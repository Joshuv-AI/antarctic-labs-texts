Spectral Analysis for Gaussian Quantum Markov Semigroups
Franco Fagnola, Zheng Li

Abstract
We investigate the spectrum of the generator induced on the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators by a Gaussian quantum Markov semigroup with a faithful normal invariant state in the general case, without any symmetry or quantum detailed balance assumptions. We prove that the eigenvalues are entirely determined by those of the drift matrix, similarly to classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups. This result is established using a quasi-derivation property of the generator. Moreover, the same spectral property holds for the adjoint of the induced generator. Finally, we show that these eigenvalues constitute the entire spectrum when the induced generator has a spectral gap.



Keywords:
Open Quantum Systems; Gaussian quantum Markov Semigroups; GKSL generators; Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Semigroups; Spectral Analysis.

1
Introduction

Quantum Markov semigroups (QMSs) are a key framework for describing the dynamics of open quantum systems. They are now widely used to model processes involving decay, decoherence, and dissipation. With the development of quantum computing and the implementation of quantum algorithms (see
[
DLL25
]
and the references therein), their importance has steadily increased. However, our understanding of their structural properties, particularly those with unbounded generators, remains surprisingly limited
[
Dav77
,
CF98
,
CFL00
,
SHW17
]
. This is also the case of spectral analysis, whose fundamental role in quantum physics is indisputable. Such analysis plays a crucial role in studying dissipative quantum evolution, where the eigenvalues of QMS generators provide comprehensive information about the dynamical properties of the open quantum system
[
BDSP24
,
Chr+21
]
.

Gaussian QMSs, sometimes called quasi-free in the literature
[
DVV79
]
, form a particularly important, perhaps the most important, subclass within this framework. They play a crucial role in quantum mechanics due to their mathematical simplicity, which allows for many explicit computations, their physical relevance, and their broad applicability in the analysis of quantum systems of bosons
[
DVV79
,
Ter27
,
BW24
]
.

A key feature of Gaussian QMSs is that they preserve Gaussian states
[
Pol22
]
: if the initial state of a quantum system is Gaussian, its evolution under these semigroups remains within the family of Gaussian states, with the mean and covariance matrix given explicitly. In the Heisenberg picture, Weyl operators are mapped to scalar multiples of Weyl operators, as shown in formula (
2.3
) below. Moreover, their generators map non-commutative polynomials in creation and annihilation operators to polynomials of the same degree (at least formally, since such polynomials are unbounded operators) and possess other useful properties. These features make this class particularly tractable.

In this paper, we consider a Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with a faithful normal invariant state
\rho
. The semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
acts on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, the algebra of all bounded linear operators on the one-mode bosonic Fock space
\mathsf{h}
. We focus on the one-mode case for clarity of exposition; however, similar results can be derived for the
n
-mode case using the same approach, albeit with more complicated computations and heavier notation. The generator of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, denoted by
\mathcal{L}
, is in the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) form
[
GKS76
,
Lin76
]
, and is given as follows:

\mathcal{L}(X)=\mathrm{i}[H,X]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}X-2L_{\ell}^{*}XL_{\ell}+XL_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right),\quad X\in\dom\mathcal{L},

(1.1)

where

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\Omega a^{\dagger}a+\frac{\kappa}{2}a^{\dagger 2}+\frac{\overline{\kappa}}{2}a^{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}a^{\dagger}+\frac{\overline{\zeta}}{2}a,

(1.2)

\displaystyle L_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\overline{v}_{\ell}a+u_{\ell}a^{\dagger},\quad\overline{v}_{\ell},u_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C},\quad\ell=1,2,

(1.3)

with parameters
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
\kappa,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
. Here,
a
and
a^{\dagger}
denote the annihilation and creation operators, respectively.

Gaussian QMSs provide a quantum analog of classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups (see
[
Fag+24
, Section 3]
). Indeed, it is well-known that a classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup can be regarded as the restriction of a Gaussian QMS, specifically one whose generator lacks a Hamiltonian part, to the algebra generated by the position operator . A detailed investigation of the spectrum of classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck generators in the
L^{2}
-space with respect to invariant measures is presented in
[
MPP02
]
. This study shows that, under suitable conditions, the spectrum of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck generator is entirely determined by the eigenvalues of the so-called “drift matrix”, which is a matrix associated with the parameters of the generator’s drift term.

In the quantum setting, as an analogue of the classical
L^{2}
space with respect to an invariant measure, we consider the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on the Fock space
\mathsf{h}
. We then define a new semigroup on this Hilbert space, denoted by
(T_{t}^{(s)})_{t\geq 0}
, as follows:

T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}):=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}(X)\rho^{(1-s)/2},\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}),

(1.4)

where
s\in[0,1]
. Throughout this paper, unless explicitly stated otherwise, the parameter
s
is assumed to range within
[0,1]
. The technique of constructing a new semigroup in this manner has a long history. In (
1.4
), the embedding
X\mapsto\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
, which maps the space of bounded operators to the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators, is known as the GNS embedding (see
[
Fri77
]
) when
s=0
, and as the KMS embedding (see
[
Cip97
]
) when
s=1/2
. Depending on the chosen embedding, the semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)})_{t\geq 0}
exhibits slightly different properties. In particular, the spectral gap may depend on
s
(see
[
Fag+24
]
).

According to
[
CF00
, Theorem 2.3]
, for any
s\in[0,1]
, the semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)})_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly continuous contraction semigroup and will be referred to as the
induced semigroup
. Its generator, denoted by
L^{(s)}
, is called the
induced generator
.

We find that the eigenvalues of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
are determined by the eigenvalues of the
drift matrix

\mathbf{Z}
, given by

\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma-\imaginary\kappa&\real\kappa-\Omega\\
\real\kappa+\Omega&-\gamma+\imaginary\kappa\end{bmatrix},

(1.5)

where

\gamma:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left(\absolutevalue{v_\ell}^{2}-\absolutevalue{u_\ell}^{2}\right).

(1.6)

The matrix
\mathbf{Z}
is called the drift matrix because its exponential changes the argument of Weyl operators, on which the explicit action of the Gaussian QMS is given by (
2.3
). In physical terms, it acts as a translation.

If
\lambda,\mu
are two distinct eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Z}
, then they are also eigenvalues of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
. This follows from the fact that the restriction of
L^{(s)}
to the two-dimensional subspace spanned by
\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2}
and
\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is similar to
\mathbf{Z}
. These eigenvalues are referred to as
base eigenvalues
because they serve as the building blocks for constructing other eigenvalues. Moreover, the eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
are completely characterized by the set

\left\{n\lambda+m\mu:n,m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

(1.7)

This extends the analysis of the spectrum of a GKLS generator
\mathcal{L}
(see, e.g.,
[
MGKC25
]
) to an infinite-dimensional framework. A key ingredient in the computation of eigenvalues is the
quasi-derivation property
of the generators of Gaussian QMSs. Specifically, if
X
and
Y
are two polynomials in the annihilation operator
a
and the creation operator
a^{\dagger}
, then we have

\mathcal{L}(XY)=X\mathcal{L}(Y)+\mathcal{L}(X)Y+\text{l.o.t.},

(1.8)

where
l.o.t.
denotes a polynomial of lower degree in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. We emphasize that this algebraic formula will be made rigorous when used in the spectral analysis of the induced generator.

In the classical case, the adjoint operator of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck generators, known as Fokker-Planck generators, shares the same spectral properties as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operators. For example, see
[
AEW18
, Theorem 3.1]
. In the quantum case, we will show that the eigenvalues of the adjoint operator of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
, denoted by
L^{(s)*}
, can also be fully described by a set of the form given in (
1.7
). The main reason for this phenomenon is that the adjoint semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)*})_{t\geq 0}
is the induced semigroup of the
dual QMS

(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
. The dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is also Gaussian, allowing us to use its quasi-derivation property to study the eigenvalues of
L^{(s)*}
, just as we did for
L^{(s)}
. We recall that the dual QMS is constructed from
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
through the following duality relation (see
[
AHK78
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
FU10
, Theorem 1]
):

\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}Y\right)=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}(Y)\right),\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

(1.9)

Moreover, the generator of the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.

Significant challenges arise when attempting to prove that the spectra of
L^{(s)}
and
L^{(s)*}
consist only of the aforementioned eigenvalues. On the one hand, the induced generator
L^{(s)}
is generally not a symmetric operator. In
[
CFL00
]
, the spectrum of a very specific self-adjoint induced generator was investigated. On the other hand, in the classical setting, the compactness of the semigroup can often be deduced from Sobolev compact embedding theorems, which are well-established in both finite- and infinite-dimensional spaces
[
MPP02
,
DPZ95
]
. Unfortunately, quantum analogues of these theorems remain incomplete, necessitating alternative approaches. To address this, we employ the following inequality relating
L^{(s)}
to
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
:

\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{-2}\leq\lambda^{-1}\left(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})\right)^{-1},\quad\lambda>0,

(1.10)

and demonstrate that
L^{(s)}
has a compact resolvent when
L^{(s)}
possesses a spectral gap. This implies that the eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
constitute its entire spectrum. The same result applies to the adjoint generator
L^{(s)*}
.

We recall that for a maximally dissipative operator
L
, its
spectral gap
is defined as follows:

\gap L:=\inf\left\{-\real\left\langle x,Lx\right\rangle:x\in\dom L,~\norm{x}=1,~x\in(\ker L)^{\perp}\right\}.

(1.11)

We say that
L
has a spectral gap if
\gap L>0
.

The paper is structured as follows:

We begin in Section
2
by introducing the necessary notation and reviewing the main properties of Gaussian states and Gaussian QMSs. In particular, the details of the quasi-derivation property, i.e., (
1.8
), are provided in Theorem
2.7
.

In Section
3
, we first outline the assumptions required for the subsequent analysis. Next, we investigate the eigenvalues of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
. Specifically, we establish that the eigenvalues of the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
are also eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
, referred to as the base eigenvalues (Theorem

). Additionally, we construct other eigenvalues of

. Moreover, the adjoint semigroup
(T_{t}^{*})_{t\geq 0}

(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
, which is also Gaussian. Therefore, using the same strategy, we deduce that the eigenvalues of
L^{(s)*}
are the complex conjugates of the eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
(Theorem
3.8
).

In Section
4
, we focus on the cases of KMS embedding (
s=1/2
) and GNS embedding (
s=0
), since these embeddings are the most natural and well-studied methods for mapping bounded operators to Hilbert-Schmidt operators with respect to the Gaussian invariant normal state
\rho
.

For the KMS embedding, the operator
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
retains the quasi-derivation property, which allows us to determine all its additional eigenvalues directly from its base eigenvalues (Theorem
4.5
).

For the GNS embedding, although the operator
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
does not appear to have the quasi-derivation property, we can still use the identity
\mathcal{L}_{*}(\rho)=0
, where
\mathcal{L}_{*}
denotes the generator of the

, to significantly simplify the explicit form of

on certain operators. Consequently, we are able to find the eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
by its base eigenvalues (Theorem
4.11
).

Finally, we demonstrate that the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
has a compact resolvent if and only if the induced generator
L^{(s)}
possesses a spectral gap (Theorem
4.13
). Using (
1.10
), we establish that when
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap, it also has a compact resolvent. This implies that the spectrum of
L^{(s)}
consists entirely of the eigenvalues we have identified. The same conclusion applies to the adjoint generator
L^{(s)*}
. However, it remains unclear whether the spectrum of
L^{(s)}
is solely composed of eigenvalues when it does not have a spectral gap. This question remains an open problem.

In Section
5
, we present the proofs of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
. The main focus of this section is to make sense of algebraic computations involving polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
when they are embedded into the Hilbert-Schmidt space, and to explain how to work with the induced generator
L^{(s)}
. The key idea is to start from the explicit action of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on Weyl operators, and then derive the corresponding action of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
on expressions of the form
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
, where
X
is a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. We stress that if
X
is such a polynomial, then
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
defines a Hilbert–Schmidt operator. Moreover, both
\rho^{s}X\rho^{-s}
and

remain polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, where

denotes the Weyl operator associated with the complex number

.

2
Gaussian States and Gaussian QMSs

We consider the one-mode Boson Fock space
\mathsf{h}:=\Gamma(\mathbb{C})
, with the canonical orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. The inner product on the space
\mathsf{h}
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Two dense subspaces of
\mathsf{h}
are frequently used later. The first is the linear span of the canonical orthonormal basis, denoted by
D:=\myspan\{(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\}
. The second is the linear span of all exponential vectors, denoted by
\mathcal{E}
. For a complex number
f\in\mathbb{C}
, the exponential vector
e(f)\in\mathsf{h}
is defined as

e(f):=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{f^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}e_{k}.

We consider the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, which is the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\mathsf{h}
. Additionally, we denote the space of trace-class operators on
\mathsf{h}
by
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathsf{h})
and the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
\mathsf{h}
by
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. The inner product on the space
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
. By convention, we use
a
to denote the annihilation operator and
a^{\dagger}
to denote the creation operator. These operators are defined on
\mathsf{h}
as follows:

ae_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,e_{n-1},\quad n\geq 1,\quad ae_{0}=0;\quad a^{\dagger}e_{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\,e_{n+1},\quad n\geq 0.

The number operator is defined as
N:=a^{\dagger}a
, the momentum operator as
p:=\mathrm{i}(a^{\dagger}-a)/\sqrt{2}
, and the position operator as
q:=(a^{\dagger}+a)/\sqrt{2}
. Furthermore, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the expression
\exp{z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a}
defines the unitary Weyl operator on
\mathsf{h}
, denoted by
W(z)
. Weyl operators play a fundamental role in the study of Gaussian QMSs.

Definition 2.1
.

A normal state
\rho

if there exists
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
and a real-linear, symmetric, invertible operator

\Tr(\rho W(z))=\exp{ - \frac{1}{2} \Re\langle z, S z \rangle- \mi\Re\langle\omega, z \rangle},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}.

Definition 2.2
.

A QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
is said to be
Gaussian
if its predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
maps Gaussian states to Gaussian states.

We recall that the predual semigroup of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, denoted by
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
, is defined by the following relation:

\Tr\left(\mathcal{T}_{*t}(\psi)X\right)=\Tr\left(\psi\mathcal{T}_{t}(X)\right),\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathsf{h}),\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Gaussian QMS on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
. Then, its generator
\mathcal{L}
is given by (

). The generator of its predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
, denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{*}
, is given by

\mathcal{L}_{*}(\psi)=-\mathrm{i}[H,\psi]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\psi-2L_{\ell}\psi L_{\ell}^{*}+\psi L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right),\quad\psi\in\dom\mathcal{L}_{*},

(2.1)

where operators

are given by (

) and (

)

The following theorem specifies the parameters, appearing in the Hamiltonian part (

) and the noise part (
1.3
) of the generator of the Gaussian QMS, for which a normal invariant state can be found.

Theorem 2.3
.

A normal invariant state
\rho
for
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
exists if and only if

\gamma>0,\quad\gamma^{2}+\Omega^{2}-\absolutevalue{\kappa}^{2}>0,

where

). Moreover,

.

The following theorem explicitly describes the action of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the Weyl operator
W(z)
. In this context, the real-linear operator
Z
appears and it plays a central role in the spectral analysis of Gaussian QMSs.

Theorem 2.4
.

The action of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on

is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}(W(z))

\displaystyle=\phi_{t}(z)W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(z)

\displaystyle:=\exp{ -\frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \Re{\overline{\me^{s Z} z} C \me^{s Z}z} \md s + \mi\int_0^t \Re{\overline{\zeta} \me^{s Z} z} \md s},

where

and

Zz=\left(\mathrm{i}\Omega-\gamma\right)z+\mathrm{i}\kappa\overline{z},\quad Cz=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}\left((\absolutevalue{u_\ell}^{2}+\absolutevalue{v_\ell}^{2})z+2v_{\ell}u_{\ell}\overline{z}\right).

(2.4)

Remark 2.5
.

The identification of the real-linear operator

) (see Appendix

.

In what follows, we introduce some useful algebraic properties of the generator of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. The next lemma follows directly from Theorem
C.1
and Lemma
B.1
. Additionally, Theorem
2.7
establishes a property that we call the

of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. In the following,
\deg
denotes the degree of a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, with degree
n
in
a^{\dagger}
and degree
m
in
a
. Then,
\mathcal{L}(X)
, as algebraically defined in (
1.1
), remains a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, and

\deg(\mathcal{L}(X))\leq m+n.

Proof.

By Theorem
C.1
, it suffices to prove the statement for the Wick normal-ordered product
a^{\dagger n}a^{m}
, which has degree
n
in
a^{\dagger}
and degree
m
in
a
.

We first note that the generator (
1.1
) can be equivalently expressed as

\mathcal{L}(X)=\mathrm{i}[H,X]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left([X,L_{\ell}^{*}]L_{\ell}-L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]\right).

(2.5)

Thus, we only need to compute
\mathcal{L}(a^{\dagger n}a^{m})
, which is straightforward but tedious. As an example, we compute
[X,L_{\ell}^{*}]
. When

, Lemma

\displaystyle[a^{\dagger n}a^{m},L_{\ell}^{*}]

\displaystyle=[a^{\dagger n}a^{m},v_{\ell}a^{\dagger}+\overline{u_{\ell}}a]

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger n}[a^{m},v_{\ell}a^{\dagger}+\overline{u_{\ell}}a]+[a^{\dagger n},v_{\ell}a^{\dagger}+\overline{u_{\ell}}a]a^{m}

\displaystyle=mv_{\ell}a^{\dagger n}a^{m-1}-n\overline{u_{\ell}}(a^{\dagger})^{n-1}a^{m},

so

is a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
and

\deg\left([X,L_{\ell}^{*}]L_{\ell}\right)\leq m+n.

Similar computations apply to
L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]
and

, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
X,Y
be polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,

\mathcal{L}(XY)=X\mathcal{L}(Y)+\mathcal{L}(X)Y+\sum_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},Y],

(2.7)

where
\sum_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},Y]
is a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
satisfying

\deg\left(\sum_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},Y]\right)\leq\deg(XY).

(2.8)

Proof.

Using (
2.5
) we compute:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(XY)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}HXY-\mathrm{i}XYH

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left(X[Y,L_{\ell}^{*}]L_{\ell}+[X,L_{\ell}^{*}]YL_{\ell}-L_{\ell}^{*}X[Y,L_{\ell}]-L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]Y\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}X[H,Y]+\mathrm{i}[H,X]Y

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left(X[Y,L_{\ell}^{*}]L_{\ell}-XL^{*}_{\ell}[Y,L_{\ell}]+[X,L_{\ell}^{*}]L_{\ell}Y-L^{*}_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}]Y\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell}\left(XL_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}Y-XL_{\ell}^{*}YL_{\ell}-L_{\ell}^{*}XL_{\ell}Y+L_{\ell}^{*}XYL_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=X\mathcal{L}(Y)+\mathcal{L}(X)Y+\sum_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},Y],

which completes the proof of (
2.7
).

By Theorem
C.1
and equation (
2.6
), we immediately obtain

\deg\left([X,L_{\ell}^{*}]\right)\leq\deg\left(X\right),\quad\deg\left([L_{\ell},Y]\right)\leq\deg\left(Y\right),

which implies (
2.8
).
∎

3
Eigenvalues of Induced Generator
L^{(s)}
and Its Adjoint Operator
L^{(s)*}

Before discussing the eigenvalues of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
, we introduce the following hypotheses, which will hold throughout the paper.

Hypothesis 1
.

The drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
(
1.5
) is stable.

Note that a matrix is said to be
stable
if the real parts of all its eigenvalues are strictly negative.

It is straightforward to verify that
\mathbf{Z}
is stable if and only if (
2.2
) holds. By Theorem
2.3
, there exists a normal invariant state
\rho
for the QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Furthermore,
\rho
is a Gaussian state and is the unique normal invariant state of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

In the following hypothesis, we require
\rho
to be faithful as well. A detailed discussion of faithfulness is provided in
[
AFP21
, Proposition 5]
. For most parameter choices, faithfulness can be ensured.

Hypothesis 2
.

The normal invariant state
\rho
is also faithful.

Moreover, we assume that the Gaussian state
\rho
is diagonal with respect to the canonical orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. This assumption is adopted for clarity of exposition, as it significantly simplifies the computations, and will be removed in Appendix
D

, the state

is diagonal with respect to the canonical orthonormal basis

if and only if the parameters

satisfy the following conditions:

\zeta=0,\quad\kappa=\mathrm{i}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell}u_{\ell}v_{\ell}\right),

(3.1)

where

is given by

\beta:=-\log\left(\frac{\sum_{\ell}\absolutevalue{u_\ell}^{2}}{\sum_{\ell}\absolutevalue{v_\ell}^{2}}\right).

(3.2)

In this way,
\rho
can be simply expressed as

\rho=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})\mathrm{e}^{-\beta N}.

Given a QMS

by (

Theorem 3.1
.

The eigenvalues of the drift matrix

are eigenvalues of the induced generator

. Moreover, the corresponding eigenvectors take the form
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
, where

is a first-order polynomial in
a
and

.

We are now in a position to demonstrate how to determine the remaining eigenvalues of

Theorem 3.2

Let

\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}

We emphasize

Lemma 3.5

The generator of

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X)=\mathrm{i}[H^{\prime},X]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left(L_{\ell}^{\prime*}L_{\ell}^{\prime}X-2L_{\ell}^{\prime*}XL_{\ell}^{\prime}+XL_{\ell}^{\prime*}L_{\ell}^{\prime}\right),\quad X\in\dom\mathcal{L}^{\prime},

Proof.

Note that (

\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}Y\right)=\Tr\left(\mathcal{T}_{*t}(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2})Y\right),\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}),

\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}=\mathcal{T}_{*t}(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}),\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

Moreover, if

\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathsf{h})

\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}=\mathcal{L}_{*}(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}).

Defining

\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}=G\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}+\sum_{\ell}L_{\ell}\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}L_{\ell}^{*}+\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}G^{*},

and

\displaystyle G^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}{2}(\mathrm{i}\kappa-\sum_{\ell}u_{\ell}v_{\ell})a^{\dagger 2}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta}}{2}(\mathrm{i}\overline{\kappa}-\sum_{\ell}\overline{u_{\ell}}\overline{v_{\ell}})a^{2},

The drift matrix of

\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma-\imaginary\kappa&\real\kappa+\Omega\\

Consequently,

\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Z}^{*},

where

denotes the conjugate transpose of the drift matrix of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Proof.

Let us first compare (
3.14
) with (

) and (

). From

to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, the parameter
\Omega
changes to

remains unchanged. The parameter

(T_{t}^{(s)})_{t\geq 0}

, with respect to embedding parameter

Lemma 3.7
.

We have

T_{t}^{(s)*}\left(\rho^{(1-s)/2}X\rho^{s/2}\right)=\rho^{(1-s)/2}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{t}(X)\rho^{s/2},\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

Proof.

Let

. Then, we have

\displaystyle\quad\left\langle\rho^{(1-s)/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X^{*})\rho^{s/2},\rho^{s/2}Y\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\rho^{s/2}Y\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}Y\right)=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}(Y)\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}Y\rho^{(1-s)/2})\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle T_{t}^{(s)*}(\rho^{(1-s)/2}X^{*}\rho^{s/2}),\rho^{s/2}Y\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right\rangle_{2},

Since
\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Therefore, from Lemma

and Theorem

Theorem 3.8

The eigenvalues of the adjoint generator

4
Spectrum of the Operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}

In this section, we aim to demonstrate that the eigenvalues identified for
L^{(s)}
and
L^{(s)*}
constitute their entire spectra. To this end, it suffices to prove that
L^{(s)*}
and
L^{(s)}
possess compact resolvents. Specifically, we show that the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
has a compact resolvent and then utilize the aforementioned inequality (
1.10
). Clearly, the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
is at least symmetric on a certain domain, which simplifies its analysis.

In general, although
L^{(s)}
generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup, the intersection of the domains of
L^{(s)*}
and
L^{(s)}
can be as small as

(see

]
for details). However, this is not the case here, as the induced generator
L^{(s)}
shares a sufficiently large domain with its adjoint
L^{(s)*}
.

We define the subspace
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
as follows:

\mathcal{X}:=\left\{\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}:X\text{~polynomial in~}a\textrm{~and~}a^{\dagger}\right\}.

Note that the definition of
\mathcal{X}
above is independent of the choice of the embedding parameter
s
. Furthermore, Theorem
3.2
ensures that
\mathcal{X}
is contained in the domain of both
L^{(s)*}
and
L^{(s)}
. Thus, the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
is naturally defined on the subspace
\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma
4.1
demonstrates that
\mathcal{X}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
, and Lemma
4.3
establishes that
\mathcal{X}
serves as a common core for both
L^{(s)*}
and
L^{(s)}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

\mathcal{X}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
be a vector such that
v\perp\mathcal{X}
. By Lemma
5.6
we know that
v\perp\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. On the other hand, by the celebrated von Neumann density theorem (see
[
BR12
, Corollary 2.4.15]
), the linear span of Weyl operators is
\sigma
-weakly dense in
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
. So, the linear span of Weyl operators, left-multiplied
\rho^{s/2}
and right-multiplied by
\rho^{(1-s)/2}
, is dense in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. Thus,
v=0
, and we conclude that
\mathcal{X}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

By Corollary
C.2
, we know that if
X
and
Y
are first-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, then the collection

\left(\mathds{1},X,Y,X^{2},XY,Y^{2},X^{3},X^{2}Y,XY^{2},Y^{3},\cdots\right)

(4.1)

forms a basis for the vector space of polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Moreover, if we embed (
4.1
) into the space
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
via the
s
-embedding, as in (
3.11
), then, by Lemma
4.1
, we can obtain an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
through the Gram-Schmidt process. Therefore, we conclude that the eigenvalues identified in Theorem
3.3
are indeed all the eigenvalues of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
. The same conclusion applies to the adjoint generator
L^{(s)*}
, whose eigenvalues are given in Theorem
3.8
.

Lemma 4.3
.

\mathcal{X}
is a common core for
L^{(s)}
and
L^{(s)*}
.

Proof.

As noted in Remark
4.2
, the (generalized) eigenvectors of
L^{(s)}
provide a sufficient number of invariant subspaces to apply
[
Jr76
, Theorem 2]
. Consequently,
\mathcal{X}
serves as a core for
L^{(s)}
. The same conclusion applies to
L^{(s)*}
.
∎

Next, we will examine the eigenvalues of the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, which involves understanding its explicit action on certain operators. In this section, we focus exclusively on the KMS embedding (
s=1/2
) and the GNS embedding (
s=0
). For other embedding parameters
s
, the explicit action of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
does not admit a concise form.

It is worth noting that, unlike the induced generator
L^{(s)}
, the eigenvalue
0
can serve as a base eigenvalue of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, as will be demonstrated later.

4.1
Eigenvalues of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
: KMS Embedding

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. By Theorem
3.2
and Lemma
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle L^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2})

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(X)\rho^{(1-s)/2},

(4.2)

\displaystyle L^{(s)*}(\rho^{(1-s)/2}X\rho^{s/2})

\displaystyle=\rho^{(1-s)/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{s/2}.

(4.3)

Here,
\mathcal{L}
is the generator of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is the generator of the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
.

If we consider the KMS embedding, i.e., when
s=1/2
, (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) imply the following important identity:

(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}X\rho^{1/4})=\rho^{1/4}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X)+\mathcal{L}(X))\rho^{1/4}.

(4.4)

As we will demonstrate imminently, (
4.4
) ensures that the quasi-derivation property remains valid for the operator
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
. Consequently, we can deduce the other eigenvalues of
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
from its base eigenvalues by following the method described in the proof of Theorem
3.3
. Furthermore, the base eigenvalues of
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
can be determined directly from the following results, which are immediate consequences of (
4.4
).

Lemma 4.4
.

We have

\displaystyle(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}a\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}(-2\gamma a-2\mathrm{i}\kappa a^{\dagger})\rho^{1/4},

(4.5)

\displaystyle(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}a^{\dagger}\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}(-2\gamma a^{\dagger}+2\mathrm{i}\overline{\kappa}a)\rho^{1/4}.

(4.6)

Then, if we consider the restriction of the operator
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
to the subspace spanned by the orthonormal vectors

\sqrt{2\sinh(\beta/2)}\,\rho^{1/4}a\rho^{1/4},\quad\sqrt{2\sinh(\beta/2)}\,\rho^{1/4}a^{\dagger}\rho^{1/4},

its matrix representation is given by:

\begin{bmatrix}-2\gamma&2\mathrm{i}\overline{\kappa}\\
-2\mathrm{i}\kappa&-2\gamma\end{bmatrix}.

(4.7)

The eigenvalues of the matrix above are
\lambda=-2\gamma+2\absolutevalue{\kappa}
and
\mu=-2\gamma-2\absolutevalue{\kappa}
, which are guaranteed to be non-positive since both
L^{(1/2)*}
and
L^{(1/2)}
are dissipative. Note that
\lambda
and
\mu
cannot be equal to
0
simultaneously.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be first-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
such that

\displaystyle(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}X\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\lambda\rho^{1/4}X\rho^{1/4}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\quad(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}Y\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\mu\rho^{1/4}Y\rho^{1/4},

(4.9)

with
\mu\leq\lambda\leq 0
, and such that
\mu
and
\lambda
are not simultaneously equal to
0
. For all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=(n\lambda+m\mu)\rho^{1/4}X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle\quad+\rho^{1/4}R_{n,m}\rho^{1/4},

(4.10)

where
R_{n,m}\in\myspan\{X^{i}Y^{j}:i+j\leq n+m-2\}
for
n+m\geq 2
or
R_{n,m}=0
otherwise. Moreover,
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
are the eigenvalues of
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
.

Proof.

Note that the existence of embedded polynomials satisfying (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) is guaranteed by Lemma
4.4
. Consequently, (
4.5
) holds for
(n,m)=(1,0)
and
(n,m)=(0,1)
. Assuming that (
4.5
) holds for a certain
(n,m)
, and using (
4.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\quad(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}X^{n+1}Y^{m}\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(XX^{n}Y^{m})+\mathcal{L}(XX^{n}Y^{m}))\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}(X\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X^{n}Y^{m})+\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(X)X^{n}Y^{m}+\sum_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}^{\prime*}][L_{\ell}^{\prime},X^{n}Y^{m}]

\displaystyle\quad+X\mathcal{L}(X^{n}Y^{m})+\mathcal{L}(X)X^{n}Y^{m}+\sum_{\ell}[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}])\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}X\rho^{-1/4}\cdot(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle\quad+(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}X\rho^{1/4})\cdot\rho^{-1/4}X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle\quad+\rho^{1/4}\sum_{\ell}([X,L_{\ell}^{\prime*}][L_{\ell}^{\prime},X^{n}Y^{m}]+[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}])\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}((n+1)\lambda+m\mu)X^{n+1}Y^{m}\rho^{1/4}+\rho^{1/4}XR_{n,m}\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle\quad+\rho^{1/4}\sum_{\ell}([X,L_{\ell}^{\prime*}][L_{\ell}^{\prime},X^{n}Y^{m}]+[X,L_{\ell}^{*}][L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}])\rho^{1/4}.

Similar to the case in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, we have

XR_{n,m},[L_{\ell}^{\prime},X^{n}Y^{m}],[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]\in\myspan\{X^{i}Y^{j}:i+j\leq(n+1)+m-2\}.

Therefore, considering the matrix representation of the operator
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
with respect to the independent vectors in (
3.11
), we obtain a triangular matrix with a diagonal given by
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
. This implies that
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
are eigenvalues of
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
. Furthermore, for the same reason stated in Remark
4.2
,
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
are all the eigenvalues of
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
.
∎

4.2
Eigenvalues of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
: GNS Embedding

Unlike the case of KMS embedding (
s=1/2
), for other values of the embedding parameter, we do not have a simple formula like (
4.4
) that gives
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
the quasi-derivation property. However, in the case of GNS embedding (
s=0
), we have found that the action of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
on
X\rho^{1/2}
, where
X
is an arbitrary polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, is relatively concise. This simplicity enables us to determine the remaining eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
using its base eigenvalues.

Although we know that
L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})
can be computed directly using Theorem
3.2
and Lemma
3.5
, in this section we will compute
L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})
through the predual generator
\mathcal{L}_{*}
, as defined in (
2.1
). This approach is preferred for two reasons. First, we aim to show that the adjoint semigroup
(T^{(s)*}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is closely related to the predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
. Second, as we will see later, this method allows us to use the identity
\mathcal{L}_{*}(\rho)=0
, making it much easier to derive a simplified form of
L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})
.

The following results establish the relationship between the adjoint semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)*})_{t\geq 0}
and the predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
for an arbitrary embedding parameter and address the relevant domain issues.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
s\in[0,1]
and any
Y\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
, we have

\rho^{s/2}T_{t}^{(s)*}(Y)\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\mathcal{T}_{*t}(\rho^{s/2}Y\rho^{(1-s)/2}).

Proof.

For any
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
and
Y\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
we have

\displaystyle\Tr( X^* \cT_{*t} (\rho^{s/2} Y \rho^{(1-s)/2} ) )

\displaystyle=\Tr( \rho^{(1-s)/2} \cT_t (X^*) \rho^{s/2} Y )

\displaystyle=\left\langle T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}),Y\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr( X^* \rho^{s/2} T_t^{(s)*} (Y) \rho^{(1-s)/2} ),

By the arbitrariness of
X
we conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
s\in[0,1]
and any
X
that is a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, we have

\rho^{s}X\rho^{1-s}\in\dom\mathcal{L}_{*}.

Proof.

Let
X
be an arbitrary polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. By Lemma
3.7
and Theorem
3.2
, we know that
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)*}
. Note that both the predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and the adjoint semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)*})_{t\geq 0}
are strongly continuous.

Then, by Hölder inequality

\|\rho^{s/2}Y\rho^{(1-s)/2}\|_{1}\leq\|\rho^{s/2}\|_{2/s}\|Y\|_{2}\|\rho^{(1-s)/2}\|_{2/(1-s)},\quad\forall\,Y\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h}),

with the understanding
2/s=+\infty
(resp.
2/(1-s)=+\infty
)
if
s=0
(resp.
s=1
), and by Lemma
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\norm{ \frac{\cT_{*t} (\rho^s X \rho^{1-s}) - \rho^{s} X \rho^{1-s}}{t} - \rho^{s/2} L^{(s)*} (\rho^{s/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2}) \rho^{(1-s)/2}}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\norm{ \frac{T_t^{(s)*} (\rho^{s/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2})- \rho^{s/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2}}{t} - L^{(s)*} (\rho^{s/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2}) }_{2}=0,

which implies that
\rho^{s}X\rho^{1-s}\in\dom\mathcal{L}_{*}
.
∎

We apply Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.7
, setting
s=0
, to determine the action of the adjoint generator
L^{(0)*}
on
X\rho^{1/2}
, and subsequently the action of the operator
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
on
X\rho^{1/2}
, in which the Hamiltonian
H
vanishes, as shown in (
4.14
).

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. We have

L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})=-\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left([X,L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}+2[L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}\right).

(4.11)

Proof.

By (
2.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})\rho^{1/2}=\mathcal{L}_{*}(X\rho)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho-\mathrm{i}X[H,\rho]+\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\frac{1}{2}L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}X\rho+XL_{\ell}\rho L_{\ell}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}X\rho L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right).

(4.12)

Notice
\rho
is an invariant state of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, so we have
\mathcal{L}_{*}(\rho)=0
, i.e.,

\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left(\rho L^{*}_{\ell}L_{\ell}-2L_{\ell}\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\rho,

which implies

\mathrm{i}X[H,\rho]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left(X\rho L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}-2XL_{\ell}\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}XL^{*}_{\ell}L_{\ell}\rho.

(4.13)

Substituting (
4.13
) into (
4.12
) we get

L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})\rho^{1/2}=-\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho+\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}[X,L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}]\rho\right).

Since
\rho^{1/2}
is a separating vector, we can conclude

L^{(0)*}(X\rho^{1/2})=-\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left([X,L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}+2[L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}\right),

which is exactly (
4.11
).
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. We have

(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X\rho^{1/2})=\sum_{\ell}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}+[L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}\right).

(4.14)

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
we obtain

\displaystyle L^{(0)}(X\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(X)\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell}^{*},X]L_{\ell}\rho^{1/2}+L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}\right).

Along with Proposition
4.8
one gets

\displaystyle\quad(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell}^{*},X]L_{\ell}\rho^{1/2}+L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{i}[H,X]\rho^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\left([X,L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}+2[L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}+[L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

The computations for the base eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
are straightforward by Proposition
4.9
and (
3.1
).

Lemma 4.10
.

We have

\displaystyle(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(a\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=-2\gamma a\rho^{1/2}-\mathrm{i}\kappa(1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})a^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2},

(4.15)

\displaystyle(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(a^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=-2\gamma a^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2}+\mathrm{i}\overline{\kappa}(\mathrm{e}^{\beta}+1)a\rho^{1/2}.

(4.16)

Then, if we consider the restriction of the operator
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
to the subspace spanned by the orthonormal vectors

\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{\beta}-1}\,a\rho^{1/2},\quad\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}\,a^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2},

its matrix representation is given by:

\begin{bmatrix}-2\gamma&2\cosh(\beta/2)\mathrm{i}\overline{\kappa}\\
-2\cosh(\beta/2)\mathrm{i}\kappa&-2\gamma\end{bmatrix},

(4.17)

whose eigenvalues are
\lambda=-2\gamma+2\cosh(\beta/2)\absolutevalue{\kappa}
and
\mu=-2\gamma-2\cosh(\beta/2)\absolutevalue{\kappa}
. These eigenvalues are non-positive since both
L^{(0)*}
and
L^{(0)}
are dissipative.

Then, we show that, although the operator
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
does not exhibit an obvious quasi-derivation property, we can still use (
4.14
) to determine other eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
by its base eigenvalues.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
X
and
Y
be first-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
such that

(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X\rho^{1/2})=\lambda X\rho^{1/2},\quad(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(Y\rho^{1/2})=\mu Y\rho^{1/2},

(4.18)

with
\mu\leq\lambda\leq 0
, and such that
\mu
and
\lambda
are not simultaneously equal to
0
. For all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2})=(n\lambda+m\mu)X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2}+R_{n,m}\rho^{1/2},

(4.19)

where
R_{n,m}\in\myspan\{X^{i}Y^{j}:i+j\leq n+m-2\}
for
n+m\geq 2
or
R_{n,m}=0
otherwise. Moreover,
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
are the eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.9
and (
4.18
) one gets

\displaystyle\lambda X\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-L_{\ell}^{*}[X,L_{\ell}]\rho^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-[L_{\ell},X]L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{1/2}-[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X]]\rho^{1/2}\right)

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell},X]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-[L_{\ell},X]L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{1/2}\right).

(4.21)

Note that the term
[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X]]
in (
4.20
) equals to
0
because
[L_{\ell},X]
is a multiple of the identity operator
\mathds{1}
. Similarly, we have

\mu Y\rho^{1/2}=\sum_{\ell}\left([L_{\ell},Y]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-[L_{\ell},Y]L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{1/2}\right).

(4.22)

By Proposition
4.9
and Lemma
B.4
we have

\displaystyle\quad(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}([L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-L_{\ell}^{*}[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}([L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}(X^{n}[L_{\ell},Y^{m}]+[L_{\ell},X^{n}]Y^{m})(\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(mX^{n}Y^{m-1}[L_{\ell},Y]+nX^{n-1}Y^{m}[L_{\ell},X])\cdot\sum_{\ell}(\rho L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{-1/2}-L_{\ell}^{*}\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}.

By (
4.21
) and (
4.22
), the above expression becomes

\displaystyle\quad(L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)})(X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2})

(4.23)

\displaystyle=m\mu X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2}+n\lambda X^{n-1}Y^{m}X\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=m\mu X^{n}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2}+n\lambda X^{n-1}\left([Y^{m},X]+XY^{m}\right)\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(n\lambda+m\mu)X^{n}Y^{m}+n\lambda X^{n-1}[Y^{m},X]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}.

(4.24)

We then prove that the tail part in (
4.24
) lies in
\myspan\{X^{i}Y^{j}:i+j\leq n+m-2\}
. Further computations yield the following result:

\displaystyle n\lambda X^{n-1}[Y^{m},X]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=nm\lambda[Y,X]X^{n-1}Y^{m-1}\rho^{1/2},

\displaystyle[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=[L_{\ell}^{*},X^{n}[L_{\ell},Y^{m}]+[L_{\ell},X^{n}]Y^{m}]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=m[L_{\ell},Y][L_{\ell}^{*},X^{n}Y^{m-1}]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+n[L_{\ell},X][L_{\ell}^{*},X^{n-1}Y^{m}]\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=m(m-1)[L_{\ell},Y][L_{\ell}^{*},Y]X^{n}Y^{m-2}\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+mn[L_{\ell},Y][L_{\ell}^{*},X]X^{n-1}Y^{m-1}\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+nm[L_{\ell},X][L_{\ell}^{*},Y]X^{n-1}Y^{m-1}\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+n(n-1)[L_{\ell},X][L_{\ell}^{*},X]X^{n-2}Y^{m}\rho^{1/2}.

By defining

R_{n,m}:=n\lambda X^{n-1}[Y^{m},X]\rho^{1/2}-\sum_{\ell}[L_{\ell}^{*},[L_{\ell},X^{n}Y^{m}]]\rho^{1/2},

we complete the proof of (
4.19
).

By (
4.19
), the matrix expression of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
with respect to the linearly independent vectors in (
3.11
), is a triangular matrix whose diagonal entries are given by
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore,
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
are eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
. Furthermore, for the same reason stated in Remark
4.2
,
(n\lambda+m\mu)_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
are all the eigenvalues of
L^{(0)*}+L^{(0)}
.
∎

4.3
Spectral Gap of
L^{(s)}
and
L^{(s)*}
, and Compact Resolvent of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}

As shown in the proof of Theorem
4.5
for
s=1/2
and Theorem
4.11
for
s=0
, we have identified sufficient many eigenvectors of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
to generate an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. This leads to the following result:

Lemma 4.12
.

L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
with domain
\mathcal{X}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.5
for
s=1/2
and Theorem
4.11
for
s=0
we know that the eigenvectors of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. Since these eigenvectors are automatically analytical vectors for
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, we invoke the celebrated Nelson’s analytical vector theorem (see, for example,
[
RS75
, Theorem X.39, p. 202]
), which implies that
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
is essentially self-adjoint.
∎

From this point onward, we consider the closure of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, while continuing to denote it by the same symbol. Consequently,
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
is now a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
.

Theorem 4.13
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap.

2.

L^{(s)*}
has a spectral gap.

3.

0
is a simple eigenvalue of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
.

4.

L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
has a compact resolvent.

Proof.

(
1
) implies (
2
). If the induced generator
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap, then the semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)}\restriction\ker(L^{(s)})^{\perp})_{t\geq 0}
is exponentially stable. Therefore, its adjoint semigroup,
(T_{t}^{(s)*}\restriction\ker(L^{(s)})^{\perp})_{t\geq 0}
, is also exponentially stable, which implies that the adjoint generator
L^{(s)*}
has a spectral gap. Similarly, we can prove (
2
) implies (
1
).

(
1
) implies (
3
). By (
1.11
) and Lemma
4.3
we have

\displaystyle\gap L^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{-\real\langle x,L^{(s)}x\rangle_{2}:x\in\dom L^{(s)},\norm{x}_{2}=1,x\perp\rho^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\langle x,(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})x\rangle_{2}:x\in\mathcal{X},\norm{x}_{2}=1,x\perp\rho^{1/2}\right\}.

(4.25)

On the other hand, note that there exists an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
consisting of eigenvectors of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, denoted by
(\widetilde{e}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, with corresponding eigenvalues
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. In particular,
\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}=0
and
\widetilde{e}_{0}=\rho^{1/2}
. Now suppose that
0
is not a simple eigenvalue of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
. Then, there exists another eigenvector
\widetilde{e}_{m}\perp\rho^{1/2}
for some
m\geq 1
such that
(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})(\widetilde{e}_{m})=0
. By (
4.25
) we have
\gap L^{(s)}\leq-\langle\widetilde{e}_{m},(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})\widetilde{e}_{m}\rangle_{2}/2=0
, which shows that
L^{(s)}
does not have a spectral gap.

Conversely, (
3
) implies (
1
). Suppose that
0
is a simple eigenvalue of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
. Then
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}<0
for all
k\geq 1
. Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
satisfy
\norm{x}_{2}=1
and
x\perp\rho^{1/2}
. Then
x
admits an expansion
x=\sum_{k\geq 0}x_{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}
with
\sum_{k\geq 0}\absolutevalue{x_k}^{2}=1
and
x_{0}=0
. Therefore,

\langle x,(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})x\rangle_{2}=\langle\sum_{j\geq 0}x_{j}\widetilde{e}_{j},(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})\sum_{k\geq 0}x_{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}\rangle_{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}\absolutevalue{x_k}^{2}.

(4.26)

By (
4.25
) and (
4.26
), it follows that
\gap L^{(s)}>0
.

(
3
) implies (
4
). If
0
is a simple eigenvalue of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, then both base eigenvalues of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
are strictly negative. By Theorem
4.5
for
s=1/2
, or Theorem
4.11
for
s=0
, all eigenspaces of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
is finite-dimensional in this case. Therefore, the resolvent of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
can be approximated by finite-rank spectral projections (see, for example,
[
Sch12
, Proposition 5.12]
), which implies that
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
has a compact resolvent.

Finally, (
4
) implies (
3
). If
0
is not a simple eigenvalue of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, then one of its base eigenvalues must be
0
. By Theorem
4.5
for
s=1/2
, or Theorem
4.11
for
s=0
, all eigenspaces of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
are infinite-dimensional. Consequently,
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
cannot have a compact resolvent.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

From (
4.25
) and (
4.26
), we observe that the spectral gap of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
is determined by the smallest base eigenvalue of the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
. By (
4.7
) and (
4.17
), we see that for a fixed choice of parameters
\Omega,\kappa,u_{\ell},v_{\ell}
, the following inequality always holds:

\gap L^{(1/2)}\geq\gap L^{(0)},

i.e., the spectral gap in the KMS embedding is always greater than or equal to the spectral gap in the GNS embedding. This follows from the elementary inequality
\cosh(\beta/2)>1
for all
\beta>0
. This result confirms the conjecture stated in
[
Fag+24
, Section 7]
, at least for the case of a diagonal
\rho
.

In the final step, we show that the entire spectrum of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
is determined by the eigenvalues identified in Theorem
3.3
, provided that
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap. By the same reasoning, the result also applies to the adjoint generator
L^{(s)*}
.

Theorem 4.15
.

If the induced generator
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap, then it has a compact resolvent.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be an arbitrary positive number. We know that
\lambda
belongs to the resolvent set of
L^{(s)}
,
L^{(s)*}
, and
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
.

From Lemma
4.12
, we know that
\mathcal{X}
is a core for the self-adjoint operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, and therefore, a form core for
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
(see
[
RS80
, Problem 16, p. 314]
), and consequently, a form core for
\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})
.

On the other hand, by Lemma
4.3
,
\mathcal{X}
is a core for
L^{(s)}
, and thus also a core for
\lambda\mathds{1}-L^{(s)}
. Furthermore, by
[
RS75
, Theorem X.25 and its Corollary]
, we conclude that
\mathcal{X}
is a form core for the self-adjoint operator

(\lambda\mathds{1}-L^{(s)*})(\lambda\mathds{1}-L^{(s)})=:\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{2}.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle x,\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{2}x\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle x,(\lambda^{2}\mathds{1}-\lambda(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})+L^{(s)*}L^{(s)})x\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle x,\lambda(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}))x\right\rangle_{2}+\left\langle L^{(s)}x,L^{(s)}x\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle x,\lambda(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}))x\right\rangle_{2}\geq\lambda^{2}\left\langle x,x\right\rangle_{2}.

(4.27)

By (
4.27
), we know that the form norm generated by
\lambda(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}))
is controlled by the form norm generated by
\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{2}
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is a common form core, we conclude that
\dom\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}\subset\dom\left(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})\right)^{1/2}
, and therefore,
\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{2}\geq\lambda(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}))
. Once the order relation between the self-adjoint operators is established, by
[
Sch12
, Corollary 10.12]
, we can conclude that

\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{-2}\leq\lambda^{-1}\left(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})\right)^{-1},

which is precisely the crucial inequality (
1.10
) introduced in the introduction.

Since
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap, Theorem
4.13
implies
\left(\lambda\mathds{1}-(L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)})\right)^{-1}
is compact. By the above inequality, we know
\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{-2}
is compact. Observe that
\absolutevalue{ (\lambda\1 - L^{(s)*} )^{-1} }^{2}=\absolutevalue{\lambda\1 - L^{(s)}}^{-2}
, and since
\absolutevalue{ (\lambda\1 - L^{(s)*} )^{-1} }^{2}
is compact, we have
\absolutevalue{ (\lambda\1 - L^{(s)*} )^{-1} }
is also compact as the square root of a positive compact operator. By the polar decomposition, we know that
(\lambda\mathds{1}-L^{(s)*})^{-1}
is compact. Finally,
(\lambda\mathds{1}-L^{(s)})^{-1}
is also compact as the adjoint of a compact operator.
∎

By the above theorem, we know that when the induced generator
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap, its resolvent is compact. Similarly,
L^{(s)*}
also has a compact resolvent. Consequently, the spectra of
L^{(s)}
and
L^{(s)*}
consist entirely of the discrete eigenvalues identified in Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.8
, respectively. However, when
L^{(s)}
does not have a spectral gap, it is unclear whether
L^{(s)}
has a compact resolvent, leaving this as an open problem.

5
Proofs of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2

In this section, we primarily focus on proving Theorem
3.2
, with the proof of Theorem
3.1
arising as a byproduct. Notably, if
X\in\dom\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, the expression (
5.1
) follows directly.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
X\in\dom\mathcal{L}
, then
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}
and

L^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2})=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(X)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

(5.1)

Proof.

Let
X\in\dom\mathcal{L}
and
Y\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\langle Y,\frac{T_{t}(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2})-\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{t}\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Tr\left(\rho^{(1-s)/2}Y^{*}\rho^{s/2}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(X)-X}{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{(1-s)/2}Y^{*}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(X)\right)=\left\langle Y,\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(X)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right\rangle_{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

We will demonstrate that (
5.1
) holds for a much broader class of
X
. Specifically,
X
can be any polynomial in the annihilation operator
a
and the creation operator
a^{\dagger}
. Correspondingly,
\mathcal{L}(X)
is algebraically defined as in (
1.1
).

5.1
Weyl Operators and Induced Generator
L^{(s)}

We show that, when
\rho^{s/2}
is left-multiplied and
\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is right-multiplied, polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
become Hilbert-Schmidt operators, and the Weyl groups
(W(rz))_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
become differentiable with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, remaining within the domain of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
.

We begin with some elementary results that will be extensively used throughout this paper.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider
X
as a monomial in
a^{\dagger}
and
a
, where the degree of
a^{\dagger}
is
m
and the degree of
a
is
n
, with
m,n\geq 0
. The operator
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is well-defined on the dense subspace
D\subset\mathsf{h}
. Moreover, there exists
\ell\leq n
and
P
, a polynomial function of degree
m+n
, such that

Xe_{k}=0,\quad\forall k<\ell;\quad Xe_{k}=\sqrt{P(k)}e_{k+m-n},\quad\forall k\geq\ell.

(5.2)

Consider
x\in D
with
\norm{x}=1
, meaning there exists
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{C}
with only a finite number of non-zero elements such that
x=\sum_{k\geq 0}x_{k}e_{k}
and
\sum_{k\geq 0}\absolutevalue{x_k}^{2}=1
. Then, by (
5.2
) we have

\displaystyle\quad\norm{\rho^{s/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2} x}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}\norm{\me^{-s \beta N / 2} X \me^{-(1-s)\beta N /2} \left( \sum_{k = 0}^\infty x_k e_k \right) }

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}\norm{ \sum_{k=\ell}^\infty x_k \me^{-s \beta( k + m - n) / 2} \sqrt{P(k)} \me^{-(1-s) \beta k /2} e_{k+m-n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta(m-n)/2}\sum_{k=\ell}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{\me^{- \beta k / 2} \sqrt{P(k)} \cdot x_k}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta(m-n)/2}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta k}P(k)\right)^{1/2}<+\infty,

indicating that
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
extends to a bounded operator on
\mathsf{h}
. Similarly, we can compute that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\norm{\rho^{s/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k }^{2}=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta(m-n)}\sum_{k=\ell}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{\me^{-\beta k} P (k)}<+\infty,

which implies that
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, and let
0<s\leq 1
. Since
\rho
is a Gaussian state, it is easy to see that
\rho^{s}
is a trace-class operator. Moreover, the proof of Lemma
5.2
shows that, in fact, the operator
X\rho^{s}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. Consequently,
X\rho^{s}
is also a trace-class operator, as it can be written as
X\rho^{s/2}\cdot\rho^{s/2}
. Similarly, using the same approach, the operator
\rho^{s}X
is also a trace-class operator.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, and let
s\in[-1/2,1/2]
. Then,
\rho^{s}X\rho^{-s}
is also a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the statement holds for monomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Let
X
be a monomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. For
s=0
, it is clear that
\rho^{s}X\rho^{-s}
is a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. For
0<s\leq 1/2
, we first show the existence of another polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, denoted by
\widehat{X}
, such that

\rho^{s}X=\widehat{X}\rho^{s}.

(5.3)

Assume that (
5.3
) holds for a certain
X
. Then, by Lemma
B.3
, we have

\rho^{s}Xa=\widehat{X}\rho^{s}a=\mathrm{e}^{s\beta}\widehat{X}a\rho^{s},\quad\rho^{s}Xa^{\dagger}=\widehat{X}\rho^{s}a^{\dagger}=\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta}\widehat{X}a^{\dagger}\rho^{s}

on the dense subspace
D
, and these equalities extend to the entire Hilbert space
\mathsf{h}
. By induction, we conclude the proof of (
5.3
). Finally, multiplying both sides of (
5.3
) by
\rho^{-s}
on the right-hand side, we obtain

\widehat{X}=\rho^{s}X\rho^{-s},

which is another polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. For
-1/2\leq s<0
, the proof follows similarly, completing the argument.
∎

The following lemma is an immediate consequence of Lemma
B.2
. Since the proof is similar to that of Lemma
5.4
, we omit it.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, and let
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Then,
W(rz)^{-1}XW(rz)
is also a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
.

Since
za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a
generates the Weyl unitary group
(W(rz))_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
, and by Lemma
5.2
, we know that
\rho^{s/2}(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator, we can now show that
\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
can be expressed as a power series.

Lemma 5.6
.

We have

\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{s/2}r^{k}(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{k}\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{k!}

(5.4)

in the space
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
.

Proof.

Let us first prove that the right-hand side of (
5.4
) defines a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

\displaystyle\quad\norm{\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{\rho^{s/2} r^k (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a)^k \rho^{(1-s)/2}}{k!} }_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\absolutevalue{r}^{k}}{k!}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\norm{ \rho^{s/2} (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a)^k \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_n }^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\absolutevalue{r}^{k}}{k!}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)\beta n}\norm{ \me^{-s \beta N / 2} (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a)^k e_n }^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\absolutevalue{r}^{k}}{k!}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)\beta n}\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta(n-k)}(2\absolutevalue{z})^{2k}\frac{(n+k)!}{n!}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\absolutevalue{r z})^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{e}^{s\beta k/2}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta n}\frac{(n+k)!}{n!}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(2\absolutevalue{rz}\mathrm{e}^{s\beta/2}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{1/2}\right)^{k}}{(k!)^{1/2}}<+\infty.

On the other hand, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
e_{n}
is an analytical vector for
(W(rz))_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
, because

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\norm{r^k (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a)^k e_n}}{k!}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\absolutevalue{2 r z}^{k}\sqrt{\frac{(n+k)!}{n!}}}{k!}<+\infty.

Therefore,
\rho^{(1-s)/2}e_{n}=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{(1-s)/2}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)\beta n/2}e_{n}
is also an analytical vector. In other words,

\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}e_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{s/2}r^{k}(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{k}\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{k!}e_{n},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since the linear span of
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
, by linearity, we can conclude the proof of (
5.4
).
∎

It is well-known that, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the map
r\mapsto W(rz)
is strongly continuous. Now, we will demonstrate that
r\mapsto\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
exhibits enhanced regularity.

Proposition 5.7
.

The map
r\mapsto\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is differentiable with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm and

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

(5.5)

Proof.

Let
t>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
0\leq s<1
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\norm{ \frac{1}{t} \int_{r}^{r + t} \rho^{s/2} (W(\tau z) - W(r z)) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k \, \md\tau}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\int_{r}^{r+t}\norm{ \rho^{s/2} (W(\tau z) - W(r z)) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a)\rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k }\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\int_{r}^{r+t}\norm{\rho^{s/2}}\norm{W(\tau z) - W(rz)}\norm{ (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k }\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\norm{\rho^{s/2}}^{2}\norm{(z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k}^{2}.

(5.6)

For
s=1
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\norm{ \frac{1}{t} \int_{r}^{r + t} \rho^{1/2} (W(\tau z) - W(r z)) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) e_k \, \md\tau}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\int_{r}^{r+t}\norm{ \rho^{1/2} (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) W(\tau z) e_k}\mathrm{d}\tau\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\int_{r}^{r+t}\norm{ \rho^{1/2} (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) W(rz) e_k}\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}.

(5.7)

By Remark
5.3
, we know that the right-hand sides of both (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) are summable with respect to
k
. Note that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm is independent of the choice of the orthonormal basis, and, in (
5.7
), the collections
(W(\tau z)e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(W(rz)e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
form two orthonormal bases in
\mathsf{h}
.

Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem and the strong continuity of Weyl groups, for
0\leq s\leq 1
we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\norm{ \rho^{s/2} \left( W((r+t)z) - W(rz) - t W(rz) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a ) \right) \rho^{(1-s)/2} }_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\norm{ \rho^{\frac{s}{2}} \left( W((r+t)z) - W(rz) - t W(rz) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a ) \right) \rho^{\frac{1-s}{2}} e_k }^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\norm{ \frac{1}{t} \int_r^{r+t} \rho^{s/2} (W(\tau z) - W(rz)) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k \, \md\tau}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\norm{ \lim_{t \rightarrow 0} \frac{1}{t} \int_r^{r+t} \rho^{s/2} (W(\tau z) - W(rz)) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \rho^{(1-s)/2} e_k \, \md\tau}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=0,

(5.8)

which implies that (
5.5
) holds in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.
∎

The next result is a generalization of Proposition
5.7
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)X\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)X\rho^{(1-s)/2},

(5.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}XW(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}XW(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

(5.10)

Proof.

By Lemma
5.4
, Remark
5.3
and following the proof of Proposition
5.7
, for
0\leq s<1
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\norm{ \rho^{s/2} \left( W((r+t)z) - W(rz) - t W(rz) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \right) X \rho^{(1-s)/2} }_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\rho^{s/2}}_{2}\norm{\rho^{(1-s)/4}\cdot\rho^{-(1-s)/2} X \rho^{(1-s)/2}}_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\norm{ \left( W((r+t)z) - W(rz) - t W(rz) (z a^\dagger- \overline{z} a) \right) \rho^{(1-s)/4} }_{2}=0,

which implies (
5.9
). For
s=1
, we need to write
\rho^{1/2}W(rz)X
as

\rho^{1/2}W(rz)X=\rho^{1/2}W(rz)XW(-rz)\rho^{-1/2}\rho^{1/4}\cdot\rho^{1/4}W(rz),

noting that

is another polynomial in

and
a^{\dagger}
, according to Lemma
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
. Thus, the proof of (
5.9
) follows the same argument for

.

Moreover, (
5.10
) follows immediately from (
5.9
) and Lemma
5.5
.
∎

In the next step, we aim to investigate the explicit action of the induced generator

Now, we introduce the following result, adapted from the proof in

of the Gaussian QMS

Proposition 5.9
.

Letting
\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}
, we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\langle\xi,\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(W(z))-W(z)}{t}\eta\rangle=\langle\xi,W(z)Y(z)\eta\rangle,

where

\displaystyle Y(z)

\displaystyle=(Zza^{\dagger}-\overline{Zz}a)+\frac{1}{2}(\overline{z}Zz-\overline{Zz}z)-\frac{1}{2}\Re{\overline{z} C z}+\mathrm{i}\Re{\overline{\zeta} z}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}(\Im{Z z}q-\Re{Z z}p)+\frac{1}{2}(\overline{z}Zz-\overline{Zz}z)-\frac{1}{2}\Re{\overline{z} C z}+\mathrm{i}\Re{\overline{\zeta} z}.

(5.11)

Fortunately, in Theorem
5.12
, we will demonstrate that the Weyl operator, wrapped by fractional powers of

Lemma 5.10
.

The set
\left\{\outerproduct{\xi}{\eta}:\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}\right\}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in\mathsf{h}
. Consider two sequences
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{E}
such that
\xi_{n}\rightarrow u
and
\eta_{n}\rightarrow v
. It suffices to demonstrate that every rank-one operator
\outerproduct{u}{v}
can be approximated by
(\outerproduct{\xi_n}{\eta_n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\norm{ \ketbra{\xi_n}{\eta_n} - \ketbra{u}{v} }_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}(\norm{\eta_n}^{2}\norm{\xi_n}^{2}+\norm{v}^{2}\norm{u}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\Re{ \langle v, \eta_n \rangle\langle\xi_n, u \rangle})=0.

Therefore,
\left\{\outerproduct{\xi}{\eta}:\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}\right\}
is dense in the space of finite-rank operators with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Given that density is transitive, we have demonstrated the density of
\left\{\outerproduct{\xi}{\eta}:\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}\right\}
in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

The map

t\mapsto\frac{1}{t}\left(T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})-\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

(5.12)

is uniformly bounded in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm for all
t>0
.

Proof.

By (
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\quad\norm{t^{-1} \left(T_t^{(s)} (\rho^{s/2} W(z) \rho^{(1-s)/2}) - \rho^{s/2} W(z) \rho^{(1-s)/2} \right)}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{t^{-1} \rho^{s/2} \left( \cT_t (W (z)) - W(z) \right) \rho^{(1-s)/2} }_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{t^{-1} \rho^{s/2} \left( \phi_t (z) W(\me^{t Z} z) - \phi_t (z) W(z) + \phi_t (z) W(z)- W(z) \right) \rho^{(1-s)/2} }_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{t^{-1} \rho^{s/2} (W(\me^{t Z} z) - W(z)) \rho^{(1-s)/2}}_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+t^{-1}\absolutevalue{\phi_t (z) - 1}\norm{\rho^{s/2} W(z) \rho^{(1-s)/2}}_{2}.

(5.13)

Notice that in the last step of (
5.13
)
we have used the inequality
\absolutevalue{\phi(t)}\leq 1
. It can be easily checked that
t^{-1}\absolutevalue{\phi_t (z) - 1}
is uniformly bounded in
t
, and we only need to show the first term in (
5.13
) is uniformly bounded in
t
.

Given the proof of
[
Par92
, Proposition 20.14]
, we confirm that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}e(f+\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)=(Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)a^{\dagger}e(f+\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z),

(5.14)

where
f\in\mathbb{C}
. By (
5.14
) and Lemma
B.2
we have the following explicit computations of the derivative

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)e(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp{ - \frac{1}{2} \abs{\me^{t Z} z}^2 - \overline{\me^{t Z} z} f}e(f+\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)

\displaystyle=\exp{ - \frac{1}{2} \abs{\me^{t Z} z}^2 - \overline{\me^{t Z} z} f}\left(-\Re{\overline{\me^{t Z} z} Z \me^{t Z} z}-\overline{Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z}f\right)e(f+\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)

\displaystyle\quad+\exp{ - \frac{1}{2} \abs{\me^{t Z} z}^2 - \overline{\me^{t Z} z} f}(Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)a^{\dagger}e(f+\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)

\displaystyle=W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)\left(-\Re{\overline{\me^{t Z} z} Z \me^{t Z} z}-\overline{Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z}a\right)e(f)+\left(Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z\right)a^{\dagger}W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)e(f)

\displaystyle=W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)\left(Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}za^{\dagger}-\overline{Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z}a+\mathrm{i}\Im{ \overline{\me^{t Z} z} Z \me^{t Z} z}\right)e(f).

(5.15)

By Lemma
5.10
we have the density of
\left\{\outerproduct{\xi}{\eta}:\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}\right\}
in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. Define the constant
M_{z}
by

M_{z}:=\max_{\tau\geq 0}\left\{\absolutevalue{Z \me^{\tau Z} z},\absolutevalue{\overline{\me^{\tau Z} z} Z \me^{\tau Z} z}\right\}.

We know that such an
M_{z}
exists and is finite because of Hypothesis
1
. Employing (
5.15
) we have, for
0\leq s<1
,

\displaystyle\quad\norm{t^{-1} ( \rho^{s/2} W(\me^{t Z} z) - W(z) ) \rho^{(1-s)/2} }_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \langle t^{-1} \rho^{s/2} ( W(\me^{t Z} z) - W(z) ) \rho^{(1-s)/2}, \ketbra{\xi}{\eta} \rangle_2}}{\norm{\ketbra{\xi}{\eta}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \langle\rho^{s/2} ( W(\me^{t Z} z) - W(z) ) \rho^{(1-s)/2} \eta, \xi\rangle}}{t\norm{\xi}\norm{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \langle\int_0^t \rho^\frac{s}{2} W(\me^{\tau Z} z) ( Z \me^{\tau Z} z a^\dagger- \overline{Z \me^{\tau Z} z} a + \mi\Im\{ \overline{\me^{\tau Z} z} Z \me^{\tau Z} z \} ) \rho^\frac{1-s}{2} \eta\md\tau, \xi\rangle}}{t\norm{\xi}\norm{\eta}}

\displaystyle\leq M_{z}\norm{\rho^{s/2}}\left(\norm{ a^\dagger\rho^{(1-s)/2}}+\norm{a \rho^{(1-s)/2}}+\norm{\rho^{(1-s)/2}}\right).

According to Remark
5.3
,
a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}
and

are bounded operators. So, we have demonstrated that (
5.12
) is uniformly bounded in

with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. For the case

5.15

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)e(f)=\left(Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}za^{\dagger}-\overline{Z\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z}a-\mathrm{i}\Im{ \overline{\me^{t Z} z} Z \me^{t Z} z }\right)W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z)e(f),

so the proof of the uniform boundedness of (

∎

Theorem 5.12
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
belongs to the domain of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
.

Proof.

We first investigate the time derivative of
T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})
in a weak form. Observe that, for an arbitrary exponential vector
e(f)
, we have

\displaystyle\rho^{s/2}e(f)

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{s/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta\ell/2}\outerproduct{e_\ell}{e_\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}e_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{s/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{k}(\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta/2})^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}e_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})^{s/2}e(\mathrm{e}^{-s\beta/2}f).

Therefore, for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}
, by linearity we know
\rho^{s/2}\xi\in\mathcal{E}
and
\rho^{(1-s)/2}\eta\in\mathcal{E}
. So, by Proposition
5.9

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\langle\xi,\frac{T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})-\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{t}\eta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\langle\rho^{s/2}\xi,\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(W(z)-W(z))}{t}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\eta\right\rangle

(5.16)

where

is defined as in (

\rho^{s/2}W(z)Y(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

Since
(T_{t}^{(s)})_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
, it is sufficient to show
\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}
is in the domain of its weak generator. Let
y\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. By Lemma
5.10
we are able to find a sequence
(\outerproduct{\xi_n}{\eta_n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\xi_{n},\eta_{n}\in\mathcal{E}
for all

such that
\outerproduct{\xi_n}{\eta_n}\rightarrow y
in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. By (
5.16

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\langle y,\frac{T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})-\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{t}\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\langle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\outerproduct{\xi_n}{\eta_n},\frac{T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})-\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{t}\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\langle\xi_{n},\frac{T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})-\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{t}\eta_{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left\langle\xi_{n},\rho^{s/2}W(z)Y(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\eta_{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left\langle\outerproduct{\xi_n}{\eta_n},\rho^{s/2}W(z)Y(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle y,\rho^{s/2}W(z)Y(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right\rangle_{2},

which implies that
\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}
and

L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)=\rho^{s/2}W(z)Y(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2},

(5.19)

where

is as in (
5.11

5.11
provides the uniform boundedness of (

∎

Lemma 5.13
.

The map

r\mapsto L^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2})

By Proposition

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{i}\Re{\overline{\zeta} z}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

(5.21)

Lemma 5.16

Proof of Theorem
3.1

\displaystyle L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-\gamma+\mathrm{i}\Omega)\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2},

\displaystyle L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega)\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2},

which implies that
-\gamma\pm\mathrm{i}\Omega
are both eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
.

Assume
\kappa\neq 0
. Letting
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\displaystyle L^{(s)}(c_{1}\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}+c_{2}\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2})

\displaystyle=(c_{1}(-\gamma+\mathrm{i}\Omega)-c_{2}\mathrm{i}\kappa)\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle\quad+(c_{2}(-\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega)-c_{1}\overline{\mathrm{i}\kappa})\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

To find an eigenvalue of
L^{(s)}
, we require that there exists a
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that

c_{1}(-\gamma+\mathrm{i}\Omega)-c_{2}\mathrm{i}\kappa=\lambda c_{1},\quad c_{2}(-\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega)-c_{1}\overline{\mathrm{i}\kappa}=\lambda c_{2}.

(5.27)

If
\lambda=0
, it follows that
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
, and no eigenvalue can be found. If
\lambda\neq 0
, then, since
\kappa\neq 0
, (
5.27
) gives an equation for
\lambda
:

\lambda^{2}+2\gamma\lambda+\left(\gamma^{2}+\Omega^{2}-\absolutevalue{\kappa}^{2}\right)=0,

which admits two roots:

\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\absolutevalue{\kappa}^{2}-\Omega^{2}}.

(5.28)

Notice that the expressions in (
5.28
) are precisely the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Z}
, thus concluding the proof.
∎

As established in Theorem
3.1
, the base eigenvectors of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
are first-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, left-multiplied by
\rho^{s/2}
and right-multiplied by
\rho^{(1-s)/2}
. Our goal now is to show that the remaining eigenvectors are higher-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
embedded in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt space in the same manner, with their corresponding eigenvalues derived from the base eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
. The main result is stated in Theorem
3.3
. To establish this, we analyze higher derivatives of the Weyl unitary groups
(W(rz))_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
, ensuring the emergence of higher-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
and proving Theorem
3.2
.

Proposition 5.17
.

For any
n\geq 1
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)},

and

L^{(s)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right).

(5.29)

Proof.

From Proposition
5.8
we know, for any
n\geq 1
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)=\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

(5.30)

Now, assume that

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}

and (
5.29
) holds for a certain
n
. Let
(s_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a sequence with
s_{k}\rightarrow 0
. By (
5.30
) we know

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\rho^{s/2}W((r+s_{n})z)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}-\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{s_{n}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n+1}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n+1}}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2},

as
k\rightarrow+\infty
in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. By the assumption we have

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}L^{(s)}\left(\frac{\rho^{s/2}(W((r+s_{k})z)-W(rz))(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{s_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{s_{k}}\left(L^{(s)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}\rho^{s/2}W((r+s_{k})z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{s_{k}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}\left(L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W((r+s_{k})z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}L^{(s)}\left(\frac{\rho^{s/2}W((r+s_{k})z)\rho^{(1-s)/2}-\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}}{s_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n}}L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n+1}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n+1}}L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right).

The existence of
\mathrm{d}^{n}L^{(s)}(W(rz)\rho^{1/2})/\mathrm{d}r^{n}
is established in Corollary
5.14
. Since the induced generator
L^{(s)}
is a closed operator, we know that

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n+1}}{\mathrm{d}r^{n+1}}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}

and (
5.29
) holds for
n+1
. By induction we conclude the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.18
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}
belongs to
\dom L^{(s)}
.

Proof.

In the proposition above, we have shown that

\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)},\quad\forall r\in\mathbb{R},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

To prove this corollary, it suffices to show that
\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}
for all
X
that are monomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
.

By setting
r=0
and fixing an arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we observe that

\rho^{s/2}(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}z^{k}(-\overline{z})^{n-k}\rho^{s/2}Y_{k,n-k}\rho^{(1-s)/2},

where
Y_{k,n-k}
denotes the sum of all monomials with the degree
k
in
a^{\dagger}
, degree
n-k
in
a
, and coefficient
1
. For example,

Y_{0,n}=a^{n},\quad Y_{1,n-1}=a^{\dagger}a^{n-1}+aa^{\dagger}a^{n-2}+\cdots+a^{n-2}a^{\dagger}a+a^{n-1}a^{\dagger}.

Since
z^{0}(-\overline{z})^{n},z^{1}(-\overline{z})^{n-1},\dots,z^{n}(-\overline{z})^{0}
are linearly independent, by varying
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we can obtain
n+1
linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Thus, by considering linear combinations of these vectors, we conclude that

\rho^{s/2}Y_{k,n-k}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)},\quad\forall\,0\leq k\leq n.

Note that
Y_{k,n-k}
can be expressed as a finite sum of Wick normal ordered products (see Appendix
C
for details). Since we know from Lemma
5.16
that
\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}
and
\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\in\dom L^{(s)}
, using the fact that
\rho^{s/2}Y_{k,n-k}\rho^{(1-s)/2}
belongs to
\dom L^{(s)}
and by induction, we can conclude the proof.
∎

Before proceeding, we require the following technical lemma, which shows that, similar to
L^{(s)}
, the order of the generator
\mathcal{L}
and the differential operator
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}r
can be interchanged. Note that in Lemma
5.19
, Proposition
5.20
, and Theorem
3.2
, the action of
\mathcal{L}
is algebraically defined as in (
1.1
).

Lemma 5.19
.

Let
X
be a polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)X\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)X\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}W(rz)X\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

(5.31)

Proof.

By (
1.1
) we have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(W(rz)X)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}(\mathrm{i}\rho^{s/2}HW(rz)X\rho^{(1-s)/2}-\mathrm{i}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)XH\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\rho^{\frac{s}{2}}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}W(rz)X-2L_{\ell}^{*}W(rz)XL_{\ell}+W(rz)XL_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right)\rho^{\frac{(1-s)}{2}})

(5.32)

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)X\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

(5.33)

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}W(rz)X\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2},

which implies (
5.31
). Note that in the transition from (
5.32
) to (
5.33
), we applied Lemma
5.5
and Proposition
5.8
. For instance, we can write

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}HW(rz)X\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\cdot W(rz)^{-1}HW(rz)\cdot X\rho^{(1-s)/2}

to apply Proposition
5.8
.
∎

Proposition 5.20
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

(5.34)

Proof.

Following (
5.19
), Proposition
5.9
, Theorem
5.15
, and Lemma
5.19
, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)Y(rz)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(W(rz))\rho^{(1-s)/2}=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2},

(5.35)

where
Y(z)
is defined as in (
5.11
). Then, (
5.35
) implies that (
5.34
) holds for
n=1
. Now, suppose that (
5.34
) holds for a certain
n
, by Proposition
5.17
and Lemma
5.19
we have

\displaystyle\quad L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n+1}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=L^{(s)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}L^{(s)}\left(\rho^{s/2}W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n}\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}\left(W(rz)(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)^{n+1}\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

We conclude the proof of (
5.34
) by induction.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.2
. In fact, this follows directly from Proposition
5.20
, as we have already demonstrated how to construct all polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
by taking linear combinations of

and

.

Appendix A
Real-Linear Operators

Let
S
be a real-linear operator defined on
\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma A.1
.

There exist two unique complex numbers
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

Sz=s_{1}z+s_{2}\overline{z},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},

(A.1)

in which

s_{1}=\frac{S(1)-\mathrm{i}S(\mathrm{i})}{2},\quad s_{2}=\frac{S(1)+\mathrm{i}S(\mathrm{i})}{2}.

In practice it is useful to identify
S
and
z
with a real-valued matrix
\mathbf{S}
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a vector
\mathbf{z}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where

\mathbf{S}:=\begin{bmatrix}\real s_{1}+\real s_{2}&\imaginary s_{2}-\imaginary s_{1}\\
\imaginary s_{1}+\imaginary s_{2}&\real s_{1}-\real s_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{z}:=\begin{bmatrix}\real z\\
\imaginary z\end{bmatrix}.

(A.2)

Conversely, given a real-valued matrix
\mathbf{S}
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can induce a real-linear operator
S
on
\mathbb{C}
.

The following identities are useful but we do not explicitly cite them when performing computations.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
S
be a real-linear operator and
y,z\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
be their corresponding identifications, respectively. We have

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\real Sz\\
\imaginary Sz\end{bmatrix},\quad\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\rangle=\real\langle y,z\rangle.

Consequently, we can identify the
n
-th power and the exponential of the real-linear operator
S
in the same manner. Specifically, we have

\mathbf{S}^{n}\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\real S^{n}z\\
\imaginary S^{n}z\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\real\mathrm{e}^{S}z\\
\imaginary\mathrm{e}^{S}z\end{bmatrix}.

Appendix B
Some Commutation Relations

In this section, we list some commonly used commutation relations, many of which are straightforward. As will become evident, in the problems we are addressing, although two terms may be non-commutative, exchanging their order often results only in multiplication by a scalar or the addition of a constant multiple of the identity. This significantly simplifies many problems.

Lemma
B.1
, Lemma
B.2
and Lemma
B.3
are well-known, so their proofs are omitted.

Lemma B.1
.

[(a^{\dagger})^{n},a]=-n(a^{\dagger})^{n-1}
and
[a^{n},a^{\dagger}]=na^{n-1}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma B.2
.

[a,W(z)]=zW(z)
and
[a^{\dagger},W(z)]=\overline{z}W(z)
.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\mathrm{e}^{-sN}a=\mathrm{e}^{s}a\mathrm{e}^{-sN},\quad\mathrm{e}^{-sN}a^{\dagger}=\mathrm{e}^{-s}a^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{-sN}.

(B.1)

The following lemma demonstrates that the commutation relation between a first-order polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
and the power of another first-order polynomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
takes a simple form.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be first-order polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,

[X,Y^{n}]=n[X,Y]Y^{n-1},\quad\forall n\geq 0.

(B.2)

Notice that
[X,Y]
is a multiple of the identity operator
\mathds{1}
.

Proof.

It is evident that (
B.2
) holds for
n=0
and
n=1
. Let us write
X=b_{1}a+b_{2}a^{\dagger}+b_{3}
and
Y=c_{1}a+c_{2}a^{\dagger}+c_{3}
, where
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
. Consequently, we immediately have

[X,Y]=[b_{1}a+b_{2}a^{\dagger}+b_{3},c_{1}a+c_{2}a^{\dagger}+c_{3}]=(b_{1}c_{2}-b_{2}c_{1})\mathds{1}.

Assuming that (
B.2
) holds for some
n
, and using the identity above, we deduce

[X,Y^{n+1}]=[X,Y^{n}Y]=Y^{n}[X,Y]+[X,Y^{n}]Y=(n+1)[X,Y]Y^{n}.

By induction, we conclude that (
B.2
) is valid.
∎

Appendix C
Wick Normal Ordering

In this section, we introduce the notion of Wick normal ordering. A product of creation and annihilation operators is said to be
Wick normal ordered
when all creation operators appear to the left of all annihilation operators. In other words, a product is Wick normal ordered if it takes the form
a^{\dagger n}a^{m}
, where
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. The following Wick’s theorem is well-known. However, since it is difficult to find a precise reference, we provide a proof in our own terms.

Theorem C.1

(Wick’s Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a monomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, with degree
n
in
a^{\dagger}
and degree
m
in
a
. Then,
X
can be expressed as a finite sum of Wick normal-ordered products whose degrees are at most
n+m
.

Proof.

It is clear that the statement holds for both
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Now, let
X
be a monomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
with degree
n
in
a^{\dagger}
and degree
m
in
a
, and assume it can be written as the finite sum

X=\sum_{k}c_{k}a^{\dagger n_{k}}a^{m_{k}}

for some
(c_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}
,
(n_{k})_{k},(m_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}
with
n_{k}+m_{k}\leq n+m
for all
k
. Then, the statement automatically holds for
Xa
. Furthermore, by Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle Xa^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{k}a^{\dagger n_{k}}a^{m_{k}}a^{\dagger}=\sum_{k}c_{k}a^{\dagger n_{k}}(m_{k}a^{m_{k}-1}+a^{\dagger}a^{m_{k}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}m_{k}c_{k}a^{\dagger n_{k}}a^{m_{k}-1}+\sum_{k}c_{k}a^{\dagger(n_{k}+1)}a^{m_{k}},

which is still a finite sum of normal ordered products whose degrees are at most
n+m+1
. By induction, the proof is complete.
∎

The following variation of Wick’s theorem is also highly useful:

Corollary C.2
.

Assume that
X
and
Y
are linearly independent first-order polynomials without constant terms. Let
W
be a monomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Then,
W
can be expressed as the following finite sum:

W=\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}},

(C.1)

where
(c_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}
,
(n_{k})_{k},(m_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}
. Note that
X^{0}Y^{0}
is the identity operator
\mathds{1}
.

Proof.

Since
X
and
Y
are linearly independent, both
a
and
a^{\dagger}
can be uniquely expressed as linear combinations of
X
and
Y
. We can write
a=b_{1}X+b_{2}Y
and
a^{\dagger}=c_{1}X+c_{2}Y
, where
b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
. So, (
C.1
) holds for
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. Now, suppose that (
C.1
) holds for a certain monomial in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
,
W
. Observe that, by Lemma
B.4
,

\displaystyle Wa

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}}a=\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}}(b_{1}X+b_{2}Y)

\displaystyle=b_{1}\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}}X+b_{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}+1}

\displaystyle=b_{1}\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}\left([Y^{m_{k}},X]+XY^{m_{k}}\right)+b_{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}+1}

\displaystyle=b_{1}[Y,X]\sum_{k}m_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}-1}

\displaystyle\quad+b_{1}\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}+1}Y^{m_{k}}+b_{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}X^{n_{k}}Y^{m_{k}+1}.

We emphasize that
[Y,X]
is a multiple of the identity operator
\mathds{1}
. Therefore, (
C.1
) holds for
Wa
. Similarly, we can prove the case for
Wa^{\dagger}
. By induction we conclude the proof of (
C.1
).
∎

Certainly, Theorem
C.1
and Corollary
C.2
above can easily be extended to polynomials in
a
and
a^{\dagger}
.

Appendix D
Diagonalization of Invariant States

Let
\rho
be a Gaussian state on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
with mean vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
and covariance operator
S
. As stated in Definition
2.1
,
S
is a real-linear, symmetric, and invertible operator. Let
\mathbf{S}
denote the identification of
S
. We refer to
\mathbf{S}
as the
covariance matrix
of the Gaussian state
\rho
. According to the celebrated Williamson’s theorem (see
[
Wil36
]
for details), we know that
\mathbf{S}
can be symplectically diagonalized. Specifically, there exists a real number
\nu\geq 1/2
and a symplectic matrix
\mathbf{G}
such that

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{G}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}\nu&0\\
0&\nu\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{G}.

(D.1)

Every Gaussian state can be diagonalized with respect to the canonical orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and is a unitary conjugate of the thermal state with
inverse temperature

\beta
, i.e.,

\rho=W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})\mathrm{e}^{-\beta N}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1},

(D.2)

where
\mathbf{M}:=\mathbf{G}^{-1}
,
B(\mathbf{M})
is the
Bogoliubov transformation
associated with the matrix
\mathbf{M}
(see
[
BD07
,
Par21
]
for details), and
\beta
is the inverse temperature satisfying
\coth(\beta/2)/2=\nu
. Note that
B(\mathbf{M})
is a unitary operator, and we have
B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}=B(\mathbf{M}^{-1})
.

Let
M
denote the real-linear operator identified with
\mathbf{M}
, let
m_{1},m_{2}
be complex numbers such that
Mz=m_{1}z+m_{2}\overline{z}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
\boldsymbol{\omega}
denote the identification of the mean vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Gaussian QMS with the invariant normal state
\rho
. In
[
AFP21
, Theorem 8]
, the authors demonstrated that the mean vector and covariance matrix of
\rho
can be fully characterized by the parameters
\Omega,\kappa,\zeta,u_{\ell},v_{\ell}
, which appear in the Hamiltonian part (
1.3
) and the noise part (
1.2
) of the generator of the Gaussian QMS. In the following result, we will examine the relationships among these parameters when
\rho
is a diagonal state. We recall that in the previous sections, we have always assumed that
\rho
is diagonal with respect to the canonical orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma D.1
.

The Gaussian invariant normal state
\rho
of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is diagonal with respect to
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
if and only if (
3.1
) holds.

Proof.

We will first show that
W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})=\mathds{1}
if and only if
\omega=0
and
\mathbf{M}=\mathds{1}_{2}
, where
\mathds{1}
denotes the identity operator in
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, and
\mathds{1}_{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix.

If
\omega=0
and
\mathbf{M}=\mathds{1}_{2}
, it is clear that
W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})=\mathds{1}
. Conversely, suppose
W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})=\mathds{1}
. Then,
W(\omega)=B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}
and
W(\omega)^{-1}=B(\mathbf{M})
. By
[
Par21
, Theorem 1]
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

W(M^{n}\omega)=(B(\mathbf{M}))^{n}W(\omega)(B(\mathbf{M}))^{-n}=W(\omega)^{-n}W(\omega)W(\omega)^{n}=W(\omega).

Clearly, the above identity implies
\mathbf{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This holds if and only if
\mathbf{M}=\mathds{1}_{2}
or
\omega=0
. In either case, from
W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})=\mathds{1}
, we conclude that
\mathbf{M}=\mathds{1}_{2}
and
\omega=0
.

By (
D.1
), we then have
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{M}=\mathds{1}_{2}
, so the Gaussian invariant normal state
\rho
is diagonal if and only if

\omega=0,\quad\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{2}\coth\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{2}.

(D.3)

From
[
AFP21
, proof of Theorem 8]
, it is known that the invariance of
\rho
is equivalent to the following equations:

\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\mathbf{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega},\quad\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{C}=0,

(D.4)

Recalling that the real-linear operators
Z
and
C
are defined in (
2.4
), and putting (
D.3
) into (
D.4
) we obtain

\zeta=0,\quad\kappa=\mathrm{i}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell}u_{\ell}v_{\ell}\right),\quad\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}\sum_{\ell}\absolutevalue{v_\ell}^{2}=\sum_{\ell}\absolutevalue{u_\ell}^{2},

which proves (
3.1
).
∎

Remark D.2
.

When dealing with a non-diagonal Gaussian state
\rho
characterized by mean vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}=\nu\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{M}
, one needs to consider the orthonormal basis
(W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
instead of the canonical orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Similarly, instead of the annihilation operator
a
and the creation operator
a^{\dagger}
, one needs to work with their unitary conjugations
B(\mathbf{M})W(\omega)aB(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}
and
B(\mathbf{M})W(\omega)a^{\dagger}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}
.

The following result shows that
a
and
a^{\dagger}
can be expressed as linear combinations of
B(\mathbf{M})W(\omega)aB(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}
,
B(\mathbf{M})W(\omega)a^{\dagger}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}
, and the identity operator
\mathds{1}
.

Proposition D.3
.

The following identities hold:

\displaystyle W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})aB(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-m_{2}a^{\dagger}+\overline{m_{1}}a+m_{2}\overline{\omega}-\overline{m_{1}}\omega,

\displaystyle W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})a^{\dagger}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}

\displaystyle=m_{1}a^{\dagger}-\overline{m_{2}}a-m_{1}\overline{\omega}+\overline{m_{2}}\omega.

Recall that
m_{1},m_{2}
are complex numbers such that
Mz=m_{1}z+m_{2}\overline{z}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

By
[
Par21
, Theorem 1]
, for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

B(\mathbf{M})W(rz)B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}=W(M(rz))=W(rM(z)),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(D.5)

Differentiating with respect to
r
on both sides of (
D.5
) and evaluating at
r=0
, we obtain

B(\mathbf{M})(za^{\dagger}-\overline{z}a)B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}=M(z)a^{\dagger}-\overline{M(z)}a,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(D.6)

Letting
z=1
and
z=\mathrm{i}
in (
D.6
) and by Lemma
A.1
, we derive

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{M})aB(\mathbf{M})^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}M(\mathrm{i})+M(1)}{2}a^{\dagger}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\overline{M(\mathrm{i})}+\overline{M(1)}}{2}a=-m_{2}a^{\dagger}+\overline{m_{1}}a,

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{M})a^{\dagger}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{M(1)-\mathrm{i}M(\mathrm{i})}{2}a^{\dagger}+\frac{-\overline{M(1)}+\mathrm{i}\overline{M(\mathrm{i})}}{2}a=m_{1}a^{\dagger}-\overline{m_{2}}a.

Finally, by Lemma
B.2
we compute

\displaystyle W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})aB(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}

\displaystyle=W(\omega)(-m_{2}a^{\dagger}+\overline{m_{1}}a)W(\omega)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-m_{2}a^{\dagger}+\overline{m_{1}}a+m_{2}\overline{\omega}-\overline{m_{1}}\omega,

and similarly, we have

W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})a^{\dagger}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1}=m_{1}a^{\dagger}-\overline{m_{2}}a-m_{1}\overline{\omega}+\overline{m_{2}}\omega,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark D.4
.

Using (
D.4
) or, equivalently,
\mathcal{L}_{*}(\rho)=0
, the complex numbers
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
can also be fully characterized by the parameters
\Omega,\kappa,\zeta,u_{\ell},v_{\ell}
. However, the computations are quite cumbersome, and we do not really need to explicitly express
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
in terms of these parameters.

By following the strategy introduced in Remark
D.2
, we can still construct the induced semigroup as in (
1.4
), despite
\rho
being non-diagonal. Consequently, all the results in Section
5
remain valid. Of course, in the proofs of Lemma
5.16
and Theorem
3.1
, we must also account for the parameter
\zeta
, which is no longer zero due to the non-diagonality of
\rho
. However, since the proofs remain essentially the same, we omit them.

The more challenging part is studying the eigenvalues of the adjoint generator
L^{(s)*}
when
\rho
is non-diagonal. In the diagonal case, according to Corollary
3.6
, the drift matrix of the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is simply the complex conjugate of the drift matrix of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. However, in the non-diagonal case, explicitly computing (
3.18
) to determine the parameters in the generator of the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is generally very difficult. Therefore, we employ the standardization process introduced in
[
GP24
, Section 2]
, which is relatively simpler, to determine the drift matrix of
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
.

We define a new semigroup
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by

\mathcal{S}_{t}(X):=U^{*}\mathcal{T}_{t}(UXU^{*})U,\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

(D.7)

where

U:=W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M}).

The unitary operator
U
is associated with the complex number
\omega
and the symplectic matrix
\mathbf{M}
, but for simplicity of notation, we denote it simply by
U
. The following result, due to
[
GP24
]
, can be obtained by explicitly analyzing the action of
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on Weyl operators.

Lemma D.5
.

The semigroup
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Gaussian QMS. Its normal invariant state is given by the diagonal operator
(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta})\mathrm{e}^{-\beta N}=:\rho_{0}
, and its drift matrix is
\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{M}
.

Borrowing terminology from classical probability, we shall refer to
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as the
standardized
QMS of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Since
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
possess a diagonal invariant state, all the results we obtained in the previous sections can be directly applied to
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. By convention, we use
(\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
to denote the dual QMS of
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. By Corollary
3.6
we know that the drift matrix of
(\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is
\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{Z}^{*}(\mathbf{M}^{*})^{-1}
.

Next, we show that the standardized QMS (with respect to the same mean vector
\omega
and symplectic matrix
\mathbf{M}
) of
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is precisely
(\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
.

Proposition D.6
.

For all
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, we have

S_{t}^{\prime}(X)=U^{*}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(UXU^{*})U.

(D.8)

Proof.

By (
1.9
) and (
D.7
), and noting that
\rho=U\rho_{0}U^{*}
, for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, we have:

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime}(U^{*}XU)\rho^{1/2}_{0}U^{*}YU\rho^{1/2}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}_{0}U^{*}XU\rho^{1/2}_{0}\mathcal{S}_{t}(U^{*}YU)\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho_{0}^{1/2}U^{*}XU\rho^{1/2}_{0}U^{*}\mathcal{T}_{t}(Y)U\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}(Y)\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}Y\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(U^{*}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)U\rho^{1/2}_{0}U^{*}YU\rho^{1/2}_{0}\right).

Since the map
X\mapsto U^{*}XU
is a bijection on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, by the arbitrariness of
Y
, we conclude that
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime}(U^{*}XU)=U^{*}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)U
. Replacing
X
with
UXU^{*}
completes the proof of (
D.8
).
∎

By Proposition
D.8
we can also easily reconstruct the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
from the standardized dual QMS
(\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
using

\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)=U\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime}(U^{*}XU)U^{*}.

Since
U^{*}=B(\mathbf{M})^{*}W(\omega)^{*}=B(\mathbf{M}^{-1})W(-\omega)
, and applying Lemma
D.5
with replacing
U
with
U^{*}
, we know the the drift matrix of the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is given by

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{Z}^{*}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*})^{-1}.

Since
\mathbf{Z}^{*}
and
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{Z}^{*}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*})^{-1}
have identical eigenvalues, it follows that Theorem
3.8
remains valid even when
\rho
is non-diagonal.

Finally, following the strategy outlined in Remark
D.2
, the results in Section
4
can be easily extended to the case of non-diagonal states, except that Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.10
are no longer valid. However, the proofs of Theorem
4.5
and Theorem
4.11
do not rely on the explicit form of the base eigenvectors of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
since the existence of such base eigenvectors can be easily inferred from the invariance of the three-dimensional subspace, spanned by
\rho^{s/2}a\rho^{(1-s)/2}
,
\rho^{s/2}a^{\dagger}\rho^{(1-s)/2}
and
\rho^{1/2}
, under the operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
.
