Stability and Heavy-traffic Delay Optimality of General Load Balancing Policies in Heterogeneous Service Systems
Yishun Luolabel=e2]luo00329@umn.edu, Martin Zubeldialabel=e1]zubeldia@umn.edu

Abstract
We consider a load balancing system consisting of n n single-server queues working in parallel, with heterogeneous service rates. Jobs arrive to a central dispatcher, which has to dispatch them to one of the queues immediately upon arrival. For this setting, we consider a broad family of policies where the dispatcher can only access the queue lengths sporadically, every T T units of time. We assume that the dispatching decisions are made based only on the order of the scaled queue lengths at the last time that the queues were accessed, and on the processing rate of each server. For these general policies, we provide easily verifiable necessary and sufficient conditions for the stability of the system, and sufficient conditions for heavy-traffic delay optimality. We also show that, in heavy-traffic, the queue length converges in distribution to a scaled deterministic vector, where the scaling factor is an exponential random variable.

Introduction

We consider a load balancing system with
n
heterogeneous servers, each equipped with its own queue and an infinite buffer. A central dispatcher receives a single stream of incoming jobs and immediately assigns each job to a server based on a load balancing policy. A wide range of policies and their derivatives has been studied, including Random Routing (RAND) policy, Round Robin (RR) policy, Join the Shortest Queue (JSQ) policy, Join the Shortest Expected Delay (JSED) policy, Power of
d
Choices (PO

To evaluate such policies, the first fundamental performance metric is maximum stability, or the size of the system’s stability region. A policy is considered throughput optimal (definition
4.1
) when it maximizes this region. JSQ is a well-known throughput optimal polciy, while some policies like Po

are throughput optimal in homogeneous systems, but not necessarily in heterogeneous systems
[
1
,
2
,
3

[

. Theoretically, a policy is throughput optimal if it performs better than RAND
[

6
,
7

. However, such criteria require a specific policy design and are not applicable to general policies. In addition, the existing literature predominantly focuses on a limited set of well-structured policies. As a result, the underlying relationship between load balancing policies and stability remains unclear, and a general characterization of the stability region for arbitrary policies is still lacking.

For policies that are throughput optimal, the second fundamental performance metric is the average job delay. JSQ and JSED are known to be delay optimal in certain settings
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Since exact analysis for a policy is often intractable, several asymptotic regimes have been explored in the literature
[
11
]
, including the classical heavy-traffic regime and the many-server heavy-traffic regime. JSQ and Po

[

,

,

5

. These conditions also depend on tailored policy designs and are not applicable to general policies. It remains an open question how to determine whether a general policy is delay-optimal in heavy-traffic.

Our research is motivated by the lack of analytical studies characterizing the stability and delay performance of general load balancing policies. To address these challenges, we propose a general framework that systematically analyzes stability and delay performance across a wide variety of load balancing policies.

1.1
Our contribution

This paper develops a unified analytical framework that bridges and generalizes a wide range of dispatching and load-balancing policies including RAND, RR, JSQ, JSED, and Po
d
within a single coherent structure. Beyond unifying existing approaches, our framework reveals the fundamental principles that govern stability and delay performance in heavy traffic, providing both conceptual clarity and analytical tractability.

At a high level, our results expose a simple yet powerful organizing principle: the performance of a broad class of policies can be understood through the geometry of their ordering rules and the majorization relations they induce among queues. Within this framework, complex policy behavior can be captured and compared through explicit stability and delay characterizations.

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

Unified modeling framework.
We introduce a general formulation for dispatching policies that base their decisions on the order of (possibly scaled) queue lengths, updated periodically over multiple time intervals. This structure encompasses most known policies such as Po
d
and JSED as special cases and enables systematic exploration of new ones.

2.

Explicit stability characterization.
Under a mild symmetry assumption, we derive a closed-form expression for the stability region of any policy in the framework. This leads to a simple majorization condition that serves as both an intuitive and exact test for throughput optimality.

3.

Heavy-traffic delay optimality.
We establish that when the majorization condition holds with strict inequality, the corresponding policy not only achieves maximum throughput but is also delay-optimal in the heavy-traffic limit.

4.

Queue-length asymptotics.
For heavy-traffic delay-optimal policies, we further derive the asymptotic queue-length distribution, quantifying how optimality manifests at the distributional level.

Methodologically, our analysis blends Lyapunov-drift and transform techniques. We refine the Lyapunov drift analysis in
[
16
]
and extend it to arbitrary policies, which allows us to uncover structural relationships between throughput, delay, and symmetry that were previously obscured in the study of dispatching systems.

2.2
Stability of load balancing policies

In queueing theory, a system becomes unstable when the arrival rate to any server exceeds its service rate over time, leading to unbounded growth of the queue. On the one hand, policies such as Weighted RAND aim to achieve throughput optimality by controlling the load directed to each server. On the other hand, JSQ is known to be throughput optimal in general settings
[
39
]
, indicating that systems are stable if jobs are sent to the shortest queue. Other policies like Po
d
and JIQ combined these two ideas in such a way that they tend to send more jobs to shorter queues while fewer jobs to longer queues than RAND.

In homogeneous systems,
[

-allocation policy where the

l

is throughput optimal if it outperforms RAND. In addition, they showed that policies that assign more jobs even to the second shortest queue may not be throughput optimal.

In heterogeneous systems,
[
5

characterize throughput optimality of a policy by its tendency to assign more jobs to shorter queues than to longer ones, relative to the Weighted RAND policy. This condition requires a specifically tailored policy design.
[
6

show that a policy is throughput-optimal if it outperforms RAND in steady state, which requires evaluating the average service rates across the
l

[
32

established a relationship between memory requirements, message exchange rate, and throughput-optimality. For the policies mentioned above,
[

]
provided a necessary and sufficient condition for the stability of the Po

Queueing Model and General Dispatching Framework

In this section, we will first introduce the discrete time queuing model and a general dispatching framework.

3.1
Discrete-time queueing model

We consider a discrete time queueing system consisting of
n
single-server FIFO queues of infinite capacity. Servers are indexed as
[n]
. We denote the queue length vector at the beginning of time slot
t
by
{\bf Q}(t)
, where
Q_{l}(t)
is the length of the
l
-th queue. Jobs arrive to the system as an i.i.d. process
\{A(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, with
\mathbb{E}[A(1)]=n\lambda
, Var
(A(1))=n\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}
, and
A(1)\leq nA_{\max}
almost surely. When
A(t)
jobs arrive, they are all dispatched to a queue with index
I^{*}(t)
according some dispatching policy, to be described later. Once the jobs join the queue, the servers serve up to
{\bf S}(t)
jobs, with
\mathbb{E}[{\bf S}(1)]=\bm{\mu}
, Var
(S_{l}(1))=\sigma_{l}^{2}
, and
S_{l}(1)\leq S_{\max}
almost surely, for any
l\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the service rates are ordered from smallest to largest, i.e., we have
\mu_{1}\leq...\leq\mu_{n}
. Similar to the arrival process, the potential services are also independent and identically distributed across time slots. Moreover, these potential services are independent from the arrival process. To describe the queue dynamics, we denote the “dispatching action” chosen by the dispatcher by
{\bf Z}(t)\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that,
Z_{j}(t)=1
for
j=I^{*}(t)
and
Z_{j}(t)=0
otherwise. Using this, the queue length process is given by

{\bf Q}(t+1)=[{\bf Q}(t)+A(t){\bf Z}(t)-{\bf S}(t)]^{+}={\bf Q}(t)+A(t){\bf Z}(t)-{\bf S}(t)+{\bf U}(t),

(1)

where the operation
[\,\cdot\,]^{+}
above is used because the queue lengths cannot be negative, and the term
{\bf U}(t)
are the unused services that arise because there might not be enough jobs to serve. Note that, the unused service term
U_{i}(t)
is positive only if
Q_{i}(t+1)=0
, which implies
Q_{i}(t+1)U_{i}(t)=0
for all
i
, or simply
\langle{\bf Q}(t+1),{\bf U}(t)\rangle=0
for any
t\geq 0
. Also, the unused service cannot be larger than the service itself, so we have
0\leq U_{i}(t)\leq S_{i}(t)\leq S_{\max}
. We drop the dependence on
t
to denote the variables in steady state, i.e.,
{\bf Q}
follows the steady state distribution of the queue length process
\{{\bf Q}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
.

(a)

A positive integer

2.

(a)

A vector with all positive components

In this section, we use our framework to provide a sufficient and a necessary condition for stability and then analyze the delay performance of load balancing policies that are throughput optimal in the heavy-traffic regime. To derive our theorem, we first introduce several new notations.

For a load balancing policy
\pi\in\Pi
, after sampling the queue length at time
kT
for all

\eta_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}

\mathbf{N}_{k,\eta_{k}}

N_{k,\eta_{k}}(l)=\sum_{t=kT}^{T(k+1)-1}Z_{\eta_{k}(l)}(t)

to
(k+1)T-1
. Note that, if
\eta_{k}=\eta_{k^{\prime}}

\mathbf{N}_{k,\eta_{k}}
and
\mathbf{N}_{k^{\prime},\eta_{k^{\prime}}}
have the same distribution. Consequently, under the same permutation
\eta
, the policy applies the same dispatching rule to assign jobs. With this in mind, we define

\displaystyle f_{l,\eta}=E\left[\left.\frac{N_{k,\eta_{k}}(l)}{T}\right|\eta_{k}=\eta\right],\qquad\tau^{2}_{l,\eta}=\mathrm{Var}\left(\left.\frac{N_{k,\eta_{k}}(l)}{T}\right|\eta_{k}=\eta\right),\qquad\forall\,l\in[n],\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{n},

4.1
Stability Region

We first give a formal definition of throughput optimality.

Definition 4.1
.

A load balancing policy
\pi
is throughput optimal if the Markov Chain
\{\mathbf{X}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is positive recurrent for all
n\lambda\in C
, where

\displaystyle C:=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}:n\lambda<\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}\mu_{l}\right\}.

Common policies like RAND, RR, JSQ, Po
d
, JSED are all throughput optimal in a homogeneous system, but some of them may not be in a heterogeneous system. For a general load balancing policy in
\Pi
, the following theorem provides a sufficient condition for any policy to be stable.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any load balancing policy
\pi\in\Pi
, if

\displaystyle n\lambda<\min\limits_{\eta\in S_{n}}\left\{\min\limits_{m\in[n]}\left\{\frac{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\mu_{\eta(l)}}{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}f_{l,\eta}}\right\}\right\},

(3)

then the Markov Chain
\{\mathbf{X}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is positive recurrent.

The proof is given in Appendix
A
. Note that Equation (
3
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle n\lambda\left(\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}f_{l,\eta}\right)<\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\mu_{\eta(l)},\quad\forall\,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{n},m\in[n].

The interpretation of this sufficient condition is that the system is stable when the arrival rate to any part of the system is less than the corresponding partial sum of service rates for all permutations
\eta
and all
m\in[n]
, and it can be seen as a majorization condition.

The following corollary provides the condition for a policy to be throughput optimal.

Corollary 4.2
.

If

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}f_{l,\eta}\leq\frac{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\mu_{\eta(l)}}{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}\mu_{\eta(l)}},\qquad\forall\,m\in[n],\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{n},

then the policy is throughput optimal.

The proof is given in appendix
B
. This corollary implies that if a load balancing policy sends fewer jobs to a longer scaled queue, the saved fraction of jobs can be redirected to a shorter scaled queue. The shortest queue can receive all incoming jobs, while other longer scaled queue have their own thresholds.

Next, we establish a necessary condition for stability. For any given
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
m\in[n]
, let

\displaystyle h(\eta,m):=\frac{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\mu_{\eta(l)}}{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}f_{l,\eta}}

be the function that is minimized in Equation (
3
). We denote the optimal value of Equation (
3
) by
h^{*}
, and the set of optimal solutions by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{*}:=\underset{\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{n},m\in[n]}{\arg\min}\left\{h(\eta,m)\right\}.

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that for each

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\dagger}}f_{l,\eta^{\prime}}=\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\dagger}}f_{l,\eta^{\dagger}}

such that

is transient.

This theorem states that, if the arrival rate to the longest
m^{\dagger}
queues does not depend on the relative order of those servers, and the arrival rate of those servers is larger than the service rate of the slowest
m^{\dagger}
servers, then the underlying Markov chain is transient. This is because, if the slowest servers happen to be the longest servers, then their queue lengths will diverge in time. The proof is given in Appendix

4.2
Heavy-traffic Optimality and Queue Length Distribution

To prove delay optimality in heavy-traffic, we first show that the steady-state scaled queue length vector
\mathbf{O}
at the time that the queues are sampled satisfies a state space collapse property. That is, the
n
-dimensional steady-state scaled queue length vector collapses along the
1
-dimensional subspace defined by the vector
\mathbf{c}:=(\sqrt{\gamma_{l}})_{l=1}^{n}
, in heavy-traffic. We define the projection of vector
\mathbf{O}
along this vector
\mathbf{c}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{\parallel}(t)=\frac{\left<\mathbf{c},\mathbf{O}(t)\right>}{\left<\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c}\right>}\mathbf{c}=\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{Q}(t)\|_{1}}{\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{1}}\sqrt{\gamma_{l}}\right)_{l=1}^{n}.

Moreover, we define the perpendicular component as

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{\perp}(t)=\mathbf{O}(t)-\mathbf{O}_{\parallel}(t)=\left(\frac{\tilde{Q}_{l}(t)}{\sqrt{\gamma_{l}}}-\frac{\|\mathbf{Q}(t)\|_{1}}{\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{1}}\sqrt{\gamma_{l}}\right)_{l=1}^{n}.

In the theorem below, we show that the second moment of the steady-state random variable
\|\mathbf{O}_{\perp}\|_{2}
is upper bounded by a constant.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}f_{l,\eta}<\frac{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\mu_{\eta(l)}}{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}\mu_{\eta(l)}},\qquad\forall\,m\in[n]\setminus\{n\},\,\eta\in S_{n}.

(4)

Then, for all
\epsilon
small enough,
there exists a function
N_{\perp}
independent from
\epsilon
, such that

\displaystyle E[\|\mathbf{O}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq N_{\perp}^{2}(n,T,\bm{\gamma},\mathcal{F})

for all
n,T\geq 1
and
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, with

\displaystyle N_{\perp}(n,T,\bm{\gamma},\mathcal{F})\in\Theta(Tn^{4})

The proof is in Appendix
D
.

On the other hand note that, for any dispatching policy, the sum of the queue lengths is lower bounded by the queue length of a single server queue with the same arrivals
A(t)
, and services
S(t)=\sum_{l=1}^{n}S_{l}(t)
. This implies that
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}\|_{1}]
scales at least as fast as

, when
\epsilon\to 0
. Combining this with Theorem
4.4

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}_{\perp}\|_{1}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}\|_{1}]}\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}_{\perp}\|_{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}\|_{1}]}\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{n\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}_{\perp}\|^{2}_{2}]}}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{O}\|_{1}]}=0.

This means that, when
\epsilon

\mathbf{O}_{\perp}
is negligible with respect to the scaled queue length vector itself. Therefore, we have
\mathbf{O}\approx\mathbf{O}_{\parallel}
when
\epsilon
approaches zero, which can be interpreted as the collapse of the state space towards its projection onto a one-dimensional vector.

5
Conclusions

This paper develops a unified analytical framework for studying the stability and heavy-traffic delay optimality of general load balancing policies in heterogeneous service systems. Unlike prior works that focus on specific policies such as JSQ, JSED, or Po
d
, our framework captures a broad class of dispatching rules defined by ordered and periodically updated queue-length information. This abstraction enables a systematic characterization of both the stability region and delay performance across heterogeneous systems.

Our main theoretical contributions include (i) a closed-form, easily verifiable condition for stability that generalizes existing majorization-based criteria; (ii) a sufficient and necessary condition for throughput optimality under symmetry; (iii) a proof that strict majorization implies heavy-traffic delay optimality; and (iv) an explicit characterization of the steady-state queue-length distribution under such policies. Together, these results reveal that the geometric structure induced by the ordering and scaling of queue lengths governs both throughput and delay properties in a unified manner.

Methodologically, the work extends classical Lyapunov drift and transform techniques to a much broader class of policies, establishing tight upper and lower bounds that coincide in heavy traffic. Beyond their theoretical appeal, these results provide actionable insights for designing scalable dispatching schemes that trade off between communication cost and delay performance, especially in modern distributed systems with heterogeneous servers and limited information sharing.

Future directions include relaxing the periodic observation assumption, extending the analysis to systems with multiple dispatchers or networked queues, and exploring reinforcement learning approaches that learn policy parameters within our analytical framework. By revealing the geometric and probabilistic principles underlying optimal load balancing, this work provides a foundation for both analytical generalization and algorithmic innovation in stochastic resource allocation systems.
