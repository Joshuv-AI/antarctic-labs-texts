How to simulate Lévy flights in a steep potential: An explicit splitting numerical scheme
Ilya Pavlyukevich, Olga Aryasova, Alexei Chechkin, Oleksii Kulyk

Abstract
We propose an effective explicit numerical scheme for simulating solutions of stochastic differential equations with confining superlinear drift terms, driven by multiplicative heavy-tailed Lévy noise. The scheme is designed to prevent explosion and accurately capture all finite moments of the solutions. In the purely Gaussian case, it correctly reproduces moments of sub-Gaussian tails of the solutions. This method is particularly well-suited for approximating statistical moments and other probabilistic characteristics of Lévy flights in steep potential landscapes.



Keywords:

Heavy tails, splitting method, explicit numerical scheme, superlinear drift, Lévy flights, steep potential, confining potential,
stiff system.

1
Introduction

Non-Gaussian, heavy-tailed Lévy processes have been a central focus of research across
various scientific fields over the past two decades. These are random jump processes with stationary
and independent increments, characterized by algebraically decaying probabilities of large deviations —
unlike Gaussian diffusions, which exhibit super-exponentially light tails.

One of the most prominent examples of such processes is the class of so-called

\alpha
-stable Lévy motions, also known as Lévy flights
[
UZ99
,
Nol20
]
.
These are sometimes described as free anomalous diffusion.
They are characterized by
a continuous flow of tiny, incremental movements interspersed
with occasional large jumps that correspond to rare but significant (often catastrophic) events,
evoking a diffusive clustering behaviour punctuated by abrupt shifts.

Mathematically,
\alpha
-stable Lévy motions emerge as scaling limits in the generalized central limit theorem
(see, e.g., Section 4.5 in
[
Whi02
]
), or via random time changes (subordination)
applied to a free diffusion (see, e.g., Example 30.6 in
[
Sat99
]
). Analytically, they can be treated by means
of fractional calculus (see, e.g.,
[
MS12
]
).

In applications, heavy tailed statistics have been observed in
teletraffic
[
Res97
,
CMP+02
]
, finance

[
Rac03
,
Nol14
]
,
biology
[
KWK06
]
,
physics
[
SZF95
,
CMKG08
,
Sch13
]
,
optimization
[
KZYU13
,
CD18
]
, and
artificial intelligence
[
ŞSG19
,
RBE21
]
.

In the presence of external force fields, particularly external potential fields,
Lévy flights exhibit more complex random dynamics.
Notable examples include overdamped Lévy motion in periodic potentials (Lévy ratchets)
[
PDCS10
,
PLXC15
]
,
overdamped non-linear Lévy oscillators
[
CGK+02
]
, and Lévy models of nonlinear friction

[
CGKM05b
,
KP19
]
.
Of particular interest is the behavior of Lévy flights in
steep potentials
or systems with nonlinear dissipativity,
which has become an important topic of ongoing research, see, e.g.,

[
CGK+04
,
DGNH07
,
DS13
,
GŻ20b
]
.
It is well known that steep
potentals can effectively
confine
Lévy flights. For example, whereas free Lévy flights generally fail finite variance,
the variance of the confined motions becomes finite when subjected to sufficiently steep potentials. In particular,
for the quartic potential
U(x)\sim x^{4}
, the variance remains finite over an infinite time horizon for any stability
index
\alpha
,
see
[
CGK+04
]
.

The numerical approximation of heavy-tailed dynamics in steep potentials is of utmost importance.
However, many standard methods — such as the classical Euler scheme (e.g., see Section 6.3 in
[
JW94
]
) —
often exhibit numerical instability or fail to accurately capture essential characteristics of the underlying random dynamics,
such as the variance or higher-order moments.

In this note, we introduce a novel explicit numerical scheme for the efficient simulation of confined, Lévy-driven
heavy-tailed dynamics, which overcomes these deficiencies.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin with an illustrative example of a numerial approximation
for Cauchy noise in a steep potential
U(x)=\frac{x^{10}}{10}
.
In Section
2
, we discuss the limitations of the widely used Euler scheme and
highlight its deficiencies in capturing key properties of the random dynamics.
In Section
3
, we introduce the direct and reverse splitting schemes for this class of equations.
Through numerical experiments, we show that the direct splitting scheme is the only method that is numerically stable and
accurately reproduces the moments of the original process.
In Section
4
, we perform a comparative error analysis of the Euler, direct and and reverse
splitting schemes for a linear system driven by
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise.
Section
5
contains the formulation of the direct splitting scheme in a general setting.
In Section
6
, we provide a collection of ready-to-use examples that can be employed
to simulate a variety of widely used nonlinear systems.
Finally, Appendix
A
lists
the set of rigorous mathematical assumptions under which convergence of the direct splitting method has been established in

[
AKP25
]
.
Appendix
B
describes several methods for simulating increments of heavy-tailed Lévy processes.

2
Cauchy noise in a steep potential: Euler schemes

Let
X
be the solution to the one-dimensional SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X^{9}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\quad X_{0}=x,

(2.1)

where the drift term
A(x)=-x^{9}
is a (minus) gradient of a steep confining potential
U(x)=\frac{x^{10}}{10}
.
The driving noise
Z
is a Cauchy process, a heavy-tailed Lévy process with characteristic function

\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda Z_{t}}=\mathrm{e}^{-t|\lambda|}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Existence and uniqueness of solutions to this SDE follow from well-known results in the theory of Lévy-driven SDEs; see, e.g.,

[
App09
]
.

The Cauchy process
Z
is a purely discontinuous Lévy process
with heavy tails. It is integrable only up to order
q=1
: specifically, for
q\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}|Z_{t}|^{q}=t^{q}(\cos(\pi q/2))^{-1},

(2.2)

while for
q\in[1,\infty)
, we have
\mathbf{E}|Z|^{q}=+\infty
.
In particular, the Cauchy process lacks both a finite mean and variance.
A typical sample path of
Z
, shown in Fig.
1
(left), is characterized by rare but pronounced large jumps.

Figure 1:
Left: A sample path of a Cauchy process
Z
on the time grid
\{kh\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
h=10^{-3}
.
Right: The absolute moments of the limit distribution
\mathbf{E}|X_{\infty}|^{q}
,
q\in[0,9)
.

On the other hand, it is known, see, e.g.,
[
CKG+03
,
SG03
,
CGK+04
,
CGKM05b
,
KP21
]

that the fast growing confining drift
A

has a
confining
effect on the heavy-tailed noise.
Specifically, the solution
X
of (
2.1
) possesses all moments up to order
q=9
, uniformly bounded in time.
That is, for any
q\in[0,9)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,\infty)}\mathbf{E}|X_{t}|^{q}<\infty.

(2.3)

Moreover, the threshold
q=9
is sharp: for every
t\in(0,\infty)
and
q\in[9,\infty)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}|X_{t}|^{q}=+\infty.

(2.4)

This phenomenon — where the solution exhibits finite moments beyond the integrability of the driving process
Z
—
is known as the
confinement
or
tail-improving effect
.

Although the moments
\mathbf{E}|X_{t}|^{q}
cannot, in general, be determined analytically for a fixed
t\in(0,\infty)
,
their asymptotic values as
t\to\infty
can be obtained.
Specifically, as a consequence of the superlinear confinement due to the
drift,
the distribution of
X_{t}
converges exponentially fast to a stationary distribution
X_{\infty}
as
t\to\infty
.

The law of
X_{\infty}
is known explicitly and has the density

\displaystyle m_{\infty}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})(1-2x^{2}\cos(\frac{\pi}{9})+x^{4})(1-2x^{2}\cos(\frac{5\pi}{9})+x^{4})},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(2.5)

see Eq. (38) in
[
DS07
]
.
This formula allows for the exact computation of the absolute moments of
X_{\infty}
.
Fig.
1
(right) illustrates these stationary moments, with specific numerical values given for moments of orders

q=0.5
, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8.

A commonly used approach for numerically solving Lévy-driven SDEs is the
straightforward explicit Euler scheme on an equidistant time grid
\{kh\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
; see, e.g.,

[
CGKM05a
,
CGK+04
,
dCNGC08
,
DGNH07
,
DGN09
,
PDCS10
]
.
For our example (
2.1
), the explicit scheme
X^{\mathrm{E},h}
is defined
by the recursive relation

\displaystyle X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{0}

\displaystyle=x,

(2.6)

\displaystyle X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle=X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}-(X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh})^{9}h+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

The heavy tail increments in (
2.6
) can be efficiently simulated as i.i.d. Cauchy random variables scaled by the time step
h
. Specifically,

\displaystyle Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}h\xi_{k+1},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(2.7)

where
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. standard Cauchy distributed random variables with the probability density

p(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}
.

Practical simulations of sample paths of
X^{\mathrm{E},h}
reveal a significant drawback of the Euler scheme — namely,
its susceptibility to numerical instability. This issue is not unique to Lévy-driven systems and is known to occur
in the Gaussian case, as well; see, e.g.,
[
Pet98
]
.
Due to the unbounded nature of the noise increments and the superlinear growth of the drift,
there is a positive probability that the numerical approximation explodes,
leading to divergence in finite time.
In such scenarios, the scheme typically terminates with a NaN (”not a number”) value,
indicating numerical blow-up.

Let us provide a quantitative explanation of the blow-up phenomenon using a concrete example; see Fig.
2
.

In other words, the numerical sign alternating instability develops rapidly once the current value
X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}

exceeds a certain threshold

. This threshold can be estimated via a
simple one-step analysis as follows. Suppose, for definiteness, that
X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}=x\in(0,\infty)
.
For instability to occur, the next step

X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{(k+1)h}
must change sign and have a larger magnitude than
x
.
In other words, the following inequality must hold:

\displaystyle x-x^{9}h+h\xi_{k+1}<-x.

(2.9)

Since the noise increment
h\xi_{k+1}
is of order
h
in probability at each step, we can neglect it
to obtain a rough estimate for the instability threshold:

\displaystyle|x|>(2h^{-1})^{1/8}=M_{h}.

(2.10)

Due to confinement, the process

X^{\mathrm{E},h}
reaches
the instability level
M_{h}
only when
the noise increment
h\xi
attains a relatively large value,

|h\xi|\gtrsim z_{h}
for some
effective
threshold
z_{h}
.

Consequently, the probability that the Euler scheme

X^{\mathrm{E},h}
remains

stable
over the interval
[0,t]
can be estimated by
the probability that the noise increments never exceed
z_{h}
for all
kh\leq t
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|X^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}|<M_{h},\ kh\leq t)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{P}(|h\xi_{k}|\leq z_{h},\ kh\leq t)

(2.11)

\displaystyle\approx\Big(1-\mathbf{P}(|\xi|>z_{h}/h)\Big)^{t/h}

\displaystyle\approx\Big(1-\frac{2h}{\pi z_{h}}\Big)^{t/h}

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{\pi z_{h}}t}.

This simple argument shows very good agreement with the empirical data. In Fig.

we

X^{\mathrm{E},h}

on the interval
[0,t]
,

, for different
time steps
h
.
Empirically, the thresholds for the noise outliers were detremined as

for
h=10^{-3}
,

for
h=10^{-4}
,
and
z_{h}=4.5
for
h=10^{-5}
.
The agreement between the theoretical estimate (

) and the empirical data is within the line thickness of the plot.

, which is bounded by

, see Fig.

3
Cauchy noise in a steep potential: Splitting schemes

To overcome the difficulties outlined above,
we propose an explicit
splitting
numerical scheme for simulating solutions of (
2.1
) that respects all the
moments of the true solution.

Our scheme is based on the well-known Lie–Trotter type approximations for semigroups, see, e.g.,
Section VIII.8 in
[
RS80
]
and

[
MQ02
]
for the general theory.
The idea behind this method can be explained as follows.
The process
X
is the Markov process with generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f(x)=\mathcal{A}f(x)+\mathcal{D}f(x),

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{A}
stands for the drift part

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x)=A(x)f^{\prime}(x)=-x^{9}f^{\prime}(x)

(3.2)

and

\mathcal{D}
stands for the noise part, in our example,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}f=-(-\Delta)^{1/2}f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(x+y)-2f(y)+f(x-y)}{y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(3.3)

The semigroup (propagator)
P
of
\mathcal{L}
is expressed in terms of the unknown process
X
started at
X_{0}=x
as

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x)=\mathbf{E}f(X_{t}(x))=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}f(x),

(3.4)

whereas the propagators of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
can be calculated directly, namely,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{A}}f(x)

\displaystyle=f(\Phi(t,x)),

(3.5)

where
\Phi=\Phi(t,x)
is the solution of the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}(t,x)

\displaystyle=A(\Phi(t,x)),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Phi(0,x)

\displaystyle=x,

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{D}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}f(x+Z_{t})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{t}{t^{2}+z^{2}}f(x+z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

(3.7)

Then, the Lie–Trotter approximation theorem (the Lie–Trotter product formula)
states that for each
t\in[0,\infty)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}=\mathrm{e}^{t(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{D})}=\lim_{h\to 0}(\mathrm{e}^{h\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{e}^{h\mathcal{D}})^{\frac{t}{h}},

(3.8)

see, e.g., Section 2.12 in
[
Var13
]
, Section 5.3 in
[
Kol11
]

or Section VIII.8 in
[
RS80
]
.

In other words, by taking
h
small enough such that
t=kh
, we can approximate the propagator of
X
by the
composition of propagators of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle P_{t}\approx P^{h}_{t}=(\mathrm{e}^{h\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{e}^{h\mathcal{D}})^{k}.

(3.9)

Equation (
3.9
) provides an approximation of
X
in terms of semigroups, i.e., in the weak sense.

We propose an analogue of the Lie–Trotter approximation (
3.9
) formulated directly
in terms of the processes, i.e., in the strong sense.

More precisely, we split the dynamics
X
into two parts: one following the integral curves of the ODE

\dot{\Phi}=A(\Phi)

and the other given by the noise
Z
.
In our example, since
A(x)=-x^{9}
, the solution of the ODE
(
3.6
)
can be found explicitly as

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x):=\frac{x}{\sqrt[8]{8tx^{8}+1}}.

(3.10)

Hence, we approximate the dynamics of
X
in two steps by means of the following
direct splitting
scheme:

\displaystyle X_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle:=x,

(3.11)

\displaystyle Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=X^{h}_{kh}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh},

\displaystyle X^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=\Phi(h,Y^{h}_{(k+1)h})=\frac{Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}}{\sqrt[8]{8h(Y^{h}_{(k+1)h})^{8}+1}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

We emphasize that the order of these operations is crucial. Since the function
x\mapsto\Phi(h,x)
is
bounded by
K_{h}:=1/\sqrt[8]{8h}
for
h\in(0,1]
, the scheme
\{X^{h}_{kh}\}
is bounded with probability one
and possesses all moments.

Moreover, all moments of order
q\in(0,9)
of the scheme converge to the corresponding moments
of the process
X
. More precisely, there is a convergence rate
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
T\in[0,\infty)

\displaystyle\sup_{kh\in[0,T]}\mathbf{E}|X^{h}_{kh}-X_{kh}|^{q}=\mathcal{O}(h^{\gamma}),\quad h\to 0,

(3.12)

see statements 2, 3, and 4 in Appendix
A
for a precise formulation. This result is illustrated
in Fig.
6
, where we observe exponentially fast convergence
of empirical moments
\langle|X^{h}_{kh}|^{q}\rangle

to the corresponding moments of the limit distribution
X_{\infty}
,
with the variance of the approximations increasing as the order

q
approaches the critical value
9
.

We note that the order of operators
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
in the classical Lie–Trotter approximations is arbitrary, and we
can also consider the
reverse splitting
approximations

\displaystyle P_{t}\approx\widehat{P}^{h}_{t}=(\mathrm{e}^{h\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{e}^{h\mathcal{A}})^{k},

(3.15)

that correspond to the
reverse splitting
scheme

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle:=x,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=\Phi(h,\widehat{X}^{h}_{kh}),

\displaystyle\widehat{X}^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{Y}^{h}_{(k+1)h}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

It is clear that the scheme (
3.16
) has the same structure as the tamed Euler scheme
(
2.12
), namely, it is given by a sum of a
bounded term
\widehat{Y}^{h}_{(k+1)h}=\Phi(h,X^{h}_{kh})
and an increment
Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}

which is integrable only up to order
q=1
. Therefore, the scheme
\widehat{X}^{h}
cannot capture the moments
of the solution
X
for orders
q\in[1,9)
.
The empirical absolute moments computed using the reverse splitting scheme
\widehat{X}^{h}

are presented in Fig.
7
, where they attain very large values for
q\in[1,9)
.

We note that a variant of the reverse splitting scheme (
3.16
) has been used in
[
DGNS07
]
,
where the authors also separated the noisy and deterministic dynamics and
cleverly combined the direct integration of the ODE
\dot{\Phi}=A(\Phi)
for large
x
with the
standard Euler approximation of the noisy part.

Empirical moments of the reverse splitting scheme
\widehat{X}^{h}
defined
in
3.16
for
h=10^{-5}
,
N=10^{6}
,
x=0
.
The dashed lines represent the corresponding moments of the limit distribution
X_{\infty}
.

At the end of this section, we note that the explicit Euler scheme
X^{\mathrm{E},h}
can be viewed as a first order
reduction of the splitting schemes
X^{h}
and
\widehat{X}^{h}
. Indeed, expanding the function
\Phi
in a Taylor series yields

\displaystyle\Phi(h,x)=x-x^{9}h+\frac{9x^{17}}{2}h^{2}-\frac{51x^{25}}{2}h^{3}+\dots.

(3.17)

Therefore, neglecting the higher order terms formally in the reverse splitting scheme (
3.16
) immediately recovers
the Euler scheme:

\displaystyle\widehat{X}^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle\approx\widehat{X}^{h}_{kh}-(\widehat{X}^{h}_{kh})^{9}h+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.18)

For the direct splitting scheme (
3.11
), recovering the Euler scheme requires two approximations:

\displaystyle X^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle\approx(X^{h}_{kh}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh})-(X^{h}_{kh}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh})^{9}h

(3.19)

\displaystyle\approx X^{h}_{kh}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}-(X^{h}_{kh})^{9}h.

Thus, one sees that the direct splitting scheme (
3.11
) treats noise in a more sofisticated, nonlinear manner.

We also emphasize that the general theory guarantees convergence
in probability
of all the schemes
(
2.6
), (
2.12
), (
3.11
), and (
3.16
).
However, among these, only the direct spitting scheme
(
3.11
) is free from explosion and successfully captures the higher moments of the process
X
.

4
Error analysis of a linear system

The second example focuses on the error analysis of a one-dimensional linear SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-MX_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\quad X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.1)

where the drift
A(x)=-Mx
a (minus) gradient of the parabolic confining potential
U(x)=\frac{M}{2}x^{2}
,
with
M\in(0,\infty)
, and the driving process is
an additive symmetric
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
Z
,
\alpha\in(0,2]
, with the characteristic function

\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda Z_{t}}=\mathrm{e}^{-t|\lambda|^{\alpha}}
. Note that when
\alpha=2
,
Z
corresponds to a Brownian motion with variance

\mathbf{E}Z_{t}^{2}=2t
.

First we note that the solution
X
of equation
(
4.1
)
is the Ortnstein–Uhlenbeck type process, which has the following form:

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mt}x+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-M(t-s)}\,\mathrm{d}Z_{s}.

(4.2)

In particular, for each
h\in(0,1]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have the following
exact
representation of the solution:

\displaystyle X_{kh}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}x+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{(j-1)h}^{jh}\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk+Ms}\,\mathrm{d}Z_{s}.

(4.3)

To study the splitting schemes, we find the solution of the ODE
\dot{\Phi}=-M\Phi
explicitly:

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-Mt},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\ t\in[0,\infty).

(4.4)

Then the Euler, direct and reverse splitting numerical approximations take the following form for the given initial
value
X_{0}^{\mathrm{E},h}=X_{0}^{h}=\widehat{X}_{0}^{h}=x
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

\displaystyle X_{(k+1)h}^{\mathrm{E},h}

\displaystyle=X_{kh}^{\mathrm{E},h}-MX_{kh}^{\mathrm{E},h}\cdot h+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh},

(4.5)

\displaystyle X_{(k+1)h}^{h}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mh}(X_{kh}^{h}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}),

(4.6)

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{(k+1)h}^{h}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mh}\widehat{X}_{kh}^{h}+Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}.

(4.7)

Since the approximations (
4.5
), (
4.6
), and (
4.7
)
are linear systems, we can find them explicitly:

\displaystyle X_{kh}^{\mathrm{E},h}

\displaystyle=(1-Mh)^{k}x+\sum_{j=1}^{k}(1-Mh)^{k-j}(Z_{jh}-Z_{(j-1)h}),

(4.8)

\displaystyle X_{kh}^{h}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}x+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-M(k-j+1)h}(Z_{jh}-Z_{(j-1)h}),

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{kh}^{h}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}x+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-M(k-j)h}(Z_{jh}-Z_{(j-1)h}).

These closed form formulae allow us to carry out the error analysis of the approximations. We begin with the direct
splitting scheme (
4.6
). Define the global error of the direct splitting scheme at time
t=kh
by

\displaystyle e^{h}_{kh}:=|X_{kh}-X_{kh}^{h}|.

(4.9)

Due to linearity, both the exact solution
X
and ist approximation
X^{h}
can be decomposed into the sum
of a deterministic part and a random part.
Accordingly, the global (cumulative) error also splits into two components:

e^{h,\mathrm{det}}
and
e^{h,\mathrm{ran}}
.
We first observe that the direct splitting scheme reproduces the deterministic solution

t\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{Mt}x
exactly on the discrete grid
\{kh\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, so the
deterministic component of the global error vanishes:

\displaystyle e^{h,\mathrm{det}}_{kh}=|\mathrm{e}^{-Mkh}x-\mathrm{e}^{-Mkh}x|\equiv 0.

(4.10)

The random global error for the direct splitting method (
4.6
) is computed as follows:

\displaystyle e^{h,\mathrm{ran}}_{kh}

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{0}^{kh}\mathrm{e}^{-M(kh-s)}\,\mathrm{d}Z_{s}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-M(k-j+1)h}(Z_{jh}-Z_{(j-1)h})\Big|

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{(j-1)h}^{jh}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-M(kh-s)}-\mathrm{e}^{-M(k-j+1)h}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}Z_{s}\Big|

\displaystyle=:\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{k}N_{k,j}^{h}\Big|.

All the summands
\{N_{k,j}^{h}\}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, on the right-hand side of (
4.11
)
are independent
\alpha
-stable random variables with the characteristic function

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda N_{k,j}^{h}}

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\mathrm{i}\lambda\int_{(j-1)h}^{jh}(\mathrm{e}^{-Mkh+Ms}-\mathrm{e}^{-M(k-j+1)h})\,\mathrm{d}Z_{s}\Big)

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-|\lambda|^{\alpha}\int_{(j-1)h}^{jh}|\mathrm{e}^{-Mkh+Ms}-\mathrm{e}^{-M(k-j+1)h}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-|\lambda|^{\alpha}h\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha Mhk}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha Mh(j-1)}\int_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{e}^{Mhu}-1)^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

In other words, each
N_{k,j}^{h}
is
\alpha
-stable distributed with the scale parameter

\displaystyle\sigma^{h}_{k,j}=h^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\mathrm{e}^{Mh(j-1)}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{e}^{Mhu}-1)^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(4.13)

so the random global error of the direct splitting method at time
kh
equals the absolute value of the
\alpha
-stable random variable
with the scale parameter

\displaystyle\sigma^{h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\sigma^{h}_{k,j})^{\alpha}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=h^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha Mhk}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha Mh}-1}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{e}^{Mhu}-1)^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(4.14)

\displaystyle e^{h,\mathrm{ran}}_{kh}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\sigma^{h}_{kh}|\xi|,\quad\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda\xi}=\mathrm{e}^{-|\lambda|^{\alpha}}.

For
\alpha=1
(Cauchy noise) and
\alpha=2
(Gaussian noise), the function

u\mapsto(\mathrm{e}^{Mhu}-1)^{\alpha}
can be integrated explicitly, yielding
closed form expressions for
\sigma^{h}_{kh}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{Mhk}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-1-Mh}{M},\quad\alpha=1,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\sigma^{h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2Mhk}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2Mh}-1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2Mh}-4\mathrm{e}^{Mh}+2Mh+3}{2Mh}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\alpha=2.

(4.16)

Analogously, we obtain the global error of the reverse splitting method (
4.7
):

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=h^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha Mhk}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha Mh}-1}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-\mathrm{e}^{Mhu})^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(4.17)

\displaystyle\widehat{e}^{h,\mathrm{ran}}_{kh}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\widehat{\sigma}^{h}_{kh}|\xi|,\quad\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda\xi}=\mathrm{e}^{-|\lambda|^{\alpha}},

and, in particular,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{Mhk}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-1}\frac{Mh\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-\mathrm{e}^{Mh}+1}{M},\quad\alpha=1,

(4.18)

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=h^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2Mhk}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2Mh}-1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2Mh}(2Mh-3)+4\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-1}{2Mh}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\alpha=2.

(4.19)

The deterministic component of the global error for the Euler approximations satisfies the following relation:

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{E},h,\mathrm{det}}_{kh}

\displaystyle=|\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}-(1-Mh)^{k}||x|.

(4.20)

It is evident, that the Euler approximation is valid only for
Mh\in(0,1)
; in particular for
Mh\in(1,\infty)
, the deterministic
Euler approximation exhibits a sign-alternating instability.

For the random component of the global error of the Euler method, we have the following equality:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{(j-1)h}^{jh}\Big|\mathrm{e}^{-M(kh-s)}-(1-Mh)^{k-j}\Big|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(4.21)

however, unfortunately, a closed-form expression for the scale parameter
\sigma^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}

cannot be obtained, except in the case
\alpha=2
,
where we get

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{E},h}_{kh}=\Big(\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2hkM}}{2M}-\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{-Mhk}(\mathrm{e}^{Mh}-1)(\mathrm{e}^{Mhk}-(1-Mh)^{k})}{M(\mathrm{e}^{Mh}+Mh-1)}+\frac{1-(1-Mh)^{2k}}{M(2-Mh)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.22)

The approximation errors exhibit different behaviors as functions of
kh
depending on whether
M
is small or large.
In Fig.
8
, we compare the scale parameters of the global errors for
\alpha=1
, with step size
h=0.001
,
under two confinement regimes: a moderate confinement
M=1
and a strong confinement
M=2000
. In the latter case, the
original SDE (
4.1
) is ill-conditioned in the computational sense, i.e.,
stiff
, see
[
Mir81
]
.

For
M=1
the scale parameters
\sigma^{h}
and
\widehat{\sigma}^{h}
are
computed using the formulae (
4.15
) and (
4.18
), respectively, while the scale parameter

\sigma^{\mathrm{E},h}
is obtained numerically from the formula (
4.21
).
In all three cases, the error scale parameters exhibit a comparable order of magnitude,

\mathcal{O}(h)
.

In the stiff regime,
M=2000
, the Euler method fails to converge, whereas the global errors of the splitting schemes
stabilize exponentially fast about a level of order
\mathcal{O}(h)
.

In summary, the splitting
schemes should be preferred as the effective choice for simulating systems in the stiff regime.

4.7
)
for
\alpha=1
(Cauchy noise) for
M=1
(left) and
M=2000
(right).

5
The direct splitting scheme in a general setting

Finally, we provide a formulation of the direct splitting scheme (
3.11
), (
4.6
) in a general setting.

We solve numerically a
d
-dimensional SDE with multiplicative noise:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=A(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+a(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+c(X_{t-})\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\quad X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5.1)

The process
B=(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, and

Z
is an independent
d
-dimensional pure jump Lévy process with the characteristic function

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle Z_{t},\lambda\rangle}=\exp\Big(t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle\lambda,z\rangle}-1-\mathrm{i}\langle\lambda,z\rangle\mathbb{I}(\|z\|\leq 1)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(5.2)

We assume that the function
A
is
C^{2}
-smooth, one-sided Lipshitz continuous and

\kappa
-confining for
\kappa\in(1,\infty)
, the coefficients
a
,
b
, and
c

are bounded and Lipschitz continuous, and for some
p\in(0,\infty)

the Lévy process
Z
has a finite
p
-th moment.

Our direct splitting numerical scheme has the following form. Let
\Phi=\Phi(t,x)
be the solution of the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}(t,x)

\displaystyle=A(\Phi(t,x)),

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Phi(0,x)

\displaystyle=x.

Then we set

\displaystyle X_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle:=x,

(5.4)

\displaystyle Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=X^{h}_{kh}+a(X^{h}_{kh})h+b(X^{h}_{kh})(B_{(k+1)h}-B_{kh})+c(X^{h}_{kh})(Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}),

\displaystyle X^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=\Phi(h,Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

In other words, at the intermediate step
Y^{h}
we calculate
the Euler–Maruyama approximation of the random dynamics with bounded coefficients and then, in the second step, we
apply the deterministic
flow
\Phi
to the intermediate result.

The numerical scheme (
5.4
) exhibits the following tail-preserving behaviour.
Under some additional technical assumptions that can be found in Appendix
A
,
for any
q\in(0,p+\kappa-1)
the convergence

\displaystyle\sup_{kh\in[0,T]}\mathbf{E}\|X_{kh}^{h}-X_{kh}\|^{q}=\mathcal{O}(h^{\gamma}),\quad h\to 0,

(5.5)

holds,
with a certain positive convergence order
\gamma
.

A detailed mathematical analysis of convergence is available in
[
AKP25
]
. We refer the reader to
Appendix
A
for the complete set of mathematical assumptions and the analysis of the convergence rates.

Finally, we emphasize that the direct splitting scheme (
5.4
) can be equally well
applied to Gaussian SDEs (i.e., for
c(\cdot)\equiv 0
). In this case, due to the action of the confining drift
the marginal distributions of the process
X
become
sub-Gaussian
, i.e., their probability tails are lighter than Gaussian
ones, see Chapter 1 in
[
BK00
]
.
The scheme (
5.4
) effectively captures these very light tails, see Appendix
A
.

6
Ready-to-use examples

In this section, we provide several explicit formulae for the function
\Phi
.

1. Linear drift.
Let
M
be a real
d\times d
-matrix, and define the linear drift
A(x)=Mx
.
Then,
A
is Lipschitz continuous. Moreover, if all eigenvalues of
M
have negative real parts,
A
is also confining.
The linear ODE
\dot{\Phi}=M\Phi
has the solution
\Phi(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{Mt}x
, where the matrix exponential
can be computed using standard linear algebra techniques.

This type of random dynamics describes, for example, the motion of a random charged particle in an external magnetic field;
see
[
CG00
,
CGS02
]
.

The advantages of the splitting schemes over the Euler method in the linear (globally Lipschitz case) have been already
discussed in Section
4
.

2. Symmetric steep one-well potential,
d=1
.
For
c\in(0,\infty)
and
\kappa\in(2,\infty)
, let
U(x)=\frac{c}{\kappa+1}|x|^{1+\kappa}
, so that

\displaystyle A(x)=-U^{\prime}(x)=-c|x|^{\kappa}\operatorname{sign}(x).

(6.1)

In this case, the ODE
\dot{\Phi}(t,x)=A(\Phi(t,x))
has the closed form solution

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{\Big(c(\kappa-1)t|x|^{\kappa-1}+1\Big)^{\frac{1}{\kappa-1}}},

(6.2)

compare with (
3.10
) for
\kappa=9
.
The condition
\kappa\in(2,\infty)
is required for
C^{2}
-smoothness of
A
.

Random dynamics in such potentials have been studied, for example, in

[
CGK+02
,
CGK+04
,
GŻ20b
,
CKG+03
,
DS07
]
.
By choosing
\kappa
large, we can effectively approximate the so-called infinite potentials; see

[
DHH08
,
GŻ20a
,
KDD+16
,
PD24
,
DZ23
]
.

3. Symmetric steep double-well potential,
d=1
.
For
c\in(0,\infty)
and
\kappa\in(2,\infty)
, let
U(x)=\frac{c}{\kappa+1}|x|^{1+\kappa}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}x^{2}
, so that

\displaystyle A(x)=-c|x|^{\kappa}\operatorname{sign}(x)+c_{1}x.

(6.3)

If
c_{1}\in(0,\infty)
,
A
is a gradient of a symmetric double-well potential with a saddle at
x=0
and local minima at

x=\pm\big(\frac{c_{1}}{c}\big)^{\frac{1}{\kappa-1}}
. The mapping
\Phi
has the explicit form

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle c_{1}^{\frac{1}{\kappa-1}}x\Big(c|x|^{\kappa-1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}t(\kappa-1)})+c_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}t(\kappa-1)}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{\kappa-1}},\quad&\displaystyle x\neq\pm\Big(\frac{c_{1}}{c}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\kappa-1}},\\
x,&\displaystyle x=\pm\Big(\frac{c_{1}}{c}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\kappa-1}}.\end{cases}

(6.4)

In particualar, for
\kappa=3
,
the drift
A(x)=-cx^{3}+c_{1}x
,
c>0
,
c_{1}>0
, corresponds to the stochastic Ginzburg–Landau equation, see Eq. (4.52) in

[
KP95
]
. We mention here the works
[
Dit99
,
BS02
,
CGK+04
,
Pav07a
,
dAD25
]
.

4. Symmetric steep one-well potential with “quadratic” minumum,
d=1
. As in the previous example, we chose

c_{1}\in(-\infty,0)
, so that

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x)=|c_{1}|^{\frac{1}{\kappa-1}}x\Big(c|x|^{\kappa-1}\mathrm{e}^{|c_{1}|(\kappa-1)t}-1)+|c_{1}|\mathrm{e}^{|c_{1}|(\kappa-1)t}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{\kappa-1}}.

(6.5)

In this context, see
[
CGKM05b
,
Lin08
,
CD19
]
.

5. Asymmetric steep potentials,
d=1
. Assume that

\displaystyle U(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\kappa+1}|x|^{1+\kappa}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}x^{2}-c_{0}x,

(6.6)

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=-c|x|^{\kappa}\operatorname{sign}(x)+c_{1}x+c_{0},

with
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. The ODE
\dot{\Phi}=A(\Phi)
cannot be solved explicitly for
c_{0}\neq 0
, but we can use our scheme
effectively assigning
A(x):=-c|x|^{\kappa}\operatorname{sign}(x)+c_{1}x
and
a(x):=c_{0}
.
This setting is particularly useful for analyzing bifurcations of the solution
X
;
see
[
CKG+03
,
DGN09
,
XLF+13
,
LRPK25
]
.

6. Nonlinear friction, see
[
CGKM05b
,
Lin08
,
EG15
,
KP19
]
.
Consider a random motion of a one-dimensional particle subject to a non-linear friction force

A(v)
that depends on the particle’s velocity
v
. Its dynamics is given by the kinetic Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=v,

(6.7)

\displaystyle\dot{v}

\displaystyle=A(v)+\mathrm{noise}.

Clearly, the drift
\mathbf{A}(x,y)=(v,A(v))
of the two-dimensional system
satisfies all the assumptions that hold for
A
.
The solution of the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Phi}}(t,x,v)=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{\Phi}(t,x,v))

(6.8)

is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}(t,x,v)=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle x+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(s,v)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
\Phi(t,v)\end{pmatrix}.

(6.9)

For the power drift
A(v)=-c|v|^{\kappa}\operatorname{sign}(v)
from Example 2 we can find that

\displaystyle x+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(s,v)\,\mathrm{d}s=x+\frac{|v|^{2-\kappa}}{c(\kappa-2)}\Big((ct(\kappa-1)|v|^{\kappa-1}+1)^{\frac{\kappa-2}{\kappa-1}}-1\Big)\operatorname{sign}(v).

(6.10)

7. Random motion in a rough potential,
d=1
.
Here it is assumed that the drift
A+a
is a gradient of a potential
U

\displaystyle U(x)=U_{0}(x)+U_{1}(x),

(6.11)

where
U_{0}
is a quadratic or steep potential of the type considered in the previous examples, and
U_{1}
is an
oscillatory perturbation composed of terms like
\cos(\omega x)
,
\sin(\omega x)
, with various frequences
\omega
,
see

[
Zwa88
,
LXKY16
,
DZ23
]
.
In this case we set
A(x)=-U^{\prime}_{0}(x)
and
a(x)=-U^{\prime}_{1}(x)
, and then apply the direct splitting scheme accordingly.

8. Rotationally invariant
d
-dimensional potential.
Let
U
be a steep one-dimensional potential, so that
\mathbf{U}(x)=U(\|x\|)
is a steep rotationally invariant potential,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(x)=-\nabla U(\|x\|)=-(U^{\prime}(\|x\|)x^{1},\ldots,U^{\prime}(\|x\|)x^{d}).

(6.12)

Let
\Phi(t,r)
be the solution of the one-dimensional ODE
\dot{\Phi}=-U^{\prime}(\Phi)
,
\Phi(0,r)=r\in[0,\infty)
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}(t,x)=\Phi(t,\|x\|)\frac{x}{\|x\|}

(6.13)

is the deterministic flow generated by the drift
\mathbf{A}
.

9. Diagonal
d
-dimensional potential.
Assume that

\mathbf{A}(x)=(A_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,A_{d}(x_{d}))
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}(t,x)=(\Phi_{1}(t,x^{1}),\dots,\Phi_{d}(t,x^{d})).

(6.14)

10. In some applications, the drift can be expressed as a sum of three terms:

A(x)+Mx+a(x)
, where
A
is a superlinearly growing confining function,
a
is bounded, and
M
is a constant matrix.
Clearly, the linear term
Mx
can be absorbed into
A
, so our theory still applies. However, especially in multidimensional
systems it is not always possible to find a solution to the
ODE
\dot{\varphi}=A(\varphi)+M\varphi
, while the solution of the simpler ODE
\dot{\Phi}=A(\Phi)
can often be computed explicitly.
In this case, we propose applying an additional splitting to the deterministic flow.
Let
\Phi
be the solution of the ODE
\dot{\Phi}=A(\Phi)
and
\Psi(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{Mt}x
be the solution of the linear ODE

\dot{\Psi}=M\Psi
.
Then we modify our scheme to obtain:

\displaystyle X_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle:=x,

(6.15)

\displaystyle Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=X^{h}_{kh}+a(X^{h}_{kh})h+b(X^{h}_{kh})(B_{(k+1)h}-B_{kh})+c(X^{h}_{kh})(Z_{(k+1)h}-Z_{kh}),

\displaystyle X^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{e}^{Mh}\Phi(h,Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

or

\displaystyle X^{h}_{(k+1)h}

\displaystyle:=\Phi(h,\mathrm{e}^{Mh}Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(6.16)

In both cases, it is essential that the random intermediate step
Y^{h}_{(k+1)h}
is evaluated before applying the flow
\Phi
.

For example, this type of splitting can be used to simulate a two-dimensional system exhibiting dissipative synchronization,
see

[
CKN08
,
LDLK10
]
, given by the equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}=A_{1}(x)+\mu(y-x)+\mathrm{noise},

(6.17)

\displaystyle\dot{y}=A_{2}(y)+\mu(x-y)+\mathrm{noise}.

The functions
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
here are steep potentials as in Examples 2. or 4.,
and the linear term is given by the matrix

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}-\mu&\mu\\
\mu&-\mu\end{pmatrix},\quad\mu\in(0,\infty).

(6.18)

Then, the matrix exponential can be computed explicitly as

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{Mt}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\mu t}&1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\mu t}\\
1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\mu t}&1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\mu t}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(6.19)

Appendix B
Simulation of some heavy tailed Lévy processes

In this Appendix we collect several methods for simulation of increments of
a heavy-tailed Lévy process
Z
.

1. Simulation of 1-dimensional
\alpha
-stable Lévy processes.
One dimensional
\alpha
-stable random variables can be simulated with the help of the methods developed in

[
IC59
,
Zol64
,
CMS76
]
, see Sections 1.7 and 3.3.3 in
[
Nol20
]
.

A one-dimensional symmetric
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
Z
with the scale parameter

\sigma\in(0,\infty)
has the characteristic function

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda Z_{t}}=\mathrm{e}^{-t\sigma^{\alpha}|\lambda|^{\alpha}}

(B.1)

and can be simulated as

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\sigma t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{\sin(\alpha U)}{(\cos U)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\cos(U-\alpha U)}{E}\Big)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}},

(B.2)

and, in particular, for
\alpha=1
,

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\sigma t\tan U,

(B.3)

where

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\sim U\Big[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\Big],

(B.4)

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1).

In general, the characteristic function of
Z_{t}
(in the so-called 1-parameterization) has the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda Z_{t}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\exp\big(-t\sigma^{\alpha}|\lambda|^{\alpha}\big[1-\mathrm{i}\beta\tan(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2})\operatorname{sign}\lambda\big]\big),\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2),\\
\displaystyle\exp\big(-t\sigma|\lambda|\big[1+\mathrm{i}\beta\frac{2}{\pi}\ln|\lambda|\operatorname{sign}\lambda\big]\big),\quad\alpha=1.\end{cases}

(B.5)

For
\alpha\neq 1
and
\beta\in[-1,1]
we define

\displaystyle\theta_{0}:=\frac{\arctan(\beta\tan(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}))}{\alpha}

(B.6)

and we get

\displaystyle Z_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sigma t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{\sin(\alpha(U+\theta_{0}))}{(\cos(\alpha\theta_{0})\cdot\cos(U))^{1/\alpha}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\cos(\alpha\theta_{0}+(\alpha-1)U))}{E}\Big)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}},\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2),\\
\displaystyle\sigma t\frac{2}{\pi}\Big[\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\beta U\Big)\tan U-\beta\ln\Big(\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}E\cos U}{\frac{\pi}{2}+\beta U}\Big)\Big],\quad\alpha=1.\end{cases}

(B.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda Z_{t}}=\exp\Big(t\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda z}-1)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=\Big(\frac{c_{-}}{|z|^{1+\alpha_{-}}}\mathbb{I}(z\leq-\sigma_{-})+\frac{c_{+}}{z^{1+\alpha_{+}}}\mathbb{I}(z\geq\sigma_{+})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}z,

with

where

\displaystyle c=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=\frac{c_{-}}{\alpha_{-}\sigma_{-}^{\alpha_{-}}}+\frac{c_{+}}{\alpha_{+}\sigma_{+}^{\alpha_{+}}}\in(0,\infty),

and independent jumps

\displaystyle J=\begin{cases}-J_{-},\quad\text{ with probability }p_{-}=\frac{c_{-}}{\lambda\alpha_{-}\sigma_{-}^{\alpha_{-}}},\\
J_{+},\quad\text{ with probability }p_{+}\frac{c_{+}}{\lambda\alpha_{+}\sigma_{+}^{\alpha_{+}}},\\

with
