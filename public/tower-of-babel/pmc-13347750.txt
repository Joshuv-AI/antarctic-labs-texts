Unveiling the Functional Role of KIF21B in Microglia Inflammatory Response
Authors: Danny Ganchala, Alvaro Bautista‐Abad, Daniel Pinto‐Benito, Julia Simon‐Areces, Daniela Grassi, Francesco Paolo Ulloa Severino, Maria‐Angeles Arevalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicroglia are brain immune cells that maintain homeostasis and respond to injury, changing cell morphology to drive inflammation, migration, and phagocytosis. This study examined the role of the kinesin KIF21B in microglial activation, demonstrating for the first time its expression in microglial cells in two in vivo neuroinflammatory models: TBI (focal inflammation) and LPS administration (diffuse inflammation). While TBI provoked a significant increase in KIF21B/Iba1 colocalization in the tissue around the lesion exclusively in females, LPS administration did not alter KIF21B expression in either sex. Given the importance of cytoskeleton remodeling for microglial migration and phagocytosis, this work investigates whether KIF21B contributes to these actions. Downregulating KIF21B in primary cultured mouse microglia had sex‐specific effects. In females, KIF21B silencing reduced both migratory capacity and phagocytosis of E. coli ‐coated spheres and neuronal debris. In males, it exacerbated migration and selectively increased neuronal debris phagocytosis, while E. coli ‐coated sphere uptake remained unaffected. These functional differences were accompanied by sex‐dependent morphological alterations, quantified through area, circularity, Feret's diameter, and perimeter: KIF21B silencing blocked the transition to amoeboid morphology in females while inducing hyperpolarized elongation in males. Finally, LPS treatment increased KIF21B colocalization with microtubules and reduced its colocalization with F‐actin in females, while neither interaction was significantly altered in males. Overall, the findings suggest that KIF21B regulates microglial function in a sex‐dependent manner through its effects on cytoskeletal organization.

Introduction

Kinesin 21B (KIF21B) is a plus‐end‐directed kinesin that belongs to the kinesin‐4 family and is expressed exclusively in the brain, spleen, and testes. In the central nervous system, KIF21B is expressed in the hippocampus, cerebellum, cortex, spinal cord, retina, and olfactory bulb (Ghiretti et al. 2016; Labonté et al. 2013). In addition to its localization in neurons, KIF21B has been detected in other brain cells such as oligodendrocyte precursors and astrocytes (Dugas et al. 2006; Kreft et al. 2014). The expression of KIF21B in microglial cells has not been previously characterized; however, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of microglia obtained from mouse brains have revealed the presence of this kinesin with sex differences in its expression in the hippocampus (Guneykaya et al. 2018).

In neurons, KIF21B is particularly enriched in dendrites, where it plays a fundamental role in the development and maintenance of dendritic spines and synaptic function. The importance of this protein has been evidenced through studies in knockout mice, where its absence causes significant alterations in neuronal morphology, including a reduction in dendritic branching and a decrease in spine density. These structural alterations are accompanied by deficiencies in synaptic transmission in hippocampal neurons, resulting in deficits in learning and memory, especially in processes such as spatial memory acquisition and fear conditioning (Muhia et al. 2016).

At the molecular level, KIF21B executes several critical regulatory functions. One of its main functions is the regulation of retrograde transport of BDNF–TrkB signaling complexes, which depends on neuronal activity (Ghiretti et al. 2016). Additionally, it participates in the clearance of ELMO1 from dendritic spines, an essential process for long‐term depression (LTD) (Morikawa et al. 2018). The interaction of KIF21B with the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM3 is also relevant, as it regulates the transport of the GABAaRγ2 receptor to the cell surface (Labonté et al. 2013).

Furthermore, KIF21B has been shown to participate in the transport of monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) to the myelin membrane, a critical process for the metabolic support that oligodendrocytes provide to axons (Chapple et al. 2024; Kreft et al. 2014). This transport function is fundamental for maintaining balance in synaptic transmission and ensuring neuronal survival. In addition, KIF21B controls microtubule dynamics in neurons (Muhia et al. 2016) and regulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics through direct binding (Gromova et al. 2023; Rivera Alvarez et al. 2023).

KIF21B also plays significant functions in astrocytes, where its expression increases significantly during astrocytic activation. Elevated levels of KIF21B correlate with GFAP expression in multiple sclerosis (MS) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) lesions, suggesting an important role in astrocyte‐mediated pathology in these diseases (Kreft et al. 2014). Despite these findings in astrocytes and the well‐characterized neuronal functions, the role of KIF21B in microglia, the primary immune effector cells of the brain, has not been investigated.

Microglia are a population of cells that constitute the immune system of the brain. Normally, microglia are found in a resting state, where they are responsible for surveilling the CNS to maintain homeostasis, regulating synaptic plasticity and neuronal activity, and extending and retracting their ramifications over large areas of the brain. This is possible due to a variety of surface pattern‐recognition receptors, PRRs, known as the sensome (Nimmerjahn et al. 2005; Hickman et al. 2013). In response to detected damage, microglia rapidly migrate unidirectionally toward the injury site (Nimmerjahn et al. 2005), thereby limiting damage spread. Upon activation, they undergo morphological transformation accompanied by alterations in gene expression and subcellular transcript organization, acquiring functional capacities including phagocytosis of cellular debris and protein aggregates, tissue repair, and cytokine release (Colton and Wilcock 2010; Henze et al. 2026).

Phagocytosis is a dynamic and versatile cellular process, dependent on actin (Aderem and Underhill 1999), mediated by receptors, through which microglia recognize, engulf, and digest particles larger than 0.5 μm (Sierra et al. 2013). These particles include apoptotic cells, cellular and myelin debris, protein aggregates, invading microorganisms, and synapses of living neurons marked with complement (Maguire et al. 2022). This process is a component of the innate and adaptive immune response to pathogens, thus constituting the first line of protection against infection. Furthermore, phagocytosis is established as an indispensable process to facilitate the repair of neural circuits in the tissue after damage to the CNS. Although KIF21B has not previously been linked to microglial function, other kinesins have been implicated in regulating macrophage morphology, migration, and phagocytosis. Large‐scale proteomic analyses revealed a significant increase in KIF5B association with microtubules following macrophage activation, identifying KIF5B as a major microtubule motor involved in directing vesicles to active regions of the plasma membrane during phagocytosis (Silver and Harrison 2011). Furthermore, macrophage activation promotes both microtubule stabilization and kinesin recruitment, thereby facilitating the efficient vesicular transport required for cell migration (Chapple et al. 2024; Kreft et al. 2014; Hanania et al. 2012).

Building on these findings, we hypothesize that KIF21B plays a critical regulatory role in microglial activation during neuroinflammatory processes. Specifically, we propose that KIF21B acts as a key mediator of the cytoskeletal reorganization required for microglial migration toward inflammatory stimuli and for the efficient phagocytosis of cellular debris and protein aggregates. To investigate this hypothesis, we first examined KIF21B expression in microglia using two in vivo models of neuroinflammation: traumatic brain injury (TBI), representing a focal inflammatory model, and systemic LPS administration, mimicking infection‐induced diffuse neuroinflammation. These models elicit fundamentally distinct inflammatory responses. In the LPS model, microglial activation is primarily characterized by the release of cytokines and chemokines (Sharma et al. 2021). In contrast, the TBI model induces robust microglial accumulation at the lesion site, accompanied by pronounced morphological changes that enable migration and phagocytosis of cellular debris, functions that require extensive cytoskeletal remodeling (Hernandez‐Ontiveros et al. 2013; Nimmerjahn et al. 2005). To further assess the specific contribution of KIF21B to these aspects of microglial function, we used an in vitro inflammatory model consisting of LPS‐treated primary microglial cultures, a widely employed system for studying microglial activation processes (Gumbs et al. 2022).

Methods

Animals

The mice used for this study were bred in the animal facility of the Cajal Institute. The animals received water and food ad libitum. They were maintained under ambient temperature conditions at 22°C ± 2°C and in a light/dark period of 12 h each. The procedures for the care, welfare, and appropriate use of all experimental animals followed the European Council and Parliament Directive (2010/63/EU), Spanish Regulation (R.D. 53/2013 and Law 6/2013, June 11), and were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of the Cajal Institute and by the Ministry of Environment and Territory (Community of Madrid, PROEX 059.5/21).

Systemic Inflammation Model With LPS

C57‐BL6J mice of 3 months of age of both sexes were used. They were injected intraperitoneally with two doses of LPS (1 mg/kg in saline solution; isotype 055‐B5, L2880, Sigma‐Aldrich), which has been demonstrated to produce neuroinflammation (Pinto‐Benito et al. 2022; Hasel and Liddelow 2021; Tapia‐Gonzalez et al. 2008) following the procedure shown in Figure 1A. Control animals received the same volume of saline solution. After 7 days, the animals were sacrificed, perfused, and brains were processed for histology.

Impact‐Induced Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) Model

C57‐BL6J mice of 3 months of age of both sexes were used to establish a TBI model in mice using the controlled cortical impact (CCI) method, as we previously published (Pinto‐Benito et al. 2025). Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction at 5% and maintenance at 2% in O2) and placed on a stereotaxic apparatus (David Kopf Instruments) with a thermal blanket at 37°C. After disinfection of the mouse's head, the skull was exposed with a sagittal incision of approximately 1.5 cm along the midline of the upper surface of the animal's head. The head was leveled, the cranial fissures were located, and the injury site was marked according to the coordinates of the mouse brain atlas (Paxinos and Franklin 2013): bregma −1.5 mm anteroposterior (AP), −1.5 mm mediolateral (ML), over the dorsal hippocampus of the left hemisphere (Figure 1B). The craniotomy was performed with a drill and a 2.3 mm bit, taking care not to damage the meninges. After exposing and cleaning the brain surface, the 2 mm piston from the impactor system (Impact One, MyNeuroLab, Leica) was placed on the exposed brain, contacting the surface. The firing pin was retracted, and the piston was lowered to the required depth of 1 mm. The impact was then performed at the determined coordinates, with a depth of 1 mm, at a velocity of 3.6 m/s, and a latency of 0.1 s. Once the lesion was made, the blood was cleaned, and the skin was closed with a 5/0 silk suture. After 7 days, the animals were sacrificed, perfused, and the tissue was processed for histology.

Immunohistochemistry

Seven days after injury or 7 days after the first intraperitoneal injection with LPS, animals were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg body weight, Dolethal, Vetoquinol) and perfused through the left cardiac ventricle, first with pre‐warmed (37°C) 0.9% NaCl and then for 5 min with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4; 37°C). Brains were post‐fixed for 4 h at 4°C in the same fixative and washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4 at room temperature. Coronal sections of the brain, 50 μm thick, were obtained using a Vibratome (VT 1000S, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Immunohistochemistry experiments were carried out on free‐floating sections in 24‐well plates with gentle agitation. All washes were performed 3 times with potassium‐supplemented PBS buffer, KPBS (5% PB 0.2 M pH 7.4, 0.8% NaCl, 0.02% KCl). Permeabilization, blocking, and incubation of primary antibodies were performed in KPBS buffer supplemented with triton, gelatin, and 5% normal donkey serum, KPGT (KPBS buffer, 0.36% gelatin, 0.5% triton X‐100). Subsequently, primary antibodies rabbit α‐KIF21B (1:350; HPA027249; Sigma‐Aldrich) and goat α‐Iba1 (1:1000; 011‐27,991; Fujifilm, Wako) were incubated at 4°C overnight with agitation. Then, the sections were washed with KPBS and the secondary antibodies donkey α‐rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000; 711‐545‐152; Jackson Inmuno‐Research) and donkey α‐goat IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000; Ab150132, Abcam) were incubated in KPBS for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the sections were washed and mounted with gerbatol and DAPI (4′, 6‐diamino‐2‐phenylindole; Calbiochem, #268289) on gelatinized slides.

Images were acquired on a Leica TCS‐SP5 confocal microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective and a resolution of 2048 × 2048 pixels, 8 bits, a speed of 4 Hz, and serial planes of 1 μm, using LASX software. For colocalization analyses of KIF21B in microglia, at least 9 photographs of the cortex from each animal (four animals per experimental group) were taken, covering the lower and upper regions of CA1, of the dorsal hippocampus, from the beginning of the cortex to the hippocampus, in equivalent histological sections between animals, in both in vivo inflammation models (Figure 1C). These images were processed using FIJI software (ImageJ 1.52n, National Institutes of Health).

Primary Microglia Cultures

Primary microglial cultures were prepared following the modified shake‐off protocol of (McCarthy and de Vellis 1980), as previously described (Molina‐Holgado et al. 2002). Briefly, CD1 strain mice were sexed between postnatal days 0 and 2. Animal sexing was performed by observing the anogenital distance. The cortices of the mice were dissected in HBSS, removing the hippocampus and meninges. After several washes with HBSS, the tissue was dissociated in complete DMEM (Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium 1×, 31053‐028, Gibco), which consisted of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum Heat Inactivated, 10500‐064, Gibco), 10% HS (Horse Serum Heat Inactivated, 26050‐088, Gibco), and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic. The cells were seeded in 75 cm2 flasks (353135, Corning) that were previously treated with 10ug/mL of poly‐L‐lysine (P2636, Sigma‐Aldrich). This mixed glial cell culture was allowed to grow for 10 days at 37°C and 5% CO2 in an incubator (RS Biotech Galaxy S). On the sixth day, half of the medium in the flasks was replaced with fresh medium. On day 10, the flasks were shaken on an orbital shaker at 230 RPM (Infors HT) and 37°C overnight.

The supernatant from the flasks containing all the cells that detached from the surface (mostly microglial cells) was collected. The surface of the flasks was carefully washed with 5 mL of PBS, which was added to the rest of the supernatant. The cells were centrifuged for 10 min at 340 g at room temperature (Rotina 46‐R, Hettich). The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in complete DMEM, then counted in a Neubauer chamber and seeded according to the experiment to obtain a > 98% pure microglial culture according to OX‐42 staining.

Migration Study Through Transwell (Chemotaxis)

Microglia were electroporated and seeded on the transwell membrane (Transwell permeable supports, 6.5 mm insert, 24‐well plate, 8.0 μm polycarbonate membrane, 3422, Costar) at a density of 500 cells/mm2. After 2 DIV, LPS (0.2 μg/mL) was added to the medium in the lower chamber, and the cells were incubated overnight. The next day, the insert was washed twice with PBS, and the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min. Subsequently, the inserts were washed again with PBS, and the upper part of the membrane was cleaned with a swab moistened with DMEM medium without additives to remove cells that had not migrated. Finally, the membranes were removed from the insert by cutting them with a scalpel and mounted on a slide using gerbatol with DAPI as mounting medium.

The number of transfected cells that migrated was quantified by calculating the percentage of GFP+ cells with respect to the total number of migrated cells (DAPI‐positive nuclei), thereby normalizing for variability in transfection efficiency between independent experiments. To evaluate the specific effect of KIF21B downregulation on migration, KIF21B‐siRNA transfected cells were compared against non‐target siRNA transfected cells under identical transfection conditions, controlling for any non‐specific effects of the transfection procedure itself.

RNA Extraction, Reverse Transcription, and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted and purified (Illustra RNAspin Mini RNA isolation kit, 25‐0500‐72, GE Healthcare) and quantified by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop One, ND‐1000, Thermofisher). 1 μg of RNA per sample was reverse transcribed to cDNA (complementary DNA) in 20 μL reaction using M‐MLV reverse transcriptase (Moloney murine leukemia virus, M1705, Promega) and deoxynucleotides (dNTPs, 11922505001, Roche), following the manufacturer's instructions in a thermocycler (Eppendorf 5332). The RNA was incubated with random primers for 5 min at 70°C for denaturation, followed by cooling on ice. Subsequently, 5× reaction buffer, dNTPs, and reverse transcriptase were added and incubated at 37°C for reverse transcription. The reaction was stopped by inactivating the enzyme, heating at 70°C for 15 min.

The qPCR reactions were performed on the 7500 Real‐Time PCR System using SYBR Green Universal PCR Master Mix (4367659, Applied Biosystems) or TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (4369016, Applied Biosystems). The cDNA was amplified in a reaction volume of 15 μL, with each reaction containing 5 μL of sample and 10 μL of the mixture of Master Mix and primers (forward, FW and reverse, RV). The primers (Table 2) were designed using the digital application Primer‐Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (Applied Biosystems), selecting primer pairs that span an exon‐exon junction to restrict amplification of genomic DNA impurities. The TaqMan probes for the primers corresponding to KIF21B were commercially acquired. Real‐time PCRs were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, followed by 1 min at 60°C). For all primers, amplification with 95%–100% efficiency was verified by performing a curve with four calibration points, with a slope of −3.2. The optimal concentration of the samples was selected based on C t values between 18 and 32 cycles. All reactions were performed in duplicate and from at least four different cultures. The relative quantification of mRNA expression was determined using the Ct method, using the BestKeeper index (Pfaffl et al. 2004), calculated for each sample from the values of 18S rRNA (18 Svedberg ribosomal RNA) and RPL13a (Ribosomal Protein L13A) as housekeeping control genes. Male control samples were used as the reference group.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were fixed for 20 min at room temperature in 4% PFA previously warmed to 37°C. They were washed and permeabilized for 4 min with 0.12% Triton‐X in 0.12% gelatin/PBS. They were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in PBS/gelatin and incubated for another hour with the primary antibodies rabbit anti‐KIF21B (1:350; HPA027249; Sigma‐Aldrich) and mouse anti‐α‐tubulin (1:1000; T5168; Sigma‐Aldrich). After several washes with PBS/gelatin, the cells were incubated at room temperature for 45 min with the corresponding secondary antibodies goat α‐rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000; Ab175471, Abcam) and goat α‐mouse H&L Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000; 115‐545‐166; Jackson Immuno‐Research). In parallel experiments, to label the actin filaments of microglia, a green fluorescent phalloidin (Actin‐stain 488 fluorescent phalloidin, PHDG1, Cytoskeleton Inc.) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions instead of anti‐α‐tubulin. Finally, they were washed with PBS, and the glass coverslips were mounted with gerbatol and DAPI on slides.

Image Acquisition and Analysis In Vitro

For phagocytosis analysis, photomicrographs were obtained with a Leica DMI6000 fluorescence microscope, using a 20× dry objective and a Leica DFC350 FX digital camera. Image acquisition parameters, including exposure time, laser intensity and detector gain, were kept constant across all experimental conditions and independent cultures, ensuring that fluorescence intensity measurements were comparable between preparations. Within each image, the ROI was defined for each GFP+ cell, where the integrated intensity of Cy3 fluorescence from the debris or LPS‐coated spheres was quantified. For chemotaxis analysis, mosaic photographs were obtained using a Leica Thunder Imager DMi8 microscope, with a Leica DFC 9000GT camera, of the fixed membranes from the Transwell wells. The number of transfected cells that migrated was quantified by calculating the percentage of GFP+ cells with respect to the total number of migrated cells, obtained by counting DAPI‐labeled nuclei. Variability in transfection efficiency between experiments was controlled by normalizing migration data to the percentage of GFP+ cells transfected with non‐target siRNA, with respect to total DAPI+ nuclei in each independent preparation.

Morphological Analysis of Microglial Cells

Pre‐Processing

Original microscopy images were processed in Fiji/ImageJ to correct illumination artifacts. Flat‐field correction was applied using the BaSiC algorithm with bright‐field and offset reference images to homogenize background intensity.

Supervised Segmentation

Microglial cell identification was performed using supervised machine learning in Ilastik (v1.x, Pixel Classification workflow). To ensure unbiased segmentation across groups, a Random Forest classifier was trained using a representative subset of images randomly selected from all experimental conditions (both sexes and treatments). The model was trained with manual annotations to distinguish two classes: “Microglia” and “Background”. The trained model was applied via batch processing to generate probability maps assigning each pixel to the most probable class.

Post‐Processing and Quantification

Segmentation maps were imported into Fiji, thresholder to isolate microglia‐classified pixels, and converted to binary masks. Morphological refinement included opening (1 iteration) to remove noise, closing (1 iteration) to smooth contours, and hole filling to consolidate soma structures.

Morphometric analysis was performed using the Analyze Particles tool. Regions of Interest (ROIs) were generated from cell silhouettes, excluding edge‐contacting objects and those < 4000 pixels2. The following parameters were quantified: Area, Circularity, Feret Diameter, and Perimeter. Segmentation accuracy was validated by overlaying binary masks onto original images.

Cytoskeleton Colocalization

At 3 DIV, the colocalization of KIF21B with actin filaments and microtubules in microglia was observed. For this analysis, photomicrographs were taken on a Leica TCS‐SP5 confocal microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective and a resolution of 2048 × 2048 pixels, 8 bits, a speed of 4 Hz, and serial planes of 1 μm, using LASX software. The planes were superimposed to improve signal intensity.

Colocalization Analysis

Colocalization analysis for both in vivo and in vitro experiments was performed using SynBot software, which quantifies the number of pixels that coincide in two channels through the comparison of the coordinates (x, y) of the location of a pixel or a set of them (Savage et al. 2024). For this, 10–15 planes were obtained, where each plane consisted of the superposition of 3 consecutive microphotographs to optimize the signal.

To quantify the colocalization of KIF21B with Iba1, the Manders coefficient M2 was calculated, normalized for the total Iba1 pixels M2=Pixels that colocalizeTotal pixelsKIF21B Hybrid coefficient=M2Total pixelsIba1

To validate colocalization analysis, two complementary approaches were applied. First, thresholds were determined independently for each channel using manual thresholding in SynBot software, based on visual inspection of signal‐to‐noise ratio in each image, to minimize background signal and ensure that only specific immunostaining was included in the colocalization analysis. Negative controls omitting primary antibodies were processed in parallel in all experiments to confirm the specificity of immunostaining signals and the absence of non‐specific colocalization. Second, a 90‐degree rotation control was applied to generate a spatially randomized reference, and the Manders M2 coefficient was recalculated on the rotated image pair. The observed M2 values were consistently higher than the rotation controls across all experimental groups (real M2: 0.208 ± 0.079 vs. rotated M2: 0.059 ± 0.021, t(423) = 35.13, p < 0.0001), confirming that the colocalization detected reflects true spatial overlap between KIF21B and Iba1/α‐tubulin/F‐actin signals rather than random coincidence. This validation was applied to all in vivo and in vitro colocalization datasets.

Statistical Analysis

The data shown in the figures are presented as the mean ± SEM. The size of the experimental groups corresponds to the number of animals or number of independently prepared cultures. For in vitro experiments, individual cells were averaged per independent culture prior to statistical analysis to avoid pseudo replication. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.0 for Windows and R statistical software (version 4.5, packages nlme). The normality of the data was analyzed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Differences between two experimental groups were analyzed through Student's unpaired t‐test. Interactions between sex and treatments were determined using the two‐way ANOVA, followed by the corresponding post hoc test. Regional colocalization data were analyzed using linear mixed‐effect models (lme function, nlme package) with animal as a random effect followed by Tukey post hoc comparisons. In cases where the data did not conform to normality, non‐parametric analyses were performed (Kruskal–Wallis and post hoc comparisons with Dunn's test). Differences between two experimental groups were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney test. Statistical significance was considered as p < 0.05 in all cases.

Results

Microglia KIF21B Expression in Brains Exposed to a Proinflammatory Challenge

To investigate whether KIF21B is involved in the microglia inflammatory response to different stimuli we selected two well‐characterized neuroinflammatory models: systemic inflammation‐induced neuroinflammation (LPS administration) and lesion‐induced neuroinflammation (TBI).

Immunohistochemical analysis to evaluate KIF21B expression in microglia in vivo was performed using antibodies against KIF21B (red) and Iba1 (specific microglial marker, green) (Figure 2). The analyses revealed the presence of KIF21B in microglia under all conditions studied, evidenced by its colocalization with Iba1 (Figure 2A,D). Quantification of this colocalization in the tissue around the lesion in the TBI model showed that injury provoked a significant increase in KIF21B/Iba1 colocalization levels only in females (Two‐way ANOVA, F(interaction) = 6.727, p = 0.0.0235; F(injury) = 14.72, p = 0.0024; F(sex) = 2.275, p = 0.1574; followed by Bonferroni post hoc test, p = 0.0013) (Figure 2B). To further characterize the spatial distribution of KIF21B expression, we analyzed region as divided into three sampling zones: proximal (~200 μm), medial (~650 μm), and distal (~850 μm) from the lesion border. The data show that KIF21B/Iba1 colocalization decreases with increasing distance from the lesion in both sexes, although this reduction reaches statistical significance only in males (one way ANOVA followed by uncorrected Fisher's LSD, p = 0,0228) (Figure 2C).

In the LPS model, a different response pattern was observed. Colocalization of KIF21B with Iba1 was maintained in both control animals and those treated with LPS, without significant differences between sexes or experimental conditions (Figure 2E).

Contribution of KIF21B to Microglial Function

To determine the specific role of KIF21B, we performed a series of in vitro experiments focused on fundamental aspects of microglial function: migration, phagocytosis, and morphology under basal and inflammatory conditions. First, we assessed whether LPS treatment modified KIF21B expression in primary microglial cultures, and we did not find any significant change (Figure 3A) consistent with the in vivo data. We then downregulated KIF21B expression using siRNA, the efficacy of which was demonstrated by RT‐qPCR in both female and male microglia (unpaired t‐test, p = 0.0002 for both females and males) (Figure 3B). The functional readouts were assessed 24 h after transfection and LPS addition. During this time, the culture medium was DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS. Under this low‐serum condition, we observed no cell proliferation under either basal or LPS‐stimulated conditions in cultures from either sex. Therefore, cell proliferation did not influence the results. In addition, at this timepoint, KIF21B protein levels are expected to be substantially reduced, as it is known that the turnover rate of the protein occurs 24 h after protein synthesis inhibition (Labonté et al. 2013).

KIF21B Regulates Microglial Migration in a Sex‐Dependent Manner

The ability of microglial cells to migrate toward the lesion focus was assessed using in vitro chemotaxis assays in Transwell chambers, a system that permits quantitative evaluation of cell migration driven by chemotactic gradients. For this, we used LPS as a chemoattractant. The chemotactic activity of LPS was validated by counting the total number of cell nuclei in the insert with or without LPS in the lower chamber. LPS significantly increased the number of migrated cells in both sexes (unpaired t‐test, p = 0.0008 in females and p = 0.0061 in males) (Figure 3C), confirming its effectiveness as a chemoattractant under our experimental conditions.

Migratory capacity in transfected microglia was quantified as the percentage of GFP+ cells (treated with siRNAs) that crossed the porous membrane, normalized with respect to the total DAPI‐positive nuclei. Results showed a sex‐specific role of KIF21B in microglia migration (two‐way ANOVA, F(sex) = 5.285, p = 0.0403; F(silencing) = 5.385, p = 0.0387; F(interaction) = 20.22, p = 0.0007, followed by Fisher's LSD post hoc test) (Figure 3D). In female microglia, KIF21B silencing did not affect migratory behavior. In contrast, male microglia exhibited a distinct response: KIF21B silencing significantly enhanced their migration capacity (p = 0.0004). These results suggested that KIF21B was dispensable for microglial migration in females but played a critical role in regulating migration in males.

Microglia Phagocytosis Is Affected by KIF21B in a Sex‐Specific Way

Previous studies in our lab found sex differences in the phagocytic activity of microglia in developmental stages (Yanguas‐Casás et al. 2018) and in aged animals (Yanguas‐Casás et al. 2020); however, the molecular mechanisms implicated in sex differences in microglia phagocytosis had not been assessed. Therefore, we decided to figure out whether KIF21B is involved in the phagocytic responses of microglial cells. The study evaluated two types of substrates: synthetic spheres coated with E. coli with a pH‐sensitive fluorescent marker (simulating the LPS inflammation model in vivo) (Figure 4A) and neuronal debris labeled with Cy3 (simulating the brain injury model) (Figure 4C). Subsequently, we measured the number of internalized particles in actively engulfing microglial cells in the different experimental conditions.

Experiments with E. coli ‐coated spheres revealed that silencing of KIF21B significantly reduced phagocytosis in females, while it did not affect the phagocytic capacity in males (two‐way ANOVA F(interaction) = 9.438, p = 0.0097; F(silencing) = 8.851, p = 0.0116; followed by Bonferroni post hoc test, p = 0.0022 in females) (Figure 4B).

In the case of neuronal debris, we quantified the contribution of KIF21B to microglial phagocytic capacity in control and in inflammatory conditions. Two‐way ANOVA showed an effect of treatment (silencing and/or LPS stimulation): F = 58.91, p < 0.0001; an effect of sex: F = 38.45, p < 0.0001 with interaction: F = 36.91, p < 0.0001. Bonferroni's multiple comparisons post hoc test revealed that under control conditions, KIF21B silencing reduced the phagocytic capacity of microglia in females (p < 0.0001) while increasing it in males (p < 0.0001). LPS treatment increased phagocytosis in both sexes (p = 0.0028 in females and p < 0.0001 in males) as expected (Yanguas‐Casás et al. 2020, 2018). Under inflammatory conditions, KIF21B downregulation also decreased phagocytosis in females (p = 0.0002) while it did not affect the phagocytic capacity in males (Figure 4D). These data suggested the existence of sex‐specific molecular mechanisms in the regulation of KIF21B‐mediated microglial phagocytosis.

Role of KIF21B in Microglial Morphology

The marked effect of KIF21B silencing on migration and phagocytosis suggested possible alterations in the underlying cellular organization. For this reason, we analyzed the following parameters related to cell morphology, seeking to correlate alterations in migration and phagocytic capacity with possible structural changes in the cell.

Area, Two‐way ANOVA showed an effect of treatment (silencing and/or LPS stimulation): F = 80.27, p < 0.0001 with interaction: F = 61.06, p < 0.0001. Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test showed that microglia cells treated with LPS maintained their area at baseline levels in females. However, KIF21B silencing significantly reduced cell area in both basal and under inflammatory conditions (p < 0.0001). On the contrary, in males, LPS treatment reduced the cell area (p < 0.0001), and KIF21B downregulation did not produce significant changes in either the control or inflammatory condition, demonstrating morphological resistance to kinesin loss (Figure 5A).

Circularity, calculated as 4π × Area/Perimeter2, yields values between zero (extremely irregular shape with extensive ramifications) and one (perfect circle) (Zanier et al. 2015). This parameter is particularly informative for microglial morphology, as surveillance‐state cells exhibit low circularity due to multiple ramified processes, while LPS‐stimulated cells adopt higher circularity by retracting processes and reorganizing toward an amoeboid state optimized for phagocytosis, but only in females (p < 0.0001) (Two‐way ANOVA F(interaction) = 49.81, p < 0.0001; F(treatment) = 36.38, p < 0.0001; F(sex) = 72.89, p < 0.0001; followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test). Downregulation of KIF21B in control conditions moderately increased circularity only in females (p = 0.0274) (Figure 5B). However, in inflammatory conditions, it blocked the transition to ameboid morphology in females (p < 0.0001), resulting in ineffective phagocytic ability. On the contrary, in males, it produced a circularity increase (p < 0.0001). These findings are consistent with the results observed in the phagocytosis evaluation (Figure 4).

Feret Diameter, a measure of the maximum distance between two cellular points, which provides information about the spatial reach of the processes (Zanier et al. 2015). This parameter defines the functional extent to which cells can explore their environment, contact targets distant from the soma, and coordinate responses across wide tissue volumes. Basal control females presented a high Feret diameter, reflecting the long‐ramified processes characteristic of active surveillance. KIF21B silencing considerably reduced this diameter (p < 0.0001) (Two‐way ANOVA F(interaction) = 20.19, p < 0.0001; F(treatment) = 11.92, p < 0.0001; followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test) indicating that cells could not reach large distances, thereby limiting their monitoring and response capacity. During inflammation, KIF21B silencing reduced Feret diameter, though without reaching statistical significance. Males exhibited a different pattern. Basally, male cells possessed less reach than females, but LPS significantly expanded it (p < 0.0001). In these conditions KIF21B silencing blocked expansion (p = 0.0121), demonstrating that KIF21B is necessary for male‐specific reorganization during activation, albeit in a different manner than in females (Figure 5C).

Perimeter measures the total cell outline length, reflecting not only size but also shape complexity (Zanier et al. 2015). High perimeter indicates complex contours with multiple extensions, invaginations, and ramifications, translating into greater surface area for environmental contact. Two‐way ANOVA showed an effect of treatment (silencing and/or LPS stimulation): F = 108.3, p < 0.0001; an effect of sex: F = 24.72, p < 0.0001 with interaction: F = 35.00, p < 0.0001. Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test showed that LPS treatment modified cellular perimeter only in females (p < 0.0001). KIF21B silencing reduced the cell perimeter under both basal and inflammatory conditions and in both sexes (p < 0.0001 in all cases), indicating a loss of membrane complexity (Figure 5D). These findings suggest that when the KIF21B do not reach the right level, cells could not maintain the structural membrane complexity necessary for phagocytosis, limiting themselves to simple, low‐capacity environmental interactions.

The observed effects on phagocytosis, migration, and morphology suggested a fundamental role of KIF21B in regulating the microglial cytoskeleton. To establish the mechanisms underlying these functional observations, we analyzed the direct interaction of KIF21B with the main components of the cytoskeleton: microtubules and actin microfilaments.

Microtubules were visualized by immunostaining against α‐tubulin (Figure 6A) and actin filaments using fluorescent phalloidin (Acti‐stain 488) (Figure 6C). The colocalization analysis with microtubules revealed that under basal conditions, female microglia showed less colocalization of KIF21B with α‐tubulin than male microglia (p = 0.0005). LPS treatment significantly increased this colocalization only in females (p < 0.0001), while in males it did not produce significant changes (two‐way ANOVA, F(sex) = 6.609 (p = 0.0245); F(interaction) = 22.29, p = 0.0005, followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparisons post hoc test). On the contrary, the interaction with actin microfilaments showed that under basal conditions, females presented higher levels of KIF21B/F‐actin colocalization than males (p = 0.0052) and LPS treatment reduced this colocalization specifically in females until reaching the levels of males, which did not show significant changes with inflammation (two‐way ANOVA, F(sex) = 15.54, p = 0.0020; F(treatment) = 10.42, p = 0.0011, followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparisons post hoc test). This differential regulation of KIF21B interaction with α‐tubulin and F‐actin provided a molecular basis to explain the sexual dimorphism observed in microglial functions.

Discussion

The role of KIF21B in neuronal development and function has been extensively studied (Ghiretti et al. 2016; Labonté et al. 2013, 2014; Muhia et al. 2016; Gromova et al. 2018, 2023; Morikawa et al. 2018; Rivera Alvarez et al. 2023). Nevertheless, its function in glial cells remains practically unexplored. The results presented in this study revealed, for the first time, the expression and function of KIF21B in microglia, with its role varying according to inflammatory context and sex.

The in vivo characterization of KIF21B in two inflammation models revealed contrasting expression patterns. In the TBI model, only females responded with a significant increase in KIF21B/Iba1 colocalization. In contrast, in the systemic inflammation model, LPS administration did not cause any change in KIF21B/Iba1 colocalization in either sex. This disparity in regulation between models suggests that the expression of KIF21B in microglial cells differs according to the type of inflammatory stimulus, consistent with previous demonstrations that microglial activation patterns vary significantly between local and systemic inflammation. The LPS‐induced neuroinflammation model used in this study has been previously employed in our laboratory, where it was shown to induce neuroinflammation and glial activation involving cytokines release and changes in cell morphology (Arevalo et al. 2012; Pinto‐Benito et al. 2022; Tapia‐Gonzalez et al. 2008). This model has also been widely used by other groups to mimic systemic infection‐associated immune activation in the CNS, consistently inducing microglial activation (Moon et al. 2025; Qiao et al. 2026), although with some differences in experimental timing. The absence of KIF21B upregulation during systemic LPS challenge may reflect the less pronounced morphological demands of this dispersed microglial activation or alternatively, that transcriptional and phenotypic reprogramming linked to innate immune memory, prime the brain for long‐term vulnerability, though such changes arise only during the acute phase of inflammation. Focal traumatic damage triggers robust microglial accumulation with pronounced morphological transformation and phagocytic activity at the injury site (Hernandez‐Ontiveros et al. 2013; Nimmerjahn et al. 2005), whereas systemic LPS administration produces a more dispersed activation pattern across brain regions (Colton and Wilcock 2010). Although previous studies reported that microglia do not express KIF21B (Kreft et al. 2014), subsequent transcriptomic analyses have revealed KIF21B mRNA expression in microglia from 13‐month‐old mice, exhibiting sex differences in the hippocampus (Guneykaya et al. 2018). These findings align with the sexual dimorphism observed in our TBI model, where we found differential expression of KIF21B between females and males in microglial colocalization. The sex‐specific upregulation of KIF21B observed exclusively in the TBI model suggests that this kinesin may be particularly critical for the robust cytoskeletal reorganization required during focal microglial responses, where morphological transformation is essential for migration and efficient debris clearance.

The in vitro studies allowed us to delve deeper into the molecular mechanisms underlying these observations. Consistent with our in vivo findings, LPS did not modify the KIF21B expression in cultured microglia. To functionally evaluate the consequences of KIF21B silencing in a TBI‐relevant context, we used neuronal debris as a phagocytic substrate, modeling a key consequence of TBI: the accumulation of cellular debris requiring microglial clearance.

The colocalization analysis with cytoskeleton components revealed divergent patterns according to sex. While under basal conditions, female microglia showed less colocalization of KIF21B with microtubules but greater with F‐actin than male microglia, LPS treatment induced differential responses. Specifically, in female microglia, LPS significantly increased colocalization with microtubules and reduced association with F‐actin, while in males, it did not cause significant changes in either of these interactions. This cytoskeletal reorganization is particularly relevant considering previous studies that have demonstrated that pseudopod formation during phagocytosis requires precise coordination between microtubules and F‐actin (May and Machesky 2001; Castellano et al. 2001). The importance of this coordination has been shown in studies where microtubule depolymerization suppressed phagocytosis by reducing pseudopod formation (Khandani et al. 2007), supporting the functional significance of the cytoskeletal reorganization we observed in KIF21B‐expressing microglia. The reduction in KIF21B colocalization with F‐actin and increased colocalization with microtubules in LPS‐treated female microglia provides a mechanistic explanation for the greater functional dependence of female microglia on KIF21B for phagocytosis. This shift suggests that KIF21B facilitates the transition from an actin‐dominated ramified state to a microtubule‐integrated amoeboid state necessary for efficient phagocytosis. Based on structural and functional similarities among kinesin family members, KIF21B may function similarly to KIF5B, another kinesin involved in phagocytosis. KIF5B regulates endosome traffic in pseudopods and possibly delivers F‐actin regulatory proteins to the phagocytic cup (Silver and Harrison 2011). Similarly, the reduction in phagocytosis upon KIF21B silencing in females suggests a comparable role in facilitating the delivery of cytoskeletal or membrane components necessary for efficient particle engulfment.

The in vitro functional characterization also revealed distinctive patterns in phagocytosis according to the type of substrate. Using neuronal debris as a substrate for phagocytosis, which models TBI, KIF21B silencing specifically impaired female microglia. Similarly, in experiments with E. coli ‐coated spheres, female microglia showed greater basal phagocytic capacity than males, an advantage that was completely lost upon KIF21B silencing. These findings are consistent with previous reports that female microglia present greater phagocytic capacity and expression of phagocytosis receptors (Yanguas‐Casás et al. 2018) and extend these observations by identifying KIF21B as a critical mediator of this sex difference.

The similar KIF21B‐dependence observed with both neuronal debris and bacterial particles suggests that this kinesin plays a general role in microglial phagocytosis in females, rather than being substrate‐specific. This finding indicates that KIF21B likely regulates core cytoskeletal machinery essential for phagocytic cup formation and particle engulfment, independently of the recognition receptors involved in binding different substrates.

Simultaneous analysis of multiple morphological parameters reveals that the morphological transformation of microglia during inflammatory activation is not a simple collapse or activation, but a coordinated reorganization of multiple architectural aspects (Figure 5E,F). The quantitative relationships between these parameters allowed identifying the KIF21B involvement in the response of microglia to inflammatory challenges: (1) Female microglia with downregulated KIF21B showed a reduced area, limiting the membrane surface available for phagocytic receptor expression and reducing cytoplasmic space for phagosome accumulation. This size constraint is functionally relevant, as increased microglial cell area is associated with enhanced phagocytic capacity (Lee et al. 2024), consistent with greater available intracellular space for processing phagocytosed material. (2) These cells failed to complete the transition to amoeboid morphology during inflammatory activation. Amoeboid microglia are characterized by an expanded soma surrounded by folded membrane sheets enriched in F‐actin at membrane edges essential for phagocytic cup formation (Levtova et al. 2017; Abd‐El‐Basset and Fedoroff 1994). Without this morphological transformation, which normally facilitates both phagocytosis and migration (Vidal‐Itriago et al. 2022), KIF21B‐silenced cells lacked the specialized membrane architecture required for efficient particle engulfment. (3) The reduced perimeter and Feret diameter indicate an inability to extend processes toward distant substrates, thereby impairing localization and contact with phagocytic targets, steps as critical to phagocytosis as engulfment itself (Vidal‐Itriago et al. 2022; Mylvaganam et al. 2021). Collectively, these morphological deficits established a clear structure–function relationship: KIF21B‐silenced female microglia lacked the coordinated morphological platform to execute effective phagocytosis during inflammatory responses.

In contrast, male microglia demonstrated remarkable resistance to KIF21B silencing, maintaining effective phagocytic capacity both basally and during LPS stimulation. This functional preservation occurred despite morphological alterations, indicating that male microglia possess compensatory mechanisms absent in females. Morphologically, KIF21B‐silenced microglia cells from males treated with LPS showed increased circularity and reduced perimeter, superficially similar to the defects in females. However, the critical distinction was that male cells achieve circularity values compatible with phagocytic cup formation. While the reduced perimeter indicated loss of extensive membrane projections, the remaining membrane maintained sufficient functional organization to support effective phagocytosis. This suggests that male microglia may have a more robust basal cytoskeletal architecture that does not require KIF21B‐mediated reorganization to achieve functional morphology.

Chemotaxis assays revealed sex‐specific roles of KIF21B in migration. In females, KIF21B silencing significantly reduced migration under both basal and inflammatory contexts. The observed morphological defects explained this loss: KIF21B‐silenced female microglia, under inflammatory conditions, showed reduced Feret diameter and perimeter, limiting the extension capacity necessary for migration with long processes, while simultaneously exhibiting insufficient circularity and reduced area, defaulting thereby failing to achieve the amoeboid migration strategy. Microglia utilize distinct morphologies for migration: polarized morphology with anterior lamellum correlates with greater motility in alternatively activated microglia, while amoeboid morphology is associated with classically activated microglia (Lively and Schlichter 2013; Kettenmann et al. 2011). KIF21B‐silenced female cells failed to achieve either morphology, resulting in impaired migration regardless of activation state. Males showed a radically different and paradoxical pattern. Basally, male cells demonstrated complete KIF21B independence for migration. However, during LPS stimulation, a functional transformation emerged: while control cells reduced their migration, KIF21B silencing not only prevented such inhibition but resulted in hypermigration of male microglia, consistent with the increased circularity observed. This hypermigration suggests that KIF21B normally constrains migratory responses in males specifically during inflammation, a regulatory function that is unmasked upon its silencing.

Colocalization data of KIF21B with cytoskeleton components provided the molecular mechanism explaining these sex‐specific morphological and functional patterns. In females, under basal conditions, KIF21B showed lower colocalization with α‐tubulin compared to males, but significantly higher colocalization with F‐actin. The basal actin‐centered architecture explained the extensive ramified morphology characteristic of resting female microglia. Critically, during LPS treatment, female microglia executed coordinated cytoskeletal reorganization: KIF21B colocalization with α‐tubulin increased while colocalization with F‐actin decreased. This reorganization is consistent with known changes during microglial activation, in which reorganization of the microtubule cytoskeleton has been shown to occur from distributed acentrosomal arrays toward stable radial arrays centrally anchored (Adrian et al. 2023; Rosito et al. 2023), while actin filaments reorganize during phagocytosis, forming accumulations around phagocytic cups (Abd‐El‐Basset and Fedoroff 1994). Both processes are critical for achieving functional activated morphology required for phagocytosis and migration (Adrian et al. 2023). Multiple cytoskeletal regulators facilitate dynamic interaction between microtubules and actin, including Rho GTPases, crosslinker proteins (Adrian et al. 2023; Dogterom and Koenderink 2019; Socodato and Relvas 2024), and actin nucleation factors. These proteins maintain microglial morphology and functional plasticity by coordinating cytoskeletal dynamics (Bobotis et al. 2023). KIF21B could function as one of these integrators, coordinating the actin polymerization necessary for migration with the regulation of microtubules that control force generation and cell adhesion (Bachmann and Straube 2015; Seetharaman and Etienne‐Manneville 2020; Rosito et al. 2023).

This study reveals that KIF21B functions as a sexually dimorphic regulator of microglial cytoskeletal dynamics, acting as an essential facilitator in females while serving as a contextual brake in males during inflammatory responses. In females, KIF21B coordinates the transition from an actin‐centered ramified morphology to a microtubule‐integrated amoeboid morphology, enabling efficient phagocytosis and directed migration. In males, KIF21B appears dispensable under basal conditions but prevents hypermigration during inflammation, suggesting distinct cytoskeletal regulatory strategies between sexes.

These findings have implications for understanding sex differences in neuroinflammatory diseases. The greater dependence of female microglia on KIF21B for phagocytic clearance could contribute to differential outcomes in conditions where debris clearance is critical, such as traumatic brain injury (Yatoo et al. 2024), stroke (Carcel et al. 2026), or neurodegenerative diseases with protein aggregates (Lopez‐Lee et al. 2022). Conversely, the different regulatory role of KIF21B in male microglia during inflammation suggests fundamentally different mechanisms of cytoskeletal regulation between sexes.

Our primary microglia cultures are derived from neonatal mice (P0‐P2), prior to gonadal sexual maturation. The sex differences observed in neonatal microglia are therefore unlikely to be driven by circulating gonadal hormones at the time of culture. Several mechanisms may contribute to the observed sex differences at this developmental stage. First, direct chromosomal effects, differences in XX versus XY gene dosage, can drive sex‐specific transcriptional programs independently of hormonal influence. Genes escaping X‐inactivation may be expressed at higher levels in female microglia (Arnold 2022). Transcriptomic analysis has revealed sex‐specific gene expression patterns in microglia that are present in early developmental stages (Guneykaya et al. 2018; Bobotis et al. 2023). Second, prenatal gonadal hormone secretion during fetal development [from embryonic day 7 to postnatal day 10 in the rodent brain] is essential for building and organizing sex‐differentiated brain circuits and establishing lasting epigenetic differences that persist later in life (Cabrera Zapata et al. 2022; Sosa et al. 2026). Together, these mechanisms could explain the sex differences in KIF21B‐dependent microglial functions observed in our neonatal cultures. Nevertheless, it is important to note the limitations of our study. Our in vitro experiments were conducted primarily in pure microglial cultures which lack the multicellular interactions present in vivo. Astrocytes modulate microglial polarization and function (Liddelow et al. 2017; Damisah et al. 2020), and neurons provide critical signals that influence microglial behavior. Therefore, the complete cellular context in vivo could modify the KIF21B‐dependent responses we observed. Second, our in vivo models examined only acute inflammatory responses (7 days post‐injury or post‐LPS). Chronic neuroinflammatory conditions may reveal different KIF21B expression patterns and functional requirements. Given that different microglial subtypes show variable efficiencies in phagocytosis according to substrate and time (Li et al. 2022), the role of KIF21B may also vary across inflammation progression stages.
