Quantum aspects of heterotic G 2 G_{2} systems
Xenia de la Ossa, Magdalena Larfors, Matthew Magill, Eirik E. Svanes

Abstract
Compactifications of the heterotic string, to first order in the α ​ ’ \alpha\textquoteright expansion, on manifolds with integrable G 2 G_{2} structure give rise to three-dimensional 𝒩 = 1 {\cal N}=1 supergravity theories that admit Minkowski and AdS ground states. As shown in de la Ossa et al. (2020) , such vacua correspond to critical loci of a real superpotential W W . We perform a perturbative study around a supersymmetric vacuum of the theory, which confirms that the first order variation of the superpotential, δ ​ W \delta W , reproduces the BPS conditions for the system, and furthermore shows that δ 2 ​ W = 0 \delta^{2}W=0 gives the equations for infinitesimal moduli. This allows us to identify a nilpotent differential, and a symplectic pairing, which we use to construct a bicomplex, or a double complex, for the heterotic G 2 G_{2} system. Using this complex, we determine infinitesimal moduli and their obstructions in terms of related cohomology groups. Finally, by interpreting δ 2 ​ W \delta^{2}W as an action, we compute the one-loop partition function of the heterotic G 2 G_{2} system and show it can be decomposed into a product of one-loop partition functions of Abelian and non-Abelian instanton gauge theories.

Introduction

There has been a lot of recent interest in both the physics and mathematics literature in studying coupled systems of gauge theory and gravity (geometry). The heterotic string provides a fertile ground for this endeavour. Heterotic strings come with a gauge bundle, whose connection should, for supersymmetric solutions, satisfy an instanton condition that depends on the geometry.
Moreover, the coupling of the gauge sector with the geometry through the heterotic Bianchi identity forces the geometry to be generically torsional - a further layer of complication which has made understanding mathematical properties of these solutions, such as the moduli problem, more challenging.

In this paper we study the moduli of heterotic string compactifications on manifolds endowed with a
G_{2}
structure, leading to a system of equations called the heterotic
G_{2}
system
de la Ossa et al. (2018)
.
This system corresponds to minimally supersymmetric, three-dimensional solutions of the heterotic string theory to first order in the
\alpha^{\prime}
expansion, as explored in e.g.
Gunaydin and Nicolai (1995)
;
Friedrich and Ivanov (2003)
;
Gauntlett et al. (2004)
;
Gurrieri et al. (2004)
;
Micu (2004)
;
Gran et al. (2006)
;
Gran et al. (2007)
;
Lukas and Matti (2011)
;
Gray et al. (2012)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2015)
;
Gran et al. (2016)
.
An interesting aspect of these systems is that they admit an

{\cal N}=1
three dimensional effective field theory on both anti de Sitter (AdS
3
) and Minkowski (
\mathbb{M}_{3}
) spacetimes. The curvature radius of the AdS
3
space is related to a (scalar) torsion class which vanishes only in the case of
\mathbb{M}_{3}

de la Ossa et al. (2020)
. In contrast,
{\cal N}=1
compactifications of the heterotic strings to four dimensions, which are known as Hull-Strominger systems
Hull (1986)
;
Strominger (1986)
, do not admit AdS
4
solutions.

In the last decade, progress has been made towards a better understanding of heterotic systems. For Hull–Strominger systems, the infinitesimal moduli were parameterised as the first cohomology of a double extension sequence
Melnikov and Sharpe (2011)
;
Anderson et al. (2014)
;
de la Ossa and Svanes (2014)
. The moduli problem has been shown to be elliptic, and connected to moduli spaces of solutions of suitable Killing spinor equations on a (holomorphic) Courant algebroid
Garcia-Fernandez et al. (2015)
. Finite deformations have been explored
Ashmore et al. (2018)
, alongside the moduli space geometry, moment maps, stability, flow equations, and further connections with generalised geometry, see e.g.
Candelas et al. (2019)
;
Garcia-Fernandez et al. (2020)
;
Ashmore et al. (2020)
;
Garcia-Fernandez et al. (2024b)
;
McOrist and Svanes (2022)
;
Garcia-Fernandez et al. (2024a)
;
Kupka et al. (2024)
and references therein.

For heterotic
G_{2}
systems, the infinitesimal moduli were shown to be parametrised by the first cohomology of an operator
\check{\cal D}
, which operates on a bundle
\cal Q
which couples geometric and gauge degrees of freedom
de la Ossa et al. (2016b)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2018)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2017)
. For
\check{\cal D}
to be nilpotent, and thus define a cohomology, it is important that its curvature satisfies the
G_{2}
instanton condition. When this is the case, the related
\check{\cal D}
complex is elliptic
de la Ossa et al. (2016b)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2018)
. An interesting consequence of this is that the
\cal Q
bundle of the heterotic
G_{2}
system satisfies the same instanton condition as does the heterotic gauge bundle, and, by dimensional oxidation, a similar result can be shown for the Hull–Strominger system
de la Ossa et al. (2017)
. This is an example of what has come to be known as “instantons for instantons” and has been explored in depth in
Silva et al. (2024)
. Further investigations of heterotic
G_{2}
geometry and its moduli, and their connections to generalised geometry has since been explored, both from a mathematical and physical standpoint, see
Clarke et al. (2016)
;
Fiset et al. (2018)
;
Clarke et al. (2022)
;
McOrist et al. (2024)
. Explicit local and global solutions to the system have also been constructed, e.g.
Fernandez et al. (2011)
;
del Barco et al. (2020)
;
Klaput et al. (2011)
;
Lotay and Earp (2023)
;
de la Ossa and Galdeano (2021)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2022)
;
Fino et al. (2023)
;
Galdeano and Stecker (2024)
. In spite of this progress, the mathematical structures governing these
G_{2}
geometries are more mysterious than their six-dimensional
SU(3)
counterparts, and tools for understanding them are sorely needed.

As history has shown, physics can provide useful insights into the understanding geometric structures. Examples of this include mirror symmetry
Candelas et al. (1991)
, and the use of topological field theory to study geometric invariants, such as Chern–Simons theory and the Jones polynomial of knots
Witten (1989)
, or holomorphic Chern–Simons theory and Donaldson–Thomas invariants
Thomas (1997)
;
Donaldson and Thomas (1998)
. In the case of heterotic solutions, physics provides us with a useful functional, the superpotential, whose critical points are exactly the solutions of the heterotic BPS equations
de la Ossa et al. (2020)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2016a)
. In the six-dimensional case, the superpotential was essential in the derivation of the Maurer–Cartan equation of the
L_{\infty}
algebra governing finite deformations
Ashmore et al. (2018)
. It is natural to ask if similar progress can be made in the
G_{2}
case. However, the six-dimensional
SU(3)
case has an advantage over the
G_{2}
solutions: it preserves more supersymmetry, and as a result, the superpotential is required to be a holomorphic functional of the fields. This provides a useful parametrisation of the moduli which can be exploited in understanding higher order deformations. For the
G_{2}
case, an analogous parametrisation is not yet known. Yet, we may explore the deformations to quadratic order, and we will do this in the present paper.

Following this perturbative strategy, we will, in this paper, study classical and quantum aspects of the three-dimensional
{\cal N}=1
supergravity with superpotential
W
. We will work perturbatively around a supersymmetric vacuum of the theory. To first order, the variation of the superpotential reproduces the BPS conditions for the system, and, as we will see, the second order variations give the equations for moduli. Moreover, the analysis allows us to construct a bicomplex, or a double complex, which also encodes the gauge symmetry of the theory. Using this double complex, we can determine obstructions to infinitesimal deformations, and make remarks about finite deformations.

Finally, by interpreting the second order variation of the superpotential as an action, we can explore quantum aspects of the system.
This direct computation of the quadratic action has two advantages, compared to the recent generalised geometry analysis of Refs.
Kupka et al. (2024)
. Firstly, it becomes clear that the heterotic
G_{2}
moduli problem is governed by a double complex, as was suggested in
Kupka et al. (2024)
. Secondly, we will see that upon a field redefinition, the total complex can be diagonalised into three separate complexes. This makes it easier to compute and express the one-loop partition function in terms of determinants of Laplacians on more familiar bundles, as has been done in
Ashmore et al. (2023)
in the case of type II compactifications on
SU(3)
structure manifolds. We will compute the absolute value of the one-loop partition function here, and leave the study of its phase and
\eta
-invariant, and the extent to which it defines topological invariants, to future work.

The paper is organised as follows. In section
2
we introduce the heterotic
G_{2}
superpotential, compute its first and second order variations and study its relevance for the moduli problem. We then explore the deformation theory to quadratic order, and define a double complex, or bicomplex, that encodes the action and symmetries of this theory, in section
3
. With this in hand, we discuss infinitesimal moduli and their obstructions. In section
4
we compute the one-loop partition function of the quadratic theory. Section
5
contains our conclusions and a discussion of open problems. Two proofs of propositions are provided in appendix
A
and
B
.

2
Variations of the superpotential and equations for moduli

Our aim in this paper is to study
the three-dimensional
{\cal N}=1
supergravity with superpotential
W
, which is associated to the heterotic
G_{2}
system. We will work perturbatively around a supersymmetric vacuum of the theory. To first order, the variation of the superpotential reproduces the BPS conditions for the system, and the second order variations give the equations for moduli, which we compute in the latter half of this section. To start, we recapitulate how the superpotential is defined.

2.1
The superpotential and the heterotic
G_{2}
system

Let

In

\alpha^{\prime}

In this paper we use this superpotential to obtain an action whose equations of motion are equivalent to those describing the infinitesimal deformations of the
G_{2}
heterotic system. In this subsection we summarise briefly the results of
de la Ossa et al. (2020)
as they are the starting point of the research presented in this paper.

Consider, then, the superpotential

\displaystyle{\rm d}(e^{-2\phi}\,\psi)=0\qquad\iff\qquad\tau_{2}=0~,\qquad 2\,\tau_{1}={\rm d}\phi~,

3.1
Defining the differential operator
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
of the complex

We will now return to the precise definition of the differential

1.

In addition, we can interpret the

Our main task now is to determine the differential

3.1.2

Given our symplectic form, the maps

This is

3.2
Nilpotency and ellipticity

Having defined the components of a self-adjoint differential, we must now ensure that it is, in fact, nilpotent.
We can write our differential as a matrix:

{\check{\mathbb{D}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\check{{\rm d}}&\mathcal{T}&0\\
0&\check{\cal D}&\mathcal{S}\\
0&0&-\check{{\rm d}}\end{array}\right)

where we redefine our notation so that, in order to match the middle complex of (

\check{\cal D}=\{{\cal D},\Psi\wedge{\cal D},{\cal D}\}
at homological degree

This squares to:

{\check{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\check{{\rm d}}^{2}&\check{{\rm d}}\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{T}\check{\mathcal{D}}&\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}\\

(49)

We know well that the contributions on the diagonal vanish, which means that we need to verify the remaining entries are identically zero.
That means verifying that the compositions
\mathcal{T}^{(p+1)}\circ\mathcal{S}^{(p)}
vanish,
and that each of the squares in total complex (

) anticommute.

3.2.1
The
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{S}
compositions

Nilpotency requires that the compositions
\mathcal{T}^{(1)}\circ\mathcal{S}^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\circ\mathcal{S}^{(-1)}
both vanish.
In fact, it suffices to check only one of these compositions, because they are adjoint to each other.
The argument we give will make this adjointness manifest.

Indeed, non-degeneracy of the symplectic form implies that
\mathcal{T}^{(1)}\mathcal{S}^{(0)}\beta=0
if and only if
\omega(\mu,\mathcal{T}^{(1)}\mathcal{S}^{(0)}\beta)=0
for all
\mu\in\Omega^{1}
.
By definition, this is true if and only if
\omega(\mathcal{S}^{(-1)}\mu,\mathcal{S}^{(0)}\beta)=0
.
It can be noted that starting with
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\mathcal{S}^{(-1)}
and taking the adjoint of
\mathcal{T}
would give precisely the same pairing here.
Expanding this out, we have:

\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{S}^{(-1)}\mu,\mathcal{S}^{(0)}\beta)

\displaystyle=\int(\hat{d}\mu)^{a}\wedge(\hat{d}\beta)_{a}\wedge\Psi\,,

which is a
G_{2}
equivariant map
\mathbf{1}\mathbf{4}\otimes(\mathbf{7}\oplus\mathbf{2}\mathbf{7})\rightarrow\mathbf{1}
and must, therefore, be vanishing.

3.2.2
Anticommuting squares

The final check of nilpotency is that each of the squares in diagram (

While the computations are straightforward, and similar to the ones presented above (i.e. we will make use of
G_{2}

Gauge contributions to the squares

The maps
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
act trivially on the gauge sector, so it may appear that gauge fields only appear in the
\check{\mathcal{D}}^{2}
computation.
This is not true, however, because
\check{\mathcal{D}}
intermixes the gauge and vector field sectors.
Nevertheless, the only possible problems are in checking anticommutativity of the squares; composing the operators we see that one leg trivially vanishes, while the vanishing of the other must be verified.

The trivial directions arise because the
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
maps are identically zero on gauge fields.
The nontriviality arises in two ways: the
\check{\mathcal{D}}
operator maps gauge sector to the vector field sector (relevant for the
\mathcal{T}

\check{\mathcal{D}}
operator maps vector fields to the gauge sector (relevant for the
\mathcal{S}

We will start with a section

and must verify that
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\check{\mathcal{D}}\alpha=\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\mathcal{D}\alpha=0

Recall from section

that the vector field component of
\mathcal{D}\alpha

(\mathcal{D}\alpha)^{a}\propto{\rm tr}(\alpha F^{a})\,,

(50)

where

G_{2}
instanton by the equations of motion.

Then:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\mathcal{D}\alpha\propto\pi_{\bf 7}d\pi_{R}({\rm tr}(\alpha\wedge F^{a})\wedge\psi^{\prime}_{a})\;=0\,.

The result follows trivially from the observation that, for a one form valued in the tangent bundle, say

is independent of the
\mathbf{1}\mathbf{4}

F^{a}
is a one form valued in the tangent bundle comprised only of the
\mathbf{1}\mathbf{4}

In other words, this map vanishes because there are no nontrivial
G_{2}
maps
\mathbf{1}\mathbf{4}\otimes\mathbf{1}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}\oplus\mathbf{7}\oplus\mathbf{2}\mathbf{7}

Consider now

and its contribution to
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(1)}(\mathcal{D}\wedge\Psi)\kappa

\displaystyle\propto d\pi_{\bf 14}({\rm tr}(\kappa\wedge F^{a})\wedge\Psi)_{a}

\displaystyle=d\pi_{\bf 14}({\rm tr}(\kappa_{a}\wedge F^{a})\wedge\Psi)=0\,,

where we used that

to get to the second line, and then used that the resulting expression is clearly in the
\mathbf{7}

We now turn to the possible contributions in the
\mathcal{S}
-squares, where the fact that
\mathcal{D}
maps a vector field to a gauge field is the new concern.
In particular:

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{S}^{(-1)}\mu

\displaystyle\propto(\hat{d}\mu)^{a}\wedge F_{a}\wedge\Psi

where we used that contracting an index obeys the Leibniz rule, and that each term vanishes separately due to both

\mathbf{1}\mathbf{4}

The final gauge theoretic concern is:

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{S}^{(0)}\beta=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{S}^{(0)}\beta=(\hat{d}\beta)^{a}\wedge F_{a}\wedge\Psi\;=0\,,

where we have made the simple observation that there are no non-zero
G_{2}

(\mathbf{7}\oplus\mathbf{2}\mathbf{7})\otimes\mathbf{1}\mathbf{4}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}

Possible vector field contributions

The remaining obstructions to nilpotency are vector field contributions, i.e.

We will start in the top-left corner of Diagram

3.4
Infinitesimal moduli and obstruction theory

As mentioned at the end of section
2
, with the constraint
\beta=0=\hat{\delta}\phi
, the infinitesimal moduli of the heterotic
G_{2}
system are captured by
H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{Q})

de la Ossa et al. (2018)
. The action (
37
), and associated gauge symmetries given by
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
-exact forms,
make it clear that the full infinitesimal moduli are captured by

H^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})=\frac{\{{\cal Z}^{0}\in{\cal E}^{0}\;|\;{\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}^{0}=0\}}{\{{\cal Z}^{0}\in{\cal E}^{0}\;|\;{\cal Z}^{0}={\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}^{-1}\}}

where the superscript denotes homological degree (note that the fields in
{\cal E}^{0}
, say, have different form degree).

To understand the higher order deformations requires analysing finite order deformations
\cal Z
of the superpotential of a background solution. The corresponding equation of motion is then a Maurer-Cartan equation of the form

\check{\mathbb{D}}{\cal Z}+{\mathcal{O}}({\cal Z}^{2})=0\>.

(53)

Solutions to this equation parameterises finite deformations of the system, i.e. which of the infinitesimal moduli can be integrated to a finite deformation. Figuring out the explicit form of the higher order terms
{\mathcal{O}}({\cal Z}^{2})
requires an investigation of the full deformation theory of heterotic
G_{2}
systems and its associated
L_{\infty}
-algebra. This is beyond the scope of this paper. We can however make some remarks.

Note first that a perturbative expansion of the Maurer-Cartan equation (
53
) together with general deformation theory arguments leads to an obstruction, or Kuranishi, map

{\cal O}\>:\;H^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})\rightarrow H^{1}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})\>,

where the un-obstructed deformations lie in the kernel of this map. The details of this map depends of course on the deformation problem at hand. For “generic” Maurer-Cartan equations, the expectation is that this map will be injective for
h^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})\leq h^{1}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})
and surjective for
h^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})\geq h^{1}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})
. In particular, for
h^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})=h^{1}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})
the map is “generically” a bijection, just like a generic quadratic matrix is invertible. The expected, or “virtual”, dimension of the moduli space in this case is zero, consisting of (possibly infinitely many) points. If this holds, and the moduli space can be compactified in some appropriate sense, there is then a natural enumerate invariant for topological spaces admitting heterotic
G_{2}
solutions. Namely, how many such solutions exist for a given topology?

We thus want to establish the following:

Proposition 4
.

For the heterotic
G_{2}
system,
h^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})=h^{1}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})
.

Proof.

That this is true follows from the existence of the non-degenerate pairing
\omega
, and Hodge theory. To invoke Hodge theory, we need to introduce a positive definite pairing
\tilde{g}
on the complex. Using the same notation as in (
40
) for the elements in
\cal{E}
, we pick the following pairing

{\tilde{g}}({\cal Z}_{1},{\cal Z}_{2})=\int\tilde{g}_{\cal Q}(X_{1}\wedge*X_{2})+\xi_{1}\wedge*\xi_{2}+\eta_{1}\wedge*\eta_{2}=\int\tilde{g}_{AB}{\cal Z}^{A}_{1}\wedge*{\cal Z}^{B}_{2}\>,

(54)

where
\tilde{g}_{\cal Q}
is the pairing on
\cal Q
defined in (
31
), but with the sign of the trace flipped on the
{\rm End}(TY)
part, so that it is positive definite. The reason for this choice will become clear below.

Let us first compute how the adjoint of
{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}
acts with respect to this inner product. This is a bit subtle, as we need to invoke
\omega
to perform the integration by parts. Let
\omega_{AB}
denote the point-wise components of the pairing
\omega
, with inverse
\omega^{AB}
. We compute

\displaystyle\tilde{g}({\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}_{1},{\cal Z}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int{\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}^{A}_{1}\wedge*{\cal Z}_{2}^{B}\tilde{g}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\int{\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}^{A}_{1}\wedge*{\cal Z}_{2}^{B}\tilde{g}_{BC}\omega^{CD}\omega_{DA}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|{\cal Z}_{1}|}\int{\cal Z}^{A}_{1}\wedge{\check{\mathbb{D}}}\left(*{\cal Z}_{2}^{B}\tilde{g}_{BC}\omega^{CD}\right)\omega_{DA}\>.

(55)

It is then clear that for
{\cal Z}
in the kernel of the adjoint requires

{\check{\mathbb{D}}}*\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\cdot{\cal Z}\right)=0\>.

(56)

Let us define the map

\Theta\>:\;\;\;{\cal E}^{0}\rightarrow{\cal E}^{1}\>,\;\;\;\;\Theta({\cal Z})=*\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\cdot{\cal Z}\right)\>,

(57)

which is an isomorphism. If we now take
{\cal Z}
to be harmonic, then
\Theta({\cal Z})
is
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
-closed. To check coclosure, we must compute the adjoint action on
\Theta({\cal Z})
. We get

{\check{\mathbb{D}}}*\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\cdot\Theta({\cal Z})\right)={\check{\mathbb{D}}}\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\circ\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\cdot{\cal Z})\right)\>.

(58)

For our particular choice of
\tilde{g}
, it is straight forward to check that
\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\circ\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}
is the identity. Hence

{\check{\mathbb{D}}}*\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}\cdot\Theta({\cal Z})\right)={\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}=0\>,

(59)

so
\Theta({\cal Z})
is both closed and co-closed.

It follows that the map
\Theta
maps harmonic forms to harmonic forms. This establishes the above isomorphism between
H^{0}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})
and
H^{1}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})
.
∎

As a corollary, by a similar argument, one can also establish the more general Serre duality-like result

h^{p}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})=h^{1-p}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})\>.

(60)

The corresponding Euler characteristic, or “index”

\chi({\check{\mathbb{D}}},{\cal E})=\sum_{p=-2}^{3}h^{p}_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}(Y,{\cal E})\>,

(61)

therefore vanishes. This shows that the moduli problem of the heterotic
G_{2}
system is an “index zero moduli problem”. This is encouraging, as such moduli problems have a tendency to lead to mathematically well-behaved topological invariant theories and enumerative geometry, such as, for example, in the corresponding moduli problems of ordinary Chern-Simons theory
Witten (1989)
, holomorphic Chern-Simons theory
Donaldson and Thomas (1998)
;
Thomas (1997)
, and more speculatively
G_{2}
instantons
Donaldson and Segal (2009)
.

4
1-loop partition function

We have seen that the second order variations heterotic
G_{2}
superpotential can be interpreted as the action (
37
), which we repeat here for convenience,

S=\langle{\cal Z},{\check{\mathbb{D}}}\,{\cal Z}\rangle_{\cal E}~.

(78)

We will now use this quadratic action to compute the absolute value of the one-loop partition function of the heterotic
G_{2}
system. For a quadratic action, this can be done using formal path integral manipulations, resulting in a rigorous end result. Our analysis will be modelled on the corresponding computation for three-dimensional Chern-Simons theory
Witten (1989)
, which we repeat for the reader’s convenience. The reader is referred to
Pestun and Witten (2005)
for a more extended review.

4.1
Warm up: Abelian Chern-Simons theory

To set the stage, we start by recalling Abelian Chern-Simons theory on a three-manifold
{\cal M}_{3}

Witten (1989)

S[\alpha]=\int_{{\cal M}_{3}}\alpha\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha\>.

(79)

The absolute value of the partition function is formally defined as the Euclidean path integral

|Z|=\frac{1}{{\rm Vol}(G)}\int{\cal D}\alpha\>e^{-S[\alpha]}\>,

(80)

where
{\rm Vol}(G)
denotes the infinite volume of gauge transformations to be determined.

Picking a metric, we can filter the path-integral under the Hodge-decomposition as

\displaystyle|Z|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\rm Vol}(G)}\int_{\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0}}{\cal D}\alpha\int_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{2}}{\cal D}\alpha\>e^{-S[\alpha]}=\frac{{\rm Vol}(\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0})}{{\rm Vol}(G)}\int_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{2}}{\cal D}\alpha\>e^{-S[\alpha]}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm Vol}(\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0})}{{\rm Vol}(G)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\det\left(\mathrm{d}|_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{2}}\right)}}}\>,

(81)

where we use that
S[\alpha]
is independent of exact modes, and we ignore potential finite-dimensional contributions from harmonic on-shell modes. The determinant is defined as

\det\left(\mathrm{d}|_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{2}}\right):=\det\left(\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}|_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{2}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\>.

(82)

Note then that, due to standard Hodge theory, the determinant of the Laplacian on one-forms,
\Delta^{1}
, decomposes as

\det\left(\Delta^{1}\right)=\det\left(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}|_{\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0}}\right)\det\left(\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}|_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{2}}\right)\>.

(83)

The partition function can therefore be written as

|Z|=\frac{{\rm Vol}(\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0})}{{\rm Vol}(G)}\frac{\det\left(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}|_{\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}{{\det\left(\Delta^{1}\right)}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}\>.

(84)

Let us then consider
\det\left(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}|_{\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0}}\right)
. If
\alpha=\mathrm{d}\gamma
is an eigenvector of
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}|_{\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0}}
, then

\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}\gamma=\lambda\mathrm{d}\gamma\>.

(85)

We can assume that
\gamma\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{d}^{\dagger})
, and
\mathrm{d}
is invertible on this image, so this equation is equivalent to

\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}\gamma=\Delta^{0}\gamma=\lambda\gamma\>.

(86)

It follows that
\det\left(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}|_{\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0}}\right)=\det(\Delta^{0})
.

Next let’s consider
{\rm Vol}(\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0})
. Elementary linear algebra states that

{\rm Vol}(\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0})=\frac{\det\left(\mathrm{d}|_{\mathrm{d}^{\dagger}\Omega^{1}}\right)}{\ker({\rm d}:\Omega^{0}\rightarrow\Omega^{1})}{\rm Vol}(\mathrm{d}\Omega^{0})=\det\left(\Delta^{0}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}{\rm Vol}(\Omega^{0})\>,

(87)

where we have ignored the finite-dimensional kernel of constant functions. Collecting everything, one finds

|Z|=\frac{{\rm Vol}(\Omega^{0})}{{\rm Vol}(G)}\frac{\det\left(\Delta^{0}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{4}}}{\det\left(\Delta^{1}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}\>.

(88)

By choosing
{\rm Vol}(G)={\rm Vol}(\Omega^{0})
, as the volume of gauge transformations, we get

|Z|=\frac{\det\left(\Delta^{0}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{4}}}{\det\left(\Delta^{1}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}\>.

(89)

Upon regularisation of the determinants of the Laplacians, this is precisely the Ray-Singer torsion of the manifold
{\cal M}_{3}
.

4.2
1-loop partition function of the heterotic
G_{2}
system

The computation of the absolute value of the partition function of the theory (
78
) proceeds in exactly the same fashion as for three-dimensional Chern-Simons theory. The only difference being that the theory (
78
) is one-reducible, i.e. it has gauge of gauge symmetries, in addition to gauge symmetries. Keeping this in mind, and the fact that our fields take values in level zero
\mathcal{E}^{0}
of the complex (
38
), the formal computation of the (absolute value of the) partition function gives

|Z|=\frac{\det\left(\Delta_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}^{(-1)}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{4}}}{\det\left(\Delta_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}^{(-2)}\right)^{\tfrac{5}{4}}\det\left(\Delta_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}^{(0)}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}\>.

(90)

This is the same expression as the partition function computed in
Kupka et al. (2024)
using generalised geometry techniques, except that the differential
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
is slightly different from the one appearing in
Kupka et al. (2024)
. We expect the results to agree upon an appropriate field redefinition of the quadratic action.

Here the Laplacians are defined as

\Delta_{{\check{\mathbb{D}}}}={\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\check{\mathbb{D}}}^{\dagger}+{\check{\mathbb{D}}}^{\dagger}{\check{\mathbb{D}}}\>,

(91)

where the adjoint
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}^{\dagger}
is defined using a
positive definite
inner product on
\mathcal{E}^{*}
. For example, we can use the inner product given by (
54
)

({\cal Z}_{1},{\cal Z}_{2})=\int\big(\tilde{g}_{\cal Q}(X_{1}\wedge*X_{2})+\xi_{1}\wedge*\xi_{2}+\eta_{1}\wedge*\eta_{2}\big)\>,

(92)

where
{\cal Z}_{i}=(\xi_{i},X_{i},\eta_{i})\in\mathcal{B}^{*,1}\oplus\mathcal{B}^{*,0}\oplus\mathcal{B}^{*,-1}
. Note that this is not the pairing
\omega
, which is not positive definite, and therefore does not give rise to an appropriate Hodge decomposition.

We would like to be a bit more explicit, and write this partition function in terms of determinants of more familiar operators. To do so, it is convenient to use the field-redefinition introduced in section
3.5
, which decouples the rows of the
{\mathcal{E}^{*}}
complex.
To proceed, recall that the original action (
78
) is invariant under shifts by
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
-exact forms of the fields. This is in fact the gauge symmetry of the heterotic theory. In particular, fields in the lowest row of the
{\cal E}^{*}
complex are shifted by
\check{{\rm d}}
-exact forms. Let
\beta\in\Omega^{2}_{\bf 7}
correspond to this part of the of the field
{\cal Z}
. If we ignore a finite-dimensional part given by harmonic forms, we can Hodge decompose
\beta
as

\beta=\check{{\rm d}}^{\tilde{\dagger}}(f\Phi)+\check{{\rm d}}\gamma\>,

(93)

where
\gamma_{1}\in\Omega^{1}
, and
\Phi=\tilde{*}\Psi
is the Hodge-dual of
\Psi
with respect to the metric given by
\Psi
. The adjoint is also defined with respect to this metric. Without loss of generality, we can pick a gauge where
\beta
does not have any
\check{{\rm d}}
-exact part (this does not change the action). Since the field redefinitions corresponding to
G
and
G^{-1}
do not touch the bottom row of
{\cal E}^{*}
, it leaves
\beta
unchanged. It follows that the gauge choice for
\beta
is preserved: the
\check{{\rm d}}\gamma
-term cannot appear in the action after the field redefinition.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega^{4}_{\bf 1}
be the top-row part of
{\cal Z}
, and
\tilde{\alpha}\in\Omega^{4}
be the part of the field after the field redefinition. This
\tilde{\alpha}
need not be a singlet. The part of the action involving
\beta
after a field redefinition is then

S[\beta,\tilde{\alpha}]=\int\beta\wedge{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha}\>.

(94)

Since the
\check{{\rm d}}
-exact part of
\beta
does not appear, this term is the same as

\displaystyle S[\beta,\tilde{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\check{{\rm d}}^{\tilde{\dagger}}(f\Phi)\wedge{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha}=({{\rm d}}^{\tilde{\dagger}}(f\Phi),\tilde{*}{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha})=(\tilde{*}{\rm d}(f\Psi),\tilde{*}{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha})

\displaystyle=({\rm d}(f\Psi),{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha})=(f\Psi,{\rm d}^{\tilde{\dagger}}{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha})=(f,\Psi\lrcorner{\rm d}^{\tilde{\dagger}}{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha})\>,

(95)

where
(\>,\>)
is the positive definite pairing induced by the metric
\tilde{g}
of
\Psi
, and we have not been careful with signs. We can assume without loss of generality that
f
does not have a constant part (as it would drop out of (
93
)). If we Hodge decompose
\Psi\lrcorner{\rm d}^{\tilde{\dagger}}{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha}
, we get

\Psi\lrcorner{\rm d}^{\tilde{\dagger}}{\rm d}\tilde{\alpha}={\rm d}^{\tilde{\dagger}}{\rm d}g+{\rm constant}\>.

(96)

Note that while
g
is a singlet, it can get contributions from the
(\mathbf{7}+\mathbf{2}\mathbf{7})
-representations of the middle row of the complex, via the field redefinition.

We thus get

\displaystyle S[\beta,\tilde{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=(f,{\rm d}^{\tilde{\dagger}}{\rm d}g)=({\rm d}f,{\rm d}g)=(\Psi\wedge{\rm d}f,\Psi\wedge{\rm d}g)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{*}{\rm d}(f\Psi),\tilde{*}{\rm d}(g\Psi))=\int\beta\wedge{\rm d}(g\Psi)\>.

(97)

This shows that the
\mathbf{7}
-valued two-form
\beta
can only couple to a singlet, also after the field redefinition! As the singlet has no gauge transformations, except for a finite-dimensional space of translations by constants which we ignore, we will take this as our redefined singlet in the top row of the original complex
{\cal E}^{*}
, now using the diagonal differential on
{\cal E}^{*}
.
By slight abuse of notation we shall also refer to this singlet as
\alpha\in\Omega^{4}_{\bf 1}
.

The above computations shows that the original classical action (
78
) can be written as

S=\langle Z,\check{\cal D}Z\rangle_{\cal Q}+\int\beta\wedge\check{{\rm d}}\alpha\>,

(98)

for
\alpha\in\Omega^{4}_{\bf 1}
. The gauge transformations in the current form of the action the
\beta
-fields are precisely as before, shifts by
\check{{\rm d}}
-exact forms. The gauge symmetries of (
98
) are given by
\mathbf{d}
-exact forms, where
\mathbf{d}
refers to the diagonal differential introduced in section
3.5
.

We had to pick a particular
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
-gauge to write the action in the form of (
98
). However, the field redefinitions we have done only include translations corresponding to the upper-triangular matrices
G
and
G^{-1}
, which therefore have a trivial Jacobian in the change of the path integral measure. A general
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
-gauge transformation will add terms to the action (
98
), but these terms can, as the above computations show, be absorbed again by field redefinitions, which again, do not change the path integral measure. The theories are therefore equivalent as quantum theories.

The action (
98
) is simpler to quantise in terms of determinants of more familiar Laplacians. Furthermore, it is easier to study its BV quantisation,
\eta
-invariants, etc. We will leave this for future work, but compute the (absolute value of the) partition function here. A straight-forward computation then gives

|Z|=\frac{\left(\det\Delta_{\check{\cal D}}^{(-1)}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{4}}}{\det\left(\Delta_{\check{\cal D}}^{(0)}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}\times\frac{\left(\det\Delta_{\bf 7}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}{\left(\det\Delta_{\bf 1}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{2}}}\>,

(99)

where the superscript denotes the Laplacian at the corresponding entry in the
{\cal E}^{*}
complex, and the subscripts denote the irreducible representation.
Here we have used that the Laplacians acting on one-forms and
\mathbf{7}
-valued two-forms are equivalent, and similarly with functions and singlet valued four-forms
Ashmore et al. (2022)
.

It is now straight-forward to compare the factors in (
99
) with the terms in the action (
98
). The first factor in (
99
) derives from the first term in (
98
), and is the one-loop partition function of an instanton gauge theory
de Boer et al. (2008b)
, with instanton connection
\cal D
on the bundle
\cal Q
. The second factor derives from the second term in (
98
). It is the inverse of the square of the result of an Abelian instanton gauge theory. The square comes from the fact that we have two fields,
\beta
and
\alpha
, while the inverse is due to the fields being shifted by one form degree. The result may also be compared with previous quantization of type II strings on
G_{2}
manifolds
de Boer et al. (2008c)
;
de Boer et al. (2008a)
;
Ashmore et al. (2022)
.

5
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we have used the superpotential we defined in
de la Ossa et al. (2020)
, for three-dimensional,
{\cal N}=1
compactifications of heterotic supergravity on 7-manifolds
Y
with
G_{2}
structure, to develop a deformation theory of such heterotic
G_{2}
systems. We have shown that the vanishing of the second order variation of the superpotential encodes the equations for infinitesimal moduli of these string configurations, as the constraint

{\check{\mathbb{D}}}{\cal Z}=0\;.

Here
{\cal Z}\in\mathcal{E}^{0}=\Omega^{4}_{\bf 1}\oplus\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{Q})\oplus\Omega^{2}_{\bf 7}
is constructed from the deformations of the supergravity fields (dilaton, metric, gauge field and
B
-field). We have shown, by explicitly constructing the relevant nilpotent differential
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
, and the required symplectic pairing, that this constraint can be encoded in an elliptic double complex (or the associated total complex). This is in accordance with more formal reasoning, based on generalised geometry, for heterotic
G_{2}
compactifications
Kupka et al. (2024)
. We have also noted that the nilpotency of
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
can be interpreted as a
G_{2}
instanton condition, as has been observed previously for complexes related to heterotic systems
de la Ossa et al. (2017)
;
Silva et al. (2024)
.

Our bicomplex encodes the equations for infinitesimal moduli of heterotic
G_{2}
systems at zero homological degree. At lower homological degree one finds the gauge, and gauge for gauge, symmetries of the theory. It thus follows that the infinitesimal moduli of heterotic
G_{2}
systems are captured by
H^{0}_{\check{\mathbb{D}}}(Y,{\cal E})
where the superscript encodes homological degree. Moreover, the obstruction space is given by
H^{1}_{\check{\mathbb{D}}}(Y,{\cal E})
. We have shown that the dimensions of these two cohomology groups are the same, showing that the expected, or “virtual”, dimension of the infinitesimal moduli space is zero. This generalizes to a Serre-like duality and shows that the complex has vanishing Euler characteristic
\chi({\check{\mathbb{D}}},{\cal E})
. This indicates that there might be natural enumerative invariants for heterotic
G_{2}
systems. Computing
H^{p}_{\check{\mathbb{D}}}(Y,{\cal E})
in explicit solutions, such as
de la Ossa and Galdeano (2021)
;
Lotay and Earp (2023)
, is an interesting avenue for future work.

As we discussed already in
de la Ossa et al. (2020)
, one motivation for our study of heterotic superpotentials is that these functionals allow us to explore finite deformations of heterotic systems. This has been established for the Hull-Strominger system in
Ashmore et al. (2018)
: by choosing a parametrisation for finite deformations suggested by holomorphy, it was shown that the superpotential expansion truncates at cubic order, and that the deformation algebra is an
L_{3}
algebra. In
de la Ossa et al. (2020)
we conjectured that there should be a similar convenient parametrisation for the finite deformations of heterotic
G_{2}
systems, which should lead to a superpotential expansion that truncates at fourth order. While we have not been able to confirm this suspicion in this paper, we have taken an important step towards its resolution by identifying a field redefinition that diagonalises the differential appearing in the second order variation of the superpotential.

By interpreting the second order variation of the superpotential as a classical quadratic action in
\cal Z
, we have initiated a study of quantum aspects of the heterotic
G_{2}
system. Using the formal analogy of the action with three-dimensional Chern-Simons theory, we could express the absolute value of the one-loop partition function in terms of determinants of Laplacians
\Delta^{p}_{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
. This result has the same functional form as obtained in
Kupka et al. (2024)
using generalised geometry techniques. With our explicit double complex in hand, we can go further: we have shown that we may perform a field redefinition that diagonalizes
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
. Using this, the one-loop partition function can be rewritten in terms of more familiar Laplacians. This is along the lines of what is obtained from quantization of type II strings on
G_{2}
spaces
de Boer et al. (2008c)
;
de Boer et al. (2008a)
;
Ashmore et al. (2022)
.

In this paper, we have not performed the full BV quantisation of the heterotic
G_{2}
systems. This is a natural next step to take. We expect that, by using the double complex in its diagonalised format, it will be relatively straight forward to compute the phase of the one-loop partition function (i.e. the
\eta
invariant). We may then compare with the results obtained from topological
G_{2}
strings
de Boer et al. (2008b)
;
de Boer et al. (2008a)
. Since, in contrast to these topological studies, our bicomplex encodes both gauge and geometric degrees of freedom, we may be able to provide new insights to possible geometric anomalies of the heterotic
G_{2}
system. The intricate coupling between these degrees of freedom may also help expose to what extent topological invariants and enumerative geometry may be defined for instantons in the
G_{2}
context
Donaldson and Segal (2009)
;
Joyce (2016)
.

Appendix B
Ellipticity

In this appendix, we prove that the
(\cal{E},{\check{\mathbb{D}}})
complex is elliptic. Good introductions to the notions of ellipticity of operators and complexes,
can be found in the book of Gilkey
Gilkey (1996)
, and the lecture notes of Pati
Pati ()
. A very brief summary of most of the notions we need can also be found in appendix B of
de la Ossa et al. (2016b)
.

Recall first that a complex
(E,D)
is elliptic if the induced complex
(E,\sigma_{L}(D))
of leading (or principal) symbols
\sigma_{L}(D)
is exact. Reyes-Carrion
Reyes Carrion (1998)
;
Reyes Carrion (1993)
proved this is the case for the canonical
G_{2}
complex with differential
\check{{\rm d}}
, and in
de la Ossa et al. (2016b)
;
de la Ossa et al. (2018)
this was used to prove ellipticity of the
\check{\cal{D}}
-complex.

Second, we will make use of

Lemma 1
.

Let
E:=\bigoplus L_{i},\,F:=\bigoplus M_{j}
two vector bundles, given by a direct sum with the same index set.
Let
D:E\rightarrow F
a differential operator such that, with respect to the above decomposition:
D
is upper triangular; all entries,
D_{ji}:L_{i}\rightarrow M_{j}
, have the same degree; and, the diagonal entries
D_{ii}
are elliptic.
Then, the total operator,
D
, is elliptic.

Proof.

Given our assumptions, the symbol of
D
is a matrix
\sigma_{\xi}(D)
with entries
\sigma_{\xi}(D)_{ij}=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{ij})
, so the result follows by linear algebra.

∎

Clearly,
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
is upper triangular, and by the results quoted before the Lemma, the diagonal entries of
{\check{\mathbb{D}}}
are elliptic. Moreover, the off-diagonal elements are given by the
\cal{S}
and
\cal{T}
maps, which are all degree-1 operators. Thus, the conditions for Lemma
1
are fulfilled, and we conclude the
(\cal{E},{\check{\mathbb{D}}})
complex is elliptic.

Lemma
1
on the level of the Laplacians
gives the same result.
