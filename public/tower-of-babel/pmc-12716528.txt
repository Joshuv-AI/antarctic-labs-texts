Prognostic value of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio for mortality in acute respiratory distress syndrome: a retrospective observational study in a lower-middle-income country
Authors: Chinh Quoc Luong, Co Xuan Dao, My Ha Nguyen, Dung Thi Pham, Quynh Thi Pham, Tai Thien Vu, Hau Thi Truong, Hai Hoang Nguyen, Thao Thi Phuong Nguyen, Hoai Thi Thu Luong, Cuong Ba Nguyen, Dai Quoc Khuong, Hien Duy Dang, Cong Hong Tran, Tu Thanh Nguyen, Tuan Anh Nguyen, Thach The Pham, Giang Thi Huong Bui, Cuong Van Bui, Quan Huu Nguyen, Thong Huu Tran, Tan Cong Nguyen, Khoi Hong Vo, Lan Tuong Vu, Nga Thu Phan, Phuong Thi Ha Nguyen, Trong Dinh Nguyen, Cuong Duy Nguyen, Anh Dat Nguyen, Chi Van Nguyen, Tuan Quoc Dang, Binh Gia Nguyen, Son Ngoc Do
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the accuracy of the arterial oxygen partial pressure/inspired oxygen fraction (PaO2/FiO2) ratio in predicting mortality among acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients in Vietnam.DesignA retrospective observational study.SettingA central hospital in Vietnam.ParticipantsAdult patients diagnosed with ARDS based on the Berlin definition and admitted to Bach Mai Hospital between August 2015 and August 2023. ARDS severity was converted from descriptive categories to the Berlin score, ranging from 1 (PaO2/FiO2>300 mm Hg) to 4 (PaO2/FiO2≤100 mm Hg).Primary outcomeAll-cause hospital mortality.ResultsOf 345 patients, 67.5% were male, and the median age was 55.0 years (IQR: 39.0–66.0). Hospital mortality was 61.2% (211/345). On the first day of admission, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio (areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROC): 0.585 (95% CI 0.522 to 0.649)) showed limited predictive ability for hospital mortality. Incorporating the PaO2/FiO2 ratio into the Berlin score did not substantially improve accuracy (AUROC: 0.578 (95% CI 0.516 to 0.641)). Both measures were less accurate than Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) (AUROC: 0.650 (95% CI 0.590 to 0.711)), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) (AUROC: 0.685 (95% CI 0.628 to 0.742)) and Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L (20 mg/dL), Respiratory rate ≥30 breaths/min, Blood pressure (systolic <90 mm Hg or diastolic ≤60 mm Hg) and Age ≥65 years (CURB-65) (AUROC: 0.689 (95% CI 0.617 to 0.762)). Higher PaO2/FiO2 values (adjusted OR, AOR: 0.988 (95% CI 0.979 to 0.996)) were independently associated with lower mortality risk, while higher Berlin (AOR: 2.477 (95% CI 1.190 to 5.156)), SOFA (AOR: 1.278 (95% CI 1.102 to 1.482)), APACHE II (AOR: 1.236 (95% CI 1.108 to 1.379)) and CURB-65 (AOR: 7.142 (95% CI 2.581 to 19.763)) scores were associated with increased mortality risk.ConclusionsIn this study of ARDS patients in Vietnam, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio demonstrated limited discriminatory ability for hospital mortality, and incorporating it into the Berlin score did not meaningfully improve performance. While less accurate than SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and Berlin score remained independently associated with mortality risk. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the retrospective design, single-centre setting and potential selection bias; further validation in larger, multicentre studies is warranted.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was first described in 1967 by Ashbaugh et al in 12 patients who exhibited new-onset hypoxaemia that did not respond to supplemental oxygen, along with bilateral infiltrates on chest radiographs and reduced respiratory system compliance. Inflammation, oedema and hyaline membranes were uniformly present in the lungs of those who did not survive.1 Over time, the diagnosis of ARDS transitioned from informal pattern recognition to formalised clinical definitions. The Lung Injury Score (LIS), introduced in 1988,2 was replaced in 1994 by the American-European Consensus Conference definition,3 which was further refined in 2012 by the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine into what is known as the ‘Berlin definition’.4 5 Since the 2012 Berlin definition of ARDS was published, various advancements have highlighted the necessity for an updated definition. These include the adoption of high-flow nasal oxygen, the increased use of pulse oximetry instead of arterial blood gases, the application of ultrasound for chest imaging and the requirement for relevance in resource-limited settings. Consequently, the ‘New Global Definition’ introduced in 2023 builds on the earlier Berlin definition.6 7

ARDS is a life-threatening form of respiratory failure characterised by the acute onset of bilateral alveolar opacities and hypoxaemia. The crucial variable in diagnosing and classifying ARDS is the arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2) to the inspired oxygen fraction (FiO2) ratio (PaO2/FiO2 ratio). This ratio is a component in various definitions and classifications of ARDS (eg, LIS, Berlin definition and New Global Definition) and is used to evaluate the severity of hypoxaemia.27 The treatment for ARDS depends on the severity of the disease. Thus, it is essential to assess the ARDS severity score as soon as the patient is stable to standardise the clinical classification of ARDS patients. Various grading systems are used for this purpose, with the most recognised one being the grading system (ie, Berlin definition) proposed by the ARDS Definition Task Force.4 5 The PaO2/FiO2 ratio is associated with an increased risk of death at different thresholds.3 5 6 8 However, its reliability is subject to caution due to the mismeasure of all things oxygen,9 which can lead to conflicting opinions about its usefulness for diagnosing and predicting the prognosis of ARDS.8 9 Regardless, a global study of 3022 ARDS patients found that mortality increases with the severity of hypoxaemia; the unadjusted hospital mortality rate was 34.9% (249/714) among those with mild (200 < PaO2/FiO2≤300 mm Hg), 40.3% (446/1106) for those with moderate (100 < PaO2/FiO2≤200 mm Hg) and 46.1% (257/557) for patients with severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2≤100 mm Hg).10 Many studies have identified various factors during acute illness that can predict mortality. These factors fall into three categories: patient-related, disease-related and treatment-related. However, no single factor has shown clear superiority over the others. Common predictors of death from ARDS include increasing age, comorbidities, poor nutrition, severe ARDS, infection and multiorgan failure (MOF).

Vietnam, classified as a lower-middle-income country (LMIC), is the 15th most populous nation globally and the 3rd in Southeast Asia, with a population of 96.462 million.11 Despite its recent economic growth spurt,12 the country faces challenges in providing sufficient resources and effective diagnostic, prognostic and treatment strategies for ARDS patients at both local and central levels.1315 Additionally, central hospitals in Vietnam are responsible for receiving patients who cannot be adequately treated in local hospitals,16 leading to delays in the diagnosis, prognosis and initiation of treatment for ARDS patients. In resource-limited settings, it is crucial to identify ARDS patients at risk of death using readily available and accurate scoring systems for better treatment decisions. This study, therefore, aimed to investigate the mortality rate and evaluate the accuracy of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and Berlin definition in predicting mortality in patients with ARDS in Vietnam.

Methods

Source of data

This retrospective observational study is the major update of our previously published paper,14 which collected data on all ARDS patients admitted to the Bach Mai Hospital in Hanoi, Vietnam, between August 2015 and August 2017. To further investigate the mortality rate and evaluate the accuracy of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and Berlin definition in predicting mortality in patients with ARDS in Vietnam, we continued to collect retrospective data on these patients admitted to the Bach Mai Hospital between September 2017 and August 2023. Additional updated details are available in online supplemental file 1. The Bach Mai Hospital is designated as a central general hospital with 3200 beds in northern Vietnam by the Ministry of Health. In the healthcare system of Vietnam, the central hospitals (level I) are responsible for training hospital staff and treating patients who are unable to be adequately treated in local hospital settings, including provincial and district hospitals (levels II and III, according to the Ministry of Health of Vietnam).16 17

Participants

This study included all patients aged 18 years or older who received a diagnosis of ARDS at Bach Mai Hospital. The ARDS diagnosis was established by expert clinicians at the study site. The diagnosis of ARDS was based on the Berlin definition,4 5 which encompasses: (1) Timing: The onset should occur within 1 week following a recognised clinical insult or the emergence or aggravation of respiratory symptoms; (2) Chest imaging: The presence of bilateral opacities on imaging that cannot be entirely attributed to effusions, collapse of a lobe or the entire lung or nodules; (3) Oedema origin: The respiratory failure should not be completely explainable by cardiac failure or excess fluid. An objective evaluation, such as echocardiography, is required to rule out hydrostatic oedema in the absence of any risk factors; and (4) Oxygenation: The oxygenation criterion is the PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≤300 mm Hg, with the patient receiving a minimum of 5 cmH2O of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Data collection

We used the unified case record form (CRF) to collect data on common types of variables from medical records. We used the EpiData Entry software (EpiData Association, Denmark, Europe) to enter data into the study database, which allowed for simple or programmed data entry and documentation that could prevent data entry errors or mistakes. We did not enter patient identifiers into the database to protect patient confidentiality.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measured in this study was hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause during hospitalisation. At the study site, the attending physician decided whether to transfer or discharge patients with ARDS, considering bed availability and overall hospital capacity. Additionally, we assessed secondary outcomes, including complications and the length of hospital stay (LOS).

Predictor measures

The CRF included exposure and confounding variables mainly based on the Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines on the Use of Mechanical Ventilation (MV) in Adult Patients with ARDS18 and collected by fully trained clinicians, such as information on:

We defined the exposure variables as the worst PaO2/FiO2 and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) to FiO2 ratios recorded on the first day of admission. In addition, we considered several severity scoring systems as exposure variables, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score,19 the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score20 and the Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L (20 mg/dL), Respiratory rate ≥30 breaths/min, Blood pressure (systolic <90 mm Hg or diastolic ≤60 mm Hg) and Age ≥65 years (CURB-65) score.21 These were all measured from admission up to 24 hours later. To classify ARDS, we applied the Berlin criteria, which categorise ARDS severity based on the PaO2/FiO2 ratio as follows: mild (200<PaO2/FiO2≤300 mm Hg), moderate (100<PaO2/FiO2≤200 mm Hg) and severe (PaO2/FiO2≤100 mm Hg), with a minimum PEEP of 5 cmH2O applied to the lungs at the end of each breath.4 5 The SOFA score evaluates the severity of organ dysfunction and predicts mortality in critically ill patients. It includes six components: (1) respiratory system (eg, PaO2/FiO2 ratio); (2) cardiovascular system (eg, amount of vasoactive medication required to prevent hypotension); (3) hepatic system (eg, serum bilirubin level); (4) coagulation system (eg, platelet concentration); (5) neurological system (eg, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)) and (6) renal system (eg, serum creatinine or urine output). Each component is scored from 0 (normal function) to 4 (severe abnormalities), resulting in a total SOFA score ranging from 0 (optimal) to 24 (most severe). Higher scores indicate greater severity. The APACHE II score is used to assess disease severity in intensive care units (ICUs) and is calculated based on twelve physiological variables and two disease-related factors. Scores range from 0 to 71 points, with higher scores indicating more severe conditions. The CURB-65 score is based on five easily measurable factors from which its name is derived: (1) confusion; (2) urea >7 mmol/L (20 mg/dL); (3) respiratory rate (RR) ≥30 breaths/min; (4) blood pressure (BP) (systolic <90 mm Hg or diastolic ≤60 mm Hg) and (5) age ≥65 years. Each criterion met scores of 1 point, resulting in a total CURB-65 score ranging from 0 to 5; higher scores indicate greater severity. For score calculations, we used the worst values for each parameter within the 24-hour window. We used the assumed GCS to estimate the neurological status of sedated patients.

We identified confounding factors as variables collected on a unified CRF, which comprised four sections as follows:

The first section focused on baseline characteristics, such as interhospital care (eg, prior hospitalisation, inter-hospital transportation, interhospital care provider, interhospital airway and respiratory care during the transport), demographics (ie, age and gender), documented comorbidities (eg, cerebrovascular disease, chronic cardiac failure, coronary artery disease (CAD)/myocardial infarction, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)/asthma, other chronic pulmonary diseases, tuberculosis, active neoplasm, chronic renal failure, ulcer disease, diabetes mellitus, immunoincompetence and haematological disease) and details of admission. We also used the 19 comorbidity categories to compute the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, which measures the predicted mortality rate based on the presence of comorbidities.22 A score of 0 indicates that no comorbidities were detected; the higher the score, the higher the expected mortality rate is.2224

Sample size

In this retrospective observational study, we aimed to determine the hospital mortality rate as the primary outcome. To calculate the minimum sample size needed for estimating a population proportion with a 95% confidence level and a margin of error (CI) of ±5.275%, we assumed a population proportion of 47.8% based on the hospital mortality rate reported in a previously published study.27 As a result, our sample size should be at least 345 patients, which should be sufficient to represent a normal distribution.

Statistical analyses

We conducted data analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics V.22.0 (IBM) and Analyse-it statistical software (Analyse-it Software, Leeds, UK). Categorical variables are presented as counts (n) and percentages (%), while continuous variables are reported as medians with IQRs or means with SDs, as appropriate. To compare survival and death in the hospital for each variable, we used the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. For continuous data, we employed the Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test or one-way analysis of variance as needed. We assessed normality using histograms, Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parametric tests were applied when the assumptions of normality were satisfied; otherwise, non-parametric alternatives were used.

To evaluate the ability of the PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 ratios to predict hospital mortality among ARDS patients on admission, we plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated areas under the ROC curves (AUROCs). In this analysis, a lower ratio indicates a more positive test result. We converted the descriptive categories for ARDS from the Berlin definition,4 5 which relies on the severity of hypoxaemia, into a numerical grading score known as the Berlin score (online supplemental table S1). The Berlin score ranges from 1 (with a PaO2/FiO2 ratio >300 mm Hg) to 4 (with a PaO2/FiO2≤100 mm Hg). To predict hospital mortality among ARDS patients on admission, we plotted ROC curves and calculated AUROCs to determine the discriminatory ability of the Berlin, SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores, where a higher score signifies a more positive test result. Using ROC curve analysis, we determined the optimal cut-off value for each variable, whether a ratio or score, which identified it as the point that maximised Youden’s index (ie, sensitivity+specificity – 1). To compare the AUROCs, we used Z-statistics to assess the differences in the AUROC values between the PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 ratios. We also compared the predictive accuracy of the Berlin, SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores in determining hospital mortality. Lastly, we examined the relationships among the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, SpO2/FiO2 ratio, Berlin score, SOFA score, APACHE II score and CURB-65 score using correlation analysis. We applied Pearson’s correlation to variables that satisfied the assumptions of normality and linearity, while using Spearman’s r for variables that violated these assumptions.

We conducted logistic regression analyses to assess the associations between each exposure variable (PaO2/FiO2 ratio, SpO2/FiO2 ratio, Berlin score, SOFA score, APACHE II score and CURB-65 score) and hospital mortality, adjusting for the same set of confounders. These included prior hospitalisation, interhospital transfer, demographics, documented comorbidities, clinical and laboratory features, CXR findings, oxygen supplementation, MV, adjunctive therapies and complications. We first performed univariable logistic regression for each exposure variable and confounder. For multivariable modelling, we included each exposure variable alongside confounders with a p<0.25 in univariable analyses comparing survival and death in the hospital, as well as clinically essential variables informed by prior literature and domain expertise.2830 The former comprised demographics (age), comorbidities (active neoplasm, chronic renal failure and haematological diseases), adjunctive therapies (antiviral drugs, NMBAs and tracheostomy), and complications (secondary bacterial infections, septic shock and acute kidney injury). The latter included demographics (gender)28 and comorbidities (chronic cardiac failure29 and CCI score).30 Thus, the initial multivariable model for each exposure incorporated the following confounders: demographics (age and gender); comorbidities (chronic cardiac failure, active neoplasm, chronic renal failure, haematological diseases and CCI score); adjunctive therapies (antiviral drugs, NMBAs and tracheostomy) and complications (secondary bacterial infections, septic shock and acute kidney injury). To refine the models and mitigate overfitting while preserving clinical relevance, we used stepwise backward elimination, beginning with all selected variables and iteratively removing those with the lowest statistical significance until only predictors independently associated with mortality remained (p<0.05). Model robustness was evaluated through sensitivity analyses, including a complete-case analysis that excluded patients with missing covariates, to confirm consistency with the primary multivariable results. We also assessed multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor, with a threshold of <5 indicating acceptable independence, and goodness-of-fit measures, including log-likelihood, pseudo-R2 and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, to verify calibration and model adequacy. Results are reported as ORs with 95% CIs for univariable models and adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs for multivariable models.

The significance levels were two-tailed for all analyses, and we considered the p<0.05 as a statistically significant value.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

In our study, we initially included data from 374 ARDS patients. However, we excluded five patients who were under 18 years old. Additionally, we removed twenty patients (5.4%; 20/369) due to missing data for most variables or crucial data (ie, PaO2 and FiO2) needed to calculate the PaO2/FiO2 ratio on the first day of admission. Furthermore, we eliminated four duplicate entries (1.1%; 4/369). Consequently, our analyses included 345 eligible patients (figure 1).

Clinical characteristics and management

In this study, most patients (90.1%; 311/345) were transferred from local hospitals (online supplemental table S2). The study cohort consisted of 67.5% males (233/345) with a median age of 55.0 years (IQR: 39.0–66.0 years) (table 1 and online supplemental table S3). The most common comorbidities included hypertension (29.1%; 65/223), diabetes mellitus (20.2%; 45/223), chronic renal failure (9.7%; 22/227), haematological diseases (7.4%; 17/230) and COPD/asthma (5.4%; 12/223) (table 1). The primary cause of ARDS was pneumonia (91.0%; 202/222) (table 2). The predominant pathogens identified were influenza A(H1N1) virus (13.0%; 29/223), CMV (2.8%; 6/218), influenza B virus (1.8%; 4/223) and parainfluenza virus (1.3%; 3/223) (online supplemental table S4). On the first day of admission, the mean SpO2/FiO2 ratio was 120.41 (SD: 44.77), while the mean PaO2/FiO2 ratio was 108.42 mm Hg (SD: 56.28), with the PaO2/FiO2 ratio of ≤100 mm Hg that was most commonly observed (56.2, 194/345) (table 3). The median Berlin score was 4.0 (IQR: 3.0–4.0), while the median SOFA score was 10.0 (IQR: 7.0–12.0), the median APACHE II score was 18.0 (IQR: 13.0–23.0), and the mean CURB-65 score was 3.0 (IQR: 3.0–4.0) within the first 24 hours of admission (table 3). Most patients (96.1%; 318/331) received invasive MV on the first day of admission (table 4). Additionally, 24.6% (83/338) of patients underwent prone positioning, and 17.3% (59/341) used recruitment manoeuvres while on MV (table 4). A majority of patients (100%; 137/137) received RRT, and 36.9% (72/195) underwent ECAT during the ICU stay (table 4). We also compared the clinical characteristics and management of patients who survived with those who did not, as detailed in tables 1–4 and online supplemental tables S2–S6.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Of the 345 eligible patients, 211 (61.2%) died in the hospital, with a mean LOS of 10.34 days (SD: 11.60) (figure 1 and table 4). During the study period, ARDS patients commonly experienced several complications. These included HAP in 59.1% (204/345) of patients, secondary bacterial infections in 17.4% (60/345), septic shock in 47.2% (163/345), acute kidney injury in 29.3% (101/345) and liver dysfunction in 11.9% (41/345) (table 4). Additionally, comparisons of these complications between patients who survived and those who died in the hospital are available in table 4.

Overall prognostic performance of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and severity scoring systems

Figures 2 and 3 illustrate the overall performance of severity scoring systems in predicting hospital mortality. Figure 2 shows an inverse correlation between ratios and hospital mortality, indicating that these ratios had poor discriminatory ability for hospital mortality. Specifically, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio demonstrated an AUROC of 0.585 (95% CI 0.522 to 0.649) with a cut-off value of ≥121.85. This ratio achieved a sensitivity of 76.8% and a specificity of 45.5% (PAUROC=0.007). Similarly, the SpO2/FiO2 ratio exhibited a similar AUROC of 0.585 (95% CI 0.523 to 0.647) with a cut-off value of ≥159.67, yielding a sensitivity of 87.1% and a specificity of 27.6% (PAUROC=0.008). The Z-statistics confirmed that there was no significant difference between the AUROC curves of the PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 ratios (AUROC difference: 0.001; 95% CI −0.045 to 0.046; Z-statistic: 0.02; p=0.981) (online supplemental table S7).

Associations between the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and various severity scoring systems with hospital mortality

In the univariable logistic regression analyses, higher PaO2/FiO2 (OR: 0.993; 95% CI 0.989 to 0.997; p<0.001) and higher SpO2/FiO2 ratios (OR: 0.994; 95% CI 0.990 to 0.999; p=0.022) were significantly associated with a decreased risk of hospital mortality (table 6). Conversely, higher scores on the Berlin (OR: 1.680; 95% CI 1.194 to 2.363; p=0.003), SOFA (OR: 1.170; 95% CI 1.092 to 1.253; p<0.001), APACHE II (OR: 1.104; 95% CI 1.066 to 1.144; p<0.001) and CURB-65 (OR: 2.131; 95% CI 1.551 to 2.929; p<0.001) were significantly associated with an increased risk of hospital mortality (table 6). In the multivariable logistic regression analyses, adjusting for the same set of confounding factors, higher PaO2/FiO2 (AOR: 0.988; 95% CI 0.979 to 0.996; p=0.004) and higher SpO2/FiO2 ratios (AOR: 0.985; 95% CI 0.973 to 0.997; p=0.015) remained independently associated with a lower risk of hospital mortality (table 6). Similarly, higher scores on the Berlin (AOR: 2.477; 95% CI 1.190 to 5.156; p=0.015), SOFA (AOR: 1.278; 95% CI 1.102 to 1.482; p=0.001), APACHE II (AOR: 1.236; 95% CI 1.108 to 1.379; p<0.001) and CURB-65 (AOR: 7.142; 95% CI 2.581 to 19.763; p<0.001) were independently associated with an increased risk of hospital mortality (table 6). Discussion

In this study, the PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 ratios appeared to have limited ability to predict hospital mortality on the first day of admission (figure 2). Incorporating the PaO2/FiO2 ratio into the Berlin score did not substantially improve its predictive performance (figure 3). Overall, these measures demonstrated lower accuracy compared with the SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores (figures 2 and 3, and online supplemental table S7). Nonetheless, higher PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 values were associated with a lower risk of hospital mortality (table 6 and online supplemental tables S8, S9, whereas higher Berlin, SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores were associated with an increased mortality risk (table 6 and online supplemental tables S10 and S13).

In this study, we observed that over three-fifths of patients with ARDS died in the hospital (figure 1), consistent with our previously reported mortality rate of 57.1% (72/126).14 This consistency is attributed to identical inclusion criteria and the same hospital setting. However, our observed mortality rate is higher than those reported in several other studies: the Validation of Biomarkers in Acute Lung Injury Diagnosis (VALID) in the USA reported a rate of 23.7% (153/646),31 the Acute Lung Injury: Epidemiology and Natural History (ALIEN) in Spain reported 47.8% (122/255),27 and the Large Observational Study to Understand the Global Impact of Severe Acute Respiratory Failure (LUNG SAFE) reported 40.1% (952/2377).10 Several factors may contribute to these disparities. First, most ARDS patients in our study were transferred from local facilities to central hospitals (online supplemental table S2); this resulted in a highly selective cohort, as not all ARDS patients from local facilities were transferred.14 16 In Vietnam, ARDS patients are initially diagnosed with severe pneumonia at local hospitals and are transferred if their condition worsens, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment. second, transferring ARDS patients can exacerbate their condition. In our study, essential interventions such as intubation (60.5%; 115/190) and MV (25.6%; 40/156) were limited during transfers (online supplemental table S2). A US study also found that delayed intubation was associated with higher mortality.32 Limited data on patient transfers in Vietnam highlight risk factors for mortality, such as increased transfers and suboptimal care quality.33 Finally, differences in patient demographics, pathogens and clinical care between LMICs and high-income countries (HICs) contribute to the observed variations. The primary causes of death in ARDS patients are MOF, sepsis/septic shock and pneumonia. While previous studies consistently identify pneumonia as the primary cause of ARDS, such as the VALID (19.3%; 125/646),31 the ALIEN (42.4%; 108/255)27 and the LUNG SAFE study (59.4%; 1794/3022),10 our study found that nearly all ARDS patients had pneumonia as the primary cause (table 2). Additionally, the SOFA score in our cohort (table 3) aligns with the score reported in the LUNG SAFE study (10.1 (95% CI 9.9 to 10.2)).10 However, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio (table 3) is lower than that reported in the LUNG SAFE study (161 mm Hg (95% CI 158 to 163)).10 This difference may be due to a higher rate of pneumonia in our study (table 2), which was the primary cause of ARDS. Therefore, despite similar illness severity scores between the two studies, our hospital mortality rate is higher. The rate of patients receiving invasive MV in our study (table 4) is comparable to that in the LUNG SAFE study (84.5%; 2377/2813).10 However, a much higher rate of patients in our cohort received NMBAs (table 4) compared with the LUNG SAFE study (21.7%; 516/2377),10 possibly due to the lower PaO2/FiO2 ratio observed in our patients (table 3). NMBAs are not routinely recommended for ARDS patients due to insufficient evidence of their benefits and potential adverse effects. Previous studies have indicated that paralysis and muscle relaxants are risk factors for HAP and sepsis/septic shock.34 35 In our study, HAP and septic shock were common complications (table 4), significantly contributing to the elevated mortality rate.36 Overall, the higher hospital mortality rate observed in our cohort compared with the VALID, ALIEN and LUNG SAFE studies may reflect regional differences in patient characteristics, pathogens and healthcare resources. However, differences in study design, referral patterns and patient selection could also contribute.

Predictive scoring systems, such as the PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 ratios, the Berlin definition, along with the SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores, are essential tools for assessing disease severity and predicting outcomes, particularly mortality, in critically ill ICU patients.5 10 19 20 37 These measurements help standardise research, enhance treatment decisions and evaluate patient care quality across ICUs. This study identified an inverse association between the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and hospital mortality (figure 2 and table 6), consistent with previous findings that mortality rates increase with worsening hypoxaemia.5 10 37 For instance, a multicentre study of 3022 ARDS patients reported mortality rates of 34.9% for mild ARDS (200<PaO2/FiO2≤300 mmHg), 40.3% for moderate ARDS (100<PaO2/FiO2≤200 mm Hg) and 46.1% for severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2≤100 mm Hg).10 However, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio showed only modest discriminatory ability for hospital mortality (figure 2). This observation is consistent with ongoing debates about its reliability.93847 For example, a Chinese study demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUROC: 0.865, 95% CI 0.748 to 0.941) for predicting mortality in COVID-19 ICU patients,39 while an Italian study reported poor discrimination (AUROC: 0.688, 95% CI 0.650 to 0.846).38 An earlier critique noted that while PaO2 reflects oxygenation, its reliability decreases when expressed as a PaO2/FiO2 ratio,9 leading to inconsistent data.38 39 47 Over the past decade, the SpO2/FiO2 ratio has increasingly replaced the PaO2/FiO2 ratio for measuring hypoxaemia,4851 especially in resource-limited settings. A previous study used a non-linear technique to estimate PaO2/FiO2 from SpO2/FiO2, outperforming other methods.50 Our study supports these findings, showing a strong correlation between SpO2/FiO2 and PaO2/FiO2 (table 5) and identifying the higher SpO2/FiO2 ratio as an independent predictor of hospital mortality (table 6). The ‘New Global Definition’ now includes SpO2/FiO2 for oxygenation assessment.6 However, this study found that the SpO2/FiO2 ratio exhibited poor discriminatory ability for hospital mortality (figure 2), possibly due to measurement inaccuracies, especially in patients with darker skin, shock or poor distal perfusion.52 These findings suggest further studies to develop newer scoring systems to improve the accuracy of predicting outcomes for ARDS patients.

The PaO2/FiO2 ratio is a key component of the Berlin definition and is associated with an increased mortality risk at various thresholds.4 5 However, a previous study found that neither the stratification by severity nor the PaO2/FiO2 ratio at the time of study entry is independently associated with mortality.53 In contrast, another study indicated that the Berlin definition’s classification of ARDS severity, specifically a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of ≤100 mm Hg, effectively identifies patients at high risk of death.54 Nevertheless, it is less effective in differentiating between mild ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 200–300 mm Hg) and moderate ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 100–200 mm Hg).54 Our study was consistent with these findings, suggesting that a higher Berlin score was associated with an increased risk of hospital mortality (table 6). Notably, an optimal cut-off value of ≥3.5 (which corresponds to a score of 4, indicating a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of ≤100 mm Hg) was particularly effective in identifying severe ARDS patients at high risk of death (figure 3). Nevertheless, the overall discriminatory performance of this score appeared limited (figure 3). Other severity of illness scores are also associated with mortality in ARDS patients. For example, higher APACHE III scores indicate an increased risk of death,55 a higher SOFA score is associated with worse outcomes,56 and the CURB-65 score assesses mortality risk in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), with a higher score indicating a greater risk of death.57 In a complementary analysis of this extended cohort of ARDS patients at Bach Mai Hospital, we previously assessed the predictive performance of the SOFA score, emphasising its utility for evaluating multi-organ dysfunction and its comparative accuracy with other severity metrics for hospital mortality in a resource-limited setting like Vietnam.58 This parallel investigation provides foundational insights into prognostication based on organ failure, which informs the current focus on oxygenation parameters. In this study, the APACHE II, SOFA and CURB-65 scores demonstrated better prognostic accuracy than the Berlin score (figure 3 and online supplemental table S7); however, all had poor discriminatory ability in predicting hospital mortality (figure 3). Accurate data collection is complicated by the challenges of determining whether deaths are directly attributable to ARDS or comorbidities such as cancer or organ dysfunction. The APACHE scoring system, which predicts mortality in ICU patients, has four versions (APACHE I-IV). Although APACHE IV is the most recent version, some facilities still use older versions, such as APACHE II. Previous studies have shown that the APACHE II score has good prognostic value for acutely ill or surgical patients.20 59 60 However, it is less effective in distinguishing between sterile and infected necrotising pancreatitis and predicting the severity of acute pancreatitis after hours.20 59 60 Our study was in line with these findings, suggesting that although the APACHE II score was independently associated with hospital mortality (table 6), its discriminatory ability for predicting this outcome was modest (figure 3). The SOFA score, initially designed to assess organ dysfunction severity in sepsis patients, is now also used to predict mortality in patients with organ failure from various causes, including ARDS.10 26 61 62 A retrospective study using data from different databases found that the SOFA score had poor predictive ability for hospital mortality in ARDS patients, with AUROC values of 0.610, 0.620 and 0.650 across different cohorts.63 Our study aligns with these findings, demonstrating that the SOFA score also had poor discrimination for hospital mortality (figure 3). The CURB-65 score is primarily designed to assess the severity of CAP and is not typically employed to evaluate ARDS patients.21 However, some components of this score, such as RR and BP, remain relevant for monitoring pneumonia-induced ARDS.64 65 These components can help determine the need for intensive care and predict mortality risk. A retrospective study on hospitalised COVID-19 patients found that the CURB-65 score had good discrimination for predicting hospital mortality, with an AUROC of 0.85 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.89).64 In contrast, a cross-sectional study on patients with COVID-19-induced ARDS revealed that the CURB-65 score only showed fair discrimination for hospital mortality, with an AUROC of 0.77 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.89).65 While a higher CURB-65 score was identified as an independent predictor of hospital mortality (table 6), our findings indicated its poor discriminatory ability for this outcome (figure 3). This trend of decreasing accuracy in predicting hospital mortality may be attributed to the different inclusion criteria used across studies. These findings suggest the need for further studies to develop new scoring systems that enhance the accuracy of predicting outcomes for ARDS patients.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective design constrained both the availability and completeness of data across multiple variables (online supplemental table S14). For example, CURB-65 scores were available for only 218 patients. Such data gaps may have introduced selection bias into the multivariable logistic regression analysis, as missing variables could affect both the patient’s initial clinical status and their subsequent risk of hospital mortality. Consequently, some clinically relevant predictors may have been excluded, which could potentially undermine the robustness of the model. To mitigate this issue, we conducted a complete-case analysis by excluding patients with missing covariate data. The results closely aligned with those of the primary analysis, suggesting that our conclusions are stable and not materially affected by the approach used to handle missing data (online supplemental tables S15–S20). Moreover, we evaluated model performance using several diagnostic criteria, including the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, pseudo R2 values and log-likelihood statistics. These evaluations demonstrated satisfactory calibration and discrimination. Further details are provided in the note boxes accompanying online supplemental tables S8–S13. Second, the study was conducted at a single centre in Hanoi, Vietnam, and focused on a highly selective population of cases transferred from local hospitals to the highest-level public sector hospitals in the country. As a result, the actual number of ARDS patients is likely much higher. Third, not all ARDS patients from local hospitals were referred to the central hospital; only those with worsening conditions were transferred. This selection bias and incomplete inclusion of patients in the study database may have influenced the observed mortality rate, potentially leading to an overestimation of fatalities. Lastly, although the sample size was sufficient, the CI was slightly wide (±5.275%), which may influence the sample’s normal distribution. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes may be necessary to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective single-centre study of ARDS patients in Vietnam, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio showed only modest ability to predict hospital mortality, and incorporating it into the Berlin score did not substantially enhance its accuracy. Compared with SOFA, APACHE II and CURB-65 scores, the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and Berlin score were less effective overall, though both remained independently associated with mortality risk. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to the retrospective design, the selective patient population and the potential for overestimation of mortality. Future multicentre and prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and to refine prognostic tools for ARDS in resource-limited settings.
