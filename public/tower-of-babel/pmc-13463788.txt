Behavioral factors associated with artemisinin resistance: linking community treatment practices with pfkelch13 mutations in Northern Uganda
Authors: Khin Zar Khaing Thein, Naoyuki Fukuda, Betty Balikagala, Naoko Yoshida, Makoto Kubo, Brenda Anena, Denis Arony Anywar, Emmanuel Odongo Aginya, Toshihiro Mita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUganda is listed among the few African countries where artemisinin partial resistance has already emerged. It has been increasing there since 2017, providing a unique opportunity to track the dynamics of its spread. Although selection of artemisinin resistant parasites by sub-optimal dosing has been suggested in vitro, epidemiological evidence that community knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward treatment seeking or adherence affect the spread of artemisinin resistance is lacking.MethodsIn 2023, we conducted active case detection of Plasmodium falciparum (Pf) malaria in 15 villages of Gulu city, Northern Uganda. With informed consent, blood samples were collected and used for 18S ribosomal DNA PCR for Pf detection and subsequent genotyping of pfkelch13 gene by direct sequencing. Structured interviews were administered to household heads to assess their KAP regarding malaria prevention and treatment-seeking behaviors. Factors associated with Pf infection and the presence of WHO-validated artemisinin partial resistance mutations were analyzed using multiple linear regression models.ResultsA total of 1249 participants from 298 households were enrolled, and the overall Pf prevalence by PCR was 20.7%. Prevalence was highest in school-aged children (6–17 years; 31.5%), followed by adults (≥ 18 years; 18.2%). The risk of Pf infection among the general population was 10% higher in households lacking sustained bed net use for children (95% CI 1.1–18.9), 6.9% higher in households with lower educational levels (95% CI 0.8–12.9), and 9.7% higher in rural households (95% CI 4.5–14.9). The overall prevalence of C469Y and A675V validated pfkelch13 mutations was 23.9% and was highest in school-aged children (30.5%). Importantly, the prevalence of C469Y or A675V mutation was 13.1% higher in households where individuals did not consistently take the correct antimalarial dosage (95% CI 0.5–25.6).ConclusionsImproper bed net use and non-adherence to the antimalarial regimen were associated with a higher risk of Pf infection and artemisinin resistance mutations, underscoring the need for community-based education to reduce the further spread of these mutations. As school-aged children represent the largest reservoir of Pf and artemisinin resistance mutations presence, targeted preventive measures and interventions for this age group are essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05976-z.

Background

Malaria remains a major global challenge, causing 282 million infections and 610,000 deaths in 2024, with over 90% occurring in the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region. Uganda ranks among the highest‑burden countries globally [1]. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), with artemether-lumefantrine as the first-line treatment regimen and dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine as the second-line, have been used in Uganda since 2004 [2, 3]. However, artemisinin partial resistance, defined as parasite clearance half-life > 5 h or day-3 positivity following artemisinin-based treatments, emerged in 2017 [4–6] and is associated with C469Y and A675V mutations in the Plasmodium falciparum (Pf) kelch13 gene [4, 5, 7–9]. They have spread rapidly to neighboring countries: the Democratic Republic of Congo and South Sudan [10] while independent local emergence of another mutation, R561H, was evidenced simultaneously in Tanzania and Rwanda [11, 12]. These mutations have been validated as artemisinin resistance mutations by the WHO, based on their clinical association with artemisinin partial resistance and reduced artemisinin susceptibility in in vitro/ex vivo ring-stage survival assay [13].

Artemisinin partial resistance can accelerate the development of partner drug resistance by reducing the synergic effect to prevent resistance in combination therapies. This pattern has been observed in the Greater Mekong Subregion, where the declining efficacy of partner drugs necessitated shifts to alternative ACT regimens [14, 15], highlighting the urgency of controlling artemisinin partial resistance in Africa. Although lumefantrine resistance resulting in clinical treatment failure has not yet been reported, studies in Uganda have already identified reduced lumefantrine susceptibility associated with the pfkelch13 C469Y mutation [9, 16].

Behavioral factors such as poor treatment-seeking behavior and inadequate adherence to antimalarial regimens may be associated with higher prevalence of drug-resistant parasites [17]. Poor adherence may expose parasites to sub-therapeutic drug concentrations or shortened treatment duration. In vitro studies suggest that such conditions facilitate the selection and proliferation of parasites harboring artemisinin resistance mutations [18]. In clinical settings, such incomplete treatment often results from inadequate dosing or self-medication [19, 20]. In rural Uganda, seeking treatment at drug shops without proper diagnostic testing is also common [21], further contributing to inappropriate antimalarial use. Despite the potential importance of these behaviors, to our knowledge, direct epidemiological comparisons between knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding malaria and the prevalence of pfkelch13 mutations remain lacking. This gap limits our understanding of the extent to which behavioral factors contribute to the spread of drug resistance. Furthermore, the recent shift in malaria burden towards older children in Uganda [22, 23] may reflect poor adherence to prevention measures in this age group. Unlike younger children, who remain the primary focus of malaria control programs, older children and adolescents are less frequently targeted and often exhibit lower adherence to preventive measures such as bed nets [24, 25], which may facilitate increased parasite prevalence in this age group.

Artemisinin partial resistance has emerged in Uganda, while reported occurrences remain localized to a few African countries [26]. This makes Uganda a critical site for investigating the factors which may influence selection pressure of artemisinin resistance, providing early insights to prepare for potential spread to other countries. Although sub-optimal treatment has been documented in our study area [27], its association with artemisinin resistance remains unclear. The objective of this study was to examine the association between KAP and risk of resistant infections. To address this, we integrated a questionnaire‑based sociological approach with molecular epidemiology to identify behavioral and parasitological factors that may facilitate the spread of resistance, particularly among asymptomatic and untreated populations, which account for the predominant source of malaria transmission [28].

Methods

Study sites, recruitment and sampling procedures

A community-based cross-sectional study was performed during the wet seasons between May and September 2023 in Gulu city, Northern Uganda, which is 332 km from Kampala, the capital of Uganda (Fig. 1). Among 128 villages under Gulu city [29], 15 villages were selected based on accessibility and availability of functioning village health teams (VHTs). In each village, 20 households were selected by systematic random sampling. Households were recruited across multiple visits to each village. However, each household was enrolled only once, and individuals were not resampled. We performed continuous sampling of participants throughout the study period rather than a single visit per village. Blood samples were collected from two to five participants per household by finger pricking. Participants aged ≥ 6 months were included even if they had taken antimalarial drugs but excluded if they had fever at the time of sampling. The required individual sample size was estimated as 1249 using Cochran’s sample size formula (30), based on the Pf prevalence of 18.8% in children under five years in indoor residual spraying (IRS) districts [31] and a pfkelch13 mutations prevalence of 19.8% in a Gulu hospital-based study [5]. The calculation assumed a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of 1.5% (Supplementary Table 1). Informed consent was obtained from adult participants (≥ 18 years) or from legal guardians of participating children. Additionally, assent was obtained from children aged 7–17 years. KAP data collection

The household head or a representing family member was interviewed about their KAP related to malaria. Questionnaires were developed by adjusting relevant questions related to KAP about malaria in Tanzania, Uganda, and Nigeria [32–34]. It consisted of 20 general questions, 16 precaution questions about KAP regarding malaria prevention and control, and 23 treatment questions about KAP regarding treatment (Supplementary Table 2). Precaution/treatment scores, defined as the numbers of correct answers to precaution/treatment questions, were used to assess participants’ overall KAP level regarding malaria prevention and treatment. The data collection protocol was distributed to all interviewers prior to fieldwork. Each interviewer underwent a two-day standardized training session covering survey procedures, ethical considerations, blood sample collection, and data recording methods. KAP data were based on participants’ self-reported experiences without a strictly defined recall period or checking medical records. However, to improve data reliability, some items were rephrased across knowledge, attitude, and practice questions to confirm participants’ understanding. To minimize recall bias, trained interviewers used standardized questionnaires and probing techniques to aid recall. To further ensure data quality, a local staff member revisited some of the selected households and re-administered the interview to compare responses with the original data.

Parasite detection and genotyping

Blood samples were obtained by finger pricking, and blood smears and dry blood spots (DBS) were prepared at the study site. Parasite density was determined using thick smears stained with 2% Giemsa by two researchers pre-validated for inter-observer consistency. Parasite density was determined by the number of parasites per 200 white blood cells (WBCs) count using the following formula (5). Parasite DNA was extracted from a quarter of DBS (25µL) using Kaneka Easy DNA Extraction Kit Version 2 (Kaneka Corporation, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. We utilized a polymerase-chain-reaction (PCR)-based assay against the 18S ribosomal RNA gene for parasite detection and species identification (Supplementary Table 3 and 4). The PCR protocol was validated using serially diluted Pf culture in quintuplicate. The detection limit, defined as the parasite density for 95% positivity in the sigmoid curve fitting, was 0.24 parasites/μL. Polymorphism in the propeller domain of the pfkelch13 gene was examined for Pf positive samples by direct sequencing as previously described [5]. Moreover, 47 samples that failed direct sequencing were genotyped by allele-specific quantitative PCR for the C469 and A675 loci [35].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed with R (Version 4.3.1). Fisher's exact test, Wilcoxon rank sum test, Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test, Cochran-Armitage test, and Spearman’s rank correlation test were used for univariate analysis. Standard linear regression was applied for multivariate analysis, while multilevel linear mixed-effects regression models with a random intercept for village were fitted since households were nested within villages. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated from empty models containing only random intercept. Since the number of participants per household ranged from 2 to 5, total number of participants per household was incorporated as an analytic weight to account for variation in the precision of outcomes. Since sociodemographic and behavioral information was obtained from the household head or a representative family member, the relationship between these factors and the prevalence of Pf and C469Y/A675V mutations was analyzed at the household level. To determine the median household Pf prevalence, the proportion of Pf-infected members relative to total participants was calculated for each household. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

In 27.2% of households, the heads had completed high school or higher education. Malaria prevalence

Among the 1249 recruited asymptomatic participants, the overall malaria prevalence was 18.4% (n = 230) by microscopy and 22.6% (n = 282) by PCR; 18.8% of the PCR positive cases (irrespective of parasite species) were malaria-negative by microscopy. (Supplementary Table 6). Pf was the most common species (91.8%), followed by Plasmodium malariae (Pm) (7.1%) and Plasmodium ovale (Po) (5.3%) including mixed infections of multiple species (Fig. 2A). For the following analysis, Pm and Po cases were excluded because pfkelch13 gene is Pf-specific and our study focused on artemisinin resistance in Pf. The overall Pf prevalence was 20.7% (n = 259), with significant variation among villages, ranging from 6.2 to 37.9% (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). Pf prevalence also varied across age groups, with the highest in school-aged children of 6–17 years (31.5%), exceeding that in children ≤ 5 years and adults (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Prevalence of artemisinin resistance mutations in pfkelch13

Validated artemisinin resistance mutations in pfkelch13 [36] were detected in 62 out of 259 Pf-positive samples (23.9%). Among them, C469Y and A675V accounted for 67.6% and 33.9%, respectively; the percentages were not mutually exclusive since one isolate carrying both mutations (Fig. 2B). No further validated or candidate artemisinin resistance mutations were identified, and mutations of uncertain significance were described in Supplementary Table 7. The prevalence of either C469Y or A675V mutations (C469Y/A675V) tended to be higher in school-aged children (30.5%), although this was not statistically significant (P = 0.07) (Fig. 3D). In contrast, antimalarial treatment within 4 weeks, considered as a proxy for drug pressure regardless of regimens or adherence, was more frequent in children ≤ 5 years (57.6%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3E). No association was observed between recent drug pressure and the prevalence of C469Y/A675V mutations (26.6% in participants with treatment history within 4 weeks vs. 23.7% in those without, P = 0.65). Furthermore, no clear pattern was observed at the village level, although analyses were limited by the small number of villages (n = 20); prevalence of C469Y/A675V mutations was not correlated with either Pf prevalence (r = 0.21, P = 0.45) (Supplementary Fig. 1) or recent drug pressure (r = −0.07, P = 0.79) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Household-level prevalence of Pf and artemisinin resistance mutations in relation to sociodemographic and behavioral factors

Pf infection was detected in 162 households, with a median household Pf prevalence of 20% (IQR: 0–33%). In univariate analyses, the household Pf prevalence was associated with the educational level of the household head (median: 0% in high school education or above vs. 20% in others; P = 0.002), area of residence (median: 22.5% in rural vs. 0% in semi-urban; P < 0.001), and children’s bed net use (median: 20% in complete use vs. 25% in incomplete use; P = 0.01) (Supplementary Table 8). These associations remained significant in the multivariable analysis. Pf prevalence was 6.9% (95% CI 0.77–13.0, P = 0.03) higher in households where the heads had less than a high school education, 9.7% (95% CI 4.5–14.9, P < 0.001) higher in rural households, and 10.0% (95% CI 1.1–18.9, P = 0.03) lower in households where children consistently sleep under bed nets (Table 2). Estimates from multilevel models with villages as a random effect were similar in magnitude and direction to those obtained from standard multiple linear regression (Supplementary Table 9), suggesting that village-level clustering had little influence on the observed associations. Village analysis

The relationship between Pf or C469Y/A675V mutations prevalence and proportion of households with relevant behavioral factors was examined at the village level. While no correlations reached statistical significance, the sample size (n = 15) was insufficient to provide definitive inferential conclusions, even regarding the absence of associations. Within a descriptive context, however, lower Pf prevalence was recorded in villages with higher reported use of bed net, coils, or repellent (Supplementary Fig. 3). Also, proportion of households reporting incorrect dosing, sharing drugs or stopping treatment after symptoms relief had high C469Y/A675V mutations prevalence. However, treatment without a blood test and purchasing drugs at drug shops exceptionally reduced C469Y/A675V mutations prevalence (Supplementary Fig. 4). This unexpected trend may reflect small sample size or reporting bias and should be interpreted with caution.

KAP on malaria prevention and treatment

Most respondents (n = 277/298, 93.0%) recognized that malaria can be transmitted by mosquito bite, and (n = 225/298, 75.5%) correctly reported that mosquitoes mostly feed at night. While 98.3% (291/296) of respondents were aware that sleeping under bed nets can prevent malaria, only 84.5% (251/297) reported that their children sleep under bed nets every night. About half of respondents acknowledge the usefulness of mosquito coils and repellents; however, consistent use was reported by only 4.0% (12/298) for mosquito repellent/coils and 1.3% (4/298) for spray.

Regarding treatment seeking behavior, although 90.3% (269/298) were aware that they should visit a health center first, 70.0% (207/296) reported consistently doing so in practice. Similarly, while 75.8% (225/297) replied that they indicated a preference against buying antimalarial drug from drug shops, in practice, 54.4% (162/298) had done so without prescription, and 43.6% (130/298) had taken antimalarial drugs without prior blood test. Knowledge of treatment was generally high; 99.3% (296/298) correctly identified artemether-lumefantrine (Coartem®) as the recommended drug, and 79.2% (236/298) were aware of the full 3-day regimen. However, 19.5% (58/298) still reported not completing the full course of antimalarial treatment. Moreover, 28.5% (85/298) reported discontinuing treatment when symptoms improved, and 18.5% (55/298) reported sharing antimalarial drugs with family members. These findings suggest that existing knowledge is not consistently translated into appropriate malaria treatment-seeking practice.

The median precaution score was 11 (IQR: 9–12) out of 16, and the treatment score was 20 (IQR: 18–22) out of 23. The two scores showed a weak correlation (r = 0.15, P = 0.01). Although statistically significant, the correlation between two scores was weak, indicating that knowledge of prevention and treatment behaviors was largely independent. Higher educational attainment of the household head and smaller household size were associated with higher precaution score (Supplementary Table 12). In contrast, neither precaution score nor treatment score was correlated with Pf prevalence or the prevalence of the C469Y/A675V mutations.

Discussion

Northern Uganda [37], along with Rwanda [12], is one of the earliest regions in Africa where artemisinin partial resistance emerged since 2017. Mutations in the pfkelch13 gene, associated with artemisinin partial resistance have been shown to be increasing in this region. These features make Northern Uganda a relevant setting to investigate behavioral drivers of artemisinin partial resistance. This study revealed that school-aged children constitute the largest reservoir of artemisinin resistant parasites and represent a key target for intervention: e.g., education to promote adherence to antimalarial regimens and proper treatment seeking. It also provided the first evidence of a relationship between KAP and the prevalence of pfkelch13 mutations, highlighting a potential association between improper drug use and higher prevalence of mutations.

We observed that asymptomatic Pf infections were most prevalent among school-aged children, consistent with previous studies in East Africa [22, 23, 38]. Notably, artemisinin partial resistance mutations in pfkelch13 were also most prevalent in this age group, despite antimalarial drug use being more common in children under five years. High drug pressure typically drives the selection of resistant parasites. However, our results suggest that the relatively lower drug pressure in school-aged children may paradoxically contribute to the persistence of artemisinin resistance mutations. Although the drug pressure estimates were based on a 4-week recall of antimalarial drug use, without accounting for specific regimens or adherence, they serve as a crude proxy for the overall frequency of drug exposure in this age group. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that school-aged children could act as an important reservoir for parasites harboring pfkelch13 mutations. Children ≤ 5 years often develop clinical symptoms due to limited immunity and thus receive treatment with artemisinin and partner drugs [39], which clears most parasites even with partial resistance. In contrast, our data demonstrate that school-aged children are less frequently subjected to this treatment-mediated clearance, due to their largely asymptomatic nature. This lower drug pressure allows artemisinin partial resistance strains to persist and circulate within this age group without being eliminated. Furthermore, school-aged children were reported to be bitten more often by Anopheles mosquitoes than children under five years in Uganda [40, 41], increasing their likelihood of acquiring multiple strains, including artemisinin resistance [42]. Supporting this, we observed a high proportion of mixed infections comprising both wild-type and mutant parasites in this age group. In addition, submicroscopic (PCR-positive but microscopically negative) infections make malaria control more challenging since they are frequently missed by routine diagnosis and can contribute to ongoing carriage and transmission. The prevalence observed in our study (18.8%) aligns with previous studies in Uganda [43, 44] showing that submicroscopic infections are common and represent a substantial hidden reservoir of Pf including pfkelch13 mutant parasites. Collectively, these factors suggest that school-aged children constitute the largest reservoir of pfkelch13 mutations associated with artemisinin partial resistance.

Several sociodemographic and behavioral factors were identified as determinants of Pf or C469Y/A675V mutations prevalence. In particular, households that did not consistently take the correct antimalarial dose had higher prevalence of C469Y/A675V mutations, suggesting an association between poor adherence and pfkelch13 mutations associated with artemisinin partial resistance. These findings highlight the potential role of adherence in influencing these markers. Although the village-level analysis did not show a statistically significant correlation likely due to the small sample size, villages with higher C469Y/A675V mutations similarly tended to have poor adherence. Moreover, taking antimalarial drugs without a blood test or purchasing them directly from drug shops did not increase the likelihood of C469Y/A675V mutations prevalence when treatment was properly completed. However, this trend should be interpreted cautiously given potential reporting bias and limited sample size. Additionally, Pf prevalence was higher in households where children did not use bed nets properly, consistent with the previous observations from Uganda and Rwanda [45–47]. Finally, higher education of household heads was associated with lower Pf prevalence consistent with other studies [25, 48, 49]. However, although higher education was correlated with higher precaution score (Supplementary Table S12), the score itself was not directly associated with Pf prevalence. These findings suggest that the protective effect of education may be mediated by other socio-economic factors not fully captured by the scoring system. In interpreting these findings, associations are difficult to detect because individuals with good practices may still be exposed to Pf or C469Y/A675V mutations from neighbors with poor practices through mosquito transmission. Despite this limitation, these associations were still observed, underscoring their importance.

To address these challenges, a set of community‑oriented interventions is required. Taken together, these findings underscore the potential importance of community based strategies aimed at improving treatment adherence and promoting appropriate antimalarial drug use to help limit the spread of artemisinin partial resistance. Given that primary sources of health information were health centers and radios, leveraging these channels to strengthen public awareness and social education on malaria would be highly beneficial. Health education directly for school-aged children is also essential, complementing efforts directed at their parents or guardians. Nevertheless, bridging the persistent gap between knowledge and practice remains a major challenge. Although directly observed therapy is an ideal solution to prevent sub-optimal dosing, it would be unrealistic in settings where access to health centers is limited and only about 70% of individuals seek care. Therefore, engaging trained staff at drug shops could represent a potentially feasible strategy to improve adherence and rational antimalarial use, although implementation and effectiveness would need further evaluation.

A limitation of this study is the non-random selection of 15 out of 128 villages, due to accessibility and local engagement constraints, resulting in potential sampling bias. In addition, the cross-sectional design did not allow us to monitor changes in C469Y/A675V mutations prevalence or treatment-seeking behavior over time. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, this study identified key factors associated with Pf prevalence and artemisinin resistance mutations in pfkelch13, providing valuable insights to guide the implementation and strengthening targeted interventions.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that inadequate adherence to antimalarial regimens is strongly associated with higher prevalence of pfkelch13 mutations linked to artemisinin resistance within communities. These findings underscore the role of human behavior in influencing the effectiveness of ACTs in real world settings, alongside biological and vector‑related determinants. Furthermore, at the household level, improper bed net use and lower educational attainment were associated with higher Pf prevalence among members, highlighting the importance of health education. Since school-aged children emerged as the highest-risk group for both Pf infection and artemisinin resistance mutations, they should be prioritized as a key population for targeted interventions.
