Attention Via Convolutional Nearest Neighbors
Mingi Kang, Jeová Farias Sales Rocha Neto

Abstract
The shift from Convolutional Neural Networks to Transformers has reshaped computer vision, yet these two architectural families are typically viewed as fundamentally distinct. We argue that convolution and self-attention, despite their apparent differences, can be unified within a single k k -nearest neighbor aggregation framework. The critical insight is that both operations are special cases of neighbor selection and aggregation; convolution selects neighbors by spatial proximity, while attention selects by feature similarity, revealing they exist on a continuous spectrum. We introduce Convolutional Nearest Neighbors ( ConvNN ), a unified framework that formalizes this connection. Crucially, ConvNN serves as a drop-in replacement for convolutional and attention layers, enabling systematic exploration of the intermediate spectrum between these two extremes. We validate the framework’s coherence on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 classification tasks across two complementary architectures: (1) Hybrid branching in VGG improves accuracy on both CIFAR datasets by combining spatial-proximity and feature-similarity selection; and (2) ConvNN in ViT outperforms standard attention and other attention variants on both datasets. Extensive ablations on k k values and architectural variants reveal that interpolating along this spectrum provides regularization benefits by balancing local and global receptive fields. Our work provides a unifying framework that dissolves the apparent distinction between convolution and attention, with implications for designing more principled and interpretable vision architectures.



1
Introduction

Deep learning architectures for computer vision have undergone a fundamental transformation over the past two decades. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) dominated through pioneering architectures like AlexNet
19
, VGG
27
, and ResNet
14
. More recently, Vision Transformers (ViT)
9
have reshaped the landscape with attention-based models achieving state-of-the-art performance across numerous vision tasks. Yet despite these successes, convolution and attention are typically treated as fundamentally distinct mechanisms suited for different situations.

Traditionally, convolution operates through local spatial aggregation, where each output is computed from a fixed spatial neighborhood. In contrast, self-attention
29
enables global feature aggregation by computing similarity-based weighted combinations across all spatial positions. However, beneath these surface differences lies a common principle: both operations aggregate features from selected neighbors. The distinction is in neighbor selection–convolution selects by spatial proximity, while attention selects by learned feature similarity.

We propose
Convolutional Nearest Neighbors (
ConvNN
)
, a unified framework that formalizes the interconnection between convolution and attention through
k
-nearest neighbor (
k
-NN) selection. Our key insight is that both operations are specialized instances of neighbor aggregation, differing in their selection strategy. Crucially,
ConvNN
can be exactly configured to recover standard convolution and exactly recover standard attention, proving both are points on a continuous spectrum.

The flexibility of
ConvNN
enables systematic exploration of this spectrum. Rather than choosing between convolution or attention, we can design hybrid configurations that combine spatial and feature-based neighbor selection. Our experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 demonstrate that these configurations achieve consistent improvements in accuracy when integrated into both CNN (VGG) and Transformer (ViT) architectures. Beyond performance gains, hybrid configurations provide interpretability by explicitly balancing local spatial features with global feature dependencies. This unified framework provides a foundation for designing next-generation operations and architectures that leverage the strengths of both mechanisms.

2
Related Works

Convolution and Attention in Vision

Convolution has dominated computer vision through architectures like AlexNet
19
, VGG
27
, and ResNet
14
, providing efficient local feature extraction with spatial inductive biases. Recently, Vision Transformers (ViT)
9
and other attention-based models
21
;
20
have achieved comparable or superior performance through self-attention
29
, which captures long-range dependencies without spatial constraints, but at a larger computational requirement.

Several variants of the self-attention mechanism have been proposed to reduce its quadratic computational and memory complexity.

Local Attention

1
restricts each query to attend only to nearby tokens within a fixed window, thereby exploiting locality while lowering the attention cost from
O(n^{2})
to
O(nw)
, where
w
is the window size.

Sparse Attention

6
, further generalizes this idea by enforcing structured sparsity patterns in the attention matrix, allowing each token to attend to a predefined subset of positions.
These approaches preserve most of the modeling capacity of global attention while making Transformer architectures more scalable for long sequences.

Connections Between Convolution and Attention

Recent work began exploring the theoretical relationships between these seemingly distinct operations. Cordonnier et al.
8
provided evidence that self-attention with relative positional encoding can express any convolutional operation, suggesting convolution and attention may exist on a spectrum.
Non-Local Neural Networks

31
and channel-based attention mechanisms
16
introduced global aggregation mechanisms, demonstrating that operations beyond local convolution can enhance feature learning. Furthermore, recent architectures have explicitly combined both operations.
Attention Augmented Convolutional Networks
2
augment convolutional layers with self-attention.
CoAtNet

32
systematically studies depthwise convolutions followed by attention blocks, showing that this combination achieves strong performance.
ConViT

10
introduces gated positional self-attention that gradually transitions from convolutional-like to attention-like behavior.
Neighborhood Attention

13
employs sliding windows to reduce attention’s computational cost while preserving spatial locality. While these hybrid architectures demonstrate the benefits of combining local and global operations, they treat convolution and attention as distinct operations combined through architectural composition rather than unified through a common theoretical principle.

k
-Nearest Neighbors in Deep Learning

The
k
-nearest neighbor (
k
-NN) principle has recently reemerged as a valuable design component in neural network architectures.

Neural Nearest Neighbors Networks

23
first explored the integration of
k
-NN reasoning primarily for few-shot learning.
More recently,
k
-NN mechanisms have been incorporated into Transformers to mitigate the quadratic computational and memory costs of self-attention.
In particular,
k
-NN Attention for Boosting Vision Transformers (KVT)

30
enhances Vision Transformers by introducing a
k
-nearest-neighbor attention mechanism that restricts each token’s attention to its most relevant local neighbors rather than the entire sequence.
Formally, KVT introduces a
k
-NN masking function that constrains each query to attend only to its
k
most similar keys, according to the traditional scaled dot-product similarity.
The selected neighbor values are then normalized via a softmax operation, and the overall attention is computed with a reduced complexity of
O(nk\log k)
—lower than the traditional
O(n^{2}d)
of full self-attention—where
n
denotes the number of tokens and
d
the embedding dimension. By adaptively selecting the
k
most similar keys for each query in feature space, KVT effectively balances local and global context modeling, leading to better performance and faster convergence compared to standard ViTs.

Similarly,
Unlimiformer

3
addresses attention computation complexity by using
k
-NN indices to compute only
k
-NN distances.
Routing Transformers

25
rely on
k
-means clustering to model sparse attention matrices; this allows each token to attend to its
k
closest tokens. Furthermore, in
k
-NN Attention Demystified
12
, a theoretical framework for
k
-NN attention is presented, reframing self-attention as expectations and leveraging lazy Gumbel sampling for efficient approximation.
Memory-efficient Transformers

11
also addresses this complexity through a highly accurate approximation for vanilla attention with top-
k
scores in chunks of queries. These works demonstrate the value of neighbor selection as a design principle, though they treat
k
-NN as an enhancement to existing architectures rather than a unifying framework for understanding different operations.

Notation

Scalar variables, vectors, and matrices, are denoted in plain lowercase (e.g.,

\mathbf{m}_{i}=0

A vector

\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{x})_{i}=\exp(x_{i})/\sum_{j}\exp(x_{j})

3.2
k
-Nearest Neighbors

Let

Correspondingly,
\text{$k$-argmax}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
returns a multi-hot encoding

indicating the positions of these top-

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\subset X

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

3.3
Convolution and Attention

3.3.1
Convolution

Let
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
be a 1D tensor with
c
channels and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times c}
a convolutional kernel with bias
b\in\mathbb{R}
. A 1D convolution (
Conv1D
) slides
W
across
Z
, computing local dot products and adding
b
, to produce
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times c^{\prime}}
, where
n^{\prime}
depends on the kernel size, stride, and padding.
In 2D (
Conv2D
), the input
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{w\times h\times c}
is convolved with kernels
W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k\times c}
across both spatial dimensions, yielding
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{w^{\prime}\times h^{\prime}\times c^{\prime}}
.
For
k=1
, the operation reduces to a pointwise linear projection across channels (a
1\times 1
convolution).

In
depthwise convolution

7
, instead of learning kernels that span all input channels, we assign one kernel per channel. That is, each channel
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is convolved with its own kernel
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times 1}
, yielding
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times 1}
. These
c
independent convolutions capture spatial correlations within each channel separately, without mixing information across channels.

3.3.2
Attention

Given query, key, and value matrices
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times h}
,

K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times h}
, and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times v}
, respectively,
the dot-product attention
29
computes

A=\operatorname{softmax}(QK^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},

(2)

and produces the output
AV\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times v}
.
We omit the usual scaling factor
\sqrt{h}
for simplicity, as it does not affect our proposed formulation in the following.

4.1
Core Framework

We now present
ConvNN
, an operation that generalizes both convolution and attention, is built upon three core steps:
similarity computation
,
neighbor selection and modulation
, and
weighted aggregation
.
Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
denote the input feature matrix.
ConvNN
operates as follows:

1.

Similarity computation
:

Let
f_{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times h}
,
f_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times h}
, and be learnable linear projections. Let
Q=f_{Q}(X)
and
K=f_{K}(X)
. After applying
\ell_{2}
normalization to the rows of
Q
and
K
, feature similarities are computed in
\mathbb{R}^{h}
rather than
\mathbb{R}^{c}
, and the neighbors of each feature
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are extracted from
V
using these indices of these projected representations.

ConvNN
employs a cosine similarity between these normalized features, which means that:

S=QK^{\top}.

(3)

2.

Neighbor selection and modulation
:

Let
f_{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times v}
be a another learnable linear projection, and define
V=f_{V}(X)
. For each feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in X
, let
\mathcal{N}^{\text{proj}}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
denote the set of
k
rows of
V
corresponding to the
k
rows of
K
most similar to
Q[i,:]
. We refer to the elements of
\mathcal{N}^{\text{proj}}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
as the
projected neighbors
of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. The similarity values and indices of these neighbors are respectively denoted as:

\mathbf{s}_{i}=\text{$k$-max}_{k}(S)[i,:],

(4)

I_{i}=\text{$k$-argmax}_{k}(S)[i,:].

(5)

We introduce a placeholder function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
that defines the weighting scheme, instantiated either as a constant vector (
\rho(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{1}_{k}
) or a softmax normalization (
\rho(\mathbf{z})=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})
).
Let
S_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(\rho(\mathbf{s}_{i}))
denote the resulting diagonal weighting matrix.

ConvNN
then constructs, for each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, a
neighborhood matrix

X_{\text{nn},i}
defined as:

X_{\text{nn},i}=S_{i}\,V[I_{i},:]\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times c},

which represents the
k
projected neighbors of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, each scaled by the corresponding weight in
\rho(\mathbf{s}_{i})
and ordered by similarity.

4.

Weighted aggregation
:

Let
X_{\text{nn}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(kn)\times c}
denote the concatenation of all neighborhood matrices
X_{\text{nn},i}
along the spatial dimension.

ConvNN
applies a
Conv1D

4.2
Extensions

To enhance the expressiveness of
ConvNN
, we introduce two extensions that enable it to interpolate between purely feature-based and spatially-aware neighbor selection.

4.2.1
Positional Encoding

To incorporate spatial structure when beneficial,
ConvNN
optionally concatenates positional coordinates to the input along the channel dimension.
For a 1D input
[c,l]
, a single channel increasing linearly with position is added, while for a 2D input
[c,w,h]
, two channels encoding horizontal and vertical coordinates
(i,j)
are appended.
The resulting tensors have shapes
[c+1,l]
and
[c+2,w,h]
, respectively.
This positional encoding enables
ConvNN
to blend feature-based and spatially aware similarity computations.

4.2.2
Sparse Neighbor Search

To reduce the computational burden of
k
-NN search from
O(n^{2}\log n)
to
O(nr\log r)
, we restrict the search to a set of
r\ll n
candidate neighbors. We select these candidates using one of two strategies:

•

Random Selection
(see Fig.
2(b)
): We randomly sample
r
features from
X
to form
X^{\text{sparse}}
, i.e.,
X^{\text{sparse}}=X[I_{r},:]
where
I_{r}
contains
r
indices drawn uniformly without replacement. This strategy preserves feature diversity while maintaining a reduced search space.

•

Spatial Selection
(see Fig.
2(c)
): For a 1D tensor
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times l}
and a subsampling rate
r
, we extract a reduced tensor

\mathbf{X}^{\text{sparse}}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times\frac{l}{r}}
such that

\mathbf{X}^{\text{sparse}}[:,i]=\mathbf{X}[:,ri]
, effectively selecting features at regular intervals along the sequence.
Analogously, for a 2D tensor
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times w\times h}
and
r^{\prime}=\sqrt{r}
, we extract a subtensor

\mathbf{X}^{\text{sparse}}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times\frac{w}{r^{\prime}}\times\frac{h}{r^{\prime}}}
such that

\mathbf{X}^{\text{sparse}}[:,i,j]=\mathbf{X}[:,r^{\prime}i,r^{\prime}j]
, thereby subsampling features at uniform spatial intervals.

4.2.3
Hybrid Branching Layer

A key distinction between
ConvNN
and standard convolution lies in their receptive behavior: while convolutions aggregate information locally from spatially adjacent features,
ConvNN
captures long-range dependencies, allowing global context to influence each representation.

Building upon prior work that integrates local feature extraction with global feature modeling
28
;
22
;
4
, we introduce a hybrid
branching layer
that combines convolutional and
ConvNN
components. We first set the
ConvNN
projectors as identity functions. Then this layer processes the input through two parallel branches—one convolutional and one
ConvNN
—and fuses their outputs via a
1\times 1
convolution. A parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
controls the proportion of output channels allocated to each branch, ensuring a balanced contribution between local and global processing. Both branches use the same kernel size (or neighbor count), keeping the parameter budget comparable while regulating their relative influence through
\lambda
.

5
Connection to Prior Literature

5.1
Traditional Convolution

Traditional convolution emerges as a special case of
ConvNN
where neighbors are selected by spatial proximity rather than feature similarity. This can be achieved by using positional encoding, choosing projections
f_{K}
and
f_{Q}
that filter all channels except spatial dimensions, setting
f_{V}
as the identity function and choosing
\rho=\mathbf{1}_{k}
(constant weighting). In this configuration, the
k
-nearest neighbors of a feature are necessarily the spatially adjacent features, reducing
ConvNN
to standard convolution with receptive field size given by
k
.

5.2
Attention Mechanism

Next, we show that the attention mechanism in Eq.
2
can be recovered as a special case of
ConvNN
by setting
k=n
,
\rho=\operatorname{softmax}
, and applying a depthwise convolution with unit weights and zero bias during the weighted aggregation phase. Consider first the multiplication
AV
in Eq.
2
.
The
i
-th row of this product can be viewed as the sum of the rows of
V
, each scaled by the corresponding attention weight in
A[i,:]=\operatorname{softmax}(Q[i,:]K^{\top})
.

Under the above settings,
ConvNN
reproduces this computation.
In Eq.
6
, the modulation step scales the same rows of
V
, now arranged as
V[I^{\text{proj}}_{i},:]
, by the values in
\rho(\mathbf{s}_{i})=\operatorname{softmax}(Q[i,:]K^{\top})
.
Applying a bias-free depthwise convolution with constant weights equal to one effectively performs the same summation across the scaled rows of
V
.
When applied to all
i
, this process yields exactly
AV
.
Furthermore, relaxing the condition
k=n
naturally recovers the localized attention formulation proposed in KVT
30
.

7
Discussion

Our experiments reveal both the promise and limitations of unifying convolution and attention through
k
-NN. The
ConvNN
framework successfully demonstrates that these operations exist along a spectrum of neighbor selection strategies, with convolution representing fixed spatial selection and attention representing global, feature-based selection.

A key finding is the strong performance of random selection in
ConvNN
compared to considering all samples. This highlights that when employing a global receptive field, selecting the closest neighbors based on learned feature is more effective than arbitrary spatial or attention-based patterns. Notably, in the
ConvNN
-Attention variant, which fully utilizes its linear projectors and softmax normalization, random selection outperforms all other attention baselines, suggesting that attending to all positions may be unnecessary for learning global dependencies. This insight challenges the assumption underlying standard attention that all positions should influence the output.

Ablation studies on
k
values reveal an important distinction between convolution and attention. While larger convolutional kernels consistently improve performance (e.g., kernel size
1\rightarrow 2\text{ or }3
), the gains from increasing
k
in
ConvNN
are modest. Interestingly, in ViT-Tiny experiments,
ConvNN
using all features underperforms KVT, whereas the random selection variant outperforms all attention baselines. This indicates that
ConvNN
’s advantage arises from flexible neighbor selection rather than the number of neighbors considered.

We also observe significant regularization effects, particularly in convolutional architectures. Adding global receptive fields through
ConvNN
acts as an implicit regularizer, forcing the network to balance spatial and feature-based aggregation. This prevents over-reliance on either mechanism alone, leading to more robust feature learning.

Our work also highlights several limitations. The hard selection mechanism in
ConvNN
limits gradient flow and may cause training instability in certain settings. Additionally, the computational cost of
k
-NN search remains higher than standard convolution, though lower than full self-attention. Extension to tasks requiring dense predictions (e.g., segmentation) will require careful consideration of how to maintain spatial correspondence during neighbor selection.

8
Conclusion

We introduced
Convolutional Nearest Neighbors
(
ConvNN
), a unified framework bridging convolution and attention through
k
-nearest neighbor selection.
By showing that both operations can be viewed as forms of neighborhood aggregation—based respectively on spatial proximity and feature similarity—
ConvNN
offers new theoretical insight into the connection between CNNs and attention-based architectures.
Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 demonstrate its flexibility and strong empirical performance, achieving competitive accuracy and runtime relative to both convolutional and attention baselines.
Future work will leverage the
ConvNN
framework to systematically analyze how different architectural components of CNNs and Transformers contribute to their representational strengths, as well as to design intermediate models that extend our branching strategy to more general convolution–attention hybrids.
