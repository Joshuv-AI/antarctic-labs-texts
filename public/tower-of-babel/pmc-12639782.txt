Mental health first aid training to improve depression literacy among health ambassadors in Iran: study protocol for a cluster-randomized controlled trial
Authors: Fatemeh Hallajian, Ensiyeh Jamshidi, Azadeh Sayarifard, Laleh Ghadirian, Ayat Ahmadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveOne of the strategies for enhancing the mental health literacy of the community could be capacity building in the field of mental health first aid (MHFA) training among health ambassadors, who serve as volunteers between health service providers and families. The present study aims to investigate the effectiveness of MHFA training packages for depression prevention using critical thinking methods on the depression literacy (as a key component of mental health literacy) of health ambassadors to assist in the early identification and timely referral of individuals with depressive disorders.Depressive disorders are among the most prevalent mental health conditions, yet many individuals delay or avoid seeking treatment due to stigma and limited awareness. Improving mental health literacy is therefore essential to promote early recognition and timely help-seeking. Health ambassadors, as community volunteers linking families to health services, are uniquely positioned to support this goal. This study applies a culturally adapted MHFA training package for depression, delivered through critical thinking and role playing, in improving the depression literacy (as a key component of mental health literacy) of health ambassadors. The trial seeks to determine whether this approach enhances their ability to recognize symptoms, provide initial support, and facilitate referral to professional care.MethodologyThis is a study protocol for a cluster-randomized controlled trial conducted in 10 comprehensive health service centers (CHSCs) in Islamshahr, Iran. A computer-generated random sequence will assign five centers to the intervention group and five to the control group. The allocation sequence will be prepared and concealed by an independent epidemiologist not involved in recruitment or intervention delivery. From each center, 25 eligible health ambassadors (women aged 25–45 years with ≥ 2 years of experience) will be randomly selected, yielding a total sample of 250 participants.The intervention group will receive an eight-session, culturally adapted MHFA training package delivered using problem-based learning and role-playing methods. Depression literacy (as a key component of mental health literacy) will be assessed at baseline and 2 months post-intervention using the questionnaire developed by Reavley et al. (2014). For statistical inference, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) will be employed to examine group differences, and multivariate linear regression will be applied to adjust for potential confounders, using SPSS software version 24. A p-value of < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.DiscussionThis protocol describes a trial designed to evaluate whether mental health first aid (MHFA) training with a critical thinking approach can improve depression literacy (as a key component of mental health literacy) among health ambassadors. Findings from this study may provide evidence to guide future mental health promotion strategies, particularly for the early identification and referral of individuals with depression and the prevention of suicide.Trial registrationIran Randomized Clinical Trial Center IRCT20231225060521N1. Registered on 21 January 2024 (current status: ongoing).

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Depression is considered as a public health concern and one of the most common mental disorders worldwide [1]. It is estimated that 3.8% of the global population is experiencing depression [2, 3]. In Iran, depression accounts for approximately 35 to 45% of mental health issues, with approximately 8 to 20% of the Iranian population facing this problem. Unfortunately, this rate is increasing and concerning [4]. This disorder is considered a significant problem not only due to its impact on quality of life and disability but also because of its substantial economic consequences, including increased utilization of healthcare services, treatment costs, and reduced workforce productivity [5, 6]. Estimates indicate that major depressive disorder (MDD) accounted for approximately 37.2 million years lived with disability (YLDs) globally in 2019, representing the largest share of disability among mental disorders (~29.7%) in that year [7].

In addition, major depressive disorder is associated with a high suicide rate—approximately 15%—making it one of the leading causes of preventable mortality among individuals with psychiatric conditions [8].

This disorder is a multifactorial disorder arising from interactions between genetic vulnerability, biological changes, and psychosocial stressors such as poverty, chronic illness, and exposure to trauma [9].

Given the significance of the issue, the gap between service delivery and actual utilization underscores the urgent need for community-level interventions to improve recognition and reduce barriers to help-seeking. Several studies have shown that despite the availability of effective and evidence-based treatments for mental disorders, more than 75% of individuals in low- and middle-income countries do not receive appropriate care. Contributing factors include limited investment in mental health, shortages of trained providers, low public awareness, and pervasive stigma, all of which prevent individuals from seeking timely and effective support [10, 11].

Fear and embarrassment from the stigma of mental illness deter patients from seeking treatment for their condition, and relatives may even advise them to discontinue treatment to avoid stigma. Furthermore, stigma can lead to personal and family issues, causing patients to ignore their illness to avoid the burden of stigma; they may avoid consulting psychiatrists, potentially leading to the chronicity of their condition over time. Additionally, they may request that psychiatrists not use insurance for psychiatric medications upon consultation. Patients with mental illness may also feel ashamed to seek financial support from their families for treatment due to the associated stigma [12, 13]. Despite the high number of mental disorders and individuals in need of counseling, very few patients seek consultation and treatment. This indicates that stigma may be more prevalent [13]. In addition, the knowledge and beliefs among individuals in society regarding psychiatric disorders are limited and often incorrect, leading to their underuse and delayed use of psychiatric services [14]. The lack of timely diagnosis and receipt of care and treatment services by the community affected by depressive disorder can have consequences [8]. In addition to damaging the psyche of individuals, this disorder also affects other aspects of health, including physical and social well-being [6].

Multiple interventions have proven effective in reducing depression, including pharmacotherapy, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and collaborative care models, many of which are cost-effective across diverse settings [15].

Low-intensity and digital approaches, such as internet-based CBT and guided self-help, also show moderate symptom reduction and favorable cost profiles [16].

In low- and middle-income countries, school- and community-based mental health literacy and education programs have improved knowledge and reduced stigma, offering scalable and feasible solutions [17].

Among these strategies, health education is especially promising as a low-cost approach to enhance mental health literacy, promote early recognition, and encourage help-seeking, particularly in low-resource settings [18].

When people lack mental health literacy—whether in the ability to recognize symptoms, in holding stigmatizing beliefs, or in awareness of available treatment options—they are less likely to seek timely help. Improving mental health literacy is therefore a critical strategy to promote early recognition, reduce stigma, and enhance help-seeking behavior at the community level. Greater literacy not only enables individuals to recognize and manage psychological symptoms in themselves but also equips them to support others experiencing difficulties. Consequently, the activities people undertake to assist themselves and those around them in managing symptoms are strongly influenced by their level of mental health literacy, which, if improved, may help reduce suffering, disability, and both the short- and long-term consequences of psychiatric disorders [19].

Despite the importance of mental health literacy, its insufficiency is a global problem, especially in developing countries [20, 21]. Numerous studies have assessed the relationship between low literacy and mental illness [22, 23], indicating that individuals with low health literacy are three times more likely to suffer from depression [24]. In Iran, depression literacy is reported to be low, and stigma is high among various population groups [25, 26].

One of the strategies for enhancing depression literacy is mental health first aid (MHFA) training, which is defined as “assisting an individual who is facing a mental health issue or is in a mental health crisis” [27]. First aid continues until the individual receives appropriate specialized treatment or until the crisis is resolved [19]. The concept of MHFA is derived from the well-known concept of first aid for physical health crises. However, first aid for psychiatric disorders is taught in only a limited number of countries [28].

MHFA training has been implemented in diverse target groups such as employees, students, refugees, and the general public [29–38]. Evidence consistently shows that such training enhances knowledge of mental disorders, increases confidence to provide support, and improves intentions to seek professional help. However, these interventions have not yet been extended to health volunteers who operate at the family and community level, despite their unique position to influence health behaviors. Moreover, most studies provide limited information about the specific training methods used, with only a few briefly mentioning role-play exercises without further detail. To date, no interventional studies on MHFA training have been conducted in Iran [24, 26, 39–42]. Given the high burden of psychiatric disorders and the barriers to accessing professional care, there is a clear need for community-based interventions targeting health ambassadors, and for evaluating their effectiveness in improving mental health literacy.

Early intervention in mental disorders is crucial. Therefore, family, friends, and other community members can be the first agents of early intervention before contacting health professionals. Many individuals with mental disorders delay seeking help, but when assistance is offered by someone with whom they have a close relationship, they are more likely to be inclined to accept help. However, many people in the community lack the skills to facilitate early intervention in the field of mental health [27, 43, 44].

One approach to educating the community could be to increase mental health literacy among health volunteers as liaisons between health centers and families. In Iran, the health ambassador program is a national primary healthcare strategy that mobilizes trained volunteers, mostly women, to act as liaisons between households and comprehensive health service centers (CHSCs). Ambassadors disseminate health information, encourage self-care, and facilitate referrals, representing a structured model of community participation in health promotion [45].

Their embedded role within families makes them well positioned to recognize early health problems and promote timely help-seeking, which has been demonstrated to reduce unnecessary doctor visits for minor ailments [46].

Leveraging this infrastructure for MHFA training therefore provides a culturally grounded opportunity to enhance depression literacy as a component of mental health literacy and improve early detection and referral in the community.

Since health ambassadors are selected from the family and have close relationships with family members, training them in MHFA could increase their awareness in the field of mental health. Consequently, if signs of mental disorders are present in the family or even within the ambassadors themselves, they could actively seek out these healthcare centers and initiate early interventions at the first opportunity.

The reviewed studies indicate that public knowledge about how to prevent psychiatric disorders, disease recognition, available treatments, self-help strategies, and skills in providing first aid to support individuals affected by psychiatric disorders is limited. A wide range of interventions can enhance mental health literacy in society, including educational interventions in mental health and MHFA [47].

In the context of health education, critical thinking enables learners to question assumptions, assess the reliability of health information, and adapt knowledge to real-life situations. This approach was selected for our intervention because depression literacy and MHFA skills require more than memorization of self-care strategies; they demand reflective judgment, problem-solving, and the capacity to respond flexibly in diverse scenarios. In fact, critical thinking refers to the ability to actively and systematically analyze, evaluate, and apply information in order to make informed judgments and decisions in various situations [48].

Previous studies in Iranian settings have shown that critical thinking skills training can significantly improve mental health outcomes, stress management, and decision-making [49].

Integrating this framework into MHFA training is therefore expected to enhance health ambassadors’ ability to learn how to help others to recognize depressive symptoms, respond empathetically, overcome stigma, and encourage appropriate help-seeking. By fostering active engagement through problem-based learning and role-playing, the critical thinking approach strengthens the translation of MHFA knowledge into effective supportive actions within families.

Considering the aforementioned points, the current study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the MHFA training package for depression with a critical thinking approach to improving the depression literacy (as a key component of mental health literacy) of health ambassadors in Islamshahr County located in southwest Tehran Province, the capital of Iran. Due to the nature of health literacy, which requires critical thinking and the active participation of individuals in accessing, understanding, evaluating, and applying mental health information to improve health, the educational methods employed in this study are based on problem-solving and role-playing. This protocol describes a trial designed to evaluate whether MHFA training delivered with a critical thinking approach can improve depression literacy among health ambassadors. Findings from the trial study may provide evidence for developing programs to increase depression literacy among health ambassadors. While the immediate outcomes of this trial are limited to literacy-related variables, such improvements may in the longer term contribute to better early identification, appropriate response, timely referral of individuals with depressive disorders, and ultimately support suicide prevention efforts at the community level.

Trial design {8}

This is a study protocol for a cluster-randomized controlled trial. This protocol has been developed in accordance with the SPIRIT 2013 (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) statement and checklist. Sampling will be performed by cluster randomization. In cluster sampling, the units of randomization are groups or clusters, not individual participants. Initially, a list of all CHSCs, which includes 14 urban and five rural CHSCs (the centers are considered as clusters), will be prepared. CHSCs are those that provide primary health and medical services and play a significant role in people’s access to these services and in maintaining and improving the community’s health level. Enhancing health levels through these centers can lead to increased life expectancy and reduced costs due to mortality caused by the absence or shortage of primary health services. After coordination with the Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) and the Islamshahr health network, based on greater interest and willingness to cooperate in the implementation of the project, 10 centers will be selected, and 5 centers will be randomly chosen as the intervention group and 5 as the control group.

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This study will be conducted in the urban and rural CHSCs of Islamshahr County. This county, located in the southwest of Tehran Province, the capital of Iran, is part of the greater Tehran metropolitan area and is characterized by features such as being a recipient of migrants and having social and ethnic differences within the community. These conditions have led to various issues and problems across different dimensions. Among the most significant problems of this county are the lack of access to facilities and services, its inappropriate distribution, and its unsatisfactory economic situation [40]. Economic status is recognized as a fundamental factor affecting mental health and depression [41]. In this county, there are 14 urban CHSCs and five rural CHSCs.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Initially, a list of all CHSCs, comprising 14 urban centers and five rural centers (considered as clusters), will be prepared. All comprehensive health service centers (CHSCs) in Islamshahr were approached, and 10 centers that agreed to participate were included to ensure feasibility and compliance with intervention delivery. To reduce bias, randomization of CHSCs to intervention and control arms will be performed only after center recruitment. Using a random number table, five centers will be randomly assigned to the intervention group and five to the control group. From the list of health ambassadors at the intervention group centers, 25 individuals who meet the study’s entry criteria will be randomly selected, totalling 250 individuals (125 in each group) across 10 clusters invited to participate in the study. Participants in the control arm will not receive the MHFA training package. Instead, they will continue with routine training and educational activities delivered as part of the National Self-Care Program for health ambassadors. These sessions, provided periodically by health network staff, cover general knowledge and advice about healthy lifestyles and self-care in mental well-being. However, this routine training does not include structured MHFA content or specific training on depression using critical thinking strategies.

To benefit the participants of the control group centers from the educational intervention, the educational package will be made available to these health centers after the study concludes.

Intervention description {11a}

In the intervention group, training will be provided based on the MHFA package for depression, which was developed by Jorm and Reavley from the University of Melbourne, Australia (2008), and was culturally adapted by Ghadirian and Siyarifard in 2021 for a group of individuals with a history of depression and their families.

The educational sessions will be conducted over eight times (each lasting one and a half hours), with a focus on critical thinking and the application of problem-based learning methods and role-playing in 2 months by the researchers. Between the educational sessions, participants will be given 1 week to study and practice with one another (Table 1).

Description of training sessions

Two members of the research team (EJ, FH) have completed courses in educational technology and teaching methods and will also participate in a MHFA facilitator training course organized by the Health Education and Promotion Department of TUMS before the start of the intervention (April–May 2024). This facilitator-level training is intended to strengthen the researchers’ competence, ensure fidelity, and standardize the delivery of the intervention. It is important to note that the health ambassadors will not directly participate in this facilitator-level course, as its content is designed for trainers and differs from the culturally adapted educational package for ambassadors. As mentioned earlier, the ambassadors’ training package has been culturally adapted and simplified for this group by Ghadirian and Sayarifard (2021), focusing on practical application, critical thinking, and role-playing exercises in family and community contexts. This distinction ensures that ambassadors receive content tailored to their needs, while facilitators are fully equipped to deliver it consistently.

The training will be based on a combination of problem-based learning methods and role-playing. In each session, a problem is presented in the form of a scenario with its surrounding conditions outlined. The learning objectives are defined. Learners are asked to reflect on the scenario and the actions that should be taken; they will be asked to gather necessary information about the problem and solutions. Then, learners perform the proposed actions through role-playing, and the execution by other learners will be considered, discussed, and evaluated. Ultimately, problem-solving strategies are suggested through brainstorming and mind mapping. The facilitators compile and present appropriate confrontations based on feedback received from learners and the MHFA educational package for depression. Intervention fidelity will be monitored as described in the oversight and monitoring section to ensure consistency of delivery.

It is important to distinguish MHFA training intervention from the routine training courses that health ambassadors receive. Routine courses primarily provide general knowledge and advice about healthy lifestyles and personal mental well-being. In contrast, our program focuses on the structured principles of MHFA, which emphasize practical skills for recognizing depressive symptoms, reducing stigma, offering immediate support, and encouraging timely referral to professional care. Moreover, this training incorporates critical thinking, problem-based learning, and role-playing, which foster active engagement, scenario-based practice, and application of knowledge to real-life situations. The MHFA package used has also been culturally adapted for the Iranian context, ensuring greater relevance compared to generic materials. These distinctions are expected to provide participants with deeper, more practical, and community-focused competencies than routine training courses.

To benefit the participants in the control group, the educational package will be made available to the control CHSCs after the study’s completion.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

The absence of more than two educational sessions, discontinuation of collaboration as a health ambassador at CHSCs, death or migration of the individual from the area, and a diagnosed history of depression or suicide in the individual will be considered criteria for exclusion from the study.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Participants will be reminded to complete the questionnaire at appropriate times via a short message service (SMS) and a group formed on the WhatsApp social network. In the intervention group, the date and time of the educational classes will also be communicated through SMS and the WhatsApp group. During the educational sessions, participants will be encouraged to engage in discussions; they will also be invited to express their opinions and suggestions and to contact the researcher/implementer in case of any issues.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Participants in the research were registered as health ambassadors in the SIB system of the Ministry of Health. This system is utilized in CHSCs across Iran. Healthcare providers serving at these centers are required to record information related to health records and services provided to the public in Iran’s integrated health system. These individuals receive routine care, which is not an obstacle; however, if, during the educational sessions, any of the participants are diagnosed with a need for depression treatment, it will be considered when analyzing the results.

Provisions for post‑trial care {30}

At the end of the study, to benefit the participants of the control group centers from the educational materials, the educational package will be made available to the related health centers.

Outcomes {12}

All depression-literacy outcomes (primary and secondary) will be measured using the depression-related component of the mental health literacy questionnaire developed by Reavley et al. Sample size {14}

The study population includes health ambassadors from the Comprehensive Health Service Centers (CHSCs) of the Islamshahr district health network. To compare the mean scores of depression literacy, the sample size was calculated with a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 90%, and an assumed intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.2. Based on prior MHFA trials, which reported small-to-moderate improvements in knowledge, attitudes, and literacy outcomes [28, 29, 34, 36, 52], we assumed a minimum detectable effect size of 0.25 (Cohen’s d) with a standard deviation of 0.2. This yielded a required sample of 200 participants across 10 clusters (20 per cluster). Allowing for a 20% attrition rate, the final sample size was increased to 250 participants (125 per group), equivalent to 25 participants per cluster.

Recruitment {15}

In this study, we will enlist the assistance of healthcare providers from CHSCs to collaborate in recruiting health ambassadors. We will explain the objectives and procedures of the study to the healthcare providers and ask them to participate in the random selection process of the participants. Follow-ups will be conducted through the healthcare providers until the expected sample size is reached. Subsequently, the potential benefits and risks of participating in the study, the assurance of confidentiality, and the study process will be explained to the healthcare providers to inform the health ambassadors. If individuals are interested and eligible to participate in the study, they will be asked to complete a consent form. Before the intervention commences, the CHSCs will be randomly allocated to either the intervention or control group (Fig. 1).

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Initially, a list of all CHSCs will be prepared, which includes 14 urban and 5 rural centers (the centers are considered clusters). After coordination with TUMS and the Islamshahr health network, based on interest and a greater inclination to collaborate in the implementation of the plan, 10 centers will be selected. Using a random number table, five centers will be randomly allocated to the intervention group and five to the control group. The CHSCs are located in various urban and rural geographical areas of Islamshahr. Each center serves its population, and health services are not provided to the population covered by other centers. As far as there is an average distance of 5 km between centers, there is no exchange of information between them. Health ambassadors at the intervention group centers are also randomly invited to participate in the study from among those who meet the entry criteria, using a random number table across 10 clusters.

Matching was not performed in this study. After identifying the 10 CHSCs that agreed to participate, a random number table was used to allocate five CHSCs to the intervention group and five to the control group. We chose not to use matching because the relatively small number of clusters could reduce the benefits of randomization. Instead, to ensure comparability, baseline demographic and socio-economic variables will be collected, and any significant differences between groups will be statistically adjusted for in the outcome analysis.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Each randomly generated sequence is assigned a unique numerical code. Upon the entry of any center into the study, the group to which individuals will be allocated will be declared by contacting the study epidemiologist. The name of the center, along with the generated code for them and the type of intervention received, will be recorded and maintained by the study epidemiologist who possesses the randomization sequence.

Implementation {16c}

Participants will be invited to participate in the study by the healthcare providers of the CHSCs.

Assignment of interventions: blinding {17}

Blinding was not implemented due to the nature of the intervention. The educational sessions are interactive and involve role-playing, group discussions, and scenario-based exercises, making it practically impossible to blind participants or facilitators to group allocation. To minimize bias, cluster randomization will be used to prevent contamination across groups. Also, clusters will be assigned simultaneously; there will be no risk that knowledge of allocation influences participant behavior or study outcomes.

Data collection will be conducted using standardized self-administered questionnaires independent of facilitators. The intervention content is based on a culturally adapted MHFA package, ensuring consistency of delivery across groups. To minimize bias, allocation concealment will be applied during randomization, and data analysis will be performed with blinded group codes. In fact, methodological rigor will be maintained through randomization, validated instruments, and statistical adjustment for potential confounders.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes{20a}

For data analysis, SPSS software version 24 and appropriate statistical tests will be utilized. For statistical analysis, after data collection and ensuring the accuracy of data entry, descriptive information of participants will be reported using the central index mean and the dispersion index standard deviation or in the form of numbers and percentages. Following the examination of necessary assumptions for analyzing relationships between variables, covariance analysis tests will be employed. To control for confounding variables, multivariate linear regression analysis will be used. The significance level for analyses will be considered to be P < 0.05.

In addition to general demographic variables, we will assess socio-economic indicators (education, occupation, household economic status) and cultural characteristics (e.g., migration background, language spoken at home) at baseline. If these factors differ significantly between groups, they will be included as covariates in multivariate regression models to control for potential confounding. Randomization at the cluster (CHSC) level is expected to distribute such contextual factors across groups, and the relatively uniform structure of CHSC services in Islamshahr will further mitigate the influence of economic and cultural disparities.

Discussion

Depression remains a major public health challenge globally, with high prevalence, persistent stigma, and delays in help-seeking contributing to its burden. Improving community-level mental health literacy is therefore an essential strategy to promote early recognition and timely referral for care. Health ambassadors, as community-based volunteers, are in a unique position to influence awareness and health behaviors within families and neighborhoods. This protocol describes a trial designed to evaluate whether MHFA training delivered with a critical thinking approach improves depression literacy among health ambassadors. Findings from this study may provide evidence that contributes to shaping future strategies aimed at enhancing mental health literacy and reducing stigma in community settings.

Early intervention in mental disorders is crucial. Therefore, family, friends, and other community members can be the first agents of early intervention before contacting health professionals. Many individuals with mental disorders delay seeking help, but when assistance is offered by someone with whom they have a close relationship, they are more likely to be inclined to accept help. However, many community members lack the skills to facilitate early intervention in mental health [27, 43, 44]. The research team reviewed several studies, including those by Reavley et al. [29], Svensson and Hanson [30], Jensen et al. [31], Burns et al. [32], Kitchener and Jorm [54], McKiernan et al. [33], Wang et al. [34], Hong et al. [35], Morgan et al. [36], Subedi et al. [37] and Marsico et al. [38]. These studies have implemented MHFA training for target groups such as employees, students, refugees, and the general public, but these interventions do not cover health ambassadors at the family level. The method of instruction was less mentioned in these studies, and only a few studies referred to role-playing without providing details. This educational approach has been effective in improving the recognition of depression symptoms and beliefs about depression treatment.

Studies have also been conducted in Iran, including Taheri Miregaaed et al. [40], Tehrani et al. [24], Ghadirian and Siyarifard [26], Siyarifard et al. [41], Siyarifard and Ghadirian [39], Ghadirian and Siyarifard [42], Faghihi et al. [55], and Shakiba et al. [46]. Studies in Iran examining the knowledge and attitudes of the community regarding MHFA indicate a low level of these indicators. Unfortunately, our search did not identify any interventional studies on MHFA training in Iran, and health ambassadors have not received training on that for early identification of these disorders at the family level. When faced with crises, they are unable to assist the affected individual; therefore, given the high burden of psychiatric disorders in Iran, there is a perceived need for training and studying its effectiveness.

Capacity building in mental health literacy and training among health ambassadors, who act as a link between health centers and families, assists in actively seeking related centers and initiating early interventions at the first opportunity when signs of mental disorders are present in the family or even in themselves. The choice of teaching method is a crucial step in the transfer of an educational package. Training in the MHFA for Depression can be based on educational frameworks that help individuals think of appropriate problem-solving methods and show the right response when faced with issues of depression or the risk of suicide. They learn practical skills to establish proper communication without judgment, listen, prevent stigmatization of the patient, and suggest suitable strategies depending on the situation. Educational methods that can enhance individuals’ critical thinking skills are needed. To achieve these objectives, problem-based and role-playing methods can be beneficial. This study protocol was developed in response to the stated necessities, aiming to determine the effectiveness of MHFA training for depression with a critical thinking approach to the depression literacy of health ambassadors in Eslamshahr County.

The Iranian context presents both opportunities and challenges for implementing MHFA training with a critical thinking approach. On the one hand, Iran faces a high burden of depressive disorders, with low depression literacy and high stigma reported across different groups [26].

These factors underscore the urgency of community-based strategies to promote early identification and timely referral. The country’s health ambassador program, which recruits volunteers as liaisons between health centers and families, provides a unique and well-established infrastructure that makes MHFA training highly suitable. Since health ambassadors are already embedded in communities, they can use MHFA skills to recognize early symptoms, provide nonjudgmental support, and encourage professional help-seeking, which is especially valuable in contexts where individuals may be reluctant to approach mental health services directly.

On the other hand, cultural stigma surrounding mental illness remains a significant barrier to open discussion and help-seeking [12].

Limited availability of specialized mental health services in some areas, particularly outside major urban centers, could reduce the impact of timely referrals [56].

To mitigate this challenge, our study will embed MHFA training within the existing primary health care and community health structures, enabling health ambassadors to act as the first point of recognition and support, thereby decreasing reliance on specialists for initial detection. In addition, by using a critical thinking approach and participatory teaching methods such as role-playing and problem-solving, this program is designed to address these challenges by equipping ambassadors with practical skills to respond to local realities, overcome stigma, and promote culturally sensitive mental health literacy.

Strengths and limitations

This trial has several notable strengths. It is the first study in Iran to evaluate a culturally adapted MHFA training package for depression that incorporates a critical thinking approach using problem-based learning and role-play. Targeting health ambassadors, who serve as trusted community volunteers, enhances the real-world relevance and potential scalability of the intervention within the primary healthcare system. Methodologically, the study employs a cluster-randomized controlled design to reduce contamination between participants and uses random sampling of ambassadors within centers. Intervention fidelity will be supported through standardized training manuals, facilitator preparation, and monitoring checklists. Furthermore, outcomes will be measured with a questionnaire that has demonstrated strong psychometric properties and has been validated for use in the Iranian context.

At the same time, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, although a cluster-randomized design was chosen to minimize contamination, there remains some risk that health ambassadors from different centers may share information, which could dilute differences between groups. Second, all outcomes are based on self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. To mitigate this, we will use a validated instrument with established psychometric properties in the Iranian context. Third, selection bias may have been introduced by recruiting only centers that expressed willingness to participate; while necessary to ensure feasibility and adherence, this may limit generalizability. Despite these limitations, the trial’s use of cultural adaptation, standardized training, and rigorous randomization procedures is expected to enhance the reliability and relevance of findings.
