Tangency counting for well-spaced circles
Dominique Maldague, Alexander Ortiz

Abstract
. In the late 90’s, Tom Wolff introduced the circle tangency counting problem in his expository article on the Kakeya conjecture. For collections of well-spaced circles, we break the N 3 / 2 N^{3/2} -barrier, proving that a set of N N well-spaced circles has at most N 25 / 18 + ε N^{25/18+\varepsilon} sites of internal tangency. The circle tangency problem can be related to a problem about incidences between points in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} and light rays. For this problem, we introduce a stopping time argument to extract maximal information about well-spaced points from a refined decoupling theorem for the light cone in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , leading to sharp bounds on the number of μ \mu -rich tangency rectangles.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Circle tangencies and unit distances

In 1999, Wolff introduced the following discrete tangency counting problem for sets of circles in the plane.

Problem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of
N
circles in the plane, no three of which are internally tangent at a point. Estimate the cardinality of the set

\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C})=\{(C,C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}^{2}:\text{$C,C^{\prime}$ are internally tangent}\}.

Figure 1.
A clamshell of
5
circles.

The condition that no three circles are tangent at a point is a non-degeneracy condition. Without it, there are examples of collections of circles that have many tangency pairs. See Figure
1
for an image of a
clamshell
configuration, where every pair of distinct circles are internally tangent.

The tangency counting problem can be phrased as a problem about a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
light rays
, lines making an angle of
45^{\circ}
with the
xy
-plane. To do this, we associate to the circle
C_{z,r}
in the plane with center
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and radius
r>0
the point
(z,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. The condition that
C_{z,r},C_{z^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
are internally tangent becomes the quadratic constraint:

|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}=|r-r^{\prime}|^{2}.

Equivalently, the points
(z,r),(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})
both must lie on a common light ray. In this formulation, Problem
1
is a cousin of the famous unit-distance problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, first posed by Erdős.

The unit distance problem:
In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, Erdős’ unit-distance problem asks: given a discrete set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
|X|=N
, what is the maximum number of pairs
(x,x^{\prime})\in X^{2}
whose distance is exactly
1
?

The unit-distance requirement can be expressed as a quadratic equation

|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}=1,

and the goal is an estimate for the cardinality of the set

U(X)=\{(x,x^{\prime})\in X^{2}:|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}=1\}.

Problem
1
and the unit-distance problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
have historically been attacked with similar algebraic–combinatorial techniques. Indeed, Wolff
[
21
]
pointed out that the cellular partitioning technique of Clarkson, Edelsbrunner, et al.
[
4
]
, which achieves
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon})
unit-distances in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, can be adapted in a straightforward way to the formulation of Problem
1
in terms of points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and light rays to prove that
|\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C})|=O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon})
for collections
\mathcal{C}
, no three of which are tangent at a point. Both bounds are believed to be too big, but have been hard to improve. More recently, Ellenberg, Solymosi, and Zahl used a different approach in the tangency counting problem, removing the
\varepsilon
, showing
|\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C})|=O(N^{3/2})

[
7
]
.

In
[
23
]
, Zahl used a more refined approach to the unit-distance problem, combining ideas from
[
7
]
with the method of polynomial partitioning to get a small improvement in the exponent of the unit-distance problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, showing
|U(X)|=O_{\varepsilon}(N^{295/197+\varepsilon})
. For the circle tangency problem, Problem
1
,
N^{3/2}
remains the bound to beat to this day. In this paper, we break the
3/2
-barrier for Problem
1
for collections of circles that are
well-spaced
. We introduce the setup for well-spaced circles now.

Definition 1.1
.

If
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a unit cube, then a subset
X\subset Q
is
well-spaced
if
X
is
\rho
-separated for some
\rho\in(0,1)
, and moreover,
X
is maximal with respect to this property. In particular,
c_{n}\rho^{-n}\leq|X|\leq C_{n}\rho^{-n}
for dimensional constants
0<c_{n}<C_{n}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
A_{\varepsilon}
so that the following holds.
Let
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
be well-spaced, and let
\mathcal{C}_{X}
denote the collection of circles

\{C_{z,r}:(z,r)\in X\},

where
C_{z,r}
denotes the circle with center
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and radius
r>0
.

If no three circles of
\mathcal{C}_{X}
are tangent at a point, then the following estimate holds:

|\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\leq A_{\varepsilon}|X|^{25/18+\varepsilon}.

Theorem
1.1
will be deduced from Theorem
1.2
, a sharp result for a continuum version of the problem we describe momentarily.

In addition to upper bounds for circle tangencies or unit-distances, one could ask about lower bounds. In this regard, well-spaced sets are important because they give the current best known lower bounds for both problems. In
[
8
]
, Erdős showed that amongst the points in a
N^{1/3}\times N^{1/3}\times N^{1/3}
integer lattice, the most popular distance (and hence by a rescaling, the unit distance) appears at least
cN^{4/3}
times. In
[
20
]
, Schlag showed that with
X
equal to a
N^{1/3}\times N^{1/3}\times N^{1/3}
lattice inside
[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{X}
of circles has at least
cN^{4/3}
-many pairs that are internally tangent. Both these examples are well-spaced in the sense of Definition
1.1
.

X
(points above) and a selected subset of circles
\mathcal{C}_{X}
(below), with black dots marking the sites of internal tangencies between circles.

See Figure
2
for an illustration of the grid
X=(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z})^{3}\cap[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
and a selection of some of the corresponding circles in the plane.

1.2.
Discrete versus continuum problems

Wolff introduced Problem
1
as a discrete analog of the following Kakeya-type problem for circles. Define a BRK set (after Besicovitch–Rado
[
1
]
and Kinney
[
16
]
who initiated the study of these sets) to be a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
containing circles of every radius in the interval
[1,2]
. Every BRK set is a
1
-parameter family of
1
-dimensional objects (circles in this case), so whether such a set has dimension
2
can be seen as a borderline question.

By taking the
\delta
-neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(E)
of a BRK set
E
, determining the dimension of
E
reduces to the geometry problem of understanding how thin neighborhoods of circles overlap. If two
\delta
-annuli overlap “transversely,” then their region of overlap is contained in the union of
O(1)
-many
\delta\times\delta
-squares. If two
\delta
-annuli overlap “tangentially,” then that means both annuli contain a common rectangle of dimensions approximately
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
. Intuitively, for a set of circles to have small dimension, many pairs of circles should overlap tangentially, minimizing how much the set “spreads out.”

Wolff gave two proofs that the dimension of a BRK set is
2
. To describe one of his approaches, we introduce a variant of Problem
1
that focuses on the points of tangency for a collection
\mathcal{C}
, rather than the number of pairs of tangent circles.

Problem 2

(Counting tangency sites)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of
N
circles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, determine the number of sites of internal circle tangency, i.e., the cardinality of the set

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\text{at least two circles of $\mathcal{C}$ are internally tangent at $z$}\}.

See Figure
3
for an illustration of a collection of
6
circles with
6
sites of internal tangency.

Figure 3.
A set of
6
circles with
|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})|=6
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a family of circles, no
3
of which are tangent at a point, then actually
|\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C})|=|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})|
, so without the assumption that no three circles are tangent at a point, Problem
1
can be seen as a version of Problem
2
with multiplicity. The current best bound for Problem
2
is
|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})|=O(N^{3/2})
by the work of Ellenberg–Solymosi–Zahl, and the conjecture is that
|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})|=O(N^{4/3})
on the basis of the lattice example.

In his paper “Local smoothing type estimates on
L^{p}
for large
p
”
[
22
]
, Wolff addressed a continuum analog of Problem
2
using the cellular partitioning technique of Clarkson, Edelsbrunner, et al.

Problem 3
.

Let
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
be such that for every
x\neq x^{\prime}\in X
, we have

d(x,x^{\prime})\geq\delta,

for a small parameter
\delta>0
(so
X
is a
\delta
-separated set), and consider the collection
\mathcal{C}_{X}
of circles defined by

\mathcal{C}_{X}=\{C_{x}:x\in X\}.

Say a
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
-rectangle
\Omega
in the plane is a
\mu
-rich tangency rectangle for
\mathcal{C}_{X}
if

\Omega\subset\mathcal{N}_{10\delta}(C_{x})

holds for at least
\mu
points
x\in X
. (The notation
\mathcal{N}_{r}(E)
is used for the open
r
-neighborhood of a set
E
.)

Say a collection
\mathcal{R}
of
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
-rectangles in the plane is
pairwise incomparable
if no two rectangles of the collection are contained in the
10
-fold dilation of the other about their centers.

Problem:
Estimate the maximal cardinality of a collection
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
of pairwise incomparable
\mu
-rich tangency rectangles for
\mathcal{C}_{X}
.

We digest the statement of Problem
3
a bit. Without the assumption that
X
is
\delta
-separated, every circle of
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is allowed to be an infinitesimal perturbation of a single fixed circle, and then there are approximately
\delta^{-1/2}
-many
\delta\times\delta^{1/2}
-rectangles contained in the
10\delta
-neighborhood of the fixed circle, regardless of the cardinality of
X
.

Heuristically, Wolff proved
1

1

Actually, Wolff proved a bound for the following bipartite variant of Problem
3
. Given
X,Y\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
with
d(X,Y)\geq 0.1
, each of which is
\delta
-separated, and a pairwise incomparable collection
\mathcal{R}_{\geq\mu,\geq\nu}
of

-rectangles

that if

is a pairwise incomparable collection of

-rich

which we should think of as the analog of the

-bound for Problem

. As a consequence of his estimate for tangency rectangles, Wolff proved a sharp

estimate for the truncated light cone

Decoupling (also known as Bourgain–Demeter decoupling
[
2

) for the light cone, is a phenomenon in Fourier analysis which is a measurement of orthogonality between different frequency localized pieces of a solution
u
to the wave equation in

for

.

We prove a sharp bound for the number of
\mu
-rich tangency rectangles for collections of well-spaced circles, which apart from
\varepsilon
is the tangency rectangle analog of the conjectured
O(N^{4/3})
bound for Problem
2
for well-spaced circles.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
A_{\varepsilon}
so the following holds for every well-spaced
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
. For any
\mu\geq 1
, and
\tau\in[|X|^{-1/3},1)
, let
\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\tau}
be an arbitrary pairwise incomparable set of
\mu
-rich
\tau^{2}\times\tau
-rectangles for the collection
\mathcal{C}_{X}
. Then the following estimate holds:

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\tau}|\leq A_{\varepsilon}|X|^{4/3+\varepsilon}.

The estimate of Theorem
1.2
is sharp in the sense that for any
N>1
, there is a well-spaced set
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
of cardinality
N
with the following property. For each
\tau\in[N^{-1/3},1)
, there exists
\mu\geq 1
such that every
\tau^{2}\times\tau
-rectangle in a maximal pairwise incomparable collection
\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\tau}
is approximately
\mu
-rich, and for which we have the lower bound

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\tau}|=\Omega_{\varepsilon}(N^{4/3-\varepsilon}).

(See Theorem
4.1

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\tau}|\leq A_{\varepsilon}|X|^{4/3+\varepsilon}
of Theorem
1.2
to the range
\tau\in(0,|X|^{-1/3})
would yield corresponding improvements to the exponent in Theorem
1.1
for well-spaced circles. Such an extension requires new ideas, and we plan to investigate this range of
\tau
in future work.

1.3.
Decoupling and the proof of Theorem
1.2

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
is based entirely on Fourier analysis and the lifting procedure that replaces circles by points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We rely on an extension of this basic correspondence due to Wolff
[
22
]
. If
\Omega
is a
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
-rectangle, and
C_{z,r}
is a circle whose
10\delta
-neighborhood contains
\Omega
, then there is a
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}\times 1
-box
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which is tangent to a light cone, and intersects the
10\delta
-ball centered at
(z,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. We refer to such a box in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as a
lightplank
or a
cone plank
. We say a lightplank is
\mu
-rich for a collection
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
if its
C\delta
-neighborhood contains at least
\mu
points of
X
. For more details on the correspondence between tangency rectangles and lightplanks, we refer to the article
[
17
]
of the second author.

Lightplanks are significant because in the restriction theory of the light cone, a
wave packet
is essentially supported in a lightplank. The duality between tangency rectangles and lightplanks allows us to count
\mu
-rich tangency rectangles by counting the corresponding
\mu
-rich lightplanks, for which tools from restriction theory become available. If
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
is a pairwise incomparable collection of
\mu
-rich lightplanks for
X
, we can form a function
f=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}}\phi_{P}
, where each function
\phi_{P}
is a smooth approximation of the indicator function of a lightplank
P
, but with Fourier transform
\widehat{\phi}_{P}
supported in a dual lightplank of dimensions
1\times\delta^{-1/2}\times\delta^{-1}
centered at the origin.

The Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\xi)
is supported in a union of
1\times\delta^{-1/2}\times\delta^{-1}
-lightplanks containing the origin. We study
f
using the well-known technique of high–low frequency analysis and consider the various frequency components of
f
, roughly corresponding to the regions
\{\frac{\delta^{-1}}{2K}<|\xi_{3}|<\frac{\delta^{-1}}{K}\}
as
K
varies. See the papers
[
5
,
14
,
10
,
18
,
11
]
for some select examples of this approach to problems in continuum incidence geometry, projection theory, and geometric measure theory. For our purposes, we need a more refined approach than has been considered previously. In particular, our high–low analysis relies on a version of
refined decoupling
involving square functions of
f
(Theorem
3.1
).

Briefly, we consider the local behavior of the function
f
near each point
x\in X
, and we use a stopping time algorithm to sift out the dominant frequency component of
f
which is
active
near
x
. Once we have localized
f
in frequency near
x
, we apply our refined decoupling theorem for the light cone formulated in terms of square functions (Theorem
3.1
). The stopping time argument we use in the proof of Theorem
1.2
echoes the structure of square function estimates in the spirit of the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality
[
3
]
. This viewpoint does not seem to have been widely explored in the Fourier analytic context. We believe this strategy, especially the use of refined decoupling as it is formulated in Theorem
3.1
in terms of square functions, will be useful in approaching other continuum incidence geometry problems as well.

2.
Estimate of
\mu
-rich tangency rectangles

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.2
. As a corollary, we will obtain Theorem
1.1
, breaking the 3/2-barrier on Problem
2
for well-spaced circles.

We employ a lifting technique, originally introduced by Wolff, to convert
\mu
-rich tangency rectangles into
\mu
-rich lightplanks for the set of points
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
that encodes our collection of circles. We then apply a stopping time argument, in combination with a new refined decoupling theorem for the light cone (Theorem
3.1
), to establish the main estimate.

Throughout, we will identify points of
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with their corresponding circles in the plane with given center-radius pairs. For
x\in X
, written in coordinates as
(\bar{x},x_{3})\in[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
, let

C_{\delta,x}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:||z-\bar{x}|-x_{3}|<\delta\}

be the
\delta
-thick annulus with core circle
x
. If
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})<\delta
, then
C_{10\delta,x}\cap C_{10\delta,x^{\prime}}
contains a
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
-tangency rectangle in the plane.

We adopt the notation and terminology from the second author’s article
[
17
]
,
where Wolff’s lifting procedure—translating circle tangencies into equivalent statements
about points and lightplanks—is described in detail.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
rectangle, and set

Q:=[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2].

Define

\mathbf{D}_{10\delta}(\Omega)\;:=\;\{x\in Q:\Omega\subset C_{10\delta,x}\},

the set of points in
Q
whose associated annulus
C_{10\delta,x}
contains
\Omega
.

If

|X\cap\mathbf{D}_{10\delta}(\Omega)|\gtrsim\mu\quad\text{(or $\sim\mu$)},

we say that
\Omega
is a
\mu
-
rich tangency rectangle
(respectively,
\sim\mu
-
rich
). When
\Omega
is a
\delta\times\sqrt{\delta}
rectangle,

\mathbf{D}_{10\delta}(\Omega)
is a
\sim 1\times\sqrt{\delta}\times\delta
lightplank.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma\colon[-\pi,\pi)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the unit-speed curve defined by
\gamma(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,1)
. If for some
A,B>0
,
\theta_{P}\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

P=v+\{a\gamma(\theta_{P})+b\gamma^{\prime}(\theta_{P})+c(\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime})(\theta_{P}):|a|\leq\frac{A}{2},|b|\leq\frac{\sqrt{AB}}{2},|c|\leq\frac{B}{2}\},

then we say
P
is a
A\times\sqrt{AB}\times B
-lightplank (or cone plank)
with
center

v
.

By rescaling, letting
R:=1/\delta
, instead of considering
\delta
-neighborhoods of circles with center-radius pairs in
[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
, we will consider the unit-neighborhoods of circles with center-radius pairs in the cube
[0,R]^{2}\times[R,2R]
. This is done to match the notation more common to restriction theory, where we consider functions with frequency support in the unit ball, and physical scale features in a large ball of radius
R>1
.

We say two
1\times\sqrt{R}\times R
-lightplanks are
K
-incomparable
if neither is contained in the
K

K

We let
\Gamma=\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\xi_{3}=\sqrt{\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}},1\leq\xi_{3}\leq 2\}
be the usual segment of the light cone. Our proof of Theorem
1.2

A sketch of the high-frequency case:

Suppose
X

\sqrt{R}
-separated in
[0,R]^{2}\times[R,2R]

\mathcal{P}_{\mu}

\mu

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lessapprox|X|^{4/3}.

Define
f=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}}\phi_{P}
where each function
\phi_{P}
is a smooth approximation of the indicator function of a lightplank
P
, but with Fourier transform
\widehat{\phi}_{P}
supported in a dual lightplank of dimensions
1\times R^{-1/2}\times R^{-1}
centered at the origin.

The starting point of our estimate is the calculation of the incidences between the disjoint unit-balls of the
1
-neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
and the
\mu
-rich lightplanks in
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
:

(2)

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lesssim\int_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)}f.

We assume
f=f_{high}
, where

f_{high}(x)=\int_{|\xi_{3}|\geq 1}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi

is the

f

f
is Fourier-supported in the
R^{-1}
-neighborhood of the truncated light cone

\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}):\xi_{3}^{2}=\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2},1\leq|\xi_{3}|\leq 2\},

where decoupling estimates can be applied. This is the motivation for considering the high–low method in our problem. By dyadic pigeonholing, we can assume that every ball of
\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
is contained in exactly
M
lightplanks of
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
, for some
M\in[1,R^{1/2}]
. In this scenario, we can apply refined decoupling for the light cone, which gives an improved bound over Bourgain–Demeter’s decoupling inequality when the region in physical space we integrate over only receives contributions from a few wave packets (see Theorem A.1 from Harris’ article for a clean statement that applies here
[
15
]
).

Since we assume every ball of
\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
is
M
-rich, and
|\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)|\sim|X|
, from (
2
) we obtain

(3)

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lessapprox M|X|.

Refined decoupling is an
L^{6}(X)
estimate. By refined decoupling, and the calculation
\|\phi_{P}\|_{6}^{6}\sim|P|=R^{3/2}
,

\displaystyle M^{6}|X|\sim\int_{X}|f|^{6}\lessapprox(\frac{M}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|})^{2}(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}}\|\phi_{P}\|_{6}^{2})^{3}\sim(\frac{M}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|})^{2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|^{3}R^{3/2}.

Rearranging and combining like terms, we have

M^{4}|X|\lessapprox|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|R^{3/2}.

Applying (
3
) (to lower bound
M^{4}\geq(\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|/|X|)^{4}
), and combining like terms, we get

\mu^{4}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|^{3}\lessapprox R^{3/2}|X|^{3}.

Finally, by the well-spaced assumption,
|X|\sim R^{3/2}
, so we confirm

\mu^{4}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|^{3}\lessapprox|X|^{4},

and taking
3
rd-roots yields the desired bound.
\square

We have sketched the proof of the estimate
\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lessapprox|X|^{4/3}
in the high–frequency dominated case, where the Fourier support of
f
is essentially the
R^{-1}
-neighborhood of
\Gamma
, and refined cone decoupling is strongest. If
f=f_{K}
dominates the integral
\int_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)}f
, where
K\gg 1
, and

f_{K}(x)=\int_{|\xi_{3}|\sim 1/K}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi,

then the bounds we get from refined decoupling are a priori weaker. To compensate for this, we use the observation that
f_{K}
is locally constant on balls of radius
K
(Proposition
2.2
), and the separation assumption of
X
to get an additional gain; namely that

\int_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)}f_{K}\approx\frac{1}{K^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{K}(X)}f_{K},

which holds as long as
K
is less than the separation between distinct points of
X
. If
f
is dominated by even lower frequencies, then a short direct argument yields the desired bound, and we will treat this case separately at the end of the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Another wrinkle in making the argument we sketched in the high–case work is that we assumed every point of
X
was
M
-rich in the lightplanks
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
. What we will do instead is for each
x\in X
, determine the dominant frequency component
f_{K(x)}
near
x
to begin, and dyadically pigeonhole so that
K(x)
is constant for every
x\in X
. To execute this strategy, we will define a family of averages of
f
which are controlled by certain square functions of
f
in the same spirit as the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy square function estimate. We will set up the estimate precisely in order to apply refined decoupling in the form of Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
so the following holds for every
R\geq 1
.

Suppose
X\subset[0,R]^{3}
is a disjoint union of unit balls which are
1\leq\rho\leq R^{1/2}
-separated and such that
|X|\sim(R/\rho)^{3}
. For every
\mu\in[1,R^{3/2}]
, if
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
is an arbitrary pairwise incomparable collection of
\mu
-rich
1\times R^{1/2}\times R
-lightplanks for
X
, then

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\leq C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}|X|^{4/3}.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
R\geq R_{0}(\varepsilon)
is sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon
.

For each
\sim 1\times R^{-1/2}\times R^{-1}
cone plank
\theta
in a partition of
\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(\Gamma)
, write
\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\theta}
for the subcollection of planks
P
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
for which the corresponding
\theta_{P}
from Definition
2.1
lies in the angular sector corresponding to
\theta
. Define
f_{\theta}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\theta}}\phi_{P}
, where for each
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
,
\phi_{P}\geq\mathbf{1}_{P}
is a Schwartz function with Fourier support in
\theta-\theta\subset B(0,2)
. Some properties of
f
we shall use are as follows:

(1)

\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\sim\mathrm{vol}(P)|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|=R^{3/2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|

(2)

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lesssim\int_{X}f
.

Consider the following martingale-like sequence constructed from
f
, a sequence of scales
\{\rho_{k}\}
, and a Fourier decomposition of
B(0,2)
. On a first read, we recommend reading
\mathcal{A}_{k}f(x)
as
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{k}}f(x):=\frac{1}{|B(x,\rho_{k})|}\int_{B(x,\rho_{k})}f
, which is morally the same as
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
up to a small error.

Let
\eta
be a radial bump function supported in
B(0,1)
, satisfying
\eta\equiv 1
on
B(0,1/2)
. Let
\eta_{r}(x)=\eta(x/r)
be the
r
-ball localized version of
\eta
, satisfying
\eta_{r}\equiv 1
in
B(0,r/2)
. Assume that
R,\rho\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
. For
R=2^{m}
, let
\rho_{k}=2^{k\lceil{m\varepsilon}\rceil}
. Let
N
satisfy
\rho_{N-1}<\rho\leq\rho_{N}
, so
N\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
, in particular. For
\rho^{-1}\leq\rho_{k}^{-1}\leq 1
, write
\omega_{k}(x)=\frac{\rho_{k}^{-3}}{(1+|\rho_{k}^{-1}x|^{2})^{\varepsilon^{-1}}}
and note that
|\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.48265pt\hbox{$\eta$}\hskip-2.48265pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{15.30566pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}_{\rho_{k}^{-1}}|(x)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\omega_{k}(x)
. We define a version of
\omega_{k}
which has nice Fourier support and is uniformly pointwise comparable to
\omega_{k}
.

Let
\tilde{\omega}_{k}(x)
be defined by

\tilde{\omega}_{k}(x)=\rho_{k}^{-3}|\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.48265pt\hbox{$\eta$}\hskip-2.48265pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{15.30566pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}|^{2}(\rho_{k}^{-1}x)+\rho_{k}^{-3}\sum_{j>0}\frac{|\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.48265pt\hbox{$\eta$}\hskip-2.48265pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{15.30566pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}|^{2}(2^{-j}\rho_{k}^{-1}x)}{2^{2\varepsilon^{-1}j}}.

It is immediate from the definition that
\tilde{\omega}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

(W1)

Frequency localization.

The Fourier support of
\tilde{\omega}_{k}
is contained in
B(0,2\rho_{k}^{-1})
. This follows from the identity
|\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.48265pt\hbox{$\eta$}\hskip-2.48265pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{15.30566pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}|^{2}=\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.48265pt\hbox{$\eta$}\hskip-2.48265pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{15.30566pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}^{2}
, so that
\widehat{|\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.48265pt\hbox{$\eta$}\hskip-2.48265pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{15.30566pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}|^{2}}=\eta*\eta
, which is supported in
B(0,2)
.

(W2)

Spatial decay comparable to
\omega_{k}
.

For
|x|\lesssim\rho_{k}
, we have
\tilde{\omega}_{k}(x)\sim\omega_{k}(x)\sim\rho_{k}^{-3}
. More generally, if
C>1
with
C\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
|x|\sim C\rho_{k}
, then

\tilde{\omega}_{k}(x)\sim\omega_{k}(x)\sim_{\varepsilon}\rho_{k}^{-3}C^{-2\varepsilon^{-1}}.

In particular,
\tilde{\omega}_{k}(x)\sim\omega_{k}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we will define a stopping time
\mathbf{k}(x)
which measures the first time at which the local behavior of
f
goes from being “crowded” to “sparse” in transition from scales
\rho_{k-1}
to
\rho_{k}
. Intuitively,

(4)

f(x)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}f(x)\approx\dots\approx\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{k}(x)-1}}f(x)\gg\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{k}(x)}}f(x).

The condition (
4
) also morally ensures that we have control over the Fourier support of
f
:

f(x)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{k}(x)-1}}f(x)\approx(\widehat{f}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\rho_{\mathbf{k}(x)}^{-1}<|\xi|<\rho_{\mathbf{k}(x)-1}^{-1}\}})^{\vee}(x).

To make this rigorous, and to define the stopping time
\mathbf{k}(x)
precisely, we will use the weights
\tilde{\omega}_{k}
. Since

f=\sum_{\theta}f_{\theta}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{f_{\theta}})\subset\theta-\theta\subset B(0,2),

we have the pointwise estimate

f(x)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}f*\tilde{\omega}_{0}(x).

Fix
x\in B_{R}
. We now describe a
stopping time algorithm
that iteratively measures decay of the localized averages of
f
at
x
. Define the initial average

\mathcal{A}_{0}f(x):=\sum_{\theta}f_{\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{0}(x).

For each
k\geq 1
, define

\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}:=|\det T_{k,\theta}|\,\tilde{\omega}_{0}\circ T_{k,\theta},

where
T_{k,\theta}
is an affine transformation, and
\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
is spatially localized to an origin-centered
\rho_{k}\times R^{1/2}\times R
stack of
1\times R^{1/2}\times R
dual planks to
\theta
. To align with the notation when
k=0
, we define
\tilde{\omega}_{0,\theta}=\tilde{\omega}_{0}
for every
\theta
.

For
k\geq 1
, set

\mathcal{A}_{k}f:=\sum_{\theta}f_{\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{0,\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{1,\theta}*\cdots*\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}.

Since
k\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
,

\tilde{\omega}_{0,\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{1,\theta}*\cdots*\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}(x)\sim_{\varepsilon}\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}(x).

Each
\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
is Fourier supported in the ball
B(0,2\rho_{k}^{-1})
, and consequently,
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
is also Fourier supported in this ball. Note that
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
differ by convolution with
\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
, where the somewhat intricate form of
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
is chosen to align with the weights in the refined decoupling Theorem
3.1
when we make the key estimate (
12
).

Stopping Time Algorithm.
Let
\delta=\varepsilon^{2}
. We define the stopping time
\mathbf{k}(x)
by the following rule:

—

If there exists
1\leq k<N
such that

\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(x)>R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{k}f(x),

then define
\mathbf{k}(x)
to be the smallest such
k
.

—

If no such
k<N
exists, set
\mathbf{k}(x):=N
.

Stopping Regions.
Define measurable regions where the algorithm halts at each step:

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\mathcal{A}_{0}f(x)>R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{1}f(x)\right\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{aligned} x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\quad&\mathcal{A}_{j}f(x)\leq R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{j+1}f(x)&&\text{for all }0\leq j<k-1,\\
&\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(x)>R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{k}f(x)\end{aligned}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for }2\leq k<N,

\displaystyle\Omega_{N}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{aligned} x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\quad\mathcal{A}_{j}f(x)\leq R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{j+1}f(x)&&\text{for all }0\leq j<N\end{aligned}\right\}.

This procedure partitions
\mathbb{R}^{3}
into disjoint stopping regions
\{\Omega_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq N}
, with each
\Omega_{k}
corresponding to points where the function
f
goes from being “crowded” to “sparse” in transition from scales
\rho_{k-1}
to
\rho_{k}
for the first time. Motivated by the analogy
\mathcal{A}_{k}f\approx\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{k}}f
, we should think of each region
\Omega_{k}
as being approximately a union of
\rho_{k-1}
-cubes (see Proposition
7
for the precise formulation used in the proof).

Since there are
N\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
regions
\Omega_{k}
, by pigeonholing, we may select
k\in[N]
such that

\int_{X}f\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\Omega_{k}\cap X}f.

It follows by the definition of
\Omega_{k}
that

(5)

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}R^{(k-1)\delta}\int_{\Omega_{k}\cap X}\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f.

Note that
R^{k\delta}\leq R^{C\varepsilon}\approx 1
for all
k\in[N]
. The rest of the analysis splits into two cases. The first case is that
1\leq k<N
, which we treat presently.
Define
\mathcal{H}_{k}f=\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f-\mathcal{A}_{k}f
and note that for
x\in\Omega_{k}
,

\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(x)\sim\mathcal{H}_{k}f(x)=|\mathcal{H}_{k}f(x)|\geq R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{k}f(x),

so

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}R^{(k-1)\delta}\int_{\Omega_{k}\cap X}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|.

The Fourier support of
\mathcal{H}_{k}f
is contained in the annulus
\{\rho_{k}^{-1}<|\xi|<\rho_{k-1}^{-1}\}
. Consider a smooth partition of unity
\sum_{\lambda}\psi_{\lambda}=1
into dyadic annuli
\{|\xi|\sim\lambda\}
in the range between
\rho_{k}^{-1}
and
\rho_{k-1}^{-1}
(where the
\psi_{\lambda}
are dilations of a single function). Since
\mathcal{H}_{k}f=\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{H}_{k}f\ast\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-5.8785pt\hbox{$\psi_{\lambda}$}\hskip-5.8785pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{17.94456pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}
, after dyadically pigeonholing, we may assume that the function
\mathcal{H}_{k}f
is Fourier supported in the
\lambda
-dilation of
\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}/\lambda^{2}}(\Gamma)
(technically, we have to replace
\mathcal{H}_{k}f
by
\mathcal{H}_{k}f*\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-5.8785pt\hbox{$\psi_{\lambda}$}\hskip-5.8785pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{17.94456pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}
, which by abuse of notation we will continue to denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}f
).

Each
\theta
has a corresponding angular sector of size
R^{-1/2}
. Write
\tau_{k}
for angular sectors of size
R^{-1/2}/\lambda
, and
\theta\subset\tau_{k}
if the sector corresponding to
\theta
is contained in
\tau_{k}
, so that

f=\sum_{\tau_{k}}\sum_{\theta\subset\tau_{k}}f_{\theta}=:\sum_{\tau_{k}}f_{\tau_{k}},

and let

\mathcal{A}_{j}f_{\tau_{k}}:=\sum_{\theta\subset\tau_{k}}f_{\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{0,\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{1,\theta}*\cdots*\tilde{\omega}_{j,\theta}.

(We will only need
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f_{\tau_{k}},\mathcal{A}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}.)

Likewise, define

\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}:=\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f_{\tau_{k}}-\mathcal{A}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}},

so that

\mathcal{H}_{k}f=\sum_{\tau_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}.

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}
is Fourier supported in a
\lambda\times R^{-1/2}\times R^{-1}\lambda^{-1}
-cone plank contained in
\{|\xi|\sim\lambda\}
, lying above the angular sector
\tau_{k}
.

By incorporating the frequency localization of
\mathcal{H}_{k}f
, we are now positioned to apply the refined cone decoupling theorem (Theorem
3.1
) in order to further estimate
\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|
. More precisely, we will apply Theorem
3.1
to a level set
U_{\alpha,\beta}\subset\Omega_{k}
, where
|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|\sim\alpha
and

\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\sim\beta.

Here, the weight functions
W_{M,\tau_{k}}(x)=w_{M,\tau_{k}}(\lambda x)
, with
w_{M,\tau_{k}}
given by Theorem
3.1
.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|\leq\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|\leq(\#\tau_{k})^{1/2}(\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2})^{1/2}\leq R^{1/4}(\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2})^{1/2}.

Furthermore, note that
|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}
is Fourier supported in an origin-centered
\sim\lambda\times R^{-1/2}\times\lambda^{-1}R^{-1}
-cone plank, so we have
|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}\lesssim|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}\ast W_{M,\tau_{k}}
pointwise uniformly. Hence, if
\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\leq R^{-100}
, then
|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|\leq R^{-90}
. By a round of dyadic pigeonholing, we obtain for some
\alpha,\beta>R^{-100}
,

(6)

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lessapprox\alpha|U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap X|+R^{-90},

in which

U_{\alpha,\beta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|\sim\alpha,\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*{W}_{M,\tau_{k}}\sim\beta\}.

Before applying refined decoupling to bound
|U_{\alpha,\beta}|
, we exploit the heuristic that
|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|
and
\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}
are locally constant on
\rho_{k-1}
-balls to study the structure of
U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap X
.

Proposition 2.2

(Locally constant property)
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
K=K_{\varepsilon}>1
such that the following holds. Suppose
x\in U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}
. Then for any
\rho_{k-1}
-cube
Q
containing
x
, we have

Q\subset\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k},

where

\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|\sim_{K}\alpha,\quad\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\sim\beta,\\
&\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f\sim_{K}\alpha,\quad\mathcal{A}_{k}f\lesssim_{K}R^{-\delta}\alpha\end{aligned}\right\},

and
a\sim_{K}b
means
K^{-1}b\leq a\leq Kb
.

In particular, for every
\rho_{k-1}
-cube
Q
and every unit ball
q
with
q\cap Q\neq\emptyset
, we have

(7)

\frac{|\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}\cap Q|}{|Q|}\gtrsim|U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap q|.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
; the locally constant property.

We begin by noting that for any
\theta
, any nonnegative function
g
, and any
y,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
|z-z^{\prime}|\lesssim\rho_{k-1}
, we have

g(y)\tilde{\omega}_{k-1,\theta}(z-y)\sim g(y)\tilde{\omega}_{k-1,\theta}(z^{\prime}-y).

It follows by nonnegativity of
f
that
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(z)\sim\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(z^{\prime})
for any such
z,z^{\prime}
. Similar reasoning applied to
\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
shows that
\mathcal{A}_{k}f(z)\sim\mathcal{A}_{k}f(z^{\prime})
if
|z-z^{\prime}|<\rho_{k-1}
(actually, this holds as long as
z,z^{\prime}
belong to the same translation of a
\rho_{k}\times R^{1/2}\times R
-stack of lightplanks parallel to
\theta^{*}
, but we have no use for this stronger fact). For the statement about
\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}
, we note that
W_{M,\tau_{k}}
is approximately constant (depending only on
M=M(\varepsilon)
) on planks with dimensions
\lambda^{-1}\times R^{1/2}\times\lambda R
, which contain
\rho_{k-1}
-balls since
\lambda^{-1}\geq\rho_{k-1}
and
\lambda R\geq\rho^{-1}R\geq R^{1/2}\geq\rho_{k-1}
.

Since
x\in\Omega_{k}
, for any
y\in Q
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(y)\sim\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(x)\sim|\mathcal{H}_{k}f(x)|
, and
\mathcal{A}_{k}f(y)\sim\mathcal{A}_{k}f(x)<R^{-\delta}\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(x)
. In particular, for any
y\in Q
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}f(y)<\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(y)
, since we may assume from the onset that
R\geq K^{\varepsilon^{-2}}
. Hence, for any
y\in Q
, by the reverse triangle inequality and our assumption that
x\in U_{\alpha,\beta}
,

|\mathcal{H}_{k}f(y)|\geq\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f(y)-\mathcal{A}_{k}f(y)\gtrsim\alpha.

This finishes the proof of the containment
Q\subset\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}
. The proof of (
7
) follows immediately.
∎

By refined decoupling from Theorem
3.1
, we have

(8)

\alpha^{6}|\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}|\lesssim\int_{\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f|^{6}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}\beta^{2}\sum_{\tau_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}.

Note that
\beta\sim\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}
on
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}
, so
\beta\sim\|\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k})}
.

We will first bound the
L^{2}
integral on the right hand side of (
8
). Note that the weight functions are
L^{1}
-normalized, meaning that
\tilde{\omega}_{j,\theta}
satisfy
\max_{j,\theta}\int\tilde{\omega}_{j,\theta}\sim 1
. Since
k<N\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
, and
f_{\theta}\geq 0
, we have

(9)

\|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\|\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\mathcal{A}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\sum_{\theta\subset\tau_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\theta}.

Therefore, since each
f_{\theta}\lesssim 1
, and
\|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\#(\theta\subset\tau_{k})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{\tau_{k}}\|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\cdot\sum_{\tau_{k}}\big(\|\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\mathcal{A}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{\tau_{k}}\#(\theta\subset\tau_{k})\sum_{\tau_{k}}(\sum_{\theta\subset\tau_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\max_{\tau_{k}}\#(\theta\subset\tau_{k})\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\sim\max_{\tau_{k}}\#(\theta\subset\tau_{k})R^{3/2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|.

Hence we have the bound

(10)

\displaystyle\alpha^{6}|\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}|

\displaystyle\lessapprox\max_{\tau_{k}}\#(\theta\subset\tau_{k})^{3}\|\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k})}^{2}R^{3/2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|.

Next, we apply the triangle inequality and nonnegativity of the functions
f_{\theta},\tilde{\omega}_{j,\theta}
to say that

(11)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|*W_{M,\tau_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f_{\tau_{k}}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}+\sum_{\tau_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}.

The function
\sum_{\tau_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}
is pointwise comparable to
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
, since
\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\sim\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
for
M>\varepsilon^{-1}
and
\theta\subset\tau_{k}
. This holds because
\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
is localized to an origin-centered
\rho_{k}\times R^{1/2}\times R
box, while
W_{M,\tau_{k}}
is localized to an origin-centered
\lambda^{-1}\times R^{1/2}\times\lambda R

axis-parallel
subset of that box.

The key point.
Since
\rho_{k}^{-1}<\lambda<\rho_{k-1}^{-1}
and
\rho_{k}=R^{\varepsilon}\rho_{k-1}
, it follows that for
M>\varepsilon^{-1}
and
\theta\subset\tau_{k}
,

(12)

\tilde{\omega}_{k-1,\theta}*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\lesssim R^{\varepsilon}\,\tilde{\omega}_{k-1,\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}.

Indeed, the left-hand side is approximately
\lambda/R^{3/2}
on a
\lambda^{-1}\times R^{1/2}\times R
block, while the right-hand side (before inserting the
R^{\varepsilon}
factor) is approximately
\rho_{k}^{-1}/R^{3/2}
on a larger
\rho_{k}\times R^{1/2}\times R
block. Since
\lambda<R^{\varepsilon}\rho_{k}^{-1}
, the left-hand side may exceed
\tilde{\omega}_{k-1,\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{k,\theta}
by at most a factor of
R^{\varepsilon}
, which justifies the inequality.

Therefore, picking up from (
11
), we have

\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\lesssim R^{\varepsilon}\,\mathcal{A}_{k}f.

Now, by the definition of
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}
,

\|\sum_{\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{H}_{k}f_{\tau_{k}}|*W_{M,\tau_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k})}\lesssim R^{\varepsilon}\|\mathcal{A}_{k}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k})}\lesssim R^{\varepsilon}R^{-\delta}\alpha.

The number of
\theta\subset\tau_{k}
is at most
\sim\lambda^{-1}
, so (
10
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\lessapprox\lambda^{-3}\alpha^{2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|R^{3/2}

and hence

(13)

\alpha^{4}|\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}|\lessapprox\lambda^{-3}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Summing over dyadic
\rho_{k-1}
-cubes, we have by the locally constant property Proposition
2.2
(and the assumption that
k<N
, so there are at most
O(1)
-many unit balls
q\subset X
in any
\rho_{k-1}
-cube),

(14)

\displaystyle|\tilde{U}_{{\alpha,\beta}}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}|\geq|Q|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\ \text{dyadic}\ \rho_{k-1}\text{-cube}\\
Q\cap X\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{|\tilde{U}_{{\alpha,\beta}}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{k}\cap Q|}{|Q|}\gtrsim\rho_{k-1}^{3}|U_{{\alpha,\beta}}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap X|.

Plugging (
14
) into (
13
) and using
\lambda\geq\rho_{k}^{-1}=R^{-\varepsilon}\rho_{k-1}^{-1}
, we get

(15)

\alpha^{4}|U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap X|\lessapprox\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Plugging (
6
) into (
15
), we get

\bigg(\frac{\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|}{|U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap X|}\bigg)^{4}|U_{\alpha,\beta}\cap\Omega_{k}\cap X|\lessapprox\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

so

\mu^{4}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|^{4}\lessapprox|X|^{3}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\sim|X|^{3}\cdot R^{3/2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|.

Since we assume
|X|\sim(R/\rho)^{3}
, where
1\leq\rho\leq R^{1/2}
, we obtain the desired bound

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lessapprox|X|^{4/3}.

It remains to treat the case
k=N
, where the stopping time algorithm does not terminate before reaching the top scale. Since
\mathbf{k}(x)=N
means that the inequality

\mathcal{A}_{j}f(x)\leq R^{\delta}\mathcal{A}_{j+1}f(x)

holds for all
0\leq j<N
, we may iterate this bound and apply linearity and
\delta=\varepsilon^{2}
to deduce

(16)

\mu|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}R^{N\delta}\int_{\Omega_{N}\cap X}\mathcal{A}_{N}f\lessapprox\sum_{\theta}\int_{X}\mathcal{A}_{N}f_{\theta}.

We introduce one last piece of notation. For each
\theta
, and
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\theta}
, let

\mathcal{A}_{N,\theta}\phi_{P}:=\phi_{P}\ast\tilde{\omega}_{0,\theta}*\tilde{\omega}_{1,\theta}*\dots*\tilde{\omega}_{N,\theta}.

Note that
\mathcal{A}_{N,\theta}\phi_{P}(x)\sim\frac{|P\cap B_{\rho}|}{|B_{\rho}|}=\rho^{-1}
for
x
in a concentric plank
\tilde{P}
of approximate dimensions
\rho\times R^{1/2}\times R
containing
P
, and that
\mathcal{A}_{N,\theta}\phi_{P}(x)\leq R^{-50}
if
x\notin C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}\tilde{P}
for
C_{\varepsilon}>1
a large enough constant.

By the
\rho
-separation of the set
X
, we have for each fixed plank
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\theta}
,

(17)

\int_{X}\mathcal{A}_{N,\theta}\phi_{P}\lessapprox\rho^{-1}|X\cap R^{\varepsilon}\tilde{P}|+R^{-50}\lessapprox\rho^{-1}\frac{R^{3/2}}{\rho^{2}}+R^{-50}.

Summing inequality (
17
) over all planks
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\theta}
and all
\theta
, and using (
16
), we obtain

\mu\lessapprox\frac{R^{3/2}}{\rho^{3}}.

Since the family
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
consists of
K
-incomparable planks, we have the upper bound
|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lesssim_{K}R^{2}
. Combining this trivial upper bound for
|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|
with the above estimate for
\mu
, and recalling that
|X|\sim(R/\rho)^{3}
, we conclude that

\mu^{4/3}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|\lessapprox\left(\frac{R^{3/2}}{\rho^{3}}\right)^{4/3}R^{2}=\frac{R^{4}}{\rho^{4}}\sim|X|^{4/3}.

This completes the analysis of the case
k=N
and finishes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

By rescaling, Theorem
2.1
implies Theorem
1.2
stated in the introduction. To see this, assume Theorem
2.1
, and let

be well-spaced, and let
\tau\in[|X|^{-1/3},|X|^{-1/6}]
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\tau}
be a collection of pairwise incomparable
\mu
-rich
\tau\times\tau^{2}
tangency rectangles. By point-lightplank duality,
\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\tau}
can be identified with a collection
\mathcal{P}
of pairwise incomparable
\mu
-rich (in
X
)

-lightplanks. Rescaling by

yields a set
\tilde{X}\subset[0,R]^{2}\times[R,2R]
which is maximally
\rho:=|X|^{-1/3}\tau^{-2}\in[1,R^{1/2}]
-separated, and a set
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
of
\mu
-rich
1\times R^{1/2}\times R
lightplanks.

If
\tau\in(|X|^{-1/3},1)
, then the estimate of Theorem
1.2
follows immediately using the well-spaced condition, since every
\tau\times\tau^{2}
rectangle is automatically
\sim|X|\tau^{3}
-rich, and there are
\sim\tau^{-4}
-many such
\tau\times\tau^{2}
rectangles.

We will use Theorem
1.2
to break the 3/2-barrier of Problem
2

for well-spaced sets of circles.
The following is Theorem
1.1
, restated for convenience.

Theorem 2.3
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
A_{\varepsilon}
so that the following holds.
Let
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
be well-spaced, and let
\mathcal{C}_{X}
denote the collection of circles

\{C_{z,r}:(z,r)\in X\},

where
C_{z,r}
denotes the circle with center
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and radius
r>0
.

If no three circles of
\mathcal{C}_{X}
are tangent at a point, then the following estimate holds:

|\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\leq A_{\varepsilon}|X|^{25/18+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We will actually show that for an arbitrary well-spaced set of circles
X\subset[0,1]^{2}\times[1,2]
, the set

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\text{at least two circles of $\mathcal{C}$ are internally tangent at $z$}\}

has cardinality
O_{\varepsilon}(|X|^{25/18+\varepsilon})
. By the discussion after the statement of Problem
2
, this implies the corresponding estimate for
|\mathcal{T}_{pair}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|
when no three circles of
\mathcal{C}_{X}
are tangent at a point.

Begin by letting
\mathcal{P}
be a maximal pairwise incomparable collection of
2
-rich (in
X
)
1\times|X|^{-1/3}\times|X|^{-2/3}
lightplanks. If
q\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})
is given, then there necessarily exists a lightplank
P\in\mathcal{P}
and two points
(x,r),(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in X\cap P
such that the circles
C_{x,r},C_{x^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
are internally tangent at
q
. Therefore, we have the following union bound for
|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|
:

(18)

|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\leq\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X\cap P})|.

By dyadically pigeonholing, we may assume that every
P
in the sum in (
18
) satisfies
|X\cap P|\sim\mu
for some
\mu\geq 1
. Denote the collection of such planks as
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
, so that

(19)

|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\lessapprox\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}}|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X\cap P})|

Applying Wolff’s bound
|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X\cap P})|\lessapprox|X\cap P|^{3/2}\sim\mu^{3/2}
for each
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
, we obtain

|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\lessapprox\mu^{3/2}|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}|.

Applying Theorem
1.2
, we obtain

|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\lessapprox\mu^{1/6}|X|^{4/3}.

Since
X
is well-spaced, it is
\sim|X|^{-1/3}
-separated, so
\mu\lesssim|X|^{1/3}
. Plugging this into the preceding expression yields

|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C}_{X})|\lessapprox|X|^{4/3+1/18}=|X|^{25/18},

as claimed.
∎

3.
Decoupling

There is a natural resemblance between the sequence
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
and its increments
\mathcal{H}_{k}f:=\mathcal{A}_{k-1}f-\mathcal{A}_{k}f
appearing in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, and the classical setting of martingales and their quadratic variations. In probability theory, the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequalities compare the size of a martingale to the square function formed from its increments
[
3
]
. Our setup, though entirely deterministic, echoes this structure:
\mathcal{A}_{k}f
behaves like a smoothed or “filtered” version of
f
, while the increments
\mathcal{H}_{k}f
measure the energy transferred between scales. This analogy is purely heuristic, but we hope it helps place the arguments of Section
2
in a natural light.

Refined high–low frequency analysis, particularly the interplay between a function
f
and its square function
\sum_{\theta}|f_{\theta}|^{2}
, emerged in earlier works such as
[
6
,
12
]
, and featured implicitly in the improved decoupling theorem for the parabola by Guth–Maldague–Wang
[
13
]
, though the structural framework was not explicitly isolated there. A related approach, with a clearer articulation of the relationship between
f
and its square function, appears in the work of Fu–Guth–Maldague
[
9
]
on decoupling inequalities for short Dirichlet sequences.

3.1.
Proof of the refined decoupling Theorem
3.1

Let

\Gamma=\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\xi_{3}=\sqrt{\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}},1\leq\xi_{3}\leq 2\}

be the usual segment of the cone. For
\{a_{\theta}\}
a partition of
S^{1}
into
R^{-1/2}
-arcs (assuming
R
is of the form
2^{2^{k_{0}}}
), define the set of cone planks
\Theta_{\Gamma}(R)=\{\theta\}
to be

\theta=\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(\Gamma)\cap\{\arg(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in a_{\theta}\}.

Let
w_{M,\theta}=|\det A_{\theta}|w_{M}\circ A_{\theta}
be weight functions, in which
A_{\theta}
is a linear function mapping the origin-centered
1\times R^{1/2}\times R
-cone plank
\theta^{*}
to the unit cube, and where
w_{M}(x)=\frac{1}{(1+|x|)^{M}}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Refined decoupling for
\Gamma
)
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\infty)
so that the following holds. Let
R\geq 1
and consider
\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(\Gamma)
. For each
M\geq C_{\varepsilon}
and each Schwartz function
g_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
with Fourier support in
\theta
we have

\int_{U_{\beta}\cap B_{R}}|\sum_{\theta}g_{\theta}|^{6}\leq C_{\varepsilon}R^{\varepsilon}\beta^{2}\sum_{\theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|g_{\theta}|^{2}

for each
R
-ball
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where

U_{\beta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\beta\leq\sum_{\theta}|g_{\theta}(x)|^{2}*w_{M,\theta}\leq 2\beta\}.

We prove Theorem
3.1
in two parts, first by establishing Theorem
3.2
, an analogous refined decoupling theorem for the parabola, and then using a lifting technique based on cylindrical decoupling to deduce Theorem
3.1
for the cone from Theorem
3.2
.

3.2.
Refined decoupling for the parabola (Proof of Theorem
3.1
part 1/2)

Let
\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}({Par})=\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(\{(t,t^{2}):-1\leq t\leq 1\})

\theta=\theta(l)
be the intersection of
\left([lR^{-1/2},(l+1)R^{-1/2})\times\mathbb{R}\right)\cap\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(Par)
and the end pieces, so
\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(Par)=\bigsqcup\theta
.

Let
w_{M}
be the weight function
w_{M}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
defined by
w_{M}(x)=\frac{1}{(1+|x|)^{M}}
. For each
\theta=\theta_{l}
, let
A_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the linear transformation

A_{\theta}\colon(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto x_{1}R^{1/2}(1,2lR^{-1/2})+x_{2}R(-2lR^{-1/2},1).

Let
w_{M,\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
denote

Theorem 3.2

(Refined decoupling for
{Par}
)
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
and
M>0
sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon,M}\in(0,\infty)
so that the following holds. Let
R\geq 1
and consider
\mathcal{N}_{R^{-1}}(Par)
. For each Schwartz function

with Fourier support in
\theta
we have

\int_{U_{\beta}\cap B_{R}}|\sum_{\theta}g_{\theta}|^{6}\leq C_{\varepsilon,M}R^{\varepsilon}\beta^{2}\sum_{\theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|g_{\theta}|^{2}

for each
R
-ball
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where

U_{\beta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\beta\leq\sum_{\theta}|g_{\theta}|^{2}*w_{M,\theta}(x)\leq 2\beta\}.

We prove Theorem
3.2
by defining

to be the smallest constant such that

\int_{U_{\beta}\cap B_{R}}|\sum_{\theta}g_{\theta}|^{6}\leq D(M,\varepsilon,R)\beta^{2}\sum_{\theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|g_{\theta}|^{2}

for each
g_{\theta}
and
U_{\beta}
from the set-up of Theorem
3.2
. We will first perform canonical decoupling into
\tau
which are parabola blocks in
\mathcal{N}_{R^{1/8}}(Par)

\tau
has approximate dimensions

Proposition 3.3

(Multiscale inequality)
.

For each

,

Proof.

Let
\phi_{R^{-1/4}}

\phi_{b}

|\hbox{\hskip 8.33334pt\hskip-2.97917pt\hbox{$\phi$}\hskip-2.97917pt\hskip-8.33334pt\raisebox{17.94456pt}{\hbox{$\scalebox{-1}{\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}}$}}\hskip-8.33334pt\hskip 8.33334pt}_{R^{-1/4}}|^{2}(x-c_{b})

\tau

\int_{U_{\beta}\cap b}|g|^{6}\lesssim(\log R)^{6}\int_{U_{\beta}\cap b}|\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(b)}g_{\tau}|^{6}+R^{-100}\max_{\theta}\|g_{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{6},

in which

4.
Sharpness

In this section, we show that Theorem
1.2
is sharp by constructing a random set of (almost)
\rho
-separated circles such that with high probability, there is only one value of
\mu
such that every
1\times R^{1/2}\times R
-lightplank contains about
\mu

.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, the following holds for each
R\geq R_{0}(\varepsilon)
sufficiently large and each
R^{\varepsilon}\leq\rho\leq R^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

With probability at least
.9
, there is a random set
X\subset[0,R]^{3}
of cardinality
\sim R^{3+\varepsilon}\rho^{-3}
such that every
\rho
-cube contains at most
R^{\varepsilon}

X
, and such that every
1\times R^{1/2}\times R
-lightplank
P
in a maximal pairwise incomparable collection of such lightplanks contained in
[0,R]^{3}
contains
\sim R^{3/2+\varepsilon}\rho^{-3}
-points of
X
. In particular,

(R^{3/2+\varepsilon}\rho^{-3})^{4/3}|\mathcal{P}|\gtrapprox|X|^{4/3}.

Proof.

We may assume
R>10
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\rho\geq R^{\varepsilon}
, and consider the grid in
[0,R]^{3}
formed by taking all closed cubes
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
of side length
100\rho
. Within each cube
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
, choose a concentric sub-cube
Q_{i}
of side length
\rho
. The resulting cubes
Q_{i}
are at least
10\rho
-separated (being generous). Let
Y=\bigsqcup_{i}Q_{i}
, so

. We will choose points randomly and independently from

with probability

be the random set of points we obtain. By Proposition
A.1
, with probability at least

Appendix A
Large deviations for sums of Bernoulli random variables

Here we record the estimates of large deviations for sums of Bernoulli random variables we need to establish the sharpness of Theorem
1.2
. These are standard applications of Chernoff bounds, but we include the proofs for completeness.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\{\xi_{i}:i=1,\dotsc,n\}
be independent 0,1-valued random variables of mean
p
, and let
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
. The following estimates hold

(i)

p_{n}=\Prob(S_{n}>10np)\leq e^{-5np}

(ii)

q_{n}=\Prob(S_{n}<\frac{1}{10}np)\leq e^{-\frac{np}{2}}
.

Proof.

First we prove (i). Let
t>0
be an extra parameter we have at our disposal. By Tchebychev’s inequality and independence of the
\xi_{i}
,

\displaystyle p_{n}=\Prob[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}>10np]

\displaystyle\leq\Prob[e^{t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}}\geq e^{10tnp}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-10tnp}\E[e^{t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}}]

\displaystyle=e^{-10tnp}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\E e^{t\xi_{i}}.

A direct calculation shows

\E e^{t\xi_{i}}=e^{t}p+(1-p)=1+(e^{t}-1)p.

By what we have so far,

p_{n}\leq e^{-10tnp}[1+(e^{t}-1)p]^{n}\leq e^{-10tnp}e^{(e^{t}-1)np}=[e^{-10t+(e^{t}-1)}]^{np}.

We could optimize to choose the best value of
t
, but setting
t=1
is sufficient because it shows

p_{n}\leq[e^{-8.28...}]^{np}\leq e^{-5np}.

The proof of (ii) is very similar. Let
\eta_{i}=p-\xi_{i}
, and let
T_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
. Then the reader can easily verify

\Prob(S_{n}<\frac{1}{10}np)=\Prob(T_{n}>\frac{9}{10}np).

By Tchebychev and independence of the variables
\eta_{i}
:

\displaystyle\Prob(T_{n}>\frac{9}{10}np)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{9}{10}tnp}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}e^{t\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{9}{10}tnp}(e^{tp}(1-p)+e^{t(p-1)}p)^{n}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{1}{10}tnp}(1+(e^{-t}-1)p)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{1}{10}tnp}e^{(e^{-t}-1)np}=[e^{\frac{t}{10}+(e^{-t}-1)}]^{np}.

Setting
t=1
, we have

q_{n}=\Prob(T_{n}>\frac{9}{10}np)\leq[e^{-0.532...}]^{np}\leq e^{-\frac{np}{2}}.\qed
