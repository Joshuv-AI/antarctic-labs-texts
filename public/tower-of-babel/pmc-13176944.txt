Inflammatory Myofibroblastic Tumor of the Gallbladder Mimicking Malignancy: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Hina Khalid, Muhammad Husnain Ahmad, Siraj Haider, Muhammad Hassan, Muhammad Arsalan, Muhammad Bilal Khalid, Abdul Rauf Khalid, Maryam Khalid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) of the gallbladder is an exceptionally rare benign tumor, often misdiagnosed as malignancy due to its radiologic and clinical features. Fewer than ten cases have been reported in the literature. IMT is characterized histologically by a proliferation of myofibroblastic spindle cells with inflammatory cell infiltration. We report the case of a 71‐year‐old male presenting with upper abdominal pain for 1 month. Imaging revealed a hetero‐echoic mass replacing the gallbladder and invading the adjacent liver, initially suspected to be gallbladder carcinoma. The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy, extended cholecystectomy, and Roux‐en‐Y hepaticojejunostomy. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor of the gallbladder. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient remains recurrence‐free at 2 years follow‐up. Gallbladder IMTs are rare entities that clinically and radiologically mimic carcinoma. Surgical resection remains the mainstay for diagnosis and treatment. Despite being histologically benign, IMTs have potential for local invasion and recurrence, warranting long‐term follow‐up.

Introduction

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm composed of spindle‐shaped myofibroblastic cells and a variable inflammatory infiltrate. Initially identified in the lungs, IMTs have been reported in multiple extrapulmonary sites, including the abdomen and pelvis. Gallbladder involvement, however, is exceedingly rare, with fewer than ten reported cases to date [1, 2]. The etiology of IMT remains unclear but may involve autoimmune or infectious triggers. While most IMTs are histologically benign, their imaging features and local invasiveness can mimic malignant neoplasms, leading to diagnostic and management dilemmas [3].

Histopathologically, IMTs can resemble malignant tumors due to cellular atypia, mitotic activity, and infiltration into surrounding tissues. Immunohistochemistry, particularly anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) staining, is a helpful diagnostic adjunct, as ALK positivity is seen in about 50% of cases, especially in younger patients [3, 4]. The differential diagnosis includes calcifying fibrous pseudotumor, inflammatory fibrosarcoma, follicular dendritic cell tumor, and gastrointestinal stromal tumors [5]. Among these, calcifying fibrous pseudotumor can be distinguished by dense hyalinized collagen, psammomatous calcifications, and sparse cellularity [6].

Due to their nonspecific presentation and resemblance to malignancy, IMTs are often diagnosed postoperatively. Surgical resection remains the gold standard for both diagnosis and treatment. We report a rare case of gallbladder IMT in an elderly male, which radiologically mimicked gallbladder carcinoma and required extensive surgical management.

Case History/Examination

A 71‐year‐old male presented with upper abdominal pain and discomfort of 1 month's duration. He denied fever, jaundice, vomiting, or weight loss. Physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory values, including liver function tests and tumor markers, were within normal limits (Table 1).

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, Treatment)

Ultrasonography of the abdomen revealed a hetero‐echoic mass arising from the gallbladder with suspected liver invasion (Figure 1). Contrast‐enhanced CT confirmed a hypodense lesion replacing the gallbladder and infiltrating hepatic segments IV and V (Figure 2). No distant metastasis or significant lymphadenopathy was detected. A provisional diagnosis of gallbladder carcinoma was made.

Preoperative ultrasonography demonstrating a hetero‐echoic gallbladder mass with suspected liver invasion.

Contrast‐enhanced CT of the whole abdomen showing a hypodense mass replacing the gallbladder, with invasion into hepatic segments IV and V.

The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperatively, a firm, infiltrative mass involving the gallbladder and liver was identified. An extended cholecystectomy with Roux‐en‐Y hepaticojejunostomy was performed. Intraoperative cholangiography via the cystic duct stump showed no leakage and free flow into the duodenum. Frozen section analysis of lymph nodes was negative, but regional lymphadenectomy was performed due to the high suspicion of malignancy.

Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Histopathological analysis revealed fibroblast‐like spindle cell proliferation, small lymphocytes, and plasma cells located in the gallbladder adventitia. The mucosa and muscularis propria were histologically unremarkable (Figures 3 and 4). Immunohistochemistry showed scattered CD3 and CD20 staining, and diffuse kappa and lambda expression, indicating a reactive inflammatory background and excluding hematologic malignancy. ALK staining was negative (Figures 3 and 4). A final diagnosis of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor of the gallbladder was established. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the fifth day. At two‐year follow‐up, he remains asymptomatic and recurrence‐free.

Discussion

Gallbladder IMTs are rare tumors that can present with features mimicking malignancy. The majority of cases present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain or discomfort, as seen in our patient. Although tumor markers and liver function tests may remain within normal limits, imaging often shows a mass lesion with local infiltration—leading to a presumptive diagnosis of carcinoma [1, 2].

The choice of extended cholecystectomy in our patient was guided by established oncological principles for suspected gallbladder carcinoma. Per current guidelines, extended cholecystectomy—incorporating a hepatic parenchymal resection margin of at least 2 cm and regional lymphadenectomy of the hepatoduodenal ligament—is the standard of care for T2 and T3 gallbladder malignancies [7, 8]. Because the preoperative imaging demonstrated transmural invasion of hepatic segments IV and V indistinguishable from T3 carcinoma, this approach was oncologically appropriate and consistent with published surgical standards. The addition of Roux‐en‐Y hepaticojejunostomy was necessitated by intraoperative findings: the infiltrative mass had involved the hepatic hilum with disruption of the normal cystic duct anatomy, precluding primary biliary repair. Hepaticojejunostomy in this context provides a tension‐free biliary‐enteric anastomosis and is the reconstruction of choice when the common hepatic duct cannot be preserved [7]. Intraoperative cholangiography confirmed anastomotic integrity with free flow of contrast into the duodenum.

Histologically, IMT is characterized by myofibroblastic spindle cell proliferation within an inflammatory milieu of plasma cells, lymphocytes, and occasionally eosinophils. Although ALK positivity supports the diagnosis in younger individuals, adult‐onset IMTs may be ALK‐negative, as observed in our case [3, 4]. Immunohistochemistry plays a critical role in distinguishing IMT from other mimics such as lymphoma or gastrointestinal stromal tumors.

The significance of ALK expression in IMT extends beyond diagnosis. In ALK‐positive cases—identified in approximately 50% of IMTs, particularly in younger patients—ALK rearrangements result from fusions involving the ALK gene on chromosome 2p23 with various partner genes, leading to constitutive kinase activation [3, 4]. This molecular subgroup is clinically important because ALK‐positive IMTs may respond to targeted therapy with ALK inhibitors such as crizotinib, which has demonstrated efficacy in unresectable or recurrent ALK‐positive IMTs in case series and small cohort studies [9]. This therapeutic option is particularly relevant in cases where complete surgical resection is not achievable. In our patient, ALK staining was negative, which is consistent with the recognized lower rate of ALK positivity in older adults; however, ALK testing remains an essential component of the immunohistochemical workup because a positive result carries direct therapeutic implications. Adult‐onset ALK‐negative IMTs are diagnosed on the basis of histomorphology and the supporting immunohistochemical panel, as in the present case [4].

The differential diagnosis includes calcifying fibrous pseudotumor, which can be ruled out based on the absence of psammomatous calcifications and hyalinized stroma [5, 6]. Inflammatory fibrosarcomas, on the other hand, show higher mitotic activity and cellular atypia, which were absent in our specimen.

Our review of the literature shows that gallbladder IMTs typically occur in middle‐aged or older individuals, frequently involve a single adjacent organ, and often lead to aggressive surgical management due to malignancy concerns [10, 11]. Long‐term outcomes are generally favorable after complete resection. However, recurrence has been reported, reinforcing the need for long‐term follow‐up.

In conclusion, IMT of the gallbladder is an uncommon, benign tumor that mimics malignancy. Surgeons and radiologists should consider it in the differential diagnosis of gallbladder masses. Surgical resection remains both diagnostic and curative. Awareness of this rare entity can guide appropriate management and avoid overtreatment.

Conclusions

Gallbladder IMTs are rare entities that clinically and radiologically mimic carcinoma. Surgical resection remains the mainstay for diagnosis and treatment. Despite being histologically benign, IMTs have the potential for local invasion and recurrence, warranting long‐term follow‐up.
