Introduction

Background and rationale

Breastfeeding is widely recognised as the optimal form of infant nutrition and provides significant health benefits for both mothers and children. For infants, breastfeeding is associated with reduced risks of infectious diseases, lower mortality rates and improved cognitive development.1 For mothers, breastfeeding has been linked to decreased risks of breast cancer, ovarian cancer and type 2 diabetes.1 2 Based on this evidence, the WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life, with continued breastfeeding alongside complementary foods up to 2 years of age or beyond.3

Despite these well-established benefits, there remains a substantial gap between the recommended targets and actual breastfeeding rates worldwide. According to recent data from UNICEF and WHO, only 48% of infants under 6 months were exclusively breastfed globally in 2023, which falls considerably short of the 70% target set by the Global Breastfeeding Collective for 2030.4 In China, the situation is similarly concerning, with the 2019 national report showing an exclusive breastfeeding rate of only 29.2% for infants under 6 months.5

The reasons behind these low breastfeeding rates are complex and multifactorial. Lactation is not simply a matter of personal choice or biological capacity. Rather, it is a dynamic process that is shaped by an interconnected web of biological, psychological, social-cultural and systemic factors. However, current research on this topic tends to be fragmented. Biomedical studies typically focus on hormonal regulation and pharmacological interventions. Psychological research examines maternal self-efficacy6 and emotional well-being.7 Public health studies evaluate the effectiveness of interventions designed to increase breastfeeding rates at the community or population level.8 Meanwhile, traditional Chinese medicine research investigates the clinical applications of acupuncture, acupressure and herbal remedies within a distinct theoretical framework that includes concepts such as meridian theory and chronotherapy.9

Each of these research streams makes valuable contributions, but their isolated nature creates what has been termed a ‘silo effect’ that prevents us from seeing the full picture. This fragmentation has real consequences for clinical practice and policy. When we fail to recognise the interconnected nature of lactation difficulties, we risk placing undue blame on individual mothers for what are often systemic failures. This approach not only adds to maternal distress but also leads to ineffective interventions that address symptoms rather than root causes.

Existing reviews and knowledge gaps

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined specific aspects of lactation. Miao et al10 conducted a systematic review focused specifically on risk factors for delayed onset of lactogenesis II. The US Preventive Services Task Force published an updated evidence report in 2025 that evaluated support interventions for breastfeeding.11 The Cochrane Collaboration has published reviews on oral galactagogues (2020)12 and breastfeeding support (2022).13 These reviews provide valuable evidence on their respective topics.

However, there are several notable gaps in the existing literature. First, no previous review has attempted to map the breadth of evidence across the full spectrum of factors affecting lactation during the first 6 months post partum. Second, existing reviews have generally not included both English and Chinese language literature, which means that evidence from traditional Chinese medicine interventions has not been systematically characterised within the broader evidence landscape. Third, there is a lack of theoretical frameworks that can help organise and interpret the diverse body of evidence in a clinically meaningful way. A scoping review is particularly well-suited to address these gaps, as its primary purpose is to map the extent, range and nature of research activity rather than to answer a specific clinical question or provide definitive recommendations.

Methods

Protocol design and registration

This protocol was developed following the updated Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews.14 The review will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.15 This protocol will be registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to the completion of formal data synthesis.

We acknowledge that scoping reviews differ from systematic reviews in their purpose and methods.16 While systematic reviews aim to answer a specific clinical question and often include formal quality assessment and meta-analysis, scoping reviews aim to map the available evidence on a topic, identify gaps and clarify key concepts.17 Consistent with this purpose, our review will focus on describing the characteristics and distribution of the available research rather than providing pooled effect estimates or formal recommendations for clinical practice.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies will be included if they meet all of the following criteria:

Population: Perinatal women (from late pregnancy through 6 months post partum) and their infants. This includes mothers of both term and preterm infants, as breastfeeding challenges are particularly relevant in the preterm population18 and this group is often cared for in neonatal intensive care settings where specific interventions have been developed.

Concept: Studies examining factors that influence lactation outcomes or studies evaluating interventions aimed at improving lactation outcomes.

Context: Any healthcare or community setting, including hospitals, clinics, home-based care and community health programmes.

Study types: Systematic reviews, meta-analyses or randomised controlled trials (RCTs). To ensure comprehensive mapping of the evidence landscape, we will not impose a minimum sample size threshold for RCTs, as this would be inconsistent with the exploratory purpose of a scoping review and would risk excluding important emerging evidence, particularly in areas such as traditional Chinese medicine and psychological interventions where large-scale trials are less common. For the identification of risk factors (Aim 1), we will primarily rely on synthesised evidence from existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses, as most risk factor research is derived from observational studies that are best captured through such syntheses. We acknowledge that novel factors appearing only in primary observational studies not yet incorporated into systematic reviews may be missed; this limitation is inherent to our focus on synthesised evidence and will be noted in the final report.

Language: English or Chinese.

Publication date: 1 January 2020 to 1 October 2025.

Information sources and search strategy

We will conduct systematic searches in the following electronic databases:

English databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus and Web of Science.

Chinese databases: China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data and VIP Database.

The search strategy will combine Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. The core English search terms will include “lactation”, “breastfeeding”, “breast milk”, “perinatal”, “postpartum”, combined with “factors”, “determinants”, “predictors”, “interventions”, “support”, “randomised controlled trial”, “systematic review” and “meta-analysis”.

For Chinese databases, we will use equivalent Chinese terms including: “泌乳” (lactation), “母乳喂养” (breastfeeding), “哺乳” (nursing), “产后” (post partum), “围产期” (perinatal), “缺乳” (insufficient lactation), “催乳” (promoting lactation), “影响因素” (influencing factors), “干预” (intervention), “随机对照” (randomised controlled), “系统评价” (systematic review) and “Meta分析” (meta-analysis). We will also include terms specific to traditional Chinese medicine interventions such as “穴位” (acupoint), “耳穴” (auricular acupoint), “针灸” (acupuncture) and “中药” (Chinese herbal medicine).

The detailed search strategies for PubMed and CNKI are provided in onlinesupplemental appendices 1 2, respectively. Search strategies for other databases will be adapted as appropriate. We will also manually check the reference lists of included systematic reviews to identify any additional relevant studies. The search will be updated prior to manuscript submission to ensure currency of the evidence.

Study selection process

All citations retrieved from the database searches will be imported into EndNote reference management software and duplicates will be removed. The selection process will proceed in two stages.

In the first stage, two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Studies that clearly do not meet the criteria will be excluded. Studies that appear potentially relevant or where there is uncertainty will be retained for full-text review.

In the second stage, the same two reviewers will independently assess the full texts of potentially relevant studies. Disagreements at either stage will be resolved through discussion. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted to make the final decision. We will record the reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage.

Approach to characterising the evidence

Consistent with the methodological guidance for scoping reviews, we will not conduct formal quality assessment or meta-analysis. The primary purpose of a scoping review is to map the breadth of evidence rather than to critically appraise its quality or synthesise effect sizes. Formal quality assessment tools such as AMSTAR-2, Cochrane Risk of Bias or Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) are designed for systematic reviews that aim to answer specific clinical questions and make recommendations, which is beyond the scope of this review.

Instead, we will characterise the evidence base descriptively by reporting:

The volume of research: number of studies identified for each factor or intervention type.

The volume of research: number of studies identified for each factor or intervention type.

The nature of research: predominant study designs (RCTs vs systematic reviews/meta-analyses), typical sample sizes and geographical distribution.

The nature of research: predominant study designs (RCTs vs systematic reviews/meta-analyses), typical sample sizes and geographical distribution.

The consistency of findings: whether studies generally report similar directions of effect.

The consistency of findings: whether studies generally report similar directions of effect.

Research gaps: areas where few or no studies have been conducted.

Research gaps: areas where few or no studies have been conducted.

This approach will provide readers with a clear picture of where the evidence is concentrated, where it is sparse, and what types of studies have been conducted, without making judgements about quality that would require the more rigorous methods of a systematic review. However, by restricting our inclusion to systematic reviews and RCTs, which occupy the higher levels of the evidence hierarchy, we ensure a baseline level of methodological rigour in the studies we examine.

Data synthesis and presentation

Given the expected heterogeneity of included studies in terms of study designs, populations, factors examined and outcome measures, we will use a narrative synthesis approach. The findings will be organised according to our proposed BPSE framework, which comprises four interconnected dimensions:

Biological dimension: This includes genetic and physiological factors, endocrine regulation (particularly the roles of prolactin and oxytocin), anatomical factors, mode of delivery, maternal health conditions (such as obesity and diabetes) and pharmacological interventions affecting lactation physiology.

Discussion

Rationale for methodological choices

We have made several methodological choices that warrant explanation. First, we chose to conduct a scoping review rather than a systematic review because our primary aim is to map the breadth of evidence on this broad topic rather than to answer a narrow clinical question. The scoping review methodology is particularly appropriate when the literature is diverse and spans multiple disciplines, as is the case for lactation.

Second, we chose not to impose sample size restrictions on included RCTs. While large sample sizes generally provide more precise estimates, many important areas of lactation research (particularly psychological interventions and traditional Chinese medicine) have been studied primarily in smaller trials. Excluding these studies would create a biased picture of the evidence landscape and would be inconsistent with the comprehensive mapping aim of a scoping review.

Third, we chose not to conduct formal quality assessment or evidence grading. This decision is consistent with the JBI methodological guidance and reflects the different purposes of scoping reviews and systematic reviews.16 Quality assessment is resource-intensive and is most valuable when the goal is to make clinical recommendations based on the best available evidence. For a scoping review aimed at mapping the evidence landscape, a descriptive characterisation of study types and designs is more appropriate and provides the necessary context for interpreting the findings.

Fourth, for the identification of risk factors, we chose to rely primarily on existing systematic reviews rather than primary observational studies. This approach ensures that we are capturing synthesised, high-quality evidence while keeping the review manageable in scope. We recognise that this may result in missing very recent or novel factors that have not yet been systematically reviewed, and we will note this as a limitation.

Anticipated limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in this protocol. As a scoping review aimed at mapping the breadth of evidence, we will not conduct meta-analyses or formally appraise study quality. Readers seeking clinical recommendations should consult the relevant systematic reviews we identify. Second, while including English and Chinese literature covers a large portion of the field, we may miss relevant studies published in other languages. Third, focusing on synthesised evidence (systematic reviews and meta-analyses) for risk factors means we might not capture novel factors that have only been examined in recent primary observational studies. Finally, restricting our search to the 2020–2025 period excludes older primary studies, although we expect most historical evidence will be captured within the included systematic reviews.

Timeline

The review is planned to be conducted over a 6-month period commencing on protocol registration. The proposed schedule is as follows:

Month 1: Finalisation of search strings, protocol registration with OSF and calibration of eligibility criteria among reviewers.

Month 2: Systematic search of electronic databases; export of citations to reference management software and removal of duplicates.

Month 3: Title and abstract screening; retrieval of full texts for potentially eligible studies.

Month 4: Full-text screening against inclusion and exclusion criteria; commencement of data extraction using the standardised form.

Month 5: Completion of data extraction; data synthesis and evidence mapping within the BPSE framework.

Month 6: Drafting of the manuscript, internal review and final revision for submission.
