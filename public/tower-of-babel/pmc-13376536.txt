A laccase–mediator system based on caffeic acid for the synthesis of benzimidazoles
Authors: Mansour Shahedi, Dina Darabian, Zohreh Habibi, Zhohreh Habibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, a laccase–mediator system utilizing caffeic acid as a mediator was developed for the green synthesis of benzimidazole derivatives. The reactions were carried out under mild conditions at room temperature, using citrate buffer as the reaction medium and aerial oxygen as the oxidant. Caffeic acid effectively mediated electron transfer between the enzyme and substrates, significantly enhancing reaction yields. Different benzimidazole derivatives were synthesized with good to excellent yields (54–93%) without the use of toxic chemicals. The results highlight the efficiency, environmental compatibility, and synthetic potential of this laccase–mediator system approach in organic synthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53113-2.Keywords: Biocatalysis, Laccase, Laccase–mediator system, Caffeic acid, BenzimidazoleSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Laccase, as one of the key oxidase enzymes with copper-containing active sites, is capable of oxidizing a range of organic compounds under mild conditions1–4. However, the range of compounds that can be directly oxidized by laccase is relatively limited due to constraints in its redox potential5. To overcome this limitation, the laccase–mediator system (LMS) was introduced6,7. In this system, a low-molecular-weight organic compound is first oxidized by laccase, and the resulting radical species is then transferred to the substrate8(Fig. 1a). Several examples of such mediators are shown in Fig. 1b. These systems are particularly valuable in the selective oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes or ketones, which is a crucial transformation in the synthesis of fine chemicals, pharmaceuticals9, and agrochemicals10,11. Additionally, LMS is employed in asymmetric synthesis, allowing the production of optically active alcohols and amines through sequential multi-enzymatic processes12. It also plays a significant role in the formation of C–N and C–C bonds through chemoenzymatic processes13,14, aromatization reactions15,16, and the oxidative coupling of phenolic compounds17, expanding its use in the development of bioactive molecules and natural product derivatives. Due to the use of molecular oxygen as a green oxidant and water as the only by-product, LMS aligns well with the principles of green chemistry and is increasingly used in both academic research and industrial applications4,18. The use of aerial oxygen as a green oxidant has been explored in various catalytic systems, emphasizing its role in sustainable chemical transformations19–21.

Laccase-mediator systems (a) and common mediators (b).

Benzimidazoles are heterocyclic aromatic compounds formed by the fusion of a benzene ring with an imidazole ring, specifically at the 4 and 5 positions22. These molecules are notable for their versatility, especially in medicinal chemistry, where their derivatives exhibit a wide range of therapeutic activities, including antibacterial23,24, antiviral25, antifungal26, anticancer27, and antiparasitic28 effects. Several benzimidazole-based compounds have been developed into commercial pharmaceuticals29, and their utility extends beyond medicine into areas such as material science30,31, and dye manufacturing32,33. Given their broad spectrum of applications, benzimidazole derivatives have drawn considerable interest from researchers.

A wide range of strategies have been developed for the synthesis of benzimidazole derivatives, with the most established method being the condensation of o-phenylenediamine with aldehydes34,35. This approach remains popular due to its simplicity, broad applicability, and generally high yields, often carried out in the presence of acids36 or oxidants37. In recent years, nanomaterial-based catalysts such as zinc oxide38, cobalt composites39, and nickel40 or copper41-supported systems have gained attention for offering benefits like high efficiency, solvent-free conditions, and catalyst recyclability. However, these nanocatalysts often require complex preparation, may involve costly materials, and sometimes pose environmental or health risks due to nanoparticle toxicity. Metal-catalyzed systems using zirconium42, or palladium43 compounds can deliver diverse benzimidazole structures under mild conditions, but their use may be limited by the high cost of metals, difficulty in catalyst separation, and potential metal contamination in final products. Acid catalysis, particularly using sulfonic acid-functionalized materials44 or solid acids45, improves reaction rates and selectivity, yet strong acids can lead to corrosion issues, hazardous waste, and lower substrate compatibility. Green and sustainable approaches such as those using plant-based catalysts offer eco-friendly alternatives with mild conditions and minimal byproducts46. Nonetheless, these methods may suffer from longer reaction times, limited substrate scope, lower yields in some cases, or inconsistent catalytic performance due to the variability in natural extracts. Despite these challenges, ongoing research continues to optimize these methods for more practical, scalable, and sustainable benzimidazole synthesis35,47.

Laccases have been investigated for the synthesis of benzimidazoles in previous studies. Leutbecher et al. (2011) employed laccase from Agaricus bisporus in the absence of a mediator, leading to the formation of 2-benzimidazoles, alongside 1,2-disubstituted benzimidazoles as byproducts48(Fig. 2). In a more detailed study, Maphupha et al. (2018) examined the reaction using three different laccases: Novoprime Base 268, Suberase®, and Denilite® II Base. Their findings revealed that Novoprime Base 268 was the most effective in promoting the formation of 2-benzimidazoles, while minimizing the production of 1,2-disubstituted benzimidazoles49. However, in some derivatives, 1,2-disubstituted benzimidazoles were found to be the predominant product. Additionally, the use of co-solvents such as acetonitrile, although sometimes necessary, poses safety concerns due to its toxicity.

Formation of 2-benzimidazoles and 1,2-disubstituted benzimidazoles in the presence of laccases.

Caffeic acid is one of the most important naturally occurring phenolic compounds, widely found in organic plants50. This compound is present in many fruits (such as apples, grapes, and pears), vegetables (such as cabbage and celery), and herbal beverages (including coffee and tea)51. Furthermore, its presence in seeds and medicinal plants plays a key role in the antioxidant and defensive activities of plants, making it one of the most important secondary metabolites in the plant kingdom52.Structurally, caffeic acid is a derivative of hydroxycinnamic acid that consists of an aromatic ring bearing two hydroxyl groups in the ortho positions (3 and 4) and a propenoic acid side chain at the para position (Fig. 3)53. This structural arrangement gives caffeic acid strong antioxidant properties, as the hydroxyl groups in these positions enable radical coupling and electron transfer reactions. The conjugated bond between the carboxyl group and the aromatic ring also plays a critical role in the stability and biological activity of the molecule54.

The structure of caffeic acid.

Numerous studies have explored the use of caffeic acid as a mediator in laccase–mediator systems, with the majority focusing on food processing or polymerization reactions55–57. However, a few investigations have examined its role in the synthesis of organic compounds. One such study, conducted in 2013 by Künst and co-workers, investigated the oxidation of NADH using a laccase–mediator system58. Among the 18 mediators screened, acetosyringone, syringaldehyde, and caffeic acid demonstrated superior activity and stability under the conditions tested. This study aims to explore two key aspects: first, the potential use of caffeic acid as an intermediate in the synthesis of organic compounds, particularly benzoimidazoles (Fig. 4); and second, whether its use as a mediator can prevent the formation of 1,2-dibenzyl as an unwanted by-product.

Result and discussion

A model reaction was carried out by combining o-phenylenediamine (1, 0.1 mmol) and benzaldehyde (2a, 0.1 mmol) in a citrate buffer solution 0.01 M pH 4.5 mixed with acetonitrile as a co-solvent, at 25 °C. The reaction was catalyzed by laccase (1 mL, 1000 U), in the presence of caffeic acid (0.006 g, 0.035 mmol) as a mediator. The reaction progress was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC), confirming complete consumption of the starting materials within 1 h. After purification, the desired product 3a was isolated in 63% yield (Table 1, Entry 1). Interestingly, there was no formation of 1,2-disubstituted benzimidazole as a byproduct, which had been encountered in previous studies48,49. When acetonitrile was replaced with ethyl acetate as the co-solvent, no product formation was observed (Table 1, Entry 2). Interestingly, eliminating the co-solvent led to a substantial improvement in yield, affording product 3a in 91% yield (Table 1, Entry 3). To assess the role of the mediator, the amount of caffeic acid was halved to 0.003 g (0.018 mmol), which resulted in a reduced yield of 63% (Table 1, Entry 4). Omitting caffeic acid entirely caused a further decrease in yield to 45% (Table 1, Entry 5), underscoring the critical role of the mediator in facilitating efficient product formation. The solvent system was further evaluated by replacing citrate buffer with phosphate buffer 0.01 M pH 7.2. In the absence of co-solvent, this change led to a significant drop in yield to 21% (Table 1, Entry 6). This reduced efficiency in phosphate buffer, relative to citrate buffer 0.01 M pH 4.5, can be attributed to the pH preference of fungal laccases, which typically exhibit optimal activity under acidic conditions, generally within the pH range of 3.0–5.559. When acetonitrile was reintroduced as a co-solvent, the yield further declined to 18% (Table 1, Entry 7), and no product was obtained with phosphate buffer and ethyl acetate (Table 1, Entry 8). Lastly, conducting the reaction without the laccase enzyme resulted in no product formation (Table 1, Entry 9), confirming the indispensable catalytic role of laccase in this transformation.

Following the optimization of reaction conditions, a series of substituted benzaldehydes were examined to evaluate the substrate scope (Table 2). Electron-donating groups such as 4-OMe (3f, 61%), 2,4-OMe (3j, 54%) and 2,5-OMe (3k, 60%) increase the electron density on the aromatic ring, which in turn diminishes the electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon. This reduction in electrophilic character slows the nucleophilic attack by the amine group of o-phenylenediamine, resulting in a slower formation of the reaction intermediate and consequently a lower overall yield. In contrast, electron-withdrawing groups including 4-Cl (3 h, 93% yield), 3-Br (3d, 84% yield), and 3-NO2 (3c, 90% yield) decrease the electron density on both the aromatic ring and the carbonyl group. This enhances the electrophilic nature of the carbonyl, thereby accelerating the nucleophilic attack by the amine and leading to significantly improved reaction yields. Alkyl substituents, such as methyl (3b, 79%) and isopropyl (3 g, 84%), act as weak electron-donating groups through inductive effects. Their influence on the reactivity is minimal, and they do not substantially hinder the progress or efficiency of the reaction.

Based on previous studies involving the oxidation of caffeic acid to its corresponding radical species60 and the oxidation of other phenolic derivatives by laccase61, a plausible mechanism is proposed (Fig. 5). Initially, an o-phenylenediamine reacts with the corresponding aldehyde to form intermediate 4a. The orthoquinone species that produced by laccase, abstracts a single electron from intermediate 4a, leading to the formation of radical cation 5a and intermediate 6. Subsequent hydrogen atom transfer and proton loss could then lead to the formation of product 3a.

Conclusion

This study presents the development of a laccase–mediator system utilizing caffeic acid for the sustainable synthesis of benzimidazole derivatives under mild conditions. The results demonstrate the potential of this system, offering good to excellent yields (54–93%) without the need for toxic chemicals or metal catalysts, aligning well with the principles of green chemistry. The use of caffeic acid as a natural mediator enhances both the yield and selectivity of the reaction, providing a promising alternative to traditional methods that produce undesirable byproducts. However, there are several limitations to the method that should be noted. First, the substrate scope is currently limited to aromatic aldehydes, and further studies are needed to explore the reaction’s generality with heteroaromatic, aliphatic, or sterically hindered aldehydes. Additionally, the electronic nature of the aldehyde substrates significantly influences the reaction efficiency, as electron-withdrawing groups led to higher yields compared to electron-donating groups. Despite these limitations, the method offers a promising and environmentally friendly approach to benzimidazole synthesis. Future studies will focus on expanding the substrate scope, and obtaining additional mechanistic data to further improve the robustness and versatility of the system.

Experimental

General remarks

All reagents were commercially available and used without further purification. Solvents used for extraction and purification were distilled before use. Myceliophthora thermophila laccase (Novozym 51003) was a generous gift from Novozymes (part of Novonesis Group) (Copenhagen, Denmark). Reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using silica gel 60 F254. All organic synthesis products were purified by preparative thin-layer chromatography (TLC), (CAMAG ® instrument, in-house prepared 20 × 20 cm silica plates) and characterized by NMR spectroscopy. 1HNMR spectra were recorded at 300 MHz on a Bruker Avance spectrometer using DMSO-d6 as a solvent. The chemical shifts were referenced to the solvent signals at δH/C 2.49/39.50 ppm (DMSO-d6) relative to TMS.

General procedure for benzimidazoles synthesis

To prepare the benzimidazoles, 0.1 mmol of ortho-phenylenediamine (0.0110 g) was mixed with 0.1 mmol of the corresponding aldehyde in the presence of 8 mL of 0.01 M citrate buffer pH 4.5 in a 100 mL round bottom flask at 25℃. Then, 1 mL of Laccase (1000 U) and 0.035 mmol (0.0060 g) of caffeic acid were added to the mixture. The reaction progress was tracked by TLC. Upon confirming the completion of the reaction, the mixture was extracted three times with ethyl acetate using a separatory funnel. The organic phase was subsequently evaporated under reduced pressure to a minimum volume. The purified product was isolated by preparative TLC (n-hexane: ethyl acetate, 3:1) and characterized via1 H NMR spectroscopy.
