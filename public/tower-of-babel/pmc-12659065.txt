Enrichment of vitamin B12-producing Porphyrobacter in the phycosphere microbiome promotes microalgal stress adaptation to antibiotic exposure
Authors: Qilu Cheng, Junwei Ma, Yuyi Yang, Jinchuan Ma, Hans-Peter Grossart, Ligen Xu, Hui Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlanktonic microalgae deploy multifaceted responsive and adaptive strategies against anthropogenic pollutants; however, current understanding of antibiotic resistance mechanisms remains predominantly focused on intrinsic physiological adaptations. While microalgae maintain intimate relationships with the phycosphere microbiome, the ecological roles of these associated microbes in mediating host adaptation to polluted environments are inadequately characterized.ResultsWe identified a phycosphere microbiome-involved antibiotic resistance mechanism in Dictyosphaerium sp., a pollution-tolerant Chlorophyta microalgae exhibiting remarkable enrofloxacin (ENR) tolerance. Microalgal growth displayed initial inhibition followed by significant promotion under 5 mg/L ENR exposure. This resilience was associated with the restructuring of phycosphere microbiome, characterized by Porphyrobacter enrichment and functional enhancement of algal fitness-promoting pathways, including upregulation of cobalamin biosynthesis genes (log2FC = 7.76) and a 33.3-fold increase in extracellular B12 accumulation. Consequently, we isolated the ENR-selected microbial taxa to elucidate their roles in microalgal stress adaptation. Co-culturing axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. with Porphyrobacter enhanced microalgal growth by 36.5% after 8-day ENR exposure, whereas non-dominant bacteria exhibited negligible effects. Based on the transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of the algal system when Porphyrobacter was dominant, we subsequently compared the growth of axenic microalgae with and without B vitamin (B1, B6, B7, B12) supplementation. Experimental validation demonstrated the pivotal role of B12-producing Porphyrobacter in enhancing microalgal ENR adaptation through (i) stimulating extracellular polymeric substances production and subsequently enhancing ENR removal via EPS-mediated adsorption and (ii) alleviating intracellular oxidative stress via elevating superoxide dismutase and peroxidase activities and reducing malondialdehyde levels. Additionally, this B12-producing bacteria/B12-mediated adaptability exhibited cross-species conservation, improving ENR resistance in Chlorella vulgaris and Scenedesmus quadricauda, with analogous protection observed under ciprofloxacin and norfloxacin exposures.ConclusionCollectively, our findings establish stress-induced enrichment of B12-producing Porphyrobacter within the phycosphere microbiome as a pivotal mechanism underlying microalgal antibiotic adaptation. This insight facilitates the rational development of microalgae-microbiome systems for enhanced wastewater treatment and sustainable bioproduction, with applications in aquatic feed supplementation, biofuel production, and biofertilizer development. Background

Global aquatic ecosystems confront escalating threats from emerging pollutants driven by extreme weather events, urbanization, and anthropogenic activities [1]. These contaminants include pharmaceutical residues (e.g., amoxicillin decreasing the population growth rate of macroinvertebrates) [2], perfluorinated compounds (e.g., perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids altering planktonic microbial communities) [3], and microplastics (e.g., polyvinyl chloride facilitating pathogenic bacterial proliferation and antibiotic resistance dissemination in aquatic biota) [4]. Synthetic antibiotics, extensively employed in medical and agricultural sectors, represent particularly critical environmental hazards. They enter aquatic systems via multiple routes, including metabolic excretion, inefficient waste management, and industrial effluents [5]. Surface water concentrations have ranged from several ng/L to μg/L levels over the past two decades, with concentrations as high as 50 mg/L reported in specific domestic and pharmaceutical wastewater effluents [5, 6]. This enduring global crisis chronically impairs both target and non-target organisms while potentiating the evolution of antimicrobial resistance [7].

Planktonic microalgae, as ancient photosynthetic organisms on Earth, contribute nearly 50% of global oxygen production while fulfilling pivotal ecological and biogeochemical functions [8, 9]. Their structural simplicity and ubiquitous distribution confer particular vulnerability to pollutants, including antibiotics. Although not direct antibiotic targets, microalgae experience profound effects through (i) evolutionary homology of organelle structures (mitochondria and chloroplasts) sharing bacterial origins via endosymbiosis [10], (ii) bioaccumulation-induced oxidative stress cascades [11], and (iii) disruption of microbial symbiosis critical for nutrient acquisition [12]. Current research documents antibiotic-induced microalgal perturbations spanning population, morphology, photosynthetic pigment profiles, and metabolic processes [11, 13, 14]. In response, microalgae have developed multilayered defense systems. Notable extracellular strategies involve structural modification of cell walls coupled with enhanced biosynthesis of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS, macromolecules containing amino, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups). These substances immobilize contaminants through adsorption/accumulation mechanisms, effectively mitigating intracellular pollutant penetration and enhancing microalgal stress adaptation [13, 15]. Intracellularly, microalgae activate coordinated antioxidant defenses through upregulation of enzymes (e.g., superoxide dismutase, catalase, and cytochromes P450) and non-enzymatic substances (e.g., glutathione, proline, and carotenoid) to neutralize reactive oxygen species and maintain redox homeostasis [14]. Furthermore, enzymatic biodegradation pathways enable metabolic detoxification, as demonstrated in Chlorella vulgaris, which employs dealkylation, decarboxylation, and dehalogenation processes to transform fluoroquinolone antibiotics into less toxic derivatives while providing carbon substrates for microalgal growth [16]. However, current investigations often focus solely on microalgal individual-level responses, largely neglecting the microalgae-associated microbiome’s essential contributions to microalgal survival in contaminated environments.

The phycosphere, a microalgal niche analogous to the plant rhizosphere, serves as a hotspot for intimate microalgae-microbe interactions [17]. The phycosphere microbiome operates as a functional extension of the algal genome, establishing complex relationships with its host spanning mutualistic cooperation to competitive antagonism [18], with probiotic interactions often prevailing based on species-specific associations and metabolic reciprocity between bacteria and microalgae [19, 20]. Thereby, like terrestrial plant-microbe systems, microalgal productivity and stability are fundamentally modulated by these microbial communities [17, 21, 22]. Emerging evidence highlights the ecological role of the phycosphere microbiome in enhancing microalgal fitness under pollution. Experimental data confirm associated bacteria-mediated attenuation of pollutant toxicity, particularly against metal-pesticide complexes [23], with microalgae-microbiome synergy frequently observed in contaminated waters [24]. Furthermore, phycosphere bacteria exhibiting aromatic hydrocarbon stress-alleviating capabilities on microalgae have been successfully characterized [25]. Despite these advances, the mechanisms underpinning phycosphere microbiome-enabled microalgal antibiotic resistance constitute a critical knowledge gap, necessitating targeted and systematic investigation.

The intricate interplay between microalgae and their phycosphere microbiome underscores the significance of this relationship in aquatic ecosystems. Although host genetics regulate microbiome assembly [26, 27], environmental factors also predominantly shape its structure and function [12, 28]. For instance, microalgae originating from the same non-axenic cultures, yet subjected to distinct geographical locations and cultivation regimes, develop divergent bacterial taxa abundance [29]. Antibiotic exposure induces phycosphere microbiome restructuring, as evidenced in Chlorella vulgaris, demonstrating reduced bacterial diversity coupled with stress-adapted taxa enrichment [12]. However, the links between these phycosphere microbiome shifts and microalgal adaptation under antibiotic exposure remain poorly understood. Enrofloxacin (ENR), a representative fluoroquinolone antibiotic, exhibits pervasive aquatic contamination across surface waters, groundwater, and aquaculture effluents due to veterinary overuse. Its environmental persistence, characterized by context-dependent half-lives ranging from 1155 to 3466 days [30], poses significant ecotoxicological risks to various aquatic organisms, including microalgae, even at trace concentrations [13, 31], while concurrently promoting the transfer of multiple-antibiotic resistance genes and the emergence of novel resistant bacteria in aquatic ecosystems [32]. Our prior work identified exceptional ENR tolerance in Dictyosphaerium sp., a pollution-resistant Chlorophyta microalgae. Drawing parallels to beneficial rhizobacteria-mediated adaptation in terrestrial plants [33–35], we hypothesize that stress-induced phycosphere microbiome remodeling contributes to microalgal adaptation to antibiotics.

This study elucidates ENR-driven phycosphere microbiome succession and its functional linkage to microalgal stress adaptation. The community-level microbial dynamics and associated shifts in functional gene expression supporting microalgal fitness were tracked. Subsequently, representative bacterial isolates were selected and co-cultured with axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. By comparing the growth patterns of axenic microalgae cocultured with and without bacteria under ENR exposure, we first tested whether and how the specifically enriched phycosphere bacteria mediate microalgal resistance to ENR. Thereafter, the functional substances, notably vitamin B12, provided by core-associated bacteria (e.g., Porphyrobacter) and its underlying mechanisms in enhancing microalgal stress adaptation were revealed. The ENR-induced symbiotic association between microalgae and vitamin B12-producing Porphyrobacter exemplifies pollution-mediated phycosphere microbiome engineering that bolsters host resilience via targeted metabolic support. These findings provide insights into pollutant-driven microalgal-bacterial coevolution while establishing a synthetic biology framework for designing robust microalgae-bacteria consortia capable of simultaneous microalgal biomass production and antibiotic bioremediation in polluted aquatic systems.

Methods

Microalgal culture and antibiotic treatments

A controlled cultivation experiment was conducted to assess the responses of Dictyosphaerium sp. to ENR exposure. The test microalgae, originally isolated from agricultural wastewater in Zhejiang University, was pre-cultured in BG11 medium to logarithmic phase. Free-living bacteria were eliminated from microalgal cultures according to a method described by Xue et al. [12], involving sequential filtration through a 3-μm pore-size polycarbonate membrane, which is a validated methodology for retaining microalgae and their attached microbes [12, 36, 37]. Microscopic analysis confirmed complete retention of Dictyosphaerium sp. (cell diameter ≈ 10 μm) with no observable algal cells in the filtrate. Due to their small cell size and loose attachment to microalgae, free-living bacteria were easily excluded through this filtration regime. Then, retained biomass was rinsed with sterile BG11 medium, followed by resuspension in axenic medium. After 48-h incubation, the cultures underwent filtration-rinsing cycles to ensure complete removal of free-living bacteria, as confirmed by the absence of bacteria in the filtrate under a microscope. The final filter was suspended in sterile medium, creating an inoculum that contained microalgae and their tightly associated microbes, excluding free-living bacteria.

Microalgae were cultivated in 100-mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of sterile BG11 medium (autoclaved at 121℃, 103 kPa, 15 min). Initial cell density was standardized to OD680 = 0.06 using a Thermo Scientific Evolution 220 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (1-cm cuvette path length), following OECD 201 guidelines for algal growth inhibition assays (https://www.oecd.org). Three ENR (Sigma-Aldrich) concentration gradients were established: 0.05 mg/L, reflecting an ambient aquatic level (4.8–54.0 μg/L) [38]; 0.5 mg/L, simulating a typical wastewater concentration (210–900 μg/L) [6, 39]; and 5 mg/L, representing acute stress concentration for microalgae (approaching maximum wastewater concentration of 5.718 mg/L) [13, 16, 40]. Triplicate cultures per treatment were maintained in a condition-controlled incubator (25°C) under 60 µm photons m−2s−1 illumination with a 14-h photoperiod.

Microalgal growth was determined using an Nexcelom Bioscience mini-cell counter. The detailed protocols and equations for calculating specific growth rate and inhibition rate were referred to Cheng et al. [41]. Photosynthetic pigments were extracted from centrifugally harvested algal pellets (5500 rpm, 10 min) via 95% ethanol thermal extraction (water bath, 80 °C, 2 min), followed by spectrophotometric quantification of chlorophyll a/b and carotenoids [42]. For ultrastructural analysis, algal cells washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) were immediately fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (4°C, 12 h). Post-fixation was conducted with 1% OsO4 (1–2 h), followed by graded ethanol dehydration (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100%). After critical point drying, samples were coated with gold-palladium using a Gold Sprayer (MC1000, Hitachi) and then observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU-8010, Hitachi).

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

Phycosphere bacterial communities were characterized through 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V5–V7 regions) on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. Microalgae-microbe complexes sampled at designed time points were filtered through 0.22-μm membranes, with genomic DNA extracted using the FastDNA Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA) and amplified via 799F/1193R primers. Sequencing yielded 61,692 reads per sample (mean), and the raw sequence data were processed through FASTQ-based quality filtering and FLASH-mediated read merging prior to UPARSE (v7.0.1090) clustering into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. Bioinformatic analysis is detailed in Text S1.

RNA sequencing and metabolomics analysis

ENR-induced changes in gene expression patterns accompanying the shifts of phycosphere microbiome were analyzed via transcriptome sequencing across ENR-treated groups (0, 0.05, and 5 mg/L) using Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol RNA Extract Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. High-quality RNA samples (OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, RIN ≥ 6.5, 28S:18S ≥ 1.0) were verified by Agilent 5300 Bioanalyser and NanoDrop ND 2000. mRNA was enriched using the polyA selection method with oligo (dT) beads, following the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation (San Diego, CA). Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript Double-Stranded cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen, CA) with random hexamer primers. The sequencing library was performed using the NovaSeq Reagent Kit. The average sequencing depth was 7.89 Gb (52.2 million reads), and the raw data was trimmed and quality controlled using FASTQ (version 0.23.4) with default parameters. Trinity (v2.8.5)-assembled transcripts were mapped to reference genomes using Bowtie 2 (v2.5.4). Gene expression levels were calculated using RSEM (v1.3.1) and normalized using fragments per kilobase per million fragments, followed by DEseq 2(v 1.42.0) differential expression analysis (|log2 (fold change) > 1|, Padj < 0.05, FDR ≤ 0.001) and functionally annotated via GO and KEGG databases.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed to identify variations in metabolite profiles in algal cultures exposed to ENR. Briefly, 2-mL culture supernatants (n = 6/group) were filtered (0.22 µm) after centrifugation (4°C, 15 min). Metabolites in the filtrate were extracted ultrasonically using a methanol:acetonitrile: water solution (v:v:v = 2:2:1) for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was then subjected to vacuum drying, followed by re-dissolution in 50% acetonitrile and subsequently analyzed by UHPLC-QTOF-MS (1290 Infinity LC, Agilent). The UHPLC system was equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phases consisted of (A) 25-mM ammonium acetate with 0.5% formic acid and (B) methanol. The mass spectrometry signals were collected using an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer in dual electrospray ionization modes, with significantly different metabolites (Student’s t test, P < 0.05, VIP > 1) via KEGG databases. Detailed detection and analysis are described in Text S2.

Isolation of phycosphere bacteria, establishment of axenic Dictyosphaerium sp., and co-culture experiment

To investigate the role of phycosphere microbiome in microalgal ENR resistance, bacterial isolates were obtained from 8-day ENR-exposed cultures (5 mg/L) through cultivation and identified via Sanger sequencing using the ABI 3730xI DNA Analyzer (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai) (more information is given in Text S3). Universal primers 27 F (AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG)/1492R (TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT) were utilized for 16S rDNA gene amplification. The sequencing data were then blasted against the NCBI nucleotide collection database. A 97% sequence similarity threshold was used for assigning bacterial isolates to a specific taxonomic level. Antibiotics including ampicillin (100 mg/L), kanamycin (100 mg/L), streptomycin (50 mg/L), and enrofloxacin (20 mg/L) were applied to generate axenic microalgae according to a previous report with minor modifications [43]. After treatment for 48 h, 5-mL microalgal samples were subcultured in 50-mL sterile antibiotic-free BG11 medium until reaching exponential growth (12 days). These antibiotic-treatment and sterile-cultivation cycles were repeated three times. Axenic microalgae were validated through culture-based sterility assays and scanning electron microscope-confirmed absence of bacteria (more details are provided in Text S4; Fig. S1). Seven bacterial isolates and two commercial Porphyrobacter strains (P. sanguineus and P. neustonensis) were tested for ENR resistance and degradation capacity (Text S5) prior to algae-bacteria co-cultivation experiments.

ENR-tolerant bacteria were co-cultured with axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. to elucidate their roles in enhancing microalgal ENR adaptation. Axenic microalgae (OD680 = 0.06) were inoculated into 50 mL of sterile BG11 medium. Bacterial inoculum (OD600 = 1, corresponding to (1.3 ~ 2.2) × 108 cfu/mL) underwent two sterile BG11 wash cycles was added to axenic microalgae cultures at a 1% (v/v) inoculum size, establishing algae:bacteria ratios of 1:4 ~ 1:10 consistent with established mutualism models [44, 45]. Experimental groups included ENR-amended (5 mg/L) and ENR-free co-cultures, with axenic algal cultures serving as controls. Each treatment has 12 replicates, and cultivation lasted 8 days followed by conditions as described in Sect. 2.1.

Quantification of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), intracellular oxidative stress biomarkers, ENR, and vitamins B12

Triplicate samples (30 mL each) were randomly selected from each treatment group (n = 12) at 2-day intervals during the 8-day co-cultivation period. EPS was extracted by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 4 °C, 15 min) according to a previous report [46]. The obtained EPS solution was filtered through 0.45 μm filters and quantified via a MULTIN/C®3100 total organic carbon analyzer (Analytik Jena, Germany). The algal pellets were flash-frozen in liquid N2 (5 s) and processed through triple freeze-thaw cycles (− 20 °C, 30 s). The supernatant collected by centrifugation was subjected to quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and peroxidase (POD), following the protocols of assay kits (Jian Cheng Bioengineering, Nanjing).

ENR was extracted and quantified according to the method of Chen et al. [47] with some modifications. Specifically, a 2-mL sample was centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min) to separate the supernatant, which was used for medium-phase ENR quantification. Cell surface-adsorbed ENR was extracted from algal pellets via ultrapure water (2 mL) rinse and centrifugation. ENR was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (Alliance 2695, Waters). The detection system was equipped with an Eclipse C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Agilent Technologies) and a UV detector. The mobile phases consisted of acetonitrile and 0.1% o-phosphoric acid (pH 3.0, triethylamine-adjusted) at a ratio of 85:15 (v/v). ENR was detected at a wavelength of 271 nm, with a 20-µL injection volume. ENR removal rates were calculated via initial/final (specific time) concentration differentials.

Bacterial-derived vitamin B12 was quantified via targeted metabolomics using Multiple Reactions Monitoring (MRM), with a mass spectrometer configured to monitor the specific precursor-to-product ion transition at m/z 678.5 → 147.1. Retention time and peak area were extracted using Multiquant 3.0.2 software. Vitamin B12 was quantified using the calibration curve (Y = 0.08863X—6.64660, r = 0.99869), with detection and quantification limits of 0.1 and 0.5 ng/mL, respectively. Experimental protocols are detailed in Text S6.

Verification of B vitamins’ effects on axenic Dictyosphaerium sp.’s growth, EPS production, and ENR removal

Based on transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, B-vitamin supplementation experiments (B1/B6/B7/B12) were conducted in axenic microalgae under 5 mg/L ENR exposure. Considering the concurrent elevation in B12 level alongside Porphyrobacter enrichment and its environmentally relevant concentrations in aquatic ecosystems (0.03 ~ 200 µg/L, with typical levels below 1 µg/L [48]), experimental concentrations of B vitamins (0.05, 0.1, 10, 100 µg/L) were strategically evaluated. Although B1, B6, and B7 showed negligible growth modulation across tested concentrations, B12 enhanced algal growth at 0.1–10 µg/L but inhibited it at 100 µg/L in our preliminary experiments. Crucially, 0.1 µg/L B12 elicited microalgal growth responses comparable to those observed in Porphyrobacter-algae associations; thus, this ecologically relevant concentration was selected for subsequent B vitamin supplementation experiments to explore the role of stress-select bacteria in microalgal adaptation. This 8-day experiment assessed algal density, EPS production, oxidative stress biomarker levels, and ENR dynamics.

The experimental framework was extended to phylogenetically diverse phytoplankton species (Chlorophyta: Chlorella vulgaris, Scenedesmus quadricauda; Cyanophyta: Anabaena azotica, Microcystis aeruginosa) following Dictyosphaerium sp. investigations. All test microalgae maintained well growth in B12-free BG11 medium. The effects of B12-producing bacteria (1%, v/v) and B12 (0.1 μg/L) on microalgal ENR (5 mg/L) adaptation were evaluated. Additionally, 5 and 10 mg/L ciprofloxacin (CIP) and norfloxacin (NOF) were employed to assess bacterial mediation of stress on Dictyosphaerium sp. under exposure to other fluoroquinolones. All cultivations were under the parameters replicated ENR exposure conditions: 50 mL sterile BG11 in 100-mL flasks, initial OD680 of 0.06, standardized photobioreactor settings.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were presented as means ± standard deviation (n ≥ 3) with graphical representations generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad, USA). Statistical analyses employed one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc tests. Statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were indicated with different letters.

Results

Temporal response of Dictyosphaerium sp.’s growth under ENR exposure

Dictyosphaerium sp. exhibited dose- and time-dependent growth responses to ENR exposure across an 8-day experiment (Fig. 1). Specifically, 0.05 and 0.5 mg/L ENR enhanced specific growth rates (d−1) by 1.04–1.80 and 1.02–3.52-fold, respectively, compared to controls (CK) (Fig. 1a), consistent with the low-dose antibiotic-induced hormesis effect known to stimulate metabolic activity [49]. Conversely, 5 mg/L ENR exposure initially suppressed growth (9.4% and 6.9% reductions at days 2 and 4) before subsequent recovery, ultimately achieving 68.3% biomass increases versus CK by day 8, which surpassed lower ENR concentration effects (18.5% and 7.6 increases for ENR-0.05 and ENR-0.5) (Fig. 1a,b). This response pattern was mirrored in the content dynamics of photosynthetic pigments across different ENR treatments (Fig. 1c,d), with chlorophyll a/b and carotenoid contents consistently increased in the ENR-0.05 and ENR-0.5, but showing an initial decline, then an increase in the 5 mg/L ENR treatment within the 8-day incubation. These results, combined with the phenotypic observations of algal growth (Fig. 1e,f), demonstrated the exceptional tolerance and resilience of Dictyosphaerium sp. to ENR stress. This can be attributed to the initial oxidative damage-induced growth inhibition, followed by the subsequent restoration of stress adaptability mediated by the phycosphere microbiome, which was particularly elucidated in our study.

ENR selects for specific bacterial taxa in the phycosphere microbiome of Dictyosphaerium sp.

To investigate the role of the associated microbiome in microalgal adaptation to ENR, shifts in phycosphere bacterial communities upon ENR addition were characterized. Scanning electron micrographs revealed a pronounced decrease in bacterial abundance and diversity after 8-day ENR exposure (Fig. 2a–c), consistent with changes in the alpha diversity indices of microalgae-associated bacteria (Table S1). In particular, the control group exhibited various spherical and rod-like bacteria adhering directly to the algal surface or clustering in the phycosphere of Dictyosphaerium sp. (Fig. 2a). In contrast, sparse microbial colonization was evident in ENR-treated samples, especially at higher ENR concentrations (Fig. 2b,c).

(d) Principal coordinates analysis of phycosphere bacterial communities across different culture durations and ENR concentrations based on Weighted Unifrac analysis (ANOSIM, R=0.8267, P=0.001). (e) Relative abundance of bacterial taxa (top 20) at the genus level. (f) Co-occurrence network analysis of phycosphere bacterial community in CK, L, and H treatment groups

Phycosphere microbiome restructuring under ENR exposure was characterized via 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing across treatments (0 mg/L, CK; 0.05 mg/L, L; 5 mg/L, H) on days 4 and 8. Consistent with microscopical observation, α-diversity metrics confirmed decreased bacterial richness following ENR exposure (Table S1), while β-diversity divergence (PCoA, ANOSIM, R = 0.827, P = 0.001) further revealed distinct bacterial community shifts, particularly in high-ENR groups (H4 and H8, Fig. 2d). Furthermore, ENR exposure induced significant taxonomic restructuring, featured by phylum-level collapse of Cyanobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidota after 8 days of 5 mg/L ENR exposure. Conversely, Proteobacteria abundance surged from 63.2 to 99.9% in ENR-5 treatment (Fig. S2a). Genus-level analysis revealed elimination of Leptolyngbya, Pseudomonas, and Agrobacterium in high-ENR treatments (H8), while selecting stress-tolerant taxa Porphyrobacter, Sphingomonas, Brevundimonas, and Microbacterium (Fig. 2e). Co-occurrence network analysis revealed reduced network complexity in ENR-treated phycosphere microbiome (Fig. 2f; Table S2), indicating attenuated ecological connectivity and reduced functional diversity and synergy within the bacterial communities. Notably, the absolute abundances of residual ENR-tolerant genera in H8 exhibited strong algal EPS-yield covariation (pairwise F-test, R2 = 0.8347, P < 0.0001), with Porphyrobacter demonstrating absolute dominance (99.3% relative abundance) and significant algal density correlation (pairwise F-test, R2 = 0.7927, P < 0.0001) (Fig. S2b–d). These findings imply that stress-driven selection of the phycosphere microbiome could be involved in microalgal antibiotic adaptation.

ENR-mediated selection of the phycosphere microbiome facilitates functions related to microalgal fitness under stress

Transcriptomic profiling revealed differential gene expression patterns associated with ENR-driven phycosphere microbiome remodeling. Specifically, low-dose ENR treatments showed a reduced number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) over time (3110 at day 4; 265 at day 8) (Fig. S3). In contrast, high ENR (5 mg/L) induced a sustained increase in DEG number, with 8419 and 9684 in H4 and H8, respectively. Comparative analysis (Benjamini-Hochberg, FDR correction, Padj < 0.05) of DEGs between L and H treatments revealed significant upregulation of genes involved in algal-bacterial pathways that are beneficial to microalgal fitness under high ENR stress (Fig. 3a), despite the decrease in microbial abundance and diversity (Fig. 2). For example, flagellar morphogenesis genes, including those involved in basal body P-ring formation (log2FC = 8.9) and motor switch proteins (log2FC = 8.5), exhibited pronounced overexpression in H4. Additionally, genes encoding flagellar-associated proteins showed persistent upregulation in both H4 and H8 (Fig. 3b; Table S3). This coordinated expression suggests enhanced bacterial motility apparatus assembly [50], potentially facilitating physical algal shielding or nutrient-rich niche relocation under ENR stress. Concurrent upregulation of genes encoding two-component system sensor histidine kinase/response regulator (log2FC = 10.2) and secretion system components (major facilitator superfamily domain-containing protein, bacterial type II secretion system protein, and general secretion pathway protein) in H4/H8 indicates activation of stress sensing/responding pathways and extracellular compound exchange [51–53], which may contribute to maintain algal-bacterial symbiosis while mitigating ENR toxicity. High-dose ENR exposure also stimulated polysaccharide metabolism, characterized by upregulation of degrading/modifying enzyme genes in H4 and H8 (Fig. 3b; Table S4). For example, β-glucosidase genes exhibited log2FC values of 8.2 and 8.7 compared to L4 and L8, respectively, coinciding with elevated phycosphere polysaccharide levels in microalgae under ENR exposure [13]. This may increase extracellular carbon flux, fueling algal carbon assimilation and assisting in stress mitigation. Simultaneous activation of B vitamin synthesis/metabolism pathways was observed. Genes ThiGH, BioA, and CobT encoding vitamins B1, B7, and B12 showed significant elevations in H4, while H8 demonstrated upregulated genes encoding enzymes of pyridoxal 5’-phosphate synthase (vitamin B6 synthesis) and cyanocobalamin biosynthesis protein (vitamin B12 synthesis) (Fig. 3b; Table S4). This metabolic reprogramming suggested microbiome-mediated nutrient provisioning, potentially potentiate algal stress tolerance and resilience via symbiotic metabolic coupling [17, 54].

PICRUSt analysis corroborated these findings, showing enhanced bacterial functions critical for microalgal stress adaptation over an 8-day period, despite the reduction of taxonomic abundance and diversity in high-dose ENR treatment (Fig. 2e; Table S1). Functional augmentation in pathways of cell motility, signaling and secretion, polysaccharide metabolism (galactose, fructose, and mannose), and vitamins synthesis/metabolism were identified (Fig. 3c). These functional compensation through stress-driven phycosphere microbiome restructuring, particularly enrichment of stress-tolerant taxa, suggests a phycosphere-mediated adaptation mechanism underpinning microalgal antibiotic resistance.

Co-cultivation analyses suggested a supportive capacity of Porphyrobacter in the growth of Dictyosphaerium sp. under ENR exposure

Functional validation of ENR-selected phycosphere microbiota was conducted through systematic co-cultivation of axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. with bacteria isolated from 8-day ENR-exposed microalgal cultures. Seven Microbacterium strains demonstrated neutral growth effects on microalgae regardless of ENR presence (Tukey’s post hoc test, P > 0.05; Fig. 4a). Isolates of ENR-enriched Porphyrobacter (Fig. 2) may contribute to stress adaptation of Dictyosphaerium sp., as evidenced by significant algal density correlation (Fig. S2d). Though native Porphyrobacter strain isolation failed due to limitations of culture conditions, two commercially sourced strains (P. sanguineus and P. neustonensis) were employed to gain insights into the Porphyrobacter-specific functions in enhancing microalgal ENR tolerance and resilience. Given the ENR sensitivity of P. neustonensis (Fig. 4b), the ENR-resistant P. sanguineus was selected for microalgal co-cultivation. Notably, axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. demonstrated 36.5% and 15.8% density enhancement under ENR-stressed and control conditions, respectively, when co-cultured with P. sanguineus over 8 days (Fig. 4c–e). Bacterial augmentation further increased ENR-exposed algal growth by 0.9–36.5% during 8-day cultivation (Fig. 4f), while reducing ENR-induced growth inhibition duration from 4 to 2 days (Tukey’s post hoc test, P < 0.05; Fig. S4). Moreover, comparative analysis revealed significantly elevated growth inhibition rates in axenic (18.6–43.6%) versus non-axenic microalgae (6.9–9.4%, cf. Figure 1b), conclusively establishing Porphyrobacter’s indispensable role in microalgal antibiotic adaptation.

Vitamin B12 is a functional metabolite provided by Porphyrobacter in enhancing Dictyosphaerium sp.’s ENR adaptation

The above findings establish stress-selected phycosphere bacteria, particularly ENR-enriched Porphyrobacter, as pivotal mediators of microalgal stress resilience. Further targeted investigation into Porphyrobacter-derived functional metabolites was conducted. Consistent with established roles of B vitamins in beneficial algal-bacterial interactions [24], our transcriptomic analysis revealed significant activation of B vitamin (B1/B6/B7/B12) biosynthesis pathways during Porphyrobacter enrichment. Interestingly, metabolomic profiling also unveiled the dynamics of B vitamin-related metabolites under ENR exposure. Specifically, different forms of vitamin B6, including pyridoxal and pyridoxine, as well as intermediate metabolite 4-pyridoxic acid, showed reduced relative abundance. In contrast, cobalamin (vitamin B12) transiently reduced at day 4 but exhibited a 33.3-fold increase at day 8 compared to the controls (Fig. 5a). These metabolic shifts suggest bacterial-mediated B vitamin provision as a key mechanism underlying microalgal stress adaptation.

B-vitamin supplementation assays in axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. under ENR stress revealed vitamin-specific growth modulation. While B1/B6/B7 exhibited negligible growth-promoting effects on microalgae under ENR exposure or control conditions (Fig. S5), B12 supplementation (0.1 µg/L) significantly enhanced algal biomass by 11.4–24.3% in ENR-treated cultures over 8 days (Tukey’s post hoc test, P < 0.05, Fig. 5b–d). Algal growth responses to B-vitamin supplementation exhibit species-specific patterns governed by vitamin type and concentration thresholds [55, 56]. The observed B12-mediated growth enhancement likely stems from its dosage optimization and distinct stress-alleviating biochemical properties, whereas B1/B6/B7 lack comparable functionality under these experimental conditions, showing no significant growth modulation. Given B12’s exclusive prokaryotic biosynthesis and its established role as a key mediator in algae-bacteria symbiosis [21], we hypothesize that Porphyrobacter-derived vitamin B12 mediates microalgal ENR adaptation and resilience. Notably, targeted metabolomics confirmed B12 production in the culture of P. sanguineus (Fig. 5e), supporting the hypothesis that bacterial B12 provisioning facilitates Dictyosphaerium sp. adaptation to ENR stress (Fig. 5f).

Vitamin B12-producing Porphyrobacter facilitates EPS production, ENR removal, and attenuates oxidative stress in Dictyosphaerium sp.

Co-cultivation of axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. with P. sanguineus significantly enhanced EPS production under both ENR-stressed and control conditions (Fig. 6a; Tukey’s post hoc test, P < 0.05). EPS content demonstrated a positive correlation with ENR adsorption, constituting the dominant ENR dissipation pathway in algal systems [13]. Notably, co-culture with P. sanguineus increased extracellular ENR adsorption, corresponding to enhanced ENR removal efficiency (Fig. 6b, Tukey’s post hoc test, P < 0.05). The axenic Dictyosphaerium-Porphyrobacter association achieved 30.5% ENR removal by day 8, which was 4.9% higher than non-axenic Dictyosphaerium sp. systems (Fig. 6b; Fig. S6a). B12 supplementation similarly elevated microalgal EPS biosynthesis, ENR adsorption, and removal (Fig. 6c,d, Tukey’s post hoc test, P < 0.05), while B6 (non-effective in growth promotion) showed no significant impact on ENR dissipation (Fig. 6d). Monocultured Porphyrobacter also failed to enhance ENR removal (Fig. S6b). These indicated that B12-producing Porphyrobacter/B12-mediated microalgal EPS overproduction drives ENR removal via adsorption, thereby reducing membrane-ENR interaction and associated cytotoxicity, enhancing microalgal stress adaptability (Fig. 6e). Concurrently, B12-producing Porphyrobacter/B12 reduced lipid peroxidation, as evidenced by 21.4% and 24.1% lower MDA levels in ENR-exposed microalgae, while upregulating antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD: 1.30 ~ 1.52-fold; POD: 1.24 ~ 1.29-fold) (Fig. S7), directly mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing microalgal adaptation under ENR exposure.

Cross-species universality of B12-producing Porphyrobacter/B12 in enhancing microalgal adaptation to ENR

To assess the cross-species ecological significance of vitamin B12-producing Porphyrobacter in microalgal antibiotic adaptation, we investigated functional conservation on diverse microalgae beyond Dictyosphaerium sp. Coculture with P. sanguineus significantly attenuated ENR toxicity in Chlorella vulgaris and Scenedesmus quadricauda, yielding 8.3% and 11.2% density increases versus controls without bacterial inoculation after 8-day ENR exposure (Fig. S8a, P < 0.05). This growth enhancement was further supported by exogenous B12 supplementation, which showed comparable algal density increases in ENR-stressed microalgae (Fig. S8b). The conserved stress-adaptive responses spanning Chlorophyta (Dictyosphaerium sp., C. vulgaris, and S. quadricauda) demonstrate a universal stress-adaptation strategy mediated by B12-producing Porphyrobacter/B12 rather than species-specific traits.

Discussion

Stress-induced enrichment of Porphyrobacter within the phycosphere microbiome underpins microalgal antibiotic adaptation

While prior investigations have predominantly addressed intrinsic microalgal stress-response mechanisms [11, 14], this study elucidates the critical role of phycosphere microbiome in mediating microalgal antibiotic adaptation. Employing ENR-tolerant Dictyosphaerium sp. model systems, we firstly give a comprehensive insight into characterizing microbial community succession under ENR exposure. Results revealed the stochastic assembly of phycosphere community in Dictyosphaerium sp., dominated by the drift process irrespective of ENR presence (Fig. S9), aligning with the ecological lottery hypothesis wherein initial colonizers dictate community structure [57]. Serial microalgal passage only ensures the passage of bacteria attached at that time; thus, the significant effects of drift may result in the stochastic assembly of the community under ENR stress. Crucially, deterministic selection intensified with ENR levels, potentially driven by dual antibiotic-mediated elimination of sensitive taxa and host-selected enrichment of mutualistic partners. These results establish an integrative framework where environmental conditions, host selection pressures, and microbial resistance traits dynamically modulate the stochastic-deterministic equilibrium in phycosphere microbiome assembly [12, 58–60]. This stress-induced microbiome restructuring optimized niche packing, thereby modulating competitiveness among microbial taxa and facilitating ecological functions. For instance, cyanobacteria inherently compete with eukaryotic microalgae for light, oxygen, and nutrients [61]. ENR-mediated elimination of cyanobacterial competitors (e.g., Leptolyngbya) may alleviate resource competition, indirectly supporting Dictyosphaerium sp.’s stress adaptation. Concurrently, ENR-selected mutualists like Porphyrobacter, exhibiting antibiotic resistance and metabolic provisioning (e.g., B12), achieved symbiont dominance. Notably, this specialized restructuring characterized by reduced diversity and simplified ecological interactions, while enhancing microalgal stress adaptation, could precipitate ecological fragility via diminished functional redundancy and increased obligate dependence on keystone taxa [62]. The high-efficiency, low-resilience partnerships may compromise microalgal stability and adaptive plasticity to new perturbations. Additionally, specialized mutualists could inadvertently drive algal blooms through stabilized cross-kingdom metabolic coupling, potentially exacerbating biogeochemical cycle perturbations and expanding the environmental resistome [63]. These cascade effects necessitate evaluation of ecological trade-offs between transient stress mitigation and ecosystem destabilization risks.

Functional exploration of stress-selected microbiota identified Porphyrobacter as a keystone taxon in microalgal antibiotic adaptation. This genus demonstrates phycosphere dominance across aquatic ecosystems [64, 65], with transcriptomic evidence establishing it as the most active bacterium in beneficial interactions with microalgae, such as Chlorella and Scenedesmus [65]. Our study provides the first empirical evidence of ENR-driven Porphyrobacter enrichment in the phycosphere and its functional role in enhancing the adaptability of Chlorophyta species (Dictyosphaerium sp., C. vulgaris, and S. quadricauda) under antibiotic stress. Notably, the observed Porphyrobacter dominance may transcend ENR-specific adaptation, as evidenced by its cross-fluoroquinolone resistance and conserved growth-promoting effects in CIP/NOF-stressed Dictyosphaerium sp. (Fig. S10). The similar structure and conserved mode of bactericidal action among these fluoroquinolones may lead to a stress-selected phycosphere microbiome enriched with Porphyrobacter [66]. Future investigations should delineate the ecological generality of this phycosphere mechanism across a broad-spectrum antibiotic or other xenobiotic stressors. Notably, ENR-tolerant Microbacterium strains exhibited neutral growth modulation in binary algal co-cultures, yet demonstrated putative metabolic cross-feeding potential through algal exudate utilization [67]. Their persistence maintained phycosphere biodiversity (Shannon index 0.09, Table S1), potentially sustaining functional redundancy critical for microalgal resilience against secondary perturbations [68]. Additionally, other residual stress-resistant taxa (Sphingomonas, Brevundimonas) showed putative ENR degradation capacity and synergistic metabolic network formation with microalgae/bacteria [69–71], though their roles require validation. Prioritizing investigation on the interactions of these non-dominate microbes with Porphyrobacter-microalgae symbioses is critical to decode the collective role of stress-selected taxa in microalgal stress adaptation.

Porphyrobacter-mediated B12 provision improves microalgal antibiotic adaptation

Evolving from the established paradigm of core microbiome shaping in terrestrial plants, where recruited rhizobacteria enhance host fitness under salt/drought stress via phytohormone production, antioxidant modulation, and engagement in other beneficial processes [33, 72, 73], our study extends these principles to aquatic ecosystems. We identify Porphyrobacter-produced vitamin B12 (herein B12) as a critical mediator of microalgal antibiotic adaptation. B12 is an essential micronutrient universally required but exclusively synthesized by archaea, α/γ-proteobacteria, and Cyanobacteria [21]. Most B12-auxotrophic marine microalgae acquire it through symbiotic partnerships with phycosphere bacteria like Ruegeria pomeroyi [74], Halomonas sp. [21], and Dinoroseobacter shibae [75]. Intriguingly, while Dictyosphaerium sp. exhibited B12-independent growth in BG11 medium (which is B12-free), enrichment of B12-producing Porphyrobacter in the phycosphere was observed. This may be attributed to the evolutionarily conserved metabolic coupling wherein the B12 provider undergoes spatial optimization to maximize metabolic cross-feeding efficiency [76]. Our findings further demonstrated that B12-producing Porphyrobacter/B12 significantly enhanced the stress fitness of Chlorophyta species (Dictyosphaerium sp., C. vulgaris, S. quadricauda) under ENR exposure (Fig. 5; Fig. S8), contrasting with the absence of protective effects in prokaryotic microalgae (A. azotica, M. aeruginosa). This interspecific variability likely stems from differential antibiotic susceptibility, species-dependent microalgae-bacteria interaction, and B12 requirement/acquisition efficiency [11, 21, 54], collectively constraining the universality of B12-producing Porphyrobacter/B12-mediated protection. These results, along with the adaptive benefits observed across fluoroquinolone antibiotics (CIP, NOF), suggest B12 provisioning, whether through symbiotic exchange or exogenous supplementation, constitutes an adaptive strategy in specific Chlorophyta confronting fluoroquinolone pollution. Such universality could sustain algal primary productivity and oxygenic photosynthesis under antibiotic stress, thereby stabilizing the aquatic ecosystem’s biodiversity and functions. Future investigations should validate this mechanism across non-fluoroquinolone antibiotic classes and phylogenetically diverse microalgae to assess ecological universality.

The cross-species and cross-antibiotic applicability of B12-producing Porphyrobacter in enhancing microalgal antibiotic adaptation positions it as a keystone partner for effective pollutant bioremediation and microalgal cultivation in multi-antibiotic-loaded wastewater treatment systems. While exogenous B12 supplementation achieves comparable microalgal stress adaptation with advantages including precise dosing protocols, simplicity of application (no symbiotic maintenance requirements), and mitigated antibiotic-resistant bacteria/genes dissemination risks, this approach incurs ecological costs: (i) perturbation of natural B12 metabolic coupling essential for microbial loop stability [77] and (ii) unintended selection for B12-auxotrophic bloom-forming species that disrupted nutrient stoichiometry and accelerate eutrophication [56, 78]. Such trade-offs necessitate rigorous assessment to optimize the risk–benefit equilibrium. Furthermore, beyond B12 production, Porphyrobacter likely enhanced microalgal fitness by physical protection, dissolved carbon provisioning [65], and ENR derivative detoxification through co-metabolic transformation, which necessitates a comprehensive investigation to fully elucidate stress-responsive algae-Porphyrobacter mutualism mechanisms.

B12 enhances microalgal antibiotic adaptation by facilitating EPS production, adsorption-mediated ENR removal, and alleviating oxidative stress

B12-producing bacteria, or B12, critically enhance organismal stress resistance across biological systems. In Chlamydomonas, thermal tolerance augmentation occurs through B12-producing bacteria-mediated interactions or direct exogenous B12 supplementation [79]. B12 can also directly mitigate environmental stressors on phycosphere bacteria while stimulating their proliferation [80]. Zebrafish studies demonstrate probiotic-derived B12 reinforces gut microbiota interactions, enhancing host-pathogen resistance [81]. These findings, along with our experimental evidence, highlight B12 as a universal mediator of cross-kingdom stress adaptation. Nevertheless, the mechanistic basis of B12-producing bacteria or B12-mediated microalgal antibiotic adaptation requires further elucidation. Notably, prior studies linked microalgal antibiotic resistance to EPS [13]. Our findings corroborate this paradigm by Porphyrobacter/B12-mediated microalgal growth improvement coupled with elevated EPS production under ENR stress. The three-dimensional EPS matrix enriched with carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amino moieties, which facilitates ENR adsorption through (i) hydrogen bonding via polar group interactions, (ii) OH-π electron coupling between ENR’s heterocyclic aromatic ring and EPS polysaccharides, and (iii) π-π interaction mediated by aromatic amino acid in EPS proteins and ENR’s electron-rich aromatic ring [13, 82]. In our microalgal system, the adsorption mechanism dominated ENR dissipation. Porphyrobacter/B12-mediated microalgal EPS overproduction significantly enhanced extracellular ENR adsorption and has a decisive influence on ENR removal from culture medium. This process enabled reduced direct membrane-ENR interaction, decreased ENR cellular accumulation, and mediated associated toxicity, thereby enhancing microalgal adaptability. These insights advance our understanding of stress-resilient algal-bacterial symbiosis while establishing a framework for EPS-targeted engineering strategies that optimize microalgae-mediated wastewater remediation through simultaneous contaminant adsorption and biomass harvesting.

Notably, B12 exhibits antioxidant properties with demonstrated oxidative stress mitigation in prokaryotic cells and animal models [83, 84]. Our findings corroborated this through Porphyrobacter/B12-mediated reduction of lipid peroxidation and upregulation of antioxidant enzymes (Fig. S7) in ENR-stressed microalgae. This activated redox homeostasis mechanism attenuates ENR-induced oxidative damage, thereby constituting an intracellular pathway to enhance microalgal antibiotic adaptation. B12 further orchestrates epigenetic regulation via methionine synthase-dependent DNA methylation [85], potentially strengthening algal stress-defense pathways (EPS biosynthesis, antioxidant systems) through upregulating genes. Systematic transcriptomic validation of the B12-supplemented culture system is required to delineate these regulatory networks in microalgal stress adaptation. Additionally, while Porphyrobacter enrichment concurrent with the presence of detoxified ENR derivatives (including CIP and two metabolic products) (Table S5; Fig. S11–12), the mechanistic role of B12 provisioning in algal xenobiotic metabolism remains uncharacterized. Targeted metabolomic flux analysis is essential to dissect B12’s function in ENR detoxification in the microalgal system. Collectively, although high-dose ENR exposure initially suppressed microalgal growth through photosynthetic inhibition (chlorophyll and carotenoids biosynthesis attenuation) and nutrient assimilation impairment (membrane lipid peroxidation induction), phycosphere microbiome-mediated stress mitigation mechanisms, encompassing extracellular EPS-dependent ENR adsorption/removal and intracellular enzymatic antioxidant defense activation, ultimately restored and promoted microalgal growth. This stress adaptation manifested as recovered photosynthetic pigments levels (Fig. S13) and reduced stress-responsive DEGs number during Porphyrobacter enrichment (Figs. S14–S15). Concomitantly, nutrient flux from microbial lysis and detoxified ENR derivatives synergistically potentiated microalgal metabolic reactivation, driving biomass recuperation under sustained antibiotic stress.

Conclusion

Employing ENR-tolerant Dictyosphaerium sp. as a model system, this study reveals that stress-induced enrichment of B12-producing Porphyrobacter within the phycosphere microbiome underpins microalgal antibiotic adaptation through dual extracellular-intracellular mechanisms: enhanced EPS biosynthesis driving ENR adsorption/removal, coupled with enzymatic antioxidant defense activation. While Porphyrobacter enhances microalgal growth under CIP and NOF exposure, its protective efficacy against non-fluoroquinolone antibiotics (e.g., β-lactams, tetracyclines) and other complex pollutants remains unverified. Critical knowledge gaps persist in three domains: the formation of pollutant-driven phycosphere core microbiome across diverse exposures, molecular drivers (e.g., quorum-sensing molecules, algal exudates) governing microbiome assembly dynamics, and stress-selected consortia’s ecological resilience for post-disturbance functional restoration. To advance applications, we proposed engineering Porphyrobacter-microalgae consortia or B12 supplementation into (i) advanced photobioreactors and constructed wetlands for targeted antibiotic removal from high-risk effluents (aquaculture/hospital wastewater) and (ii) aquafeed and biofertilizer production systems to synergistically increase biomass productivity, CO₂ fixation rates, and bioenergy (biofuel/bioelectricity) output while addressing plant-based aquaculture nutritional deficits. However, implementation challenges including spatiotemporal environmental heterogeneity, indigenous microbiota antagonism, and multilevel contamination risks (e.g., pathogens, invasive species, pollutants) demand mitigation strategies integrating (i) niche engineering through physicochemical parameter optimization, (ii) microalgae-microbiome symbiont network stabilization, and (iii) real-time biosensor arrays and metagenomic surveillance for functional consortium tracking. Ecologically, microscale alterations in the phycosphere microbiome may cascade through aquatic ecosystems, necessitating multiscale investigative frameworks that synergize controlled mesocosm experiments, field trials across representative ecosystems (e.g., intensive aquaculture ponds, wastewater treatment lagoons), and machine learning-enabled predictive modeling to quantify cross-scale impacts on biogeochemical cycling (C/N/P fluxes), primary productivity, and ecosystem biodiversity.
