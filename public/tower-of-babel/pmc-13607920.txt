Degenerative lumbosacral spondylolisthesis: Instability as the primary pathology and stabilization as definitive treatment. Experience with 28 cases treated by only fixation without any decompression
Authors: Atul Goel, Apurva Prasad, Abhidha Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:The objective of the study was to evaluate clinical outcomes in patients with degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis associated with lumbar canal stenosis treated exclusively with multisegmental spinal stabilization, without conventional decompressive procedures involving bone, ligament, or disc resection. This strategy is based on the hypothesis that spinal instability, rather than neural compression, is the primary pathological driver of symptoms. Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis is considered part of a broader spectrum of multilevel “vertical” spinal instability, frequently associated with adjacent segment involvement and lumbar canal stenosis.Materials and Methods:Between January 2017 and August 2025, 28 patients with symptomatic lumbar canal stenosis and degenerative spondylolisthesis underwent surgical treatment. Fixation was restricted to the affected listhesis level and adjacent unstable segments. No decompressive bone, ligament, disc, or osteophyte resection was performed. Instability was identified using clinical evaluation, radiological assessment, and intraoperative findings. Stabilization was achieved using transarticular facetal screw fixation. Fixation spanned two segments in 9 patients, three segments in 10 patients, four segments in 8 patients, and five segments in 1 patient. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index, Visual Analog Scale, and patient-reported satisfaction scores.Results:All patients demonstrated immediate postoperative clinical improvement. At a mean follow-up of 29 months, sustained symptom relief and high patient satisfaction were observed. Solid fixation and successful arthrodesis were achieved in all cases. No patient required reoperation or additional surgical intervention.Conclusions:“Vertical” spinal instability is the principal pathogenic factor in degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis and associated canal stenosis. Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis rarely exists in isolation and commonly involves multisegmental instability. Stabilization of unstable segments alone is the treatment, and decompressive procedures are unnecessary.

INTRODUCTION

Lumbar spondylolisthesis is conventionally classified into four grades based on the extent of vertebral slippage.[1] When listhesis is associated with an isthmic defect or pedicular fracture, it represents a distinct clinicopathological entity – namely, isthmic (lysis) spondylolisthesis. Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis, in contrast, is most commonly limited to Grade I, whereas isthmic spondylolisthesis typically presents with a higher grade of slip.[2] The present study focuses exclusively on lumbar spondylolisthesis related to degenerative spinal disease; cases of isthmic spondylolisthesis were excluded.

Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis is rarely an isolated phenomenon and is frequently associated with lumbar canal stenosis.[3] We propose that although degeneration-related spinal instability may be radiologically apparent as single-level spondylolisthesis, the underlying pathological process is usually multisegmental and generalized rather than focal. Instability is commonly present not only at the level of listhesis but also at one or more adjacent segments, even in the absence of established radiological criteria indicating spinal canal stenosis or instability. While instability at adjacent levels may be relatively subtle, the listhesis-involved segment typically represents an advanced stage of instability. This pattern reflects the multisegmental nature of the primary etiological factor – paraspinal muscle weakness and dysfunction. True focal instability is uncommon and is usually encountered in younger patients, in whom muscle involvement is limited to one or a few spinal segments.[4]

The predominant clinical manifestations of degeneration- related lumbar spondylolisthesis closely resemble those observed in lumbar canal stenosis. Neurogenic claudication was the principal presenting symptom and was observed in all patients. The presence of degenerative listhesis generally indicates an advanced stage of multisegmental vertical facetal instability, and symptoms related to lumbar canal stenosis are typically more pronounced.

In accordance with our previously published concept, the musculoskeletal alterations and neural “compressive” elements identified on imaging are considered secondary to segmental spinal instability.[5678] These changes are naturally protective and adaptive and potentially reversible following stabilization of the affected segments. Consequently, decompressive procedures aimed at enlarging the spinal canal or neural foramina are unnecessary. This treatment philosophy contrasts with conventional surgical strategies that prioritize decompression through resection of bone, soft tissue, or disc material and realignment of the vertebral bodies at the level of spondylolisthesis. In the present study, stabilization was achieved using transarticular facetal screw fixation as described by Camille.[9] This technique stabilizes the fulcrum of spinal motion, secures the strongest part of the vertebra, and can be performed safely and efficiently under direct vision without the need for intraoperative imaging or neurophysiological monitoring. The clinical outcomes of this management strategy are presented.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient population

Between January 2017 and August 2025, 28 consecutive patients with symptomatic degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis were surgically treated at our institution and included in this retrospective analysis. All patients demonstrated clinical and/or radiological evidence of lumbar canal stenosis. Only patients with Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis were included. Patients younger than 50 years and those with isthmic spondylolisthesis were excluded. The minimum follow-up duration was 4 months. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. All clinical evaluations and surgical procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical evaluation

All patients presented with symptoms characteristic of lumbar canal stenosis. Neurogenic claudication with low back pain radiating to one or both lower limbs was present in all cases. Motor weakness was observed in 5 patients, and sensory deficits were noted in 3 patients.

Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and a patient-reported satisfaction questionnaire administered in the local vernacular language. All patients had progressive symptoms refractory to conservative treatment.

Radiological evaluation

Preoperative and postoperative evaluations included dynamic plain radiographs (flexion–extension views), computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Spondylolisthesis was present at L3–4 in 4 patients, L4–5 in 18 patients, and L5–S1 in 6 patients. None of the patients demonstrated mobile instability of the facet joints or vertebral bodies on dynamic imaging. Although the listhesis involved a single level in all patients, adjacent segment instability and lumbar canal stenosis was suspected and eventually treated based on clinical, radiological, and intraoperative findings.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed with the patient in the prone position. Lumbar lordosis was reduced by adjusting the operating table. The operative level was confirmed using intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Stabilization was performed using Camille’s transarticular facet screw fixation technique.[9] A double-insurance construct (two transarticular screws across a single facet joint) was used in all patients.[10] In selected cases, a triple-insurance construct was employed. Self-tapping monoaxial screws measuring approximately 18 mm in length and 2.6 mm in diameter were utilized [Figure 1].

Levels requiring fixation were determined based on clinical findings, radiological features, and direct intraoperative inspection and manual manipulation of the facet joints. Indicators of instability included osteophyte formation, reduced disc space height, ligamentum flavum bulging, intra-articular fluid, parafacetal cysts, and abnormal facet mobility. No decompressive resection of bone, disc, ligament, or osteophytes was performed. No special attempts were made to realign the vertebral segments. Autologous bone graft was harvested from transected spinous processes and applied after decortication of the laminae and facet joint surfaces.

Postoperative management

Patients were mobilized early and advised to use an external lumbar brace for 6 weeks. Postoperative imaging was obtained immediately after surgery and during follow-up to assess implant position and fusion.

RESULTS

Follow-up duration ranged from 4 to 96 months (mean: 29 months). Independent observers not involved in the study performed clinical and radiological assessments.

All patients demonstrated clinical improvement in the immediate postoperative period. Mean ODI and VAS scores showed significant improvement at final follow-up compared with preoperative values. Patient-reported satisfaction scores indicated high levels of perceived clinical benefit [Tables 1-3].

Patient satisfaction score

During the follow-up period, all patients were satisfied with their surgical treatment and clinical outcome and there was no recurrence of major symptoms in any patient. Arthrodesis of the treated spinal segments was considered successful when, at a minimum follow-up of 6 months, screw position remained unchanged, bone fusion across the facet joints was evident, and dynamic imaging demonstrated absence of relative motion between vertebral components. Based on these criteria, successful segmental arthrodesis was achieved in all cases. None of the patients required repeat surgery or any additional surgical procedure at the operated or adjacent spinal levels.

DISCUSSION

The term spondylolisthesis is derived from the Greek word olisthanein, meaning “to slip.”[11] Lumbar and lumbosacral spondylolisthesis are relatively common clinical conditions and represent a frequent cause of back pain in the adult population.[1213] Among the various etiological factors implicated, trauma and spinal degeneration are the most prevalent. Given the high clinical burden of spondylolisthesis and its frequent indication for surgical intervention, continued critical evaluation of its pathogenesis and treatment strategies remains essential.

Degeneration-related lumbar spondylolisthesis is most commonly encountered in the elderly population and is frequently associated with lumbar canal stenosis.[121314] Despite this frequent coexistence, the conceptual unification of degenerative spondylolisthesis and lumbar canal stenosis as manifestations of a single pathological process has not been clearly articulated.[14] In prevailing literature, these entities are often considered related but discrete conditions.

The pathogenesis of lumbar canal stenosis has been extensively studied.[1516] The conventional understanding proposes that spinal degeneration begins with age-related intervertebral disc dehydration or disc herniation, followed by a cascade of secondary changes including reduction in disc height, bulging of the posterior annulus and posterior longitudinal ligament, facet joint overriding, and infolding of the ligamentum flavum. These degenerative alterations are collectively believed to reduce spinal canal dimensions, ultimately resulting in neural compression and clinical symptoms.

Several studies have documented the occurrence of adjacent-segment instability following surgical fixation of a spondylolisthesis-involved segment.[1718] We contend that instability is not merely a postoperative phenomenon but is commonly present at both the listhesis-involved level and adjacent spinal segments at initial presentation. Accurate identification of instability through a combination of clinical assessment, radiological evaluation, and intraoperative findings, followed by stabilization of all unstable segments, is fundamental to effective treatment. Instability at adjacent levels is common and represents a key mechanism underlying the development of lumbar canal stenosis.

Although spinal instability has been discussed in various contexts, its role as the primary pathogenetic factor in degenerative lumbar disease has neither been universally accepted nor systematically explored. Conventionally, the lumbar spine in canal stenosis is regarded as structurally stable. However, the posture-dependent nature of symptom exacerbation during standing or walking and relief with rest or flexion suggests a dynamic rather than a fixed pathological process.[719] Symptoms that manifest predominantly after physical activity imply that canal compromise is not static or intrinsic but activity-dependent. Weakness and early fatigue of the paraspinal musculature during exertion may result in facetal overriding or telescoping, supporting the concept that lumbar canal stenosis is fundamentally a dynamic disorder.

Our earlier publications emphasize the pivotal role of spinal instability in the genesis of pain and in the evolution of lumbar canal stenosis.[567] The posterior paraspinal musculature plays a crucial role in maintaining the uniquely human upright, bipedal posture. The facet joints serve as the principal fulcrum through which these muscles exert their stabilizing action. In contrast, the anterior flexor musculature, located ventral to the vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs, is comparatively thin and biomechanically weaker.

Beyond age-related sarcopenia, disuse, misuse, or injury to the paraspinal musculature can precipitate spinal instability that is primarily focused at the facet joints. This results in difficulty with prolonged standing and ambulation. Progressive muscle weakness leads to narrowing of the facetal joint space, erosion of articular cartilage, and the development of subtle or overt facetal listhesis.[7] We have termed this phenomenon vertical facetal listhesis and identify it as the nodal event in the pathogenesis of the entire spectrum of degenerative spinal disease.[20]

The earliest pathological changes occur at the facet joints, which constitute the central stabilizing elements of the spinal motion segment. Progressive facet incompetence leads to vertical and central instability characterized by loss of facet overlap, telescoping of vertebral bodies, retrolisthesis of facets, and abnormal micromotion. Anterolisthesis of the vertebral bodies, when present, represents a late and visible manifestation of this instability rather than its primary cause. Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis has traditionally been regarded as a discrete clinical entity; however, its role as a marker of generalized multisegmental spinal instability has not been adequately recognized.

Several changes conventionally labeled as “degenerative” or “pathological,” including ligamentous buckling, osteophyte formation, disc space reduction, and subsequent narrowing of spinal and neural canals, appear to be secondary, adaptive biological responses to vertical instability. As muscular support deteriorates, the ability to sustain physiological load diminishes, leading to progressive instability and the clinical manifestation of claudication. With further muscle fatigue, claudication distance progressively shortens.

Vertical spinal instability is inherently dynamic and becomes clinically evident when fatigued musculature fails to maintain spinal alignment under load. Lumbar canal “stenosis” thus represents the cumulative effect of multilevel muscle weakness–induced vertical instability. Reduction in disc height, vertebral body listhesis, and canal narrowing are expressions of this process rather than independent pathological entities.

Because facet joints are laterally located and obliquely oriented, early vertical facetal instability is often subtle and difficult to detect on conventional imaging. Overt vertebral body listhesis represents an advanced stage of this process. Isthmic or pedicle fracture-related spondylolisthesis constitutes a further progression, wherein facetal malalignment compromises pedicular load-bearing capacity, culminating in structural failure.

Conventionally, surgical decision-making has been guided by the degree of vertebral slip, sagittal alignment, and the presence of canal or foraminal stenosis. Neural decompression has long been considered the principal surgical objective, with stabilization added variably. Attempts at vertebral realignment are often undertaken to improve sagittal balance and facilitate arthrodesis. However, this approach assumes neural compression as the primary pathological driver.[21222324252627]

In 2011, we were the first to describe facetal distraction using the Goel facet distraction spacer for the treatment of cervical and lumbar canal stenosis.[5282930] This technique resulted in unbuckling of ligaments, restoration of intersegmental height, and indirect neural decompression without any bone, disc, ligament, or osteophyte resection. This represented the first surgical strategy for canal stenosis that did not advocate direct decompression.

Subsequent publications emphasized that spinal instability, rather than neural compression per se, is the principal cause of symptoms.[313233] Neural tissues can tolerate significant distortion, particularly when changes develop gradually. In contrast, instability causes repetitive micro-injuries and represents the critical factor in symptom initiation and progression. Based on this understanding, we further refined our surgical strategy and now advocate stabilization alone, without even indirect decompression.[313233]

Radiological identification of vertical instability is often unreliable, and the parameters discussed by us earlier have not been validated; therefore, direct intraoperative assessment of facetal stability can be a dependable method.[34] Our four-decade-long experience with facet manipulation across the craniovertebral and subaxial spine has enabled reliable intraoperative identification of instability.

Among available fixation techniques, we employed the transarticular facetal screw fixation described by Camille.[910] As the facet joints represent the strongest structural component and the fulcrum of spinal motion, facetal fixation provides robust stabilization and an optimal environment for arthrodesis. Reinforcement with additional screws (double or triple insurance) provides reinforced biomechanical strength and stability to the implant.

Our earlier work on atlantoaxial instability and basilar invagination similarly demonstrated that instability is the primary pathology and stabilization the definitive treatment.[353637] Reduction or realignment, while desirable, is secondary to achieving durable arthrodesis. This principle applies equally to degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis.

The favorable outcomes observed in our previous cervical and lumbar degeneration series, together with the uniformly satisfactory results in the present cohort, strongly support this concept. Early and sustained symptomatic improvement indicates that direct neural decompression may be unnecessary when instability is adequately addressed.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, lack of a control group, and relatively short follow-up in some patients. Prospective, comparative studies with longer follow-up are required to further validate these findings and define appropriate patient selection criteria.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on our current observations and cumulative prior experience, we conclude that vertical spinal instability manifesting as telescoping or overriding of the facet joints and accentuated during physiological loading and physical activity constitutes the primary pathogenetic mechanism underlying claudication pain and the development of lumbar canal stenosis. Degeneration-related lumbar spondylolisthesis represents an advanced and overt expression of this vertical instability. Surgical fixation directed at the fulcrum of spinal motion alone provides a rational, sufficient, and effective treatment strategy. Procedures aimed at direct decompression of neural structures are unnecessary.
