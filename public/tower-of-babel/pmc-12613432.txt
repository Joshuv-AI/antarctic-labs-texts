Seroprevalence of coronaviruses of cattle and pigs in Belgian herds
Authors: Ruth M Mumo, Stefaan Ribbens, Fabien Grégoire, Sebastiaan Theuns, Bart Pardon, Kristien Van Reeth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBovine coronavirus (BCoV), porcine haemagglutinating encephalomyelitis virus (PHEV), and porcine respiratory coronavirus (PRCV) are common coronaviruses of cattle and swine, but they are rarely monitored. From December 2021 to March 2022, we collected 2,639 bovine and 2,633 porcine serum samples from Belgian herds to determine the current seroprevalence of BCoV, PHEV and PRCV in their respective hosts. The samples were categorised by age and province. Sera were tested individually in virus neutralisation (VN) assays against recent virus isolates.ResultsThe seroprevalence rates of BCoV, PHEV and PRCV at the individual level were 95.3%, 96.2% and 76.4%, respectively. The seroprevalence of BCoV and PRCV was significantly lower in calves and fattening pigs than in adult animals. The seroprevalence tended to be greater in provinces with higher livestock densities. Most Belgian pigs (74.2%) were seropositive for both PHEV and PRCV.ConclusionThe three animal coronaviruses, BCoV, PHEV and PRCV, are enzootic in Belgian cattle and swine herds. Age and animal density were associated with seropositivity. There is cocirculation of PHEV and PRCV in Belgian swine herds.Keywords: BCoV, PHEV, PRCV, Virus neutralisation, Age-stratified, Seroprevalence

Background

Coronaviruses are single-stranded, positive-sense RNA viruses that infect a wide range of species. These viruses are categorised into 4 genera, known as alpha-, beta-, gamma- and deltacoronaviruses.

Bovine coronavirus (BCoV) is a betacoronavirus that was first isolated in 1972 in the United States [1, 2]. It is associated with both enteric and respiratory illness in cattle. Infection with BCoV presents as 3 disease syndromes: calf diarrhoea, winter dysentery and bovine respiratory disease. Diarrhoea in calves reduces weight gain [3]. Winter dysentery reduces milk output [4, 5]. Bovine respiratory disease, although not solely caused by BCoV, may result in reduced growth rates, carcass quality and yield [6]. The virus is shed in both nasal secretions and faeces and can be transmitted through respiratory particles, the faecal-oral route, and contact with fomites [7]. High BCoV prevalence rates have been reported in Africa [8], Asia [9, 10], Europe [11–14], North America [7, 15] and South America [16, 17]. However, the seroprevalence of BCoV in Belgian cattle has not been previously reported.

Porcine haemagglutinating encephalomyelitis virus (PHEV), a betacoronavirus, was first isolated in 1962 in Canada [18]. Approximately one decade later, in 1972, PHEV was isolated in Belgium [19]. Transmission of PHEV occurs through nasal secretions and saliva [20]. Suckling piglets from PHEV-naïve sows may exhibit clinical signs such as coughing, sneezing, nasal discharge, vomiting, diarrhoea, wasting and neurological manifestations with high mortality rates [19, 21, 22]. Clinical signs are rarely observed in older pigs [23, 24]. Infection with PHEV has been documented in Asia [25–28], Australia [29], Europe [30–34], North America [35, 36] and South America [37]. The last seroprevalence study for PHEV in Belgium was conducted in the late 1970 s [34]. Recent data is therefore lacking.

Porcine respiratory coronavirus (PRCV), an alphacoronavirus, is another coronavirus found in pigs. It is a member of the porcine respiratory disease complex, which encompasses several viral and bacterial pathogens contributing to respiratory illness in pigs, including porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus, porcine circovirus type 2, and Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae, among others [38]. This porcine coronavirus emerged in Belgium in 1984 as a mutant of the transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) [39]. A deletion of 621 to 681 nucleotides in the N-terminal domain of the spike protein of TGEV resulted in a virus with an altered tropism for the respiratory tract rather than the gastrointestinal tract. Hence, the virus was named PRCV [40, 41]. Unlike its predecessor, PRCV induces mild or subclinical infection. Antibodies against PRCV confer protection against TGEV because the antigenic site A, the main target of neutralising antibodies, within the spike protein is conserved in the 2 viruses [42–44]. Following its emergence, PRCV has been reported, in some cases with high prevalence, in several countries in Asia [45–49], Europe [12, 50–52] and the United States [53, 54]. This coincided with a decline in TGEV. As the prevalence of TGEV decreased, so did its clinical and economic relevance [39, 52]. However, there are no recent data on the seroprevalence of PRCV in Belgium.

Despite the importance of BCoV, PHEV and PRCV in livestock-producing regions, their current seroprevalence is unknown in most countries, including Belgium, which has a high livestock density. The purpose of this study was to determine the seroprevalence of these 3 viruses in cattle and swine herds across the provinces of Belgium via an age-stratified cross-sectional serological survey spanning December 2021 to March 2022.

Results

Seroprevalence of BCoV in Belgian cattle

All 264 cattle herds sampled had at least 1 seropositive animal. Therefore, the herd seroprevalence was 100%, with a 95% confidence interval of 98.6–100%. In the analysis of the individual level seroprevalence, 3 samples were excluded, making the total samples 2,636. In each of the provinces of Antwerp, Hainaut and Luxembourg, 1 serum sample was excluded. The antibody titre could not be reliably determined in those 3 samples due to the HRT-18 cells in the wells corresponding to those samples detaching. In 84.1% of the herds, all the sampled individuals were seropositive. This may reflect widespread historical exposure and the potential ease of transmission. In line with this finding, 95.3% of the individual cattle sampled had antibodies against BCoV (Table 1).

The seroprevalence at the individual level showed minimal variation between provinces. The percentage was lower in Flemish Brabant (90.0%) than in provinces with higher cattle populations such as West Flanders (98.9%) in the Flanders region. In the Wallonia region, the seroprevalence was marginally lower in Walloon Brabant (90.0%) than in Namur (97.3%), with higher cattle population. Cattle from the other provinces in Wallonia were not more likely to be seropositive than those from West Flanders (p > 0.05). These results suggest an association between cattle density and seropositivity. Similarly, age was associated with seropositivity. The 6–12-month-olds had the lowest seroprevalence at 86.8%, while 99.4% of the cattle over 2 years were seropositive. In line with the seroprevalence rates, the geometric means of virus-neutralising antibody titres increased with age from 127.3 in the calves to 443.4 in the cattle over 2 years of age (P < 0.0001).(Fig. 1). Higher titres in older cattle might reflect repeated exposures, boosting already existing antibodies.

Seroprevalence of PHEV in Belgian swine

We analysed all 2,633 serum samples. However, for 3 samples, the VN antibody titre could not be determined as the cells in the wells corresponding to those samples detached in the VN assay. Therefore, the number of viable samples was 2,630. Only 1 herd, in East Flanders, out of the total 263 sampled was seronegative for PHEV. The herd seroprevalence was 99.6% with a 95% confidence interval of 97.9–100%. In majority of the herds (83.3%), all the pig samples were seropositive for PHEV. The highest seroprevalence rates were reported in Antwerp (97.8%) and West Flanders (97.7%) (Table 2). A significantly lower seroprevalence of 82.6% was detected in Flemish Brabant (p < 0.0001), which has a lower pig population. The seroprevalence of PHEV in fattening pigs was 94.4%, comparable to sows (97.8%). The geometric mean VN antibody titre of the fattening pigs was significantly lower, at 70.3, than that of the sows at 200.0 (Fig. 2). This finding revealed an association between age and exposure to PHEV in Belgian swine herds.

Seroprevalence of PRCV in Belgian swine

The number of viable samples for the PRCV seroprevalence was 2,619, out of the total 2,633 collected. This is because 14 samples were too heavily contaminated with bacteria to enable visualisation of the CPE in the VN assay and were thus excluded. Among the 263 herds sampled, 4 were seronegative for PRCV, with a herd seroprevalence of 98.5% and a 95% confidence interval of 96.2–99.4%. The 4 seronegative herds were distributed equally between East Flanders and Limburg. Approximately half of the sampled herds had more than 80% of individuals seropositive for PRCV. The overall seroprevalence for individual pigs was 76.4% with significant differences between the provinces (Table 3). West Flanders and Antwerp, with the highest pig densities, had higher seroprevalence rates than East Flanders, Limburg and Flemish Brabant. Compared with sows, fattening pigs had a lower likelihood of being seropositive (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the seroprevalence is associated with age. The geometric mean titre of virus-neutralising antibodies against PRCV was also significantly lower in fattening pigs than in sows (Fig. 3). In fact, in 30 herds, fattening pigs were seronegative.

After exclusion of the indeterminate porcine samples, we had matched serostatus results for PHEV and PRCV in 2,619 samples. Out of these 74.2% had antibodies against both viruses. These represented 97.3% of the 263 herds sampled. This finding indicates cocirculation of the 2 viruses in most herds.

Discussion

This study examined the seroprevalence of BCoV, PHEV and PRCV in Belgian livestock and their associations with province and age. These findings indicate that BCoV, PHEV and PRCV are highly prevalent in Belgian herds. These results support the hypothesis that age and animal density influence seropositivity rates. While most studies use ELISA to determine seroprevalence, we investigated the presence and titre of virus-neutralising antibodies for all 3 viruses.

Our findings of high BCoV seroprevalence rates at the individual level are broadly consistent with reports from countries such as Italy [55], Poland [11], and Sweden [56]. While these studies differ in terms of sampling design and herd structure, such as age distribution, their shared detection of high individual-level seropositivity supports the notion that BCoV is enzootic across various cattle populations in Europe. Additionally, a recent European survey covering 17 countries from 2021 to 2022 reported a herd-level BCoV seropositivity of 80%, reinforcing the widespread circulation of the virus [14]. Furthermore, comparably high herd-level seroprevalence rates have also been reported in Thailand [57], Norway [58], Sweden [59], England and Wales [60]. Seropositivity for BCoV seemed to be associated with age in our study, with older animals exhibiting a greater likelihood of seropositivity as well as elevated VN antibody titres. Age-related trends in seropositivity have also been documented in other studies [56, 57, 61]. Given the apparent enzootic nature of BCoV, the likelihood of an animal being exposed to the circulating virus increases cumulatively over time.

In Belgium, BCoV was previously identified as the most frequently occurring virus involved in respiratory disease outbreaks in calves, either as a single infection or a coinfection with other pathogens [62]. This could explain the high seroprevalence detected among calves in this study. In line with previous studies, provinces with higher animal population densities presented higher seroprevalence rates, as increased contact between animals facilitates virus transmission [11, 55, 58]. The lowest BCoV seroprevalence was unexpectedly recorded in Hainaut. This province has a comparatively high cattle density in the Wallonia region [63, 64]. Our analysis did not reveal a reduced likelihood of cattle from Hainaut being seropositive. In the absence of a plausible explanatory factor, this result might reflect chance or unmeasured confounders.

There are commercially available BCoV vaccines such as the inactivated Bovilis® Rotavec® Corona vaccine, which is used in pregnant cows for the prevention of neonatal calf diarrhoea. This vaccine elicits neutralising antibodies in cows for passive transfer to calves through colostrum [65]. The impact of this vaccination on our findings in cattle over a year old is unclear because of the unavailability of vaccine coverage data during the study period. However, based on the seroprevalence observed in calves, vaccination likely had minor impact on our findings in the older animals. Maternal antibodies in calves have been shown to wane over time up to 4 months, coinciding with seroconversion [65]. The live attenuated Bovilis® Nasalgen®-C vaccine for the prevention of BCoV-induced respiratory disease in calves became available in 2023, after the study period. We could therefore conclude that all antibody titres detected in the 6–12-month-old calves were caused by infection.

The seroprevalence of PHEV determined in this study was comparable with that reported in a previous study conducted in Belgian sows in 1979 (95%) [34]. The comparable seroprevalence rates suggest that transmission patterns may have remained unchanged, although epidemiological studies are needed for definitive confirmation. Fattening pigs showed statistically lower seroprevalence compared to sows. However, the absolute difference was small and both groups exhibited high overall seroprevalence, suggesting limited biological relevance. In support of this, a study conducted in Argentina reported no association between PHEV seropositivity and age [66]. This finding supports the idea of early exposure, where a switch from passive to active immunity was reported in pigs as young as 8 weeks of age and maintenance of PHEV on farms [34]. This coincides with when maternal antibodies start to become undetectable, although in a minority of pigs, this passive immunity could last until 15 weeks [34, 67]. The herd seroprevalence obtained in our study (99.6%) was also comparable to that of pigs in the United States (96.2%) [68]. The outbreaks of PHEV in Argentina (2006) and Canada (1998) caused significant economic losses [37, 69]. Despite PHEV being enzootic, no outbreaks have been documented in Belgium. The most likely explanation is that suckling pigs born to immune sows are protected from disease [20]. In the absence of a commercial vaccine, maintaining immunity against PHEV in breeding sows may be beneficial for conferring lactogenic immunity to piglets.

For PRCV, comparable results were previously reported for the seroprevalence of sows in Belgium in 1990 (98.2%) [52]. Until this study, there have been no seroprevalence studies for PRCV in Belgium. However, recent studies conducted in Serbia (2022) and Denmark (2021–2023) reported similar seroprevalence rates as those reported in our study [50, 70]. Comparable seroprevalence rates after 3 decades suggest that transmission patterns may be stable, although further studies on incidence and transmission dynamics are needed for confirmation. The lower seropositivity in fattening pigs than in sows suggests that the virus had not circulated in those herds for 4–5 months prior to sample collection. In line with this, seasonal disappearance of PRCV on swine farms has been reported in Belgium, with reintroduction in cooler seasons [52].

In swine production regions, PRCV may be clinically inconsequential. However, maintaining immunity against PRCV may be beneficial in preventing the resurgence of TGEV by establishing immunological memory, yielding better protection to offspring [71, 72]. The seroprevalence of TGEV in Belgium decreased from 12 to 24% before 1984 to 7.6% in 1990, after the emergence of PRCV [39, 52]. For more than 20 years, there have been no documented outbreaks of TGEV in Belgium, and it is no longer regarded as an important pathogen of pigs. The current seroprevalence of TGEV in Belgium is unknown. By using the VN assay, we were unable to distinguish between PRCV and TGEV antibodies. Recent data from Europe indicate low levels of TGEV circulation. Differential enzyme-linked immunoassays, which detect antibodies against antigenic site D of the spike protein (a region deleted in PRCV and thus unique to TGEV), have revealed TGEV seroprevalence rates of 5.5% in southern Italy and 2.2% in Poland [73–75]. On the basis of those reports, the influence of circulating TGEV on the present results was likely minimal.

We used the same porcine sera to determine the seroprevalence of PHEV and PRCV. Our findings indicate that the 2 viruses cocirculate in Belgian swine farms. The seroprevalence at the herd level was comparable between PHEV and PRCV. However, at the individual level, PHEV had a higher seroprevalence than PRCV. This difference in seroprevalence may be attributed to the ease of transmission of PHEV, its higher incidence in swine populations, or a combination of both. The cocirculation of PHEV and PRCV in swine herds warrants comparative studies on their transmission efficiency. Understanding why PHEV maintains higher individual-level seroprevalence than PRCV could reveal critical insights into virus-host dynamics.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the most extensive seroprevalence study conducted within a single European country for BCoV, PHEV, and PRCV, using virus neutralisation assays. The findings provide current insights into the prevalence of these coronaviruses in Belgian cattle and swine. These data indicate that all 3 viruses are enzootic, reflecting sustained circulation. While herd-level seroprevalence rates for PHEV and PRCV were comparable, the individual-level seroprevalence was notably higher for PHEV, suggesting differences in transmission dynamics between the two porcine coronaviruses. Seropositivity was also positively associated with increasing age and animal population at the provincial level. Owing to the absence of detailed herd-type data, further stratification of results by production system was not possible, limiting the ability to assess system-specific risk factors. Nonetheless, given the high seroprevalence rates observed, it is plausible that production system differences may have limited impact on the overall likelihood of cattle and pigs being seropositive for BCoV, PHEV, and PRCV in Belgium.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

We designed our study as a cross-sectional study using a 2-stage clustering method to randomly select samples in both Flanders and Wallonia. First, herds were randomly selected from farms on the basis of the distribution of animal populations in each province. Second, a systematic random sampling stratified by two age classes was then applied to obtain the individual animals within the herds [76]. In the cattle herds, 5 calves aged 6–12 months and 5 cows older than one year were sampled. In pig herds, 5 fattening pigs aged 14–26 weeks and 5 sows over one year of age were included. This age stratification enabled assessment of age-related variation in antibody profiles. Calves and fattening pigs represented growing animals, whereas cows and sows represented long-term herd residents with cumulative exposure. The age selection for the younger animals was based on minimising the influence of maternal antibodies. In herds where this selection was not possible due to the unavailability of calves or fattening pigs, the number was made up with older animals, so each herd would be represented by 10 individual animal sera. Sampling 10 animals per herd provides a ≥ 95% probability of detecting at least one seropositive individual when the true within-herd prevalence is ≥ 30%, on the basis of binomial probability theory. This sample size strikes a balance between epidemiological sensitivity and logistical feasibility and reflects common surveillance practices in livestock studies [77].

The cattle samples from Flanders were selected from the annual winter screening for notifiable diseases [78, 79]. Winter screening typically targets animals that are 12–24 months old and over 24 months old. However, samples from Wallonia were selected from the routine surveillance for infectious bovine rhinotracheitis (IBR). The sampling method for IBR surveillance has been previously described [80, 81]. Porcine samples were selected from the national surveillance collection for Aujeszky’s disease. The sampling method for Aujeszky’s surveillance has been previously described [82]. These surveillance programs target clinically healthy animals. Bias from preferentially targeting animals showing signs of disease was therefore prevented. Bovine and porcine sera were collected in Flanders by Dierengezondheidszorg Vlaanderen (DGZ), the regional animal health association. The counterpart organisation in Wallonia, Association Régionale de Santé et d’Identification Animales (ARSIA), collected bovine sera in Wallonia. All samples were collected between December 2021 and March 2022. The cattle sera were used to determine the seroprevalence of BCoV, whereas the pig sera were used to determine the seroprevalence of PHEV and PRCV.

Pig sample demographics

A total of 2,633 pig serum samples were selected from 263 herds (Table 5). These sample numbers per province were proportionately allocated according to the population of pigs. During sample collection, the number of sera collected deviated slightly from the original design. This adjustment was to ensure sufficient number of samples for both the PHEV and PRCV seroprevalence analysis in each province in case of any technical issues encountered. The samples were only collected from pigs in Flanders, as the region accounts for approximately 93% of the pig population in Belgium. Therefore, the sampling of Flanders alone was still representative of Belgium.

Virus neutralisation assays

Antibody titres in the animal sera were determined by virus neutralisation (VN) assays. The VN assay is generally considered the gold standard for determining neutralising antibodies. The VN assays were performed against recent Belgian isolates of BCoV, PHEV and PRCV designated BCoV-Gent/PS-666/2020, PHEV-Gent/PS-412/2020 and PRCV-Gent/PS-071/2020, respectively. All the serum samples were inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min. Two-fold serial dilutions in 96-well microtitre plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with 100 TCID50 of BCoV, PHEV or PRCV. All the samples were individually titrated. The starting serum dilution was 1/4. For the BCoV VN assay, the bovine serum-virus mixture was transferred to microtitre plates with confluent human rectal tumour (HRT-18) cells [83–85]. For PHEV and PRCV, rein de porc diploid (RPD) and swine testis (ST) cells, respectively, were then added to the porcine serum-virus mixture at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/ml and incubated at 37 °C [86]. The PRCV plates were observed daily for cytopathic effects (CPE) up to 7 days. Our BCoV and PHEV VN assays usually do not exhibit CPE. After 4 days of incubation, the BCoV and PHEV plates were dry-fixed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at −20 °C. The visualisation of infected cells was conducted by immunohistochemical staining with 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) (Sigma, Cat. No. A5754) as previously described [87]. For BCoV, the primary and secondary antibodies used were mouse anti-CoV monoclonal antibody (Sigma, MAB9013) and polyclonal goat anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase (Dako, Cat. No. P0447), respectively, each at a 1/1,000 dilution. The primary antibody for the PHEV assay was swine antiserum against the Belgian PHEV strain VW572 diluted 1/10,000, and the secondary antibody was goat anti-swine peroxidase (Jackson, Cat. No. 114-0035-003) diluted 1/500. Antibody titres were determined as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution showing 50% neutralisation of the virus. Titres ≥ 4 were considered positive [88, 89]. Samples with titres >512 were assigned a titre of 512, and those with titres < 4 were assigned a titre of 2 for the calculation of the geometric mean titres.

Production of virus stocks

To produce the virus stocks of the isolates used for the VN assays, we propagated BCoV in HRT-18 cells, PHEV in RPD cells, and PRCV in ST cells. The cell culture medium of the HRT-18 cells comprised RPMI 1640 with GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 72400021), 10% heat-inactivated horse serum (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 16050-122), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 15140-122) and 0.5% gentamicin (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 15710049). The cell culture medium for the RPD cells comprised RPMI 1640 with GlutaMAX, 10% foetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. A2720803), 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 0.5% gentamicin. The cell culture medium of the ST cells comprised MEM with GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 41090028), 10% foetal bovine serum, 1% sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 11360039), 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 0.5% gentamicin. Each of the 3 cell lines was grown for 4 days in separate T175 cell culture flasks. Once confluent, the growth medium was removed and the cell monolayers washed with PBS containing Mg2+ and Ca2+. The respective monolayers were inoculated with 10 ml of the virus isolates diluted 1/10. After 1 h of incubation at 37 °C, 40 ml of medium was added to the flasks. For BCoV in HRT-18 cells, the medium comprised RPMI 1640 with GlutaMAX, 2.5 µg/ml trypsin (Sigma, Cat. No. T4799), 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 0.5% gentamicin. For PHEV in RPD cells, the medium comprised RPMI 1640 with GlutaMAX, 5% foetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 0.5% gentamicin. For PRCV in ST cells, the medium consisted of MEM with GlutaMAX, 5% foetal bovine serum, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 0.5% gentamicin. The flasks were further incubated for 4 days for BCoV and PHEV, and the supernatants were collected as no apparent CPE was observed. The visualisation of infected cells was conducted as described in the previous section. For PRCV, the flask was incubated until approximately 80% CPE was achieved, and then the culture supernatant was collected for virus titration. The culture supernatants were aliquoted into 1 ml vials and stored at −70 °C as virus stocks. Virus stocks were titrated in HRT-18 (BCoV), RPD (PHEV) and ST (PRCV) cells, and titres were calculated by the Reed and Muench method.

Production of control antisera

In each assay, we included control swine antisera produced against each of the virus isolates in the VN assays. To produce these antisera, 5-week-old piglets received 2 doses of 1 × 107 TCID50 of virus formulated in Emulsigen as an adjuvant at an 80:20 ratio. The doses were administered intramuscularly at 4-week intervals. The pigs were euthanised 2 weeks after the second dose and exsanguinated. This protocol was adapted from a previously described method for producing swine influenza A virus antisera in pigs [90]. The blood obtained was processed into serum. The serum was heat inactivated for 30 min in a warm water bath at 56 °C. Aliquots were made and stored at −20 °C. The ethical committee of Ghent University approved this animal experiment (EC 2022-041).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were initially performed to summarise the distribution of serostatus across age categories and provinces. Proportions were calculated for categorical data. Associations between age category and serostatus were assessed using a multiple logistic regression model employing the glm function from the R stats package. In this model, both age category and province were treated as categorical fixed effects. We had 3 levels for the age of cattle, 6–12 months, 1–2 years and over 2 years with the over 2 years as the reference level. For the pigs, we had 2 age levels, fattening pigs and sows with the sows as the reference level. For the provinces, West Flanders was the reference. The results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values. Before comparing the geometric means for the VN antibody titres stratified by age category, the test for normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (shapiro.test()), visually Q-Q plots (functions qqnorm () and qqline () from stats package) and histogram (function hist () from stats package). Because the distribution showed significant departures from normality, non-parametric Mann-Whitney tests (wilcox.test ()) or Kruskal-Wallis tests (kruskal.test ()) where there were more than 2 means were used. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with p-value adjustment using the dunn.test function. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 and GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
