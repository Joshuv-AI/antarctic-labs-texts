Bilateral choanal atresia in a low-resource environment: A case report and call for global health equity

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Bilateral choanal atresia is a rare but life-threatening congenital anomaly characterized by complete obstruction of the posterior nasal airway [1]. As neonates are obligate nasal breathers, this condition constitutes a respiratory emergency requiring prompt intervention. Reported in approximately 1 in 5000 to 8000 live births with a recognized female predominance, its management is critical to prevent severe complications such as respiratory distress, hypoxia, and feeding difficulties [1], [2] (See Table 1.)

The standard of care is well-defined in well-resourced settings, involving timely diagnosis with advanced imaging and definitive surgical repair. However, in resource-limited environments, the pathway to effective care is often obstructed by systemic challenges. These include limited access to specialized healthcare services, inadequate medical infrastructure, and significant financial constraints, which collectively delay diagnosis and compromise optimal management [5], [6].

This case report details the management of a neonate with bilateral choanal atresia within such a constrained setting. We aim to elucidate the specific diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered and describe the adaptive strategies employed. By highlighting this disparity, we underscore the urgent need for innovative, context-sensitive approaches to improve outcomes and advance global health equity for neonates with this critical condition.

The main objective of reporting quantitative outcomes data from this case is to contribute to the limited evidence base on long-term results in such contexts. By doing so, we aim to underscore the broader implications for global health equity and advocate for systemic changes to address health disparities.

Case presentation

Patient background

According to the referral documentation, a full-term female neonate was born via spontaneous vaginal delivery at a district hospital in a resource-limited setting. The infant was delivered by a 36-year-old multiparous woman following an uneventful pregnancy with regular antenatal care; there were no reported teratogenic exposures or maternal infections. The newborn weighed 3.2 kg, with APGAR scores of 7 and 8 at 1 and 5 min, respectively, and required only minimal resuscitation. Other anthropometric measurements, including birth length and head circumference, were not specified in the notes.

Within minutes of birth, the infant developed severe respiratory distress marked by cyanosis, nasal flaring, and substernal retractions. Pre-ductal oxygen saturation (SpO₂) was 82% on room air. Notably, cyanosis worsened during feeding attempts (SpO₂ ∼75%) and improved when crying, suggesting nasal airway obstruction—a classic sign of choanal atresia in an obligate nasal breather.

Physical examination revealed normal external nasal anatomy and no syndromic stigmata. A bedside diagnostic attempt to pass a 6-French nasogastric catheter met bony resistance at approximately 3 cm in both nostrils, strongly indicating choanal atresia. Immediate stabilization was achieved with a size 00G oral airway, improving SpO₂ to 94%. Supplemental oxygen via headbox at 2 L/min maintained SpO₂ > 92%, and feeding was initiated via an orogastric tube.

Due to limited resources, definitive imaging was delayed. An initial lateral neck X-ray with radiopaque dye instilled into the nares confirmed complete posterior nasal obstruction. After a 48-h period, the neonate was transferred to a tertiary center where a non-contrast CT scan (Fig. 1) confirmed the diagnosis of bilateral bony choanal atresia, with a mean posterior choanal stenosis of <1 mm and no apparent associated anomalies (Fig. 2). Radiographic technique for the lateral neck study and CT scanner parameters (slice thickness; field of view) were not available. Given the absence of known associated features, audiology and CHARGE screening were not performed.

Management and challenges [1], [2], [6]

The management pathway was significantly affected by systemic constraints. A 5-day delay occurred between initial presentation and definitive surgical planning, attributable to referral logistics and financial coordination. The total estimated cost of care (imaging, transfer, surgery) was approximately USD 800, representing over four months of the family's income, a catastrophic health expenditure. The total cost of USD 800 was derived from hospital receipts and caregiver interview. The monthly household income of approximately USD 200 was directly reported by the mother, placing the treatment cost at four months' income, well above the threshold for catastrophic health expenditure as defined by WHO.

During this interim period, innovative stabilization was necessary. The medical team fashioned a modified oral airway from a shortened 3.0-mm endotracheal tube (ETT Length 7.5 and ETT Diameter 3.0) to maintain a patent airway. The neonate was sustained on orogastric feeds, achieving an average daily weight gain of 15 g, with close monitoring to prevent aspiration or failure to thrive.

Definitive intervention and outcome

On day 7 of life, definitive surgical correction was performed at the tertiary center. Regarding the management of bony atresia, under general anesthesia, the surgical technique employed was a stepwise approach using manual instrumentation, as powered drills were unavailable in our setting. Under endoscopic visualization (4-mm 0° endoscope), a curved trocar was initially used to create a perforation through the bony atretic plate. This was followed by sequential manual dilation using progressively larger urethral dilators from 6-Fr (2.0 mm), with careful rotational movements to enlarge the neochoana without fracturing surrounding structures. The procedure was performed under direct endoscopic control to ensure midline orientation and to avoid injury to the skull base or sphenopalatine vessels. While manual dilation of bony atresia carries a theoretical risk of fracture or inadequate opening, we achieved a satisfactory anteroposterior diameter of >5 mm bilaterally, confirmed by postoperative endoscopy. This technique, though adapted, successfully created a patent nasal airway and is consistent with previously described approaches in resource-constrained settings where powered instruments are not accessible.

Nasal patency was successfully restored, and silastic tubing with unknown inner/outer diameter was fashioned into nasal stents which were fixed with sutures. Note that any devices were used off-label on this child. The antibiotic/analgesic regimens were made with ceftriaxone 50 mg per kg OD for five days associated paracetamol IV 15 mg per kg BID for 2 days. Normal saline was used for care.

The neonate was discharged on post-operative day 5, maintaining SpO₂ of 97% at baseline and 99% on room air with successful oral feeding established. The family received comprehensive counseling on stent care and complication recognition.

Stent-related considerations

The use of an improvised stent fashioned from a shortened endotracheal tube (ETT) was an adaptive measure necessitated by the unavailability of commercial silicone stents in our setting. We acknowledge that ETTs are not designed for long-term nasal placement and carry inherent risks, including mucosal irritation, granulation tissue formation, infection, and accidental distal migration. To mitigate these risks, we implemented a rigorous monitoring protocol throughout the 6-month stenting period. The stent was carefully trimmed to an appropriate length (approximately 2 cm beyond the choanae) and secured externally with a trans-septal suture and adhesive tape to prevent displacement.

Postoperative care included a structured protocol for nasal stent maintenance, which was communicated to the family in a standardized, reproducible manner. The protocol comprised twice-daily nasal saline instillation (2 mL of 0.9% saline per nostril) using a sterile syringe, followed by gentle suctioning with a neonatal suction catheter to remove crusts and secretions and maintain stent patency. Caregivers were instructed to perform this sequence immediately after feeding to minimize the risk of aspiration. In addition, they were trained to observe for signs of stent displacement, unilateral obstruction, bleeding, or fever, and to report these promptly to the clinical team. Weekly clinic visits were scheduled for endoscopic inspection and removal of any accumulated crusts by the attending clinician. At each visit, stent patency was confirmed by airflow assessment, and the stent was repositioned if any signs of migration were noted. No granulation tissue or infection was observed during the stenting period. While this improvised approach introduced additional monitoring demands compared to standardized silicone stents, it proved feasible with close family engagement and regular clinical oversight. This experience underscores both the resourcefulness required in low-income settings and the importance of structured follow-up to safely use adapted materials.

Long-term follow-up over 3 years demonstrated an excellent outcome. Stents were removed after 6 months. Criteria for stent removal 1) timing (≥6 months for bilateral cases to prevent restenosis); 2) Clinical evaluation (endoscopic confirmation of patent neo choanae without significant granulation tissue or infection. Patient stability with good feeding and no respiratory distress); 3) Endoscopic confirmation (Patent nasal airway with stable, healed neo choanae; absence of restenosis or significant granulation). Serial endoscopic evaluations at 6, 12, and 36 months showed a patent nasal airway with a minimal anteroposterior diameter of >5 mm means bilaterally (measured endoscopically). The child experienced no episodes of aspiration pneumonia, respiratory distress, or failure to thrive. At the 3-year mark, weight and height were at the 50th percentile with age-appropriate developmental milestones. No post-operative complications or lesion recurrence were observed.

Patient/Family perspective

The infant's mother described the experience as profoundly distressing: “When I saw my baby turning blue, I thought I would lose her. No one had explained this could happen.” She reported that learning to clean the nasal stents at home was frightening at first, as she feared causing harm. The mother's statement was originally expressed in Kinyarwanda (local language) and translated into French by a bilingual clinician. The translation was reviewed by a second bilingual team member to ensure accuracy and fidelity to the original sentiment.

Despite clear instructions, the lack of access to a nearby health facility made her feel isolated when questions arose. She noted that each follow-up visit required significant travel expenses and time away from work, adding strain to an already difficult situation. Reflecting on the outcome, she expressed deep gratitude for her child's recovery but emphasized that more community-based support and affordable local care would have eased the journey. Her experience underscores the critical need for accessible post-operative follow-up and family-centered support in resource-limited settings.

Discussion: Contextualizing management in a resource-limited Setting [1], [2], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]

This case underscores the profound disparities in managing a life-threatening congenital condition based on setting. In high-income countries, the standard pathway involves rapid diagnosis with advanced CT imaging, prompt surgical repair by multidisciplinary teams using powered drills and balloon dilation, and structured post-operative care in neonatal intensive care units, all underpinned by comprehensive insurance.

Alignment with World Health Organization (WHO) goals and objectives [8], [9]

This case underscores the imperative of SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) by demonstrating that timely neonatal surgical care—a core component of the WHO Every Newborn Action Plan (ENAP)—is achievable even in resource-limited settings when adaptive strategies are employed. Yet the delays and financial hardship experienced directly contravene SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities), revealing how systemic barriers disproportionately affect neonates in low-resource environments and undermine progress toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC). Finally, the case highlights the critical role of SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals): collaboration between referring and tertiary centers, as well as the leveraging of locally available resources and informal networks, enabled a life-saving intervention. Strengthening such partnerships, alongside targeted investments in surgical infrastructure and workforce, is essential to translate global equity targets into tangible neonatal survival gains.

Neonates in resource-rich settings benefit from advanced diagnostics, skilled teams, and state-of-the-art equipment, while those in underserved regions rely on improvisation and face significant barriers to timely care. This case was managed at a tertiary referral center, which provided essential foundational elements, including a functional operating room, pediatric anesthesia capable of safely intubating and ventilating a neonate, a 4-mm 0° rigid endoscope for visualization, and basic urethral dilators for manual dilation. These components, though basic, are not universally available in district hospitals in many resource-limited settings. We propose that the minimum viable setting for replicating this protocol requires: [1] a dedicated neonatal airway management team with experience in endoscopic techniques; [2] access to a rigid endoscope or at minimum a headlight and nasal speculum to guide the puncture; [5] basic surgical instruments (trocar, urethral dilators, suction); [6] pediatric anesthesia capability with neonatal airway supplies; and [7] a structured post-operative follow-up system with trained personnel to monitor stent integrity and detect complications. Without these essential elements—particularly the endoscope and skilled personnel—replication in a district hospital would be challenging and potentially unsafe. While our approach demonstrates that advanced powered instruments are not mandatory, it also underscores that some level of specialist expertise and equipment is indispensable. The role of improvisation

We emphasize that the improvised techniques employed in this case—including the modified oral airway, urethral dilators, and custom-fashioned silastic stents—were contingency measures necessitated by the acute unavailability of standard equipment. Their successful use in this instance should not be interpreted as validating these adaptations as equivalent replacements for established surgical and postoperative infrastructure. Rather, improvisation represents an adaptive bridge, designed to provide life-saving care when standard resources are inaccessible, while acknowledging the inherent limitations and additional monitoring burdens that such adaptations introduce. The favorable outcome in this case was contingent on close clinical oversight, caregiver engagement, and access to a tertiary center with basic endoscopic and anesthetic capabilities. In settings where even these minimal enabling factors are absent, improvisation alone is unlikely to compensate for systemic deficits. Therefore, we advocate those adaptive strategies be viewed as temporary solutions within a broader framework of urgent investment in surgical workforce training, equipment procurement, and supply chain strengthening, to ensure that all neonates with bilateral choanal atresia ultimately receive care aligned with international standards.

Conclusion

This case report vividly illustrates the severe challenges inherent in managing bilateral choanal atresia in neonates when operating in a resource-limited setting. The document details how timely recognition, innovative adaptations in airway management, and prompt surgical intervention can serve as lifesaving measures, even when essential equipment and specialized personnel are scant. The successful outcome achieved in this neonate's care is a testament to the ingenuity and perseverance of healthcare providers facing infrastructural and economic constraints. However, it also exposes how gaps in resources and standardized protocols can jeopardize neonatal survival and long-term health.

The analysis further underscores that while clinical innovation can temporarily bridge some shortcomings, sustainable improvements in neonatal outcomes must involve a wholesale strengthening of health systems. This report calls upon local and global stakeholders, from frontline health workers and hospital administrators to international policymakers and donor agencies, to prioritize investments in neonatal care. Efforts should focus on enhancing diagnostic capabilities, ensuring the availability of essential surgical tools, and providing specialized training to healthcare professionals. Such steps are not merely technical fixes but fundamental strategies to redress broader global health inequities. By amplifying commitment to neonatal health, we pave the way for more equitable, resilient healthcare systems that offer every newborn, regardless of geography or socioeconomic status, the best possible start in life.

Looking forward, this case report challenges us to reimagine how global health initiatives can integrate capacity building with policy reform. Future research should aim to develop context-specific guidelines and support networks that empower resource-limited centers to manage rare congenital anomalies efficiently. In a world where neonatal health directly influences the well-being of entire communities, it is imperative to translate the lessons learned from this case into robust, actionable strategies. Together, clinicians, researchers, and global health leaders can transform isolated successes into a broad movement for health equity, ensuring no neonate is left behind due to systemic shortfalls.
