A novel approach to soil nutrients prediction model for Bezuidenhout Park, Johannesburg, Gauteng Province, South Africa: attention temporal neural networks (ATNN)
Authors: Bamidele A Dada, Nnamdi I Nwulu, Seun O Olukanmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe agricultural sector is moving from the Agriculture 4.0 model to the Agriculture 5.0 model because of advances in machine learning (ML), big data, and remote sensing. The purpose of this study is to introduce and validate a novel Attention Temporal Neural Network (ATNN) framework for the high-resolution prediction of soil nutrient concentrations, demonstrating its practical value using a real-world Digital Soil Mapping (DSM) dataset from Bezuidenhout Park, Johannesburg. The ATNN framework explicitly models temporal and contextual dependencies in multisource predictors, which include a Digital Elevation Model (DEM), spectral indices from Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery, and meteorological covariates. The method involves combining the deep feature extraction capabilities of the ATNN with the strength of gradient-boosting regressors, specifically XGBoost, to leverage both architectures for robust tabular regression. The resulting ATNN–XGBoost hybrid model delivered the best performance in the experiments. It significantly reduced prediction error and improved agreement with laboratory measurements, achieving, for example, an RMSE ≈ 1.98 ppm, MAPE ≈ 2.81%, CCC ≈ of 0.76, and R2 ≈ of 0.69 for aluminium. This approach materially improved nutrient estimation accuracy over baseline models, including Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), and AdaBoost (ADB). The key contributions to this work are threefold: (1) the development of a compact ATNN architecture tailored for soil nutrient time series and spatial covariates; (2) a practical hybridisation strategy that pairs attention-based feature encoding with XGBoost (XGB); and (3) an empirical demonstration of superior performance on a real South African DSM dataset. Introduction

Global food security is still at risk. Recent evaluations show that Sustainable Development Goal 2 ending hunger and achieving food security will not be met by 2030. The expected growth in agricultural output is not enough to close the gaps that already exist1,2 Soil degradation and nutrient depletion are central bottlenecks to sustainable production, with the FAO and its allied programs identifying widespread risks to soil health and substantial data gaps that hinder timely intervention and policy formation3. Rapid urbanisation and changing land-use patterns further complicate nutrient management by increasing spatial heterogeneity and introducing novel anthropogenic drivers that are poorly represented in conventional monitoring systems4–6. Hence, accurate prediction of soil nutrient status is challenging because soil properties are controlled by complex, interacting factors (topography, parent material, land management, and microclimate) and because observational data are frequently sparse, noisy, and spatially non-stationary. These issues lead to large local variability and sampling biases that reduce the transferability of empirically fitted models across landscapes and land-use types3,5,6. In urban environments specifically, highly variable microtopography and mixed land uses create conditions that standard regional models do not reliably capture, calling for methods that explicitly account for multi-scale heterogeneity.

ML methods (e.g., SVM, RF, ANN, XGB)7–10 and classical regression approaches (e.g., MLR, PLSR)11,12 have substantially advanced predictive mapping of soil properties by exploiting large multisource datasets and remote sensing proxies12–17. However, reliance on large labelled training sets, sensitivity to feature selection and sampling design, limited interpretability, and variable generalisation to new contexts remain important limitations. Ensemble and stacked approaches have improved accuracy in some studies, yet few investigations have rigorously evaluated such methods within the distinctive conditions of urban green spaces where rapid anthropogenic change and fine-scale variability dominate18–23.

Materials and methods

Description of the site

Bezuidenhout Park in Johannesburg, South Africa (Longitude 28.09163° or 28° 5′ 30″ east and Latitude −26.18309° or 26° 10′ 59″ south), offers a diverse climate and varying weather conditions, making it an ideal study area for examining soil nutrient dynamics and their environmental influences. The park’s typical temperature fluctuates between 25 and 30 °C during the day, with occasional higher values. Winter is marked by low precipitation and moderate temperatures, with daytime temperatures ranging from 16 to 20 °C and nighttime temperatures dropping to 4 °C. The park receives an average annual rainfall of 713 mm, with the highest amount during summer months, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The study area, Bezuidenhout Park, Johannesburg, Gauteng Province, South Africa.

Sample and data collection

This study used unconventional satellite imagery and APIs to assess soil nutrients and environmental conditions at Bezuidenhout Park, Johannesburg. The study area was delineated using high-resolution satellite imagery and GIS. Initial boundaries and terrain features were assessed via Google Earth Pro, followed by processing in GIS (ArcMap v10.8) for cartographic accuracy and vector boundary generation. Ground-truth field surveys planned for June 2024 aim to validate remote sensing data and address micro-topographical issues related to urban canopy cover, establishing a georeferenced baseline for future spatiotemporal modeling and soil sampling. The Simple Random Sampling (SRS) method was employed to assess soil nutrients, with each member having an equal and independent probability of being chosen for a sample. The methodology involved selecting 15 specific longitudes and latitudes covering the study area. Soil samples was collected, analysed them in a laboratory, and mapped their coordinates using the proposed method. A secondary dataset was compiled using International Soil Data Africa (iSDA) soil’s API for data augmentation and validation, which provided nutrient values in parts per million (ppm), g/kg and Cmol+/kg attached to the coordinates. The iSDA API was used to improve the accuracy of climate conditions44. The Open Weather API was used for months to gather spatial data and environmental information, and data from Earth Explorer and Copernicus was combined to improve the accuracy of climate conditions. A total of 2000 randomly selected samples of surface soil were collected from two depth ranges: 0.00–0.20 m (active layer for major plant soil interactions) and 0.20–0.50 m (active layer for plant growth, nutrient cycling, water dynamics, and long-term health of soil) as illustrated in Fig. 2. Data collection was meticulous, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the study area. The Open Weather API provided detailed climate data for each sampling point, including temperature, humidity, and precipitation, enabling a robust analysis of environmental covariates. Key outcomes include synchronised inputs, reduced mismatches, and improved accuracy, which enhance soil nutrient predictions in dynamic urban environments like Bezuidenhout Park.

Variables transformation

Changing all the variables to a 10-m raster format helped create a consistent layout and allowed for detailed geospatial analysis, which is important for accurately predicting soil nutrients. We generated the vital factors by converting all the variables to a raster format with a resolution of 10 m. This conversion ensured spatial uniformity and facilitated precise geospatial analysis. The high-resolution raster format was chosen to capture fine-scale variations in environmental covariates, which are crucial for accurate soil nutrient predictions. Each raster data set showed a specific environmental factor, like height from the Digital Elevation Model (DEM), colour bands from Landsat and Sentinel-2, and other calculated measures, like the Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Normalised Difference Water Index (NDWI). To integrate these variables with soil sample data, pixel values corresponding to the exact locations of the soil samples were extracted from the raster data sets. This extraction process involved overlaying the soil sample points onto the raster grids and retrieving the pixel values for each variable at these points. By doing so, we ensured that each soil sample was associated with a comprehensive set of environmental covariates, reflecting local conditions accurately. The high-resolution 10-m data allowed micro-environmental variations that might significantly influence soil nutrient dynamics. This granularity is particularly important in heterogeneous landscapes where soil properties can change abruptly over short distances.

Finer resolutions can lead to microtopographic noise and overparameterisation, which causes machine learning models to overfit rather than generalise biogeochemical gradients. The chosen resolution balances the nearest-neighbour distance of field observations with the soil sample size, avoiding representation errors and Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) complications. By calibrating the grid to the dataset’s spatial density and nutrient autocorrelation range, the model maximises the signal-to-noise ratio, thereby ensuring reliability by representing the pedological environment and preventing spatial overfitting in the ATNN framework.

Derivation of environmental covariates

The approach to predicting soil nutrients involves meticulous data preparation using features from Digital Elevation Models (DEM), Landsat, and Sentinel-2 images. RF

RF is a robust ensemble ML algorithm that has been widely used in soil nutrient prediction studies. The algorithm integrates multiple decision trees to form a forest, where each tree contributes to the final prediction, thereby yielding an accurate result45. Various environmental and agricultural contexts, including soil nutrient mapping, have applied this methodology. For example45,46, RF has been used to predict soil’s chemical and physical properties at different sizes and show how these models can effectively map soil nutrients. Additionally47, RF is very effective for both sorting data into categories and making numerical predictions, which makes it a good choice for estimating both continuous and categorical variables about soil nutrient levels. Finally48,49, shows that RF is effective at predicting total phosphorus levels in the soil, emphasising its ability to handle large amounts of data and complicated relationships between different soil features. In general, in soil nutrient predictions, RF is compelling due to several reasons: it can model multi-way interactions within its input variables quite well, maintain robustness around outliers, and generate excellent results related to soil nutrients.

ADB

ADB, or adaptive boost, is an ensemble learning technique that leverages the predictions of many weak classifiers to generate a strong classifier, therefore improving accuracy in ML models. It was introduced by50, and its basic idea involves iteratively training weak classifiers, usually decision stumps, in weighted versions of the training dataset. Initially, all instances are given equal weights, but in subsequent iterations, ADB increases the weights of misclassified instances, forcing the next classifier to focus on the hard-to-classify examples. The final model is a weighted sum of these weak classifiers, where each classifier’s contribution is proportional to its accuracy. The result is an adaptive process that works to reduce bias and variance effectively, making ADB compelling for binary classification tasks though it can be extended to multiclass problems. However, ADB has some disadvantages: ADB’s performance can deteriorate due to its sensitivity to noisy data and outliers.

GB

GB is an ML method that optimises soil nutrient prediction by building decision trees to correct residual errors. It models complex relationships between soil properties and nutrient levels, increasing precision. GB’s key mechanisms include loss minimisation, additive training, and hyperparameters. Its advantages include feature importance, robustness, and accuracy. Applications include precise fertilising and sustainable practices51. Challenges include computational intensity and the risk of overfitting with noisy data. Many environmental studies have applied this approach to the complex interactions between soil properties and nutrients. For instance52, showed that arbuscular mycorrhizas play an essential role in minimising aluminium phytotoxicity in acidic soils; therefore, it is necessary to determine the correct soil nutrient predictions. At the same time, adding gradient enhancements to soil nutrition prediction models allows researchers to fully consider how soil properties, like aluminium content and nutrient levels, interact, which improves the accuracy and reliability of soil nutrition predictions.

XGB

XGB refers to the implementation of GB machines, which aim to optimise computational speed and model performance. Originally suggested in53, XGB, it became well-known mainly because it [a] effectively manages sparse data and [b] can handle large data sets while enhancing predictions in different ML tasks. It works by sequentially adding new models, typically decision trees, to correct the errors of the previously made models. The system uses a type of gradient descent algorithm to reduce the loss function. Key features of XGB include regularisation to prevent overfitting, parallel tree construction, and intrinsic capabilities for handling missing data54. So far, XGB has been successful in the implementation of GB in ML for soil nutrient prediction. Most fields, including soil science, have applied it. For instance39, researchers applied Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosting (GB) using machine learning methods to predict soil properties. Similarly55, showed XGB’s performance in predicting soil organic matter content, even outperforming other ML methods. Moreover, according to56, the hierarchical ensemble model tree structure, XGB is well-suited for spatial adjustment problems, which are common in soil mapping applications. Among geostatistical methods57, kriging stands out for its ability to provide unbiased predictions with minimal variance, making it ideal for soil nutrient mapping. Soil nutrients, essential for crop productivity, exhibit spatial dependence, which Kriging effectively captures through variogram analysis, a widely used method for estimating soil nutrient values. Variants include Ordinary Kriging (OK), Universal Kriging (UK), Co-Kriging (CK), and Regression Kriging (RK). Ordinary kriging was implemented as shown in Fig. 4. Ordinary kriging, a popular choice for spatial interpolation, balances simplicity and statistical rigour, providing accurate predictions with uncertainty estimates, making it a popular choice in geostatistics. ATNN

The ATNN is an innovative but sophisticated type of neural network that handles data in a sequence by combining attention features, time-based processing, and flexible components. It is suitable for tasks like time-series forecasting, natural language processing (NLP), and speech recognition58. The ATNN has model consists of several components, including an input layer for raw sequential data, an embedding layer, positional encoding, a time processing layer, an attention mechanism, adaptive features, variable parameters, memory improvement techniques, an output layer, and training methods. Fundamental components include multi-head attention, adaptive attention, dynamic parameters, conditional computation, and memory augmentation. The output layer has task-specific heads such as regression, classification, and autoregressive decoding. Training methods include loss functions, optimisation, regularisation, and adaptive training. Applications include time-series forecasting, NLP, speech processing, and healthcare. Advantages of ATNN include context awareness, flexibility, efficiency, scalability, and adaptation. Attention highlights critical sequence segments, handles diverse inputs and tasks, reduces redundancy, and handles long sequences better than pure RNNs59. There are simpler versions called ATNNs that use special kinds of convolutions or sparse attention for devices with less power. Additionally, there are mixed models that combine ATNN with CNNs for various data types, and reinforcement learning is used for making decisions over time. The three main methods for training a hybrid ATNN are ADB, XGB/GB, and RF. ADB involves iteratively training ATNNs, reweighting misclassified samples, and combining weighted predictions. XGB corrects residuals with additive ATNNs, allowing for time-series regression and forecasting. RF combines ATNNs with static features, allowing for average predictions in noisy sequential data. Data preparation involves aligning sequential and static features, and the model design includes an ATNN core, a temporal layer, and attention. Boosting involves weighted/residual-driven training, while bagging involves bootstrap sampling. The process of inference involves analysing ensemble outputs by weighing or averaging them. The best ensemble method depends on the data type, such as rare events in sequences, regression with complex residuals, or robustness in noisy/multimodal data. This research work addresses all the stated shortcomings.

The existing temporal attention models, such as Transformers, Temporal Convolutional Networks, and LSTM+Attention, are limited in environmental and nutrient prediction tasks due to their data-hungry nature and susceptibility to overfitting, particularly with moderately sized tabular datasets. These models struggle with the heterogeneity of tabular data and are unable to establish effective decision boundaries within features like chemical concentrations and temperature. Furthermore, deep neural networks generally face the “black box” dilemma, lacking the interpretability necessary to understand variable contributions in environmental science. The new ATNN framework addresses these shortcomings by conceptualising temporal modelling and prediction as interconnected processes instead of merely employing end-to-end learning. Key innovations of the ATNN framework include targeted temporal feature extraction that assigns dynamic weights to historical data, enhancing prediction accuracy; algorithmic symbiosis for better context awareness, where temporally weighted features are handled by tree-based ensemble learners; and dynamic noise reduction techniques to eliminate irrelevant historical data, which improves metrics like Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Positioning the ATNN framework in relation to existing literature reveals it as a merging of Sequence Representation Learning with Advanced Ensemble Modeling. This approach surpasses traditional machine learning by utilising crucial temporal memory and weighting, allowing for improved time-series data processing without the computational burdens and overfitting risks associated with deep learning. Moreover, it presents a more efficient, localised predictive solution that can effectively manage complex, non-linear, multidimensional datasets, such as those involved in soil nutrient forecasting, by introducing a hybrid feature-fusion mechanism that differentiates between temporal feature extraction and predictive mapping, setting it apart from standard temporal attention methods.

Methodological novelty and positioning of the ATNN framework

The proposed ATNN framework offers a methodological innovation in analysing static, tabular pedological datasets. Unlike traditional attention methods focused on sequential data, the ATNN emphasises feature-wise attention, assessing relationships among soil parameters like pH and moisture. It incorporates a localised self-attention module to dynamically evaluate feature interactions, allowing adaptive feature weighting that minimises the influence of redundant data. This framework addresses gaps in existing ensemble models, which rely on global feature evaluations and often struggle with heterogeneous soil survey data. By integrating strengths from temporal attention and ensemble methods, ATNN preprocesses inputs to create context-aware feature representations for baseline ensemble trees, merging the adaptive learning of deep networks with the robustness of established techniques. Ultimately, it recontextualises the attention mechanisms for complex biogeochemical data, optimising the model architecture for high-dimensional soil chemistry.

The ATNN is a dynamic feature generator that improves gradient boosting and bagging models. Instead of producing a direct prediction, it outputs attention-weighted contextual vectors that are combined with the original feature set and used by foundational learners, like Random Forests (RF), AdaBoost (ADB), Gradient Boosting (GB), and XGBoost (XGB). This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of neural attention for temporal sequencing along with the nonlinear mapping capabilities of tree-based methods.

Cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning

To ensure robust evaluation while accounting for spatial autocorrelation typical of soil datasets, samples were partitioned using a spatially stratified scheme. The fully labelled dataset was split into training (70%), validation (15%), and independent test (15%) sets using spatial block sampling such that nearby samples were placed in the same fold whenever possible to avoid information leakage. For hyperparameter selection and model development, we adopted a fivefold spatial cross-validation on the training set (i.e., the 70% subset was further divided into five spatial folds). This spatial CV preserves local structure and provides conservative estimates of out-of-sample performance compared with random folds. The validation set was used to stop the process early and choose the best model, while the held-out test set was used to report the final performance. All splits used a fixed random seed (seed = 42) to ensure reproducibility, and continuous predictors were standardised (zero mean, unit variance) using parameters estimated only from the training set. Geospatial data management requires distinct geographic blocks for training and testing to avoid memorising location-specific traits. In time-series forecasting, it is essential to train solely on historical data to avoid look-ahead bias. Additionally, maintaining similar statistical distributions across data splits is essential for handling skewed datasets, thereby preventing bias from unrepresentative samples.

Discussion

This study establishes that an integrated approach, fusing multi-source geospatial data with advanced ML, significantly enhances the prediction of soil nutrient levels. The core of our methodological success lies in the ATNN, whose consistent variance outperformed other classifiers, indicating its superior ability to model the complex, non-linear relationships within the data. Our findings robustly align with the broader scientific consensus on the power of remote sensing and ML. However, this study moves beyond confirmation by implementing a more comprehensive data fusion strategy. By synergistically combining DEM, Landsat, and Sentinel-2 imagery, we have captured a wider spectrum of topographic and spectral features, thereby achieving a tangible increase in prediction accuracy. A critical finding is the exceptional performance of the XGB algorithm. As quantified by a suite of robust metrics including RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, alongside higher CCC and R2 scores XGB demonstrated remarkable stability and predictive power across all tested nutrients. This positions it as a premier, reliable tool for digital soil mapping. The most significant advancement of this work is its scope. While previous studies have often been restricted to Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) and basic texture fractions, our analysis provides a comprehensive profile of 12 macro- and micronutrients. This comprehensive evaluation provides an integrated perspective on soil health, representing a significant advancement over the disjointed nutrient analyses prevalent in current literature. Finally, the empirical success of ensemble learning methods, specifically RF and GB, confirms their theoretical advantages. Their strong performance validates that these ensemble techniques are exceptionally well-suited for modelling the high-dimensional, noisy data characteristic of geospatial and environmental science, effectively reducing variance and bias and yielding more generalisable models. This study has several limitations, despite its promising results. Reliance on satellite-based remote sensing data may be subject to various biases because of atmospheric interference, sensor noise, and issues of temporal resolution. The performance might also be site-specific since the model performance will depend considerably upon the quality and resolution of input data. Even though our models used many different environmental factors, the complicated ways they interact and change over time might not be completely reflected, which could affect how well the results apply in other situations. To mitigate data leakage and target redundancy in ATNN-enhanced models, stringent protocols were enacted due to their high predictive performance (often R2 > 0.95). A pre-modelling collinearity audit was performed, removing input covariates with high correlation (r > 0.85) to prevent trivial proxies. Additionally, data splitting was conducted using a specified method, ensuring that testing data remained independent from the training set in both feature space and geographic proximity. All normalisation and hyperparameter tuning were confined to the training folds to maintain the integrity of the blind test set.

Future studies should investigate the use of hyperspectral imagery and on-site soil measurements as supplementary data sources to improve nutrient predictions. Combining deep learning methods with traditional ML algorithms in hybrid models could make them even more accurate and reliable. Further, it would be worth extending the study to different geographical regions with varying climatic and soil conditions so that enough insight may be garnered for the adaptability and scalability of proposed methods. Looking at how soil nutrients change over time, considering seasonal shifts and how land is used, could provide more profound insights and enhance our ability to make predictions. Three synergistic factors account for the superior performance of the ATNN–XGB hybrid in most of these nutrient predictions.

ATNN’s feature-level temporal attention learns to weight spectral indices, topographic derivatives, and meteorological covariates differently across time steps so that the most informative feature-time combinations are in the learning encoding. ATNN is able to fuse these time-varying and time-invariant signals into compact, discriminative representations. By producing low-dimensional encodings that capture both long-range temporal dependencies and cross-feature interactions, ATNN reduces noise and redundancy in the input space while amplifying the predictive signal available to the downstream regressor.

Recommendations

This work is expected to serve as a key foundation for precision agriculture practices and sustainable land management in the near future. Future studies could refine models for other variables, enabling more spatially disaggregated predictions for localised applications. This research highlights that, by using remote sensing technologies, combining environmental data, and applying ML algorithms, we could significantly improve how we understand localised soil nutrient changes. Furthermore, we should explore advanced optimisation techniques, real-time data integration, and simple models to enhance predictions across various fields. Our findings have established a foundation for informed decision-making in agriculture, conservation, and environmental policy, promoting resilience and sustainability not only within our case study area, Bezuidenhout Park in South Africa, but also across wider ecosystems and agricultural landscapes. The implications of this research will deeply influence soil management practices and agronomic strategies. High accuracy in nutrient predictions can lead to more strategic and resource-efficient fertiliser practices, which result in better crop production and lower environmental risks. Our findings pave the way for precision farming, which optimises resource usage for sustainability by combining ML-based models with comprehensive remote sensing data. Future efforts should focus on further refining predictive models by incorporating additional variables, such as soil microbial activity and climate change projections, and enhancing the resolution for local applications. Extending our scope to other regions with diverse environmental conditions will help validate and generalise our findings. This study thus provides a foundation for informed decision-making in agriculture, conservation, and policy-making, contributing to the resilience and sustainability of both urban and rural ecosystems.

Conclusion

The results of this study show that ML methods can effectively analyse different types of environmental data and accurately predict soil nutrient levels as long as the data is thoroughly prepared and the model is carefully checked. In this paper, we looked at how well ML algorithms can predict soil nutrient levels by using a detailed dataset that includes environmental information from DEM, Landsat, and Sentinel-2 images, as well as real-time environmental data from APIs. The integration of these diverse data sources has allowed us to capture nuanced spatial and temporal variations in soil properties, which are critical for effective agricultural management and environmental sustainability. Our approach involved comprehensive data preprocessing, including feature extraction from raster images and API data integration, followed by rigorous analysis and modelling. Choosing and refining the right features was crucial for making our models work better, allowing them to effectively use the complex environmental data. Techniques such as PCA and feature importance analysis guided the selection and optimisation of ML models by offering more profound insights into the key drivers of soil nutrient variability. In our analysis, ATNN-XGB emerged as the top-performing model, consistently delivering superior predictive accuracy across various soil nutrients. Its ability to handle complex interactions among predictors and adapt to nonlinear relationships was crucial for minimising prediction errors and maximising model interpretability. The comparison with RF, ADB, and GB showed that it was very effective at identifying important patterns in soil nutrient changes. Additionally, this study stressed the need to evaluate our model’s accuracy using strong measures like RMSE, MAE, CCC, MAPE, and R2, which helped confirm that our predictions were reliable and could be applied in different situations. By systematically assessing model performance across multiple metrics and nutrient types, we emphasised the versatility and applicability of our methodology in a wide range of agricultural and environmental contexts.
