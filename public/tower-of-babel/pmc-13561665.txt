A Context‐Based Narrative Moral Distress Scale for Nurses and Nursing Students
Authors: Hsun‐Kuei Ko, Wei‐Ting Lai, Shu‐Li Lee, Min‐Tao Hsu, Hsiu‐Yueh Wu, Li‐Min Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraditional moral distress scales primarily measure sources rather than contextualized responses. This study developed and validated the Narrative Moral Distress Scale, a context‐based instrument for assessing multidimensional moral distress in nurses and nursing students. In Phase 1, narrative stories and items were generated from previous qualitative transcripts of 32 nurses, followed by expert validation and face validation. Phase 2 involved testing with 291 nurses and 52 nursing students in Taiwan using Exploratory Factor Analysis, Confirmatory Factor Analysis, and measurement invariance tests. The final 14‐story 29‐item scale comprises five factors. The second‐order model demonstrated acceptable fit, good internal consistency, and robust measurement equivalence, confirming the model's stability. Participants reported moderate moral distress, primarily in value conflict and emotional distress. In clinical practice, the scale could identify moral distress factors driving burnout, enabling managers to design targeted interventions to improve interprofessional communication. In nursing education, it provides a framework for quantifying the efficacy of ethics curricula, while supporting administrative policy efforts to enhance supportive ethical environments and improve professional retention.

Introduction

Moral distress is a pervasive ethical challenge experienced by healthcare professionals, particularly clinical nurses and nursing students. Moral distress has been a core focus of nursing clinical work since the concept was first proposed by Jameton (1984). Moral distress is traditionally defined as the challenge that arises when an individual knows the morally correct action but is prevented from pursuing it by institutional or contextual constraints (Jameton 1984).

While the impact of moral distress on professional nurses is well documented (Aljabery et al. 2024; Giannetta et al. 2020; Houle et al. 2024; Morley et al. 2021; Salari et al. 2022; Tian et al. 2021), a significant methodological gap exists: most traditional measurement tools primarily assess the barriers or sources (antecedents) of moral distress rather than providing a contextualized evaluation of the phenomenon or its multidimensional consequences (Hamric et al. 2012; Houle et al. 2024; Tian et al. 2021). Moral distress is also acute among nursing students during their clinical internships, but existing scales are rarely utilized to evaluate student learning or the efficacy of ethics education (Sasso et al. 2016). This study therefore developed and validated the Narrative Moral Distress Scale (NMDS) through an initial scale development process to address gaps in current moral distress measurement scales.

Background

Moral distress was first proposed by Jameton (1984). Wilkinson (1987) later expanded on this concept by incorporating negative feelings such as anger, frustration, powerlessness, and self‐blame, ensuring that both “moral” and “distress” were explicitly defined. Nathaniel (2006) included the physical and relational consequences in the definition. Austin et al. (2009) introduced the notions of internal constraints and external barriers, as well as the cause of moral obligation. Barlem and Ramos (2015) suggested that moral distress could lead to complex moral actions and behavioral manifestations. The Canadian Nurses Association (2017) added moral reflection to its definition of moral distress. Through the historical development of the above definition of moral distress, it can be seen that moral distress includes several defining characteristics.

The primary distinction between moral distress and general emotional distress is that moral distress arises from the physical and emotional suffering caused by unmet moral values, rather than from a general reaction to workplace stress (Ko et al. 2018; Ko et al. 2019; Morley et al. 2019). Nurses experience moral distress when their altruistic moral values cannot be fulfilled, leading to distress caused by this moral pressure (Ko et al. 2018; Ko et al. 2019). This demonstrates that moral values and moral judgment play a crucial role in moral distress.

The main reasons for moral distress, as summarized in the literature, encompass some key aspects. At the individual level, distress is influenced by personal moral sensitivity, competency, self‐efficacy, and the perceived threat to one's moral integrity (Deschenes et al. 2020; Ko et al. 2018; Lotfi‐Bejestani et al. 2023; Wilkinson 1987; Wilson et al. 2022). At the relational or team level, it emerges from value conflicts between nurses and patients, families, and healthcare peers, often exacerbated by poor communication (Epstein et al. 2019; Ghazanfari et al. 2021; Ko et al. 2019; Usberg et al. 2021). Finally, institutional or systemic barriers—such as resource shortages, hierarchical power structures, and rigid organizational policies—function as potent external barriers that prevent healthcare professionals from fulfilling their altruistic obligations (Aljabery et al. 2024; Hamric et al. 2012). These barriers prevent nurses from fulfilling their commitment to act in patients' best interests, as dictated by their moral values.

The consequences of moral distress include internal emotions such as self‐blame (Aljabery et al. 2024; Ghazanfari et al. 2021) and external conflicts with others (Ko et al. 2018; Nathaniel 2006). Moral distress can lead to physical symptoms, such as insomnia (Jameton 1993; Nathaniel 2006). Over time, these physical and emotional stresses can lead to burnout, manifesting as self‐doubt, diminished life satisfaction, conflicts with medical team members, and even leaving the nursing profession (Aljabery et al. 2024; Austin et al. 2009; Prompahakul et al. 2021). Conversely, some literature highlights the potential positive impacts of moral distress, including learning from failures, recognizing and reflecting on personal moral values, and fostering both personal and professional growth (Ko et al. 2018; Morley et al. 2021).

Based on the literature above and systematic reviews published between 2020 and 2024 (Giannetta et al. 2020; Houle et al. 2024; Tian et al. 2021), nine key factors associated with the moral distress continuum were identified for support instrument development. These factors and their definitions, summarized from the literature, were presented in Table 1.

Various instruments have been developed to quantify moral distress, primarily focusing on clinical triggers and the psychological intensity of professional responses. Foundational tools such as the Moral Distress Scale (MDS) and the Moral Distress Scale‐Revised (MDS‐R) employ dual Likert scales to create a composite measure of frequency and intensity (Corley et al. 2001; Hamric et al. 2012; Tian et al. 2021). Some tools have been adapted for specific environments, such as psychiatric care (MDS‐P) and long‐term care (MDDCS) (Awosoga et al. 2018; Ohnishi et al. 2010). Others include the Moral Distress Thermometer (MDT) for rapid real‐time screening (Wocial and Weaver 2013) and the Moral Distress Risk Scale (MDRS), which identifies distress through underlying risk factors (Schaefer et al. 2019). More recently, the Measure of Moral Distress for Healthcare Professionals (MMD‐HP) was introduced to integrate system‐, unit‐, and patient‐level root causes (Epstein et al. 2019). Despite this variety, systematic reviews highlight a critical methodological gap that the exit scales often conflate the causes of moral distress with its multidimensional factors (Giannetta et al. 2020; Houle et al. 2024; Tian et al. 2021). They also cannot be applied to nursing students.

To fully assess the multifaceted factors of moral distress and recognize that it originates from moral situations, a context‐based assessment approach is essential. The empirical foundation of this study was based on prior qualitative research involving intensive interviews with 32 clinical nurses (Ko et al. 2018; Ko et al. 2019), which yielded rich verbatim transcripts. These transcripts provided a rich source for developing the moral stories and items for the moral distress scale. We utilized these verbatim texts and nine‐factor definitions (Table 1) to construct narrative stories and item sets, aimed at developing a context‐based moral distress scale that uses clinical narratives as a contextual framework to evoke complex, personal experiences of complete moral events. The scale was developed and tested through an initial scale construction process to comprehensively assess the moral distress of nurses and nursing students.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a cross‐sectional design to develop and validate the Narrative Moral Distress Scale (NMDS). The instrument development and validation process was structured in two distinct phases: Phase 1 focused on scale development and initial validity, and Phase 2 involved comprehensive psychometric validation.

Phase 1: Scale Development Stage

Item Generation and Initial Structure

The development of the scale began with a review of the literature on moral distress and the induction of nine factors in Figure 1. To ground these factors in empirical reality, a meticulous rereading of verbatim transcripts and story restoration from 32 clinical nurses (Ko et al. 2018; Ko et al. 2019) was conducted. These transcripts, which restored nearly 100 narrative stories, served as the authentic substrate for identifying the situations of moral distress. From this foundation, 20 narrative stories representing diverse moral situations—such as disease diagnosis, treatment decisions, pain control, and terminal stage care from various clinical units—were purposively selected for the initial version (V1) of the scale. Each story fully incorporated all nine factors of moral distress detailed in Table 1. This approach requires respondents to first process a complex moral story. By anchoring responses to a rich, lived context, the NMDS ensured that each item's development was grounded in the temporal and relational dynamics of clinical practice, thereby providing respondents with a robust, context‐based situational framework.

Development process of the Narrative Moral Distress Scale (NMDS).

The initial version (V1) of the NMDS comprised 20 stories and 190 items (166 forward and 24 reverse questions) (Figure 1). Items were designed using a story item set, aiming to develop 9 to 10 items per story and approximately 1 to 2 items for each factor. Taking NMDS Story 1 as an example, the development of the first item for value conflict was based on the story's contexts (Supporting Information 1). Responses were measured on a 4‐point Likert scale, with 4 indicating strong agreement and 1 indicating strong disagreement.

Expert Validity

Expert validation was conducted by five nursing scholars or clinical nurse experts familiar with moral distress. Each item was rated on a 1‐to‐4 scale based on three criteria: “relevance with the factor concept,” “accuracy in conveying the factor concept,” and “alignment with the meanings of the factor concept.” Items with a Content Validity Index (CVI) below 0.80 were eliminated (Cronbach and Meehl 1955). Following this process, the scale achieved an overall CVI of 0.98 (Figure 1).

Face Validity

Face validity was performed to evaluate the readability of the scale items. Ten clinical nurses and ten nursing students were invited to rate each item on a 1‐to‐4 scale based on the “clarity in understanding the items,” “ease of understanding the items,” and “ease of responding.” Items receiving a score of 2 or below were removed based on participant feedback (Figure 1).

Phase 2: Psychometric Testing and Data Analysis

Sample and Setting

The study recruited a diverse validation sample of clinical nurses in a teaching medical center and final‐year undergraduate nursing students at a university in southern Taiwan. Inclusion criteria for nurses required that participants be on active clinical duty, with no restrictions on age or years of professional seniority, to ensure broad representation of experience levels. Inclusion criteria for the student group were nursing seniors in their final year, as this population is highly susceptible to moral distress during clinical internship. Those who were unwilling to answer or whose answers were incomplete were excluded.

Data Collection

A total of 405 scales were distributed: 353 were provided to clinical nurses and 52 to nursing students. A sample size exceeding 300 is considered sufficient to avoid offending estimates in data analysis (Hair et al. 2019). For nurses, data collection spanned diverse units, including intensive care units, emergency rooms, operating rooms, oncology wards, hospice departments, medical‐surgical wards, obstetrics and gynecology units, and psychiatric wards. For students, the scale was distributed and collected in a classroom. To justify the statistical aggregation of the two groups, the Independent Samples t‐test was conducted to compare mean distress scores. Furthermore, a multigroup confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to test for measurement invariance across groups. A measurement weights invariance model was tested by constraining all factor loadings to be equal across groups (Putnick and Bornstein 2016).

Ethical Considerations

The study received approval from the Kaohsiung Medical University Institutional Review Board (KMUHIRB‐E(I)‐20 210 382) in Taiwan. Ethical guidelines were adhered to by providing a detailed “participant instruction” section on the first page of the scale to obtain informed consent. To ensure privacy and confidentiality, participants sealed completed scales in the original envelope and returned them to the researchers. Furthermore, participant rights were safeguarded by ensuring that no direct relationship of interest existed between the researchers and participants.

Item Analysis and Internal Consistency Reliability

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics 20.0. Item analysis was performed for each factor, calculating the mean, maximum, and minimum values. Items were removed if they failed to discriminate between high‐scoring and low‐scoring groups, or if the item‐to‐total correlation coefficient was lower than 0.40 or the inter‐item correlation coefficient was lower than 0.30 (Hair et al. 2019) (Figure 1). Internal consistency reliability for the factors and the overall scale was assessed using Cronbach's alpha.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

According to Hair et al. (2019), when the goal is to identify latent structures for the first time, principal component analysis (PCA) results are highly consistent with those of principal axis factoring (PAF), which typically yields only slightly lower factor loadings. Therefore, for data reduction and factor identification, principal component analysis (PCA) was utilized as the primary EFA extraction method (Hair et al. 2019). Rotation method: Varimax with Kaiser normalization. Retention criterion: Eigenvalue > 1 (Kaiser 1960). The recommended threshold of the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.60 (Kaiser 1974). The significant indicator of Bartlett's test of sphericity was p < 0.05 (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019). In the rotated component matrix, loadings below 0.40 were suppressed as recommended by Stevens (2002) (Figure 1). Parallel analysis was also performed to ensure the robustness of the factor structure.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and Construct Validity

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using Structural Equation Modeling and path analysis in SPSS Amos 29.0. CFA was performed using Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation. To confirm construct validity, several criteria were applied: Standardized factor loadings (Std.), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) (Hair et al. 2019; Fornell and Larcker 1981; Kenny 2024).

Model fit was assessed using established criteria: The normed chi‐square (χ 2/DF), absolute fit indices (GFI, AGFI), RMSEA and SRMR, and incremental fit indices (TLI and CFI) (Hair et al. 2019) (Figure 1).

Discriminant validity was evaluated using both the Fornell and Larcker (1981) criterion and the HTMT ratio. Measurement invariance of the second‐order NMDS model was tested through a multi‐group analysis based on a 50% random split of the sample. Robust measurement equivalence was confirmed by testing for minimal changes in Δχ 2 and ΔCFI/ΔTLI across increasingly restrictive constraints (Putnick and Bornstein 2016) (Figure 1).

Results

A total of 346 scales were returned, resulting in a response rate of 85.4%. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion. After excluding three incomplete scales, 343 valid responses were analyzed. The participants consisted of 291 nurses and 52 final‐year undergraduate nursing students. There was no significant difference in the mean moral distress scores between the nurses and students. A multigroup confirmatory factor analysis indicated the chi‐square difference test between the configural invariance model and the measurement weights invariance model was non‐significant, Δχ 2 (24) = 26.938, p = 0.307. The data were combined for analysis. The mean age of the participants was 34.0 years, and the average nursing seniority was 13.2 years. The participants' characteristics were described in Table 2.

Item Analysis and Scale Validation

The initial nine‐factor structure of the Narrative Moral Distress Scale (NMDS) was reduced through item analysis, exploratory factor analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis (Figure 1).

Following the analysis, the factors “internal barriers” and “value reflection” were removed because they did not meet the criteria for satisfactory validation and reliability. Furthermore, the original factors of “moral value,” “moral judgment,” and “moral conflict” exhibited excessively high correlation coefficients. Consequently, “moral value” was removed, and “moral judgment” and “moral conflict” were merged and renamed as “value conflict.” All reverse questions were ultimately excluded from the final version, primarily because their item discrimination was statistically insignificant or their factor loadings were low.

The final validated scale structure of the NMDS consists of 14 stories and 29 forward items (Figure 1). The final scale measures five factors: 5 items on value conflict (VC); 8 items on emotional distress (ED); 6 items on self‐doubt (SD); 5 items on physical symptoms (PHS); and 5 items on career harm (CH) (Figure 2).

Exploratory Factor Analysis

The five extracted components cumulatively explained 64.10% of the total variance (Table 3). Rotation sums of squared loadings represented the post‐rotation variance distribution, which balanced component contributions. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.923, which was classified as marvelous (Hutcheson and Sofroniou 1999). Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ 2 = 5737.538, df = 406, p < 0.001), indicating that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis (Table 3). Parallel analysis confirmed that the actual eigenvalue for the fifth factor is 1.41 (Table 3), higher than the mean eigenvalue (1.33) and the 95th percentile eigenvalue (1.36), supporting the 5‐factor structure (Supporting Information 2). Most retained loadings exceeded 0.60, indicating good practical significance (Hair et al. 2019). The pattern showed a structure with each item loading cleanly onto one factor (Table 4).

Reliability and Descriptive Statistics of the Scale

Internal consistency reliability was acceptable. The Cronbach's alpha for the overall scale was 0.924, and for the individual factors listed from highest to lowest were as follows: physical symptoms (α = 0.895), self‐doubt (α = 0.802), career harm (α = 0.797), emotional distress (α = 0.794), and value conflict (α = 0.724).

To facilitate clinical interpretation, we split the degree of moral distress following the guidelines of Gelman and Park (2009). The sample was divided according to the 75th and 25th percentiles to define high, medium, and low levels of moral distress. The higher NMDS scores indicate higher moral distress. The participants in this study reported moderate levels of moral distress (Table 2). Discussion

This study aimed to initially develop and validate the Narrative Moral Distress Scale (NMDS), a context‐based instrument designed to assess moral distress in nurses and nursing students comprehensively. The NMDS differentiates itself by moving away from situational triggers within individual items of the existing scales toward a structured, context‐based approach. The NMDS yields a stable second‐order factor structure comprising five measurable factors: value conflict, emotional distress, self‐doubt, physical symptoms, and career harm. This structure, which can assess the multifaceted factors of moral distress, contrasts sharply with traditional instruments (Corley et al. 2001; Epstein et al. 2019; Hamric et al. 2012; Schaefer et al. 2019; Tian et al. 2021).

The NMDS demonstrated acceptable psychometric characteristics, supporting its use as a reliable and valid tool. From a psychometric perspective, while individual narratives introduce clinical diversity, the hierarchical second‐order measurement model is designed to isolate the shared variance belonging to the latent construct of moral distress. The acceptable model fit and significant factor loadings obtained suggest that responses are not merely reflections of scenario‐specific characteristics but are anchored in the underlying dimensions of moral distress (Hair et al. 2019). By providing a consistent set of items across different narratives, the NMDS ensures that the narrative design facilitates deeper engagement without compromising the construct validity of the latent measurement model.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) results explained 64.10% of the total variance, exceeding the 60% threshold recommended in the social sciences (Hair et al. 2019). The Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) results confirmed a good model fit. Although the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for emotional distress (0.468) was slightly below the ideal threshold of 0.50 (Fornell and Larcker 1981), it remains within the acceptable psychometric range for newly developed tools (Chin 1998; Hair et al. 2019). Based on its favorable CR value and significant factor loading, this factor was retained. Internal consistency of NMDS was acceptable. The items within each factor effectively converge on their respective latent constructs. The NMDS further established discriminant validity and robust measurement invariance, indicating the model's stability and generalizability. The results address a widespread lack of measurement equivalence tests of moral distress tools (Tian et al. 2021), offering future researchers a distinct alternative to other moral distress scales.

The item analysis and reliability testing process led to the exclusion of the original factors “internal barriers” and “value reflection.” The concept of “value reflection” is not part of the original theory of moral distress; its inclusion is based on recent research findings (Canadian Nursing Association 2017; Ko et al. 2018). It was reduced to only four items after expert and face validation, but ultimately was not included in the final version of the NMDS. The results suggest that consistently quantifying these self‐reflection domains remains a psychometric challenge. Reflection remains essential for fostering moral growth through education. It is more likely a dynamic psychiatric and cognitive process, highlighting the need for longitudinal assessment to capture the temporal progression of moral distress or the effectiveness of educational applications (Ko et al. 2018; Nathaniel 2006).

Among the five factors, “Value Conflict” and “Emotional Distress” exhibited the highest mean scores. This highlights that nurses and students perceive the most intense impact of moral distress through internal value conflicts and subsequent emotional distress. Of the 29 items, 10 had an average score exceeding 3.0, with “Value Conflict” and “Emotional Distress” each containing five items. On the other hand, six items had a mean score below 2.45, including five items from the “physical symptoms” and one from the “career harm.” Although moral distress might influence career harm (Austin et al. 2009; Jameton 1993; Ko et al. 2018; Prompahakul et al. 2021), it was one of several factors influencing career choices rather than having a definitive impact. The factor of “physical symptoms” was identified as having the lowest scores, indicating that while participants experience moral distress, its impact on physical symptoms was minimal, with the predominant effects being emotional. However, these low scores may also reflect participants attributing physical symptoms to illness rather than moral distress. This suggests that low scores on the physical symptoms in the NMDS should still be interpreted with caution, as the underlying physical damage may be masked.

The narrative scenarios in the NMDS introduce cultural interpretability. For instance, scenarios involving treatment decisions may be shaped by the Eastern virtue of filial piety, where family members may conceal a terminal diagnosis from the patient (Supporting Information 1). While these narratives evoke moral distress among nurses in Taiwan, the psychometric functioning may vary in different cultures (Houle et al. 2024). While further research on cross‐cultural measurement invariance is necessary, the NMDS may offer a culturally congruent assessment option tailored for Eastern healthcare contexts.

Participants in this study reported moderate levels of moral distress, a finding consistent with other research involving nurses globally (Keilman et al. 2023; Salari et al. 2022; Showalter et al. 2022; Tomura 2023).

To address potential differences in how indicators were interpreted, a multigroup confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to test for measurement invariance across nurse and student groups. The chi‐square difference test was non‐significant, indicating that the equality constraints did not significantly worsen model fit. This result supports the retention of the null hypothesis that factor loadings are equivalent across groups, suggesting that the NMDS items carry the same psychological meaning for both populations (Putnick and Bornstein 2016). Furthermore, no significant differences in mean moral distress scores reinforce this aggregation. The results suggest that despite differences in clinical responsibility and autonomy, both nurses and students undergo similar experiences when faced with moral distress (Mæland et al. 2021; Sasso et al. 2016).

During student internships, they engage in patient care but, unlike nurses, cannot make care decisions and are primarily observers and learners. Despite this, students hold their moral values and judgments. Mæland et al. (2021) found that when nursing students describe their most challenging clinical situations, these situations were often related to moral distress. Students experienced conflicts between their moral judgments regarding patient treatment and those of the medical teams, leading to self‐blame. Such conflict can diminish patient care satisfaction, lead to physical issues, and provoke emotional responses, such as depression and anxiety (Mæland et al. 2021; Sasso et al. 2016), similar to those experienced by nurses. If students do not learn to recognize and manage moral distress during their education, it may continue to impact their physical and emotional well‐being, as well as the effectiveness and safety of patient care once they enter clinical practice. Students might also fear entering the clinical workforce and lack the confidence and courage to act (Sasso et al. 2016). Given that nursing students are the future workforce in clinical nursing, experiencing similar moral distress to that of nurses could affect their willingness to pursue a nursing career and contribute to a shortage of nursing staff. Therefore, it is crucial for nursing education to focus on helping students recognize their personal moral values, develop moral judgment, perform moral actions, and address moral distress through reflection (Ko et al. 2020). However, given that students account for 15.2% of the total sample, pooling nurses and students as a research group remains a limitation of interpretation. While the non‐significant chi‐square difference (p = 0.307) supports the retention of the null hypothesis regarding factor loading equivalence across groups (Putnick and Bornstein 2016), the small size of the student group may limit the statistical power to detect subtle variations in how moral conflict is perceived during clinical socialization. Therefore, these explanations should be considered as directions for future research, rather than conclusions for the student population as a separate entity.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged in this study. First, the absence of comparisons with existing moral distress instruments limits the evidence for external and criterion‐related validity. Second, the use of the same dataset for both EFA and CFA may result in overfitting, potentially capturing sampling errors unique to this cohort rather than generalizable population trends. Third, the temporal stability of the NMDS remains unknown, as test–retest reliability was not assessed. Therefore, the current findings should be considered preliminary psychometric validation. Future longitudinal studies with independent cross‐validation samples will be essential to ensure the NMDS's stability and universal applicability.

Participants were exclusively recruited from institutions in southern Taiwan, primarily affiliated with a single medical center and a single medical university. This highly localized sampling and affiliation with a teaching hospital may limit the generalizability of the results to other countries, healthcare systems, or academic settings. Another limitation related to generalizability is the inherent narrative cultural differences in the NMDS scale. Because the NMDS relies on narrative context, cultural differences in moral judgments may influence participants' responses, thus limiting the scale's applicability across different cultures. Future research should focus on testing for cross‐cultural measurement invariance.

Although the inclusion of final‐year undergraduate nursing students improved the applicability of this scale to nurses and students, the small student population limits the ability to draw independent psychometric conclusions for this specific group. Future research should recruit a larger student sample and replicate its psychometric performance to ensure robust validity and draw definitive conclusions regarding its applicability as an independent tool for the student population.

Conclusions

This study initially developed and validated the Narrative Moral Distress Scale (NMDS). The NMDS comprises five factors demonstrated through 14 narrative scenarios and 29 forward‐scored items measured on a 4‐point Likert scale. The reliability and validity of the NMDS were acceptable. The NMDS is recommended for assessing moral distress among nurses and students in clinical practice and educational settings to evaluate the efficacy of moral education and clinical interventions, and to become a reliable psychometric tool for future moral distress research.

Given the limitations of this study, future research is recommended to employ longitudinal designs to explore the time‐dependent relationships between moral stress and outcome progression, thereby significantly enhancing the clinical or educational utility of the NMDS. Furthermore, to further refine the psychometrics and theoretical framework, future studies should apply the NMDS to populations in different geographic regions to enhance its external validity and universality.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

In nursing education, the NMDS provides a framework for assessing student learning and professional readiness. Ethics nursing educators can develop narrative scenarios integrated into problem‐based learning and simulation‐based training, helping students develop the “phronesis” or practical wisdom needed to navigate complex clinical situations and defend patient rights before they enter the workforce. For clinical managers, the Narrative Moral Distress Scale serves as a diagnostic tool to identify the specific components of moral distress. Administrators can design targeted interventions, such as communication workshops or ethics meetings, to improve interprofessional collaboration and safeguard nursing autonomy. In nursing policy, the NMDS supports administrative efforts to foster supportive ethical environments that enhance professional identity and retention. By acknowledging the comprehensive spectrum of moral distress, policymakers can better advocate for systemic changes that protect the integrity of the nursing workforce and address the global nursing shortage.
