Podcast Episode 1: A Pragmatic Overview of Heart Failure in Patients with Diabetes for Endocrinologists, Primary Care Physicians and Noncardiologist Clinicians
Authors: Nihar R. Desai, Silvio E. Inzucchi, Eugene E. Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure is a growing major public health concern. Total medical costs for heart failure in the USA are expected to rise to more than US $70 billion by 2030, and this is expected to rise as the prevalence of heart failure increases owing to an aging population and the concurrent increase in risk factors for heart failure such as obesity, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. Heart failure affects 22% of patients with type 2 diabetes and is frequently the first presentation of cardiovascular disease in these patients. One of the key challenges in heart failure treatment is early detection and diagnosis. Heart failure can present similarly to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and is often misdiagnosed. Late diagnosis of heart failure can lead to delays in treatment initiation and poor patient outcomes. Clinicians other than specialist cardiologists, particularly endocrinologists and primary care physicians in the case of patients with diabetes, can play a key role in the early detection and diagnosis of heart failure. In this podcast series of three episodes, a cardiovascular specialist, an endocrinologist, and a primary care physician will provide practical guidance for primary care physicians on optimal identification and management of heart failure in patients with diabetes. In the first episode, we give a pragmatic overview of heart failure and practical guidance on how clinicians can identify patients most at risk of developing heart failure, tips for early detection and screening, and ultimately, how to prevent progression of heart failure in these patients. Dr Eugene Wright [0:08:58]

So, based on that, Nihar, now that we’ve identified the patient at high risk, as a primary care physician or a noncardiology specialist, what screening and testing should we be focusing on?

Dr Nihar Desai [0:09:11]

So, I think biomarkers are really important to support the detection of cardiac dysfunction at an early stage.

Measurement of the natriuretic peptide or high sensitivity cardiac troponin on at least a yearly basis is recommended to identify the presence of early-stage heart failure [7, 9]. Assessing natriuretic peptides may be done more frequently, if there’s a change in clinical status or concern for heart failure emerges [9]. Natriuretic peptides are released from the heart and cleaved into the hormonally active B-type natriuretic peptide, or BNP, and the inactive N-terminal prohormone of BNP, or the NT-proBNP. Both BNP and NT-proBNP are viewed as comparable in their utility in detecting heart failure but differ in their circulating levels [18].

To determine heart failure risk, a useful cutoff value for BNP is over 50 pg/mL. For NT-proBNP, this is over 125 pg/mL, and for high sensitivity, cardiac troponin is over the 99th percentile for the healthy patient population [9].

Serial measurements and looking at biomarker levels as continuous variables have been shown in clinical trials to be a key tool in early detection of heart failure. I’ll give you one example from the EXAMINE trial in patients with type 2 diabetes, two NT-proBNP measurements spaced 6 months apart were able to identify risk for heart failure. Those with two elevated measurements were at highest risk and those patients with an increased measurement relative to baseline had rising risk for heart failure [19].

It’s also worth mentioning that biomarker results should be analyzed in the context of the complete clinical examination. Factors such as age, the presence of kidney disease or atrial fibrillation may lead to increased levels of these prognostic biomarkers without necessarily being an indication of progressing heart failure [20–22]. On the other hand, obesity may lower natriuretic peptide concentrations, even if there is significant risk of heart failure [23].
