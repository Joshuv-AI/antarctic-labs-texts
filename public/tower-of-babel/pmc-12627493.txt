Whistles characterisation using artificial intelligence reveals responses of short-beaked common dolphins to a bio-inspired acoustic mitigation device for fishing nets
Authors: Loïc Lehnhoff, Hervé Glotin, Yves Le Gall, Eric Menut, Hélène Peltier, Alain Pochat, Krystel Pochat, Olivier Van Canneyt, Bastien Mérigot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding cetacean whistles is crucial for assessing their social interactions, behaviours, and responses to anthropic activities. Identifying the various types of whistles present in acoustic recordings is often challenging, but necessary for this purpose. To facilitate this process, we have developed a semi-automated deep learning approach called the “Draw Your Own Contours” (DYOC) method. This is available as an open-source software along with its associated dataset. It utilises YOLOv8m and ResNet18 to identify whistle contours. DYOC was applied to 808 minutes of audio recordings of wild, free-ranging short-beaked common dolphins from the Bay of Biscay, France. It enabled the annotation of 8,730 whistle contours, six times faster than manual annotation. These recordings were associated with observations of dolphin behaviour, the presence of fishing nets, and the activation of the DOLPHINFREE acoustic beacon. Analyses revealed that these variables affected the signal-to-noise ratio, the number of inflections, and the frequency and/or duration of recorded whistles. This study provides the first characterisation of whistle features for a population of short-beaked common dolphins in the Bay of Biscay. The annotation of whistle contours using the DYOC method helped reveal the complex acoustic behaviour of dolphins in response to external variables.

Introduction

Sounds are extensively used by cetacean species in all aspects of their lives. Echolocation clicks are mainly used by odontocetes for spatial orientation1–3 and prey detection4, but can also be used in social interactions in the form of buzzes5. On the other hand, whistles are used most often to communicate between individuals6–8 during social interactions. Whistles are frequency-modulated narrow-band signals, with durations ranging from tenths of a second to several seconds9. For cetaceans, their duration varies from about 0.1 to 5 seconds, with very distinct frequency ranges depending on species. For example, whales are known to emit low-frequency whistles (<200 Hz10), while delphinids emit whistles at higher frequencies (1–25 kHz11). Traditionally, whistle contours are annotated manually in order to study them in detail. However, this initial step is particularly time-consuming and repetitive, making it an ideal task for the development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) models and other algorithms with the aim of facilitating or speeding up the process. Several fully automated approaches already exist: based on deep learning methods (e.g12–15.), machine learning techniques (e.g16,17.), or more analytical methods (e.g18–21.). Some are even integrated into software, such as PAMGUARD (e.g22.). However, while these methods facilitate the initial annotation of whistle contours, they are not equally effective for all datasets. Furthermore, using fully automated annotation tools can make manual corrections difficult, because they are not part of the prediction process. Contour extraction is a challenging task, even for a human annotator, and further developments are still needed to improve the identification and characterisation of whistles in audio recordings.

Whistles of delphinids have been extensively studied through research carried out on the common bottlenose dolphin Tursiops truncatus (Montagu, 1821)23, with numerous experiments conducted on both captive and free-ranging individuals. Whistles of other delphinid species are relatively less well-documented. Those of the short-beaked common dolphin Delphinus delphis (Linnaeus, 1758) were first described from audio recordings made on captive dolphins off the coast of California6. Since then, many studies have described the repertoire of this species24,25 for a variety of populations. The main results from past literature showed that those whistles vary for neighbouring and distant populations of common dolphin subspecies9,26–30 and other variables. Short-beaked common dolphins located in South Africa and in the Western North Atlantic Ocean are also believed to produce signature whistles similar to those described for common bottlenose dolphins31,32, with highly stereotyped shapes.

In the Bay of Biscay, France, short-beaked common dolphins are threatened by fishery bycatch with worrying levels of strandings since 201633. The latest reliable estimates show that 8,950 (CI95% [6,710; 12,630]) individuals died as a result of bycatch in 202134. The population of short-beaked common dolphins in the Bay of Biscay is estimated at around 634,286 (CI95%: 352,227 - 1,142,213) individuals33. However, this number varies depending on year and sources33–35. In addition, some models are rather pessimistic concerning the long term state of this population of dolphins11. In response, numerous projects have been launched in an attempt to find ways of keeping these dolphins away from fishing nets. During the DOLPHINFREE project (“Dolphins free from fishery bycatch”), a bio-inspired acoustic beacon prototype, emitting an informative signal, was developed and tested at sea from research boats and onboard fishing vessels36

As part of the DOLPHINFREE project, we recorded the signals emitted by wild free-ranging short-beaked common dolphins in Brittany, France36. The whistle repertoire of this population is poorly known, even though it could provide insights into dolphin social behaviours, group dynamics, responses to environment stimuli and individual recognition9,26,27,37, and could even be linked to bycatch events38. We therefore aimed at conducting a comprehensive analysis of the whistles of these animals as part of the DOLPHINFREE experiments.

However, annotating animal vocalisations that vary in time and frequency is a difficult and time-consuming task, regardless of the species being studied. As stated above, further developments are still needed to improve the accuracy of models used for the identification of whistles in audio recordings. In order to accurately study the whistle contours of dolphins that were recorded during the DOLPHINFREE experiments, we created a semi-automatic method to speed up the annotation process. The method has been designed to represent a compromise between accuracy and complexity. It utilises AI models to predict whistle contours, and incorporates manual correction steps to directly modify prediction errors, within the prediction process. This tool was applied to the DOLPHINFREE dataset, with the objective of investigating the characteristics of the whistles emitted by short-beaked common dolphins in response to the DOLPHINFREE bio-inspired beacon, the presence or absence of a fishing net, and the animal’s behavioural states when conducting experiments.

Methods

Data collection

Experiments were conducted with wild free-ranging short-beaked common dolphins at sea off the coast of Brittany, France (Figure S1, supplementary material), during the summers of 2020, 202136 and 2022. Experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (see Institutional Review Statement). The experimental procedures were approved under request no.26568 by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation of Languedoc-Roussillon (CEEA-LR).

Opportunistic encounters enabled us to test prototypes of a bio-inspired beacon for the DOLPHINFREE project. Tests involved visual observations from the boat and the recording of acoustic signals emitted by dolphins. They were based on the observation of three main variables: behavioural state of the dolphins (attracted to the boat, foraging, socialising, travelling or milling/resting), absence/presence of a fishing net, and activation state of the beacon. These experiments are described in detail in a previous publication36. In total, 83 groups of dolphins were encountered during the experiments, and 105 trials were conducted (some groups were recorded several times).

A total of 808 minutes of audio recording were collected using a single hydrophone ’Ocean Sonics icListen HF’ (dynamic range: 118 dB, sensitivity: −170 dB re V/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upmu }$$\end{document}Pa) with 512 kHz sampling rate and 32-bit depth. We chose to group observations and audio recordings into 1-minute bin intervals as a more practical solution. Thus, each audio file is associated with the behavioural state observed during its recording, as well as with the activation state of the DOLPHINFREE acoustic beacon during that minute: off (before or after its activation), on (while activated) or switched on/off during a recording (which is the moment when the beacon’s signal is introduced into the acoustic environment).

Data annotation

Annotating all the whistles in our whole dataset manually would be very time-consuming. In this context, AI based models are of particular interest. Pre-trained models can be fine-tuned on a small sample of a dataset in order to make predictions on the whole dataset. Therefore, we annotated the whistle contours of a small sample of the DOLPHINFREE dataset (43 files or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}5% of the recordings, chosen arbitrarily) using a custom-made annotation tool39. Annotations were made on spectrograms computed from raw audio recordings resampled at 96,000 Hz, with the Hann window, a window size of 1024 samples (11 ms) and a hop length of 512 samples (5 ms), represented on a linear frequency scale.

It should be noted that dolphins often vocalise in groups, meaning that whistles of different individuals overlap. Therefore, during the annotation process, the bins of the spectrograms were limited to a minimum value of −60 dBFS (Decibel relative to Full Scale) in order to only consider the whistles of highest energy for contour extraction. For similar reasons, only the fundamental frequencies of the whistles were manually annotated. While harmonics could be studied, their energy decreases drastically with distance and dolphin orientation, more so than fundamentals40. This resulted in the manual annotation of 1,750 whistles in total.

Due to the presence of many individuals vocalising simultaneously during the experiments, whistle contours often overlap. Even for human annotators, annotating these cases is a difficult task. Therefore, we considered segments of whistles that could not easily be connected to other whistles as independent, regardless of their sizes. However, to avoid the over-representation of these potential fragments of other whistles in the training dataset, hard thresholds were applied in order to keep only the whistles with a duration>100 ms and a Signal-to-Noise Ratio >20 dB (SNR, computed as the difference in dB between the mean level of a signal and the mean level of ambient noise, for the frequency range). Thus, a subset of 431 whistles corresponding to these constraints was kept for training DYOC (distributions shown in Figure S2, supplementary material).

The ”Draw Your Own Contours” (DYOC) extraction method

The detection and extraction of whistle contours is challenging. Whistles in spectrograms can be compared to objects in images. To identify these objects, a model has to achieve satisfactory performance in both object detection and instance segmentation tasks. Object detection is the task of locating objects within an image by drawing bounding boxes around them. Instance segmentation is a more advanced task that precisely identifies an object within a bounding box, pixel by pixel. When applied to whistle contours, this means that the method will need to define a bounding box around each whistle and then identify the contours of the whistle within this box.

The DYOC structure

The DYOC (“Draw Your Own Contours”) method uses two efficient models: YOLOv8m41,42 and ResNet1843, which are trained and tested on our dataset using PyTorch44. The efficiency of YOLO applied to marine bioacoustic data was inspired by recent works45 and has already been demonstrated in other studies46–52. ResNet is a standard model, widely used for image processing tasks. Pretrained weights were used for these models, as transfer learning has been shown to be more effective than using randomly assigned weights for similar tasks53. The structure of DYOC was designed to be minimalist.

In order to predict the contours of whistles, the method directly takes audio files (resampled at 64 kHz) as inputs, and converts them to spectrograms (Hann window, size: 1,024 samples, hop length: 512 samples). Each 6-second segment of these spectrograms is then resized to a 640x640 pixels image in order to match YOLO’s input size. In order to facilitate the recognition of whistles despite ambient noises, contrast is also added using a gamma correction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} = 0.5) before making predictions. YOLO then predicts the time-frequency limits of the whistles within each square image using bounding boxes (bboxes). YOLO only predicts a single ’whistle’ class. Then, an image is extracted from the coordinates of each bbox, and resized to 224x224 pixels that can be used by ResNet18. Finally, ResNet18 performs a regression on these images and outputs a prediction of the whistle contour coordinates: 112 y-positions corresponding to uniformly distributed points along the x-axis. Initially, YOLO was designed to predict the position of objects in complex landscapes and photographic scenes using bboxes. Thus, we expected that a simple whistle detection task should be a fairly straightforward process for this model. With regard to ResNet18, the model performs a regression to predict the contours of the whistles extracted from YOLO predictions. Its purpose is to achieve accurate annotation of the simplest isolated whistles. It is not expected that the model will be able to make accurate predictions in complex situations where whistles are overlapping with other sounds, as these situations are also challenging for a human annotator to judge. This is why steps with manual checks are included in DYOC (see Fig. 1), allowing for rapid correction of its predictions. By design, DYOC is a semi-automatic method. Intended as a tool to assist annotation, not as a fully automated annotation tool.

DYOC training and performance

DYOC is trained in two parts, by independently training YOLO and ResNet18. YOLO uses segmented spectrograms as input and output bboxes. ResNet18 uses images of isolated whistles to predict the coordinates of their frequencies along the time axis of the image. We used ground truth bboxes to train ResNet18 in order to avoid propagating YOLO’s prediction errors to the ResNet18 training. In order to train DYOC, the subset of 431 whistles was split into train/test/validation subsets at proportions of 80%/10%/10%. Test scores were computed using an 8-fold cross-validation.

The YOLO component of DYOC was trained using yolov8m with the following hyperparameters: a maximum of 500 epochs, a batch size of 32, a patience of 50 epochs and an SGD optimiser. The remaining parameters were left at their default values. During the evaluation process, the TP (true positive), FP (false positive), and FN (false negative) rates were utilised to calculate precision, defined as the proportion of relevant detections in predictions (or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$$\end{document}) and recall, defined as the proportion of relevant detections made (or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$\end{document}). In addition, YOLO uses a specific metric for its evaluation: the mAP (mean Average Precision), which is the average of the area under precision-recall curves for different confidence thresholds.

ResNet18 was trained with the following hyperparameters: a maximum of 500 epochs, a batch size of 64, a learning rate of 0.001, a patience of 50 epochs and a Adam optimiser with a learning rate scheduler (gamma: 0.98). For evaluation, we mainly used the RMSE (Root Mean Square Error, where the errors are the distances between predicted and ground truth coordinates), as it could easily be interpreted as a proportion of the image size.

Spectrograms were converted to and treated as black and white images (0–255 pixel values correspond to [−60, −40] dBFS values in spectrograms). To enhance performances, common data augmentation transformations were included for the training of our models: white padding, vertical and horizontal flipping as well as slight colour changes. For ResNet18, we created synthetic whistle contours in order to train the model on a wide variety of shapes, not present in our initial dataset. These were obtained by generating points at random coordinates in an image, which were then linked by a quadratic spline. Columns of black pixels were added to simulate echolocation clicks, and white noise to simulate ambient noise. Finally, we added contrast to all our images using a gamma correction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} = 0.5). DYOC in practice

DYOC predicted whistle bboxes in a spectrogram with a mAP50 of 69.2% on average. Precision and recall scores in Table 1 are comparable to those of other approaches on neighbouring populations of dolphins21. Speed performances are described in the supplementary material. These scores show that YOLO makes few errors, but this is not sufficient to permit its use in DYOC without verifying its predictions. Even if there is still room for improvement, these results are satisfactory for our purposes, as our aim is only to accelerate the annotation process, not to make perfect predictions. It should also be noted that there were many overlapping whistles in the initial manually annotated dataset. These cases are difficult to handle. Manual annotation relies on contextual information, which was not provided to DYOC.

ResNet18 regression makes only a few errors: RMSE is 6.1% of the image size on average. This is satisfactory for further use: predicted contours are arguably of equal or better quality than human labels in easy cases of annotation (see Fig. 4a & b), but predictions are poor when the model is confronted with low-SNR whistles (Fig. 4c) or overlapping signals (Fig. 4d). This means that, depending on YOLO bboxes predictions, the annotator will only need to correct the predictions in the most difficult cases, thus saving a good amount of time. We were therefore able to apply it to the DOLPHINFREE dataset. For the most complex cases, however, manual annotation verification was still needed. False positive identifications of harmonics, for example, were manually removed. In all, around 60 hours were required to manually annotate the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}1500 whistles used in training. Using DYOC, it took only 10 hours to annotate the same quantity of whistles. Therefore, the approach enabled us to annotate whistle contours in audio recordings 6 times faster than using manual annotation. The method could be further improved by using more training data or by filtering the audio data, but DYOC is only a semi-automated annotation method, with no need for perfect annotation of whistle contours. Its aim is to facilitate the annotation process, thereby expediting the examination of whistle contours, which constitutes the core objective of this investigation. Case study: DOLPHINFREE experiments

Overview of results

Using DYOC trained on our manually annotated dataset, we were able to detect and verify the whistle contours extracted from 808 minutes of audio recordings, collected during the DOLPHINFREE experiments. In total, 13,584 whistles were extracted with DYOC. These whistles have durations ranging from 0.02 to 3.37 seconds, and frequencies from 1.71 to 31.8 kHz.

We retained 8,730 whistles for further analyses (constraints: signal >−60 dB, SNR >10 dB, duration >200 ms). We then extracted classic characteristics from these contours54: duration, minimum/starting/ending/maximum frequencies, frequency range, number of inflections and frequency gradient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {end frequency}-\text {start frequency}}{\text {duration}}$$\end{document}). We analysed these features depending on the behavioural state of the dolphins observed, the presence/absence of a fishing net and the activation state of the DOLPHINFREE bio-inspired beacon. Then, we used a dimension reduction technique (UMAP) to represent whistle shapes in a 2D space.

Effects on whistles

We compared the features of detected whistles on the basis of three variables of interest: the observed behaviour of the dolphins, the state of activation of the DOLPHINFREE acoustic beacon and the presence/absence of a fishing net. Results are presented in Figs. 5 & 6. In addition, the number of whistles per category are available in Table S1 (supplementary material). Frequency ranges and frequency means are not shown in order to make the figures easier to read. In Fig. 6, we chose to represent whistle features depending on travelling and foraging behaviours only, because these behaviours were the most likely to be associated with bycatch. Figure 6 shows less variables than Fig. 5 for easier readability. Violin plots represent data with kernel density estimates of the distributions and box-plots in their centres. Kruskal-Wallis tests and Dunn’s post-hoc tests were computed for each variable in order to determine whether there were differences between the distributions of features.

Our results highlighted differences in the features of detected whistles depending on the behaviour of dolphins (Fig. 5). The average detected SNRs were lowest when dolphins were attracted by the boat or travelling, median during socialising and milling activities and the highest during foraging behaviours (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 522, d.f. = 4, p-value < 0.001), with SNRs ranging from 17.4 to 22.2 dB depending on the behavioural state. Whistles recorded from milling dolphins were, on average, slightly longer (by 0.1 s) than those recorded when other activities were observed (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 38, d.f. = 4, p-value < 0.001). Slight variations of the average frequency gradient can be observed depending on behavioural activities: dolphins attracted to the boat emitted whistles with globally decreasing frequencies as opposed to relatively stable whistles when they were travelling (Fig. 5, Dunn’s post-hoc test, p-value < 0.05). However, these mean values are associated with fairly wide confidence intervals, showing great variations within each behavioural state. Conversely, smaller intervals around the mean numbers of inflections per whistle enabled us to show high variations of this feature depending on behavioural activities. Whistles emitted during socialisation, milling activities, or when dolphins were attracted to the boat had the highest mean numbers of inflections measured. These numbers were lower when dolphins were travelling and lowest when dolphins were foraging (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 77, d.f. = 4, p-value < 0.001). Whistle minimum and maximum frequencies also showed subtle differences depending on behavioural states (Fig. 5, K-W test, p-values < 0.05), which was not the case for starting and ending frequencies. Additionally, whistles detected during milling activities showed slightly higher maximum frequencies and lower minimum frequencies than whistles detected during other behavioural activities. This reflected a slightly wider frequency range during milling activities (K-W test, H = 81, d.f. = 4, p-value < 0.001) (frequency ranges not shown on Fig. 5 for readability).

During experiments, when a fishing net was set underwater, detected whistles had an SNR that was clearly lower (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 959, d.f. = 1, p-value < 0.001), and a higher number of inflections per whistle on average (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 21, d.f. = 1, p-value < 0.001). Frequency features were less affected by the presence of a fishing net: the average of maximum frequencies shows a subtle decrease (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 16, d.f. = 1, p-value < 0.001), while other frequency features did not show changes in presence or absence of a fishing net (Fig. 5, K-W tests, p-values > 0.05). In addition, neither the duration nor the frequency gradients had statistically different distributions when a fishing net was absent/present (Fig. 5, K-W tests, p-values > 0.05).

Distributions of whistle features also varied depending on the behaviour state of dolphins in the presence (or absence) of a fishing net (i.e. interaction of these two variables, see Fig. 6). Travelling dolphins emitted longer whistles than foraging individuals when the fishing net was absent (0.7 s & 0.62 s, respectively). However, this effect reversed when a fishing net was present (Fig. 6, K-W test, H = 46, d.f. = 3, p-value < 0.001), although the differences are slighter (i.e. 0.65 s & 0.7 s, respectively). The same inversion can be observed for the number of inflections and frequency features extracted from the whistles (see Fig. 6) with very little difference between each modality. Finally, the SNRs also showed significant variations (Dunn’s post-hoc test, p-value < 0.05): detected whistles were recorded with lower SNRs when a fishing net was present underwater (17.95 dB). Conversely, in absence of a net, whistles of travelling dolphins were recorded at significantly higher SNRs (22.49 dB), and those of foraging dolphins at even higher SNRs (23.19 dB).

The use of the bio-inspired DOLPHINFREE beacon also had an effect on detected whistles. The mean SNR decreased upon activation of the beacon (i.e. from 21.3 dB to 19.4 dB), and then rose to levels that were higher than before its use (Fig. 5, K-W test, H = 109, d.f. = 4, p-value < 0.001) with large variations (from 19.4 to 22.7 dB). Duration and average numbers of inflections per whistle followed the same trend: whistles were slightly longer with noticeably more inflections at beacon activation/deactivation than when the beacon remained activated or after its deactivation (K-W tests, H = 41 & H = 63 respectively, d.f. = 4, p-values < 0.001). All measured frequency features varied depending on the activation state of the beacon during the experiments (K-W tests, p-values < 0.05). They followed the same relative pattern as the duration of detected whistles: measured frequency features were on average distinctly higher before the activation of the beacon. The features of detected whistles from foraging and travelling dolphins showed little difference depending on the activation state of the DOLPHINFREE beacon (Fig. 6). Frequency features showed some difference in distributions depending on behaviours within each modality of activation (K-W tests, H = 45, d.f. = 9, p-value < 0.001). Namely, whistles minimum frequencies were higher (10 kHz) for travelling dolphins than foraging ones (9 kHz) after the beacon’s deactivation (Dunn’s post-hoc test, p-value < 0.05). Detected whistles of travelling dolphins showed a distinctly higher number of inflections compared to foraging dolphins when the beacon was switched on/off, but followed the same patterns for the rest of the experimental sequences (Dunn’s post-hoc tests, p-values > 0.05). Similarly, durations of recorded whistles remained mostly similar during the sequences of activation between foraging and travelling dolphins, with little to no variation detectable (Dunn’s post-hoc tests, p-values > 0.05). For the SNRs, recorded levels of sound intensity were lower by 4.8 dB on average for travelling dolphins compared to foraging dolphins over the whole experimental sequence (see Fig. 6). For all modalities in the activation sequence, distributions of recorded SNRs were statistically different when comparing between foraging and travelling dolphins, except for before the activation of the beacon (Dunn’s post-hoc tests, p-values > 0.05). After the use of the DOLPHINFREE signal, the detected SNR of whistles emitted by travelling dolphins declined, while it increased for foraging dolphins.

Whistles shapes

In order to visualise overall differences in the shapes of the whistles, we applied a dimension reduction method to the 8,730 whistle contours extracted with DYOC. To this end, we first calculated the Dynamic Time Warping (DTW55) metric between all pairs of contours (with normalised frequencies). This metric is commonly used to compare two temporal sequences that are not perfectly synchronised. Then, we performed a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for dimension reduction (UMAP56) on the contours (with the following parameters: number of neighbours: 50, minimum distance: 0.5, number of components: 2, metric: DTW). Our goal was to group together whistles that have similar shapes, with no a priori information on these data. This representation did not highlight any relation between the shapes of the annotated whistles and our experimental modalities (Fig. 7a, b & c). However, it enabled us to manually differentiate groups of similar shapes (associated with different colours in Fig. 7d). Upsweeps and downsweeps are easily clustered together in this representation. However, it is interesting to note that convex and concave whistles appear as clear gradients linking upsweeps and downsweeps shapes. Sinusoidal whistles, which can be seen as concatenations of simpler concave and convex contours, stand out in the centre of the UMAP representation. This last group is the one whose forms vary the most. Sinusoidal whistles typically have three or more inflections, and some contours shapes are completely unique, which could partially explain why this group is so scattered.

Discussion

In this study, we propose a new semi-automatic annotation method, DYOC, that combines deep learning models and manual annotation steps to efficiently speed up the extraction process of whistle contours. This semi-automatic method facilitated the annotation of whistle contours at a rate that was six times faster than manual annotation. DYOC was applied to a dataset comprising 808 minutes of audio recordings of short-beaked common dolphins, and enabled the extraction of 13,584 whistles. The whistles were studied with regard to the variables that were observed during the DOLPHINFREE experiments. The contours show significant variations in acoustic features depending on dolphin behaviour, the activation of the DOLPHINFREE bio-inspired beacon, and the presence of a fishing net.

The main criticism that can be made regarding the use of YOLOv8m and ResNet18 in DYOC is that they are not initially intended to be applied to sounds, but rather to images. Here, we made predictions using spectrograms that are considered to be images, which means that the models do not differentiate between the temporal and frequency axes (i.e. images are not read from left to right as they are by humans). Nevertheless, the results of the regression part of DYOC showed very good prediction scores on test data (RMSE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$=$$\end{document} 6.1% of image size). Visualisations (Fig. 4) also showed that the model makes predictions that have a polynomial-like appearance, even for images that are difficult to predict. It underlines that the ResNet part of DYOC has successfully integrated the general pattern of a whistle, and can predict plausible contours despite limited information. Predictions made by YOLO were less convincing than expected (mAP50 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$=$$\end{document} 69.2%), but are satisfactory for our purposes.

Both models used in DYOC are fully operational without having to create a deep learning architecture from scratch for each particular use, which is why we chose them. Ultralytics, the company which develops YOLO, recommends training YOLOv8 with 10,000 instances per class in order to obtain good results. Despite using a very small number of whistle instances for our training (431), we were still able to obtain satisfactory results, proving the efficiency of a generalist method of this kind. Knowing that the whistles annotated for the training of DYOC had varying shapes, SNR, and overall visual qualities, our results could probably be improved by using a larger training dataset.

Overall, these high-performance models are well-suited for our general task, but are not good enough to be entirely relied upon, that is why DYOC is a semi-automated method. Designing a fully automated AI tool for the annotation of whistle contours is a complex and very specific task, that was not the aim of this study. Results show that, the YOLO part of DYOC can make false positive and false negative detections (see Table 1), and ResNet18 has difficulty making predictions on low-energy whistles or in presence of overlapping noises (see Fig. 4c & d). Without being perfect, the DYOC method enabled us to correctly detect the coordinates of whistles in spectrograms and to make annotations of their contours. The steps included to facilitate the manual correction of predictions, directly within DYOC, enabled us to greatly speed up our annotation process (Fig. 1). When making predictions, these steps were the slowest, as YOLO and ResNet forward passes are almost instantaneous, but they were necessary to ensure the good quality of our contours for our analyses.

Using the whistle contours obtained from DYOC, features commonly used in previous studies were extracted. Duration and frequency ranges of our whistles were similar to those collected from different populations of short-beaked common dolphins in other zones26,27,30,54. However, common dolphin whistling rates are known to vary in response to anthropogenic acoustic stimuli36,57. In this study, the aim was to study whistle responses in more detail, by comparing their characteristics as a function of different variables: behaviour state, presence/absence of a fishing net and activation state of a bio-inspired acoustic beacon to limit bycatch. We used non-parametric statistical tests to account for differences in data sampling.

We showed that almost all features extracted from detected whistles (except starting and ending frequencies) varied depending on the behaviour of dolphins. Namely, milling dolphins emit longer whistles, on average, than dolphins in other behavioural states (0.74 compared to 0.65 seconds on average for the other states, respectively), with a larger mean frequency range (6.1 compared to 4.9 kHz, respectively) and a higher mean number of inflections (2.2 compared to 2, respectively). This means that despite being in an apparent resting state, dolphins continue to exchange information. These whistles could also help dolphins to stay grouped together40,58,59 when they emit fewer echolocation clicks and are not actively travelling. Whistles also had a similarly higher number of inflections when dolphins were socialising and attracted to the survey boat, which is comparable to other populations of the same dolphin species26,60. In addition, mean SNRs varied greatly between 3 modes: 19 dB when travelling or when attracted by the boat, 20.6 dB when socialising or resting and 22.4 dB during foraging activities. These differences are greater than what we would have expected from variations between the emissions of on-axis and off-axis dolphins towards the hydrophone used for experiments61. Furthermore, an analysis of the SNR of the whistles in relation to the distances of the dolphins from the hydrophone, estimated visually by on-board observers, shows that distance alone cannot explain these differences in SNR (spearman correlation coefficient: −0.006, p-value: 0.57). It indicates that travelling and attracted dolphins were either emitting whistles at a lower sound intensity level than foraging dolphins, or from a greater distance, hence reducing the signal’s intensity. Bottlenose dolphins have been documented to produce whistles at a higher rate when feeding in order to attract conspecifics62. The elevated SNRs recorded during our experiments while dolphins were foraging could play a similar role.

The estimated gradient of frequency of detected whistles did not show noticeable variations (Fig. 5), which is why we did not further consider this variable (Fig. 6). Overall, its only significant difference in distribution was between travelling dolphins and those attracted to the boat: attracted animals emitted whistles that rapidly decreased in frequency, but travelling animals emitted whistles that remained at a relatively constant frequency in time. Whistles recorded during foraging, travelling or attraction behaviour represented \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}90% of the detections. We chose to focus our analyses on foraging and travelling states as those behaviours are more likely to occur during interaction with fishing activities.

The use of the bio-inspired DOLPHINFREE beacon led to considerable variations in the observed characteristics of the detected whistles (Fig. 5). Whistles were slightly longer when the beacon was switched on/off compared to when it was fully activated or after its use (mean durations of 0.72s and 0.73s compared to 0.65s and 0.63s, respectively). A similar pattern, with greater differences, was observed for the numbers of inflection per whistle (2.45 inflections at beacon activation/deactivation compared to 1.9 inflections during and after use). This underlines that dolphins communicated differently when the signal was introduced rather than during the whole emission sequence of the acoustic beacon. In addition, SNRs of recorded whistles decreased strongly when the beacon was activated (19.4 compared to 20.3 dB before activation), but showed very high SNRs after the beacon was turned off (22.7 dB). These variations show a response to the signal emitted by the beacon but it is more difficult to explain why SNRs were higher after the use of the beacon. Odontocetes have highly variable recovery rates to return to their initial behavioural state after an acoustic event63 ranging from a couple of minutes to a couple of hours. In this experiment, we could only wait for up to 5 minutes after the extinction of the beacon, as dolphins left the emission zone after generally less than 1 minute, which could be why these higher SNRs can still be observed. Finally, frequency features showed a shift to lower frequencies when the beacon was activated (e.g. mean of maximum recorded frequencies: 14.7 kHz before the activation of the beacon compared to 14 kHz for other modalities). This difference is further accentuated at the activation and deactivation of the beacon. This response could be interpreted as a way to contact dolphins further away regarding the presence of a particular acoustic event37.

Our results showed some differences in the distribution of the frequency and number of inflections of whistles among activation sequences during foraging and travelling behaviours, while those of duration and SNRs presented slight differences (see Fig. 6). The number of inflections and the frequency features of the whistles varied more in function of behaviour and the activation state of the beacon alone, rather than their interaction. Interestingly, after the deactivation of the beacon, travelling dolphins emitted whistles at considerably lower SNRs than foraging dolphins (16.8 vs. 25.8 dB respectively), a difference that can be explained by our visual observations: travelling dolphins exhibited a tendency to move away from the beacon faster than foraging dolphins. In contrast, the numbers of inflections per whistle were significantly higher for travelling dolphins compared to foraging ones at the activation/deactivation of the beacon, showing that more complex whistles are emitted by travelling dolphins in response to the DOLPHINFREE signal.

Finally, whistle characteristics varied significantly in response to the presence of a fishing net: detected signals were quieter but more complex. When a fishing net was set underwater, detected whistles had a lower mean SNR (18.2 compared to 23 dB) but a higher mean number of inflections (2.1 compared to 1.8), and a slightly lower mean maximum frequency (13.9 compared to 14.1 kHz), while other frequency features showed no significant changes. The maximum frequencies varied slightly but could have two origins: an acoustic response of dolphins to the presence of the fishing net, or dolphins emitting from farther away when a fishing net was present (high frequencies are the first that are absorbed by the environment). The fishing net was set underwater from the boat, from which the hydrophone was also set. Thus, the lower SNRs corroborates the hypothesis of dolphins avoiding the area when a fishing net is present. Unfortunately, our data does not enable us to estimate precisely the distance from which the whistles were emitted. According to previous research, dolphins can detect fishing nets only from a few metres away64. When considered in relation to dolphins’ behaviour (Fig. 6), detected whistles of foraging dolphins had a higher SNR on average than those of travelling dolphins when no fishing net was set. This reflects the observed higher SNR during foraging behaviours. However, despite this difference of SNR between behaviours in absence of fishing net, the average SNR decreased to a similar level when a fishing net was present, showing an equal acoustic response to the presence of a fishing net whether dolphins were travelling or foraging. Moreover, the duration of whistles decreases slightly when travelling dolphins encounter a fishing net, while detected whistles of foraging dolphins become longer (from 0.62 to 0.7 seconds). Similar relative patterns of change can be observed for the number of inflections per detected whistles. In addition, maximum frequencies (with ending frequencies and frequency ranges, not shown in Fig. 6 for easier readability) vary depending on absence/presence of the fishing net and the behaviour of dolphins. When there is no fishing net, measured maximum frequencies are higher for travelling than foraging dolphins but when a fishing net is present, it is the exact opposite. Overall, these differences show complex whistling responses of dolphins to the presence of an object in their environment (the fishing net) and depending on their behaviour. Foraging dolphins modulate their whistles sharply in presence of a fishing net compared to travelling dolphins. This tends to show that dolphins are more responsive to the presence of a fishing net when they are hunting than when they are travelling.

We did not find any direct link between the general shapes of the whistles and our experimental variables. Assessing in detail the repertoire of short-beaked common dolphins was not the main aim of this study. However, using an unsupervised clustering technique with no a priori information, we showed that unsupervised methods can be used to group whistles in categories similar to those previously reported for other populations of short-beaked common dolphins26,27,54. Moreover, this technique highlights that the categorisation of whistles into different types appears to be overly simplistic as some whistles exhibit a more gradual transition between classes (especially for concave and convex whistles, see Fig. 7). Recent studies31,32 also showed that signature whistles could be identified among common dolphin whistles. Stereotyped whistles could be included in small clusters in Fig. 7.

Ultimately, the findings of this study indicate that generalist computer vision deep learning models are well-suited for use on spectrograms, even when trained on limited datasets. Using pre-trained models in conjunction with a limited number of manual annotations is a lightweight approach, but it proves adequate for predicting annotations in a much larger dataset. The application of this approach to a case study enabled us to substantially accelerate our annotation process. Detected whistle characteristics were shown to vary very differently depending on the behaviour of dolphins, the presence/absence of a fishing net and the activation state of a DOLPHINFREE bio-inspired beacon. Interactions between these variables show a complex acoustic behaviour of dolphins in response to environmental and acoustic conditions. Regarding the DOLPHINFREE acoustic beacon, these results suggest that emitting its signal periodically elicits more pronounced acoustic responses from short-beaked common dolphins compared to a continuous emission. For that reason, the latest version of the beacon includes a passive acoustic monitoring module, thus the emission of the signal only occurs when dolphins are detected within a radius of 250 m from the beacon. Variations observed in whistles characteristics suggested that dolphins could use these signals to inform their conspecifics about a change in their environment, notably the emissions of the DOLPHINFREE bio-inspired beacon37,62. It could potentially enhance their alertness and increase their chances of identifying fishing nets. An action plan introduced by the French government, planned for 2024-2026 in collaboration with scientists and fishers, aims to assess the efficiency of the bio-inspired acoustic beacon DOLPHINFREE to limit bycatch of short beaked common dolphins in the Bay of Biscay. More generally, these results can be applied to other species and geographical areas, studying the variation of whistle contours is a mean to understand social interactions, behaviour, and responses to anthropogenic activities, which informs conservation policies.
