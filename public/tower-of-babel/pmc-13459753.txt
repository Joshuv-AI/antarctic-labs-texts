Case report: anesthetic management for a partial nephrectomy in a patient with tracheobronchopathia osteoplastica
Authors: Yushan Luo, Zhen Wu, Xiaoyuan Zhang, Hui Li, Xiaohua Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case report describes the anesthetic management of a middle-aged woman with the rare condition tracheobronchopathia osteoplastica (TPO) who underwent a left partial nephrectomy. The patient presented with severe airway stenosis, with the narrowest tracheal segment measuring approximately 0.40 cm. After a comprehensive multidisciplinary preoperative evaluation, an individualized anesthetic plan was formulated according to a difficult airway management protocol, incorporating the use of a narrow-gauge endotracheal tube and video laryngoscopy. General anesthesia was administered uneventfully, enabling the surgery to proceed smoothly while ensuring perioperative safety. This case underscores the critical importance of meticulous planning and tailored airway management strategies in patients with TPO and severe airway compromise.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04388-8.

Case presentation

The patient was a 43-year-old woman (height, 155 cm; weight, 55 kg) admitted for an elective left partial nephrectomy due to a renal angiomyolipoma diagnosed more than five months earlier. The patient had no significant dyspnea before surgery. Her medical history revealed that a previous surgical attempt at another hospital had been aborted after unexpected severe airway stenosis prevented successful tracheal intubation. Two months before the current admission, bronchoscopy confirmed the diagnosis of TPO, revealing extensive multifocal nodular and exophytic lesions involving the trachea, main bronchi, and segmental bronchi (Fig. 1), consistent with complex and severe airway pathology.

Further preoperative investigations included chest computed tomography (CT), which demonstrated mid-tracheal stenosis, with the narrowest segment measuring approximately 0.40 cm in diameter, it is 2.9 cm away from the tracheal prominence (Fig. 2). Pulmonary function testing showed mild obstructive ventilatory impairment with increased airway resistance, while arterial blood gas analysis indicated mild hypoxemia (PaO₂: 77.8 mmHg). Other preoperative laboratory and imaging findings were unremarkable.

After multidisciplinary evaluation by the Pulmonology and Anesthesiology departments, the airway was classified as an anticipated difficult airway. However, there was no evidence suggesting potential difficulty with mask ventilation, enabling the formulation of a detailed and individualized anesthetic management plan. Given the multiple, irregular, and rigid nodules within the airway that precluded passage of any tube exceeding the tracheal lumen diameter, a carefully calculated intubation strategy was essential. The narrowest segment of the airway, measuring 0.40 cm in diameter, was located 2.9 cm above the carina. With a planned insertion depth of 19 cm at the incisors, a 5.5-mm internal diameter (OD: 7.6 mm) endotracheal tube was selected, anticipating that its tip would remain securely above the stenotic segment. Endotracheal tubes with smaller internal diameters of 5.0 mm (OD: 7.0 mm) and 4.5 mm (OD: 6.3 mm) were prepared as backups (Fig. 3).

Anesthetic management

The central challenge in this case was the management of an extremely narrow, non-compliant airway secondary to TPO. Standard intraoperative monitoring included electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry (SpO₂), and temperature. Upon arrival in the operating room, the patient’s vital signs were stable. Accordingly, a laryngeal mask airway (sizes 3# and 4#), rigid bronchoscope, jet ventilation device, and emergency tracheostomy kit were prepared as rescue options. The crisis algorithm was: (1) LMA if mask ventilation failed; (2) jet ventilation if LMA failed; (3) surgical airway if CICO occurred. Fortunately, none of these were needed.Induction was achieved with ciprofol 15 mg, remimazolam 4 mg, sufentanil 20 µg, and cisatracurium 12 mg. Hemodynamics remained stable throughout induction. For analgesia and maintenance, sufentanil 20 µg was given at induction, followed by continuous infusion of remifentanil and intermittent top-ups of sufentanil as needed. Sevoflurane was inhaled for maintenance of anesthesia, and cisatracurium was administered intermittently. Postoperative NRS pain score was 0–2, and no rescue analgesia was required. After anesthetic induction, mask ventilation was confirmed to be adequate, providing an essential safety assurance before attempting intubation. Based on preoperative CT measurement showing a minimum tracheal diameter of 0.40 cm located approximately 2.9 cm above the carina, we formulated a stepwise intubation strategy. First, we prepared three endotracheal tubes (ETTs) of decreasing sizes (5.5 mm, 5.0 mm, and 4.5 mm internal diameter) with the distal bevels pre-cut to create a smoother. Second, we planned a graded intubation approach: starting with the largest tube that might potentially pass (5.5# ETT, outer diameter 7.6 mm) under fiberoptic bronchoscopic guidance to objectively assess the stenotic segment, with immediate down-sizing to 5.0# ETT (outer diameter 7.0 mm) or 4.5# ETT if resistance was encountered. Third, we pre-determined the insertion depth based on CT measurement to position the tube tip safely above the stenotic segment. Finally, we used fiberoptic bronchoscopy to confirm the final tube position before initiating mechanical ventilation. This strategy allowed us to successfully intubate the patient with a 5.0# ETT after the 5.5# ETT failed to pass the stenotic segment. The tube tip was positioned just above the stenotic segment and secured at 19 cm at the incisors (Fig. 4). After connection to the anesthesia circuit, the airway pressure was 9 cm H₂O, end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) was 36 mmHg, and SpO₂ was 99%. When the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position and pneumoperitoneum was established, the peak airway pressure rose to 17 cm H₂O. The surgery proceeded uneventfully. At the end of the procedure, the patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) with the tracheal tube in place. After confirmation of full recovery of consciousness, spontaneous breathing, and adequate muscle strength, the patient was extubated smoothly in the PACU. She was observed for 30 min without any signs of laryngeal edema, respiratory distress, or desaturation, and then transferred to the ward in stable condition. She achieved a full recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 5. No adverse or unanticipated events occurred during anesthesia, surgery, or the recovery period.

Discussion

This case exemplifies a structured and systematic approach to managing the unique challenges of a difficult airway in a patient with TPO. TPO is an exceptionally rare benign disorder characterized by the formation of ectopic bone and cartilage within the tracheobronchial submucosa [1, 2]. These pathological changes lead to diffuse wall thickening, irregular luminal narrowing, and, most importantly, a progressive loss of airway compliance [3, 4]. As a result, tracheal intubation becomes a high-risk procedure, with potential complications including mucosal injury, bleeding, and even complete airway obstruction [5]. Our experience underscores that successful anesthetic management in such cases depends on meticulous, multidisciplinary preoperative planning and the integration of multiple complementary techniques.

Quantitative assessment of the stenotic airway on chest CT (minimum diameter: 0.40 cm) served as the foundation of our strategy [6], guiding the selection of appropriately sized endotracheal tubes (ETTs). Implementation of the plan followed a carefully coordinated, multimodal approach. Intubation was conducted in a deliberate, stepwise fashion—initially with a 5.5-mm ETT, not with the expectation of success but to objectively evaluate the severity and precise location of the stenosis. When resistance was encountered, prompt substitution with a 5.0-mm ETT allowed successful passage through the glottis. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy played a pivotal role by providing real-time visualization and definitive confirmation of ETT positioning [7, 8]. This ensured that the tube tip was situated safely just above the stenotic segment [9]—deep enough to maintain ventilation but without risking mucosal trauma—forming a key element of our airway safety protocol.

Our management philosophy extended beyond the act of successful intubation. Continuous attention to airway safety was maintained throughout the perioperative course—from confirming adequate mask ventilation prior to muscle relaxation [10], to selecting a neuromuscular blocking agent, and performing an awake, closely monitored extubation in the post-anesthesia care unit. Although quantitative neuromuscular monitoring was not performed during this case, extubation was successfully carried out after clinical confirmation of adequate recovery (e.g., return of spontaneous breathing, head lift for 5 s, adequate tidal volume) [11]. Such comprehensive, end-to-end vigilance is indispensable when caring for patients with high-risk airways [12].

In conclusion, this case highlights that managing rare and complex airway disorders such as TPO demands not only advanced technical proficiency but also a proactive safety culture grounded in thorough preparation, judicious use of technology, and sustained intra- and postoperative vigilance. Our experience demonstrates that a structured, multi-pronged strategy can effectively mitigate risk and enhance patient safety in the context of an anticipated extremely difficult airway.

Conclusion

For patients with a difficult airway secondary to severe TPO, successful anesthetic management depends on meticulous multidisciplinary preoperative evaluation and the formulation of a highly individualized airway management strategy. Such a strategy should include the preparation and appropriate modification of narrow-gauge endotracheal tubes, coupled with the combined application of fiberoptic bronchoscopy to optimize tube placement and confirm positioning. Ultimately, the careful execution of this plan—supported by seamless team coordination and vigilant intraoperative monitoring—is essential to maintaining patient safety throughout the perioperative period. This case provides valuable clinical insights and a practical framework for managing patients with similarly complex and high-risk airway conditions.
