Real-world provider experiences with hemophilia A gene therapy: administration of valoctocogene roxaparvovec
Authors: Flora Peyvandi, Robert Klamroth, Johannes Oldenburg, Anita Rajasekhar, Andrea Buxton, Matteo Nicola Dario Di Minno, Saskia Gottstein, Amit Soni, Ezio Zanon, Andrew D Leavitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere hemophilia A is a bleeding condition caused by a deficiency in clotting factor VIII (FVIII ≤1 IU/dL) that results in spontaneous and excessive posttraumatic bleeding. The current standard of care is prophylaxis with exogenous FVIII or bispecific antibodies that mimic FVIII function. Valoctocogene roxaparvovec is a gene therapy approved for adults with severe hemophilia A that enables endogenous FVIII production to protect against bleeding. As a newer treatment modality, workflows for gene therapy administration and postinfusion care are still unfamiliar to many health care professionals and may be perceived as implementation barriers. We are 10 hemophilia gene therapy providers across the United States, Italy, and Germany sharing our experiences of administering valoctocogene roxaparvovec in clinical practice to >25 patients with severe hemophilia A. In this study, we provide our insights into patient preparation, establishment of treatment centers and multidisciplinary teams, postinfusion management, and patient follow-up to facilitate open communication and collaboration among the treatment community.Keywords: genetic therapy, hemophilia A, valoctocogene roxaparvovec

Introduction

Hemophilia A results from clotting factor VIII (FVIII) deficiency [1]. Individuals with severe hemophilia A (FVIII ≤1 IU/dL) experience spontaneous and excessive posttraumatic bleeding [2], and repeated bleeds into joints can lead to chronic arthropathy and long-term disability [3]. The current standard of care is prophylaxis with exogenous FVIII or bispecific antibodies that mimic FVIII function [[1], [2], [3]]. Regular prophylactic infusions are burdensome and negatively impact health-related quality of life [4].

Gene therapy, an alternative to regular prophylaxis, can offer greater independence and improved quality of life [5,6]. It may enable a “hemophilia-free mind” relieved of the day-to-day burden of preventing or managing bleeds [6]. Valoctocogene roxaparvovec is a gene therapy approved for adults with severe hemophilia A in the European Union and the United States with no antibodies against adeno-associated virus serotype 5 (AAV5) [7,8]. Valoctocogene roxaparvovec uses an AAV5 vector to transfer a B-domain-deleted human FVIII coding sequence controlled by a hepatocyte-selective promoter, enabling long-term endogenous FVIII expression and bleed prevention with a single infusion [9]. The safety and efficacy of valoctocogene roxaparvovec were assessed in the multicenter, single-arm, open-label, phase 3 GENEr8-1 (NCT03370913) trial [[10], [11], [12], [13]], which demonstrated benefit in most individuals through at least 5 years of follow-up [14].

As gene therapies move from clinical trial settings to clinical practice, questions remain for providers. Unfamiliarity with preparation, administration, and postinfusion monitoring for gene therapy can act as barriers to adoption. These concerns highlight the importance of knowledge-sharing among providers and the greater treatment community.

In this study, we share our real-world experiences of administering valoctocogene roxaparvovec at centers in the United States, Italy, and Germany. We represent 10 independent, standalone, or university-associated centers, and approximately one-third of us were not part of the valoctocogene roxaparvovec clinical trials. We summarize our insights collected from an informal survey on preparing patients for gene therapy, setup and management of centers/multidisciplinary teams, postinfusion management, patient follow-up after treatment, and management of gene therapy complications.

Patient Preparation

For those centers not involved in the valoctocogene roxaparvovec clinical trials, education, preparation, and discussion on how to start gene therapy were performed through various national and international meetings and direct outreach to clinical trial centers regarding their experience. The entire center team was prepared through an intense review of available product information and conducted mock infusion days.

The time from a patient expressing interest in gene therapy to receiving infusion is typically 2 to 6 months, although this can vary (Figure). A similar shared decision-making process involving education and screening is used at all our centers. Several shared decision-making tools are available, including a web-based tool from the World Federation of Hemophilia [15]. Before gene therapy administration, we believe that providers must address patient concerns about previous treatments and verify that expectations are in line with the aims and realistic outcomes of gene therapy.

Educating patients about their options

Information about gene therapy is predominantly shared in-person across several meetings. Beyond communicating the pros and cons of gene therapy, we believe tempering expectations in advance is important [16,17]. Our patients have expressed concerns on several topics, including potential FVIII level, consequences for liver health, subsequent steroid use, the high frequency and logistical challenges of follow-up monitoring, and the uncertain duration of gene therapy benefit. In our experience, not all patients are ready for gene therapy; some are too busy to make the time commitment, while others prefer to wait for additional clinical data to feel more safe. Therefore, the shared decision-making process with the health care provider and family/friends must allow adequate time for patients to ask questions and ultimately drive the decision to undergo treatment. Based on discussions with patients who expressed an interest or curiosity in gene therapy, most of them opted to proceed for several reasons (Figure). The most common was a desire to avoid regular prophylaxis, but patients also wanted to take advantage of the independence afforded by gene therapy to achieve lifestyle changes (eg, higher level of physical activity, freedom to spontaneously travel). In short, they hoped to reduce their hemophilia burden.

Conducting eligibility screening

The eligibility criteria for gene therapy have been previously described [18]. Briefly, the main tests include companion diagnostic (CDx) AAV5 antibody testing using an appropriately validated assay and extensive liver screening (eg, laboratory testing, ultrasound, and FibroScan) to ensure optimal liver health. For most of us (80%), CDx testing is conducted only for patients who express interest in gene therapy, whereas 20% conduct CDx testing for all of their patients upfront. While some centers prefer to start the process of gene therapy after a negative AAV5 antibody test, insurance authorization, patient availability, and patient education can delay dosing after a negative result. The need for strict adherence to follow-up protocols after treatment also impacts patient eligibility.

Preparation of Center and Multidisciplinary Team

Our European centers used a hub-and-spoke model, where an expert hemophilia comprehensive care center administered the gene therapy, and patients were followed up at local treatment centers [19]. The hub-and-spoke model is not used in the United States. Within all centers, most (70%) of our teams consist of 6 to 10 people, including individuals from hematology, pharmacy, and nursing departments. Some centers also include physical therapy, hepatology, and psychology experts. One center included a social worker who played an essential role with postinfusion patient monitoring.

For about half of us, becoming a dosing center and coordinating with the relevant team members were easier than expected, especially if policies and communication were prepared in advance. Our United States centers had the opportunity to access personalized resources from a support program from the manufacturer of valoctocogene roxaparvovec [20]. The more challenging aspects of setting up a dosing center included interacting with insurance companies, procuring equipment, and acquiring necessary approvals. As we represent providers from both standalone and university-affiliated centers across different countries, the types of approvals required to administer valoctocogene roxaparvovec varied. For example, this could include approvals from the hospital or institution, high-cost medication committees, infusion centers, pharmacy centers, and in some cases, insurance-mandated referrals.

Infusion Monitoring and Management

On “infusion day,” syringe pumps are used to administer valoctocogene roxaparvovec; therefore, nurses experienced with this equipment are critical for success. Patients are then monitored within the center for infusion-related complications. The time for monitoring after infusion varied by center but typically ranged from 1 to 6 hours. While patients are usually allowed to return home on the same day, a phone call or in-person visit within 24 hours is recommended. Patients who live far from the center often stay in a nearby hotel overnight in case of delayed reactions.

Infusion day was perceived to be uneventful and easy, with no reported infusion-related reactions or important side effects to date. However, these anecdotes reflect the variability of real-world experiences. In locales using the hub-and-spoke model, the care center should ensure availability for direct hospital admission in the event of any serious reactions after dosing.

Patient Follow-up After Treatment

Routine infusion follow-up procedures for patients, including regular blood draws to monitor FVIII activity and liver function, have been described previously [21]. Implementation of monitoring varies across our centers, and we encourage other gene therapy providers to check their local regulations as these can vary regionally, even within countries. In Europe, dosing was performed mainly at hub centers with close collaboration with spoke centers for follow-up. In the United States, dosing does not have to be at a clinical trial center, but we recommend close collaboration if needed. Nearby patients can return to the dosing center for blood draws, while others can use their local commercial labs or home health care services. Either way, patients should be encouraged to schedule all blood draws in advance at the same facility to minimize reporting variability. Some of us use remote monitoring to ease the burden of postinfusion follow-up visits for patients. For example, with patients who live far from treatment centers, monthly monitoring could alternate between in-person and telemedicine visits to review liver tests and FVIII activity. Some centers enlist a social worker to review lab results with patients at their check-ins or a case manager to coordinate patient-related activities. Some of the centers have used the valoctocogene roxaparvovec patient support program (PSP) offered by the manufacturer to reduce the burden of follow-up visits to hemophilia centers. PSPs are programs offered by pharmaceutical companies to help patients and providers navigate the steps of treatment administration and/or follow-up. PSPs can vary in different countries and may not be available in all geographical areas. The valoctocogene roxaparvovec PSP is available in some countries to facilitate the collection and transport of samples from patients’ houses to the hospital. We encourage regular communication and flexibility to support patients with the monitoring requirements, but facilities should still be available in the center.

Of 10 authors, 6 of us did not observe any long-term adverse reactions in our patients after gene therapy. For those who did, flu-like symptoms and elevated liver enzymes necessitating steroid treatment were the most common and the latter required a close collaboration with hepatologists for dose and duration of the therapy. Irrespective of the need to manage reactions, a multidisciplinary team is mandatory for the administration of valoctocogene roxaparvovec.

Managing expectations after treatment can be challenging; patients may feel anxious about their FVIII levels or not comply with monitoring requirements. Even with a good response to gene therapy, an eventual return to prophylaxis is still possible and should be communicated in advance with patients.

Key benefits of gene therapy expressed by our patients include freedom from prophylaxis, improved quality of life, and easier traveling experiences. Patients generally felt a reduced burden of disease. With the focus on patients, it can be easy to overlook the impact of gene therapy on patient families and/or spouses, most of whom have provided long-term support and are just as grateful that treatment exists.

Conclusions

Although we have noted that we are sharing our personal real-world experiences of administering gene therapy, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, because we are sharing our perspectives, our interpretation is subject to bias and not necessarily representative of all centers. Second, we report on a small sample size of approximately 25 patients at the time of preparation of the manuscript. For a list of more comprehensive and detailed guidelines and recommendations on conducting gene therapy for hemophilia treatment, we refer to published clinical practice guidelines, including from the World Federation of Hemophilia [[22], [23], [24]].

Gene therapy reduces disease burden and affords individuals with severe hemophilia A more independence, but clinical practice implementation is an ongoing process. We believe that sharing provider experiences is crucial to making gene therapy more accessible. In our experience, perceived implementation barriers, including administrative work, training, dealing with compliance bodies, and patient education, can be surpassed more easily than expected. We encourage the hemophilia A treatment community to share their experiences with administering valoctocogene roxaparvovec to promote collaboration and improve accessibility.
