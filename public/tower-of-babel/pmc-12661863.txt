The long-term outcomes of radioactive iodine therapy in malignant struma ovarii with extensive abdominopelvic metastases: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jingjia Cao, Xiang Li, Yuling Wang, Huiying Song, Zhuoga Baima, Yujiao Chen, Juan Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStruma ovarii (SO) is a rare type of teratoma characterized predominantly by thyroid tissue, accounting for only 1% of all ovarian tumors and 2% of ovarian teratomas. Clinical presentation commonly includes abdominal pain or irregular menstrual cycles. While typically benign, SO may rarely undergo malignant transformation, occurring in an estimated 0.3% to 5.0% of cases. Most cases of malignant struma ovarii (MSO) are diagnosed postoperatively through histopathological examination or incidentally detected during abdominal ultrasound or computed tomography scans. Although MSO exhibits biological behavior analogous to thyroid carcinomas, considerable controversy persists regarding optimal surgical extent and postoperative management, reflecting its unpredictable clinical course, metastatic potential, and relatively high recurrence rate.Case presentationWe report the case of a 40-year-old female patient with MSO accompanied by extensive abdominopelvic metastasis. The patient underwent multiple surgeries, including tumor cytoreduction and total thyroidectomy, followed by radioactive iodine therapy (RAI). At five years of follow-up, she remained disease-free. This case is supplemented by a review of relevant literature on the diagnosis, management, and prognosis of MSO, with the aim of informing clinical decision-making for this rare condition.ConclusionsMSO presents with nonspecific symptoms that complicate preoperative diagnosis. Although current evidence questions the need for aggressive treatment in cases without extraovarian extension, we implemented a primary thyroid carcinoma protocol—including total thyroidectomy and radioactive iodine therapy—to minimize recurrence risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04104-6.Keywords: Malignant struma ovarii, Radioactive iodine therapy, Total thyroidectomy, Prognosis

Introduction

Struma ovarii (SO) is a specialized form of mature teratoma, defined by its composition being entirely or predominantly (≥ 50%) thyroid tissue. It is a relatively rare clinical entity, accounting for approximately 1% of all ovarian tumors and 2% of ovarian teratomas. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for SO. However, due to the low incidence of malignant struma ovarii (MSO) and the fact that most studies are limited to individual case reports or small case series, there are no standardized guidelines regarding the optimal extent of surgical intervention [1]. Although approximately 300 cases have been documented worldwide to date, determining the true incidence of malignancy remains challenging. Available literature suggests that malignant transformation occurs in roughly 0.3% to 5.0% of SO cases. The average age at presentation is around 50 years, with a range from 21 to 77 years, and most patients present with non-specific clinical features [2]. Despite the thyroidal origin of the tumor, hyperthyroxinemia was observed in only 5% to 8% of patients [3]. Here, we report a 40-year-old female patient with MSO accompanied by extensive abdominopelvic metastasis.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old female patient was admitted to the Second Hospital of Shandong University on April 17, 2017, reporting persistent lower abdominal distension and pain for more than three months. Physical examination revealed a slightly distended abdomen with positive shifting dullness on pelvic examination. Subsequent pelvic CT imaging demonstrated substantial fluid-density shadows in the abdominal and pelvic cavities. An abnormal soft tissue density mass containing fat density within the left adnexal region was identified, exhibiting multilobular changes. The solid component of the mass was intensely enhanced after contrast administration, with the largest nodule measuring approximately 3.1 × 3.3 × 3.5 cm. Multiple nodular soft tissue density shadows were also observed beneath the right diaphragm and within the abdominal and pelvic cavities. Ultrasound imaging revealed the presence of extensive free fluid in the abdomen, with the largest pocket measuring around 7.8 cm. Laboratory tests showed a significantly elevated CA125 level of 1238.9 IU/mL (reference value < 35.0 IU/mL), while other tumor markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), CA19-9, squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and ferritin—were within normal limits. The patient had previously undergone a right oophorectomy via laparoscopy three years earlier, with postoperative pathology confirming a mature teratoma.

On April 24, 2017, the patient underwent tumor-reductive surgery at a local hospital. The procedure encompassed total hysterectomy, left adnexectomy, right salpingectomy, appendectomy, greater omentectomy, and pelvic lymph node dissection. Intraoperative findings included approximately 4,000 mL of ascitic fluid. The right ovary was absent, with only about 3 cm of remaining fallopian tube visible. The left ovary was enlarged and exhibited multiple purple-red, mushroom-like vegetations protruding from its surface, the largest measuring approximately 3 × 2 cm. Similar lesions were observed in the left rectouterine pouch near the cervix (approximately 2 × 2 cm), as well as smaller, bubble-like growths (1 × 1 cm and 0.5 × 0.5 cm) on the right cervical side. No adhesions were observed in the pelvic region, and no noticeable nodules were detected on the surfaces of the greater omentum, mesentery, or liver. Postoperative pathological analysis revealed two primary diagnoses: hyperplastic struma ovarii with peritoneal strumosis, and a well-differentiated thyroid follicular tumor of the ovary with extra-ovarian metastasis. The pathology slides were reviewed by experts at a major hospital in Beijing, who concluded that the findings in the left adnexa were consistent with ovarian thyroid tissue altered by follicular thyroid carcinoma, demonstrating widespread pelvic involvement. The key lymph nodes showed no evidence of metastatic cancer. Immunohistochemistry results are summarized as follows: thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) (++), thyroglobulin (Tg) (++), synaptophysin (Syn) (-), inhibin (α-inhibin) (-), and Ki-67 proliferation index approximately 1–5% (+). One week later, follow-up tests showed the patient’s CA125 level was approximately 538.1 IU/mL (reference value < 35.0 IU/mL). Thyroid function tests revealed free tetraiodothyronine (FT4) at 11.62 pg/mL (reference range 5.9–12.5 pg/mL), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) at 0.46 µIU/mL (reference range 0.49–4.91 µIU/mL), and both thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody (anti-TPO) levels within normal limits.

On May 12, 2017, the patient underwent a total thyroidectomy at a local hospital. Intraoperative findings revealed both thyroid lobes to be normal in size and shape, soft in consistency, and without palpable nodules. No enlarged lymph nodes were observed in the central neck compartment, and subsequent postoperative pathological examination confirmed the absence of malignant cells. Thyroid hormone replacement was intentionally withheld postoperatively. The patient returned for RAI therapy on June 26, 2017, after one month of a low-iodine diet. Upon admission, her vital signs were stable, and routine laboratory tests were unremarkable. Thyroid function tests indicated mild hypothyroidism, though urinary iodine levels remained elevated. She received an oral dose of 110 mCi of iodine-131, following pre-medication with steroids and vitamin C to mitigate potential side effects. A post-therapy whole-body scan (Rx-WBS) performed three days later revealed iodine-avid tissue in the thyroid bed and multiple foci of uptake in the liver, abdominal cavity, and pelvis, confirming metastatic disease. The patient was subsequently started on thyroid hormone replacement. She tolerated the procedure well without immediate adverse effects. Follow-up assessments were conducted at ten days, one month, and three months post-discharge, showing no significant abnormalities and allowing for medication adjustment.

Since the metastatic tumors could not be surgically resected, the patient received a total of five treatments on the following dates: October 20, 2017; April 18, 2018; December 10, 2018; June 3, 2019; and March 25, 2020. Each treatment involved the administration of 200 mCi of iodine-131 (Fig. 1). Regular monitoring of tumor markers demonstrated a consistent decline over time, indicating a gradual reduction in disease activity (Fig. 2).

Post-therapy whole-body scans (Rx-WBS) following 131I administration demonstrated the following temporal changes: The June 2017 scan revealed residual iodine-avid tissue in the thyroid bed alongside multiple foci of uptake in the abdominopelvic cavity. By October 2017, the thyroid bed activity had resolved to background levels, though persistent abdominopelvic foci remained. The April 2018 scan showed continued presence of abdominopelvic foci, albeit with markedly reduced intensity and dilution compared to prior studies. Literature review

The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) prior to data extraction (registration number: CRD420251163564; link: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251163564).

A systematic literature search adhering to PRISMA guidelines was conducted, without restrictions on region, publication type, or language; non-English articles were translated as needed.

A comprehensive literature review was conducted using PubMed (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com/wos/), Embase (https://www.embase.com/), and ScienceDirect (https://www.sciencedirect.com/) databases, employing the following keywords: “malignant struma ovarii,” “malignant ovarian teratoma,” “thyroid carcinoma arising in struma ovarii,” “struma ovarii,” and “metastasis.” References cited within these articles were also reviewed. Only publications in English from 1970 to 2025 were included. Cases involving benign SO or MSO without significant metastasis were excluded. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 3). The detailed search strategy and PRISMA checklist are provided in the Supplementary Table 1.

Selection criteria (PRISMA flow diagram)

This search identified and summarized 13 additional cases of MSO with extensive metastasis that provided detailed clinical features, treatment information, and follow-up outcomes (Table 1). The average patient age was approximately 41.2 years (range 27–55), with the condition most commonly occurring in individuals in their 40s. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) was the predominant pathological type in both MSO and thyroid cancer cases; only one case of follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) was noted in MSO. Seven patients received RAI therapy at doses ranging from 100 to 150 mCi. The median follow-up duration was 7.9 years, with seven patients alive with no evidence of disease and five alive with persistent disease. Importantly, no deaths directly attributable to the condition were reported.

Discussion

Approximately 17% of MSO patients presented with ascites or pleural effusion, and cytological examination revealed no abnormal cells, indicating pseudo-Meigs syndrome, which may also be associated with elevated CA125 levels [17, 18]. Diagnosis is primarily established postoperatively through histopathologic examination of paraffin-embedded tissue, applying diagnostic criteria similar to thyroid carcinomas. Common histologic subtypes include papillary carcinoma, follicular carcinoma, and their variants, while mixed forms are relatively uncommon. Although MSO generally carries a favorable prognosis, it retains potential for recurrence and metastasis. Follicular carcinoma demonstrates particular propensity for distant spread to sites such as lungs, liver, and central nervous system. Presently, understanding of this disease is limited to case reports and small-scale studies, which hampers the development of standardized treatment protocols due to its rarity, variable clinical presentation, and considerations related to patient reproductive status. Surgical excision remains the primary treatment, with approaches tailored based on factors such as patient age, tumor size, and extent of metastasis. The role of additional postoperative therapy remains controversial [19]. However, the specific types of additional therapy, including pelvic radiation or other modalities, varied widely and lacked consistent patterns across cases.

For patients with tumors ≤ 2 cm confined to the ovary and without high-risk pathological features, management may consist of thyroid hormone therapy, regular pelvic imaging, and serum Tg monitoring. In contrast, for patients with larger tumors, peritoneal dissemination, or more aggressive histological features, the suggested approach involves total thyroidectomy followed by radioactive iodine therapy and thyroid hormone suppression therapy. Marti et al. analyzed data from 53 cases of MSO and proposed stratifying patients into low-risk and high-risk groups. The low-risk group was defined as patients with tumors confined to the ovary, without evidence of extra-ovarian metastasis, and with relatively small tumor volumes. Conversely, the high-risk group included patients with ruptured tumors, presence of extra-ovarian metastasis, or large tumor volumes. For low-risk cases, surgical excision alone is generally sufficient, and patients wishing to preserve fertility may opt for ovary-sparing procedures. In high-risk patients, the recommended treatment approach is total thyroidectomy followed by radioactive iodine therapy after surgery [20]. In this particular case, the patient presented with multiple peritoneal metastases, classifying her as high-risk and requiring adjuvant iodine therapy. Notably, she did not receive thyroid hormone supplementation for nearly six weeks postoperatively. At the time of iodine administration, her serum TSH level was 6.81 µIU/mL, which is below the ATA guideline recommendation of a TSH level greater than 30 mIU/L. The presence of multiple metastatic sites within the abdominal and pelvic regions may help explain the observed serum TSH levels, which appeared close to or below the normal reference range. Salvatori et al. reported a 22-year-old patient with multiple metastases of MSO. After discontinuing levothyroxine (LT4) for two months, her TSH level was 6.4 µIU/mL (reference range: 0.23–3.80 µIU/mL). Therapeutic outcomes improved following administration of recombinant human TSH (rhTSH) [21]. Given the unavailability of rhTSH for clinical use in mainland China and based on Vrachimis’s study of over 1,800 cases—which demonstrated that TSH levels above certain thresholds do not affect the efficacy of iodine therapy for residual thyroid tissue removal—medical staff administered a 110 mCi dose of iodine. Follow-up assessments conducted one month later showed a significant reduction in Tg levels, indicating a positive response.

Following a four-week low-iodine diet, the patient’s urinary iodine level remained elevated above the standard reference range on the day of the first radioiodine treatment. This was attributed to prior use of iodine-containing disinfectants during two earlier surgeries and recent contrast-enhanced CT scans before tumor resection. Iodine-based contrast agents, derived from triiodobenzoic acid, compete with radioiodine for uptake into thyroid tissue and metastases [22, 23]. In this case, the patient’s second iodine treatment showed lower urinary iodine levels despite following the same low-iodine diet. Additionally, patients with lung metastases in our department consistently exhibit baseline urinary iodine levels around 214 µg/L prior to treatment. This phenomenon may be related to the extent of thyroid-related disease spread, although the precise reasons remain unclear. There is ongoing debate regarding the optimal iodine dosage for cases with a single metastasis. While common practice typically involves doses of 100–200 mCi, limited data exist on whether higher doses should be considered when treating multiple abdominal metastases from ovarian-type tumors. In the case of a 36-year-old female patient, Carlo Gobitti et al. administered a dose of 5,550 MBq, which represents the minimum amount of radiation necessary to eradicate cancerous lesions while remaining within safety limits for healthy organs [24]. The patient’s serum TSH and serum Tg levels were 155 µIU/mL and 2,140 ng/mL, respectively. However, accurately calculating radiation absorption in practice remains challenging. The fixed-dose approach provides simplicity and manageable side effects in the treatment of thyroid cancer. Given the extensive peritoneal and liver metastases observed on the whole-body scan, a higher dose of 200 mCi was administered in subsequent treatments.

A positive correlation has been observed between serum Tg levels and tumor Tg production, with serum Tg levels influenced by both the number of Tg-producing cells and their degree of differentiation. In this case, the patient’s Tg levels tended to decrease following discontinuation of medication. The short-term efficacy of iodine therapy for metastatic DTC showed promising results, with notable reductions in metastatic lesions and serum Tg levels. However, existing literature provides limited documentation of cases involving patients with widespread MSO metastases who maintain Tg levels below 1 ng/mL after iodine treatment [25]. The patient’s inhibitory Tg level has remained stable at approximately 8 ng/mL since the third iodine treatment. Immediate side effects related to the cumulative dose, such as pronounced gastrointestinal discomfort, highlight the need for long-term follow-up. Currently, iodine treatment is considered a relatively safe modality.

The prognosis for patients diagnosed with MSO is generally favorable, however, this observation is largely based on the limited number of reported cases and the inherent challenges in accurately assessing prognosis. Consequently, all patients with MSO require long-term monitoring, even those diagnosed early or with completely excised small tumors. Indicators for ongoing follow-up include comprehensive whole-body examinations, pelvic assessments, serum Tg measurements, iodine scintigraphy, or a combination of these methods. For patients with persistently elevated Tg levels, whole-body iodine irradiation should be performed to confirm recurrence. The current average monitoring period for recurrence is approximately 4 years, during which most patients can accurately detect recurrence through Tg level assessments or iodine irradiation [26]. This patient has been followed for approximately 5 years. Steady Tg levels suggest that the iodine adjuvant therapy is effective, and the patient’s condition is considered stable.
