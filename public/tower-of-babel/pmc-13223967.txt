Red blood cell deformability as a mechanobiological marker of CFTR dysfunction in cystic fibrosis
Authors: Eleonora Pero, Giovanna Tomaiuolo, Valeria Rachela Villella, Speranza Esposito, Martina Antonietta Vetrone, Antonella Panarelli, Alice Castaldo, Angela Sepe, Immacolata Andolfo, Antonella Tosco, Giuseppe Castaldo, Valeria Raia, Stefano Guido
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the gene encoding the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR), a chloride channel that controls salt and fluid transport across membranes. While CFTR is mainly studied in epithelial tissues, it is also detectable in red blood cells (RBCs), with a reduced expression in individuals with CF. However, the impact of this reduction on RBC deformability remains unexplored. Here, we investigated RBC deformability in CF using osmotic gradient ektacytometry. We examined 70 subjects with CF or CFTR-related disorders and compared them with 46 healthy control subjects. Our findings identify significant and reproducible alterations in RBC deformability associated with CFTR dysfunction, partially restored in subjects receiving highly effective CFTR modulators. These results support RBC deformability as a functional phenotype of CFTR dysfunction with prognostic potential, encouraging further investigations into the role of RBC membrane channels in cellular mechanics.

Introduction

One of the most important properties of erythrocytes (red blood cells, RBCs) is their ability to deform and navigate capillaries narrower than their diameter, enabling efficient gas exchange between blood and tissues.1,2 Beyond oxygen delivery, mechanical deformation of RBC triggers the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which regulates vascular tone.3,4,5,6 Altered RBC deformability may reflect changes in membrane integrity, ion channel function, and cellular hydration. While many hereditary membrane disorders involving ion channel mutations are well known to affect RBC deformability, cystic fibrosis (CF) remains largely unexplored in this regard. To date, no studies have investigated RBC deformability in CF, despite reasons to expect a connection.

CF, a common genetic disease among Caucasians, is caused by mutations in the gene encoding CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator), a chloride channel primarily involved in regulating salt and fluid transport across epithelia.7,8,9,10 The most frequent mutation, F508del, leads to CFTR misfolding and prevents it from reaching its final location at the cell surface. CFTR is expressed on a variety of cells throughout the human body, and mutations in this gene affect several organs, especially the lungs. Consequently, most research regarding CFTR function has historically focused on the lung airway epithelium.11 Nevertheless, more than two decades ago, studies revealed that CFTR is also expressed and functional on RBC.12,13 Atomic force microscopy-based recognition imaging has demonstrated that membrane CFTR protein levels are reduced by approximately 70% in erythrocytes from individuals with CF carrying homozygous F508del mutation.12,14,15,16,17

In erythrocytes, CFTR modulates ATP release in response to mechanical stress.9 Sprague et al. showed that reduced ATP release from CF erythrocytes leads to decreased pulmonary nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, potentially contributing to pulmonary hypertension, oxidative stress, and disrupted inflammatory regulation.3,12 These observations suggest a significant yet understudied connection between CFTR dysfunction in RBCs and the systemic manifestations of CF.

Importantly, recent studies have shown that endothelial CFTR interacts with mechanosensitive ion channels like PIEZO1 and TRPV4,18 which are well known to regulate ion exchange in response to mechanical and osmotic stress, thereby influencing cell volume and deformability.19 These findings raise an important question: do RBCs carrying CFTR mutations exhibit pathological alterations in volume and deformability?

To address this, we assessed RBC deformability in CF subjects using osmotic gradient ektacytometry (OGE). OGE is a reliable, high-throughput technique, widely used in clinical and research settings20,21,22,23 to diagnose hereditary RBC membrane disorders.24 It is able to distinguish between spherocytosis, elliptocytosis, ovalocytosis, and stomatocytosis,25 based on differences in RBC deformability caused by deficiencies or dysfunctions in RBC membrane skeletal proteins.26 OGE provides a profile of RBC deformability across varying osmolalities and under controlled shear stress. This profile reflects the combined effect of cell volume, membrane stability and ion channel function, all closely linked to cell hydration.

We compared RBC deformability profile from CF subjects with those of healthy control subjects, employing univariate analyses, principal component analysis, receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC) analysis, and Spearman correlations to identify discriminant parameters. Furthermore, we examined the effects of two CFTR modulators, lumacaftor/ivacaftor (LUM/IVA) and elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI), on RBC deformability, comparing these effects with sweat chloride changes that reflect epithelial CFTR.

Additionally, we studied individuals with CFTR-related disorders (CFTR-RDs), who show clinical signs of CFTR dysfunction (e.g., recurrent respiratory symptoms, sinusitis, pancreatitis), carry two genetic CFTR variants with at least one mild mutation, but have normal or borderline sweat chloride levels. These patients do not fulfill the formal criteria for CF diagnosis and are frequently left in a diagnostic “gray zone,” with limited access to CFTR modulators.27,28 We explored whether OGE could detect CFTR dysfunction through RBC deformability, potentially contributing to functional assessment in this diagnostically challenging population and guiding treatment decisions.

This section describes the clinical and demographic characteristics of the study population and presents the analysis of RBC deformability, including group comparisons, multivariate analysis, correlation with clinical parameters, and response to CFTR modulator therapy.

Among the 70 enrolled subjects, 63 received a confirmed diagnosis of CF, while 7 were classified as having CFTR-RDs. Most subjects with CF carried two severe CFTR variants (class I or II), such as F508del, G542X, or N1303K, which result in absent or misprocessed CFTR protein and are associated with minimal or no residual function. A smaller number of subjects carried at least one variant associated with residual CFTR activity, such as 3849+10kbC>T or D1152H. CFTR-RD subjects had normal sweat chloride levels but presented clinical features indicative of CFTR dysfunction. All CFTR-RD subjects carried two CFTR gene variants, including at least one associated with residual or mild functional activity, such as D1152H, P5L, 5T-12TG, N703S, or K503E, often in compound heterozygosity with a severe mutation such as F508del. According to current diagnostic guidelines, these cases do not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for CF27,28 and are therefore categorized as CFTR-RD.

Among the 63 subjects with confirmed CF diagnosis, 20 were already undergoing treatment with CFTR modulators (either LUM/IVA or ETI) at the time of blood collection, while the remaining 43 had not yet started any modulator therapy. Some of these untreated subjects were later monitored after starting treatment with either LUM/IVA or ETI.32,33 For clarity, we label data from untreated subjects as CF (n = 43), those from patients treated with LUM/IVA as CF-LI (n = 10), and those receiving ETI as CF-ETI (n = 28).

Osmotic gradient ektacytometry reveals altered RBC deformability associated with CFTR dysfunction

OGE generates a deformability profile of RBC, expressed as the elongation index (EI) as a function of the osmolality of the suspension medium. It employs a Couette geometry setup (Figure 1A) to apply a controlled shear stress that induces RBC elongation (Figure 1B). From this profile, named the osmoscan curve (Figure 1C), key parameters were derived, including Omin (reflecting the surface-to-volume ratio and hydration status), EImax (maximum deformability), O(EImax) (indicative of membrane properties), and Ohyper (associated with intracellular viscosity or MCHC).Figure 1Experimental setup and representative osmoscan curve(A) Schematic illustration of the Lorrca OGE experimental setup. In the PCA biplot, the length of the loading vectors represents the contributions of each osmoscan parameters to the total variance of the dataset. The similar lengths of these vectors indicate that all variables contribute equally to explaining the dataset variance. Notably, Omin, O(EImax), Ohyper, and Area align with the direction of maximum separation between CTRL and CF clusters, suggesting their key role in driving group differentiation. Conversely, EImin and EImax vectors point in the opposite direction, implying they are more associated with variability within groups rather than with differences between them.

Group separation is further supported by the distributions of osmoscan parameters shown in Figure 2C. CF and CFTR-RD subjects display significantly lower values for Omin, O(EImax), and Ohyper and higher values for EImin compared with the CTRL group. This pattern is characteristic of an RBC dehydrated status also observed in other membrane disorders, such as dehydrated hereditary stomatocytosis (or xerocytosis).21 Additionally, decreased EImax and Area may reflect impaired RBC deformability. Note that EIhyper is excluded in our analysis because it is computationally derived by the ektacytometer as half of EImax and is therefore redundant. ROC-AUC analysis identifies discriminant osmoscan parameters in cystic fibrosis

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of osmoscan parameters to discriminate between CF subjects and healthy control subjects at the group level. ROC curves plot the true-positive rate (sensitivity) against the false-positive rate (1-specificity) across a range of threshold values. The AUC provides a summary measure of discriminatory performance. Among the osmoscan parameters, Ohyper, O(EImax), Omin, and Area (in descending order of AUC) showed the strongest discriminatory performance between CF and CTRL groups (Table 2). Correlations between osmoscan parameters and clinical data

Spearman correlation analysis between osmoscan parameters and clinical data was performed to assess whether CF alters existing relationships or introduces new associations, offering insights into disease-specific RBC biomechanics and systemic CFTR dysfunction.

Additionally, correlations among osmoscan parameters help identify redundancy among the four diagnostically relevant variables identified by the ROC analysis, aiding feature reduction for improved statistical modeling and future machine/deep learning applications. Figures 3A and 3B present heatmaps of Spearman correlation coefficients (r) between osmoscan parameters (EImin, Omin, EImax, O(EImax), Ohyper, Area), RBC indices (Ht, Hb, MCV, RDW), age, and sex in both healthy control subjects and subjects with CF. The lack of significant correlations with age or sex (|r|<0.50) further confirms that osmoscan parameters primarily reflect intrinsic RBC properties that are independent of demographic factors.34 Figure 3C, D, and E highlight the three most relevant variable pairs with strong correlations, Omin-O(EImax), Ohyper-O(EImax), and Ohyper-Area, and Table 3 presents the statistical analysis of correlations and linear regressions. The Fisher Z-transformation pvalue indicates no significant difference (Z-pvalue > 0.05) in the Omin-O(EImax) Spearman correlation coefficients, suggesting that this relationship remains stable between CF and control groups. This stability is further supported by the similarity in regression slopes (βCTRL = 1.24, βCF = 1.46, β-pvalue > 0.05). In contrast, CF induces a distinct correlation pattern in the Ohyper-Area pair (rCTRL = 0.51, rCF = 0.97, Z-pvalue < 0.05) and the emergence of a strong correlation between Ohyper and O(EImax), absent in control subjects (rCTRL = 0.19, rCF = 0.70, Z-pvalue < 0.05). Additionally, the Ohyper-Area pair exhibits a significantly steeper regression slope in CF patients (βCTRL = 0.22, βCF = 0.58, β-pvalue < 0.05), further emphasizing the altered relationship between these parameters. This suggests a disease-specific correlation pattern, reflecting CF-related alterations in RBC deformability. Impact of CFTR modulator treatment on osmoscan parameters in cystic fibrosis

A key part of this study focused on evaluating the impact of modulator therapies such as LUM/IVA and ETI on osmoscan parameters and, consequently, RBC properties. Figure 4 represents group-level analyses based on treatment status at the time of assessment, rather than longitudinal paired evaluations. At this level, no significant changes in osmoscan parameters were observed after LUM/IVA treatment (CF-LI) compared with untreated CF subjects. In contrast, ETI treatment (CF-ETI) led to significant increases in Omin, O(EImax), Ohyper, and Area, with complete restoration of Omin and O(EImax) to control levels. These findings are consistent with clinical observations, where the sweat test shows only modest changes after LUM/IVA but decreased to near the reference range (RR, <40 mmol/L)30 following ETI treatment. Notably, most CF subjects had an FEV1% within the reference range (RR) (>80%), likely due to their young age (mean 9 years old), as lung function is often preserved in children with CF at this stage. To complement the group-level comparisons, a paired pre-post analysis was conducted in a subset of subjects who initiated ETI during the study period. Discussion

While CFTR is best known for its role in regulating salt and fluid transport in epithelial tissues, it is also expressed on RBC membranes, with reduced levels in CF subjects. However, the impact of CFTR dysfunction on RBC deformability has remained unexplored. Our study shows that CFTR mutations are associated with significant alterations in RBC deformability, as measured by OGE. These findings identify RBC deformability as a distinct biophysical phenotype linked to CFTR dysfunction and extend the functional consequences of CFTR impairment beyond epithelial compartments.

Compared with healthy control subjects, CF subjects exhibited a significant leftward shift of the OGE curve, characterized by reduced values of Omin, O(EImax), Ohyper, and Area (Figure 2A). Statistical analyses confirmed clear separation between CF and control groups, as shown by univariate tests and PCA (Figures 2B and 2C). ROC analysis (Table 2) and Spearman correlations (Figure 3) highlight Ohyper as the best parameter discriminating CF subjects from control subjects, with AUC values of 0.99. The leftward shift of the OGE curve of CF subjects is consistent with RBC dehydration and increased intracellular viscosity. This pattern resembles that described in dehydrated hereditary xerocytosis, a condition caused by mutations in the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1,19,21,35 which leads to RBC dehydration. In this regard, recent studies have also reported functional interactions between CFTR and mechanosensitive channels, including PIEZO1.18 Although the aim of the present study was not to investigate the molecular mechanisms, these findings from the literature provide mechanistic support for our observations and offer a plausible explanation for the dehydration phenotype identified in CF subjects. Further studies will be needed to determine whether altered crosstalk among RBC membrane channels directly affect RBC hydration in CFTR dysfunctions.

Beyond classic CF, we also analyzed subjects with CFTR-related disorders, who carry two genetic CFTR variants with at least one mild mutation. These individuals often present with normal or borderline sweat chloride levels, despite exhibiting clinical features consistent with CFTR dysfunction. Our data show altered RBC deformability in CFTR-RD subjects, with OGE profiles largely overlapping those observed in confirmed CF patients (Figure 2A, B; Table S1). This finding suggests that RBC hydration-related parameters capture functional consequences of CFTR impairment even in individuals in whom standard clinical measures remain within the reference range. Given the diagnostic uncertainty and the limited availability of functional testing for CFTR-RD,28 the identification of a reproducible RBC deformability phenotype may open new avenues for investigating the potential role of RBC biomechanics as an adjunct functional readout in complex or borderline cases. This perspective warrant further validation in larger and independent cohorts.

We further evaluated the effects of CFTR modulator therapies on RBC deformability. CF subjects treated with ETI exhibited a significant shift of hydration-related osmoscan parameters toward the control range, whereas no significant changes were observed in subjects receiving LUM/IVA (Figure 4). These findings are consistent with the differential clinical responses reported for these therapies, including the more pronounced reduction in sweat chloride levels observed under ETI treatment. This supports the interpretation of RBC deformability as a sensitive functional readout of CFTR modulation. Notably, while Omin and O(EImax) approached control values under ETI, residual alterations in Ohyper and Area suggest that CFTR modulation may not completely normalize all aspects of RBC hydration and deformability. This observation aligns with the concept that current modulators, although highly effective, do not fully restore CFTR folding, trafficking, and channel function, highlighting the potential value of further therapeutic refinement.

The association between CFTR dysfunction, RBC deformability, and clinical status highlights the systemic dimension of CFTR impairment. Reduced CFTR expression has been associated with impaired ATP release and NO synthesis,9 key regulators of vascular tone. Altered RBC hydration and deformability could, in principle, influence microvascular flow, oxygen delivery, and inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to broader physiological consequences.3,12 While vascular and pulmonary implications are not directly addressed in this study, the observed phenotype provides a rationale for future investigations into the role of RBC biomechanics in CF-related systemic manifestations.

Future studies should aim to further elucidate the molecular basis of this phenotype, including direct quantification of CFTR protein on RBC membranes and in vitro modulation experiments using CFTR inhibitors or mechanosensitive channel modulators to assess causality. Moreover, evaluating RBC deformability in non-CF inflammatory lung diseases would help clarify the specificity of the observed alterations and distinguish intrinsic CFTR-dependent effects from secondary systemic influences.

Overall, our findings identify RBC deformability as a sensitive readout of CFTR dysfunction and modulation. The identification of a reproducible hydration-related phenotype in RBCs broadens the perspective on CFTR biology beyond epithelial tissues and supports further investigation into its mechanisms and systemic consequences.

Limitations of the study

This study was primarily observational and was not designed to define the molecular mechanisms linking CFTR dysfunction to altered RBC deformability. Direct assessment of CFTR protein on RBC membranes, together with in vitro modulation studies targeting CFTR or interacting ion channels, will be required to determine causality. Potential contributions of systemic factors associated with CF (including inflammation, oxidative stress, or chronic infection) to the observed RBC deformability alterations cannot be fully excluded. Future studies should include control cohorts with non-CF inflammatory lung diseases to distinguish CFTR-dependent RBC alterations from secondary systemic effects. In addition, the CFTR-RD and LUM/IVA subgroups were relatively small, and paired longitudinal data were available only for a subset of ETI-treated subjects. Larger, independent, genotype-stratified, and longitudinal cohorts will be needed to validate the clinical relevance and specificity of RBC deformability measurements in CFTR dysfunction.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cystic fibrosis subjects

This observational clinical cohort study was conducted on human participants undergoing cystic fibrosis (CF) diagnosis and clinical monitoring at the Cystic Fibrosis Regional Reference Center of the University of Naples “Federico II”. Diagnostic evaluation, CFTR genetic testing, hematological analyses, clinical monitoring, and therapeutic decisions were performed by the clinical center according to standard clinical practice and independently of the present study.

The study cohort included 70 subjects, 33 males and 37 females, ranging in age from 8 months to 17 years (mean age 9 years).36 Participants included subjects with confirmed cystic fibrosis and subjects with CFTR-related disorders, as defined according to clinical evaluation, sweat chloride testing, and CFTR genetic analysis. Participants were not selected a priori according to CFTR genotype and CFTR variants were summarized in the results section. At the time of blood collection, participants were either untreated (CF, n=43) or receiving CFTR modulator therapy with lumacaftor/ivacaftor (CF-LI, n=10) or elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (CF-ETI, n=28).32,33 Because some participants were sampled at more than one time point, including before and after initiation of CFTR modulator therapy, the number of analyzed blood samples exceeded the number of enrolled subjects.

For this study, a peripheral blood aliquot was used to assess red blood cell deformability by osmotic gradient ektacytometry (OGE), and the resulting data were integrated with clinical, hematological, and genetic information obtained from routine diagnostic and monitoring procedures. Hematological parameters included hematocrit (Ht), hemoglobin concentration (Hb), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and red cell distribution width (RDW). Clinical data included sweat chloride testing and pulmonary function assessment, i.e., forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1%).

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Naples Federico II (77/2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians before blood collection.

Control dataset

Data from healthy controls (CTRL) were obtained from an open-access dataset by van Cromvoirt et al.,29 including 46 healthy donors (17 males, 29 females; mean age 34 years, range 18-61). Osmotic gradient ektacytometry measurements (EImin, Omin, EImax, Omax, EIhyper, Ohyper, Area), were performed under the same experimental conditions applied in this study (shear stress 30 Pa, 37°C), along with hematological indices (Ht, Hb, MCV, and RDW). Although the Cromvoirt dataset includes only adults, its suitability for paediatric comparison was supported by consistency with reference ranges derived from an age-stratified cohort, reported by Kristiansen et al.34 (3 months-90 years), with approximately 61% pediatric participants. As shown in Table S2 of the supplemental information, the main Osmoscan parameters were highly consistent between the two datasets, with substantial overlap in the reference ranges for O(EImax), Ohyper, and Area. Minor differences were observed for EImin and Omin, with slightly higher values and wider intervals in the Kristiansen et al. cohort, likely reflecting the larger sample size and broader age range. To further exclude potential instrument- or operator-related bias, we also analyzed 9 local healthy donors under the same laboratory setting.37 Their osmoscan curves were comparable to those of the external control dataset, as shown in Table S2 and Figure S3 of supplemental information. Together, these comparisons support the use of the van Cromvoirt et al.29 dataset as an external reference control for the present study.

Method details

RBC deformability was evaluated via Osmotic Gradient Ektacytometry (OGE), which is widely used in the diagnosis of RBC membrane disorders. For OGE-based measurements, 250 μl of EDTA-anticoagulated blood was mixed with 5 ml of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, 30 mPas) and analyzed within 48 hours using the Laser-assisted Rotational Red Cell Analyzer (Lorrca MaxSis, Mechatronics Instruments BV, Zwaag, Netherlands). This device generates a deformability profile of RBC, expressed as the elongation index (EI) as a function of the osmolality of the suspension medium. It employs a Couette geometry setup (Figure 1A) to apply a controlled shear stress that induces RBC elongation (Figure 1B). Measurements are performed at 30 Pa and 37°C to generate the osmoscan curve (Figure 1C), which yields key parameters for assessing RBC surface-to-volume ratio and hydration status (Omin),21,38,39 maximum deformability (EImax), potential membrane abnormalities (O(EImax)) and intracellular viscosity or MCHC (Ohyper).21,40

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software and Python. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) extracted PC1 and PC2, two orthogonal linear combinations of the original variables that maximize variance in a lower-dimensional space.41,42,43

The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed distribution normality. Normally distributed data were analysed with an unpaired t-test, while non-Gaussian data used the Mann-Whitney test, with 1% FDR correction. ROC analysis identified the best osmoscan parameters for distinguishing CF from CTRL, optimizing clinical thresholds. Spearman correlation (with 1% FDR correction) heatmaps and linear regressions were used to examine relationships between variables.
