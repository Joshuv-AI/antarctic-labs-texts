Comparison of elastosonographic changes of the tibial nerve and Achilles tendon in patients with type II diabetes mellitus
Authors: HuiHui Yang, JianMei Huang, ShuCheng Chen, JiaYing Lin, Kuan Cai, Qing Feng, Yu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo compare the elastosonographic changes of the tibial nerve (TN) and Achilles tendon (AT) in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and explore their relationship and respective relevant factors.MethodsThis case-control study enrolled 165 subjects, comprising 126 patients with T2DM and 39 healthy controls matched for age and gender. The patients were further divided into those with and without diabetic peripheral neuropathy (PN-DM and NPN-DM groups). Clinical and laboratory data were collected. Conventional ultrasound and elastography were performed to assess the changes in the morphology and elasticity of the bilateral TN and AT. Sonographic features were compared across the three groups, relevant factors affecting the stiffness of TN and AT were analyzed, respectively.ResultsDiabetic patients exhibited significantly higher levels of HbA1C and a higher rate of smoking than healthy controls (P < 0.01 and P = 0.02, respectively). Their levels of body mass index (BMI) and total cholesterol have a significant difference between the NPN-DM group and healthy controls (both P = 0.02). The incidence of other microvascular complications in the NPN-DM group was significantly lower among diabetic patients (P = 0.04). Compared with healthy controls, the cross-sectional area (CSA) and transverse diameter of TN in diabetic patients were significantly larger (both P < 0.01), and CSA and anteroposterior diameter of AT were notably greater (P = 0.02 and P < 0.01). Besides, the stiffness of TN in the longitudinal section was significantly higher (P < 0.01), and the stiffness of AT in the cross-section was remarkably lower (P < 0.01). There was no significant difference in the morphology or elastography of TN or AT between NPN-DM and PN-DM groups. Furthermore, the stiffness of TN was not linearly related to that of AT, but independently correlated with age, HbA1C, and other microvascular complications (P < 0.05). The stiffness of AT was only independent of age (P < 0.01).ConclusionsThe size of both TN and AT in diabetic patients was significantly larger. The stiffness of TN increased, and that of AT decreased; however, these changes were independent of each other.Clinical trial numberNot applicable. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major cause of disability because of the high prevalence of microangiopathy and macroangiopathy, with diabetic foot being a terrible and serious complication. Many factors contribute to diabetic foot ulcers, including diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), trauma, and peripheral artery disease.

DPN is the strongest initiating risk factor for diabetic foot ulcers [1], affecting at least 45% of patients with T2DM [2]. DPN results pathophysiologically from metabolic disorders caused by long-term hyperglycemia. Sorbitol and fructose accumulate excessively in nerve cells, thereby increasing intracellular osmotic pressure and causing nerve cell edema [3]. This endoneurial edema elevates intraneural pressure, stiffens the nerves, and compresses the microvascular system, ultimately reducing blood flow. Therefore, DPN is regarded as another type of diabetic microvascular complication, other than diabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic nephropathy (DN) [4]. The performance of tibial nerve (TN) stiffness is also increasingly evaluated for characterizing DPN [1].

Meanwhile, hyperglycemia promotes the non-enzymatic glycation of proteins, leading to the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) in various tissues, including muscles, tendons, ligaments, and cartilage [5]. Therefore, diabetic patients have a threefold increased risk of all musculoskeletal disorders, and they are particularly prone to tendinopathy [6]. Diabetic tendinopathy is increasingly considered a significantly underappreciated non-vascular complication of diabetes, with prevalence varying between 52.5% and 85% [7, 8]. The Achilles tendon (AT) is the largest in the body and plays a critical role in human movement. Its structural changes and altered stiffness in diabetic patients may increase the foot load and accelerate the occurrence of diabetic foot [9]. Based on it, understanding the relationship between diabetic tendinopathy of AT and DPN will help in better comprehending the occurrence of diabetic foot and in preventing it more effectively.

Ultrasound examination is a real-time, non-invasive, and radiation-free method offering high resolution for observing the morphology, structure, and echogenicity of superficial tissues. Shear wave elastography (SWE) is an advanced ultrasound technique that measures changes in shear wave velocity (SWV) to assess tissue stiffness along with morphology [10], and it is now commonly applied in the breast, thyroid, liver, and other organs. It is operator-independent and highly repeatable.

Therefore, this study investigated features of the morphology and stiffness in both TN and AT among diabetic patients with and without DPN using conventional sonography and elastosonography, aiming to compare the morphology and function (SWE) changes of TN and AT in patients with T2DM and explore their clinical relationship.

Methods

This case-control study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. All examinations in this study were performed after obtaining written informed consent from all participants. 2.1 Participants.

A total of 180 inpatients diagnosed with T2DM in our hospital were enrolled from November 2023 to July 2025, matched by gender and age. Exclusion criteria were as follows: [1] Presence of severe medical conditions like heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, and tumors [2]. Known history of polyneuropathy from other causes, such as genetic, alcoholic, metabolic, inflammatory, or toxic origins [3]. Use of medications affecting nerves, such as vitamin B6, hydralazine, etc [4]. Disorders due to nerve compression, including intervertebral disc and radicular disease [5]. Presence of skin injury or swelling [6]. History of rheumatism, Achilles tendinitis, trauma, or intense physical activity [7]. Individuals with a body mass index (BMI) indicating overweight (BMI ≥ 28) [8]. Patients with moderate-severe stenosis (≥ 50%) or occlusion of lower limb arteries. Fifty-four patients were excluded, 6 for insufficient cardiac or renal function, 5 for thyroid dysfunction, 18 for peripheral arterial occlusive disease, 5 for lower limb edema, 8 for a history of tumor, and 12 for being overweight (BMI > 28). Ultimately, 126 patients were included in the study and further divided into two groups: those with DPN (PN-DM) group (n = 47) and those without (NPN-DM) group (n = 79). Additionally, 39 non-diabetic patients were recruited as the control group. And ultrasound examination and clinical data were collected by different researchers.

Diagnostic criteria

The clinical criteria for diagnosing DPN require the presence of a combination of symptoms and signs of neuropathy, including any two or more of the following: neuropathic symptoms (such as tingling, numbness, burning sensation, or ataxia), decreased distal sensation (such as impaired temperature sensation, touch, pain, and vibration), or unequivocally decreased or absent ankle reflexes [11]. The diagnostic criteria for DR are determined by dilated fundus examination [12]. DN is defined as persistently elevated urine albumin excretion (≥ 30 mg/g [≥ 3 mg/mmol]), persistently reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (< 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2), or both, for greater than 3 months in diabetic patients, excluding other primary or secondary kidney diseases [13].

Clinical profile

Detailed demographic data of all subjects were recorded, including clinical history, duration of diabetes, and anthropometric measurements. Relevant laboratory investigations, such as fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting C-peptide (FCP), haemoglobin A1c (HbA1C), blood lipid, and kidney function, were obtained within one week of ultrasound examinations.

Ultrasound examination

All examinations were performed using a 9–14 MHz linear array transducer (Resona 7 S; Mindary, High-Tech Industrial Park, Shenzhen, China). It is worth noting that the probe should be held perpendicular to the measuring position to avoid anisotropy. The probe should contact the skin without pressure; additional medical ultrasound gel was used, especially for AT, due to its shallow position. The examination was performed by two experienced doctors who were trained in ultrasound examination of TN and AT.

Morphological measurements

Subjects were positioned supine, with their lower limbs relaxed and slightly externally rotated to expose their medial ankle joints enough. First, a cross-sectional image of TN was taken 3 cm above the superior border of the medial malleolus in both the lower limbs to observe the clarity of the TN fascicle membrane, fascicle echo, and internal mesh structure, followed by freezing the image for measurement of the anteroposterior diameter (APD), transverse diameter (TD), and cross-sectional area (CSA) three times. Then subjects were in a prone position, with their feet naturally suspended outside the examination bed. A cross-sectional image of AT was obtained 4 cm proximal to its calcaneal insertion, which is vulnerable to blood supply and biomechanical factors [14]. Then APD, TD, and CSA of AT were measured three times. An average of the three readings was taken as the final reading regardless of TN or AT. A combined value of the TN or AT was also calculated using the mean of both limbs. An inter-observer reliability test was performed in 30 subjects randomly selected from three groups (10 per group).

Elastosonography measurements

When the two-dimensional image was clear and stable, the probe was held still for 3–5 s to perform elastography. The elastography imaging was frozen, different region of interest (ROI) was selected according to differences in different sections and image sizes, ensuring that all the TN and AT in the image enter the area of interest as much as possible. And stored for the SWV measurements, both the cross-sectional and longitudinal images of TN and AT were stored and analyzed (3–5 images per section).

An ROI (2 mm × 2 mm) was carefully placed within TN on the cross-section, while 5 ROIs of the same size were placed along the longitudinal section. In contrast, two ROIs (4 mm × 4 mm) were placed within AT on the cross-section, while 5 ROIs of the same size were placed along the longitudinal section to ensure AT was covered. The SWV of TN or AT for each image was measured three times to calculate the average, and then the final value of SWV was determined by averaging the results from 3 to 5 images. Similarly, a combined SWV value of the TN or AT was also calculated using the mean of both limbs. An inter-observer reliability test was performed in 30 subjects randomly selected from three groups (10 per group).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26.0. The chi-square test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. For quantitative data comparison, either ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Parameters that showed significant differences among the three groups, along with potential confounding factors such as age and BMI, were selected for correlation analysis of the SWV of TN using multiple linear regression. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant. The morphological and elastographic data of the bilateral TN and AT from different examiners were analyzed to evaluate the inter-observer reliability using the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Values less than 0.5 are indicative of poor reliability, values between 0.5 and 0.75 are indicative of moderate reliability, values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and values greater than 0.9 indicate excellent reliability.

Results

Study demographics

The demographic and laboratory characteristics of all subjects are summarized in Table 1. Diabetic patients exhibited significantly higher levels of HbA1C, a higher rate of smoking, and a greater proportion of subjects with a family history of diabetes when compared to the control group. (P < 0.01, P = 0.02, and P < 0.01, respectively). Their levels of body mass index (BMI) and triglyceride (TG) were also higher, with a significant difference between the NPN-DM group and the control group (both P = 0.02). In contrast, their levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) were lower, with a significant difference between the NPN-DM group and the control group (P = 0.01). There was a significant difference in medication between the diabetic groups (P = 0.02). The incidence of other microvascular complications in the NPN-DM group was significantly lower among diabetic patients (P = 0.04). The duration of diabetes in the PN-DM group was longer, though no significant difference was shown between diabetic groups (P = 0.09). Besides, there were no significant differences in the remaining other indicators among the three groups (P > 0.05).

Morphological measurements of conventional ultrasound

Morphological measurements of TN and AT were both performed in the cross-section. Compared with the control group, the cross-sectional area (CSA) and transverse diameter (TD) of TN in diabetic patients were significantly larger (both P < 0.01), and CSA and the anteroposterior diameter (APD) of AT were notably greater (P = 0.02 and P < 0.01). However, there was no significant difference in CSA, TD, or APD of TN or AT between diabetic groups, though they showed a modestly increasing tendency in size from the control group, NPN-DM, to PN-DM groups (Table 2; Fig. 1). The ICC of morphological measurements (TD, APD, and CSA) for the bilateral TN was 0.79–0.91, and that for the bilateral AT was 0.86–0.98 (Supplement Tables 1 and 2).

Comparison of shear wave elastography

Elastosonography (Emean, Emax, and Emin) of TN and AT was performed both in the cross-section and longitudinal section. Compared with the control group, the stiffness of TN in the longitudinal section in diabetic patients was significantly higher (P < 0.01), and the stiffness of AT in the cross-section was remarkably lower (P < 0.01). However, there was no significant difference in the above parameters between the diabetic groups, although they showed a tendency toward stiffening in TN and softening in AT compared to the control group, from NPN-DM to PN-DM. Besides, there was no significant difference in the elastography of TN in the cross-section or AT in the longitudinal section among the three groups (Table 3; Fig. 2). The ICC of the elasticity data (Emean, Emax, and Emin) for the bilateral TN were 0.97–0.99 in the longitudinal section and 0.72–0.97 in the cross-section. The ICC of the elasticity data (Emean, Emax, and Emin) for the bilateral AT were 0.77–0.85 in the longitudinal section and 0.83–0.97 in the cross-section. (Supplement Tables 3 and 3).

Discussion

Since DPN and diabetic tendinopathy of AT are both risk factors for diabetic foot and are affected by long-term hyperglycemia, in the present study, we compared the morphological and elastosonographic changes of TN and AT in patients with T2DM. Whether DPN was present or not, the results showed that the size of both TN and AT in diabetic patients was significantly larger. In contrast, the stiffness of the TN increased, and that of the AT decreased. Additionally, the elasticity changes were independent of each other.

As we know, endoneurial edema and the accumulation of AGEs in AT in diabetes can cause structural changes of TN and AT, which may explain our findings that both TN and AT appear larger in diabetic patients. However, scholars have not agreed on morphological changes in TN and AT in diabetes. Dhanapalaratna et al. reported that patients with more severe symptoms or signs of DPN exhibited larger CSA of TN [15]. D’Ambrogi’s study also showed that AT thickening was more pronounced in the presence of DPN [16]. These findings were similar to our results, however, we didn’t detect a distinct difference in the size of TN or AT between patients with and without DPN. On the contrary, Wang et al. found no statistically significant differences in CSA of TN among healthy individuals, patients without DPN, and those with DPN [17]. Furthermore, Evranos’s research suggested that there was no association between AT thickness and the occurrence of diabetic foot ulcers [9]. The reason for these conflicts among studies might result from multiple factors, such as gender, age, and weight, affecting the morphology of TN and AT [17–19], which may challenge the value of the morphological measurements by the conventional ultrasound.

Similar to other studies, our results showed that the longitudinal SWV of diabetic patients was significantly higher than that of the control group. Although the stiffness of TN showed a modest increasing trend with the presence of DPN, there were no significant differences between the NPN-DM and PN-DM groups. This was inconsistent with others who found that diabetic patients with DPN exhibited a significantly higher elastic modulus than those without [17, 20]. We speculated that a potential reason could be the clinical heterogeneity of the NPN-DM group. This notion aligns with the emerging principles of peripheral nerve ultrasound, which emphasize distinguishing structural from functional neuropathies [15, 17]. Structural neuropathy is characterized by overt morphological changes such as increased cross-sectional area (CSA), fascicular swelling, or loss of the normal honeycomb pattern, reflecting endoneurial edema, demyelination, or axonal loss [15, 20]. In contrast, functional neuropathy may present with preserved morphology but altered biomechanical properties—such as increased stiffness on SWE—indicating elevated intraneural pressure or early metabolic derangement without frank structural remodeling [17, 20]. At the nerve fascicle level, these ultrasound features can help differentiate early from late diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN). Early DPN is typically associated with reversible endoneurial edema and increased intrafascicular fluid content, leading to subtle fascicular enlargement and increased stiffness on SWE even when CSA remains within normal limits [1, 20]. Late DPN, however, involves progressive endoneurial fibrosis, fascicular convergence, and loss of distinct fascicular borders, often accompanied by nerve atrophy or persistently enlarged CSA with harder or heterogeneous elasticity [15, 21]. Previous work on ultrasound-based assessment of neuropathy has highlighted the value of combining morphological and elastographic parameters for early detection of DPN, especially in subclinical stages before electrophysiological abnormalities become evident [17, 20]. In our study, although no significant difference in TN stiffness was found between the NPN-DM and PN-DM groups, the progressive increase in stiffness from controls to NPN-DM to PN-DM suggests that SWE may detect early functional changes before structural differences become statistically significant. This interpretation supports the concept that elastosonography could serve as a complementary tool to conventional ultrasound for identifying subclinical DPN.In addition, we did not find a significant difference in the cross-sectional SWV of TN among the three groups, which could be due to the different arrangement of TN in the transverse section from the longitudinal section. The relatively small area of TN in the cross-section could also affect the placement of ROI. The ICC of the transversal SWV was also lower.

Unlike the stiffening of TN in the longitudinal section, we found that the elasticity of AT in the transversal section decreased in diabetic patients and showed a softening tendency with the presence of DPN. However, there were no significant differences between the NPN-DM and PN-DM groups, which was consistent with findings by Harish et al. [22]. In contrast, Özlem Turhan İyidir found that patients with DPN had significantly softer AT compared to those without DPN and healthy individuals [14]. In fact, the sonographic changes in diabetic patients should not be viewed as purely structural. Instead, they reflect metabolic‑mechanical coupling—the interplay between metabolic injury and biomechanical loading. For the tibial nerve, hyperglycemia induces endoneurial edema and microvascular damage, leading to increased stiffness [3, 4, 20]. For the Achilles tendon, advanced glycation end‑products alter viscoelasticity, while posture, muscle tone, and weight‑bearing further modulate its stiffness [5, 9, 16], which may explain the differences among different studies. Thus, diabetes-related imaging findings are dynamic readouts of metabolic‑mechanical coupling rather than static structural markers [23]. Besides, we found a significant difference in the stiffness of the AT in the cross-section but not in the longitudinal section. We thought that the superficial location of AT made its elastic measurements susceptible to pressure, which was more pronounced along the longitudinal axis of the AT due to a larger contact area. It turned out that the ICC of the longitudinal SWV was also lower.

To investigate the relationship of changes in elasticity between TN and AT in diabetes, we performed a subsequent multiple linear regression analysis. The results showed that the SWV (Emean value) of TN and AT was not linearly correlated.However, this apparent independence may be more apparent than real, due to mechanical interactions within the nerve‑muscle‑tendon‑fascia functional unit. Peripheral nerves do not exist in isolation; they are mechanically coupled to surrounding structures including deep fascia, synergistic and antagonistic muscles, and tendons [16, 20]. Any change in Achilles tendon stiffness or muscle tone can alter the local mechanical environment—for instance, increased tendon stiffness may modify the tensile load transmitted to the crural fascia, which in turn influences the intraneural pressure of the tibial nerve via fascial slings or compartmental pressure shift [9]. Functional entrapment during ankle movement may cause intermittent nerve compression [1, 15], and postural overload in diabetic patients can lead to indirect nerve stiffness changes [21]. Thus, the lack of linear correlation does not rule out interdependence, and a dynamic pathology model [16, 23] warrants further investigation.

In contrast, age, the level of HbA1c, and the presence of other microvascular complications were independently relevant to the elasticity of TN. As mentioned above, the alteration in the stiffness of TN is gradually becoming one of the characteristics of DPN [1]. The recently proposed concept of diabetic panvascular disease (DPD) precisely justifies the significant correlation between the TN stiffness and other diabetic microvascular complications, which may indicate that an increase in the stiffness of TN probably represent the severity of diabetes and may indicate the occurrence of other microvascular diseases [23].

Our study has some limitations. First, DPN is a multifocal peripheral neuropathy, but we only investigated the TN and AT, focusing on specific sites of these structures. Second, this study has excluded the overweight individuals (BMI ≥ 28), which may with several common comorbidities. The aim is to rule out the influence of other factors of complications on the neuromuscular system. But due to the different degrees of diabetes among the three groups, there are still differences in BMI among them, which could affect the results. Thirdly, Use of combined bilateral mean values in this study may mask side-to-side differences, more detailed research is needed in the future. Fourthly, the sample size of our study is relatively small, limiting the statistical power to detect subtle differences. Finally, although elastosonography assesses the elasticity of nerve and tendon quantitatively, we did not identify patients with subclinical DPN because of insufficient data on NCS in diabetic patients. Patients were divided into the NPN-DM and PN -DM groups based solely on clinical symptoms and signs, which may have led to the possibility of misclassifying asymptomatic DPN patients into the NPN-DM group.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the size of both TN and AT in diabetic patients was significantly larger. The stiffness of TN increased, and that of AT decreased; however, these changes were independent of each other. The stiffening of the TN was independently related to age, the level of HbA1c, and microvascular complications, which may represent the severity of diabetes and suggest whether there are any other complications. This could non-invasively assess the severity of diabetes in patients at an early stage and could also prompt doctors in choosing suitable treatment plans for patients.
