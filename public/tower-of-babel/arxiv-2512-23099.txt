Lectures on Gauge theories and Many-Body systems
Nikita Nekrasov, Igor Chaban

Abstract




1
Introduction

These lectures discuss two correspondences between gauge theories and integrable many-body systems.
The first correspondence goes back to the work of many mathematicians and physicists in the 1980-1990’s and is provided by an infinite dimensional Hamiltonian reduction and its quantum counterparts. In this approach the quantization parameters of the gauge theory coincide with the quantization parameters of the many-body system.
The second correspondence started emerging in the mid-1990’s, it involves non-trivial dualities, relating classical problems on one side to quantum on another and vice versa. This duality has various reincarnations: Fourier and Legendre transforms, Langlands duality, etc. The quantization parameters are mapped to geometric parameters. Simple questions on one side solve complicated ones on the other and vice versa.

Why many-body systems? Gauge theories describe dynamics of gauge equivalence classes of connections, the simplest observables to define in the classical gauge-theoretic setting
are the conjugacy classes of the holonomies of the dynamical gauge field along a fixed loop in space time. If this loop is moved from one location to another the conjugacy class of the holonomy changes. For classical gauge groups these conjugacy classes are in one-to-one correspondence with configurations of points on a circle modulo permutations and, possibly, other symmetries, thus the abovementioned change in the conjugacy class
becomes a dynamics of indistinguishable point particles on a circle.

In quantum context the positions of these particles becomes random. Depending on the dimensionality of spacetime, and the degree of supersymmetry of the underlying gauge theory, the mapping of spacetime gauge dynamics to that of a many-body system could be exact or approximate.

In what follows we study the Calogero-Moser-Sutherland class of
many-body systems, associated with
A
type root system
and
SU(N)
gauge theories in various spacetime dimensions, starting in
0+1
going all the way to
5+1
, with up to eight supercharges. The correspondence will
be direct in
0+1,1+1,2+1,3+1
dimensions for non-supersymmetric
matrix quantum mechanics, two dimensional Yang-Mills
theory, modified three dimensional Chern-Simons theory,
or a hybrid 3d version of 2d Yang-Mills theory, and the hybrid
4d Chern-Simons theory. The correspondence will
be of second type for
3+1,4+1,5+1
dimensional
super-Yang-Mills theories with eight supercharges, subject to
the so-called
\Omega
-deformation.

We shall introduce a class of non-local observables,
generalizing the loop operator of the old-fashioned matrix models. Specifically for supersymmetric gauge theories the correlation functions of these observables obey a non-perturbative version of the Dyson-Schwinger equations that we review. From these equations, in the
3+1
dimensional case follow the Schrödinger equation of a many-body system. The correspondence in the
4+1
and
5+1
dimensional cases remains a conjecture.

Calogero–Moser many-body system

2.1
Definition of the system

Like any classical Hamiltonian system, the Calogero-Moser (CM) system (
[
8
]
) is a symplectic manifold
(\mathcal{P},{\omega})
called
the phase space
and a function
H
on
\mathcal{P}
,

Definition 2.1
.

(Rational) Calogero–Moser system is the triple

\bullet

Phase space
\mathcal{P}=\big(T^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Delta)\big)/S_{N}

with standard coordinates
\{(p_{i},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
\Delta=\{x_{i}=x_{j}\text{ for some }i\neq j\}
is the union of diagonals and

is the symmetric group acting on
(x_{i},p_{i})
by permutations.

\bullet

The symplectic structure is defined by a canonical symplectic form

\displaystyle\omega=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}dp_{i}\wedge dx_{i}.

\bullet

The CM Hamiltonian is given by the formula

\displaystyle H=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2}+\nu^{2}\sum\limits_{i<j}\frac{1}{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}},

where
\nu
is the
coupling constant
parameter.

The system describes motion of particles (many bodies) strung on a thread and interacting pairwise with potential
V(r)=\frac{\nu^{2}}{r^{2}}
(repulsion for
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
).

2.2

Some symplectic manifolds have a certain kind of symmetry called

Definition 2.3

Let

\displaystyle dH_{\xi}=-\omega(V_{\xi},\cdot)

2.

For the discussion below it is usefull to formulate the following

Theorem 2.1
.

(Meyer
[

\displaystyle\mu^{-1}(0)^{ss}=\big\{(P,X,z)\in\mu^{-1}(0):X\text{ has different eigenvalues}\big\}.

\bullet

comes from

\bullet

6
Gauge theories and measures on partitions

Supersymmetric gauge theories in four dimensions, and five and six dimensional theories compactified to four dimensions, produce a class of complexified statistical models. The random variables of these models are collections of Young diagrams, also known as colored partitions
[
35
,
37
]
.
More specifically, this is a result of a two-step localization procedure. First of all, it is well-known since the interpretation of M. Atiyah and L. Jeffrey of E. Witten’s topological quantum field theory intepretation
[
55
]
of Donaldson theory
[
11
]
, path integral of topologically twisted
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetric gauge theory, for specific correlation functions, can be interpreted as an integral of equivariant differential forms (albeit in an infinite-dimensional setting).
This interpretation has been generalized to theories with matter hypermultiplets
[
50
,
25
,
32
,
27
]
. Whenever such interpretation holds, path integral localizes to a finite dimensional integral over the moduli space of instatons, i.e. solutions to the
F^{+}_{A}=0
equations modulo suitable gauge group. This localization is an infinite-dimensional version of the equivalence of different representatives of Euler class of a vector bundle: Poincare dual to the zero locus of generic transverse section,
and the curvature Gauss-Bonnet type form. The next step undertaken in
[
35
]
is to deform the theory in such a way that the path integral measure becomes the equivariant differential form with respect to both the local gauge symmetries and the global symmetries consisting of the rotations of
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
(more generally, isometries of the four-manifold on which one studies the theory), the rotations of gauge frame and possibly rotations of the spaces of multiplicities of matter fields. The question of framing arises when one is working on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
or other non-compact manifolds. We need to impose the boundary conditions both on gauge fields and gauge transformations one divides by. As a result, the gauge group
{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}
is a proper normal subgroup in the group
{\mathcal{G}}
of all gauge transformations defined on some compactification of
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, e.g.
S^{4}={\mathbb{R}}^{4}\cup{\infty}
. The quotient
{\mathcal{G}}/{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}
is the group of frame rotations. The equivariant parameters
\bf a
for this group are identified with the vacuum expectation value of the adjoint-valued complex Higgs field, the scalar in the vector multiplet of
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetry:

\langle{\phi}\rangle={\bf a}

(6.1)

The equivariant parameters
({{\varepsilon}}_{1},{{\varepsilon}}_{2})
for the rotation group
Spin(4)
are not present in the conventional
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theory.

The way path integral localizes onto the instanton configurations and then onto the set of special instanton configurations has been described in literature on many occasions, see
[
43
]
for a review.
Using the localization in equivariant cohomology we express the integral as a sum over the corresponding fixed points. The subtlety is that the fixed points do not belong to the actual moduli space of smooth supersymmetric gauge field configurations, such as instantons. Rather, they are parked somewhere on the compactified
moduli space. The points of the compactified moduli space no longer parametrize the solutions to the instanton equations on the smooth four dimensional spacetime. They can be interpreted in algebraic geometry as parametrizing torsion free sheaves on
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
–
a compactification of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, a complex plane, whose underlying four dimensional real space is the original spacetime. Every instanton uniquely corresponds to some locally free, and thereofore, torsion free sheaf. However, there are more general torsion free sheaves. A limit of a sequence of locally free sheaves could be a torsion free sheaf which is not locally free. Let us explain this in a little more detail.

The ordinary
SU(N)
instanton gauge field,

A=A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\,,\ F=dA+A\wedge A=\frac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}\wedge dx^{\nu}

(6.2)

(6.3)

defines a holomorphic rank
N

\mathcal{E}
by means of its space of sections. Namely, for each open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
the space
\Gamma({\mathcal{E}}|_{U})
of local holomorphic sections of
\mathcal{E}

is by definition the space of

which solve the auxiliary linear problem:

\left({\bar{\partial}}_{\bar{i}}+A_{\bar{i}}\right)s=0\,,\ {\bar{i}}={\bar{1}},{\bar{2}},

(6.4)

where we decompose
A
with respect to the complex structure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\approx{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

\displaystyle z^{1}=x^{1}+{\mathrm{i}}x^{2}\,,\ z^{2}=x^{3}+{\mathrm{i}}x^{4}\,,

\displaystyle{{\displaystyle\bar{z}}}^{\bar{1}}=x^{1}-{\mathrm{i}}x^{2}\,,\ {{\bar{z}}}^{\bar{2}}=x^{3}-{\mathrm{i}}x^{4}

as follows

A=\sum_{i=1}^{2}A_{i}dz^{i}+A_{\bar{i}}d{\bar{z}}^{\bar{i}}.

(6.6)

The space of local holomorphic sections

of local holomorphic functions on
U

{\Gamma}({\mathcal{E}}|_{U})\approx{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{\oplus N}.

(6.7)

Indeed, in that case we are able to find a fundamental matrix

of solutions (
6.4

A_{\bar{i}}=-\bar{\partial}_{\bar{i}}\Psi\Psi^{-1}.

(6.8)

The abovementioned torsion free sheaves obey a weaker condition:
if

is such, that

6.1
String theory perspective

Noncommutative gauge theories
[

require string theory for their definition.
In string theory realization of gauge theory, typically, there is an infinite number of degrees of freedom involved, of which only a finite number is relevant for low energy physics.
Specifically, the gauge origami

setup embeds the four dimensional
spacetime

as a subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}\approx{\mathbb{R}}^{8}

As a complex manifold it is the total space of direct sum of four line bundles

7
Order and Disorder

The view on quantum field theory as a limit of statistical mechanics suggests two types of interesting observables. The first type, the so-called order operators, are defined in terms of elementary fields locally, i.e. they are renormalized finite expressions in fields and their derivatives, evaluated at a point of spacetime, on a loop, a surface etc., the main feature being the locality of the associated functional.
The next class of observables, the disorder operators, are defined in a tricky way. Sometimes they can also be expressed through the elementary fields of the theory, but in a non-local way. Typically one defines them as a prescription to perform the path integration over the space of fields, which are well-defined outside a submanifold
N\subset M
of spacetime manifold,
and have a singularity along
N
of prescribed type, or asymptotics. In the following we consider examples of these operators appearing in particular quantum field theories. Then we give a hint on how equivariant localization discussed in (
6
) turns order and disorder observables in four-dimensional supersymmetric gauge theories to specific functions on the set of multipartitions. We don’t give a coherent derivation though but rather introduce the final answer for these functions as a definition. Then we study their expectation values.

7.1

A possible example of a model containing both order and disorder operators
is the two dimensional sigma model valued in

or, more generally, a torus

, where

is Euclidean vector space, and

Given

(the dual lattice is defined as a subset of the dual space

7.5
Wilson loop in non-abelian gauge theory

In non-abelian theory the situation is even more interesting.

First of all, let us unpack the definition of the Wilson loop. We work with the
compact gauge group
G
. Let us take for simplicity
R
to be an irreducible representation.
Recall that
R
can be viewed as the Hilbert space of an auxiliary quantum mechanical model, whose classical limit, i.e. the phase space, is some coadjoint orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{R}=G/H\subset{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
of
G
. The generic coadjoint orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}

is the set of covectors, conjugate to
\xi\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
by the coadjoint group action:

{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}=\{\,{\eta}\,|\,{\eta}=Ad_{g}^{*}{\xi}\ {\rm for\ some\ }g\in G\}

(7.18)

The coadjoint orbits are classified by the equivalence classes
{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}/G={\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/W
.

Example 7.1
.

Consider the case
G=SU(N)
. The coadjoint orbits
are isomorphic to adjoint orbits, the latter are classified by the collection
J=\left({\mathrm{i}\lambda}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{i}\lambda}_{N}\right)
of eigenvalues of the antihermitian
N\times N
traceless matrix
g^{-1}{\rm diag}(J)g
. Since these are purely imaginary numbers, we can order them:

{\lambda}_{1}\geq{\lambda}_{2}\geq\ldots\geq{\lambda}_{N}

(7.19)

The coadjoint orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{J}
is the space
of all antihermitian matrices
X
whose eigenvalues
are given by
J
. Remarkably, this is a complex manifold, isomorphic to the (partial) flag variety

Fl(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r})=GL(N,{\mathbb{C}})/P_{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}}

(7.20)

with

N>v_{1}>v_{2}>\ldots>v_{r}

(7.21)

where

m_{1}=N-v_{1}\,,\ m_{2}=v_{1}-v_{2}\,,\ldots\,,\ m_{r}=v_{r-1}-v_{r},m_{r+1}=v_{r}

(7.22)

are the multiplicities of the eigenvalues, ordered
as in (
7.19
)

{\lambda}_{1}=\ldots={\lambda}_{m_{1}}=l_{1}>{\lambda}_{m_{1}+1}=\ldots={\lambda}_{m_{1}+m_{2}}=l_{2}>\ldots>{\lambda}_{N-v_{r}+1}=\ldots={\lambda}_{N}=l_{r+1}

(7.23)

The points of (
7.20
) are the flags

V_{r}\subset V_{r-1}\subset\ldots\subset V_{1}\subset W

of subspaces of the fixed complex vector space
W={\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, with
{\rm dim}V_{i}=v_{i}
. Endowing
W
with hermitian structure, we identify

Fl(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r})=U(N)/\left(U(m_{1})\times\ldots\times U(m_{r+1})\right)

(7.24)

as follows:
given an operator
{\mathrm{i}}X\in End(W)
with hermitian
X=X^{\dagger}
, consider the function

h_{X}({\psi})={\psi}^{\dagger}X{\psi}

on
W\ni\psi
. It is invariant under the
U(1)
-action
{\psi}\mapsto e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}{\psi}
, this, it descends, by restricting it first to
S^{2N-1}=\{{\psi}\,|\,{\psi}^{\dagger}{\psi}=1\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, to a function
h_{X}
on
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{N-1}=S^{2N-1}/U(1)
. The critical points of the latter are
the normalized eigenvectors of
X
. The minima correspond to the lowest eigenvalues, the maxima correspond to the maximal eigenvalues. Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r+1}
be the connected components of the sets of
h_{X}
critical points, with

h_{X}(C_{i})=l_{i}\,,\ i=1,\ldots,r+1\ .

(7.25)

Let
N_{1},\ldots,N_{r+1}
be the descending manifolds for the gradient flow of
h_{X}
emanating from
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r+1}
. Their closures are compact submanifolds obeying
{\bar{N}}_{r+1}\subset{\bar{N}}_{r}\subset\ldots\subset{\bar{N}}_{1}
, (obviously
{\bar{N}}_{1}=\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
,
{\bar{N}}_{r+1}=N_{r+1}=C_{r+1}
) the projectivizations of
V_{r}\subset V_{r-1}\subset\ldots\subset V_{1}\subset W
.

The coadjoint orbits
{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
are the symplectic leaves of Kirillov-Konstant Poisson structure on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
:

\{f,g\}|_{\eta}=\left({\eta},\left[df,dg\right]\right)

(7.26)

The corresponding symplectic form
{\omega}_{\xi}

represents a nontrivial class in
H^{2}({\mathcal{O}}_{\xi},{\mathbb{R}})
.
For
G=SU(N)
the periods of
\omega_{\xi}
are linear combinations of
l_{i}-l_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
.

The coadjoint action of
G
on
{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}

preserves
{\omega}_{\xi}
and has a moment map. In fact, the moment map generating the coadjoint action is the embedding
{\mu}:{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
.

When the symplectic form is integral

[{\omega}_{\xi}]\in H^{2}({\mathcal{O}}_{\xi},{\mathbb{Z}})
, the associated orbit is called
quantizable
.
There is a holomorphic line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
, such that
c_{1}({\mathcal{L}})=[{\omega}_{\xi}]
. There is a
U(1)
-connection

\nabla
on
\mathcal{L}
, whose curvature is determined by the symplectic structure

F_{\nabla}=2\pi\mathrm{i}{\omega}_{\xi}
. Geometric quantization associates to
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
the Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}=H^{0}({\mathcal{O}}_{\xi},{\mathcal{L}})

(7.27)

The group
G_{\mathbb{C}}
acts on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
by geometrically moving the holomorphic sections. The compact group
G
preserves the inner product. The main result of geometric quantization program is that all the irreducible representations of
G

are obtained in this way,
R={\mathcal{H}}_{\xi}
, for some
{\xi}={\xi}_{R}
.

Let
A=A(t)dt
be some
\mathfrak{g}
-valued

-form
on
S^{1}
. Consider the monodromy operator
g\in G

g=P{\exp}\oint A.

(7.28)

The Wilson loop observable is given by the character of

\displaystyle W_{R}=\chi_{R}(g)={\text{Tr}}_{R}(g).

What is important for us
is the path integral representation of it:

{\chi}_{R}(g)=\int_{{\eta}:S^{1}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}}[D{\eta}(t)]\,e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\oint_{S^{1}}\,d^{-1}{\omega}_{\xi}+\mathrm{i}\oint_{S^{1}}({\eta},A)}.

One way to understand (

[
13

6

4

L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
of parametrized loops
{\eta}:S^{1}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}

{\Omega}_{L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}}({\delta}_{1}{\eta},{\delta}_{2}{\eta})=\int_{S^{1}}\,dt\,{\omega}_{\xi}\left({\delta}_{1}{\eta}(t),{\delta}_{2}{\eta}(t)\right).

(7.30)

It is invariant under the action of the loop group

by point-wise coadjoint action. It is also invariant under the

,

\Omega_{L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}}

is invariant under the action of the semi-direct

{\widetilde{LG}}=U(1)\mathbin{\hbox{\thinspace\vrule height=4.1pt,depth=-0.3pt,width=0.25pt\hskip-2.0pt$\times$}}LG

{\widetilde{LG}}
action is not actually globally defined, as the action is not Hamiltonian. It is locally Hamiltonian, though: the one-form on

{\alpha}_{\xi}=\iota_{{\dot{\eta}}}{\Omega}_{L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}}=\int_{S^{1}}dt\,{\omega}_{\xi}(\dot{\eta}(t),{\cdot})=(p_{2})_{*}{\rm ev}^{*}{\omega}_{\xi}

is closed. It is the transgression of the closed two-form
{\omega}_{\xi}
with respect to the evaluation map

{\rm ev}:S^{1}\times L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}\rightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}.

(7.32)

Fix a point
p\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
. We can define the function

{\tilde{L}}\left({\ell},[{\sf path}]_{p}^{\ell}\right)=\int_{\sf path}{\alpha}_{\xi}

(7.33)

where
{\sf path}
is a path on
L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
connecting a given loop
{\ell}
to the constant loop
{\eta}(t)=p
(note that the fundamental group of a partial flag variety is trivial for compact connected simply connected

). Since the value of the integral does not change under any homotopy preserving the endpoints

and

, it only depends on the homotopy class
[{\sf path}]_{p}^{\ell}
, also known as the point
on the universal cover of

, covering

.

The beauty of quantizable
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}

are integers. Therefore, the function

(7.34)

is well-defined on
L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
. It is this factor that we denote by

(7.35)

in (

L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}
, evaluated on the element

{\phi}={2\pi\mathrm{i}}{\partial}_{t}+A(t)\in Lie({\widetilde{LG}})={\mathbb{R}}{\partial}_{t}\oplus L{\mathfrak{g}}

We are therefore in the realm of the DH formula in the formulation of

4
]

be the torus of

generated by

(7.37)

with constant

(with generic

{L{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}}

(7.38)

Taking

(7.40)

Supposing that

Thus, the fixed points on

are the constant loops, taking values in the intersection points of the orbit

and a vector space

\displaystyle({\mathfrak{g}}/\bigoplus\limits_{\alpha}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha})^{*}\cong{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}.

Assuming (without loss of generality) that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{\xi}\cap{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}=\{w\xi:w\in W\}.

Evaluating

Summarizing, the Wilson loop observable
