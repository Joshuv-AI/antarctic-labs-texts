Combination of PARPi and topoisomerase I inhibitors is highly effective in olaparib-resistant ovarian cancer
Authors: M. Chiappa, V. Villa, F. Sias, C. Grasselli, L. Sala, S. Canesi, M. Marabese, S. Mason, D. Dibitetto, R. Fruscio, G. Damia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) often shows an initial response to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) such as olaparib, particularly in tumors with defects in homologous recombination (HR), but resistance to these drugs is a major clinical challenge. To address this, we screened over 1400 FDA-approved compounds to find agents that could restore olaparib sensitivity in resistant ovarian cancer cells. DNA topoisomerase I inhibitors (TOP1i) emerged as the most effective partner. The combination of TOP1 inhibitors with PARPi showed strong synergistic anticancer effects across multiple murine and human HGSOC models, regardless of HR status, including patient-derived xenografts made resistant to olaparib. The combination was well tolerated and primarily worked by inducing apoptosis and increased DNA damage. These findings suggest that TOP1 inhibitors combined with PARP inhibitors could be a promising treatment strategy for olaparib-resistant ovarian cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40164-026-00828-7.

To the Editor

The approval of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis) in both front-line and recurrent settings of high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) have revolutionized the treatment of BRCAmut ovarian cancer; however resistance to these drugs represents an emerging clinical problem [1]. The availability of in vitro and in vivo PARPi resistant models is extremely valuable as it allows the identification of therapeutic strategies to overcame resistance.

We recently obtained murine homologous recombination repair (HR)-deficient (HRD) and -proficient (HRP) ovarian cancer cell line resistant to PARPi through multiple in vitro olaparib (Ola) treatments (ID8 Brca1-/- OlaR and F3 OlaR, respectively) whose resistance rely on different mechanisms [2]. Using ID8 Brca1-/- OlaR cells, we performed a high-throughput screening with an FDA-approved drug library in combination with Ola and identified 50 compounds active in combination and inactive as single agents (Fig. 1A and S1A, Table S1). We selected for further validation irinotecan, rolipram, VX-680, BIRB-796 and nimesulide. An additive effect in Brca1-/- OlaR cells was observed with all the drugs, except for the topoisomerase I inhibitor (TOP1i) irinotecan, that resulted synergic in both resistant and sensitive Brca1-/- cells (Figure S1B). We tested the combination of another TOP1i, camptothecin (CPT) with Ola and saruparib (Saru) (a selective PARP1i) in both HRP and HRD cells and their corresponding OlaR sublines (i.e. Brca1-/- OlaR, F3 OlaR, PEO1 OlaR), and found a strong synergism (Figs. 1B and S1C). We then tested a panel of human OC cell lines with different HR status and sensitivities to Ola (Table S2); again, in all the cell lines tested the combination of CPT/Ola and CPT/Saru displayed a variable degree of synergy, except in Ovcar 432 cells in which an additive effect was mainly observed when both PARPi were considered (Figs. 1C and S1D). We do not have a clear explanation as to why no synergism was observed in this cell line, especially given that its sensitivity to both PARPi and TOP1i is comparable to that of other cell lines (Table S2). This could be related to the different genetic background, or possibly to the level of proteins reported to be important for both PARPi and TOP1 (e.g. SLFN11) [3]. Despite PARPi/TOP1i combination has already been reported to be active in preclinical models of cancer [4–6], to the best of our knowledge no data have been published in models of PARPi-acquired resistance.

To investigate the mechanisms underlying this synergistic effect, we treated Brca1-/- OlaR cells with Ola/Saru, CPT or their combination at doses slightly active as single agent treatment, but very effective when combined (Figure S2A) and evaluated the cell cycle perturbation. CPT single treatment did not perturb cell cycle distribution, while PARPi induced a G2/M block (Fig. 1D, Table S3). Interestingly, combo-treated cells displayed a more pronounced G2/M arrest and accumulation of a sub-G1 population from 24 h onward. These effects increased at 48 and 72 h, with the progressive expansion of the sub-G1 fraction indicating apoptotic cell death. This latter finding was confirmed both by the evaluation of the Annexin V staining (Fig. 1E) and by caspase activity (Figure S2B). The % of cells in early and late apoptosis was greater in combo treated cells (Ola/CPT and Saru/CPT) as compared untreated and single treatments already at 24 h and increased up to 72 h. Caspase activation was evident after PARPi/CPT combo treatment at 48 and 72 h. In Brca1-/- cells, PARPi, both as single treatment or in combination with CPT, induced a slight G2/M cell-cycle arrest, not reaching statistical significance. Similarly to resistant cells, sub-G1 population formation, annexin and caspase induction after combination treatment were observed also in sensitive cells (Figs. 1D, E and S2B, Table S4).

A greater γH2AX induction (read out of both DNA damage and apoptotic DNA fragmentation [7]) in combo-treated Brca1-/- OlaR and Brca1-/- cells compared to control and single agents was evident at all time points, with the most pronounced effects occurring at 24 and 48 h. (Figs. 1F and S2C). We explore the induction of replicative stress by evaluating the phosphorylation of RPA32 (pSer33-RPA32); however no clear induction of pSer33-RPA32 over untreated cells was observed except at 72 h in both single-agent treatments and in the combination groups, probably due to the high phosphorylation levels already present at basal conditions, that suggest a higher replicative stress in Ola resistant cells, as already reported [8]. In Brca1-/- cells in the Ola/CPT setting, a modest increase pSer33-RPA was detectable already 24 h after combination treatment and became more pronounced at 48 and 72 h compared with either untreated cells and single agent, although the differences did not reach statistical significance. A similar trend was observed with the Saru/CPT combination, in which pSer33-RPA levels were markedly increased at both 24 and 48 h, whereas induction occurred with slower kinetics in cells treated with either drug alone. By 72 h, pSer33-RPA levels were comparable across all treatment groups. These findings suggest that combination treatment elicits an earlier and more robust replication stress response, which may contribute to the early onset of cell death observed from 24 h after treatment (Figure S2C).

The PARPi/TOP1i combination was tested in two HRD HGSOC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) made resistant to Ola through multiple in vivo treatment cycles (#218OlaR and #230OlaR) [9]. For in vivo studies we selected topotecan, as it is approved as second-line therapy in ovarian carcinoma [10]. Although clinical data suggest that the combination of Ola and topotecan results in dose-limiting myelo- and gastrotoxicities [11], it was well tolerated in our experimental setting based on animal body weight and clinical macroscopic observations (Figure S3A, C). In PDX #218OlaR (Brca1mut), Ola treatment was ineffective, while topotecan induced tumor growth inhibition, never exceeding 50%, without leading to any survival advantage; the combination was extremely active, as tumor regressions were observed in all mice (Figs. 2A-C, F and S3B). Regressions persisted after the end of treatment with two mice sacrificed after seven months without tumor (Fig. 2C, F). Similar results with topotecan/Saru combination in PDX #230OlaR (Brca1wt, HRD). Saru treatment was almost inactive, while topotecan was moderately active with 2 tumor regressions, one stable disease and three progressive diseases (Figs. 2D-F and S3D). Again, the combination was very active as all tumors regressed and only one tumor regrew two months after the end of the treatment (Figure S3D). A trend toward increased apoptosis was observed in combination-treated mice compared to control and single-agent–treated tumors, as indicated by caspase activation; however, this did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2G). Similarly, no clear induction of γH2AX was detected following either single-agent or combination treatment, likely due to the already high basal levels observed (Figs. 2H and S3E). Increased levels of pSer33-RPA32 were observed following single-agent treatment, with a further enhancement in combination-treated tumors (Fig. 2H and S3E), again not reaching a statistical significance. This may be attributable to both the biological variability of the samples (three individual tumors), and the use of a single sampling time point (24 h after the last olaparib dose), which may not correspond to the optimal window for detecting activation of the DNA damage response following in vivo treatment. This may also account for the less pronounced modulation of γH2AX and pSer33-RPA32 observed in vivo than in the in vitro setting, where continuous drug exposure likely led to more sustained activation of these markers. The reported molecular basis of the activity of the PARPi/TOP1i combination relies on the fact that PARPi inhibits pathways involved in the removal of trapped TOP1 from DNA. These pathways include base excision repair, HR and mismatch repair [12]. Furthermore, PARP1 is essential for activating tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1), which is a key enzyme in the repair of TOP1 cleavage complexes and plays a crucial role in recruiting TDP1 to sites of DNA damage [13]. HR restoration is involved in the resistance to PARPi in Brca1-/-OlaR cells and #218OlaR, but not in #230OlaR PDX, where ola resistance correlated with an increased stability of the replication fork [2, 14]. These findings suggest that the combination may be effective in multiple settings of acquired olaparib resistance, including mechanisms other than HR restoration.

In conclusion, our in vitro data demonstrate that TOP1i synergistically enhance the antitumor activity of PARPi in OC, regardless of HR status. Moreover, the combination was highly effective in two in vivo OC models of acquired resistance to Ola, supporting its potential application in HRD tumors that have developed resistance to PARPi. Patients with HRD tumors who relapse during or after PARPi treatment due to heterogeneous resistance mechanisms—such as HR restoration, reduced PARP1 expression, or replication fork stabilization—may represent the population most likely to benefit from this therapeutic strategy. Although the clinical development of the Ola/topotecan combination is hindered by myelosuppression and gastrointestinal toxicities [11], Saru, a more potent and specific PARPi, has been associated with fewer haematological side effects [15], supporting its use in combination with TOP1i in clinical setting.
