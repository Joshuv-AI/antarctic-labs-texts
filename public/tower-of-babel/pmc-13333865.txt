Highly selective and recyclable vanadium‑promoted Mg–Sr–Al oxide catalyst for n‑butanol ethoxylation
Authors: Sara Heidari Goudarzi, Vahid Javanbakht, Sayyed Ali Monajemi, Maryam Mehrabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
2-Butoxyethanol is an important glycol ether widely used as a solvent, wetting agent, emulsifier, and chemical intermediate, and is commonly produced via the ethoxylation of n-butanol with ethylene oxide. The selection of an appropriate catalyst plays a crucial role in controlling product distribution and facilitating downstream purification. In this study, mixed metal oxide catalysts were evaluated for the selective ethoxylation of n-butanol, and a vanadium-promoted Mg–Sr–Al mixed metal oxide (AMSV) was developed as a highly efficient and recyclable heterogeneous catalyst. Under mild reaction conditions (125 °C and 0.3 MPa), the AMSV catalyst achieved 71.27% n-butanol conversion with 67.93% selectivity toward 2-butoxyethanol, significantly outperforming the unmodified Mg–Sr–Al oxide (AMS; 54.26% conversion and 62.67% selectivity) as well as the conventional homogeneous KOH catalyst. The catalysts were synthesized via a controlled co-precipitation method, calcined at 550 °C, and comprehensively characterized using FESEM, XRD, FTIR, CO2−TPD, and BET analyses. Quantitative product analysis was performed by GC-FID (Agilent 7890 A, HP-INNOWAX column, split ratio 50:1, n-hexanol internal standard), yielding calibration coefficients with R2 values exceeding 0.999. The superior catalytic performance of AMSV is attributed to its enhanced textural and surface properties, including a 7.2-fold increase in BET surface area (92.88 m2 g−1), a higher density of moderate-strength basic sites (≈ 900 µmol g−1, desorption at 400–600 °C), and the presence of Lewis basic V5+=O surface species, as evidenced by an FTIR band at approximately 995 cm−1. Reusability tests demonstrated that the AMSV catalyst retained its initial activity and selectivity over consecutive reaction cycles, confirming its excellent structural stability and recyclability. These findings highlight the potential of vanadium-promoted mixed metal oxides as sustainable heterogeneous catalysts for narrow-range alcohol ethoxylation.

Introduction

Glycol ethers, particularly 2-butoxyethanol, constitute an important class of solvent–surfactant intermediates widely applied in coatings, inks, detergents, plasticizers, and chemical processing industries owing to their amphiphilic molecular structure and tunable physicochemical properties1–3. Industrially, 2-butoxyethanol is synthesized via the ethoxylation of n-butanol with ethylene oxide (EO), a reaction that is strongly influenced by catalyst type, surface acid–base properties, and EO insertion kinetics4,5. Conventional alkoxylation processes predominantly rely on homogeneous alkaline catalysts, such as KOH, which exhibit high activity but suffer from several inherent drawbacks, including broad ethoxylate chain-length distributions, formation of higher glycol ethers, corrosion issues, and the requirement for multistep neutralization and purification procedures6,7 (Fig. 1).

These limitations have driven increasing interest in heterogeneous catalysts capable of delivering narrower product distributions, improved selectivity, and enhanced recyclability while satisfying modern sustainability and process-safety requirements. Recent studies have demonstrated that mixed metal oxides (MMOs) offer highly tunable surface basicity, good structural stability, and well-defined active-site environments, making them attractive candidates for alkoxylation reactions8–10. Compared with single-component oxides, MMOs can exhibit synergistic interactions between different cations (e.g., Mg2+–Al3+–Sr2+), leading to stronger Lewis basic sites, enhanced oxygen mobility, and more efficient EO activation pathways11,12. These features are particularly beneficial for achieving higher mono-ethoxylation selectivity and suppressing the formation of poly-ethoxylated by-products, which is essential for narrow-range glycol ether production. The incorporation of vanadium species, such as metavanadate, into mixed metal oxide catalysts derived from hydrotalcites has been widely explored to enhance catalytic activity and selectivity in alkoxylation and partial oxidation reactions. Metavanadate introduction provides redox-active centers that facilitate ethylene oxide activation via mechanisms like Mars-van-Krevelen, while stabilizing reduced vanadium species (V4+/V3+) to improve conversion and suppress poly-ethoxylate formation13. For example, decavanadate-intercalated Mg-Al hydrotalcites yield mixed oxides with superior selectivity toward mono-glycol ethers in n-butanol ethoxylation, attributed to tuned surface basicity and vanadium dispersion. Similarly, supported vanadium oxides exhibit excellent performance in environmental oxidation processes due to their dynamic electronic structures and ability to control redox cycles14. In V-containing CuMgAl-LDHs, vanadate intercalation enhances structural stability and catalytic efficiency for hydroxylation reactions15, while non-stoichiometric anion exchange with vanadates in Mg-Al hydrotalcites optimizes vanadium loading for oxidative dehydrogenation, leading to tailored pore distribution and basicity16. These properties make vanadium-promoted catalysts ideal for achieving narrow-range ethoxylation with high recyclability and selectivity. The rational design of mixed metal oxide catalysts aims to combine complementary physicochemical properties that are not attainable in single oxides. Alkaline earth oxides such as MgO and SrO provide strong surface basic sites, whereas Al2O3 contributes thermal stability, mechanical strength, and suitable surface acidity, facilitating improved dispersion of active phases. The synergistic interaction among these components enhances surface basicity, optimizes the acid–base balance, and improves resistance to sintering under ethoxylation conditions. Furthermore, incorporation of vanadium species can introduce redox-active centers and modify the surface electronic structure, thereby promoting selective EO activation17. Recent investigations have shown that vanadium-doped/promoted mixed oxides exhibit enhanced dispersion, increased defect density, and improved catalytic performance in oxidation and basic catalysis reactions18,19.

Despite these advances, relatively few studies have focused on the ethoxylation of n-butanol over multi-component Mg–Sr–Al mixed metal oxides, and even fewer have systematically examined the effect of vanadium incorporation on catalytic performance, structure–activity relationships, and mono-ethoxylate selectivity. Addressing these gaps is essential for the development of next-generation heterogeneous catalysts capable of overcoming the limitations of conventional homogeneous systems. Accordingly, the present work aims to synthesize Mg–Sr–Al mixed metal oxide catalysts (AMS) and their vanadium-promoted counterparts (AMSV) via a controlled co-precipitation route, to comprehensively characterize their structural, morphological, textural, and acid–base properties, and to evaluate their catalytic performance in the ethoxylation of n-butanol with ethylene oxide under identical reaction conditions. Particular emphasis is placed on correlating catalyst physicochemical properties—especially the nature and distribution of basic sites and the role of vanadium species—with catalytic activity, EO conversion, and selectivity toward 2-butoxyethanol. Through this approach, the study provides insight into the rational design of vanadium-promoted mixed metal oxide catalysts for selective alcohol ethoxylation processes.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals used in the synthesis and catalytic experiments were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. n-Butanol and ethylene oxide were supplied by Shazand Petrochemical Company (Arak, Iran). Potassium carbonate (K2CO3), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), nitric acid (HNO3), strontium nitrate [Sr(NO3)2], magnesium nitrate hexahydrate [Mg(NO3)2 6 H2O], aluminum nitrate nonahydrate [Al(NO3)3 9 H2O], and sodium metavanadate (NaVO3) were purchased from Merck.

Synthesis of catalyst

The catalysts were synthesized via a co-precipitation method at constant pH. An aqueous solution containing Mg(NO3)2 6 H2O, Sr(NO3)2, and Al(NO3)3 9 H2O (total metal concentration of 1 M, according to Table 1) was added dropwise (1.5 mL min−1) into a well-stirred K2CO3/NaOH solution at 50 °C, while maintaining the pH at 9.5 ± 0.1 through automated addition of 3 M NaOH. For the vanadium-promoted catalyst (AMSV), NaVO3 was introduced to achieve a vanadium loading of 5 wt%. The resulting suspension was stirred at 500 rpm for 2 h under a continuous nitrogen flow (50 mL min−1) at room temperature. The obtained slurry was aged under a nitrogen atmosphere at 40 °C for 24 h, filtered, neutralized with 0.5 M HNO3, and thoroughly washed with deionized water. The solid was dried overnight at 60 °C and subsequently calcined at 550 °C for 6 h in air using a heating rate of 1 °C min−120.

Production of 2-butoxyethanol

Ethoxylation reactions were conducted in a 500 mL stainless-steel jacketed batch reactor equipped with magnetic stirring (800 rpm) and a pressure relief valve. n-Butanol (269.80 g), catalyst (0.20 wt% relative to n-butanol), and ethylene oxide (80.08 g) were charged into the reactor, corresponding to an EO/n-butanol molar ratio of 0.5:1. The reactor was purged with nitrogen, heated to 125 ± 2 °C, and pressurized with ethylene oxide to 0.3 MPa.

The reaction time varied depending on the catalytic activity of the catalyst. Due to its high flammability and toxicity, ethylene oxide (boiling point: 10.4 °C) was handled under an inert atmosphere in a fume hood, and all experiments were performed in accordance with standard industrial safety protocols. After completion of the reaction, heterogeneous catalysts were recovered by vacuum filtration, washed with absolute ethanol (3 × 20 mL), and dried at 80 °C prior to reuse.

Reaction products were analyzed using a GC-FID system (Agilent 7890 A) equipped with an HP-INNOWAX capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Samples (1 µL) were injected in split mode (50:1) using n-hexanol as an internal standard. The oven temperature program was set from 50 °C (2 min) to 250 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, followed by a 5 min hold. Calibration curves exhibited correlation coefficients (R2) greater than 0.9992121,22.

GC results

The gas chromatography results of all ethoxylation experiments are summarized in Table 2. The catalytic performance was evaluated in terms of n-butanol conversion and selectivity toward 2-butoxyethanol. Among the investigated catalysts, the vanadium-promoted mixed metal oxide (AMSV) exhibited the highest catalytic activity, achieving an n-butanol conversion of 71.27% with a selectivity of 67.93% toward 2-butoxyethanol.

In comparison, the unmodified Mg–Sr–Al mixed oxide (AMS) resulted in a lower conversion of 54.26% and a selectivity of 62.67%, while the conventional homogeneous KOH catalyst showed a conversion of 46.89% with a selectivity of 41.06%. The higher proportion of mono-ethoxylated product obtained over AMSV indicates a more controlled ethoxylation behavior relative to both AMS and KOH.

The product distribution data reveal that the use of heterogeneous catalysts significantly suppresses the formation of higher ethoxylated by-products, such as butyl diethylene glycol (BDG) and butyl triethylene glycol (BTG), compared to the homogeneous system. In particular, AMSV favors the formation of 2-butoxyethanol while limiting consecutive ethylene oxide insertion, leading to a narrower ethoxylate distribution.

These results demonstrate that vanadium incorporation into the Mg–Sr–Al mixed oxide framework markedly enhances catalytic performance in terms of both conversion and selectivity, highlighting the effectiveness of AMSV as a heterogeneous catalyst for narrow-range ethoxylation of n-butanol.

Characterization

The as-synthesized catalysts were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (FESEM instrument/ TESCAN MIRA III) for morphology investigation, X-ray Diffraction (XRD) using PHILIPS model PW1730 at 40 kV and 35 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1540 nm) for structural study, FT-IR spectrometer by Thermo model AVATAR for functional group illustration. Surface basic properties were determined by temperature-programmed desorption TPD of probe molecules (CO2) and the evolution of desorbed molecules was followed by NanoSord, NS91 and BET surface areas were measured at liquid-nitrogen temperature using a BEL, BELSORP MINI II instrument. Textural properties of the catalyst were studied by means of low-temperature N2 adsorption. Ethoxylated products were separated from the solid catalyst by vacuum filtration and analyzed using an Agilent 7890 A GC-FID with an HP-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm). Nitrogen (1.5 mL/min) served as the carrier gas. Samples (1 µL) were injected in split mode (50:1) at 250 °C. The oven was programmed from 100 °C (1 min) to 160 °C at 15 °C/min, and the FID temperature was set to 200 °C. Quantification was based on a five-point external calibration (R2 > 0.999), and homolog identification was confirmed by co-injection and GC-MS. For quantitative evaluation, calibration was performed using a mixed standard solution of n-butanol ethoxylates with well-defined concentrations, enabling the determination of ethylene oxide (EO) unit distribution with high precision.

Results and discussion

To further elucidate the catalytic performance, the effects of reaction temperature (125 ± 2 °C) and reaction time (1.5–2.5 h) were systematically investigated using AMS and AMSV catalysts. The results indicate that increasing temperature enhances EO conversion due to improved EO activation, while excessive temperatures promote secondary ethoxylation. Similarly, prolonged reaction time increases conversion but slightly reduces selectivity toward 2-butoxyethanol. Optimal performance was achieved at 120–130 °C and 2 h reaction time, balancing conversion and selectivity.

FESEM

The FESEM images (Fig. 3a and b) depict the surface morphologies of the AMS and AMSV heterogeneous mixed metal oxide catalysts at a magnification of 40,000×, with a working distance of approximately 5.23 mm and a view field of 5.19 μm. Both images reveal a porous, agglomerated microstructure consisting of irregularly shaped nanoparticles (50–200 nm) forming sponge-like clusters26. The AMS sample (a) exhibits looser aggregation with distinct particle boundaries and larger inter-particle voids, whereas the AMSV sample (b) shows denser clusters with reduced voids, attributed to vanadium incorporation27. This densification likely enhances structural stability and catalytic durability, although it may slightly decrease initial porosity. The similarities in nanoparticle size suggest that vanadium refines rather than radically alters the morphology, potentially improving conductivity and ion diffusion.

CO2-TPD

CO2-TPD profiles (Fig. 4; Table 4) revealed three desorption regions: weak (100–400 °C), moderate (400–600 °C), and strong (> 600 °C) basic sites. AMS exhibited the highest total basicity (2797.1 µmol g−1), with a significant contribution from strong sites (peak at ~ 780 °C, ~ 800 µmol g−1), likely due to isolated O2− anions. AMSV showed slightly lower total basicity (2631.5 µmol g−1) but a higher proportion of moderate-strength sites (400–600 °C, ~ 900 µmol g−1 vs. ~800 µmol g−1 in AMS). The high-temperature peak in AMSV shifted to 750 °C and decreased in intensity, suggesting that vanadium partially neutralizes strong basic sites while generating new Lewis basic V5+=O sites.

Moderate basic sites are optimal for ethoxylation, as very strong sites promote over-ethoxylation to polyglycols28. This explains the higher selectivity of AMSV (67.93%) despite its lower total basicity.

BET

The textural properties of AMS (Mg–Sr–Al) and AMSV (Mg–Sr–Al–V) catalysts, evaluated using BET (Fig. 5) and BJH (Fig. 6) analyses, reveal distinct differences. AMS exhibits a surface area of 12.89 m2 g−1, an average pore size of 27.463 nm, a pore volume of 2.9616 cm3 g−1, and a total pore volume of 0.0885 cm3 g−1, indicating limited mesoporosity. In contrast, AMSV shows a higher surface area of 92.88 m2 g−1, a smaller average pore size of 20.346 nm, a pore volume of 21.339 cm3 g−1, and a total pore volume of 0.4724 cm3 g−1, suggesting enhanced mesoporosity due to vanadium doping29. The BJH plots confirm this, with AMS displaying a narrow peak at ~ 3–5 nm and AMSV showing a broader distribution peaking at ~ 2 nm, reflecting increased pore diversity and connectivity in AMSV30. The higher pore volume observed for AMSV may be associated with structural defects or the formation of additional mesoporosity (Table 5) induced by vanadium incorporation, consistent with vanadium’s role in refining pore structure via oxygen vacancies17. This enhancement likely improves catalytic performance, although the smaller pore size in AMSV may affect diffusion31.

FTIR

FTIR diagrams of the synthesized catalysts of AMS and AMSV are illustrated in the Fig. 7. As shown in this figure, the broad band at 3440–3460 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching of surface hydroxyl groups and physisorbed/structural water involved in hydrogen bonding32. AMS exhibits peaks at 2921 cm−1 (C–H stretching), 1637 cm−1 (H–O–H bending), and 1060 and 705 cm−1 (M–O vibrations in Mg–Al lattices)33. AMSV displays additional peaks at 1720 cm−1 (C = O stretching), 1475–1384 cm−1 (CO32− stretching, suggesting enhanced CO2 adsorption), 901 cm−1 (V = O stretching), and 479 cm−1 (V–Mg–Al bonds)34. The increased carbonate bands in AMSV indicate that vanadium doping enhances basicity and CO2 interaction, likely via oxygen vacancies35. A sharp absorption at ~ 1500 cm−1 is assigned to the asymmetric stretching (ν3) mode of residual carbonate species remaining from the co-precipitation step32. The 901 cm−1 V = O band confirms vanadium incorporation36. Weak C–H stretching bands (2850–2950 cm−1) originate from residual organic impurities and are not related to the catalyst surface.

Surface area-normalized activity and the dominant role of basicity modification

The substantial difference in BET surface area between AMS (12.89 m2 g−1) and AMSV (92.88 m2 g−1) prompted a normalized comparison of catalytic activity to isolate the influence of textural properties from intrinsic site efficiency. Surface area-normalized turnover frequencies (TOF per m2), calculated based on n-butanol conversion and assuming active sites proportional to total basic site density from CO2-TPD, are presented in Table 6. While AMSV exhibited a lower normalized TOF (0.26 h−1 m−2) compared to AMS (1.43 h−1 m−2), its overall higher absolute conversion and improved selectivity demonstrate that vanadium modification primarily enhances activity through increased site accessibility and, more critically, through optimization of the basicity profile (shift toward moderate-strength sites) and introduction of Lewis basic V5+=O centers. These factors dominate over mere surface area effects in promoting selective mono-ethoxylation and suppressing over-ethoxylation to polyglycols17,28. The surface area-normalized TOF values were calculated based on the conversion achieved after 2 h of reaction at 125 °C, assuming that the number of active sites is proportional to the total basic site density determined by CO2-TPD.

Reaction mechanism

The enhanced catalytic performance of AMSV can be rationalized by a cooperative bifunctional surface mechanism arising from the synergistic interaction between alkaline-earth basic sites (MgO–SrO) and vanadium-derived Lewis acidic/redox centers. As evidenced by CO2-TPD analysis, AMSV exhibited a higher proportion of moderate-strength basic sites (400–600 °C) compared to AMS, which are mainly associated with Mg2+–O2− and Sr2+–O2− surface pairs. These sites are sufficiently basic to promote alcohol deprotonation while avoiding excessive stabilization of alkoxide intermediates. In the initial step, n-butanol adsorbs on surface O2− basic sites and undergoes deprotonation to form surface-bound butoxide species. Concurrently, ethylene oxide is activated through coordination to electron-deficient V5+=O species, which polarize the epoxide C–O bond and lower the activation barrier for ring opening. This dual-site activation facilitates a controlled nucleophilic attack of the butoxide on the activated EO, favoring selective mono-ethoxylation.

Importantly, vanadium incorporation shortens the lifetime of alkoxide intermediates by promoting faster proton transfer and product desorption, thereby suppressing consecutive EO insertions and limiting the formation of higher ethoxylates. In contrast, the unmodified AMS catalyst, characterized by a higher fraction of strong basic sites and lower surface area, stabilizes alkoxide species for longer durations, which favors anionic polymerization and broader ethoxylate distributions.

Consequently, AMSV exhibited higher n-butanol conversion (71.27%) and improved selectivity toward 2-butoxyethanol (67.93%), in agreement with GC-FID results. These findings demonstrate that catalytic performance is governed not only by surface area but also by the nature and distribution of active sites, confirming the dominant role of vanadium-induced basicity modulation in directing the ethoxylation pathway37,38.

The reusability of AMS and AMSV was evaluated under identical reaction conditions (120–130 °C, 0.3 MPa, 2 h, EO/n-butanol = 0.5:1). Discussion

The preservation of catalytic activity and surface area after reuse demonstrates that vanadium incorporation enhances catalyst stability. The synergistic interaction between Mg–Sr mixed oxides and vanadium species likely suppresses sintering and maintains active basic sites during reaction.

AMS exhibited a decline in performance in the second cycle, with conversion dropping from 54.26% to 52.18% and selectivity from 62.67% to 60.60%, indicating pore blockage by polymeric residues and particle agglomeration, expected given its low initial surface area and absence of structural stabilizers.

In contrast, AMSV maintained stability, with only marginal drops in conversion (71.27% → 68.91%) and selectivity (67.93% → 66.94%). The presence of surface vanadium oxide species likely prevents particle sintering and facilitates desorption of heavy adducts, preserving accessible basic sites. These results highlight the role of vanadium modification in enhancing both activity and recyclability, making AMSV a suitable heterogeneous catalyst for industrial ethoxylation processes.

Conclusions

The vanadium-promoted Mg–Sr–Al mixed metal oxide (AMSV) demonstrated superior catalytic performance in the narrow-range ethoxylation of n-butanol with ethylene oxide under mild conditions (120–130 °C, 0.3 MPa), achieving 71.27% n-butanol conversion and 67.93% selectivity to 2-butoxyethanol. This outperformed the unmodified AMS (54.26% conversion, 62.67% selectivity) and homogeneous KOH (46.89% conversion, 41.06% selectivity). The primary product was 2-butoxyethanol, with minimal formation of higher homologs (average EO units ≈ 1.2–1.5), as confirmed by GC-FID analysis.

The enhanced activity and selectivity of AMSV are attributed to its optimized structural and surface properties: a 7.2-fold increase in BET surface area (92.88 m2 g−1) for improved reactant accessibility, elevated moderate basic site density (≈ 900 µmol g−1, 400–600 °C) that favors selective mono-ethoxylation, and Lewis basic V5+=O centers (FT-IR band at ≈ 995 cm−1) that facilitate controlled EO ring-opening. In contrast, the stronger basic sites in AMS promoted over-ethoxylation, reducing selectivity.

The vanadium-induced synergy enhances catalytic efficiency and thermal stability while ensuring excellent recyclability, with AMSV retaining initial activity in the second consecutive cycle after simple filtration and ethanol washing. These results establish AMSV as an environmentally benign alternative to homogeneous catalysts for industrial narrow-range ethoxylation, offering reduced waste, lower energy consumption, and potential for continuous operation.
