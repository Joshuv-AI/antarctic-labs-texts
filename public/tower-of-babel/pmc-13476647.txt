Introduction

Epidermoid cysts are rare, benign, slow-growing lesions of ectodermal origin. Histologically, they are characterized by a lining of stratified squamous epithelium filled containing keratin debris and desquamated epithelial cells (Hoang et al., 2019). While intracranial epidermoid cysts have been well studied, spinal epidermoid cysts are much less common, accounting for less than 1% of all spinal tumors. Spinal epidermoid cysts may be congenital or acquired. Congenital lesions have been associated with spinal dysraphism, particularly in the lumbosacral region. Acquired cysts typically result from trauma, such as lumbar puncture or spinal surgery, where epithelial elements are inadvertently introduced into the spinal canal (Sîrbu et al., 2020).

Radiologically, these lesions usually appear isointense to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) on MRI and often show restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), a feature that helps in differentiating them from other cystic lesions such as arachnoid cysts ((Osborn); Phillips and Chiu, 1987).

The ventriculus terminalis, also known as the persistent terminal ventricle or “fifth ventricle”, is a focal dilation of the central canal in the conus medullaris that usually regress after birth(Coleman et al., 1995). Persistence into adulthood is rare. Symptom onset is often delayed because of the indolent growth of these lesions. Epidermoid cysts may remain asymptomatic for years or decades, enlarging slowly until the produce mass effect on the conus medullaris or nerve roots (Ganau et al., 2012).

Given the rarity, a comprehensive understanding of these lesions is essential for accurate diagnosis, surgical planning, and management. In this study, we present a clinical case of an epidermoid cyst located in the conus medullaris and discuss the findings considering the limited number of previously published cases.

Case Presentation

A 56-year-old man presented to our neurosurgical clinic with progressive neurogenic claudication. Neurological examination revealed no motor or sensory deficits. Lumbosacral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a well-circumscribed, intramedullary lesion measuring 11.0× 13.7 × 9.8 mm within the conus medullaris at the level of Th12-L1 (Fig. 1). The lesion appeared hyperdense on CT imaging, with internal calcifications, raising suspicion for an epidermoid cyst (Fig. 2). No associated congenital malformations were identified.

CT of the thoracolumbar spine demonstrating an hyperdense lesion with internal calcification a: axial view, b: sagittal view.

Following diagnostic confirmation, the patient underwent surgery through a posterior midline approach with hemilaminectomy at Th12-L1 level to expose the conus medullaris. Intraoperative neuromonitoring, including motor-evoked potentials (MEPs), somatosensory-evoked potentials (SSEPs), and direct electrical stimulation, was used through the procedure. Intraoperatively, a well-defined cystic lesion was identified. Microsurgical techniques were employed to carefully open the dura mater and achieve complete excision of the cyst. The dura was then closed with a continuous watertight suture, and a TachoSil patch was applied over the suture line to reinforce dural sealing. The wound was closed in anatomical layers. Histopathological examination showed a cyst lined by stratified squamous epithelium, confirming the diagnosis of an epidermoid cyst (Fig. 3).

Histopathological examination (Hematoxylin-Eosin) showing a cystic lesion lined by stratified squamous epithelium (Scale bars: a: 200 μm and b: 50 μm).

The early postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 with complete resolution of symptoms. However, two weeks after surgery, he developed severe headache and swelling at the surgical site. On readmission, a spinal MRI (Fig. 4) demonstrated a complete resection of the cysts and an extraspinal fluid collection along the surgical site, consistent with a postoperative seroma. Because of the suspected CSF leak, a lumbar drain was placed and maintained for 3 days. After 5 days of in-hospital observation, the patient was discharged in good clinical condition without further complications. At the 3-month clinical follow-up, he was asymptomatic, and the surgical wound was unremarkable.

Discussion

Spinal epidermoid cysts are rare lesions and are frequently underdiagnosed. Although intracranial epidermoid cysts are better documented, intramedullary spinal epidermoid cysts, particularly those arising in the conus medullaris, remain uncommon, with only isolated case reports and small case series in the literature. Our case illustrates this rarity, as a systematic search of PubMed and Google Scholar identified fewer than a dozen well-documented adult cases of epidermoid cysts of the conus medullaris without associated malformations.

Clinical presentation

Clinical presentation is typically insidious. Patients most often present with slowly progressive course of back pain, gait disturbance, motor weakness, and/or bladder dysfunction, reflecting gradual compression of the conus medullaris or adjacent neural structures. Ohara et al. (2015) described a 63-year-old woman with gait disturbance, urinary retention, and leg weakness evolving over six months, while Cincu et al. (2007) reported a 28-year-old female with hip pain and urinary incontinence due to a conus–epiconus lesion. Aggarwal et al. (2021) noted a 32-year-old man with hip pain and gait disturbance. Fleming et al. (2011) highlighted the problem of recurrence in a 24-year-old woman who required re-exploration of an intramedullary conus lesion, presenting again with back and hip pain and gait difficulties. Moon et al., in contrast, described a 43-year-old female who developed complete paralysis below T12 following surgery, underscoring the risks of operating in this eloquent region (Moon et al., 2006). Compared to these reports, our patient manifested only progressive claudication without major neurological deficit, emphasizing how such lesions may remain clinically subtle until advanced stages.

Imaging

MRI remains the diagnostic modality of choice for spinal epidermoid cysts. Typical findings include a well-circumscribed lesion that is hypointense or isointense on T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2-weighted images, and demonstrates restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), which is particularly helpful in distinguishing epidermoid cysts from arachnoid cysts and other cystic lesions (Jamjoom et al., 2022). In the present case, the preoperative MRI was obtained externally and did not include DWI sequences, so diffusion restriction could not be assessed, and the initial diagnosis remained uncertain regarding the exact type of cystic lesion.

CT myelography can provide additional information in selected cases, especially when MRI is contraindicated, and can delineate intradural filling defects, spinal canal compromise, and osseous anatomy with high spatial resolution (Patel et al., 2020).

Surgical management and outcomes

Surgical resection remains the treatment o choice for symptomatic lesions. The extent of resection must be balanced against the risk of neurological injury, particularly when the capsule is adherent to the conus medullaris or nerve roots. In such situations, subtotal removal of the capsule may be safer than aggressive dissection, although residual capsule carries a risk of recurrence. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring is especially valuable in surgery involving the conus medullaris, where eloquent neural pathways are densely concentrated. Aggarwal et al. (2021) achieved only partial removal due to adhesion. Their patient initially worsened but improved partially at six months. Cincu et al. (2007) reported near-total excision with neurological improvement. Fleming et al. (2011) performed partial resection for recurrence, with mild postoperative benefit but persistence of disease. By contrast, Moon et al. described poor outcome after attempted excision, with complete paralysis below T12 (Moon et al., 2006). These variable results illustrate the balance between aggressive excision to prevent recurrence and the risk of catastrophic deficits when capsule dissection jeopardizes functional tracts.

In the present case, complete excision was achieved. Nevertheless, the patient developed a postoperative CSF leak requiring lumbar drainage. Postoperative CSF leakage is a recognized complication of spinal tumor surgery and is associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and the need for additional intervention. Reported rates of CSF leak requiring active intervention after spinal tumor resection vary considerably between series, ranging from 0% to 28.6%, reflecting differences in tumor type, surgical approach and definitions used across series (Barber et al., 2019).

Meticulous dural closure and close postoperative monitoring are essential to minimize CSF leakage and related complications (Castillo et al., 2024).

Future directions

Although surgery remains the standard treatment, management strategies continue to evolve. Experimental reports of recombinant hydrolytic enzymes (hyaluronidase, collagenase, lipase) suggest a potential non-surgical option for dissolving cyst contents in high-risk patients (Castelanich et al., 2024). However, such approaches remain in early stages and require further validation before clinical adoption.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while intramedullary epidermoid cysts of the conus medullaris are exceedingly rare, awareness of their clinical, radiological, and surgical features is critical for accurate diagnosis and management. This case contributes to the limited literature and underscores the importance of careful microsurgical technique and vigilant postoperative monitoring, especially with regard to CSF leak.
