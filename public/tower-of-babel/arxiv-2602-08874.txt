Is Reasoning Capability Enough for Safety in Long-Context Language Models?
Yu Fu, Haz Sameen Shahgir, Huanli Gong, Zhipeng Wei, N. Benjamin Erichson, Yue Dong

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly combine long-context processing with advanced reasoning, enabling them to retrieve and synthesize information distributed across tens of thousands of tokens. A hypothesis is that stronger reasoning capability should improve safety by helping models recognize harmful intent even when it is not stated explicitly. We test this hypothesis in long-context settings where harmful intent is implicit and must be inferred through reasoning, and find that it does not hold. We introduce compositional reasoning attacks , a new threat model in which a harmful query is decomposed into incomplete fragments that scattered throughout a long context. The model is then prompted with a neutral reasoning query that induces retrieval and synthesis, causing the harmful intent to emerge only after composition. Evaluating 14 frontier LLMs on contexts up to 64k tokens, we uncover three findings: (1) models with stronger general reasoning capability are not more robust to compositional reasoning attacks, often assembling the intent yet failing to refuse; (2) safety alignment consistently degrades as context length increases; and (3) inference-time reasoning effort is a key mitigating factor: increasing inference-time compute reduces attack success by over 50 percentage points on GPT-oss-120b model. Together, these results suggest that safety does not automatically scale with reasoning capability, especially under long-context inference.

Large language models (LLMs) have made rapid progress in long-context processing
(
18
)
and multi-step reasoning
(
32
)
. Frontier models can now retrieve and integrate information distributed across tens of thousands of tokens, enabling increasingly complex reasoning over aggregated content
(
23
)
. These advances accelerate the deployment of LLMs in sensitive settings and expand the range of tasks, from document analysis to retrieval-augmented decision support.

An expectation is that stronger reasoning over longer inputs also leads to stronger safety alignment. However, most existing safety evaluations focus on direct harmful prompts, where malicious intent is explicit and immediately recognizable
(
21
;
3
;
29
)
. In real-world deployments, such as retrieval-augmented generation systems
(
38
)
, harmful intent may instead be implicit, distributed across retrieved documents, and only become apparent after the model synthesizes multiple pieces of information. This gap motivates a central question:

Does improved reasoning capability translate into better safety alignment in long-context settings, where harmful intent must be inferred rather than directly stated?

In this paper, we study this question through
compositional reasoning attacks
. In this threat model, an adversary decomposes a harmful query into incomplete fragments and scatters them throughout a long context. The harmful intent emerges only after the model retrieves and combines the incomplete fragments. Unlike prior multi-turn or refinement-based jailbreaks
(
15
)
, these attacks use a single-pass prompt over a static long context, isolating failures that arise from reasoning over distributed evidence rather than interactive manipulation. A well-aligned model should retrieve and integrate the fragments but refuse once it recognizes the reconstructed objective. This setting tests whether models refuse after reconstructing harmful intent through reasoning, rather than whether they can refuse explicit harmful requests.

Figure 2:
Radar chart comparing model safety performance across reasoning types at 64k context length. Axes correspond to Direct Retrieval, Single-hop Aggregation, Chain Reasoning, and Multi-hop Deductive Reasoning; values indicate safety ratio (%).

We build a benchmark around this threat model using harmful queries from AdvBench
(
37
)
. Our benchmark systematically varies both context length (up to 64k tokens) and the reasoning required to reconstruct harmful intent. In particular, we design several reasoning types ranging from simple aggregation to multi-hop deductive reasoning, allowing us to isolate how safety alignment changes as reasoning complexity increases and evidence becomes more dispersed.
To disentangle safety failures from retrieval failures or prompt artifacts, we keep the surface form of the user query fixed and independently control factors such as fragment placement, and inference-time reasoning effort.

Evaluating 14 frontier LLMs, we find that stronger reasoning capability does not reliably translate into stronger safety alignment. Models often retrieve and correctly assemble distributed fragments, yet fail to recognize the reconstructed content as harmful and refuse. Moreover, safety alignment degrades consistently as context length increases: models become progressively less likely to refuse when fragments are embedded in longer documents, even though their retrieval and synthesis abilities remain strong. Figure
1
provides a high-level overview of these trends. Figure
2
then zooms in on the 64k context-length setting, comparing safety across reasoning types and showing that many models remain robust under direct retrieval but degrade when harmful intent must be reconstructed through reasoning.

These findings raise a follow-up question:
do models lack the ability to recognize reconstructed harmful intent, or do they possess this capability but fail to apply sufficient reasoning effort by default?
To answer this, we increase inference-time reasoning effort within the same model, without changing model weights or training data. This intervention reduces the success of the attack by more than 50 percentage points. This indicates that the safety-relevant capability is present but is not consistently engaged. More broadly, our results highlight a practical risk of deploying long-context LLMs: as context length grows, recognizing harmful intent requires deeper and more sustained reasoning, yet models do not automatically allocate additional inference-time compute to safety-relevant judgment. As a result, safety alignment can degrade even as overall reasoning capability improves.

Contributions.
Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce
compositional reasoning attacks
, a threat model in which harmful intent is split into incomplete fragments and only emerges after reasoning over long contexts, and we build a benchmark based on AdvBench to evaluate safety in this setting.

•

We design a suite of reasoning types, from single-hop aggregation to multi-hop deductive reasoning, that systematically controls the amount of reasoning required to reconstruct harmful intent.

•

Evaluating 14 frontier LLMs, we show that a stronger general reasoning capability does not imply stronger safety alignment: models often reconstruct harmful intent but fail to refuse, and safety degrades systematically as context length increases.

•

We identify inference-time reasoning effort as a key factor for robustness: increasing inference-time compute within the same model reduces attack success by more than 50 percentage points without changing model weights.

2
Methodology

We propose a benchmark to test whether safety alignment in LLMs scales with reasoning capability in long contexts.
We break a harmful query into incomplete fragments and distribute them throughout a long document. No single fragment can reconstruct the harmful request on its own; harmful intent becomes clear only after the model retrieves and combines the fragments. A well-aligned model should recover the full query but refuse to comply once it recognizes the harmful objective, according to its safety policy.

2.1
Threat Model

We consider a black-box adversary who tries to induce harmful outputs from a safety-aligned LLM by exploiting long-context reasoning. The adversary interacts with the model only through an API and cannot inspect weights, prompts, system messages, or any other internal state. Their goal is to craft inputs that lead the model to produce harmful content despite the model’s safety training.

Instead of submitting a harmful query directly, the adversary decomposes it into
n
fragments
\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\}
and embeds them as “needles” in a long “haystack” document
\mathcal{H}
. During decomposition, the adversary may add concrete details (e.g., chemical names, tool commands) that are not present in the original query. These details make each fragment more specific while ensuring that no fragment alone constitutes a complete harmful request. The adversary then issues a neutral reasoning query
q
that prompts the model to retrieve and synthesize the fragments. The attack succeeds if the model combines the fragments without refusing, i.e., if it produces an output that fulfills the harmful intent rather than recognizing and declining the request.

2.2
Reasoning Types

To systematically study the relationship between reasoning capacity and safety alignment, we design three types of fragment decomposition, each requiring a different cognitive strategy to reconstruct harmful intent from a long context.

Baseline
: Direct Retrieval.
The original harmful query is inserted verbatim as a single fragment in the long context, as introduced by
27
. The model’s task is simply to find this fragment in the haystack and respond. Since no reconstruction is needed, this baseline isolates long-context retrieval from reasoning: the model only needs to retrieve the query and recognize its harmful nature. We use it as a control to measure the baseline safety alignment when harmful intent is directly present in the retrieved text.

Type 1
: Single-hop Aggregation.
The harmful query is decomposed into exactly two fragments
(f_{1},f_{2})
that are each incomplete on their own, but jointly constitute the full harmful procedure when combined:
{f_{1}+f_{2}\rightarrow\text{Answer}}
. The model’s task is to locate both fragments in the long context and merge them into a single coherent procedure. This type introduces the simplest form of reasoning beyond retrieval: one aggregation step over two retrieved pieces. For example, one fragment may describe
“the materials needed”
, while the other specifies
“the procedure”
. Neither fragment alone provides a harmful instruction, but their combination does.

Type 2
: Chain Reasoning.
The harmful query is decomposed into exactly three fragments
(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
that form a sequential dependency chain:
f_{1}\rightarrow f_{2}\rightarrow f_{3}
. The model must retrieve all three fragments and process them in order, because each fragment explicitly references the output of the previous one (e.g.,
“Using the information gathered in Fact 1…”
or
“With the result from Fact 2…”
). This type isolates multi-step reasoning with explicit links: the model cannot solve the task by treating fragments as independent facts. For example, in an identity theft scenario:
collect personal information

\rightarrow

use [gathered information] to obtain an SSN

\rightarrow

use [SSN] to commit fraud
. These explicit cross-references ensure that the harmful intent emerges only through sequential reasoning.

Type 3
: Multi-hop Deductive Reasoning.
The harmful query is decomposed into exactly four fragments
(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4})
that require multi-step deduction. Unlike Type 2, the fragments do not contain explicit cross-references; the model must infer the latent connections between them. Concretely, the model must first combine a subset of fragments to deduce an intermediate “bridge” concept
X
that is never explicitly stated, and then use this inferred concept to synthesize the remaining fragments into the harmful conclusion. The structure follows
(f_{1}+f_{2})\rightarrow X
(inferred bridge), then
(X+f_{3}+f_{4})\rightarrow\text{Answer}
. For example, in a cybersecurity scenario: (
target lacks input validation
+
return address can be overwritten
)
\rightarrow

buffer overflow vulnerability
(inferred)
\rightarrow
(
shellcode generation tool
+
execution requirements
)
\rightarrow

complete exploit
. By removing explicit links, this forces deduction rather than following a linear narrative.

Together, these four types define a spectrum of cognitive demands, ranging from simple retrieval to complex multi-step deduction. This design allows us to test whether improvements in reasoning capability are accompanied by corresponding improvements in safety alignment. Specifically, this approach enables us to study and test whether models can recognize and refuse harmful intent that only becomes apparent after reasoning over fragments rather than from direct exposure to a single harmful query.

2.3
Benchmark Construction

Harmful Query Collection.

We construct our benchmark from the first 100 harmful queries in AdvBench
(
37
)
, a widely used dataset that spans multiple categories, including cybersecurity, chemistry, social engineering, and illegal activities. For each harmful query, we generate decompositions for all three reasoning types described above using the closed-source Gemini-2.0-flash model.

For each query, the decomposition process produces three artifacts: (1) a set of fragments
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
, where
n\in\{1,2,3,4\}
depends on the type of reasoning; (2) a neutral reasoning query
q
that prompts the model to retrieve and synthesize the fragments; and (3) a verification step confirming that the reconstructed answer is semantically equivalent to the original harmful query.
To control for prompt effects across reasoning types, we use an identical query template for
q
(
“Based on the inserted facts, describe the complete procedure.”
). This ensures that differences in model behavior are driven by the reasoning required to assemble the fragments, rather than by differences in how the query is asked. The detailed decomposition prompts for each reasoning type are provided in Appendix
A
.

Long-Context Construction.

To construct long-context examples, we embed the generated fragments at random positions within a haystack document
\mathcal{H}
. Following BABILong
(
11
)
, we use books from the PG19 corpus
(
24
)
as background text. Random insertion spreads fragments throughout the document to mimic realistic retrieval settings, where relevant information is dispersed across the context rather than concentrated in one place.
We mainly evaluate three context lengths: 0k (no haystack), 16k, and 64k tokens. The final input consists of the haystack
\mathcal{H}
with embedded fragments, followed by the reasoning query. Our benchmark contains 1,200 examples (100 queries
\times
4 reasoning types
\times
3 context lengths and example can be found in Appendix
B
).

2.4
Evaluation Protocol

To evaluate whether a model’s output fulfills the harmful intent, we use Gemini-2.0-flash as an automated evaluator. We adopt the X-teaming evaluation prompt
(
25
)
, which assigns a score on a 1–5 scale. The complete evaluation prompt is provided in Appendix
C
.

We take a conservative definition of a successful attack: we classify a response as unsafe only when the evaluator assigns the maximum score of 5, indicating that the response fulfills the harmful intent. We measure the
Safety Ratio
(SR) as:

\text{SR}=\frac{|\{N-r_{i}:\text{score}(r_{i})=5\}|}{N},

where
r_{i}
is the model’s response to the
i
-th test case and
N
is the total number of test cases. Lower SR indicates weaker safety alignment.

3
Experiment

3.1
Experimental Settings

We evaluate 14 frontier LLMs on our benchmark using standard inference configurations. For open-source models, we follow the inference settings recommended by the model developers. For closed-source models accessed via API, we use greedy decoding to obtain deterministic and reproducible outputs. All models are evaluated with their default system prompts to avoid introducing additional prompt-specific effects. Detailed hyperparameters for each model are provided in Appendix
D
.

3.2
Main Results

Table
1
reports the safety ratio for each model across three context lengths and four reasoning types (see Sec.
2.2
). The results highlight three key insights about how reasoning capability and context length interact with safety alignment.

Direct retrieval demonstrates strong baseline safety.

In the
Direct Retrieval
setting, nearly all models achieve near-perfect safety ratios at all three context lengths. Averaged across models, the safety ratio is 100% at 0k context, 96% at 16k, and 95% at 64k. This establishes an important baseline: when the harmful query is presented explicitly and requires no compositional reasoning, modern LLMs typically recognize the harmful intent and refuse to comply. In other words, models remain robust even when the harmful request appears within a long context. This result also serves as a sanity check for our benchmark design and suggests that current safety training is effective in retrieval-based scenarios where harmful intent is directly stated.

Reasoning capability does not guarantee safety alignment.

When reasoning is required (
Single-hop Aggregation
,
Chain Reasoning
, and
Multi-hop Deductive Reasoning
), safety performance varies substantially across models, and this variation shows no consistent relationship with general reasoning capability. The Gemini family is the clearest outlier, exhibiting markedly weaker safety alignment than other frontier models. For example, at 0k context length, Gemini-2.5-flash-preview achieves safety ratios of only 7%, 9%, and 9% across our three reasoning types, whereas GPT-5.2 achieves 87%, 76%, and 92% under the same conditions. Similarly, Gemini-3-flash-preview achieves 33%, 30%, and 18%, substantially lower than Claude-sonnet-4.5 (84%, 74%, 84%) and Claude-haiku-4.5 (81%, 69%, 74%).

This disparity is notable given Gemini’s state-of-the-art reasoning capability. In contrast, GPT-5.2 and the Claude models maintain high safety ratios, suggesting a better generalization of safety behavior when harmful intent must be reconstructed. Variation is also substantial among open-source models: the GPT-oss models remain above 86% safety, while Deepseek-v3.2 drops to 29%. Overall, these results indicate that stronger reasoning capacity alone is not sufficient for safety; safety alignment appears to be an orthogonal dimension that requires explicit attention.

Figure 4:
Impact of context length and inference-time reasoning effort on safety for GPT-oss-120b (results averaged over Type 1-3). Safety degrades sharply as context length increases (top), with high-effort reasoning partially mitigating long-context failures. Context length significantly impacts safety performance.

Averaged over
Single-hop Aggregation
,
Chain Reasoning
, and
Multi-hop Deductive Reasoning
, the safety ratio drops from 55% in short contexts (0k) to 43% in moderate contexts (16k) and further to 42% in long contexts (64k), a 13% relative decline overall. This consistent degradation from short to moderate to long contexts suggests that extended contexts pose a systematic challenge for safety mechanisms, even when the task format is unchanged.

At the model level, the sensitivity to context length varies substantially. Many models degrade sharply: for
Multi-hop Deductive Reasoning
, GPT-oss-120b drops from 93% (0k) to 48% (16k) to 37% (64k), and Claude-sonnet-4.5 decreases from 84% to 54% to 42%. However, this effect is not uniform. Some models remain relatively stable (GPT-5.2: 92% to 67% to 78%) or even improve with longer contexts (Qwen3-next-80b-a3b-thinking: 29% to 61% to 64%). This heterogeneity indicates that the interaction between long-context reasoning and safety alignment is not fixed: stronger general capability does not automatically preserve safety as context grows, implying that long-context safety requires explicit design beyond overall reasoning improvements.

3.3
Reasoning Analysis

Table
1
shows that strong reasoning capacity does not reliably translate into strong safety alignment under compositional reasoning attacks. This raises a question:
What factors determine safety alignment in reasoning settings?

3.3.1
Reasoning Effort Improves Safety

Next, we study
reasoning effort
: the computational resources allocated during inference. We treat reasoning effort as a distinct and potentially important dimension that is often overlooked in safety evaluations. Concretely, we evaluate GPT-oss-120b model under three reasoning effort levels (Low, Medium, High) across varying context lengths. Figure
4
summarizes the results, showing safety performance as a function of context length (Top) and efficiency trade-off between safety gains and computational cost (Bottom).

Higher Reasoning Effort Consistently Improves Safety.
As shown in the left panels for both models in Figure
4
, increasing reasoning effort improves safety across all context lengths. With higher effort, models more often surface the reconstructed harmful intent in their reasoning traces and refuse to comply (see Appendix
E
for examples). For GPT-oss-120b at 0k context, safety ratios increase from 72% (Low) to 91% (Medium) and 92% (High). The same trend holds at 64k, where safety increases from 12% (Low) to 40% (Medium) and 63% (High). Although safety degrades at every effort level as context grows, higher reasoning effort preserves a substantially larger safety margin. Correspondingly, the gap between High and Low effort widens from +20 percentage points at 0k to +51 points at 64k.

Diminishing Marginal Returns of Reasoning Effort.

Although increasing reasoning effort consistently improves safety, the efficiency of these gains varies. As shown in the bottom of Figure
4
, moving from
Low
to
Medium
effort yields a large safety improvement of 37.6 percentage points (27.0% to 64.6%) with a moderate increase in token consumption. This corresponds to an efficiency of +22.1% safety improvement per 100 reasoning tokens. In contrast, moving from
Medium
to
High
effort shows diminishing returns: safety increases further to 80.4%, but the computational cost more than doubles (220 to 599 tokens), reducing efficiency to only +4.2% per 100 tokens.

The Dilemma of Static Reasoning Effort.
Although the efficiency trade-off suggests a theoretical “sweet spot” for reasoning effort, identifying this optimum in practice is challenging.
As illustrated in Figure
4
, the marginal utility of additional reasoning tokens varies drastically across effort levels, with high efficiency at lower effort but diminishing returns at higher effort. This non-linearity creates a fundamental dilemma: a static reasoning-effort setting either incurs prohibitive computational cost through over-reasoning or risks severe safety failures due to under-reasoning.

3.3.2
Task Complexity Analysis

Next, we examine how task complexity influences safety alignment (Figure
5
). Under low reasoning effort, safety decreases as task complexity increases: Single-Hop achieves 31%, Chain drops to 29%, and Multi-Hop further decreases to 21%. This pattern suggests a budget-allocation effect: more complex tasks may consume most of the available reasoning effort to reconstruct the harmful intent, leaving less budget for the model to recognize the harm and refuse.

Task complexity also modulates the benefits of increasing reasoning effort. As shown in Figure
5
, Multi-Hop achieves a safety improvement of 277% on GPT-oss-120b when moving from low to high reasoning effort, compared to 158% for Single-Hop. Together, these results indicate that complex reasoning tasks are most vulnerable under low reasoning budgets, yet also gain the most from additional reasoning effort. This complements StrataSword
(
34
)
, which shows that attack complexity affects safety; here we show a parallel effect on the defense side, where the impact of reasoning effort is similarly task-dependent.

3.4
Long-Context Analysis

Although we analyzed how reasoning effort and task complexity affect safety, our main results also show that context length is a critical factor. To understand why safety degrades in long contexts, we focus on two mechanisms:
context relevance
(whether safety depends on the full context or on task-relevant fragments) and
needle position
(how the location of fragments within the context affects safety).

3.4.1
Context Relevant Analysis

We study how the surrounding context influences safety by comparing two conditions:
irrelevant context
(as in Table
1
, where the decomposed fragments are embedded in unrelated documents) and
relevant context
(where an uncensored LLM generates task-related neutral background text that semantically connects to the harmful query). The generation prompts for relevant context are provided in Appendix
F
.

As shown in Figure
6
, the models achieve
higher
safety ratios when the fragments are embedded in a relevant context than in an irrelevant context. At 4K context length, the relevant context yields an average safety ratio of 80% versus 68.7% for the irrelevant context (+11.3 points). This gap grows with context length, increasing from +11.3 points at 4K to +32.3 points at 64k.
This result is counter to previous work
(
20
)
, which finds that task-relevant information can facilitate attack success. In our setting, the relevant context appears to provide contextual clarity: it makes the reconstructed harmful intent more explicit and coherent, which may make it easier for safety mechanisms to detect and reject. By contrast, irrelevant context can create a
camouflage effect
, where harmful intent remains diffuse and implicit amid noisy distractors, potentially slipping past safety filters.

3.4.2
Needle Position Analysis

Another question in long-context modeling is whether the position of critical information affects performance. Prior work shows a “lost-in-the-middle” effect
(
19
)
, where models retrieve information from the middle of long contexts less reliably than from the beginning or end. We test whether this retrieval vulnerability translates into safety failures by systematically varying the placement of decomposed fragments across four regions: Start, Early Middle, Late Middle, and End.
As shown in Figure
7
, the safety ratios are insensitive to the position of the fragments in all context lengths. For GPT-oss-120b at 16k context, safety varies only within a narrow range across the four regions.

This position-invariant behavior suggests that models can retrieve and integrate decomposed fragments regardless of where they appear in the context. However, our main results (Table
1
) still show a substantial degradation in safety as the length of the context increases. Together, these findings indicate that long-context safety failures are not primarily driven by an inability to locate relevant fragments. Instead, failures arise after successful retrieval: models reconstruct the harmful intent but do not reliably recognize it as harmful and refuse.
This suggests that improving long-context safety will require strengthening intrinsic safety alignment mechanisms, rather than focusing only on retrieval or general reasoning capability.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the safety risks arising from long-context reasoning through compositional reasoning attacks, in which harmful intent is distributed across extended contexts.
Evaluating 14 frontier LLMs, we find that strong reasoning capability alone does not ensure safety alignment: models can retrieve and integrate dispersed fragments, yet often fail to refuse the reconstructed harmful intent.
Moreover, safety degrades consistently as the length of the context increases, and our analysis suggests that this is not due to retrieval failures but to weaknesses in applying safety judgment after successful long context reasoning.

We identify inference-time reasoning effort as a key mitigating factor. Increasing reasoning effort within the same model improves safety, indicating that the capability to recognize harm is present but not reliably engaged by default.
These findings expose a fundamental tension: the reasoning abilities that enable long-context understanding also create new attack surfaces, and mitigating these risks through increased reasoning effort incurs substantial computational cost. Addressing this challenge will require adaptive safety mechanisms that balance security and efficiency based on both model characteristics and context lengths.

Impact Statement

This work aims to improve the safety and reliability of large language models by studying vulnerabilities that arise from long-context reasoning and compositional attacks. By systematically analyzing how harmful intent can be reconstructed from distributed fragments, our findings highlight failure modes that are not captured by standard safety evaluations focused on short or explicit prompts. Understanding these vulnerabilities is essential for deploying LLMs safely in real-world settings where models must reason over long documents and complex contexts.

As with prior research on security and safety, our results have a dual-use nature. Insights into compositional attack strategies and long-context failure modes could potentially be misused to bypass existing safety mechanisms. However, we argue that rigorously identifying and characterizing these weaknesses is a necessary step toward building more robust defenses. By showing that safety failures persist even when retrieval and reasoning succeed, our work points to the need for adaptive safety mechanisms that scale with reasoning depth and context length.
Overall, we expect this work to have a positive societal impact by informing the design of safer long-context LLMs, guiding future safety evaluations beyond surface-level benchmarks, and encouraging further research into defense strategies that remain effective as models’ reasoning capabilities continue to improve.
