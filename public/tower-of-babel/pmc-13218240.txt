Provoking disulfidptosis-enhanced mitophagy by manganese-based nanoplatform via regulating cellular energy supply and redox homeostasis
Authors: Zhenying Diao, Bin Feng, Yanfei Fu, Youcheng Liang, Long Qiu, Jianbo Sun, Yanlei Liu, Daxiang Cui, Ting Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic reprogramming and reinforcing redox defense mechanisms in tumors remains major obstacles to oxidative stress therapies in Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). To address these obstacles, we strategically targeted cystine transporter SLC7A11, which plays a central role in cellular redox homeostasis. Herein, guided by bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, we identified that concurrent activation of SLC7A11 and inhibition of glucose transporter GLUT1 synergistically delays NSCLC progression. We introduced an effective strategy to effectively target SLC7A11 and GLUT1 for synergistic chemodynamic therapy (CDT) and disulfidptosis. A RNA-seq-guided nanoplatform was constructed from manganese dioxide (HMnO2) with hollow structures and loaded with GLUT1 inhibitor Bay876 (HMnO2@Bay). Upon tumor cell uptake, HMnO2 continuously generates cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals through Fenton-like reaction and activates the expression of SLC7A11. The Bay876 released from the nanoplatform blocks glucose uptake, downregulates NADPH supplementation, and disrupts the SLC7A11-mediated GSH synthesis antioxidant pathway. This boosts disulfidptosis via increased cystine accumulation while concurrently enhancing CDT through the attenuation of GSH-mediated ROS exhaustion. Additionally, RNA-seq explored changes in the transcriptome of tumor cells treated with HMnO2@Bay, revealing that the synergistic therapy of CDT and disulfidptosis promotes mitophagy in tumor cells. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations reveal the treatment effectively suppresses tumor growth with minimal systemic toxicity. This study provides a promising NSCLC therapy paradigm to induce disulfidptosis-enhanced mitophagy by simultaneously targeting oxidative and glucose dyshomeostasis.Keywords: RNA-Sequencing, Metabolic regulation, Mitophagy, Disulfidptosis, Synergistic therapies

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), a predominant subtype of lung cancer, presents significant clinical challenges including diagnostic difficulties, drug resistance, poor prognosis, and high risk of metastasis [1,2]. Driven by metabolic disturbances from disordered growth, tumor cells generally experience higher levels of oxidative stress than normal cells [3,4]. This characteristic of the tumor microenvironment (TME) has been exploited in therapeutic approaches such as chemodynamic therapy (CDT), which effectively kills tumor cells by generating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) [5]. However, tumor cells counteract ROS through adaptive mechanisms, including mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy) to remove damaged mitochondria and sustain redox balance, alongside increased glucose uptake and glutathione (GSH) production [6,7]. In this process, A key player in their antioxidant defense is the cystine transporter SLC7A11, which is overexpressed on tumor cell membranes [8]. SLC7A11 imports cystine, converting it to cysteine for GSH synthesis, thereby neutralizing ROS and promoting survival [9]. While mitophagy helps tumor cells mitigate oxidative damage, excessive ROS can overwhelm this system, triggering lethal mitochondrial dysfunction. Thus, simultaneously targeting SLC7A11-mediated cystine metabolism and disrupting mitophagy may synergistically disrupt redox homeostasis, enhancing tumor sensitivity to CDT.

In the SLC7A11/Cystine/GSH antioxidant mechanism, Gan et al. discovered a novel cell death mode, characterized by disulfide bond accumulation and actin contraction, which they named disulfidptosis [10]. Disulfidptosis is predominantly triggered in tumor cells by the combination of elevated SLC7A11 levels and a depleted nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) pool. Highly expressed SLC7A11 is able to translocate cystine into the cell, which can be reduced to cysteine in the cytoplasm with the assistance of NADPH. When the supply of NADPH is inhibited, cystine cannot be reduced to cysteine in a timely manner, leading to the accumulation of cystine. Excessive accumulation of cystine may cause actin cytoskeletal proteins to have abundant disulfides and disulfide bonds, triggering the collapse of the actin network and inducing disulfide death in tumor cells. In addition, glutathione reductase maintains redox homeostasis by catalyzing the reduction of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) back to its reduced form (GSH), using NADPH as the electron donor [11]. Recently, several studies have explored biomaterial-mediated disulfidptosis for cancer therapy. Zhang et al. developed a hydrogen-bonded organic framework (HOFs) biocatalyst system that disrupts redox homeostasis and induces glucose deprivation, thereby activating disulfidptosis-mediated antitumor immunotherapy [12]. Zhang et al. reported a manganese-based nanoadjuvant (BMP-Au) that synergistically triggers disulfidptosis-mediated immunogenic cell death and activates the cGAS-STING pathway [13]. Yu et al. constructed a copper single-atom nanozyme integrated with glucose oxidase (GOx) to interrupt cellular energy supply and NADPH generation, thereby provoking tumor disulfidptosis [14]. These studies highlight the potential of inducing disulfidptosis through glucose deprivation or redox disruption. Therefore, blocking NADPH biosynthesis is important for interfering with redox homeostasis, optimizing ROS generation and inducing disulfidptosis for effective cancer therapy.

With elevated ROS levels, tumor cells respond to metabolic stress and maintain cell survival by increasing mitophagy activity [15]. However, mitophagy manifests dual roles in the tumor microenvironment (TME), with its different levels potentially steering tumors toward different fates. When over-activated mitophagy levels, beyond a critical threshold, result in the removal of healthy mitochondria, which can impair normal cell function and survival [6,16,17]. In order to fully utilize death-promoting mitophagy, fundamental solutions to ROS generation and mitochondrial dysfunction need to be integrated into material function. The current study found that excess manganese (Mn) leads to cellular mitochondrial dysfunction, manifested as elevated mitochondrial ROS levels alongside reductions in mitochondrial mass and membrane potential [18,19]. A promising therapeutic strategy for NSCLC involves using Mn-based nanomaterials in CDT to elevate ROS levels, induce mitophagy, and ultimately eradicate tumor cells.

Here, our bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis identified that concurrent expression of SLC7A11 and GLUT1 is associated with NSCLC progression. We developed a multifunctional nanoplatform by encapsulating the glucose transporter inhibitor Bay876 within hollow manganese dioxide (HMnO2@Bay). This nanoplatform is designed to synergistically integrate disulfidptosis induction and CDT through a self-sustaining ROS-generating cascade, ultimately triggering mitophagy for potent tumor cell elimination. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), HMnO2@Bay demonstrated efficient cellular uptake by A549 lung cancer cells. The acidic TME triggered the degradation of the HMnO2 releasing Mn2+ ions which catalyzed a Fenton-like reaction to convert endogenous H2O2 into highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Concurrently, the released Bay876 effectively suppressed GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake, disrupting glycolysis and NADPH regeneration. These reactions further lead to depletion of the NADPH pool and accumulation of cystine, which triggers disulfidptosis of tumor cells through SLC7A11 functions. Severe blockade of GSH synthesis coupled with GSH consumption by Mn2+-mediated oxidative stress, amplifying CDT efficacy. The resultant collapse of redox and metabolic homeostasis exacerbated mitochondrial damage, driving aberrant mitophagy activation. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments provide evidence that HMnO2@Bay is effective in killing tumor cells, providing an innovative therapeutic approach against NSCLC.

Results and discussion

Bioinformatic analysis of the effect on the NSCLC

From existing research literature, we identified 36 genes associated with disulfidptosis (Table S1). A total of 508 lung cancer patients were randomly split into a training cohort of 356 and a test cohort of 152. The gene expression matrix derived from the training group samples was analyzed. Through an iterative LASSO Cox regression model, six key prognostic genes (GYS1, ME1, MTHFD1, GLUT1, SLC3A2, SLC7A11) were screened from the 36 candidate DRGs. (Fig. 1A and B). The survival analysis revealed a markedly inferior overall survival for high-risk patients in the training cohort relative to their low-risk counterparts, supported by a model AUC of 0.763 (Fig. 1C and D and S1B). The prognostic model demonstrated robust predictive performance in the test cohort (AUC = 0.753), with patients stratified into the low-risk group exhibiting significantly improved survival rates (Fig. 1E and S1C). These results validated the prognostic model's accuracy and indicated a strong link between the expression level of six DRGs and lung cancer patient survival prognosis. According to Fig. 1F, the PPI network graph demonstrated that the six prognostic genes have strong protein interactions. GLUT1 and SLC7A11 were central nodes in the PPI network graph, suggesting their significant influence on the poor prognosis of lung cancer patients through the regulation of other prognostic genes. The gene matrix correlation analysis showed a strong co-expression link between GLUT1 and SLC7A11 (Fig. 1G). Moreover, we identified that GLUT1 and SLC7A11 were significantly up-regulated and expressed in lung cancer patients, which suggests they may be potential target genes for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) therapy (Fig. 1H and I).

Preparation and characterization of HMnO2@Bay

Hollow HMnO2 nanoparticles were fabricated through a stencil-based process involving the synthesis of a silicon dioxide template and its subsequent etching (Scheme 1). The successful synthesis and stimuli-responsive behavior of the HMnO2@Bay nanoparticles were systematically characterized, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images provide direct visual evidence of the HMnO2@Bay nanoparticles. HMnO2@Bay nanoparticles exhibited a well-defined and uniform spherical morphology (Fig. 2A). Upon exposure to a mildly acidic environment (pH = 6.5), the structural integrity of the nanoparticles begined to compromise, as indicated by the appearance of fragmented and disintegrated structures (Fig. 2B). This degradation process becomed markedly more pronounced at pH 5.5, where the nanoparticles were almost completely dissolved, confirming the pH-dependent disintegration of the HMnO2 (Fig. 2C). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of HMnO2 revealed Mn 2p peaks at 642.36 eV (Mn 2p3/2) and 654.05 eV (Mn 2p1/2) with a spin-orbit splitting of 11.69 eV (Fig. S2A–C). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) imaging revealed a clear lattice fringe with an interplanar spacing of 0.25 nm (Fig. S2D and E). TEM-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping demonstrated that Mn and O were uniformly distributed across the entire nanoparticle (Fig. S2F). In addition, the characteristic fluorine (F) signal from Bay was also homogeneously colocalized with Mn and O, confirming that Bay was evenly loaded. DLS measurements showed an increase in the average hydrodynamic diameter from 134 nm of HMnO2 to 151 nm of HMnO2@Bay (Fig. 2D). Zeta potential of Bay was −3.7 ± 2.5 mV, that of HMnO2 was approximately −30.2 ± 2.5 mV, and that of HMnO2@Bay was −39.1 ± 1.4 mV (Fig. S2G). The optimized capability of Bay was approximately 51.1%, indicating the high loading efficiency of HMnO2 (Fig. S2H). The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopic profiles for HMnO2, Bay, and HMnO2@Bay are presented in Fig. 2E. Compared to free Bay, the UV absorption peak of Bay loaded into HMnO2 displayed a red shift from 340 nm to 350 nm. The dialysis bag diffusion method was utilized to assess the release profile of Bay from the HMnO2@Bay. Under acidic conditions (pH 5.5) with GSH, a cumulative release of approximately 58% was achieved within 24 h (Fig. S2I). To accurately assess the colloidal stability, the size distribution of HMnO2@Bay was monitored in saline, PBS (pH 7.4), and cell culture medium over 7 days. In all three media, no significant increase in particle size or aggregation was observed (Fig. S3).

Due to the high intracellular levels of GSH in tumor tissues, which scavenge hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and render its generation ineffective, depleting GSH has emerged as a key strategy to enhance ·OH production [20]. HMnO2 depletes GSH through reduction to Mn2+ (eqn (1)), which in turn facilitate ·OH production. Additionally, HMnO2 exhibits catalytic activity toward ·OH production. To evaluate the GSH-depleting activity of HMnO2@Bay, DTNB was employed as an indicator of GSH content (Fig. 2F). Upon reaction with GSH, DTNB forms the yellow derivative TNB. This product is soluble in NaOH and formaldehyde and generates a detectable absorption peak at approximately 412 nm. Therefore, GSH consumption by HMnO2@Bay, can be quantified by measuring the absorbance at 412 nm. Upon addition of HMnO2@Bay to the mixture of DTNB and GSH, a significant decrease in absorbance at 412 nm was observed, indicating the excellent GSH depletion capacity of HMnO2@Bay. Mn2+ is a highly efficient As a highly efficient Fenton reagent, Mn2+ catalyzes the degradation of H2O2 to generate highly toxic ·OH (eqn (1)), a reaction harnessed for chemokinetics-based cancer therapy [21]. To ensure the improved CDT effects, we employed 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as an indicator to assess the ability of HMnO2@Bay to generate ·OH (Fig. 2G). As shown in Fig. 2G, there was no significant absorption was observed in TMB and HMnO2@Bay. The TMB + H2O2 and TMB + H2O2+Bay group exhibit similar and relatively weak absorption peaks at 652 nm. However, more significant absorption peak at 652 nm was observed in TMB + H2O2+HMnO2@Bay and TMB + H2O2+HMnO2, which indicating that Mn2+ is a key factor in the generation of ·OH via the Fenton-like reaction. To further investigate, electron spin resonance spectroscopy (ESR) was employed to quantify the generation of ·OH. The generation of ·OH in H2O2 and HMnO2@Bay solutions was measured under different pH conditions (Fig. 2H). HMnO2@Bay exhibited the strongest ESR signal in acidic solution (pH = 5.5), indicating significant generation of ·OH. In contrast, only a weak ESR signal was observed in neutral solution (pH = 7.4). These results confirm that the Mn2+-catalyzed Fenton-like reaction efficiently generates highly ·OH under acidic conditions. The MRI capability of HMnO2@Bay was evaluated by measuring its longitudinal relaxation time utilizing a clinical MR scanner (Fig. 2I). A concentration-dependent increase in MR signal intensity was observed for HMnO2@Bay, with brighter images obtained at higher concentrations (up to 9.2 μg/mL). The signal intensity showed a linear relationship with concentration, confirming the strong in vitro MRI capability of HMnO2@Bay. These results indicate that HMnO2@Bay possesses both excellent biocompatibility and high performance for potential bioimaging applications.

CDT-disulfidptosis therapy performance

In order to illustrate the anticancer mechanism of HMnO2@Bay, we first analyzed the internalization process of HMnO2@Bay by A549 lung cancer cells over time by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) and flow cytometry analysis. As shown in Fig. 3A, CLSM images revealed a progressive increase in intracellular red fluorescence from 2 h to 4 h and further to 8 h, indicating sustained internalization of the nanoplatform over time. Flow cytometric analysis corroborated these observations, demonstrating a time-dependent enhancement of the mean fluorescence intensity in cells incubated with HMnO2@Bay (Fig. 3B). The uptake efficiency at 4 h was markedly higher than that at 2 h, and a further notable increase was observed at 8 h, confirming that the nanoplatform is efficiently and continuously internalized by cells in a time-dependent manner. These results implied that the efficient cellular internalization of HMnO2@Bay was a crucial step toward achieving its potent therapeutic outcomes in vivo.

The CCK-8 assay was employed to assess the synergistic effect of CDT and disulfidptosis on cell survival. Results revealed a dose-dependent decrease in viability with increasing HMnO2 concentration during in vitro CDT, with modest toxicity observed at 4 μg/mL (Fig. S4). Subsequently, the in vitro therapeutic efficacy of HMnO2@Bay was evaluated (Fig. 3C). A549 cells were treated with different treatments, and the survival rate decreased significantly as the concentration of Bay was elevated. Notably, in the presence of cystine, the HMnO2@Bay + Cys group exhibited the lowest cell survival rate compared to all groups. To further investigate the synergistic antitumor effects of CDT and disulfidptosis, the cytotoxicity of different treatments was assessed using live/dead cell staining assays (Fig. 3D). There were only a few red spots in the HMnO2 group, suggesting that CDT alone was weakly killing tumor cells. Furhermore, we performed rescue experiment using a reducing agent (TCEP). The addition of TCEP markedly reduced the number of dead cells, indicating partial rescue of cell viability. Analysis of Annexin V-FITC/PI staining showed that A549 cells treated with HMnO2@Bay + Cys mainly experienced necrosis (11.5%) and apoptosis (13.2%), while the corresponding necrosis and apoptosis rates in the PBS group were only 2.7% and 1.7% (Fig. S5). These results indicate that HMnO2@Bay + Cys treatment leads to enhanced overall cell death burden. Notably, disulfidptosis, which does not involve early phosphatidylserine externalization, cannot be accurately captured by Annexin V/PI flow cytometry, and the apoptotic cells detected may be attributed to ROS induced apoptosis [10].

Next, we investigated the effects of using nanomedicines on glycolysis and oxidative stress in tumor cells, which were mainly attributed to impaired glucose uptake and the presence of excess ROS. The ability of HMnO2@Bay to generate ·OH has been demonstrated by TMB as well as ESR and has shown excellent anti-tumor therapeutic potential in CCK-8 assays. Therefore, intracellular ·OH were determined using CLSM and flow cytometry. As illustrated in Fig. S6, FL intensity of the HMnO2 group, HMnO2@Bay group, and HMnO2@Bay + Cys group was significant, suggesting that it could exert excellent CDT therapeutic effects. A recent report on the characteristics of disulfide-induced cell death suggests that the depletion of intracellular NADPH, coupled with the significant accumulation of intracellular disulfide molecules (such as cysteine), can lead to the contraction of the cytoskeleton, thereby triggering disulfide cell death. Recent reports about the characterization of disulfidptosis propose that depletion of intracellular NADPH coupled with significant accumulation of intracellular disulfide molecules (e.g., cystine) leads to contraction of the cytoskeleton, which triggers disulfidptosis [7,10]. These reports are consistent with our findings. In contrast to the unfolded cytoskeleton in control group, A549 cells treated by HMnO2@Bay + Cys exhibited significant cytoskeletal contraction and condensation (Fig. 3E). However, when combined with TCEP treatment, this phenotype was partially reversed. We further employed non-reducing western blotting to validate that HMnO2@Bay can induce disulfide bond formation in the actin cytoskeletal protein through glucose deprivation. Fig. 3F revealed distinct migration patterns of Filamin A (FLNA) and Talin-1 (TLN1) on immunoblotting membranes following different treatments. Following treatment with HMnO2@Bay and HMnO2@Bay + Cys, the actin-binding proteins FLNA and TLN1 showed reduced electrophoretic mobility and the appearance of anomalous high-molecular-weight bands near the stacking gel, implying the formation of intermolecular disulfide bonds (Fig. S7). These results can be attributed to the following three factors. First, tumor cells maintain redox homeostasis and cell survival in response to ROS generated by HMnO2@Bay-mediated CDT via xCT-SCL7A11-mediated cystine uptake. Fig. 3G showed that the uptake efficiency of cystine was significantly higher in cells treated with HMnO2@Bay than in cells treated with PBS. A significant decrease in intracellular NADPH resulted from the inhibition of glucose uptake by Bay, following its release from the degrading HMnO2 (Fig. 3H and I, and S8). Depleted NADPH in the HMnO2@Bay + Cys group impeded the intracellular reduction of cystine to cysteine, thereby causing a pronounced decrease in cysteine availability (Fig. 3J). Next, we further investigated the intracellular redox balance. Compared to the PBS control, the HMnO2@Bay + Cys group showed a reduction in GSH levels and the most elevated GSSG/GSH ratio among all groups (Fig. S9A and B). These results suggest that HMnO2@Bay were able to effectively inhibit intracellular GSH levels in A549 cells by blocking the substrates for GSH synthesis as well as depleting endogenous GSH, which can be attributed to the blocked glucose uptake, the induction of disulfidptosis, and the glutathione peroxidase-like activity activity of HMnO2. The accumulation of nanoparticles in tumor cells induced the onset of disulfidptosis and significantly disturbed the intracellular redox homeostasis, thereby promoting tumor cell death and further enhancing the efficacy of CDT.

Anti-tumor mechanism of HMnO2@Bay + Cys using transcriptomics analysis

To further investigate the underlying therapeutic mechanisms of HMnO2@Bay + Cys, comprehensive transcriptomic analysis was performed using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), which enabled systematic investigation of mRNA expression patterns associated with the observed biological effects. A total of 1351 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the HMnO2@Bay + Cys and PBS groups through bioinformatic analysis, consisting of 702 downregulated and 652 upregulated genes (Fig. 4A and B). Analysis of gene expression patterns revealed distinct regulatory effects in A549 cells treated with HMnO2@Bay + Cys. As illustrated in Fig. 4C and D, the HMnO2@Bay + Cys group exhibited notable elevation in transcriptional levels of disulfidptosis-related genes, including SLC7A1, SLC7A11, and ACTG1P20, while showing significant suppression of GLUT1 expression. Utilizing the DEGs identified through comparison of HMnO2@Bay + Cys and the PBS groups, gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were subsequently conducted to interpret the biological implications of transcriptional alterations. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that HMnO2@Bay + Cys treatment regulated various signaling pathways related to the glucose metabolism of A549 cells, including Central carbon metabolism in cancer and Pl3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 4E). These biological processes are fundamentally involved in cellular nutrient processing, particularly in the assimilation and metabolic conversion of essential substrates such as glucose and glutamine [21]. Notably, under conditions of impaired glucose uptake, significant enrichment was observed in disulfidptosis-related processes and pathways associated with actin cytoskeleton organization and cellular adhesion mechanisms, such as focal adhesion and regulation of actin cytoskeleton signaling pathway [10]. The results from the GO enrichment analysis are in agreement with these findings (Fig. 4F). In addition, KEGG and GO enrichment analysis both revealed that biological processes and signaling pathways associated with death of tumor cells are determined to be significantly changed, such as the apoptosis signaling pathway, the TNF signaling pathway, the p53 signaling pathway, cellular response to oxidative stress and so on. In order to validate the above-mentioned results, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were utilized (Fig. 4G). The results suggested that HMnO2@Bay + Cys treatment significantly disrupted glycolytic pathways in tumor cells, leading to impaired NADPH biosynthesis. The subsequent depletion of intracellular NADPH levels disturbed sulfur metabolism, resulting in two critical consequences: reduced conversion of cystine to cysteine and consequent GSH biosynthesis deficiency, coupled with enhanced disulfide bond formation, thus promoting actin contraction. Notably, we observed that mitochondrial autophagy was recorded in the KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 5A). Therefore, we hypothesize that the antitumor mechanism of HMnO2@Bay may also be associated with mitochondrial autophagy.

To validate our hypothesis, we conducted a series of in vitro experiments, including immunofluorescence, western blot, bio-transmission electron microscopy (Bio-TEM), and flow cytometry analyses. Compared to the control group, HMnO2@Bay + Cys affected the expression of pathways associated with mitochondrial autophagy, with a normalized enrichment score (NES) for mitochondrial electro transport NADH to_ubiquinone pathway was −2.00, and the NES for regulation of mitochondrial memberane potential pathway was −1.73 (Fig. 5B and C). A decline in mitochondrial membrane potential serves as an early hallmark of mitophagy [6]. To assess changes in mitochondrial membrane potential in A549 cells after distinct treatments, the JC-1 fluorescent probe was employed (Fig. 5D). In the control group and the HMnO2 group, distinct red fluorescence was observed, which was attributed to the JC-1 fluorescent probe forming a polymer (J-aggregates) in the mitochondrial matrix under conditions of elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby emitting red fluorescence. In the HMnO2@Bay and HMnO2@Bay + Cys groups, red fluorescence was barely detectable, while green fluorescence increased significantly. This was due to the JC-1 fluorescent probe existing in its monomeric form within the mitochondrial matrix under conditions of low mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby emitting green fluorescence. Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were further validated by subsequent flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5E). In the HMnO2@Bay and HMnO2@Bay + Cys groups, A549 cells exhibited a tendency toward the lower right quadrant, suggesting a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential. Using Bio-TEM as the gold standard, we observed a rise in autophagosomes/lysosomes in treated A549 cells versus controls (Fig. 5F and G). Compared to other groups, HMnO2@Bay + Cys treatment triggered a more severe accumulation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes, indicating excessive autophagic activity (Fig. 5F and G). In addition, we examined the expression levels of Light Chain 3 (LC3) through western blot. LC3 serves as an autophagy marker protein primarily involved in autophagosome formation. Its precursor undergoes cleavage by the ATG4B enzyme to generate the cytoplasmic form LC3-I. Upon autophagy activation, LC3-I binds to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and converts to the membrane-bound form LC3-II. Consequently, the LC3-II/LC3-I conversion ratio is considered the gold standard for detecting mitophagy [22,23]. In the HMnO2@Bay + Cys group, A549 cells also exihibited the significant expression of LC3-II (Fig. 5H and S10). In summary, these findings indicate that mitophagy ultimately leads to abnormal lysosomal accumulation and dysfunction of mitophagy homeostasis, further validating the efficacy of CDT-disulfidptosis therapy in activating mitophagy (Fig. 5I).

Real-time FL/MRI imaging

Whole-body MRI/FL imaging was employed to profile the biodistribution of HMnO2@Bay, providing critical insights into its in vivo behavior before assessing therapeutic efficacy. T1-weighted MRI was conducted on A549 tumor-bearing mice to observe signal changes after intravenous administration of HMnO2@Bay. Fig. 6A revealed that the MRI signal gradually intensified over time. Owing to the accumulation of HMnO2@Bay in tumors via the EPR effect, the signal intensity reached maximum at 4 h post-injection (Fig. S11). Consequently, HMnO2@Bay nanoparticles demonstrated highly effective tumor targeting capability. Subsequently, FL imaging was carried out at 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection. As illustrated in Fig. 6B and C, while free Cy5.5 was rapidly cleared from circulation, HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5 maintained a longer half-life in vivo. At 24 h post-injection, the mice were euthanized, and key organs were harvested for subsequent biodistribution analysis. FL imaging demonstrated preferential accumulation of HMnO2@Bay in tumor sites (Fig. 6D and E). Additionally, strong intensities were detected in the liver and kidneys, implying that HMnO2@Bay could be metabolized and eliminated through hepatic and renal clearance pathways in vivo.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of HMnO2@Bay

Given the promising efficacy of HMnO2@Bay-mediated synergistic CDT and disulfidptosis in inducing cell mitophagy, we next evaluated whether this therapeutic effect could be replicated in A549 tumor-bearing nude mice. We conducted in vivo experiments following the treatment protocol outlined in Fig. 7A. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: PBS, HMnO2, Bay, and HMnO2@Bay. To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of HMnO2@Bay, we systematically assessed tumor growth inhibition by analyzing tumor volume over time, followed by measurement of excised tumor weights. As shown in Fig. 7B and C, the tumor growth of the HMnO2 and Bay groups displayed neutral suppression, while the HMnO2@Bay group exhibited better tumor inhibition due to the promising synergetic lung cancer CDT and disulfidptosis regimen. The therapeutic efficacy of HMnO2@Bay was further substantiated by digital images, which showed that it produced the smallest tumors among all groups in both mice and excised specimens (Fig. 7D and S12). Body weight remained comparable across all groups during the entire treatment period, implying no significant systemic toxicity (Fig. S13). To elucidate the tumor suppression mechanisms of HMnO2@Bay, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumor tissues was conducted at the end of treatment to evaluate antitumor efficacy of nanomaterials at the histological level. (Fig. 7E). H&E staining revealed the most severe tumor tissue damage in the HMnO2@Bay group, while the Bay and HMnO2 groups exhibited moderate tumor organ injury. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of TUNEL staining showed significant cellular apoptosis in HMnO2@Bay-treated tumors, as evidenced by the substantial increase in TUNEL-positive signals (red fluorescence) compared to PBS groups. Concurrently, IF of Ki67 showed markedly reduced proliferative activity in these tumors, with noticeably weaker green fluorescence intensity compared to PBS groups. These results collectively suggest that the HMnO2@Bay possesses dual capabilities of inducing programmed cell death while simultaneously inhibiting tumor cell multiplication, thereby validating its potent antitumor efficacy. To elucidate the mechanism of HMnO2@Bay, we employed IF staining to evaluate GLUT1 expression levels. A significant downregulation of GLUT1 was observed in the tumors treated by HMnO2@Bay, confirming the inhibitory role of the loaded Bay. Immunohistochemistry staining analysis of A549 tumors was performed to assess the expression of SLC7A11 (Fig. S14). Immunohistochemistry staining showed that SLC7A11 were significantly upregulated in tumors of the HMnO2@Bay group, which was owing to the HMnO2@Bay-mediated enhancing CDT. Decreased GLUT1 expression accompanied by impaired glucose uptake by tumor tissues, as well as increased SLC7A11 expression accompanied by increased cystine within tumor tissues, led to decreased tumor nutrient supply and disulfidptosis in the tumors. In addition, Fig. S15 showed that HMnO2@Bay treatment led to a significant accumulation of cystine, along with elevated glucose levels, while NADPH and GSH levels decreased, indicating inhibition of glycolysis and enhanced oxidative stress. The favorable in vivo antitumor efficacy of HMnO2@Bay can be attributed to the intrinsic properties of the hollow MnO2 architecture. This nanoplatform enabled pH/GSH-responsive drug release while simultaneously functioning as a Fenton-like nanocatalyst to generate ·OH for CDT and deplete intracellular GSH. Additionally, the released Mn2+ ions provided T1-weighted MRI imaging capability, allowing real-time biodistribution monitoring. Importantly, CDT, disulfidptosis, and mitophagy were integrated within this single nanoplatform. RNA-seq-guided discovery, together with both in vitro and in vivo mechanistic studies, revealed that the combined therapy activated mitophagy, which serves as an additional cell death mechanism beyond disulfidptosis alone. Therefore, the synergistic effects of CDT, disulfidptosis, and mitophagy collectively contribute to the potent tumor suppression observed in the HMnO2@Bay group.

Biosafety analysis of HMnO2@Bay

After administering various treatments to mice (including PBS, HMnO2, Bay, and HMnO2@Bay), a thorough biotoxicity evaluation was performed to investigate the biosafety of HMnO2@Bay. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidneys) were collected for H&E staining and histopathological assessment. Fig. 8A demonstrates that the HMnO2@Bay group did not exhibit significant organ damage compared to the PBS group. Additionally, assessment of blood biochemical parameters revealed normal hepatic and renal function in HMnO2@Bay-treated mice, with markers (AST, ALT, CREA, UREA) showing no significant deviation from the PBS group (Fig. 8B–D). These findings verify the high biocompatibility of the formulation and the success of the HMnO2 delivery system in attenuating biotoxicity of Bay.

Experimental section

Chemicals and cell culture

Bay876 and cystine-FITC probes were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), 2,7-Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), and DAPI were obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). A Calcein-AM/PI Double Stain Kit and SF594 Phalloidin was brought from Solarbio Reagent Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Glucose assay kit was bought from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). GSH assay kit and cysteine assay kit was bought from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). An Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit was obtained from KeyGEN BioTECH Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). Anti-LC3A/B Rabbit pAb and and β-actin Rabbit pAb were obtained from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The A549 lung cancer cells were bought from the BeNa Culture Collection (China).

Prognostic modeling analysis

Data on expression profiles and clinical information for lung cancer patients were gathered from the Cancer Genome Atlas database (https://www.cancer.gov/), and after excluding those without survival information, 508 samples were used for subsequent analysis. LUAD patients were randomly partitioned into training and test sets in a 70:30 ratio via the “caret” package in R studio. The disulfidptosis-related genes (DRG) were sourced from previous research and arranged into a matrix that includes survival details of LUAD patients.

Following construction using the training set, the model's performance was assessed on the separate test set. We employed LASSO regression analysis on the DRG matrix to streamline and filter variables, and subsequently built the prognostic model with COX regression, iterating the process 1000 times to determine the best gene features. A risk score was calculated for each lung cancer patient according to the expression levels of the identified prognostic genes. Patients were then dichotomized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median of these scores. The survival rates of patients in high-risk and low-risk groups were evaluated using the R package “survival”, and the reliability of the prognostic model was confirmed by ROC curves.

Using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), we built a prognostic gene-protein interaction network and imported it into Cytoscape software. The PPI network was then analyzed with the cytohubba plug-in to identify hub prognostic genes [24].The Spearman method was used to analyze the co-expression relationships of hub prognostic genes.

Synthesis of HMnO2

SiO2 nanoparticles were prepared by hydrolyzing tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 1 mL) in a mixture of deionized water (10 mL), ethanol (1.25 mL), and ammonium hydroxide (0.32 mL, 25%) under stirring for 72 h at room temperature. The product was washed (8000 g, 15 min) and stored in water. Subsequently, an aqueous KMnO4 solution (50 mg) was added to the SiO2 suspension (6.5 mg). Following ultrasonication, poly allylamine hydrochloride (PAH) was added dropwise until a light-yellow color appeared, and stirring continued in the dark for 24 h to form the MnO2 coating. PAH intercalates between MnO2 layers to maintain structural stability and prevent aggregation, while also balancing the negative charges on the MnO2 framework through its -NH3+Cl− ion pairs [25,26]. The silica core was then etched by stirring the SiO2@MnO2 nanoparticles (20 mg) in Na2CO3 solution (2 mM) at 60 °C for 12 h. After centrifugal washing (9000 g, 15 min) with water, hollow MnO2 (HMnO2) nanoparticles were obtained.

Synthesis of HMnO2@Bay and HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5

A DMSO solution of Bay876 was prepared and then used for drug loading. Specifically, 100 μL of Bay876 solution was added to 2 mL of HMnO2 nanoparticle suspension (1 mg/mL), and the mixture was incubated with gentle agitation for 12 h. Following centrifugation (5000 rpm, 5 min), the obtained HMnO2@Bay nanoparticles were washed multiple times with ultrapure water and ultimately redispersed in 2 mL of fresh ultrapure water via ultrasonication. For fluorescence labeling, the HMnO2@Bay suspension was mixed with Cy5.5 and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h with gentle stirring. The mixture was dialyzed for 12 h to remove unbound dye, followed by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min) and thorough washing with ultrapure water to obtain the final HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5 nanoparticles.

Characterization of HMnO2@Bay

The morphology of HMnO2@Bay was observed using a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan). XPS was performed on a ESCALAB QXi XPS Microprobe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). HRTEM images were obtained using a Talos F200X G2 TEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). UV-Vis absorption spectra of Bay876, HMnO2, and HMnO2@Bay were recorded by a spectrophotometer (UH5700, Hitachi, Japan). The hydrodynamic sizes and zeta potential of HMnO2 and HMnO2@Bay were detected by dynamic light scattering (Malvern Instruments, UK). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was determined utilizing electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, BRUKER EMXPLUS). Furthermore, T2-weighted magnetic resonance (MR) imaging was performed in vitro through a 3.0 T clinical scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany).

To assess the pH-dependent drug release, the release profile of Bay876 from HMnO2@Bay was evaluated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 and 5.5. Briefly, the nanoparticles were placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO: 1.0 kDa) and immersed in 30 mL of the respective PBS buffer under gentle agitation at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, 3 mL of the external release medium was sampled and replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer. The amount of Bay876 released was quantified by measuring its UV-Vis absorption.

HMnO2@Bay-mediated GSH exhaustion

To investigate Mn2+-mediated GSH consumption, HMnO2@Bay (1 mg/mL) in PBS (pH 5.5) was first incubated for 12 h. Following the addition and 1 h reaction with GSH (200 μL, 100 μM), the mixture was sequentially treated with NaOH (0.15 mol/L) and formaldehyde. After 2 min, DTNB (10 μmol/L) was added, and the resultant TNB was detected by its characteristic UV-Vis absorption at 412 nm.

Catalase activity assay

To investigate ·OH generation of HMnO2@Bay, TMB was utilized as a probe. Both Bay and HMnO2@Bay were treated with TMB in an acetic acid buffer (pH 5.5). Subsequently, H2O2 was added to above solution. After 1 h of reaction, the absorbance at 648 nm was measured to analyze the generation of ·OH.

Cell culture

The human lung cancer cell line A549 was acquired from the BeNa Culture Collection. Cells were propagated in F-12K medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2).

Cellular uptake

Cells were cultured on chambered coverglass (5 × 104 cells per well) for 12 h to perform subsequent cellular uptake experiments. Then, solutions of HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5 at a Cy5.5 concentration of 5 μg mL−1 were replaced with the origin F-12K culture medium, and cells were incubated for 1, 2, and 4 h. Subsequently, cells were washed with fresh PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI. Imaging was performed using a CLSM (Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

Cells were cultured in 24-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well) for 12 h to prepare for subsequent flow cytometry analysis. Then, cells were treated with HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5 for the designed time (1, 2, and 4 h). Finally, the cells were harvested and collected to evaluate the cellular internalization of HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5 using flow cytometer (FACSCanto II, BD Biosciences, USA).

Cell viability and death assays

CCK-8 assays were performed to investigate cytotoxicity. Cells were planted in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well). After 12-h incubation, the original culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing Bay or HMnO2@Bay (Bay dose: 0–40 ng/mL, HMnO2 dose: 4 μg/ml) with or without addition of cystine (5 mM). For the Bay + Cys and HMnO2@Bay + Cys treatment groups, free cystine was directly added to the culture medium simultaneously with Bay or HMnO2@Bay. The purpose of exogenous Cys supplementation was to enhance the substrate supply for SLC7A11, thereby facilitating disulfidptosis under glucose-deprived and NADPH-depleted conditions [10,27,28]. Cell viability was assessed after 24 h of incubation using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (BioTek, USA). For live/dead staining, cells were treated with a Calcein-AM/PI double-staining kit following various interventions, and the fluorescence images of live/dead cells were captured using an EVOS FL microscope (Thermo Scientific, USA).

For apoptotic cell death detection, apoptosis detection kit was used to investigate the apoptotic status of cells in the different experimental groups. Subsequently, the apoptosis rate of stained cells from each group was then quantified by flow cytometry.

Intracellular generation of ·OH

The intracellular ROS generation was assessed using a hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF) probe. Cells were plated in chambered coverglass at 5 × 104 cells per well, cultured for 12 h, and subsequently incubated for 24 h with the respective agents, including PBS, HMnO2, Bay, Bay + Cys, HMnO2@Bay, and HMnO2@Bay + Cys. After that, the cells were stained by HPF, and images of stained cells were captured using CLSM. Additionally, the intracellular ·OH levels of cells with distinct treatments were detected using the flow cytometry.

Cytoskeletal protein observation

Actin Tracking Stains were used to stain the F-actin of A549 cells. The cells were washed with PBS and subsequently incubated with Actin Tracking Stains for 30 min. The images of changes in cytoskeletal protein of the cells were captured by CLSM.

Glucose, NADPH, cysteine, and GSH level detection

The cells were cultured in 6-well plate (1 × 106 cells per well) for 12h, and then treated with PBS, Bay, Bay + Cys, HMnO2@Bay, and HMnO2@Bay + Cys for 24 h. After that, the original F-12K medium was harvested. Cells were then washed with PBS and detached using trypsin for collection. Extracellular glucose concentration was evaluated by a glucose assay kit following the manufacturer's directions. Intracellular NADPH, cysteine, and GSH levels were measured using a NADP+/NADPH assay kit, a cysteine assay kit, a GSH assay kit following the manufacturer's directions.

Measurement of cystine uptake

The cells were cultured in 6-well plate (1 × 106 cells per well) for 12h, and then various groups including PBS, HMnO2, Bay, and HMnO2@Bay groups were incubated with 5 μM cystine-FITC probe for 4 h. After that, cells were washed using PBS, harvested by trypsinization, and subjected to flow cytometric analysis to quantify cystine uptake.

RNA sequencing

The A549 cells were treated by PBS and HMnO2@Bay + Cys for 24h, and their RNA were was isolated using Trizol reagent for RNA sequencing according to the requirement of Novogene, Inc. (Beijing, China).

Western blot

Treated A549 cells were collected, lysed in RIPA buffer, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm to collect the protein supernatant. Following separation by SDS-PAGE and transfer to PVDF membranes, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (1 h, room temperature). They were then successively incubated with primary antibodies (overnight, 4 °C) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (0.5 h, 37 °C). Signal visualization was performed using a chemiluminescence imaging system (ChemiScope 6000, Clinx, China).

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Cells were plated in 6-well plates and treated by PBS, HMnO2, Bay, Bay + Cys, HMnO2@Bay, or HMnO2@Bay + Cys. Following treatment, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were incubated with 1 mL of JC-1 staining solution for 20 min. After removing the staining solution, the cells were washed with PBS and subsequently observed under a CLSM. In addition, quantitative analysis was performed using flow cytometry.

Establishment of the tumor model

BALB/c female nude mice (6-8 weeks old) were procured from BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (Zhuhai, China). To establish xenograft models, A549 lung cancer cells (5 × 106 cells suspended in 100 μL PBS) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right dorsal flank of each mouse. Treatment protocols were initiated when the tumors reached an average volume of approximately 80 mm3, as measured by digital calipers. All experiments complied with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Medical University (Permit No. SYXK 2019-0213). The procedures were conducted at the University's Experimental Animal Center.

In vivo FL/MRI imaging

To dynamically monitor the tumor accumulation and retention of the nanoparticles, in vivo MRI was performed on A549 tumor-bearing mice following intravenous injection of HMnO2@Bay. The mice were scanned using a MR scanner at predetermined time points post-injection. For FL imaging, mice with a volume of about 200 mm3 were randomly divided into groups (n = 3) and intravenously injected with free Cy5.5 (2 mg kg−1) or HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5. The in vivo biodistribution of HMnO2@Bay@Cy5.5 was then monitored at predetermined time points (4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h) using an IVIS Lumina II optical imaging system (Caliper, USA). Finally, excised tumors and major organs were subjected to ex vivo imaging for quantitative analysis of FL signals.

Synergistic CDT and disulfidptosis therapy

In previous study, physiological levels of cystine were sufficient to trigger effective disulfidptosis without the need for exogenous cystine supplementation. Therefore, only four groups were included in the in vivo experiment [10,28]. Specifically, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group) receiving intravenous administration: PBS, HMnO2, Bay, and HMnO2@Bay (HMnO2: 0.5 mg/kg, Bay: 7.5 mg/kg). During the 24-day experiment, tumor volume and animal body weight were recorded every other day. At the study terminus, mice were euthanized and tumors were harvested for further analysis. NADPH (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd, China), and GSH (UpingBioHANGZHOU, China) levels in tissues were measured using commercially available kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. Terminal necropsy yielded tumor specimens for histopathological examination via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Immunofluorescent staining with TUNEL, Ki-67, and GLUT1 and immunohistochemistry staining with SLC7A11 in tumors was further performed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy in vivo.

In vivo safety assessment

At the experimental endpoint (24 days post-treatment), mice were euthanized for blood collection. The serum was subsequently analyzed for key clinical biochemical markers of hepatic and renal function, including ALT, AST, CRE, and BUN. The major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were excised and subjected to histological evaluation through H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using the Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons across multiple groups.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully designed a multifunctional Mn-based nanoplatform, HMnO2@Bay, that integrates self-sustained ROS generation, glucose metabolism inhibition, and SLC7A11-targeted disulfidptosis induction for synergistic anti-tumor therapy against NSCLC. The nanoplatform demonstrates GSH/pH-responsive degradation in the acidic TME, releasing Mn2+ ions for enhanced Fenton-like catalytic activity and Bay876 for selective GLUT1 blockade. This dual action disrupts both redox and metabolic homeostasis, leading to NADPH depletion, cystine accumulation, and subsequent disulfidptosis via the SLC7A11 pathway. Furthermore, the sustained oxidative stress and metabolic suppression synergistically trigger mitophagy, resulting in irreversible mitochondrial damage and tumor cell death. Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirm the potent antitumor efficacy and favorable biosafety of HMnO2@Bay. This work presents a novel therapeutic strategy that concurrently targets multiple vulnerabilities of tumor cells, offering a promising translational approach for the treatment of NSCLC and potentially other malignancies characterized by SLC7A11 overexpression and metabolic dysregulation.
