Lost in Aggregation: The Causal Interpretation of the IV Estimand
Danielle Tsao 1 , Krikamol Muandet 2 , Frederick Eberhardt 3 , Emilija Perković 1 1 University of Washington, Seattle, USA 2 CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security, Saarbrücken, Germany 3 California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, USA dltsao@uw.edu

Abstract
Instrumental variable based estimation of a causal effect has emerged as a standard approach to mitigate confounding bias in the social sciences and epidemiology, where conducting randomized experiments can be too costly or impossible. However, justifying the validity of the instrument often poses a significant challenge. In this work, we highlight a problem generally neglected in arguments for instrumental variable validity: the presence of an “aggregate treatment variable”, where the treatment (e.g., education, GDP, caloric intake) is composed of finer-grained components that each may have a different effect on the outcome. We show that the causal effect of an aggregate treatment is generally ambiguous, as it depends on how interventions on the aggregate are instantiated at the component level, formalized through the aggregate-constrained component intervention distribution. We then characterize conditions on the interventional distribution and the aggregate setting under which standard instrumental variable estimators identify the aggregate effect. The contrived nature of these conditions implies major limitations on the interpretation of instrumental variable estimates based on aggregate treatments and highlights the need for a broader justificatory base for the exclusion restriction in such settings.



Keywords:
Aggregate treatments, Causal inference, Exclusion restriction, Instrumental variables, Structural causal models, Two-stage least squares

1
Introduction

Instrumental variables (IV) allow practitioners to mitigate confounding bias when estimating the causal effect of a treatment
A
on some outcome
Y
from observational data alone. The technique is widely used across disciplines, notably in the social sciences
(
3
;
1
)
and epidemiology
(
24
;
22
)
, where randomized experiments can be too costly or even impossible to conduct.
IV estimation relies on a random variable
I
, called an instrument, that stands in a particular relationship to the treatment and outcome:
I
is associated with the treatment
A
(relevance), but not with the outcome
Y
(exchangeability), except through its relationship with
A
(exclusion restriction) (
25
).

Famously,
3
use the quarter-of-birth
as an instrument to estimate the effect of education on wages.
1
use the national savings rate as an instrument to estimate the causal effect of gross domestic product (GDP) on the degree of democracy of a country.
24
uses air quality as an instrument for estimating the causal effect of caloric intake on body mass index (BMI).
22
, in turn, use particular genotypes as instruments for estimating the causal effect of body mass index (BMI) on ischemic heart disease.
While the relevance condition can be tested explicitly, exclusion and exchangeability are not testable unless one makes additional assumptions about the IV set-up; see, e.g., the approaches by
17
;
9
. As a result, establishing the validity of the instrument constitutes a substantive epistemic challenge, grounded in expert knowledge and background assumptions, and often highly contested; see, e.g.,
7
and
8
with respect to the analysis in
2
.

In the above examples, the treatment (education, GDP, caloric intake and BMI, respectively) is an aggregate of many finer-grained quantities into a single univariate variable, where the individual components can have markedly different effects on the outcome. For example, as
11
note,
education
, measured in units of time, summarizes many different types of education in different domains, which very plausibly have different influences on future income.
Given that the IV estimates are used to inform real-world interventions, two important questions arise:

(a)

What is the nature of the causal effect of interest when the treatment is an aggregate?, and

(b)

Under what circumstances does the classical IV estimator identify this aggregate causal effect?

The first question relates closely to fundamental assumptions about the nature of causal effects used in the literature on potential outcomes. Originally articulated as part of the
single unit treatment value assumption
(SUTVA),
26
requires for the estimation of a causal effect that “the value of
Y
for unit
u

when exposed to treatment
t
will be the same no matter what mechanism is used to assign treatment
t
to unit
u
[…]”.
35
isolates the concern more precisely in what he calls
treatment variation invariance
, namely, that the potential outcome takes the same value irrespective of what means are used to instantiate the cause. While VanderWeele considers different protocols of instantiating the treatment (such as different ways of performing medical surgery), the point similarly applies to aggregate treatments where an aggregate treatment value may be instantiated by a variety of different configurations of its components. In many social science settings where IV estimation techniques are used, it is highly plausible that treatment variation invariance is violated.

The second question connects back to the literature in economic theory on aggregation bias, which investigates the mismatch between estimands for macro-relations and aggregated estimands for the corresponding micro-relations in the context of common estimation methods such as
ordinary least squares (OLS)
(
32
;
21
)
and two-stage least squares (2SLS)
(
33
)
. As this literature highights, the relation between aggregate and components has to be highly constrained in order to attribute meaningful causal effects to aggregate treatments.

We make the following contributions: We formalize the linear IV setting with an aggregate treatment in Section
2
and derive the standard IV estimand applied to this setting. In Section
3
we then motivate and justify what we take to be the ground-truth causal effect of the aggregate treatment on the outcome that would provide a suitable basis for policy advice, addressing the first question above.
Section
4
then identifies what additional assumptions about the aggregate setting are necessary in order for the IV estimand to correctly identify the aggregate causal effect, addressing the second question. We show that there are two such scenarios: proportional aggregation (Section
4.1
) and instrument-tuned interventions (Section
4.2
). Proportional aggregation amounts roughly to the assumption that either each component variable has the same effect on the outcome, or, that the effect on the outcome is mediated by the aggregate treatment. That is, proportionality renders the aggregation claim close to trivial. In contrast, instrument-tuned interventions require a highly contrived intervention policy, which is implausible in real-world practical cases.

In Section
4.3
we show how IV estimates diverge from the target aggregate causal effect when the particular assumptions of Sections
4.1
and
4.2
are not satisfied.
In Section
5
we connect these findings to the exclusion restriction for IV validity to show that these circumstances are untestable in practice and that, consequently, assumptions about aggregation need to be addressed when the exclusion assumption is justified for a domain of application. We also argue that standard techniques to mitigate violations of the exclusion restriction will in general not apply in this aggregate setting. Finally, in Section
6
we consider several related IV settings.
We conclude with a strong note of caution: If IV estimates that involve aggregate treatment variables are taken to provide sound policy advice, then rather strong assumptions about the nature of the aggregation or the policy intervention have to be justified.

The Aggregate Setting

Consider the setting illustrated in Figure
1
: From the researcher’s perspective (top) it is a standard IV setting. To estimate the causal effect of treatment
A
on outcome
Y
the researcher uses an instrumental variable
I
to mitigate the potential confounding due to unobserved variable
U
where
I
only directly causally affects
A
and is not confounded with
Y
. However, suppose that (unbeknownst to the researcher) treatment
A
is made up of
k>1
components
A_{1},\ldots A_{k}
that may be subject to different influences
\delta_{j}
and
\gamma_{a_{j}}
from the instrument
I
and confounder
U
, respectively, and that may have different effects
\beta_{j}
on outcome
Y
(bottom). That is, for the linear case the data generating process is given by the following structural causal model (SCM):

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\leftarrow\epsilon_{u}\quad I\leftarrow\epsilon_{i}

(1a)

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\delta_{j}I+\gamma_{a_{j}}U+\epsilon_{a_{j}},\quad j\in\{1,...,k\}

(1b)

\displaystyle A\-\

\displaystyle=\-\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}A_{j}

(1c)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\leftarrow\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}A_{j}+\gamma_{y}U+\epsilon_{y}.

(1d)

Intercepts are omitted for simplicity and without loss of generality. The relation (
1c
) between
A
and its components
A_{j}
is stated as an equation, rather than as a causal effect, indicating that this relation is definitional or constitutive, rather than causal, and consequently cannot be broken by experimental intervention. Following
11
, we refer to (
1c
) as the
aggregation rule.
For simplicity we consider the aggregation rule to be a simple weighted linear sum, and that the error terms
\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{y},\epsilon_{a_{j}}
for
j\in\{1\ldots,k\}
are mutually independent with mean zero and finite variance.

Given that the aggregate setting is linear with a single instrument, all standard IV estimation techniques, such as two-stage least squares (2SLS)
(
31
;
4
)
and indirect least squares
(
13
;
2
)
, target the same estimand
(
10
)
. We denote this general linear IV estimand as
\beta_{\text{IV}}
.
Applying 2SLS, for example, to derive
\beta_{\text{IV}}
in the aggregate setting amounts to first regressing
A
on
I
to obtain linear predictor
\tilde{A}
of
A
, and then regressing
Y
on
\tilde{A}
to obtain the estimand.
For the aggregate setting this implies:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}=\frac{\text{cov}(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}A_{j},I)}{\text{var}(I)}I,

(2)

and hence

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{IV}}=\frac{\text{cov}(Y,\tilde{A})}{\text{var}(\tilde{A})}=\frac{\text{cov}(Y,I)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\text{cov}(A_{j},I)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}\delta_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\delta_{j}}.

(3)

For a standard IV model as in the top of Figure
1
,
\beta_{\text{IV}}

is interpreted as the causal effect of the treatment on the outcome (defined formally in Section
3
).
However, for the aggregate setting at the bottom of Figure
1
, it is unclear which interventions (if any) correspond to this estimand. In particular, the intervention on
A
has to be realized in its components with possibly many ways of instantiating a single value.

3
The Causal Effect of an Aggregate Treatment

We use the do-notation
\text{do}(A=a)
of
23
to denote the intervention that sets the value of treatment
A
to
a
. In linear models, the target causal effect is normally defined as the expected change in outcome
Y
resulting from a unit change of the intervention on the treatment
A
:

\displaystyle\E[Y\,|\,\text{do}(A=a+1)]-\E[Y\,|\,\text{do}(A=a)].

(4)

This type of causal effect is often referred to as the average causal effect, or the average treatment effect
(
16
)
. However, given that
A
is an aggregate, an intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
can be instantiated by many combinations of values for the components
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
, with each satisfying the aggregation rule (
1c
). The same applies to
\text{do}(A=a+1)
, which implies that a unit change in
A
may involve more (or less) than a unit change in any one
A_{j}
.

29
refer to this type of effect as
ambiguous
. The effect is not well-defined as it is dependent on the particular values of the component
A_{j}
’s that instantiate the intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
, or in other words, it violates treatment variation invariance
(
35
)
.

In addition to this value-ambiguity of the components, one has to determine whether under the intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
the values of the components
A_{j}
are fixed by interventions
\text{do}(A_{j})
that make the
A_{j}
independent of their causal parents (here represented by
I
and
U
), or whether
\text{do}(A=a)
merely enforces the aggregation rule on the values of the
A_{j}
without making the
A_{j}
independent of their causal parents. Both of these considerations highlight the need for an explicit distribution relating the intervention on the aggregate
A
to the change of its components
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
.

3.1
Aggregate-constrained Component Intervention Distribution (ACID) and the Aggregate Causal Effect

For a given intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
on the aggregate
A
, we introduce the
aggregate-constrained component intervention distribution (ACID)
to describe how the intervention is instantiated in terms of the components
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
, and how the components’ relations to all other variables in the causal system change under intervention.
That is, for
\mathbf{V}=\{I,U,A_{1},...,A_{k},Y\}
, the ACID is given by an interventional distribution:

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{v})_{\text{do}(A=a)}=P(i,u,a_{1},...,a_{k},y\ |\ \text{do}(A=a)).

(5)

We emphasize that
any
causal claim about
any
aggregate treatment presupposes an ACID, even if it is generally left implicit.

The following three common desiderata, discussed in more detail in Section
3.3
, strike us as essential assumptions about an ACID in the context of IV estimation of linear causal effects:

Surgicality

-
The intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
is instantiated in terms of (possibly correlated) interventions on the
A_{j}
that make the
A_{j}
independent of their observational causes (
I
and
U
).

Aggregation Restriction

-
\text{do}(A=a)

satisfies the aggregation rule, i.e. the ACID lacks support for any combination of values of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
that do not satisfy the aggregation rule (
1c
).

Value Independence

-
The causal effect of treatment
A
on
Y
is independent of the particular value
a
that
A
is set to in the intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
. So,
for all
a,a^{\prime}
that can be assigned:

\displaystyle\E[Y|\text{do}(A=a+1)]-\E[Y|\text{do}(A=a)]=\E[Y|\text{do}(A=a^{\prime}+1)]-\E[Y|\text{do}(A=a^{\prime})].

An ACID that satisfies all three desiderata is henceforth referred to as a
valid ACID
.
A valid ACID in (
5
) will factor in the following way:

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{v})_{\text{do}(A=a)}

\displaystyle=P(i,u|do(A=a))P(a_{1},...,a_{k}|i,u,do(A=a))P(y|a_{1},...,a_{k},i,u,do(A=a))

(6a)

\displaystyle=P(i,u)P(a_{1},...,a_{k}|do(A=a))P(y\ |a_{1},...,a_{k},u)

(6b)

\displaystyle=P(i)P(u)P^{*}(a_{1},...,a_{k};a)P(y\ |a_{1},...,a_{k},u),

(6c)

where we use the chain rule and the
Surgicality
assumption to transform the interventional distributions into their observational counterparts.
Note that the intervention value
a
is a parameter of
P^{*}
because the
Aggregation Restriction
must be satisfied for
a
.
Since the
P^{*}
-term marks the crucial (and only) difference between the ACID and the observational distribution of
\mathbf{V}
, we will refer to both (
5
) and the specific
P^{*}
distribution as the ACID, as long as it is clear from context.

The SCM corresponding to an intervention on the aggregate
A
for the system described by (
1
) is then given by:

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\leftarrow\epsilon_{u}\quad I\leftarrow\epsilon_{i}

(7a)

\displaystyle(A_{1},...,A_{k})

\displaystyle\sim P^{*}(a_{1},...,a_{k};a)

(7b)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\leftarrow\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}A_{j}+\gamma_{y}U+\epsilon_{y}.

(7c)

Furthermore,
\E[Y\,|\,\text{do}(A=a)]
is now computed as

\displaystyle\E[Y\,|\,\text{do}(A=a)]

\displaystyle={\int}(\beta_{1}a_{1}+...+\beta_{k}a_{k})dP^{*}(a_{1},...,a_{k};a)

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int\beta_{j}a_{j}dP^{*}(a_{j};a),

(9)

where
U
and
\epsilon_{y}
can be dropped from the calculation.
Then for any
P^{*}
that in addition satisfies
Value Independence
, we have a well-defined
aggregate causal effect
as follows.

Definition 1
(Aggregate Causal Effect)

The causal effect of an aggregate treatment
A
on an outcome
Y
, referred to as the aggregate causal effect and denoted by
\text{ACE}(A,Y)
, is taken to be:

\displaystyle\text{ACE}(A,Y)

\displaystyle=\E[Y\,|\,\text{do}(A=a+1)]-\E[Y\,\,|\text{do}(A=a)],

(10)

where the expectations of
Y
are computed as in (
9
) under a valid ACID.

For a non-aggregate treatment
A
, the aggregate causal effect corresponds to the standard average causal effect
(
16
)
.

With the aggregate causal effect defined, we can now formulate our second question of the introduction precisely:
Under what conditions does an investigator facing the aggregate setting in Figure
1
, where the
A_{j}
are unobserved, correctly identify the aggregate causal effect in (
10
) using classic IV estimation applied to the aggregate
A
?
More precisely, what are the constraints on SCM (
1
) and its ACID in (
7b
) such that the aggregate causal effect corresponds to the IV estimand
\beta_{\text{IV}}
in (
3
)?

3.2
A Gaussian ACID Example

Consider an ACID
P^{*}
that, given intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
on the aggregate
A
, sets the values of the components
A_{1},...,A_{k}
according to a multivariate
Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}\\
\vdots\\
A_{k}\end{bmatrix}\sim N_{k}\left(\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}+ad_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{k}+ad_{k}\end{bmatrix}\hskip-2.84526pt,\mathit{\Sigma}\right),

(11)

where to satisfy the
Aggregation Restriction

the constants
c_{j},d_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
need to satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}c_{j}=0,\quad\kern 5.0pt\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}d_{j}=1,

(12)

as well as the following constraint on the covariance matrix for
\mathbf{\alpha}:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})^{\top}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\alpha}^{\top}\mathit{\Sigma}=0,\text{where of course $\mathit{\Sigma}\succeq 0$.}

(13)

Surgicality
of this ACID is satisfied by construction when the factorizations in (
6
) are satisfied, and

Value Independence
is satisfied
as seen by the fact that

\displaystyle\eqref{def:aggcausaleff2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int\beta_{j}a_{j}dP^{*}(a_{j};a)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}c_{j}+a\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}d_{j}

(14)

and hence by (
10
),

\displaystyle\text{ACE}(A,Y)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}d_{j},

(15)

which no longer depends on
a
.

In Appendix
B.3
we show how the distribution in (
11
) can also, if desired, be parameterized to match the first and second moments of the
observational distribution over
(A_{1},...,A_{k})^{\top}
conditional on the aggregate
A=a
.
Note from (
15
) that the ACE does not depend on the choice of
\mathit{\Sigma}
beyond (
13
) and thus also holds for the deterministic case where
\mathit{\Sigma}=0
.

3.3
Discussion of the ACID Assumptions

While the assumption of
Surgicality

is quite standard
(
23
)
, it is a strong assumption in the current context: It requires that an intervention on the aggregate amounts to a surgical intervention on each component even though the experimenter may only have direct control over the aggregate. However, any weakening of this desideratum risks mistaking confounding for a causal effect. To take one extreme, if the intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
merely amounted to conditioning on the aggregation rule, i.e,

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{v})_{\text{do}(A=a)}=P(i,u,a_{1},...,a_{k},y\ |\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}a_{j}=a)

(16)

then intervening on an aggregate would be equivalent to Monte Carlo sampling. In other words, setting
A=a
would amount to waiting for the observational distribution to bring about suitable values of
A_{j}
such that the aggregation rule is satisfied. But that mistakes a sampling technique for an experimental intervention. Moreover, if the
A_{j}
had no causal effect on
Y
(i.e.,
\beta_{j}=0;j\in\{1,...,k\}
), but there was confounding between the
A_{j}
and
Y
, then such an ACID would incorrectly suggest that there is an aggregate causal effect of
A
on
Y
when there is no directed causal path from
A
or its components to
Y
.
While this does not apply to our linear Gaussian setting, for an “observationally-tuned” ACID it may be possible that the observational distribution has no support for any value combination of the components
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
that satisfies the aggregation rule for the intervened value
a
. Hence, making such out-of-distribution
interventions impossible, when those are often the motivation for causal estimation in the first place.

We acknowledge that there may be circumstances where an intervention can be appropriately defined that does not
fully
sever the relationships of the
A_{j}
with
U
and
I
, but we note that many of the above concerns can be generalized if confounding influences are not broken by the intervention.

Satisfying the
Aggregation Restriction
ensures that the aggregate variable being considered under intervention is in fact the same as that measured under observation. Without this requirement the aggregate causal effect would be highly underdetermined, since the observational relation no longer constrains the interventional one. There may be specific circumstances where modeling a change in the constitutive relations between the
A_{j}
and
A
is appropriate. For example, when a manager wants to increase overall sales and instantiates that by changing the composition of the underlying product palette. In that case the intervention includes a change in the aggregation weights
\alpha_{j}
. But this example already indicates that under such circumstances, any inference from previous observational data of the aggregate is likely to provide only very limited guidance about the effect of the intervention unless the disaggregated product categories were already tracked in the first place (in contrast to our setting where the
A_{j}
are unobserved). Given our focus on IV estimation in observational data, we leave such examples aside here. We note that we assume that the aggregation rule is satisfied for each interventional datapoint, not merely in expectation of the interventional distribution, though none of our results hinge on this assumption.

Finally, we focus on causal effects that satisfy
Value Independence
of the particular intervention value because we are interested in the possible interpretations of classic linear causal model assumptions
where the estimated causal effect is reported in terms of a single parameter that specifies the causal effect independently of the intervention value.
Unsurprisingly, this demand for value independence of the causal effect is a particularly strong assumption and imposes strong limitations on the ACIDs that can be considered.

Related Work

34

also consider variations in how a treatment is instantiated and offer two settings. In their Section 4 they define their causal effect using a known pre-specified distribution over the different versions of the treatment. This distribution is what we refer to as the ACID. The causal effect is then a mixture of the effects of the components according to this distribution. In their Section 5, which more explicitly considers aggregate treatments, they consider as their ACID the observational distribution over the components conditional on the aggregation rule being satisfied. A similar approach is taken by
39
. As we discussed in the context of such an ACID in (
16
), this approach requires some care in ensuring that the interventional value is within the observational support, and that the intervention on the components still breaks confounding. In Appendix
B.3
we consider such an ACID explicitly. Neither of these articles consider the conditions under which such causal effects can be accurately estimated using IV techniques.

11
also consider aggregates of component treatments, but do not give a full causal interpretation of the aggregate causal effect. Instead they consider various types of averages over the effects of the components as their target of estimation. If these averages are to be understood as a marginal causal effect that can inform policy, then their implicit ACID is the distribution of weights in their averages.
11
note that the issue of defining aggregate causal effects needs to be distinguished from treatment effect heterogeneity, which usually refers to different responses to treatment across (subpopulations of) units. In contrast, for aggregate causal effects the variation arises due to different instantiations of the same treatment value
within
a unit. Formally we agree with this distinction, but in practice it is less clear, especially if the subpopulations are not known or sharply delineated: On the standard view of heterogeneous treatment effects we have a population of units that (in fact) divide into subpopulations with different causal effects between subpopulations, but the same effect within each subpopulation. One can view the aggregate setting as modeling a population of units where the causal effect of the treatment is an average across units, but each individual may instantiate the treatment differently in its components. In many ways the aggregate setting we describe can provide an explanation for heterogeneous treatment effects
because
different subpopulations cluster units that instantiate the (aggregate) treatment differently in their components.

The approach in
5
most closely resembles ours here in that they explicitly denote the ACID describing the relation between intervened aggregate and its components as their
intervention distribution
, but they do not relate their formal framework to effect estimation.

4
When Do IVs Correctly Identify the Aggregate Causal Effect?

We next consider settings where the IV estimand may correctly identify the causal effect. The first of these, which we refer to as
proportional aggregation
, does not depend on the specific form of the ACID; any valid ACID will do. We explore this setting in Section
4.1
below.

Next, using the Gaussian ACID from Section
3.2
, we show that there are specific ACIDs that ensure that the IV estimand corresponds to the aggregate causal effect. We refer to these settings
as
instrument-tuned interventions
and explore them in Section
4.2
.

4.1
Proportional Aggregation

To motivate the case of proportional aggregation, consider the aggregate setting with
\beta_{j}=\tau
and
\alpha_{j}=1
for all
j
. In this case, the components
A_{j}
simply split the aggregate
A
into parts that have the same causal effect
\tau
on
Y
and so it does not matter how an intervention
\text{do}(A=a)
is instantiated
in terms of its components. A generalization of this case is what we refer to as
proportional aggregation
, where

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}=\cdots=\frac{\beta_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}=\vcentcolon\tau.

In this case the IV estimand in (
3
) is

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{IV}}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tau\alpha_{j}\delta_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\delta_{j}}=\tau,

and the aggregate causal effect under proportional aggregation is also

\displaystyle\text{ACE}(A,Y)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\tau\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}A_{j}\ \bigg|\ \text{do}\big(A=a+1\big)\right]-\E\left[\tau\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}A_{j}\ \bigg|\ \text{do}\big(A=a\big)\right]

(17a)

\displaystyle=\tau(a+1)-\tau a=\tau.

(17b)

Notice that in this setting, as long as the aggregation rule is respected, any valid ACID can be assumed to relate the intervention on the aggregate
A
to its components
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
.

There are several ways to interpret the assumption of proportional aggregation. In the simplest case where
\alpha_{j}=c
for some constant
c
, it just means that all components of
A
have the same causal effect on
Y
. More generally, proportional aggregation amounts to an assumption that the components have the same causal effect once rescaled by their share of the aggregate. Alternatively, since under proportional aggregation
A
(with
U
) screens off the
A_{j}
from
Y
(i.e.
A_{j}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|A,U
), the aggregate
A
can also be interpreted as mediating all the causal effects from
A_{j}
to
Y
. So, instead of
Y
being an effect of the
A_{j}
(and the confounder) in SCM (
1
), we would have:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\leftarrow{\tau}A+\gamma_{y}U+\epsilon_{y}.

(18)

This is a common way of interpreting aggregate treatments: the effect of the components is mediated by the aggregate.
14
take this route, as do the approaches that develop “Bartik instruments” (see e.g.
15
). In all these cases there only is a causal effect between the aggregate and the outcome, but no component causal effects as the
\beta_{j}
in our setting.

Thus, the assumption of proportional aggregation offers an interpretation of the aggregate setting in Figure
1
that would ensure that the IV estimate can support a policy intervention no matter how the intervention on the aggregate is instantiated in the components. Using our example from the introduction, proportional aggregation would imply that while
education
is made up of many different types of learning, for the assessment of the effect on future wages, one can interpret the IV analysis as assuming that the overall duration of education is the relevant cause, while the specific types of education are irrelevant. Formulated in this way it becomes obvious that this is a rather strong assumption about the nature of the causal effects of different types of education that a student experiences in years K-12: the claim would be that it doesn’t matter to future wages whether one adds extra time in kindergarten or in highschool, both would be equally beneficial.

5
The Exclusion Assumption and its Justifications

We have shown that unless proportional aggregation is satisfied or a valid ACID suitable for an instrument-tuned intervention is provided, the difference between the IV estimate of the causal effect and the aggregate causal effect is unbounded. What are the implications for IV studies that involve aggregate treatment variables?

It is tempting to see the aggregation problem as another variation of the ways in which the exclusion restriction
can be violated, and to propose the corresponding remedies. The exclusion restriction requires that the instrument
I
is independent of the outcome
Y
given the treatment
A
and confounder
U
. In our setting, while
I\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|A_{1},...,A_{k},U
, the exclusion restriction is indeed violated with respect to the aggregate treatment, since we have that
I\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|A,U
for
A=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}A_{j}
with arbitrary
\alpha_{j}
. This is a familiar problem that can also occur when a continuous treatment variable is discretized. Moreover, the Gaussian observational joint distribution over
I,U,A,Y
in the aggregation setting of Figure
1
(SCM (
1
)) is equivalent to the Gaussian observational joint distribution of the causal system in Figure
3
(SCM (
21
) below) that violates the exclusion restriction by having an extra edge for a direct effect of the instrument on the outcome, as reflected by the following result. See Appendix
D
for the proof.

Consider SCM (

In the case of proportional aggregation (where

However, for any other choice of non-zero coefficients of the aggregation setting, the exclusion restriction violation persists. In particular, even though
\beta_{\text{IV}}=\text{ACE}(A,Y)
under an instrument-tuned intervention, the observational setting still violates the exclusion restriction with respect to the aggregate treatment. The ACIDs derived in Section
4.2
are just chosen in such a way that the IV estimand coincides with the aggregate causal effect.

This point highlights the crux of considering existing remedies that address the exclusion restriction violations in other contexts:
The adjustment that is needed to ensure that the IV estimand matches the aggregate causal effect depends on the particular ACID used to define the causal effect
.
Thus, although extensive work addresses the exclusion restriction violations in linear SCMs, such as those from treatment measurement error, instrument endogeneity, or direct instrument effects
(
36
;
40
;
6
;
12
;
38
)
, these methods do not carry over here unless they coincide with a suitable ACID.
Put differently, the
\beta^{\prime}
of Proposition

Researchers often make a significant effort to provide domain-specific justifications for why they believe the exclusion restriction
is satisfied in their application. However, a discussion of aggregate treatments does not generally appear in these justificatory arguments.
In the over-identified case (when there are more instruments than treatments), practitioners often apply what is known as the “Sargan test” to argue that the exclusion restriction is satisfied
(
27
)
. The null hypothesis of the Sargan test is that the IV estimands corresponding to different instruments are equal
(
37
)
. Rejecting the null hypothesis then indicates a violation of the exclusion restriction. However, not rejecting the null hypothesis does not directly imply that the exclusion restriction is satisfied
(
19
)
. In the next section, we explore how one might use the Sargan test to check for proportional aggregation, and what additional justifications may be needed.

The classic IV setting considers linear causal relations with additive Gaussian noise. It is natural to ask whether this parametric restriction unnecessarily complicates the inference. The first thing to note is that without linearity and Gaussianity we generally lose
Value Independence
(see Appendix
B.1
), that is, the true target causal effect is no longer well-defined for continuous treatment
A

that the aggregate
A

Moreover, non-linearities in the causal effects will in general mean that the aggregate causal effect is going to become far more sensitive to how the aggregate value is realized through the ACID in the components. The consideration of non-linear causal effects then does not appear to be a promising route to achieve a match between the IV estimand and the aggregate causal effect. However, as in other causal discovery settings, non-linearities may help identify the components of the treatment, and, once the components are identified, the causal effects can be estimated for each component individually.

This last point leads to a more general point about the aggregate setting. If proportional aggregation cannot reasonably be assumed, then an understanding of the aggregate-component relation becomes crucial: Is it possible to identify the components and estimate their effects separately? Is it possible to instantiate a specific ACID? Or is it possible to change the aggregation rule such that proportional aggregation can be achieved? Each of these possibilities takes us beyond the setting we consider here, where the components
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
are not known to the researcher.

7
Conclusion

Instrumental variable analyses have long served as a compass for social scientists, economists, and epidemiologists, providing orientation in settings with unobserved confounding where causal identification would otherwise be elusive. Despite sustained scrutiny of instrument validity, the conditions of relevance, exclusion, and exchangeability offer a clear causal framework that has enabled impactful policy interventions, ranging from education and labor-market reforms to public health interventions, that would otherwise be infeasible using standard regression analysis alone.

However, when treatments are aggregates of multiple components that have different effects on the outcome, our results show that classical IV methods identify causal effects only under highly restrictive assumptions about the system or about how the instrument relates to the aggregation. We have described these assumptions as
proportional aggregation
(Section
4.1
) and
instrument-tuned interventions
(Section
4.2
). On the one hand, these findings raise concerns about whether IV analyses can continue to provide clear directional guidance in settings with aggregated treatments, a common feature of social science applications. In such cases, aggregation in the linear Gaussian case is formally equivalent to a violation of the exclusion restriction (Section
5
), rendering statistical testing infeasible without additional assumptions. On the other hand, our results also provide guidance for analyses that may have become “lost in aggregation” by emphasizing that any claim that the exclusion restriction holds must not only rule out direct effects of the instrument on the outcome, but also justify why the treatment should not be viewed as an aggregate; alternatively, assumptions such as proportional aggregation or instrument-tuned interventions must be made explicit and defended.

These assumptions are strong and reflect fundamental limitations of the IV setting; in the absence of additional evidence, IV estimates should therefore be used with caution as guides for policy intervention when the treatment is plausibly an aggregate.

B.2
Example of a Valid Non-Gaussian ACID

Consider an ACID that satisfies
Surgicality
and the aggregation rule, such that the marginal distributions of
\alpha_{1}A_{1},...,\alpha_{k}A_{k}
under the ACID are identical. Such an ACID would satisfy value independence. To see this, observe that
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\E[A_{j}|\text{do}(A=a)]=a
and
\alpha_{i}\E[A_{i}|\text{do}(A=a)]=\alpha_{j}\E[A_{j}|\text{do}(A=a)]
for all
i,j
. Hence,
\E[A_{j}|\text{do}(A=a)]=\frac{a}{k\alpha_{j}}
for
j=1,...,k
. It follows that
\text{ACE}(A,Y)=\sum_{j}\frac{\beta_{j}}{k\alpha_{j}}
, which does not depend on
a
.

B.3
The “Natural” Gaussian ACID

One may want to consider an ACID that (surgically) sets the component variables
(A_{1},...,A_{k})
to the values they would have in the observational distribution, conditional on the aggregate constraint
A=a
. For our linear Gaussian setting that amounts to appropriately parameterizing the
multivariate Gaussian distribution in (
11
) to match the conditional distribution of
(A_{1},...,A_{k})
given the aggregation rule from their observational joint distribution in SCM (
1

Let
\nu_{j}
denote the observational variance of
A_{j}
for
j=1,...,k
and let
\rho_{ij}
denote the observational covariance of

with

1

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}\\
\vdots\\
A_{k}\end{bmatrix}\sim N\left(\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}+ad_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{k}+ad_{k}\end{bmatrix},\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}=\begin{bmatrix}\nu_{1}&\ldots&\rho_{1k}\\
&\ddots&\\
\rho_{k1}&\ldots&\nu_{k}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(41)

Let
\mathbf{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})^{\top}
. Given the aggregation rule,
(A_{1},...,A_{k})
has the conditional mean vector:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\\
\vdots\\
d_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{\alpha}}{\mathbf{\alpha}^{\top}\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}{\mathbf{\alpha}}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\rho_{ij}\alpha_{j}}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j}\rho_{1j}\alpha_{j}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j}\rho_{kj}\alpha_{j}.\end{bmatrix}

(42)

\displaystyle\mathit{\Sigma}=\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}-\frac{\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{\alpha}\mathbf{\alpha^{\top}}\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}}{\mathbf{\alpha}^{\top}\mathit{\Sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{\alpha}}.

The “Natural ACID” to consider is thus a multivariate Gaussian as in (

(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})^{\top}
as in (

\mathit{\Sigma}
as in (

\displaystyle\text{ACE}(A,Y)=\frac{\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\text{cov}(A_{j},A)}{\text{var}(A)}.

In general, this will not be equal to the IV estimand in (
