Exploring the diversity of leafhoppers (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae) in the Eastern Canadian Arctic
Authors: Abraão Almeida Santos, Nicolas Plante, Sarah Parceaud-May, Jordanne Jacques, Amélie Grégoire-Taillefer, Edel Pérez-López, Brian Bahder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diversity of leafhoppers (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae) in the Arctic is likely underestimated, limiting the ability to document community changes due to uncertainty in species occurrence and a lack of long-term monitoring data. The Nunavik Sentinels community science program documents pollinator diversity across the Boreal, Subarctic, and Arctic regions of Québec (Eeyou Istchee and Nunavik) and Newfoundland and Labrador (Nunatsiavut). Although not a target, leafhoppers were incidentally collected, providing a unique resource for surveying this overlooked taxon. Here, we report leafhoppers collected by the Nunavik Sentinels from 2018 to 2023, comprising 25 identified species and another 7 at the genus level. Out of the 25 species collected, 14 are new records for the Québec and Labrador region. Nearly all species have records in other provinces and territories, indicating extensive Boreal or Nearctic distributions. Species richness per site per year ranged from 1 to 12, with the highest diversity recorded in the Taiga Shield Subarctic (20 species). Species richness declined with increasing latitude, but notably, two species [Colladonus torneella (Zetterstedt) and Latalus missellus (Ball)] were found in the coldest Arctic ecozones. Our study provides the first baseline for leafhopper diversity in the Eastern Canadian Arctic, highlighting the value of citizen-science programs and Indigenous community knowledge in documenting and monitoring insect biodiversity amid the rapidly transforming Northern environment.Keywords: Arctic arthropods, biodiversity, citizen science, indigenous knowledge

Introduction

Insect biodiversity decline is a critical global concern (Wagner 2020, Saunders et al. 2025). This decline is driven by multiple factors, including climate change, habitat loss and fragmentation, and pollution (Outhwaite et al. 2022, Rumohr et al. 2023). However, detecting actual declines in insect populations remains challenging due to the complex nature of these trends (Didham et al. 2020). This issue is particularly critical for Canadian Arctic fauna, where documentation of community changes is limited by uncertainty in species occurrence and a lack of long-term monitoring data (Hodkinson et al. 2013, Gillespie et al. 2020). Because available population trends for the Arctic arthropods reveal nonlinear responses that vary significantly by taxa and habitat (Gillespie et al. 2020, Høye et al. 2021), monitoring insect fauna has become imperative for tracking population shifts and assessing the drivers of change (Hodkinson et al. 2013, Gillespie et al. 2020, Høye et al. 2021).

One solution to this challenging task is community-based participatory research in Arctic environments, which would use local networks to document and monitor biodiversity (Balazs and Morello-Frosch 2013). The Nunavik Sentinels program exemplifies this approach. This community science initiative, officially launched in 2019 and coordinated by the Montréal Insectarium—Espace pour la vie, involves community members (including local Indigenous youth and elders), organizations, and scientists working together to monitor insect biodiversity in the Canadian northern territories above the 52nd parallel across Eeyou Istchee, Nunavik (Québec), and Nunatsiavut in Labrador (Newfoundland and Labrador) (Grégoire Taillefer et al. 2024). The territory studied encompasses the Arctic, Subarctic, and Boreal regions (McLennan et al. 2018). A detailed report on the Nunavik Sentinel’s history, origins, and testimonies is available in Grégoire Taillefer et al. (2024). Nonetheless, while focusing on specific taxa, primarily butterflies and other pollinators, this program ­naturally collects a wide variety of non-target insects, creating a unique collection of Arctic insect biodiversity in Eastern Canada.

Here, we present one component of this unique collection: leafhoppers (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae). Leafhoppers are a diverse group with over 1,200 species in Canada and Alaska (United States), and their distribution closely matches that of their host plants (Hamilton 1997, Maw et al. 2000). Recently, this group was proposed as a marker of the impacts of climate change on agriculture (Plante et al. 2024). This proposition was associated with the apparent increases in plant diseases transmitted by leafhoppers in the Nearctic region (Olivier et al. 2011, 2014, Brochu et al. 2023, Duffeck et al. 2025) and with their recent northward range shifts (Faris et al. 2024, Jacques et al. 2025). Nevertheless, this approach could be extended to Arctic areas, as leafhoppers, including both pest species and known vectors of phytoplasmas, are predicted to expand their ranges northward under warm-climate change scenarios (Santos et al. 2024). Hence, documenting leafhopper communities in the Arctic is a starting point for testing both predictions.

Despite the potential of leafhoppers as indicators of climate change, comprehensive baseline data for the Canadian Arctic remain critically lacking. The most comprehensive northern leafhopper baselines are limited to Yukon and Alaska (Hamilton 1997, Maw et al. 2000), leaving a large geographic gap in other Arctic regions, particularly the eastern part. Our research addresses this gap by providing the first report of leafhopper species collected by the Nunavik Sentinels, thereby establishing an initial assessment in this area. Accordingly, we aim to (i) document the leafhopper species collected by the Nunavik Sentinels, (ii) annotate new regional and provincial distribution records, and (iii) discuss biogeographical patterns of species occurrence in comparison with the Yukon fauna.

Materials and Methods

Nunavik Sentinels Framework Overview

The Nunavik Sentinels operate under a four-pillars framework: (i) training land camp, (ii) summer employment, (iii) educational kit, and (iv) research (Grégoire Taillefer et al. 2024). Pillar (i) refers to training camps for indigenous youth, with knowledge transfer on traditional land activities provided by elder indigenous community members and on scientific disciplines by external researchers. Additionally, at this camp, youth are introduced to various aspects of entomology through field expeditions for collections, using standardized collection protocols defined by the program’s coordination (see sampling methods section). Pillar (ii) focuses on hiring indigenous youth (aged 15 to 30 years) during the summer to participate in insect monitoring in their own communities, making them ‘Sentinels’ who collaborate in the research process to assess and mitigate climate change on their ancestral territory. Pillar (iii) involves providing educational kits to integrate the discovery of nature, insects, and other arthropods in northern school curriculum and day camp activities, to introduce indigenous youth to entomology. Finally, pillar (iv) integrates the previous pillars by compiling and analyzing traditional knowledge and scientific data collected during training land camps, by Sentinels, and other community members. This compiled knowledge is shared with all participants, collaborators, community members, and local governments, and all specimens collected are either added to the Insectarium’s scientific collection or returned to the communities where they were collected (Grégoire Taillefer et al. 2024).

Sampling Sites and Methods

Sampling took place in the Eeyou Istchee and Nunavik regions of Québec (QC) from 2018 to 2023, and in Nunatsiavut in the Labrador (LB) region of Newfoundland and Labrador (NL) from 2022 to 2023. The number of Sentinels has been continuously evolving since the program was officially launched in 2019, with five Sentinels in three communities reached by 2023. However, activities were delayed between 2020 and 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic (Grégoire Taillefer et al. 2024). As a result, variations occurred between the regions and the number of sites during the reporting period (2018 to 2023).

The number of sites where leafhoppers were collected ranged from one to eight each year, located at elevations from 0 up to 458 m above sea level (458 masl), spanning three biogeographic regions: the Boreal, Subarctic and Arctic; and six Canadian terrestrial ecozones: Boreal Shield, Hudson Plains, Taiga Shield, Southern Arctic, Northern Arctic and Arctic Cordillera (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Sampling occurred from mid-June to mid-August, lasting up to eight weeks, with sampling at each site timed to the peak insect activity period. The recommended weather conditions for beginning sampling included no rainfall, temperatures of 10 °C or higher (though 7 °C was acceptable on sunny days), and a clear or partly cloudy sky.

Although the sampling methods could target overall insect biodiversity, the Nunavik Sentinels’ primary focus is on butterflies and other pollinators. Thus, among the three sampling methods used, nets, pan traps (blue, yellow, and white of 30 mL), and bucket light traps, leafhoppers were only captured by the first two (see Results section). Here, we outline the guidelines for using these two methods. Netting was performed 3 to 4 times per week, with each session lasting one hour and covering a nonlinear walking distance of 1 to 2 km. The sweep net used had a diameter of 18 inches (≈45.7 cm) and was a standard sweep net typically employed for actively sampling flying insects; no vegetation sweeping was done. For the pan trap method, a 150 m linear transect was established, with 30 traps, 10 of each color, spaced 5 m apart. The traps were arranged in an alternating color sequence: yellow, white, and blue. Soapy water was added to half the volume of each pan trap, and a rock was placed inside to prevent wind from spilling it. This method was used only in open habitats twice a week, and the traps remained in the field for 24 h after placement. In all cases, specimens (except Lepidoptera) were stored in small vials with propylene glycol.

Samples Processing and Species Identification

Each year, samples were sent to the Montréal Insectarium—Espace pour la vie (Montréal, QC) at the end of summer, where they were transferred and preserved at room temperature in 95% ethanol. They were then sorted and initially identified at the order level. Samples from 2018 to 2023 containing any Hemiptera were then sent to our institution by November 2024. Initially, leafhoppers were separated from the primary Hemiptera collection, and the number of adults and nymphs was recorded.

Adult specimens were grouped based on morphological similarities and identified by dissecting the male genitalia by opening the abdominal genital capsule to observe lateral and ventral views of the aedeagus and the shape of the styles. Identification was performed by using Beirne’s identification key for leafhoppers of Canada and Alaska (Beirne 1956) and further complemented with online identification platforms: BugGuide (https://bugguide.net), British Bugs (https://britishbugs.org.uk), Hoppers of North Carolina (https://auth1.dpr.ncparks.gov/bugs), and World Auchenorrhyncha database (Dmitriev et al. 2025). We confirmed species name classification using the World Auchenorrhyncha database due to multiple synonyms. The final classification adopted here was the accepted species name from the World Auchenorrhyncha database (Dmitriev et al. 2025).

To verify whether a species was a new record, we checked the occurrence initially reported in the Checklist of the Hemiptera of Canada and Alaska (Maw et al. 2000) and double-checked with its updated version, provided by Dr. Joel Kits, the leafhopper specialist at the Hemiptera division/Canadian National Collection of Insects, Arachnids, and Nematodes in 2025. Specimens were deposited at the Montréal Insectarium scientific collection under the voucher numbers IMQC1016789 to IMQC1016968.

We summarized the number of species collected over the years, sampling sites, and specimen counts (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Then, we photographed species with no damage or missing body parts using a Canon EOS R6 Mark II equipped with a Laowa 25 mm f/2.8 2.5-5X ultra-macro lens. We visualized species richness per sampling point/year and occurrence records of identified species by creating maps based on the provincial territories of Québec and Newfoundland and Labrador, as well as Canadian ecozone boundaries provided by Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/7ad7ea01-eb23-4824-bccc-66adb7c5bdf8). Figures were generated using R (version 4.4.2) and RStudio (version 2024.12.1) (R Core Team 2024), along with the ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), rnaturalearth (Massicotte and South 2023), and terra (Hijmans 2025) packages.

Results

Leafhoppers Identification

A total of 745 leafhoppers (606 adults and 139 nymphs) were collected over six years, primarily in July and August. Leafhoppers were mainly collected by pan traps (172/180 of capture events compared to 8/180 by sweep net) (Supplementary Table S1). We identified 25 species, with another 7 at the genus level, classified into five subfamilies: Aphrodinae, Deltocephalinae, Evacanthinae, Eurymelinae, Megophthalminae, and Typhlocybinae (Table 1). We recorded 10 species for LB and 27 for QC (Fig. 2), with the most abundant species being Hebecephalus algidus DeLong & Davidson and Lymotettix myralis Medler (Figs 2 and 3).

Leafhopper Species Richness, New Records, and Biogeography

Species richness at each sampling site per year ranged from 1 to 12 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S2), with the highest final species richness (all years combined) in three ecozones: Taiga Shield (20 species), Boreal Shield (12 species) and Southern Arctic (9 species) (Fig. 4). Two species were found in the coldest ecozones: Colladonus torneella (Zetterstedt) in the Arctic Cordillera and Latalus missellus (Ball) in the Northern Arctic, with the latter being the northernmost record (Fig. 4). Macrosteles quadrilineatus (Forbes) complex had a wide distribution across the ecozones, with records from five sites within the 50° to 58° N latitude range, as well as Limotettix myralis between 52° and 58°N (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The diversity of leafhoppers in the Canadian Arctic territories is likely underestimated. Variations in documented species across these regions suggest that differences in counts may result from insufficient survey effort rather than from actual differences in biodiversity. For instance, recorded occurrences include 12 species in Nunavut, 88 in the Northwest Territories, 156 in Yukon, and 158 in Alaska (Maw et al. 2000). In this context, our examination of leafhoppers collected from the Nunavik Sentinels indicates the presence of at least 25 species across the regions of Eeyou Istchee, Nunavik (QC), and Nunatsiavut (LB). This represents a doubling of the fauna known from adjacent Nunavut, yet remains lower than totals from more intensively surveyed regions such as Yukon. Critically, although leafhoppers were not the primary focus of the Nunavik Sentinels, their incidental collection during the short-term surveys provides valuable natural records, including 14 new geographical occurrences.

While leafhoppers are diverse across Canada and Alaska, their diversity and abundance decline at higher latitudes. The historical sampling frontier, noted by Hamilton (1997), lies near 70°N in Alaska and 63°N in Baffin Island (Nunavut, Canada), north of which no formal records exist. To date, the leafhopper fauna of Canada’s Northern territories has been extensively documented only in Yukon (Hamilton 1997). The assemblage we documented here is consistent with this broader northern fauna. Of 25 confirmed species, 24 are also recorded across Yukon, other Canadian provinces, and Alaska, indicating they occupy extensive Boreal, Subarctic, Arctic, or Nearctic distributions (Fig. 5). A single species, Macrosteles variatus, presents a more complex case. It has been flagged as a potential regional endemic species. Still, its status is considered uncertain due to possible misidentifications (Maw et al. 2000). Thus, our study extends the documented range of widespread Arctic-Boreal/Nearctic species and provides a new dataset for examining ecological patterns.

We identified a latitudinal gradient in species richness across ecozones, which aligns with expectations from the Yukon, where 88% of species are concentrated in the warmer, southern valley bottoms (Hamilton 1997). Relatively high species richness was documented mostly in the Taiga, followed by the Boreal Shield, with a decline in the Southern Arctic. Only Latalus missellus was recorded in the Northern Arctic, and Colladonus torneella in the Arctic Cordillera (Fig. 4). This gradient suggests that similar ecological drivers, particularly temperature and habitat structure, shape leafhopper communities in the North (Bale et al. 1997, Hamilton 1997). At the microhabitat scale, most of our collections originated from low-elevation sites in open fields near bays or rivers. At our highest elevation site (458 masl in the Boreal Shield), only one species, Macrosteles quadrilineatus, was found. This suggests that within these landscapes, local factors, especially warmer microclimates, host plant availability, and proximity to water, are key drivers of leafhopper presence, consistent with patterns observed in southern Yukon (Hamilton 1997), and overall Arctic arthropod fauna (Bale et al. 1997). Nonetheless, the occurrence of species at elevations up to 1600 m in South Yukon (Hamilton 1997) highlights a potential elevational range beyond the scope of our sampling, suggesting an opportunity for future studies.

Our results show that leafhoppers are present in nearly all ecozones of the study area, except the unsampled Hudson Plains, confirming Cicadellidae as a widespread family of Hemiptera in the northeastern Boreal, Subarctic, and Arctic biogeographic regions. More significantly, our study extends the documented range of Boreal-Arctic/Nearctic leafhoppers into northeastern Canada, providing the first records for 14 species in QC and LB. While a core of widely distributed species like the Macrosteles quadrilineatus complex occurs across multiple ecozones, species richness sharply declines into the Arctic, where only two species, Colladonus torneella and Latalus missellus (the northernmost record), were detected (Fig. 4). Importantly, nearly all species we report are known from other northern regions (Fig. 5), strongly suggesting that their previous apparent absence was likely due to sampling effort rather than actual biogeographic boundaries. However, we emphasize that these distributional records form a presence-only dataset. Confirming the spatial and temporal patterns would require targeted, multi-year surveys explicitly designed for Cicadellidae across the northern areas sampled in this study. Nonetheless, our report provides a baseline for understanding the northern limits of leafhopper distribution in eastern North America, serving as a reference point for monitoring future shifts in these boundaries under climate change.

In addition to documenting northern biodiversity, our findings open new avenues for investigating leafhopper-borne plant diseases in Arctic and Subarctic ecosystems. Several genera detected in this study, including Colladonus spp. and Limotettix spp., are known vectors of economically important phytoplasmas such as X-disease in stone fruits and false blossom disease in cranberries (Clarke et al. 2024, Polashock and Wei 2026). As the Northern regions warm and new leafhopper species expand their ranges, the potential introduction or emergence of these pathogens could pose risks to culturally and nutritionally important berry species. Such impacts would directly threaten Inuktitut and Cree ethnocultural traditions tied to berry harvesting, a cornerstone of food sovereignty, health, and intergenerational knowledge in northern communities (Dowsley et al. 2022).

The Nunavik Sentinels have provided a unique resource to uncover leafhopper diversity in the Northern regions of QC and LB. This case highlights how citizen-science initiatives can accelerate biodiversity documentation within the Arctic, particularly for understudied taxa (Hodkinson et al. 2013). Nonetheless, the leafhopper fauna of the region remains underestimated and lacks the long-term monitoring needed to detect climate-driven community changes, including the arrival/introduction of new species (Hodkinson et al. 2013). The fundamental question of whether a species is novel or previously undocumented cannot be addressed without adequate long-term datasets or phylogeographic studies. Accordingly, this study, built on opportunistic sampling, establishes a baseline that will serve as a critical reference for future ecological monitoring and for detecting shifts in leafhopper communities as the Arctic continues to undergo rapid environmental transformation. These accelerating climatic changes, along with the associated risks to ecosystem health, are particularly acute in the extreme northern latitudes and may exceed current model predictions (Kornhuber et al. 2024).
