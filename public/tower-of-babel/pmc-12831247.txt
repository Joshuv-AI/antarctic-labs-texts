Healthfulness of street food outlets around public schools in Mexico
Authors: Catalina Medina, Citlali González, Ana Laura González-Alejo, Uzzi López, Martín Romero, Tania Aburto, Simón Barquera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisparities in the availability, distribution, and density of formal unhealthy and healthy food outlets around schools have been observed based on sociodemographic neighborhood characteristics; however, little is known about street food outlets (SFOs).ObjectiveTo analyze the spatial characteristics of healthy and unhealthy SFOs around public schools in a Mexico City neighborhood and their association with environmental factors.MethodologyA cross-sectional study was conducted to identify SFOs located within a 500-meter buffer around 67 public schools situated in a neighborhood with diverse levels of marginalization in Mexico City. The nutritional healthfulness index (NH-index)—defined as the percentage of available healthful food and beverage groups—was calculated for each SFO using a validated tool for the Mexican context. Logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate the association between null healthfulness (null-NH), defined as 0% availability of healthy food groups, and environmental characteristics. Additionally, spatial analyses were conducted to examine the distribution and density of SFOs around schools.ResultsIn total, 1296 SFOs were identified within 500 m around schools. In total, 42.4% of them were classified as unhealthy. SFOs located in areas with a very high SDI, farther from schools, and with a higher total population per block were less likely to be classified as null-NH. ConclusionsUnhealthy street food outlets are highly concentrated near public schools in vulnerable areas of Mexico City. While new regulations aim to reduce exposure to these products, further research is needed to evaluate their impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25932-x.Keywords: Food environment, Street food, Food availability, Healthfulness, Mexico

Introduction

In 2024, Mexico updated the guidelines regulating the sale of foods and in and around schools [1]. While educational authorities can enforce compliance with the guidelines inside schools, enforcement outside schools is expected to be more challenging, as school authorities lack jurisdiction over public vendors. Moreover, most of these vendors are informal, particularly street food outlets (SFOs) [2, 3]. Consequently, even if the guidelines successfully improve the availability of healthy foods and limit the unhealthy options inside schools, children may still be exposed to unhealthy food and beverage choices nearby, potentially limiting the effectiveness of this policy. Assessing the types of products sold on the way to and from school, is important as they may influence children’s food choices [2] and health outcomes [4–7].

Studies from high-income countries have reported a higher availability of unhealthy foods from formal establishments within walking distance (≤ 1000 m) of schools, [8, 9] especially in neighborhoods with lower socioeconomic status (SES) [2, 8]. One study in Chile assessed the availability of healthy and unhealthy food within SFOs around schools in vulnerable areas of southern Santiago and found that none of the evaluated schools had more than very low availability of healthy food options in these outlets [10]. Evidence from low-middle income countries (LMICs) shows that street food in cities such as Maputo, Mozambique, is dominated by junk food, [11] while in Indonesia [12] and Kyrgyzstan, [13] the foods tend to be high in fat and energy. However, these findings may not accurately reflect the specific availability of foods in SFOs around schools.

In Mexico, few studies have assessed food availability around schools, and among these, none have documented differences by socioeconomic level [7, 14]. One study in Mexico City assessed the availability, density, and distribution of SFOs, and found that SFOs were mainly located near areas with homes, transportation centers, and workplaces, while their availability near schools was limited, possibly due to study design [14]. Another study conducted in Guadalajara and Cuernavaca, Mexico, reported that 67% of public schools are surrounded by food vendors, compared to private schools [7]. Furthermore, most mobile vendors around public schools have been found to offer predominantly unhealthy food options [7].

To our knowledge, there is no available information on the nutritional quality of food and beverages sold in SFOs, nor on their spatial distribution by socioeconomic level around schools in Mexico City. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to analyze the spatial characteristics of healthy and unhealthy SFOs around public schools in a Mexico City neighborhood and their association with environmental factors.

Methodology

Municipal area description

Mexico City, the capital of Mexico, has a population of 9,209,944 inhabitants [15]. It is divided into 16 municipal areas, each of which may contain one to five levels of social development commonly associated with income levels within the Mexican context [16].

The city is home to 6,003 elementary private and public schools (preschool and primary), serving approximately 895,316 students [17]. Public elementary schools typically operate on a morning schedule, from 8:30 a.m. to 12:30 p.m. [17].

Selection of the municipality area

Municipality areas containing neighborhoods with varying levels of marginalization were prioritized, based on the Marginalization Index (MI) derived from the 2020 Population and Housing Census [18]. The MI considers factors such as educational attainment, housing conditions, and perceived income, and classifies areas into five levels: very low, low, medium, high, and very high. Although Mexico City and its 16 municipality areas are generally classified as having very low marginalization, among its 2,019 neighborhoods, 48.9% are categorized as low, 24.6% as medium, 20.2% as very low, and 5.2% as high or very high. Municipality areas with neighborhoods spanning all five levels (Tlalpan, Tláhuac, and Xochimilco) were considered for inclusion to ensure diversity in marginalization levels. While Iztapalapa holds the highest number of elementary schools, none are located in neighborhoods with very high marginalization. In contrast, Tlalpan is the only municipality area with two schools in such areas and ranks second, after Xochimilco, in the proportion of schools in highly marginalized neighborhoods. Based on these criteria, Tlalpan was selected for the study.

Selection of the elementary public schools

The target population for the sample size calculation consisted of 116 schools in the Tlalpan municipality area, [19] with the parameter of interest being the proportion of food and beverage vendors classified as healthy. Data from previous pilot tests suggest that 25% of vendors in public spaces in Mexico City meet this criterion, that is, they sell a higher proportion of foods and beverages recommended for daily consumption. The sample size estimation was based on the following assumptions: the proportion of healthy vendors is uniformly distributed between 0 and ½; half of the vendors around each school are sampled (M = 10, m = 5); and variance (V) was estimated using the formula for two-stage sampling.

where N is the total number of schools, n is the sample size, M is the total number of vendors per school, and m is the number selected per school.

To achieve a confidence interval half-width of 5% and a coefficient of variation below 10%, a sample of 50 schools was selected. Additionally, to allow comparisons between schools in neighborhoods with varying levels of marginalization, all schools located in areas with a very high (n = 2) and high (n = 8) level of marginalization were included. An additional 50 more schools were then randomly selected within Tlalpan, along with 7 more to account for a potential 20% loss. This represented almost 90% of primary public schools in Tlalpan.

Selection of Street Food Outlets (SFOs) around schools

From the selected elementary public schools, a circle was drawn (Euclidean buffer) with a 500 m radius around each school using Tableau online [20]. Maps were created and saved within this program. Trained personnel printed maps and marked streets before data collection. The 500 m buffer was chosen based on the regular walking distance that families could walk from home to school [21].

For this study, SFOs were defined as those that include ready-to-eat food or beverages prepared and/or sold in streets and public spaces by vendors or hawkers [22]. These vendors usually use portable booths, mobile food outlets such as food carts, or trucks. In addition, open-air markets were also included [23]. From September 2022 to June 2023, we mapped and evaluated SFOs found within the buffer during weekdays, from 8:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m., to document food and beverage availability during morning school hours.

Evaluation of SFOs

Three trained personnel evaluated SFOs using the previously validated SFBT (Street Food and Beverage Tool) [24]. This is an audit tool consisting of two sections. The first section includes the oral informed consent to vendors. Then, if accepted, it was asked if they had been previously evaluated, the days of operation, sales elsewhere, and authorization to photograph what they offer. In addition, trained personnel observed the type of SFOs, context around the SFOs, name, address, structural and working characteristics, presentation of available food and/or beverages, vendors, and SFOs’ hygiene practices, and trash can and gel-type disinfectant availability.

The second section includes a checklist of 18 food and beverage groups sold in SFOs. These groups were classified as recommended or non-recommended to be consumed daily based on Eat-Lancet guidelines, [25] Gaona-Pineda et al., [26] NOVA classification, [27]and a group of experts’ opinions. Recommended foods and beverages included: natural/or sparkling water; natural fruit and vegetable juices; sugar-free milk, coffee, tea, and other beverages; preparations without red or processed meat; fruits; vegetables; cereals with or without dairy products; non-fried snacks; and plain yogurts. Non-recommended foods and beverages included: ultra-processed beverages with added sugars; homemade sweetened beverages with added sugar; alcoholic beverages; preparations with red or processed meat, deep-fried dishes; sweets and desserts; sweet bread and pastries; fried salty snacks and other ultra-processed foods; and fruit-flavored yogurts. The nutritional healthfulness index (NH-index) of SFOs was obtained based on a percentage of available recommended foods and beverages groups (i.e., presence) (= 0% null-NH, >0% to < 51% low-NH, ≥ 51% high-NH). Further information is described elsewhere [24].

Personnel visited the area surrounding each school for 4 to 5 days, continuing until the entire 500-meter buffer zone was covered and all SFOs within the buffer were evaluated.

Environmental covariates

Social Development Index (SDI)

This index served as a proxy variable for the income area. The SDI is calculated based on the method of Unsatisfied Basic Needs, which is part of the Integrated Poverty Measurement. The SDI measures poverty in Mexico City and evaluates sociodemographic dimensions, including social security, health, housing, education, durable goods, and energy. A detailed description of this index can be found on the EVALUA Ciudad de México web page [28]. For this study, the SDI was used and categorized into three groups: low-SDI areas (i.e., very low and low), middle-SDI areas (i.e., medium), and high-SDI areas (i.e., high). The SDI was calculated based on the neighbourhood in which each SFO was located.

Density

The density was expressed as the number of SFOs per hectare, providing a quantitative measure of outlet concentration within each buffer zone.

Total population per block (in the Mexican context: colonias) and total number of students per buffer

Blocks are administrative and territorial units mainly used in urban areas to organize space within municipalities. Residents per block (colonias), expressed as a continuous variable, were obtained from the 2020 Population and Housing Census from the National Institute of Statistics and Geography in 2020 [18]. The total number of students within each buffer was calculated by assigning to each street vendor the number of students enrolled in schools located within the vendor’s area of influence [19].

Total and percentage of commercial economic units per block

Based on the National Statistical Directory of Economic Units (DENUE), [29] 131 businesses that fit within wholesale trade (code 43) and retail trade (code 46) were selected [30]. The percentage of commercial units relative to the total economic units surveyed per block was calculated and divided into quantiles. Supplementary Table 1 contains detailed information on both categories.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses (percentages and confidence intervals) were used to characterize the sample. Logistic regression was used to estimate the association between null-NH SFOs (yes/no) and environmental characteristics, including distance, total population per block, total economic units per block, and percentage of commercial economic units per block. In the multivariate model, each environmental characteristic was examined as the main independent variable while adjusting for the remaining factors. Additional logistic regression was used to assess the association between high-NH SFOs (yes/no) and environmental characteristics.

A comprehensive spatial analysis approach was used to examine the distribution and density of SFOs around schools. The density analysis of SFOs was calculated within 100 m, 200 m, 300 m, 400 m and 500 m buffers and stratified by NH-index (null, low, and high) around schools. A comparison between buffer zones was performed to determine the type of SFOs (NH-index: null, low, and high) most available nearby schools. The average distance between schools and different types of SFOs (NH-index: null, low, and high) was calculated using Euclidean distance. Ripley’s K and L function was used to estimate the spatial clustering or dispersion of SFOs around schools. To further examine the spatial relationship between these SFOs and schools, the K-cross function was applied. This method allows for the assessment of spatial dependence between two different types of point patterns—in this case, schools and SFOs —by comparing the observed distribution to what would be expected under spatial randomness. A higher-than-expected concentration of SFOs near schools may suggest spatial clustering, while a lower concentration may indicate spatial dispersion.

Analysis was performed with SPSS version 25, RStudio 2024.12.1 + 563, and QGIS, version 3.4.5 - Bratislava. Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

A total of 1,419 SFOs were identified within a 500 m Euclidean buffer around the 67 selected schools; of these, 1,296 SFOs were correctly mapped and had an SDI index. According to the SDI, 22% of schools were located in areas classified as very high, 22% as high, 25% as medium, 15% as low, and 15% as very low (data not shown).

Table 1 describes the distribution of SFOs by NH-index and environmental characteristics. Overall, 42.4% of SFOs were classified as having null-NH. A lower proportion of null-NH SFOs was observed in areas with high SDI (30.3%) compared to areas with very low (48.6%) and low SDI (48.8%). In contrast, a higher proportion of SFOs with a low-NH was found in areas with a very high SDI (57.4%) compared to areas with very low (40.3%) and low SDI (41.7%). In terms of proximity to schools, a higher proportion of null-NH SFOs were located within the nearest buffer to schools (≤ 100 m) (48.7%) compared to those in the furthest buffer (34.0%). Although not statistically significant, a lower proportion of null-NH SFOs was found in areas with a higher population per block (37.5%) and a higher number of economic units per block (35.3%) compared to the quantiles with lower population and economic units. There were no significant differences in the proportion of null-, low-, and high-NH SFOs in areas with a higher population per block, total economic units per block, and percentages of commercial economic units per block relative to lower quantiles.

Figure 1 shows the distribution of SFOs located at a 500 m radius around the schools by NH-index. The results of the spatial dependence analysis, using Ripley’s cross-type Kij(r) function, reveal a clear aggregation of all three types of SFOs—high-NH, low-NH, and null-NH—around the studied schools, in contrast to a completely random spatial distribution.

Spatial analysis of SFOs distribution within a 500 m radius by NH-index. Tlalpan, Mexico City 2022-2023

To assess the significance of this spatial clustering, the observed patterns were compared to a homogeneous Poisson process, which serves as a null model in spatial statistics. In a Poisson process, events (in this case, SFOs) are assumed to occur completely at random, independently of one another, and with equal probability across space. This comparison allows us to determine whether the actual distribution of SFOs reflects random variation or if it reveals an underlying spatial structure.

At all evaluated distances (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 m), the observed values of K(r) were significantly higher than those expected under the Poisson process, indicating strong positive spatial dependence.

Among the categories analyzed, null-NH SFOs exhibited the highest K(r) values across all distance ranges, suggesting a particularly dense concentration of these outlets near school locations. Low-NH SFOs, while present in the surrounding areas of schools, showed relatively lower levels of aggregation, especially at shorter distances.

Discussion

This study examined the spatial characteristics of healthy and unhealthy SFOs around public schools in a Mexico City neighborhood and their association with environmental factors. Overall, 42.4% of the SFOs located within a 500 m radius around schools were classified as null-NH, meaning that all of the products sold in these outlets are unhealthy. SFOs located in areas with a very high SDI, farther from schools, and with a higher total population per block were less likely to be classified as null-NH. Finally, a significant clustering of SFOs was observed around schools.

A higher density of SFOs was found within the buffer closest to schools, which is in line with what previous studies have reported [7, 14, 31–33]. Multiple studies from Latin America [7, 14, 31] and the U.S. [32, 33] have found that street food vendors commonly cluster near schools, [7, 33] where [7, 33] pedestrian activity is highest, [31, 34] especially in low- to middle-income areas [31]. However, patterns vary by city, [7, 14, 31–33] country, [7, 14, 31–33] and the buffer size used for analysis [7, 10, 14, 32]. Vendors are often strategically located in high-traffic areas, such [31] as school zones, [14, 31] public transport hubs, [14] and worksites [14]. Research shows that smaller buffer zones (e.g., 100–400 m) reveal higher vendor density, while larger ones dilute this effect [7, 10, 14, 32]. Variations in findings are also influenced by vendor operating hours [14] and seasonal factors such as school vacations [14].

Within a 500-meter radius of the schools, SFOs predominantly offer unhealthy foods and beverages, such as ultra-processed products, particularly sweetened beverages, candies, and salty snacks, while healthier options are scarce [35]. This pattern is consistent with findings from studies in high- and middle-income countries, [2, 10, 36–38] and raises public health concerns, as the concentration of establishments offering predominantly unhealthy food options, with few healthy alternatives, can significantly influence dietary behaviors [3, 39]. Increased consumption of such products is associated with adverse health outcomes, including obesity [40, 41] and type 2 diabetes [42]. Evidence from a meta-analysis suggests that interventions targeting the school food environment can promote healthier eating behaviors, such as increased fruit intake, and contribute to reductions in BMI Z-scores [43].

In this study, SFOs with no offer of health food and beverages were more commonly found in areas with lower SDI, reflecting structural inequalities in access to healthy food. Residents in these areas are disproportionately exposed to unhealthy food environments, a pattern also observed in previous research in Mexico City, which found that low-income neighborhoods had the lowest density of outlets selling cooked meals, fruits, and vegetables [14]. These disparities could contribute to poor dietary habits and a higher prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and other diet-related noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), placing additional strain on already burdened public health systems. Although SFOs provide affordable food options for low-income families, their predominance in unhealthy offerings near schools raises concerns about children’s diet quality [35]. Thus, enhancing enforcement and offering incentives for healthier options could help reduce nutritional inequities and support better health outcomes in low-SDI communities [44].

In countries such as Mexico, SFOs play a vital role in preserving national culinary traditions. As these informal outlets are widespread and often serve as a primary source of income for many families, changing their supply dynamics presents significant challenges. Moreover, the types of products sold are largely shaped by consumer demand. Nevertheless, SFOs also have the potential to serve as platforms for promoting healthier eating habits. In the United States, some programs have been implemented to improve access to healthy foods in low-income areas. For instance, the Green Carts initiatives in the Bronx sought to address food swamps and deserts by increasing the availability of fresh produce [45, 46]. Similarly, school-based programs that subsidize healthy food have been shown to encourage greater consumption of nutritious meals among students [47].

The higher concentration of unhealthy SFOs closest to schools [7, 10, 14, 32] could be shaped by socio-cultural habits, commercial strategies, and, significantly, by political and regulatory environments. Many countries have implemented policies to limit the sale of unhealthy food and beverages in [48] and around schools to reduce children’s exposure to obesogenic environments [48–54]. A global study on competitive policies in schools reported that 48 countries have national regulations restricting the sale of specific foods or beverages, including soft drinks, energy drinks, chips, chocolate, and fried foods [48]. In terms of policies around the schools, India bans the sale of high-fat, salty, and sugary pre-packaged foods within 50 m of school campuses [49]. At the same time, Cabo Verde prohibits the distribution of low-nutritional beverages and alcohol within a 200-meter radius [51]. Jamaica restricts the sale of sweetened drinks during school hours and activities, [52] and Malaysia enforces a 40-meter buffer zone around schools where food and drink sales are banned [53]. Barbados goes further by prohibiting vending within 91 m unless vendors are licensed and comply with national nutrition standards [54].

In contrast, Mexico’s General Law of Adequate and Sustainable Nutrition was approved in 2024 [1]. This law mandates restrictions on the marketing and distribution of unhealthy prepackaged foods near schools; however, the specific regulations and the operational procedure for its implementation have not yet been established [1]. Previous examples demonstrate that while school-based food regulations exist in several countries, it remains unclear whether they are effective or properly enforced.

Based on our studies, more than 40% of SFOs near schools sold only unhealthy foods, primarily in low-income areas. Research indicates that proximity to such outlets may increase students’ consumption of unhealthy foods [55]. Limiting the availability of these products has the potential to positively impact children’s health outcomes [55]. Therefore, it is crucial to strengthen regulations that address not only the sale and marketing of products within the schools but also those sold in the surrounding areas, [48] particularly in vulnerable and low-income communities. Potential interventions could include zoning laws that limit the density of unhealthy SFOs near schools, [51, 53, 54, 56] mandatory nutrition standards for vending operating near school zones [54] educational campaigns, [56] and incentives for small businesses to offer healthier food options [44]. Furthermore, identifying barriers and promoting affordable, accessible, culturally appropriate healthy alternatives could be essential for effectively reducing the consumption of unhealthy foods [56]. Public policies informed by these findings could support cross-sector collaboration between the Ministry of Health, Education, and Urban Planning to create a healthier food environment for children.

Strengths and limitations

Although this study assessed a high number of SFOs around schools in a neighborhood that includes all socioeconomic levels, this may not represent the SFOs of all schools in Mexico City. A major strength of this research lies in its comprehensive mapping of SFOs across 67 schools in southern Mexico City, encompassing a diverse mix of urban and peri-urban environments. These contexts highlight the challenges associated with food availability near schools.

While the SFBT was used to classify SFOs as healthy or unhealthy, it does not consider proportion size, sales volumes, or nutrient profile of the products offered, factors that are crucial for a more accurate assessment of food nutrition quality. Additionally, the study employed a 500-meter Euclidean buffer to define the surrounding food environment, a method used in similar research [21]. However, this approach may underestimate walkable distances and overestimate accessibility in poorly connected street networks, potentially inflating outlet densities and attenuating proximity effects.

In the case of Tlalpan municipality, where approximately 45% of schools are private and 55% are public, the study focused exclusively on public schools. This decision was based on previous research showing that 67% of public elementary schools are surrounded by food vendors [7]. However, this focus represents a key limitation, as the exclusion of private schools may restrict the ability to detect differences in food access between public and private educational setting in Mexico City.

Conclusions

There is a high concentration of unhealthy street food outlets near public schools in a Mexico City neighborhood, particularly in more vulnerable areas. Although Mexico has recently implemented regulations to limit students’ exposure to unhealthy products around schools, the extent to which these measures influence children’s purchasing behaviors a dietary intake remains unclear. Evaluating the implementation and effectiveness of these policies, especially in low-income settings, will be essential to inform targeted interventions and ensure that current regulations translate into meaningful health outcomes for children.
