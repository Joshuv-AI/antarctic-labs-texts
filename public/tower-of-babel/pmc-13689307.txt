The influence of community engagement on stakeholder perspectives in cross-sector integrated care: an integrative review
Authors: Vanessa Wright, Kelly Metcalfe, Whitney Berta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity engagement represents a highly relevant way to integrate care across sectors and address social and structural determinants of health with populations. Yet, advancement of integrated care remains a challenge, particularly across health and social service organizations. Situating social cognition as a key element of integrated care, this paper explores the act community engagement within cross-sector integrated care.MethodsAn integrative review was conducted to determine what is known about the influence of community engagement on stakeholder perspectives in cross-sector integrated care, and to contribute to a more comprehensive evidence base for building and operationalizing equitable integrated care. In March 2022, four data bases were systematically searched, applying date limits 1990–2022, for English language articles that described community engagement in relation to integrated care and resulting stakeholder perspectives. Using matrices, numerous variables were extracted and synthesized using thematic analysis derived from the Rainbow Model of Integrated Care and a continuum of community engagement.ResultsIn total, 13 studies were included in this analysis. Two studies included the hospital as a partner, and the rest were a mix of public, private health, and social service sectors. Positive stakeholder perspectives (N = 6) were found in studies that were consultative or collaborative, and led with social capital, shared reciprocity, and trust. Moderate and negative perspectives (N = 7) were found in studies that led with a utilitarian stance and lacked collective leadership, governance, longitudinal planning, and joint evaluations.ConclusionsThis review makes a singular contribution to cross-sector integrated care literature, utilizing perspectives from health and social service organizations to map what is known about the influence of community engagement on cross-sector integrative care. Perspectives from this review support calls for additional integrative care research exploring community-hospital relationships, and how power dynamics influence proximal and distal relationships, capabilities, motivations, and opportunities for collaboration.

Background

For decades, the damaging effects of health system fragmentation have been recognized by front line providers, policy makers, health and social care organizations and their recipients [1–3]. Care fragmentation arises from health system fragmentation, when health care delivery is spread across multiple, poorly coordinated providers. This lack of cohesion has been shown to result in adverse consequences including health inequities, increased hospital admission and mortality [4]. Strategies to improve care coordination and address system fragmentation have been implemented across North America, the United Kingdom, Europe, Australia, and New Zealand [5, 6], yet many patients continue to navigate complex health systems in isolation, determining adequate entry points and pathways to support their health outcomes [7]. Patients report wanting care that is easy to understand and access, where housing, social services, and health work in close partnership [8]. Characterizing this request, is the siloed nature of many North American healthcare systems, that are marked by limited flow of information and resources, reducing efficiency, effectiveness, and quality of health care provision [9, 10]. Health systems are responding through goals to improve access to services, create smoother transitions between services and strive for patients to receive the ‘right’ care at the ‘right’ time, reducing the number of times patients must tell their story [11]. These overarching goals support what is best known in literature as integrated care, a term that denotes health system efforts around the world to improve effectiveness, efficiency, and outcomes [12].

Integrated care has demonstrated effectiveness for those with complex heath conditions ranging from diabetes, influenza, and cardiovascular disease [13]. Extending the benefits of integrated care beyond the clinical domain has allowed for the inclusion of population health as a central pillar to integrated care efforts [14–16]. Population health takes into consideration the wide range of factors that influence the health of populations and communities, including physical, social, economic, and cultural environments [14]. In order to address the full array of factors that can affect individual and community health, interaction among various sectors is required, recognizing that no one service can adequately respond to the diverse health needs of individuals and communities [3, 17]. As such, enhancing the ability for providers to work together, across sectors, has been heralded as the solution [1]. Herein, a shift in our health care system is seen as we move beyond the traditional biomedical model, and pay particular attention to cross-sector collaboration, and the influence of public policy, to address the social and structural determinants of health and health equity [15].

Despite imperative to advance integrated care toward policy making and system design, an assortment of terms is often used interchangeably to describe collaboration of providers across sectors including collaborative care, coordinated care, and partnerships [18–20]. For the purposes of this study, the term cross-sector integrated care is used, recognizing that a ‘collaboration’ occurs in the midrange of a continuum of how organizations work on public issues [21]. Whereas, integrative care may take different forms of vertical and horizontal integration, through different levels of integration (micro, meso, macro) and occurring at varying stages of intensity [22]. We define cross-sector integrated care through the work of Bryson et al., [21] as the linking or sharing of information, resources, activities, and capabilities by organizations in two or more sectors to achieve jointly an outcome that could not be achieved by organizations in one sector separately.

Although integrated care and population health are not novel concepts, realizing their operational synergy remains a challenge [17]. Integrated care is difficult to achieve, particularly through the lens of population health where care coordination emphasizes the integration of two service platforms: health and social care [23]. Health and social services traditionally have long-standing institutional separation, and distinct funding streams and accountability arrangements, making it difficult to seamlessly integrate cross-sector care, even if all have the same end goal to improve health of communities and populations [23, 24]. To that end, community engagement is seen as an effective strategy for harnessing community potential, allowing individuals to regain control of their health and improve population health outcomes [25]. What remains to be uncovered, is what kind of cross-sector collaborations work, with whom, and by what way of community engagement.

As cross-sector integrated care arrangements continue to develop between the health and social service sectors, there is a need to move well beyond optimizing organizational and process integration, to that of social cognition, which considers what is shared and valued between parties in the commitment to co-ordinating efforts [26]. The ethos of shared values and commitment in care delivery is known as normative integration [27]. It has been suggested that normative integration may be the key to addressing ongoing integrative challenges through the development and creation of trust. Trust herein is seen as underpinning quality interactions and relationships necessary for sustained cross-sector integrated care efforts [28].

The emphasis on trust as critical to the success of integrated care has been well documented, yet integrated care studies have shown that trust is a moving mark, highly contextual, and difficult to fully achieve [29, 30]. These findings speak to the complexity of integrated care and the careful attention that is required to build and cultivate trust through transparent communication, inclusivity, establishing a shared vision, leadership, and governance in long term agenda making [22, 31, 32]. When asymmetries present in these areas, reliance is inadvertently placed on interpersonal trust as a compensatory mechanism for challenges shaped by inter-organizational engagements [30]. While interpersonal trust may form the basis for establishing interpersonal and interorganizational trust [33], it is the application of governance models, particularly collective/collaborative governance, that allow for meaningful progress in integrated care. Ansell and Gash [34] outline collaborative governance as a participatory approach in decision making where stakeholders across sectors work together to identify common goals, share resources, responsibilities, and develop policies and programs aimed at addressing complex societal challenges. Collaborative governance is seen as an opportunity to advance trust through acknowledging the complex histories of resource and power imbalances known to public health service organizations, as well as histories of antagonism and cooperation [34]. Considering potential lies in advancing cross-sector integrated care through revealing what is shared among care partners, there is a need to better understand the collective beliefs, motivations, and perspectives of those involved in cross-sector integrated care initiatives. A review of the literature was conducted with a goal of summarizing stakeholder perspectives found in community centred cross-sector integrative care research.

Conceptual framework

Community engagement continuum

Community engagement may be seen on a continuum as illustrated by the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (modified from the International Association for Public Participation’s Community Engagement Continuum), ranging from outreach (provision of one-way information), to consultation, involvement (two-way flow of information), collaboration (partnership on all aspects), and shared leadership, inclusive of longer-term partnerships and shared decision making [35], Fig. 1. Among the diverse processes of community engagement, two methodological approaches have emerged. The first being the ‘utilitarian’ perspective, where the community is approached to improve the effectiveness of the intervention and invited for their views, but the intervention has not been designed by the community themselves [36]. The second approach is grounded in the development of social capital. From this perspective, community members are empowered to determine their priorities for health and service redesign [36]. The Community Engagement Continuum (CEC) stems from Arnstein’s [37] eight domains in the “ladder of citizen participation” and has been used by researchers to support categorization of community engagement across translational research [35, 38]. Building on this framework, Key et al. [39], developed the Community Engagement Research Continuum to assist researchers identify and place their work on the continuum, assess appropriate access points, and opportunities for progression related to context. Rainbow model of integrated Care

The Rainbow Model of Integrated Care (RMIC) is a comprehensive framework used to assess and evaluate integrated care dimensions based on the Triple Aim Framework for primary care (Fig. 2) [27]. The model was validated through a series of Delphi panels and seeks to align integrated care within micro, meso, and macro system levels [40]. As such, the micro-level refers to levels of clinical integration among professionals in patient care coordination across the health care system. The meso-level refers to both organizational integration and professional integration. The former refers to networks formed between organizations, and the latter as partnerships between health care professionals in integrated care efforts. At the macro-level, system integration refers to linkages between the health care system and external partners. Functional and normative integration span micro, meso, and macro system levels. Functional integration includes key supports that are needed in integrative operations, such as financing, management, and information systems. Normative integration, as referred to earlier, is based on the shared vision, values, and mission of efforts through cultural and social attention. Key features of integrated care have been developed into 59 taxonomies by Valentijn et al. [40] to assist classifying and categorizing the broad spectrum of approaches used in various projects (Fig. 3). The RMIC and related taxonomy provides a framework to evaluate integration and outcome measures from the perspectives of both patients and health care providers [40]. Although the validation of this model has been extensive, including various clinical domains and chronic disease specific states (diabetes, renal disease, and Parkinson’s) as well as in primary care settings [41–44], there has been little critique of the RMIC. Aim

Integrated care literature over the past two decades has enabled advances in the field through identification of barriers and enablers to system integration. Despite these efforts, considerable difficulty establishing collaboration across organizational boundaries remains [26]. Beyond normative integration, exploring differing perspectives of multiple stakeholders learning with communities, rather than from communities may provide the contextual evidence needed to advance cross-sector integrated care through heath policy and practice [46, 47]. This review aims to expand the understanding of cross-sector integrated care and normative integration through the application of the RMIC and CEC. In this regard, stakeholder perspectives are mapped onto both frameworks providing a unique overlay of integrated care dimensions with community engagement categorization. In addition, this review aims to provide comprehensive evidence to build and operationalize equitable cross-sector integrated care initiatives grounded in respect and allyship, with the communities served by heath care systems. Objectives developed to meet these aims include i) identification of studies and reviews focusing on cross-sector integrated care utilizing community engagement and participation, ii) critique of quality studies, and iii) identification of themes found within the included research studies that align with the RMIC and CEC frameworks to provide new findings in the expanding field of cross-sector integrated care.

Methods

An integrative literature review was conducted using Whittemore and Knalf’s framework [48] as a guide. This integrative review is classified as a mixed studies review, through analysis of both qualitative and quantitative study findings [49]. The literature included in this integrative review met clear and specific criteria to support the inclusion and exclusion of articles chosen to represent the data in an unbiased manner [48]. To gather relevant data for the review, key terms were searched on select data bases to yield a cohort of articles for data extraction. Thematic analysis was used through reading and re-reading the articles to identify key themes and findings that emerged from stakeholders regarding their perceptions of cross-sector integrated care efforts [50].

Search strategy

Relevant articles were searched using a pre-planned literature search in four data bases: OVID – MEDLINE, OVID – EMBASE, EBSCO-CINHAL and PSYCH-INFO, from 1990-present. The year 1990 was chosen, as the time-period when integrated care was introduced as a concept in medical and social service literature. Data bases were searched using key concepts deemed most relevant to yield data in according with the aim of the review [48]. Key concepts included “community engagement”, “community participation”, “community involvement”, “community mobilization”, “community representation”, “collaborative care”, “integrated care”, “coordinated care”, “collaborative care”, “collaborative care”, “collaborative partnership”, “inter-sectoral care”, “inter-agency care”, “cross-sector collaboration”. Key concepts were combined with Boolean operator AND, and the search words within each concept were combined with OR.

Study selection criteria

This review focuses on the perspectives of all stakeholders involved in cross-sector integrative care initiatives, where the community was actively engaged and included as a partner. Inclusion criteria was devised as: studies that were evaluative in nature, explored an intervention, utilized community and population health interventions, published between January 1990 and Feb 2022, empirical research written in the English language from Canada, USA, Western Europe, UK, Australia, and New Zealand. In addition, inclusion criteria included studies that focused on horizontal integration (collaborating organizations, networks, groups in the health system). The exclusion criteria were editorials, opinion pieces, conference abstracts, review papers, non-English papers, dissertations, and those articles with focus on health outcomes only.

Search outcomes

Per PRISMA guidelines, eight articles were removed due to duplication [51] (see Fig. 4 for PRISMA flow diagram). Titles and abstracts were checked for relevance based on the study inclusion and exclusion criteria. A total of 2410 articles were rejected as they did not meet the objectives laid out in the inclusion criteria. This process yielded 103 articles for full text screening. These articles were read for their relevance, and of these, 90 further studies were rejected as they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 12 qualitative and 1 quantitative study met the criteria for this integrative review. Quality appraisal

The Mixed Method Appraisal Tool 2018 (MMAT) was used to evaluate the research included in this review. Although no specific standard for assessing methodological quality in an integrative review exists [48], the MMAT is a validated critical appraisal tool that is designed for systematic mixed review studies [64]. The MMAT leads appraisal with two standard screening questions for all studies under review, then incorporates five tailored screening questions for each study type: qualitative, quantitative randomized control trial, quantitative non-randomized control trial, quantitative descriptive, and mixed method. Eleven of the thirteen studies included in this review were deemed high quality. The two studies that were deemed low quality (data collection methods inadequately described) have remained in this analysis, however these findings were taken into consideration during data analysis.

Data abstraction and synthesis

To facilitate analysis, both qualitative and quantitative data were extracted into an evidence table. This table is also known as a matrix, used to organize, support data analysis, and develop themes [48]. Codes were deductively generated to support thematic analysis, built using RMIC taxonomy and spectrum of community engagement categories. In addition, stakeholder perspectives were coded as positive, moderate, and negative dependent on each study’s overall findings related to successes and challenges of employing cross-sector integrated care with communities. After deductively coding each study, the codes were clustered to develop themes through use of a matrix. Matrices were used in three stages to support the fusion of narrative and statistical data [48]. Initially, articles were grouped based on setting, methods, and results. This preliminary data tabulation, allowed for a second matrix to develop and demonstrate similarities and patterns within the data, using the CEC, the RMIC, and 59 key feature integrated care taxonomy to code data within the studies. The third matrix allowed for the emergence of themes to present among the studies, where significance, relevance, and novel representation was found related to stakeholder perceptions of cross-sector integrated care. Results

Characteristics of the included studies

Of the 13 studies included in this review, seven were based in the USA, three in Europe, two in the UK, and one in Canada. Publication years ranged from 2001 - 2021 and included 11 qualitative studies, one quantitative study and one mixed method study. The focus of each study explored stakeholder perspectives in cross-sector integrated care and collaboration through population health programs, in community settings. Study topics included: teen pregnancy, domestic violence, breast and prostate cancer, obesity (n = 2), chronic disease prevention, transportation and health, early childhood intervention, social prescribing, and care-dependent seniors. All 13 studies were involved in the integration of health, social care, and wider community service, and utilized a horizontal integrated care approach. Two studies included the hospital as a partner, the rest included a combination of private and community health sectors, social care organizations, and regional/municipal governments. Regarding stakeholder perspectives of cross-sector integrated care initiatives, six studies detailed positive stakeholder perspectives, two studies detailed moderate stakeholder perspectives, and five studies detailed negative stakeholder perspectives. Moreover, all studies addressed micro, meso, and macro level constructs of integrated care, reflecting the complex needs of the populations targeted within the studies.

Integration processes

Clinical integration (micro-level)

This dimension represents the heart of integration efforts and is the interface where patients and their families receive care from the health system [46]. All studies described a clinical integration process to address population health needs. Community health workers (CHW) were utilized in four studies as a key group to mobilize outreach efforts [55, 59, 61, 65]. In addition to providing direct clinical integration, CHWs acted as case managers [61] and were hired to create linkages among organizations [55, 61]. These findings are synonymous with the CHW role that has been carved across the globe as an expansive and critical mechanism to boost health efforts within community settings [66]. CHWs are often members of the communities they serve and possess a unique understanding of the local context, including barriers and facilitators to health care access [66].

Despite three studies undertaking a community based participatory research (CBPR) focus [54, 55, 59], the use of client participation in co-design was used minimally in studies with and without a CBPR focus [52, 58]. These findings are significant as the premise of CBPR is built on equitable approaches that involve community members, organizational representatives, and researchers in all aspects of the research process. Through this collaborative process, partners contribute unique strengths and shared responsibilities to enhance the understanding of a particular phenomenon with the goal of improving community health and well-being [67].

Furthermore, three studies addressed continuity of care as an objective of fluid care delivery. Two of these studies aim to do this through CHWs [55, 61], and through cross-sector collaboration [53]. The central element to each of these studies was their longitudinal nature in establishing a commitment to community mobilization through a collective impact model [53] and continued follow up support offered by CHWs [55, 61]. Centrality of client needs was a notable guiding feature in six studies, that sought to integrate and scale client care at micro, meso, and macro levels, which was particularly successful when diversified across sectoral organizations [24, 54, 56, 62, 63, 65].

Professional integration (meso-level)

Personal and professional characteristics are often seen as a driving force behind many cross-sector integrated care efforts that may hinge on misalignment of collaborative governance [30]. In several studies, interpersonal characteristics were seen as a supportive element, and provided advantage to a well-functioning partnership, and influenced acquired learning in partner organizations [54, 65]. In this regard, the success of these partnerships was situated in individual reflexivity and employing a strengths-based approach with collaborative working group members, and community members. In other studies, champions or change agents were identified as key agents in intersectoral collaboration. CHWs’ fulfilled this role as they recognized the importance of both individual and organizational relationship building [61, 62]. That said, there may be reasons for this emergent leadership style as Zhu et al. [63] found, that is situated in a lack of shared governance or decision making [63]. As a result, reliance on interpersonal characteristics to build individual relationships when organizational relationships lacked was common finding [61]. Herein, there was an over-reliance on skills of CHWs’ as sole ‘problem solvers and emotional supports’, while the responsibilities of others in the partnership were negated [58, 59].

Shared vision between professionals was often established from the outset in collaborative partnerships [52–54, 57], [59, 65]. These early formalities can be captured through agreements of interdisciplinary collaboration or memorandums of understanding [52, 53]. However, early establishment of shared vision did not necessarily equate with collaborative success in short- or long-term project indicators [53, 57, 59]. Within these studies, barriers to cross-sector integrated care were sighted as time and resource misalignment, overt power dynamics, lack of community representation, and intermediate outcome assessment. Of particular importance is who is creating a shared vision from the outset, where excluding key community organizations led to detrimental outcomes [59]. Shared vision was found to expand and deepen over time in positive, collaborative working relationships [52, 54]. Furthermore, interprofessional education provided an avenue to facilitate increased awareness of services, inner workings of health systems, and cultural relevance if all parties were present [55, 58, 65]. When interprofessional education narrowly addressed access to services, rather than cultural community-based teachings, project scope and reach were limited [55, 58]. Similarly, multidisciplinary guidelines provided a neutral reference for organizations to situate their work, advance understanding in evidenced based practice, strengthen relationships, and increase community capacity [55, 56].

Organizational integration (meso-level)

Nomenclature used to describe cross-sector integrated care initiatives varied widely across studies. The term ‘collaboration or collaborative’ was used most frequently [24, 54, 62, 63], followed by ‘partnership’ [52, 59, 65], then ‘program’ [57, 61], ‘initiative’ [53] and ‘coalition’ [58]. Terms were defined and labelled according to overarching goals of the integrated group or partners themselves. For example, Crooks et al. [54], referred to their group as a collaborative, a small agile group of individuals, dedicated and responsive to one another needs. Shan et al. [65], equally described accountability measures taken in community partner selection, however partnership inclusivity remained at the forefront. Grounded in anticolonialism and indigenous health principles, Shan et al. [65], chose to refrain from using the term ‘collaborative’. Even among partners within the same study various terms were used dependent on perceived goals and desires. One hospital partner referred to their interaction with community services as a collaborative, when further analysis by Zhu et al. [63] revealed the level of engagement was in fact cross-sector interaction, a unidirectional level of outreach. In addition, poor interorganizational governance between the health and community sectors was causal for group reorientation and labelling. Pursell and Kearns [58], demonstrated how the community sector can break ties with an organizational working group and refer to themselves as a ‘coalition’ in effort to build their own capacity.

Attention, dedication, and alignment of interorganizational governance played a significant role in the positive perspectives of stakeholders in community centered integrated care. The opportunity for all partners to meet regularly, co-design programs, and share decision making allowed for greater understanding of capabilities within the group [52, 54, 57, 65]). Moreover, attention placed on the general SDOH within a community, rather than a narrow programmatic focus allowed stakeholders to unite through a common vision, act with honesty, and value local, bottom-up experiences of health promotion [52, 54, 65]. Progress reports, and annual work plans provided added support and direction within partnerships to create learning organizations to advance collective research, and program development [54, 56]. Conversely, the exclusion of key working group partners from the outset fractured trust and momentum in advancing project goals [58, 59]. Negative perspectives of stakeholders were found in studies suggested to be based in CBPR and consensus building but lacked attention to these efforts throughout [53, 59]. Negative perspectives among stakeholders were also found in studies whose outcome presented as value creation for organization. Herein, a beneficial outcome for the organization is sought after verses overarching population health features. This demonstrated paralysation of community social capital and health equity, as well as disabling trust, at the hand of academic, health, and municipal partners [55, 58, 59].

System integration (macro-level)

Equal to the importance of robust interorganizational governance in community centred integrated care, is diverse stakeholder management. Programs, partnerships, and collaborations that upheld commitment from diverse stakeholders were resoundingly positive through stakeholder perspectives. These initiatives included engagement from municipalities [52, 54, 65], and public and private organizations [24, 56, 63]. Importantly, each study referenced above referred to community organization representatives as key stakeholders. Of interest, the involvement of external funding partners (state, government and not for profit) and government legislators did not necessarily equate to program longevity or positive experiences among project partners. This was particularly true when direct funding partners and those in decision making authority were not involved in proximal stakeholder management [53, 55, 59, 62]. Conversely, funding that was diversified and secured across sectors created opportunity for program partners to plan longitudinally, develop trust, and cultivate relationships with each other, and their communities [24, 52, 54, 56, 63]).

Programmatic alignment and leadership style

The context in which programs are created directly sets them up for success or failure. Those designed to respond to gaps in care were often seen as bridging rather than building sustainable solutions. This is detailed through reliance on CHWs in the context of deepening austerity measures [61], and competitive tender practices to appoint new partners in care, which limits foundational integrated care development [62]. Programs that were created through existing structures and systems, governed by institutional polices, had a greater reach and opportunity to evolve through stakeholder acceptance and comfort [54, 56, 65]. Here, institutional and government support were recognized by stakeholders as a commitment in longevity. Stakeholders then felt a willingness and assurance to invest, particularly with community members/representatives. Although diverse stakeholder representation was deemed beneficial at the proximal level in community centred integrated care, Middleton et al. [57] called for a single leader to champion efforts. In this example, public health and local authorities worked ‘side by side’, while the community voice was absent in leadership representation. Conversely, Crooks et al. [54], called for distributed leadership, that is emergent and context dependent. Here, reliance on trust and foundational relationships was seen as pivotal to enacting a distributed leadership style, which requires shared project completion by partners in varying roles, also known as socially distributed cognition [54].

Functional integration (macro-level)

Functional integration links micro, meso, and macro domains at the clinical, professional, organization, and system level [27]. More specifically, functional integration refers to mechanisms by which financing, organizational and informational management systems are structured around the primary process of service delivery to coordinate and support accountability between organizations [27]. Indices of functional integration were found in 9/13 studies. A variety of human resource management structures were employed, where community partners or representatives were included in joint planning and hiring processes [52, 54, 56, 62, 65]. Each of these studies, except for one [62] resulted in positive stakeholder perspectives where community engagement ranged between involvement, collaboration, and shared leadership. Despite the early inclusion of community members as central to the planning process, van Dijk et al. [62], noted misalignment between micro-level ‘bottom up’ initiatives with top-down incentives. This study points to the continual and iterative orientation that is required overtime amongst all stakeholders to re-establish commitment and shared understanding.

Moreover, studies that contracted community organizations as a third party to complete outreach and engagement work garnered negative stakeholder perspectives, utilizing outreach and consultation as a form of community engagement. Precisely, this refers to community organizations being engaged as an afterthought to support community engaged activity, rather than from the outset to mobilize a shared vision. Of note, all stakeholders in these arrangements spoke to power imbalances within their projects as they progressed [53, 55, 58, 59]. Although few projects demonstrated regular feedback and performance indicators [54, 56], recognition of their importance to conduct quality improvement and celebrate success was noted [57, 63]. Projects that had regular feedback mechanisms reported positive perspectives from stakeholders [54, 56], whereas the development of joint evaluative frameworks, with community partners, to address soft versus hard performance indicators was seen as a necessary endeavor to create neutral ground among cross-sector partnerships [57, 63]. In addition, to support the complex and tailored approaches that integrated care requires, several studies with negative stakeholder perspectives reflected on the need for a collective governance framework to guide professionals toward developing mutually agreed upon goals to sustain relationships in urban and rural cross-sector partnerships [55, 62].

Normative integration (macro-level)

Normative integration refers to the development and maintenance of a common frame of reference between organizations, individuals, and professional groups. Elements of normative integration can be established through a shared vision, reliable behaviour, visionary leadership, and linking diverse values and norms within the integrated service model [27]. As described earlier, normative integration is at the crux of all integrated care efforts. Evidence indicates that when normative integration is focal, advances within health system collaborative processes are observed [68]. Although normative integration was referred to explicitly in only one study [62], an abundance of shared stakeholder attributes, values and desires presented across the studies. Specifically, the concept of trust was found in 9/13 studies and was seen as a multilayered construct, influenced by the development of a shared vision, influenced by reliable behaviour and leadership styles. Cicognani et al. [52] found that trust was associated with health partnership performance, and tied to efficacy, commitment, and a sense of belonging within community. Similarly, studies that exemplified the deepening of previously established relationships overtime reported the presence of trust in all levels and systems of care integration [24, 54, 56].

Among all studies, trust was coveted but unachievable in some settings due to power inequities, the temporal nature of some projects, equitable distribution of finances, and lack of time or investment with community partners from the outset [53, 55, 59, 61]. Each of these studies spoke to lessons learned and as a result, viewed trust as moving and perhaps unachievable target to continually strive toward in community based cross-sector integrated care. Of particular significance, front-line service providers and CHWs’ described their trust as waning toward project partners when they were left in precarious and isolated positions as the ‘community representative’ in studies with negative stakeholder perspectives. In these circumstances, front-line workers represent both the community and project objectives, hence the utmost care must be taken to support co-design and inclusivity so to not fracture the trust of the community from the outset.

The importance of being flexible, reinventing roles as needed, and transcending domain perceptions was highlighted by several studies, which speaks to the extent agreements are upheld in integrated service models [24, 54, 56, 58, 62]. Plausibility to transcend one’s professional domain perceptions comes with good governance, partnership security, and visionary leadership, however few studies identified such a leader [54, 56]. Lastly, integrated care projects within studies were enacted without a sense of urgency identified and conducted between a two to eight-year timeframe, with two years being the average length of time. Studies in this review demonstrate that there does not appear to be a direct correlation between project duration, level of community engagement, and stakeholder perception. In this regard, positive stakeholder perspectives were found in studies that were two, six and eight years in length. Further research is needed to explore the relationship between length of integrated care projects on the impact of stakeholder perspectives and community engagement.

Among the studies, there was a direct relationship between the level of community engagement and the resulting perceptions of stakeholders. Specifically, the further along the continuum of community engagement, the more positive were stakeholders’ perceptions of their integrated care experiences (Table 3). Lessons learnt offered by stakeholders provide a constructive view of the collaborative process known to integrative care and opportunities to evaluate intermediate outcomes. These are sentinel reflections by stakeholders given the importance of creating a collective attitude and shared vision at the beginning of a partnership to support momentum, longevity, and integration of existing partnerships [54, 65]. Discussion

The purpose of this integrative review was to identify what is known about the influence of community engagement on stakeholder perspectives involved in cross-sector integrated care initiatives. Thirteen studies were identified that explored this concept from North America, Europe, and the United Kingdom. Through this methodology and data analysis, new information has been uncovered which provides new insight and relevance for providers in health and social services, at the clinical, organizational and system level. Positive stakeholder perspectives were found in six studies, moderate or mixed stakeholder perspectives were found in two studies, and negative stakeholder perspectives were found in five studies. All studies supported the client through a population health focus, yet only half the studies attempted to scale client care and address ongoing needs through micro, meso, and macro system levels [24, 54, 56, 62, 63, 65]. According to several studies, the focus on clinical versus population health programming was detrimental through increased reliance on a small group of front-line service providers, namely CHWs, to bridge notable gaps in care and serve as problem solvers with their unique interpersonal skill sets [55], [59, 61]). This suggests that there is merit in solidifying, expanding, and amplifying the role and cadre of nonclinical providers, like CHWs, in integrated services to address the structural and social determinants of health specific to communities, and advance social capital through health equity approaches [69].

Formalities established at the beginning of collaborative care partnerships are integral to create conditions of trust, conflict, and efficacy that become resources or liabilities during continued efforts [34]. However, it appears an overemphasis on ‘setting the ground rules’ in early collaborative process may overshadow the continual work that is required as collaborative process ensue to align values and shared vision. Several studies reflected on this through their lessons learned and highlighted the importance of empowering all those involved in cross-sector integrated care to address wayward team function as projects evolve, not only in the early stages [53, 57, 59]. This leads to question, who is at the table in these early conversations, who is brought in as programs unfold, and how can cross-sector integrated care partners realign once mistakes are made?

The importance of governance as it relates to the perspectives of stakeholders within community-centred integrated care cannot be understated. Studies that established a commitment toward governance from all stakeholders at the outset demonstrated resoundingly positive perspectives through community centred co-design [52, 54, 56, 65]. Interestingly, studies that involved less than five stakeholders were able to utilize and rely on a distributive leadership style [54], whereas studies that reflected the multisectoral nature of integrated care, requested the emergence of one leader, to act as a facilitator [57, 63]). This was particularly important in new intersectoral partnerships, where relationships and trust had yet to be established [53, 55, 59, 61]. This finding is of interest and merits further exploration as to why a single leader would be chosen to govern these new partnerships, particularly considering international calls to advance distributive leadership in integrated care. Distributive leadership has been increasingly recognized as a method where multiple actors work together across professional and organizational boundaries to achieve people centred and integrated health services [70]. Further, distributive leadership is an approach that recognizes the importance of spontaneous collaborations among intuitive working relationships [71, 72]. While seen to be an advantageous approach for instilling collective governance in complex health systems, it appears that the practice of distributive leadership is still in its infancy in cross-sector integrated care practices.

The opportunity for program partners to forge relationships, plan longitudinally, and develop trust was also evident in studies that included committed and diverse stakeholder representation, municipal or region representation, and funding security [52, 54, 56, 65]. Considering the integral role that hospitals play in rural and urban health outcomes, the fact they were included in only two studies is a cause for greater reflection. This dynamic can be captured in a quote from a community partner in one study, ‘the hospital gets recognition, the community gets served’ [55]. Herein lies the foundation of community-centred integrated care. The very inequities that integrated care is attempting to solve can be easily recreated in project work when inadequate attention is paid to power imbalances within partnerships and structures themselves [53, 55, 57–59, 61]. Lastly, there is need to develop joint evaluative frameworks to assist with evaluation, share educational resources, and training programs to support the institutionalization within cross-sector integrated care [56, 57, 63]. These are salient findings when exploring dimensions for collaborative governance within integrated care, put forth by Ansell and Gash [34]. The evaluation of intermediate outcomes is a key dimension of Ansell and Gash [34] collaborative governance process and used as method to help illustrate group progress. Ideally, determinants for outcome design are crafted during the ‘institutional design’ where basic protocol and ground rules are established. Nevertheless, a framework that allows cross-sector partners to jointly establish outcome measurements and set shared goals would be of great service and thus requires greater attention.

Implications for research and theory

Over the past three decades, integrated care has taken strides toward blending community health and social services models, improving quality of care using patient centred measures, and addressing underlying cultural attitudes and norms within specific populations [73]. Yet, the reality of establishing collaborative interagency relationships with communities as partners is a challenging, long term and complex process [74]. Stakeholders typically define integrated care around patient needs [23] and research tends to highlight perspectives of single stakeholder groups in analysis rather than cross-sector evaluations. This review contributes to new insights gained in cross-sector integrated care evaluation and highlights the lack of hospital perspectives as a key partner in collaborative care. Hospitals traditionally have focused on vertical or disease specific pathways, observing hospital to home transitions [75, 76], [77]). Studies that have explored horizontal hospital based integrated care are situated in austerity, reflecting opportunities to improve efficiency through cost saving measures [78–80], rather than from a population health perspective. Considering the expansive resources that hospitals offer and potential reach with communities, the hospitals’ role as cross-sector integrated care partner requires further evaluation, as does their influence on proximal and distal relationships with collaborative teams.

Behaviour is a key determinant of individual and collective action. Multiple theorists over the past decades have put forth behaviour change theories and models to describe human decision making, information processing, and related influences [81]. Moreover, they consider the wide range of psychological, social, societal, and contextual factors that influence one’s behaviour development and change over time. Findings from this integrative review support tenets of social cognitive theory (SCT), which considers the unique way individuals acquire and maintain behaviour within social environments [82]. The notion of trust amongst stakeholders as a recurring theme in this review highlights the merit of SCT’s concept of self-efficacy. Trust is seen as integral to building efficacy and has the potential to generate social capital within communities grounded in these constructs [83]. To greater explore the influence of trust and self-efficacy within cross-sector integrated care contexts, a variety of determinant frameworks, situated in behavioural science, would be well adept evaluative tools. Examples include Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services [84], the Transtheoretical Domains Framework [85], Capabilities, Opportunities and Motivations for Behaviour Change [86] and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [87].

Implications for policy and practice

Health systems globally are reorienting services to achieve quadruple aim outcomes [88]. Perspectives from stakeholders in this review support calls to promote collaborative governance models that are situated in trust, collaborative commitment, and a distributive leadership style. The influence that policy makers have on this work is immense. In this regard, policy makers are encouraged to honour enduring collaborative governance structures among health and social service organizations, which is truly essential in the establishment of trust, equity, and sustainable integrated care practice [12].

Considering the benefits and necessity of collaborative care are increasingly recognized across health systems, a significant opportunity presents for hospitals, community health and social care organizations to devise collective strategies for partnership. Creation of joint evaluative frameworks and collaborative training programs, with community partners, to support knowledge transfer and establish leadership within community-centred integrated care are needed [89]. Organizations and institutions involved in integrated care would benefit in viewing themselves as a complex and adaptive system, with agility and readiness to respond to local circumstance, through reliance on emergent ways of thinking and sense making [90]. Moreover, when organizations recognize their role as a collective learning partner, effective cross-sector partnerships emerge [2]. Despite well intentions of leveraging CBPR to develop equitable partnership between academic researchers and community members, review findings demonstrate this is not always the case. The foundation of community-centred integrated care lies in recognizing where unearned advantage and disadvantage are placed within social structures. Those involved in cross-sector integrated care need to recognize their positions of privilege and work in solidarity to collectively act against systems of inequity [91]. Organizations and institutions embarking on cross-sector integrated care would benefit in exploring principles of critical allyship with community partners and acknowledge the possibility of institutionalized tension before the collaboration process begins [12].

Limitations and strengths

One of the strengths of this study is the unique combination of the RMIC conceptual framework and taxonomy with the CEC for coding and analysis. The widespread use and recognition of the RMIC as validated framework adds value to the expanding body of research on normative integration included in this study and current literature. Limitations of this study include that data extraction was completed by one researcher, however the coding review process through matrices was systematic and repeated three times in sequence. In addition, only English language studies were reviewed, as it is the primary working language of this study’s authors. Another limitation of this study is that all 13 studies analyzed were predominately written from an academic perspective. In one of the studies, an academic author also identified as a community member, however given the lack of community or professional authorship, hard conclusions regarding stakeholder’s perspectives of community-centered integrated care are challenging to deduce. This could be a topic for additional research. Namely, exploring the influence of community members as researchers on perceived self-efficacy and resultingly, their motivation to build social capital.

Conclusion

This review identified the collective influence community engagement has on stakeholder perspectives involved in cross-sector integrated care. Positive stakeholder perspectives were noted to a greater degree when the community is engaged in a collaborative sense or when shared leadership ensues. Diverse stakeholder management, government and political support, secure funding structures, collaborative governance, and distributive leadership were seen to foster the trust and build relationships necessary for positive stakeholder experiences in cross-sector integrated care. Community-centred integrated care requires a multifaceted approach, situated in critical allyship, to advance population health outcomes through research, education, policy, and practice. This review demonstrates the need to further evaluate the roles that hospitals play in micro, meso, and macro level community-centred integrated care, and their impacts and perspectives as integrated care partners. Further research is also required to support the creation of tools and frameworks cross-sector partners may use when devising joint proximal and distal outcome assessment. Lastly, additional research is required to explore optimal methods for realignment of shared values and vision that are often required in the collaborative process trajectory. The application of the Rainbow Model of Integrated Care within this paper provides a foundation to explore the experience of community-centred integrated care among diverse stakeholders in novel and established programs. Such findings will continue to advance our understanding of core tenants needed to enact equitable community-centred integrated care to promote community health and well-being.
