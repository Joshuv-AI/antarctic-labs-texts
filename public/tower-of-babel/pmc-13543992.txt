Zein-based ternary complex stabilized oleogels for saturated fat reduction: fabrication, characterization, and application in chocolate spread
Authors: Qian Zhang, Qiming Wang, Zhenan Rao, Xiaojuan Lei, Jichun Zhao, Lin Lei, Kaihong Zeng, Jian Ming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solid fats in chocolate spreads contribute high levels of saturated fatty acids, posing cardiovascular health concerns. This study developed zein/sodium caseinate/soybean soluble polysaccharide ternary nanoparticle-stabilized oleogels via emulsion templating to replace butter in chocolate spreads. Increasing nanoparticle concentration (2–6 wt%) reduced droplet size (100 → 0.4 μm) and enhanced oil-binding capacity (89.5 → 99.8%), attributed to β-crystalline domains, hydrogen bonding, and interfacial layers. Oleogels showed viscoelastic solid behavior with shear-thinning and thixotropic recovery (>50%). Spreads with ≤50% oleogel substitution exhibited comparable color (ΔE < 1.4) and texture to butter controls, while improving the ΣPUFA/ΣSFA ratio (0.89 vs. 0.41 in commercial spread). Findings demonstrate protein-based oleogels as feasible fat alternatives, though long-term stability and in vivo effects need validation.

Introduction

Chocolate spreads are widely consumed by consumers worldwide. This product can serve as a delicious confectionery item or be used by the food industry as a filling ingredient in formulations such as biscuits and cakes. Solid fat is a crucial component of chocolate spreads, with fat content reaching up to 60% (Fernandes Almeida et al., 2024). The texture and sensory properties of chocolate spreads are closely related to their solid fat content (Fayaz et al., 2017). However, solid fats contain high levels of saturated and trans fatty acids. Due to health concerns associated with cocoa butter and other saturated fats (Wang et al., 2022), researchers have begun exploring alternative approaches to reduce fat content in chocolate spreads while maintaining low production costs and improving sensory/textural properties under reduced saturated fatty acid conditions. Notably, directly replacing solid fats with liquid oils may significantly compromise product performance and quality (Fernandes Almeida et al., 2024). With growing consumer awareness of balanced diets, the food industry urgently needs to develop optimized fat formulations that avoid negative impacts on chocolate spread quality characteristics.

An emerging strategy involves using oleogelation technology to transform liquid oils (e.g., vegetable oils) into solid fat-like materials as partial/total replacements for saturated fat-rich solid fats in chocolate spreads. Several oleogelators have been reported for chocolate spread formulations, including carnauba wax (Roufegarinejad et al., 2024), shellac (Patel et al., 2014), monoglycerides (Fayaz et al., 2017), and rice bran wax (Prakansamut et al., 2024). However, most of these oleogelators belong to aliphatic compounds and their derivatives, limiting their food applications (Zhao et al., 2023). Moreover, their preparation typically requires high temperatures and mechanical agitation, which may induce oil oxidation. To date, limited research exists on biopolymer-based oleogels for chocolate spreads. Bascuas et al. (2021) developed chocolate spreads using hydroxypropyl methylcellulose and xanthan gum-based oleogels as solid fat replacements. Similarly, Wu et al. (2024) incorporated methylcellulose and xanthan gum-based oleogels into chocolate spreads, significantly enhancing their textural and rheological properties. However, no studies have investigated protein-based oleogels in chocolate spreads. Our previous research demonstrated the potential of zein-based oleogels as fat replacers in food applications (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). To our knowledge, no systematic investigation has focused on protein-based Pickering emulsion-templated oleogels for chocolate spread applications. Therefore, this study focuses on exploring the application of zein-based oleogels as fat substitutes in chocolate spreads.

Zein, a natural whole-grain protein extracted from corn, exhibits amphiphilicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability (Ren et al., 2022). It can form oleogels via Pickering emulsion templating. However, low solubility due to aggregation/precipitation limits its application in colloidal systems (Li et al., 2019). Sun et al. (2018) found that zein‑sodium caseinate-polyglycolic acid ternary nano-coordination showed superior stability and unique properties as natural interfacial stabilizers for emulsions compared to binary complexes. This suggests that oleogels prepared with ternary nanoparticles may possess improved gel network structures. Sodium caseinate (NaCas), a water-soluble milk protein, serves as an effective natural emulsifier to stabilize zein-based nanoparticles and emulsions (Patel et al., 2010). Soluble soybean polysaccharide (SSPS), a natural anionic polysaccharide containing hydrophobic protein fragments (∼6.3%), can promote protein-polysaccharide complexation and form denser interfacial layers around oil droplets, providing enhanced steric stabilization (Ding & Yao, 2013). Sun et al. (2018) found that, compared with binary nanoparticles, ternary nanoparticles composed of zein, sodium caseinate, and polyethylene glycol ester exhibited higher stability and unique properties as natural interfacial stabilizers for emulsions. Therefore, it is speculated that oleogels prepared from the ternary composite may also possess a well-developed gel network structure. Yan et al. (2024) produced oleogels with an oil binding capacity of 98.4% by combining egg white protein, gallic acid, and xanthan gum, and all the oleogels exhibited strong gel network structures. In addition, resveratrol, as a natural antioxidant, has positive effects on inhibiting lipid peroxidation. In this study, natural antioxidant components such as resveratrol were added to the liquid oil with the aim of improving the lipid oxidation stability of the oleogels, thereby providing broader application prospects for the use of oleogels as substitutes for solid fats.

Despite extensive studies on wax- and monoglyceride-based oleogels for spreads, systematic investigations on protein-based Pickering emulsion-templated oleogels are lacking. In this study, we fabricated Z/N/SNPs-stabilized oleogels through the emulsion template method and evaluated their performance as butter replacers in chocolate spreads. We systematically characterized emulsion and oleogel properties-droplet size, rheology, microstructure, thermal behavior, texture, processing stability, and oxidative stability. Subsequently, chocolate spreads with 0–100% oleogel substitution (based on total weight) were analyzed for color, rheology, texture, flavor (electronic nose), sensory acceptance, and fatty acid composition. The research gap addressed here is the lack of systematic data on protein-based oleogels in spreads, particularly regarding the interplay between ternary nanoparticle concentration, network interactions, and their translation to product quality. The zein-based ternary complex was confirmed to maintain structural integrity, thus enabling a reduction in saturated fat content without compromising sensory characteristics.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sodium caseinate (NaCaS), Soluble soybean polysaccharide (SSPS), Resveratrol (purity 95%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Zein was purchased from Hefei Bomer Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). According to previous studies, zein was primarily composed of α-zein (Wang et al., 2024). Corn oil was purchased from local supermarkets (Chongqing, China). Commercial chocolate spread was purchased from Seasir (Shanghai, China). Butter was purchased from Angie's Yeast Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China).

Preparation of Zein/NaCaS/SSPS ternary nanoparticles (Z/N/SNPs)

Z/N/SNPs prepared by pH-driven method (Yuan et al., 2021). The prepared composite nanoparticles were subjected to particle size, transmission electron microscopy, contact angle, Fourier infrared and x-ray diffraction. A thorough account of the experimental methodology was described in “Supporting Information: Fabrication and characterization of Z/N/SNPs (DOC)”.

Emulsion templates and oleogels preparation

The lyophilised Z/N/SNPs powders were dissolved in deionised water at 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 wt%. Resveratrol at 0.2 mg/ml was first added to the corn oil to mix with the nanoparticle solution at 40% of the fixed oil fraction and then processed in a 600 W cell crusher for 4 min to obtain an emulsion. The emulsion was air-dried in a fume hood at 25 °C for 72 h until reaching a consant weight and then homogenised using a JYL-C50T grinder for 1 min to obtain oleogel.

Characteristics of emulsions

Average droplet size of emulsions

The droplet size distribution of the emulsions was determined using a MasterSizer 3000 (Malvern, UK),with pure water as the dispersing medium and a refractive index of 1.450 for the emulsion.

Rheological measurements

Rheological measurements were analyzed by rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar Co., Austria) (Wang et al., 2024). Experimental details were seen in “Supporting Information: Rheological determination of Pickering emulsion template (DOC)”.

Microstructure observation

Briefly, the emulsion was imaged using an Olympus BX43 microscope with a magnification of 100.

Characteristics of oleogels

The morphology of the oleogels

The microstructural features of the oleogels were characterized by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Model LSM710, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) according to the procedure reported by Wang et al. (2024). Experimental details were seen in “Supporting Information: CLSM analysis measurements (DOC)”.

XRD analysis

The samples were analyzed at 25 °C by an X-ray diffractometer (Panat, The Netherlands). The measurement conditions were based on those reported by Chen et al. (2025), with minor adaptations. The test conditions comprised a Cu-Kα radiation source and 2θ angular scanning, with a range of 5.0–55.0° and a scanning step size of 0.02°.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The chemical structures of pure oil, resveratrol, nanoparticles and oleogels were obtained by FTIR (Perkin-Elmer, USA) with a scanning range of 600–4000 cm−1.

Functional properties of oleogels

Rheological characterization

The rheological properties of oleogel samples was determined using a rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar Co., Austria) with a gap height of 1 mm. Amplitude sweeps (stress = 0.01–1%, frequency = 1 Hz), frequency sweeps (0.1–10 Hz, stress = 0.01%), flow behavior (shear rate = 1–100 s−1) and thixotropic responses (first stage: 0.1 s−1, 120 s; second stage: 10 s−1, 60 s; third stage: 0.1 s−1, 180 s) were measured at 25 °C. The temperature responsiveness was measured with a temperature ramp from 20 °C to 80 °C and back to 20 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min.

Thermal behaviors observation

The samples were heated from 30 °C to 600 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C/min using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Instrument, Delaware, USA).

Texture properties

The texture properties of oleogels were calculated by a texture analyzer (TA. XT Plus990000, UK)(Zhu et al., 2024). Test conditions for texture are 5.0 mm/s pre-test, 1.0 mm/s test, and 50% deformation.

The processing stability of oleogels

Oil-binding capacity (OBC). Oleogel samples (1 g) were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min (Meng et al., 2018). After inversion to drain free oil, OBC was calculated as:OBC%=m−m1−m2m×100%where m: sample mass; m1: total mass pre-centrifugation; m2: mass post-draining.

Storage Stability. Oleogels were stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for 28 days, with macroscopic, rheological, and OBC analyses at days 0/14/28.

Freeze-thaw stability. Samples underwent 4 cycles of freezing (−20 °C/24 h) and thawing (25 °C/4 h), followed by macroscopic/rheological/OBC characterization post-cycle 4.

The oxidative stability of oleogels

Determination of peroxide value (PV) and TBA value was carried out according to the procedure described by Ma et al. (2023), with slight adjustments. Corn oil (Oil), corn oil with added resveratrol (Oil-R), and oleogel without added resveratrol (Ole) were all placed in the dark at 50 °C as a control with the oleogel (Ole-R) to promote oxidative reactions, and the samples were periodically removed for oxidation experiments to be determined. Experimental details of the peroxide value (PV) TBA value were seen in “Supporting Information: Oxidative stability of oleogels (DOC)”.

Preparation and quality determination of chocolate spreads

Preparation of chocolate spreads

The chocolate spread was produced according to the method described by Bascuas et al. (2021) with slight modifications. For each sample, a total of 60 g of chocolate spread was prepared using the following recipe: 35% butter, 22% skimmed milk, 30% icing sugar, 8% cocoa powder, and 5% skimmed milk powder. On a total weight replacement basis (w/w, i.e., equal weight substitution), butter was replaced by oleogel at levels of 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, respectively. The samples were named as follows: substitution 0% (0O/100B): 0% oleogel +100% butter; substitution 25% (25O/75B): 25% oleogel +75% butter; substitution 50% (50O/50B): 50% oleogel +50% butter; substitution 75% (75O/25B): 75% oleogel +25% butter; substitution 100% (100O/0B): 100% oleogel +0% butter. In the example codes, O stands for Oleogels and B for butter.

Initially, the sugar, cocoa and skimmed milk powder were stirred with the skimmed milk in a water bath at 60 °C for 10 min until thoroughly mixed and removed from the water to cool to ambient temperature. The butter/oleogel mixture was then added and mixed with a hand-held homogeniser at 2 (∼900 rpm) for 2 min before increasing the speed to 4 (∼1100 rpm) for 1 min. Afterwards, the chocolate spreads were stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis the following day.

Color and microscopic observation

The color of the chocolate spread was measured with a colourimeter (UltraScan PRO, HunterLab, USA) according to the L* a* b* color system. The total color difference ∆E* is denoted by ∆E∗=∆L∗2+∆a∗2+∆b∗2. CLSM is used to observe the microstructure of chocolate spread. Experimental details were seen in “Supporting Information: CLSM analysis measurements (DOC)”.

Macrostructure studies (mechanical properties): Rheological and instrumental textural measurements

Frequency sweeps (constant strain of 0.01%, frequency range 1–10 Hz) and flux measurements (shear rate variation from 1 to 100 s−1) of the samples were performed by a MCR302 rheometer (Anton Paar, Germany). Parallel plates of 25 cm were taken with a gap height of 1 mm and a test temperature of 25 °C. Textural analysis was conducted employing a texture analyzer under preset conditions: 5.0 mm/s pre-test, 1.0 mm/s test speed, and 25% deformation.

Flavor and sensory analysis

Sample flavor was collected by an electronic nose system (Shanghai Baosheng Industrial Development Co., Ltd., China). Sensor cleaning time 100 s, injection time 60 s, gas flow rate 1 l/min. The response substances for each sensor in the electronic nose are shown in Revised Table S1.

Sensory evaluation (including color, fragrance, state of organisation, spreadability and overall acceptability) was conducted utilising a hedonic 10-point scale, where 1 represented the least preferred option and 10 represented the most preferred option, according to Shahamati et al. (2024). Written/verbal informed consent was obtained from twenty voluntarily recruited students at Southwest University prior to the sensory testing. The sensory protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of College of Food Science, Southwest University (IRB No. HF20250706; see Certificate in Supplementary Materials).

Fatty acid composition

The fatty acid (FA) composition of oleogels/butter chocolate spreads and commercial chocolate spreads was analyzed using gas chromatography (GC) (Lu et al., 2023). Firstly, total lipids were extracted from the respective samples. Subsequently, fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were prepared by adding boron trifluoride-methanol solution heated for 2 min at 80 °C in a water bath. After cooling to room temperature, saturated NaCl solution and n-hexane were added to facilitate phase separation. Finally, the upper organic solvent layer was collected for FA composition determination. The FA composition of the extracted lipids was then analyzed by GC (Agilent 7890B，Agilent Technologies，USA) using an instrument equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). FAs were identified by matching retention times to those of a FAME standard mixture (GLC NESTLE 37 MIX).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed for sample variability by ANOVA one-way ANOVA and Duncan's test using SPSS 23.0 software, with the significance level set at P < 0.05; plotted using Origin 2021.

Results and discussion

Particle size,wettability analysis of ternary nanoparticles

The stability and rheological properties of Pickering emulsions were governed by the size and charge density of the nanoparticle (Li et al., 2019). And as demonstrated in Fig. S1A, the Z/N/SNPs exhibited a particle of 108 ± 1.25 nm and ζ-potential of −18.83 mV. Meanwhile, the lower PDI (< 0.3) and the single-peak distribution indicated that the nanoparticles were well dispersed. TEM images (Revised Fig. S1B) showed that Zein NPs were spherical but with uneven size and aggregation, whereas Z/N/SNPs were spherical with a core-shell structure and uniform distribution. Li et al. (2020) demonstrated that negatively charged SSPS self-assembled to coat the surface of in positively charged zein nanoparticles.

Particle wettability critically influenced emulsion stability. The three-phase contact angle (θ) of Z/N/SNPs measured 86.66 ± 1.19° (Revised Fig. S1C), near the optimal 90° for Pickering stabilization (Wang et al., 2016). In contrast, unmodified zein particles showed strong hydrophobicity (θ = 114.90 ± 5.40°) due to >50% hydrophobic amino acid content (Li et al., 2021). The hydrophilic groups of SSPS enhanced zein's water affinity (Yuan et al., 2021), and the near-neutral wettability indicated that the Z/N/SNPs were good stabilizers for Pickering emulsions (Meng et al., 2020).

Particles size, microstructure and rheological analysis of emulsions

The emulsion was completely dispersed in water and showed spherical in oil droplets (Revised Fig. 1A), thereby confirming the emulsion type was O/W emulsion. As observed in Revised Fig. 1B–C and Revised Fig. S2, the emulsion droplets (D[4,3]) were spherical in shape, with a size range from 0.4 μm to 100 μm. And the D[4,3] of the emulsion decreased with increasing Z/N/SNPs concentration, which may be attributed to the stable interfacial coverage provided by higher concentrations of Z/N/SNPs at the oil–water interface, thereby preventing the dispersed oil droplets from coalescing. At 2 wt% Z/N/SNPs, bimodal distribution and droplet aggregation indicated incomplete interface coverage (Revised Fig. S2). Monodisperse droplets were obtained by increasing the nanoparticle concentration to 4–6 wt%. This result suggested that the concentration of Z/N/SNPs had a strong effect on the emulsion particle size due to more nanoparticles attaching to the interfacial layer and forming a stronger coverage magnitude (Zhu et al., 2024). Rheological properties could reflect the structure and flow characteristics of Pickering emulsions. As shown in Revised Fig. 1D, all emulsions had a LVR of <10% strain, and within the LVR the samples had a higher energy storage modulus (G′) than loss modulus (G″) and appeared as solid-like gels (Zhu et al., 2019). The G′ of emulsions increased with increasing Z/N/SNPs concentration, indicating that the gel network generated by the composite nanoparticles improved the ability of the emulsion to resist external deformation. The frequency scanning results (Revised Fig. 1E) showed that the gel strength of the emulsion had a low frequency dependence. It could be seen from Revised Fig. 1F that all emulsions had shear thinning phenomenon, and the viscosity of the emulsion increased with the increase of nanoparticle concentration. Similarly, a study by Xu et al. (2024) indicated that an increase in nanoparticle concentration promoted the formation of more entangled network structures, which inhibited droplet aggregation and led to an increase in emulsion viscosity. Thus, Z/N/SNPs concentration can well control the viscoelastic behavior and stability of emulsions.

Characteristics of oleogels

The morphology of oleogels

As shown in Revised Fig. 2A, as the water in the emulsion evaporated, the volume of the dried product shrank to form a solid-like sample. From the macroscopic morphology, all the oleogel samples were yellow in color, and the oleogels showed better self-supporting and extrusion properties with the increase of Z/N/SNPs concentration. CLSM revealed compact oil droplets (red) encapsulated by nanoparticle networks (green; Revised Fig. 2B). Interestingly, the oil droplets became larger after drying the emulsion template, and the oil droplets in the oleogel were mostly non-spherical, which was due to the deformation of the oil droplets caused by the water evaporating and squeezing the oil droplets (Wang et al., 2022). As the concentration of Z/N/SNPs increased, the red oil droplets became smaller and more homogeneous, indicating that the network structure became denser and more capable of tightly wrapping the oil. XRD allowed further elucidation of the crystal morphology of oleogels, which was essential for understanding the structural and physical properties of the oleogel. All the oleogels exhibited broader peaks around 19.6°, i.e., d = 4.5 Å (Revised Fig. 3B), which is indicative of the presence of β-polycrystalline crystals in the oleogels. These crystals are associated with good thermal stability, a high melting point, and a triclinic subunit cell arrangement (Chen et al., 2024). Functional properties of oleogels

Rheological properties

Flow tests, frequency scans, amplitude scans, thixotropy tests and temperature scans were used to characterise the rheological behavior of the oleogels. Consistent with the macroscopic morphology, all oleogels showed elastic solid properties (Revised Fig. 4A), and with the increase of Z/N/SNPSs concentration, both G′ and G″ increased, and the mechanical strength and structural stability of the prepared oleogels increased. The higher G′ values of the oleogel systems than the corresponding emulsion systems, indicating the formation of a denser network structure. In the 0.1–10 Hz range, the samples' G′ values showed a weak frequency dependence with a small change in frequency increase, indicating that the oleogels had strong mechanical strength (Revised Fig. 4B), which was similar to other oleogels formed by the emulsion templating method (Li et al., 2023). As shown in Revised Fig. 4C, all oleogel samples exhibited shear-thinning behavior, a phenomenon attributable to progressive disruption of their structural networks under elevated shear conditions. Thixotropic reversibility denoted the ability of oleogel networks to exhibit viscosity reduction under high shear rates and regained viscosity at rest, serving as a critical parameter for evaluating their properties (Naderi et al., 2024). As shown in Revised Fig. 4D, the recovery rate of the oleogels exceeded 50%, indicating the good structural recovery of the oleogel (Meng et al., 2018). As the concentration of Z/N/SNPs increased, the degree of recovery of oleogel viscosity increased. The reason was that the high concentration of composite nanoparticles forms a stronger reversible elastic gel network, which results in better damage resistance to strong external forces (Chen et al., 2024). The temperature scan plot (Revised Fig. 4E) showed the effect of temperature variation (20 to 80 °C) on the viscoelasticity of the oleogels (Wang et al., 2022). Within this temperature range, all samples demonstrated higher storage moduli (G′) than loss moduli (G″), confirming the dominance of elastic behavior in oleogels. The temperature-dependent decline in G′ values suggested progressive weakening of hydrogen bonds between nanoparticles and oil molecules, leading to structural deformation (Wang et al., 2024). Whereas, the G′ value rebounded with decreasing temperature. In other studies, oleogels had been observed to have similar thermal reversibility (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). The results suggested that the oleogels prepared by Z/N/SNPs had reversible structural damage and recovery and could be used as a substitute for butter or cream.

Thermal properties

The thermogravimetric results of the oleogel were displayed in Revised Fig. 4F, where the weight loss of the oleogel occurred in three stages. The initial thermal event (0–175 °C) was primarily attributed to the evaporation of both internal and surface moisture, with negligible oleogel mass loss (≤1.5%) confirming effective residual water elimination during pre-drying. Subsequent weight stability ceased at 175 °C, transitioning to a second degradation phase (175–250 °C) aligning with established vegetable oil thermal decomposition profiles (Ding et al., 2019). At 250 °C, the residual mass of all the oleogels was close to 100%, indicating that the physical barrier provided by the three-dimensional network of oleogels was sufficient to retard oil degradation. In the final stage, significant weight loss of the oleogels occurred at 300–500 °C, related to the thermal degradation of proteins and polysaccharides in the composite nanoparticles. In this stage, the polysaccharides and proteins underwent carbonization, the three-site network structure of the oleogel was degraded, and the oil droplets were exposed and rapidly lost (Wang et al., 2022). Similarly, Zou et al. (2022) prepared emulsion-templated oleogels that successfully resisted high temperatures and avoided mass loss. Whether used as carriers or solid fat substitutes, oleogels with high thermal stability could be applied to a wider range of processing procedures and techniques (Tan et al., 2024).

Texture properties

The mechanical behavior of the gels was studied by textural analysis (Revised Table S2). The trend of hardness was consistent with the change in storage modulus observed in the rheological evaluation. When the concentration of Z/N/SNPs in the oleogels was increased from 2 wt% to 6 wt%, the hardness values increased from 81.11 N to 599.21 N respectively. This indicated that the hardness of the oleogel could be adjusted by regulating the concentration of nanoparticles. Meanwhile the properties directly related to taste such as adhesion, elasticity, and chewability showed the same pattern with the addition of nanoparticles as hardness. Cohesion indicated the strength of the internal bonding of a sample and its ability to maintain its integrity when subjected to an external force (Xie et al., 2023).

The processing stability of oleogels

In this study, the processing stability of oleogel samples was assessed by centrifugation, storage, and freeze-thaw treatment. From the visual appearance, the oleogels prepared by 2 wt% and 3 wt% of Z/NSNPs showed more obvious oil leakage by structural collapse after centrifugation treatment, 28 days storage treatment, and three freeze-thaw cycles, while the higher concentration of Z/NSNPs still maintained good solid properties (Revised Fig. S3A and S3C). The OBC of the oleogels (Revised Fig. S3E) increased from 89.5% to 99.8% for the composite nanoparticle contents from 2 wt% to 6 wt%, saturating at 5 wt% nanoparticles. Similar findings were made by Feng et al. (2025) who reported that higher myofibrillar protein content resulted in the formation of a more stable three-dimensional network structure formation, enhancing intermolecular interactions thereby increasing OBC. In addition, the G′ curves of all the samples remained high and higher than the corresponding G″ after treatment (Revised Fig. S3B and S3D), indicating that the oleogels still maintained a good gel structure. The results indicated that the Z/N/SNPs-stabilized oleogels had great physical stability, beneficial food processing and transportation application.

The oxidative stability of oleogels

In order to investigate the different roles of resveratrol and ternary nanoparticles in the resistance to lipid oxidation of oleogels, oxidative stability experiments were carried out (Shuai et al., 2023). As evidenced in Fig. 5A, the significantly slower PV increase in oleogels versus liquid oil systems demonstrated that the gel network effectively suppressed oxidative deterioration through restricted oil mobility and molecular diffusion constraints. At all stages of the storage process, the PV magnitude of the samples was in the order of Oil ≥ Oil-R > Ole > Ole-R, which indicated that the bulk oil with resveratrol added did not show significant antioxidant effects, whereas resveratrol exerted better antioxidant effects in the oleogel system. Bulk oil was directly exposed to air for a long time, and resveratrol was unstable and easily degraded during storage. The oleogel structure encapsulated the oil and the resveratrol in it, enabling it to better perform its antioxidant effects (Chen et al., 2023). As shown in Revised Fig. 5B, the TBA values of the four samples showed the same change rule regarding the secondary reaction products. During lipid oxidation chain propagation, peroxyl radical migration proceeded via collisional transfer between adjacent triglyceride molecules (Zhao et al., 2023). The network structure formed by the composite nanoparticles, however, acted as a physical barrier, disrupting the contact between the triglyceride molecules and preventing oxygen radical transfer, which inhibited the formation of secondary oxidation products (Yan et al., 2024). This phenomenon was in agreement with the results of Chen et al. (2024), who observed that the network structure consisting of soya isolate proteins and monolaurate glycerides effectively restricted the flow of the oil phase and also reduced the contact of the oil with, for example, oxygen, thereby slowing down the oxidation process. Meanwhile the resveratrol encapsulated in the oleogel was oxidized first when it came into contact with oxygen radicals, thus playing a powerful role in delaying the oxidation of the oleogel. Application of oleogels in the chocolate spread

Changes of morphology and color

Fig. S4 showed the macroscopic and CLSM of the chocolate spread. All samples showed a uniform and smooth condition with no delamination or fat precipitation. The chocolate spread paste added with the oleogel showed similar color and appearance to the control sample, and could be evenly applied to the sliced bread. CLSM was used to study the distribution of ingredients in spreads with different oil-gel substitution ratios (Fig. S4B). Lipids in the 0O/100B sample were amorphously distributed among the green proteins. With the increase of the oleogels substitution ratio, the sample showed uniformly distributed spherical fat droplets. The 100O/0B sample showed a tightly packed network of fat droplets, and the interaction between droplets was enhanced, the cohesion was enhanced.

Color could influence consumer perception and was one of the key commercial features of chocolate spread. Color is a critical sensory attribute that influences consumer perception and purchase intention, as lighter shades are often associated with freshness and higher quality in chocolate-based products (Tirgarian et al., 2023). As shown in Revised Table 1, there was no significant difference in L∗, a∗ and b∗ between chocolate spread (25O/75B) and butter chocolate spread (0O/100B) at a substitution level of 25%. ΔE parameter was used to assess the color similarity of the spreads. Chocolate spreads with substitution levels of 25% (25O/75B) and 50% (50O/50B) showed low ΔE values (below 1.4), indicating a high degree of similarity to butter spread (0O/100B). A ΔE value of less than 3 meant that it was difficult for the consumer to detect the color difference (Wang et al., 2025). Changes of rheological behaviors

The frequency scans of cocoa butter prepared with different oleogel substitution ratios were shown in Revised Fig. 5C, in which all the chocolate spread samples showed a consistent trend. Both G′ and G″ were found to be independent of frequency, with G′ consistently greater than G″ for all samples during the frequency scan. This showed that the chocolate spread with the addition of oleogel retained its solid properties and frequency independence (Wang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, as the oleogel substitution ratio increased, a continuous increase in G′ from 1.7 × 103 Pa was observed, and G′ reached 1.0 × 104 Pa for the 100O/0B sample. The chocolate spreads with 25% substitution (25O/75B) and 50% substitution (50O/50B) and butter (0O/100B) showed a more similar viscoelastic solid behavior. Fayaz et al. (2017) used monoglyceride oleogels to partially replace palm oil. The synergistic effect of the molecular affinity between palm oil and the oleogelator, coupled with the formation of hydrogen bonds, stabilized the formation of lamellar crystalline domains, thus improving the viscoelasticity of the chocolate spread. All the chocolate spreads exhibited pseudoplastic (shear thinning) behavior (Revised Fig. 5D), as the apparent viscosity decreased with increasing shear rate.

The texture of chocolate spreads was one of the most important parameters for sensory acceptance. Based on the results shown in Revised Table 1, having 25% substitution (25O/75B) and 50% substitution (50O/50B) better mimicked the textural properties of butter cocoa spreads. In contrast, hardness, viscosity and cohesion were significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the 100% oleogel (100O/0B) and 75% substitution (75O/25B) spread than the other samples. Acan et al. (2021) reported that the smaller the fat droplets, the greater the interaction between the droplets, which limits the mobility of the solid particles in the chocolate spread, leading to higher hardness.

Sensory qualities

As shown in Fig. S5, the radar images of the five chocolate spreads showed similar profiles, indicating that the oil-gel chocolate spreads were similar to the control samples in terms of their main flavor components. The response values of W5S and W1C were significantly higher for the butter chocolate spread than for the other samples, suggesting that the electronic nose was able to effectively differentiate between the flavours of the butter chocolate spread and that of the chocolate spread with the addition of oleogel. Sensor response modes (W1W/W3C/W2S/W6S/W3S) showed comparable volatile profiles for the oleogel replacement spreads (25O/75B, 50O/50B) and the all-butter control group (0O/100B), suggesting that typical aroma substances (hexanal, dimethyl trisulphide) and texture-related volatiles (octanal, nonanal) were retained during the butter replacement process. The flavouring substances in the chocolate spread might include nitrogenous, aromatic compounds, alkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones.

The results of sensory evaluation (Revised Table 1) showed that although the addition of oleogels would slightly reduce the scores of color and flavor, it would make the texture of coated chocolate higher, and all scores were within an acceptable range, which indicated that it was feasible to produce coated chocolate with oleogels instead of butter. Similarly, Shahamati et al. (2024) showed that replacing coconut oil with oleogel did not reduce the overall acceptability of chocolate spreads, which proved to be an effective substitute for solid fats in chocolate spreads.

Changes of FAs

The 50% degree of substitution (50O/50B) oleogel chocolate spread was selected for comparison with the commercially available chocolate spread. The FA composition of the chocolate spread with oleogel added and the commercially available chocolate spread was provided in Table S3. While commercial chocolate spreads were made primarily from cocoa liquor mass and hazelnuts, oleogel chocolate spreads were made from a mixture of corn oil and butter. Compared to commercial chocolate spreads, oleogel chocolate spreads contained a lower fat content. Whereas in the distribution of various fats, commercial chocolate spreads exhibited higher saturated fat content (lauric acid, myristic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid) and monounsaturated fatty acid content (oleic acid), oleogel chocolate spreads exhibited higher polyunsaturated fatty acid content (linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid). These differences in FA composition were caused by differences in the ingredients used in the production of chocolate spread.

The ratio of polyunsaturated fatty acids to saturated fatty acids (∑PUFA/∑SFA) was commonly used to assess the impact of diet on cardiovascular health (CVH), the recommendation was that the ∑PUFA/∑SFA ratio should to be higher than 0.45, or at least 0.4 (Flores et al., 2025). The ΣPUFA/ΣSFA value of 0.89 in oleogel chocolate spread was much higher than that of commercial chocolate spread (0.41). This ratio was quite high in commercially available chocolate spreads at 46.55, reflecting the predominance of omega-6 fatty acids. The ratio of Σn−6/Σn−3 in oleogel chocolate spread was 39.48, which was lower than the ratio in commercially available chocolate spreads. Thus, these results suggested that the 50% oleogel-substituted chocolate spread exhibited a lower saturated fatty acid content and a higher unsaturated fatty acid content compared with the commercially available spread, and that replacing cocoa butter with oleogel might be a viable approach to formulating chocolate spreads with modified fatty acid profiles.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the potential design of protein-based oleogels for application in the preparation of chocolate spread by replacing solid fats, for which an oleogel system based on Zein/NaCaS/SSPS composite nanoparticles was constructed. The results showed that the concentration of the composite nanoparticles significantly affected the structure, texture and rheological properties of the emulsion and the oleogel. 5% (wt) and 6% (wt) Zein/NaCaS/SSPS nanoparticles prepared oleogels with strong oil binding capacity (∼99%) and high mechanical strength. When replacing butter in the preparation of chocolate spreads, oleogels showed good appearance and quality. The structural and organoleptic properties of the oleogel were similar to those of butter-based chocolate spreads when the substitution rate was less than 50%. In addition, the oleogel-substituted (50%) chocolate spread exhibited a fatty acid composition with a higher ΣPUFA/ΣSFA ratio (0.89 vs 0.41 in commercial), which aligns with dietary recommendations for reducing saturated fat intake.

In summary, zein/NaCas/SSPS ternary nanoparticles can stabilize emulsion-templated oleogels with tunable mechanical properties. This study provided a feasible formulation strategy for producing reduced-saturated-fat chocolate spreads with desirable textural properties. It is noteworthy that this study still has certain limitations. Given the differences in fat content and moisture between butter and oleogel, the replacement description in this study is limited to a mass basis; further optimization using a fat-equivalent replacement model is warranted in future studies. In this study, only short-term storage stability was evaluated, and no in vivo or clinical studies were performed to validate the health implications. Future work may prioritize long-term shelf-life assessments, blinded consumer trials, and nutritional intervention investigations.
