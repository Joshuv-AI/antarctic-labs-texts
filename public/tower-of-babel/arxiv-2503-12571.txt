Rafał Filipów, Małgorzata Kowalczuk, Adam Kwela

Abstract
. For each countable ordinal α \alpha , we introduce an ideal conv α \mathrm{conv}_{\alpha} and use it to characterize the class of all compact countable spaces which are homeomorphic to the space ω α ⋅ \omega^{\alpha}\cdot n + 1 n+1 with the order topology. The characterization is expressed in terms of finding a convergent subsequence defined on a set not belonging to conv α \mathrm{conv}_{\alpha} .

Primary: 54A20, 03E05. Secondary: 03E10, 03E15, 03E17, 03E75, 40A05, 54H05.

1.
Introduction

See Sections
2
and
3
for notions and notations used in the introduction.

For an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
, we define the class

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
of all topological spaces
X
having the property that for every sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
X
there exists
A\notin\mathcal{I}
such that the subsequence
(x_{n})_{n\in A}
is convergent in
X
(
[
11
, Definition 1.1]
).

We see that
\FinBW(\mathrm{Fin})
coincides with the class of all sequentially compact spaces, and one can show that
\FinBW(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})
coincides with the class of all finite spaces, whereas
\FinBW(\mathrm{BI})
coincides with the class of all boring spaces (
[
11
, Theorem 6.5]
).
Using the Katětov order one can show
that the above mentioned ideals are critical, in a sense, for considered classes of spaces.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
11
, Theorem 6.5]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\omega
.

(1)

\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathrm{Fin}

\iff

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
coincides with the class of all sequentially compact spaces.

(2)

\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}

\iff

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
coincides with the class of all finite spaces.

(3)

The following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

\mathrm{BI}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
and
\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.

(b)

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
coincides with the class of all boring spaces.

In
[
14
, Section 2.7]
, the author proved that

\mathrm{conv}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff[0,1]\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I}).

Using the above equivalence, one can show that the ideal
\mathrm{conv}
turns out to be critical for uncountable compact metric spaces as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\omega
.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\mathrm{conv}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.

(2)

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
contains an uncountable compact metric space.

(3)

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
contains all compact metric spaces.

In the realm of compact countable spaces, we have the following theorem
of Mazurkiewicz and Sierpiński
which characterizes these spaces in terms of countable ordinals with the order topology.
Note that every compact countable space is metrizable (see e.g.
[
16
, Proposition 8.5.7]
), and consequently it is sequentially compact.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
13
]
, see also
[
16
, Theorem 8.6.10]
)
.

A countable space
X
is compact if and only if

X
is homeomorphic to the space
\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n+1
with the order topology for some countable ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\omega
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
denote the class of all countable compact spaces
and

\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}

denote the class of all compact spaces which are homeomorphic to

\omega^{\beta}\cdot n+1
for some
\beta\leq\alpha
and
n\in\omega
.
By Mazurkiewicz-Sierpiński theorem we obtain the equality:

\mathbb{K}=\bigcup_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}.

The main objective of this paper is to define ideals
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
and prove the following theorem which shows that
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is a critical ideal for
\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}
.

Theorem
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\omega
and
\alpha
be a countable ordinal.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\mathrm{conv}_{(1+\alpha)+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
and
\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.

(2)

\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}.

The next theorem (interesting in its own right) is a crucial ingredient in the proof of the above theorem.

Theorem
.

For any ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
and countable ordinal
\alpha
,

\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\omega^{\alpha}+1\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I}).

The critical ideals
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
are defined in Section
3
where we also prove some basic properties of these ideals. The proofs of the above theorems are given in Section
4
.
In Section
5
, we prove that the sequence
(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}
of the critical ideals is strictly decreasing in the Katětov order.
In Section
6
, we show that the sequence
(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}
is critical for the space
\omega_{1}
, but there is no single ideal which is critical for the space
\omega_{1}
.
In Section
7
, we present some additional properties of the critical ideals concerning the property Kat, the property
P^{-}
, Borel complexity and cardinal characteristics.

2.
Preliminaries

All topological spaces considered in the paper are assumed to be Hausdorff.
A sequentially compact space
X
is called
boring
(
[
11
, Definition 3.21]
) if there exists a finite set
F\subseteq X
such that each one-to-one convergent sequence in
X
converges to some point from
F
.

Recall that an ordinal number
\alpha
is equal to the set of all ordinal numbers less than
\alpha
.
In particular, the smallest infinite ordinal number
\omega=\{0,1,\dots\}
is equal to the set of all natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
, and each natural number
n=\{0,\dots,n-1\}
is equal to the set of all natural numbers less than
n
.
Using this identification, we can for instance write
n\in k
instead of
n<k
and
n<\omega
instead of
n\in\omega
or
A\cap n
instead of
A\cap\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
Moreover, for ordinals
\alpha<\beta
, we write

[\alpha,\beta]
to denote the set of all ordinals
\xi
such that
\alpha\leq\xi\leq\beta
, and similarly for
(\alpha,\beta)
and other intervals.
If
A
is a set of ordinals, we write
\ot(A)
to denote the
order type
of
A

i.e. the unique ordinal
\alpha
such that
A
and
\alpha
are isomorphic.

An
ideal
on a set
X
is a nonempty family
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
which is closed under taking finite unions (i.e. if
A,B\in\mathcal{I}
then
A\cup B\in\mathcal{I}
)
and subsets (i.e. if
A\subseteq B
and
B\in\mathcal{I}
then
A\in\mathcal{I}
).
For an ideal
\mathcal{I}
, we write
\mathcal{I}^{+}=\{A\subseteq X:A\notin\mathcal{I}\}
and call it the
coideal of
\mathcal{I}
,
and we write
\mathcal{I}^{*}=\{A\subseteq X:X\setminus A\in\mathcal{I}\}
and call it the
dual filter of
\mathcal{I}
.
An ideal
\mathcal{I}
is
tall
(a.k.a.
dense
) if for every infinite
A\subseteq X
there is an infinite
B\in\mathcal{I}
such that
B\subseteq A
.

By identifying sets of natural numbers with their characteristic functions,
we equip
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
with the topology of the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\omega}
and therefore
we can assign topological complexity to ideals on
\omega
.
In particular, an ideal
\mathcal{I}
is Borel (
F_{\sigma}
, analytic, resp.) if
\mathcal{I}
is Borel (
F_{\sigma}
, analytic, resp.) as a subset of the Cantor space.

Example 2.1
.

(1)

\mathrm{Fin}(X)
is the ideal of all finite subsets of
X
.
We write
\mathrm{Fin}
instead of
\mathrm{Fin}(\omega)
.

(2)

If
\phi:\mathcal{P}(\omega)\to[0,\infty]
is a countably additive measure such that
\phi(\omega)=\infty
, then

\mathcal{I}_{\phi}
given by:

A\in\mathcal{I}_{\phi}\iff\phi(A)<\infty

is a
summable ideal
.
\mathrm{Fin}
is a summable ideal given by the measure
\phi(A)=|A|
for all
A\subseteq\omega
. Each summable ideal is
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
.

(3)

\mathcal{I}_{d}
is the ideal of all subsets of
\omega
of asymptotic density zero:

A\in\mathcal{I}_{d}\iff\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap n|}{n}=0.

It is a
\Pi^{0}_{3}
ideal.

(4)

\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
is the ideal on
\omega\times\omega
defined by

A\in\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}\iff\forall i\,(|\{j:(i,j)\in A\}|<\omega).

It is a
\Pi^{0}_{3}
ideal.

(5)

\mathrm{Fin}^{2}=\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
is the ideal on
\omega\times\omega
defined by

A\in\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}\iff\exists i_{0}\,\forall i\geq i_{0}\,(|\{j:(i,j)\in A\}|<\omega).

It is a
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
ideal.

(6)

\mathrm{BI}
is the ideal on
\omega\times\omega\times\omega

introduced in
[
11
, Definition 4.1]

and defined by

\begin{split}A\in\mathrm{BI}\iff\exists i_{0}\,\left[\right.&\,\,\forall i<i_{0}\,(\,\{(j,k):(i,j,k)\in A\}\in\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})\ \land\\
&\left.\forall i\geq i_{0}\,(|\{(j,k):(i,j,k)\in A\}|<\omega)\right].\end{split}

It is a
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
ideal.

The vertical section of a set
A\subseteq X\times Y
at a point
x\in X
is defined by
A_{(x)}=\{y\in Y:(x,y)\in A\}
.
For ideals
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
on
X
and
Y
respectively, we define the following ideal (called the
Fubini product
of
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
):

\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{J}=\{A\subseteq X\times Y:\{x\in X:A_{(x)}\notin\mathcal{J}\}\in\mathcal{I}\}.

Using the notion of the Fubini product, one can see that (
[
11
]
)

\mathrm{BI}=(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}^{2})\cap(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(\omega^{2})).

An ideal
\mathcal{I}
is a
P-ideal
if for every sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\omega}
of elements of
\mathcal{I}
there is
A\in\mathcal{I}
such that
A_{n}\setminus A
is finite for all
n\in\omega
. The ideals
\mathrm{Fin}
,
\mathcal{I}_{d}
,
\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
and all summable ideals are P-ideals, whereas
\mathrm{Fin}^{2}
and
\mathrm{BI}
are not P-ideals.

Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be ideals on
X
and
Y
respectively.
We say that
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are
isomorphic
(in short
\mathcal{I}\approx\mathcal{J}
)
if there exists a bijection
f:X\rightarrow Y
such that
A\in\mathcal{I}\iff f[A]\in\mathcal{J}
for every
A\subseteq X
.
We say that
\mathcal{J}
is below
\mathcal{I}
in the Katětov order
(in short
\mathcal{J}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
) if there is a function
f:X\rightarrow Y
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{I}
for every
A\in\mathcal{I}
.
We say that ideals
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are
\leq_{K}
-equivalent
if
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{J}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.

A relationship between Katětov order and classes
\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
is expressed in the following theorem which will be used in other sections.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
11
, Corollary 10.2]
)
.

If
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{J}
, then
\FinBW(\mathcal{J})\subseteq\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
.

3.
Critical ideals

For a topological space
X
,
we write
c(X)
to denote the set of all convergent sequences in
X
.
For a subset
D\subseteq X
of a topological space
X
, we write
\mathrm{conv}(D)
to denote
the ideal on
D
consisting of all subsets of
D
which can be covered by ranges of finitely many sequences in
D
which are convergent in
X
i.e.

A\in\mathrm{conv}(D)

if and only if
A\subseteq D
and there exist
k\in\omega
and sequences
(d^{(i)}_{n})_{n\in\omega}\in c(X)\cap D^{\omega}
for
i<k
such that

A\subseteq\bigcup_{i<k}\left\{d^{(i)}_{n}:n\in\omega\right\}.

A point
p\in X
is an
accumulation
(a.k.a.
limit
)
point
of a set
A\subseteq X
in a topological space
X
if
p\in\closure(A\setminus\{p\})
.
By
A^{d}
we denote the
derived set of
A
i.e. the set of all accumulation points of
A
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be a sequentially compact space.
Let
D\subseteq X
be a countable infinite subset of
X
.

(1)

For every
A\subseteq D
,

A\in\mathrm{conv}(D)\iff A^{d}\text{ is finite}.

(2)

For every infinite set
A\subseteq D
there exists an infinite set
B\subseteq A
such that
B\in\mathrm{conv}(D)
. In particular, the ideal
\mathrm{conv}(D)
is tall.

Proof.

(
1
,
\implies
) Take any
A\in\mathrm{conv}(D)
. There exists
k\in\omega
and sequences
(d^{(i)}_{n})_{n\in\omega}\in c(X)\cap D^{\omega}
for
i<k
such that
A\subseteq\bigcup_{i<k}\{d^{(i)}_{n}:n\in\omega\}.
The sequence
(d^{(i)}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is convergent for each
i
, so let
\{d_{i}:i<k\}
be the set of their limits. Then
A^{d}\subseteq(\bigcup_{i<k}\{d^{(i)}_{n}:n\in\omega\})^{d}\subseteq\{d_{i}:i<k\}
which is finite.

(
1
,
\impliedby
) Assume that
A
is infinite,
A^{d}
is finite and let
k
be the cardinality of the set.
We will prove the statement by induction on
k\in\omega
.

For
k=1
, let
A^{d}=\{d\}
. Since
A
is countable infinite, we can enumerate its elements injectively as
\{a_{n}:n\in\omega\}
. We claim that the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is convergent to
d
. Let
U
be an open neighborhood of
d
and suppose for the sake of contradiction that there exists infinitely many elements of the set
\{a_{n}:n\in\omega\}
that are not in
U
. Since
X
is sequentially compact, there exists an infinite set
C\subseteq\omega
such that
(a_{n})_{n\in C}
is convergent to a point
p
that is different from
d
, so
p\in A^{d}
which is a contradiction with our initial assumption. Consequently we can cover
A
with a convergent sequence, so
A\in\mathrm{conv}(D)
.

Suppose the statement is true for
k
. Take
A^{d}=\{d_{i}:i<k+1\}
. Since
X
is Hausdorff we can find a collection of open sets
\{U_{i}\subseteq X:d_{i}\in U_{i}\}
that are pairwaise disjoint. Define a partition of
A
by
B_{1}=A\cap U_{k}
and
B_{2}=A\setminus U_{k}
.

We claim
B_{1}^{d}\subseteq\{d_{k}\}
and
B_{2}^{d}\subseteq\{d_{i}:i<k\}
.
Take
i<k
. Then
B_{1}\cap U_{i}\subseteq U_{k}\cap U_{i}=\emptyset
, so
d_{i}\notin B_{1}^{d}
.
Similarly,
B_{2}\cap U_{k}=\emptyset
, so
d_{k}\notin B_{2}^{d}
.

So by the induction hypothesis
B_{1}\in\mathrm{conv}(D)
and
B_{2}\in\mathrm{conv}(D)
and therefore
A\in\mathrm{conv}(D)

(
2
)
Take an infinite set
A\subseteq D
.
Take distinct points
a_{n}\in A
for
n\in\omega
.
Since
X
is sequentially compact, there is a convergent subsequence
(a_{k_{n}})_{n\in\omega}
. Then
B=\{a_{k_{n}}:n\in\omega\}
is an infinite subset of
A
such that
B\in\mathrm{conv}(D)
.
∎

Definition 3.2
.

(1)

If
X=[0,1]
with the Euclidean topology, we define the ideal

\mathrm{conv}=\mathrm{conv}(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]).

(2)

For a countable ordinal
\alpha\geq 2
and
the space
X=\omega^{\alpha}+1
with the order topology, we define the ideal

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}=\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\alpha}+1).

In the rest of the paper, we write “
\alpha
” to mean ”a countable ordinal
\alpha\geq 2
”.

Below we show that the Borel complexity of
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is at most
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
, and in Corollary
7.4
we will show that this complexity is not lower.

Proposition 3.3
.

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\in\Sigma^{0}_{4}
for every
\alpha
.

Proof.

We use a standard quantifier-counting argument. Let
\{U_{n}:n\in\omega\}
be a countable basis for the topology of
X=\omega^{\alpha}+1
.
By Lemma
3.1
,
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\iff|A^{d}|<\omega
, hence

A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\iff\exists F\in[X]^{<\omega}\,\forall\beta\in X\setminus F\,\exists n\in\omega\,\left(\beta\in U_{n}\land\forall\gamma\in U_{n}(\gamma\notin A)\right).

Since the sets
\{A\subseteq X:\gamma\notin A\}
are closed in
X
for every
\gamma
, we can count quantifiers to obtain that
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is
F_{\sigma\delta\sigma}
.
∎

The following
purely combinatorial
characterization of members of the topologically defined ideal
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
will be used repeatedly in the next sections.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1
.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.

(2)

For every increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha}
, the set

A\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]

is finite for all but finitely many
n
.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)

Take
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
and suppose for the sake of contradiction that there exists
an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha}
and an infinite set
B\subseteq\omega
such that
the set
A\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]

is infinite for every
n\in B
.
Since
\omega^{\alpha}+1
is sequentially compact, we can find an accumulation points of
A
in
(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]
for every
n\in B
.
Then
A^{d}
is infinite, so
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
by Lemma
3.1
, a contradiction.

(2)\implies(1)

Take
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.
By Lemma
3.1
,
A^{d}
is infinite, so we can pick a strictly increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
A^{d}
.
Since successor ordinals are isolated points,
\lambda_{n}
is a limit ordinal for each
n
.
Then
(\xi,\lambda_{n+1}]
is a neighborhood of
\lambda_{n+1}
for every
\xi\in(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})
,
and consequently

A\cap(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]
is infinite for every
n\in\omega
.
∎

In the case of successor ordinals, we can obtain the following extension of the above characterization.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha+1}+1
.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
.

(2)

For every increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha+1}
, the set

A\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]

is finite for all but finitely many
n
.

(3)

(a)

A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)]
is finite for all but finitely many
n
, and

(b)

A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)]\in\mathrm{conv}((\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)])
for all
n
.

Proof.

(1)\iff(2)
This is proved in Proposition
3.4
.

(2)\implies(2a)
Take
\lambda_{n}=\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n
and apply item (2).

(2)\implies(2b)
Let
n\in\omega
. Then we obtain item (3b) by applying item (2) to every increasing sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\omega}
in
(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)]
.

(3)\implies(1)

Let
n_{0}
be such that

A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)]
is finite for every
n\geq n_{0}
.
Then

A^{d}\subseteq\bigcup_{n<n_{0}}(A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)])^{d}\cup\{\omega^{\alpha+1}\}.

Since

A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)]\in\mathrm{conv}((\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)])

for every
n<n_{0}
,
we obtain that
A^{d}

is finite, hence
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
.
∎

Since
\omega^{\alpha}+1
and
(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)]
are order isomorphic, we obtain that the ideals
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
and
\mathrm{conv}((\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)])
are isomorphic for every
n
.
Then we can use item (3) of the above proposition to obtain an ideal isomorphic to
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
.

Corollary 3.6
.

The ideal
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
is isomorphic to the ideal

\left(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(\omega^{\alpha}+1)\right)\cap\left(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\right).

We finish this section with a proposition which shows that the results from Section
4
extend Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The ideals
\mathrm{conv}_{2}
and
\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
(
\mathrm{conv}_{3}
and
\mathrm{BI}
, resp.)
are isomorphic.

Proof.

Apply Corollary
3.6

and the fact that

\mathrm{BI}=(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}^{2})\cap(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(\omega^{2}))
.
∎

4.
Proofs of the main theorems

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\omega
.
If
f:\omega\to\omega^{\alpha}
is a function such that
f^{-1}(\xi)\in\mathcal{I}
for each
\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}
, then for any set
B\subseteq\omega
such that
B\notin\mathcal{I}

there exists an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha}
such that

(1)

B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]\in\mathcal{I}
for each
n
, and

(2)

B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{0},\lambda)]\notin\mathcal{I}
, where
\lambda=\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
\alpha
.
For
\alpha=1
, the sequence
\lambda_{n}=n
works.
Assume the lemma holds for any
\beta<\alpha
.

Successor case:

\alpha
is a successor ordinal, so
\alpha=\delta+1
.

If
B\cap f^{-1}[[\omega^{\delta}\cdot n,\omega^{\delta}\cdot(n+1))]\in\mathcal{I}
for each
n<\omega
, the sequence
\lambda_{n}=\omega^{\delta}\cdot n
works.
Now, assume there is
n<\omega
with
C=B\cap f^{-1}[[\omega^{\delta}\cdot n,\omega^{\delta}\cdot(n+1))]\notin\mathcal{I}
.
Since
[\omega^{\delta}\cdot n,\omega^{\delta}\cdot(n+1))
is order isomorphic to
\omega^{\delta}
, we can apply the inductive hypothesis to obtain an increasing sequence

(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
[\omega^{\delta}\cdot n,\omega^{\delta}\cdot(n+1))
such that

B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]=C\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]\in\mathcal{I}
for each
n
, and

B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{0},\lambda)]=C\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{0},\lambda)]\notin\mathcal{I}
, where
\lambda=\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}
. Hence the proof is finished in this case.

Limit case:

\alpha
is a limit ordinal, so there is an increasing sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n<\omega}
in
\alpha
such that
\alpha_{0}=0
and
\alpha=\sup_{n}\alpha_{n}
.

If
B\cap f^{-1}[[1,\omega^{\alpha_{n}})]\in\mathcal{I}
for each
n<\omega
, the sequence
\lambda_{n}=\omega^{\alpha_{n}}
works.
Now, assume there is
n<\omega
with

C=B\cap f^{-1}[[1,\omega^{\alpha_{n}})]\notin\mathcal{I}

If we apply the inductive hypothesis to the set
C
, we can obtain an increasing sequence

(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
[1,\omega^{\alpha_{n}})
such that

B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]=C\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]\in\mathcal{I}
for each
n
, and

B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{0},\lambda)]=C\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{0},\lambda)]\notin\mathcal{I}
, where
\lambda=\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}
. Hence the proof is finished in this case.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

For any ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
,

\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\omega^{\alpha}+1\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I}).

In particular, if
\alpha
is infinite, then

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\omega^{\alpha}+1\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I}).

Proof.

(
\implies
) Let
f:\omega\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
. We need to find
D\notin\mathcal{I}
such that the subsequence
f\restriction D
is convergent.

If there exists
\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}+1
such that
D=f^{-1}(\xi)\notin\mathcal{I}
, then

f\restriction D
is convergent to
\xi
, so we are done.
Now, suppose
f^{-1}(\xi)\in\mathcal{I}
for each
\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}+1
.

We define

A_{\xi}=f^{-1}(\xi)

for each
\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}+1
,

Let
g:\omega\to\omega^{1+\alpha}+1
be a function such that

(1)

g[A_{\xi}]\subseteq[\omega\cdot\xi,\omega\cdot(\xi+1))
for each
\xi<\omega^{\alpha}
,

(2)

g[A_{\omega^{\alpha}}]\subseteq\{\omega^{1+\alpha}\}
, and

(3)

g\restriction\omega\setminus A_{\omega^{\alpha}}
is injective.

Since
\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\nleq_{K}\mathcal{I}
, there exists a set
B\notin\mathcal{I}
such that
g[B]\in\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha}
such that
B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]\in\mathcal{I}
for each
n
and
C=B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{0},\lambda)]\notin\mathcal{I}
, where
\lambda=\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}
.
Since
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is an increasing sequence in
\omega^{\alpha}
,
the sequence
(\omega\cdot\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is increasing in

\omega^{1+\alpha}
,
so by Proposition
3.4
, there is
n_{0}\in\omega
such that

g[B]\cap[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})
is finite for each
n\geq n_{0}
.
Since
g
is injective on
\omega\setminus A_{\omega^{\alpha}}
, the set

B\cap g^{-1}[[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})]=B\cap\bigcup_{\lambda_{n}\leq\xi<\lambda_{n+1}}A_{\xi}

is also finite for each
n\geq n_{0}
.

Let

D=C\setminus\bigcup_{n<n_{0}}\left(\bigcup_{\lambda_{n}\leq\xi<\lambda_{n+1}}A_{\xi}\right).

Since
C\notin I

and for each
n

C\cap\bigcup_{\lambda_{n}\leq\xi<\lambda_{n+1}}A_{\xi}=B\cap f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]\in\mathcal{I},

we conclude that
D\notin\mathcal{I}
.
Once we show that
f\restriction D
is convergent to
\lambda
, the proof will be finished.

Let
U
be an open neighborhood of
\lambda
. Then there exists some
k_{0}
such that
(\lambda_{k_{0}},\lambda]\subseteq U.

Notice that

\begin{split}\{n\in D:f(n)\notin U\}&\subseteq\{n\in D:f(n)\leq\lambda_{k_{0}}\}\\
&\subseteq D\cap\bigcup_{n<k_{0}}\left(\bigcup_{\lambda_{n}\leq\xi<\lambda_{n+1}}A_{\xi}\right)=\bigcup_{n<k_{0}}\left(D\cap\bigcup_{\lambda_{n}\leq\xi<\lambda_{n+1}}A_{\xi}\right).\end{split}

Since the set
D\cap\bigcup_{\lambda_{n}\leq\xi<\lambda_{n+1}}A_{\xi}
is finite for each
n
, we get that
\{n\in D:f(n)\notin U\}
is finite as well.

(
\impliedby
)
Suppose that
\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
and let
f:\omega\to\omega^{1+\alpha}+1
be a witnessing function, meaning that
B\notin\mathcal{I}
implies that
f[B]\notin\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}
.
Let
g:\omega^{1+\alpha}+1\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
be a function such that
g(\omega^{\alpha+1})=\omega^{\alpha}
and

g^{-1}(\xi)=[\omega\cdot\xi,\omega\cdot(\xi+1)))\quad\text{ for each $\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}$.}

Let
h=g\circ f
.
Then
h:\omega\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
, so
once we show that the subsequence
h\restriction A
is not convergent for any
A\notin\mathcal{I}
,
the proof will be finished.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there exists some
A\notin\mathcal{I}
such that
h\restriction A
is convergent to some
\beta\in\omega^{\alpha}+1
.

Case (1).

\beta
is a successor ordinal or
\beta=0
.

Let
U=\{\beta\}
. As
U
is a neighbourhood of
\beta
, there exists a finite set
K\subseteq\omega
such that
h[A\setminus K]=\{\beta\}.
Then
f[A\setminus K]\subseteq g^{-1}(\beta)=[\omega\cdot\beta,\omega\cdot\beta+\omega)
. But then
f[A\setminus K]^{d}\subseteq[\omega\cdot\beta,\omega\cdot\beta+\omega)^{d}=\{\omega\cdot\beta+\omega\}
. Then
f[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
(by Lemma
3.1
), a contradiction with
f
being the witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.

Case (2).

\beta
is a nonzero limit ordinal.

Let
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
be any increasing sequence in
\beta
such that
\beta=\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}
.
We claim that the set
f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})
is finite for all
n
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})
is infinite for some
n
. Then the set
A\cap f^{-1}[[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})]
is infinite. The set
V=(\lambda_{n+1},\beta]
is an open neighborhood of
\beta
, but

A\cap f^{-1}[[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})]\subseteq\{i\in A:h(i)\notin V\},

which leads to a contradiction with
\beta
being the limit of the subsequence
h\restriction A
.

If we show that
f[A]^{d}
is finite, then
f[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
(by Proposition
3.1
), which will lead to a contradiction with
f
being the witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.

Since

f[A]^{d}=(f[A]\cap[0,\omega\cdot\beta])^{d}\cup(f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot\beta,\omega\cdot(\beta+1)])^{d}\cup(f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot(\beta+1),\omega^{1+\alpha}])^{d},

it is enough to show that sets from the above union are finite.

First, we show that
(f[A]\cap[0,\omega\cdot\beta])^{d}\subseteq\{\omega\cdot\beta\}.

Take any
\gamma\in[0,\omega\cdot\beta)
. Then
\gamma<\omega\cdot\lambda_{k}
for some
k
. Let
U=[0,\omega\cdot\lambda_{k})
. Then
U
is an open set containing
\gamma
. Moreover,
f[A]\cap U=\bigcup_{n<k}f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot\lambda_{n},\omega\cdot\lambda_{n+1})
which is finite. Hence
\gamma
is not an accumulation point of
f[A]
.

Second, we observe that

(f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot\beta,\omega\cdot(\beta+1)])^{d}\subseteq[\omega\cdot\beta,\omega\cdot(\beta+1)]^{d}=\{\omega\cdot(\beta+1)\}.

Third, we show that
(f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot(\beta+1),\omega^{1+\alpha}])^{d}=\emptyset
.
Let
K=\{n\in A:h(n)>\beta\}
. Since
U=[0,\beta]
is an open neighborhood of
\beta
,
K
must be finite, so the set
f[A]\cap[\omega\cdot(\beta+1),\omega^{1+\alpha}]
is finite as well, and consequently its derived set is empty.
∎

The
disjoint union of topological spaces

X_{i}
for
i<n
(
n<\omega
) is a topological space on the set

\bigsqcup_{i<n}X_{i}=\bigcup_{i<n}X_{i}\times\{i\}

with open sets of the form

\bigcup_{i<n}U_{i}\times\{i\}\quad\text{ for any open sets $U_{i}$ in $X_{i}$.}

Lemma 4.3
.

If
X\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
, then
\bigsqcup_{i<n}X\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
for any
n<\omega
.

Proof.

Take any sequence
f:\omega\to\bigsqcup_{i<n}X
and let
\pi:\bigsqcup_{i<n}X\to X
be the projection onto the first coordinate.
Then there exists
A\notin\mathcal{I}
such that the subsequence
(\pi\circ f)\restriction A
is convergent to some
p\in X
.
Let’s observe that
f^{-1}[X\times\{i\}]\cap A\notin I
for some
i<n
.
Let
C=f^{-1}[X\times\{i\}]\cap A\notin\mathcal{I}
. We claim that the subsequence
f\restriction C
is convergent to
(p,i)
. Take any open neighborhood
U
of
(p,i)
. Then
\pi[U]
is an open neighborhood of
\pi(p,i)=p
, so there exists some finite set
K
such that
(\pi\circ f)[A\setminus K]\subseteq\pi[U]
, and it follows that
f[C\setminus K]\subseteq U
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\omega
.

(1)

\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\implies\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}\subseteq\FinBW(\mathcal{I}).

(2)

\mathrm{conv}_{(1+\alpha)+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\implies\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}.

(3)

\mathrm{conv}_{(1+\alpha)+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\text{ and }\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}.

In particular,
if
\alpha
is infinite, then

(1)

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\implies\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}\subseteq\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
.

(2)

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\implies\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}
.

(3)

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\text{ and }\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

(1)
If
\mathrm{conv}_{1+\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
then by Theorem
4.2
,
\omega^{\alpha}+1\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
which implies that
\omega^{\beta}+1\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
for any
\beta\leq\alpha
.
Since

\omega^{\beta}\cdot n+1
is homeomorphic to a disjoint union of finitely many copies of the space
\omega^{\beta}+1
, we can apply Lemma
4.3
to obtain
\omega^{\beta}\cdot n+1\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
for any
\beta\leq\alpha
and
n\in\omega
. Consequently
\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}\subseteq\FinBW(\mathcal{I}).

(2)
If
\mathrm{conv}_{(1+\alpha)+1}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
then by Theorem
4.2
,
\omega^{\alpha+1}+1\notin\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
, which implies that
\omega^{\beta}\cdot n+1\notin\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
for each
\beta\geq\alpha+1
and
n\in\omega\setminus\{0\}
, which leads to
\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}
by Theorem
1.3
.

(3)
It follows from items (1) and (2) and Theorem
4.2
.
∎

5.
Katětov order among the critical ideals

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\alpha<\beta
, then
\mathrm{conv}_{\beta}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

It is enough to notice that the function
f:\omega^{\alpha}+1\to\omega^{\beta}+1
given by
f(\xi)=\xi
is a witness
for
\mathrm{conv}_{\beta}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}
.
If the order types of
A
and
B
are smaller than
\omega^{\alpha}
, then the order type of
A\cup B
is smaller than
\omega^{\alpha}
, and
consequently

\omega^{\alpha}\setminus(A\cup B)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that the order type of
A\cup B
is
\omega^{\alpha}
.
Since
\omega^{\alpha}
is an indecomposable ordinal, we obtain (see e.g.
[
10
, Exercise 5 in Chapter I]
) that
the order type of
A
is
\omega^{\alpha}
or the order type of
B
is
\omega^{\alpha}
, a contradiction.
∎

Definition 5.3
.

For every
\alpha
and every ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
by
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
we define the ideal on
\omega^{\alpha+1}+1
given by

\begin{split}A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}\iff&\{i\in\omega:|A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot i,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(i+1)]|=\omega\}\in\mathcal{I}\quad\text{and}\\
&A\cap(\omega^{\alpha}\!\!\cdot\!i,\omega^{\alpha}\!\!\cdot\!(i+1)]\in\mathrm{conv}((\omega^{\alpha}\!\!\cdot\!i,\omega^{\alpha}\!\!\cdot\!(i+1)])\text{ for each }i\in\omega.\end{split}

The following straightforward proposition reveals a relationship between the ideal
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
and
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{I}
, and provides a counterpart of Corollary
3.6
.

Proposition 5.4
.

(1)

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}=\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathrm{Fin}}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
.

(2)

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
is isomorphic to the ideal

(\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(\omega^{\alpha}+1))\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\omega
. If a function
f:\omega^{\alpha+1}+1\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
and an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
are such that
\lambda_{0}=0
,

\sup\{\lambda_{n}:n<\omega\}=\omega^{\alpha}
and
the order type of the set

f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]

is smaller than
\omega^{\alpha}

for each
n<\omega
, then there exists
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
f[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

First, we notice that by Lemma
5.2
,

[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(k+1))\setminus\bigcup_{i\in F}f^{-1}[[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})]\neq\emptyset

for every finite set
F\subseteq\omega
and
k\in\omega
.
Now, we take any function
g:\omega\to\omega
such that
g^{-1}(n)
is infinite for each
n\in\omega
, and inductively pick elements
a_{n}\in\omega^{\alpha}
for
n\in\omega
in such a way that

a_{n}\in[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot g(n),\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(g(n)+1))\setminus\bigcup\left\{f^{-1}[[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})]:\exists j<n\,(f(a_{j})\in[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1}))\right\}.

Then the set
A=\{a_{n}:n\in\omega\}
has the following properties:

(1)

A\cap[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(k+1))
is infinite for each
k\in\omega
,

(2)

for each
n\in\omega
there is
i\in\omega
such that
f(a_{n})\in[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})
,

(3)

|f[A]\cap[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})|\leq 1
for each
i\in\omega
.

By property (
1
), we get that
[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(k+1)]\cap A^{d}\neq\emptyset
for each
k\in\omega
, and consequently
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
.
By properties (
2
) and (
3
), we get that

(f[A])^{d}=\{\omega^{\alpha}\}
, and consequently
f[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
for every
\alpha
and every ideal
\mathcal{I}
.
In particular,
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
for every
\alpha
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\alpha
.
First, we consider the successor case. We assume that

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
for every ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
and want to show that

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}\not\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+2}^{\mathcal{I}}
for every ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
.
Let
\lambda_{n}=\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n
for each
n\in\omega
.
Then
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
is an increasing sequence in
\omega^{\alpha+1}
such that
\lambda_{0}=0
and
\sup\{\lambda_{n}:n<\omega\}=\omega^{\alpha+1}
.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+2}^{\mathcal{I}}
for some ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
, and let
f:\omega^{\alpha+2}+1\to\omega^{\alpha+1}+1
be a witness for this.
We have two cases.

Case (1).
The order type of
f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]
is smaller than
\omega^{\alpha+1}
for each
n\in\omega
.

In this case, we can use Lemma
5.5
, to obtain a set
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+2}^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
f[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
. But this contradicts the fact that
f
is a witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+2}^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case (2).
The order type of
f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]
is greater than or equal to
\omega^{\alpha+1}
for some
n\in\omega
.

Let
A\subseteq f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]
be a set which has the order type equal to
\omega^{\alpha+1}
. Then the order type of

is at most
\omega^{\alpha}
as

\ot(f[A])\leq\ot([\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}))=\ot([\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)))=\omega^{\alpha}.

Then using the function
f\restriction A
, we can obtain
a witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
, a contradiction with the inductive hypothesis.

Finally, we consider the limit case. We assume that
\alpha
is a limit ordinal and
\mathrm{conv}_{\beta}\not\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\beta+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
for each
\beta<\alpha
and each ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
.
Let
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
be an increasing sequence in
\omega^{\alpha}
such that
\lambda_{0}=0
and

\sup\{\lambda_{n}:n<\omega\}=\omega^{\alpha}
.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
for some ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\omega
, and let
f:\omega^{\alpha+1}+1\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
be a witness for this.
We have two cases.

Case (1).
The order type of
f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]
is smaller than
\omega^{\alpha}
for each
n\in\omega
.

In this case, we can use Lemma
5.5
, to obtain a set
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
f[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
. But this contradicts the fact that
f
is a witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha+1}
.

Case (2).
The order type of
f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]
is greater than or equal to
\omega^{\alpha}
for some
n\in\omega
.

Let
A\subseteq f^{-1}[[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})]
be a set which has the order type equal to
\omega^{\alpha}
. Then the order type of
f[A]
is smaller than
\omega^{\alpha}
as

\ot(f[A])\leq\ot([\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}))\leq\ot(\lambda_{n+1})=\lambda_{n+1}<\omega^{\alpha}.

Let
\beta<\alpha
be such that
\lambda_{n+1}<\omega^{\beta}
.
Then using the function
f\restriction A
, we can obtain
a witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{\beta}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.
Since
\beta+1<\alpha
, we get
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\beta+1}
by Corollary
5.7
.
Consequently
\mathrm{conv}_{\beta}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\beta+1}
, a contradiction with the inductive hypothesis.
∎

.

, then
\mathrm{conv}_{\beta}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}

It follows from Proposition

.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}
be ideals on
\omega

\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a tall ideal on a countable set

such that

\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}((\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction A)\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I})

for every
A\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
Assume also that
\mathrm{Fin}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1}
,
\mathrm{Fin}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{I}
and
X\notin\mathcal{I}
.
Then

(\mathcal{I}_{0}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I})\not\leq_{K}(\mathcal{I}_{1}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I}).

Proof.

Denote
\mathcal{J}_{i}=(\mathcal{I}_{i}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I})
for
i=0,1
. Let
f:\omega\times X\to\omega\times X
be arbitrary. If there is
n\in\omega
such that
f[\{n\}\times X]\cap(\{k\}\times X)
is finite for all
k\in\omega
, then
C=\{n\}\times X\notin\mathcal{J}_{1}
, but
f[C]\in\mathcal{J}_{0}
, so we are done. Hence, assume that

E_{n}=\{k\in\omega:f[\{n\}\times X]\cap(\{k\}\times X)\text{ is infinite}\}

is non-empty for all
n\in\omega
and put
T=\{n\in\omega:E_{n}\text{ is infinite}\}
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}
, either
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction T
or
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
.

Assume first that
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction T
. In particular,
T
is infinite in this case. For each
n\in T
inductively find
g(n)\in\omega
and
A_{n}\subseteq X
such that:

(a)

g(n)\in E_{n}
,

(b)

g(n)<g(n+1)
,

(c)

f^{-1}[\{g(n)\}\times A_{n}]\cap(\{n\}\times X)
is infinite,

(d)

A_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
,

(note that (c) is possible to obtain as (a) implies that
f[\{n\}\times X]\cap(\{g(n)\}\times X)
is infinite, while (d) is possible to obtain as
\mathcal{I}
is tall). Since
g:T\to\omega
and
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction T
, there is
B\subseteq T
such that
g[B]\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
, but
B\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Then

C=\bigcup_{n\in B}\{g(n)\}\times A_{n}\in\mathcal{J}_{0}

by
g[B]\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
and items (b) and (d), while
f^{-1}[C]\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X)
by
B\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}
and item (c), so also
f^{-1}[C]\notin\mathcal{J}_{1}
.

Assume now that
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
. For each
n\in\omega\setminus T
define

B_{n}=\left(f^{-1}[f[\{n\}\times X]\setminus(E_{n}\times X)]\right)_{(n)}.

For every
n\in\omega\setminus T
the set
f[\{n\}\times X]\setminus(E_{n}\times X)
belongs to
\mathcal{J}_{0}
, so if
B_{n}\notin\mathcal{I}
for some
n
, then we are done (as
\mathcal{J}_{1}\not\ni\{n\}\times B_{n}\subseteq f^{-1}[f[\{n\}\times X]\setminus(E_{n}\times X)]
). Hence, assume that
B_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
for every
n\in\omega\setminus T
.

Define
H_{k}=\{n\in\omega\setminus T:\max E_{n}\leq k\}
for every
k\in\omega
.

Consider first the case that
H_{k}\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
for some
k
. Observe that
\mathcal{I}\restriction(\omega\setminus B_{n})
is
\leq_{K}
-equivalent to
\mathcal{I}
, for every
n\notin T
(as
B_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
), so

((\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction H_{k})\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap\left(\left(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I}\right)\restriction\left(\bigcup_{n\in H_{k}}\{n\}\times B_{n}\right)\right)

is
\leq_{K}
-equivalent to
((\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction H_{k})\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap((\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I})\restriction(H_{k}\times X))
. Since
\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}((\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction H_{k})\otimes\mathrm{Fin}(X))\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I})
(by the assumptions of this Lemma) and
\mathcal{J}_{0}\restriction((k+1)\times X)
is
\leq_{K}
-equivalent to
\mathcal{I}
, we can find
A\in\mathcal{J}_{0}\restriction((k+1)\times X)
such that
f^{-1}[A]\notin\mathcal{J}_{1}
.

Assume from now on that
H_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
for every
k
. Let
\phi
be a measure on
\omega\setminus T
such that
\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)=\{A\subseteq\omega\setminus T:\phi(A)<\infty\}
. For each
k
find a finite set
G_{k}\subseteq H_{k}\setminus H_{k-1}
such that

\phi(G_{k})\geq\phi(H_{k}\setminus H_{k-1})-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}.

Define
G=\bigcup_{k\in\omega}G_{k}
and observe that
\phi((\omega\setminus T)\setminus G)\leq 1
. Indeed, if
\phi((\omega\setminus T)\setminus G)>1
, then there should exist some finite
F\subseteq(\omega\setminus T)\setminus G
such that
\phi(F)>1
. Since
\omega\setminus T=\bigcup_{k\in\omega}H_{k}
and
H_{k}\subseteq H_{k+1}
, there is
m
such that
F\subseteq H_{m}\setminus G=H_{m}\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq m}G_{k}
. But we have

\begin{split}\phi(H_{m})&\geq\phi(F)+\phi\left(\bigcup_{k\leq m}G_{k}\right)=\phi(F)+\sum_{k\leq m}\phi(G_{k})\\
&\geq\phi(F)+\sum_{k\leq m}\left(\phi(H_{k}\setminus H_{k-1})-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right)\\
&=\phi(F)-\frac{2^{m+1}-1}{2^{m+1}}+\phi\left(\bigcup_{k\leq m}H_{k}\setminus H_{k-1}\right)\\
&=\phi(F)-\frac{2^{m+1}-1}{2^{m+1}}+\phi(H_{m}),\end{split}

which gives us
\phi(F)\leq(2^{m+1}-1)/2^{m+1}<1
, a contradiction.

Since
(\omega\setminus T)\setminus G\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
, the restrictions
\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction G
are isomorphic (by
[
12
, Proposition 1.2]
and the fact that
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is tall).
Let
h:G\to\omega
be given by
h\restriction G_{k}=k
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{0}\not\leq\mathcal{I}_{1}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
, there is
B\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
such that
g^{-1}[B]\subseteq G
and
g^{-1}[B]\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}
.

For every
k\in B
and
n\in G_{k}
, using tallness of
\mathcal{I}
, find some
A_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
(f^{-1}[\{k\}\times A_{n}])_{(n)}
is infinite and
\{k\}\times A_{n}\subseteq f[\{n\}\times X]
(this is possible as
n\in G_{k}\subseteq H_{k}\setminus H_{k-1}
means that
k\in E_{n}
). Define

C=\bigcup_{k\in B}\bigcup_{n\in G_{k}}\{k\}\times A_{n}=\bigcup_{k\in B}\left(\{k\}\times\bigcup_{n\in G_{k}}A_{n}\right).

Then
C\in\mathcal{J}_{0}
, but
f^{-1}[C]\notin\mathcal{J}_{1}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.1

\mathrm{conv}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}

Corollary 6.4
.

(1)

For any ideal
\mathcal{I}
,

\omega_{1}\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\iff\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\text{ for any $\alpha$.}

(2)

There is

single ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\omega_{1}}

\omega_{1}\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})\iff\mathcal{I}_{\omega_{1}}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}.

for any ideal
\mathcal{I}

Proof.

(1,

(1,

(2)

Lemma 6.5
.

If
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are tall ideals,
\mathcal{J}
is a P-ideal and
\mathcal{J}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
then
\mathcal{J}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
.

Proof.

Let
f:\omega\times\omega\to\omega
be arbitrary. We need to find
A\in\mathcal{J}
such that
f^{-1}[A]\notin\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
. Denote

T=\{n\in\omega:f\restriction(\{n\}\times\omega)\text{ is finite-to-one}\}.

There are two possibilities: either
T\in\mathcal{I}
or
T\notin\mathcal{I}
.

If
T\in\mathcal{I}
, for each
n\in\omega\setminus T
find an infinite
A_{n}\subseteq\{n\}\times\omega
and
g(n)\in\omega
such that
f[A_{n}]=\{g(n)\}
. Since
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
are isomorphic (by
[
12
, Proposition 1.2]
as
T\in\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}
is tall)
and
\mathcal{J}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
, there is
A\in\mathcal{J}
such that
g^{-1}[A]\notin\mathcal{I}\restriction(\omega\setminus T)
. Then

f^{-1}[A]\supseteq\bigcup_{n\in g^{-1}[A]}A_{n}\notin\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathrm{Fin},

so
A
is the required set.

If
T\notin\mathcal{I}
, using tallness of
\mathcal{J}
, for each
n\in T
find infinite
B_{n}\in\mathcal{J}
such that
B_{n}\subseteq f[\{n\}\times\omega]
. Since
\mathcal{J}
is a P-ideal, there is
A\in\mathcal{J}
such that
B_{n}\setminus A\in\mathrm{Fin}
for all
n\in\omega
. In particular,

f^{-1}[A]\cap(\{n\}\times\omega)\supseteq f^{-1}[A\cap B_{n}]\cap(\{n\}\times\omega)\notin\mathrm{Fin}

for all
n\in T
. Hence,
f^{-1}[A]\notin\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
.
∎

Theorem 6.6

There are

\mathcal{J}_{\beta}=(\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})\cap(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{d})

7.
Additional properties of critical ideals

7.1.
Critical ideals have the property KAT

We say that an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
X

contains an isomorphic copy
of an ideal
\mathcal{J}
on
Y
(in short
\mathcal{J}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{I}
) if there is a bijection
f:X\to Y
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{I}

for every
A\in\mathcal{J}
.
In
[
1
, Lemma 3.3]
, the authors showed that
if
\mathcal{J}
is tall, then we can only require that
f
is one-to-one in the definition of
\sqsubseteq
.

Proposition 7.1
.

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\sqsubseteq\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\otimes\{\emptyset\}
for every
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
f:(\omega^{\alpha}+1)\times\omega\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
be a one-to-one function such that

f[\{\omega^{\alpha}\}\times\omega]=[0,\omega)

and
for each
\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}
we have

f[\{\xi\}\times\omega]=[\omega\cdot(1+\xi),\omega\cdot(1+\xi+1)).

We claim that
f
is a witness for
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\sqsubseteq\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\otimes\{\emptyset\}
.
Since the ideal
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is tall
(by Lemma
3.1
(
2
)) and the function
f
is one-to-one, we only need to show that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\otimes\{\emptyset\}
for every
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
.
Take any
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
and suppose for the sake of contradiction that
f^{-1}[A]\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\otimes\{\emptyset\}
.
Then

B=\{\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}+1:f^{-1}[A]\cap(\{\xi\}\times\omega)\neq\emptyset\}\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}.

By Proposition
3.4
, there exists an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha}

and an infinte set
C\subseteq\omega
such that
the intersection
B\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})
is infinite for each
n\in C
.
Then for each
n\in C
we can find
\tau_{n}\in[\omega\cdot(1+\lambda_{n}),\omega\cdot(1+\lambda_{n+1}+1)]\cap A^{d}
.
Consequently,
A^{d}
is infinite, so
A\notin\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
, a contradiction.
∎

The following corollary shows that the ideals
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
have the property
Kat
i.e. we can replace arbitrary function by a bijection or a finite-to-one function when comparing the ideals
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
with other ideals in the Katětov order (this phenomenon for arbitrary ideals was introduced and examine in details in
[
1
]
).

We say that an ideal
\mathcal{J}
on
Y
is below an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
X
in the
Katětov-Blass order
(in short
\mathcal{J}\leq_{KB}\mathcal{I}
) if there is a finite-to-one function
f:X\to Y
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{I}
for every
A\in\mathcal{I}
.

Corollary 7.2
.

For any ideal
\mathcal{I}
,

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{KB}\mathcal{I}\iff\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{I}.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
7.1
and
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
.
∎

Using Proposition
3.7
, we can see that the above corollary extends
[
4
, Theorem 6.2]
and
[
11
, Proposition 4.4]
, where the authors proved the above corollary for
\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}\approx\mathrm{conv}_{2}
and
\mathrm{BI}\approx\mathrm{conv}_{3}
, respectively.

7.2.
Borel complexity and
P^{-}
property

An ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
X
is
P^{-}
(a.k.a.
hereditary weak P
) if for every partition
\mathcal{A}
of any set
C\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
into sets from
\mathcal{I}
there exists
B\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
such that
B\subseteq C
and
B\cap A
is finite for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
(see
[
8
, p. 2030]
and
[
11
, Definition 4.8]
, resp.).
It is known that
\mathrm{conv}
is not a
P^{-}
ideal (see e.g.
[
11
, proof of Proposition 4.10(b)]
), however there are
P^{-}
ideals which are above
\mathrm{conv}
in the Katětov order
(for instance,
\mathcal{I}_{d}
is
P^{-}
and
\mathrm{conv}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}_{d}
by Proposition
6.1
).
Every ideal
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is above the ideal
\mathrm{conv}
in the Katětov order, however there is no
P^{-}
ideal above any
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
ideal as shown by the following proposition.

Proposition 7.3
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is a
P^{-}
ideal, then
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\nleq_{K}\mathcal{I}
for every
\alpha
.
In particular,
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is not
P^{-}
for any
\alpha
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{I}
is a
P^{-}
ideal, then
\omega_{1}\in\FinBW(\mathcal{I})
by
[
11
, Proposition 6.1]
. Thus, Corollary
6.4
finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 7.4
.

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\in\Sigma^{0}_{4}\setminus\Pi^{0}_{4}
for every
\alpha
.

Proof.

In
[
11
, Proposition 4.9]
, the author proved that every
\Pi^{0}_{4}
ideal is
P^{-}
, so
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is not
\Pi^{0}_{4}
by Proposition
7.3
. On the other hand,

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
by Proposition
3.3
.
∎

7.3.
Cardinal characteristics of critical ideals

Some properties of ideals can be described by cardinal characteristics associated with them. There are four well known cardinal characteristics called additivity, covering, uniformity and cofinality defined in the following way
for an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
X
(see e.g.
[
2
]
):

\add(\mathcal{I})=\min\left\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\bigcup\mathcal{A}\notin\mathcal{I}\right\}
,

\cov(\mathcal{I})=\min\left\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\bigcup\mathcal{A}=X\right\}
,

\non(\mathcal{I})=\min\{|A|:A\notin\mathcal{I}\}
,

\cof(\mathcal{I})=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(B\subseteq A)\}
.
These characteristics are useful in the case of ideals on an uncountable set
X
(for instance in the case of the
\sigma
-ideal of all meager sets and the
\sigma
-ideal of all Lebesgue null sets). However, they are (but
\cof
) useless in the case of ideals on countable sets as
\add(\mathcal{I})=\cov(\mathcal{I})=\non(\mathcal{I})=\aleph_{0}
for every ideal
\mathcal{I}
on a countable set.
Fortunately,
Hernández and Hrušák
introduced in
[
7
]
(see also
[
9
]
) certain versions of these characteristics more suitable for tall ideals on countable sets
(
A\subseteq^{*}B
means that
A\setminus B
is finite in this definitions):

\begin{split}\adds(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\neg\exists B\in\mathcal{I}\,\forall A\in\mathcal{A}\,(A\subseteq^{*}B)\},\\
\covs(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in[\omega]^{\aleph_{0}}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(|A\cap B|=\aleph_{0})\},\\
\nons(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(|A\cap B|<\aleph_{0})\},\\
\cofs(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(B\subseteq^{*}A)\}.\end{split}

There are some inequalities holding among these characteristics for all ideals (see also Figure
1
):

\aleph_{0}\leq\adds(\mathcal{I})\leq\covs(\mathcal{I})\leq\cofs(\mathcal{I})\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}

and

\aleph_{0}\leq\adds(\mathcal{I})\leq\nons(\mathcal{I})\leq\cofs(\mathcal{I})\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
(see e.g.
[
9
, p. 578]
).

It is known (see e.g.
[
9
]
) that

\adds(\mathrm{conv})=\adds(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\aleph_{0}
,

\nons(\mathrm{conv})=\nons(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\aleph_{0}
,

\covs(\mathrm{conv})=\cofs(\mathrm{conv})=2^{\aleph_{0}}
,

\covs(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}
and
\cofs(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}
, where

\mathfrak{b}
is the
bounding number
i.e. the smallest cardinality of any unbounded family in the poset
(\omega^{\omega},\leq^{*})
and

\mathfrak{d}
is the
dominating number
i.e. the smallest cardinality of
any dominating family in the poset
(\omega^{\omega},\leq^{*})
(for more on these cardinals see for instance
[
3
]
).
Moreover, if
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{J}
, then
\covs(\mathcal{I})\geq\covs(\mathcal{J})
and
\nons(\mathcal{I})\leq\nons(\mathcal{J})
(
[
7
, Proposition 3.1]
).

The following theorem provide the values of the above mentioned characteristics for critical ideals considered in the paper.

Proposition 7.5
.

For every
\alpha
,

\nons(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\adds(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\aleph_{0},\quad\covs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{d}.

Proof.

(
\nons(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\aleph_{0}
)
Since
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{2}\leq_{K}\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
, we obtain
\aleph_{0}\leq\nons(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\nons(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\aleph_{0}
.

(
\adds(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\aleph_{0}
) Since
\aleph_{0}\leq\adds(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\nons(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\aleph_{0}
, it follows that
\adds(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\aleph_{0}
.

(
\covs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{b}
) Since
\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathrm{conv}_{2}\leq_{K}\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin}
, then
\covs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})\geq\covs(\mathrm{Fin}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}
. We will show the reverse inequality by induction on
\alpha
.
Assume the statement holds for any
\beta<\alpha
.

Successor case:

\alpha
is a successor ordinal, so
\alpha=\gamma+1
.
Let’s notice that for any infinite set
X\in\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1}
, either there is some
n
such that the set
X\cap\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n
is infinite, or the set
X\cap[\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n,\omega^{\gamma}\cdot(n+1)]
is finite for each
n
.
Since
\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma}
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n)
, by inductive hypothesis we obtain
a witness, say
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n)
, for
\covs(\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n))=\mathfrak{b}
for each
n\geq 1
.
Since
\covs(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}
, there exists a family
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega^{\gamma+1}+1)
with the following properties:

(1)

|\mathcal{B}|=\mathfrak{b}
,

(2)

if
F\in\mathcal{B}
then the set
F\cap[\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n,\omega^{\gamma}\cdot(n+1))
is finite for each
n
,

(3)

for any infinite
A\subseteq\omega^{\gamma+1}+1
, if the set
A\cap[\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n,\omega^{\gamma}\cdot(n+1))
is finite for each
n
, then there exists some
F\in\mathcal{B}
such that the set
A\cap F
is infinite.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}\cup\bigcup_{n<\omega}\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is a subfamily of
\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1}
of cardinality
\mathfrak{b}
having the property that for any infinite
D\in\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1}
there exists some
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that the set
D\cap C
is infinite, so it follows that
\covs(\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1})\leq\mathfrak{b}
.

Limit case:

\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n<\omega}
be a strictly increasing sequence that is cofinal in
\alpha
with
\alpha_{0}=0
. Let’s notice that for any infinite set
X\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
, either there is some
n
such that the set
X\cap\omega^{\alpha_{n}}
is infinite, or the set
X\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}}]
is finite for each
n
.
By inductive hypothesis, we can find a witness, say
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha_{n}}
, for
\covs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha_{n}})=\mathfrak{b}

for each
n\geq 1
.
Since
\covs(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}
, there exists a family
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega^{\alpha}+1)
with the following properties:

(1)

|\mathcal{B}|=\mathfrak{b}
,

(2)

if
F\in\mathcal{B}
then the set
F\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}})
is finite for each
n
,

(3)

for any infinite
A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1
, if the set
A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}})
is finite for each
n
, then there exists some
F\in\mathcal{B}
such that the set
A\cap F
is infinite.

Analogically to the previous case, the family
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}\cup\bigcup_{n<\omega}\mathcal{A}_{n}
is a witness for the required inequality.

(
\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{d}
) First, we prove that
\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\mathfrak{d}
by induction on
\alpha
. Assume the statement holds for any
\beta<\alpha
.

Successor case:

\alpha
is a successor ordinal, so
\alpha=\gamma+1
. Let’s notice that for any set
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1}
there is some
n
such that
A\cap\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n\in\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n)
and for any
k\geq n
the set
A\cap[\omega^{\gamma}\cdot k,\omega^{\gamma}\cdot(k+1))
is finite.
Since
\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma}
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n)
, by inductive hypothesis we obtain a witness, say
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n)
, for
\cofs(\mathrm{conv}(\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n))=\mathfrak{d}
for each
n\geq 1
.
Since
\cofs(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}
, there exists a family
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega^{\gamma+1}+1)
with the following properties:

(1)

|\mathcal{B}|=\mathfrak{d}
,

(2)

if
F\in\mathcal{B}
then the set
F\cap[\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n,\omega^{\gamma}\cdot(n+1))
is finite for each
n
,

(3)

for any
A\subseteq\omega^{\gamma+1}+1
, if the set
A\cap[\omega^{\gamma}\cdot n,\omega^{\gamma}\cdot(n+1))
is finite for each
n
then there exists some
F\in\mathcal{B}
such that
A\subseteq^{*}F
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\{A\cup B:A\in\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{A}_{n},B\in\mathcal{B}\}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is a subfamily of
\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1}
such that
|\mathcal{C}|=\mathfrak{d}
and for any
D\in\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1}
there exists some
C\in\mathcal{C}
with
D\subseteq^{*}C
, so it follows that
\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\gamma+1})\leq\mathfrak{d}
.

Limit case:

\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n<\omega}
be a strictly increasing sequence that is cofinal in
\alpha
with
\alpha_{0}=0
. Then

\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\left\{A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1:A\cap\omega^{\alpha_{n}}\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha_{n}}\ \land\ \forall_{k}\geq n\ A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k+1}})\in\mathrm{Fin}\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha_{n}}
be a witness for
\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha_{n}})=\mathfrak{d}
. Since
\cofs(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathrm{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}
, there exists a family
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega^{\alpha}+1)
with the following properties:

(1)

|\mathcal{B}|=\mathfrak{d}
,

(2)

if
F\in\mathcal{B}
then the set
F\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}})
is finite for each
n
,

(3)

for any
A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1
, if the set
A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}})
is finite for each
n
, then there exists some
F\in\mathcal{B}
such that
A\subseteq^{*}F
.

Analogically to the previous case, the family
\mathcal{C}=\{A\cup B:A\in\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{A}_{n},B\in\mathcal{B}\}
is a witness for the required inequality.

Second, we prove that
\mathfrak{d}\leq\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the witness for
\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})
. For any
g\in\omega^{\omega}
we define the set
B_{g}=\bigcup_{n<\omega}[\omega\cdot n,\omega\cdot n+g(n)]
and for any
A\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
we define
f_{A}=\{(n,\max\{k:\omega\cdot n+k\in A\}):n\in\omega\}
, with the convention that
\max\emptyset=0
and
\max(C)=0
for any infinite set
C
. We will show
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{A}:A\in\mathcal{A}\}
to be the dominating family in
\omega^{\omega}
. Take any
g\in\omega^{\omega}
. Since the derivative of the set
B_{g}
is contained in
\{\omega^{2}\}
, it follows that
B\in\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha}
, so there exists some set
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
B_{g}\subseteq^{*}A
, then
g(n)\leq f_{A}(n)
for all but finitely many
n
. Hence
\mathfrak{d}\leq|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{A}|=\cofs(\mathrm{conv}_{\alpha})
.
∎
