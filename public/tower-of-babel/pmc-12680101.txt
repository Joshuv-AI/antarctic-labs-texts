Introducing Stewardship Early: Development and Evaluation of a Multimodal Curriculum for Second-Year Medical Students
Authors: Marcia Epstein, Thien-Ly Doan, Henry Donaghy, Pranisha Gautam-Goyal, Amanda Jezek, Thomas Kwiatkowski, Priya Nori, Doreen M Olvet, Amanda Schorr, Joanne M Willey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn antimicrobial stewardship session for second-year medical students incorporating case-based learning, patient testimonials, and advocacy was developed. The session significantly improved students' knowledge, attitudes, and self-efficacy, supporting early integration of stewardship education in medical curricula.Keywords: antimicrobial curriculum, antimicrobial education, antimicrobial stewardship

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing global health threat. Both the World Health Organization (WHO) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have recognized AMR as a major public health crisis, with resistant organisms such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) causing serious morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. This highlights the urgency for strategic interventions, including integration of comprehensive antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) education early in medical training. AMS programs aim to optimize the use of antimicrobial agents to achieve favorable patient outcomes and mitigate AMR [3, 4].

Although AMS training traditionally targets postgraduate learners and practicing physicians, evidence suggests that incorporating these principles into undergraduate medical education more effectively influences prescribing behavior [2, 4]. Despite the recognized importance of AMS, many medical curricula still lack consistent, structured AMS training, leaving students underprepared to prescribe responsibly [2, 4, 5].

To address this gap, we developed an AMS module within the second-year microbiology and infectious diseases course at the Zucker School of Medicine at Hofstra/Northwell. By building on foundational knowledge of antimicrobial use, the session was designed to help students develop the skillset for judicious antimicrobial prescribing. The module emphasized strategies to prevent AMR using evidence-based decision-making. Here, we detail the structure and effectiveness of the program, with our objective being to assess the session's impact on students' knowledge and preparedness to implement AMS in clinical practice.

METHODS

A required 2-hour large-group interactive session was delivered in the latter half of an 8-week microbiology and infectious diseases course. The session aimed to help students understand the impact of AMR, AMS, and drug development on clinical outcomes and public policy. Launched in 2019, the session has been delivered on an annual basis. Each year's session included: (1) an overview of AMR and the One Health initiative, (2) case-based discussions of healthcare-associated infections (e.g., those caused by CRE and emerging pathogens like Candida auris), (3) presentations by patients with a history of AMR infections, and (4) an introduction to AMR legislative advocacy by the Senior Vice President for Public Policy and Government Relations at the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA). Learning objectives included: (1) discussing the importance and consequences of AMR; (2) defining AMS, its core elements as defined by the CDC [1], and the One Health approach to curbing AMR; (3) recognizing the impact of resistant infections on patients and their families; and [4] appreciating the need for policy advocacy to influence new antimicrobial drug development.

Module Structure

In preparation for the module, students viewed four short videos addressing the (1) scope of AMR in both the US and globally, (2) the One Health Initiative, (3) the core elements of AMS programs [1], and (4) the Pasteur Act [6]. The session began with two infectious disease clinicians discussing the implications of AMR and the role of One Health in addressing this global problem, followed by two case presentations. Cases included a postoperative CRE infection and a fatal C. auris bloodstream infection. Interwoven into these case presentations were patient testimonials that ranged from an individual detailing the impact a treatment-resistant MRSA infection had on his young family, a woman describing a recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection that resolved with a fecal microbiome transplant, to a patient explaining the impact of treatment resistant recurrent urinary tract infections. The session concluded with a presentation on legislative advocacy by an IDSA representative, who used the Pasteur Act [6] as a case-study.

Participants

Presession and postsession data were collected from 401 second-year medical students across four cohorts: 2019 (N = 100), 2020 (N = 97), 2021 (N = 103), and 2023 (N = 101) (classes of 2022, 2023, 2024, 2026, respectively). Data from 2022 (class of 2025) were excluded due to unpaired anonymous responses. Third-year medical students were invited to participate in an additional survey based on their clerkship experiences, with data collected from this latter group of students in 2023 (N = 95) and 2024 (N = 93) (classes of 2024 and 2025, respectively).

Evaluation

Students completed 9 survey items via Qualtrics before and after the AMS session, using a 5-point Likert scale (1, strongly disagree; 3, neutral; 5, strongly agree). Postsession surveys included three additional questions inquiring about the impact of the session on students' future clinical work. Third-year students received the postsession survey with an additional six clerkship-specific questions.

Statistical Analysis

Data was statistically evaluated using IBM SPSS Statistics (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA, Version 28.0). Descriptive statistics were presented as the frequency and percent of each response, as well as the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD). A Wilcoxon Signed Rank test was performed to compare presession and postsession data for each survey item. A Mann–Whitney U-test was performed to compare postsession and clerkship data in a small sample of third-year medical students. The clerkship survey was collected anonymously; therefore we could not link individual data over time. For all tests, a P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Hofstra University Institutional Review Board (HUIRB Approval Ref#: 20230517-SOM-WIL-1) and was granted exempt status. No student names, personal information, or other identifiers were available to the researchers.

RESULTS

Second-Year Student Presession and Postsession Data

Out of 401 participants, matched presession and postsession data were available from 169 students: 46 from the 2019-2020 academic year (46% response rate), 48 from the 2020-2021 academic year (49% response rate), 33 from the 2021-2022 academic year (32% response rate), and 42 from the 2023-2024 academic year (42% response rate). Statistically significant improvements were observed in awareness of AMR's impact on mortality, recognition of the physician's role in promoting stewardship, comfort in explaining the concept of AMS to a layperson, and appreciation for interdisciplinary stewardship efforts as well as the need to educate policymakers (Table 1). The session had the most impact on students reported ability to explain AMS to a layperson with only 48% agreeing or strongly agreeing to this item prior to the session and 95% doing so in the postsession survey (Figure 1). Conversely, postsession scores declined in two areas: believing that drug development could keep pace with resistance and favoring physician autonomy to prescribe independently of evidence-based guidelines. In further support of the session's effectiveness, 78% said they would change their personal antibiotic use, 64% reported increased interest in physician advocacy, and 92% believed the session would affect their future clinical prescribing. By contrast, there was no change in student belief that AMS programs are expensive.

Third-Year Student Clerkship Data

Data was available from 42 students after completing their internal medicine clerkship: 18 from the class of 2024 (19% response rate) and 24 from the class of 2025 (26% response rate). When comparing clerkship student responses to postsession scores obtained 1 year prior, students' confidence explaining AMS to laypersons continued to increase, but their certainty that AMR will be a major cause of death in the future declined (Figure 1). More students also expressed the belief that drug development is keeping pace with resistance—opposite the trend seen in the second-year data. In response to the additional clerkship-specific survey questions, 81% believed that clinical exposure reinforced their support of physician advocacy for stewardship. Clerkship students also reported feeling comfortable interpreting an antibiotic sensitivity report (98%) and an antibiogram (86%), as well as valuing the use of rapid diagnostic tests to reduce the use of unnecessary antibiotics (95%); however, less than half of students reported being familiar with concepts of the One Health Initiative (48%) Notably, 100% of clerkship students said the AMS session helped them identify inappropriate antibiotic use during clinical work. For instance, students reported witnessing the prescription of antibiotics for viral infections due to patient expectations, maintenance of unnecessarily prolonged antibiotic courses, and the inappropriate usage of broad-spectrum antibiotics for surgery patients who likely did not need them.

DISCUSSION

Our results support the growing evidence that early AMS education improves medical students' knowledge and attitudes toward responsible prescribing [7–9]. Our findings suggest that integrating a structured AMS session into the second year of medical school helps shape future physicians' stewardship behaviors, aligning with recommendations that emphasize early intervention as more effective than attempting to change prescribing habits later in a physician's career [10]. While the clerkship survey findings are largely consistent with those obtained from the preclerkship survey, a few important exceptions warrant further investigation. Interestingly, students were less likely to see AMR as a leading future cause of death and were more confident in the pace of drug development, possibly reflecting limited exposure to resistant infections and experience with novel antibiotics used to treat them. Indeed, these trends reinforce the need for ongoing AMS education during clinical training [11], and educational programs such as that described herein should be the foundation of a continuum of training that extends throughout a physician's career [12].

LIMITATIONS

This study is not without significant limitations. These include the small sample size, particularly of clerkship students at a single institution, highlighting the need for further research with broader program implementation and participation across institutions to more accurately assess long-term impact.

CONCLUSION

It is critical that AMS education be evidence-based [4]. By reporting outcomes of a curriculum introducing AMS concepts early in medical education, we hope to foster a generation of clinicians better equipped to address the growing challenge of AMR through stewardship, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes.
