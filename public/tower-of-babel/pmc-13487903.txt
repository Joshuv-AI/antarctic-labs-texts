Accurate Solution‐Phase Thermodynamics via Composite Quantum Chemistry and Machine Learning: Application to Biomass Decomposition
Authors: Mikito Fujinami, Akihiko Okubo, Shuhei Ogo, Ayumu Onda, Hiromi Nakai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThermodynamic quantities in the hydrated state provide essential reference information for understanding hydrothermal biomass decomposition. However, accurately estimating thermodynamic quantities in the hydrated state using computational methods remains challenging. In this study, we develop a general computational framework to evaluate accurate solution‐phase thermodynamics in the standard state for compounds involved in the decomposition of xylose and glucose in water by combining gas‐phase free‐energy calculations with machine‐learning prediction of hydration free energies. Gas‐phase thermodynamic quantities are evaluated by a composite method designed to reproduce coupled‐cluster theory with singles, doubles, and perturbative triples in the complete basis set limit (CCSD(T)/CBS) accuracy. Hydration free energies are predicted using molecular descriptors and machine learning. The resulting approach enables efficient evaluation of large reaction networks and is demonstrated for a dataset comprising 84 molecules and 73 reactions. Comparison with experimentally‐derived free‐energy differences for sugar isomerization indicates that the method achieves an accuracy within 3.7 kcal/mol in aqueous solution. The computed thermodynamic data further identify furfural and 5‐hydroxymethylfurfural as thermodynamically favorable products in the xylose and glucose systems, respectively. This framework provides a practical route to accurate standard‐state solution‐phase thermodynamics by combining high‐accuracy gas‐phase thermochemistry with machine‐learning prediction of hydration free energies.Keywords: biomass decomposition, composite quantum chemistry, hydration free energy, machine learning, solution‐phase thermodynamics

Introduction

Plant biomass, including lignocellulosic biomass, is the largest biomass resource on Earth. Its reuse, through conversion into fuels and gases, is important for realizing a sustainable society. Lignocellulosic biomass mainly contains cellulose and hemicellulose, which can be converted into small molecules by chemical, physical, or biological processes. In chemical conversion, cellulose and hemicellulose are decomposed by pyrolysis [1, 2] or hydrothermal [3, 4] treatment. Improvement of conversion efficiency requires an understanding of the decomposition process and its control. To clarify the reaction mechanisms, thermodynamics, and kinetics of biomass decomposition, various experimental [5, 6, 7, 8, 9] and theoretical [10] studies have been reported. Theoretical calculations are particularly effective in identifying intermediates and transition states that are difficult to observe experimentally.

Thermodynamic quantities are fundamental to understanding chemical phenomena but must be evaluated with high accuracy for reliable predictions. In practice, an accuracy of approximately 1 kcal/mol—commonly referred to as chemical accuracy—is generally considered sufficient. In quantum chemistry, coupled‐cluster theory with singles, doubles, and perturbative triples in the complete basis set limit, CCSD(T)/CBS, is known to satisfy chemical accuracy relative to experimental values. This level of accuracy enables reliable evaluation of thermodynamic quantities, particularly for gas‐phase molecules. High‐accuracy analyses of biomass pyrolysis based on such calculations have also been reported [11, 12].

In contrast, evaluation of hydrothermal decomposition requires thermodynamic quantities in aqueous solution, where accurate estimation of solvation (hydration) free energies remains a major challenge in computational chemistry. Numerous approaches based on quantum chemistry, molecular dynamics, ab initio molecular dynamics [13, 14, 15, 16], and their combination with machine learning [17, 18] have been investigated for this purpose. These approaches typically entail a trade‐off between computational cost and accuracy, and efficient evaluation is particularly desirable for complex reaction networks such as biomass decomposition, which comprise a large number of molecular species. Machine learning schemes that directly predict experimental values have been explored and are expected to provide rapid, accurate estimates for molecular systems with available experimental data [19, 20, 21].

Previous computational studies have reported thermodynamic properties and aqueous‐phase reaction energetics for selected biomass‐related compounds, including glucose, furan derivatives, sugar alcohols, and organic acids [22, 23]. However, a systematic approach that enables high‐accuracy evaluation of solution‐phase thermodynamic quantities across extensive reaction networks while maintaining computational efficiency is still lacking.

In this study, we develop a computational framework to evaluate accurate solution‐phase thermodynamics by combining gas‐phase free‐energy calculations using the composite quantum chemistry method [24, 25], which was developed to efficiently achieve CCSD(T)/CBS‐level accuracy with machine‐learning predictions of hydration free energies.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The Methods section describes the analyzed reaction pathways, the composite method, and the experimental data and machine‐learning method used to predict hydration free energies. The Results section presents the predictive accuracy for hydration free energy, the calculated reaction free energies, and the stable compounds identified from these calculations. Finally, the conclusions are given.

Methods

This section outlines the reaction pathways examined for the decomposition of xylose and glucose, the computational procedure of the composite method, and the dataset and method used for machine‐learning prediction of hydration free energy. Figure 1 presents representative reaction pathways for the decomposition of xylose and glucose. The structural formulas of the compounds, the molecular names used in this paper, and leaving molecules such as H2O, CO, and formaldehyde are shown. Most of the reaction pathways were taken from previous studies [11, 26], and some additional reactions were included. All reactions, including equilibrium processes, are represented by single arrows for simplicity. The upper part of Figure 1 shows the reaction pathways of xylose. β‐d‐xylopyranose and d‐xylose are highlighted in red. For visual clarity, these two molecules are drawn separately, although they are in chemical equilibrium. The lower part of Figure 1 shows the reaction pathways of glucose. β‐d‐glucopyranose and d‐glucose are highlighted in red. When one carbon atom is removed by formaldehyde elimination, some of the resulting molecules overlap with those appearing in the xylose pathways shown in the upper part. All pathways shown in Figure 1 were included in the calculations, resulting in a dataset comprising 84 molecules and 73 reactions.

The composite quantum chemistry method [21] employed in this study is a scheme for estimating the CCSD(T)/CBS energies by combining lower‐cost electron‐correlation methods with smaller basis sets. The complete‐basis‐set limit of the Hartree–Fock (HF) energy was obtained with an extrapolation formula [27] based on HF energies calculated with cc‐pVDZ, cc‐pVTZ, and cc‐pVQZ basis sets. To estimate the electron‐correlation energy, the composite method applies the empirical relationship expressed by Equation (1).

In Equation (1), E represents the correlation energies computed at the MP2/cc‐pVXZ (X = D, T, Q), CCSD/cc‐pVYZ (Y = D, T), and CCSD(T)/cc‐pVDZ levels, and c denotes the corresponding coefficients. The coefficients c are empirical parameters, and values previously determined to reproduce the correlation energies of small organic molecules [25] were adopted in this study. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used for geometry optimization and frequency calculations, with ωB97X‐D as the exchange‐correlation functional and aug‐cc‐pVTZ as the basis set. The vibrational contributions to the enthalpy and Gibbs free energy were evaluated from the DFT frequencies. All thermodynamic quantities were evaluated at 298.15 K. The reliability of the composite method for thermochemical calculations has been demonstrated in previous studies. Chemical accuracy was reported for formation enthalpies [25] of small molecules relative to experimental values, and reaction enthalpies [28] differed by only about 0.2 kcal/mol on average from independently computed CCSD(T)/CBS‐level values. These reported results support the use of the composite method as an efficient approximation to CCSD(T)/CBS calculation.

Hydration free energies were obtained from the FreeSolv dataset [29]. This dataset contains experimental hydration free energies of small organic molecules in the standard state, from which a subset of 300 molecules composed of H, C, and O atoms was extracted. The details of the extracted FreeSolv subset are included in the Supporting Information. Table S1 lists the SMILES representations of the molecules with the experimental hydration free energies. Figure S1 shows histograms of the molecular weights and the target hydration free energies. For these molecules, two‐dimensional descriptors were computed using the Python libraries RDKit [30] (version 2025.09.6) and Mordred (version 1.2.0) [31, 32]. The use of two‐dimensional descriptors enables efficient prediction while retaining sufficient topological and physicochemical information relevant to hydration free energies. The applied descriptors are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. LightGBM [33, 34] was used as the machine‐learning method. Model performance was evaluated using double cross‐validation with five folds in both the outer and inner loops. Hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna [35, 36]. The optimized hyperparameters used to construct the final model were listed in Table S4.

Results and Discussion

This section presents the predictive accuracy of hydration free energies obtained by machine learning and the resulting reaction free energies in aqueous solution for xylose and glucose decomposition, evaluated by combining the composite quantum chemistry method and machine‐learning predictions.

Figure 2 presents the predicted hydration free energies for the test data in the double cross‐validation of the FreeSolv subset. The horizontal axis represents the experimental hydration free energy, and the vertical axis represents the value predicted by machine learning. The mean absolute error (MAE) and maximum absolute error (MaxE) were 0.55 and 10.56 kcal/mol, respectively. The coefficient of determination (R2) was 0.89. Overall, the machine‐learning model shows high predictive accuracy, although three outliers are observed for molecules with exceptionally large hydration free energies. Even including these outliers, the MAE remained 0.55 kcal/mol, indicating robust predictive performance for small molecules. Two of these three exceptional molecules were xylopyranose and glucopyranose, suggesting that relatively large cyclic molecules with multiple hydroxyl groups are more challenging to predict. Using the optimized hyperparameters, the final model was retrained on the full dataset and applied to predict hydration free energies for the molecules involved in the decomposition reactions of xylose and glucose.

Predictive accuracy of hydration free energies obtained by machine learning.

The machine‐learning‐based approach for estimating hydrated‐state free energies was validated against free‐energy differences derived from experimentally measured equilibrium concentrations of sugars in aqueous solution [37, 38]. Its accuracy was also benchmarked against commonly used DFT‐based methods for calculating hydration free energies. For the DFT‐based methods, geometry optimizations and free‐energy calculations in water were performed using the polarizable continuum model (PCM), conductor‐like polarizable continuum model (CPCM), and solvation model based on density (SMD). Hydration free energies were obtained from the differences between the aqueous‐ and gas‐phase free energies. To ensure a consistent comparison, gas‐phase free energies from the composite method were used throughout, and only the method used to evaluate the hydration free energy was varied.

Table 1 summarizes the mean absolute errors relative to experimental reaction free energy in the hydrated state, ΔG aq for the isomerization of glucose, xylose, ribose, talose, and arabinose. The α‐ and β‐d‐pyranose, α‐ and β‐d‐furanose, and open‐chain forms were considered, with the corresponding β‐d‐pyranose form used as the reference. Reaction free energies for the individual sugars are provided in Table S5. Because the training set contains only a limited number of glucose and xylose structures and none of the remaining sugar structures, this comparison also assesses the predictive performance of the model beyond the compounds represented in the training data. The present approach yields mean absolute errors of up to 3.69 kcal/mol, comparable to those of the DFT‐based methods. The experimental free‐energy differences for anomerization, defined here as interconversion between the α and β forms, are less than 1 kcal/mol. None of the methods examined resolves these small differences with sufficient quantitative accuracy. Nevertheless, the machine‐learning‐based approach maintains comparable performance for sugar structures absent from the training set, demonstrating useful transferability to related molecular systems.

Overall, the proposed approach provides predictive accuracy comparable to that of commonly used DFT‐based solvation models. A notable advantage of the present approach is observed for the open‐chain forms, for which it provides the lowest errors, whereas the DFT‐based methods tend to underestimate the reaction free energies. Open‐chain sugars are highly flexible and possess many accessible conformations; consequently, reliable DFT‐based hydration free energies may require extensive conformational sampling. In contrast, a model trained directly on experimental hydration free energies may implicitly account for conformational effects reflected in the experimental data contained in the training set. Because the proposed approach requires neither aqueous‐phase geometry optimization nor conformational sampling, it enables rapid evaluation and is particularly advantageous for large, conformationally flexible molecules.

According to ΔH gas in Table 3, formation of 2C03 and LA (lactic acid) is thermodynamically most stable in the xylose system in terms of enthalpy, whereas β‐d‐glucopyranose, the reactant, is most stable in the glucose system. Comparison of ΔH gas and ΔG gas indicates that an increase in the number of molecules contributes significantly to stabilization of the reaction free energy through entropic effects. In terms of ΔG gas, reactions producing furfural (FF) and 5‐hydroxymethylfurfural (5‐HMF), respectively, accompanied by the elimination of three H2O molecules, are thermodynamically favorable in both the xylose and glucose systems. Comparison of ΔG gas and ΔG aq reveals that the hydration effects vary significantly for the molecular species. Changes of up to ~6 kcal/mol are observed, indicating a thermodynamically significant contribution. For example, in the xylose system, the reaction producing Fu08 and two H2O molecules is thermodynamically unfavorable in the gas phase (ΔG gas = +2.2 kcal/mol) but becomes favorable in the hydrated state (ΔG aq = −4.0 kcal/mol). Among the species considered in terms of ΔG aq, FF in the xylose system and 5‐HMF in the glucose system are identified as the most stable products, with stabilities enhanced relative to those predicted from ΔG gas.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a computational framework for evaluating the thermodynamic quantities in aqueous solution and applied it to the decomposition reactions of lignocellulosic biomass. Gas‐phase thermodynamic quantities were calculated using a composite quantum chemistry method capable of approaching chemical accuracy. Hydration free energies were predicted by machine learning trained on experimental data, yielding a mean absolute error of 0.55 kcal/mol for the test data. Thermodynamic quantities in aqueous solution were evaluated for a dataset comprising 84 molecules and 73 reactions. The results identify FF as the most stable product in the xylose system, whereas 5‐HMF in the glucose system.

The present results are subject to important limitations. The obtained free energies represent standard‐state hydrated conditions at 298.15 K, whereas actual hydrothermal decomposition occurs at elevated temperatures and pressures. The framework also assumes that molecular structures can be represented without explicit solution‐phase optimization or conformational sampling and does not currently include protonated or ionic species, explicit catalytic effects caused by acids, or transition states. Consequently, the dataset should be regarded as a reference for thermodynamic trends rather than a complete description of hydrothermal reactivity. These limitations should be addressed by incorporating temperature‐ and pressure‐dependent hydration free energies and thermal corrections, performing solution‐phase geometry optimization or conformational sampling when required, and explicitly treating protonation, ionic species, or water molecules. Extension of the framework to transition states and activation free energies would further enable kinetic analysis. Despite these limitations, the resulting dataset provides useful standard‐state aqueous thermodynamic reference data for intermediates and reaction pathways relevant to hydrothermal biomass decomposition.
