Evidence-Based Perspectives on Complementary and Alternative Medicine in Cancer Treatment: Current Advances and Clinical Relevance
Authors: Adil Farooq Wali, Sirajunisa Talath, Nawar Nezar Alaani, Omnia Ibrahim Mohamed, Tiba Humam Alhamadani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer is a leading cause of death worldwide with considerable physical, psychological and socioeconomic impact. In parallel to traditional treatments such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery and immunotherapy, patients with cancer have increasingly turned towards Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM) in their quest for better quality of life, symptom relief and whole-person oriented approaches. In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of the role of CAM therapies in cancer management, including Ayurvedic medicine, Traditional Chinese Medicine (eg, acupuncture), naturopathy, homeopathy, yoga and tai chi, dietary interventions and mind–body therapies. Available literature indicates that several CAM modalities may alleviate the side effects of treatment including pain, fatigue, anxiety and insomnia while decreasing nausea and depression as well as lymphedema, improving emotional health and clinical trial overall satisfaction. Positive supportive effects through symptom relief have been shown for herbal medicines and acupuncture while yoga, meditation as well as relaxation therapies show benefits related to psychological resilience and stress reduction. The evidence for the effectiveness of individual interventions (homeopathy, certain dietary therapies, etc.) are inconsistent and controversial. Adverse herb–drug interactions, non-adherence to curative treatment schedules and safety issues further emphasise the need for clinical oversight and evidence-informed incorporation of CAM into cancer care. Global prevalence, the state of CAM in the UAE, together with obstacles related to regulation and clinical practice are also reviewed. Recent advances in complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) have increasingly focused on evidence-based integrative oncology, where selected CAM interventions are incorporated alongside conventional cancer therapies to improve patient outcomes. Growing clinical evidence supports the use of acupuncture, standardized herbal formulations, yoga, mindfulness-based interventions, and nutritional approaches for managing cancer-related symptoms, reducing treatment-associated toxicities, and enhancing quality of life. Furthermore, advances in phytochemical research, molecular pharmacology, and systems biology have improved the understanding of the mechanisms underlying CAM therapies, facilitating their rational integration into multidisciplinary cancer care. Despite these promising developments, further well-designed clinical trials and standardized treatment protocols are required to establish their long-term efficacy and safety. Although several complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) interventions have demonstrated encouraging clinical outcomes, the quality of the available evidence varies considerably. While randomized controlled trials support the use of acupuncture, yoga, and mindfulness-based interventions for symptom management and quality-of-life improvement, many studies evaluating herbal medicines are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, and inadequate long-term follow-up. Therefore, additional well-designed multicenter clinical trials are required to establish the efficacy and safety of these interventions.In summary, as long as CAM therapies are used appropriately and in conjunction with healthcare professionals, they may be beneficial adjuncts to standard cancer therapy. The role of these agents in integrative oncology care will require additional well-designed clinical trials and mechanistic studies to determine efficacy and safety profiles.

Introduction

Cancer is the major cause of death all over the world, taking the lives of an astounding 1.69 million people every year (WHO, 2018). Malignancies of the breast (28.0%), colon (16.9%), uterine (6.2%), and lungs (6.0%) are the most common types of malignancy in women. It has been estimated by the World Health Organization (WHO) that new cancer cases may raise to 21 million by the end of 2030. Cancer pain is one of the most severe pains felt by the patient, as reported by researchers.1

Despite its rising popularity, traditional or alternative medicine is often excluded from developed countries’ public health systems. WHO defines Complementary and Alternative Medicines (CAM) as “any diagnosis, treatment, or prevention that complements conventional medicine by expanding medicine’s conceptual framework”.1 Modifying one’s diet, using herbal remedies, trying homeopathy, hypnosis, or practicing mental imaging or visualization are some of the most often employed treatments under CAM.1

Most cancer patients who utilized CAM have improved their overall well-being and lifestyle. However, other factors, such as dissatisfaction with conventional medicine, a poor doctor-patient relationship, a lack of perceived effectiveness, and sheer desperation, may play a role. Various cultural beliefs, indigenous experiences, and ideas form the basis for the body of knowledge and expertise known as complementary and alternative medicine (CAM). People worldwide have turned to CAM to improve physical or mental health.2

Investigating the effectiveness of CAM in cancer treatment necessitates a comprehensive examination of numerous non-traditional therapies that patients frequently include into their mainstream medical regimens.3 A variety of practices are included in CAM, such as homeopathy, herbal medicine, acupuncture, mind-body techniques, or nutritional supplements. Oncology patients are increasingly turning to these treatments in the hopes of reducing treatment-related side effects, improving quality of life, and alleviating symptoms. Patients undergoing chemotherapy often experience nausea, discomfort, and exhaustion; nevertheless, acupuncture has shown promise in alleviating these side effects and improving overall health. Cancer patients can benefit greatly from mind-body techniques like tai chi, yoga, and meditation in terms of lowering stress, anxiety, and despair and increasing their emotional and physical resilience.

It is important to carefully examine the use of dietary supplements including omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals to avoid unfavorable interactions with conventional medicines, since they may boost immune function and mitigate treatment-related toxicities. There is little and conflicting evidence about the effectiveness of homoeopathy in cancer treatment, and the practice continues to be controversial. Rigid clinical research is necessary to confirm the efficacy and safety of CAM therapies, especially since they are being integrated with traditional oncology practices.4 Additionally, this method stresses the significance of patients and doctors keeping lines of communication open in order to guarantee safe and coordinated treatment. Research into CAM treatments is urgently needed to confirm their efficacy in cancer treatment and determine the best way to incorporate them into integrated cancer care. Nevertheless, certain CAM therapies have shown encouraging results.5

Patient’s usage of CAM, particularly in the context of cancer, might also generate safety concerns. Some complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) therapies and herbal products may interact with conventional anticancer treatments by altering hemostatic pathways or modulating drug-metabolizing enzymes. For example, Ginkgo biloba, garlic (Allium sativum), ginseng (Panax ginseng), and ginger (Zingiber officinale) possess antiplatelet properties that may increase the risk of bleeding, particularly in patients receiving anticoagulants or undergoing surgical procedures. Likewise, St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) is a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4) and P-glycoprotein, resulting in reduced plasma concentrations and therapeutic efficacy of several chemotherapeutic agents, including irinotecan and imatinib. Conversely, herbal products such as grapefruit-derived compounds and goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis) inhibit CYP3A4 activity, potentially increasing systemic drug exposure and toxicity. These clinically significant herb–drug interactions highlight the importance of careful medication reconciliation and physician supervision when CAM therapies are used alongside conventional cancer treatment.6 The anticancer treatment could be less successful, or the patient could be exposed to severe side effects from the chemotherapy. Therefore, this article was compiled to comprehensively review the available information regarding applying CAM therapies to cancer treatment.7

Despite the growing interest in complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) for cancer management, the available literature remains fragmented, with many reviews focusing only on individual CAM modalities, specific cancer types, or symptom management rather than providing a comprehensive evaluation of the field. In recent years, complementary and alternative medicine has evolved considerably from traditional healing practices toward evidence-based integrative oncology. Advances in clinical research have generated stronger evidence supporting selected CAM modalities, particularly acupuncture, herbal medicines with standardized bioactive constituents, yoga, meditation, and nutritional interventions, as supportive strategies for symptom management and improving quality of life in cancer patients. Simultaneously, progress in molecular biology, pharmacogenomics, metabolomics, and network pharmacology has provided deeper insights into the mechanisms by which phytochemicals and mind-body interventions influence inflammation, oxidative stress, immune modulation, apoptosis, and tumor-associated signaling pathways. These scientific developments have facilitated a more systematic evaluation of CAM therapies and promoted their integration into multidisciplinary cancer care. Nevertheless, considerable challenges remain regarding treatment standardization, quality control, safety evaluation, herb–drug interactions, and the generation of high-quality clinical evidence. Therefore, this review not only summarizes the current evidence supporting CAM in cancer treatment but also highlights recent advances, emerging research trends, and future perspectives that are shaping the development of integrative oncology.

Classification of CAM

The National Centre for Complementary and Alternative Medicine (NCCAM) developed the name “complementary and alternative medicine” (CAM).8 CAM can be broadly classified into several categories based on the types of practices and therapies they encompass. These classifications help in understanding the diverse nature of CAM and its various approaches to health and healing.9

Ayurvedic Medicine (Ayurveda) in Cancer Treatment

Ayurveda is often translated as “the science of life.” This ancient Indian practice of holistic, all-natural medicine dates back at least 3000 years, making it one of the world’s first such systems. Ayurveda is an all-encompassing medical practice that treats the whole person by focusing on restoring balance in the physical, mental, and spiritual realms. Traditional Indian medicine is based on the ancient practices of yoga and meditation developed in India. Its primary focus is on fostering wellness rather than preventing illness. It is true that many diseases and disorders, like cancer, diabetes, heart disease, and neurological illness, were once thought to be untreatable, but modern medicine has made great strides in this area. Ayurvedic practice incorporates many modalities, including diet, exercise, meditation, medicines, massage, sunbathing, and breathing techniques. Several Ayurvedic herbs, including turmeric, amla, ashwagandha, kumanjam, black cumin, rhubarb root, and Triphala, have shown potential in the treatment of many malignancies, including breast cancer.10–16 However, definitive evidence of Ayurvedic medicines’ efficacy requires more in-depth study. This process is essential to implementing a well-designed experiment, complete with sufficient samples, randomization, and control groups.17

Although numerous herbal compounds have demonstrated significant anticancer activity in preclinical studies through mechanisms such as apoptosis induction, inhibition of cell proliferation, suppression of angiogenesis, and modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways, only a limited number have progressed to well-designed clinical trials. Among the phytochemicals discussed in this review, curcumin has been the most extensively investigated in clinical studies, primarily as an adjunct to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with encouraging results in improving treatment response and reducing therapy-related adverse effects. Similarly, Boswellia serrata extracts have been evaluated in clinical settings for reducing radiation-induced cerebral edema and improving symptom management in patients with brain tumors. In contrast, compounds such as bakuchiol, shikonin, withaferin A, thymoquinone, rhein, and Xihuang Pill remain supported predominantly by in vitro and animal studies, with limited or no high-quality randomized clinical trial evidence demonstrating their efficacy in cancer patients. Therefore, despite promising preclinical findings, further multicenter randomized controlled trials are required before these agents can be routinely recommended in clinical oncology.

Traditional Chinese Medicine in Cancer Treatment

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) is a comprehensive medical system that has been practiced and continuously developed in China over thousands of years. Moxibustion, Chinese herbal medicine, massage (including tui na and t’ai chi), Acupuncture, nutritional therapy, and qi gong are only some of the various modalities that make up TCM. Common methods include tai chi massages, Acupuncture, and traditional Chinese medicine. Herbs are important in Chinese medicine, helping restore qi (vital energy) and achieving yin-yang harmony. In contrast to yang, which signifies hot, aroused, or active energy, yin is cold, slow, or inactive. Traditional Chinese medicine uses botanicals and herbs to address an illness’s symptoms and roots. They boost the immune system, which is why they are also used to prevent illness and improve health. Moschus moschiferus Linnaeus, Bos taurus domesticus Gmelin and Commiphora myrrha Engl. Moreover, Boswellia carterii is the main ingredient in the Xihuang pill, a popular traditional Chinese medication. The triple-negative breast cancer cell line Hs578T is susceptible to apoptosis induction by aqueous extract of the Xihuang tablet via intrinsic mechanisms, which reduces cell viability and stops cell cycle progression at the S phase. In an in vitro study, aqueous extract of Xihuang Pill was evaluated against the Hs578T triple-negative breast cancer cell line at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 mg/mL. Treatment significantly reduced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, with approximately 50% inhibition of cell proliferation at 2.0 mg/mL, accompanied by S-phase cell-cycle arrest and activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways through increased caspase-3 activity and mitochondrial membrane depolarization. These findings suggest that standardized TCM formulations may exhibit measurable anticancer effects and warrant further validation in preclinical and clinical studies.

Lithospermum, a popular Chinese herb, is the source of naphthoquinone shikonin. The effectiveness of this extract was evaluated by measuring exosome release in MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Shikonin slowed MCF-7 cell growth by decreasing the release of exosomes that originate from tumors.18 Shikonin has also demonstrated potent anticancer activity in breast cancer models. Treatment of MCF-7 cells with 2–10 μM shikonin significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, resulting in nearly 60–70% growth inhibition at 10 μM after 48 hours of exposure. Furthermore, shikonin markedly decreased exosome secretion and induced apoptosis through enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and activation of caspase-dependent signaling pathways. Chinese Fructus Psoraleae, or dried ripe fruit of Psoralea corylifolia L., contains bakuchiol, a meroterpene with a partial terpenoid structure. Anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anti-osteoporotic, anti-oxidant, and anti-depressant/anti-stress characteristics have all been demonstrated.19 One of bakuchiol’s anticancer properties is a marked reduction in the rate at which breast cancer cells multiply, as discovered by researchers.20 Due to its ability to activate the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, bakuchiol suppresses the development of breast cancer cells. Recent research, however, found that bakuchiol had estrogenic activity in both test tube and animal models. More research is needed to confirm bakuchiol’s potential as an anti-breast cancer drug.21 Bakuchiol has been evaluated in several breast cancer cell models at concentrations of 5–20 μM. Treatment with 20 μM bakuchiol resulted in approximately 55–70% inhibition of breast cancer cell proliferation, induced S-phase cell-cycle arrest, increased intracellular ROS production, and promoted apoptotic cell death through activation of the mitochondrial pathway. Although these findings demonstrate promising anticancer activity, additional pharmacokinetic studies and well-designed clinical trials are required before clinical translation.

Acupuncture in Cancer Treatment

Numerous acupuncturists, doctors, dentists, and others use Acupuncture to alleviate, prevent, or treat pain and other medical issues. More than two thousand years ago, this technique was developed in China.22 The Yin and Yang philosophy, which views the universe as comprised of opposing forces that must be brought into equilibrium for health and happiness, seeks to achieve this end by cultivating a state of equilibrium.23 According to acupuncturists, energy channels called meridians connect different body parts and are responsible for specific functions.24 By modulating the secretion of neurotransmitters and neurohormones, Acupuncture can influence brain and spinal cord regions responsible for processing sensory information and controlling automatic bodily processes.25 One possible mechanism by which Acupuncture produces its effects is the transmission of electromagnetic waves at a faster-than-usual frequency. An increase in electromagnetic signaling activity promotes the release of molecules that inhibit pain perception.

Breast cancer patients are often advised to try Acupuncture due to the treatment’s efficacy, low cost, lack of serious side effects, and potential for enhancing patients’ quality of life. Warm Acupuncture combines Acupuncture with moxibustion, in which heat is applied to the acupuncture point by burning the herb.26 Warm acupuncture and diosmin (a semisynthetic flavonoid) were administered to patients with breast cancer-related chronic lymphedema for one month. Treatment efficacy was evaluated using arm circumference measurements, limb volume assessment by water displacement, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores for pain, and patient-reported quality-of-life questionnaires. The warm acupuncture group demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in upper-arm circumference and limb volume, accompanied by improvements in pain scores and functional outcomes compared with the diosmin-treated group. These findings indicate that warm acupuncture may provide a beneficial supportive therapy for managing breast cancer-related lymphedema, although larger randomized controlled trials are required to validate its long-term efficacy and safety. When comparing warm Acupuncture to oral diosmin for treating chronic lymphedema induced by breast cancer, the results showed that warm Acupuncture was more beneficial. Acupuncture’s positive impacts on patients’ lives were evident early on, and the practice’s use of heated needles presented no safety concerns. The benefits of warm Acupuncture have been hypothesized, but more study is required to identify the most helpful acupoints and define the optimal treatment time.27 Side effects, both temporary and permanent, are sometimes experienced when treating breast cancer with either local or systemic therapy. Acupuncture was highly beneficial in alleviating or eliminating short-term side effects from standard breast cancer treatments.28

Saam acupuncture, the Korean variant, is currently practiced by medical professionals in that country. In one investigation, researchers explored the feasibility of incorporating Saam acupuncture as a supplementary treatment for breast cancer patients. For six weeks, these patients attended acupuncture sessions three times a week for 18. The effectiveness of this medication was monitored by regularly checking up on the patients receiving it. Breast cancer therapy often results in lymphedema, and this study suggests that Saam acupuncture can help alleviate this condition. It has been hypothesized that this therapy helps alleviate lymphedema, a common complication for women undergoing breast cancer treatment. Saam acupuncture’s efficacy and safety require a large-scale, randomized, controlled investigation.29

T’ai Chi in Cancer Treatment

Chinese martial arts and healing practice, tai chi emphasizes relaxed, flowing movements and deep breathing. Acupuncture, acupressure, and qi gong share their underlying principles. T’ai chi is a martial art form that originated in China in 1300. It is known for its emphasis on both flexibility and strength. The Yin and Yang paradigm is present, much like in Acupuncture and Chinese herbal medicine. Each movement in tai chi serves a greater purpose and is directed toward bringing harmony between the practitioner and the external environment. T’ai chi has improved several health markers, including blood flow, balance, stress and anxiety levels, aerobic capacity, and flexibility.

Interventions based on tai chi have been the subject of multiple clinical investigations, all of which have confirmed their safety and positive effects on health. Numerous clinical studies, both randomized and non-randomized, have examined the effects of tai chi on a wide range of health problems, including but not limited to cardiovascular disease,30–32 osteoarthritis,33,34 conditions such as diabetes, schizophrenia, depression, poor balance, and the avoidance of falls.35–38 People with breast cancer widely practice Tai Chi because of the favorable effects it has been demonstrated to have on the disease.39–45 The benefits of tai chi in reducing treatment-related side effects and improving the quality of life for women with breast cancer were validated by a meta-analysis of nine randomized controlled studies.

Breast cancer patients (N=322) were studied to determine the effects of tai chi on pathology. Strength in the handgrip and elbow flexion were both found to increase with tai chi training. Pathology, BMI, physical, social, and emotional health, and health-related quality of life were unaffected.41 T’ai chi practitioners improved their BMI and quality of life more than those who engaged in non-meditative light exercise, according to a recent study by Larkey et al,39 Research shows that breast cancer survivors who practice tai chi report less insomnia. One study found that tai chi practitioners had decreased cellular inflammatory responses and expression of genes encoding proinflammatory mediators. A meta-analysis of 5 randomized controlled studies involving 407 breast cancer survivors indicated that tai chi did not improve survival rates or any secondary outcomes (weight, BMI, bone mineral density, or muscle strength).46 A contemporary systematic assessment of tai chi’s efficacy in treating, preventing, and rehabilitating human health conditions is lacking. Due to the wide range of tai chi styles, intensities, durations, and approaches to learning and practice, there is a pressing need for more precise evidence of tai chi’s therapeutic effects.

Naturopathy in Cancer Treatment

Naturopathy is a fascinating alternative medicine because it primarily emphasizes the efficacy of the body’s homeostatic mechanisms. The disease’s sources are attacked, healthy bodily functions are restored, and future illnesses are avoided with the help of the treatment plan provided by this medical model. In contrast to practitioners of many other schools of allopathic and alternative medicine, naturopathic doctors tend to counsel their patients on how to live a healthy lifestyle and prevent illness in the first place. Modern naturopathic treatments that aim to alleviate physiological and psychological stress include nutritional supplements, physical therapy, homeopathy, hormone therapies, hydrotherapy, and personalized exercise routines.

In recent years, naturopathic doctors have increasingly recommended TCM treatments.47 A study of 1817 US patient visits to naturopathic doctors found that these practitioners routinely recommended plant medicines, vitamins, and minerals to treat chronic conditions such as back problems, headaches, and fatigue. The purpose of this study was to conduct a cross-sectional mail survey among naturopathic physicians who see breast cancer patients.48 Researchers from the United States and Canada discovered that these clinicians routinely conducted diagnostic imaging, physical examinations, and lifestyle behavior studies. Botanical drugs comprised 88% of all treatments, and nutritional supplements, including vitamin C and coenzyme Q-10, accounted for 84%.49,50 Although more research is needed to demonstrate that naturopathic treatments are as effective as conventional ones. Naturopathy, which emphasizes the body’s intrinsic power to heal itself, shows enormous promise to ensure patient health and offer an alternate road to well-being.

Homeopathy in Cancer Treatment

In the 19th century, German physician Samuel Christian Hahnemann developed homeopathy as an alternative medicine. The notion of Similia Similibus Curantur, “like cures like,” is central to homeopathy. Hahnemann theorized that medications should cause symptoms analogous to the disease they were meant to cure to trigger the body’s homeostatic response and bring it back into balance.51 Regarding dosage, alternative medicine practitioners strictly adhere to the “rule of minimum dose,” which states that the smaller the dose, the greater the effect. Although many Homeopathic therapies have effective ingredients, these remedies are often diluted to the point that only trace amounts of the original substance remain in the final dosage. Careful observation of his patient’s physiological and psychological circumstances led Hahnemann to develop Homeopathic treatment plans, which he then tailored to each patient depending on their responses to earlier experiments. He advocated that people in good health continually consume chemicals that induce various diseases’ symptoms.

Homeopathic medicines sometimes contain diluted versions of plant and animal ingredients like cinchona bark and phytolacca that are either orally or applied topically.52 Recent studies have looked at how Terminalia chebula homeopathic dilutions affect the expansion of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Particularly at greater dilutions, the treatment greatly reduced the proliferation of breast cancer cells.53 While several clinical studies were conducted on homeopathic therapies and around 5 million adults and 1 million children utilized homeopathic remedies in 2012, scientists still widely questioned the efficiency of this therapeutic approach.54 Despite a few high-quality studies, the available data is inadequate and unreliable.55 Homeopathy has promising results in treating various conditions, but the extent to which it works is still up for debate.56 While significant conceptual differences exist between the various comprehensive medical systems, many similarities exist in their approaches to illness prevention and treatment. The foundation of these medical tenets rests on the assumption that the human body possesses innate recuperative powers. Restoring harmony between body, mind, and soul is a common healing component. Treatment under such systems is often individualized and founded on an in-depth analysis of the patient’s symptoms. As these healing modalities are typically employed without proper experimental reasoning or isolation as standalone therapies, there is a need for more research to comprehend these systems as a whole better.

Despite its widespread use by some cancer patients for symptom relief and supportive care, the clinical effectiveness of homeopathy remains controversial. Although a few studies have reported improvements in quality of life, fatigue, anxiety, or treatment-related symptoms, the overall quality of evidence is limited by small sample sizes, methodological weaknesses, heterogeneity in study design, and a high risk of bias. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have concluded that there is insufficient high-quality evidence to demonstrate that homeopathy provides benefits beyond placebo for cancer treatment or supportive oncology care. Consequently, major oncology organizations do not recommend homeopathy as a substitute for evidence-based conventional cancer therapies. From the authors’ perspective, homeopathy should only be considered, if at all, as a complementary supportive approach under appropriate medical supervision, and patients should be encouraged to discuss its use with their oncology team to avoid delaying or replacing established treatments.

Mind-Body Interventions in Healing Cancer

The premise of mind-body therapies is that one’s state of mind can affect one’s physiological processes and symptoms. The goal of many of the methods used in these therapies goes beyond mere relaxation since they are also designed to improve health and treat various illnesses.57 Music therapy, dance and movement therapies, and other forms of this mind-body intervention were found to be particularly helpful in lowering anxiety levels in people with breast cancer.

The 13-trial study involving 606 patients, however, concluded that art therapy had no beneficial benefits on the participants’ depression due to their breast cancer.58 While there was little to no effect on the participants’ heart rates, respiration rates, or blood pressures. The study found that music therapies could improve cancer patients’ anxiety, pain, mood, and quality of life.58 In a separate study, music therapy has been shown to reduce cancer-related stress and discomfort. Some of the most debilitating side effects of cancer treatment, such as exhaustion, pain, and insomnia, are mitigated by the use of relaxation techniques like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), hypnosis, guided imagery, and others.59 However, there has been no comprehensive analysis of the existing evidence on the various art therapies, especially in women with breast cancer. Since the treatment is affordable, more people can take part in it. CBT for vasomotor symptoms in breast cancer have been demonstrated to be equally beneficial in recent studies. Vasomotor symptoms may respond well to breathing exercises, relaxation strategies, and cognitive behavioral therapy. Breast cancer patients and survivors who practice yoga report feeling less tired.60

Importance of CAM in the Treatment of Cancer

Instead of conventional medical interventions like surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation, alternative therapies are advocated or used. Other treatments are often invasive and have a significant biological effect, making them costly, risky, and without sufficient evidence. They may have adverse effects due to biological actions or patients delay appropriate treatment. The phrase “complementary medicine” describes treatments not often found in Western medicine but are provided in addition to standard therapy.61 Supplements, diets designed to combat cancer, lifestyle modifications, and other interventions are some of the alternatives to conventional anticancer treatment that can be found in alternative medicine. Changes in nutrition, herbs, homeopathy, hypnosis, and spiritual healing are a few alternative therapies that have helped many people.62

The utilization of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) varies considerably across geographical regions and cancer types. In many Asian countries, traditional medical systems such as Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda, and Kampo medicine are widely integrated into routine healthcare, whereas in North America and Europe CAM is more commonly used as supportive care alongside conventional cancer treatment. Similarly, CAM utilization differs according to cancer type, with patients diagnosed with breast, prostate, colorectal, and gynecological cancers reporting higher rates of CAM use than those with other malignancies. These regional and disease-specific differences are influenced by cultural beliefs, healthcare accessibility, physician recommendations, socioeconomic factors, and the availability of evidence-based integrative oncology services. Therefore, the interpretation of CAM utilization patterns should consider these variations when evaluating their clinical relevance and applicability.

Sometimes, conventional Western medicine and CAM are utilized together in “integrative healthcare.” Conventional medicine places a heavy emphasis on outcomes that have been shown to work in clinical trials. Still, patients may feel that their doctors and other healthcare providers do not have enough time to devote to the other areas in which they may want help, especially while dealing with cancer.63 Many CAM doctors can devote ample time to each patient and have in-depth conversations about their concerns. CAM typically considers the patient’s and family’s situation, emphasizing compassion, quality of life, and the possibility of better outcomes than conventional medicine. Summarizes the Herbal Medicines Used in Cancer Treatment

Among the various complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) interventions, acupuncture has the strongest and most consistent evidence from multiple randomized controlled trials for managing chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, cancer-related pain, aromatase inhibitor-associated arthralgia, cancer-related fatigue, and breast cancer-related lymphedema. Yoga has also demonstrated consistent benefits in randomized clinical trials by improving fatigue, sleep quality, anxiety, depression, and overall quality of life, particularly among breast cancer survivors. Similarly, mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) and meditation have shown significant improvements in psychological distress, emotional well-being, stress reduction, and health-related quality of life across several randomized controlled studies. In contrast, although certain herbal medicines, including curcumin and Boswellia serrata, have shown encouraging results as supportive therapies, the available clinical evidence remains comparatively limited because of the small number of high-quality randomized trials and variability in study design. Therefore, current evidence most strongly supports acupuncture and mind–body interventions as adjunctive therapies in integrative cancer care, whereas additional large-scale randomized controlled trials are required to establish the efficacy of many herbal medicines.

It should be noted that a substantial proportion of the clinical evidence presented in this review is derived from studies involving breast cancer patients, particularly those evaluating acupuncture, yoga, mindfulness-based interventions, and selected herbal medicines for symptom management and quality-of-life improvement. Breast cancer is one of the most extensively studied malignancies in integrative oncology, resulting in a comparatively stronger evidence base than that available for many other cancers. However, these findings should not be directly extrapolated to other malignancies because tumor biology, disease stage, treatment regimens, and patient characteristics differ considerably across cancer types. Although some CAM interventions have demonstrated potential benefits in colorectal, prostate, lung, head and neck, and gynecological cancers, the available evidence remains relatively limited and often consists of small clinical studies or preclinical investigations. Therefore, additional multicenter randomized controlled trials involving diverse cancer populations are required to determine the efficacy, safety, and clinical applicability of CAM interventions across different malignancies.

The strength of clinical evidence supporting complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) interventions varies considerably among different modalities. Acupuncture has the strongest clinical evidence for managing cancer-related symptoms, including pain, chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, cancer-related fatigue, and aromatase inhibitor-associated arthralgia. Mind–body interventions such as yoga, meditation, and mindfulness-based stress reduction are also supported by moderate-quality evidence demonstrating improvements in quality of life, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, and fatigue among cancer patients. In contrast, although several herbal medicines and dietary supplements have demonstrated promising anticancer activity in preclinical studies, their clinical evidence remains limited because of insufficient large-scale randomized controlled trials, variability in herbal formulations, and lack of standardized treatment protocols. Homeopathy and several other CAM modalities continue to have limited or inconclusive clinical evidence regarding their efficacy in cancer treatment. Therefore, the integration of CAM into oncology practice should be guided by the quality of available clinical evidence, patient preferences, and established safety considerations.

CAM in the United Arab Emirates

People in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) tend to lean toward CAM practices like traditional medicine and massage therapy because they believe these approaches are safer and more in tune with nature than conventional medicine.71 Research has shown that the high prevalence of CAM use in the UAE is due, in large part, to the population’s strong conviction in the efficacy of herbal remedies.72 For this reason, the NHS officially recognizes CAM therapies like herbal medicine, and CAM treatments like chiropractic, TCM, and homeopathy are becoming increasingly mainstream and legally recognized in the UAE.73–75 Hhomeopathy is gaining ground as a viable supplemental medical practice around the world. It is already incorporated into national insurance programs in the UK, UAE, and India. The Government of the UAE sanctioned homeopathy in the country in 2001, and the MOH there now regulates it as a valid medical practice.

Safety Considerations, Risks, and Contraindications of CAM in Cancer Care

Although complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is widely perceived as safe because many therapies are derived from natural sources, these interventions are not without risks. Herbal medicines may interact with conventional anticancer drugs through pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms, potentially altering drug efficacy or increasing toxicity. For example, St. John’s wort may induce cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4) enzymes and P-glycoprotein, reducing the plasma concentrations of several chemotherapeutic agents, whereas Ginkgo biloba, garlic, ginseng, and ginger possess antiplatelet activity that may increase bleeding risk, particularly in patients receiving anticoagulants or undergoing surgery. Variability in herbal product composition, contamination, adulteration, and lack of standardization further complicate their clinical use and may lead to unpredictable therapeutic outcomes.

Certain CAM interventions also have specific contraindications. Acupuncture should be used cautiously in patients with severe thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, bleeding disorders, or active skin infections because of the increased risk of bleeding and infection. Similarly, some herbal supplements should be avoided during chemotherapy or immunotherapy unless their safety has been established, as they may interfere with drug metabolism or immune responses. Therefore, clinicians should routinely inquire about CAM use, evaluate potential herb–drug interactions, encourage open communication with patients, and recommend evidence-based CAM interventions only under appropriate medical supervision. Continued pharmacovigilance, standardized manufacturing practices, and high-quality clinical research remain essential to ensure the safe integration of CAM into oncology practice.

The Macrobiotic Diet

Despite its modern origins, the Yin-Yang idea of balance is at the heart of the macrobiotic diet. Yin and Yang, or complementary yet opposite forces, are central to TCM since they are thought to characterize all aspects of existence. Macrobiotics is a lifestyle based on a philosophy of harmony with the natural world, and its tenets are embodied in how food is chosen, cooked, and eaten.77 Cancer cannot be cured by changing diet. The current formulation of the macrobiotic diet is somewhat similar to the dietary recommendations put forward by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) in recent years. Whole grains provide 50–60% of macrobiotic calorie consumption, 25–30% of calorie intake comes from vegetables, and the remaining 10–25% from seaweed, beans, and soups. Meat, dairy, some vegetables, and processed foods are all discouraged in favor of soy. Key to the macrobiotic diet is miso-based soups, which are made from fermented soybeans. The presence of genistein, a substance found in soybeans, has been linked to a lower risk of getting breast cancer. However, no evidence suggests that a macrobiotic diet benefits people with cancer. Not only can this diet lead to malnourished individuals losing weight, but it may also harm cancer patients.

Megavitamin Therapy

Patients and alternative health care providers may swear by vitamin C infusions or hundreds of daily capsules as a panacea for all ailments. The use of megavitamins or orthomolecular therapy to treat disease has not been supported by evidence.78

Many people in the United States are fascinated by the idea that individuals may use their thoughts to improve physical health. There are some mind-body interventions that orthodox medicine is starting to take seriously. Examples of practices with solid empirical support include meditation, biofeedback, and yoga for managing stress and other physiological responses.79,80 Others believe that patients can use their mental strengths and the results of mind-body practices to either avoid or treat their cancer. The field of study known as bioelectromagnetics examines how electromagnetic fields interact with living organisms. Replacement of Conventional Therapies by CAM

Complementary therapies can potentially reduce pain, especially in the postoperative period. One study revealed that Reiki helped cancer patients deal with pain for up to four days, while another found that Acupuncture helped patients recover faster after surgery by reducing discomfort and enhancing arm mobility. Ear acupuncture reduces pain intensity,85 and research suggests that Acupuncture and massage can help with both pain and depression.86 After breast cancer surgery, hypnosis lessens the intensity of pain, unpleasantness, discomfort, nausea, tiredness, and mental anguish.87,88 Cancer can also be alleviated by using support groups and a healing touch.

Several complementary therapies have shown promise in reducing chemotherapy’s immediate and long-term negative effects. Stimulation of the P6 point with acupuncture89,90 or self-administered acupressure91,92 alleviates nausea and vomiting. Massage and therapeutic touch reduced pain, mood disruption, and exhaustion, while reflexology reduced chemotherapy-related anxiety.93,94 Group cognitive behavioral therapy has been shown to lower external health locus of control,95 while group relaxation and guided imagery have been shown to lower fatigue and improve sleep problems. Anthroposophic mistletoe extracts have been found to improve both the quality of life and the patient’s ability to withstand the toxic effects of chemotherapy.96

According to some research, the negative effects of radiotherapy can be mitigated with the use of complementary therapies. Guided imagery and relaxation techniques have been shown to improve both the time it takes to fall asleep, and the duration of sleep, and cognitive behavioral group intervention can help to shift focus from the outside to within on the health, both of which can increase energy and alertness.95 Mistletoe extracts from an anthroposophical perspective can help ameliorate radiotherapy’s deleterious effects on quality of life.96 However, there are conflicting reports about the efficacy of herbal skin gels like Aloe vera in protecting and healing skin after radiotherapy.97,98 Therefore, this topic warrants further investigation. The skin healed faster than with a placebo after finishing radiation and using homeopathic X-rays and belladonna treatments.99 Several homeopathic treatments for itchy skin were effective.100

Hormone-treated patients experiencing menopausal symptoms commonly seek relief from complementary therapy. Acupuncture and electroacupuncture have alleviated hot flashes.101,102 When women were treated with either applied relaxation or electroacupuncture, they saw an improvement in their psychological health and vasomotor symptoms.95 Participants in an 8-week mindfulness-based stress reduction course reported improved sleep quality.103

Receipt of emotional and social support has been found to aid in healing after a breast cancer diagnosis. Complementary therapies and meditation methods may also be helpful in addition to more conventional forms of psychological help. Breast cancer patients who undergo hypnosis before surgery experience less emotional distress.104 There are several short-term advantages to aromatherapy and massage; these include improvements in one’s mental health, mood, depression, anxiety, relaxation, body image, sleep, and maybe physical ailments.105,106 Reflexology has been shown to alleviate stress and pain93,107 and offer psychological benefits. The effectiveness of reflexology in alleviating symptoms is now being analyzed.108,109

A study conducted in Colombia, including 3117 cancer patients, revealed that the prevalence of CAM use was 51.7% among cancer patients. A total of 43 of 47 breast cancer patients (91%) used BB-CAM throughout therapy. This study documented that 43 patients had 30 possibly clinically relevant interactions (70%). Challenges and Limitations of CAM

Despite the increasing acceptance of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) in oncology, several challenges limit its routine clinical implementation. Safety remains a major concern because certain herbal medicines and dietary supplements may interact with chemotherapeutic agents, immunotherapies, or targeted therapies, potentially affecting treatment efficacy or increasing the risk of adverse events. In addition, variability in the quality, composition, and standardization of herbal products makes it difficult to ensure consistent therapeutic outcomes. Patient adherence to CAM interventions may also be influenced by treatment complexity, financial burden, cultural beliefs, and unrealistic expectations regarding therapeutic benefits. Another important challenge is the limited awareness and knowledge of CAM among healthcare professionals, which may reduce their confidence in discussing CAM use with patients. As a result, many patients do not disclose their CAM use, increasing the risk of unrecognized herb–drug interactions and inappropriate self-medication. Strengthening physician education, encouraging open patient–physician communication, and developing evidence-based clinical guidelines will be essential for the safe and effective integration of CAM into multidisciplinary cancer care.

The integration of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) with conventional cancer therapy requires careful clinical oversight to minimize the risk of herb–drug interactions and treatment-related complications. Clinicians should routinely obtain a comprehensive medication history, including all herbal medicines, dietary supplements, and over-the-counter CAM products used by patients. Potential pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions, particularly those involving cytochrome P450 enzymes, P-glycoprotein transporters, and anticoagulant pathways, should be carefully evaluated before initiating CAM therapies. The use of standardized, quality-controlled herbal formulations with documented safety profiles is preferable to unregulated products of uncertain composition. Patients should be encouraged to disclose all CAM use to their oncology team, enabling shared decision-making and appropriate monitoring for adverse events or reduced therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, collaboration among oncologists, pharmacists, and integrative medicine specialists is essential to ensure that CAM interventions complement rather than interfere with evidence-based cancer treatment. The development of evidence-based clinical guidelines and continued pharmacovigilance will further enhance the safe integration of CAM into routine oncology practice.

The utilization and reported outcomes of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) are influenced by regional differences in healthcare systems, cultural practices, regulatory policies, and the availability of integrative oncology services. In countries such as China, Japan, and South Korea, traditional medical systems are formally integrated into national healthcare systems, resulting in greater acceptance, standardized clinical practice, and broader clinical experience with therapies such as Traditional Chinese Medicine and Kampo medicine. In contrast, CAM in many Western countries is primarily used as a complementary approach alongside conventional cancer treatment, with utilization often influenced by patient preference, socioeconomic status, and access to specialized integrative oncology services. Regulatory oversight and quality control of herbal medicines also differ considerably between countries, potentially affecting product consistency, safety, and therapeutic outcomes. Consequently, variations in healthcare infrastructure, cultural beliefs, practitioner training, and patient populations may contribute to differences in CAM utilization and reported clinical outcomes. Therefore, findings from one geographical region should be interpreted cautiously and validated through well-designed multicenter studies involving diverse populations before being generalized to broader clinical practice.

Perspective and Future Directions

Although complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) has gained considerable attention as an adjunct to conventional cancer therapy, the current evidence remains heterogeneous and varies substantially across different CAM modalities. While interventions such as acupuncture, selected herbal medicines, yoga, and mindfulness-based therapies have demonstrated encouraging results in symptom management and quality-of-life improvement, robust evidence supporting their direct anticancer efficacy is still limited. Many published studies are constrained by small sample sizes, methodological inconsistencies, lack of standardized formulations, inadequate long-term follow-up, and variability in treatment protocols, making direct comparison between studies challenging.

From the authors’ perspective, the future of CAM in oncology lies in its integration into evidence-based cancer care rather than its use as a replacement for conventional therapies. Advances in molecular pharmacology, systems biology, artificial intelligence, pharmacogenomics, and precision medicine offer new opportunities to identify bioactive compounds, elucidate mechanisms of action, predict herb–drug interactions, and personalize CAM interventions for individual patients. However, achieving successful clinical translation will require standardized manufacturing practices, rigorous quality control, multicenter randomized controlled trials, comprehensive safety evaluations, and harmonized regulatory frameworks. Addressing these challenges will strengthen the scientific credibility of CAM and facilitate its responsible incorporation into multidisciplinary cancer management.

The methodological quality of the studies reviewed varies substantially across different CAM modalities. Randomized controlled trials evaluating acupuncture and mind–body interventions generally demonstrate stronger methodological quality, including appropriate control groups, validated outcome measures, and reproducible intervention protocols. In contrast, many studies investigating herbal medicines are preclinical or early-phase clinical investigations with relatively small sample sizes, limited randomization, inadequate blinding, heterogeneous herbal formulations, and short follow-up durations. These methodological limitations reduce the certainty of the available evidence and highlight the need for standardized study designs, larger multicenter randomized controlled trials, and rigorous safety assessments to facilitate the integration of evidence-based CAM into oncology practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) represents a promising adjunct to conventional cancer treatment, particularly for improving symptom management, reducing treatment-related adverse effects, and enhancing patients’ quality of life. However, the available evidence varies considerably across different CAM modalities and is influenced by differences in study design, methodological quality, treatment protocols, and patient populations. Among the interventions reviewed, acupuncture and mind–body therapies, including yoga, meditation, and mindfulness-based stress reduction, currently have the strongest clinical evidence for supportive cancer care. In contrast, although many herbal medicines have demonstrated encouraging anticancer activity in preclinical studies, their routine clinical application remains limited by insufficient high-quality clinical evidence, variability in herbal formulations, and concerns regarding safety and herb–drug interactions. Future research should prioritize large-scale multicenter randomized controlled trials, standardized CAM interventions, rigorous safety and pharmacovigilance assessments, and investigations across diverse cancer types and populations. Such efforts will strengthen the evidence base and facilitate the safe, evidence-based integration of CAM into modern oncology practice.
