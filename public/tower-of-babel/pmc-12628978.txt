Mental health screening in pediatric rheumatology: a feasibility study
Authors: Sarah James, Laura Lolacher, Karen Hodge, Janine Slavec, Anna Draper, Nick McPhate, Kristin Houghton, Jaime Guzman, Lori Tucker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the call for inclusion of routine mental health screening in pediatric rheumatology practice, the practical aspects of implementation in real world clinical settings have not yet been demonstrated. The aims of this study were: (1) to determine the acceptability and practicality of mental health screening in patients with Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA) during routine visits at a pediatric hospital multidisciplinary clinic; (2) to understand the frequency and severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms within this sample.MethodsPatients with JIA aged 8–17 were invited to participate during their routine clinic appointments. Patients completed three questionnaires on an iPad: PROMIS Pediatric Anxiety (v3), PROMIS Pediatric Depression (v3), and an acceptability questionnaire. Caregivers completed an acceptability questionnaire. Participants were provided with feedback on their scoring profile and relevant mental health resources during their clinic visit.ResultsForty patient-parent dyads participated during routine follow-up appointments at the Rheumatology Clinic at BC Children’s Hospital between April and August 2024 (mean patient age = 13.5 years; 23 (57.5%) females). Overall, 92% of patients and 95% of caregivers reported the screening process to be ‘Acceptable’ or ‘Completely Acceptable’. The mean PROMIS Anxiety t-score was 51.79 (SD = 11.06) and the mean PROMIS Depression t-score was 50.54 (SD = 7.62). Six patients (15%) had elevated or very elevated anxiety scores, and two patients (5%) had elevated depression scores.ConclusionThere was high acceptability of the mental health screening process among patients and caregivers. The mean anxiety and depression scores were similar to the general population with a small proportion of patients reporting elevated scores. Mental health screening is practical within rheumatology clinic visits and should be considered by health professionals providing care for youth with JIA.

Background

Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA) is one of the most common chronic health conditions of childhood [1]. Significant advancements have been made in pharmacological treatment in recent decades, leading to improved physical outcomes for children and youth with JIA [2]. The mental health of children with JIA has been largely understudied, but emerging research highlights the need for better, more comprehensive care for this population. Research has shown that youth with JIA experience symptoms of anxiety and depression at higher rates than healthy children [3]. A Canadian single centre study exploring the prevalence of anxiety and depression in patients (n = 80) aged 12–18 years with JIA, found 40% of patients scored above the clinical threshold for at least one subscale of anxiety or depression [4]. In transition-age young adults with JIA, 45% reported problems with anxiety or depression (27% moderate to extreme) [5].

The Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) Mental Health Workgroup recently developed a priority agenda for mental health research [6]. Guidance statements from this working group call for depression and anxiety screening for youth over 12 years, management of identified symptoms and patient education [7]. Additional studies have reinforced this call to action, with McHugh and colleagues [8] calling for regular mental health screening, after their study indicated that patients with JIA and higher disease activity had worse psychological functioning in activities, school, and social settings, and high rates of internalized emotional disturbances compared to healthy controls.

Despite the call for inclusion of routine mental health screening in pediatric rheumatology practice, the practical aspects of implementation in real world clinical settings have not yet been demonstrated. Perceived barriers by pediatric rheumatology clinicians in the systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) population are limited staff resources and time. [9] Current practice related to mental health screening within our Pediatric Rheumatology Clinics at the British Columbia’s Children’s Hospital (BCCH) is typically limited to depression/suicide screening questions within the confidential HEADSS (Home & Environment, Education & Employment, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality and Suicide/Depression [10] assessment completed informally by physicians with selected patients. While this screening may identify patients with suicidal risk, this approach lacks sensitivity to pick up patients with mild or moderate anxiety or depressive symptoms, who may warrant further mental health assessment, intervention or referral to available support.

Mental health screening in the pediatric rheumatology clinic requires selection of a simple valid instrument. The Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement System (PROMIS) is a set of measures that can be used to evaluate physical, mental and social health, with pediatric validated measures available [11]. The PROMIS Pediatric PROMIS-Anxiety and PROMIS-Depression Measures have been clinically validated and used in prior studies to evaluate mental health in youth with JIA [3; 12, 13].

The pediatric rheumatology multidisciplinary team at BCCH identified mental health screening as a clinical priority to improve outcomes. However, mental health screening is not consistently performed in our clinics due to time constraints, competing demands and limited allied health staffing. This quality improvement project aimed to determine the acceptability and practicality of completing mental health screening measures in clinic to inform how to best change practice to best support the mental health of patients with JIA. The purpose of this feasibility study was to:determine the acceptability and practicality of mental health screening during routine clinical visits in the Rheumatology Clinic at BCCH; andto explore the frequency and severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms in 8–17-year-olds with JIA in this sample.

Methods

This was a prospective multi-stakeholder feasibility study. The first involved patient-caregiver dyads, to explore acceptability of the mental health screening process from their perspectives, and to understand the frequency of anxiety and depressive symptoms among patients. The second involved staff members of the BCCH Rheumatology Team to investigate practicality of the screening process within clinic.

Patient-caregiver dyads were recruited through the Rheumatology Clinic at BC Children’s Hospital in Vancouver, British Columbia. Inclusion criteria for patients were: (i) confirmed diagnosis of JIA; (ii) aged 8–17 years and (iii) attending a routine follow-up clinic appointment with a caregiver. The sole exclusion criteria were children and/or caregivers who did not have decision-making capacity. Ethics approval was obtained by The University of British Columbia, Children’s and Women’s Research Ethics Board (certificate H23-03571). Parents or guardians provided written informed consent and children provided written assent.

Study data were collected using REDCap electronic data capture tools [14; 15]. Data were collected using a digital tablet (iPad, Apple Corporation) and REDCap software in survey mode. Participants first completed a brief demographic section (age, sex, mental health diagnosis, any mental heath supports currently being accessed) and three short questionnaires: anxiety screening, depression screening and patient acceptability questionnaire (see Appendix 2), taking approximately 10-15 minutes. Follow this, the parent/caregiver completed one questionnaire: caregiver acceptability questionnaire (see Appendix 3), taking approximately 5 minutes. Patients and caregivers completed the questionnaires immediately prior or following their routine clinic visit, depending on clinic flow.

Mental health measures

Patients completed the PROMIS Pediatric Anxiety – Short Form 8a (version 3.0) and PROMIS Pediatric Depression Short Form 8a (version 3.0). Both measures are validated for use in children aged 8–17 years, and consist of eight questions regarding anxiety or depressive symptoms during the prior week. Participants respond on a 5-point scale from ‘Never’ to ‘Almost Always’. The anxiety measures focuses on fear, panic, worry, dread, hyperarousal and somatic symptoms related to arousal. The depression measure focuses on sadness, guilt, criticism, worthlessness, loneliness, interpersonal alienation, loss of interest, loss of meaning and loss of purpose. Raw scores are converted into T-scores, with higher scores reflecting greater anxiety or depressive symptoms. Cut offs are provided for within normal limits (T-score < 55), mild (T-score 55–59), moderate (T-score 60–69) and severe (T-score > 70) symptom categories. Following expert psychology review, PROMIS categories were revised for this study to align with T-score cut offs in other pediatric-focused anxiety and depression measures. The cut-offs were defined as: “Average” (T-score < 60), “High Average” (T-score 60–64), “Elevated” (T-score 65–69) or “Very Elevated” (T-score > 70). The category names were preferred as they are more reflective of a scoring label (i.e. an “elevated score” on a measure of anxiety), rather than a diagnostic label (e.g. suggesting “moderate” anxiety). The PROMIS measures are not diagnostic tools, which was made clear to participants [11].

Patient and caregiver acceptability measures

Acceptability measures were short, specifically tailored questionnaires based on relevant constructs of the ‘Theoretical Framework of Acceptability’ (TFA [16];) including affective attitude (e.g. how comfortable are you/with your child completing mental health questionnaires in clinic?), burden, perceived effectiveness and general acceptability. The measure also included a question regarding the recommended frequency of screening and a free text space for additional comments.

Patient feedback & resources

Patients were provided with feedback and resources at the same clinic visit, based on their scoring profile from the PROMIS questionnaires by the clinic occupational therapist. The resources were developed by our multidisciplinary study team. The ‘Your Mental Health Matters: Tips & Resources’ (see Appendix 1) handout was provided to patients who screened in the Average category for both measures, and the ‘Support & Crisis Number’ was additionally provided to patients who screened in the High Average, Elevated or Very Elevated category in at least one measure. If patients scored in the Very Elevated category or completion of these questionnaires identified any mental health concerns that required immediate attention, the attending rheumatologist was alerted and consulted with patient and caregiver to determine an action plan. The time taken for the feedback process was variable depending on child/caregiver preference in terms of detail provided, and also potential wait-time based on occupational therapist availability.

Practicality

The second arm of this study invited, via email, rheumatologists who staffed clinics during the study period to complete a Practicality Staff Questionnaire. This short, tailored questionnaire was used to determine facilitators and barriers of mental health screening within routine clinic appointments. Questions related to their level of comfort discussing mental health; feasibility of additional time taken; how worthwhile the screening process was overall; and recommended frequency of screening.

Results

Demographics

Forty patient-parent dyads were recruited between April and August 2024. Another 26 families were approached but declined, primarily due to time constraints. The mean patient age was 13.5 years and there were 23 (57.5%) females. Eight patients reported having a mental health diagnosis, of which four had current mental health supports. Mental health measures

The mean PROMIS Anxiety t-score was 51.79 (SD = 11.06) and the mean PROMIS Depression t-score was 50.54 (SD = 7.62), both similar to the general population (mean = 50 SD = 10). Six patients (15%) had elevated (n = 3) or very elevated (n = 3) anxiety scores, and two patients (5%) had elevated depression scores. Rheumatology physicians were notified and action plans were created for patients who had very elevated scores of anxiety.

Acceptability measures

Overall, 92% of patients and 95% of caregivers reported the screening process to be ‘Acceptable’ or ‘Completely Acceptable’. The majority of caregivers (87.5%) and just over half (57%) of patients reported that the rheumatology team should check in about mental health (see Fig. 1). Most patients (87.5%) reported it took ‘No effort at all’ or ‘A little effort’ to complete the questionnaires. Ninety percent of caregivers reported they were ‘Very Comfortable’ or ‘Comfortable’ with their child completing the questionnaires. Patient and parent responses regarding the ideal frequency of the screening were split between every visit (patient 32.5%; parent 32.5%), every six months (patient 37.5%; parent 32.5%) and every year (patient 25%; parent 32.5%); and other (patient 5%; parent 2.5%). Practicality according to staff

The majority of staff (83%) reported feeling comfortable discussing mental health issues during routine rheumatology clinic appointments. Most staff felt the additional time taken to complete questionnaires is feasible with clinic flow, however one staff did not find it feasible. Two-thirds of respondents reported that the questionnaires helped to understand how their patient was doing with their mental health. Overall, all rheumatologists reported that the mental health screening process was ‘Worthwhile’ or ‘Completely Worthwhile’. The ideal frequency of the screening among staff were every 6 months (n = 3), every visit (n = 2) and ‘Other’ (n = 1; free text explaining “within first 2 visits, when suspected problems and minimally once a year”).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the acceptability and practicality of mental health screening for patients with JIA within a pediatric rheumatology multidisciplinary clinic, and to describe the frequency and severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms among those participating in the study. Overall, there was high acceptability of the screening process from both patient and caregivers. Most rheumatology providers found the screening process feasible within clinic flow, and overall worthwhile. In this small patient sample, mean anxiety and depression symptoms scores were similar to the general population, with a minority of patients reporting elevated anxiety scores (15%) or depression scores (5%). This may not be representative of all children and youth with JIA but may reflect typical rates seen during routine screening in clinics similar to us.

While the overall acceptability of the screening was high among patients and caregivers, there was a discrepancy between patient and caregiver report on whether the rheumatology team should check in about mental health. While 87.5% of caregivers either agreed or strongly agreed, just over half of patients responded that the rheumatology team should check in, while 25% had no opinion and 17.5% disagreed or strongly disagreed. This variation among patient responses suggests that health care providers must be sensitive when approaching mental health screening, as patients may be more hesitant about undergoing mental health screening, compared to caregivers. Further study focused on youth perspectives on the issue of mental health questioning in the pediatric rheumatology clinic is important to ensure our practices remain highly patient focused.

In addition, there was variability in patient report regarding how helpful the screening measures were for patients to understand how they were doing with their mental health, with almost half feeling that it was helpful, a third with no opinion and 17.5% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing. These findings may reflect that the screening questionnaires didn’t capture all aspects of mental health, with only anxiety and depression measures; or perhaps that the patients were already aware how they were doing in terms of their mental health.

Published studies in the area of mental health screening in youth with JIA have largely been part of larger observational studies incorporating a broad range of measures, with large sample sizes but lacking the opportunity to provide individualized feedback [17; 18]. This current study differed in mimicking the actual conditions in which screening would occur during routine clinic visits, and providing patients with individualized feedback on their scoring profile and customized resources based on their scores in real time. Participants in previous studies have identified a desire for mental health resources to be tailored to patients with rheumatic conditions [19].

Despite reported symptoms of anxiety and depression in our sample being similar to the general population, these findings are interpreted with caution in the context of a small sample size and the study design. A systematic review by Fair and colleagues (2019), found children with JIA were found to have higher symptoms of anxiety and depression compared to healthy peers overall. There was significant variation among studies, likely due to methodological discrepancies and small and/or heterogeneous samples. The prevalence of clinically significant anxiety symptoms ranging from 7% to 64%, and depression from 7% − 36%, with most studies supporting an association between active disease and mental health symptoms [3]. The overall lower reported rates of anxiety and depression symptoms found in this study may relate to our small sample size and potential selection bias.

This current study did not capture disease status of participants, and therefore it is possible that recruited patients were skewed towards having inactive disease. We had a high number (n = 26) of families who declined to participate in the study, primarily due to timing constraints. Typically, when patients have active disease, their clinic visits are longer and more complex, and therefore these families may have had limited capacity to be involved in a research study. This is a further consideration regarding the practicality of the screening process within clinic. When carried out purely for clinical purposes, the screening process would require less time compared to as part of a study, which may allow more families to participate.

While mental health screening is an important in identifying patients who would benefit from additional mental health support, the screening may be of limited value and potentially even harmful, if those identified with elevated anxiety or depression scores do not have access to appropriate mental health supports. A recently published south-Swedish cohort study (n = 640; study period 1998–2019) found children with JIA were not at an increased risk of being diagnosed with depression or anxiety [20]. Interestingly, authors reports that protective factors were having preventative psychological counselling services to develop coping strategies and prompt access to psychological support as needed. Access to mental health supports however varies considerably across geographical region, and within region based on many factors, with the reality that many patients have limited access to mental health supports. Advocating for training of professionals, and access to services must continue to happen universally. Peer support is an intervention that continues to be explored and may provide an additional support option [21].

Strengths & limitations

Strengths of this study were that the study was carried out within routine clinic visits and offered not only to patients who expressed mental health concerns or physicians felt had mental health symptoms; the individualized mental health resource provision based on mental health screening profile. Limitations are acknowledged including the small sample size; lack of sociodemographic, disease activity and JIA subtype data. Recruitment was limited to clinics only at one site and the timing of completing the questionnaires during the clinic appointment varied depending on staff availability. The number of families who declined to participate is also a limitation; we do not know if these patients differ from the patients that agreed to participate.

Conclusion

We found that mental health screening in routine pediatric rheumatology clinic visits was highly acceptable by patients with JIA and their caregivers, and largely practical from a staff perspective. This study encourages healthcare providers working with children and youth with JIA to consider mental health screening as an initial step in improving the overall quality of care.
