Feasibility of an Unfunded Multicenter Trial Group for Older Adult Burn Patients: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Ashleigh Bull, Shawn Tejiram, Lauren B Nosanov, Alisa Savetamal, Dhaval Bhavsar, Sam Miotke, David M Hill, Sara Higginson, Theresa Chin, Andrea Long, Alexandra Lacey, Rosemary E Paine, Mark J Johnston, Deepak K Ozhathil, Marc G Jeschke, Lucy A Wibbenmeyer, Colette Galet, Kathleen Skipton Romanowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults make up a growing population of burn patients but pose unique physiological challenges that require specialized care. Comprehensive multicenter data are limited, and single-center studies lack the power to answer key questions for this group. This study assessed the feasibility of forming an unfunded multicenter trial group to study older burn patients and piloted a retrospective study to explore trends in their care and their clinical outcomes. After Institutional Review Board approval and executing data use agreements, 12 North American burn centers collected standardized data on patients aged 60 and older treated from 2017–2019. Demographics, burn characteristics, interventions, and outcomes were gathered from each site’s burn care quality platform submissions or electronic medical record and entered in a centralized REDCap database. Among the 1632 patients included in the database, median age was 68 years; most were male (67%) and White (73%) with a median BMI of 27.5. Median burn size was 3.5% total surface area, and patients arrived a median of 4.7 hours post-injury. The median modified Baux score was 76.2. Patients typically underwent one operation within 3 days of arrival, leading to a median hospital stay of 6 days and ICU stay of 1 day. In-hospital mortality was 10.4%, and median time to wound healing was 40 days. This pilot demonstrates that an unfunded multicenter collaboration is feasible and can generate meaningful data to guide care for older burn patients. Strong inter-institutional collaboration and communication and standardized data definitions were key to success.Keywords: burns, older adults, management, multicenter, unfunded

INTRODUCTION

The older adult population is rapidlyexpanding. As of 2022, 57.8 million Americans were over 65 years old, a figure that is projected to exceed 89 million by 2060.1 With this demographic shift, the number of older burn injured patients will likely increase. The traditional model for predicting mortality in burn patients is the Baux score which is based on patient age and burn size (%total burn surface area [TBSA]). Unfortunately, these models fail to fully account for variability between individuals. Patients of the same chronological age can have markedly different states of health and functional statuses making numerical age alone an inadequate predictor of outcome.2

Masud et al. first examined the correlation between Canadian Study of Health and Aging Clinical Frailty Scale (CSHA-CFS) and mortality.3 One of the limitations in their study was that they only looked at burns that were greater than 10% TBSA. Romanowski et al. repeated their study but included all patients regardless of burn size.4 A total of 89 patients were admitted during the study period. This study also found that patients who died had significantly higher CHSA-CFS compared to survivors (5.2 ± 1.2 vs 4.4 ± 1.2). Multivariate logistic regression, controlling for severity of cutaneous injury, presence of inhalation injury, and age, found that CSHA-CFS was independently associated with increased risk of discharge to a skilled nursing facility (SNF) and increased risk of mortality. Previous studies have established the correlation between frailty and outcomes such as mortality, discharge to a SNF, and likelihood of undergoing a goals of care discussion.5–7 More recently, a multicenter trial conducted across three specialized Dutch burn centers showed that the CSHA-CFS was both reliable and valid, including its association with adverse outcomes in patients with burn injury.8

Research now needs to move beyond simply correlating frailty with outcomes to using frailty to plan care and make decisions for older patients. In addition, more work needs to be done to examine the current practices being used to treat older adult burn patients with respect to surgical treatment of their burn wounds. Because relatively few older patients are treated at each burn center annually, single-center trials are insufficient to address the key questions about the unique care needs of this population. Additionally, the generalizability of these studies to other populations is poor due to the small sample size. We hypothesized that the development of a multicenter trial group focused on older adult burn patients could yield valuable insights into clinical outcomes, yet funding for such initiatives is limited. This study explores the feasibility of creating an unfunded multicenter trial group and conducting a retrospective pilot study to explore clinical trends and outcomes in this population.

METHODS

Multicenter study design

Following Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and the creation of data use agreements (DUAs), a retrospective pilot study was conducted across twelve burn centers in North America.

Study type

Each center was tasked with collecting standardized data on burn patients aged 60 and older admitted to their burn center from January 2017 to December 2019.

Training

Each center received a PowerPoint presentation developed by one of the lead authors (CG) describing how to score frailty using the CSHA CFS to ensure consistency in scoring between centers. Questions regarding scoring were addressed in individual center meetings with the lead investigator (KSR).

Data collection

Data included demographic information, burn characteristics (burn mechanisms, TBSA, burn depth information, modified Baux scores, and presence of inhalation injury, treatment interventions, and outcomes such as mortality, length of hospital stay, and complications. Where possible, data were sourced from each center’s submission to the Burn Care Quality Platform (BCQP). BCQP and additional data from the electronic medical records (EMRs) were manually entered into a centralized REDCap database managed by one institution. A common data dictionary was created through collaboration with participating centers with BCQP variables and definitions as its core to ease collection and homogeneity of data.

Statistical analysis

SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), was used to analyze the data by site. A Shapiro–Wilk test for normality was used to determine if continuous variables had a normal distribution. All continuous variables in this study had a non-parametric distribution; therefore, a Kruskal–Wallis Test was used to analyze continuous variables. Chi-square testing was used to determine significant differences in proportions and categorical data, except for small cell sizes. When there were small cell sizes for a particular data point, the Fisher Exact Test or the Monte Carlo Estimate for the Fisher Exact Test was used. Descriptive statistics of the pilot study are presented. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Center recruitment, institutional review board, and data use agreement process

A total of 12 burn centers (10 American Burn Association (ABA) verified centers, 2 non- verified centers) agreed to participate in this multicenter study.

The main site (University of California, Davis) obtained IRB approval in June 2022. Participating sites obtained IRB approval and DUA approvals between July 2022 and November 2023, with timeframe varying between sites.

Data collection began July 2022 and ended August 15, 2024. Each center started data collection as soon as their IRB was approved with some centers starting more than a year after the main site was approved. Five centers interested in the study started the process but did not submit any data.

Population characteristics

The study included 1632 older adult burn patients with 41 from Canada, 673 from the Midwestern region, 399 from the Northeastern region, 50 from the Southern region, and 469 from the Western region. Median age was 68 years (interquartile range [IQR] 13); 1095 patients (67%) were male and 1187 (73%) were White with a median BMI of 27.5 (IQR 7.87). The median burn size was 3.5% total body surface area (IQR 9, range 0%-100%). Patients presented a median of 4.68 hours after injury (IQR 15.35), with a median modified Baux score of 76.21 (IQR 20.5). These patients underwent a median of 1 operation (IQR 1), typically 3 days after injury (IQR 6). The median length of hospital stay was 6 days (IQR 13), with an ICU stay of 1 day (IQR 5). In-hospital mortality was 10.4%. The median time from injury to death was 15 days (IQR 131). The median time to wound healing was 40 days (IQR 48). Challenges

The biggest hurdle was contractually, specifically the time taken for each legal team to execute a signed DUA. Rules on the need for a DUA were also different at each center making this process slower than expected. Another barrier encountered was the slow pace in collecting data at some sites due to Principal Investigator availability and staffing shortages. Some sites utilized medical students and residents to complete data collection when between academic responsibilities.

DISCUSSION

Multicenter studies offer great advantages to burn research, particularly the ability to capture intra-center practice variances and by increasing the volume of patients that can be studied. In contrast, single center studies often suffer from small sample sizes as well as regional differences, which can impede statistical power and limit generalizability. However, despite these advantages, funding for multicenter trials can be difficult to obtain and sustain. The amount of indirect funds that are requested for each center to operate limits the number of centers that can participate and potentially the quality of output as each funding mechanism has its own maximum award.

This study evaluated the feasibility of creating a multicenter group to establish a large-scale retrospective cohort using registry data and chart reviews conducted without dedicated research funding.

In the absence of dedicated funding, this project relied heavily on motivated coinvestigators at each site to ensure timely collection of data. This project would not be possible to complete without multiple motivated and time-focused coinvestigators affording accountability for each other to ensure high data quality and timely collection. Success depended on coordinated efforts across burn centers, including onboarding and standardization of documents. In addition, a PowerPoint on frailty scoring as well as live Questions and Answers sessions, along with video conferencing software was used to ensure centers’ questions were addressed in a timely fashion. Rigorous data management is essential to executing a study of this size, which was facilitated by the aforementioned methods.9

The threat of missing data was primarily avoided by using burn registry data. Most sites only extracted a few data points from the EMRs, except for one which does not participate in the burn registry and therefore required manual collection of hundreds of data points on each patient directly from the EMR.

In this dataset, we observed significant differences between centers in several domains, which likely reflect variations in patient populations, institutional practices, and resource availability. While the current study was not designed to fully explore the drivers of this heterogeneity, these findings highlight an important area for future investigation. Planned follow-up analyses and future prospective studies will aim to better characterize center-level factors and assess their impact on outcomes, with the goal of identifying modifiable practices that may improve consistency and quality of care across institutions.

Barriers faced

We faced several barriers while setting up this multicenter retrospective study. The primary barrier was variability in institutional policies regarding DUA and IRB approval. Regardless of funding, this variability poses challenges to study completion.10 In our case, some centers’ IRB and DUA policies allowed for rapid completion and submission of data collection; while, in other cases, these institutional policies made it difficult or impossible for centers to participate. In fact, for some centers, the IRB and DUA are processed in parallel allowing for a faster approval of both, while for others, those two processes happened separately. Often the DUA needed to be approved, for those centers to start their IRB applications. The overall PI of the study had to work closely with the research team and legal department at their center, as well as with the participating site PIs to ensure that the process was moving forward efficiently and in accordance with each site’s policies. Notably, one center was able to collect data under the IRB while the DUA was in process, facilitating rapid data sharing with the primary center once the DUA was approved. Simultaneous collection and negotiation allowed for troubleshooting at this center that streamlined data collection at subsequent centers.

Maintaining consistent communication with multiple sites can also be challenging but it is necessary to ensure steady progress in data collection.11 Regular emails and face to face virtual meetings allowed this team to ensure completion of this multicenter cohort study. The main site principal investigator used recommendations from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors to develop guidelines regarding authorship, internal manuscript review, and timing of research dissemination to avoid any conflicts between the sites.

Ensuring consistent data collection between sites was another challenge faced early on with some of the site being unsure on how to score for frailty using the CSHA CFS. To avoid inconsistencies, a PowerPoint presentation describing how to score frailty using the CSHA CFS was developed to ensure consistency in scoring between centers. Questions or concerns regarding scoring were addressed in individual center meetings with the principal investigator.

Finally, unfunded studies can rely on motivated co-investigators as well as trainees for data collection; however, in our case, the use of trainees for data collection certainly slowed data collection and cleaning due to multiple demands on their time.

CONCLUSION

Ultimately, this was a successful unfunded multicenter study of frailty in burn care. As funding becomes increasingly difficult to obtain, it is important to share our experience in creating an unfunded multicenter review group to address new questions in burn care and beyond. Overall, the strength of the study was in the motivated co-investigators and use of existing registry data. Key challenges included variability in regulatory practices and staffing shortages across institutions, which impeded data collection. The results of this multicenter study will be disseminated in the near future.
