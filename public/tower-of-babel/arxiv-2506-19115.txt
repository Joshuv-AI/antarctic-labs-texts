A Two-Operator Calculus for Arithmetic-Progression Paths in the Collatz Graph
S. Angermund

Abstract
A recast of the standard residue-class analysis of the 3 ​ x + 1 3x+1 (Collatz) map in terms of two elementary operators on arithmetic progressions. The resulting calculus (i) splits any progression into its even and odd subsequences in a single step; (ii) gives a closed formula for every set of seeds that realises a prescribed parity word; (iii) yields a one–line affine invariant that forbids trajectories consisting of infinitely many “odd“ moves; and (iv) reduces the non-trivial-cycle problem to a pair of linear congruences.



Related Work

The Collatz function has been studied from multiple, partly overlapping,
perspectives. We outline those most relevant to the present paper.

Stopping-time and density results.

Terras pioneered the study of stopping times and proved that the set of
integers with finite stopping time has asymptotic density one

[
9
]
. Everett subsequently analysed parity sequences to obtain
upper bounds on total stopping times
[
3
]
. Lagarias gives a
thorough survey and annotated bibliography covering these and many later
developments
[
5
]
.

Residue-class-wise affine (rcwa) mappings.

A modern unifying language for Collatz-type maps views them as actions of a
subgroup of the rcwa group on
\mathbb{Z}
. Kohl’s monograph

[
4
]
formalises this viewpoint and describes algorithmic tools
for rcwa dynamics. Our operator pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
can be seen as generators
of a particularly simple rcwa subgroup acting modulo
2
.

Parity words and
2
-adic conjugacy.

The binary “parity word” encoding of trajectories dates back to Terras,
reappears in Everett’s parity-sequence bounds, and underlies Akin’s
2
-adic
conjugacy map
[
1
]
[
6
]
. We extend
this idea by giving a closed-form expression for the
number
of seeds
realising any finite word, thereby quantifying population density along each
branch of the inverse tree.

Inverse trees and graph-theoretic approaches.

Numerous authors build a directed tree whose vertices are pre-images of
1
;
see, e.g. Bernstein–Lagarias’ decomposition of the
3x+1
digraph and the
graph-theoretic study by Sultanow
[
8
]
. The tree presented
here differs in that it keeps both affine parameters
(a,b)
symbolic at
every level, allowing us to track linear invariants such as
b=a-1
.

Growth-rate versus invariant arguments.

Classical proofs rule out infinite “all-odd” branches by comparing
geometric growth factors (
3/2
versus
1/2
). Our proof replaces this
numerical estimate with a single affine constraint
b=a-1
, providing what
appears to be a new, shorter argument.

In summary, while our work overlaps with well-established tools—parity sequences,
rcwa dynamics, and inverse trees—it contributes (i) a minimal two-operator
calculus, (ii) an explicit seed-count formula for arbitrary parity words, and
(iii) a new linear-invariant proof excluding trajectories with infinitely many
odd moves.

3
Index transformations

It’s interesting for us to consider two specific transformations of
i
, let’s define them as
T_{1}:i\longmapsto 2j
and
T_{2}:i\longmapsto 2j+1
. Now
T_{1}:(ai+b)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\longmapsto(2aj+b)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
T_{2}:(ai+b)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\longmapsto(2aj+a+b)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. Or

\begin{split}&T_{1}:S(a,b)\longmapsto S(2a,b),\\
&T_{2}:S(a,b)\longmapsto S(2a,b+a).\end{split}

Example 3.1
.

Let’s look at
S(3,2)
. Apply
T_{2}:S(3,2)\longmapsto S(6,5)
and choose for example
s_{10}\in S(6,5)=65
. Now, since
T_{2}:i\longmapsto 2j+1
and we chose
j=10
then
i=21
will give us the same sequence value from the original sequence:
s_{21}\in S(3,2)=65
.

Example
3.1
demonstrates how we can track both the index and the parameters
a,b
when performing transformations on sequences.

5
Collatz

The Collatz Conjecture:
For any
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
the sequence
n_{i+1}=f_{col}(n_{i})
where

f_{col}(n)=\begin{cases}n/2,\quad&when\ 2\mid n\\
3n+1,\quad&when\ 2\nmid n\end{cases}

will reach the number 1.

When
n
is odd,
3n+1\longrightarrow 3(2\lambda+1)+1=6\lambda+4
is always even. So
f(n)
can take on a more compact form if we combine the odd-even step. Let’s define this function for future reference.

Definition 5.1
.

The compact Collatz step

n_{i+1}=\begin{cases}n_{i}/2,\quad&when\ 2\mid n_{i}\\
\frac{3n_{i}+1}{2},\quad&when\ 2\nmid n_{i}\end{cases}

Collatz possibility graph

Let’s start a Collatz sequence without making any assumptions on the seed
n_{0}
, and build up a graph of possible paths. Making no assumptions on
n_{0}
we let
n_{0}=s_{i}\in S(1,0)
be any number (note that
n_{0}
is just the sequence index

n_{0}
, we can without loss of generality first do an even and odd sub-sequence extraction: using Proposition
4.1
,

will branch of into the even sub-sequence
S(2,0)
and the odd sub-sequence

Even sub-sequence

Sub sequence extraction
:

Collatz step:

The child loops back to the parent

Odd sub-sequence

•

Sub sequence extraction

Remark 1:
Without loss of generality we have here constructed the
form
that all possible numbers
n_{1}=f_{col}(n_{0})
can take on. Using Proposition

we can keep applying this method to the child nodes, and without loss of generality we can construct the
structure

.

Remark 2:
While sub-sequence extractions will transform

f_{col}(ai+b)=(a/2)i+(b/2)
. Odd:
f_{col}(ai+b)=3ai+(3b+1)

Tracing the seed

A sequence of transformations
T_{k\in K}
, where
K
is a sequence of 1´s and 2´s, has the following property:

T_{k\in K}:i\longmapsto 2^{|K|}j+x

where
x\geq 0
is some sum of powers of 2. More generally:

Proposition 6.1

(General index formula)
.

Let
K=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{k})\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}^{k}
be a word in the
two operators, read from root to leaf, and write

s_{t}\;=\;\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\kappa_{t}=T_{2},\\
0&\text{if }\kappa_{t}=T_{1}.\end{cases}

After the
k
subsequence extractions the seed index
i
that lands on
position
j
in the target progression is

\boxed{\;i\;=\;2^{\,k}\,j\;+\;\sum_{t=1}^{k}s_{t}\,2^{\,k-t}\;}.

Proof:
Each operator doubles the running index; whenever
\kappa_{t}=T_{2}
it
adds
1
. Unrolling the recursion gives

i=2^{k}j+s_{1}2^{k-1}+s_{2}2^{k-2}+\cdots+s_{k}.

\Box

So when only
T_{1}
transformations are applied then
T_{k\in K}:i\longmapsto 2^{|K|}j,\ K=(1,1,1,...,1)
. When only
T_{2}
transformations are applied we will get the final transformation
T_{k\in K}:i\longmapsto 2^{|K|}j+2^{|K|}-1,\ K=(2,2,2,...,2)
. This follows from the fact that
2^{k-1}+...+2^{0}=2^{k}-1
.

We can now trace the seed in the graph by tracking the transformation sequence
K
on a given path. There is also an interesting result following this regarding path densities:

Corollary 6.1

(Seed density of a node)
.

For a word
K
of length
k
, the set of seeds that reach the
corresponding node is the arithmetic progression

2^{k}\mathbb{N}+\sum_{t=1}^{k}s_{t}2^{k-t}
.
Consequently the sub-tree rooted at that node has natural
density
2^{-k}
inside
\mathbb{N}
.

7
Odd steps only

A not so far fetched guess would be that there is a seed
n_{0}
such that the odd step of the compact Collatz function would be infinitely repeated: successions of multiplying by 3, adding 1 and dividing by 2 will clearly diverge to infinity as long as
n_{0}\geq 0
. We will now attempt to show that there is no such seed.

Theorem 7.1
.

There is no finite seed
n_{0}
such that Equation
1
will repeat the odd step indefinitely.

Proof:
We can assume that
n_{0}
is odd and thereafter the Collatz step will always be

n_{k+1}=\frac{3n_{k}+1}{2}.

This is the right most path in Figure
1
, and by a glance it looks like
n_{k}
always has an odd
a
and even
b
, in fact the pattern seems to be that
a
is odd and
b=a-1
. If we can show that this is always the case then, according to Proposition
4.1
, we are always doing
T_{2}
index transformations. Let’s prove this as a lemma:

Lemma 7.1
.

We want to show that for
n_{k}=ai+b
, where
a
odd and
b=a-1
, after a
T_{2}
transformation and the odd step in Equation
1
we will end up with
n_{k+1}=a^{\prime}j+b^{\prime}
where
a^{\prime}
is odd and
b^{\prime}=a^{\prime}-1
. Let’s do the full induction proof.

Base case:

n_{0}=s_{i}\in S(1,0)
. Odd sub-sequence extraction:
T_{2}:S(1,0)\longrightarrow S(2,1)
. Next we do the Collatz step on
s_{i}\in S(2,1)
:

n_{1}=\frac{3(2i+1)+1}{2}=\frac{6i+4}{2}=3i+2

3 is indeed odd and
2=3-1
so base case complete.

Induction case:

n_{k}=s_{i}\in S(a,b)
. Assume
a
odd and
b=a-1
. Odd sub-sequence extraction:
T_{2}:S(a,b)\longmapsto S(2a,b+a)
, and Collatz step on
s_{i}\in S(2a,b+a)
:

n_{k+1}=\frac{3(2ai+b+a)+1}{2}=\frac{6ai+3b+3a+1}{2}=3ai+\frac{3(a+b)+1}{2}

We can apply the transformations
a=2\lambda+1
and
b=2\lambda
as the induction hypothesis:

\begin{split}n_{k+1}&=3ai+\frac{3(a+b)+1}{2}=3(2\lambda+1)i+\frac{3(2\lambda+1+2\lambda)+1}{2}\\
&=(6\lambda+3)i+\frac{12\lambda+4}{2}=(6\lambda+3)i+(6\lambda+2)\end{split}

Now
a^{\prime}=6\lambda+3
and
b^{\prime}=6\lambda+2
, which gives the resulting sequence
S(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
where indeed
a^{\prime}
is odd and
b^{\prime}=a^{\prime}-1
.

\Box

Now, importantly, we know that we are always doing
T_{2}
index transformations before each Collatz step. So after
k
successive steps we will land at a node with sequence index
j
, and by
6.1
we can retrieve the seed as
s_{i}\in S(1,0)=i=2^{k}j+2^{k}-1
. Even if we choose
j=0
, the starting seed will clearly be unbounded when we let
k\longrightarrow\infty
. Thus there is no finite seed
n_{0}
such that the compact odd Collatz-step can be applied indefinitely.

\Box

8
Cycles

We can use the parity splitting together with the index tracing to search for cycles.

Proposition 8.1

(Cycle conditions)
.

Let
n_{k}
be a node at depth
k
with arithmetic sequence
a_{1}j+b_{1}
and index map
\alpha_{1}j+\beta_{1}
. Let
n_{k-t}
be a node at depth
k-t
with arithmetic sequence
a_{2}j+b_{2}
and index map
\alpha_{2}j+\beta_{2}
. For there to be a cycle between
n_{k}
and its ancestor
n_{k-t}
there must exist
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\begin{split}a_{1}x+b_{1}&=a_{2}y+b_{2},\\
\alpha_{1}x+\beta_{1}&=\alpha_{2}y+\beta_{2}.\end{split}

This ensures that the nodes take on equal value when spawned from the same seed.

If we solve the linear system for
x
and
y
we get an explicit formula for cycle conditions:

\begin{split}x&=\frac{a_{2}\big(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}\big)+\alpha_{2}\big(b_{2}-b_{1}\big)}{a_{1}\alpha_{2}-a_{2}\alpha_{1}},\\
y&=\frac{a_{1}\big(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}\big)+\alpha_{1}\big(b_{2}-b_{1}\big)}{a_{1}\alpha_{2}-a_{2}\alpha_{1}}.\end{split}

Notice that if
b_{1}=b_{2}
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
we get
x=y=0
. We can actually see this cycle in the first few levels of the tree graph.

j=0

9
Conclusion and Outlook

We have shown that the Collatz dynamics admit a compact
description once every integer is encoded as an arithmetic progression

S(a,b)
. A single pair of index operators,

T_{1}:S(a,b)\longmapsto S(2a,b),\qquad T_{2}:S(a,b)\longmapsto S(2a,b+a),

(i)
separates any progression into its even and odd
subsequences in one step;
(ii)
yields a closed formula

i\mapsto 2^{k}j+2^{k}-1
for the seeds that realise any prescribed
parity word (Proposition
6.1
); and
(iii)
exposes the
affine invariant
b=a-1
that rules out trajectories consisting of
infinitely many odd moves (Theorem
7.1
).

9.1
Immediate corollaries.

•

Every Collatz orbit must include at least one division by
2
; the
invariant argument replaces the classical growth-rate estimate

3/2
versus
1/2
.

•

Each node in the symbolic inverse tree carries an explicit
population count—the number of seeds that flow through
it—clarifying why some branches are denser than others.

9.2
Directions for further work.

1.

Cycle classification.

We started analysing cycles in section
8
; a systematic search within the

\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
calculus could sharpen existing cycle-length
bounds.

2.

Density estimates.

The seed-count formula suggests a probabilistic model for the
likelihood that a random integer follows a given parity word.

3.

Generalised maps.

Replacing the pair
(3n+1,n/2)
by other residue-class-wise
affine rules (e.g.
5n+1
on odd numbers) preserves the
operator framework; studying which invariants survive such
changes may shed light on why
3x+1
is special.

4.

Algorithmic applications.

Because the operators act symbolically on
(a,b)
, they can
serve as a state-space reduction in exhaustive searches or SAT
encodings of the conjecture.
