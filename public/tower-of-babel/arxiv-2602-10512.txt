Don’t Eliminate Cut: Exponential Separations in LLM-Based Theorem Proving
Sho Sonoda sho.sonoda@riken.jp, Shunta Akiyama akiyama_shunta@cyberagent.co.jp, Yuya Uezato uezato_yuya@cyberagent.co.jp

Abstract
We develop a theoretical analysis of LLM-guided formal theorem proving in interactive proof assistants (e.g., Lean) by modeling tactic proposal as a stochastic policy in a finite-horizon deterministic MDP. To capture modern representation learning, we treat the state and action spaces as general compact metric spaces and assume Lipschitz policies. To explain the gap between worst-case hardness and empirical success, we introduce problem distributions generated by a reference policy q q , including a latent-variable model in which proofs exhibit reusable cut/lemma/sketch structure represented by a proof DAG. Under a top- k k search protocol and Tsybakov-type margin conditions, we derive lower bounds on finite-horizon success probability that decompose into search and learning terms, with learning controlled by sequential Rademacher/covering complexity. Our main separation result shows that when cut elimination expands a DAG of depth D D into a cut-free tree of size Ω ⁡ ( Λ D ) \Omega(\Lambda^{D}) while the cut-aware hierarchical process has size O ⁡ ( λ D ) O(\lambda^{D}) with λ ≪ Λ \lambda\ll\Lambda , a flat (cut-free) learner provably requires exponentially more data than a cut-aware hierarchical learner. This provides a principled justification for subgoal decomposition in recent agentic theorem provers.



1
Introduction

Recent
agentic theorem provers
couple a verifier with an LLM and often include explicit
subgoal decomposition
steps such as lemma discovery, proof sketches, or draft-refine loops (e.g., Draft-Sketch-Prove
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
, DeepSeek-Prover-V2
(
Xin et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2025
)
, Hilbert
(
Varambally et al. 2025
)
, Prover Agent
(
Baba et al. 2025
)
, SeedProver
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, and Aristotle
(
Achim et al. 2025
)
).
These components can appear as ad-hoc heuristics, since generating a sketch or intermediate lemma is itself a hard task and may seem a detour toward the low-level search required to close goals. This study provides theoretical evidence that such hierarchical procedures can be
rational
and even
exponentially
more sample-efficient when the underlying proof distribution exhibits reusable high-level structure.

We model interactive theorem proving as follows. A prover state is a finite set of goals; applying a tactic rewrites the goals into a new set of goals. The kernel transition is deterministic, whereas an LLM proposes tactics stochastically, yielding a finite-horizon deterministic MDP. We evaluate a prover by its finite-horizon success probability, i.e., the probability of reaching the terminal “no goals” state within time
T
. Since tactics typically carry parameters (e.g., lemma arguments) and goal sets range over an infinite space of propositions, we formalize states and actions as
any
compact metric spaces, and restrict to Lipschitz policy classes; this abstracts the data embeddings and representations acquired in the real-world LLMs.

Because theorem proving is computationally hard and often worst-case intractable (e.g., 3-SAT problems are NP-hard, and first-order logic is undecidable in general), we explicitly incorporate
data distributions
. We introduce a reference policy
q
that generates successful proofs and induces a distribution over theorem instances. We study two regimes: a
cut-free
regime where proofs are essentially trees with an average length parameter, and a
cut-structured
regime where proofs admit reusable subproofs (cuts/lemmas/sketches) represented as a latent proof DAG
Z
, together with an observation model that generates cut-free traces from
Z
. The latent-variable formulation allows a broad family of structural biases to be described with a small number of interpretable parameters, including a depth
D
, an effective unique branching factor, and a contraction parameter governing how decomposition reduces local proof complexity.

We analyze two learning-and-search pipelines. The
flat
(cut-free) model learns a single policy by ERM on cut-free traces and performs top-
k
search. The
hierarchical
(cut-aware) model learns a high-level decomposition policy and a low-level solver policy, and alternates policy learning with latent-structure inference via an EM/VAE-style procedure. For both pipelines, we derive success probability lower bounds under top-
k
search and Tsybakov-type margin assumptions
(
Audibert and Tsybakov 2007
;
Arlot and Bartlett 2011
)
; the bounds separate intrinsic search difficulty from one-step imitation errors. We further control the latter via sequential Rademacher/covering complexity
(
Rakhlin et al. 2015a
;
Rakhlin et al. 2015b
;
Block et al. 2021
)
, yielding explicit generalization terms for posterior-weighted ERM under latent DAGs.

Our main contributions are:
(i) a compact-metric MDP formalization of LLM theorem proving with a finite-horizon success criterion;
(ii) distributional models induced by a generator
q
, including a latent proof-DAG model for cut structure;
(iii) success probability guarantees for flat and hierarchical top-
k
search under margin conditions;
(iv) sequential generalization bounds (sequential Rademacher/covering) for posterior-weighted ERM with latent DAGs, with an explicit inference error term controlled by the ELBO gap;
(v) an exponential separation showing that, under cut-elimination blow-up, flat cut-free learning requires exponentially more data than cut-aware hierarchical learning.
Together, these results support the view that subgoal decomposition leverages an inductive bias present in realistic mathematical proof distributions, without contradicting classical worst-case hardness results.

1.1
Related work (cut/lemma sharing and modern agentic provers)

The study of decomposition and sharing is motivated not only by heuristics but by proof complexity. While cut elimination is a fundamental meta-theorem, eliminating cut may cause an enormous blow-up in proof size.
Boolos 1984
’ “Don’t Eliminate Cut” exhibits an inference with a short proof using cut but with astronomically large cut-free proofs, emphasizing that cut/lemma mechanisms are essential for tractability in practice.

This classical lesson reappears concretely in modern LLM-guided formal theorem proving, where agentic pipelines explicitly produce intermediate claims and reuse them:

•

Draft, Sketch, and Prove

(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
uses informal proofs to produce formal proof sketches that guide search by explicitly decomposing into easier subproblems.

•

DeepSeek-Prover-V2

(
Ren et al. 2025
)
describes a Lean 4 prover trained with reinforcement learning in a pipeline that explicitly leverages recursive subgoal decomposition.

•

Seed-Prover

(
Chen et al. 2025
)
proposes lemma-style whole-proof reasoning with iterative refinement using Lean feedback and proved lemmas, explicitly emphasizing lemma reuse and broad/deep search.

•

Aristotle

(
Achim et al. 2025
)
integrates Lean proof search with an informal lemma-generation system and dedicated geometry solver, emphasizing lemma-based decomposition as a scaling mechanism.

These systems suggest that lemma/cut-style subgoal decomposition is not an optional refinement but a structural driver of provability at scale. The hierarchical generator below is designed to isolate and quantify this phenomenon.

1.2
Related Work (hierarchical policies and exponential separations)

Hierarchical policies via options and goal-conditioned HRL.

A large body of work studies
hierarchical
decision making through
options
(macro-actions) and related HRL formalisms. In lifelong RL,
Brunskill and Li 2014
analyze PAC-style option discovery under the assumption that there exists a small option set that is
\varepsilon
-optimal across tasks drawn i.i.d. from an unknown task distribution, making explicit how well-chosen temporal abstractions can improve sample efficiency. In online RL,
Fruit et al. 2017
study regret minimization in MDPs with options
without
prior knowledge of option duration/reward parameters, combining the semi-MDP view with the internal Markov structure of option execution. More recently,
Robert et al. 2023
derive sample-complexity lower bounds for a class of goal-conditioned hierarchical RL algorithms, clarifying when a hierarchical decomposition can help and motivating a corresponding Q-learning-type method. These works provide strong motivation and formal guarantees for hierarchical policies, but they primarily target unknown stochastic environments and regret/value criteria. In contrast, our setting is a deterministic, verifier-defined MDP arising from interactive theorem proving, and our performance metric is
finite-horizon goal-reaching probability
under top-
k
search. Conceptually, our decomposition/lemma policies play the role of high-level options, but our main separation mechanism is specific to proof systems: reusable
cut/lemma/sketch
structure yields a compact proof DAG that can be exponentially more succinct than its fully unfolded cut-free proof tree.

Exponential gains from exploiting structure: factored MDPs.

The clearest precedents for
exponential separations
in RL come from exploiting graphical structure in
factored
MDPs. Starting with
Kearns and Koller 1999
, and continuing through structure-learning and near-optimal regret analyses
(
Strehl et al. 2007
;
Osband and Roy
;
Xu and Tewari 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
, this line of work shows that when transitions and rewards factor according to a sparse dependency graph, one can obtain learning guarantees whose dependence is polynomial in the number of factors and parent-set sizes, rather than exponential in the raw state-space cardinality. Our exponential separation is analogous in spirit but arises from a different structural primitive:
sharing
in proof DAGs (cuts/lemmas) versus duplication in cut-free proof trees. Under a cut-elimination blow-up assumption, the effective number of decision points for a flat (cut-free) prover scales as
\Omega(\Lambda^{D})
while a cut-aware hierarchical prover scales as
O(\lambda^{D})
with
\lambda\ll\Lambda
, yielding an exponential gap in the one-step accuracy (and thus sample size) required to achieve a target success probability under margin-based top-
k
analysis. Unlike factored-MDP results, we do not learn the transition model (it is given by the proof assistant kernel) and instead quantify the joint effect of representation class (Lipschitz policies on compact metric embeddings), imitation learning (ERM), and search.

Proof trees and AND–OR structure.

From a search viewpoint, interactive theorem proving induces an AND–OR structure:

•

OR: at a goal state, one chooses a tactic (and its parameters).

•

AND: a chosen tactic may produce multiple subgoals, all of which must be solved.

A complete proof corresponds to a finite AND–OR proof tree. In practice, intermediate results (lemmas, proof sketches) can be reused across branches, turning the tree into a
proof DAG
. This is the operational face of
cut introduction
: one proves a lemma once and reuses it. Conversely,
cut elimination
(normalization to a cut-free proof) expands shared DAG structure into a tree, potentially causing a large blow-up in size
(
Boolos 1984
, we refer to the seminal paper “
Don’t Eliminate Cut
” by)
.
More detailed concrete examples are provided in
Appendix

A
.

Proof complexity.

Let
x_{0}
be a theorem instance. There may be more than one action sequences (tactic scripts) that close
x_{0}
. Let
A(x_{0})
be the set of successful action sequences (or proof trees) and define the (minimal) proof length

K(x_{0}):=\inf\{|a|:a\in A(x_{0})\},

with
K(x_{0})=\infty
if
A(x_{0})=\emptyset
. The parameter
K(x_{0})
is an abstract notion of proof complexity; in our analysis we use this to measure the difficulty of theorem instances
x_{0}
.

2.2
Finite-horizon deterministic MDP for theorem proving

We formalize ITP as a finite-horizon deterministic Markov decision process (MDP).
A
state
encodes the current set of goals (and their contexts). A
goal state
is the terminal “no goals” configuration.
A
tactic action
may include parameters (e.g., lemma instantiations), so the action space is typically infinite.

State and action spaces.

To abstract away the concrete representation used by modern neural policies (token sequences, learned embeddings, structured trees), we assume:

•

the state space
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
and action space
(\mathcal{A},d_{\mathcal{A}})
are compact metric spaces;

•

policies are Lipschitz in the state metric (formalized below).

This is the only structural assumption we rely on for the main theorems. A concrete and practically relevant embedding
(e.g., representing a varying-size set of goals as a measure on a goal space and using bounded-Lipschitz geometry)
is presented as an example construction in
Appendix

B
.

Deterministic kernel.

Lean’s kernel deterministically computes the next goal state given the current goals and a tactic. Hence we model transitions by a deterministic map

F:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X},\qquad x_{t+1}=F(x_{t},a_{t}),

where invalid tactics can be modeled by mapping to a designated failure state.

Policy class.

We work with policy classes
H
consisting of (possibly randomized) Lipschitz maps
x\mapsto\pi(\cdot\mid x)
, with a uniform interior condition to control log-loss:
there exists
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
\pi(a\mid x)\geq\rho
for all relevant
(x,a)
.
This is standard when one uses log-loss and top-
k
selection.

Finite horizon and success probability.

Theorem proving may not terminate; we therefore impose a time horizon
T
(or a step budget) and define performance as the finite-horizon goal-reaching probability.
Let
G\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be the terminal set corresponding to no goals.
Given a policy
\pi(a\mid x)
, we obtain a stochastic process

A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid X_{t}),\qquad X_{t+1}=F(X_{t},A_{t}),\qquad X_{0}=x_{0}.

We evaluate performance by the finite-horizon success probability

V^{\pi}(T,x_{0}):=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}\big(\exists t\leq T:\ X_{t}\in G\big),

i.e., goal-reaching probability within time limit
T
. We call
V^{\pi}(T,x_{0})
a
(pointwise) provability
and its expectation
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[V^{\pi}(T,X)]
the
statistical provability
.

Optimal policies exist but are not informative.

In deterministic finite-horizon MDPs, an optimal policy (
\pi^{*}
that attains the maximum value
V^{*}
) exists in very large classes (e.g., measurable policies). This is classical (demonstrated in
Appendix

C
), and mathematically useful (it justifies an “optimal value” benchmark), but it is not directly constructive for theorem proving. Our focus is therefore on realistic restricted classes (e.g., Lipschitz policies) and on distributional guarantees under data generation models.

3
Data generation models: a base distribution and a latent proof-DAG distribution

A central premise of this work is distributional analysis: worst-case theorem proving is intractable, but empirical benchmarks are highly non-uniform. We therefore introduce explicit data distributions.

3.1
Cut-free baseline distribution induced by a reference policy
q

Fix a reference stochastic policy
q(a\mid x)
(i.e., any proposal distribution such as
a pretrained LLM, or random draws from theorem libraries such as Mathlib
(
The mathlib community 2020
)
).
Running
q
in the deterministic prover yields trajectories (proof attempts)

y=(x_{0},a_{0},x_{1},a_{1},\dots,x_{T}),\qquad x_{t+1}=F(x_{t},a_{t}).

Conditioning on success within a truncation length
L
(or horizon
T
) induces a success-filtered distribution over theorem instances and proof traces:

Q_{\mathrm{tree}}(dx,dy)\ \propto\ \Big(\text{rollout of }q\text{ producing a successful trace }y\text{ from }x\text{ within }L\Big).

The parameter
L
controls the average proof length under
Q_{\mathrm{tree}}
.
For theoretical constructions one may also use success-conditioned sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) / Doob-transform style sampling to generate successful traces without rejection (details deferred to
Appendix

D
).

3.2
Cut-structured distributions via latent proof DAGs

To model lemma/cut/sketch structure, we introduce a latent-variable model where the latent variable is a proof DAG
Z
. We write

\displaystyle p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y,z)\propto p_{\theta}(x)\,p_{\theta}(z\mid x)\,p_{\theta}(y\mid x,z)\,\mathbf{1}\{\mathsf{Succ}(x,y,z)\},

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{DAG}}(dx,dy)=\int p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(x,y,z)\,dz.

Here
Z
captures reusable subgoals and their decomposition structure (depth-labeled DAG), while
y
is an observed cut-free trace (or decision-point log) generated by expanding
Z
into a tree and filling in low-level solver steps.
The truncation by
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Succ}(x,y,z)}
ensures the distribution is supported on successful objects.
The latent model compactly expresses a wide family of “proof-friendly” inductive biases via a small set of interpretable parameters, including:

•

D
: maximum decomposition depth,

•

b_{\mathrm{eff}}
: effective branching of
unique
subgoals (after sharing),

•

\alpha\in(0,1)
: contraction rate of local proof complexity under decomposition (shorter low-level proofs at deeper levels).

The main theorems below do not depend on a particular parametric form of
p_{\theta}(z\mid x)
beyond the induced decision counts and margin conditions.

From DAGs to observed traces.

The observation model
p_{\theta}(y\mid x,z)
can be read as “unfolding” the DAG into a cut-free proof trace (or a collection of local traces), e.g. by expanding reused lemmas into repeated subproofs. This connects directly to cut elimination: cut-free proofs correspond to (possibly much larger) trees obtained by unfolding a shared DAG.

4
Policies, learning rules, and search rules

We study two prover pipelines that correspond to two ways of interacting with cut structure.

4.1
Flat (cut-free) model: ERM + top-
k
search

The flat baseline ignores latent cut structure and learns a single policy
\pi_{\mathrm{flat}}\in H_{\mathrm{flat}}
from cut-free data (e.g., from
Q_{\mathrm{tree}}
or from unfolded traces under
Q_{\mathrm{DAG}}
).

Learning (ERM).

Given i.i.d. data from distribution
Q
, we learn a single policy
\pi_{\mathrm{flat}}\in H_{\mathrm{flat}}
by empirical risk minimization under log-loss:

\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{flat}}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{flat}}}\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{D}}-\log\pi(a\mid x),

where
\mathcal{D}
is the collection of decision-point pairs extracted from the traces. Because states depend on past actions through the deterministic kernel, the effective sample sequence is
sequential
; generalization is controlled by
sequential Rademacher/covering complexity
of
H
(details in
Section

G.2
).

Search (top-
k
).

At test time, we perform top-
k
proof search: at each state
x
, propose the
k
highest-probability actions under
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{flat}}(\cdot\mid x)
(restricted to a finite candidate set), try them with verifier feedback, and backtrack as needed. The key analytical event is a
coverage event
: the existence of a successful proof whose required actions always lie within the proposed top-
k
lists.
In our analysis, we assume a
Tsybakov-type margin condition
(explained below) on the policy’s ranking of the correct action among candidates, which converts one-step imitation error (mixture KL) into a misranking probability and then into a finite-horizon success bound.

4.1.1
Top-
k
margin condition (Tsybakov-type).

Our top-
k
analysis relies on a margin condition that controls how
stable
the correct action ranking is under small policy perturbations. Fix a finite candidate set
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cand}}(x)\subset\mathcal{A}
at each state
x
, and let
q(\cdot\mid x)
denote the reference (data-generating) action distribution restricted to
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cand}}(x)
. Write
q_{(1)}(x)\geq q_{(2)}(x)\geq\cdots
for the descending order statistics of
\{q(a\mid x):a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cand}}(x)\}
. We define the
top-
k
margin (gap)
at
x
by

\Delta_{k}(x):=q_{(k)}(x)-q_{(k+1)}(x),

with the convention
q_{(k+1)}(x)=0
if
|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cand}}(x)|=k
. Intuitively, large
\Delta_{k}(x)
means the
k
-th and
(k\!+\!1)
-th candidates are well separated under
q
, so the top-
k
set is robust to estimation error; small
\Delta_{k}(x)
indicates near-ties, where small deviations can flip the top-
k
membership. The Tsybakov-type margin condition postulates that near-ties are rare under the decision-point distribution: there exist constants
C>0
and
\beta>0
such that for all
u>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\Delta_{k}(X)\leq u\big)\leq C\,u^{\beta},

where
X
is a random decision state drawn from the induced mixture distribution over decision points (decomposition, solver, or flat, depending on the context). This assumption converts one-step imitation error (e.g. mixture KL) into a bound on the probability that a learned policy misranks the correct action outside the top-
k
list, yielding the characteristic exponent
p=\beta/(\beta+2)
in our success probability bounds.

4.2
Hierarchical (cut-aware) model: EM + subgoal decomposition + top-
k

The hierarchical model explicitly separates
high-level
and
low-level
decisions:

•

a decomposition policy
\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}\in H_{\mathrm{dec}}
proposes cuts/lemmas/sketches or other subgoal decompositions;

•

a solver policy
\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}\in H_{\mathrm{sol}}
closes terminal subgoals produced by decomposition.

Operationally, the prover alternates between (i) choosing a decomposition that produces reusable subgoals and (ii) solving the resulting subgoals, with memoization on canonicalized goals (sharing).

Learning (EM/VAE-style alternating updates).

Because the decomposition structure is not observed directly, we treat
Z
as latent and use an inference model (variational posterior)
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
. Learning proceeds by alternating:

•

E-step: update an amortized
\phi
to fit
r_{\phi}
by maximizing an ELBO;

•

M-step / policy step: update
(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}},\pi_{\mathrm{sol}})
by posterior-weighted ERM, i.e.,

\min_{\pi_{\mathrm{dec}},\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x^{(i)},y^{(i)})}\big[\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}},\pi_{\mathrm{sol}};x^{(i)},y^{(i)},z)\big],

where
\mathcal{J}
is the sum of log losses over decomposition and solver decision points induced by
z
.

Sequential Rademacher/covering complexity yields generalization bounds for this posterior-weighted ERM (treating
r_{\phi}
as fixed during the policy update), and the inference mismatch is controlled by the expected posterior KL
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{KL}(r_{\phi}\|p(z\mid x,y))]
, i.e., the ELBO gap.

Search (top-
k
at both levels).

The hierarchical prover uses top-
k
search at both levels: it proposes
k_{\mathrm{dec}}
decomposition candidates and
k_{\mathrm{sol}}
solver actions for subgoals, with verifier feedback and backtracking. The resulting success bound splits into two additive contributions (decomposition vs. solver), reflecting a balance between high-level structural exploration and low-level proof search. This decomposition is particularly important when the latent model implies that solver complexity contracts with depth (parameter
\alpha
) while the number of unique subgoals grows with
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Why hierarchy can be exponentially better.

Under cut structure, the latent DAG may have size
O(\lambda^{D})
in depth
D
(unique subgoals with sharing), whereas full cut elimination can unfold it into a cut-free tree of size
\Omega(\Lambda^{D})
with
\Lambda>\lambda
. Since our success guarantees amplify one-step errors
linearly
(explained later) in the number of required decision points, the flat (cut-free) learner must drive per-step errors much lower to compensate, resulting in exponentially larger sample requirements under standard learning curves. This yields a theoretical justification for subgoal decomposition as a distributionally rational inductive bias.

6
Conclusion

We presented a theoretical framework for LLM-guided formal theorem proving as a finite-horizon deterministic MDP over compact metric state/action spaces, together with distributional assumptions that capture “proof-friendly” structure. Modeling cut/lemma/sketch reuse via a latent proof DAG yields success guarantees for hierarchical top-
k
search in which (i) search and learning contributions separate cleanly and (ii) learning is controlled by sequential complexity measures even in the presence of latent structure. Most importantly, when cut elimination provably expands reusable DAG structure into exponentially larger cut-free trees, we obtain an exponential separation: cut-aware hierarchical learning and search can achieve a given success level with exponentially fewer samples than any flat cut-free approach under the same margin-driven analysis. This provides a principled explanation for why subgoal decomposition, despite being a difficult auxiliary task, can be a statistically and computationally rational design choice in modern agentic theorem provers.

(K,d_{K})
is compact, then
(M_{\leq W}(K),d_{\mathrm{BL}})
is a compact metric space.

This encoding is
permutation-invariant
and naturally supports variable goal counts.

B.2
Proof of
Theorem

4
: Compactness of
M_{\leq W}(K)
under
d_{\mathrm{BL}}

Lemma 1

(Arzelà–Ascoli compactness of the BL unit ball)
.

Let

\mathcal{F}:=\{f:K\to\mathbb{R}:\ \|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1\}.

Then

\mathcal{F}
is compact in
(C(K),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.

Proof.

Functions in
\mathcal{F}
are uniformly bounded
(
\|f\|\leq 1
) and equicontinuous (Lipschitz constant
\leq 1
). Since

K
is compact, Arzelà–Ascoli applies. ∎

Lemma 2

(
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
metrizes weak convergence on
M_{\leq W}(K)
)
.

Let
(\mu_{n})
and
\mu
be in
M_{\leq W}(K)
.
Then

\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu\ \text{(weakly)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0.

Proof.

we show

•

(
\Leftarrow
) If
d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
, then

\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\to 0
for all bounded Lipschitz
f
. Bounded
Lipschitz functions are dense in
C(K)
on compact metric spaces, so
convergence holds for all continuous
f
, i.e.,

\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
.

•

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
. Suppose

d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\not\to 0
. Then there exists

\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence (still indexed by
n
) and

f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\left|\int f_{n}d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }n.

By Lemma A.1,
(f_{n})
has a further subsequence converging
uniformly to some
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\int f_{n}d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|+\left|\int(f_{n}-f)d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|+\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Since
\mu_{n},\mu
are positive with mass
\leq W
,

\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mu_{n}(K)+\mu(K)\leq 2W
, and

\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
. Also,
f
is continuous so

\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\to 0
by weak convergence. Hence the right-hand
side tends to
0
, contradicting
\geq\varepsilon
.

Therefore
d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
. ∎

Lemma 3

(Compactness)
.

M_{\leq W}(K)
is compact under
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
.

Proof.

M_{\leq W}(K)
is tight and weakly sequentially
compact (by Prokhorov; on compact
K
, tightness is automatic). Any
sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence; by Lemma A.2 that
subsequence is Cauchy and convergent in
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
. Hence
sequential compactness, hence compactness. ∎

This completes the proof of
Theorem

4
.

Appendix C
Bellman equations, existence of optimal policies, and the 0–1 trivialization

C.1
Bellman recursion for reachability

Define the dynamic programming value

V^{\pi}_{t}(x):=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{x}(\exists\tau\leq t:\ X_{\tau}\in G)

for remaining horizon
t
.

In a general MDP,
V^{\pi}_{t}
satisfies a Bellman recursion. Here,
because transitions are deterministic given
(x,a)
, the recursion
takes an especially simple form.

Proposition 1

(Bellman equation for a fixed policy)
.

For any
policy
\pi
,

V^{\pi}_{0}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x\in G\},

and for
t\geq 1
,

V^{\pi}_{t}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x\in G\}+\mathbf{1}\{x\notin G\}\int_{\mathcal{A}}V^{\pi}_{t-1}(f(x,a))\pi(da\mid x).

Proof.

If
x\in G
then the event holds at time
0
. If

x\notin G
, the event “hit
G
within
t
steps” is equivalent
to “after choosing
A_{0}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)
, hit
G
within

t-1
steps from
f(x,A_{0})
”, which yields the stated conditional
expectation. ∎

C.2
Optimal policy and triviality in deterministic theorem
proving

Define the optimal value

V^{*}_{t}(x):=\sup_{\pi}V^{\pi}_{t}(x),\qquad V^{*}(T,x):=V^{*}_{T}(x).

Theorem 5

(0–1 trivialization and characterization by
reachability)
.

Assume deterministic transition. Then for any
T
and
x_{0}
,

V^{*}(T,x_{0})=\mathbf{1}\{K(x_{0})\leq T\}.

Moreover, if
K(x_{0})\leq T
then there exists a deterministic Markov
policy
\pi^{\dagger}
such that
V^{\pi^{\dagger}}(T,x_{0})=1
.

Proof.

(1)

If
K(x_{0})>T
, then there is no action sequence of
length at most
T
that reaches
G
, hence for every (possibly
randomized) policy
\pi
, the trajectory is a deterministic function
of the sampled actions and cannot reach
G
within
T
; thus

V^{\pi}(T,x_{0})=0
and
V^{*}(T,x_{0})=0
.

(2)

If
K(x_{0})\leq T
, choose a successful sequence

(a_{0}^{\ast},\dots,a_{K-1}^{\ast})
and define the corresponding
deterministic state sequence
x_{t+1}^{\ast}=f(x_{t}^{\ast},a_{t}^{\ast})

with
x_{0}^{\ast}=x_{0}
. Define a deterministic Markov policy

\pi^{\dagger}
by

\pi^{\dagger}(\cdot\mid x_{t}^{\ast})=\delta_{a_{t}^{\ast}},\qquad t=0,\dots,K-1,

and define
\pi^{\dagger}(\cdot\mid x)
arbitrarily elsewhere.
Under
\pi^{\dagger}
, the induced trajectory follows
(x_{t}^{\ast})

with probability
1
(because actions are deterministic), hence
reaches
G
by time
K\leq T
, so
V^{\pi^{\dagger}}(T,x_{0})=1
and

V^{*}(T,x_{0})=1
.

∎

Discussion: Why we introduce a data distribution and restricted learning

Theorem

5
shows that under deterministic kernel
semantics, the
unconstrained
optimal success probability is
always
0
or
1
, and thus is not informative about learning or
search. To obtain nontrivial theory, we study:

•

restricted policy classes (regularity, Lipschitzness, parametric
families), and

•

average-case behavior under a data distribution
Q
that reflects
“easy vs hard” theorem populations, together with explicit proof
search procedures (top-
k
, beam search, decomposition). The
remainder of the paper formalizes these components.

Appendix D
Examples of Data Generation Methods

In this study, it is not necessary to actually generate datasets,
but we describe two re-weighting methods as at least theoretical evidence of their constructive existence.

Define the success-to-go function

h_{r}(x):=\mathbb{P}^{q}_{x}(X_{r}\in G),\qquad r=0,1,\dots,L.

Proposition 2

(Backward Bellman recursion for
h
)
.

We have

h_{0}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x\in G\}
, and for
r\geq 0
,

h_{r+1}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}h_{r}(f(x,a))q(da\mid x).

Proof.

This is
Proposition

1
specialized to
t=r+1
and the
policy
q
with the terminal event “be in
G
at exactly
r

steps”. ∎

D.1
Conditioning on success: Doob
h
-transform

We want to sample
successful
length-
L
proof traces without
heavy rejection. The canonical construction is a Doob
h
-transform (a
change of measure on path space) for Markov processes
(
Chung and Walsh 2005
;
Rogers and Williams 2000
)

Definition 1

(Doob-transformed, success-conditioned policy)
.

For remaining horizon
r\geq 1
and state
x
with
h_{r}(x)>0
,
define

q_{r}^{\star}(a\mid x):=\mathbb{P}^{q}(A_{0}=a\mid X_{0}=x,\ X_{r}\in G)=\frac{q(a\mid x)h_{r-1}(f(x,a))}{h_{r}(x)}.

Theorem 6

(Exact conditional sampling of successful traces)
.

Fix
L
and an initial state
x_{0}
with
h_{L}(x_{0})>0
. If we
generate a trajectory by

A_{t}\sim q_{L-t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid X_{t}),\qquad X_{t+1}=f(X_{t},A_{t}),\qquad t=0,\dots,L-1,

then the law of
(X_{0:L},A_{0:L-1})
coincides with the law of the
base
q
-trajectory conditioned on
X_{L}\in G
.

Proof.

Let
(a_{0},\dots,a_{L-1})
be a valid action sequence and

x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},a_{t})
its induced state sequence. Under the conditional
law,

\mathbb{P}^{\star}(a_{0:L-1}\mid x_{0})=\prod_{t=0}^{L-1}\frac{q(a_{t}\mid x_{t})h_{L-t-1}(x_{t+1})}{h_{L-t}(x_{t})}.

This telescopes:

\mathbb{P}^{\star}(a_{0:L-1}\mid x_{0})=\frac{\prod_{t=0}^{L-1}q(a_{t}\mid x_{t})\cdot h_{0}(x_{L})}{h_{L}(x_{0})}=\frac{\prod_{t=0}^{L-1}q(a_{t}\mid x_{t})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{x_{L}\in G\}}{\mathbb{P}^{q}_{x_{0}}(X_{L}\in G)}.

This is exactly the base
q
path probability reweighted by

\mathbf{1}\{X_{L}\in G\}
and normalized, i.e., the conditional
distribution. ∎

D.2
Twisted SMC for practical construction

In realistic theorem proving,
h_{r}(x)
cannot be computed exactly (the
action space is huge; the transition is complex). We therefore use

twisted
sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) to approximate the
success-conditioned path distribution.

SMC for Feynman–Kac path measures is standard;
see e.g.
Doucet and Johansen 2011
’s tutorial for particle filtering/smoothing.
Twisted particle filters for Feynman–Kac models are a well-developed
variant designed to reduce weight degeneracy using “twisting
functions” that approximate
h

(
Whiteley and Lee 2014
)
.

D.2.1
Approximate twisting function

We assume an approximation
\hat{h}_{r}:\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
of

h_{r}
(learned or heuristic).

Define the (time-inhomogeneous) proposal kernel

\tilde{q}_{r}(a\mid x)\propto q(a\mid x)\hat{h}_{r-1}(f(x,a)),\qquad r\geq 1.

D.2.2
Algorithm (Twisted SMC with backward sampling)

Algorithm (Twisted SMC for
L
-step successful traces).

Input: generator
q
, twisting functions
\hat{h}_{r}
, horizon
L
,
particle number
N
.

1.

Initialize particles: sample
X_{0}^{(i)}\sim\mu_{0}
and set weights

w_{0}^{(i)}=1/N
.

2.

For
t=0,\dots,L-1
(remaining steps
r=L-t
):

•

Propose actions
A_{t}^{(i)}\sim\tilde{q}_{r}(\cdot\mid X_{t}^{(i)})
.

•

Propagate
X_{t+1}^{(i)}=f(X_{t}^{(i)},A_{t}^{(i)})
.

•

Update weights by importance correction

w_{t+1}^{(i)}\propto w_{t}^{(i)}\cdot\frac{q(A_{t}^{(i)}\mid X_{t}^{(i)})}{\tilde{q}_{r}(A_{t}^{(i)}\mid X_{t}^{(i)})},

and multiply by the terminal potential

\mathbf{1}\{X_{t+1}^{(i)}\neq\bot\}
(and optionally later by

\mathbf{1}\{X_{L}^{(i)}\in G\}
).

•

Resample if the effective sample size is below a threshold,
recording ancestor indices.

3.

At
t=L
, set
w_{L}^{(i)}\leftarrow 0
if
X_{L}^{(i)}\notin G
(or
multiply by
\mathbf{1}\{X_{L}^{(i)}\in G\}
) and renormalize.

4.

Sample an index
I
from
\{1,\dots,N\}
proportional to

w_{L}^{(i)}
and reconstruct the full trajectory by tracing back
ancestors (backward sampling / smoothing).

Output: a successful trace
(X_{0:L},A_{0:L-1})
.

Remark 1
.

When
\hat{h}=h
, the twisted proposal matches the
exact Doob transform, and weights become constant (no degeneracy),
recovering
Theorem

6
. In general, twisted SMC approximates the
success-conditioned Feynman–Kac path law, and backward sampling returns
an entire successful trajectory.

Details on Flat Learning and

E.1
A

We fix a “generator” policy

and use it to define a distribution
of
proved theorems
together with proof traces.
The generator itself can be an LLM, a hand-engineered heuristic, or random samples obtained from real datasets such as Mathlib
(
The mathlib community 2020
)
.

Fix a horizon

and a prior
\mu_{0}
on initial states
(a prior over theorem statements and contexts). A base trajectory

S_{0}\sim\mu_{0},\quad A_{t}\sim q(\cdot\mid S_{t}),\quad S_{t+1}=f(S_{t},A_{t}),\quad t=0,\dots,L-1.

A data point consists of a theorem (initial state) together with a
certified proof trace:

Definition 2

(Trace-labeled theorem distribution)
.

Fix a
length distribution
p_{L}
on
\mathbb{N}
with
\mathbb{E}[L]=K

(the “average proof length parameter”). Generate
(1)
L\sim p_{L}
,
(2) sample a successful length-
L
trace
(S_{0:L},A_{0:L-1})
, (3) output
z=(x,\tau)
where

is the theorem statement extracted
from
S_{0}
(or simply
x:=S_{0}
) and
\tau
is the action/state

We denote the resulting distribution by

The choice of

Remark 2
.

In this study, it is not necessary to actually generate datasets, especially

and

to support their theoretical existence.

E.2
Learning by ERM: approximation and estimation error under sequential dependence

E.2.1
Sequential generalization for imitation on proof traces

We learn a policy by imitation on certified proof traces sampled from a
generator-induced distribution
Q
. A single sample is a successful
trace

\tau=(S_{0},A_{0},S_{1},A_{1},\dots,S_{L-1},A_{L-1},S_{L}),\qquad S_{L}\in G,

with random length
L\leq T
. Let
K:=\mathbb{E}[L]
denote the
average proof length (number of OR-decisions).

Because losses will be evaluated along sequentially generated states, it
is convenient to work with the
length-biased mixture
distribution
of states (and state-action pairs) along traces.

Definition 3

(Time-mixture distribution along traces)
.

Let

Q
be a distribution over successful traces. Define a probability
measure
\nu
on states by the identity

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}[g(S)]:=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim Q}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}g(S_{t})\Big],\qquad\forall g\geq 0.

Similarly, define a probability measure
\mu
on state-action pairs
by

\mathbb{E}_{(S,A)\sim\mu}[h(S,A)]:=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim Q}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}h(S_{t},A_{t})\Big],\qquad\forall h\geq 0.

Throughout this section, we study the one-step log-loss

\ell_{\pi}(s,a):=-\log\pi(a\mid s),

and its population risk under
\mu
,

R(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{(S,A)\sim\mu}[\ell_{\pi}(S,A)].

If traces are generated by a fixed generator policy
q
, then
conditional on
S=s
we have
A\sim q(\cdot\mid s)
, hence

R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{A\sim q(\cdot\mid S)}[-\log\pi(A\mid S)]\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}\Big[H(q(\cdot\mid S))+\mathrm{KL}(q(\cdot\mid S)\|\pi(\cdot\mid S))\Big].

Define the one-step KL excess risk

\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}\big[\mathrm{KL}(q(\cdot\mid S)\|\pi(\cdot\mid S))\big],

so that

R(\pi)=R(q)+\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi),\qquad R(q):=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}[H(q(\cdot\mid S))].

Given
n
independent traces
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}\sim Q
, write

N:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}
for the total number of decision points in the
dataset. Define the empirical risk

\hat{R}_{N}(\pi):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{L_{i}-1}\ell_{\pi}(S_{i,t},A_{i,t}),

and choose an ERM solution

\hat{\pi}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in H}\ \hat{R}_{N}(\pi).

We assume a uniform lower bound to control the log-loss.

Assumption 8

(Lower-bounded probabilities)
.

There exists

\rho\in(0,1)
such that
\pi(a\mid s)\geq\rho
for all
\pi\in H

and for all
(s,a)
encountered in the data-generating process.
Consequently,
\ell_{\pi}(s,a)\in[0,B]
with
B:=\log(1/\rho)
.

To state a sequential generalization bound, we use a sequential covering number
(
Rakhlin et al. 2015a
;
Rakhlin et al. 2015b
)
.

Theorem 7

(Sequential generalization via sequential Rademacher
and Dudley)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:=\{\ell_{\pi}:\pi\in H\}
and assume

8
. There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

R(\hat{\pi})\leq\inf_{\pi\in H}R(\pi)+c\left(\mathfrak{R}_{N}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F})+B\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}\right),

where
\mathfrak{R}_{N}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F})
is the sequential
Rademacher complexity at depth
N
, and it satisfies the Dudley-type
entropy bound

\mathfrak{R}_{N}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F})\leq c\inf_{\alpha\in(0,B]}\left[\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{\alpha}^{B}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{seq}}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},N)},d\epsilon\right].

Define the one-step approximation error and estimation error

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}^{(1)}:=\inf_{\pi\in H}\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi),\qquad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}^{(1)}:=c\left(\mathfrak{R}_{N}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F})+B\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}\right).

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{\pi})\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}^{(1)}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}^{(1)}.

Proof.

Since
R(\pi)=R(q)+\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi)
and
R(q)

does not depend on
\pi
, the ERM generalization bound on

R(\hat{\pi})
directly translates to
\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{\pi})

with the same additive error. ∎

For example, when the state space
\mathcal{S}
has doubling dimension
d(>2)
and the policy
\pi
is
L
-Lipschitz, then the estimation error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
scales as
O(LN^{-1/d})
(see
Corollary

1
in
Section

E.4
).

E.3
Proof search guided by
\hat{\pi}
: top-
k
expansion

In this section we analyze the effect of
search
separate from the
policy itself, focusing on top-
k
expansions (beam search variants
fit this template).

E.3.1
A minimal top-
k
search
model

Given a state
s
, top-
k
produces the set

\mathrm{Top}_{k}(\pi(\cdot\mid s))
of the
k
most likely actions
under
\pi(\cdot\mid s)
(ties broken arbitrarily).

We idealize the verifier (Lean kernel) as providing immediate rejection
if
f(s,a)=\bot
, and we assume the search backtracks appropriately so
that if a
reference proof trace

(a_{0},\dots,a_{L-1})
is
contained in the top-
k
sets at every step along the induced states,
then the search can recover that trace.

This is a sufficient condition for success (it ignores alternative
proofs and thus leads to lower bounds).

E.3.2
A Tsybakov-type margin condition for top-
k
guarantee

By incorporating a Tsybakov-type margin (or, low-noise) condition
(
Audibert and Tsybakov 2007
;
Arlot and Bartlett 2011
)
that quantifies how often the
k
-th boundary is nearly tied, we derive a refined bound.

In practice, top-
k
is applied after a candidate generation step
(e.g., an LLM proposes a finite list of tactics). We therefore formalize
top-
k
on a finite candidate set.

Assumption 9

(Finite candidate sets)
.

There exists a
measurable mapping
\Gamma:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}^{M}
that returns a
multiset of
M
candidate actions at each state, and all policies
considered are evaluated on
\Gamma(s)
. For

p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
define its restriction

p_{\Gamma}(\cdot\mid s)
as the normalized probability on

\Gamma(s)
, and define
\mathrm{Top}_{k}(p(\cdot\mid s))
as the
top-
k
actions with respect to
p_{\Gamma}(\cdot\mid s)
.

Let
q_{(1)}(s)\geq q_{(2)}(s)\geq\dots\geq q_{(M)}(s)
denote the sorted
values of
q_{\Gamma}(\cdot\mid s)
.

Definition 4

(Top-
k
mass and gap)
.

Define

m_{k}(s):=\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Top}_{k}(q(\cdot\mid s))}q_{\Gamma}(a\mid s),\qquad\Delta_{k}(s):=q_{(k)}(s)-q_{(k+1)}(s),

with the convention
q_{(M+1)}(s)=0
.

Assumption 10

(Tsybakov-type top-
k
margin condition)
.

There exist constants
C_{0}>0
and
\beta>0
such that for all

u>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{k}(S)\leq u)\leq C_{0}u^{\beta},

where
S\sim\nu
is the time-mixture state distribution from

Definition

3
.

Theorem 8

(Top-
k
success under a Tsybakov margin condition)
.

Assume
9
and
10
. Then for any policy

\pi
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\big[V^{\mathrm{top}\text{-}k(\pi)}(T,x)\big]\geq 1-K\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}[1-m_{k}(S)]-KC_{\beta,C_{0}}\big(\bar{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi)\big)^{\beta/(\beta+2)}.

In particular, for the ERM solution
\hat{\pi}
, with probability at
least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\big[V^{\mathrm{top}\text{-}k(\hat{\pi})}(T,x)\big]\geq 1-K\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu}[1-m_{k}(S)]-KC_{\beta,C_{0}}\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}^{(1)}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}^{(1)}\big)^{\beta/(\beta+2)},

where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}^{(1)}
and

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}^{(1)}
are from
Theorem

7
(and
Corollary

1
).

The full proof is given in
Section

E.5
.

Remark 3

(Role of the three factors)
.

The bound splits the
failure probability into: (i) intrinsic top-
k
mass loss

\mathbb{E}[1-m_{k}(S)]
determined by the generator/data distribution;
(ii) a margin-controlled term depending on
\beta
and the learner’s
one-step KL error; (iii) a linear amplification by the average length

K
. Increasing
k
improves the intrinsic term

\mathbb{E}[1-m_{k}(S)]
, while better learning decreases

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
. The margin
exponent
\beta
determines how strongly estimation/approximation
error is amplified by top-
k
ranking.

E.4
Concretizing the sequential covering number via doubling dimension and Lipschitzness

We now provide an explicit upper bound on

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{seq}}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},N)
under geometric
assumptions.

We impose a doubling condition on the state space metric. Recall that
our state metric may involve Wasserstein distance on goal-measures.

Assumption 11

(Doubling state geometry)
.

The metric space

(\mathcal{S},d_{\mathcal{S}})
is compact with diameter

D:=\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{S})
and has doubling dimension

d\in\mathbb{N}
.

This implies a standard covering estimate: there exists a universal
constant
C_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
\delta\in(0,D]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{S}}(\delta)\leq\left(\frac{C_{0}D}{\delta}\right)^{d}.

(1)

We next impose Lipschitz regularity of the policy in a geometry-aware
distribution metric on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
. Let
d_{\Delta}
be
either the
1
-Wasserstein metric
W_{1}
on

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
(when
(\mathcal{A},d_{\mathcal{A}})
is
compact) or total variation. We only use that

|\pi(a\mid s)-\pi(a\mid s^{\prime})|\leq d_{\Delta}(\pi(\cdot\mid s),\pi(\cdot\mid s^{\prime}))
.

Assumption 12

(Lipschitz policy class)
.

Lemma 4

Lemma 5

For a trace

By construction, if

Therefore,

We now bound the one-step failure probability under the generator.
Conditional on

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{t}\notin\mathrm{Top}_{k}(\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t}))\mid S_{t}=s)=1-\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Top}_{k}(\pi(\cdot\mid s))}q_{\Gamma}(a\mid s).

For any state

Applying (

Appendix F
Details on Hierarchical Learning and
Theorem

2

We formulate the hierarchical model as a general latent-variable model with a
proof DAG
as latent variable, and conduct the success-probability analysis. We also sketch a variational/EM learning pipeline and discuss identifiability of structural parameters such as
(b_{\mathrm{eff}},\alpha)
.

F.1
A latent-variable formulation of hierarchical proof data

F.1.1
Observed data and latent proof DAGs

Let
X
denote a theorem instance (equivalently, an initial prover state or root goal), and let
Y
denote the observed proof-side data associated with
X
. The representation of
Y
can vary with the application; examples include:
(i) a full interactive transcript (states and actions),
(ii) a multiset of decision-point state–action pairs,
(iii) partial information such as local proof skeletons and terminal solver traces.
We intentionally keep
Y
abstract.

Let
Z
denote a
finite marked proof DAG
encoding the hierarchical proof structure with sharing. Concretely,
Z
may contain:

•

a finite DAG
(U,E)
of
unique
subgoals
u\in U
(canonicalized goals),

•

a depth map
\mathrm{depth}:U\to\{0,\dots,D\}
,

•

terminal indicators
T(u)\in\{0,1\}
,

•

decomposition actions
A_{u}^{\mathrm{dec}}
for
T(u)=0
,

•

solver lengths
L_{u}
and solver action sequences
A_{u,0:L_{u}-1}^{\mathrm{sol}}
for
T(u)=1
,

•

and any additional marks needed to reconstruct the corresponding kernel-valid proof object.

F.1.2
A general latent-variable model

Let
\theta
be a parameter vector controlling both the distribution of theorem instances and the proof-generation mechanism (including the structural parameters
(D,b_{\mathrm{eff}},\alpha)
and any additional parameters such as termination profiles, action models, and reuse mechanisms). We posit a joint distribution

p_{\theta}(x,y,z)=p_{\theta}(x)p_{\theta}(z\mid x)p_{\theta}(y\mid x,z).

(16)

The induced marginal (data) distribution is

p_{\theta}(x,y)=\int p_{\theta}(x,y,z)dz.

(17)

The success-filtered data distribution used throughout the paper corresponds to conditioning on a kernel-validity event
\mathsf{Succ}
(“the proof object verifies”).

Success-conditioned model.

Define the conditional (success-filtered) distribution

p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y,z):=p_{\theta}(x,y,z\mid\mathsf{Succ})=\frac{p_{\theta}(x,y,z)\mathbf{1}\{\mathsf{Succ}(x,y,z)\}}{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\mathsf{Succ})}.

(18)

The observed data distribution is then

Q_{\mathrm{hier}}(dx,dy)=p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(x,y)dxdy,\qquad p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(x,y)=\int p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(x,y,z)dz,

(19)

for some unknown “true” parameter
\theta^{\star}
(or a pseudo-true parameter in a misspecified setting).

Remark 4

(Doob transform / twisted SMC removes the global normalizer)
.

If the generator is
constructed
to sample only kernel-valid objects (e.g. via a Doob
h
-transform or twisted SMC that conditions on eventual closure), then
\mathbf{1}\{\mathsf{Succ}\}
is implicit in the support and the normalizing constant
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\mathsf{Succ})
can be avoided in practice. In that case one can work directly with a normalized
p_{\theta}^{+}
without explicit conditioning.

F.1.3
A structured factorization capturing
(D,\alpha,b_{\mathrm{eff}})

While (
16
) is completely general, it is useful to exhibit a factorization that makes the structural parameters explicit. One natural template is:

p_{\theta}(z\mid x)=\prod_{u\in U(z):\ \mathrm{depth}(u)<D}p_{\theta}\big(T(u)\mid\mathrm{depth}(u)\big)p_{\theta}\big(A_{u}^{\mathrm{dec}}\mid s_{u}\big)^{\mathbf{1}\{T(u)=0\}}p_{\theta}\big(\mathrm{Assign}_{u}\mid s_{u},A_{u}^{\mathrm{dec}},\mathcal{H}_{u}\big)^{\mathbf{1}\{T(u)=0\}},

(20)

where
s_{u}
is the (single-goal) state associated with node
u
,
\mathrm{Assign}_{u}
is a random mechanism that maps each generated child subgoal into either an existing node or a new node (thus modeling sharing), and
\mathcal{H}_{u}
denotes the relevant history/state of the partially built DAG at the moment
u
is expanded. The effective branching parameter
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
is then encoded in the law of
\mathrm{Assign}_{u}
through the growth rate of the number of unique nodes per depth.

The observation model can be factorized by decision type. For example, if
Y
contains the terminal solver traces (or summaries thereof), a standard form is

p_{\theta}(y\mid x,z)=\prod_{u\in U(z):T(u)=1}p_{\theta}\big(L_{u}\mid\mathrm{depth}(u);\alpha,K_{0}\big)p_{\theta}\big(A_{u,0:L_{u}-1}^{\mathrm{sol}}\mid s_{u},L_{u},\mathsf{close}\big)\times p_{\theta}\big(y_{\mathrm{aux}}\mid x,z\big),

(21)

where the depth-dependent length model makes
\alpha
explicit, e.g. via

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[L_{u}\mid\mathrm{depth}(u)=d,\ T(u)=1]=K_{0}\alpha^{d}.

(22)

F.2
Variational inference and an ELBO for success-filtered proof data

We introduce a variational posterior (inference model)
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
, with parameters
\phi
, to approximate
p_{\theta}^{+}(z\mid x,y)
. We use
r_{\phi}
to avoid notational collision with the generator policy
q
.

F.2.1
ELBO for the marginal likelihood

For any
(x,y)
and any density
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
supported wherever
p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y,z)>0
,

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\log\int p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y,z)dz=\log\int r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)\frac{p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y,z)}{r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)}dz

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,y)}\big[\log p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y,z)-\log r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)\big]=:\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x,y),

(23)

where the inequality is Jensen’s inequality. The quantity
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x,y)
is the (success-conditioned) evidence lower bound (ELBO). The gap is the posterior KL:

\log p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y)-\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x,y)=\mathrm{KL}\big(r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,y)\|p_{\theta}^{+}(\cdot\mid x,y)\big)\ \geq\ 0.

(24)

For an i.i.d. dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim(p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+})^{\otimes n}
, the empirical ELBO is

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta,\phi):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}^{+}(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}).

(25)

Maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{n}
over
(\theta,\phi)
yields a standard variational estimator of
\theta
.

F.2.2
ELBO when success-conditioning is implicit vs explicit

If
p_{\theta}^{+}
is implemented directly (e.g. via a Doob transform / twisted SMC so that only success-support is sampled), then (
23
) is directly usable.

If instead one works with an unconditioned
p_{\theta}
and explicit conditioning (
18
), then

\log p_{\theta}^{+}(x,y)=\log p_{\theta}(x,y,\mathsf{Succ})-\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\mathsf{Succ}),

(26)

and the second term is a global normalizer. One may estimate or bound
\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\mathsf{Succ})
via SMC-style estimators, or avoid it by working directly with a success-conditioned generator.

F.3
Joint learning with latent proof DAGs: EM/VAE-style training of hierarchical policies

We now describe how to learn (i) the latent-variable model parameters
\theta
and (ii) a pair of
test-time
policies
(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}},\pi_{\mathrm{sol}})
used by hierarchical proof search, using the latent-variable viewpoint.

F.3.1
A posterior-weighted ERM objective for hierarchical policies

Let
\eta
denote the parameters of
(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}},\pi_{\mathrm{sol}})
(e.g. neural parameters). When
Z
is latent, the decomposition/solver decision points are also latent, since they are functions of
Z
.

Assume that, given
(x,y,z)
, one can extract:

•

a set (or sequence) of decomposition decision points
\{(S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}},A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}})\}_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(z)}
,

•

a set (or sequence) of solver decision points
\{(S_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}},A_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}})\}_{j\in I_{\mathrm{sol}}(z)}
.

Define the complete-data imitation loss

\mathcal{J}(\eta;x,y,z):=\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(z)}-\log\pi_{\mathrm{dec},\eta}(A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\mid S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}})+\sum_{j\in I_{\mathrm{sol}}(z)}-\log\pi_{\mathrm{sol},\eta}(A_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}}\mid S_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}}).

(27)

A natural learning objective is the posterior-weighted ERM:

\min_{\eta}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x^{(i)},y^{(i)})}\big[\mathcal{J}(\eta;x^{(i)},y^{(i)},z)\big].

(28)

If
r_{\phi}
equals the true posterior, (
28
) is the Bayes-optimal risk minimization under the success-filtered data distribution; if
r_{\phi}
is approximate, (
28
) is a variationally motivated surrogate.

F.3.2
EM-style alternating optimization

A practical scheme is an EM/VAE hybrid that alternates:

•

(E-step / inference)
update
\phi
to improve
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
by maximizing the ELBO
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta,\phi)
,

•

(M-step / generative)
update
\theta
by maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta,\phi)
,

•

(Policy step)
update
\eta
by minimizing the posterior-weighted imitation objective (
28
).

When
r_{\phi}
is restricted to a tractable family and optimized to (approximate) the true posterior, this procedure is an approximate EM algorithm; when
r_{\phi}
is amortized, it is a standard variational learning pipeline.

Remark 5

(Unifying
\theta
and
\eta
)
.

One may also parameterize the generator action models inside
\theta
by the same networks used for the test-time policies (i.e. identify
q_{\mathrm{dec}}
with
\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}
and
q_{\mathrm{sol}}
with
\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}
). Then maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{n}
directly trains the policies in a generative modeling sense. The separation between ‘generator” and ‘test-time policy” is conceptually useful for analysis, but not required by the latent-variable formalism.

F.4
Estimating
(b_{\mathrm{eff}},\alpha)
and identifiability

We discuss how structural parameters can be estimated from success-filtered data and under what conditions they are identifiable. For clarity we assume
D
is known; extensions to unknown
D
are possible via model selection.

F.4.1
Sufficient statistics extracted from
Z

Let
Z
encode depth-partitioned unique-node sets
U_{d}(Z)\subseteq U(Z)
and terminal sets
U_{d}^{\mathrm{term}}(Z)\subseteq U_{d}(Z)
.
Define latent sufficient statistics:

C_{d}(Z):=|U_{d}(Z)|,\qquad T_{d}(Z):=|U_{d}^{\mathrm{term}}(Z)|,\qquad S_{d}(Z):=\sum_{u\in U_{d}^{\mathrm{term}}(Z)}L_{u}.

(29)

If
Z
is observed (e.g. because canonicalized goal identifiers are logged), these are directly computable. If
Z
is latent, one can use posterior expectations
\mathbb{E}_{r_{\phi}}[\cdot\mid x,y]
or
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}^{+}}[\cdot\mid x,y]
.

F.4.2
A homogeneous moment model

To make identifiability statements crisp, consider a homogeneous specialization:

\mathbb{E}[C_{d+1}(Z)\mid C_{d}(Z)]=b_{\mathrm{eff}}C_{d}(Z),\qquad\mathbb{E}[L_{u}\mid\mathrm{depth}(u)=d,\ T(u)=1]=K_{0}\alpha^{d},

(30)

and
\mathbb{P}(T(u)=1\mid\mathrm{depth}(u)=d)=r
constant.

Then

\mathbb{E}[C_{d}(Z)]=b_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d},\qquad\mathbb{E}[T_{d}(Z)]=rb_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d},\qquad\mathbb{E}[S_{d}(Z)]=rb_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d}K_{0}\alpha^{d}.

(31)

F.4.3
Identifiability: what can be recovered from what?

Identifiability of
\alpha
from per-terminal solver lengths.

Define the average solver length per terminal node at depth
d
:

\overline{L}_{d}(Z):=\frac{S_{d}(Z)}{T_{d}(Z)}\quad\text{(with the convention $\overline{L}_{d}=0$ if $T_{d}=0$)}.

(32)

Under (
30
), whenever
\mathbb{P}(T_{d}>0)>0
,

\mathbb{E}[\overline{L}_{d}(Z)\mid T_{d}(Z)>0]=K_{0}\alpha^{d}.

(33)

Hence, for any
d
such that both
T_{d}
and
T_{d+1}
are positive with nonzero probability,

\alpha=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\overline{L}_{d+1}(Z)\mid T_{d+1}>0]}{\mathbb{E}[\overline{L}_{d}(Z)\mid T_{d}>0]}.

(34)

Thus
\alpha
is identifiable (up to the mild regularity that terminal nodes occur at two depths).

Identifiability of
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
from unique-node counts.

From (
31
), whenever
\mathbb{E}[C_{d}(Z)]>0
,

b_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[C_{d+1}(Z)]}{\mathbb{E}[C_{d}(Z)]}.

(35)

Thus
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
is identifiable from the depth profile of unique-node counts.

Non-identifiability from aggregated solver effort alone.

If one observes only the aggregated totals
\{S_{d}(Z)\}
but not
\{C_{d}(Z)\}
or
\{T_{d}(Z)\}
(even in expectation), then (
31
) shows that only the
product

b_{\mathrm{eff}}\alpha
is identifiable from
\mathbb{E}[S_{d+1}]/\mathbb{E}[S_{d}]
:

\frac{\mathbb{E}[S_{d+1}(Z)]}{\mathbb{E}[S_{d}(Z)]}=b_{\mathrm{eff}}\alpha.

(36)

This illustrates a general principle: identifiability of
(b_{\mathrm{eff}},\alpha)
requires depth-resolved information separating ‘how many subgoals” from ‘how hard each subgoal is”.

F.4.4
Posterior moment estimators and EM updates

Given data
\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and an inference model
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
, define posterior moment estimators

\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[C_{d}]:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x^{(i)},y^{(i)})}[C_{d}(z)],\qquad\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{L}_{d}]:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x^{(i)},y^{(i)})}[\overline{L}_{d}(z)].

(37)

A direct method-of-moments plug-in estimator is

\hat{b}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[C_{d+1}]}{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[C_{d}]},\qquad\hat{\alpha}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{L}_{d+1}]}{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{L}_{d}]},

(38)

for any depth
d
where the denominators are nonzero. More robustly, one may estimate
\log b_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\log\alpha
by least-squares regression of
\log\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[C_{d}]
and
\log\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{L}_{d}]
against
d
.

In an EM-style M-step, one can instead maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood

\theta\leftarrow\arg\max_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x^{(i)},y^{(i)})}\big[\log p_{\theta}^{+}(x^{(i)},y^{(i)},z)\big],

(39)

where the dependence of
p_{\theta}^{+}
on
(b_{\mathrm{eff}},\alpha)
is made explicit by (
20
)–(
21
). Under standard regularity conditions (correct specification, identifiability, and sufficiently accurate inference), such estimators are consistent.

F.5
Success probability bounds in latent-variable form

We now preset the success probability analysis using only the latent-variable model (
16
) and its success-filtered version (
18
).

F.5.1
Hierarchical top-
k
search as a coverage event on a latent DAG

Fix a hierarchical prover that uses two policies:

•

a decomposition policy
\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}
with top-
k_{\mathrm{dec}}
lists,

•

a solver policy
\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}
with top-
k_{\mathrm{sol}}
lists,

together with backtracking and memoization that is complete with respect to these top-
k
lists.

Let
(X,Y,Z)\sim p_{\theta}^{+}
. Given
(X,Y,Z)
, extract the decomposition and solver decision points:

\{(S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}},A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}})\}_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)},\qquad\{(S_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}},A_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}})\}_{j\in I_{\mathrm{sol}}(Z)}.

Define the coverage event

\mathcal{E}(Z,Y):=\Big(\bigcap_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)}{A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\in\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}))}\Big)\cap\Big(\bigcap_{j\in I_{\mathrm{sol}}(Z)}{A_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}}\in\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{sol}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}(\cdot\mid S_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}}))}\Big).

(40)

By completeness w.r.t. top-
k
lists,
\mathcal{E}(Z,Y)
implies that the prover can reproduce the generator’s successful choices and therefore succeed. Hence the (random) success indicator satisfies

V^{\mathrm{hier}\text{-}\mathrm{top}\text{-}k}(T,X)\ \geq\ \mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}(Z,Y)\}.

(41)

Taking expectations under
p_{\theta}^{+}
yields the basic reduction:

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\theta}^{+}}\big[V^{\mathrm{hier}\text{-}\mathrm{top}\text{-}k}(T,X)\big]\ \geq\mathbb{P}_{(Y,Z)\sim p_{\theta}^{+}}\big(\mathcal{E}(Z,Y)\big).

(42)

F.5.2
Decision-mixture measures induced by the latent model

Define the random counts

N_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z):=|I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)|,\qquad N_{\mathrm{sol}}(Z):=|I_{\mathrm{sol}}(Z)|.

(43)

Let their expectations under
p_{\theta}^{+}
be

K_{\mathrm{dec}}:=\mathbb{E}[N_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)],\qquad K_{\mathrm{sol}}:=\mathbb{E}[N_{\mathrm{sol}}(Z)].

(44)

These coincide with the earlier effective counts when
Z
encodes the unique-node DAG (cf. Definition 7.12 and Proposition 7.13), but (
44
) is valid for any latent-variable specification.

Next define the induced mixture laws over states at each decision type. For any measurable
g\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}}[g(S)]:=\frac{1}{K_{\mathrm{dec}}},\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)}g(S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}})\Big],\qquad\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{sol}}}[g(S)]:=\frac{1}{K_{\mathrm{sol}}},\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j\in I_{\mathrm{sol}}(Z)}g(S_{j}^{\mathrm{sol}})\Big],

(45)

where expectations are under
(X,Y,Z)\sim p_{\theta}^{+}
.

F.5.3
Latent-variable success bound

Assume finite candidate sets for top-
k
lists, so that
\mathrm{Top}_{k}(\cdot\mid s)
and the generator gaps are well-defined on a finite set. Let the generator action models at decomposition/solver decisions be
q_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid s)
and
q_{\mathrm{sol}}(\cdot\mid s)
, understood as the conditional distributions of the corresponding actions in
Y
given the corresponding state in
Z
.

Define one-step KL errors under the latent-induced mixtures:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{dec}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}}\big[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid S)\|\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid S))\big],

\displaystyle\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{sol}}(\pi_{\mathrm{sol}})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{sol}}}\big[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\mathrm{sol}}(\cdot\mid S)\|\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}(\cdot\mid S))\big].

(46)

Define intrinsic top-
k
masses (on the generator) by

m_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}^{\mathrm{dec}}(s):=\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(q_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid s))}q_{\mathrm{dec}}(a\mid s),\qquad m_{k_{\mathrm{sol}}}^{\mathrm{sol}}(s):=\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{sol}}}(q_{\mathrm{sol}}(\cdot\mid s))}q_{\mathrm{sol}}(a\mid s).

(47)

Finally, assume Tsybakov-type margin conditions for each decision type under the induced mixture laws:
there exist
(C_{\mathrm{dec}},\beta_{\mathrm{dec}})
and
(C_{\mathrm{sol}},\beta_{\mathrm{sol}})
such that for all
u>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}^{\mathrm{dec}}(S)\leq u)\leq C_{\mathrm{dec}}u^{\beta_{\mathrm{dec}}}\quad(S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{k_{\mathrm{sol}}}^{\mathrm{sol}}(S)\leq u)\leq C_{\mathrm{sol}}u^{\beta_{\mathrm{sol}}}\quad(S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{sol}}),

(48)

where
\Delta_{k}
denotes the generator top-
k
gap on the candidate set.

Theorem 10

(Latent-variable success bound for hierarchical top-
k
search)
.

Assume completeness w.r.t. top-
k
lists, finite candidate sets, and the margin conditions (
48
). Then there exist constants
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{dec}}=\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{dec}}(C_{\mathrm{dec}},\beta_{\mathrm{dec}})
and
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{sol}}=\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{sol}}(C_{\mathrm{sol}},\beta_{\mathrm{sol}})
such that for any
(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}},\pi_{\mathrm{sol}})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\theta}^{+}}\big[V^{\mathrm{hier}\text{-}\mathrm{top}\text{-}k}(T,X)\big]\ \geq\ 1

\displaystyle-K_{\mathrm{dec}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}}[1-m_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}^{\mathrm{dec}}(S)]+\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{dec}}\big(\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{dec}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})\big)^{\beta_{\mathrm{dec}}/(\beta_{\mathrm{dec}}+2)}\Big)

\displaystyle-K_{\mathrm{sol}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{sol}}}[1-m_{k_{\mathrm{sol}}}^{\mathrm{sol}}(S)]+\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{sol}}\big(\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{sol}}(\pi_{\mathrm{sol}})\big)^{\beta_{\mathrm{sol}}/(\beta_{\mathrm{sol}}+2)}\Big).

(49)

Proof.

We follow the same coverage-event argument as in the explicit-DAG presentation, but write all expectations as marginals of the latent-variable model
p_{\theta}^{+}
.

By (
41
) and (
42
),

\mathbb{E}[V^{\mathrm{hier}\text{-}\mathrm{top}\text{-}k}(T,X)]\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}(Z,Y)^{c}).

(50)

Using
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dec}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sol}}
and a union bound,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dec}}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sol}}^{c}).

(51)

Consider
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dec}}
. By a union bound over
i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)
,

\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dec}\}^{c}}\leq\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)}\mathbf{1}\{A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\notin\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}))\}.

(52)

Taking expectations yields

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dec}}^{c})\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)}\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\notin\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}))\ \big|\ S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\big)\Big].

(53)

By definition of the generator model, conditional on
S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}=s
we have
A_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\sim q_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid s)
, hence the conditional probability equals

1-\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot\mid s))}q_{\mathrm{dec}}(a\mid s)
.
Applying the crude decomposition (as in
Lemma

7
)
pointwise in
s
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dec}}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)}(1-m_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}^{\mathrm{dec}}(S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}))\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})\neq\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(q_{\mathrm{dec}})\text{ at }S_{i}^{\mathrm{dec}}\}\Big].

(54)

Using the definition of
\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}
in (
45
) and
K_{\mathrm{dec}}=\mathbb{E}[N_{\mathrm{dec}}(Z)]
, the first term equals

K_{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}}[1-m_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}^{\mathrm{dec}}(S)]
.
The second term equals

K_{\mathrm{dec}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})\neq\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(q_{\mathrm{dec}}))

evaluated at
S\sim\nu_{\mathrm{dec}}
.

By the margin conditions (
48
) and the standard margin–KL argument (as in
Lemma

9
),
there exists
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{dec}}
such that

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})\neq\mathrm{Top}_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}(q_{\mathrm{dec}}))\leq\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{dec}}\big(\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{dec}}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})\big)^{\beta_{\mathrm{dec}}/(\beta_{\mathrm{dec}}+2)}.

(55)

Substituting into (
54
) yields the decomposition part of (
49
).

The solver part is identical, with
(\mathrm{dec})
replaced by
(\mathrm{sol})
. Combining with (
51
) and (
50
) proves (
49
).
∎

Remark 6

(Where
(D,\alpha,b_{\mathrm{eff}})
enter the bound)
.

In the latent-variable formulation, the structural parameters affect the guarantee through:
(i) the induced counts
K_{\mathrm{dec}},K_{\mathrm{sol}}
(controlled by branching/sharing and termination, hence by
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
D
),
(ii) the induced state mixtures
\nu_{\mathrm{dec}},\nu_{\mathrm{sol}}
and margins (which also depend on the geometry of the generated subgoals),
(iii) the solver-length model through

(which influences

and the distribution of solver states).
This clarifies how “proof-friendly” latent structure translates into finite-horizon provability guarantees.

Appendix G
Sequential generalization for posterior-weighted ERM with latent proof DAGs

This section refines
Joint learning with latent proof DAGs
by adding explicit generalization guarantees based on
sequential Rademacher complexity
and
sequential covering numbers
. The key message is that, once the inference model
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
is fixed (or treated as an approximation oracle), the induced training data are generated by a
predictable sequential process
, and standard sequential complexity tools yield high-probability bounds for the
one-step
imitation errors (mixture KLs) that enter the top-
k
success guarantees.

G.1
Posterior-weighted ERM as learning on a predictable sequential process

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. from the success-filtered data distribution
p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(x,y)
.
Fix an inference model
r_{\phi}(z\mid x,y)
, and define latent draws

Z_{i}\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid X_{i},Y_{i})\qquad(i=1,\dots,n).

For each decision type
\mathrm{typ}\in\{\mathrm{dec},\mathrm{sol}\}
, the pair
(Y_{i},Z_{i})
deterministically induces a finite sequence of decision points

(S_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}},A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}})_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{typ}}},

where
T_{\mathrm{typ}}
is a
fixed
horizon bound (padding with an absorbing “null” decision if needed). We emphasize that within each
i
, the state
S_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}}
may depend on past actions (e.g. via a deterministic kernel), hence the sequence is sequential.

Let
H_{\mathrm{typ}}
be a policy class and define the log loss

\ell_{\pi}(s,a):=-\log\pi(a\mid s),\qquad\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}.

(56)

Boundedness assumption.

Assume there exists
\rho\in(0,1)
such that for all
\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}
and all
(s,a)
that can occur under the model,

\pi(a\mid s)\geq\rho.

(57)

Then
\ell_{\pi}(s,a)\in[0,B]
uniformly with
B:=\log(1/\rho)
.

Population and empirical risks (fixed
\phi
).

Define the population risk under the
r_{\phi}
-induced
training distribution:

R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{typ}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{typ}}}\ell_{\pi}(S_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}},A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}})\Big],

(58)

where the expectation is over
(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}
,
Z_{i}\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid X_{i},Y_{i})
, and the randomness in
Y_{i}
if it includes stochastic action generation.

The empirical risk is

\hat{R}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi):=\frac{1}{nT_{\mathrm{typ}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{typ}}}\ell_{\pi}(S_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}},A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{typ}}).

(59)

We define the (posterior-weighted) ERM estimator

\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{typ}}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}}\hat{R}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi).

(60)

G.2
Sequential Rademacher complexity and sequential covering

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the space of observations at a decision point. We take
\mathcal{X}:=\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}\times\mathcal{A}
with an abstract measurable structure. For each
\mathrm{typ}
, define the loss class

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{typ}}:=\{(s,a)\mapsto\ell_{\pi}(s,a):\ \pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}\}\subseteq[0,B]^{\mathcal{X}}.

(61)

Definition 5

(Predictable trees)
.

An
\mathcal{X}
-valued predictable tree of depth
T
is a sequence
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
where each
\mathbf{x}_{t}:\{\pm 1\}^{t-1}\to\mathcal{X}
maps past signs to the
t
-th observation.

Definition 6

(Sequential Rademacher complexity)
.

The sequential Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[0,B]^{\mathcal{X}}
at depth
T
is

\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon_{t},f(\mathbf{x}_{t}(\epsilon_{1:t-1}))\Big],

(62)

where the supremum is over all predictable trees
\mathbf{x}
of depth
T
and
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{T}
are i.i.d. Rademacher signs.

Definition 7

(Sequential covering number)
.

Fix a predictable tree
\mathbf{x}
of depth
T
. An
\alpha
-cover of
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbf{x}
is a set
\mathcal{V}
of real-valued predictable trees of depth
T
such that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
and every path
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{T}
there exists
v\in\mathcal{V}
with

\Big(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{t}(\epsilon_{1:t-1}))-v_{t}(\epsilon_{1:t-1})\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\alpha.

(63)

Let
\mathcal{N}(\alpha,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{x})
be the minimum size of such a cover, and define the worst-case sequential covering number

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{seq}}(\alpha,\mathcal{F},T):=\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\ \mathcal{N}(\alpha,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{x}).

(64)

A standard Dudley-type bound relates (
62
) to (
64
).

Theorem 11

(Sequential Dudley bound)
.

For
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[0,B]^{\mathcal{X}}
and any
T\geq 1
,

\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F})\ \leq c\inf_{\alpha\in(0,B]}\Big[\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{\alpha}^{B}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{seq}}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},T)},d\epsilon\Big],

(65)

for a universal constant
c>0
.

G.3
Uniform generalization bounds (fixed inference model
\phi
)

We state a distribution-free generalization inequality for the posterior-weighted ERM (
60
). The proof follows the standard sequential symmetrization route and is omitted.

Theorem 12

(Sequential uniform convergence)
.

Fix
\mathrm{typ}\in\{\mathrm{dec},\mathrm{sol}\}
and assume (
57
). Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and latent draws

\sup_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}}\Big|R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi)-\hat{R}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi)\Big|\ \leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{nT_{\mathrm{typ}}}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{typ}})+B\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2nT_{\mathrm{typ}}}}.

Consequently, the ERM solution (

R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{typ}})\ \leq\inf_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}}R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi)+4\mathfrak{R}_{nT_{\mathrm{typ}}}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{typ}})+2B\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2nT_{\mathrm{typ}}}}.

(67)

G.4
From log-loss generalization to mixture KL bounds

To connect to the top-
k
success bounds, we convert log-loss control into control of the

Let
q_{\mathrm{typ}}(\cdot\mid s)

-decision state

\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}\big[\log(q_{\mathrm{typ}}(A\mid S)/\pi(A\mid S))\big]=R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi)-R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(q_{\mathrm{typ}}),

(68)

where
R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(q_{\mathrm{typ}}):=\mathbb{E}[-\log q_{\mathrm{typ}}(A\mid S)]

(Sequential generalization for mixture KL (fixed

Subtract the constant

G.5
A geometric specialization via sequential covering

We record a typical specialization when the state space has controlled metric entropy and the policy class is Lipschitz.

Assumption (Metric geometry).

Assume the
\mathrm{typ}
-state space
(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{typ}},d)
is compact with diameter
\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{typ}})
and doubling dimension
d_{\mathrm{typ}}
.

Assumption (Lipschitz policies).

Assume that for every
\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}
, the map
s\mapsto\pi(\cdot\mid s)
is
L_{\mathrm{typ}}
-Lipschitz from
(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{typ}},d)
into the probability simplex equipped with total variation distance, and that (
57
) holds.

Then
\ell_{\pi}(s,a)
is Lipschitz in
s

L_{\ell}:=L_{\mathrm{typ}}/\rho
(a standard consequence of
\nabla\log
bounded by
1/\rho
on the simplex interior). Under these assumptions one obtains a sequential covering bound of the form

\log\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{seq}}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{typ}},T)\ \leq C\Big(\frac{L_{\ell}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{typ}})}{\epsilon}\Big)^{d_{\mathrm{typ}}}\log\Big(\frac{B}{\epsilon}\Big),

(70)

for a constant
C
depending only on the doubling constant.

Combining (
65
) and (
70

\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{typ}})\ \lesssim\begin{cases}T^{-1/2},&d_{\mathrm{typ}}=1,\\
T^{-1/2}\log T,&d_{\mathrm{typ}}=2,\\
T^{-1/d_{\mathrm{typ}}},&d_{\mathrm{typ}}>2.\end{cases}

(71)

G.6
Inference error: controlling the mismatch between
r_{\phi}
and the true posterior

The preceding bounds control
\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{typ}})
, i.e. the mixture KL under the
r_{\phi}
-induced
training distribution. We now relate this to the corresponding quantity under the
true
success-filtered posterior.

Let
p^{\star}(z\mid x,y):=p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(z\mid x,y)
denote the true success-filtered posterior. Define the posterior KL (averaged over the data distribution)

\mathrm{PostKL}(\phi):=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid X,Y)\,\|\,p^{\star}(\cdot\mid X,Y)\big)\Big].

(72)

When the generative parameters are fixed at
\theta^{\star}

\mathrm{PostKL}(\phi)
equals the expected ELBO gap:

\mathbb{E}[\log p_{\theta^{\star}}^{+}(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star},\phi;X,Y)]=\mathrm{PostKL}(\phi).

Let
R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\star}(\pi)
denote the population risk defined as in (
58

instead of
r_{\phi}
.

Lemma 10

(Inference error bound via posterior KL)
.

Assume (
57

\mathrm{typ}\in\{\mathrm{dec},\mathrm{sol}\}

\sup_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}}\Big|R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\star}(\pi)-R_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi)\Big|\ \leq B\sqrt{2\mathrm{PostKL}(\phi)}.

(73)

Consequently,

\sup_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{typ}}}\Big|\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\star}(\pi)-\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\phi}(\pi)\Big|\ \leq 2B\sqrt{2\mathrm{PostKL}(\phi)}.

(74)

Proof.

For each
(x,y)
, the map
z\mapsto\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{typ}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{typ}}}\ell_{\pi}(S_{t}^{\mathrm{typ}},A_{t}^{\mathrm{typ}})
is measurable and takes values in
[0,B]
. Hence by the variational characterization of total variation and Pinsker’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p^{\star}(\cdot\mid x,y)}[\cdot]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,y)}[\cdot]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq 2B\mathrm{TV}\big(p^{\star}(\cdot\mid x,y),r_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,y)\big)

G.8
Plug-in to success probability bounds

Let
K_{\mathrm{dec}}
and
K_{\mathrm{sol}}
denote the expected numbers of decomposition and solver decision points (under the true success-filtered model), and assume the hierarchical top-
k
completeness and margin conditions that yield the latent-variable success bound (cf. Theorem

in the previous section). Then the success guarantee takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[V^{\mathrm{hier}\text{-}\mathrm{top}\text{-}k}(T,X)\big]\geq 1

\displaystyle-K_{\mathrm{dec}}\Big(\mathbb{E}[1-m_{k_{\mathrm{dec}}}^{\mathrm{dec}}(S)]+\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{dec}},\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{dec}}^{\star}(\pi_{\mathrm{dec}})^{p_{\mathrm{dec}}}\Big)

(76)

with

and
p_{\mathrm{sol}}=\beta_{\mathrm{sol}}/(\beta_{\mathrm{sol}}+2)
.

Applying Theorem

to
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{dec}}
and
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{sol}}
gives a fully explicit bound on the success probability in terms of:

•

approximation errors

\inf_{\pi\in H_{\mathrm{sol}}}\bar{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathrm{sol}}^{\star}(\pi)

Remark 7

(Interpretation: a three-way tradeoff)
.

Equation (

H.1
General latent-variable formulation and two evaluation protocols

Let

is a latent

(a finite marked DAG), and

Protocol H (hierarchical solving on the DAG).

A hierarchical prover uses two policies:

•

a decomposition policy

for lemma/cut/splitting decisions;

•

a solver policy
\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}
for tactic-level steps on terminal subgoals;

together with memoization on

(80)

where
\mathcal{T}
is a space of cut-free proof

. Intuitively,
\mathrm{CE}(Z)
replaces every shared lemma/cut-subproof by explicit duplication, producing a tree-like proof object.

A flat prover uses a
single
policy
\pi_{\mathrm{flat}}
on the cut-free tree representation. Let

N_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathrm{CE}(Z))

(81)

denote the number of flat decision points on the unfolded tree, and define

(82)

Remark 8

(what is “purely latent-variable”?)
.

The equalities (

82
) use only the latent-variable viewpoint: both protocols are measurable functions of
(Y,Z)
(hierarchical) or of
\mathrm{CE}(Z)

. No further structure has been imposed yet.

H.2

(i) a

bound (success degrades at most linearly with the number of required decisions), and

We state these assumptions in a way that is compatible with the general latent model.

Assumption 13

(Top-

Both provers (hierarchical and flat) are complete with respect to their respective top-
k

Assumption 14

(Finite candidate sets)

Top-

Assumption 15

Under the induced decision-point mixture laws (defined as in (

We write the corresponding margin-to-misranking exponents

Assumption 16

(One-step imitation errors)

Let

H.3
Lower bounding cut-elimination blow-up from latent DAG statistics

17
postulates blow-up directly at the level of decision counts. Here we give a general way to
justify
such blow-up from statistics of the latent proof DAG.

Fix a DAG instance
z\in\mathcal{Z}
with a depth partition
U_{d}(z)\subseteq U(z)
of unique subgoals (canonicalized nodes). Let
\mathrm{CE}(z)
be the unfolded cut-free tree.

Path-multiplicity statistic.

For each unique node
u\in U(z)
, define its
path multiplicity

M(u;z):=\#\{\text{directed root-to-$u$ paths in }z\}.

(96)

Intuitively,
M(u;z)
counts how many times
u
must be duplicated when shared structure is unfolded into a tree.

Lemma 11

(Unfolding multiplicity equals path multiplicity)
.

For any DAG
z
and any node
u\in U(z)
, the number of occurrences of the subproof rooted at
u
in the unfolded tree
\mathrm{CE}(z)
is at least
M(u;z)
. Consequently, the number of goal-occurrences at depth
d
in
\mathrm{CE}(z)
satisfies

\mathrm{Occ}_{d}(\mathrm{CE}(z))\ \geq\ \sum_{u\in U_{d}(z)}M(u;z).

(97)

Proof sketch.

Each distinct root-to-
u
path in
z
corresponds to a distinct context in which
u
is used. Unfolding removes sharing by duplicating each shared subproof for each distinct usage context, hence at least one copy per path. Summing over nodes at depth
d
yields (
97
). ∎

Lemma

11
implies that blow-up is controlled by how rapidly path multiplicities grow with depth. A particularly transparent sufficient condition uses a
reuse factor
.

Assumption 20

(Layered reuse lower bound)
.

There exists
r>1
such that for every depth
d\in\{1,\dots,D\}
and every node
u\in U_{d}(Z)
, the in-degree of
u
from depth
d-1
is at least
r
almost surely:

\mathrm{indeg}_{d-1\to d}(u)\ \geq\ r.

(98)

Proposition 3

(Exponential blow-up from reuse)
.

Under
20
, for every
z
,

\sum_{u\in U_{d}(z)}M(u;z)\ \geq\ r^{d},\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\mathrm{Occ}_{d}(\mathrm{CE}(z))\ \geq\ r^{d}.

(99)

In particular, the total number of tree decision points (and thus
N_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathrm{CE}(z))
in any reasonable encoding) is
\Omega(r^{D})
.

Proof.

Let
P_{d}(z):=\sum_{u\in U_{d}(z)}M(u;z)
be the total path multiplicity mass at depth
d
. By definition
P_{0}(z)=1
.
If each node at depth
d
has at least
r
parents at depth
d-1
, then each path to depth
d-1
can be extended in at least
r
ways to depth
d
, implying
P_{d}(z)\geq rP_{d-1}(z)
. Induction gives
P_{d}(z)\geq r^{d}
. Finally apply
Lemma

11
.
∎

Connection to
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
(unique branching).

In many hierarchical generators one has both:
(i) unique-node growth
\mathbb{E}[|U_{d}(Z)|]\asymp b_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d}
, and
(ii) reuse
r>1
as above.
Then the unfolded tree depth-
d
occurrences scale as
(rb_{\mathrm{eff}})^{d}
, while the DAG unique nodes scale as
b_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d}
, producing an exponential ratio
r^{d}
.

H.4
Concrete example: a layered reuse generator with
(D,\alpha,b_{\mathrm{eff}},r)

We give a simple stylized generator where the blow-up and the hierarchical advantage can be computed explicitly and connected to the parameters
(D,\alpha,b_{\mathrm{eff}})
.

Example model.

Fix integers
D\geq 1
and
b_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq 1
, and a reuse factor
r\geq 2
. Construct a layered DAG with unique nodes

|U_{d}|=b_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d},\qquad d=0,\dots,D.

(100)

Each unique node at depth
d-1
produces exactly

b:=rb_{\mathrm{eff}}

(101)

child occurrences
at depth
d
in the (conceptual) tree expansion, but canonicalization merges them so that only
b_{\mathrm{eff}}

unique
children remain. Equivalently, each unique node at depth
d
has exactly
r
parents at depth
d-1
(uniform reuse), realizing
20
with equality.

Terminal subgoals at depth
d
are solved by a solver trace of mean length
K_{0}\alpha^{d}
(with
\alpha\in(0,1)
):

\mathbb{E}[L(u)\mid\mathrm{depth}(u)=d,\ T(u)=1]=K_{0}\alpha^{d}.

(102)

Decision counts.

In this model,

K_{\mathrm{dec}}(D)\asymp\sum_{d=0}^{D-1}b_{\mathrm{eff}}^{d},\qquad K_{\mathrm{sol}}(D)\asymp K_{0}\sum_{d=0}^{D}(b_{\mathrm{eff}}\alpha)^{d},\qquad K_{\mathrm{hier}}=K_{\mathrm{dec}}+K_{\mathrm{sol}}.

(103)

If
b_{\mathrm{eff}}\alpha<1
, then
K_{\mathrm{sol}}(D)=O(1)
and
K_{\mathrm{hier}}\asymp b_{\mathrm{eff}}^{D}
.

After full cut elimination, the unfolded tree has branching factor
b=rb_{\mathrm{eff}}
, hence the number of flat decision points scales as

K_{\mathrm{flat}}(D)\asymp\sum_{d=0}^{D-1}b^{d}\asymp(rb_{\mathrm{eff}})^{D}.

(104)

Therefore the blow-up ratio is exponential:

\frac{K_{\mathrm{flat}}(D)}{K_{\mathrm{hier}}(D)}\ \gtrsim r^{D},\qquad\text{(in particular when $b_{\mathrm{eff}}\alpha<1$)}.

(105)

Exponential efficiency (plug-in).

Substituting (
104
) and (
103
) into
Theorems

15
and
16
yields:

•

an exponential gap
r^{D/p}
in the
required one-step accuracy
(KL) between flat and hierarchical proving;

•

an exponential gap
r^{D/(p\gamma)}
in the
required sample size
(or training resources) under a one-step learning curve exponent
\gamma
.

This concrete example makes transparent that the separation is driven by
reuse
: cut elimination duplicates shared subproofs, converting reuse into exponential tree size, while the hierarchical prover retains sharing and thus pays only for
unique
subgoals.

Remark 9

(balancing
\pi_{\mathrm{dec}}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{sol}}

.

In this example, when
