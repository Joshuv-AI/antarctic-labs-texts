Adipose-cartilage communication via EV-Mito–mtDNA signaling promotes osteoarthritis progression
Authors: Cheng Gu, Fawei Liao, Ganggang Kong, Shiyi Yin, Yifei Jiang, Runtao Zhou, Jingguang Zeng, Yixun Wu, Xubo Li, Bo Wang, Qiguang Pang, Ming Fu, Qingtang Zhu, Canbin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a major risk factor for osteoarthritis (OA), yet the mechanisms linking excess adiposity to joint degeneration remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify adipocyte-derived extracellular vesicles enriched in mitochondrial components (EV-Mito) as pathogenic mediators that transfer mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to chondrocytes. The internalized mtDNA activates the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, inducing inflammatory signaling, metabolic dysfunction, senescence, and cartilage degeneration in murine and human models. Genetic and pharmacological inhibition of cGAS-STING signaling attenuates cartilage damage, pain behaviors, and gait abnormalities. Population-level and interventional data further show that body fat percentage, rather than body mass index, is more closely associated with OA risk and severity. In an exercise cohort, high-intensity interval training selectively reduced adiposity and was associated with lower mtDNA abundance in synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, reduced synovial 2′,3′-cyclic GMP-AMP levels, and superior improvement in OA symptoms. These findings define a conserved EV-Mito–mtDNA–cGAS-STING axis linking adipose tissue to joint pathology and highlight fat-targeted interventions as mechanistically informed strategies for preventing and treating obesity-associated OA.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent form of chronic joint disease worldwide, leading to substantial joint dysfunction and a decline in quality of life (1). The primary pathological features of OA include chondrocyte metabolic dysregulation and cellular senescence. The etiology and pathogenesis of OA are highly complex, involving multiple interrelated factors. Common risk factors include aging, joint injury or overuse, obesity, genetic predisposition, sex, hormonal influences, mechanical stress, and joint malalignment (2, 3). Among these, obesity has emerged as a particularly strong contributor to both the onset and progression of OA (4). Traditional models have attributed this relationship to biomechanical overload of weight-bearing joints, such as the knees and hips, in which excessive mechanical stress accelerates cartilage degeneration. However, recent studies have demonstrated that obesity also influences the progression of OA in non–weight-bearing joints, such as the temporomandibular joint and hand joints (5). These observations suggest that obesity exerts systemic effects that extend beyond mechanical loading alone. Furthermore, in the context of obesity-driven OA, the underlying mechanisms of cartilage degradation and abnormal subchondral bone remodeling challenge the conventional “wear and tear” paradigm. The joint overload theory alone fails to explain the strong correlation between obesity and OA (6, 7). Collectively, these findings suggest that the systemic metabolic alterations induced by obesity play a greater role in OA pathogenesis than previously recognized.

Adipose tissue, beyond serving as an energy reservoir, functions as a dynamic endocrine organ (8). It secretes a variety of adipokines, bioactive signaling molecules that regulate inflammatory responses, systemic energy balance, and glucose and lipid metabolism. Emerging evidence indicates that obesity induces profound changes in the endocrine and paracrine functions of adipose tissue, thereby modifying its physiological effects on distant organs and tissues (9–11). For example, in obesity, the levels of adipokines, such as leptin and adiponectin, secreted by adipose tissue change. However, their effects are paradoxical, as they can drive cartilage degradation and support cartilage repair (12–15). This dual nature makes it difficult to attribute the substantial pro-degenerative impact of obesity on OA solely to these adipokines, suggesting the involvement of additional mechanisms. In addition to classical soluble factors, adipocytes secrete a substantial number of extracellular vesicles (EVs), a class of adipokines that mediate intertissue communication (16, 17). These adipocyte-derived EVs are enriched with bioactive cargos, including proteins, RNA, DNA, and lipids, and serve as important vehicles for intercellular signaling. Notably, EVs can traverse biological barriers, circulate through bodily fluids, and be internalized by recipient cells in neighboring or distant tissues, influencing their behavior and function. Crucially, studies have confirmed that obesity alters both the abundance and molecular composition of adipocyte-derived EVs, underscoring their role in adaptive cell-to-cell communication (18–21). These findings suggest the hypothesis that EVs secreted by adipocytes in obesity may constitute a key intrinsic mechanism that exacerbates chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction and promotes OA progression.

In this study, we identify adipose tissue–derived extracellular vesicles (AT-EVs) as a class of adipokines that mediate adipose-cartilage communication and regulate cartilage homeostasis. Using a genetically engineered mouse model expressing adipocyte-specific EV reporters, we demonstrate that chondrocytes internalize AT-EVs in vivo along with their mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), uncovering a previously unrecognized mechanism of intercellular signaling. Notably, mitochondrial extracellular vesicles (EV-Mito) originating from obese adipose tissue were found to exacerbate chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction and promote cellular senescence. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the mtDNA cargo of obesity-associated EV-Mito activates the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) signaling pathway in chondrocytes. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) reduced adiposity and was associated with decreased mtDNA abundance in synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, lower synovial 2′,3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) levels, and improved OA outcomes, supporting adiposity-targeted intervention as a clinically relevant strategy. Together with our mechanistic data, these findings suggest that approaches aimed at limiting adipose EV-Mito biogenesis or neutralizing EV-associated mtDNA, particularly when combined with personalized fat-reduction exercise regimens, may represent a promising approach to attenuating obesity-driven OA by disrupting pathogenic adipose-derived signaling.

RESULTS

Obesity-induced adipose signaling accelerates chondrocyte senescence and OA progression

Accumulating evidence from observational studies suggests a positive association between obesity and increased risk of knee OA (22, 23). However, these studies are inherently susceptible to confounding variables and reverse causality. To elucidate the causal relationship, we conducted a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis (fig. S1, A to C). The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) analyses and several complementary MR-Egger analyses supported a genetically predicted positive association between obesity and the risk of knee OA, providing genetic evidence supporting causality. Furthermore, leave-one-out analysis indicated that no single SNP drove the association (fig. S1D), affirming the reliability of our findings.

To experimentally model obesity, we subjected mice to a high-fat diet (HFD) for 12 consecutive weeks. Compared with control mice, HFD-fed mice exhibited significantly enlarged white adipose tissue (WAT; fig. S2A), and their body weight increased by approximately 50% (fig. S2B). Histological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed hypertrophic adipocytes with enhanced lipid droplet accumulation (fig. S2C). In addition, these mice developed impaired glucose tolerance (fig. S2D) and insulin resistance (fig. S2E), confirming successful induction of obesity. To examine whether HFD-induced obesity accelerates cartilage metabolic dysfunction and OA progression, we used destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgical model, a well-established method for inducing OA (24, 25). Mice on either a chow diet (CD) or HFD underwent DMM surgery (Fig. 1A). Eight weeks post-DMM, corresponding to late-stage OA in humans, H&E and Safranin O–Fast Green (SO/FG) staining showed that HFD-fed mice exhibited more pronounced articular surface roughening and cartilage damage compared to CD-fed controls (Fig. 1B), accompanied by significantly elevated Mankin and Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scores (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that obesity exacerbates DMM-induced cartilage metabolic deterioration. Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed that cartilage from HFD-fed mice exhibited decreased expression of key extracellular matrix components, including type II collagen (Col2) and aggrecan (ACAN), alongside a marked increase in matrix metalloproteinase–13 (MMP13) expression (Fig. 1D). Micro–computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis of knee joints demonstrated significant subchondral bone changes in HFD-fed mice, including thickened subchondral bone plates and increased osteophyte formation (Fig. 1B). Quantitative evaluation confirmed elevated tibial subchondral bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in obese mice, along with a higher number and volume of osteophytes (Fig. 1E). These findings collectively provide compelling evidence that HFD-induced obesity accelerates cartilage degradation and pathological bone remodeling in OA.

To determine whether the observed OA exacerbation was driven by adipose tissue–derived factors rather than mechanical overload from increased body mass, we performed a transplantation experiment. CD-fed recipient mice first underwent bilateral inguinal adipose tissue removal and were then assigned to three groups: sham surgery without adipose tissue transplantation, transplantation with adipose tissue from CD-fed donor mice, or transplantation with adipose tissue from HFD-fed donor mice (Fig. 1F). Posttransplantation, glucose tolerance (fig. S2F), body weight, and final graft weight (fig. S2G) were comparable across groups. Representative H&E-stained sections of transplanted CD-derived adipose tissue (CD-AT) and HFD-derived adipose tissue (HFD-AT) grafts showed no obvious inflammatory infiltration or tissue injury (fig. S2H), effectively isolating the influence of transplanted adipose tissue. Notably, mice receiving HFD-AT transplants displayed significantly more severe cartilage degeneration than those receiving CD-AT or sham transplants (Fig. 1G). Quantitative analyses revealed elevated Mankin and OARSI scores, decreased Col2 and ACAN expression, and increased MMP13 expression in HFD-AT recipients. Micro-CT analysis corroborated these results, showing elevated tibial subchondral BV/TV, Tb.Th, and osteophyte formation and volume (Fig. 1H).

To further validate these findings, we conducted in vitro experiments using primary mouse chondrocytes cocultured with adipose tissue. Coculture with HFD-derived adipose tissue significantly reduced Col2 and ACAN levels, while up-regulating MMP13 and senescence markers P16 and P21. In contrast, chondrocytes cocultured with CD-derived adipose tissue showed no significant alterations in these markers (fig. S2, I and J). Together, these results provide strong evidence that adipose tissue in obesity promotes chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction, accelerates cellular senescence, and drives the progression of OA, independently of mechanical loading.

AT-EVs are internalized by chondrocytes in vivo and in vitro

To visualize EV transfer from adipocytes, we used a genetically engineered mouse model in which a floxed stop cassette precedes a CD63 sequence fused to emerald green fluorescent protein (emGFP) at the endogenous Gt(ROSA)26Sor locus. When crossed with adipoq-CreERT2 mice, tamoxifen (TAM) induced Cre recombinase-mediated excision of the stop cassette, leading to adipocyte-specific expression of CD63-emGFP, thereby enabling in vivo tracking of AT-EVs (Fig. 2, A and B). Following TAM administration in HFD-fed reporter mice, we assessed emGFP fluorescence in cartilage tissues. In adipoq-CreERT2; CD63-emGFP mice, emGFP signals were detected in cartilage under obese conditions and were markedly enhanced following DMM surgery (Fig. 2, C and D). These findings indicate that AT-EVs are naturally transferred from adipocytes to chondrocytes in vivo and that this transfer is significantly up-regulated in response to cartilage injury.

To complement in vivo findings, we established an in vitro protocol for isolating AT-EVs using differential ultracentrifugation (UC), optimized according to established methods (Fig. 2E) (11, 26). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed typical cup-shaped vesicles, while nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed a size distribution predominantly ranging from 50 to 150 nm (fig. S3A). Western blot (WB) analysis further supported AT-EV enrichment, as evidenced by the presence of the canonical EV markers CD9, CD63, and TSG101, the absence of the negative marker Calnexin, and the detection of the AT-EV markers adiponectin and FABP4 (fig. S3B). To evaluate AT-EV uptake, primary murine chondrocytes were isolated and cultured, with cellular identity verified by toluidine blue and SO staining (fig. S3C). EVs derived from CD and HFD adipose tissues were labeled with PKH26 and incubated with chondrocytes for 24 hours (fig. S3D). Costaining with phalloidin, together with z-stack and three-dimensional reconstruction analyses, showed that AT-EVs were localized within chondrocytes rather than merely attached to the cell surface (Fig. 2F and movies S1 to S3). Quantitative analysis of PKH26 fluorescence intensity further showed that the uptake of CD-EVs and HFD-EVs by chondrocytes did not differ significantly (Fig. 2G). In addition, neither CD-EVs nor HFD-EVs detectably affected chondrocyte viability at the tested concentrations, as assessed by cytotoxicity assays (Fig. 2H). Time-course fluorescence imaging showed progressive accumulation of PKH26-labeled AT-EV signals in chondrocytes within 2 hours of treatment, with signal intensity further increasing at 4, 8, and 12 hours, consistent with time-dependent uptake of AT-EVs (fig. S3, E and F). These results confirm the successful isolation and characterization of AT-EVs with typical morphology and marker expression, as well as their efficient, time-dependent uptake by chondrocytes without cytotoxicity.

Together, these results provide robust evidence that AT-EVs from both normal and obese conditions are efficiently internalized by chondrocytes and distributed within their intracellular compartments, supporting a direct mechanism of adipose-cartilage communication.

Obesity-associated AT-EVs exacerbate chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction, senescence, and OA progression

To examine the effects of AT-EVs derived from different metabolic conditions on chondrocyte homeostasis, primary chondrocytes were isolated from human non-OA control cartilage and OA cartilage tissues and cultured in vitro. EVs were isolated from the infrapatellar fat pads (IFPs) of donors without obesity and donors with obesity, termed nonobese EVs and obese EVs, respectively, and were incubated with primary OA chondrocytes. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and WB analyses were conducted to assess the expression of anabolic cartilage matrix components (Col2 and ACAN) and MMP13 (Fig. 3A). Chondrocytes treated with obese-EVs exhibited significant down-regulation of Col2 and ACAN expression, along with a marked up-regulation of MMP13, compared to untreated controls. In contrast, nonobese-EVs did not induce statistically significant changes in these markers (Fig. 3, B to D). These findings demonstrate that AT-EVs derived from obese adipose tissue disrupt the anabolic-catabolic balance in chondrocytes, thereby exacerbating metabolic dysfunction. Notably, the absence of such effects in nonobese EVs highlights the pathological, state-dependent metabolic nature of AT-EVs and their potential role in obesity-associated cartilage degradation.

To corroborate these findings, parallel experiments were performed using murine primary chondrocytes. CD-EVs or HFD-EVs were incubated with chondrocytes (fig. S4A). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that HFD-EV–treated chondrocytes displayed significantly reduced expression of anabolic markers Col2 and ACAN, along with elevated levels of MMP13 and senescence-associated proteins P16 and P21, relative to those treated with CD-EVs (fig. S4B). These results confirm that obesity-associated AT-EVs promote both degenerative and senescent phenotypes in murine chondrocytes under metabolic stress, establishing them as key mediators of chondrocyte dysfunction.

To explore the in vivo relevance of these findings, CD-EVs and HFD-EVs were intra-articularly injected into the knee joints of mice subjected to the DMM model (Fig. 3E). After 12 weeks of EV treatment, no significant differences in body weight or blood glucose levels were observed among the groups (fig. S4, C and D), confirming that systemic metabolic variables were controlled. Histological analysis using H&E and SO/FG staining revealed significantly more severe cartilage erosion in HFD-EV–treated knees, as indicated by increased Mankin and OARSI scores compared to saline- or CD-EV–injected controls (Fig. 3, F and G). Immunohistochemistry further demonstrated decreased Col2 and ACAN expression and increased MMP13 levels in HFD-EV–treated joints (Fig. 3, F and G).

To directly determine the pathogenic role of AT-EVs in obesity-associated OA, HFD-fed DMM mice received either local injections of GW4869 into the inguinal fat depot or AAV9-sh-Rab27a–mediated Rab27a knockdown in adipose tissue to inhibit AT-EV secretion (Fig. 3H). H&E staining confirmed that repeated local injections did not induce pathological alterations in adipocyte morphology (fig. S4E), and WB analysis verified significant suppression of Rab27a expression in adipose tissue (fig. S4, F and G). Both GW4869 and AAV9-sh-Rab27a effectively reduced AT-EV release (fig. S4K) without altering the systemic levels of other adipose-derived factors, including free fatty acids (FFAs), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c). Inhibition of AT-EV secretion significantly attenuated cartilage degradation, as indicated by lower Mankin and OARSI scores, restored Col2 and ACAN expression, and decreased MMP13 levels (Fig. 3, I and J).

During obesity, in addition to the excessive release of AT-EVs, pathological expansion of adipose tissue induces local hypoxia, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and immune cell infiltration, which collectively promote the dysregulated production of adipose-derived factors, including FFAs and leptin (27). To assess the relative contribution of AT-EVs versus soluble adipokines to OA pathogenesis in vivo, mice were administered ergosterol (ES, 100 mg kg−1 day−1) via oral gavage for eight consecutive weeks to reduce circulating FFA levels, using a previously validated method (28). ES treatment did not affect AT-EV release or protein cargo (fig. S4K) but effectively reduced serum FFAs, TG, TC, and LDL-c while elevating HDL-c levels (fig. S4L). However, despite this improvement in circulating FFAs and overall metabolic profiles, cartilage degeneration remained unchanged, as Mankin and OARSI scores, along with Col2, ACAN, and MMP13 expression, showed no improvement (Fig. 3, I and J), indicating that neither elevated FFAs nor systemic metabolic dysregulation is the major driver of OA progression.

To assess the contribution of leptin receptor signaling in chondrocytes, we generated cartilage-specific Lepr conditional knockout mice (Col2a1-CreERT2; Leprfl/fl, referred to as Lepr cKO mice) and confirmed the expected genotypes by PCR and efficient reduction of Lepr protein in cartilage tissues by Western blotting (fig. S4, H to J). Lepr cKO did not alter AT-EV release, EV protein content (fig. S4K), or systemic lipid profiles (fig. S4L), but produced only a modest alleviation of OA pathology. The extent of improvement was markedly less than that achieved by EV inhibition: Decreases in Mankin and OARSI scores and increases in Col2 and ACAN expression were less pronounced, and the reduction in MMP13 was comparatively weaker (Fig. 3, I and J). These findings demonstrate that targeted inhibition of AT-EV release, rather than modulation of classical adipokines or systemic lipid metabolism, effectively reverses cartilage pathology in obesity-associated OA.

Collectively, these findings establish that AT-EVs derived from obese adipose tissue are potent pathological mediators that disrupt cartilage homeostasis by promoting chondrocyte metabolic disorder and senescence. Their effects are consistently observed in both in vitro and in vivo models, underscoring their critical role in obesity-driven OA.

AT-EVs in obesity enrich mitochondrial components and facilitate their transfer from adipocytes to chondrocytes

To identify bioactive protein cargo within AT-EVs, we performed unbiased proteomic profiling of EVs isolated from adipose tissues of CD- and HFD-fed mice using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Differential expression analysis revealed distinct protein signatures in HFD-EVs compared to CD-EVs, with subsequent analyses focusing on proteins up-regulated in HFD-EVs (Fig. 4A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis showed that a substantial subset of these differentially expressed proteins mapped to mitochondrial metabolic pathways (Fig. 4B). Gene Ontology analysis further showed that proteins up-regulated in HFD-EVs were enriched in biological processes related to mitochondrial organization, oxidative phosphorylation, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) metabolism. Notably, many of the most enriched proteins in obesity-associated AT-EVs were mitochondria-associated proteins, including components of the electron transport chain and mitochondrial membrane transporters (fig. S5, A to C). Our proteomic data thus suggest that pathological AT-EVs undergo compositional remodeling, favoring the generation of EV-Mito under obesogenic conditions.

To investigate the transfer of mitochondrial components via AT-EVs, we used an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector encoding a mitochondria-targeted DsRed2 reporter (mito-DsRed2) under the control of an adipocyte-specific adiponectin promoter (fig. S5D). Murine adipocytes isolated from HFD-induced obese mice were transduced with AAV-MitoDsRed2, and EVs isolated from their conditioned medium were incubated with murine chondrocytes for 24 hours. After incubation, recipient chondrocytes were stained with phalloidin to visualize the cytoskeleton (Fig. 4C). Confocal microscopy revealed DsRed2 fluorescence in recipient chondrocytes, and z-stack–based three-dimensional reconstruction showed that this fluorescence was localized intracellularly rather than merely attached to the cell surface (Fig. 4, D and E). To elucidate the internalization mechanism of AT-EVs, chondrocytes were pretreated with chlorpromazine, a pharmacological inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis (29, 30), which significantly reduced DsRed2 uptake (Fig. 4, D and E). These findings suggest that chondrocytes can internalize AT-EV–associated mitochondrial signals in vitro and that this process is at least partly dependent on clathrin-mediated endocytosis.

To investigate whether adipose-derived mitochondrial signals are transferred to cartilage in vivo, an AAV-MitoDsRed2 construct was locally injected into the WAT of mice to achieve adipocyte-restricted mitochondrial labeling. Eight weeks after injection, robust DsRed2 fluorescence was detected in the inguinal WAT of treated mice. In contrast, no signal was observed in controls (fig. S5E), confirming the specificity and efficiency of the reporter system. Confocal imaging of articular cartilage sections revealed no DsRed2 fluorescence in chow-fed mice, excluding off-target viral leakage. Cartilage from HFD-induced obese mice exhibited distinct DsRed2 signals that became further intensified in DMM-induced OA models (Fig. 4, F and G). To verify that the MitoDsRed2 signal was derived from AT-EVs, we performed dSTORM-based super-resolution imaging of EVs using previously reported methods (31, 32). Because DsRed2 itself does not exhibit the photoswitching behavior required for dSTORM-based single-molecule localization, an AF647-conjugated anti-DsRed2 antibody was used for indirect detection. Under nonpermeabilized conditions, DsRed2 positivity was minimal on the surface of both CD-EVs and HFD-EVs. In contrast, after permeabilization, DsRed2 positivity in HFD-EVs was markedly increased, whereas it remained minimal in CD-EVs (Fig. 4, H and I). These findings indicate that HFD-induced adipose-derived mitochondrial DsRed2 signals are detectable in cartilage in vivo and that intravesicular MitoDsRed2 signals are enriched in a subset of HFD-EVs.

However, AAV-MitoDsRed2 tracing alone cannot determine whether the DsRed2 signal detected in cartilage originates from EV-encapsulated mitochondrial material or from free mitochondrial components or debris. To further test whether mitochondrial transfer was EV dependent, control experiments were performed in which GW4869 pretreatment was used to suppress adipose EV release before AAV tracing, or isolated HFD-EVs from AAV-MitoDsRed2–labeled adipose tissue were reintroduced into mice with GW4869-suppressed adipose EV release. Confocal imaging showed that GW4869 administration markedly reduced DsRed2-positive mitochondrial signals in cartilage, whereas reintroduction of isolated DsRed2-positive HFD-EVs restored the DsRed2 fluorescence within chondrocytes (Fig. 4, J and K). These findings indicate that mitochondrial components in adipose tissue are primarily delivered to cartilage via EV-mediated transport rather than by free mitochondrial leakage or debris diffusion. Collectively, the spatial and functional correlation between adipose-derived EV-Mito trafficking and cartilage degeneration reveals a dynamic interorgan cross-talk mechanism, wherein metabolic stress enhances the EV-dependent transfer of mitochondrial components from adipose tissue to compromised articular cartilage.

Previous studies have established that EV-Mito, with diameters smaller than intact mitochondria, can contain mtDNA, mitochondrial proteins, or their complexes rather than whole organelles (33–35). To delineate obesity-associated alterations in EV-Mito composition, we quantified EV particles and protein content of adipose tissue. AT-EVs from HFD-induced obese mice showed a nearly threefold increase in EV particle count and protein concentration compared with the CD group (fig. S5F), indicating increased AT-EV output during obesity. DNA isolation from equal particle numbers of AT-EVs further revealed notable enrichment of total DNA in HFD-EVs (fig. S5, G and H). This suggests that the number of EVs released from adipose tissue, along with their contents (proteins and DNA), is significantly elevated in obesity. To distinguish nuclear DNA (nDNA) from mtDNA, we performed absolute quantification via quantitative PCR (qPCR) using plasmid-calibrated standards. Primers targeting nuclear-specific loci (B2m and Tuba1a) showed negligible nDNA in EVs (≤0.5% of total DNA) (fig. S5I). qPCR-based analysis of multiple mtDNA regions showed that HFD-EVs exhibited elevated copy numbers across 14 mitochondrial genes when normalized to equal EV input (fig. S5J). Consistently, agarose gel electrophoresis of EV-derived DNA extracted from equal numbers of murine CD-EVs and HFD-EVs revealed stronger long-range mtDNA amplification signals in HFD-EVs (Fig. 4L). Similarly, under equal EV particle input, human obese-EVs showed more intense mtDNA bands than nonobese-EVs (Fig. 4M). These findings support the conclusion that obesity increases AT-EV output and enhances mitochondrial cargo abundance, suggesting that EV-Mito may act as vectors of metabolic intercellular communication in obesity-associated pathologies.

mtDNA in adipocyte-derived EV-Mito drives chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction, senescence, and OA progression

To pinpoint the EV-Mito cargo driving chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction and senescence, we selectively depleted mtDNA or proteins from HFD-EVs by treating them with DNase I (1 U/ml) with low-dose saponin-mediated reversible permeabilization or with proteinase K (0.1 mg/ml) with low-dose saponin, respectively, following validated protocols (36, 37). TEM confirmed that the vesicular morphology remained intact after enzymatic treatment (fig. S6A), and NTA showed a comparable size distribution of EVs following processing (fig. S6B and movies S4 to S6). Quantitative analyses further verified the efficacy of proteinase K in depleting EV-associated proteins and of DNase I in eliminating mtDNA cargo (fig. S6, C to E). These results indicate that the enzymatic depletion procedure effectively reduced the targeted cargo while largely preserving gross vesicular morphology and size distribution. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that mtDNA-depleted HFD-EVs significantly restored cartilage matrix marker expression, as evidenced by increased expression of Col2 and ACAN, and reduced levels of the catabolic enzyme MMP13 and senescence markers P16 and P21 compared to untreated HFD-EVs (fig. S6, F and G). In contrast, depletion of EV-associated protein cargo did not significantly alter these markers, indicating that protein-depleted HFD-EVs retain their pathological effects (fig. S6, F and G). Together, these findings support mtDNA as a major functional component contributing to the pathological effects of EV-Mito. Furthermore, the sustained bioactivity of protein-depleted EVs implies that mtDNA may act via noncanonical, protein-independent pathways to induce metabolic and senescent changes in joint cartilage.

To examine whether this mtDNA-dependent effect was also present in human OA, EVs were isolated from the IFP of donors with obesity, subjected to enzymatic cargo depletion, and incubated with human OA chondrocytes. RT-qPCR analysis showed that mtDNA-depleted obese-EVs significantly increased the expression of anabolic genes Col2 and ACAN, while reducing MMP13 expression compared to intact obese-EVs (Fig. 5A). WB quantification corroborated these findings at the protein level (Fig. 5, B and C). Notably, depletion of protein cargo from obese-EVs did not significantly alter marker expression compared with intact obese-EVs. This cross-species concordance in murine and human chondrocyte systems supports mtDNA cargo as a conserved functional contributor to obesity-associated EV-Mito–induced cartilage pathology.

To investigate the pathological role of mtDNA in vivo, HFD-EVs selectively depleted of either mtDNA or proteins were intra-articularly injected into the knee joints of DMM model mice (Fig. 5D). Histological evaluation using H&E and SO/FG staining revealed that mtDNA-depleted HFD-EVs significantly reduced cartilage degradation, as indicated by lower Mankin and OARSI scores compared to intact HFD-EV controls (Fig. 5, E and F). Immunohistochemical analysis showed increased expression of Col2 and ACAN and reduced MMP13 levels in cartilage treated with mtDNA-depleted EVs (Fig. 5, G to I). In contrast, protein-depleted HFD-EVs did not mitigate cartilage damage and displayed scores and marker expression levels comparable to those observed in the intact HFD-EV group (Fig. 5, E to I). These in vivo findings corroborate our in vitro results and further support mtDNA cargo, rather than EV protein cargo, as a major pathogenic component of HFD-EVs that contributes to cartilage degeneration and OA progression.

To determine whether mtDNA serves as the bioactive cargo responsible for the pathogenic effects of HFD-EVs and to exclude potential off-target effects of DNase I treatment, purified mtDNA isolated from HFD-EVs was either administered as free mtDNA or loaded into CD-EVs by electroporation to generate mtDNA-loaded EVs (EVs-mtDNA), and their effects on cartilage degeneration and chondrocyte dysfunction were examined (Fig. 5J). Quantitative analyses confirmed that electroporation did not reduce the protein cargo of EVs and successfully introduced mtDNA into the vesicles (fig. S7, A and B). Histological evaluation showed that administration of purified mtDNA alone did not alter cartilage integrity compared with controls (Fig. 5, K and L). In contrast, intra-articular delivery of EVs-mtDNA resulted in increased Mankin and OARSI scores, reduced Col2 and ACAN expression, and elevated MMP13 levels (Fig. 5, K and L). Consistently, in vitro experiments using mouse chondrocytes demonstrated that treatment with purified free mtDNA did not induce degenerative changes. In contrast, EVs-mtDNA decreased Col2 and ACAN expression while increasing MMP13, P16, and P21 levels, indicating that EV-mediated mtDNA delivery was sufficient to reproduce the catabolic and senescence-associated changes induced by HFD-EVs (fig. S7, C and D). These results indicate that mtDNA functions as the principal bioactive cargo of HFD-EVs and exerts pathogenic effects only when encapsulated within EVs, whereas free mtDNA alone fails to induce cartilage degeneration, likely due to its inability to penetrate cartilage without EV-mediated transport.

To further determine whether mtDNA transfer to recipient chondrocytes depends on EV encapsulation, we used a xenogeneic incubation system (Fig. 5M). Human-derived EV-Mito incubated with murine chondrocytes resulted in the detection of human mtDNA in recipient cells, as confirmed by species-specific qPCR. Notably, free human mtDNA failed to enter murine chondrocytes, whereas EVs-mtDNA led to detectable human mtDNA signals in recipient murine chondrocytes (Fig. 5N). Conversely, incubation of murine EV-Mito with human chondrocytes led to the detection of murine mtDNA within recipient cells. Similarly, purified murine mtDNA alone was not detectably recovered from human chondrocytes, whereas EVs-mtDNA resulted in detectable murine mtDNA signals in recipient human chondrocytes (Fig. 5O). To further validate these observations, endogenous mtDNA in chondrocytes was depleted using ethidium bromide (EtBr, 100 ng/ml) (fig. S7E). In EtBr-pretreated chondrocytes, mtDNA signals were markedly reduced, whereas mtDNA signals became detectable after EV-Mito treatment, and EVs-mtDNA, but not free mtDNA, produced detectable mtDNA signals in recipient cells (fig. S7, F and G). Together, these cross-species and mtDNA rescue experiments demonstrate that EVs serve as essential carriers mediating the intercellular transfer of mtDNA, thereby enabling its active internalization by chondrocytes.

Adipocyte-derived EV-Mito delivers mtDNA to chondrocytes, activating the cGAS-STING pathway

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which mtDNA delivered via adipocyte-derived EV-Mito exacerbates chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction, we focused on the cytosolic DNA-sensing cGAS-STING signaling pathway. As a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), mtDNA can be sensed by cGAS, which catalyzes the production of the second messenger cGAMP upon binding to cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) (38, 39). This, in turn, activates STING, initiating phosphorylation of downstream effectors TBK1, IRF3, and NF-κB p65, ultimately promoting the production of type I interferons (IFN-I) and proinflammatory cytokines (40, 41). Transcriptomic analysis of articular cartilage from 23 patients with OA (3 individuals without obesity and 20 individuals with obesity) revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between the two groups (Fig. 6, A and B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated enrichment of cytosolic DNA sensing, NF-κB signaling, inflammatory pathways, IFN-α/γ signaling, and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) signaling in the group with obesity, supporting the involvement of a cGAS-STING–associated inflammatory program (Fig. 6, C and D, and fig. S8A). WB analysis showed that human chondrocytes treated with obese-EVs exhibited elevated cGAS expression and increased STING phosphorylation. In contrast, treatment with mtDNA-depleted obese-EVs largely attenuated these responses (Fig. 6, E and F). Moreover, cGAS expression in human chondrocytes was silenced with small interfering RNA (siRNA), and qPCR analysis verified the knockdown efficiency (Fig. 6G). siRNA-mediated suppression of cGAS effectively attenuated obese-EV–induced STING activation and markedly reduced the phosphorylation of downstream effectors TBK1, IRF3, and NF-κB p65 compared with scrambled siRNA controls (Fig. 6, H to L).

We further quantified cGAMP levels, a key effector of the cGAS-STING pathway, in synovial fluid and cartilage tissues. We found significantly higher concentrations in samples from individuals with obesity than in individuals without obesity (Fig. 6M). Consistently, the mRNA levels of IFN-β, IL-6, and CXCL10 were markedly increased in the group with obesity (Fig. 6N), supporting enhanced cGAS-STING–associated inflammatory signaling in obesity-associated OA samples. These findings support the conclusion that mtDNA cargo delivered by obesity-associated EV-Mito contributes to cGAS-STING activation in human chondrocytes and may thereby promote inflammatory cartilage degeneration.

To determine whether this mechanism is conserved across species, primary murine chondrocytes were treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), intact HFD-EVs, mtDNA-depleted HFD-EVs, or protein-depleted HFD-EVs. Immunofluorescence staining showed marked increases in phosphorylated STING and TBK1 signals in chondrocytes treated with intact or protein-depleted HFD-EVs, accompanied by nuclear translocation of IRF3 and NF-κB p65, and elevated expression of IFN-β and IL-6 (fig. S8, B to D). In contrast, mtDNA-depleted HFD-EVs markedly attenuated STING/TBK1 activation and downstream inflammatory responses (fig. S8, B to D).

These results support mtDNA as a major functional cargo within EV-Mito that contributes to activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in both human and murine chondrocytes. This conserved response across human and murine chondrocyte systems may contribute to obesity-associated cartilage inflammation and highlights the pathological role of adipocyte-derived EV-Mito in OA progression.

Targeting the cGAS-STING pathway and reducing body fat alleviate obesity-associated OA

To determine whether the cGAS-STING pathway mediates the pathological effects of EV-Mito, human chondrocytes treated with obese-EVs were subjected to cGAS, STING, or NF-κB inhibition via siRNA against cGAS (si-cGAS), the STING inhibitor (H-151), or the NF-κB inhibitor ammonium pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate (PDTC). WB analysis showed that all treatments attenuated the obese-EV–induced reduction in the cartilage matrix proteins Col2 and ACAN and suppressed the up-regulation of MMP13 (fig. S9, A and B). Similarly, murine chondrocytes treated with HFD-EVs exhibited restored Col2 and ACAN expression and reduced levels of MMP13, P16, and P21 following treatment with si-cGAS, H-151, or PDTC (fig. S9, C and D). These data support the conclusion that the cGAS-STING–NF-κB signaling axis contributes to EV-Mito–induced chondrocyte catabolic and senescence-associated changes, and that siRNA-mediated or pharmacological inhibition of this pathway attenuates these effects.

To validate these findings in vivo, both pharmacological and genetic approaches to STING inhibition were used. HFD-induced obese mice received either the STING inhibitor H-151 or cartilage-specific deletion of STING in Col2a1-CreERT2; STINGfl/fl mice, referred to as STING cKO mice, followed by DMM surgery (Fig. 7A and fig. S10A). Successful deletion of STING in cartilage tissues from STING cKO mice was confirmed by WB analysis (fig. S10, B and C). Histological staining with H&E and SO/FG revealed that both H-151 treatment and STING cKO improved cartilage integrity and surface smoothness, as reflected by significantly reduced Mankin and OARSI scores compared with controls (Fig. 7, B and C). Immunohistochemistry further showed increased Col2 and ACAN expression and decreased MMP13 levels in both intervention groups (Fig. 7, B and C). Micro-CT analysis showed reduced subchondral BV/TV, Tb.Th, and osteophyte formation in H-151–treated and STING cKO mice (Fig. 7, B and C). Together, these in vivo results indicate that both pharmacological and genetic blockade of STING effectively mitigates OA progression in obese mice with DMM-induced OA, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting the cGAS-STING pathway.

Given that gait parameters can provide functional readouts in murine OA models (42), we next assessed whether inhibition of STING could improve locomotor performance in obese mice with DMM-induced OA. Quantitative gait analysis showed that both H-151 treatment and STING cKO significantly increased the operated-to-contralateral hindlimb ratios of duty cycle, mean intensity, and print area, while pain behavior assessments showed increased paw withdrawal thresholds (fig. S10, D and E). These multimodal analyses indicate that pharmacological and genetic inhibition of STING not only ameliorates joint pathology but also improves locomotor function and alleviates pain, supporting a functional contribution of STING signaling to obesity-associated OA progression.

Building upon these mechanistic insights, we evaluated the clinical relevance of adiposity to OA prevalence by analyzing the relationship between body fat percentage (BFP) and OA prevalence using data from 23,431 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (Fig. 7D). Higher BFP was associated with higher body mass index (BMI) values on average, although between-group overlap suggested substantial heterogeneity (Fig. 7E). To control for BMI, OA prevalence was compared across BFP groups with comparable BMI levels. While BMI did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 7F), OA prevalence increased with higher BFP (Fig. 7G), suggesting an association that was not fully explained by BMI. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, adjusted for BMI and age, further showed a nonlinear association between BFP and OA prevalence, with OA odds increasing in the higher BFP range (Fig. 7H). These findings indicate that BFP may capture adiposity-related OA associations beyond BMI alone, supporting the clinical relevance of adipose tissue burden in OA.

To translate these findings into intervention strategies, we conducted a randomized controlled trial in a Chinese population to evaluate the impact of targeted fat-loss interventions on OA outcomes. A total of 60 individuals with obesity were assigned to a control group, moderate-intensity training (MIT), or HIIT, of whom 51 completed the trial (Fig. 7I). Gait parameters were measured using a Vicon 3D motion capture system before and after intervention (Fig. 7J). Baseline characteristics, including BMI, BFP, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores, and gait metrics, were comparable across groups (table S4). Postintervention, both exercise groups exhibited significant reductions in BMI and BFP, with HIIT producing a more pronounced decrease in BFP than MIT (Fig. 7K), indicating greater adiposity reduction. To further determine whether fat-loss interventions were associated with changes in synovial EV populations enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, we isolated synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers from knee synovial fluid using a previously reported approach (43, 44). These enriched EV fractions were characterized by TEM (fig. S10F) and NTA (fig. S10G), while nano-flow cytometry (nFCM) analysis detected canonical EV markers CD9, CD63, and TSG101, together with adipose-associated EV markers FABP4 and adiponectin (fig. S10H). Postenrichment nFCM quantification further showed that more than 90% of detected EV events in the enriched fractions were positive for FABP4 or adiponectin across the three participant groups (fig. S10I). Postintervention levels of synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers and mtDNA abundance in these fractions were lower in the exercise groups than in the control group, with the lowest levels observed in the HIIT group (Fig. 7L). Postintervention synovial fluid cGAMP levels were also lower in the exercise groups, with the lowest levels observed after HIIT (Fig. 7M). Clinically, postintervention analyses showed improved WOMAC scores in both exercise groups, with HIIT producing the greatest functional recovery. Gait analysis further revealed increased step cadence in both the MIT and HIIT groups. Single-limb support time, a parameter reflecting dynamic balance and stability, was also markedly prolonged in the HIIT group compared with both the MIT and control groups (fig. S10J).

Collectively, these findings indicate that fat-targeted exercise, particularly HIIT, is associated with lower synovial cGAS-STING pathway activity, reduced mtDNA abundance within synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, and improved clinical and gait outcomes.

DISCUSSION

OA has long been associated with obesity, yet the molecular mechanisms linking excess adiposity to articular cartilage degeneration remain incompletely understood (45, 46). In this study, we identify adipocyte-derived EV-Mito as a previously unappreciated mechanistic link between obesity-associated adipose dysfunction and OA progression. We demonstrate that EV-Mito from obese adipose tissue delivers mtDNA into chondrocytes, where it acts as a DAMP, activating the cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS and its downstream effector STING. This, in turn, drives a cascade of proinflammatory signaling and catabolic reprogramming that culminates in cartilage matrix breakdown, cellular senescence, and OA pathogenesis. Notably, blockade of cGAS-STING signaling or reduction of body fat content markedly attenuates these pathological effects, offering translational insights into potential therapeutic strategies (Fig. 8).

Schematic overview of cross-talk between adipose tissue and cartilage via EV-Mito in obesity-associated OA.

Obesity increases the release of adipose tissue–derived EV-Mito, which deliver mtDNA to chondrocytes and trigger cGAS-STING activation. This signaling cascade induces TBK1 phosphorylation and downstream activation of IRF3 and NF-κB, leading to elevated expression of type I interferons and IL-6, thereby promoting chondrocyte dysfunction and accelerating OA progression. Experimental interventions targeting this axis, including GW4869 and AAV9-sh-Rab27a to reduce AT-EV release, si-cGAS, the STING inhibitor H-151, cartilage-specific STING knockout, and the NF-κB inhibitor PDTC, attenuate this inflammatory axis. In parallel, HIIT markedly reduced adiposity and was associated with lower mtDNA abundance in synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, lower synovial cGAMP levels, and improved OA-related outcomes. Collectively, these findings support a mechanistic link between obesity and OA, highlighting EV-Mito–mediated mtDNA signaling as a promising therapeutic target.

Our findings support a pathogenic contribution of obesity-associated adipose tissue to OA progression. MR analysis provides genetic evidence consistent with a causal link between obesity and OA. In parallel, mouse experiments, particularly adipose tissue transplantation, indicate that obesity-associated adipose tissue is sufficient to aggravate cartilage metabolic dysfunction and OA progression under the tested conditions. Using in vivo genetic tracing and in vitro uptake assays, we further show that chondrocytes efficiently internalize AT-EVs. These findings highlight the role of AT-EVs as a class of signaling molecules in the cross-talk between adipose tissue and cartilage.

Multiple adipose depots maintain direct anatomical contact with articular cartilage, supporting the possibility of paracrine AT-EV transfer via synovial fluid (47, 48). In addition, vascular channels in the subchondral region may provide a route for more systemic delivery (49). Synovial fluid originates from plasma ultrafiltration through the synovial capillary network and is further enriched by synoviocyte-derived components (50). The subchondral bone contains microvascular networks (microarteries, venules, and nerve branches) that provide partial nutritional support and facilitate waste removal from overlying cartilage (51, 52). Consistent with this anatomical possibility, in our model, adipose-derived EV and mitochondrial signals were increased in OA cartilage and were reduced by blocking adipose EV release. One possible explanation is that, in addition to increased EV release from obese adipose tissue, diseased cartilage may also permit greater accumulation of nanoscale vesicles. This interpretation is indirectly supported by prior studies showing that nanoparticles preferentially diffused through degraded, ACAN-depleted cartilage explants and that OA altered intra-articular biodistribution, increasing cartilage accumulation relative to healthy joints (53, 54). Together, these anatomical and pathological features provide a plausible basis for adipose-cartilage EV trafficking in vivo.

Emerging evidence suggests that an HFD primarily induces adipocyte metabolic dysfunction, thereby triggering the secretion of EVs that have been shown to have detrimental effects on the cerebral, vascular, and intestinal systems (55). However, whether such obesity-remodeled EVs directly mediate articular cartilage degeneration has remained unclear. Across both in vitro and in vivo models, obesity-associated AT-EVs consistently exacerbated chondrocyte metabolic dysfunction, senescence, and OA-related pathology, whereas inhibition of AT-EV biogenesis attenuated these effects, supporting a functional role for AT-EVs in metabolic OA.

An important advance of the present study is not merely the observation that extracellular mitochondrial components can be inflammatory, because extracellular mitochondria-associated inflammatory signaling has already been reported in other contexts (56, 57). Rather, our study identifies an obesity-associated adipose-to-cartilage EV-Mito trafficking axis. Both in vitro tracing and in vivo reporter experiments support the transfer of adipocyte-derived mitochondrial cargo to chondrocytes, primarily through clathrin-dependent endocytosis. This aligns with recent evidence that HFD-induced obesity triggers mitochondrial fragmentation in adipose tissue (58) and that selective packaging of mitochondrial components into EVs is an adaptive response to metabolic stress (59). Emerging evidence suggests that HFD-induced alterations in the molecular composition of EVs, encompassing DNA, RNA (including noncoding RNAs), proteins, and lipids, exert systemic modulatory effects on metabolic pathways within target organs and tissues. We identified mtDNA as the key functional component within EV-Mito that drives chondrocyte metabolic disorder and senescence. Depletion of mtDNA from EV-Mito restored cartilage matrix synthesis and suppressed catabolic and senescence markers in both mouse and human chondrocytes, whereas protein depletion had only minimal effects. In vivo, mtDNA-depleted EV-Mito also attenuated cartilage degeneration. This depletion strategy should be interpreted with caution, because DNase I plus saponin treatment may not be completely selective and cannot fully exclude unintended effects on other EV-associated components or procedural carryover. Nevertheless, the complementary electroporation and free-mtDNA control experiments consistently support mtDNA as an important contributor to the pathological effects of obesity-associated EV-Mito. Together, these findings suggest that AT-EV–resident mitochondrial material may represent a biologically meaningful pathogenic signal linking obese adipose tissue to chondrocyte dysfunction and OA progression. The significance of this transfer therefore lies not merely in the movement of mitochondrial material itself, but in identifying a mechanistically plausible route through which obesity-associated adipose dysfunction contributes to cartilage degeneration.

Mechanistically, our study extends rather than overturns existing models of obesity-associated OA. Previous work has shown that metabolic abnormalities can aggravate OA beyond mechanical loading alone (60, 61) and that mechanical overload can promote extracellular mitochondrial particle release and inflammation in musculoskeletal tissues (57). In this context, the advance of the present study lies in identifying a more specific obesity-associated adipose-to-cartilage pathway, in which adipose-derived EV-Mito transfer mtDNA to chondrocytes and engage cGAS-STING signaling. Our data further place adipose-derived EV-Mito–delivered mtDNA upstream of cGAS-STING activation in chondrocytes, thereby providing a molecular route through which obesity-associated adipose dysfunction may drive sterile inflammatory responses in cartilage. Although our transplantation and EV-transfer experiments support a causal contribution of adipose-derived factors, they do not directly exclude simultaneous contributions from altered mechanical loading in obesity. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as identifying a previously underappreciated metabolic signaling axis that may act alongside, rather than in place of, biomechanical effects. Thus, rather than replacing prior metabolic or mechanical models of OA, our study refines them by defining an adipose-derived EV-Mito–mtDNA–cGAS-STING signaling axis that may operate in parallel with biomechanical stress. This mechanistic framework is also consistent with our human data, in which adiposity was more closely associated with OA risk than body weight alone, and adiposity-targeted intervention was accompanied by lower synovial cGAMP levels, reduced mtDNA abundance within synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, and improved OA outcomes. However, in the human exercise cohort, synovial EV enrichment was based on immunocapture of adipose-associated EV markers rather than direct isolation of EVs from participant-matched adipose tissue. Therefore, this part of the human EV analysis should be interpreted as supportive translational evidence from these marker-enriched synovial EV fractions, rather than as definitive lineage-tracing proof of adipose tissue–derived EV-Mito trafficking in humans. Together, these findings support adiposity-targeted strategies as a translationally relevant approach for obesity-associated OA.

While our investigation focused on delineating cartilage-centric mechanisms underlying OA pathogenesis, several important questions remain unresolved. First, the tissue specificity of EV-Mito enrichment has not been fully characterized. Although our data emphasize cartilage involvement, we cannot exclude the possibility that EV-Mito also affects adjacent joint tissues, such as the subchondral bone, synovium, or periarticular musculature, thereby contributing to OA progression through indirect or paracrine cross-talk mechanisms. Second, the functional heterogeneity among EV subtypes, including exosomes, apoptotic bodies, and microvesicles in adipose-cartilage communication, remains incompletely understood. Our isolation strategy was optimized for small EVs (<200 nm), which may have excluded larger vesicular populations that could carry distinct bioactive cargo relevant to metabolic regulation and intercellular signaling. Moreover, because no second UC PBS-washing step was included during AT-EV isolation, the potential influence on sample purity cannot be completely ruled out. Although the overall characterization results, including broader-field TEM, NTA, and EV marker analysis, supported EV enrichment, this point should still be considered when interpreting the data. In addition, although the in vitro and in vivo EV dosing regimens used in this study were selected based on preliminary optimization and previously reported protocols, their precise physiological relevance remains to be further established. Last, the intracellular fate of internalized mtDNA within chondrocytes represents a key unanswered question. Whether this exogenous mtDNA is transcribed into functional mitochondrial RNA, potentially amplifying inflammatory responses or altering cellular metabolism, remains to be determined.

Collectively, our findings refine current models of obesity-associated OA by showing that obesity may contribute to joint degeneration not only through altered joint loading, but also through a specific adipose-derived EV-Mito–mtDNA–cGAS-STING signaling route. The identification of this axis provides a mechanistic framework for adiposity-targeted and pathway-directed interventions in obesity-associated OA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study was designed to investigate whether adipocyte-derived EV-Mito mediates obesity-associated OA by transferring mtDNA to chondrocytes and activating the cGAS-STING pathway, and to evaluate the translational relevance of this axis at the population and interventional levels. Male C57BL/6 mice were fed either a CD or an HFD to induce metabolic obesity, followed by DMM surgery to model injury-accelerated OA and to permit longitudinal histological, micro-CT, and behavioral assessments of cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone remodeling; to examine adipose-specific contributions under conditions minimizing body weight–related mechanical confounding, adipose tissue transplantation experiments were conducted in adipose-depleted recipients in conjunction with DMM, and OA outcomes were compared. Adipocyte-derived EVs were isolated from adipose depots by optimized differential UC, characterized by TEM, NTA, and canonical EV markers, and traced in vivo using an adipoq-CreERT2; CD63-emGFP reporter system to visualize AT-EV transfer to cartilage after DMM. The in vivo pathogenicity of AT-EVs was tested by intra-articular injection of CD-EVs, HFD-EVs, or modified EVs into DMM mice, and the contribution of adipose EV secretion was evaluated by local GW4869 administration and AAV9-sh-Rab27a knockdown of adipose Rab27a. Primary murine chondrocytes and human OA chondrocytes were exposed to EVs from lean or obese adipose tissue as well as to DNase I– or proteinase K–treated or mtDNA-depleted EV preparations to determine cargo dependency, and downstream effects on matrix proteins, catabolic enzymes, senescence markers, and cGAS-STING signaling were assessed by qPCR, immunoblotting, and immunohistochemistry; pathway necessity was interrogated using siRNA/genetic loss or pharmacologic inhibitors (including H-151) and by conditional genetic models where available. To address clinical and population relevance, an NHANES cross-sectional analysis was performed to examine the association between BFP and OA prevalence using RCS regression, and a controlled exercise intervention cohort comparing HIIT versus moderate exercise was conducted to assess changes in adiposity, postintervention abundance of synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers, synovial cGAMP levels, and clinical and functional OA-related outcomes.

Participants

Cohort 1: Fifty-three participants undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for end-stage knee OA (Kellgren-Lawrence grade IV) were prospectively enrolled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The cohort consisted of 30 individuals without obesity (BMI < 28 kg/m2) and 23 individuals with obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2). Discarded adipose tissue, articular cartilage, and synovial fluid samples were intraoperatively collected for subsequent in vitro analyses. Inclusion required the following: (i) radiographically confirmed unilateral knee OA; (ii) absence of corticosteroid therapy within 3 months; (iii) no history of autoimmune diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus) or malignancies; and (iv) no active infections. Exclusion criteria included bilateral joint involvement or prior intra-articular interventions. In addition, knee cartilage tissues were obtained from three amputee patients who underwent limb resection due to extra-articular malignancies, serving as supplementary controls.

Cohort 2: We conducted a 12-week randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effects of different exercise modalities on metabolic and musculoskeletal outcomes in individuals with obesity-related knee OA. Eligible participants were aged 55 to 70 years, had clinically diagnosed knee OA of the index knee with radiographic evidence of OA based on Kellgren-Lawrence grading, and met the obesity criteria of BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2 and BFP ≥ 30%. Inclusion criteria also required the absence of major neurological, cardiovascular, respiratory, or psychiatric disorders; successful completion of pre-exercise safety screening for moderate-to-high-intensity exercise; ability to complete biological sample collection, exercise intervention, and follow-up assessments; and provision of written informed consent after understanding the purpose and procedures of the study. Exclusion criteria included autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus; glucocorticoid therapy, intra-articular injection, or other treatments that could affect study outcomes within 3 months before enrollment; severe cardiovascular, neurological, respiratory, or psychiatric disorders that precluded exercise participation; cognitive impairment, poor compliance, or inability to complete study procedures or follow-up; active infection or active malignancy; recent major joint surgery; and any other condition judged by the investigators to make participation inappropriate.

A total of 60 eligible participants were randomly assigned (1:1:1) to one of three groups using computer-generated random numbers: (i) nonexercise control group (C group), (ii) moderate-intensity continuous training group (MIT, M group), and (iii) high-intensity interval training group (HIIT, H group). Randomization was stratified by sex to ensure balanced distribution. Allocation was concealed using sealed opaque envelopes prepared by an independent researcher. Nine participants withdrew due to personal reasons or noncompliance, resulting in 51 participants completing the trial (C: n = 19; M: n = 15; H: n = 17). The intervention analyses reported here were conducted in the completer population, and reasons for withdrawal were recorded.

Participants in the control group maintained their habitual lifestyle without additional exercise. The MIT group performed continuous cycling on a stationary ergometer (Monark Exercise AB, Vansbro, Sweden) for 30 min at an intensity of 60 to 75% of their peak heart rate (HR_peak). The HIIT protocol consisted of four 4-min bouts at 85 to 95% HR_peak, interspersed with three 3-min recovery periods at 60 to 70% HR_peak, for a total of approximately 25 min per session. Sessions were conducted under the direct supervision of certified exercise physiologists three times per week. Heart rate was continuously monitored via chest straps (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland), and total energy expenditure was matched between MIT and HIIT sessions to ensure comparable exercise workload. Exercise adherence was defined as completion of ≥90% of scheduled sessions.

Anthropometric measurements, including height and body mass, were obtained using a wall-mounted stadiometer and a calibrated digital scale, respectively. Whole-body composition was assessed by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA). Knee function was evaluated using the WOMAC questionnaire. For gait analysis, participants were equipped with a standard 16-marker Plug-in Gait set and walked barefoot at a self-selected pace along a 10-m walkway. Motion capture was performed using a six-camera Vicon system (Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK), synchronized with embedded force plates. Marker trajectories were filtered in Vicon Nexus, and joint kinematics and spatiotemporal parameters were calculated using the Plug-in Gait model.

Synovial fluid was aspirated from the suprapatellar pouch of the index knee using a 22-gauge needle under aseptic conditions by an experienced orthopedic clinician after the 12-week intervention. To reduce viscosity, samples were treated with hyaluronidase at 37°C for 30 min immediately after collection. The samples were then centrifuged at 3000g for 15 min at 4°C to remove cells and debris, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Cell culture

For isolation and culture of primary chondrocytes, human articular cartilage tissues were aseptically harvested from surgical waste. Tissue specimens were minced into 1- to 2-mm3 fragments and subjected to sequential enzymatic digestion with 0.25% trypsin (37°C, 30 min, catalog no. 27250018, Gibco) followed by 0.2% type II collagenase (catalog no. 17101015, Gibco) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12; catalog no. 11320082, Gibco) at 37°C for 6 to 8 hours. Isolated chondrocytes were pelleted by centrifugation at 1200g for 10 min, then resuspended in complete medium composed of DMEM/F-12 (catalog no. 11320082, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog no. 10091148, Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (catalog no. 15140148, Gibco), and l-ascorbic acid (50 μg/ml; catalog no. A7506, Sigma-Aldrich), and cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Murine primary chondrocytes were similarly isolated from the knee joint cartilage of 4-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, with procedural adaptations for smaller tissue volumes. The chondrocyte phenotype was validated using SO and Toluidine Blue staining. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; catalog no. BL539A, Biosharp), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (catalog no. CT11451, Coolaber), and stained with 0.1% SO for proteoglycan detection or 0.04% Toluidine Blue for sulfated glycosaminoglycan visualization. Images were acquired using bright-field microscopy. For the culture of murine adipocytes, subcutaneous adipose tissue was excised from the inguinal region of euthanized mice, minced, and digested with collagenase D (1.5 U/ml) and Dispase II (2.5 U/ml) at 37°C for 40 min. The digest was filtered through a 100-μm mesh, centrifuged at 500g for 5 min, and the adipocyte-enriched supernatant layer was collected. Purified adipocytes were then resuspended and maintained in DMEM/F-12 (catalog no. 11320082, Gibco) containing 10% FBS (catalog no. 10091148, Gibco) pre-depleted of bovine EVs by UC at 120,000g for 18 hours at 4°C, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (catalog no. 15140148, Gibco) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For in vitro coincubation experiments, chondrocytes were incubated with AT-EVs at 1.0 × 109 particles/ml for 24 hours at 37°C. The concentration was determined based on preliminary experiments and published particle-based EV coincubation protocols in chondrocyte-related studies (62, 63).

Experimental OA in mice

DMM surgery: On the day of surgery, mice were weighed and anesthetized with 4% isoflurane in 0.8 liters/min oxygen for induction, followed by maintenance anesthesia with 2% isoflurane in 0.8 liters/min oxygen delivered via a nose cone. The right knee joint was shaved and disinfected using povidone-iodine. Under sterile conditions and microscopic guidance, a medial parapatellar arthrotomy was performed to expose the medial meniscotibial ligament. The medial meniscotibial ligament was transected using microscissors to destabilize the medial meniscus and induce joint instability. For sham surgery, the medial meniscotibial ligament was exposed but not transected. Layered closure of the joint capsule, subcutaneous tissue, and skin was performed using 6-0 absorbable sutures (Ethicon Vicryl, USA).

MR study

The data used in this study were derived from published genome-wide association studies. A two-sample MR approach was used to estimate the causal effect of obesity on knee OA. The primary analytical method was the IVW approach, supplemented by the MR-Egger regression method and the weighted median method, to assess the causal relationship between genetic variants associated with obesity and knee OA. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected based on stringent criteria: a significance threshold of P < 5.0 × 10−8 for association with the exposure, a linkage disequilibrium threshold of r2 < 0.001, and a clumping window of 10,000 kb. The MR-Egger intercept test was performed to assess potential horizontal pleiotropy. Last, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine whether any individual SNP contributed to the combined IVW estimates.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

Mouse articular and adipose tissues were fixed in 4% PFA (catalog no. BL539A, Biosharp). Articular tissues underwent EDTA (catalog no. G1105, Servicebio) decalcification for several weeks before paraffin embedding. During decalcification, the EDTA solution was replaced with fresh solution every 2 to 3 days until the bone tissues were sufficiently softened for sectioning. Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared and stained with H&E (catalog no. C0105S, Beyotime) to assess general tissue morphology. Cartilage integrity was evaluated using SO counterstained with FG (catalog no. G1053, Servicebio). For immunohistochemistry, tissue sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (catalog no. ZLI-9311, ZSGB-BIO) to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed using 0.1% trypsin (catalog no. 27250018, Gibco) for 2 hours at 37°C. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, catalog no. AM2618, Invitrogen) for 1 hour at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following day, sections were incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min and detected using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, P0203, Beyotime) substrate. Histological analysis was performed using a bright-field microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) to capture images of the tissue samples. Cartilage degeneration was evaluated using the Mankin and OARSI scoring systems based on H&E- and SO/FG-stained sections. All histological assessments were independently performed by two experienced investigators who were blinded to group allocation. The final score for each sample was calculated as the mean of the two individual scores, and in cases of discrepancy, the observers jointly reviewed the slides to reach a consensus. Immunohistochemical staining was further quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, USA) to assess relative protein expression levels.

Micro-CT analysis

Knee joint specimens were scanned using a high-resolution micro-CT system (Inveon, Siemens, Germany) with the following settings: 80 kV x-ray voltage, 500 μA current, 9.56 μm voxel resolution, 360° rotational acquisition, and 3500 ms exposure time. Adipose tissue transplantation

The adipose tissue transplantation procedure was performed according to established protocols (64, 65), with minor modifications. Inguinal WAT (iWAT) was harvested from either HFD-fed or CD-fed donor mice, trimmed of connective tissue, and cut into approximately 200-mg fragments. The grafts were briefly maintained in sterile saline for less than 5 min to preserve tissue viability. Recipient mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% for induction, 2% for maintenance) and received perioperative buprenorphine analgesia (0.1 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). Recipient mice were then assigned to sham surgery without adipose tissue transplantation, transplantation with CD-derived WAT, or transplantation with HFD-derived WAT. For transplantation groups, a total of 1 g of donor adipose fragments was implanted into the corresponding inguinal fat compartments. The incision was closed with absorbable sutures, and graft viability was verified by gross inspection and mass retention (80 to 90% of the initial weight) at the time of harvest.

Glucose tolerance test and insulin tolerance test

For glucose tolerance test, mice were fasted for 16 hours with ad libitum access to water. After measuring baseline blood glucose levels, an intraperitoneal injection of insulin at 0.70 U/kg body weight was administered. Isolation and characterization of AT-EVs

Adipose tissue from human IFP and inguinal depots of mice was dissected, washed three times in PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio), and minced into small tissue fragments under sterile conditions. Tissue fragments were precultured for 1 hour in complete medium consisting of α-MEM (catalog no. 12571071, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (catalog no. 10091148, Gibco) that had been depleted of bovine EVs by UC at 120,000g for 18 hours at 4°C (66), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (catalog no. 15140148, Gibco), then washed with PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio) and centrifuged at 300g for 10 min. After washing, adipose tissue fragments were transferred into fresh EV-depleted complete medium and cultured for 16 hours to allow EV release into the conditioned medium. The conditioned medium was first filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer (catalog no. 431751, Corning) and centrifuged at 300g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a second 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 2000g for 20 min, followed by filtration through a 0.22-μm syringe filter (catalog no. SLGP033RS, Millipore), then concentrated using 100-kDa ultrafiltration centrifugal units (catalog no. UFC810024, Millipore) by centrifugation at 3500g for 30 min at 4°C. Differential UC of the concentrate was performed using an ultracentrifuge (Beckman, Brea, USA) as follows: 300g, 10 min; 2000g, 10 min; 10,000g, 30 min; and 100,000g, 2 hours. The EV-enriched pellet was resuspended in PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio), yielding human and mouse AT-EVs. Aliquots were stored at −80°C until use. EVs were characterized using NTA, TEM, and Western blotting to assess particle size distribution, morphology, and EV marker expression. The size distribution of EVs was determined using an NTA instrument (NanoSight NS300, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Particle concentration and size distribution were calculated by tracking the Brownian motion of individual particles in suspension.

AT-EV proteomic profiling via LC-MS/MS

AT-EVs from CD- or HFD-fed mice underwent sequential reduction and alkylation using 5 mM dithiothreitol and 10 mM iodoacetamide under the indicated incubation conditions. Proteins were precipitated by acetone precipitation (6:1 v/v) at −20°C overnight, followed by centrifugation at 8000g for 10 min at 4°C. Pelleted proteins were reconstituted in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer and subjected to tryptic digestion (1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio) at 37°C for 16 hours. Digested peptides were desalted using solid-phase extraction columns, lyophilized, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Easy-nLC 1000 system coupled to a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an electrospray ionization source. Peptides were loaded onto a trap column (Acclaim PepMap C18, 100 μm by 2 cm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 10 μl/min, followed by analytical separation on a column (75 μm by 15 cm) using a 120-min linear gradient from 4 to 80% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. MS spectra were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode with HCD fragmentation (normalized collision energy: 28%). Raw MS/MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with the SEQUEST HT search engine against the mouse UniProt database. False discovery rates (FDRs) were controlled at <1% at both the peptide and protein levels. Protein quantification was performed using label-free quantification based on precursor ion intensities.

Degradation of DNA and proteins in EVs

To identify the functional cargo components contained within AT-EVs, we used enzymatic digestion to reduce EV-associated DNA or protein cargo, following previously reported methods (36, 37, 67). Purified EVs were diluted in PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio) to a final protein concentration of 0.15 mg/ml. The EV suspension was first mixed with DNase I (1 U/ml; catalog no. EN0771, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or proteinase K (catalog no. 17916, Thermo Fisher Scientific), then 0.2% saponin (catalog no. 47036, Sigma-Aldrich) was added, and the mixture was gently agitated on a shaker at room temperature for 30 min. Reactions were terminated by adding 10 mM EDTA (catalog no. G1105, Servicebio) for DNase I or 1 mM PMSF; catalog no. ST505, Beyotime) for proteinase K. The mixtures were subsequently centrifuged at 100,000g for 70 min at 4°C to remove residual enzymes and reaction by-products, and the resulting EV pellets were resuspended in PBS for downstream experiments.

DNA loading into EVs by electroporation

Purified mtDNA isolated from HFD-EVs was electroporated into CD-EVs to generate mtDNA-loaded EVs (EVs-mtDNA) (68). Briefly, CD-EVs (3 × 108 particles) were mixed with 5 μg of purified mtDNA in 50 μl of electroporation buffer (1.15 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.2; 25 mM KCl; 21% OptiPrep, catalog no. D1556, Sigma-Aldrich) and subjected to two square-wave pulses (400 V, 125 μF) using a GenePulser Xcell system (Bio-Rad, USA). This input ratio was selected based on preliminary optimization experiments and was adjusted for our experimental system, with HFD-EVs as the biological reference. Immediately after electroporation, 1 mM EDTA was added to prevent DNA aggregation, followed by incubation for 15 min at room temperature. To remove free mtDNA and residual reagents, samples were centrifuged through Nanosep filters [300 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO); catalog no. OD300C33, Pall] at 5000g for 5 min at 4°C, treated with DNase I (1.5 U/μl; catalog no. EN0521, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min at 37°C, inactivated with 20 mM EDTA, and washed three times with 1× TE buffer (10 mM tris-HCl and 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) before resuspension in 100 μl of PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio).

Intra-articular injection

Intra-articular injections into the knee joints of mice were performed twice per week for 12 weeks using a 30-gauge needle and a microsyringe (Hamilton, Switzerland) under isoflurane anesthesia. Each knee joint received 10 μl of PBS or EV suspension (1 × 1010 particles). The selected regimen was based on dosing schedules commonly used in previous preclinical studies of intra-articular EV administration in murine OA models (69, 70). All injections were carried out under aseptic conditions.

Bioinformatics analysis

Public gene expression data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE98460). Gene expression differences between cartilage samples from individuals with and without obesity were analyzed and visualized using volcano plots, with genes showing greater expression differences highlighted for visualization. Heatmaps were generated to display representative obesity-associated expression patterns. GSEA was performed using ranked gene expression changes to evaluate pathway-level differences between groups. Pathways related to cytosolic DNA sensing, NF-κB signaling, inflammatory responses, IFN-α/γ signaling, and TNF-α signaling were examined as part of the enrichment analysis.

Measurement of cGAMP

The quantification of cGAMP in synovial fluid or cartilage tissue lysates was performed using a chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay (catalog no. 501700, Cayman) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total protein concentration in each lysate, determined using a standardized BCA assay kit (catalog no. 23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific), was used for normalization. All assays were conducted in technical duplicates, with luminescent signals quantified using a multimode microplate reader.

In vivo delivery of AAV9-sh-Rab27a

AAV vectors carrying short hairpin RNA against Rab27a (AAV9-sh-Rab27a) and the corresponding negative control (AAV9-sh-NC) were used in this study, with related materials supplied by Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Viruses were packaged, purified, and provided in sterile PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio) at a titer of 1 × 1012 vg/ml. To suppress AT-EV secretion in vivo, 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice fed an HFD received bilateral injections of AAV9-sh-Rab27a (50 μl per side) directly into the inguinal fat pads under isoflurane anesthesia using a 30-gauge needle. Control mice received equal volumes of AAV9-sh-NC. Injections were performed once weekly for three consecutive weeks following established protocols for local AAV delivery to adipose depots. Rab27a knockdown efficiency in adipose tissue was assessed by Western blotting where indicated. Mice were euthanized 12 weeks after the initial injection for subsequent analyses.

dSTORM imaging of AT-EVs

dSTORM imaging of AT-EVs was performed using the EV Profiler 1.0 kit (ONI, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Coverslips were pretreated with 0.1% poly-l-lysine at room temperature for 15 min, gently rinsed three times with ultrapure water, and air-dried. Subsequently, 50 μl of EV suspension (approximately 2.0 × 108 particles) was added onto the center of each coverslip and incubated in a humidified chamber at 4°C for 2 hours to allow EV adsorption. The immobilized EVs were then fixed with the F1 solution provided in the kit for 10 min. For surface target staining, samples were blocked with 5% BSA. For intravesicular target staining, samples were permeabilized with 0.001% Triton X-100 and then blocked with 5% BSA. The samples were then incubated at room temperature for 50 min with CD9-CF488 and CD63-CF568 antibodies provided in the kit, together with an AF647-conjugated anti-DsRed2 antibody. After washing according to the manufacturer’s protocol, the stained EV samples were immediately imaged in freshly prepared dSTORM imaging buffer (provided in the kit). dSTORM was performed as previously described (71) using a Zeiss Elyra PS.1 super-resolution microscope in total internal reflection fluorescence mode. dSTORM images were acquired for 10,000 frames with 30-ms exposure. Images were processed using the PALM module of Zeiss ZEN Black software. The photon-number threshold was set to 200, and a point spread function (PSF) of 0.25 was used. EV-associated fluorescence signals were analyzed using an ImageJ macro as previously described (72).

RNA interference

cGAS expression was knocked down using siRNA-mediated silencing. On the basis of preliminary screening of three candidate siRNAs targeting human cGAS, the siRNA with the most efficient knockdown effect was selected for subsequent experiments. The target sequence of the selected cGAS siRNA was 5′-CUAGCAACUUAAUUGACAA-3′. The sequence of the scrambled control siRNA was 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′. Chondrocytes were transfected with cGAS siRNA or a nontargeting scrambled control siRNA at a final concentration of 25 nM using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (catalog no. 13778-075, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hours of transfection, knockdown efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR, and the cells were then subjected to the indicated experimental treatments and downstream analyses.

H-151 and PDTC treatment

For in vivo administration, mice received intraperitoneal injections of either STING inhibitor H-151 (210 μg per mouse, catalog no. SML2437, Sigma-Aldrich) or an equivalent vehicle containing the same concentration of DMSO in 200 μl of PBS with 10% Tween 80 three times weekly during an 8-week intervention period. To mitigate localized tissue irritation, injection sites were systematically rotated among the four abdominal quadrants, ensuring no single region received consecutive administrations. For in vitro treatment, cultured chondrocytes were exposed to 0.5 μM H-151 (catalog no. SML2437, Sigma-Aldrich) or 10 μM PDTC, an NF-κB inhibitor (catalog no. S1808, Beyotime), for 24-hour incubation cycles.

Behavioral testing

Gait analysis was performed using an automated gait-assessment system (GaitLab, WEASEL, France) to quantify locomotor function. Mice were allowed to acclimate to the testing environment before recording. Spontaneous ambulation was recorded via ventral videography as mice traversed an illuminated, transparent walkway, with software-derived algorithms analyzing spatiotemporal gait parameters, including stride dynamics and paw contact-related parameters. Mechanical allodynia, as an indicator of OA-related pain sensitivity, was assessed using the von Frey assay. An electronic von Frey aesthesiometer (Zhongshi Technology, China) was used to determine hind paw withdrawal thresholds, defined as the minimal force in grams required to elicit a nociceptive reflex withdrawal.

NHANES database analysis

The NHANES is a nationally representative, cross-sectional study of the US population that uses a complex, multistage probability sampling design to collect comprehensive health and nutritional data. This includes laboratory assays, imaging, radiography, interviews, and physical examinations. The NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board. For this analysis, we used data from the 1999 to 2006 and 2011 to 2018 NHANES cycles. After excluding individuals lacking complete data on BFP or OA status, 23,431 of 80,630 participants were included in the final analysis. Participants were stratified into three sex-specific BFP categories: normal: men <20%, women <30%; moderate: men 20 to 30%, women 30 to 40%; high: men ≥30%, women ≥40%. An RCS regression model was applied to evaluate the dose-response relationship between BFP and OA risk.

Isolation of synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers

Human synovial fluid samples were subjected to size-exclusion chromatography using a pre-equilibrated qEVoriginal column (70 nm, Izon Science, New Zealand). Fractions 7 to 11 were collected as EV-enriched fractions according to the manufacturer’s instructions and subsequently concentrated using 100-kDa MWCO centrifugal ultrafiltration units (catalog no. UFC8100, Millipore, USA). To enrich synovial EV fractions carrying adipose-associated EV markers, antibodies against previously reported adipose-associated EV markers, including FABP4, CA3, STEAP4, GGT5, and CAMKIIα (44), were biotinylated using an EZ-Link Micro Sulfo-NHS-Biotinylation Kit (catalog no. 21925, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibody information is provided in table S5. Dynabeads M-280 streptavidin magnetic beads (catalog no. 11205D, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were rinsed with PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio) and then incubated with the biotinylated antibody cocktail for 30 min at room temperature with gentle mixing. The antibody-loaded beads were collected using a DynaMag-2 magnet (catalog no. 12321D, Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed three times with 0.1% BSA (catalog no. AM2618, Invitrogen) in PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio), and resuspended in PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio). Subsequently, concentrated synovial fluid–derived EVs were incubated with the antibody-loaded beads at 4°C for 16 hours on a rotating mixer. After incubation, bead-bound EVs were magnetically separated and washed three times with 0.1% BSA in PBS (catalog no. G4202, Servicebio). To release captured EVs from the antibody-loaded beads, the bead-bound EV complexes were incubated with IgG elution buffer (catalog no. 21004, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature with gentle mixing. The beads were then magnetically removed, and the EV-containing eluate was immediately neutralized with 1 M tris-HCl buffer (pH 9.0, catalog no. T2819, Sigma-Aldrich) before downstream analyses.

nFCM analysis of enriched synovial EV fractions

For nFCM staining, antibodies against CD9, CD63, TSG101, FABP4, and adiponectin were conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 using the Lightning-Link Alexa Fluor 488 Conjugation Kit (catalog no. ab236553, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CD9, CD63, and TSG101 were used as canonical EV markers, whereas FABP4 and adiponectin were used as adipose-associated EV markers. Approximately 1.0 × 107 particles from synovial EV fractions enriched for adipose-associated EV markers were resuspended in 100 μl and fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 30 min. For the detection of nonmembrane proteins, the fixed EVs were further permeabilized with 0.2% Tween-20 (catalog no. P9416, Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 30 min. After fixation or permeabilization as appropriate, EVs were washed with 1 ml of PBS and pelleted by UC at 100,000g for 20 min at 4°C. The pellet was then resuspended in 50 μl of PBS and incubated with AF488-conjugated antibodies (5 μg/ml) at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, EVs were washed twice with 1 ml of PBS by UC at 100,000g for 20 min at 4°C using an ultracentrifuge (Optima MAX-XP, Beckman Coulter, USA). The final pellet was resuspended in 50 μl of PBS and immediately subjected to nano-flow cytometric analysis using a CytoFLEX nano flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). For each sample and antibody staining condition, an unstained EV control was processed in parallel using the same fixation, permeabilization, washing, and acquisition procedures but without AF488-conjugated antibody incubation. These unstained controls were used to define background fluorescence and to set the positivity threshold for each marker. Before data acquisition, instrument startup and daily quality control were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EV samples were acquired at a flow rate of 3 μl/min. Data were analyzed using CytExpert Nano software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise specified. In all figures, n denotes the number of individually phenotyped mice, the number of human participants, or the number of biological replicates for in vitro experiments. Mice and cells were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Outcome assessment, image quantification, and data analysis were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate, followed by the indicated multiple-comparison test, was used. Detailed information on statistical tests, sample sizes, and the number of independent replicates is provided in the corresponding figure legends. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Sample sizes were determined based on prior experience and established conventions.
