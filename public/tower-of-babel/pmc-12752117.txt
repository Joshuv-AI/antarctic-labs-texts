Predictors of complications after intramedullary nailing for lower extremity fractures in low-resource settings: a 10-year experience with the SIGN nail in Nigeria
Authors: Stephen Adesope Adesina, Isaac Olusayo Amole, Adepeju Olatayo Adegoke, Chiwendu Uchechukwu Isiakpona, Chidiebube Enyeremchi Ukejianya, Sarah Michael-Duja, Imri Goodness Adefokun, Olusola Tunde Ekunnrin, Simeon Ayorinde Ojo, Innocent Chiedu Ikem, Samuel Uwale Eyesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn low-resource countries, the incidence of lower extremity long-bone fractures exceeds that in high-resource countries due to higher rates of motor vehicle collisions (MVCs). This situation is worsened by limited resources for adequate care, resulting in significant health and economic impacts. Locked intramedullary nailing (IMN) is a widely accepted treatment for lower extremity fractures, but it carries risks of complications, which have not been well-studied in low-resource settings. Considering the resource differences between high- and low-resource settings, local research is essential for identifying modifiable factors to prevent complications. This study investigated the predictors of complications after IMN with “Surgical Implant Generation Network” (SIGN) nails (Richland, WA, USA) for lower extremity fractures in a low-resource setting.MethodsA secondary analysis was performed on prospectively collected data from 666 fractures in 603 patients treated with SIGN nails at a mission teaching hospital in southwestern Nigeria between July 2014 and June 2024. The mean age was 43.2 years, and 65.7% of the patients were male. Fractures meeting the inclusion criteria were classified as with or without complications. Two-thirds (66.8%) were femur fractures, and 33. 2% were tibia fractures. Univariate and binary logistic regression analyses identified significant predictors of complications.ResultsThe overall complication rate was 11.7%, with infection and non- union being the most common. The mortality rate was 1.1%. Independent predictors of complications included injury mechanism (p = 0.033), fracture age (p = 0.003), fracture type (p < 0.001), prior treatment elsewhere (p = 0.007), and surgery duration (p = 0.004). Fractures resulting from MVCs, older fractures, and open fractures showed higher complication rates. Each additional 30 min of surgery increased the odds of complications by 34% (95% CI, 1.003–1.017).ConclusionThis study shows that IMN with SIGN nails is an effective treatment for fractures in low-resource settings. However, challenges remain, especially with open fractures and delayed treatment. Prioritizing timely surgery, improving infection control, and enhancing surgical efficiency are essential for better outcomes. The findings inform strategies for improvement and highlight the need for further research to develop evidence-based guidelines for IMN in low-resource environments.

Background

In low-resource countries, the incidence of lower extremity long-bone fractures exceeds that in high-resource countries due to higher rates of motor vehicle collisions (MVCs), including motorcycle collisions and pedestrian collisions, as well falls and other traumatic incidents [1]. Ninety per cent of traffic deaths occur in low-resource countries [2]. The burden of lower extremity fractures in these countries is compounded by limited access to healthcare, inadequate infrastructure, shortages of trained healthcare professionals, and significant financial barriers [3]. The consequences include inadequate treatment and increased risk of long-term disability, chronic pain, limited mobility, economic burden, reduced quality of life, and mortality [4].

Locked intramedullary nailing (IMN) has emerged as a preferred treatment option for lower extremity fractures due to its benefits [5, 6]. Compared to alternative treatments, IMN offers several advantages, including reduced soft tissue dissection, fewer implants required, improved stability, better fracture alignment, simplified postoperative protocols, shorter hospital stays, and cost-effectiveness [6, 7]. The benefits of stable fixation, early mobilization, and weight-bearing provided by IMN are important in low-resource settings, where manual labour is often the main source of income. An early return to work is crucial to mitigate the impact of unemployment and poverty on individuals and families who depend on daily wages for their survival [8]. The limited need for specialized equipment makes IMN a feasible option in low-resource settings [9, 10].

The previous challenges to using IMN in low-resource settings, such as the scarcity of implants, concerns about infections, and limited access to orthopedic equipment and training, are being increasingly addressed, leading to greater adoption of the procedure [11, 12]. Western organizations have intervened to train doctors in trauma care, develop health infrastructure, and provide charitable donations of implants and equipment to numerous centers in developing countries [12–14]. Earlier studies in low-resource countries have reported acceptable infection rates and satisfactory functional outcomes, encouraging the use of IMN in these settings [9, 12, 13, 15].

Yet, despite its benefits, IMN carries risks even in high-resource countries, and these risks are amplified in low-resource settings by several ongoing challenges. These include inadequate trauma care systems, limited access to specialized equipment, and delays in access to surgery, which often require open reduction and longer operation times [16]. Additionally, there are infrastructure issues, including understaffing, outdated facilities, and unreliable access to water and electricity, as well as concerns about hygiene and infection control [10, 12, 15, 17, 18]. Moreover, the need for out-of-pocket payments hinders timely treatment and worsens outcomes [19].

Complications from IMN commonly include issues such as infection, hardware failure (i.e. screw or nail breakage), malunion, rotational malalignment, and impaired bone healing (including delayed or nonunion) [6, 20, 21]. Research in high-resource countries has identified several factors that predict poor outcomes after IMN for long-bone fractures. These factors include high-energy injuries, the use of stainless steel nails instead of titanium, fracture gaps, immediate full weight-bearing after surgery, open fractures, and smoking [22, 23].

In low-resource settings, the common complications associated with IMN are infection, nonunion, malunion, and implant failure [12, 15, 24, 25]. While some studies in low-resource settings have examined factors related to complications, particularly infections, there is a lack of research on the predictors of these complications [24, 26, 27]. Considering the differences in resources and healthcare access between high- and low-resource countries, local research is vital for identifying factors that can be modified to prevent complications. The knowledge gained can guide surgical decisions and improve outcomes. Recent calls emphasize the need for orthopedic research in low-resource settings to inform treatment and improve outcomes specific to these environments, rather than depending on findings from high-resource settings [28]. The study aims to examine the clinical predictors of complications after “Surgical Implant Generation Network” (SIGN) intramedullary nailing for lower extremity fractures (femur and tibia) in a resource-limited setting, focusing on factors related to both the fractures and the surgical procedure.

Methods

Study center

The study was undertaken at a mission teaching hospital in a semi-urban city in southwestern Nigeria. The hospital serves as a referral center for health facilities within the city and from villages and towns in the region. The hospital lacked fracture tables, C-arms and powered reaming devices. It started receiving charitable donations of SIGN implants from SIGN Fracture Care International (Richland, WA, USA) in 2014, which has led to a significant increase in patient flow from distant cities.

SIGN nailing system

The SIGN nailing system (Fig. 1) is designed to obviate the need for advanced fracture-care equipment [13]. The implants provided to our hospital include nails, interlocking screws, and fracture (Fx) plates. The SIGN nail is specifically designed for use in low-resource settings, allowing placement without the need for fluoroscopy or electrical power. It is a solid, stainless-steel intramedullary nail used to treat fractures in the femur, tibia, and humerus. Manual reamers are used to enlarge the canal, eliminating the need for power reamers that require a continuous electrical supply. Additionally, a rigid targeting side arm enables accurate drilling for screw insertion without relying on intraoperative fluoroscopy, allowing for blind drilling of proximal and distal interlocks [13].

Fracture treatment protocol

Patients with acute fractures were admitted through the Emergency Room, while those with older fractures were evaluated in the outpatient clinic for surgical clearance. All patients with open fractures began receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics upon arrival. Wound debridement and irrigation were performed as soon as possible. Primary wound closure was performed if there was no skin loss; otherwise, delayed closure using a muscle flap and split-thickness skin graft was performed during definitive fracture stabilization. Definitive surgery was carried out as promptly as possible for all consenting and medically fit patients. Many acute diaphyseal fractures underwent closed nailing, while all others received open or mini-open reduction. The nail diameters ranged from 8 to 12 mm, and all slots were locked. All patients received ceftriaxone and/or other antibiotics for five days or longer, depending on the type of fracture or any accompanying injuries. Pre- and post-operative radiographs were taken, and the core management team remained consistent throughout the study period.

Follow-up protocol

Patients began walking as soon as their condition allowed, usually on the first day after surgery, and were discharged from the hospital starting on the fifth post-operative day. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 6 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months. The lead author also maintained contact with patients by telephone to monitor their well-being and organize additional follow-ups as needed. In our setting, patients generally continued attending follow-up appointments until they achieved functional recovery and any complications had been resolved [12, 29]. During follow-up visits, clinical evaluations checked for complications and functional outcomes, while plain X-ray radiographs assessed the progress of fracture healing. For patients unable to complete 12 months of in-person follow-up, telephone interviews were carried out with either the patient or a family member to assess their well-being. Previous studies in our setting have demonstrated the effectiveness of telephone interviews for patient follow-up when they are unable to attend hospital visits due to financial constraints or perceived wellness [29].

Study design

This study was a secondary analysis of prospectively collected data from July 2014 to June 2024, following approval from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee and obtaining informed consent from participants. Data were collected post-operatively and updated after each follow-up visit. Out of a total of 707 lower extremity fractures, only 666 cases were included in the analysis. These were patients who had either up to 12 months of follow-up or who died from causes directly related to the fracture or its treatment in our center. The following groups were excluded from the study: (i) patients with less than 12 months of follow-up who could not be reached by phone, (ii) patients assessed to have recovered from their injuries and surgery during an earlier follow-up but who later died at home from unknown causes within 12 months of surgery, and (iii) patients whose deaths were attributed to pre-existing severe, life-threatening conditions such as metastatic cancer or advanced heart failure (Fig. 2). The data collected included age, sex, mechanism of injury, fracture age (classified as fresh - ≤3 weeks or old - >3 weeks), fracture type (open/closed), and location (purely diaphyseal or end segment involved), along with concomitant injuries, pre-visit treatment, time to surgery, nail diameter, reduction method (open/closed), supplemental plating, duration of surgery (from skin incision to closure), co-morbidity, and complications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We defined a complication as any issue directly related to the fracture or its treatment in our hospital that resulted in death, required additional medication, or for which further surgical intervention was strongly advised and/or performed to achieve a satisfactory outcome. This included: (i) death from suspected thromboembolic events; (ii) fracture-related infection, defined as purulent drainage from the wound, formation of a fistula, sinus, or wound breakdown with communication to the bone or implant [30]; (iii) non-union, defined as a fracture that fails to show progressive improvement in radiographic or clinical evidence of union for at least 9 months, where further intervention was strongly advised and/or performed to achieve union [31]; (iv) malunion, which refers to a non-anatomical union resulting in length, rotational, or angular defects, often leading to functional impairment [32]; (v) joint stiffness; and (vi) pain due to implant position changes, identified by comparing sequential radiographs. In cases where patients experienced multiple complications, we recorded the earliest complication that was believed to have led to the subsequent problems. For instance, if a patient had both fracture-related infection and non-union, fracture-related infection would be recorded as the complication; for a patient with non-union and implant breakage, non-union would be noted. We excluded problems caused by treatment received before arriving at our hospital, such as joint stiffness from prior treatment by a traditional bonesetter.

Statistical analysis and data management

Data were stored securely on the lead author’s laptop using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp, New York, USA) and later analyzed. Fractures were classified into two categories: those with complications and those without. Potential risk factors for complications were identified based on prior literature and variables from this study [21, 22]. These included intrinsic patient-related factors, such as sociodemographic profile and comorbidity status, along with extrinsic factors related to the fracture or surgery, including injury mechanism, fracture age, type and location, concomitant injuries, pre-visit treatments, fracture-to-fixation time (time to surgery), nail diameter, reduction methods, supplemental plating, and surgery duration. Comorbidities were categorized into two groups: (1) No/mild, which includes uncomplicated hypertension and other minor conditions that do not significantly affect overall health; (2) Significant, comprising more severe conditions such as diabetes, heart failure, stroke with residual deficit, chronic kidney disease, and benign prostate pathology requiring surgery. The variables were examined for significant associations with complications. Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) test was used for categorical variables, while the independent sample t-test was applied to numerical variables. A visual assessment of data normality was conducted using histograms to determine the appropriateness of using parametric tests. Variables that displayed significance in the univariate analyses were subsequently entered as covariates into a binary logistic regression analysis to identify significant independent predictors of complications. A p-value of < 0.05 was deemed significant.

Results

The study involved 666 fractures in 603 patients. The mean age was 43.2 years (SD, 18.24; range, 6–98), and 396 (65.7%) were males. Insignificant numbers were smokers (3) or chronic alcoholics (4). There were 445 (66.8%) femur fractures and 221 (33.2%) tibia fractures. Seventy-eight fractures experienced complications, including seven deaths related to the fracture or treatment at our center, leading to an overall complication rate of 11.7% and a mortality rate of 1.1%. Figure 3 shows that most fractures, 88.3%, did not develop complications. Infection and non-union were the most common complications, occurring in 5.6% and 2.7% of all fractures, respectively. Among all complications, infection accounted for 47.4% and non-union for 23.1%. Complications were seen in 47 (10.6%) femur fractures and 31 (14%) tibia fractures. Non-union was the most frequent complication in femur fractures, making up 3.8% of all cases. Conversely, infection was the leading complication in tibia fractures, constituting 11.8% of all cases.

Figure 4 reveals a significant relationship between the fractured bone and both injury mechanism (χ2 = 17.781, p < 0.001) and fracture type (χ2 = 66.440, p < 0.001). MVCs were more commonly associated with tibia fractures, accounting for 91.9% of tibia fractures compared to 78.9% of femur fractures. The majority of femur fractures (85.2%) were closed, whereas tibia fractures had a higher proportion of open fractures, with 31.7% being acute open fractures and 11.8% being chronic open fractures with healed wounds. In contrast, only 14.8% of femur fractures were open.

In Table 1, the univariate analysis of factors associated with complications identified several significant relationships. Fractures from MVCs had significantly more complications than those from other causes (p = 0.049). Complications were more likely to occur in fractures involving the end segment (18.3%) compared to purely diaphyseal fractures (10.3%), p = 0.016. Old fractures exhibited a higher complication rate (18.8%) than fresh fractures (8.7%), p < 0.001. Complications were more common in open fractures, especially in chronic open fractures with healed wounds (32.5%), compared to closed fractures (8.3%), p < 0.001. Additionally, complications were more frequent in fractures that had been treated elsewhere before (15.8%) compared to those that had not (10.1%), p = 0.041. Regarding treatment approaches, open reduction was associated with a higher complication rate (16.2%) than closed reduction (5.4%), p < 0.001. The other variables did not show any significant association with complications.

Table 2 shows that a statistically significant difference was found in surgery duration between fractures without complications (mean = 79.1 min, SD = 40.23) and those with complications (mean = 104.2 min, SD = 45.03) (t = -5.099, p < 0.001). However, the diameter of the nails used did not differ significantly between fractures with complications (mean = 10.4 mm, SD = 1.11) and those without complications (mean = 10.4 mm, SD = 1.14) (t = -0.384, p = 0.701). Furthermore, the mean duration of surgery for old fractures (mean = 108.9 min, SD = 41.38) was significantly longer than that for fresh fractures (mean = 70.8 min, SD = 36.18) (t = -11.229, p < 0.001). Neither the surgical approach (antegrade vs. retrograde) nor the type of nail used (standard vs. fin) had any influence on the occurrence of complications related to femur fractures. Additionally, the morphology of diaphyseal fractures (AO/OTA types A, B, or C) did not affect the likelihood of complications. The sociodemographic profiles of the groups with and without complications were not significantly different either.

Table 3 shows that bonesetters treated 18.3% of the fractures before hospital admission. Smaller groups received unsuccessful cast, internal or external fixation, or traction treatments. The median time to surgery was 5 days, with a wide range (0–7404 days). Surgery was performed promptly (within 2 days) in 25.7%, early (3–7 days) in 36.6%, delayed (8–21 days) in 8%, and late (> 3 weeks) in 29.7%. Additionally, the statistical analysis reveals significant associations between pre-surgery treatment and complications (p = 0.025) and between time to surgery and complications (p < 0.001). Fractures treated with internal fixation (35%) and traction (25%) had higher complication rates compared to those with no treatment (10.1%). Complication rates increased with delayed surgery, rising sharply from 7.8% to 17.0% between early and delayed procedures.

Table 4 displays the results of the binary logistic regression used to identify the independent predictors of complications. The overall model proved to be statistically significant compared to the null model (χ2(8) = 67.6, p < 0.001), explaining 18.8% of the variance in complications (Nagelkerke R2) and correctly predicting 89.0% of cases. Significant predictors of complications included injury mechanism (p = 0.033), fracture age (p = 0.003), fracture type (p < 0.001), prior treatment elsewhere (p = 0.007), and surgery duration (p = 0.004). Fracture location (p = 0.091) and fracture reduction method (p = 0.069) did not have a significant independent impact.

The odds of developing complications are approximately 2.5 times higher for fractures resulting from MVCs compared to those with other types of injury mechanisms (95% CI, 1.078–5.965). Old fractures (> 3 weeks) had 5.3 times the odds of complications compared to fresh fractures (95% CI, 1.776–15.954). The odds of complications for acute open fractures were 2.6 times higher than for closed fractures (p = 0.003, 95% CI, 1.383–4.936). Chronic open fractures with healed wounds were 3.9 times more likely to have complications than closed fractures (p = 0.002, 95% CI, 1.667–9.254). Fractures that had received prior treatment elsewhere had 5.0 times the odds of developing complications as untreated fractures (95% CI, 1.555–16.275). For every one-minute increase in surgery duration, the odds of complications increase by a factor of 1.01 (95% CI, 1.003–1.017), holding all other variables constant. For instance, if the surgery duration increases by 30 min, the odds of complications rise by approximately 34%, calculated as follows: ((1.01^30 − 1) * 100%).

Discussion

This study’s findings indicate that the majority (88.3%) of fractures treated with SIGN nails did not develop complications, supporting previous research in low-resource settings that suggests complication rates following orthopedic implant surgery are within acceptable limits, given the severity of trauma in these settings [12, 15]. The use of solid nails, such as the SIGN nail, which are stronger than hollow nails of the same diameter, provides excellent fixation for high-energy fractures common in low-resource settings [11]. The solid design may also reduce the risk of infection by minimizing internal dead space and the surface area available for biofilm formation [33].

Despite these benefits, the study found an overall complication rate of 11.7% and a mortality rate of 1.1%. This underscores the challenges faced by low-resource countries in providing optimal care for patients with major lower limb trauma due to resource constraints. While complications following IMN are not exclusive to low-resource countries, the inadequacy of resources to address them in these settings necessitates a critical examination of their impact [3, 23, 34]. Implementing safety measures, such as the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, has proven effective in reducing surgical complications and mortality rates. However, adopting these measures in resource-limited settings remains challenging [35]. Although current consensus guidelines for managing fracture-related infections may be applicable in low-resource settings, significant organizational and infrastructure barriers hinder standardization of diagnosis and management [36]. In these contexts, a complication can lead to increased poverty for patients and their families [3, 4].

The most common complications observed in this study were infection and non-union, consistent with existing literature. The rates of infection (5.6%) and non-union (2.7%) were comparable to previously reported rates in both low- and high-resource countries. For instance, studies have reported non-union rates ranging from 6.9% to 11% [6, 23, 37] and infection rates between 5% and 7% [12, 26, 37]. When used with appropriate indications and care, IMN offers a minimally invasive and biologically friendly technique for achieving fracture reduction and fixation, resulting in low complication rates [20]. However, when complications occur, they can lead to significant morbidity, financial burden, and psychological distress [4, 20, 31].

A notable disparity exists in complication rates between femur and tibia fractures. Femur fractures exhibited a slightly lower overall complication rate (10.6% vs. 14%), with non-union being the most common complication, whereas infection was the leading complication for tibia fractures. The higher complication rates observed in tibia fractures treated with IMN can be attributed to several factors, including compromised soft tissue envelope and blood supply, as well as a higher incidence of open fractures, which increases the risk of infection and other complications. In contrast, femur fractures tend to have better outcomes due to their more robust soft tissue envelope and blood supply [20]. As illustrated in Fig. 4, tibia fractures are more likely to result from MVCs and be open fractures compared to femur fractures. Both open fractures and high-energy trauma are established risk factors for complications [22, 31]. The high infection rate, particularly among tibia fractures, underscores the need for enhanced infection control measures. Furthermore, although not included in our data, preoperative anemia, which is common in orthopedic patients in low-resource settings, may worsen these complications. A recent study indicates a 24.1% prevalence of preoperative anemia among elective orthopedic patients [38]. This finding is consistent with previous studies underscoring inadequate infrastructure, such as unreliable access to water and electricity, and concerns about hygiene and infection control, as significant challenges in low-resource settings [10, 12].

Univariate analysis (Tables 1 and 2) showed that several clinical variables were significantly associated with complications. However, binary logistic regression analysis (Table 4) revealed that injury mechanism, fracture age, fracture type, prior treatment elsewhere, and surgery duration were the significant predictors of complications. Fractures resulting from MVCs had approximately 2.5 times higher odds of developing complications compared to those from other injury mechanisms. This increased risk is likely due to the high-energy impacts associated with MVCs, leading to more severe and complex fractures, often with concomitant soft tissue, blood vessel, and nerve injuries [22, 31]. The severity of the injury directly influences the likelihood of complications, and delays in accessing medical care when MVCs occur in remote locations can further exacerbate these complications [39].

Fractures that were older than three weeks had 5.3 times higher odds of complications compared to fresh fractures. This finding aligns with existing literature that emphasizes the increased complexity and risks associated with delayed treatment. A recent study by Subramanian et al. revealed that each week of delayed surgical intervention raises the odds of infection by 9.2% for closed diaphyseal tibial and femoral fractures treated with SIGN nails [16]. Delayed surgery is associated with longer operation times, higher rates of open reduction, and compromised fracture healing, resulting from impaired blood flow, tissue damage, and changes in bone structure [16, 40, 41]. In contrast, fresh fractures benefit from better blood supply and a more effective inflammatory response, which are crucial for early bone repair, thus reducing the likelihood of complications. In low-resource countries, late presentation is common due to financial and cultural barriers, leading to delayed treatment. This study found that 18.3% of fractures presented with abnormal unions following initial traditional bonesetter’s treatment, while many others had received unsuccessful cast, internal or external fixation, or traction treatments. The mean duration of surgery for old fractures was significantly longer than for fresh fractures. Delayed surgery was associated with increased complication rates, rising from 7.8% to 17.0% between early (3–7 days) and delayed (8–21 days) procedures (Table 3). These findings emphasise the importance of timely surgery in low-resource settings.

Open fractures carried a higher risk of complications compared to closed fractures, with acute open fractures having 2.6 times higher odds of complications. Even after wound healing, chronic open fractures had 3.9 times higher odds of complications compared to closed fractures. Overall, open fractures pose a greater risk of complications than closed ones, and the severity of the open fracture significantly increases this risk [42, 43]. Even after an open fracture wound has healed, the risk of complications remains higher than that of closed fractures. This is primarily due to the initial injury, which can damage the bone and surrounding tissues, impair blood supply, introduce bacteria, and create an environment more conducive to infection and delayed healing [44]. This situation underscores the importance of thoroughly investigating the causes of nonunion or delayed union. By understanding these factors, we can optimize treatment options before considering surgical intervention. Prompt and high-quality treatment—including debridement, stabilization, antibiotics, and appropriate follow-up—is essential to minimise complications [43]. However, in low-resource countries, limited resources for evaluation and treatment, along with delays in care, can exacerbate complication rates.

Fractures that were previously treated elsewhere had 5.0 times higher odds of developing complications compared to untreated fractures. This suggests that the prior treatments may have been ineffective or even harmful. As a referral center, our facility frequently receives fractures that were unsuccessfully treated using both conventional and unconventional methods (Table 3). Complications were most prevalent among fractures previously treated with internal fixation (35.0%), followed by traction (25.0%) and bonesetter’s manipulation (13.9%). Prior inadequate or delayed treatment can impede healing and increase the risk of complications due to impaired blood flow, tissue damage, and changes in bone structure [40, 41]. Patient-related factors, such as comorbidities, age, drug use, smoking and poor nutrition, may also contribute to complications. Notably, the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs has been associated with higher odds of fracture-healing complications [45]. Intuitively, chronic pain resulting from an inadequate management of fractures can lead to abuse of painkillers, especially in settings where the drugs are readily accessible. In low-resource settings like Nigeria, where manual labour is the primary source of income, inadequate initial management of a fracture can lead to prolonged pain, disability, or loss of limb function. This not only decreases an individual’s earning potential but also worsens issues like poverty and malnutrition. Such conditions can further impede bone healing after revision surgery.

Additionally, for fractures that fail to heal after initial surgery, infection is a serious concern, and revision surgery requires careful evaluation. Diagnostic tests, including a complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, and cultures, are vital for identifying and managing potential infections before proceeding with revision surgery [46, 47]. However, limited access to these tests and financial constraints can hinder the implementation of effective management strategies, such as staged reconstruction, which are essential for proper healing of these fractures. Surgery duration was also an independent predictor of complications, with a 34% increase in odds for every additional 30 min of operative time. Research consistently indicates that longer surgical times are associated with a higher risk of complications [48, 49]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Cheng et al. highlighted a strong correlation between extended operative time and complications across various surgical specialties [48]. Therefore, minimizing operative time should be a priority for surgeons, hospitals, and policymakers, as it can improve patient outcomes and reduce complications. By understanding both modifiable and non-modifiable factors contributing to prolonged operative duration, we can enhance preoperative planning and intraoperative efficiency, ultimately reducing overall surgical time. Enhancing operating room efficiency is particularly crucial in low-resource settings. Research from similar settings has identified several challenges, including high cancellation rates, delayed start times, prolonged turnover times, inadequate patient preparation, and facility shortages, all of which are indicative of inefficient operating room utilization [50].

Limitations and strengths

This study has limitations that must be taken into account when interpreting its findings. The single-center design may restrict the applicability of the results to other settings. The retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data may have introduced bias, and excluding patients with less than 12 months of follow-up or those lost to follow-up may have affected the accuracy of the reported complication rates. Additionally, the lack of advanced equipment, such as fracture tables and C-arms, may have influenced surgical outcomes. Furthermore, complications can overlap, potentially distorting their predictors; for instance, non-union may occur following an infection, affecting the predictors of both complications.

Despite these limitations, the study’s methodology was strengthened by several measures. Telephone interviews helped minimise loss to follow-up, and a consistent core management team ensured standardized data collection. A clear definition of complications allowed for accurate and consistent data gathering, while statistical analysis identified independent predictors of complications, controlling for potential confounding variables. The study’s strengths include its large sample size and prospective data collection, which reduced recall bias. The long follow-up period (minimum of 12 months) enabled a comprehensive assessment of complications. Conducted in a real-world, low-resource setting, this study provides valuable insights into the challenges and outcomes of fracture care in such environments, and its findings are relevant and applicable to similar settings.

Conclusions

This study shows that SIGN IMN is an effective treatment for lower extremity fractures in low-resource settings, with 88.3% of cases not developing complications. However, challenges remain, especially with tibial fractures, open fractures, and delayed treatment. Independent predictors of higher complication rates include fractures caused by MVCs, older fractures, open fractures, previous unsuccessful treatments, and longer operative times. To improve outcomes, prioritizing timely surgery, enhancing infection control, and increasing surgical efficiency are essential. Understanding these predictors helps healthcare providers develop strategies to reduce complications, long-term disability and poverty. Further research is necessary to create evidence-based guidelines and predictive models that assist surgeons in predicting and minimizing complications in low-resource settings.
