Network pharmacology analysis and in vitro experimental validation of liriodenine against non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jing Yang, Yichu Sun, Fei Liang, Ziqiang Shen, Yun Zhou, Jiangrui Zhu, Wen Sun, Youyou Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLiriodenine, a natural aporphine alkaloid from various medicinal plants, has shown antitumor activity in several cancers. However, its role and mechanisms in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remain unclear.MethodsPotential targets of liriodenine and NSCLC were retrieved from public databases. Core targets were identified using random forest and the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression algorithms. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations assessed the binding affinity and stability between liriodenine and its targets. In vitro assays were performed in A549 cells. Cell viability, proliferation, migration, and apoptosis were evaluated by CCK-8, colony formation, wound healing, and flow cytometry, respectively. Western blot was used to measure Transforming Growth Factor Beta 1(TGF-β1)expression.ResultsSixty-five overlapping targets were identified. Computational analysis showed that liriodenine strongly and stably binds TGF-β1. In vitro, liriodenine significantly inhibited proliferation and migration, induced apoptosis, and downregulated TGF-β1 expression in a dose-dependent manner. These effects provide evidence suggesting the involvement of the TGF-β1/Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition pathway.ConclusionThis study provides both computational and experimental evidence that liriodenine inhibits the growth and migration of NSCLC cells and promotes apoptosis by suppressing TGF-β1. These findings highlight its potential as a natural candidate for targeted therapy against NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15498-7.

Background

According to the latest statistics from GLOBOCAN 2022, lung cancer accounts for 12.4% of all malignant tumors globally and is responsible for 18.7% of cancer-related deaths, ranking first in both incidence and mortality among all cancers [1]. Lung cancer is primarily classified into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer, with NSCLC comprising approximately 80%–85% of all cases [2]. The therapeutic options for NSCLC include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. In recent years, the widespread adoption of molecular targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors has led to improved survival outcomes for patients with NSCLC. However, due to the complex biological characteristics of tumors and significant interpatient heterogeneity, current therapeutic strategies remain limited in terms of long-term efficacy, drug resistance, and patient applicability [3, 4]. Therefore, further investigation into novel anti-tumor agents is essential to improve the prognosis of NSCLC patients.

In recent years, increasing evidence has demonstrated that bioactive compounds derived from natural products, particularly those extracted from plants, possess significant antitumor activities and have emerged as promising candidates in cancer therapy research [5]. Liriodenine, chemically known as 8 H- [1, 3] benzodioxolo[6,5,4-de]benzo[g]quinolin-8-one, is a naturally occurring aporphine alkaloid isolated from a variety of plant species. It has demonstrated a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including antiarrhythmic, antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-platelet activating factor effects [6, 7]. Notably, its anticancer potential has garnered increasing attention. Previous studies have shown that liriodenine induces G1 phase cell cycle arrest and inhibits DNA synthesis in human hepatoma Hep G2 and SK-Hep-1 cell lines, primarily through upregulation of p53 expression and enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production, thereby suppressing tumor cell proliferation [8]. Additionally, research by Nordin et al. demonstrated that liriodenine induces apoptosis in ovarian cancer CAOV-3 cells by activating the mitochondrial pathway via caspase-3 and caspase-9, while also effectively inhibiting cell proliferation [9]. These findings underscore the broad anticancer potential of liriodenine across different malignancies.Importantly, liriodenine has been reported to exert notable antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells [10], suggesting its therapeutic potential in lung cancer. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms of liriodenine in NSCLC remain incompletely understood. In this study, we for the first time integrated network pharmacology and machine learning approaches to systematically predict the potential targets of liriodenine in NSCLC. We further experimentally validated the effects of liriodenine on cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and TGF-β1 expression, aiming to elucidate its molecular pharmacological mechanisms from multiple dimensions. Given the high incidence of NSCLC and the limitations of current therapeutic strategies, elucidating the role and underlying mechanisms of liriodenine in NSCLC is of great significance for expanding its antitumor applications and promoting the development of novel natural therapeutic agents.

Network pharmacology (NP) is an emerging research approach based on systems biology, network biology, and multi-omics technologies—including genomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics [11]. By identifying shared targets between bioactive compounds and diseases and constructing complex network models, NP facilitates the elucidation of interactions among drugs, targets, signaling pathways, and diseases [12]. Moreover, previous NSCLC studies based on TCGA transcriptomic analyses have revealed dysregulation of multiple key molecules and signaling pathways, providing reference and support for the network pharmacology analyses in this study [13, 14]. In this study, network pharmacology was employed to predict the potential targets and biological functions of liriodenine in NSCLC, with machine learning algorithms applied to further enhance the reliability of target prediction. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations were then used to evaluate the binding stability between liriodenine and the predicted targets. Finally, in vitro experiments were conducted to validate the antitumor effects of liriodenine and to explore its underlying biological mechanisms in NSCLC. This study broadens the therapeutic potential of liriodenine and proposes novel potential strategies for the treatment of NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Screening of potential targets of liriodenine against NSCLC

The chemical structure of liriodenine was first retrieved from the PubChem database and converted into its SMILES format. Potential drug targets were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction database, yielding 100 unique liriodenine-associated targets after removing duplicates. NSCLC-related target genes were obtained from the GeneCards database. The intersection of liriodenine-associated targets and NSCLC-related genes was identified and visualized using a Venny diagram.

Construction of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network between liriodenine and NSCLC targets

The overlapping target genes were uploaded to the STRING database v11.5 (https://cn.string-db.org), with the species set to Homo sapiens and the minimum required interaction score set to > 0.4. Unconnected nodes were excluded from the network to improve clarity. The resulting protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was exported in CSV format and imported into Cytoscape software for further analysis. Topological parameters, including degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), closeness centrality (CC), eigenvector centrality (EC), local average connectivity (LAC), and network centrality (NC), were calculated to identify key hub targets. Visualization and further analysis of the PPI network were performed using Cytoscape.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis included three categories: molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC). The species was restricted to Homo sapiens, with statistical significance thresholds set at p ≤ 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05. The top 10 GO terms and the top 20 KEGG pathways were selected for visualization.

Machine learning

In this study, transcriptomic data were retrieved from the TCGA LUAD and LUSC datasets. Gene expression was normalized using the TPM (Transcripts Per Million) method: raw read counts were divided by the corresponding gene length in kilobases to calculate Reads Per Kilobase (RPK), and these values were further scaled by the sum of RPKs per sample to obtain TPM. The TPM-normalized expression matrix was then used as the input for subsequent machine learning analyses. Common target genes of liriodenine associated with NSCLC were identified using two machine learning algorithms: the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE). LASSO logistic regression analysis was performed using the ‘glmnet’ package (v4.1-8) in R (v4.3.1), while SVM-RFE was implemented with the ‘e1071’ package (v1.7-16). The expression differences of the core genes were visualized using boxplots generated with the ‘ggpubr’ package.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed between liriodenine and the selected key target proteins to evaluate their binding affinities. The receptor protein structures were obtained from the RCSB PDB database, restricted to Homo sapiens and filtered for a resolution of < 3 Å to ensure high structural accuracy and reliability. PyMOL (version 3.1.3.1) was used to remove water molecules and small ligands from the protein structures, and hydrogen atoms were added using AutoDockTools. The 3D structure of liriodenine was retrieved from the PubChem database in SDF format and converted to MOL2 format using Open Babel (version 3.1.1). Molecular docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina. The binding interactions between liriodenine and the target proteins were further analyzed and visualized in 3D using PyMOL, while 2D interaction diagrams were generated using Discovery Studio (DS).

Molecular dynamics simulation

To evaluate the stability and dynamic binding behavior of the protein–ligand complexes, a 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed using GROMACS v2022.03. The AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was applied to the protein, while ligand parameters were generated using the GAFF force field via AmberTools22, with restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges calculated using Gaussian 16 W. The complexes were solvated in a TIP3P water box, with a minimum distance of 1.2 nm between the protein surface and the box edge. Sodium and chloride ions (0.154 M) were added to neutralize the system. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm, followed by 100 ps of equilibration under constant volume and temperature and constant pressure and temperature conditions. Temperature was maintained at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat, and pressure was kept at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method with a cutoff radius of 1.2 nm, while short-range non-bonded interactions were truncated at 1.0 nm, with neighbor lists updated every 10 steps. After equilibration, a 100 ns production simulation was conducted under periodic boundary conditions with a time step of 2 fs, and trajectory data were recorded every 10 ps. Trajectory analyses included root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and the number of hydrogen bonds. Gibbs free energy landscapes were calculated using the built-in GROMACS tools g_sham and xpm2txt.py based on RMSD and Rg distributions. Binding free energy calculations were conducted using the MM/PBSA approach implemented in MMPBSA.py v16.0 to assess the thermodynamic stability of the protein–ligand complexes.

Cell culture

The human non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell line A549 was purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ under standard conditions.

Assessment of liriodenine cytotoxicity on A549 NSCLC cells using the CCK-8 assay

A549 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 10³ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with serum-free DMEM containing various concentrations of liriodenine (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM) for 24 h. Untreated cells served as the control group. All procedures were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions for the CCK-8 assay kit. After treatment, plates were incubated for 1 h, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated using the formula: (OD of treated wells – OD of blank wells) / (OD of control wells – OD of blank wells). All experiments were conducted in triplicate independently (n = 3), with five technical replicates per group.

Colony formation assay to evaluate the inhibitory effect of liriodenine on the clonogenic proliferation of A549 cells

A549 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 900 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then treated with liriodenine at concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, and 5 µM, with an untreated group serving as the control. Cultures were maintained for an additional 9 days, with the medium replaced every 3 days. At the end of the incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. After removing the stain, wells were thoroughly washed with distilled water and allowed to air dry before imaging. Colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software. The relative colony formation rate (%) was calculated as (number of colonies in the treatment group ÷ number of colonies in the control group) × 100%. Comparative analysis was performed to evaluate the inhibitory effect of liriodenine on the clonogenic proliferation of A549 cells.

Wound healing assay to assess the effect of liriodenine on the migration ability of A549 cells

To investigate the effect of liriodenine on the migration ability of A549 cells, a wound healing assay was conducted. A549 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured until reaching a confluent monolayer. A sterile pipette tip was used to create a uniform scratch across the cell monolayer. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, culture medium containing varying concentrations of liriodenine (0, 1.25, 2.5, and 5 µM) was added, and the cells were further incubated. Images of the scratch were captured at 0 and 24 h using a microscope. The scratch width was measured using ImageJ software, and the cell migration rate (wound closure rate) was calculated as: [(initial scratch width − scratch width at time t) / initial scratch width] × 100%. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure the reliability of the results. This assay provided a direct evaluation of liriodenine’s impact on A549 cell migration over time, offering experimental evidence for its antitumor activity.

Apoptosis assay

To evaluate the pro-apoptotic effect of liriodenine on A549 cells, apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V-FITC single staining followed by flow cytometry analysis. Log-phase A549 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then treated with varying concentrations of liriodenine (1.25, 2.5, and 5 µM) for 24 h. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) were added according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Flow cytometry was performed to quantify the percentages of early and late apoptotic cells. Untreated cells served as the negative control.

Western blot analysis for the evaluation of cellular protein expression levels

This study employed Western blot analysis to investigate the effects of liriodenine treatment on migration-related proteins in A549 cells. Protein was extracted from cells treated with liriodenine at concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, and 5 µM, and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay kit. Total protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with blocking buffer at room temperature for 30 min, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. TGF-β1 was selected as the key migration-related target protein, with GAPDH used as the internal loading control. Following washes with buffer, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, then washed thoroughly to remove unbound antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection system, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. This assay provided a direct evaluation of the effect of liriodenine on TGF-β1 protein expression in A549 cells, providing experimental evidence for its antitumor mechanism.

Simultaneously, 6,113 NSCLC-associated targets were identified from the GeneCards database. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of predicted targets of liriodenine in NSCLC treatment

GO functional enrichment analysis of the 65 common targets was performed using the DAVID database, identifying a total of 360 significantly enriched terms (p < 0.05), including 227 BP, 35 CC, and 98 MF. After applying a FDR threshold of < 0.05, 110 BP, 21 CC, and 83 MF terms remained. The top 10 enriched categories in each ontology were visualized using bar plots (Fig. 1D). BP enrichment mainly involved critical biological processes such as signal transduction, protein phosphorylation, cell migration, cell proliferation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. CC enrichment indicated that these genes were predominantly localized to the cell membrane, cytoplasm, receptor complexes, and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complexes. MF analysis revealed that the target genes were significantly enriched in ATP binding, protein kinase activity, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, and protein tyrosine kinase activity. Additionally, activities related to phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase and histone kinase were also enriched, suggesting potential roles in signal transduction and transcriptional regulation. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified 127 significantly enriched pathways (p < 0.05, FDR < 0.05), with the top 20 pathways selected for visualization (Fig. 1E). These pathways predominantly involved cancer-related signaling, signal transduction, infection-related pathways, and regulation of cellular functions. The enrichment results indicate that these target genes may play important roles in the progression of various cancers, immune regulation, and signal transduction processes.

Machine learning and molecular screening

To enhance the reliability of predicted targets, this study employed LASSO regression and SVM-RFE algorithms to identify key targets of liriodenine against NSCLC. The LASSO algorithm identified 24 feature genes (Fig. 2A), while the SVM-RFE algorithm selected 20 feature genes (Fig. 2B), with an intersection of 17 genes (Fig. 2C). The overlapping genes were then intersected with the potential targets screened by CytoNCA, resulting in four core genes—TGF-β1, JUN, SRC, and ERBB2—identified as the final potential therapeutic targets of liriodenine for NSCLC(Supplementary Material S2). Subsequently, survival analysis was performed for the four significantly differentially expressed genes. Patients were stratified into low-expression (bottom 60%) and high-expression (top 40%) groups based on gene expression levels. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test (Fig. 2D). Notably, among all target genes, only TGF-β1 demonstrated a statistically significant association with survival (p = 0.031), suggesting its potential close association with patient prognosis.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Based on the molecular docking results, the TGF-β1–Liriodenine complex was selected for molecular dynamics simulation.

Analysis of complex stability

RMSD analysis

RMSD is commonly used to assess the accuracy of protein structure predictions and serves as a key indicator of system stability. As shown in Fig. 4A, the RMSD of the complex increased rapidly during the first 20 ns and stabilized after 40 ns, indicating that the complex gradually reached equilibrium. The ligand RMSD (Fig. 4B) fluctuated mainly between 0.015 and 0.025 nm, with only occasional peaks approaching 0.05 nm, suggesting that the ligand remained stable within the binding site without significant drift.

Rg analysis

Rg is a key indicator reflecting the compactness of protein structure. As shown in Fig. 4C, the Rg values remained relatively stable between 2.82 and 2.89 nm, with minimal fluctuations, indicating that the complex maintained a compact and stable conformation without significant unfolding or conformational relaxation was observed.

SASA analysis

SASA is a structural parameter commonly used to assess the extent of interaction between hydrophilic and hydrophobic residues of a protein and the surrounding solvent. The analysis showed that the SASA initially was approximately 360 nm² and gradually stabilized within the range of 325–335 nm² over the course of the simulation (Fig. 4D), indicating a stable binding state between the small molecule and the protein.

RMSF analysis

RMSF was used to analyze the flexibility of amino acid residues within the protein. As shown in Fig. 4E, most residues exhibited fluctuations between 0.1 and 0.3 nm, with peak values of 0.4–0.5 nm observed only at the N- and C-termini and loop regions. These findings are consistent with the dynamic characteristics of proteins and indicate overall conformational stability.

Analysis of hydrogen bond interactions between the small molecule and the protein

Hydrogen bonds play a crucial role in the binding process between proteins and ligands and are closely associated with electrostatic interactions. Therefore, the number and stability of hydrogen bonds can partially reflect the strength of these interactions. As shown in Fig. 4F, the number of hydrogen bonds between the protein and ligand remained between 1 and 3 throughout the simulation, with particularly stable interactions observed during the 40–100 ns interval. This indicates that hydrogen bonding contributes significantly to the stability of the protein–ligand complex.

Gibbs free energy analysis

The Gibbs free energy landscape was constructed to gain deeper insights into the conformational behavior of the protein–ligand complex. The shape and size of the lowest-energy regions indicate the structural stability of the complex. Based on RMSD and Rg values, the Gibbs free energy of the TGF-β1–Liriodenine complex was calculated and visualized as a free energy landscape (Fig. 4G). The results revealed that the lowest-energy region was concentrated within an RMSD range of 0.29–0.34 nm and an Rg range of 2.83–2.85 nm, with a corresponding minimum free energy between 0 and 2 kJ/mol, suggesting that the complex adopts its most stable conformation within this range.

MM/PBSA analysis

To further quantify the binding stability of the complex, molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) calculations were performed based on the 20 ns RMSD trajectory from the equilibrium phase. This method estimates the binding free energy (ΔG_total) by partitioning it into gas-phase energy (ΔG_gas, consisting of van der Waals energy ΔE_vdw and electrostatic energy ΔE_elec) and solvation energy (ΔG_solvation, including the polar contribution ΔE_GB and the nonpolar contribution ΔE_surf).

As summarized in Table 3, the total binding free energy of the TGF-β1–Liriodenine complex was calculated to be − 23.39 kcal/mol, indicating a favorable binding affinity between the ligand and its target protein. The gas-phase energy (ΔG_gas = − 46.94 kcal/mol) was the major driving force, primarily contributed by significant van der Waals interactions (ΔE_vdw = − 28.30 kcal/mol) and electrostatic interactions (ΔE_elec = − 18.65 kcal/mol). Although the solvation energy was positive (ΔG_solvation = 23.55 kcal/mol), particularly the polar component (ΔE_GB =26.66 kcal/mol), the overall negative ΔG_total suggests that the binding process is thermodynamically favorable. These findings further support the stability of the complex and the effective binding capability of Liriodenine.

Liriodenine inhibits the proliferation of A549 cells

A549 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. After cell adherence, varying concentrations of liriodenine (2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM) were added to each well. Following a 24-hour incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 1 h. The results showed that cell viability decreased significantly with increasing concentrations of liriodenine compared with the control group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5A).

As shown in the colony formation assay (Fig. 5B), the number of colonies formed in the liriodenine-treated groups decreased significantly in a dose-dependent manner compared to the control group, with the difference reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Liriodenine inhibits migration of A549 cells

In this study, the wound healing assay was employed to further evaluate the inhibitory effect of Liriodenine on A549 cell migration. The results demonstrated that after 24 h of treatment, Liriodenine significantly suppressed A549 cell migration in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5C), indicating that Liriodenine effectively reduces the migratory capacity of these cells. The differences were statistically significant (p < 0.01).

Liriodenine induces apoptosis in A549 cells

A549 cells were treated with Liriodenine at concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, and 5 µM for 24 h. Compared to the control group, the apoptosis rate of A549 cells increased in a dose-dependent manner(p < 0.01), as shown in the Fig. 5D.

Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of Liriodenine on TGF-β1 expression in A549 cells

Western blot results demonstrated that Liriodenine treatment dose-dependently downregulated TGF-β1 protein expression in A549 cells compared to the control group. Quantitative analysis using GAPDH as the internal control revealed a significant decrease in TGF-β1 levels at concentrations of 2.5 µM and 5 µM (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively; see Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that Liriodenine may exert its biological effects by inhibiting the TGF-β1 signaling pathway.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple testing.

Disscusion

NSCLC is one of the most common lung cancer types worldwide and is associated with poor prognosis, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases [1]. Despite continuous advancements in therapeutic approaches in recent years, significantly prolonging survival remains challenging for patients with advanced-stage NSCLC. Therefore, exploring alternative antitumor strategies is crucial. Liriodenine, an aporphine alkaloid extracted from various medicinal plants such as Annona and Schisandra species [15], has been reported to possess multiple pharmacological activities, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-arrhythmic, and antitumor effects [16–18]. However, its mechanisms of action in NSCLC remain unclear, limiting its clinical application. To address this gap, we employed a multidisciplinary integrative research strategy combining network pharmacology, bioinformatics, and experimental validation to systematically investigate the potential targets of liriodenine.

In this study, four key hub genes—including TGF-β1, JUN, SRC, and ERBB2—were identified through PPI network construction and machine learning-based analyses, suggesting that liriodenine may exert its antitumor effects by modulating multiple critical signaling pathways. Subsequently, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding affinities of liriodenine to these candidate targets. Among them, TGF-β1 exhibited the lowest binding free energy (− 8.6 kcal/mol), indicating a strong interaction. MD simulations further demonstrated the structural stability of the TGFB1-liriodenine complex throughout the simulation period. These findings suggest that TGF-β1 is likely the primary therapeutic target of liriodenine in NSCLC. In mammals, the TGF-β family consists of three isoforms—TGF-β1, TGF-β2, and TGF-β3—which are encoded by distinct genetic loci. Among these, TGF-β1 is the most abundantly expressed subtype across various tissues. TGF-β signaling regulates downstream gene expression via both the canonical Smad-dependent pathway—in which phosphorylated Smad2/3 form a complex with Smad4, translocate to the nucleus, and interact with transcription factors to activate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related genes—and non-canonical Smad-independent pathways, including the MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascades, which collectively enhance tumor cell motility, invasiveness, and drug resistance [19, 20]. Moreover, TGF-β can promote the enrichment of immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2-polarized macrophages, within the tumor microenvironment. This enrichment attenuates antitumor immune responses and enhances immune tolerance to tumor-associated antigens, thereby further facilitating tumor immune evasion [21].

TGF-β1 is a multifunctional cytokine that plays a pivotal role in numerous biological processes, including embryonic development, cellular and tissue differentiation, angiogenesis, wound healing, immune regulation, and tumorigenesis [22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that TGF-β1 exhibits a “double-edged sword” effect during different stages of tumor development and progression. In the early stages of tumorigenesis, TGF-β1 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting epithelial cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis. However, during tumor progression, TGF-β1 facilitates EMT, immune evasion, and angiogenesis, thereby enhancing the invasiveness and metastatic potential of cancer cells [23, 24]. In cancer patients, the activation of the TGF-β1 signaling pathway is considered one of the key mechanisms driving EMT and tumor metastasis [25, 26]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting TGF-β1 and its downstream EMT-related mechanisms are considered promising interventions for NSCLC, particularly in suppressing metastatic progression. Jeong et al. [27] investigated the regulatory effects of a novel class of inhibitors on the TGF-β1 signaling pathway. Their findings revealed that TGF-β1 stimulation significantly enhanced the migration and invasion of A549 cells, accompanied by increased phosphorylation of Smad2 and upregulation of EMT markers such as N-cadherin, MMP-2, and MMP-9, confirming its key role in EMT induction. treatment with the inhibitor markedly suppressed TGF-β1-induced migratory and invasive abilities. This was associated with increased expression of E-cadherin and reduced Smad2 phosphorylation, indicating that the inhibitor effectively blocked TGF-β1/Smad signaling and reversed the EMT phenotype induced by TGF-β1. He et al. reported similar findings, demonstrating that Bu-Fei Decoction (BFD) effectively inhibits TGF-β1-induced migration and EMT in NSCLC cells. Their study revealed that BFD suppresses the phosphorylation of Smad2/3, thereby interfering with the canonical Smad signaling pathway and dose-dependently inhibiting the TGF-β1-induced EMT process [28]. These results further confirm the critical role of TGF-β1 in lung cancer progression and provide a theoretical basis for the development of multi-component natural products targeting this pathway. Another study further supports this view, demonstrating that Cucurbitacin B (CuB) inhibits TGF-β1 expression in vitro, significantly suppressing cell migration and invasion. Moreover, CuB effectively inhibited tumor growth in a mouse model [29]. Notably, TGF-β1-induced EMT not only promotes tumor cell invasion and metastasis but has also been reported to be associated with therapeutic resistance in lung cancer. During EMT, cancer cells may acquire stem cell-like properties, exhibit increased drug efflux activity, and develop resistance to apoptosis, thereby reducing their sensitivity to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [30–32]. Taken together, the TGF-β1 signaling pathway may potentially influence pathways associated with therapeutic resistance, suggesting that targeting TGF-β1 could provide an opportunity to help certain patients overcome resistance to current therapeutic strategies. In summary, the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway serves as a central regulator of EMT and tumor metastasis in NSCLC and has emerged as a critical target for multiple natural compounds exhibiting anti-metastatic effects. These findings provide a solid theoretical foundation for the clinical translation of therapies targeting this pathway.

Following the network pharmacology prediction that liriodenine may exert therapeutic effects against NSCLC, a series of in vitro experiments were conducted to validate this hypothesis. First, the anticancer activity of liriodenine was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, and its IC50 value in A549 cells was determined. The inhibitory effect was evidenced not only by reduced short-term cell viability but also by a significant decrease in colony formation capacity, suggesting that liriodenine effectively limits tumor cell self-renewal and sustained proliferation. The wound healing assay demonstrated that liriodenine markedly suppressed the migration rate of A549 cells, indicating a potential impact on EMT-related processes, thereby inhibiting tumor cell invasion and metastatic potential. Further flow cytometry analysis revealed that liriodenine significantly induced apoptosis in A549 cells, implying that its antitumor mechanism involves not only proliferation inhibition but also activation of programmed cell death pathways. Induction of tumor cell apoptosis is a well-established mechanism of many classical anticancer agents [33], and liriodenine’s activity in this regard further supports its potential as an anticancer drug. To explore the underlying mechanism, TGF-β1 protein expression was evaluated. Western blot results showed that liriodenine significantly downregulated TGF-β1 expression in a dose-dependent manner. Combined with previous molecular docking and dynamics simulation data, these findings further support TGF-β1 as a probable direct target of liriodenine. As a key pro-metastatic factor in NSCLC, TGF-β1 is known to promote tumor progression and is suggested to contribute to therapeutic resistance through EMT and immune evasion pathways. Taken together, the analyses and experimental results of this study suggest that liriodenine may exert its antitumor effects by targeting TGF-β1 and potentially inhibiting TGF-β1–mediated EMT. This provides a molecular basis for its regulatory effects on cell migration and apoptosis and supports further investigation of anticancer strategies targeting the TGF-β1 axis.

It is noteworthy that, although this study provides preliminary evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of liriodenine against NSCLC at the cellular level, several limitations remain. First, we have not yet investigated whether downstream signaling pathways of TGF-β1—such as Smad, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK—are involved in the antitumor effects of liriodenine, and Western blot analysis focused solely on TGF-β1 without assessing downstream EMT markers such as E-cadherin, N-cadherin, vimentin, or Smad2/3 phosphorylation. Evaluating these EMT-related proteins is essential to confirm the mechanistic link between TGF-β1 inhibition and EMT regulation, which will be addressed in future studies. Second, this study employed only the A549 cell line as an in vitro model of NSCLC. Although A549 cells, derived from lung adenocarcinoma, are widely used and exhibit representative features of this subtype, NSCLC comprises a heterogeneous group of histological subtypes, including squamous cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma, which may differ substantially in their molecular mechanisms and therapeutic responses. To date, the antitumor effects of liriodenine have not been validated in other NSCLC subtypes. In future studies, a broader range of cell lines and animal models will be employed to comprehensively assess the in vivo efficacy of liriodenine. It should be noted that no normal lung epithelial cell line (e.g., BEAS-2B) was included as a toxicity control in the current study. Therefore, the potential cytotoxicity of liriodenine on normal lung cells has not been directly evaluated, which represents a limitation of our work. Future studies will include appropriate normal lung epithelial cell lines to systematically assess the safety profile of liriodenine. Furthermore, whether liriodenine exerts its antitumor activity through additional key targets or molecular pathways remains to be elucidated, which warrants further investigation using multi-omics approaches such as transcriptomics and proteomics.

Conclusions

In summary, this study preliminarily explored the potential mechanism of liriodenine in NSCLC through network pharmacology and bioinformatics analyses. In vitro experiments demonstrated that liriodenine inhibited the proliferation of A549 cells, induced apoptosis, and downregulated the expression of TGF-β1, suggesting its potential antitumor activity. Further studies using animal models and clinical samples are needed to validate its efficacy, safety, and translational potential.
