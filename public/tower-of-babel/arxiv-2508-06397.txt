The convex structure of the Parisi formula for multi-species spin glasses
Hong-Bin Chen, Victor Issa, Jean-Christophe Mourrat

Abstract
. We study the free energy of mean-field multi-species spin glasses with convex covariance function. For such models with D D species, the Parisi formula is known to be valid, and expresses the limit free energy as a supremum over monotone probability measures on ℝ + D \mathbb{R}_{+}^{D} . We show here that one can transform this representation into a supremum over all probability measures on ℝ + D \mathbb{R}_{+}^{D} of a concave functional. We then deduce that the Parisi formula admits a unique maximizer. Using convex-duality arguments, we also obtain a new representation of the free energy as an infimum over martingales in a Wiener space. Keywords and phrases: mean-field spin glass, Parisi formula, Hamilton–Jacobi equation MSC 2020: 82D30, 82B44, 35D40

Introduction

We study mean-field spin-glass models with multiple species. Fixing an integer
{D}\geqslant 1
, we write
[D]=\{1,\dots,{D}\}
to denote the set of different species of spins.
For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we give ourselves
(I_{N,d})_{d\in{[D]}}
a partition of
\{1,\dots,N\}
, and interpret each
I_{N,d}
as the set of indices that belong to the
d
-th species. For any two configurations
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
d\in[D]
, we define the overlap for the
d
-th species as

(1.1)

R_{N,d}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in I_{N,d}}\sigma_{n}\sigma^{\prime}_{n},

and we set

(1.2)

R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=(R_{N,d}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}))_{d\in[D]}.

We give ourselves a function
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and a centered Gaussian field
(H_{N}(\sigma))_{\sigma\in[-1,+1]^{N}}
with covariance

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{N}(\sigma)H_{N}(\sigma^{\prime})\right]=N\xi\left(R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\right).

Throughout, we assume that the function
\xi
admits an absolutely convergent power-series expansion and satisfies
\xi(0)=0
, and we make the key assumption that
the function
\xi
is convex on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
.
The definitions of the free energy and Gibbs measure of the spin glass require that we also choose a reference probability measure. For each species
d\in[D]
, we fix a probability measure
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
with support in
[-1,1]
that is not a Dirac mass, and let

(1.4)

\mathrm{d}P_{N}(\sigma)=\bigotimes_{d\in[D]}\bigotimes_{n\in I_{N,d}}\mathrm{d}{\bm{\pi}}_{d}(\sigma_{n}).

In words, when
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{N})
is sampled according to
P_{N}
, the coordinates of
\sigma
are independent, and the law of
\sigma_{n}
is
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
if
n
belongs to
I_{N,d}
. For each
d\in[D]
, the proportion of spins that belong to the
d
-th species is given by

(1.5)

\lambda_{N,d}=|I_{N,d}|/N,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and we set }\lambda_{N}=\left(\lambda_{N,d}\right)_{d\in{[D]}}.

We assume throughout that for some
\lambda_{\infty}=(\lambda_{\infty,d})_{d\in[D]}\in(0,1)^{D}
, we have

(1.6)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{N}=\lambda_{\infty}.

The main object of study of this paper is the limiting value as
N\to+\infty
of the free energy defined for every
t\geqslant 0
by

(1.7)

\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0})=-\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\int\exp\left(\sqrt{2t}H_{N}(\sigma)-Nt\xi\left(R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}P_{N}(\sigma).

The term
-Nt\xi\left(R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma)\right)
is introduced as a convenience to simplify the expression of the limit. One possible way to remove it a posteriori is described in
[
43
]
; we also
point out that this term is constant if each
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
is supported in
\{-1,+1\}
. In (
1.7
), the second argument of
\overline{F}_{N}
is a Dirac mass at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and we will extend this function to more general arguments below.

In order to state the Parisi formula for the limit of (
1.7
), we introduce further notation. We write
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,+\infty)
, and let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
denote the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
. We say that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
is
monotone
when there exists an increasing map
\mathsf{q}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
such that
\mu=\mathrm{Law}(\mathsf{q}(U))
with
U
a uniform random variable in
[0,1]
. Here and throughout, the word “increasing” (and likewise for “decreasing”) is understood in the sense of non-strict inequalities, that is, we are asking here that
\mathsf{q}(t)-\mathsf{q}(s)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
for every
s\leqslant t\in[0,1)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
the collection of monotone probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
.
For every
p\geqslant 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
the set of probability measures with finite
p
-th moment, by
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
the set of compactly supported probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, and we set
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})=\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})\cap\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
.

For all the models considered here, the Parisi formula is known to be valid
[
4
,
9
,
13
,
27
,
46
,
47
,
60
]
. We give two versions of the Parisi formula, the first one in (
1.11
) being the most classical, and the second one in (
1.12
) being the one which will best clarify its relationship with the sequel. For each
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
and
d\in[D]
, we denote by
\nu_{d}
the
d
-th marginal of
\nu
; in other words,
\nu_{d}
is the image of
\nu
through the mapping
x\mapsto x_{d}
.
The first term in the Parisi formula is given, for each
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, by

(1.8)

\psi(\nu)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\,\psi_{d}(\nu_{d}),

where for each
d\in[D]
, the function
\psi_{d}
is the cascade transform of the reference measure
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
defined precisely in (
2.16
) (see also Lemma
3.1
for an alternative characterization).
It follows from
[
2
]
that the functions
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{D}
are each concave over
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. The formula (
1.8
) therefore allows us to extend
\psi
into a concave (but not strictly concave) function on all of
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
.
For every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we set

(1.9)

\theta(y)=y\cdot\nabla\xi(y)-\xi(y),

(1.10)

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\{x\cdot y-\xi(x)\},

and similarly for
(t\xi)^{*}
for any
t\geqslant 0
. We note that for every
t>0
, we have
(t\xi)^{*}=t\,\xi^{*}\left({\,\cdot\,}/t\right)
.

Theorem 1.1

(Parisi formula)
.

For every
t\geqslant 0
, we have

(1.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\psi((t\nabla\xi)(\mu))-t\int\theta\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right\}

(1.12)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int(t\xi)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}

where
(t\nabla\xi)(\mu)
denotes the image of the measure
\mu
through the mapping
x\mapsto t\nabla\xi(x)
.

A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
that realizes the supremum in (
1.11
) is usually called a Parisi measure; this measure can often be interpreted as the asymptotic law of the overlap under the Gibbs measure (see
[
13
, Corollary 8.7]
or
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
for precise statements). The existence of a Parisi measure can be obtained via coercivity and compactness arguments.
For models with a single species (
D=1
), it was proved in
[
2
]
that the Parisi measure is unique. Here we extend this result to all multi-species models.

Theorem 1.2

(Uniqueness of Parisi measure)
.

For every
t\geqslant 0
, the supremum in (
1.12
) is achieved at exactly one measure, say
\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
; and the supremum in (
1.11
) is achieved at all measures
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
that satisfy
\overline{\nu}=(t\nabla\xi)(\overline{\mu})
, and only at those measures.

As already noted, although the function
\psi
is initially only defined on
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
, the formula in (
1.8
) readily allows us to extend it into a concave function defined on all of
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
. In order to show Theorem
1.2
, we aim to relax the maximization problem in (
1.12
) into one that is posed over all of
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
. In our extension to this variational problem, the last term in there is replaced by the following optimal-transport cost. For every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we denote by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
with first marginal
\mu
and second marginal
\nu
. For each
t\geqslant 0
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we set

(1.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int(t\xi)^{*}(y-x)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y).

In this introduction we have only defined
\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0})
in (
1.7
), but below we define
\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)
for all
(t,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
, and we state our next results for arbitrary arguments.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
(t,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we have

(1.14)

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)\right\}.

Moreover, the functional inside the supremum in (
1.14
) is concave in
\nu
. Finally, there is exactly one measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
that is monotone and realizes the supremum in (
1.14
).

Using convex-duality arguments inspired by
[
31
,
42
]
, we then deduce from Theorem
1.3
a representation of the limit free energy as an infimum.
We denote by
\mathfrak{X}
the set of functions
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form
\chi(x)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\chi_{d}(x_{d})
where
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{D}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
are convex,
1
-Lipschitz, increasing functions that vanish at the origin. For every
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
and
t\geqslant 0
, we let

(1.15)

\displaystyle\psi_{*}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\int\chi\mathrm{d}\nu-\psi(\nu)\right\}

and

(1.16)

\displaystyle S_{t}\chi(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}}\left\{p\cdot x+t\xi(p)-\chi^{*}(p)\right\}

(1.17)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}}\left\{\chi(y)-(t\xi)^{*}(y-x)\right\}.

In (
1.16
), the definition of
\chi^{*}
is as in (
1.10
). The equality between (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) will be explained below in Lemma
5.12
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every
(t,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we have

(1.18)

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\inf_{\chi\in\mathfrak{X}}\left\{\int S_{t}\chi\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\psi_{*}(\chi)\right\}.

Finally, we describe a more concrete representation of the limit free energy as an infimum over martingales. Let
\mathscr{P}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
be a probability space with associated expectation
\mathbf{E}
, and let

(\mathcal{F}_{1}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(\mathcal{F}_{D}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
be an independent family of filtrations over
\mathscr{P}
. We assume that all these
\sigma
-algebras are complete, that is, they all contain every subset of any null-measure set. We also assume that the probability space is rich enough to accommodate for the existence of independent Brownian motions
(B_{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(B_{D}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
that are adapted to the filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{1}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(\mathcal{F}_{D}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
respectively. We denote by
\mathbf{Mart}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{Mart}_{D}
the spaces of bounded martingales over
\mathscr{P}
with respect to the filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{1}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(\mathcal{F}_{D}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
respectively, and we set

(1.19)

\mathbf{Mart}=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mathbf{Mart}_{d}.

For every
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{D})\in\mathbf{Mart}
, we define
\chi_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for every
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{D})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
,

(1.20)

\chi_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\int_{0}^{x_{d}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{d}(t)^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

We also define, for every
t\geqslant 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(1.21)

\phi_{d}(t,x)=\log\int\exp\left(x\sigma-t|\sigma|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{\pi}}_{d}(x),

and set, for every
t\geqslant 0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(1.22)

\phi_{d}^{*}(t,y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{x\cdot y-\phi_{d}(t,x)\right\}.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every
t\geqslant 0
,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
, and
T=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{D})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
such that
\supp(\mu)+t\nabla\xi([0,1]^{D})\subseteq\prod_{d=1}^{D}[0,T_{d}]
, we have

(1.23)

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}}\bigg\{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\,\mathbf{E}\left[\phi^{*}_{d}(T_{d},\alpha_{d}(T_{d}))-\sqrt{2}\alpha_{d}(T_{d})B_{d}(T_{d})\right]\\
-\chi_{\alpha}(T)+\int S_{t}\chi_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\mu\bigg\}.

In addition the infimum in (
1.23
) is reached at a unique
\overline{\alpha}\in\mathbf{Mart}
. This martingale
\overline{\alpha}
is such that for every
d\in[D]
and almost every
t\in[0,T_{d}]
, we have
\overline{\alpha}_{d}(t)=\partial_{x}\Phi_{\overline{\nu}_{d}}(t,X_{d}(t))
, where
\overline{\nu}
is the unique monotone measure that realizes the supremum in (
1.14
),
\Phi_{\overline{\nu}_{d}}
is defined in (
3.2
) subject to the terminal condition (
3.3
), and
X_{d}
is defined in (

).

We conjecture that Theorems

to converge to the unique viscosity solution to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f-\int\xi(\partial_{\mu}f)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0&\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}),\\

The same result may also be valid in the case when

As discussed more precisely in

Proof ideas.
For the choice of
\mu=\delta_{0}
, and in view of (
1.12
), the task of showing (
1.14
) boils down to showing that

(1.25)

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int(t\xi)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int(t\xi)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}.

For each measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
, there is a unique measure
\nu^{\uparrow}
that has the same marginals as
\nu
and is a monotone measure. Due to the equality of the marginals and (
1.8
), we have that
\psi(\nu)=\psi(\nu^{\uparrow})
, so surely the most natural idea for showing (
1.25
) would be to assert that

(1.26)

\int(t\xi)^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\uparrow}\leqslant\int(t\xi)^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu.

We show in Proposition
4.1
that the inequality (
1.26
) is indeed valid when
D=2
and
\nabla\xi(0)=0
. However, we also show in Remark
4.3
that the inequality (
1.26
) is false in general, so another strategy must be developed towards the proof of our main results.

Our approach consists instead of a careful analysis of the optimizers of the variational formula in (
1.14
). First, recall that
the definition of
S_{t}\chi
from (
1.17
) implies that for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
,

(1.27)

\chi(y)-S_{t}\chi(x)\leqslant(t\xi)^{*}(y-x),

with equality when
y
is a maximizer in (
1.17
). From this, we check in Lemma
6.4
that

(1.28)

\int S_{t}\chi\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})}\left\{\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)\right\}.

Letting
\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
denote a maximizer of (
1.14
), and
\chi
denote the derivative of
\psi
at
\overline{\nu}
, we observe that, thanks to the convexity of the mapping
\nu\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)
, we have that

(1.29)

\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\overline{\nu}-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\overline{\nu})=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})}\left\{\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)\right\},

and thus, in view of (
1.28
), that

(1.30)

\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\overline{\nu}-\int S_{t}\chi\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\overline{\nu}).

Denoting by
\pi
the optimal transport plan from
\mu
to
\overline{\nu}
, the previous display tells us that the inequality (
1.27
) is in fact an equality for
\mathrm{d}\pi
-almost every
(x,y)
. Heuristically, we then want to exploit certain monotonicity properties of the mappings
\nabla\chi
and
\nabla S_{t}\chi
to deduce that if
\mu
is monotone, then so is
\overline{\nu}
. The details are a bit more subtle, as it may well be that the measure
\overline{\nu}
itself is actually not monotone, and we need to smear out the measure
\mu
in order to effectuate this transfer of monotonicity, but we ultimately show that we can build a maximizing measure for (
1.14
) that is indeed monotone whenever
\mu
itself is monotone. The bulk of the technical work consists in showing that
\nabla\chi
and
\nabla S_{t}\chi
satisfy the desired monotonicity properties. Once Theorem
1.3
is shown, we obtain Theorem
1.2
as a direct consequence, while the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
rely on convex-duality arguments that are inspired by
[
31
,
42
]
.

Related works.

The Parisi formula was initially predicted using the non-rigorous replica method in the case of the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model (i.e. for
D=1
,
\xi(r)=r^{2}
, and
P_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{-1})
) in
[
36
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
, and was later proved rigorously in
[
27
,
60
]
. A more conceptual proof based on the ultrametricity of the Gibbs measure was later developed
[
45
,
46
]
, and allowed all models with a single type of spins (
D=1
) to be covered. This ultrametricity property was further leveraged in order to identify the limit free energy of spin glasses with multiple types and convex
\xi

[
4
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
. One key ingredient of the proof consists in showing that, up to a small perturbation of the energy function, the overlaps between the different types of spins must synchronize with one another; in other words, the joint law of the overlaps must be a monotone measure.
Theorem
1.3
shows that one can in fact relax the final optimization problem into a “desynchronized” one, where the measure is no longer constrained to be monotone.
Concurrent developments for spherical models include
[
17
,
59
]
for models with a single type of spins, and
[
5
]
for multi-type models. For models with additional symmetries, the limit formula may in certain cases be simplified into an expression corresponding to an effective single-type model
[
6
,
7
,
10
,
30
,
41
]
.

Models with non-convex
\xi
are less well-understood, and even the existence of the limit free energy is not proved in general. Yet, under the assumption that the limit free energy exists, its value has been identified in
[
57
,
58
]
for all spherical models in the case when
\xi
is a monomial. Still for spherical models, the case of
D=2
and
\xi(x,y)=xy
has been obtained unconditionally in
[
1
,
3
]
, and the cases of
\xi(x,y)=x^{p}y^{q}
and
\xi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{D})=x_{1}\cdots\,x_{D}
have also been obtained unconditionally for particular choices of the parameter
\lambda_{\infty}
in
[
7
,
19
]
.

Outside of these cases, even stating an appropriate conjecture for the limit free energy is a non-trivial task. Indeed, several possible candidate variational formulas for the limit free energy, inspired by the classical Parisi formula, were shown to be invalid in
[
38
, Section 6]
. A candidate for the limit free energy, defined in terms of the solution to a Hamilton-Jacobi equation, was proposed in
[
16
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
, and this candidate was shown there to be a lower bound for the limit free energy. Under the assumption that the limit free energy exists, a representation of this limit in terms of a critical point of an explicit functional was also found in
[
9
,
13
]
. This description is in the spirit of the cavity fixed-point equations appearing in the physics literature. It does not completely characterize the limit however, as there may be more than one critical point in general.

As discussed around (
1.24
), and despite the negative results of
[
38
, Section 6]
, we now believe that there is in fact a plausible candidate variational formula for the limit free energy for non-convex
\xi
. The point is that it does
not
take a form similar to the classical Parisi formula, but rather a form as in (
1.23
). Such alternative “un-inverted” formulas first appeared in the context of models with a single type of spins in
[
42
]
, and were further developed in
[
31
]
, with results close to Theorem
1.4
for vector spin glasses. A key ingredient for our analysis as well as in
[
42
]
is the fact that the function
\psi
is concave. This was first proved for models with a single type of spins in
[
2
]
; the uniqueness of the Parisi measure was also obtained there in this case. For spherical models, the uniqueness of the Parisi measure can be seen more directly, and a similar convex-duality analysis was performed in
[
33
,
34
]
. We also mention that for vector spin glasses with convex
\xi
, the uniqueness of the Parisi measure was shown to be valid in
[
11
]
provided that the measure argument
\mu
does not charge any single point.

These “un-inverted” formulas are in turn inspired by a series of works concerning the construction of efficient algorithms that aim to identify configurations
\sigma
in the support of
P_{N}
such that
H_{N}(\sigma)
is as large as possible. For many models, an explicit quantity
\mathsf{ALG}
was identified as a variational formula analogous to the Parisi formula, such that the following holds. On the one hand, there exists an efficient algorithm that outputs a configuration
\sigma
in the support of
P_{N}
such that
H_{N}(\sigma)/N
is approximately equal to
\mathsf{ALG}
. On the other hand, for any
\varepsilon>0
, if an algorithm is Lipschitz continuous with respect to its entries, then it cannot reliably output a configuration
\sigma
in the support of
P_{N}
such that
H_{N}(\sigma)/N
exceeds
\mathsf{ALG}+\varepsilon
. Whether or not this quantity
\mathsf{ALG}
coincide with the limit of
\sup_{\sigma\in\supp P_{N}}H_{N}(\sigma)
depends on the choice of
\xi
. At least for models with a single type, this quantity
\mathsf{ALG}
can also be written as an “un-inverted” formula very similar to that found in
[
42
]
.

An algorithm that indeed achieves the correct value
\mathsf{ALG}
was first put forward in
[
56
]
for spherical models with a single type of spins. The algorithm is based on a Hessian ascent. Using a different approach based on approximate message passing algorithms, this result was extended to models with
\pm 1
spins in
[
21
,
37
,
54
]
. For models with
\pm 1
spins, a Hessian-ascent algorithm was also constructed in
[
35
]
.
The converse statement, that no Lipschitz algorithm can identify configurations with energy exceeding
\mathsf{ALG}
, is based on the notion of overlap gap property
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
29
]
.
Remarkably, the identification of this threshold
\mathsf{ALG}
has been extended to spherical models with multiple types, including for models with non-convex
\xi
for which the limit free energy is not currently known
[
28
,
29
]
.

2.
Definition of the enriched free energy

The goal of this section is to give a precise definition of the enriched free energy
\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)
. We start by giving a definition of this quantity in the case when the measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
has a finite support. For every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we write
x\leqslant y
when
y-x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
.
Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
be of the form

(2.1)

\mu=\sum_{k=0}^{K}(\zeta_{k+1}-\zeta_{k})\delta_{q_{k}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with marginals}\hskip 10.00002pt\mu_{d}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}(\zeta_{k+1}-\zeta_{k})\delta_{q_{k,d}}

for each
d\in{[D]}
, where
\zeta_{k}
and
q_{k}=(q_{k,d})_{d\in{[D]}}
satisfy

(2.2)

\displaystyle 0=q_{-1,d}\leqslant q_{0,d}<q_{1,d}<\dots<q_{K,d},

(2.3)

\displaystyle 0=\zeta_{0}<\zeta_{1}<\dots<\zeta_{K}<\zeta_{K+1}=1.

The construction of
\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)
involves a random probability measure with ultrametric properties called the Poisson–Dirichlet cascade. We briefly introduce this object and refer to
[
46
, Section 2.3]
or
[
20
, Section 5.6]
for a more detailed explanation. We define

(2.4)

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{N}^{0}\cup\mathbb{N}^{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathbb{N}^{K}

with the understanding that
\mathbb{N}^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
. We think of
\mathcal{A}
as a tree rooted at
\emptyset
such that each vertex of depth
k<K
has countably many children. For each
k<K
and
\alpha=(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, the children of
\alpha
are the vertices of the form

(2.5)

\alpha n=(n_{1},\dots,n_{k},n)\in\mathbb{N}^{k+1}.

The depth of
\alpha=(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})
is denoted by
|\alpha|=k
and for every
l\leqslant k
, we write

(2.6)

\alpha_{|l}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{l})

to denote the ancestor of
\alpha
at depth
l
. Given two leaves
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{K}
, we denote by
\alpha\wedge\beta
the generation of the most recent common ancestor of
\alpha
and
\beta
, that is

(2.7)

\alpha\wedge\beta=\sup\{k\leqslant K:\>\alpha_{|k}=\beta_{|k}\}.

We attach an independent Poisson process to each non-leaf vertex
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
with intensity measure

(2.8)

x^{-1-\zeta_{|\alpha|+1}}\mathrm{d}x.

We order increasingly the points of those Poisson processes and denote them by
u_{\alpha 1}\geqslant u_{\alpha 2}\geqslant\dots
. For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}
, we set
w_{\alpha}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}u_{\alpha_{|k}}
and define

(2.9)

v_{\alpha}=\frac{w_{\alpha}}{\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}w_{\beta}}.

The Poisson–Dirichlet cascade associated to
(\zeta_{k})_{1\leqslant K+1}
in (
2.3
) is the random probability measure on
\mathbb{N}^{K}
(the leaves of the tree
\mathcal{A}
) whose weights are given by
(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}
.

Now, let
(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}
be the Poisson–Dirichlet cascade associated to
(\zeta_{k})_{1\leqslant K+1}
in (
2.3
), chosen to be independent of
H_{N}
.
Let
(z_{\beta,d})_{\beta\in\mathcal{A},d\in[D]}
be a family of independent standard Gaussian variables. We choose
(z_{\beta,d})_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}
independent of
(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}
and
H_{N}
. For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}
, we set

(2.10)

w^{\mu_{d}}(\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}(q_{k,d}-q_{k-1,d})^{1/2}z_{\alpha_{|k},d}

with
(q_{k,d})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K}
given in (
2.2
).
The centered Gaussian process
(w^{\mu_{d}}(\alpha))_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}
has the following covariance structure

(2.11)

\mathbb{E}\left[w^{\mu_{d}}(\alpha)w^{\mu_{d}}(\alpha^{\prime})\right]=q_{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime},d}.

Let
(\omega^{\mu_{d}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. copies of
\omega^{\mu_{d}}
.
For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

(2.12)

\displaystyle W^{\mu}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha)=\sum_{d\in{[D]}}\sum_{n\in I_{N,d}}w^{\mu_{d}}_{n}(\alpha)\cdot\sigma_{n}

which is a centered Gaussian process with covariance

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W^{\mu}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha)W^{\mu}_{N}(\sigma^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\right]=Nq_{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}}\cdot R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

For
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mu
given in (
2.1
), we define the enriched Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H^{t,\mu}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha)=\sqrt{2t}H_{N}(\sigma)-tN\xi\left(R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma)\right)+\sqrt{2}W^{\mu}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha)-Nq_{K}\cdot R_{N}(\sigma,\sigma),

where
H_{N}
is given as in (
1.3
). We define

(2.14)

\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=-\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\int\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}\exp\left(H_{N}^{t,\mu}(\sigma,\alpha)\right)v_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}P_{N}(\sigma).

The expression in the previous display is Lipschitz with respect to
(t,\mu)
. More precisely, for every
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and every
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
of the form in (
2.1
), we have

(2.15)

|\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)-\overline{F}_{N}(t^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})|\leqslant\mathsf{W}_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})+|t-t^{\prime}|\sup_{|a|\leqslant 1}|\xi(a)|,

where
\mathsf{W}_{1}
denotes the Wasserstein
L^{1}
distance between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
. This is borrowed from
[
9
, Proposition 4.1]
, where the term
\mathsf{W}_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is written as
\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathsf{q}}(s)-{\mathsf{q}}^{\prime}(s)|\mathrm{d}s
with
\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Law}({\mathsf{q}}(U))
and
\mu^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Law}({\mathsf{q}}^{\prime}(U))
for a uniform random variable
U
over
[0,1]
. The equality between the two expressions can be obtained from a straightforward modification of
[
40
, Proposition 2.5]
.

As a consequence, the free energy
\overline{F}_{N}
admits a unique Lipschitz extension to
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
.

Given
w^{\mu_{d}}
in (
2.10
) and
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
in (
1.4
), each
\psi_{d}
appearing in (
1.8
) is given by

(2.16)

\displaystyle\psi_{d}(\mu_{d})=-\mathbb{E}\log\int\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}\exp\left(\sqrt{2}w^{\mu_{d}}(\alpha)\tau-q_{K,d}\tau^{2}\right)v_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\bm{\pi}}_{d}(\tau)

at every
\mu
of the form in (
2.1
). A similar property as in (
2.15
) holds for
\psi_{d}
and thus
\psi_{d}
can be extended to
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
By an invariance property of
(\nu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}
(see e.g.
[
20
, Corollary 5.26]
and
[
9
, Lemma 4.11]
), we have

(2.17)

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{N}(0,\mu)=\sum_{d\in{[D]}}\lambda_{N,d}\psi_{d}(\mu_{d}),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}).

Therefore, due to (
1.6
) and (
1.8
), we have

(2.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(0,\mu)=\psi(\mu),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}).

In order to state the Parisi formula for
\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)
at a general measure
\mu
, we introduce the following notation. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the collection of càdlàg increasing maps
{\mathsf{q}}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, and let
U
denote a uniform random variable on
[0,1]
. For any
{\mathsf{q}}\in\mathcal{Q}
, we write

(2.19)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{q}}=\mathrm{Law}({\mathsf{q}}(U))\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}).

We also set
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}=\mathcal{Q}\cap L^{\infty}([0,1);\mathbb{R}^{D})
. It is clear that
{\mathsf{q}}\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{q}}
is a bijection from
\mathcal{Q}
to
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, and also from
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
to
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
.
The following is a restatement of
[
9
, Proposition 6.1]
which adapts
[
13
, Proposition 8.1]
from the setting of vector spin glasses.
It generalizes (
1.11
) in Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.1

(Parisi formula)
.

For every
t\geqslant 0
and
{\mathsf{q}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
, denoting
\mu=\mathcal{L}_{{\mathsf{q}}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we have

(2.20)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\sup_{{\mathsf{p}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}}\left\{\psi\left({\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathsf{q}}+t\nabla\xi({\mathsf{p}})}\right)-t\int\theta\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{p}}\right\}.

In (
2.20
), the notation
\nabla\xi({\mathsf{p}})
stands for the path
s\mapsto\nabla\xi({\mathsf{p}}(s))
, and
\int\theta\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{p}}
can be explicitly written as
\int_{0}^{1}\theta({\mathsf{p}}(s))\mathrm{d}s
. The right-hand side in (
2.20
) can be connected to the unique solution of a Hamilton–Jacobi equation
[
13
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
43
]
.

3.
Properties of the enriched free energy at infinite temperature

In this section, we derive useful properties of
\psi
. Due to the decomposition in (
1.8
), we mostly focus on the study of
\psi_{d}
for each
d\in[D]
.

3.1.
Properties of
\psi_{d}

We fix
d\in{[D]}
, and use the following notation in this subsection:

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\bm{\pi}}_{\circ}={\bm{\pi}}_{d}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\psi_{\circ}=\psi_{d}.

For
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we denote by
t\mapsto\nu(t)=\nu([0,t])
its cumulative distribution function, which is a right-continuous increasing function taking values in
[0,1]
.
When
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we denote by

\displaystyle\nu^{-1}(1)=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\>1\leqslant\nu(t)\}

the right endpoint of the support of
\nu
.
For
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we consider the backward parabolic equation

(3.2)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\Phi_{\nu}(t,x)=\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi_{\nu}(t,x)+\nu(t)\left(\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}\right)^{2}(t,x)\hskip 20.00003pt

with
(t,x)
ranging in
[0,\nu^{-1}(1)]\times\mathbb{R}
, and with the terminal condition

(3.3)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\nu}(\nu^{-1}(1),x)=\log\int\exp\left(x\sigma-\nu^{-1}(1)\sigma^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{\pi}}_{\circ}(\sigma),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

We call the equation (
3.2
) the
Parisi PDE
associated with
\nu
.
The fact that the function
\Phi_{\nu}
is well-defined can be found in
[
32
]
. When the support of
\nu
is finite, one can use the Cole-Hopf transform to solve (
3.2
) on each time sub-interval of
[0,\nu^{-1}(1))
on which
\nu(\cdot)
is constant; and then one can argue by continuity to build
\Phi_{\nu}
for arbitrary
\nu
(see for instance
[
20
, Section 6.5]
).
We extend
\Phi_{\nu}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
by setting

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\nu}(t,x)=\log\int\exp\left(x\sigma-t\sigma^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{\pi}}_{\circ}(\sigma),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in[\nu^{-1}(1),\infty)\times\mathbb{R}.

A simple computation reveals that this extension also satisfies the equation (
3.2
) on
[\nu^{-1}(1),\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.
We call
\Phi_{\nu}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the
Parisi PDE solution
associated with
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
We summarize the basic properties of the Parisi PDE solution in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(Regularity of Parisi PDE)
.

For every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
k\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, the derivative
\partial^{k}_{x}\Phi_{\nu}
exists everywhere and is bounded uniformly over
\nu,t,x
.
In particular,
|\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}|\leqslant 1
everywhere.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, there is a constant
C_{k}
such that for every
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{k}_{x}\Phi_{\nu}(t,x)-\partial^{k}_{x}\Phi_{\nu^{\prime}}(t,x)\right|\leqslant C_{k}\int_{t}^{\infty}\left|\nu(\tau)-\nu^{\prime}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau.

For every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{\circ}(\nu)=-\Phi_{\nu}(0,0).

Proof.

First, assume
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
For each
\nu
, there is an explicit expression for
\Phi_{\nu}
given in
[
12
, Lemma 4.5 and Remark 4.6]
(with
D=1
and
L(t)=t
for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
therein, and with
\nu
and
P_{1}
substituted for
\alpha
and
\mu
therein). In particular, both (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are satisfied. The relation to the initial condition as in (
3.6
) is given in
[
12
, Lemma 5.4]
. The fact that
\Phi_{\nu}
satisfies the equation and the regularity of
\Phi_{\nu}
are from
[
12
, Proposition 4.7]
. The particular case
|\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}|\leqslant 1
follows from
[
12
, Proposition 4.7 (3)]
and the fact that
{\bm{\pi}}_{\circ}
is supported in
[-1,1]
.
The inequality (
3.5
) then allows us to extend the results to every
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. To extend (
3.6
), we also use the continuity of
\psi_{\circ}
.
∎

We now recall the concavity of
\psi_{\circ}
originally due to
[
2
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Concavity of
\psi_{\circ}
)
.

The function
\psi_{\circ}:\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\mathbb{R}
is concave; that is, for every
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have
\psi_{\circ}((1-\lambda)\nu+\lambda\nu^{\prime})\geqslant(1-\lambda)\psi_{\circ}(\nu)+\lambda\psi_{\circ}(\nu^{\prime})
.

The restriction of
\psi_{\circ}
to
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is strictly concave; that is, for every distinct
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have
\psi_{\circ}((1-\lambda)\nu+\lambda\nu^{\prime})>(1-\lambda)\psi_{\circ}(\nu)+\lambda\psi_{\circ}(\nu^{\prime})
.

Proof.

By the Lipschitzness of
\psi_{\circ}
in
\nu
discussed in the previous section (or implied by (
3.5
) and (
3.6
)), it suffices to prove the strict concavity on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Fix any distinct
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. By (
3.4
), we can fix any
T>\nu^{-1}(1),\nu^{\prime-1}(1)
so that
\Phi_{\nu}
and
\Phi_{\nu^{\prime}}
both satisfy the Parisi PDE (
3.2
) on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
with the same terminal condition equal to
x\mapsto\log\int\exp(x\cdot\sigma-T\sigma^{2})\mathrm{d}{\bm{\pi}}_{\circ}(\sigma)
.
Then, the concavity follows from (
3.6
) and the strict convexity of
\nu\mapsto\Phi_{\nu}(0,0)
on the set of probability measures with support in
[0,T]
, which is available from a straightforward modification of
[
2
, Theorem 4 (i)]
.
∎

In order to determine the derivative of
\psi_{\circ}
, we introduce an adjoint equation to the Parisi PDE, for each
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
Let
P:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the one-dimensional heat kernel, namely

(3.7)

P(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}.

Since
\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}
is bounded due to Lemma
3.1
, we can use a Picard fixed-point argument in a suitable function space to build a function
u_{\nu}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle u_{\nu}(t,x)=P(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{x}P(t-s,x-y)u_{\nu}(s,y)\left(2\nu(s)\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}(s,y)\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s.

In other words, the function
u_{\nu}
is a mild solution to

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{\nu}=\partial_{x}^{2}u_{\nu}-2\nu(t)\partial_{x}\left(u_{\nu}\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}\right),\qquad&\text{on $\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}$},\\
u_{\nu}(0,\cdot)=\delta_{0},\qquad&\text{on $\mathbb{R}$},\end{cases}

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac mass at zero. One can verify that
u_{\nu}
is also a weak solution to this equation.
From the mild solution formulation, one can check that
u_{\nu}
and its derivatives in
x
have exponential decay in
x
, which allows for integration by parts. We also recognize that (
3.8
) is a Kolmogorov equation and thus
u_{\nu}(t,\cdot)
can be interpreted as the probability density function of a stochastic process at time
t
. Therefore,
u_{\nu}
also satisfies

(3.9)

\displaystyle u_{\nu}\geqslant 0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\int u_{\nu}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

We use the properties of
u_{\nu}
to prove the next two lemmas.

Lemma 3.3

(Derivative of
\psi_{\circ}
)
.

Let
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and let
\nu_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)\nu+\varepsilon\nu^{\prime}
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. We have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\psi_{\circ}(\nu_{\varepsilon})\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi(t)\mathrm{d}(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)

where

(3.11)

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}(\tau,x)\right)^{2}u_{\nu}(\tau,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Notice that the right-hand side in (
3.10
) is well-defined and finite since the first moments of
\nu^{\prime}
and
\nu
exist and
|\chi(t)|\leqslant t
due to
\int u_{\nu}(\tau,x)\mathrm{d}x=1
and the boundedness of
|\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}|\leqslant 1
given by Lemma
3.1
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we write
\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{\nu_{\varepsilon}}
and
u=u_{\nu}
. For any
T>0
, we set
C_{\varepsilon}(T)=\int\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T,x)u(T,x)\mathrm{d}x
. Using
u(0,{\,\cdot\,})=\delta_{0}
and integrating by parts (or more precisely appealing to the weak formulation of (
3.8
)), we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(0,0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(0,x)u(0,x)\mathrm{d}x=C_{\varepsilon}(T)-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\Phi_{\varepsilon}u\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Using the equations satisfied by
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
and
u
in (
3.2
) and (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\Phi_{\varepsilon}u\right)=-u\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi_{\varepsilon}-\nu_{\varepsilon}u(\partial_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\Phi_{\varepsilon}\partial^{2}_{x}u-2\nu\Phi_{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}\Phi_{0}u).

Integrating this in
x
and using integration by parts, we can cancel the second-order terms and get

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(0,0)=C_{\varepsilon}(T)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\nu_{\varepsilon}a_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\nu a_{\varepsilon}a_{0}\right)u

where we used the shorthand
a_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(0,0)-\Phi_{0}(0,0)-\left(C_{\varepsilon}(T)-C_{0}(T)\right)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\underbrace{\nu_{\varepsilon}a_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\nu a_{\varepsilon}a_{0}+\nu a_{0}^{2}}_{\blacksquare}\big)u

We can rearrange
\blacksquare=(\nu_{\varepsilon}-\nu)a_{0}^{2}-(\nu_{\varepsilon}-\nu)(a_{\varepsilon}^{2}-a_{0}^{2})+\nu_{0}(a_{\varepsilon}-a_{0})^{2}
and we argue that the last two terms are of order
O(\varepsilon^{2})
uniformly in
\varepsilon,t,x
. Indeed, this follows from the facts that
\nu_{\varepsilon}-\nu=O(\varepsilon)
by definition, that
a_{\varepsilon}-a_{0}=O(|\nu_{\varepsilon}-\nu|_{L^{1}})=O(\varepsilon)
due to (
3.5
), and that
a_{\varepsilon},a_{0}=O(1)
by Lemma
3.1
. Using this and
\nu_{\varepsilon}-\nu=\varepsilon(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)
, we get
\blacksquare=\varepsilon(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)a_{0}^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
uniformly in
\varepsilon,t,x
.
Inserting this to the above display we get

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(0,0)-\Phi_{0}(0,0)-\left(C_{\varepsilon}(T)-C_{0}(T)\right)=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)a_{0}^{2}u+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e.chi=}}}{{=}}\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)\dot{\chi}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(IBP)}}}{{=}}-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\chi\mathrm{d}(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)+\varepsilon\left(\nu^{\prime}(T)-\nu(T)\right)\chi(T)++O(\varepsilon^{2}).

In the last step, we used the fact that
\chi(0)=0
. Using this relation and
\psi_{\circ}(\nu_{\varepsilon})=-\Phi_{\nu}(0,0)
(due to (
3.6
)), we can obtain (
3.10
) by first sending
T\to\infty
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
provided that we can show that, for any fixed
\varepsilon
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(T)-C_{0}(T)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\nu^{\prime}(T)-\nu(T)\right)\chi(T)

\displaystyle=0.

Hence, it remains to verify them.
Using the definition of
C_{\varepsilon}(T)
, the estimate in (
3.5
) (with
k=0
), and
\int u(T,x)\mathrm{d}x=1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|C_{\varepsilon}(T)-C_{0}(T)\right|\leqslant c_{0}\varepsilon\int_{T}^{\infty}|\nu(\tau)-\nu^{\prime}(\tau)|\mathrm{d}\tau

for some constant
c_{0}
.
Since
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\nu(\tau)-\nu^{\prime}(\tau)|\mathrm{d}\tau
is finite as it is equal to
\mathsf{W}_{1}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
, the right-hand side vanishes as
T\to\infty
, which verifies (
3.12
). To see (
3.13
), we start by recalling that
|\chi(t)|\leqslant t
as explained below Lemma
3.3
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left(\nu^{\prime}(T)-\nu(T)\right)\chi(T)\right|\leqslant T(1-\nu^{\prime}(T))+T(1-\nu(T)).

Let
X
be a random variable with law
\nu
. We have
T(1-\nu(T))=T\mathbb{P}(X>T)\leqslant\mathbb{E}[X\mathds{1}_{X>T}]
. Since
\nu
has finite first moment, the dominated convergence theorem implies
\lim_{T\to\infty}T(1-\nu^{\prime}(T))=0
. The other term can be treated similarly and thus we obtain (
3.13
). As explained previously, now that (
3.12
) and (

) are verified, the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.4

(Properties of
\chi
)
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and let
\chi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be given as in (

). Then,
\chi(0)=0
and
\chi
is increasing,
1
-Lipschitz, and convex.

Proof.

Due to
\dot{\chi}\geqslant 0
evidently from (
3.11
),
\chi
is increasing. By Lemma
3.1
,
|\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu}|\leqslant 1
everywhere. Using this and (
3.9
), we have
|\dot{\chi}|\leqslant 1
and thus
\chi
is
1
-Lipschitz. To show that
\chi
is convex, we compute
\ddot{\chi}
. For brevity of notation, we write
\Phi=\Phi_{\nu}
,
u=u_{\nu}
, and
\int=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x
. Using the equations satisfied by
\Phi
and
u
as in (
3.2
) and (
3.8
) and integration by parts (IBP), we can compute

\displaystyle\ddot{\chi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int(\partial_{x}\Phi)^{2}u=\int\left(2\partial_{t}\partial_{x}\Phi(u\partial_{x}\Phi)+(\partial_{x}\Phi_{x})^{2}\partial_{t}u\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(IBP)}}}{{=}}\int\left(-2\partial_{t}\Phi\partial_{x}(u\partial_{x}\Phi)+(\partial_{x}\Phi)^{2}\partial_{t}u\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e.Phi_nu_eqn}\eqref{e.u_nu=eqn}}}{{=}}\int\left(2\left(\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi+\nu(\partial_{x}\Phi)^{2}\right)\partial_{x}(u\partial_{x}\Phi)+(\partial_{x}\Phi)^{2}\left(\partial_{x}^{2}u-2\nu\partial_{x}(u\partial_{x}\Phi)\right)\right)

∎

3.2.
Properties of
\psi

Using the decomposition in (

), we obtain the Lipschitzness, concavity, and differentiability of
\psi
as stated below.
For each
p\geqslant 1
, let
\mathsf{W}_{p}
be the Wasserstein
L^{p}
metric over probability measures on a Euclidean space that will be clear from the context.

Recall that for any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
and
d\in{[D]}
, we denote by
\nu_{d}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
its
d
-th marginal.
Due to (
2.15
) and (
2.18
), we have, for all
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left|\psi(\nu)-\psi(\nu^{\prime})\right|\leqslant\mathsf{W}_{1}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}).

Proposition 3.5

(Concavity of
\psi
)
.

The function
\psi
is concave on
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
. Furthermore, for every
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, if there exists
d\in{[D]}
such that
\nu_{d}\neq\nu^{\prime}_{d}
and
\nu_{d},\nu^{\prime}_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, then we have, for every
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

(3.15)

\psi\left(\lambda\nu^{\prime}+(1-\lambda)\nu\right)>\lambda\psi(\nu^{\prime})+(1-\lambda)\psi(\nu).

Proof.

This follows from the decomposition in (
1.8
) and Lemma
3.2
stating that each
\psi_{d}
is concave on
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and strictly concave on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
∎

Note that the function
\psi
is not strictly concave on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
since it is possible for
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
to satisfy
\nu\neq\nu^{\prime}
while
\nu_{d}=\nu^{\prime}_{d}
for every

. However, the following is true.

Lemma 3.6

(Monotone measures characterized by marginals)
.

Let
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
. Then,
\nu=\nu^{\prime}
if and only if
\nu_{d}=\nu^{\prime}_{d}
for all

.

Proof.

By the definition of monotone measures (see the paragraph below (
1.7
)), there are two increasing maps
{\mathsf{q}},{\mathsf{q}}^{\prime}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
such that
\nu=\mathrm{Law}({\mathsf{q}}(U))
and
\nu^{\prime}=\mathrm{Law}({\mathsf{q}}(U))
where
U
is a uniform random variable in
[0,1]
. Then,
\nu_{d}=\nu^{\prime}_{d}
is equivalent to
{\mathsf{q}}_{d}={\mathsf{q}}^{\prime}_{d}
a.e. on
[0,1)
. The desired result follows from the simple observation that
{\mathsf{q}}={\mathsf{q}}^{\prime}
if and only if
{\mathsf{q}}_{d}={\mathsf{q}}^{\prime}_{d}
for all
d\in{[D]}
.
∎

The set
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
is not convex when

, so it does not make sense to talk about concavity or strict concavity of

on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
. Yet we have the following result, which is an immediate corollary of Proposition

and Lemma

.

Corollary 3.7

(“Strict concavity” of
\psi

\mathcal{P}_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
)
.

For any two distinct
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

(3.16)

\psi\left(\lambda\nu^{\prime}+(1-\lambda)\nu\right)>\lambda\psi(\nu^{\prime})+(1-\lambda)\psi(\nu).

We define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}=\left\{\chi:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}\bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}\text{$\chi(x)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\chi_{d}(x_{d})$, $\forall x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}$, where $\chi_{d}(0)=0$, }\\
\text{ $\chi_{d}$ is increasing, $1$-Lipschitz, and convex on $\mathbb{R}_{+}$}\end{array}\right\}.

Proposition 3.8

(Differentiability of
\psi
)
.

For every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, there is
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
such that, for every
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we have

(3.19)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\psi\left((1-\varepsilon)\nu+\varepsilon\nu^{\prime}\right)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\chi\mathrm{d}\left(\nu^{\prime}-\nu\right).

Proof.

This follows from the decomposition in (
1.8
) and Lemma
3.3
for the differentiability of each
\psi_{d}
, with
Lemma
3.4
ensuring the desired properties of each of the

in the decomposition of
\chi
.
∎

4.
Models with two species

In this section, we present relatively simple proofs of Theorems
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
in the special case of models with two species (
D=2
) with no external field (
\nabla\xi(0)=0
) and
\mu=\delta_{0}
. The proofs we give later of the general versions of these theorems do not make any reference to the results of this section, so one can skip this entire section if so enclined. Nevertheless, we find it interesting to also present this comparatively simpler argument, and also to explain why it does not generalize to models with more than two species.

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, recall that we write
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
to denote its marginals. Letting
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be the cumulative distribution functions of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
respectively, and
U
be a uniform random variable on
[0,1]
, we set
\mu^{\uparrow}=\mathrm{Law}(F_{1}^{-1}(U),F_{2}^{-1}(U))
. This defines a mapping from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
to
\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, and we observe that
\mu=\mu^{\uparrow}
if
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. The fundamental idea is the following observation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
D=2
and that
\nabla\xi(0)=0
. For every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, we have

(4.1)

\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\uparrow}\leqslant\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu.

As will be explained in more details shortly, Proposition
4.1
allows us to rewrite the supremum in (
1.12
) as

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int(t\xi)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int(t\xi)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu^{\uparrow}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int(t\xi)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\},

which is key to establishing Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
in this particular case. The key ingredient of the proof of Proposition
4.1
is the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
D=2
and that
\nabla\xi(0)=0
. For all real numbers
a\leqslant a^{\prime}
and
b\leqslant b^{\prime}
, we have

(4.3)

\xi^{*}(a,b)+\xi^{*}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\leqslant\xi^{*}(a^{\prime},b)+\xi^{*}(a,b^{\prime}).

The condition (
4.3
) is an integrated version of the claim that the mixed second derivatives of
\xi^{*}
are non-positive, i.e.
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{*}(a,b)\leqslant 0
. Heuristically, the mappings
\nabla\xi
and
\nabla\xi^{*}
are inverse of one another, and thus the Hessian of
\xi^{*}
at
(a,b)
should be the inverse of the matrix
A=\nabla\xi^{2}(\nabla\xi^{*}(a,b))
. Moreover, the assumption that
\xi
must allow for the existence of a Gaussian random field satisfying (
1.3
) imposes some constraints on
\xi

[
40
, Proposition 6.6]
. In particular, the Hessian of
\xi
must be a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix with nonnegative coefficients, and thus we should have

\partial^{2}_{ab}\xi^{*}(a,b)=-\det(A)^{-1}A_{12}\leqslant 0.

Relying on this heuristic, we give a rigorous proof of Proposition
4.2
in Appendix
A
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

This follows from Proposition
4.2
and
[
40
, Proposition 2.5]
applied to
c(a,b)=\xi^{*}(a,b)
. (We point out that the sentence “Examples of functions satisfying the condition (2.16) include any convex function of
x-y
.” in
[
40
, Remark 2.5]
is actually false, as explained below some convex functions fail to satisfy
[
40
, (2.16)]
.)
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We now give an example with
D=3
of an admissible function
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
for which Proposition
4.1
is invalid. Consider
\xi(x)=\frac{x\cdot Ax}{2}
where

A=\begin{pmatrix}6&2&1\\
2&5&3\\
1&3&4\end{pmatrix}.

The function
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
thus defined satisfies

\mathbb{E}H_{N}(\sigma)H_{N}(\tau)=\xi\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}\cdot\tau_{1}}{N},\frac{\sigma_{2}\cdot\tau_{2}}{N},\frac{\sigma_{3}\cdot\tau_{3}}{N}\right)

provided that we set

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{d,d^{\prime}=1}^{3}(A_{dd^{\prime}})^{1/2}J_{i,j}^{d,d^{\prime}}\sigma_{di}\sigma_{d^{\prime}j},

where the
J_{i,j}^{d,d^{\prime}}
are independent standard Gaussian random variables. We observe that
A
is positive definite, as can be checked using Sylvester’s criterion on the positivity of the leading principal minors, so the function
\xi
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We have

A^{-1}=\frac{1}{57}\begin{pmatrix}11&-5&1\\
-5&23&-16\\
1&-16&26\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
(A^{-1})_{13}>0
. Let
\overline{V}=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})
, we have that
\nabla\xi(x)=Ax
so
\overline{V}=\{Ax\big|\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}\}
. Using that
\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))=x\cdot\nabla\xi(x)-\xi(x)
(proved below in (
5.7
) in any dimension), we see that for every
y\in\overline{V}
,

\xi^{*}(y)=\frac{y\cdot A^{-1}y}{2}.

In particular
\xi^{*}
is differentiable on the interior of
\overline{V}
and
\nabla^{2}\xi^{*}(y)=A^{-1}
. Now let
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let us fix
y_{0}
in the interior of
\overline{V}
. For
\varepsilon>0
small enough we have that the points
y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1}
,
y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{3}
and
y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1}+\varepsilon e_{3}
all belong to the interior of
\overline{V}
. Let
\mu
be the uniform measure on
\{y_{0},y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1},y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{3}\}
, we have

\mu^{\uparrow}=\frac{1}{3}\left(2\delta_{y_{0}}+\delta_{y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1}+\varepsilon e_{3}}\right).

In particular,

\displaystyle\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\uparrow}-\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(\xi^{*}(y_{0})+\xi^{*}(y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1}+\varepsilon e_{3})-\xi^{*}(y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1})-\xi^{*}(y_{0}+\varepsilon e_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{13}\xi^{*}(y_{0}+t\varepsilon e_{1}+s\varepsilon e_{3})\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s.

Since
\partial_{13}\xi^{*}>0
on the interior of
V
, we obtain

\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\uparrow}>\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu,

as announced. ∎

We now prove Theorem
1.3
when
D=2
,
\nabla\xi(0)=0
and
\mu=\delta_{0}
by relying on Proposition
4.1
. As a consequence, we will be able to deduce that Theorem
1.4
also holds at
D=2
and
\mu=\delta_{0}
.

Recall from (
2.17
) that every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, we have

(4.4)

\psi(\nu)=\lambda_{\infty,1}\psi_{1}(\nu_{1})+\lambda_{\infty,2}\psi_{2}(\nu_{2}),

where
\psi_{d}
is the Parisi functional associated to
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
. The right-hand side of the previous display is well defined even when
\nu
is not monotone, in particular this gives an extension of the functional
\psi
to
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
when
D=2
,
\nabla\xi(0)=0
and
\mu=\delta_{0}
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. Since
\nu
and
\nu^{\uparrow}
have the same marginals, we have
\psi(\nu)=\psi(\nu^{\uparrow})
. Thus according to Proposition
4.1
we have

\psi(\nu)-\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu\leqslant\psi(\nu^{\uparrow})-\int\xi^{*}d\nu^{\uparrow}.

This implies

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int\xi^{*}d\nu\right\}\leqslant\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int\xi^{*}d\nu\right\}.

Since the other bound is trivial,

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int\xi^{*}d\nu\right\}=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\int\xi^{*}d\nu\right\}.

We use Theorem
1.1
to conclude that (
1.14
) holds at
\mu=\delta_{0}
.

From Lemma
3.2
, we have that
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are (strictly) concave on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and the map
\nu\mapsto\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu
is affine, thus the function inside the supremum in (
1.14
) is concave in
\nu
.

Finally, by contradiction, assume that there are two distinct monotone probability measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
that reach the supremum in (
1.14
). Consider
\nu^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\nu+\nu^{\prime}}{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
(note that
\nu^{\prime\prime}
may not be monotone). Since
\nu\neq\nu^{\prime}
and
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, we must have
\nu_{1}\neq\nu^{\prime}_{1}
or
\nu_{2}\neq\nu^{\prime}_{2}
. Since
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are strictly concave on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, this yields

\displaystyle\psi(\nu^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}\left(\frac{\nu_{1}+\nu^{\prime}_{1}}{2}\right)+\psi_{2}\left(\frac{\nu_{2}+\nu^{\prime}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{\psi_{1}(\nu_{1})+\psi_{1}(\nu^{\prime}_{1})}{2}+\frac{\psi_{2}(\nu_{2})+\psi_{2}(\nu^{\prime}_{2})}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi(\nu)+\psi(\nu^{\prime})}{2}.

By (
1.14
) at
\mu=\delta_{0}
, we thus have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi(\nu)-\int\xi^{*}d\nu+\psi(\nu^{\prime})-\int\xi^{*}d\nu^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle<\psi(\nu^{\prime\prime})-\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\psi(\rho)-\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\rho^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0}).

This is a contradiction. Therefore there is exactly one probability measure
\nu
that reaches the supremum in (
1.14
).
∎

Recall the definition of
\mathfrak{X}
from (

). For every
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
, we define
S_{t}\chi
according to (
1.17
).

Proof of Theorem
1.4
when
D=2
,
\nabla\xi(0)=0
, and
\mu=\delta_{0}
.

We assume, without loss of generality, that
t=1
. According to
[

, we have that

(4.5)

\psi_{d}(\nu_{d})=\inf_{\chi_{d}}\left\{\int\chi_{d}\mathrm{d}\nu_{d}-(\psi_{d})_{*}(\chi_{d})\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all
\chi_{d}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
that are
1
-Lipschitz, increasing, convex functions that vanish at the origin and where we have defined

(\psi_{d})_{*}(\chi_{d})=\inf_{\nu_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\left\{\int\chi_{d}\mathrm{d}\nu_{d}-\psi_{d}(\nu_{d})\right\}.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\psi(\nu)

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}(\nu_{1})+\psi_{2}(\nu_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\chi_{1}}\left\{\int\chi_{1}\mathrm{d}\nu_{1}-(\psi_{1})_{*}(\chi)\right\}+\inf_{\chi_{2}}\left\{\int\chi_{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{2}-(\psi_{2})_{*}(\chi)\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\chi\in\mathfrak{X}}\left\{\int\chi\mathrm{d}\nu-\psi_{*}(\chi)\right\}.

Plugging this into the formula of Theorem

at

, we obtain

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0})=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\inf_{\chi\in\mathfrak{X}}\left\{\int\chi\mathrm{d}\nu-\psi_{*}(\chi)+\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}.

The sets
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
\mathfrak{X}
are both convex. In addition,
\mathfrak{X}
is compact with respect to convergence in the local uniform topology. Furthermore, for every

, the map

is convex on

and for every

is concave on

. According to Sion’s min-max theorem

]

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\delta_{0})=\inf_{\chi\in\mathfrak{X}}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\int\chi\mathrm{d}\nu-\psi_{*}(\chi)+\int\xi^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}.

According to (

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\left\{\int(\chi-\xi^{*})\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left\{\chi(x)-\xi^{*}(x)\right\}=S_{1}\chi(0).

we obtain

Stability of

,

(5.1)

as was announced in (

). The fact that this quantity is equal to that in (

) for every

The first result of this section is an analysis of the optimizers in (

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
\xi^{*}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
. Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function. Let

and

be such that

(5.2)

\chi(y)-S_{t}\chi(x)-(t\xi)^{*}(y-x)=0.

If

and
x
is a differentiable point of
S_{t}\chi
, then we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\nabla S_{t}\chi(x)=\nabla(t\xi)^{*}(y-x).

If
y\in(0,+\infty)^{D}
and
y
is a differentiable point of
\chi
, then we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\nabla\chi(y)=\nabla(t\xi)^{*}(y-x).

In particular, if the two conditions hold at the same time, then we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle\nabla S_{t}\chi(x)=\nabla\chi(y).

Before showing this, we derive in the next three lemmas basic properties of
\xi
and
\xi^{*}
that will be useful throughout the rest of the paper. We say that a function
\varsigma:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
provided that, for every
M>0
, there is
R>0
such that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}:\>|x|\geqslant R}\frac{\varsigma(x)}{|x|}\geqslant M.

Lemma 5.2
.

The function
\xi^{*}
is superlinear on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
. In addition, if we assume that
\xi
is superlinear on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, then
\xi^{*}
is also continuous. The same holds for
(t\xi)^{*}
for any
t>0
.

Proof.

The function
\xi
is continuous so it is locally bounded. For every
r>0
, let
M_{r}
denote the supremum of
|\xi|
on the ball of radius
r
centered at
0
. Letting
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
and setting
x=ry/|y|
in the supremum below, we get that

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi(x)\right\}\geqslant r|y|-M_{r}.

Then (
5.6
) follows from the last display. Let us now further assume that
\xi
is superlinear. In this case, we have that for every
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x|\to+\infty\\
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\end{subarray}}\left(x\cdot y-\xi(x)\right)=-\infty.

This implies that
\xi^{*}<+\infty
on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Hence,
\xi^{*}
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
taking only finite values and thus
\xi^{*}
is continuous (see e.g.
[
20
, Proposition 2.9]
).
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\xi
is superlinear on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
and strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, then
\xi^{*}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Proof.

Let
M>0
be arbitrary and let
R>0
be such that (
5.6
) holds for
\xi
. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be such that
|y|\leqslant M
. For every
|x|\geqslant R
, we have
x\cdot y-\xi(x)\leqslant|x|(|y|-M)\leqslant 0
. Hence,

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{|x|\leqslant R}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi(x)\right\}.

Since
\xi
is assumed to be strictly convex, the variational problem in the previous display admits exactly one maximizer. It then follows from the envelope theorem
[
20
, Theorem 2.21]
that
\xi^{*}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, we have

(5.7)

\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))=x\cdot\nabla\xi(x)-\xi(x).

Furthermore, if
\xi
is strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, then we have for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
that

(5.8)

\displaystyle\nabla\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))=x,

(5.9)

\displaystyle\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))-y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+},

(5.10)

\displaystyle\nabla\xi^{*}(y)\cdot(\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))-y)=0,

(5.11)

\displaystyle\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y)))=\xi^{*}(y).

Remark 5.5
.

When
\xi
is strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, note that thanks to (
5.8
) we have in particular that for every
y\in\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
,

(5.12)

\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))=y.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, we start by proving (
5.7
). By definition of
\xi^{*}
, it is clear that the left-hand side is larger than the right-hand side. To prove the other inequality, let
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, by convexity of
\xi
we have

\xi(\lambda x^{\prime}+(1-\lambda)x)\leqslant\lambda\xi(x^{\prime})+(1-\lambda)\xi(x).

Rearranging and letting
\lambda\to 0
we get
\nabla\xi(x)\cdot(x^{\prime}-x)\leqslant\xi(x^{\prime})-\xi(x)
, in particular

x^{\prime}\cdot\nabla\xi(x)-\xi(x^{\prime})\leqslant x\cdot\nabla\xi(x)-\xi(x).

Taking the supremum over
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
in the previous display, we obtain
\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))\leqslant x\cdot\nabla\xi(x)-\xi(x)
, which thus completes the proof of (
5.8
).

Since
\xi
is assumed to be strongly on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, it is superlinear on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
and strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, so by Lemma
5.3
, the function
\xi^{*}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{++}
we can differentiate (
5.7
) to obtain

\nabla^{2}\xi(x)\nabla\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))=\nabla^{2}\xi(x)x.

Since
\xi
is assumed to be strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, the matrix
\nabla^{2}\xi(x)
is positive definite and the previous display implies (
5.8
) for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{++}
, which we can then extend to
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
by continuity.

We now turn to proving (
5.9
) and (
5.10
). Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{++}
, by definition we have

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi(x)\right\}.

Let
M>0
and assume that
|y|\leqslant M
. Since
\xi
is assumed to be superlinear, there exists
R>0
such that (
5.6
) holds for
\xi
and we have that for
|x|>R
,

x\cdot y-\xi(x)\leqslant|x|(|y|-M)<0.

Therefore,

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\\
|x|\leqslant R\end{subarray}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi(x)\right\}.

In addition since
\xi
is assumed to be strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, there is a unique maximizer

in the variational formula of the above display. According to the envelope theorem
[
20
, Theorem 2.21]
we have
\nabla\xi^{*}(y)=x_{\text{opt}}(y)
. It follows that for every

\displaystyle\xi(x_{\text{opt}}(y))

\displaystyle=x_{\text{opt}}(y)\cdot y-\xi^{*}(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant x_{\text{opt}}(y)\cdot y-(x\cdot y-\xi(x)).

Rearranging, we obtain

\xi(x)\geqslant\xi(x_{\text{opt}}(y))+y\cdot(x-x_{\text{opt}}(y)).

Chosing

for

and letting

in the previous display, we obtain (
5.9
). Furthermore, if
x_{\text{opt},d}(y)>0
for some

, then we can also take

in the previous construction (provided that

is small enough) and this yields
\partial_{x_{d}}\xi(x_{\text{opt}}(y))=y_{d}
. Therefore for every

, we have that the

\nabla\xi^{*}(y)
is

or the

-th coordinate of

Finally, by a similar proof as for (

\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))=y\cdot\nabla\xi^{*}(y)-\xi^{*}(y).

From this, (

\displaystyle\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y)))

\displaystyle=\nabla\xi^{*}(y)\cdot\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))-\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))

\displaystyle=\nabla\xi^{*}(y)\cdot\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))-\left(y\cdot\nabla\xi^{*}(y)-\xi^{*}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\xi^{*}(y)+\nabla\xi^{*}(y)\cdot(\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))-y)

\displaystyle=\xi^{*}(y),

and thus, (

∎

In the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt|y-x|-|(y-x)_{+}|\leqslant|x|.

\displaystyle|y-x|-|(y-x)_{+}|\leqslant|y-x-(y-x)_{+}|\leqslant\Big(\sum_{d=1}^{D}(y_{d}-x_{d})^{2}\mathds{1}_{0\leqslant y_{d}<x_{d}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant|x|.

\displaystyle y_{+}=\left(y_{d}\mathds{1}_{y_{d}\geqslant 0}\right)_{1\leqslant d\leqslant D}.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing function, and for every

Let

Since the supremum in the definition of

\displaystyle x\cdot y_{+}-\varsigma(x)\leqslant\widehat{x}\cdot y-\varsigma(\widehat{x})\leqslant\varsigma^{*}(y),

Proof of Proposition

.

We now turn to the main objective of this section.
As discussed around (

Definition 5.8

(
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convexity)
.

We say that a function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}

when for every

\chi(x+y+z)-\chi(x+z)\geqslant\chi(x+y)-\chi(x).

When

is
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-convex if and only if
\nabla\chi
is
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing.

Recalling the definition of
\mathfrak{X}
from (

that the derivative of
\psi
at any point in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
is a function

that belongs to

, and this implies in particular that

is indeed
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-convex. The main result of this section is as follows.

Proposition 5.9
.

For every
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
and

, the function
S_{t}\chi
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convex.

In order to prove Proposition
5.9
, we will rely on the fact that the mapping
(t,x)\mapsto S_{t}\chi(x)
can be interpreted as the solution to a Hamilton–Jacobi equation. We find it clearer to state some results in a slightly more general setting.
Let
H:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function. Thanks to
[
15
]
, we know that when
\phi
and
H
are
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation

(5.18)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-H(\nabla u)=0\on(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\\
u(0,\cdot)=\phi\end{cases}

admits a unique viscosity solution. Moreover, by
[
15
, Proposition 6.2]
, when
H
is further assumed to be convex and bounded from below, this unique viscosity solution can be represented as the following version of the Hopf-Lax formula

(5.19)

u(t,x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\{\phi(x+y)-(tH)^{*}(y)\},

where
(tH)^{*}(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot p-tH(p)\right\}
denotes the convex dual of
tH
as defined in (
5.16
). We show that, under exactly the same assumptions on
H
and
\phi
, one can represent the solution using another version of the Hopf-Lax representation.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing and Lipschitz function, and let
H:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function that is
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing, convex and bounded below. The unique
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing and Lipschitz viscosity solution
u
of

(5.20)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-H(\nabla u)=0\text{ on }(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+},\\
u(0,\cdot)=\phi,\end{cases}

satisfies, for every
(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
,

(5.21)

u(t,x)=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\{\phi(x^{\prime})-(tH)^{*}(x^{\prime}-x)\}.

Proof.

We let
RHS
denote the right-hand side in (
5.21
). It is clear from the standard Hopf-Lax representation in (
5.19
) that
u\leqslant RHS
. Let
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, we let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
denote the vector whose coordinates are positive parts of the coordinates of

. We have
(tH)^{*}(y)=(tH)^{*}(x^{\prime}-x)
by Lemma

, and since
(x+y)-x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, by monotonicity of
\phi
we have
\phi(x^{\prime})\leqslant\phi(x+y)
. This yields

u(t,x)\geqslant\phi(x+y)-(tH)^{*}(y)\geqslant\phi(x^{\prime})+(tH)^{*}(x^{\prime}-x).

Taking the supremum over
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
in the previous display, we obtain that
u(t,x)\geqslant RHS
, and thus the result.
∎

A first consequence of this remark and the previous proposition is a simple proof of the equality between (

) and (

).

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz,
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing and convex function. We have

(5.22)

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{\chi(y)-(t\xi)^{*}(y-x)\right\}=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\{p\cdot x+t\xi(p)-\chi^{*}(p)\}.

Proof.

The Fenchel-Moreau theorem states that a function

is convex and lower semi-continuous if and only it can be written as the supremum of a family of affine functions. In
[
14
]
, a similar result has been proved for functions on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, more precisely a function
h:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, lower semi-continuous and
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-increasing if and only if it satisfies

h(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot p-h^{*}(p)\right\},

where
h^{*}
is defined as in (
5.16
). From this, we have that

\displaystyle\xi(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot p-\xi^{*}(p)\right\},

\displaystyle\chi(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot p-\chi^{*}(p)\right\},

where again
\xi^{*}
and
\chi^{*}
are defined as in (
5.16
). Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\{p\cdot x+t\xi(p)

\displaystyle-\chi^{*}(p)\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{p\cdot(x+x^{\prime})-(t\xi)^{*}(x^{\prime})-\chi^{*}(p)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{p\cdot(x+x^{\prime})-(t\xi)^{*}(x^{\prime})-\chi^{*}(p)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{\chi(x+x^{\prime})-(t\xi)^{*}(x^{\prime})\right\}.

The last line is then equal to the left side of (
5.22
) thanks to Proposition
5.10
and (
5.19
).
∎

Another useful consequence of Proposition
5.10
is the following result.

Lemma 5.13

(Preservation of Lipschitzness by
S_{t}
)
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing and Lipschitz. Let
(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
be the semigroup introduced in (
5.1
) and set
C=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}:\>|z|\leqslant\|\chi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}}|\xi(z)|
. For every
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and

, we have

(5.23)

\displaystyle\left|S_{t}\chi(x)-S_{t^{\prime}}\chi(x^{\prime})\right|\leqslant C\left|t-t^{\prime}\right|+\|\chi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.10
and Remark
5.11
, the mapping
(t,x)\mapsto S_{t}\chi(x)
is the unique
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing and Lipschitz viscosity solution of the equation described therein with the choices of
H=\xi_{\big|\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}
and
\phi=\chi
. The desired result thus follows from
[
15
, Theorem 1.2 (2a)]
.
∎

Note that when
D=1
the notion of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convexity coincides with the usual notion of convexity. In this case it is clear from the alternative representation of
S_{t}\chi
obtained in Lemma

that, for every
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
, we have that
S_{t}\chi
is convex in the usual sense. In particular, when
D=1

holds. More generally, the following characterization of
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-convexity holds.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function that is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
(0,+\infty)^{D}
. We have that
\chi
is
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-convex if and only if for every
x\in(0,+\infty)^{D}
,
\nabla^{2}\chi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}_{+}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\chi
is
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
-convex. Applying (
5.17
) to
(x,ty,sy)
and letting
s\to 0
and
t\to 0
, we obtain that for every
x\in(0,+\infty)^{D}
and every
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
,

y\cdot\nabla^{2}\chi(x)z\geqslant 0.

This exactly means that
\nabla^{2}\chi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}_{+}
. Conversely, for
x\in(0,+\infty)^{D}
and
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
we have

(\chi(x+y+z)-\chi(x+z))-(\chi(x+y)-\chi(x))=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}y\cdot\nabla^{2}\chi(x+ty+sz)z\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

Thus, if
\nabla^{2}\chi
is
\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}_{+}
-valued, we obtain that the right-hand side of the previous display is nonnegative and thus (
5.17
) holds. We then obtain (
5.17
) for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
by continuity.
∎

To prove Proposition
5.9
we will make use of the following idea. Assume that the map
u:(t,x)\mapsto S_{t}\chi(x)
is smooth and let
w=\nabla^{2}u
denote its Hessian. We have

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w=\nabla w\nabla\xi(\nabla u)+w\nabla^{2}\xi(\nabla u)w\text{ on }(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}\\
w(0,\cdot)=\nabla^{2}\chi.\end{cases}

By Lemma
5.14
, we have that
w(0,\cdot)
is
\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}_{+}
-valued. Furthermore, it can be checked that the PDE in the previous display preserves positivity of the coefficients of its solutions. Indeed, on the right-hand side the first term is a plain transport term and the second term is of the form
f(t,x,w(t,x))
with
f(t,x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}_{+}
when
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}_{+}
, this is because the function
\xi
itself is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convex.

To make this observation rigorous we will have to pay attention to several details. First, we will extend
\xi
and
\chi
in order to work with PDEs on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and avoid boundary problems. Second, we will add a regularizing term of the form
\varepsilon\Delta u
on the right hand-side in the Hamilton–Jacobi equation defining
S_{t}\chi
, this will yield a family of approximations of
S_{t}\chi
that are smooth enough to perform the computations sketched above.

Observe that every
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
is
1
-Lipschitz, hence the function
S_{t}\chi
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing thanks to (
1.16
) and
1
-Lipschitz thanks to Lemma
5.13
. Thus, changing
\xi
outside the intersection of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
with the ball of radius
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
does not affect
S_{t}\chi
, this is proved rigorously in
[
15
, Theorem 1.2 (2) (c)]
. Therefore, we have some freedom on choosing how to define
\xi
outside of
B(0,1)\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, and it will be convenient for our purposes to use this freedom to modify
\xi
so that it becomes uniformly Lipschitz.
Let
L
denote the largest value taken by
|\nabla\xi|
on the set of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
such that
\sum_{d=1}^{D}x_{d}\leqslant 2D
. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, let

\xi_{\text{reg}}(x)=\begin{cases}\max\{\xi(x),\xi(0)+2L(\sum_{d=1}^{D}x_{d}-D)\}&\text{ if }\sum_{d=1}^{D}x_{d}\leqslant 2D\\
\xi(0)+2L(\sum_{d=1}^{D}x_{d}-D)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

It can be checked (see
[
40
, Proposition 6.8]
and
[
16
, Lemma 4.3]
) that
\xi_{\text{reg}}
coincides with
\xi
on
B(0,1)\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, that
\xi_{\text{reg}}
is convex and Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
, and that it is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convex and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
(this last property is referred to as “proper” in the cited references).

We define
\overline{\xi}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting

\overline{\xi}(x)=\xi_{\text{reg}}(x_{+}).

It can be easily checked that
\overline{\xi}
is locally Lipschitz and convex. We let
(\overline{S}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the semigroup associated to the equation

\partial_{t}u-\overline{\xi}(\nabla u)=0\text{ on }(0+,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{D}.

According to
[
15
]
or
[
20
]
, the semigroup
(\overline{S}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is well-defined on the set of Lipschitz functions
\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
, define
\overline{\chi}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
\overline{\chi}(x)=\chi(x_{+})
. Then
\overline{\chi}
is Lipschitz and convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and we have for every
(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
,

(5.24)

\overline{S}_{t}\overline{\chi}(x)=S_{t}\chi(x).

Proof.

In this proof, we let
\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}
denote the convex dual of
\xi_{\text{reg}}
with respect to
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, more precisely

\xi^{*}_{\text{reg}}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi_{\text{reg}}(x)\right\}.

We also let
\overline{\xi}^{*}
denote the convex dual of
\overline{\xi}
with respect to
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, more precisely

\overline{\xi}^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\{x\cdot y-\overline{\xi}(x)\right\}.

Step 1.
We show that
\overline{\chi}
is Lipschitz, convex and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Since
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}
we have
\overline{\chi}(x)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\overline{\chi}_{d}(x_{d})
, where
\overline{\chi}_{d}
is the function which coincides with
\chi_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and is constant taking the value
\chi_{d}(0)=0
otherwise. Therefore it suffices to show that
\overline{\chi}_{d}
is Lipschitz, convex and increasing on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
, we have

|\overline{\chi}_{d}(\lambda_{1})-\overline{\chi}_{d}(\lambda_{2})|=|\chi_{d}(\lambda_{1})-\chi_{d}(0)|\leqslant|\lambda_{1}|.

Now observe that
|\lambda_{1}|=\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}=|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|
, so

|\overline{\chi}_{d}(\lambda_{1})-\overline{\chi}_{d}(\lambda_{2})|\leqslant|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|.

Now observe that the previous display clearly also holds when
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
or
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
, thus
\overline{\chi}_{d}
is Lipschitz. In addition,
\chi_{d}
is increasing on
\mathbb{R}
as a composition of increasing functions and
\overline{\chi}_{d}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}
as the composition of a convex function and a convex increasing function. In conclusion,
\overline{\chi}
is Lipschitz, convex and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Step 2.
We show that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
\overline{\xi}^{*}(x)\geqslant\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(x_{+})
with equality when
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, using Lemma
5.7
in the last line we have

\displaystyle\overline{\xi}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi_{\text{reg}}(y_{+})\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi_{\text{reg}}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle=\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(x_{+}).

This proves the first part of the statement. Now assume that
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
. Since
x\cdot y\leqslant x\cdot y_{+}
we have

\overline{\xi}^{*}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi_{\text{reg}}(y_{+})\right\}\leqslant\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\{x\cdot y_{+}-\xi_{\text{reg}}(y_{+})\right\}=\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(x).

Step 3.
We show that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
,
\overline{S}_{t}\overline{\chi}(x)=S_{t}\chi(x)
.

Without loss of generality we assume that
t=1
. Since
\xi_{\text{reg}}
and
\overline{\xi}
are convex on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
and
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we are simply going to verify that the Hopf-Lax representations of
\overline{S}_{1}\overline{\chi}(x)
and
S_{1}\chi(x)
coincide. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
, thanks to the equality case in the inequality from Step 2 we have

\displaystyle\overline{S}_{1}\overline{\chi}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\{\overline{\chi}(x+y)-\overline{\xi}^{*}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}}\left\{\overline{\chi}(x+y)-\overline{\xi}^{*}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}}\left\{\chi(x+y)-\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle=S_{1}\chi(x).

In addition, thanks to the sub-additivity of the positive part and the inequality from Step 2, we have for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
that

\displaystyle x+y_{+}-(x+y)_{+}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}

\displaystyle-\overline{\xi}^{*}(y)\leqslant-\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(y_{+}).

Since
\overline{\chi}
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-increasing on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
according to Step 1, we have

\overline{\chi}(x+y)-\overline{\xi}^{*}(y)\leqslant\chi(x+y_{+})-\xi_{\text{reg}}^{*}(y_{+})\leqslant S_{1}\chi(x).

Taking the supremum over
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
in the previous display, we obtain
\overline{S}_{1}\overline{\chi}(x)\leqslant S_{1}\chi(x)
, thus the result.
∎

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}

by saying that such a function is

) holds for every

and

.

Definition 5.16

(

.

is
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}

and

.

Reproducing the proof of Lemma

, one can check that a

is

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
H:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
such that all the derivatives of order
\geqslant 1
of
H
are in
L^{\infty}
and all the derivatives of order
\geqslant 2
of
g
are in
L^{\infty}
. Consider the Cauchy problem

(5.26)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-H(\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}\text{ on }(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\\
u^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=g.\end{cases}

There is exactly one function
u^{\varepsilon}
that is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
smooth with bounded derivatives of order
\geqslant 2
and that satisfies (
5.26
). Furthermore, if
H
and
g
are
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convex, then for every
t\geqslant 0
, the function
u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D}
-convex.

The well-posedness of (
5.26
) is well known in many contexts and is a basic result in the theory of viscosity solutions to Hamilton–Jacobi equations
[
22
,
23
]
. For a pedagogical and detailed proof of this result under some slightly different boundedness assumptions, we refer to the lecture notes
[
8
]
. In the usual approach to well-posedness, the main ingredient is Duhamel’s formula which allows to show that the solutions of (
5.26
) are the fixed points of a certain functional. For the sake of completeness, we explain in the proof below how to adapt the estimates of
[
8
, Section 3]
yielding existence of a fixed point.

Proof of Lemma
5.18
.

Up to modifying
H
and rescaling time, we can assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon=1
. In this proof we will thus not explicitly make the
\varepsilon
dependence of
(
5.26
)
apparent and simply write
u
instead of
u^{\varepsilon}
. We let
P
denote the
D
-dimensional heat kernel, more precisely

P(t,x)=\frac{1}{\left(4\pi t\right)^{\frac{D}{2}}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}}.

Given two functions
a,b:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, we let
a*b
denote their additive convolution, namely

a*b(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}a(y)b(x-y)\mathrm{d}y.

Step 1.
Existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
5.26
).

Given a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function
u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, we let for every
t\geqslant 0

S(u)(t,\cdot)=P(t,\cdot)*g+\int_{0}^{t}P(t-s,\cdot)*H(\nabla u(s,\cdot))\mathrm{d}s.

We let
u^{(-1)}=0
and for every
k\geqslant 0
,
u^{(k)}=S(u^{(k-1)})
. By construction of
S
, for every
k\geqslant 0
, we have

(5.27)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u^{(k)}-\Delta u^{(k)}=H(\nabla u^{(k-1)})\text{ on }(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\\
u^{(k)}(0,\cdot)=g.\end{cases}

Therefore, it suffices to show that the sequence
(u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
converges in an appropriate sense to a function
u
to get the existence of a solution for (
5.26
).
To do so, let us fix
T>0
. We will prove that for every
n\geqslant 2
, the sequence
(\nabla^{n}u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
. From this we will deduce that the sequence
((\partial_{t},\nabla)^{2}u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Since
S(u)(0,\cdot)=g
and
\nabla S(u)(0,\cdot)=\nabla g
, thanks to the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, those estimates will yield local uniform convergence up to extraction of the sequence
(\nabla^{n}u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
for all
n\geqslant 0
, which will conlude the proof of existence of a solution with bounded derivatives of order
\geqslant 2
for (
5.26
).

We define

L_{n}(k,t)=\|\nabla^{n}u^{(k)}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

We now explain how to prove bounds that are uniform in
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,T]
for
L_{2}(k,t)
following the proof of
[
8
, Claim 3.6]
, those estimates can easily be generalized to any
n\geqslant 2
. Differentiating twice the recurrence relation
u^{(k)}=S(u^{(k-1)})
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{d}\partial_{d^{\prime}}u^{(k)}

\displaystyle=P(t)*\partial_{d}\partial_{d^{\prime}}g+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{d^{\prime}}P(t-s)*\left(\nabla H(\nabla u^{(k-1)})(s,\cdot)\cdot\nabla\partial_{d}u^{(k-1)}(s,\cdot)\right)\mathrm{d}s

There exists a constant
c>0
depending only on
D
such that
\|\nabla P(t)\|_{L^{1}}\leqslant ct^{-1/2}
. Therefore it follows from Young’s inequality
\|a*b\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\|a\|_{L^{1}}\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}
and the previous display that

L_{2}(k,t)\leqslant\|\nabla^{2}g\|_{L^{\infty}}+c|\nabla H|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t-s}}L_{2}(k-1,s)\mathrm{d}s.

After further manipulations of this inequality and induction on
k
, we obtain the existence of a constant
\Lambda>0
depending only on
D
and
|\nabla H|_{\infty}
such that

L_{2}(k,t)\leqslant 2\|\nabla^{2}g\|_{L^{\infty}}e^{\Lambda t}.

More generally, by induction on
n
using a similar argument, we can show that for every
T>0
and every
n\geqslant 2
there exists
C(n,T)>0
such that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every
t\in[0,T]
,

L_{n}(k,t)\leqslant C(n,T).

Those are the announced estimates for
\nabla^{n}u^{(k)}
. To convert this into estimates for
(\partial_{t},\nabla)^{2}u^{(k)}
we use (
5.27
) to deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nabla u^{(k)}=\nabla^{2}u^{(k)}\nabla H(\nabla u^{(k)})+\nabla\Delta u^{(k-1)},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial_{t}u^{(k)}=\partial_{t}\nabla u^{(k)}\cdot\nabla H(\nabla u^{(k)})+\partial_{t}\Delta u^{(k-1)}.

In addition, using (
5.27
) once more we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Delta u^{(k-1)}=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\nabla u^{(k-1)}\cdot\nabla^{2}H(u^{(k-1)})\partial_{i}\nabla u^{(k-1)}+\nabla H(\nabla u^{(k-1)})\cdot\nabla\Delta u^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle+\Delta\Delta u^{(k-2)}.

Since
\nabla H\in L^{\infty}
and
\nabla^{2}H\in L^{\infty}
, it follows from the previous two displays and our previous estimates that
(\partial_{t}\nabla u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
and
(\partial_{t}\partial_{t}u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
are uniformly bounded on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Therefore,
((\partial_{t},\nabla)^{2}u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
, so
((\partial_{t},\nabla)u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
is uniformly Lipschitz on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Since at
t=0
,
(\partial_{t},\nabla)u^{(k)}(0,\cdot)=(\Delta g+H(\nabla g),\nabla g)
, we deduce that
((\partial_{t},\nabla)u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
converges uniformly on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
thanks to the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. In addition, it follows from the uniform Lipschitzness that
((\partial_{t},\nabla)u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
is locally uniformly bounded on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
, so
(u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
is uniformly locally Lipschitz on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Since
u^{(k)}(0,\cdot)=g
, we can apply the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem once more to obtain local uniform convergence of
(u^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
This concludes the existence part of the proof. For the uniqueness part we can proceed as in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 3.2]
but using weighted
L^{p}
norms instead of the
L^{\infty}
norm.

Step 2.
Let
g_{\alpha}(x)=g(x)+\alpha\left(\sum_{d}^{D}x_{d}\right)^{2}
and let
u_{\alpha}
the unique solution of (
5.26
) with initial condition
g_{\alpha}
. We show that for every
t\geqslant 0
,
\nabla^{2}u_{\alpha}(t,\cdot)\to\nabla^{2}u(t,\cdot)
pointwise up to extraction as
\alpha\to 0
.

To prove this, we perform estimates as in Step 1, but this time for

\widetilde{L}_{n}(k,t)=\|\nabla^{n}u^{(k)}_{\alpha}(t,\cdot)-\nabla^{n}u^{(k)}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(w)},

with
n=1,2
, where
L^{p}(w)
denotes the
L^{p}
space with weight
w(x)=e^{-|x|}
and
\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)}=\|fw\|_{L^{p}}
. In this context we can replace the usual Young inequality by the following weighted version,

\|a*b\|_{L^{p}(w)}\leqslant\|a\|_{L^{1}(\overline{w})}\|b\|_{L^{p}(w)},

where
\overline{w}(x)=e^{|x|}
. This weighted inequality can be deduced from the usual Young inequality after observing that
w(x+y)\leqslant\overline{w}(x)w(y)
, we refer to
[
44
, Theorem 2.4]
for a full proof. As previously, this yields estimates that are uniform in
t\in[0,T]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
and that vanish in the limit

Recall also the notation

.
The key step towards the derivation of our main results is as follows.

Proposition 7.1
.

Assume that

\xi
is strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
.
Let

be given by (
6.6
). For every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
{\mathsf{q}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
, and for
\mu={\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{q}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
, we have

(7.1)

\displaystyle h(t,\mu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)\right\}

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\mathsf{p}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}}\left\{\psi\left({\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathsf{q}}+t\nabla\xi({\mathsf{p}})}\right)-t\int_{0}^{1}\theta\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{p}}\right\}.

Moreover, the suprema in (

As discussed below (
1.30
), the key point in showing (

into the monotonicity of a maximizer in the definition of

. In order to do so, we will smear out the measure

Lemma 7.2

(Characterization of monotonicity)
.

Let

and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
. The measure
\mu
is monotone if and only if, for every
i,j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,

(7.3)

\displaystyle\mu^{\otimes 2}\left(\left\{\,(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\>\ y_{i}\geqslant y^{\prime}_{i}\ \text{ or }\ y_{j}\leqslant y^{\prime}_{j}\,\right\}\right)=1.

Remark 7.3
.

Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
two independent random variables with law
\mu
. Then, (
7.3
) can be rewritten as
\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}\geqslant Y^{\prime}_{i}\text{ or }Y_{j}\leqslant Y^{\prime}_{j})=1
. ∎

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

We proceed in two steps. First, we show that (
7.3
) is equivalent to a stronger formulation and then we apply results from
[
40
, Section 2]
.

Step 1.

We show that the condition specified in (
7.3
) is equivalent to

(7.4)

\displaystyle\mu^{\otimes 2}\left(\left\{\,(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\>\ a\cdot y\geqslant a\cdot y^{\prime}\ \text{ or }\ b\cdot y\leqslant b\cdot y^{\prime}\,\right\}\right)=1

for every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
.
One direction is obvious and we focus on deducing (
7.4
) from (
7.3
).

It is more convenient to work with random variables and we start with some notation. For a pair
(U,V)
of real-valued random variables, we say that
(U,V)
is monotone if, given an independent copy
(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})
, we have almost surely that
\{U\geqslant U^{\prime}
or
V\leqslant V^{\prime}\}
.
It is clear that
(U,V)
is monotone if and only if
(V,U)
is so.
We need the following property. Given three random variables
U,V,W
, we have, for every
s,t\geqslant 0
,

(7.5)

if both
(U,W)
and
(V,W)
are monotone, then so is
(sU+tV,W)
.

This immediately follows from the definition. Indeed, let
(U^{\prime},V^{\prime},W^{\prime})
be an independent copy and there is nothing to show when

. Otherwise, we must have
U\geqslant U^{\prime}
and
V\geqslant V^{\prime}
, which implies
sU+tV\geqslant sU^{\prime}+tV^{\prime}
.

Let
Y
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mu=\mathrm{Law}(Y)
. The condition in (
7.3
) is equivalent to that
(Y_{i},Y_{j})
is monotone for every
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d
. Fix any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
. For each

, iteratively applying (
7.5
) to pairs
(Y_{i},Y_{j})
for

, we can get that
(a\cdot Y,Y_{j})
is monotone. Similarly, we can get that
(a\cdot Y,b\cdot Y)
is monotone, which is equivalent to (
7.4
).

Step 2.

We use results from
[
40
, Section 2]
to conclude. We start with some definitions.
Let
S^{d}
be the linear space of real symmetric matrices endowed with the Frobenius inner product, namely,
a\cdot b=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}b_{ij}
for
a,b\in S^{d}
. Let

be the set of positive semi-definite matrices. Let
\overline{\mu}
be the image of
\mu
through the map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni x\mapsto\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
.
Since
\overline{\mu}
is supported on diagonal matrices and diagonal entries of

are nonnegative, we can see that (

) is equivalent to

(7.6)

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}^{\otimes 2}\left(\left\{\,(y,y^{\prime})\in S_{+}^{d}\times S_{+}^{d}:\>\ a\cdot y\geqslant a\cdot y^{\prime}\ \text{ or }\ b\cdot y\leqslant b\cdot y^{\prime}\,\right\}\right)=1

for every
a,b\in S^{d}_{+}
.

Monotone probability measures on

are defined in the paragraphs above
[
40
, Proposition 2.3]
and
[
40
, (2.1)]
. The condition in (
7.6
) exactly matches this definition and thus
\overline{\mu}
is monotone on

. By
[
40
, Proposition 2.4]
, this monotonicity is equivalent to the existence of an increasing path

such that
\overline{\mu}=\mathrm{Law}(\overline{{\mathsf{q}}}(U))
, where the monotonicity of

is understood as
\overline{{\mathsf{q}}}(s^{\prime})-\overline{{\mathsf{q}}}(s)\in S^{d}_{+}

. Since

is supported on diagonal matrices,

8.
Explicit representation in terms of martingales

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
. One ingredient of the proof of Theorem
1.4
is a representation of the function
\psi
as, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})
,

(8.1)

\psi(\mu)=\inf_{\chi\in\mathfrak{X}}\left\{\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\psi_{*}(\chi)\right\}.

In this representation, the set
\mathfrak{X}
and function
\psi_{*}
are not uniquely determined. In order to derive Theorem
1.5
, the main task compared with the proof of Theorem
1.4
is to derive a more explicit representation of this function
\psi
. To do this, we will rely heavily on ideas developed in
[
42
]
, where very similar results are proved for models with scalar spins.

We recall that we give ourselves a probability space
\mathscr{P}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
with associated expectation
\mathbf{E}
, and
(\mathcal{F}_{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(\mathcal{F}_{D}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
an independent family of complete filtrations over
\mathscr{P}
, which each comes with an adapted Brownian motion
(B_{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(B_{D}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. We denote by
\mathbf{Mart}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{Mart}_{D}
the spaces of bounded martingales over
\mathscr{P}
with respect to the filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{1}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
, …,
(\mathcal{F}_{D}({t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
respectively, with
\mathbf{Mart}=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mathbf{Mart}_{d}
.
When we want to make the dependence on the underlying probability space explicit we write
\mathbf{Mart}(\mathscr{P})
in place of
\mathbf{Mart}
.

We recall that the functions
\psi_{d}:\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\mathbb{R}
,
\phi_{d}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\phi_{d}^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are defined in (
2.16
), (
1.21
), and (
1.22
) respectively. Since we have assumed that
{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
is not a Dirac mass, it follows that for every
t\geqslant 0
,
\phi_{d}(t,\cdot)
is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}
. Adapting the proof of
[
42
, Lemma 2.2]
, we obtain the following explicit representation for
\psi_{d}
.

Proposition 8.1
.

For every
d\in[D]
,
T_{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
\nu_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
supported in
[0,T_{d}]
, we have

(8.2)

\psi_{d}(\nu_{d})=\inf_{\alpha_{d}\in\mathbf{Mart}_{d}}\mathbf{E}\bigg[\phi^{*}_{d}(T_{d},\alpha_{d}(T_{d}))-\sqrt{2}\alpha_{d}(T_{d})\cdot B_{d}(T_{d})\\
-\int_{0}^{T_{d}}\nu_{d}[0,t]\alpha_{d}^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg].

In addition, there is a unique minimizer
\alpha_{d}\in\mathbf{Mart}_{d}
in (
8.2
) and it must satisfy
\alpha_{d}(t)=\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu_{d}}(t,X_{d}(t))
a.s. for almost every
t\leqslant T_{d}
, where
X_{d}
is the unique strong solution to

(8.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX_{d}(t)&=2\nu_{d}[0,t]\,\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu_{d}}(t,X_{d}(t))\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_{d}(t),\\
X_{d}(0)&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

We next recall that, for each
\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}
, we have defined
\chi_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
1.20
).

Proposition 8.2
.

For every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{\uparrow}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{D})
with marginals

and

such that for every

, we have

(8.4)

\psi(\nu)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}}\bigg\{\int\chi_{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\chi_{\alpha}(T)\\
+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\mathbf{E}\left[\phi^{*}_{d}(T_{d},\alpha_{d}(T_{d}))-\sqrt{2}\alpha_{d}(T_{d})\cdot B_{d}(T_{d})\right]\bigg\}.

In addition, there is a unique minimizer

in (
8.4
) and it must satisfy
\alpha_{d}(t)=\partial_{x}\Phi_{\nu_{d}}(t,X_{d}(t))
a.s. for every

and almost every

is the solution to (

Proof.

By integration by parts, we have for every

and thus, due to (

Using this, the decomposition of

into

as in (

), and the formula (

), we obtain (

).

Given
T=(T_{1},\dots,T_{d})
, for every
\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}
, we let

\varphi(\alpha)=-\chi_{\alpha}(T)+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\mathbf{E}\left[\phi^{*}_{d}(T_{d},\alpha_{d}(T_{d}))-\sqrt{2}\alpha_{d}(T_{d})\cdot B_{d}(T_{d})\right],

K_{0}=\Big\{(\varphi(\alpha),(\mathbf{E}[\alpha^{2}_{1}(t)])_{t\leqslant T_{1}},\dots,(\mathbf{E}[\alpha^{2}_{D}(t)])_{t\leqslant T_{D}})\ \Big|\ \alpha\in\mathbf{Mart},\\
\,\mathbf{E}[\phi_{d}^{*}(T_{d},\alpha_{d}(T_{d}))]<+\infty\Big\}.

We also let

denote the closed convex hull of

. Since

depends on the underlying probability space

, the sets

and

and

Proposition 8.3
.

For every

and

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\inf_{(\varphi,\gamma)\in K}\left\{\int S_{t}\chi_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\mu-\varphi\right\},

where

is given by
\chi_{\gamma}(x)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\int_{0}^{x_{d}}\gamma_{d}(s)\mathrm{d}s

Proof.

According to Theorem

, we have

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+})}\left\{\psi(\nu)-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)\right\}.

In view of the more precise statement of the Parisi formula for multi-species spin glasses given in

So, the supremum in the first display of this proof can be restricted to

and thus, thanks to Proposition

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K}_{T})}\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}}\bigg\{\int\chi_{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\chi_{\alpha}(T)\\
+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_{\infty,d}\mathbf{E}\left[\phi^{*}_{d}(T_{d},\alpha_{d}(T_{d}))-\sqrt{2}\alpha_{d}(T_{d})\cdot B_{d}(T_{d})\right]-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu)\bigg\}.

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K}_{T})}\inf_{(\varphi,\gamma)\in K_{0}}G_{t,\mu}(\nu;\varphi,\gamma),

Proposition 8.4

Since

So we only need to prove the converse bound. Let

be the convex hull of

Now, let

as the convex hull of

\displaystyle\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\varphi(\alpha^{(i)}),

\displaystyle\gamma_{d}(t_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mathbf{E}[(\alpha_{d}^{(i)}(t_{d}))^{2}].

to the sets of martingales in

\mathbf{Mart}_{d}(\mathscr{P})
that is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{d}(0)
. Now, by the assumption on

in the statement of Proposition

such that

\mathbb{P}(I=i)=c_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall 1\leqslant i\leqslant n,

and such that

is
\mathcal{F}_{d}(0)
-measurable for every

Since

\displaystyle\varphi=\varphi(\beta),

\displaystyle\gamma_{d}(t_{d})=\mathbf{E}[(\beta_{d}(t_{d}))^{2}].

\int S_{t}\chi_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\mu-\varphi=\int S_{t}\chi_{\beta}\mathrm{d}\mu-\varphi(\beta)\geqslant\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}(\mathscr{P})}\left\{\int S_{t}\chi_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\mu-\varphi(\alpha)\right\}.

∎

Proposition 8.5

There exists a probability space
\overline{\mathscr{P}}
such that (

Proof.

We let
\mathscr{P}
be a probability space such that Proposition

Since Proposition
8.4

We are now ready to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem

We let

\Gamma(\nu,\alpha)=\int\chi_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\nu-\varphi(\alpha)-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mu,\nu).

Let
\overline{\mathscr{P}}
be a probability space such that Proposition

holds. This means that we have

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}(t,\mu)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbf{Mart}(\overline{\mathscr{P}})}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K}_{T})}\Gamma(\nu,\alpha),

and that the infimum in the previous display is reached at some
\overline{\alpha}

In particular

Appendix A
Proof of the
2
-dimensional inequality for
\xi^{*}

In this section, we give a proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let us first show that without loss of generality we may assume that
\xi
is strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Assume that Proposition
4.2
holds when
\xi
is further assumed to be strongly convex. Applying Proposition
4.2
to the function
\xi_{\lambda}=\xi+\frac{\lambda}{2}|\cdot|^{2}
, we obtain for all real numbers
a\leqslant a^{\prime}
and
b\leqslant b^{\prime}
,

\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(a,b)+\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\leqslant\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(a^{\prime},b)+\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(a,b^{\prime}).

Thus, to show that (
4.3
) holds for
\xi
, it is enough to show that for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
\xi^{*}_{\lambda}(y)\to\xi^{*}(y)
as
\lambda\to 0
. We fix
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, the sequence
(\xi^{*}_{\lambda}(y))_{\lambda}
increases as
\lambda
decreases to
0
and is upper-bounded by
\xi^{*}(y)
, we denote by
\ell\in[0,\xi^{*}(y)]
its limit. In addition, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have
\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(y)\geqslant x\cdot y-\xi(x)-\frac{\lambda}{2}|x|^{2}
letting
\lambda\to 0
in this inequality yields

\ell=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(y)\geqslant x\cdot y-\xi(x).

Taking the supremum over
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
in the last display, we obtain
\xi^{*}(y)\leqslant\ell
. Thus
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\xi_{\lambda}^{*}(y)=\xi^{*}(y)
, as desired.

In view of the previous argument, we will assume that
\xi
is
\lambda
-strongly convex for some
\lambda>0
for the rest of this proof.

Step 1
. We show that for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, the supremum in the definition of
\xi^{*}(y)
is reached at a unique point
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and that we have
|x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)|\leqslant|y|/\lambda
.

We fix
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Since
\xi
is strongly convex, the map
x\mapsto x\cdot y-\xi(x)
is strongly concave on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
; so it has at most one maximizer. In addition, we have for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
that
\xi(x)\geqslant\frac{\lambda}{2}|x|^{2}
. Hence for
|x|>2|y|/\lambda
, we have

x\cdot y-\xi(x)\leqslant|x||y|-\frac{\lambda}{2}|x|^{2}\leqslant|x|(|y|-\frac{\lambda}{2}|x|)<0\leqslant\xi^{*}(y).

It follows that

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\\
|x|\leqslant\frac{y}{\lambda}\end{subarray}}\left\{x\cdot y-\xi(x)\right\}.

The optimization problem in the last display has an optimizer
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
satisfying
|x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)|\leqslant|y|/\lambda
.

Step 2.
We show that
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)=0
if and only if
y=0
.

If
y=0
, the condition
|x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)|\leqslant|y|/\lambda
imposes
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)=0
. Conversely, if we have
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)=0
, then
\xi^{*}(y)=0
. We will show that
\xi^{*}(y)>0
for
y\neq 0
. Let
r>0
, the function
\xi
is
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on the ball
0
of center and radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. In particular, since we have assumed that
\nabla\xi(0)=0
, there exists
\theta=\theta(r)>0
such that for every
|x|<r

\xi(x)\leqslant\xi(0)+\nabla\xi(0)\cdot x+\frac{\theta}{2}|x|^{2}=\frac{\theta}{2}|x|^{2}.

For
|y|<\lambda r
, chosing
x=\varepsilon y
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we have
|x|<r
and
|x|\leqslant|y|/\lambda
, this yields

\xi^{*}(y)\geqslant x\cdot y-\xi(x)\geqslant\varepsilon|y|^{2}-\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{2}}{2}|y|^{2}=\varepsilon|y|^{2}\left(1-\frac{\theta\varepsilon}{2}\right)>0.

Step 3.
We show that
\xi^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular,
\xi^{*}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and its gradient is a Lipschitz function which is itself differentiable almost everywhere.

We know that
\xi^{*}
is convex, so it is enough to show that the function
y\mapsto\xi^{*}(y)-\frac{1}{2\lambda}|y|^{2}
is concave on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\xi^{*}(y)-\frac{1}{2\lambda}|y|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left\{x\cdot y-\frac{1}{2\lambda}|y|^{2}-\xi(x)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{y}{\sqrt{\lambda}}-\sqrt{\lambda}x\right|^{2}-\left(\xi(x)-\frac{\lambda}{2}|x|^{2}\right)\right\}.

Thus,
y\mapsto\xi^{*}(y)-\frac{1}{2\lambda}|y|^{2}
is the supremum of a jointly concave functional on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
so it is concave on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Step 4.
We show that for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
,
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)=\nabla\xi^{*}(y)
.

We fix
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
, we have for every
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
,

\displaystyle\xi^{*}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geqslant x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\cdot y^{\prime}-\xi(x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y))

\displaystyle=x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\cdot y-\xi(x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y))+x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\cdot(y^{\prime}-y)

\displaystyle=\xi^{*}(y)+x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\cdot(y^{\prime}-y).

This means that for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
|v|=1
, we have for
\varepsilon>0
small enough
y+\varepsilon v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
and

\frac{\xi^{*}(y+\varepsilon v)-\xi^{*}(y)}{\varepsilon}\geqslant x_{opt}(y)\cdot v.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain

\nabla\xi^{*}(y)\cdot v\geqslant x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\cdot v.

Since the last display is valid for all
v
in the sphere of radius
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it imposes that
\nabla\xi^{*}(y)=x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)
.

Step 5.
We show that
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{*}\leqslant 0
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

Step 5.1.
Letting
V=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})
, we show that
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}=U_{a}\cup U_{b}
where
U_{a}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}\big|\,x_{\mathrm{opt},a}=0\}
and
U_{b}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}\big|\,x_{\mathrm{opt},b}=0\}
are disjoint open sets.

Clearly
U_{a}\cup U_{b}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}
. Conversely, let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}
, and by contradiction assume that
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
. Perturbing slightly around
x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y)
, we get the first order condition and
\nabla\xi(x_{\mathrm{opt}}(y))=y
, this is a contradiction. Hence, we have
x_{\mathrm{opt},a}(y)=0
or
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)=0
. This proves that
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}=U_{a}\cup U_{b}
. By Step 2, we must have
U_{a}\cap U_{b}=\emptyset
. Finally, let us show that
U_{a}
is open. Given
y\in U_{a}
, we have
x_{\mathrm{opt},a}(y)=0
and
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)>0
. By Step 4,
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)
is a continuous function of
y
so for every
y^{\prime}\in B_{r}(y)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}\setminus\overline{V}
with
r>0
small we have
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y^{\prime})>0
. This imposes
x_{\mathrm{opt},a}(y^{\prime})=0
as otherwise
y^{\prime}\in V
and thus
B_{r}(y)\subseteq U_{a}
. This proves that
U_{a}
is an open set and by symmetry we also have that
U_{b}
is an open set.

Step 5.2.
We show that
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{*}\leqslant 0
on
V=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})
.

According to Step 3,
\xi^{*}
is differentiable with Lipschitz gradient on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. According to (
5.7
) in Lemma
5.4
, we have that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
,

\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))=x\cdot\nabla\xi(x)-\xi(x).

Differentiating the expression in the previous display, we obtain
\nabla\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x))=x
. Now let
y\in V
, there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
such that
y=\nabla\xi(x)
, and we have

\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))=\nabla\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(\nabla\xi(x)))=\nabla\xi(x)=y.

Finally, since
\xi
is strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, the function
\nabla\xi
is continuous and injective on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, so by invariance of domain,
V
is an open set. Differentiating the last display with respect to
y\in V
, we obtain that for almost all
y\in V
,

\nabla^{2}\xi^{*}(y)=[\nabla^{2}\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))]^{-1}.

The
2\times 2
matrix
A=\nabla^{2}\xi(\nabla\xi^{*}(y))
is symmetric positive definite and has non-negative coefficients, this is because the condition (
1.3
) imposes some positivity constraint on the coefficients of the power series expansion of
\xi
as explained in Remark
5.11
. Since
(A^{-1})_{ab}=-\det(A)^{-1}A_{ab}
, we obtain that
(A^{-1})_{ab}\leqslant 0
, as desired.

Step 5.3.
We show that
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{*}\leqslant 0
almost everywhere on
U_{a}\cup U_{b}
.

Let
y\in U_{a}
, we have
x_{\mathrm{opt},a}(y)=0
and
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)>0
, so

\xi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x_{b}>0}\left\{x_{b}y_{b}-\xi(0,x_{b})\right\}.

The value of the unique optimizer in the previous display is
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)
. In particular,
x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)
is independent of the value of
y_{a}
and by Step 4, we have
\partial_{b}\xi^{*}(y)=x_{\mathrm{opt},b}(y)
. Thus, for almost all
y\in U_{a}
,
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{*}(y)=0
. By symmetry, this also holds for every
y\in U_{b}
.

Step 5.4.
We show that the boundary of
V
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
is a Lebesgue negligible set.

Let us show that the boundary of
V
is
\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})
. Since
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
is Lebesgue negligible and
\nabla\xi
is a smooth function, the desired result will follow. Let
\overline{V}
denote the closure of
V
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
and let
y\in\overline{V}
. There exists a sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
such that
\nabla\xi(x_{n})\to y
. The sequence
(\nabla\xi(x_{n}))_{n}
is bounded and
\nabla\xi(x_{n})-\lambda x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
so the sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
is bounded. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
denote a subsequential limit of
(x_{n})_{n}
, up to extraction we have
y=\lim_{n}\nabla\xi(x_{n})=\nabla\xi(x)
. Hence
\overline{V}\subseteq\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. Conversely given
y\in\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have
y=\nabla\xi(x)=\lim_{n}\nabla\xi(x_{a}+\frac{1}{n},x_{b}+\frac{1}{n})
, so

.
Thus
\overline{V}=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
\overline{V}\setminus V=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\setminus\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})
. Since
\xi
is assumed to be strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
\nabla\xi
is injective on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and we have
\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\setminus\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})
. Thus
\overline{V}\setminus V=\nabla\xi(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++})
as announced.

Step 6.
We show that (
4.3
) holds when
\xi
is
\lambda
-strongly convex.

We let
a\leqslant a^{\prime}
,
b\leqslant b^{\prime}
, such that
a\neq a^{\prime}
and
b\neq b^{\prime}
. This way
a^{\prime}=a+h
and
b^{\prime}=b+k
for

. We then have

\displaystyle\big(\xi^{*}(a,b)-\xi^{*}(a^{\prime},b)\big)-\big(\xi^{*}(a,b^{\prime})-\xi^{*}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}hk\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{*}(a+th,b+sk)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

According to Step 5, the right-hand side in the previous display is the integral of an almost everywhere nonnegative function, so it is nonnegative and

\big(\xi^{*}(a,b)-\xi^{*}(a^{\prime},b)\big)-\big(\xi^{*}(a,b^{\prime})-\xi^{*}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)\geqslant 0.

Rearranging the terms in the previous display, we obtain (

) when

and
b\neq b^{\prime}
. Finally, by continuity of
\xi^{*}
, the inequality (
4.3
) holds even when

or
b=b^{\prime}
.
∎
