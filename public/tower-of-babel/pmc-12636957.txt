Association between the atherogenic index of plasma and long-term prognosis in acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yiwen Zheng, Liya Wang, Xinling Tan, Zijun Chen, Zihan Zhang, Wei Yu, Xiujuan Jiang, Xian Yu, Jing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was aimed to evaluate the association between the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).DesignA retrospective cohort study.SettingThe study utilised data from two tertiary care centres in China, which were retrospectively analysed.ParticipantsThis retrospective cohort study initially enrolled 953 adult (≥18 years) patients with acute STEMI undergoing PCI. After applying exclusion criteria (including prior myocardial infarction, severe hepatic/renal dysfunction, lost follow-up and so on), 784 participants with complete clinical data comprised the final cohort and were divided into three AIP groups based on tertiles of AIP levels: low AIP group (n=260), medium AIP group (n=261) and high AIP group (n=263).ResultsHigh AIP patients exhibited significantly higher 2-year MACEs rates versus lower tertiles (p<0.05). After adjusting for age, sex, smoking and other factors, high AIP remained an independent MACEs predictor (OR:2.44; 95% CI 1.55 to 3.84; p<0.001). After adjustment, each one-unit increment in the AIP was associated with an adjusted OR of 3.295 (95% CI 1.683 to 6.449; p=0.001) for MACEs. In subgroup analyses, the high AIP level was a risk factor for the development of MACEs in patients of all ages, males, smokers, non-diabetic group and high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol group (p<0.05). Restricted cubic spline analysis showed a linear association between AIP levels and MACEs (p overall <0.05, p non-linear >0.05).ConclusionOur study demonstrates the AIP independently predicts 2-year MACEs in STEMI patients post-PCI, particularly recurrent acute coronary syndrome, suggesting its utility as a cost-effective prognostic tool complementary to traditional lipid metrics.

Introduction

Acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is one of the most common causes of death and disability globally.1 2 Early identification and timely treatment significantly impact the prognosis of STEMI patients, and direct percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is preferred for STEMI patients; however, the administration of medication post-PCI in acute STEMI patients is strongly associated with long-term prognosis.3 Reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is crucial for improving long-term outcomes in STEMI patients, yet the risk of cardiovascular events remains even after standardised lipid-lowering therapy.3 4

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) indirectly reflects the level of small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL), considered an alternative and simple marker of plasma atherogenicity, calculated from the formula log10(TG/HDL-C)58 (triglycerides, TG; high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, HDL-C). sdLDL particles are a subtype of LDL particles and are regarded as a more accurate biomarker for overall coronary artery disease prognosis than total LDL-C because of their stronger atherogenic effects.9 The stronger atherogenic effect of sdLDL is due to its smaller size and diameter, higher susceptibility to oxidative modification and glycation, lower affinity for LDL receptors and enhanced binding with proteoglycans, which facilitate the entry of sdLDL particles into the arterial intima and their prolonged residence in the subendothelial space, leading to increased lipid accumulation and atherosclerotic plaque formation.912 Duran and colleagues13 suggest that sdLDL-C is linked to the instability and susceptibility to rupture of atherosclerotic plaques due to its greater propensity for oxidative modification. The complexity and cost associated with measuring sdLDL make its implementation difficult and limit its widespread adoption. In contrast, AIP can be calculated from parameters obtained through routine lipid profile.

AIP levels have been found to be strongly associated with the risk of coronary artery disease in the elderly, higher the Synergy between PCI with Taxus and Cardiac Surgery (SYNTAX) scores, the development of coronary artery disease in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, the rapid progression of coronary atherosclerosis, the no-reflow phenomenon in patients with acute STEMI undergoing PCI and so on.1418 However, there are few studies on the relationship between AIP levels and the long-term prognosis of acute STEMI patients after PCI. This study aims to investigate whether the AIP can serve as an independent predictor of long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in STEMI patients undergoing PCI.

Methods

Study design

This study enrolled 784 patients who were diagnosed with acute STEMI and underwent PCI during the period from January 2016 to January 2022. The diagnostic criteria for STEMI include acute severe chest pain lasting more than 20 min, accompanied by an ECG showing ST-segment elevation, according to contemporary guidelines (ie, ≥1 mm (0.1 mV) in all non-V2-V3 leads, ≥2 mm (0.2 mV) in leads V2–V3 for men, and ≥1.5 mm (0.15 mV) in leads V2–V3 for women), in at least two contiguous leads as well as dynamic changes in markers of myocardial injury.1 Laboratory data prior to PCI treatment and perioperative information were collected, see figure 1.

Study flowchart. AIP, atherogenic index of plasma; PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention; STEMI, ST-elevation myocardial infarction.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosed acute STEMI patients undergoing PCI treatment; (2) complete and available laboratory test data; (3) age ≥18 years.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with a history of myocardial infarction, status postcoronary stent implantation or cardiovascular events in the past 3 months; (2) past or newly diagnosed autoimmune diseases, haematologic disorders, rheumatic heart disease, congenital heart diseases, malignancies, acute or chronic infectious diseases, severe hepatic renal insufficiency (alanine aminotransferase >5 upper limit of normal or total bilirubin >3 upper limit of normal or Child-Pugh score ≥10 or creatinine clearance rate ≤30 mL/min); (3) patients who had taken steroids or immunosuppressants in the past 3 months; (4) patients with a recent history of surgery, trauma or transfusion; (5) patients with incomplete clinical data, missing information, poor compliance or who were lost to follow-up.

This study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. This study was reviewed and approved by the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University with the approval number: 2024–46, dated 2 July 2024. Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was not required.

Data collection

Data for all enrolled patients were collected by specialised data collectors following a standardised data collection process, including basic patient information, personal history, medical history and auxiliary examination details. Smoking history was defined as ≥1 cigarette per day for more than 6 consecutive months; or previously met smoking criteria with cessation less than 6 months ago. Diseases were defined according to guidelines, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation and hyperlipidaemia. In this study, cerebrovascular disease was defined as encompassing cerebral infarction, transient ischaemic attack, intracerebral haemorrhage and subarachnoid haemorrhage. Chronic respiratory diseases were defined as including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchial asthma, bronchiectasis and interstitial lung disease. Intraoperative SYNTAX scores were calculated using the website (http://www.syntaxscore.com), based on the weighting factors of each segment and the scoring of lesion characteristics. Triple-vessel or left main coronary disease was defined as narrowing of the anterior descending branch, circumflex branch and right coronary artery all greater than 50% or left main coronary artery narrowing greater than 50%. Killip classification was diagnosed by the attending physician based on the patient’s symptoms and signs at admission: Class I—no signs of heart failure, no rales in the lungs; class II—signs of heart failure, bilateral basal rales not exceeding 50% of the lung fields; class III—significant heart failure, bilateral rales exceeding 50% of the lung fields; class IV—cardiogenic shock.

AIP was calculated using the formula AIP=log10(TG/HDL-C), TG and HDL-C units are in mmol/L. Based on AIP levels, subjects were divided into three groups defined by tertiles as follows: Group 1 (low AIP group) AIP≤0.009, group 2 (medium AIP group) 0.009<AIP≤0.221, group 3 (high AIP group) AIP>0.221.

Follow-up

All patients post-PCI were followed up through electronic medical records, phone calls and outpatient visits. The follow-up period is 2 years postsurgery, with the endpoint being the occurrence of MACEs or until the end of the 2-year follow-up, documenting the incidence of MACEs.

Primary endpoint: MACEs were defined as all-cause mortality, cardiac rupture, acute non-fatal myocardial infarction, unstable angina, target vessel reperfusion treatment, acute ischaemic stroke, acute heart failure, severe arrhythmias (multifocal ventricular premature beats, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, high-grade atrioventricular block, sick sinus syndrome) and cardiogenic shock.

Endpoints of special interest: recurrent ACS (unstable angina, STEMI, non-STEMI), acute heart failure and all-cause mortality post-operation.

Statistical analysis

Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of data. Data conforming to a normal distribution were presented as mean±standard deviation (X̄±s), with independent sample t-tests used to compare differences between two groups and one-way analyzed of variance for differences among three groups. Non-normally distributed data were presented using median and IQR (P25, P75), with the Mann-Whitney U test comparing differences between two groups and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for differences among three groups. Categorical data were presented as percentages (%) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using binary logistic regression, with a significance level of α=0.05, and p<0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.

Based on one-way logistic regression analysis and reflecting the consistency of OR of AIP level in different subgroups of the population and the interaction with different subgroups by forest tree. The relationship between AIP level and OR of occurrence of MACE and secondary endpoints in patients with acute STEMI was assessed by restricted cubic spline (RCS) and the OR with 95% CIs was calculated by logistic regression analysis. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

SPSS (V.26.0) and R studio (R V.4.4.1) were used to perform all statistical analyses.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the study.

Univariate and multivariate analyses and primary and secondary endpoints

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of MACEs occurrence, clarifying the association between the AIP and MACEs occurrence 2 years post-PCI in acute STEMI patients. As shown in table 3, univariate analysis demonstrated that age, hypertension, prior coronary heart disease, Killip class, SYNTAX score and postoperative use of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), statins and β-blockers were associated with MACEs occurrence over 2 years post-PCI in acute STEMI patients, and these associations were statistically significant (p<0.05). The high AIP level was significantly associated with an increased risk of MACEs in the univariate analysis (OR: 1.570, 95% CI 1.080 to 2.283, p=0.018). After adjusting for factors such as gender, age, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, previous coronary artery disease, Killip class (II or higher), SYNTAX score, left main or triple vessel disease, postoperative DAPT, β-blockers, statins and ACEI/ARB use, the high AIP level remains an independent risk factor for MACEs (OR: 2.458, 95% CI 1.566 to 3.858, p<0.001). Moreover, after adjustment, each one-unit increment in the AIP was associated with an adjusted OR of 3.295 (95% CI 1.683 to 6.449; p=0.001) for MACEs, see table 3. The high AIP level is also an independent risk factors for ACS (OR: 2.082, 95% CI 1.209 to 3.586, p=0.008) and all-cause death (OR: 2.573, 95% CI 1.006 to 6.581, p=0.049) (see tables3 4). Differences in acute heart failure before and after adjustment were not statistically significant across groups (see table 4).

RCS and subgroup analysis

Due to the discovery of a non-linear relationship between medium and high AIP levels compared with low AIP levels, the R V.3.4.2 software was used to conduct RCS analysis to determine the association between the ORs for MACEs, recurrent ACS, all-cause mortality and acute heart failure and AIP levels. The AIP level has statistical significance in predicting the occurrence of MACE 2 years post-PCI in acute STEMI patients (p overall=0.001), and it shows a linear association with the occurrence of MACEs (p non-linear=0.099>0.05). Furthermore, when AIP>0.12, the ORs for MACEs significantly increase, see figure 2. The AIP level is statistically significant in predicting the occurrence of recurrent ACS (p overall=0.010), and it has a linear relationship with the occurrence of recurrent ACS (p non-linear=0.143>0.05). Additionally, when AIP>0.12, the ORs for recurrent ACS significantly increase, see figure 3. However, the prediction of all-cause mortality and acute heart failure based on AIP levels is not statistically significant (p overall>0.05), see figure 4.

Discussion

This study primarily focuses on the relationship between the occurrence of MACEs after PCI in acute STEMI patients and AIP levels, aiming to develop evidence-based guidance for clinical practice. Key findings of the study include in patients with acute STEMI, the high AIP level is an independent risk factor for the occurrence of MACEs within 2 years post-PCI, primarily manifesting as recurrent ACS; the high AIP levels indicate a higher risk of poor prognosis in male acute STEMI patients of all ages who are non-diabetic, smokers and have high LDL-C; a possible linear relationship exists between AIP and the ORs for MACEs and recurrent ACS, with significant increases in the ORs for both when AIP exceeds 0.12. This confirms that AIP can be utilised to predict outcomes post-PCI in acute STEMI patients and could be integrated with other factors to offer a more precise and dependable prognostic tool.

The study revealed significant statistical differences in the rates of MACEs and recurrent ACS among acute STEMI patients post-PCI across different AIP level groups, with the high AIP level emerging as a risk factor for MACEs and recurrent ACS after PCI. Even after adjusting for traditional cardiovascular prognostic factors such as gender, age, smoking history, hypertension and diabetes, an increased OR persists, showing that high AIP levels are independent risk factors for MACEs, recurrent ACS and all-cause mortality 2 years post-PCI in acute STEMI patients. Additionally, RCS analysis revealed that when AIP levels exceed 0.12, the ORs for MACEs and recurrent ACS significantly increase 2 years post-PCI in acute STEMI patients, whereas there is no statistical significance for acute heart failure or all-cause mortality. This finding is consistent with prior research that showed high levels of sdLDL-C primarily impact overall cardiovascular event development by raising the risk for fatal and non-fatal myocardial infarctions (the hazard ratio for the fourth quartile group: 3.71, 95% CI 1.59 to 8.63).13

The mechanism underlying this phenomenon could be explained by the calculation of AIP from TG and HDL-C levels. HDL-C exerts a protective effect against atherosclerosis through its roles in promoting reverse cholesterol transport, bile secretion, anti-inflammatory actions and antioxidation. In contrast, elevated TG levels contribute to atherogenic damage.1922 Chylomicrons and very low-density lipoprotein containing TG undergo lipid exchange with cholesterol in LDL-C and HDL-C due to the action of cholesterol transport proteins. Higher TG levels intensify this exchange, raising sdLDL-C levels and lowering HDL-C levels23 which in turn promotes atherosclerosis. Higher AIP levels indicate higher TG and lower HDL-C levels, indirectly reflecting higher sdLDL-C levels. The susceptibility of sdLDL-C to oxidative modification can lead to larger fibrous plaque lipid cores and more foam cells, both of which, along with a thin fibrous cap, are considered key characteristics of vulnerable plaques.2426 In summary, high AIP levels can increase the instability and likelihood of plaque rupture through the mechanisms described above, and one of the critical drivers of acute STEMI is the rupture of these unstable plaques. Thus, it is hypothesised that elevated AIP levels might primarily increase the incidence of MACEs by promoting the instability of atherosclerotic plaques, primarily leading to recurrent ACS, while showing no significant statistical relevance for heart failure and all-cause mortality.

The study observed no protective effect when AIP levels were below 0.12, potentially attributable to the functions of HDL-C. Helgadottir and colleagues discovered that loss-of-function mutations in the gene encoding SR-BI increase HDL-C levels, yet do not mitigate the risk of coronary artery disease.27 These phenomena may be attributed to HDL-C subtypes; specific subtypes with different properties may be crucial in determining the effects of HDL-C.28 HDL initially exists as β-HDL, which matures into α-HDL through cholesterol acyltransferase activity. α-HDL represents the predominant form in plasma and comprises two major subclasses: HDL2 and HDL3.29 30 HDL2 particles are larger with lower density, while HDL3 represents an early maturation product with smaller size and higher density. HDL3 can further convert to HDL2 under cholesterol acyltransferase activity.29 31 Early studies indicated that elevated β-HDL levels served as a positive risk marker for ischaemic heart disease.32 33 However, recent research suggests that low β-HDL levels may predict more severe coronary artery stenosis.34 While these findings appear contradictory, they indicate that β-HDL may play different roles across various stages and populations. Regarding HDL2, previous studies demonstrated that high HDL2 levels significantly correlate with reduced cardiovascular risk,3537 reflecting its cardioprotective effects. In contrast, HDL3 shows no significant association with cardiovascular risk,37 and its role requires further investigation. The cardiovascular protective effects of HDL actually depend on subtype distribution, protein composition and functional characteristics, rather than total HDL-C levels alone.28 However, current routine clinical HDL-C measurements cannot adequately reflect these crucial biological indicators.

Different studies have arrived at various conclusions regarding AIP cut-off values. Zheng et al.38 noted a significant increase in the risk of MACEs post-PCI for non-diabetic patients with coronary artery disease when AIP exceeds 0.18. Some research suggests using 0.24 as the optimal cut-off for predicting MACEs in chronic coronary syndromes,39 while other studies identify high AIP levels as those exceeding 0.34.40 Currently, there is no clear definition for AIP cut-off values. The differing conclusions regarding AIP cut-offs in the aforementioned studies and this study are likely due to differences in the study populations. Like the current guidelines that recommend specific lipid-lowering targets for different strata of cardiovascular risk, AIP might also benefit from having varied cut-off values and management goals for different clinical characteristics and risk levels. This also indicates the need for large-scale studies involving diverse clinical characteristic samples to further explore the cardiovascular risks associated with different AIP levels in various populations, aiming to more precisely manage lipid levels and thereby prevent cardiovascular events.

The RCS analysis in this study revealed no statistically significant non-linear association between AIP and MACEs (p non-linear > 0.05). However, the shape of the curve suggested a potential J-shaped relationship. It is noteworthy that our findings clearly demonstrate that the high AIP level (AIP> 0.221) is an independent risk factor for MACEs in patients with acute STEMI. This underscores the clinical value of identifying and actively managing individuals with elevated AIP in practice. At the same time, the observed trend towards a J-shaped association implies that the relationship between AIP and MACEs may be more complex than a simple linear model, indicating that very low AIP levels may not confer additional benefit. Therefore, in patients with already low AIP, overly aggressive intervention should be avoided—though this hypothesis warrants further validation in prospective studies.

The study’s subgroup analysis, which considered different clinical characteristics, indicated that the high AIP level could pose varying degrees of risk to different groups. High AIP levels are likely to suggest a considerably high risk of adverse outcomes in male acute STEMI patients across all age groups who are non-diabetic, with LDL-C levels ≥1.8 mmol/L and who smoke. However, the subgroup analysis did not find a significant interaction between the subgroups and the high AIP level. Some previous findings that AIP is closely and independently related to cardiovascular risk in patients with type 2 diabetes,40 41 but subgroup analysis in this study did not replicate the aforementioned association. This may be due to the small sample size of subgroups in this study and a lack of adjustment for other prevalent risk factors. Thus, further research is needed to verify the above statement.

Recent studies have found that in populations with normal glucose metabolism, AIP levels are independently associated with the severity of newly diagnosed coronary heart disease.42 Min et al43 demonstrated that poor control of AIP levels is a strong predictor of newly developed cardiovascular diseases in populations with diabetes or pre-diabetes. This study also found a linear relationship between AIP levels and the occurrence of MACEs and recurrent ACS following PCI in acute STEMI patients. Some studies have also found that AIP levels are associated with an increase in epicardial fat tissue and negatively correlated with the incidence of heart failure and can also predict the risk of metabolic syndrome.4446 All these studies affirm that high AIP levels are closely linked to cardiovascular disease risk and outcomes. However, the causes may extend beyond lipid abnormalities to include other metabolic anomalies such as insulin resistance, calling for more foundational research and larger clinical studies to delve into these mechanisms. Few studies have addressed the link between AIP levels and adverse outcomes following PCI in patients with acute STEMI, and there is no established cut-off for AIP. This study finds that AIP levels independently correlate with poor outcomes post-PCI in acute STEMI patients, demonstrating a linear relationship and providing an evidence-based approach to prognostic assessment.

Limitation

This research is retrospective and subjected to certain inherent biases associated with retrospective studies, and it also has a limited sample size. Due to data unavailability, this study did not analyse several conventional prognostic indicators for acute STEMI patients, such as the time of onset, time from admission to guidewire passage through the culprit vessel, troponin and N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide. No further animal experiments or basic research were conducted to explore the mechanisms, and the possible mechanisms discussed are inferred from previous research findings.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that the AIP independently predicts 2-year MACEs in STEMI patients post-PCI, particularly recurrent ACS, suggesting its utility as a cost-effective prognostic tool complementary to traditional lipid metrics. However, further prospective studies are needed for more detailed analyses.
