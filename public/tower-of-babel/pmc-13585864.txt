A Lipoylation–PDH–TCA Transcriptional Deficit in the Alzheimer’s Disease Cortex — Donor-level Multi-cohort Evidence with Neuronal-composition, Disease-specificity and Matched-null Controls
Authors: Tao Zhang, Xiaodong Chen, Yi Wang, Yu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCuproptosis, a copper-dependent form of regulated cell death acting through lipoylated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle proteins, has been proposed as a molecular link between type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease. We asked whether cuproptosis effector genes are dysregulated in a cell-type-specific manner in the human Alzheimer’s disease cortex, and whether any such signal survives controls for neuronal composition and disease specificity. Single-nucleus data from 21 prefrontal cortex donors were aggregated to donor-level pseudobulk within each cell type. Four independent bulk cohorts, each contributing one observation per donor, entered a random-effects meta-analysis. Adjustment for estimated neuronal content, competitive and expression-matched null gene-set testing, and comparison against Huntington’s disease and vascular dementia were applied as controls. The donor was the unit of replication throughout. No gene–cell-type pair survived correction across the 75 prespecified tests; PDHA1 in inhibitory neurons showed the largest nominal reduction (− 0.418, q = 0.829). In donor-level meta-analysis PDHA1 was lower in Alzheimer’s disease in all four cohorts (− 0.457, 95% CI − 0.712 to − 0.201, I2 = 0%) but did not survive correction (q = 0.086). A post hoc lipoylation/pyruvate dehydrogenase/TCA module did reach the adjusted threshold (− 0.245, 95% CI − 0.363 to − 0.127, q = 0.035), whereas the copper modules pooled to zero and moved in opposite directions across cohorts. Comparable reductions occurred in Huntington’s disease and vascular dementia, and the disease-control contrasts were not significant. These transcriptional associations do not demonstrate cuproptosis, are not specific to Alzheimer’s disease, and are hypothesis-generating only.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12031-026-02598-7.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, PDHA1, Pyruvate dehydrogenase, Lipoylation, Donor-level analysis, Transcriptomic meta-analysis

Introduction

Cortical inhibitory interneurons exhibit disproportionately high metabolic demands relative to their excitatory counterparts and are increasingly recognized as determinants of cognitive integrity in aging and neurodegeneration (Kann 2016; Hu et al. 2014). Their tonic firing patterns, particularly in parvalbumin-expressing (PVALB +) populations, place a sustained load on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, rendering these neurons susceptible to bioenergetic stress (Inan et al. 2016; Yao et al. 2009). In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), interneuron loss and altered network rhythmicity have been demonstrated in both human postmortem studies and animal models (Verret et al. 2012; Palop and Mucke 2016), and single-nucleus transcriptomic atlases have since resolved these changes at cell-type resolution (Mathys et al. 2019; Grubman et al. 2019). Yet the molecular events that translate systemic metabolic dysfunction into selective interneuron vulnerability remain incompletely defined.

A complementary line of inquiry has linked peripheral metabolic disease, in particular type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), to elevated risk of AD (Chatterjee et al. 2017; Biessels and Despa 2018; Sims-Robinson et al. 2010). Although meta-analyses indicate a 1.5- to twofold increased AD risk in T2D populations (Zhang et al. 2017; Gudala et al. 2013), recent large-scale Mendelian randomization studies have not supported a direct genetic causal effect (Larsson and Markus 2018; Østergaard et al. 2015). This pattern is compatible with the epidemiologic association being mediated by specific molecular intermediaries rather than by a monolithic causal pathway, although it is equally compatible with residual confounding in the observational literature. Impaired insulin and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signalling in the AD brain has been proposed as one such intermediary and has been characterized in both postmortem tissue and mechanistic reviews (Talbot et al. 2012; Monte and Wands 2008; Fernandez and Torres-Alemán 2012; Arnold et al. 2018), but the cellular level at which any such defect operates has not been resolved.

Among the candidate molecular bridges, copper homeostasis has attracted renewed interest following the identification of cuproptosis, a copper-dependent form of regulated cell death distinct from apoptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis (Tsvetkov et al. 2022; Ge et al. 2022). Cuproptosis is triggered by direct binding of intracellular copper to lipoylated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle proteins, leading to lipoylated protein aggregation, iron–sulfur cluster destabilization, and proteotoxic stress (Tsvetkov et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2022). Its effectors include FDX1 (a regulator of lipoylation via LIAS), the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex subunits PDHA1, PDHB, DLAT, and DLD, and the copper transporters SLC31A1, ATP7A, and ATP7B. (Patel et al. 2014) Because cuproptosis depends on intact mitochondrial respiration, it has been proposed as a process that might preferentially affect neurons reliant on oxidative metabolism, including cortical interneurons.

Serum copper and ceruloplasmin are elevated in T2D (Squitti et al. 2014), copper accumulates in amyloid plaques (Bagheri et al. 2018; Brewer 2012), and dysregulation of copper transporters has been documented in both T2D and AD (Lutsenko 2010; Squitti et al. 2014). Despite these parallel observations, the cell-type specificity of cuproptosis effector dysregulation in the human AD cortex has not been systematically examined.

An additional consideration motivates the design of this study. The effectors of cuproptosis are, almost without exception, components of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex and the lipoylation machinery — that is, genes that are highly expressed in neurons and central to mitochondrial energy metabolism. Any transcriptional analysis of these genes in a tissue that loses neurons, as the AD cortex does, will therefore recover a signal for reasons that have nothing to do with copper. Distinguishing a copper-related process from neuronal composition, and from the general transcriptional consequences of neurodegeneration, is not a peripheral control but the central analytical problem. We designed the study accordingly.

We had three objectives. First, to identify which cell types in the human AD prefrontal cortex exhibit cuproptosis effector dysregulation, using a 167,290-nucleus single-nucleus RNA sequencing dataset with the donor as the unit of replication. Second, to determine whether any such signal survives adjustment for neuronal composition, competitive testing against the transcriptome-wide background, replication in independent cohorts, and comparison against non-Alzheimer neurodegenerative disease controls. Third, to place these findings in a population-level epidemiological context using a survey-weighted analysis of serum copper and cognitive performance. We did not perform genetic instrumental-variable analysis in this study. The resulting framework is intended to delimit which components of the proposed T2D–copper–AD hypothesis are supported by available human data and which are not (Figs. 1 and 2).

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Analysis Units, and Terminology

This study is an integrative computational analysis of publicly available datasets. No new experimental data were generated. Throughout, we distinguish three levels of inference and we use terminology that reflects what each analysis can and cannot establish.

First, all inferential statistics comparing disease and control groups in the single-nucleus data are performed with the donor (sample) as the unit of replication. Individual cells or nuclei are never treated as independent observations for any hypothesis test reported in this manuscript. Where cell-level distributions are displayed (Fig. 4B–D), they are presented as descriptive visualisations of the underlying data spread and the accompanying statistic is reported as descriptive only; no biological conclusion rests on it. The cell-level density panel of the composite score shown in the original submission has been removed. The replicate unit applied to every dataset, including the bulk cohorts in which a single donor contributes more than one array, is stated explicitly in Supplementary Table S23 and is discussed in the Limitations.

Second, we use the term cuproptosis-related transcriptional signature rather than “cuproptosis” when describing our own observations. We measured transcript abundance and gene-set scores. We did not measure intracellular copper, copper-induced toxicity, lipoylated DLAT or DLST aggregation, Fe–S cluster protein loss, FDX1 dependency, or copper-induced cell death; the full register of quantities this study does not measure is given as Additional file 4. Accordingly, the transcriptional profile described here is an expression-level phenotype enriched for cuproptosis effectors; it is not, and is not presented as, functional evidence that cuproptosis occurs in these cells.

Third, all pathway quantities are reported as transcriptomic pathway scores. No flux measurement (isotope tracing, extracellular flux analysis, or metabolomics) was performed; all pathway quantities are gene-expression module scores.

Datasets, sample sizes, and the replicate unit used for each analysis are summarised in Supplementary Table S23. Five machine-readable files accompany this manuscript and are cited individually where they apply: the results master table, to which every estimate reported here resolves (Additional file 1); the analysis plan, which assigns each analysis to a primary, secondary, exploratory or withdrawn tier (Additional file 2); the full membership of every gene set used (Additional file 3); the register of quantities this study does not measure (Additional file 4); and the minimum detectable effect for each null result (Additional file 5). Source data for each panel of Supplementary Figures S1 and S2 are provided as a separate workbook.

Single-nucleus RNA Sequencing (Primary Discovery Cohort)

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from 21 prefrontal cortex donors (12 AD, 9 control) were obtained from GSE157827 (Lau et al. 2020). Processing with Seurat v5 (Stuart et al. 2019) comprised quality control (200–6,000 detected genes, 500–30,000 UMIs, < 15% mitochondrial reads), SCTransform v2 normalisation, principal component analysis (50 components), Harmony (Korsunsky et al. 2019) batch correction with donor as the batch covariate, uniform manifold approximation and projection embedding, and Louvain clustering (resolution = 0.5). This yielded 167,290 nuclei annotated into eight major cell types using canonical lineage markers. The quality-control cut-offs are the conventional values for post-mortem single-nucleus cortex and were fixed before any group comparison; they were not tuned to the result. Because the primary tests are donor-level pseudobulk aggregates, moderate variation in these cut-offs changes the number of nuclei contributing to a donor's aggregate but not the number of independent observations, which is 21 throughout. Nuclear preparations retain little cytoplasmic RNA, so the mitochondrial fraction is low by construction and the 15% ceiling removes few nuclei.

Formal doublet classification (for example scDblFinder or DoubletFinder) was not performed, because nucleus-level count matrices were not regenerated for this revision. A cluster-level proxy screen was used instead and is reported as a sensitivity analysis; we do not describe it as formal doublet correction, and residual doublet contamination remains a limitation.

Donor sex was not available in the GSE157827 metadata and was inferred from XIST and Y-chromosome gene expression; sex-adjusted models are reported as sensitivity analyses and the inferred nature of this variable is stated wherever it is used. Donor age was not available for this cohort, so no age adjustment was possible at single-nucleus resolution.

Differential expression of cuproptosis-related genes within each cell type was assessed by pseudobulk DESeq2: (Love et al. 2014) counts were summed within each donor × cell-type combination, and the resulting donor-level matrix was analysed with donor as the unit of replication. Multiplicity was controlled across the full gene × cell-type family by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

The prespecified cuproptosis gene set contains 16 genes as defined by Tsvetkov et al. (Tsvetkov et al. 2022) Thirteen of these 16 genes passed the prespecified expression filter (detected in ≥ 10% of nuclei in at least one cell type) and are reported in the pseudobulk analysis (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S11a). LIPT1, ATP7A and GCSH did not pass this filter in any cell type and were therefore not testable at the single-nucleus level; none was removed post hoc on the basis of its result. All three are retained in every bulk-tissue analysis, where they are adequately detected, and appear in the full 16-gene panels (Figs. 2A and 3D). Six of the eight annotated cell types yielded enough nuclei per donor to be tested; endothelial cells and pericytes did not. Of the resulting 78 gene–cell-type combinations, three were not estimable, giving the family of 75 tests to which Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied. The complete flow from 16 predefined genes to 13 testable genes and 75 tests is set out in Supplementary Table S11a.

As a complementary donor-level test that does not depend on the DESeq2 model, mean normalised expression per donor was compared between groups by Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Supplementary Table S11b), and cell-type-level cuproptosis module scores (Seurat AddModuleScore) were compared between donors by the same test (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S10). Inhibitory Neuron Sub-clustering and Co-expression Analysis

Inhibitory neurons (n = 24,510 nuclei) were sub-clustered into 17 populations and annotated by canonical GABAergic markers (SST, PVALB, VIP, LAMP5, ADARB2). Three-axis correlation analysis (IGF/insulin × glycometabolism × cuproptosis-related genes) used Spearman correlation. Cells were stratified into IGF/insulin signal tertiles, and a PDHA1-centred co-expression network was constructed across five functional modules.

These co-expression, tertile-stratification, and network analyses are descriptive and exploratory. They are computed across pooled nuclei and are therefore subject to donor-level dependence; the associated p values are not corrected for the clustering of nuclei within donors and are reported as nominal descriptive statistics only. No claim of statistical significance at the donor level is made for these panels (Fig. 4B–D), and no mechanistic conclusion in this manuscript rests on them.

Cell–cell Communication Analysis

CellChat v2 (Jin et al. 2021) was used to infer ligand–receptor interactions between conditions across the eight cell types. Pathway-level information-flow differences were quantified (Supplementary Table S14) and inhibitory-neuron-specific interactions were enumerated (Supplementary Table S15).

CellChat infers communication probability from the co-expression of annotated ligand–receptor pairs. A pathway-level probability of zero indicates that no constituent ligand–receptor pair reached the detection and permutation thresholds in that condition; it does not demonstrate that the pathway is functionally inactive, and it is not evidence of abolished signalling. Seven of the 50 detected pathways had zero inferred probability in AD (ANGPTL, VEGF, IGF, BMP, COMPLEMENT, THY1, ITGB2); all are listed in Supplementary Table S14. IGF is not among the 15 pathways ranked by absolute probability difference that are displayed in Fig. 4A, because its absolute difference (− 0.055) is small relative to the highly expressed adhesion and neurotrophic pathways shown there; the IGF result is therefore reported from Supplementary Table S14.

Bulk Transcriptomic and Additional Single-nucleus Cohorts

Bulk-tissue analyses used GSE33000 (Narayanan et al. 2014) (prefrontal cortex; 310 AD, 157 non-demented controls, one array per donor; Illumina microarray), analysed with limma (Ritchie et al. 2015) and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA; GSVA package (Hänzelmann et al. 2013)); and GSE161355 (Bury et al. 2021) (11 donors: 6 with type 2 diabetes and 5 controls, each contributing neuronal, astrocytic and vascular laser-captured fractions, 33 arrays in total; Affymetrix GPL570). The counts of 18 and 15 given in the original submission were array counts rather than participant counts; this cohort contains no cognitive assessment and is used only as an exploratory diabetes-versus-control comparison.

For the revision, four additional independent cohorts were analysed. GSE138852 (Grubman et al. 2019) is a single-nucleus dataset of the entorhinal cortex comprising 6 Alzheimer’s disease and 6 control donors and 13,214 nuclei; in the published cell-type composition of this dataset neurons constitute approximately 5% of nuclei, which is the reason that most neuronal gene–cell-type combinations, including PDHA1 in inhibitory neurons, could not be evaluated (see Results). GSE5281 (Liang et al. 2008) comprises 161 arrays of laser-capture-microdissected pyramidal neurons (87 Alzheimer, 74 control) sampled from six anatomically distinct brain regions, with multiple regions contributed by each donor; donor identity is not recorded in the GEO metadata for this series, and a candidate donor key built from the sample titles was inconsistent with the recorded donor age, so this cohort is analysed within region, where each donor contributes exactly one array. GSE122063 (McKay et al. 2019) comprises 136 arrays of frontal and temporal cortex from 31 donors (12 with Alzheimer’s disease, 8 with vascular dementia and 11 with no dementia) assayed together, with two brain regions per donor and technical replicates within region; donor identity is recorded in the subject-identifier field, and the counts of 56 Alzheimer and 44 control given in the original submission were array counts, not donor counts. Two further bulk cohorts entered the cross-cohort meta-analysis only: GSE132903, comprising 195 arrays of middle temporal gyrus with one array per donor (97 Alzheimer, 98 control), on which 14 of the 16 prespecified genes are measurable; and GSE48350, comprising 253 arrays from 84 donors (28 Alzheimer, 56 control) sampled from up to four cortical regions per donor, standardised within region and averaged to one observation per donor. The Huntington’s disease arm of the discovery collection was analysed on the same platform as GSE33000 (624 arrays in the combined analysis, one array per donor). GSE44770 was excluded a priori from all meta-analyses because it derives from the same sample collection as GSE33000 and would not constitute independent replication.

Replication, Meta-analysis, and Specificity Analyses

Four sets of analyses were added in response to review. All were prespecified as families for multiplicity control, and complete numerical results including all null findings are provided in Supplementary Tables S17–S23.

Independent Single-nucleus Evaluation

Donor-level pseudobulk analysis was repeated in GSE138852. Only gene–cell-type combinations passing the same prespecified expression filter were tested; every tested combination is reported (Supplementary Fig. S1A, Supplementary Table S16), and minimum detectable effects for untested combinations are given in Supplementary Table S16 and, for every null result in this manuscript, in Additional file 5.

Cross-cohort Meta-analysis

Random-effects meta-analysis with the restricted maximum likelihood estimator of between-study variance and the Knapp–Hartung adjustment to confidence intervals and tests (metaphor (Viechtbauer 2010)) of the 16 prespecified genes and of the module scores was performed across independent cohorts, excluding the discovery cohort GSE33000 (Supplementary Figs. S1B, S1C; Supplementary Tables S17, S18). For this revision the whole meta-analysis was recomputed from the public data so that every contributing cohort enters it at donor level. Cohort-level effects were estimated on within-cohort standardised expression using the module score definition given above and the model recorded for each cohort in Supplementary Table S23: a mixed-effects model with the donor as a random intercept in GSE122063; separate linear models within each of the six brain regions in GSE5281, where donor identity is not recorded and each donor contributes one array per region, combined across regions by averaging with the standard error taken as the larger of the mean regional standard error and the between-region standard deviation, because the same donors recur in every region; a linear model on the single array per donor in GSE132903; and a donor-level linear model after within-region standardisation in GSE48350. Between-cohort heterogeneity is reported as I2, with the number of contributing cohorts (k), the 95% prediction interval, the number of cohorts sharing the pooled direction, and the leave-one-cohort-out range. Genes not measurable on a platform contribute no estimate, so k is 4 for 13 genes, 3 for DLAT and SLC31A1, and 2 for GCSH.

Module Score Definition

Each module score is the mean of the within-cohort standardised expression values of its member genes, after collapsing probes to genes and standardising each gene to zero mean and unit variance within the cohort; the number of member genes present is recorded for every cohort in Supplementary Table S19. The five module scores are post hoc subdivisions of the 16 prespecified cuproptosis genes and of two KEGG-curated metabolic gene sets, they were not prespecified, and they are labelled as post hoc wherever they are reported. Neuronal-composition sensitivity. Because AD cortex loses neurons, any neuron-enriched transcript will fall in bulk tissue for reasons unrelated to a disease-specific mechanism. We therefore (i) re-estimated module effects in GSE33000 with adjustment for an estimated neuronal-content score (Supplementary Fig. S2A, Supplementary Table S19); (ii) repeated the module analysis in GSE5281, where laser capture removes glia from the input material so that glial dilution cannot generate a difference, estimating the effects separately within each of the six brain regions so that each donor contributes exactly one array (Supplementary Fig. S2B, Supplementary Table S20); and (iii) tested each module competitively against the transcriptome-wide background (camera (Wu and Smyth 2012)) and against 1,000 random gene sets matched for expression level and neuronal specificity (Supplementary Fig. S2C, Supplementary Table S21). Confidence intervals were not produced for the neuronal-content-adjusted estimates by the original analysis pipeline. For this revision the whole adjustment was recomputed from the public data with age and sex as covariates, and confidence intervals are reported for every adjusted estimate (Supplementary Table S19).

Disease Specificity

Module effects were compared between Alzheimer’s disease and two disease controls — Huntington’s disease, analysed within the discovery collection on the same platform, and vascular dementia, analysed in GSE122063 where all three groups were assayed together — and the formal Alzheimer’s-versus-disease-control contrasts were tested directly (Supplementary Fig. S2D, Supplementary Table S22).

Diabetes–Cuproptosis Impairment (DCI) Score and Machine Learning

The operational DCI Score was calculated from 19 genes that were detected above the expression threshold in the single-nucleus data and annotated on the GSE33000 platform. The operational axes were IGF/insulin signalling (PTEN, INSR, IRS2, AKT1, IRS1, IGF1R), cuproptosis pro-death (PDHA1, DLAT, LIAS, DLD, PDHB, FDX1), copper transport/protection (SLC31A1, ATP7A, ATP7B), and mitochondrial regulators (PPARGC1A, FOXO3, ESRRA, NRF1). Axis scores were Z-standardised and combined as DCI = Z(Cup_ProDeath) − Z(IGF_Signal) − Z(Cup_Protect) − Z(Mito_Regulator). The DCI Score is a prespecified composite of curated gene sets, not a data-derived classifier; its operational membership and axis direction are stated here in full so that the score does not depend on an unavailable supplementary table. All group comparisons of the DCI Score and its axes are performed at the donor level (Fig. 5A and C).

A random forest classifier was trained to separate DCI-High from DCI-Low inhibitory neurons using 119 features: the 19 DCI genes together with the top 100 genes derived from independent component analysis. The balanced dataset (n = 4,620) was split 70/30 for training and testing, with leave-one-sample-out cross-validation across 20 donors and cross-cell-type transfer without retraining. Receiver operating characteristic curves and the areas under them were computed with pROC (Robin et al. 2011); every area is reported with a 95% confidence interval in Supplementary Table S24.

This Analysis is Internally Circular

The classifier’s features included the 19 DCI genes that define the score being predicted, so the reported discrimination largely reflects reconstruction of a deterministic function of those same genes rather than independent biological validation. It is retained only as a technical demonstration that the composite is a learnable, internally consistent function of its inputs, and is not interpreted as biological validation of the DCI Score. Feature selection in the original analysis was performed once on the full dataset rather than within each cross-validation fold, which inflates cross-validated performance; this is stated as a limitation. Confirmatory analyses that would break the circularity — retraining after removal of the DCI genes, and nested cross-validation with feature selection inside each fold — are identified in the Discussion as required before any predictive claim can be made.

In silico Perturbation and Driver Ranking

In silico perturbation modelled the predicted downstream effect of shifting each of the 19 operational DCI genes from AD to control levels, using linear regression fitted in control inhibitory neurons; a gene was counted as “rescued” if the predicted change opposed the observed AD direction. Housekeeping genes (ACTB, GAPDH, UBC) were included as negative controls.

These Controls did not Behave as Controls

They achieved reversal fractions of 64.0–71.9% and the largest cumulative reversal magnitudes in the entire ranking (Fig. 5E). This indicates that the metric is driven substantially by the connectivity and expression level of a gene within the co-expression structure rather than by specific causal involvement. This quantity is therefore reported as a descriptive perturbational connectivity score for experimental follow-up only. The name “Driver Causality Score” used in the original submission has been withdrawn from the manuscript, the figures and the supplementary material, and no causal interpretation is placed on this quantity anywhere.

Population-level Analysis (NHANES)

NHANES 2011–2014 participants aged ≥ 60 years with a Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) score and a serum copper measurement were included; after exclusion for missing covariates, 945 participants remained (277 with diabetes, 668 without). Serum copper was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry and natural log-transformed. Linear regression models were fitted sequentially: Model 1 (crude); Model 2 (age, sex, race/ethnicity, education); Model 3 (additionally poverty–income ratio, body mass index, hypertension, smoking, drinking, HbA1c). Restricted cubic splines with four knots assessed non-linearity. Sensitivity analyses used alternative cognitive outcomes, exclusion of extreme copper values, survey-weighted models incorporating the NHANES design, and a restricted diabetes definition based on self-reported physician diagnosis alone.

This Analysis is Exploratory

The primary adjusted association is null in the full sample and does not reach conventional significance in the diabetes subgroup; the survey-weighted estimate is close to the null; and the mediation analysis is not statistically significant for any candidate mediator. Subgroup and interaction tests were not corrected for multiplicity and are hypothesis-generating. No causal or confirmatory interpretation is placed on these results.

Network Pharmacology, Enrichment, and Molecular Docking

Copper-related targets (n = 4,522) were retrieved from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (Davis et al. 2023) (Supplementary Table S4). Disease targets for T2D (n = 5,980) and cognitive impairment (n = 567) were obtained from GeneCards (Stelzer et al. 2016) at a relevance score > 15. The triple intersection defined 192 core targets (Supplementary Table S5), and this is the only core target set used in this manuscript.

The gene sets used at each step are defined as follows, and the complete membership of each is listed in Additional file 3. The functional enrichment analysis was not performed on the 192 core targets. It was performed on the union of the pairwise intersections, of which 1,522 genes mapped to Entrez identifiers and entered clusterProfiler, (Yu et al. 2012) and this is the denominator shown in every GeneRatio in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8. The background universe was the set of 9,564 genes annotated in at least one KEGG pathway, which is the denominator of every BgRatio. Enrichment used a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05, at which 209 KEGG pathways were significant.

A pathway was labelled “DCI-related” by the following prespecified criterion: a significant pathway was so labelled if it contained at least one gene from the union of the four DCI axis gene sets and belonged to one of three KEGG categories — insulin/IGF and glucose metabolism, mitochondrial energy metabolism, or programmed cell death. Thirty-seven of the 209 significant pathways met this criterion (Supplementary Table S8). Broad signalling pathways such as PI3K–Akt and MAPK are reported for completeness but are not treated as independent mechanistic evidence: they contain hundreds of genes, overlap heavily with one another, and are enriched in most large target lists.

Cross-pathway hub genes were defined as genes participating in ≥ 6 significant DCI-related pathways; 32 genes met this criterion and the 25 with the highest degree are displayed (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S9).

Molecular docking used CB-Dock2 (Liu et al. 2020) to estimate binding affinities between five copper-modulating compounds (D-penicillamine, trientine, tetrathiomolybdate, elesclomol, disulfiram) and three targets (FDX1, PDB 2Y5C; DLAT, PDB 1Y8N; CDKN2A, PDB 1BI7). Docking scores are computational estimates of geometric and energetic complementarity. They are not evidence of physical binding, target engagement, or functional modulation, and are reported here only as a hypothesis-generating triage step.

Computational Drug Repurposing

Connectivity scoring assessed 13 candidate drugs across five pharmacological categories against the 89-gene AD inhibitory neuron signature. Drug target sets were curated from PharmGKB and DrugBank (Wishart et al. 2018), and pre-ranked GSEA (fgsea (Subramanian et al. 2005), 10,000 permutations) provided complementary evidence. Connectivity scores rank transcriptional signature opposition; they do not account for pharmacokinetics, blood–brain barrier penetration, or toxicity, and they are not predictions of clinical efficacy.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses used R 4.3.1 with Seurat v5, DESeq2, limma, GSVA, clusterProfiler, fgsea, metafor, lme4 and pROC; the exact package versions and the random seed are recorded by sessionInfo() in every script deposited in the reproducibility archive. Every estimate reported in this manuscript, in the figures and in the supplementary material resolves to a single row of the results master table (Additional file 1). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. Multiplicity was controlled by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure within prespecified families, which are defined explicitly for each analysis in the corresponding Supplementary Table. Three further thresholds are used, and each is stated where it applies rather than left implicit: detection in at least 10% of nuclei in at least one cell type, which is the filter that determines which genes are testable at single-nucleus resolution; a false discovery rate of 0.05 for the competitive gene-set tests, applied on the same scale as every other family in this work; and 1,000 permutations for the expression- and specificity-matched empirical null, which sets the resolution of that empirical p value at 0.001. No analysis in this manuscript uses a relaxed discovery threshold such as a false discovery rate of 0.25. Effect estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals and sample sizes throughout, and the covariates entered into each model are stated where the analysis is reported and summarised for every cohort in Supplementary Table S23. Receiver operating characteristic curves were computed with pROC (Robin et al. 2011). Every area under the curve reported in this manuscript is accompanied by a 95% confidence interval in Supplementary Table S24. For the six module-level curves in GSE33000 these are DeLong intervals; the areas were also recomputed for this revision directly from the public data and agree with the archived values to within 0.03 across all six modules, and both sets are reported. For the four donor-level curves in the discovery cohort, which are computed on 12 Alzheimer and 9 control donors, intervals were obtained by the method of Hanley and McNeil from the areas and the group sizes, and are correspondingly wide. AUCs are interpreted descriptively and no validation or predictive claim rests on them. Group comparisons of disease versus control in the single-nucleus data use the donor as the unit of replication throughout; the unit applied in each bulk cohort is stated in Supplementary Table S23.

Results

PDHA1 shows the Largest Nominal Reduction among Cuproptosis-related Genes in AD Inhibitory Neurons, but no Gene–Cell-Type Pair Survives Correction

Clustering of 167,290 nuclei from GSE157827 resolved eight cell types: excitatory neurons (35.0%), oligodendrocytes (25.6%), inhibitory neurons (14.6%), astrocytes (11.4%), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (7.4%), microglia (4.6%), endothelial cells (0.9%), and pericytes (0.5%).

Donor-level pseudobulk DESeq2 analysis across the 13 cuproptosis-related genes that passed the prespecified expression filter and the six cell types with sufficient nuclei identified PDHA1 in inhibitory neurons as the pair with the largest reduction (effect − 0.418, 95% CI − 0.748 to − 0.088, p = 0.013; Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S11a). Three of the 78 possible gene–cell-type combinations were not estimable, giving 75 tests in the prespecified family. No gene–cell-type pair survived Benjamini–Hochberg correction across these 75 tests (PDHA1 in inhibitory neurons q = 0.829; the estimate in excitatory neurons was − 0.303, p = 0.051, q = 0.943), a formal inhibitory-versus-excitatory contrast was null (p = 0.666), and we therefore do not claim inhibitory-neuron selectivity. LIPT1, ATP7A and GCSH did not pass the expression filter in any cell type and were therefore not testable at single-nucleus resolution (Methods, “Single-nucleus RNA sequencing”); all three are retained in the bulk-tissue analyses and in the full 16-gene panels.

Two further donor-level analyses were performed and are reported in full because they qualify this result. At the donor level the lipoylation/PDH/TCA module score was lower in AD inhibitory neurons (− 0.319, 95% CI − 1.006 to 0.369, p = 0.341) but no cell type reached the adjusted threshold (all q ≥ 0.883; Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S10). The nominal p = 0.043 reported in the original submission came from a cell-level test in which nuclei were treated as independent observations; it is withdrawn, and Supplementary Table S10 has been replaced by the donor-level table. A donor-level Wilcoxon test on mean PDHA1 expression, which does not rely on the DESeq2 dispersion model, did not reach significance in inhibitory neurons (p = 0.060; Supplementary Table S11b). The PDHA1 signal is therefore consistent in direction across both donor-level approaches but is significant under one model and not the other, and it should be regarded as suggestive rather than established. With 12 AD and 9 control donors, this cohort has limited power to distinguish a modest cell-type-specific effect from noise.

At the bulk-tissue level in GSE161355 (11 donors: 6 with type 2 diabetes and 5 controls; 33 laser-captured fraction arrays), none of the 16 cuproptosis-related genes was differentially expressed (all p > 0.05; Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S6), and the composite cuproptosis ssGSEA score did not differ between groups (p = 0.63). This cohort provides no support for the hypothesis.

Molecular Context of PDHA1 in Inhibitory Neurons (Exploratory)

Sub-clustering of 24,510 inhibitory neurons identified 17 populations annotated by canonical GABAergic markers. PDHA1 expression was lower in AD across all IGF/insulin signal tertiles (Fig. 4C), and genome-wide co-expression placed PDHA1 within a network spanning cuproptosis effectors, IGF/insulin components, glycometabolism enzymes, and neuronal genes, with PPARGC1A (PGC-1α) the most strongly correlated transcription factor (Fig. 4B, D).

These analyses pool nuclei across donors and do not account for donor-level dependence; the accompanying p values are nominal and are reported as descriptive only (Methods, “Inhibitory neuron sub-clustering and co-expression analysis”). The correlation coefficients themselves are modest (PPARGC1A–PDHA1 r = 0.148), and the very small p values attached to them are a function of the large number of nuclei rather than of effect size. No conclusion in this manuscript rests on these panels.

Inferred Cell–cell Communication

CellChat analysis detected 50 signalling pathways, most with reduced inferred communication probability in AD. Seven pathways had an inferred probability of zero in AD (ANGPTL, VEGF, IGF, BMP, COMPLEMENT, THY1, ITGB2; Supplementary Table S14). For IGF specifically, the inferred probability was 0 in AD versus 0.055 in control. Inhibitory neurons showed a net reduction of 27 ligand–receptor pairs (148 in AD versus 175 in control; Supplementary Table S15). The 15 pathways with the largest absolute probability differences, all reduced in AD, are shown in Fig. 4A; IGF is not among them.

As set out in Methods, “Cell–cell communication analysis”, a zero inferred probability reflects failure to reach detection and permutation thresholds and is not evidence that a pathway is functionally abolished. We therefore describe this result only as a loss of detectable inferred IGF ligand–receptor communication in this dataset.

Bulk-tissue Association in GSE33000

In GSE33000 (310 AD, 157 controls), 15 of 16 prespecified cuproptosis-related genes met the adjusted threshold (LIPT1 did not, q = 0.057; Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table S13). Transcriptome-wide, 11,363 of 17,405 genes met FDR < 0.05, so this proportion is not by itself informative and competitive gene-set testing was used instead. Positive regulators were predominantly lower in AD: PDHB (− 0.131, 95% CI − 0.158 to − 0.104, q = 4.1 × 10 − 19), DLAT (− 0.129, 95% CI − 0.157 to − 0.101, q = 2.8 × 10 − 17) and PDHA1 (− 0.085, 95% CI − 0.104 to − 0.067, q = 2.7 × 10 − 17); CDKN2A was higher (+ 0.050, 95% CI 0.031 to 0.069, q = 1.3 × 10 − 6). Effects are differences in log-ratio on a two-colour platform and are adjusted for age and sex. All four cuproptosis ssGSEA composites differed between groups (all p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S12).

These are Whole-tissue Measurements and they do not Establish the Cellular Origin of the Signal

GSE33000 contains no cell-type information. The concordance in direction between GSE33000 and the single-nucleus inhibitory neuron result is compatible with an inhibitory-neuron origin, but it is equally compatible with a contribution from excitatory neurons, from any other cell population, or from a change in cellular composition. The section “Neuronal composition accounts for the copper-related signal but not for the lipoylation/PDH/TCA signal” reports analyses designed specifically to test the composition explanation.

Neuronal Composition Accounts for the Copper-related Signal but not for the Lipoylation/PDH/TCA Signal

Because AD cortex loses neurons, a neuron-enriched transcript will decline in bulk tissue irrespective of any disease-specific mechanism. Three analyses were performed to test this explanation (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Tables S20–S22).

Adjustment for an estimated neuronal-content score in GSE33000 attenuated every module (Supplementary Fig. S2A, Supplementary Table S19). The copper import and metal-sensing module was reduced to approximately zero (0.314 → − 0.001, adjusted p = 0.981), and the copper homeostasis module was non-significant both before and after adjustment (0.084 → 0.058, p = 0.190). The lipoylation/PDH/TCA module was attenuated by more than half but remained detectable (− 0.552 → − 0.254, p = 4.2 × 10 − 5; the independent recomputation reported below, which is the estimate we treat as primary because it carries a confidence interval and reproduces from the public data, gives − 0.113, 95% CI − 0.213 to − 0.013, p = 0.026), as did oxidative phosphorylation (− 0.789 → − 0.234, p = 2.6 × 10 − 7); the TCA cycle module was attenuated to − 0.108 (p = 0.025). For this revision the entire adjustment was recomputed independently from the public GSE33000 data under the module score definition given in Methods, with age and sex as covariates and with confidence intervals for every estimate (Supplementary Table S19). Five of the six modules reproduced. The copper import and metal-sensing module was again reduced to zero and became non-significant (0.504 → − 0.002, 95% CI − 0.106 to 0.102, p = 0.970), the copper homeostasis module was again null after adjustment (p = 0.293), and the lipoylation/PDH/TCA and oxidative phosphorylation modules again survived (− 0.113, 95% CI − 0.213 to − 0.013, p = 0.026 and − 0.163, 95% CI − 0.239 to − 0.086, p = 3.6 × 10 − 5). The adjusted TCA cycle estimate did not reproduce (− 0.044, 95% CI − 0.109 to 0.021, p = 0.187) and is therefore reported as a point estimate without a significance claim. The composition conclusion below rests only on the modules that were stable under both implementations.

In GSE5281, where laser capture of pyramidal neurons removes glia from the input material so that glial dilution cannot generate a difference, the module analysis was repeated for this revision at the donor level. Donor identifiers are not provided in the GEO metadata for this series, and a candidate donor key constructed from the sample titles proved inconsistent with the recorded donor age, so a donor random-effects model across regions cannot be fitted. Because each donor contributes at most one array per brain region, the effects were therefore estimated separately within each of the six regions, in which the array is the donor and pseudoreplication cannot arise (Supplementary Fig. S2B, Supplementary Table S20). The oxidative phosphorylation module was lower in Alzheimer’s disease in all six regions (− 0.482 to − 1.210, all p ≤ 0.018) and the TCA cycle module in all six (− 0.391 to − 0.928, all p ≤ 0.048). The lipoylation/PDH/TCA module was lower in direction in all six regions but reached significance in only one, the superior frontal gyrus (− 0.895, 95% CI − 1.447 to − 0.343, p = 0.002, q = 0.007), where it also survived adjustment for neuronal content (− 0.600, 95% CI − 1.004 to − 0.196, p = 0.005). The copper import and metal-sensing module was not elevated in any region. The pooled estimates reported in the original submission, which treated the array as the unit of analysis (lipoylation/PDH/TCA − 0.384, 95% CI − 0.566 to − 0.202, q = 9.5 × 10 − 5; oxidative phosphorylation − 0.766, q = 2.2 × 10 − 12; TCA cycle − 0.587, q = 1.4 × 10 − 11; copper import + 0.007, q = 0.941), are retained in Supplementary Table S20 for comparison; their intervals do not account for within-donor correlation. In this cohort the broader mitochondrial signal is therefore robust to donor-level re-estimation across all six regions, whereas the narrower lipoylation/PDH/TCA subdivision is resolved in one region only. Other cohort, platform, and technical differences between GSE5281 and GSE33000 also cannot be excluded as contributors.

The behaviour of the copper modules across cohorts is not neutral but inconsistent. In the discovery cohort the copper import and metal-sensing module was higher in Alzheimer’s disease before adjustment (+ 0.314) and fell to zero after adjustment for estimated neuronal content (− 0.001, p = 0.981). In the independent donor-level cohort GSE48350 both copper modules were significantly lower rather than higher in Alzheimer’s disease (copper import and metal sensing − 0.296, q = 0.009; copper homeostasis − 0.243, q = 0.009). In the laser-captured pyramidal neurons of GSE5281, estimated within region at donor level, the copper homeostasis module reached the adjusted threshold in three of six regions and the copper import module in two, in each case in the direction of a reduction, whereas the lipoylation/PDH/TCA module reached it in one. Pooled across the four independent cohorts the two copper modules therefore cancel to approximately zero (− 0.022 and − 0.001, q = 0.994 for each) with moderate between-cohort heterogeneity (I2 = 59% and 66%). A module whose constituent transcripts move in opposite directions in different cohorts, and which is reduced rather than elevated wherever it does reach significance at donor level, does not behave as an active copper-import programme would predict.

Competitive testing against the transcriptome-wide background gave the same pattern (Supplementary Fig. S2C, Supplementary Table S21): oxidative phosphorylation (camera FDR = 1.3 × 10−11; empirical p = 0.001), the TCA cycle (FDR = 1.3 × 10−3; p = 0.001), and lipoylation/PDH/TCA (FDR = 6.2 × 10−3; p = 0.004) exceeded both the transcriptome-wide background and random gene sets matched for expression level and neuronal specificity, whereas the copper import and metal-sensing (FDR = 0.318; p = 0.255) and copper homeostasis modules (FDR = 0.375; p = 0.550) did not. These tests establish that the mitochondrial and lipoylation-related changes exceed the general transcriptional alteration present in this tissue; they provide no functional evidence of cuproptosis.

Taken together, the copper-transport component of the original hypothesis is not supported once neuronal composition is accounted for. The mitochondrial and lipoylation component survives these controls.

The Lipoylation/PDH/TCA Signature is not Specific to Alzheimer’s Disease

Module effects were compared with two disease controls (Supplementary Fig. S2D, Supplementary Table S22). In the discovery collection the lipoylation/PDH/TCA module was reduced to a greater degree in Huntington’s disease (− 0.751, q = 4.5 × 10 − 32) than in Alzheimer’s disease (− 0.552, q = 1.0 × 10 − 15). GSE122063 was re-estimated at donor level for this revision, using a mixed-effects model with donor as a random intercept. The module was lower in Alzheimer’s disease (− 0.419, 95% CI − 0.693 to − 0.145, q = 0.034; 12 Alzheimer and 11 control donors) and lower to a comparable degree in vascular dementia, although with only eight vascular dementia donors that estimate did not reach the adjusted threshold (− 0.232, 95% CI − 0.608 to 0.144, q = 0.456). The TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation modules behaved in the same way in both disease controls (Alzheimer’s disease − 0.644 and − 0.855, both q = 0.032; vascular dementia − 0.547 and − 0.659, q = 0.074 and 0.092). The copper import module was elevated in Huntington’s disease (+ 0.384, q = 8.1 × 10 − 11) and in Alzheimer’s disease within the discovery cohort (+ 0.314, q = 4.9 × 10 − 7), but at donor level in GSE122063 it was not significantly elevated in either Alzheimer’s disease (+ 0.257, q = 0.321) or vascular dementia (+ 0.161, q = 0.645).

The formal Alzheimer’s-versus-disease-control contrasts for the lipoylation/PDH/TCA module were not statistically significant (AD versus Huntington’s disease q = 0.145; AD versus vascular dementia at donor level − 0.188, 95% CI − 0.575 to 0.198, q = 0.587). The same held for the TCA cycle (AD versus Huntington’s disease q = 0.337; AD versus vascular dementia q = 0.780). For oxidative phosphorylation the Alzheimer’s-versus-Huntington’s contrast did reach the adjusted threshold in the discovery collection (+ 0.252, q = 0.042, indicating a smaller reduction in Alzheimer’s disease), whereas the Alzheimer’s-versus-vascular-dementia contrast did not (− 0.197, q = 0.700), so the equivalence claim is stated for the lipoylation/PDH/TCA and TCA cycle modules and qualified for oxidative phosphorylation. This transcriptional signature is therefore a shared correlate of neurodegeneration in these datasets and is not specific to Alzheimer’s disease or to a diabetes-related mechanism. Any interpretation of the signature as an Alzheimer’s-specific or diabetes-specific axis is not supported by these data.

Independent Single-nucleus Evaluation and Cross-cohort Meta-analysis

In the independent single-nucleus cohort GSE138852 (entorhinal cortex; 6 AD and 6 control donors, 13,214 nuclei), 20 gene–cell-type combinations passed the expression filter and were tested. No combination reached the multiplicity-adjusted threshold (minimum q = 0.580; Supplementary Fig. S1A, Supplementary Table S16). PDHA1 was testable in a single cell type (oligodendrocytes) and was not replicated, with an effect in the opposite direction to the discovery finding (log2FC = + 0.321, 95% CI − 0.868 to 1.511, p = 0.597, q = 0.948). Because neurons constitute only about 5% of the nuclei recovered in this dataset, PDHA1 was not testable in inhibitory neurons and the discovery finding was not directly interrogated; the cohort neither replicates nor refutes it, and its six donors per group and sparse neuronal representation provide limited power (minimum detectable effects in Supplementary Table S16).

Random-effects meta-analysis across independent cohorts, excluding the discovery cohort GSE33000 and its sister collection GSE44770, is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1B and Supplementary Table S17. The meta-analysis comprised four independent cohorts (GSE5281, GSE132903, GSE122063 and GSE48350) and was recomputed in full for this revision so that all four enter at donor level. PDHA1 was lower in AD (− 0.457, 95% CI − 0.712 to − 0.201, k = 4, I2 = 0%, 95% prediction interval − 0.802 to − 0.112, same direction in 4 of 4 cohorts, leave-one-cohort-out range − 0.511 to − 0.417) but did not survive multiplicity correction within the 16-gene prespecified family (q = 0.086). The next-ranked genes were ATP7B (− 0.325, q = 0.086), FDX1 (− 0.501, q = 0.110), DLD (− 0.781, q = 0.110) and PDHB (− 0.720, q = 0.110). No individual gene met the adjusted threshold. At the module level the post hoc lipoylation/PDH/TCA module did reach the adjusted threshold (− 0.245, 95% CI − 0.363 to − 0.127, k = 4, I2 = 0%, 95% prediction interval − 0.405 to − 0.086, same direction in 4 of 4 cohorts, leave-one-cohort-out range − 0.280 to − 0.226, q = 0.035; Supplementary Fig. S1C, Supplementary Table S18). Oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle were pooled in the same direction but did not reach it and were heterogeneous (− 0.581, q = 0.083, I2 = 75%; − 0.415, q = 0.085, I2 = 78%). Both copper modules pooled to essentially zero with no evidence of an effect in either direction (copper import and metal sensing − 0.022, 95% CI − 0.357 to 0.314, q = 0.994; copper homeostasis − 0.001, 95% CI − 0.238 to 0.237, q = 0.994). The contrast between the module and the gene results is the expected one: the module aggregates ten correlated transcripts and is estimated with much greater precision than any single gene, which is why it survives correction where its members do not.

The four independent cohorts entering the meta-analysis were GSE5281 (87 Alzheimer and 74 control arrays of laser-capture-microdissected pyramidal neurons), GSE132903 (97 Alzheimer and 98 control donors; 14 of the 16 prespecified genes were measurable on this platform), GSE122063 (12 Alzheimer and 11 non-demented donors, from a series of 31 donors that also includes 8 with vascular dementia; the counts of 56 and 44 given in the original submission were array counts) and GSE48350. GSE48350 contains up to four cortical regions from the same individual. Diagnosis was taken from the sample title and source name; the individual field of this series encodes donor race rather than diagnosis and was not used. Expression was standardised within region and averaged to a single observation per donor before testing, giving 28 Alzheimer and 56 control donors from 253 arrays; arrays were never treated as independent observations. GSE44770 was excluded a priori because it derives from the same collection as GSE33000, and the Huntington's disease arm was analysed only as a disease control because it also derives from the discovery collection.

The Cross-cohort Evidence for PDHA1 Specifically is therefore Weak

The gene shows a consistent direction of effect but does not meet the prespecified adjusted significance threshold in independent data.

DCI Score: Donor-level Performance

The DCI Score ranged from − 10.2 to + 10.4 across 167,290 nuclei. The cell-level density panel shown in the original submission is withdrawn, because the statistic it carried (p = 1.41 × 10−31) reflects the number of nuclei, not the number of donors, and is not interpreted.

At the Donor Level, No Cell Type Reached Significance

Inhibitory neurons and microglia showed the largest separation, both at p = 0.095 (Fig. 5A). Axis decomposition in inhibitory neurons showed that the only nominally significant component was the IGF/insulin axis (p = 0.041); the cuproptosis pro-death axis did not reach significance (p = 0.111), nor did the copper protection (p = 0.464) or mitochondrial regulator axes (p = 0.808; Fig. 5B). The cuproptosis component of the composite is thus not statistically supported at the donor level, and the composite as a whole is not significant in any cell type. The mean axis and composite differences across all eight annotated cell types are shown for completeness in Fig. 5D.

Donor-level ROC analysis in inhibitory neurons gave AUC = 0.722 for the composite, which was lower than the IGF/insulin axis alone (AUC = 0.769) and similar to the cuproptosis pro-death axis (AUC = 0.713), while the mitochondrial regulator axis was near chance (AUC = 0.537; Fig. 5C). These values come from 21 donors, so the corresponding intervals are wide: 0.503 to 0.941 for the composite, 0.566 to 0.972 for the IGF/insulin axis, 0.491 to 0.935 for the cuproptosis pro-death axis and 0.283 to 0.791 for the mitochondrial regulator axis (Supplementary Table S24). Every interval spans 0.5 except that of the IGF/insulin axis, which only just excludes it.

The composite therefore does not outperform its best single component even within the cohort in which it was constructed, and we no longer describe it as a validated classifier or propose it as a framework. A cross-platform transfer of the composite to GSE33000 was reported in the original submission. On re-examination the transferred composite could not be reconstructed from the axis definitions given in Methods, and when those definitions are applied to the public GSE33000 data the resulting score runs in the direction opposite to the one its construction implies, so that analysis and its figure panels are withdrawn in full. Module-level discrimination in GSE33000, which reproduces both from the archived outputs and from an independent recomputation, is reported instead with DeLong 95% confidence intervals in Supplementary Table S24.

Machine Learning: An Internally Circular Analysis

A random forest classifier separated DCI-High from DCI-Low inhibitory neurons with AUC = 0.992 and 95.2% accuracy (Supplementary Fig. S3A), 96.0% mean leave-one-sample-out accuracy across 20 donors (Supplementary Fig. S3B), and 93.2% mean cross-cell-type transfer accuracy (Supplementary Fig. S3C). These values are reported only to document the original computational result; the feature-level output was not retained as a complete auditable table and is not used to support a biological or predictive claim.

This Result is a Circularity Artefact and is not Biological Validation

The labels were defined by thresholding the DCI Score, and the classifier’s feature set included the 19 genes from which that score is computed; the model therefore reconstructs a deterministic function of its own inputs. The near-perfect AUC and the dominance of DCI genes among the important features are the expected consequences of this design, not evidence about disease biology. Feature selection was additionally performed once on the full dataset rather than within each cross-validation fold, which inflates the cross-validated estimates further. The high cross-cell-type transfer accuracy indicates that the composite is computable in any cell type, not that a shared vulnerability state exists. This analysis therefore provides no evidence that the DCI Score captures a generalisable cell state.

In silico Perturbation Ranking

Pre-ranked GSEA against 3,384 perturbation gene sets supported a mitochondrial basis for the 89-gene AD inhibitory neuron signature (WONG_MITOCHONDRIA_GENE_MODULE, NES = − 1.87, adjusted p = 1.18 × 10 − 7); as stated in Methods, that signature was derived at cell level and is descriptive. Among the 19 operational DCI genes, in silico perturbation ranked PPARGC1A first by reversal fraction (77.5%), followed by PDHA1 (75.3%) and AKT1 (68.5%). By cumulative reversal magnitude the three highest-ranked entries in the entire analysis were the housekeeping negative controls (Fig. 5E). No integrated causality score is reported; the composite score presented in the original submission is withdrawn together with its name.

The Negative Controls Invalidate a Causal Reading of this Ranking

The housekeeping genes ACTB, GAPDH, and UBC, which have no specific role in this biology, achieved reversal fractions of 64.0%, 71.9%, and 66.3% respectively and the three largest cumulative reversal magnitudes in the analysis (Fig. 5E). The metric is therefore substantially driven by expression level and network connectivity rather than by causal involvement. This analysis is retained as a descriptive prioritisation heuristic only. The term “Driver Causality Score” has been withdrawn from the manuscript, the figures and the supplementary material, and the quantity is now named the perturbational connectivity score.

Network Pharmacology and Computational Drug Repurposing

The triple intersection of copper (4,522), T2D (5,980), and cognitive impairment targets (567) defined 192 core targets (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Tables S1, S5). Six of the 16 cuproptosis-related genes were present among them (FDX1, LIPT1, DLAT, CDKN2A, ATP7A, ATP7B), an over-representation relative to chance (Fisher’s exact OR = 5.93, p = 0.002; Fig. 2E). Because the copper target list was itself an input to the intersection that defines the core set, this over-representation is expected in part by construction and is reported descriptively. In GSE161355, the cuproptosis pathway score correlated with insulin signalling (ρ = 0.680, p < 0.0001) and mitochondrial function (ρ = 0.649, p = 0.0001) and inversely with neuroinflammation (ρ = − 0.602, p = 0.0003; Fig. 2B); these are correlations among ssGSEA scores computed from partially overlapping gene sets in 33 samples.

Enrichment analysis of the 1,522 mapped genes against a 9,564-gene KEGG background identified 209 significant pathways, of which 37 met the prespecified DCI-related criterion (Methods, “Network pharmacology, enrichment, and molecular docking”; Supplementary Tables S7, S8). PI3K–Akt (180 genes), MAPK (155 genes), and AGE–RAGE signalling in diabetic complications (69 genes) were among the most significant (Fig. 2C). As set out in Methods, these broad pathways are reported descriptively and are not treated as independent mechanistic evidence. Thirty-two genes participated in ≥ 6 DCI-related pathways, and the 25 with the highest degree are shown (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S9).

Connectivity scoring ranked tetrathiomolybdate (0.319), trientine (0.208), and elesclomol (0.192) above the remaining ten candidates (Supplementary Fig. S3D), with mitochondria-targeted agents next (MitoQ 0.129; NAD⁺ precursors 0.069) and approved AD drugs lower (memantine 0.052; donepezil 0.012). Pre-ranked GSEA gave nominal support for elesclomol (NES = − 1.54, p = 0.018) and tetrathiomolybdate (NES = − 1.51, p = 0.024). Docking estimated affinities for elesclomol of − 6.3 kcal/mol (FDX1), − 7.2 kcal/mol (DLAT), and − 7.4 kcal/mol (CDKN2A), and − 5.8 kcal/mol for trientine against DLAT (Fig. 3C).

These analyses were seeded with copper-related target sets, so the recovery of copper modulators at the top of the ranking is expected by construction and is not independent evidence for a copper mechanism. Only 13 drugs were evaluated, the candidate list was assembled by hand, and no correction was applied across the ranking. Docking scores are geometric estimates and not evidence of target engagement (Methods, “Network pharmacology, enrichment, and molecular docking”). These results nominate compounds for experimental testing and nothing more.

Discussion

This study set out to test whether cuproptosis effector genes are dysregulated in a cell-type-specific manner in the human Alzheimer’s disease cortex, and whether such dysregulation could constitute a molecular bridge between type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease. The controls we applied constrain the answer substantially, and we set out below which components of the hypothesis they leave standing.

What the Data Support

Two findings survive the controls we applied, and one widely quoted observation does not. The observation that does not survive is PDHA1 itself. In donor-level pseudobulk analysis of 167,290 nuclei, PDHA1 in inhibitory neurons showed the largest nominal reduction among the 75 prespecified gene–cell-type tests, but the association did not survive multiple-testing correction (q = 0.829), and no formal evidence of inhibitory-neuron selectivity was obtained (inhibitory versus excitatory p = 0.666). First, a transcriptional module comprising lipoylation machinery and pyruvate dehydrogenase and TCA cycle components is consistently lower in Alzheimer’s disease cortex, and this reduction is not merely a consequence of neuronal loss: it persists after adjustment for estimated neuronal content, it is present in laser-captured pyramidal neurons where glia are absent from the input material, and it exceeds both the transcriptome-wide background and random gene sets matched for expression level and neuronal specificity. Second, the direction of effect for pyruvate dehydrogenase subunits is consistent across every cohort examined. Within that surviving programme the oxidative phosphorylation and TCA cycle components are the most robust, being reduced at donor level in every brain region of the laser-captured cohort, whereas the narrower lipoylation/PDH/TCA subdivision that carries the overlap with cuproptosis effectors is supported in the two cohorts in which donor-level analysis is possible but is resolved in only one of six laser-captured regions.

The biological plausibility of a pyruvate dehydrogenase deficit in cortical interneurons remains sound. Fast-spiking PVALB⁺ interneurons sustain high tonic firing rates and depend disproportionately on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (Kann 2016; Inan et al. 2016), and PDHA1 encodes the E1α subunit that gates pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle and is itself a principal point of regulatory control on the complex (Patel et al. 2014; Harris et al. 2002). A reduction in this program is a coherent correlate of the bioenergetic failure repeatedly described in the Alzheimer’s disease cortex (Qin et al. 2009; Sweeney and Song 2016). What our data add is that the reduction is detectable in neuronal material specifically and is not an artefact of cellular composition.

What the Data Do Not Support

Four candidate claims are not supported by these data.

The Copper and Cuproptosis-specific Component is not Supported

The copper import and metal-sensing module, which appeared elevated in Alzheimer’s disease in the original bulk analysis, falls to approximately zero once neuronal content is taken into account (0.314 → − 0.001, p = 0.981), and neither copper module exceeds the transcriptome-wide background or matched random gene sets. In laser-captured neurons the copper import module is null. The copper-transport arm of the proposed mechanism is therefore most parsimoniously explained by cellular composition rather than by a copper-handling defect. We note further that nothing in this study measures copper. Intracellular copper, copper-induced toxicity, lipoylated DLAT or DLST aggregation, Fe–S cluster protein loss, FDX1 dependency, and copper-induced cell death were all unmeasured. The transcriptional signature described here is enriched for cuproptosis effectors because those effectors are pyruvate dehydrogenase and lipoylation components (Tsvetkov et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2022); enrichment for the substrates of a process is not evidence that the process is occurring.

The Signature is not Specific to Alzheimer’s Disease

This is the most consequential of these findings. Reductions in the lipoylation/PDH/TCA module of comparable or greater magnitude occur in Huntington’s disease (− 0.751) and, at donor level, in vascular dementia (− 0.232), and the formal Alzheimer’s-versus-disease-control contrasts are not significant (q = 0.145 and 0.587). The copper import module is elevated in Huntington’s disease and in Alzheimer’s disease within the discovery cohort, and is not significantly elevated in either Alzheimer’s disease or vascular dementia at donor level in GSE122063. A signature shared across a polyglutamine disease, a vascular dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease is better interpreted as a common correlate of cortical neurodegeneration, plausibly reflecting the metabolic state of surviving or dying neurons, than as a mechanism specific to Alzheimer’s disease, and it cannot be specific to a diabetes-related pathway when it is equally present in conditions with no diabetes link.

The Independent Evidence for PDHA1 is Limited

In the independent single-nucleus cohort GSE138852, no gene–cell-type combination reached the adjusted threshold, and PDHA1 was testable only in oligodendrocytes, where it was not replicated. That cohort comprises six donors per group and recovers few neurons, so it did not interrogate inhibitory neurons at all; it neither replicates nor refutes the discovery finding and simply provides no support. In donor-level cross-cohort meta-analysis PDHA1 showed a consistent direction in all four cohorts with no detectable heterogeneity but did not survive correction within the prespecified 16-gene family (− 0.457, q = 0.086, k = 4, I2 = 0%), and no individual gene met the adjusted threshold. The post hoc lipoylation/PDH/TCA module, which aggregates ten correlated transcripts and is therefore estimated far more precisely than any one of them, does survive correction in the same analysis (− 0.245, q = 0.035). Within the discovery cohort itself, the two donor-level tests disagree: DESeq2 gives p = 0.013, which becomes q = 0.829 after correction across the 75 prespecified gene–cell-type tests, while a Wilcoxon test on per-donor mean expression gives p = 0.060. With 12 AD and 9 control donors, this is the resolution the data can offer.

The DCI Score and its Machine-learning Evaluation do not Support a Predictive Claim

The composite is not significant in any cell type at the donor level, its cuproptosis component is not significant, and it is outperformed by its own individual components in the discovery cohort, in which the composite reaches AUC = 0.722 against 0.769 for the IGF/insulin axis alone. A composite that does not outperform its parts has no demonstrated utility, and we no longer propose it as a framework. The cross-platform transfer analysis reported in the original submission has been withdrawn in full: on re-examination the composite could not be reconstructed from the axis definitions given in Methods, and when those definitions are applied to the public GSE33000 data the resulting score runs in the direction opposite to the one its construction implies. The random forest analysis is circular by construction: labels were defined by thresholding the score, and the feature set contained the genes from which the score is computed, so near-perfect discrimination was guaranteed before any biology was involved. Similarly, the in silico perturbation ranking is invalidated as a causal instrument by its own negative controls, since ACTB, GAPDH and UBC achieved the three largest cumulative reversal magnitudes in the analysis.

Population-level Evidence

The NHANES analysis is null in the full sample and does not reach significance in the diabetes subgroup after full adjustment, the diabetes × copper interaction is not significant, and the survey-weighted estimate is close to the null. The mediation analysis yields no significant Sobel test and mediates a total effect that is itself non-significant. These data carry little evidential weight; a subgroup pattern of this kind is what would be expected by chance across the strata examined.

Pharmacological Analyses

The connectivity-based ranking placed three copper modulators at the top. This is expected by construction rather than informative: the candidate list and target sets were seeded with copper-related genes, only thirteen compounds were evaluated, the list was assembled by hand, and no correction was applied across the ranking. Docking scores estimate geometric and energetic complementarity and are not evidence of physical binding or target engagement (Liu et al. 2020). These analyses nominate compounds for experimental testing and support no clinical or mechanistic inference (Pushpakom et al. 2019).

Relation to the Established Literature on Pyruvate Dehydrogenase in Alzheimer’s Disease

The observation that survives our controls is not new. Reduced activity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex in Alzheimer’s disease cortex was first reported four decades ago (Perry et al. 1980) and has been replicated at the level of enzyme activity and protein abundance (Sheu et al. 1985; Bubber et al. 2005), which is a stronger class of evidence than transcript abundance. It has also been known since the earliest of those studies that the deficit is not confined to Alzheimer’s disease: reduced pyruvate dehydrogenase complex activity was described in Huntington’s disease brain in the same report that described it in Alzheimer’s disease (Sorbi et al. 1983), and comparable reductions in the closely related α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex have been documented across several neurodegenerative conditions (Gibson et al. 2005). Our disease-specificity result therefore recovers, in transcriptomic data and in an independent cohort in which all three groups were assayed together, a non-specificity that the enzymological literature had already reported.

What the present analysis adds is not the observation but its boundary conditions. The earlier studies measured whole-tissue homogenates without adjustment for the loss of neurons that accompanies the disease, did not test the deficit competitively against the transcriptome-wide background or against gene sets matched for expression level and neuronal specificity, and did not formally contrast Alzheimer’s disease with a second dementia assayed alongside it. We show that the reduction persists in laser-captured neuronal material in which glial dilution cannot operate, that it exceeds both null distributions, and that it nonetheless fails a formal test of disease specificity. We also show that the copper-handling arm of the cuproptosis hypothesis, which is the genuinely novel element of the proposal, is not supported once cellular composition is taken into account.

Limitations

The lipoylation/PDH/TCA deficit was still present in laser-capture-microdissected pyramidal neurons, an input that contains no glia and in which a fall in neuronal proportion cannot operate. However, adding a neuronal-content score attenuated the effect substantially in that cohort, from −0.384 (p = 5.7 × 10–5) to −0.141 (p = 0.158) in the superseded pooled array-level model, and the donor-level within-region re-estimation shows the same attenuation in the one region where the module reaches significance (−0.895 to −0.600). The deficit therefore exceeded random gene sets matched for expression level and neuronal specificity, but we cannot fully separate a specific metabolic deficit from a general decline in neuronal transcriptional output.

In GSE48350 the Alzheimer arm is defined clinically rather than neuropathologically: Braak staging is recorded only for cases (61 of 80 arrays at stage IV or above, 16 at stages I to III) and is absent for all 173 control arrays. Donor age was available for every array in this cohort and was included as a covariate.

A comparable or larger decrease was observed in Huntington's disease (−0.751) and, at donor level, in vascular dementia (−0.232), and neither the Alzheimer-versus-Huntington contrast (q = 0.145) nor the Alzheimer-versus-vascular-dementia contrast (q = 0.587) reached the adjusted threshold. This signature therefore accompanies neurodegeneration generally and does not identify an Alzheimer-specific state.

FDX1 and the lipoylation machinery are required to execute cuproptosis, and in this cortex they are lower rather than higher (FDX1 −0.027, q = 0.011). Together with the loss of the copper-import signal after adjustment for neuronal content, these data do not indicate a cuproptosis-vulnerable state; the direction of cuproptosis susceptibility cannot be determined from transcript abundance alone. Intracellular copper, aggregation of lipoylated DLAT/DLST, loss of Fe-S cluster proteins and FDX1 dependency were not measured in this study.

Beyond the specific withdrawals above, the following limitations apply. All findings derive from computational analysis of public datasets; no experimental validation was performed, and no functional evidence of cuproptosis exists in this work. The discovery cohort comprises 21 donors, which limits power for cell-type-specific effects and produces wide confidence intervals for every AUC reported. Postmortem tissue introduces confounding from agonal state and perimortem events.

The unit of analysis differs between cohorts. All single-nucleus hypothesis tests use the donor as the replicate unit, and GSE33000 and the Huntington’s disease arm contribute one array per donor. In GSE5281 and GSE122063 a single donor contributes several arrays, up to six brain regions per donor in GSE5281 and two cortical regions with technical replicates in GSE122063, and both cohorts have been re-estimated at donor level for this revision. GSE122063 records donor identity in its subject-identifier field, so the module effects were re-derived with a mixed-effects model using the donor as a random intercept; brain region is a within-donor factor contributed in equal numbers by every donor in this series and is therefore orthogonal to the group contrast. GSE5281 does not record donor identity, and a candidate donor key built from the sample titles was inconsistent with the recorded donor age, so the effects were re-derived separately within each of the six brain regions, in which each donor contributes exactly one array. Both re-estimations are reported alongside the original array-level estimates in Supplementary Tables S20 and S22. The re-estimation leaves the disease-specificity conclusion unchanged and reduces the precision of the two cohorts, as expected; it also shows that in GSE5281 the lipoylation/PDH/TCA subdivision is resolved in only one of six regions, which is stated in the Results.

Between-cohort heterogeneity in the recomputed donor-level meta-analyses is zero for PDHA1 and for the lipoylation/PDH/TCA module but substantial for several other quantities, reaching I2 = 90% for individual genes and 75–78% for the oxidative phosphorylation and TCA cycle modules, so those pooled estimates should be read with that in mind. The module-level result rests on a post hoc subdivision and on four cohorts, which is the minimum at which a prediction interval carries much meaning. The neuronal-content adjustment relies on an estimated score rather than measured cell counts. Confidence intervals were not produced for the adjusted estimates by the original pipeline; the adjustment has been recomputed for this revision with intervals for every estimate, and the two implementations agree for five of the six modules. GSE5281 differs from the discovery cohort in platform, tissue handling, and era, so agreement between them is reassuring but not decisive. The disease-control comparisons use cohorts of unequal size and design, and the non-significant Alzheimer’s-versus-disease-control contrasts should be read as failure to demonstrate specificity rather than as proof of identity across diseases. The five module scores are post hoc subdivisions of the 16 prespecified cuproptosis genes and of two KEGG-curated metabolic gene sets; they were not prespecified, and the analysis plan filed with this submission records them as such (Additional file 2). Finally, the exploratory analyses reported in “Molecular context of PDHA1 in inhibitory neurons”, “Transcriptomic pathway scores associated with PDHA1”, “Network pharmacology and computational drug repurposing”, and “Population-level analysis” were not corrected for multiplicity and are not evidence.

The scale of the single-nucleus data should not be mistaken for statistical power. The discovery dataset contains 167,290 nuclei, but nuclei from the same person are not independent replicates; the effective sample size for every hypothesis test is 21 donors. With that number the study is exposed to type II error, the confidence intervals are wide, and individual estimates are unstable. The null results reported here should therefore be read as failure to detect an effect in a cohort powered only for the effect sizes given in the minimum detectable effect tables, not as evidence that no effect exists.

The independent single-nucleus cohort is small. GSE138852 contributes six Alzheimer and six control donors, and PDHA1 was testable in only one cell type. A cohort of this size can neither confirm nor refute a cell-type-specific effect of the magnitude observed in the discovery data, and we do not treat the absence of replication in it as disconfirmation.

Cell-type identities in the single-nucleus data were assigned from canonical marker expression and cluster-level module scores. We did not cross-check these assignments with reference-based mapping, automated annotation, or an independent deconvolution method, and misassignment of a minority of nuclei — particularly between inhibitory neuron subtypes and between oligodendrocyte lineage states — cannot be excluded. Endothelial cells and pericytes yielded too few nuclei per donor to be tested at all.

The neuronal-content covariate is a transcriptome-derived module score, not a deconvolution estimate and not a measured cell count. Module scores of this kind approximate composition and are themselves sensitive to the expression of their member genes, so adjusting for one can remove part of the biological signal as well as the compositional confound. The 63% attenuation observed in laser-captured neurons should be read with that ambiguity in mind; a dedicated deconvolution method, or matched cell counts, would be required to separate the two contributions properly.

Several thresholds in this work are conventional rather than derived: the expression filter applied before single-nucleus testing, the cut used to define DCI-High and DCI-Low nuclei, and the 0.05 threshold applied to Benjamini–Hochberg q values. We have not systematically varied them, and the analyses that depend most heavily on them are the exploratory ones.

For the random forest analysis, the class labels were derived by thresholding a score computed from genes that are themselves in the feature set, and feature selection was performed once on the complete dataset rather than inside each cross-validation fold. Both are forms of information leakage between the label, the features, and the evaluation, and the reported accuracy cannot be interpreted as generalisation performance.

Finally, this study applies a large number of computational frameworks — pathway scoring, competitive gene-set testing, ligand-receptor inference, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and random forest classification. Agreement in direction between several such tools raises the plausibility of a hypothesis but does not substitute for independent data or experimental measurement, because the tools share input data, gene-set definitions, and assumptions. We have therefore not treated convergence across methods as an additional line of evidence.

What would Test the Hypothesis

The analyses that would convert the surviving observation into a mechanistic claim are specific. Direct measurement of copper, lipoylated DLAT and DLST aggregation, Fe–S cluster protein integrity, and FDX1 dependency in cortical interneurons would establish whether cuproptosis occurs at all in this setting; without such measurements the term should not be applied. Pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and flux measurement, as distinct from transcript abundance, would establish whether the transcriptional reduction has a functional correlate. Cell-type-resolved data from additional cohorts, with inhibitory neurons adequately represented, would determine whether the PDHA1 signal is real, since no existing independent single-nucleus dataset interrogated it. Comparison against a broader panel of neurodegenerative and non-neurodegenerative disease controls would establish whether any component of the signature is disease-specific. Recovery of donor identity for GSE5281, which is not available in the public metadata, would allow that cohort to be analysed across regions rather than within them. For the DCI Score, retraining after removal of the defining genes and nested cross-validation with feature selection inside each fold would be required before any predictive claim could be entertained. We regard the first and third of these as prerequisites for any further claim about cuproptosis in Alzheimer’s disease.

Conclusions

Across single-nucleus, bulk transcriptomic and population-level layers, this study identifies a reduction in a mitochondrial and lipoylation-related transcriptional program, centred on pyruvate dehydrogenase subunits including PDHA1, in the Alzheimer’s disease cortex. This reduction is detectable in neuronal material and is not attributable solely to neuronal loss. It is, however, not specific to Alzheimer’s disease: comparable or larger reductions occur in Huntington’s disease and vascular dementia, and the formal contrasts between them are not significant. The copper-transport component of the originally proposed mechanism does not survive adjustment for neuronal composition, no measurement in this study addresses cuproptosis functionally, and the independent evidence for PDHA1 specifically does not survive multiplicity correction.

We therefore conclude that these data support a transcriptional association rather than a copper-mediated mechanism, and we present them as hypothesis-generating with that limitation stated plainly. The lipoylation and pyruvate dehydrogenase programme, and PDHA1 within it as a nominal observation only, remain reasonable targets for experimental interrogation in models of neurodegeneration, but the case for a diabetes-specific, copper-mediated, Alzheimer’s-specific mechanism is not made by this work.
