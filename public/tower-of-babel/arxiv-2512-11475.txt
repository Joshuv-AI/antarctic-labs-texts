Candidate set sampling: A note on theoretical guarantees
Shifeng Xiong

Abstract




Abstract
In this note we introduce a simple numerical sampling method, called candidate set sampling, which is based on an straightforward discretization to the density function. This method requires the knowledge of the density function (up to an unknown normalizing constant) only. Furthermore, candidate set sampling is non-iterative, dimension-free, and easy to implement, with fast convergence and low computational cost. We present its basic convergence properties in the note.

KEY WORDS:
Bayesian computation; discretization; Monte Carlo; quasi-Monte Carlo.

1
Introduction

We consider the issue of sampling from a multivariate density function, which plays an important role in Bayesian computation (Ghosh, Delampady, and Samanta 2006), as well as other problems in
computational statistics and data analysis, including high-dimensional integration and optimization problems (Fang and Wang 1993).
Many numerical methods and algorithms have been developed in the literature, most notably the
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. However, in real applications the MCMC methods may have practical difficulties for complex distributions, as well as other technical problems such as convergence (e.g., O’Hagan, Murphy, and Gormley 2012).

To overcome difficulties encountered in many MCMC algorithms, non-iterative sampling methods have been proposed in the literature. They use some simplified distribution to approximate the target distribution. Such simplification includes normal distribution via Laplace approximation (Bornkamp 2011) and variation inference (Blei, Kucukelbir, and McAuliffe 2017),
mixture of uniform distributions via discretization (Fu and Wang 2002; Wang and Lee 2014), and mixture of normal distributions via Gaussian process interpolation (Joseph 2012), among many others.

In this note we propose another sampling method, called candidate set sampling, which is based on an straightforward discretization to the density function. This method requires the knowledge of the density function (up to an unknown normalizing constant) only. Furthermore, candidate set sampling is non-iterative, dimension-free, and easy to implement, with fast convergence and low computational cost. We present its basic convergence properties in the note.

2
Sampling from a candidate set

We consider the problem of generating random variables from a probability density function
f(\mathbf{x})
, whose support is denoted by
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, up to an (unknown) constant. For unbounded
\mathcal{S}
, we can find a bounded set that contains the significant region of
f
. Without loss of generality, we set
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{d}
. Let
\mu_{f}
denote the probability measure on
[0,1]^{d}
corresponding to
f
.

For a large integer
M
, take a candidate set
\mathcal{A}_{M}=\{\mathbf{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, whose elements are uniformly scattered on
[0,1]^{d}
. Define a probability measure
\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
on
\mathcal{A}
as

P(\mathbf{X}_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}=\mathbf{a}_{i})=p_{i}=\frac{f(\mathbf{a}_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}f(\mathbf{a}_{j})},\ \ i=1,\ldots,M,

(1)

where the random vector
\mathbf{X}_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
follows from
\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
. We can prove that, if
\mathcal{A}_{M}
possesses some dense properties, then
\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
converges to
\mu_{f}
as
M\to\infty
. For a sample size
m
, let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{m}
be independently and identically distributed according to (
1
). The sample
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is the desired output of the proposed candidate set sampling approach.

The candidate set
\mathcal{A}_{M}
can be generated by randomly sampling from the uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{d}
, quasi-Monte Carlo sequence (Niederreiter 1992), support points (Mak and Joseph 2018), or other space-filling designs (Santner, Williams, and Notz 2018). Here we adopt quasi-Monte Carlo sequences since they yield better approximations to numerical integrals than the Monde carlo method and are easy to construct deterministically. Let
\mathcal{A}_{M}
be a quasi-Monte Carlo sequence satisfying the error bound of integration approximation,

\left|\frac{g(\mathbf{a}_{1})+\cdots+g(\mathbf{a}_{M})}{M}-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}g(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|\leqslant\delta_{M,d}V(g)

(2)

for any integrable
g
and sufficiently large
M
, where
\delta_{M,d}
is a positive number dependent only on
M
and
d
,
\delta_{M,d}\to 0
as
M\to\infty
for fixed
d
, and
V(g)
is the variation of
g
in the sense of
Hardy and Krause; see for instance Niederreiter (1992). In this paper we assume
V(f)<\infty
. The number
\delta_{M,d}
represents the convergence rate of a quasi-Monte Carlo sequence. For example, the Halton sequence
gives
\delta_{M,d}=C_{d}(\log M)^{d}/M
, where
C_{d}
depends only on
d
(Dick, Kuo, and Sloan 2013).

Let
d_{K}(\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})},\ \mu_{f})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}\left|\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}\left([\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)-\mu_{f}\left([\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)\right|
denote the Kolmogrov distance between
\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
and
\mu_{f}
, where
[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]=[0,x_{1}]\times\cdots\times[0,x_{d}]
for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\prime}
.

Theorem 1
.

For sufficiently large
M
,

d_{K}(\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})},\ \mu_{f})\leqslant C\delta_{M,d},

(3)

where
C
is a positive constant independent of
M
and
d
.

Proof.

Denote
J=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\in(0,\infty)
. Let
I
represent the indicator function. For any
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
, let
J_{\mathbf{x}}=\int_{[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]}f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
. We have

\displaystyle\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}\left([\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}I\left(\mathbf{a}_{i}\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}f(\mathbf{a}_{i})\right)^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}f(\mathbf{a}_{i})I\left(\mathbf{a}_{i}\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(J+\Delta_{1})^{-1}(J_{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta_{2}),

where

\Delta_{1}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}f(\mathbf{a}_{i})-J,\quad\Delta_{2}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}f(\mathbf{a}_{i})I\left(\mathbf{a}_{i}\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)-J_{\mathbf{x}}.

By (
2
),
|\Delta_{1}|,\ |\Delta_{2}|\leqslant\delta_{M,d}V(f)
. Therefore, for sufficiently large
M
,

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}\left([\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)-\mu_{f}\left([\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]\right)\right|=\left|(J+\Delta_{1})^{-1}(J_{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta_{2})-J^{-1}J_{\mathbf{x}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J|\Delta_{1}|+J_{\mathbf{x}}|\Delta_{2}|}{J|J+\Delta_{1}|}\leqslant\frac{J|\Delta_{1}|+J_{\mathbf{x}}|\Delta_{2}|}{J^{2}/2}\leqslant\frac{2V(f)}{J}\delta_{M,d},

which implies (
3
).∎

Let
\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
denote the empirical probability measure of the sample
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
from
\mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
in (
1
). The following theorem indicates that
\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
is asymptotically equivalent to the empirical measure of the desired sample from
\mu_{f}
.

Theorem 2
.

If
\delta_{M,d}=O(1/\sqrt{m})
, then
d_{K}(\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})},\ \mu_{f})=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{m})
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove
d_{K}(\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})},\ \mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})})=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{m})
. By DKW’s inequality, for any
\epsilon,z>0
and
m
, there exists a positive constant
C_{\epsilon,d}
, not depending on
M
, such that
d_{K}(\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})},\ \mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})})\leqslant C_{\epsilon,d}e^{-2(2-\epsilon)mz^{2}}
, which implies
E\left[\sqrt{m}d_{K}(\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})},\ \mu_{(f,\mathcal{A}_{M})})\right]^{2}=O(1)
(Shao 1999). This completes the proof. ∎

3
Sampling in a deterministic way

Usually deterministic approximation, instead of random sampling, via some design strategy can improve the representativeness of
\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
. Here we begin with the minimax distance design (Johnson, Moore, and Ylvisaker 1990) on
[0,1]
, i.e.,
u_{i}=(2i-1)/(2m),\ i=1,\ldots,m
, and generate
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
in the following deterministic manner. Let
q_{0}=0
and
q_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}
for
i=1,\ldots,M
. Clearly,
u_{1}<\cdots<u_{m}
and
q_{0}\leqslant q_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant q_{M}
.

For
j=1
, search from
\{1,\ldots,M\}
in the increasing order, and find the minimal number
i_{1}
such that
u_{1}\in[q_{i_{1}-1},q_{i_{1}})
, let
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}
;

for
j=2
, search from
\{i_{1}+1,\ldots,M\}
in the increasing order, and find the minimal number
i_{2}
such that
u_{2}\in[q_{i_{2}-1},q_{i_{2}})
, let
\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}
;

\vdots

for
j=m
, search from
\{i_{m-1}+1,\ldots,M\}
in the increasing order, and find the minimal number
i_{m}
such that
u_{m}\in[q_{i_{m}-1},q_{i_{m}})
, let
\mathbf{X}_{m}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{m}}
.

If
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is generated from this deterministic process, then the empirical measure
\mu_{(m,\mathcal{A}_{M})}
is non-stochastic. We can prove its deterministic convergence properties in the follow-up work.
