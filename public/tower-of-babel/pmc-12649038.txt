The impact of the February 6, 2023, earthquake in Türkiye on the nursing workforce: brain drain and strategic solutions for sustainability in the healthcare sector
Authors: Bircan Kara, Senem Yüksel, Alev Semah Renkli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the globalizing world, significant advances have been made in health services with the development of technology. These advances have led to an increase in the demand for qualified manpower, such as nurses, to ensure the sustainability of health systems. Disasters, especially disasters such as earthquakes, can cause the migration of qualified manpower out of the city. After earthquakes, the destruction of city infrastructure and the emergence of housing needs increase migration rates. This study aims to analyze the migration of nursing manpower after the February 6, 2023, earthquake and to present solution suggestions for institutions.MethodThis study was planned with a retrospective method. After the February 6, 2023, earthquake until August 2024, the loss of manpower in nursing services in a university hospital was retrospectively examined through personnel records. A total of 68 nurses were included in the study. Data such as age, gender, marital status, type of departure, post-earthquake damage status of their homes (housing problem), and place of migration of the nurses who left the institution were statistically compared.ResultsThe mean age of the nurses included in the study was 34.7 years, 70.6% were female, and 86.8% resigned after the earthquake. After the earthquake, 69.1% migrated to Turkey and 30.9% migrated abroad. It was found that the house where 44.1% of the nurses lived was damaged in the earthquake. There was a statistically significant difference between the place of migration and the way of leaving (p = 0.02). Still, there was no significant difference between gender, marital status, and the method of travel (p > 0.05).DiscussionDisasters often lead to large human migrations. Deniz et al. (2017) reported that 250,000 people migrated after the Van earthquake. Katayanagi et al. (2020) reported that 19.1% of the working population became unemployed and migrated after the earthquake in Japan. In this study, the domestic migration rate was found to be 69.1%, which is consistent with the literature. However, it was a striking finding that the majority of nurses who migrated after the earthquake were women and had children.ConclusionAccording to the results of the study, more than 80% of the nurses resigned and migrated to Türkiye; the majority of these nurses were women, and the rate of those who migrated abroad was 30.9%. Introduction

In the globalizing world, with the rapid development of technology, health services have also experienced rapid transformation. This development has increased the need for qualified manpower for the sustainability of health systems [1]. One of the most important factors in the provision of health services is the presence of qualified and educated health workers. In this context, the increasing demand for qualified nurses to work in the health sector has become a factor that directly affects the performance of health systems [2].

The demand for qualified nurses has steadily increased in recent years due to population growth, aging demographics, and the rising burden of chronic diseases [3]. Simultaneously, the intensification of global crises such as pandemics, Disasters and conflicts has further strained healthcare systems, exposing their vulnerabilities. The COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, not only amplified the global need for nurses but also tested the resilience and adaptive capacities of national healthcare systems [4]. These overlapping pressures highlight the critical role of nurses in maintaining continuity of care and ensuring community health during crises.

The return of nurses after disasters is of great importance not only for the health sector but also for the protection of the general health level of society [5]. However, nurse attrition and migration have become growing concerns, especially in regions vulnerable to disasters. Earthquakes, floods, and armed conflicts can cause major disruptions in urban infrastructure, leading to the displacement of large populations and the exodus of qualified healthcare professionals [6]. These challenges are often compounded by the lack of institutional preparedness, inadequate working conditions, and the absence of support mechanisms for healthcare workers [7].

The migration of health workers after natural disasters is not a new phenomenon, and various studies have attempted to address it. Yet, comprehensive research focusing specifically on nurse migration after large-scale disasters in Türkiye remains limited. In this regard, the February 6, 2023, earthquake, one of the deadliest and most destructive in Türkiye’s modern history, provides a critical case for examining the dynamics of post-disaster health workforce displacement. Over 50,000 people lost their lives, and hundreds of thousands were displaced, placing immense pressure on hospitals and health workers in the affected regions [8]. Initial reports suggest that over 30% of nurses in some institutions relocated domestically or abroad [9, 10].

The departure of nurses from healthcare institutions can severely impair service delivery, especially in critical units such as operating theaters, emergency care, and maternal health [11]. Moreover, workforce shortages negatively impact patient safety and satisfaction, leading to prolonged treatment durations and increased healthcare costs [12].

From a theoretical standpoint, this study adopts two key frameworks to understand the underlying motivations behind post-disaster nurse migration: Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs and the Risk Perception Paradox. According to Maslow (1943), individuals prioritize basic physiological and safety needs during periods of crisis. If these needs—such as shelter, security, and family protection—are not met, individuals are less likely to focus on higher-order needs such as career fulfillment or professional loyalty. In the context of post-disaster nursing, unmet basic needs often override institutional loyalty [13, 14].

Complementing this is the Risk Perception Paradox, which suggests that people do not always act in proportion to objective risk assessments but are often guided by subjective perceptions shaped by cognitive biases, emotions, and social influences [13, 14]. Thus, even minimal home damage may trigger migration if perceived threats to children’s safety or trust in public systems remain unresolved. These frameworks enable a more nuanced interpretation of migration behavior beyond surface-level triggers [15].

Globally, brain drain in the healthcare sector—particularly in low- and middle-income countries—has emerged as a major policy challenge. The World Health Organization [16] has highlighted the need for strategic investments in workforce retention and resilience-building, particularly in disaster-prone regions. Countries like Haiti (post-2010) and Japan (post-2011) have faced similar challenges in retaining health professionals, with significant long-term implications for public health infrastructure [16, 17].

In the context of Türkiye, the migration of nurses following the February 2023 earthquake can be seen as a microcosm of broader systemic vulnerabilities. The fact that many nurses migrated despite having only slightly damaged homes suggests that infrastructural conditions alone do not determine retention. Psychological trauma, childcare disruptions, and institutional inflexibility were also key drivers of nurse migration.

To prevent future losses in nursing human capital, institutions must adopt a multi-level approach, involving collaboration between local governments, national policymakers, and health sector administrators [18]. Such approaches may include emergency housing programs, educational support for children, mental health services for staff, and region-specific salary scales [19]. Furthermore, the prestige and working conditions of the nursing profession must be strengthened to attract and retain talent, especially in high-risk areas.

Despite the relevance and urgency of this issue, there is a noticeable gap in the academic literature that integrates both quantitative and qualitative data to assess post-disaster nurse migration and its institutional implications in Türkiye. This study aims to fill that gap by retrospectively examining the case of a university hospital affected by the 2023 earthquake. It seeks to identify the underlying causes of nurse migration, analyze demographic and institutional patterns, and offer practical recommendations to improve retention in future crises.

The findings are expected to contribute to both scholarly understanding and policy development, serving as a foundation for disaster preparedness planning and long-term workforce strategies. Ultimately, resilient healthcare systems require not only physical infrastructure but also well-supported and secure personnel.

Türkiye is currently in a post-impact phase, aiming to rebuild infrastructure and revise its national disaster preparedness plans. Given the lessons learned from the February 6, 2023, earthquake, this study provides valuable insight into how institutional and social factors influence nurse migration during crises. By identifying these dynamics, the study aims to contribute to the formulation of evidence-based policies and workforce strategies for future disaster scenarios.

This study aims to investigate the patterns and causes of nurse migration following the February 6, 2023, earthquake in Türkiye. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following research question: “What demographic and institutional factors influenced the migration decisions of nurses after the earthquake?”

Metod

Research design

This study was designed as a descriptive and retrospective study. The nurses included in this study left the institution between February 6, 2023, and August 31, 2023. The reasons why nurses left the hospital were retrospectively scanned and analyzed through the records kept by the personnel unit.

Study group

Before the earthquake, 395 nurses were employed at the hospital. During the disaster, 24 nurses unfortunately lost their lives. An evaluation was conducted on the remaining 371 nurses. Of these, 72 nurses officially left the hospital between February 2023 and August 2024. Among them, 68 nurses with complete and accessible personnel data were included in the final sample. Nurses with missing post-earthquake records or unclear departure status were excluded. A formal sample size calculation was not conducted due to the retrospective nature of the study and the total population being predefined. The study included all accessible nurses (n = 68) who left the institution after the earthquake and met the inclusion criteria. As such, the analysis represents a total population sample rather than a selected subset, which reduces sampling bias but limits generalizability.

Data collection tool

Data were retrospectively extracted using a structured data collection form developed specifically for this study. The form was used to collect information from institutional personnel records and included the following variables: age, gender, marital status, number of children, type of departure, post-earthquake housing damage status, migration destination, and date of departure. A sample of the form is provided in Appendix A.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 21 software was used for statistical analysis of the data. The data were summarized with basic statistics such as number, percentage, mean, and frequency. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine whether the data were normally distributed. As a result of the test, it was determined that the data were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Since the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used. The Pearson Chi-Square test was used to examine the relationships between categorical variables. When expected cell counts were below 5, Fisher’s Exact Test was applied automatically by SPSS to ensure accuracy. This test was used to determine whether the factors affecting nurses’ decisions to leave the hospital showed significant differences according to demographic groups. The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05 in all tests. This means that the findings of the study are not random and are statistically significant.

In addition to significance testing, effect sizes were calculated for key comparisons. For non-parametric tests, the effect size r was computed using the formula r = Z /√N. For chi-square tests, Cramér’s V was calculated to assess the strength of association.

Data accessibility limitation

Of the 72 nurses who left the institution after the earthquake, complete data were available for 68. The remaining four cases were excluded due to administrative limitations (e.g., missing personnel records or incomplete exit documentation). These exclusions were unrelated to demographic or institutional factors, suggesting random omissions. Ethical considerations

Ethics committee approval

This study was approved by the Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee. The ethics committee decision date and number are as follows: (30.10.2024/26). Ethics committee approval was obtained to ensure that the rights of the participants were protected throughout the study and that the research procedures complied with ethical and legal requirements. However, since this study was conducted with data obtained from the hospital, it does not require the consent of the individuals to participate. Considering the nature of the study, the ethics committee approved that it was not necessary to obtain consent from the individuals.

Institutional permission

Before the start of the study, the necessary permissions were obtained by the Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Hospital administration. The necessary written approval and permission for the study were obtained from the relevant institutions, and in this process, the institutions were committed to the safety, privacy, and confidentiality of the data of the participants. The date and number of the permission were recorded as: (411492/ 21.08.2024).

Anonymity and confidentiality

The anonymity and confidentiality of participants were strictly maintained throughout the study. Personal data belonging to the nurses were not accessed, and all records were fully anonymized before analysis. No names, identification numbers, or other personally identifiable information were included in the dataset. A formal commitment was made to the hospital administration, ensuring that the data obtained from the personnel unit would be used exclusively for scientific purposes and handled in strict compliance with confidentiality protocols.

Confidentiality and privacy

All data were collected, recorded, and analyzed in a fully anonymized format, strictly aligned with the research objectives. At no stage of the study were participants’ identities revealed or accessible. Access to the data was limited to the principal investigator and an authorized analyst, both of whom were affiliated with the institution. The data were stored on a secure, password-protected server. In compliance with institutional policy, the data will be retained for five years and will not be shared with any third parties. At the end of the retention period, all data will be permanently deleted.

Results

Of the nurses who left the hospital after the earthquake, 86.8% resigned. The rate of nurses who migrated domestically was 69.1%, and the rate of those who migrated abroad was 30.9%. Discussion

Natural disasters impose significant negative consequences on a global scale, exacerbating socio-economic vulnerabilities and inducing displacement crises [20]. Post-disaster scenarios, particularly earthquakes, underscore the heightened need for a skilled healthcare workforce. Nurses are critical to maintaining the continuity of healthcare delivery in affected regions and establishing a disaster-resilient health infrastructure [21]. Post-disaster, healthcare workers—particularly nurses—play a critical role in sustaining patient care, ensuring service continuity, and facilitating emergency response. However, the global migration of nurses, especially to developed countries, may have long-term adverse effects on the health systems of developing nations. This trend poses a significant threat, potentially pushing some countries’ healthcare systems to the brink of collapse [22]. Another risk jeopardizing the sustainability of healthcare services due to nurse migration is the difficulty in securing the return flows of healthcare workers, which impedes the rapid recovery of local health systems following disasters.

Current scholarship underscores the imperative for governmental strategic planning to mitigate the emigration of domestic nursing personnel [23]. Government interventions are crucial to limit international nurse migration and prevent permanent workforce depletion in local health systems. These strategies not only curb migration but can also enhance healthcare workers’ motivation, thereby strengthening the long-term sustainability of health systems.

This study examines the causes of nurse migration and the profiles of migrating nurses, with findings aligning with prevailing trends in the literature. Post-disaster attrition appears closely linked to factors such as housing insecurity and familial obligations. Although psychological distress is not directly assessed, previous studies suggest it may also play a role in nurses’ decisions [24, 25]. This analysis situates these determinants within established theoretical frameworks and highlights the critical importance of nurse retention strategies for effective crisis management in healthcare systems.

Some studies have also explored nurse migration in disaster or crisis settings. While Xu et al. (2005) focused on general migration trends, their demographic findings may be reflective of patterns seen post-disaster [26]. Galbany et al. (2024) also mention structural and environmental pressures that, although not disaster-specific, are often intensified in post-crisis settings. Therefore, these works, while not directly focused on disaster-induced migration, help contextualize the profile of nurses likely to migrate under strained circumstances [27].

The findings of this study show parallels with the literature. The average age of nurses who migrated after the earthquake was determined to be 34.75, indicating that younger nurses tended to migrate. Additionally, 70% of migrating nurses were female, and 84% had children. While the gender distribution may in part reflect the broader demographic composition of nurses in Türkiye—where the majority are female—the overall profile of migrating nurses aligns with existing literature that characterizes nurse migrants as predominantly young and female.

However, the high number of married nurses with children who migrated after the earthquake is a particularly noteworthy and counterintuitive finding. Traditionally, in Turkish culture, factors such as age, marital status, and parenthood—especially for women—are seen as barriers to migration due to responsibilities tied to family stability and caregiving. Contrary to this norm, our study found a significant number of such individuals among the migrant nurse population.

This phenomenon may be better understood through a cause-and-effect lens by considering the combined pressures of post-disaster family vulnerability and institutional inadequacy. Studies show that disruptions in housing and education, and fear for children’s safety, may outweigh traditional cultural constraints in post-crisis decisions [28, 29].

Moreover, based on Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs, unmet safety and physiological needs may have overridden professional obligations or cultural reluctance. The decision to migrate, especially among women with children, appears to be driven less by dissatisfaction with the profession and more by the desire to secure a predictable and safer environment for their families. In this sense, the migration of married female nurses with children reflects a protective, family-centered adaptation strategy rather than a simple career-based emigration choice.

This interpretation is consistent with existing literature, yet our findings show a comparatively higher migration rate among married female nurses with children.

Unlike studies that suggest cultural norms act as barriers to migration (e.g., Thomas et al., 2006), our data demonstrate that extreme post-disaster conditions may override these cultural constraints.

This divergence emphasizes the need to view migration decisions not merely through cultural expectations but through the lens of survival priorities and institutional failures.

In conclusion, strategic measures need to be taken in the healthcare sector to prevent nurse migration. Establishing collaboration between the education sector and local governments, and developing projects and programs to improve post-earthquake education and social services are of great importance.

A review of research on nurse migration reveals that Thomas et al. (2006) examined Indian nurses aspiring to migrate to the United States, investigating migration intentions among both public and private sector nurses. According to their findings, public sector nurses demonstrated lower migration intention scores than their private-sector counterparts, primarily due to concerns about adapting to conditions abroad. However, no statistically significant difference was found [30].

Similarly, Jose et al. (2008) reported that over 50% of Indian nurses maintained their migration applications for over three years, despite facing communication barriers and language difficulties. About the present study, while the sample does not include sectoral differentiation, the persistence of migration intentions, even under difficult personal or institutional conditions-mirrors the resilience seen in Jose et al.‘s findings [31]. Additionally, the motivations found in our study (e.g., housing insecurity, family concerns) can be viewed as functionally parallel to adaptation anxieties described by Thomas et al. (2006), particularly about perceived stability and external constraints [30].

Rolle Sands et al. (2020) examined nurses in the Caribbean region and determined that 80% of Caribbean nurses migrated to countries such as the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom. Their study emphasized the need to address unfavorable working conditions in source countries experiencing significant nurse migration [32]. Research indicates that natural disasters are among the contributing factors to skilled healthcare worker migration. The impact of disasters on healthcare services may result in increased demand for the health workforce due to rising health threats in affected regions. Consequently, studies emphasize the necessity to enhance both the quantity and quality of healthcare personnel [33]. In our study, despite the increased need for health services after the February 6 earthquake, which was described as the disaster of the century, there was a tendency to migrate to a region not affected by the earthquake within the country and to migrate completely out of the country. The fact that the migration rate in the country is close to 70% of the total migration can be explained by the fact that even if the conditions were similar, going to a new institution could only be explained by the collapsed infrastructure problems of the city, the housing problems experienced by the nurses with their families and the inadequacy of the temporary shelters created. In addition, it is thought that the internal migration may be due to the efforts of the nurses to protect the unity of the family. Family unity is very important in Turkish culture, and this value is even higher, especially in families with children. In this cultural context, it is possible to explain the fact that nurses only migrate internally and change places in institutions with similar working conditions in the same country. Nevertheless, the fact that 30.9% of nurses migrated abroad despite these cultural values signals the limits of cultural barriers in crisis contexts. This suggests that post-disaster migration is not purely a sociological outcome but is structurally driven by systemic inadequacies, such as prolonged shelter insecurity and institutional silence.

Based on these findings, it is important to make strategic recommendations for health institutions and local governments to prevent nurse migration. Steps should be taken to prevent internal migration of nurses by determining targets such as increasing housing opportunities and providing social and educational opportunities. Projects and programs should be developed in cooperation with local governments and government officials to achieve these targets. These steps can increase the contribution of nurses to post-disaster health services and ensure the sustainability of services.

Burnett et al. (2024) stated in their study that 24% of nurses’ reasons for migration were poor working conditions [34]. Valizadeh et al. (2016) listed the reasons for nurses’ migration as working conditions, facilities of the institution, wages paid, and institution policies, respectively [15]. Another study was conducted by Hirohara et al. (2019), which examined nurses who left their city after an earthquake in Japan. It was found that the primary reasons for migration were concerns over their children’s education and health. However, once housing issues were resolved and better opportunities became available, many nurses returned and resumed work at their institutions [35]. These findings offer valuable comparative insight for the present study, which also identified housing insecurity and family-related concerns—particularly related to children’s well-being—as major migration drivers. While our study focused on outward migration, the observations by Hirohara et al. emphasize how institutional responses and infrastructural recovery can influence nurses’ willingness to return, pointing to the dynamic and reversible nature of migration decisions in post-disaster contexts.

This comparative case highlights an important point: the reversibility of migration decisions is highly contingent upon the speed and adequacy of post-disaster interventions. In the Turkish context, the absence of comprehensive post-disaster planning may have contributed to the permanence of migration, particularly among nurses with families. This underscores the critical role of rapid recovery planning in health workforce retention.

The findings of this study are consistent with similar trends reported in the literature. In particular, it was observed that the migration rate among nurses with children is relatively high. However, a notably large proportion of nurses chose to leave the city despite their homes being only slightly damaged and potentially made safe with minimal repairs. This finding may be explained by the prioritization of children’s safety, education, and healthcare needs. Familial factors such as the provision of secure housing conditions and improved educational opportunities for children appear to be key determinants influencing nurses’ decisions to migrate.

An important consideration in the context of disaster response is that nurses residing in affected regions are themselves disaster victims. As such, expecting them to continue providing care without first ensuring their own and their families’ safety, housing, and psychosocial needs may compromise both staff well-being and the healthcare system’s resilience. Our findings support the view that institutions and policymakers must prioritize the dual identity of these professionals—as both care providers and survivors—when designing post-disaster healthcare strategies. Collaborating with local governments to improve access to safe housing, educational facilities, and social amenities may serve as a strong incentive for nurses to remain in their positions. Such strategies would help create a safer and more supportive post-disaster working environment for nurses, thereby contributing to the sustainability of healthcare services.

Conclusion

This study examined the dynamics of nurse migration following the February 6, 2023, earthquake in Türkiye, with a specific focus on identifying the factors influencing nurses’ decisions to migrate. The findings reveal that over 30% of the nurses migrated abroad, and a substantial proportion relocated domestically. Most of the migrating nurses were married women with children, and their decisions were primarily realized through resignation rather than temporary leave, due in part to structural constraints that limited return opportunities. Interestingly, although many reported minimal physical damage to their homes, the lack of a perceived safe environment-even one that could be restored through minor repairs, played a critical role in prompting migration.

These results demonstrate that nurse migration in the aftermath of a disaster is shaped not only by physical loss but also by perceived family insecurity, caregiving responsibilities, and institutional inadequacy. While this study is limited to a single hospital, it offers critical insights into how disaster-related displacement and weak systemic support mechanisms contribute to long-term workforce attrition. Limitations

This study investigates the loss of the nursing workforce following the major earthquake that occurred in Türkiye on February 6, 2023; however, it has certain limitations. Since the research was conducted with nurses from a single university hospital, the findings are limited to this specific institution and region. Moreover, the study covers only the period up to August 2024, and the lack of long-term follow-up prevents the observation of changes over time.

The data collection process was limited to personnel records from the human resources department, which may include potential inaccuracies or omissions due to personal reporting errors. The study focused primarily on housing-related issues and individual factors, without taking into account other influential elements such as economic or familial conditions.

Although demographic variables such as gender and marital status were examined, broader variables like socioeconomic status were not addressed. Furthermore, while the statistical analyses revealed significant differences regarding types of migration, these differences were not supported by in-depth social or psychological analyses. Consequently, these limitations restrict the scope and generalizability of the study’s findings, highlighting the need for more comprehensive and longitudinal research in the future.
