Effects of centralized multidisciplinary surgical endometriosis care: a retrospective 3-year follow-up cohort study
Authors: Sari Koivurova, Johanna Makelä-Kaikkonen, Johanna Laru, Pasi Ohtonen, Outi Uimari, Maarit Niinimäki, Tero Rautio, Anna Terho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to study the impact of centralizing surgical endometriosis care, including possible improvements in patient outcome.MethodsThis study is a single tertiary centre retrospective cohort study with a 3-year postoperative follow-up conducted at the University hospital district of Oulu, Finland. The cohorts consisted of 215 women operated before centralization (January 2013 – June 2016) and 246 women operated after centralization (July 2016 – December 2019). Background, intraoperative and postoperative 3-year data were collected from hospital records. NRS and EHP-30 questionnaires were sent to patients to study long-term patient outcome. Comparisons were made between the two time periods. Main outcome measures were rates of referrals from secondary to tertiary level hospital, changes in diagnostics, implementation of multidisciplinary approach and patient access to specialized endometriosis care. Data were analysed via SPSS version 28.0 using independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test and Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable adjusted models were used to control for possible confounding factors when comparing the two time periods.ResultsAfter centralization, referrals from secondary level hospitals tripled (4.6% vs. 12.6%, p = 0.003). Preoperative endometriosis diagnosis using MRI increased (24.2% vs. 48.8%, p < 0.001) and complex surgery was more often planned and performed by a multidisciplinary team (3.3% vs. 18.3%, p < 0.001; 8.8% vs. 18.4%, p = 0.008). A slight non-significant reduction in complication risk was detected after centralization (aOR 0.95, 95% CI 0.50–1.78). Hormonal treatment was more common pre- and postoperatively (35.8% vs. 52.7%, p = 0.002; 47.9% vs. 69.9%, p < 0.001). Postoperative follow-up at tertiary level hospital was longer (8.2 mo vs. 12.2 mo, p = 0.002).ConclusionsCentralization of endometriosis surgery was achieved in terms of increased number of referrals. Multidisciplinary approach was implemented more often in diagnostics, preoperative counselling and surgery. Preoperative and postoperative hormonal treatments were conducted more thoroughly. Postoperative specialized care surveillance was increased after centralization allowing chronically symptomatic patients a better access to an individualized treatment plan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04558-0.

Background

Endometriosis affects around 10% of fertile-aged females [1]. The symptoms are cyclic or non-cyclic pelvic pain: dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria and infertility [1, 2]. Despite the defined pelvic symptomology, there is still significant diagnostic delay due to lack of knowledge and overlooking of the symptoms among health care professionals. Previous data shows that endometriosis causes economic burden to societies e.g., increased sick leaves [3, 4]. To date, endometriosis should be considered a chronic disease with severe co-morbidity [5], warranting just as meticulous care as any chronic illness [3, 6].

Medical treatment of endometriosis is often complex, requiring tailored plans including hormonal and analgesic drugs. About 30% of affected women experience infertility that must be taken into consideration along endometriosis treatment [1]. Pain and infertility healthcare specialists are required for holistic endometriosis care. Deep endometriosis (DE) commonly affects extragenital pelvic organs, therefore surgical treatment requires skillful pelvic floor surgery. ESHRE Guideline 2022 states that deep endometriosis should be treated in a centre of expertise [7], and additional recommendations on multidisciplinary management of endometriosis exist [1, 2, 8], aiming to facilitate diagnostics, reduce morbidity and improve quality of life [9].

Endometriosis centres have been established around Europe. However, research showing the efficacy of multidisciplinary care is scarce, though some studies have indicated medical improvement and reduction in the use of health care services with multidisciplinary models [2, 10, 11]. A French study showed a decrease in postoperative complication rate after implementing a multidisciplinary approach [12]. In addition, quality-of-life improvements have been shown after surgery at an interdisciplinary centre of expertise [13]. According to French professionals, objectives of an Expert Centre include training other health professionals, developing basic and clinical research, multidisciplinary team meetings, informing the public, facilitating patient access and determining a tailored long-term treatment strategy [14].

In Finland, demanding endometriosis care has been referred to five university hospitals and to a few secondary level hospitals, without any nationally established criteria. Treatment of severe endometriosis in our district has been centralized to Oulu University Hospital (OUH) since spring 2016. To evaluate the quality of diagnostics and treatment in OUH Endometriosis Centre, we conducted a retrospective cohort study comparing two time-periods before (January 2013 – June 2016) and after (July 2016 – December 2019) centralization. Our primary aims were to compare the utilization rate of centralized care, development of diagnostics, achievement of multidisciplinary approach and holistic care between the time periods. Secondly, we aimed to compare the long-term surgical outcomes between the groups to study whether centralization results in measurable benefits for the patients.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective hospital register-based cohort study was conducted in OUH which is a public tertiary hospital in Northern Finland. OUH district includes four secondary level hospitals. Treatment of severe endometriosis was centralized to OUH Endometriosis Centre during spring 2016 and within our unit the endometriosis care was appointed to a designated team. Our multidisciplinary team (MDT) consists of a surgical team with two gynaecological endometriosis surgeons who also bear the total responsibility of the patient care, and two colorectal surgeons of which one is also a gynaecologist. In addition, a gynaecologic endocrinologist, radiologist, anesthesiologist, urologist and thorax surgeon are included in the team when needed. Outpatient clinic patient flow is organized by two dedicated endometriosis nurses. Additionally, our team includes a pelvic floor physiotherapist with a subspecialty in sexual therapy and psycho-physical physiotherapy. MDT meetings (gynaecology, gyneacological endocrinologist, colorectal surgeon and radiologist) with patient- specific treatment planning are conducted monthly. MDT outpatient clinics with colorectal surgeon occur twice per month. After the initial gynecological evaluation the most demanding DE cases are referred to a MDT meeting, where surgery plan is made taking into account the patient’s symptoms as well as the clinical, ultrasound and the MRI findings. A multidisciplinary pre-surgery visit is organized if needed, and surgery is then performed with colorectal contribution when needed. Postoperative follow-up according to patients needs is performed by dedicated endometriosis specialists and nurses. Endometriosis patients are linked to a digital endometriosis site, where they can self-initiatively acquire information on the disease and treatment options. Description of OUH Endometriosis Centre is presented in Table S3.

Patient population

All women having undergone surgery for endometriosis in OUH between 2013 and 2019 were included in the study. Endometriosis related ICD-10 (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision) N80 codes linked to surgical procedures were used to find the patients from OUH records. Patients were divided into two groups according to the timing of the surgical treatment: January 2013 – June 2016 (Period I) and July 2016 – December 2019 (Period II). Women with visible endometriosis at the operation were included in the analyses even though the histological diagnosis was unspecified or absent. Women with repeated surgery after index surgery were also included.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were achievement of centralization (measured by the number of referrals from secondary level hospitals), development of diagnostics and increase in the multidisciplinary teamwork as well as the achievement of a comprehensive endometriosis care in a three-year follow-up. Secondary outcome was the long-term patient reported outcome measured by NRS (Numeric Rating Scale) and EHP-30 (Endometriosis Health Profile) questionnaires.

Data including the background characteristics (Table 1), diagnoses and surgical procedures (Table 2), intraoperative (Table 3) and postoperative aspects (Table 4a, 4b) were manually collected from hospital records by one researcher (SK) during September 2022 to February 2025. Women’s overall health was categorized according to ASA (The American Society of Anesthesiologists) classification: 1 (normal healthy patient), 2 (patient with mild systemic disease), 3 (patient with severe systemic disease), 4 (patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life) and 5 (moribund patient not expected to survive without the operation). Comorbidity included all chronic somatic and psychiatric illnesses. Infertility was defined as having an infertility-related diagnosis. Difficulty of the surgery was categorized into three complication risk levels based on the perioperative ICD-10 diagnoses: (1) low risk (R10.3, pain localized to lower abdomen; N80.3, endometriosis of the pelvic peritoneum; N80.6, endometriosis of cutaneous scar; N80.89, other endometriosis; N80.9, unspecified endometriosis), (2) moderate risk (N80.0, endometriosis of uterus; N80.1, endometriosis of ovary; N80.2 endometriosis of fallopian tube) and (3) high risk (N80.4, endometriosis of rectovaginal septum and/or vagina; N80.5, endometriosis of intestine; N80.80, endometriosis of bladder; N80.81, endometriosis of uterosacral ligaments). Postoperative data were collected up to three years after index surgery.

After register-based data collection all women in both cohorts were mailed questionnaires concerning their long-term outcome: NRS measuring subjective bodily pain on a scale of 0–10 [15] and EHP-30 measuring a variety of aspects regarding endometriosis-related quality of life (QoL). EHP-30 consists of a core instrument with five subscores covering pain, control and powerlessness, social support, emotional well-being and self-image. The total scale ranges from 0 to 100, with lower scores indicating better QoL [16].

Statistical methods

Summary measurements are presented as means with standard deviations (SD). Independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test were used to analyse continuous variables, and Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical variables (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4). Regarding immediate postoperative outcomes (Table 5) multivariable adjusted logistic regression method (for complications) or linear regression method (for surgery time) or negative binomial model (for discharge day) were used to control for possible confounding when comparing the two time periods. The known factors affecting surgical outcome: ASA (1/2/3), BMI (body mass index), smoking (no/yes) and surgical difficulty level (low/moderate/high risk of complications), were used as adjusting factors. The results for logistic regression analyses are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR), and for the negative binomial model adjusted rate ratio (aRR) is presented. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) are presented for the aOR, regression coefficient and aRR.

For the patient reported NRS and EHP-30 questionnaires, missing data was accounted for using a multiple imputation (MI) method, assuming missing at random (MAR) with full conditional specification using the variables of age at operation, time from surgery to fulfilling the questionnaires, treatment period, BMI, ASA and urgency to model the EHP-30 variable. Fifty different data sets were created, and pooled results are presented (Table S2). The results of the MI analysis are treated as sensitivity analysis. Two-tailed p-values are reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS for Windows (IBM Corp. Released 2021. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) was used for analyses.

Results

Background characteristics

The former cohort before centralization consisted of 215 surgical endometriosis patients while the latter cohort after centralization consisted of 246 patients. Referrals from secondary level hospitals increased significantly after centralization (4.6% vs. 12.6%, p = 0.003). There were no significant differences between the groups regarding preoperative background characteristics: BMI, ASA, parity and smoking. Women in the latter cohort tended to be slightly younger (NS, not significant) but more morbid, since co-morbidity was significantly increased in the latter cohort (48,6% vs. 59,3%, p = 0.024), migraine being the most prominent co-existing disease (9.3% vs. 13.8%, p = 0.044). Preoperative hormonal treatment, as well as a combination of hormonal drugs, was significantly more common after centralization (35.8% vs. 52.7%, p = 0.002; 2.4% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.006; respectively). Preoperative diagnostics included more often MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) during the latter study period (24.2% vs. 48.8%, p < 0.001) and surgery was more often planned by the multidisciplinary team (3.3% vs. 18.3, p < 0.001) (Table 1.).

Surgical treatment

Age at surgery was two years younger in the latter cohort (NS) (Table 1). Pain was the primary indication for surgery in both cohorts, but even more so during the latter study period (p = 0.01). During both periods, the most frequent surgical diagnoses were peritoneal, ovarian and bowel/rectovaginal endometriosis, but the frequencies of peritoneal and bowel/rectovaginal diagnoses were increased in the latter cohort (40.0 vs. 53.7, p = 0.003; 6.5% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.004; respectively). The amount of multiple surgical endometriosis diagnoses was significantly increased in the latter cohort compared to the former cohort (20.9% vs. 35.0%, p < 0.001) (Table 2). During both study periods endometriosis surgery was principally performed by sparing some or all reproductive organs. After centralization the type of surgery was significantly more often a combination of procedures (30.2% vs. 43.9%. p = 0.002). Peritoneal surgery (16.3% vs. 37.9%, p < 0.001) as well as vaginal (0.5% vs. 3.3%, p = 0.031) and bowel resections (3.3% vs. 8.1%, p = 0.032) were significantly more common in the latter cohort (Table 2).

There were no significant differences regarding the surgical method between the time periods with laparoscopy being the principal method. Robotic surgery non-significantly increased towards the latter period (Table 2). The rate of intraoperative complications and conversions did not differ between the periods, nor did the operating room or surgery times. Women in the latter cohort were operated by more experienced surgeons (p < 0.001), more often by a team of gynaecological endometriosis surgeons (7.4% vs. 29.3%, p < 0.001) and more often by a multidisciplinary team with colorectal surgeons (8.8% vs. 18.4%, p = 0.008) (Table 3).

Short-term postoperative data

There were no significant differences between the study groups regarding time to discharge, re-admission, number of sick leave days or complications (Table 4a). The regression analyses showed no significant differences regarding surgery time or discharge day. A slight non-significant reduction in the risk of complications was found during the latter period (aOR 0.95, 95% CI 0.50–1.78) (Table 5). Histological verification of the disease was obtained significantly more often after centralization (69.7% vs. 80.1%, p = 0.002). Postoperative hormonal treatment was provided significantly more often after centralization (47.9% vs. 69.9%, p < 0.001) (Table 4a).

Discussion

This study is one of the first studies to report long-term follow-up data on the effect of centralization and multidisciplinary approach concerning endometriosis surgery in a single tertiary hospital. Our study shows significant development in the care of surgical endometriosis patients after implementing a centralized multidisciplinary model. Referrals from secondary level hospitals were increased and diagnoses concerning especially DE increased. The planning and execution of surgeries was increasingly subjected to a multidisciplinary approach. Patients were followed up for a longer time after surgery, suggesting that endometriosis was treated as a chronic illness according to current guidelines with long-standing individualized treatment plans.

The need for centralization of endometriosis care has been acknowledged widely [8, 17–21]. Expertise endometriosis centres have been implemented in several European countries; Denmark [22], Germany [23], United Kingdom [24], France [14] and the Netherlands [9]. French professionals have established further goals: to train health professionals, to develop basic research and participate in clinical research, to coordinate MDT meetings, to inform the public, to facilitate patient access and tailor a long-term individualized treatment strategy [14]. Having a sufficient workload is also important to maintain sufficient surgical skills [24]. Some studies have shown that the increasing volume of DE colorectal surgical activity correlates to a decrease in complication rates [25, 26].

At our university hospital district, the implementation of endometriosis expertise centre was considered beneficial by the secondary level as the referrals from those hospitals tripled. Earlier, demanding endometriosis surgery was also performed at smaller secondary level hospitals. We were able to improve medical treatment before proceeding to surgery, hormonal treatment being the first line therapy in our practice. Women with endometriosis are recommended to be offered hormonal treatment to reduce pain and perhaps to prevent or postpone surgery [7]. In addition to first line transvaginal sonography, preoperative diagnostics included MRI with multidisciplinary approach more often. Dedicated TVS and MRI have been shown to be similarly effective to detect DE but suggested to be used according to the expertise in the region [27], both methods being examiner dependent. Increased MRI usage in our study resulted in an increase of preoperative DE diagnosis, possibly enabling better surgical planning and preoperative patient information. European Endometriosis League (EEL) states in their consensus that MDT establishes a basis for an individualized endometriosis care driven by guidelines and multidisciplinarity [28]. The general development of MRI diagnostics over time, irrespective of centralization, may also have had an impact on the increased detection of DE in our study.

There was a tendency of having surgery at a younger age after centralization, though not reaching statistical significance within this sample size. It is probable that the establishment of an endometriosis expertise centre may have raised awareness of the disease among regional primary health care professionals and resulted in an earlier referral of patients with endometriosis-like symptoms to our centre. Increasing endometriosis awareness at the primary health care is an important tool to shorten the well-known diagnostic delay of the disease [14].

The main indication for surgery was increasingly pain, indicating a more conservative approach towards symptomless imaging findings, but an active approach towards symptomatic patients. The increased number of peritoneal endometriosis diagnoses and peritoneal surgery in the latter cohort reflects the increased number of women with endometriosis-like symptoms referred to our outpatient clinic (data not shown). The increased number of bowel endometriosis is probably due to referrals from secondary level hospitals. After centralization the patients had an increasing number of different endometriosis diagnoses per surgery, probably reflecting more detailed intraoperative diagnostics. The surgical procedures performed are in line with the intraoperative diagnoses, including more often peritoneal and bowel surgery as well as vaginal resections. The surgical team was significantly more experienced after centralization with an increasing use of endometriosis surgeon teamwork as well as multidisciplinary co-operation with colorectal surgeons. As a result of the increased operative experience and teamwork, combined surgery with coinciding surgical procedures was significantly increased after centralization, aiming at complete removal of the endometriotic tissue to diminish the risk of recurrent disease. Experience in DE surgery is of utmost importance considering operating time, complications, surgical completeness and recurrence rate [29] and with comprehensive surgery it is possible to minimize the number of lifetime operations [30]. Therefore, DE should be managed in a tertiary referral centre in a multidisciplinary context [31, 32]. Postoperative epidural analgesia was also increasingly used after centralization reflecting multidisciplinary approach with anesthesiologic collaboration and better conception of postoperative pain management.

Short-term postoperative outcome was similar between the groups. Although having performed more demanding surgery during the latter period, complication rate remained similarly low regarding complications needing further surgical care (Clavien Dindo III) [33]. The latter cohort in general had more severe disease, and thus short-term postoperative outcome was adjusted by three-step severity-level of the disease. Disease-specific classifications were not useful in this retrospective study. The low complication rate probably partly reflects the effect of more experienced surgical team after centralization, but this finding may also be affected by temporal changes in the surgical skill development. The rate of severe Clavien Dindo III postoperative complications of the present study (1.4% and 3.3%, respectively) is comparable to others [34]. The quality of postoperative endometriosis care was significantly improved after centralization with a high coverage (70%) of postoperative hormonal treatment as suggested by ESHRE guideline on endometriosis to prevent the recurrence of the disease and relating symptoms when pregnancy is not desired [7].

Our three-year follow-up data showed a significant increase in the utilization of endometriosis centre services, both at medical and nursing levels. The nurse-level contacts exceeded that of the doctors, being in concordance with our aim to create a low-threshold contact channel that would diminish the need for medical outpatient clinic visits. An Australian study group showed a reduction in emergency visits, short admissions and daily opiate use of women with chronic pelvic pain after the establishment of a multidisciplinary clinic for patients with persisting pain [10]. Unfortunately, we could not distinguish emergency and elective visits, neither do we have access to data on possible basic health care or secondary-level hospital visits. Rather, we see the prolonged postoperative follow-up time with the increased number of outpatient clinic visits in our endometriosis centre after centralization as a sign of a long-term individualized treatment plan based on patients’ needs, and perhaps partly as a reflection of our own medical interest on the postoperative outcome. Endometriosis is a complex entity warranting holistic approach with multidisciplinary teamwork, pharmaceutical, surgical and non-pharmaceutical interventions tailored according to patients’ unique needs [35]. In addition, endometriosis should be recognized as a chronic condition with adequate follow-up. Patient education is also important to be able to involve the patients themselves to the treatment plan and execution. Additionally, our results are in accordance with previous clinical experience showing that surgical patients with especially peritoneal endometriosis quite often need further conservative interventions, such as pelvic floor physiotherapy, for pain management.

Unfortunately, the response rate to the mailed questionnaires (NRS and EHP-30) was low with limited significance in the results and therefore this supplementary patient reported outcome data should be interpreted with caution. NRS questionnaire measuring bodily pain levels (scale 0–10) showed similar satisfactory results between the cohorts. Dyschezia, however, was rated lower by the latter cohort. This may reflect a more complete surgical treatment of rectovaginal and bowel endometriosis after centralization. Endometriosis-related general health (EHP-30) was similarly quite good in both cohorts indicating that the outcome of endometriosis care has been at an acceptable level during the entire study period. The three-year postoperative EHP-30 scores in the present study are similar to a Canadian study with a two-year follow-up after conservative endometriosis surgery [13].

The post-centralization data showed numerous changes in the examination and treatment path of surgical endometriosis patients with an increase in the health care utilization. These changes are to be seen as process change indicators that do not automatically produce patient benefits. However, centralization resulted in clinically important effects in pharmaceutical care, preoperative evaluation, MDT approach, surgical experience and simply in the patients’ access to specialized care. Further, these changes show dedication among health care professionals to develop and improve endometriosis care which, in our opinion, enables and is essential to patient benefit.

In addition to the novelty of our data, the strengths of our study are comprehensive manual data collection by a researcher who was familiar with the medical working environment in question throughout the study periods. Data was very detailed requiring clinical perception and understanding of the patient process. Rather than being evaluated in pre-defined research setting, the results of this study depict real-world effects of implementing a new utility in a single tertiary referral hospital. Follow-up data covering a three-year period postoperatively was collected for all patients, shedding light on long-term surgical outcome.

As for limitations, due to the relatively small study sample the detection and comparison of rare incidents may be difficult. In this dataset, we had no access to patients’ visits in basic health care or secondary level hospitals. The present study is retrospective, non-randomized and conducted in a single centre setting making it susceptible to selection bias. Also, temporal chances in clinical practice, that are independent of centralization, such as increased awareness of endometriosis, improvements in imaging and surgical expertise, may confound the observed outcomes. Additional limitation is the relatively low response rate to the mailed questionnaires (NRS and EHP-30) that may have caused selection bias concerning patient-reported results. However, there were no significant differences in the background characteristics between respondents and non-respondents, nor between the results of imputed and available data. Furthermore, being a study conducted in a tertiary centre that offers both conservative and operative care with individualized follow-up, the results may not be generally applicable.

Conclusion

The results of our study are encouraging, as several improvements were achieved in the endometriosis patient care process in our setting after centralization. Evidence on the possible patient benefit, however, remains limited. Number of referrals from central hospitals tripled indicating that our multidisciplinary approach was considered beneficial by the secondary level hospitals. Additionally, we were able to achieve most of the goals [14] set for an endometriosis centre. Endometriosis is a complex and challenging medical entity, and holistic approach with experienced multidisciplinary medical team should be offered to these patients for optimizing their care. More research is required to establish clear guidelines for centralizing endometriosis care for the benefit of patients.
