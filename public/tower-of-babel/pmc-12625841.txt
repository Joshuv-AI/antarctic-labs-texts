Patient education series: understanding trauma and emergency surgical conditions—emergency general surgery and the emergency general surgeon
Authors: Jennifer Hubbard, Shannon M Foster
Published: 2025

What is an emergency general surgeon?

An emergency general surgery surgeon, or EGS surgeon, is a doctor who helps people with sudden, serious health problems that might need surgery right away. They help patients who come to the hospital with things like severe belly pain, a bad infection that needs surgery, or other problems requiring surgery that may happen while someone is already in the hospital. EGS surgeons are trained to act fast and are very skilled at deciding if surgery is needed and what type of surgery to do. They treat a wide variety of problems throughout the body. Even if surgery is not needed, their decisions help guide the care patients receive while they are in the hospital.

What is the difference between a general surgeon and an emergency general surgeon?

An EGS surgeon is a general surgeon but one who works primarily in the inpatient/hospital setting rather than in the office/clinic. Many general surgeons usually work in a clinic or an office to see patients who are referred from another doctor with a problem that may need surgery but are not in any immediate danger. A visit to the general surgeon’s office allows scheduling of tests, pictures, and operations into the future based on symptoms and availability. Being seen by the surgeon in the hospital, also called a ‘consult’, means tests and studies will happen during the hospital stay as quickly as possible to help guide treatment. While many general surgeons sometimes cover consults and calls for emergencies, EGS surgeons do so all the time. They treat hospital patients at all hours of every day.

What is an acute care surgeon?

An acute care surgeon is a general surgeon who has done extra training on how to treat sick patients in the hospital. They can work as an EGS surgeon. They can also work as a trauma surgeon or as the leader of a team taking care of a very ill patient.

How are emergency surgeries different from elective surgeries?

Emergency surgeries/operations, like those handled by an EGS surgeon, are often done urgently (within a few hours or within the same day) or emergently (as soon as possible). The patient is often very sick because the surgical problem they are having is bad enough to make the whole body sick. The surgeries are difficult and the patients can have long, hard recoveries with many complications.

Elective surgeries, on the other hand, are planned and scheduled in advance. These types of surgeries are usually done on patients who are healthy enough to be at their baseline of activity, so the surgeon has time to prepare them for surgery and the patient has a better chance of recovering from surgery without as many complications. The recovery from elective surgery can be easier because it was not an emergency where the whole body was sick at the time of surgery.

What is an EGS service?

In addition to the EGS surgeon, the EGS service is a team that can include other healthcare members, such as resident doctors, advanced practitioners (APs), specialized nurses, social workers, and case managers. Surgical residents, doctors who are training to become surgeons, help with patient care as they learn about surgery under the guidance of EGS surgeons. APs are nurse practitioners or physician assistants who have finished training and work as part of the surgery team. Nurses on an EGS team often have extra training in wound and nutrition problems. Other team members are there to aid in discharge planning, setting up care outside the hospital, and billing questions. The size of the team depends on the size of the hospital, as larger hospitals may have larger EGS teams. The EGS team visits each patient and checks on their progress by a process called ‘rounds’. They talk about each patient’s needs, make plans for their care and answer questions. Family members and healthcare surrogates/designees/power of attorney will also be given important updates whenever possible (in person or by phone) from a rounding team member to keep everyone informed. Even if you see or speak to different people from the team each day, everyone is up to date on the case and care plan.

Will I see the same surgeon every day?

Since EGS surgeons and team members work in the hospital to provide care 24/7, they work in shifts. A shift may be daytime, nighttime, or even 24–36 hours long. When the shift is over, they go home to rest and recover and transfer patient care to another team member. This means you might not see the same surgeon or team member each day. For example, one EGS surgeon might be working at the time of the first consult in the emergency department, another may perform surgery the next day, and another might round afterward. This system is called ‘shared coverage’ and means that the surgeons all communicate about each patient’s case, so every surgeon knows the details about the care and plan.

Why are different levels of care and hospital locations on one service?

Hospitals have different areas where patients stay based on how much care they need. The areas often have different monitors, different room layouts, and different visitor allowances. Most importantly, they have different skilled nursing needs. The EGS team decides which area is best based on how sick the patient is and how much nursing care and vital sign monitoring is needed. Observation/emergency room: for patients whose condition is expected to improve or change in a short time frame that impacts further decisions and care plan.

The EGS team decides every day which location is needed and will continue to take care of the patient in all of these areas. The care level may change multiple times over the course of a hospital stay based on changes in the patient’s condition.

Do EGS surgeons and services see patients after they leave the hospital?

After discharge, the EGS service might ask the patient to come back to the hospital clinic for a follow-up appointment. This visit will be with one of the EGS care team members to make sure all is healing well and to answer any questions. Since EGS surgeons and teams work in shifts, the person at the clinic appointment might be different from the surgeon who performed the operation. Regardless of which team member is in the clinic—surgeon, resident, nurse, or AP, they will know all about the case and what is needed. The patient’s primary care doctor might also be involved in follow-up care. Any other specialist involved while in the hospital will likely also be seen at a follow-up appointment.
