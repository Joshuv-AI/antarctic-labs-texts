The Perceived Influences of Environment on Health in Italy: a Penalized Ordinal Regression Approach
M. Stival, A. Andreella, G. Bertarelli, C. Midões, S.F. Tonellato, S. Campostrini

Abstract
Understanding how individuals perceive their living environment is a complex task, as it reflects both personal and contextual determinants. In this paper, we address this task by analyzing the environmental module of the Italian nationwide health surveillance system PASSI (Progressi delle Aziende Sanitarie per la Salute in Italia), integrating it with contextual information at the municipal level, including socio-economic indicators, pollution exposure, and other geographical characteristics. Methodologically, we adopt a penalized semi-parallel cumulative ordinal regression model to analyze how subjective perceptions are shaped by both personal and territorial determinants. The approach balances flexibility and interpretability by allowing both parallel and non-parallel effects while regularizing estimates to address multicollinearity and separation issues. We use the model as an analytical tool to uncover the determinants of positivity and neutrality in environmental perceptions, defined as factors that contribute the most to improving perception or increasing the sense of neutrality. The results are diverse. First, results reveal significant heterogeneity across Italian territories, indicating that local characteristics strongly shape environmental perception. Second, various individual factors interact with contextual influences to shape perceptions. Third, hazardous environmental factors, such as higher PM2.5 levels, appear to be associated with poorer environmental perception, suggesting a tendency among respondents to recognize specific environmental issues. Overall, the approach demonstrates strong potential for application and provides useful insights for environmental policy planning. 1 1 1 This project is developed under the project Planet4Health, which is funded by the European Commission grant 101136652. The five Horizon Europe projects, GO GREEN NEXT, MOSAIC, PLANET4HEALTH, SPRINGS, and TULIP, from the Planetary Health Cluster.



1
Introduction

Understanding how citizens perceive the environment in which they live is of fundamental importance, as such perceptions are directly linked to individual and collective well-being.
They not only reflect the quality of life in a given context but also shape awareness of risks and opportunities, guide daily choices that affect health, and inform the design of policies and communication strategies in the domains of health promotion, environmental protection, and social equity, which in turn affect the acceptability of these policies by the population.

Despite its relevance—recognized in early works by
Buttel and Flinn, 1976
;
Van Liere and Dunlap, 1981
;
Dunlap and Liere, 1984
;
Mohai and Twight, 1987
—environmental perception is still far from fully understood,
being the combination of individual-level factors—such as age, gender, education, socio-economic status, psychological traits, political orientation—interacting with contextual features regarding the environment, e.g., urban form, quality of natural resources, exposure to pollutants, and territorial vulnerabilities.
Reflecting this complexity, the literature has proposed a wide range of methods to study environmental perception.
Some studies rely on surveys that directly ask citizens to evaluate
specific aspects of their environment.
For instance, while
Ammons et al., 2022
concentrate on the perception of worry regarding harm from air pollution using cross-sectional data from the Health Information National Trends Survey,
Raker and Bozick, 2025
study the temporal evolution in patterns and socio-demographic determinants of concerns for air pollution in Houston.
Furthermore,
D’Uggento et al., 2023
focus on young people, concluding that students are motivated to protest and demand a greener future for the global community, being concerned about the harmful effects of climate change, such as natural disasters, and aware that the crisis will spiral out of control if immediate action is not taken.
Other studies employ instruments to measure attitudes toward pro-environmental orientation
(
Dunlap et al., 2000
)
and environmental concerns
(
Van Liere and Dunlap, 1981
)
. Others attempt to combine subjective perceptions with objective environmental indicators, arising from diverse sources, including monitoring stations

(
Dons et al., 2018
)
, national reports
(
Pu et al., 2019
)
, and citizens science reporting tools
(
Grossberndt et al., 2020
)
.
Far from being exhaustive in presenting existing works on the topic
(see
Cori et al., 2020
;
Cruz and Manata, 2020
;
Perea et al., 2025
, for recent topic-related reviews)
, these differences highlight not only different disciplinary traditions but also the nature of available data sources, which are often heterogeneous, fragmented, and complex to harmonize.

While surveys and institutional sources, such as national well-being indicators
(
Istat, 2023a
, e.g.)
, are fundamental tools for monitoring populations and tracking changes in people’s living conditions, perceptions, and behaviors, they often lack in describing with sufficient granularity the surrounding environment, which is crucial to assess whether such beliefs are supported.
Conversely, administrative and environmental datasets alone may fail to capture subjective experiences and perceptions of risk.
A fundamental challenge lies in integrating different sources of information to develop a more comprehensive understanding of environmental perception and to produce evidence that can inform effective interventions.
Increasingly available data integration approaches and methods for dealing with multi-source data sets offer promising perspectives in the literature.
For instance,
Salvatore and Moretti, 2024
propose to use small area estimation techniques to obtain reliable estimates of the proportion of people who are very and extremely worried about climate change at regional level using data from the
European Social Survey, 2022
. In contrast,
Hannibal et al., 2016
use data from nationally representative surveys and government agency data
to examine the extent to which local environmental stressors, such as air pollution and industrial waste, are related to individual environmental concern.
Finally,

Midões et al., 2024
construct a data set, the SHARE-ENV data set based on SHARE data
(
Börsch-Supan et al., 2022
)
, which is granular and comprehensive to study how climate change interacts with other environmental stressors and vulnerability factors.

In this work, we propose a framework for studying environmental perceptions and beliefs, in which large survey data are combined with diverse exogenous information describing the territory.
We consider the Italian case and exploit the data from the nationwide health surveillance system PASSI (Progressi delle Aziende Sanitarie per la Salute in Italia).
PASSI is a surveillance system designed to continuously collect information on the health, behaviors, and perceptions of the Italian adult population
(
Baldissera et al., 2011
)
.
The survey includes a module on environmental perception aimed at investigating how individuals evaluate their surrounding environment in relation to health.
Motivated by the wide territorial coverage achieved by this environmental module in 2023, we contribute to the literature by examining how Italian citizens perceive their living environment, in relation to their health and socio-economic characteristics.

To the best of our knowledge, in Italy, national-level studies remain relatively scarce, as attention has been devoted mainly to sub-national domains
(
Coi et al., 2016
)
.
The few national studies are outdated, e.g.,
Ottone et al., 2017
, or do not explicitly examine the relationship between environmental perception, health outcomes, individual characteristics, and the actual environment in which people live
(
Istat, 2024a
, e.g.)
.
Our study seeks to fill this gap by providing evidence at the national level on these aspects.
However, understanding these relationships is challenging, as diverse territorial contexts characterize Italy.
Italy encompasses highly diverse geographical settings—from coastal zones to the highest peaks in
Europe—as well as marked differences in population composition, wealth, risk exposure, lifestyles, and health conditions
(
Istat, 2023a
;
Istat, 2024c
;
OECD, 2024
)
.
Given the considerable heterogeneity of the territory, PASSI alone can capture only some of the aspects mentioned.
To address these information gaps, we integrate the PASSI survey data with a range of exogenous variables at the municipal level, i.e., the smallest local units for which we have information about the individuals observed.

Our analytical aim is to disentangle how the perceived effects of environment on personal health relate simultaneously to individual features and to broader contextual characteristics.
Clarifying these relationships is important for identifying the drivers of perceived inequalities and for informing policies that can effectively address both personal vulnerabilities and contextual disadvantages.
This requires a framework able to capture the interplay of both individual and territorial determinants, which can be numerous and interact in ways that make the analysis challenging.
In this sense, refined statistical approaches are needed, as they make it possible to disentangle the interplay of multiple individual and contextual dimensions, without losing interpretability.
From a methodological point of view, our analysis relies on well-established ordinal regression models
(
Agresti, 2010
)
, and in particular on the semi-parallel model proposed by
Wurm et al., 2021
, which offers a flexible balance between interpretability and good predictive performance.
Leveraging a three-category ordinal response regarding the perceived influence of the environment on health—categorized as negative, neutral, or positive, see Section
2
—we describe how such models can be used to identify two complementary dimensions of perception:
the determinants of
positivity
and of
neutrality
.
The first reflects characteristics that, if positive (or negative), lead to a positive (or negative) attitude toward the surrounding environment, while the seconds capture those characteristics that are mostly associated with an increased sentiment of neutrality.
We argue that examining both determinants is fundamental for understanding how citizens relate to their environment.
Positivity indicates satisfaction and adaptation, whereas its negative counterpart points to underlying concerns. Departing from neutrality, in turn, may represent an initial step toward heightened awareness and active engagement.

In summary, our study would like to contribute to the literature in several ways.
First, it exploits the richness of micro-level evidence offered by the PASSI system, a resource that has so far received limited attention in the field of environmental perception.
Second, it proposes an integration of subjective survey responses with contextual indicators, thereby offering a more comprehensive approach to the study of environmental attitudes.
Third, it develops a methodological framework that allows to identify and interpret two key dimensions of perception—positivity and neutrality—which we consider crucial for understanding the interplay between environmental beliefs, health, and awareness.
Ultimately, we analyze and discuss the Italian case, highlighting the potential of the proposed approach and its limitations, and suggesting possible future developments of interest.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces the PASSI dataset and its environmental module, together with the complementary data sources used in the analysis; Section
3
describes the methodological framework; Section
4
reports the results and offers a critical discussion; and Section
5
concludes with implications and directions for future research.

2.1
The PASSI surveillance system and the external datasets

PASSI is a nationwide surveillance system that, since 2008, has continuously collected data on behavioral risk factors and health among Italian adults aged 18-69 years
(
Baldissera et al., 2011
)
. Based on the US Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance, the PASSI surveillance system aims to establish a continuously updated and local-level database to monitor trends in health issues, risk factors, and preventive measures in Italy.
The sampling design is a stratified sampling (by gender and age) at the Local Health Unit (LHU) level, using the health registries. The gender-age specific strata, used to group the population from the health registries and from which the sample is drawn, must include at least six categories (Men aged 18–34, Men aged 35–49, Men aged 50–69; Women aged 18–34, Women aged 35–49, Women aged 50–69). In this way, the sample will reflect the proportions of the population, ensuring representativeness by gender and age. The survey is designed to obtain reliable estimates at LHU level. Every year, over 90% of the LHUs operating across Italy take part in the surveillance, providing information on over 90% of the country’s resident population. The response rate is consistently above 85%, while the refusal rate never exceeds 10%
(
Istituto Superiore di Sanità, 2023
)
.
In 2023, the total sample size is
31532
.
Due to differences in population size across regions, sample sizes vary accordingly, from
244
respondents in Molise to
3546
in Emilia Romagna.
On behalf of the LHUs, data are collected via a telephone survey administered by trained staff. The survey, which lasts about 30 minutes, poses a series of predefined questions on various topics.
The questions cover a broad range of topics, including chronic disease prevention, lifestyle behaviors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity), screening participation, mental health, and social determinants.
Being a rich source of information, PASSI data are a well-established resource used by researchers in several fields in Italy.
They have been used to study morbidity and morbidity patterns
(
Pastore et al., 2023
;
Andreella et al., 2023
;
Stival et al., 2024
)
, health in urban areas
(
Nobile et al., 2022
)
, and behavioral profiles
(
Minardi et al., 2011
;
Minardi et al., 2022
;
Gorini et al., 2023
;
Andreella et al., 2023
)
.

In addition to these core domains, the system includes optional modules on emerging public health priorities, which can be activated by regions or LHUs based on local needs.
Among them, module 18 investigates how individuals perceive the environment in relation to their personal health.
However, because this module was optional until 2023 and not consistently implemented across regions, studies concerning these aspects are few and dated
(
Ottone et al., 2017
)
.

In this work, we focus on the first item of the environment module (module 18) from the PASSI 2023 survey, which in English translates as:

Question 1.
Thinking about the environment of your neighborhood or the area where you live, how do you think it influences your current state of health?

Respondents could choose among four options:
i

ii
) it does, positively;
iii

iv
) I don’t know.
To exploit the ordinal nature of the variable, we focus on the first three categories, which implies discarding approximately 5% of the sample.
In this way, we focus on the
respondent’s belief about the environment’s influence on their health
as being either
positive
(if
ii
),
neutral
(if
i
), or
negative

).
Unlike subsequent questions that focus on general and specific concerns, this item is particularly appealing as it allows respondents to express their beliefs not only in terms of worries but also with respect to potential positive aspects of their living environment.
Our interest lies in analyzing variation in the response by jointly considering individual and contextual data, detailed in Table

Individual data comes from the PASSI dataset and includes socio-demographic variables (age, sex, occupation, citizenship, educational level, economic status, and co-living situation), spatial information (the respondent’s Local Health Unit), and a health indicator (reporting at least one chronic disease).
In addition, we incorporate municipal-level contextual variables coming from the SHARE-ENV
(
Midões et al., 2024

and ISTAT (Italian National Institute of Statistics) databases.
These include the yearly average of PM2.5 concentration by municipality, derived from Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) global reanalysis

Our starting point is to recognize that, in the Italian case as well, perception is linked to both contextual and individual factors.

While this industrial activity correlates with higher pollution levels, these areas also have higher income per capita and overall socio-economic development, resulting in lower MFI, better economic conditions, and other correlated aspects that may improve environmental perception as well.
On the one hand, this implies that these indicators alone are insufficient to capture the full range of dynamics present in the data; on the other hand, it is important to recognize that these factors are correlated and linked to the population characteristics of different geographical areas.
This last aspect can be understood by looking at the data from different perspectives.

As explained in more detail below, the framework is flexible enough to include numerous covariates, consider potential interactions, and depart from the typical assumption of parallel odds while maintaining interpretability.

3.1
The cumulative ordinal logistic regression model

We modeled the answer

y

to Question
2.1
using a categorical response variable. This variable takes a value of
-1
,
0
, or
1
, corresponding to a perceived negative, neutral, or positive influence of the environment on health. This information is accompanied by the individual vector of
P
covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
related to respondent
i
.
We treat the response variable as being a realization of a multinomial regression model such that

\displaystyle{y}_{i}\sim\text{Multinomial}_{3}(1,{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})),\quad i=1,\dots,n

where
n
denotes the sample size, with

{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})=(\pi_{-1,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}),\pi_{0,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}),\pi_{+1,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}))^{\top},

such that
\sum_{j=-1}^{+1}\pi_{j,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})=1
, for any linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}=(\eta_{-1,i},\eta_{0,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
having “+1” as reference category.

One common assumption for dealing with an ordinal response is to equate the cumulative odds to the linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\log\bigg(\frac{\Pr(y_{i}\leq j)}{\Pr(y_{i}>j)}\bigg)=\eta_{j,i}=c_{j}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},

(1)

for
j\in\{-1,0\}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and derive the multinomial probabilities as

\displaystyle\pi_{-1,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})

\displaystyle=1-\Pr(y_{i}>-1)

(2)

\displaystyle\pi_{0,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\Pr(y_{i}>-1)-\Pr(y_{i}\leq 0)

\displaystyle\pi_{+1,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\Pr(y_{i}>0).

The parameters
c_{j}
are
ordered intercepts
, such that
c_{-1}<c_{0}
, and are generally referred to as
thresholds
(or cutoffs) of a latent construction of the model
(
Agresti, 2010
)
.
The parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
are regression parameters, with generic element
{\beta}_{j,p}
with
p\in\{1,\dots,P\}
, representing the effect of
p
–th covariate on the cumulative log-odds, when other covariates are considered fixed.
Notably, the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
vary across outcome thresholds
j
. Consequently, the overall parameter
\boldsymbol{B}=({\boldsymbol{\beta}_{-1}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}})
forms a
P\times 2
matrix.
Without additional constraints, the parameters in
\boldsymbol{B}
remain unrestricted.
In this formulation, the model can be seen as a reparameterization of a multinomial logistic model with constrained intercepts, which preserves the natural ordering of the response categories when
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Under this construction, a positive (negative) value of
{\beta}_{j,p}
indicates a linear increase (decrease) of the cumulative log-odds presented in Equation (
1
), when the
p
–th covariate increases from
x_{p}
to
x_{p}+\delta
, for some
\delta>0
.
As usual in logistic models, this
translates into an increased (decreased) probability of observing
y\leq j
.

Notably, we are not requiring the typical assumptions of parallelism, also known as the assumption of proportional odds, for which
\beta_{-1,p}=\beta_{0,p}
,
\forall p\in\{1,\ldots,P\}
.
Since
\beta_{j,p}
depends on the margin
j
, interpreting a positive value of
\beta_{j,p}
as a worsening of perception due to an increase in the covariate
x_{p}
is not correct or straightforward. Such interpretation also depends on
\beta_{j^{\prime},p}
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
.

To clarify the interpretation of the regression parameters, we first note that the parameters
\beta_{j,p}
and
\beta_{j^{\prime},p}

In brief, the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{p}=({\beta}_{-1,p},{\beta}_{0,p})^{\top}
of each covariate can be classified according to the Cartesian quadrant in which it lies.
The first quadrant (
\beta_{-1,p}>0
,
\beta_{0,p}>0
) indicates increased probabilities of both negative and neutral environmental quality perception as
x_{p}
increases.
The second quadrant (
\beta_{-1,p}<0
,
\beta_{0,p}>0
) is associated with an increased tendency toward a neutral environmental quality perception when
x_{p}
increases.
The third quadrant (
\beta_{-1,p}<0
,
\beta_{0,p}<0
) indicates a decreased probability of both negative and neutral perceptions, and hence a shift toward a positive feeling.
Finally, the fourth quadrant (
\beta_{-1,p}>0
,
\beta_{0,p}<0
) indicates a reduced tendency toward neutrality, with responses polarized between negative and positive perception.
This may reflect ambivalence or a lack of clear positioning that
leads people with similar characteristics to respond in opposite ways.

Notably, coefficients that lie close to the bisector between the first (
\beta_{-1,p}>0
,
\beta_{0,p}>0
) and third (
\beta_{-1,p}<0
,
\beta_{0,p}<0
) quadrants indicate unidirectional changes toward positive or negative perception.
Projecting the coefficients along this bisector provides a way to identify the
determinants of positivity
, i.e., the explanatory variables that mostly contribute to an improvement in perception in the logit-scale.
The limiting case under which all coefficients lie in this bisector line is the
parallel odds
model.
This specification assumes that changes in
\boldsymbol{x}
produce equal shifts in both log-odds functions.
Under these restrictions, the link between the covariates and the cumulative odds is represented by the linear predictor

\displaystyle\eta_{j,i}=c_{j}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta},

where only the intercepts (or thresholds) vary across outcome margins, while the regression coefficients remain constant.
Despite its widespread use in practice, the simplicity of this model comes at the cost of restrictions that are often too stringent, especially given the complexity of survey data and the variety of scenarios illustrated in Table
2
.
For instance—due to different degree of awareness, in terms of recognition, understanding, and knowledge that individuals have about environmental issues and their relation with health
(
Perea et al., 2025
;
Mónus, 2021
)
—people with higher education may be more likely to say the environment is either good or bad for health, but not more likely to choose a neutral answer, if the unobserved environment in which they live is effectively good or bad for their health, respectively.
Under this perspective, we connect coefficients that mostly deviate from the bisector—those in which the assumption of parallelism appears to be violated—to the determinants that increase or decrease neutrality the most.

While a widely used test against the
parallel odds
assumption is given by the Brant test
(
Brant, 1990
)
, it is often the context that suggests which covariates are subject to the assumption of parallelism.
In such contexts, a commonly applied approach is the
partially parallel
odds model
(
Fullerton and Xu, 2016
)
, in which certain parameters are constrained to be equal across categories, while others are allowed to vary freely.
In the absence of this substantial knowledge, it is possible to adopt the
semi-parallel odds model
proposed by
Wurm et al., 2021
, based on a penalized likelihood approach, as explained in the next subsection.

3.2
The semi-parallel cumulative ordinal logistic regression model

Although the model presented in Section
3.1
offers the interpretative advantages previously discussed, it is important to recognize that the non-parallel cumulative ordinal regression model also presents several limitations.

First, the inclusion of multiple covariates—especially dichotomous ones and their interactions—can lead to computational issues such as separation or quasi-separation, resulting in empty cells or outcome categories with only a single response type. These problems are well documented in logistic regression
(see, e.g.
Cessie and Houwelingen, 1992
;
Heinze and Schemper, 2002
, among others)
, and are commonly addressed through penalization methods that shrink parameter estimates toward zero
(
Harrell, 2015
;
Archer and Williams, 2012
)
.
Second, the evident correlations among our predictors raise natural concerns about multicollinearity, being of interest to provide reliable uncertainty measures. Standard remedies such as dimensionality reduction techniques often sacrifice interpretability, while pre-selecting a limited set of predictors is not feasible, as in our application, when multiple correlated covariates are expected to jointly explain variability in the response.
Another way to address multicollinearity is through penalization approaches
(
Harrell, 2015
;
Archer and Williams, 2012
, see, e.g., ridge regression for ordinal models)
.
Further, the lack of specific literature in this applied setting also provides little guidance on model specification, specifically on which predictors might reasonably satisfy the parallelism assumption required for partial parallel models.

In alternative,
Wurm et al., 2021

introduced an elastic-net formulation that acts as a compromise between the parallel and non-parallel ordinal models, while simultaneously regularizing the regression parameters to manage multicollinearity and the high number of predictors.
This approach, also known as the
semi-parallel
model,
begins by defining an over-parameterized linear predictor as follows:

\displaystyle\eta_{j,i}=c_{j}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},

(3)

in which the linear predictor is composed of both shared
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and margin-specific effects
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
.

Wurm et al., 2021
proposed then to minimize, with respect to
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B})
, the objective function

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B})=-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B};\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{X})+\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B};\lambda,\alpha,\rho),

which is a sum of a multinomial log-likelihood
\mathcal{L}
and a penalty function
\mathcal{P}
, with parameters defined in Subsection
3.1
,
\boldsymbol{y}
the vector with responses of all individuals, and
\boldsymbol{X}
the matrix with their explanatory variables.
We denote with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{c}}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}
, the results of the minimization procedure.

The—weighted and rescaled—multinomial log-likelihood equals

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B};\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\Big[\sum_{j=-1}^{1}\mathbbm{1}(y_{i}=j)\log\big(\pi_{j,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})\big)\Big],

with weights
w_{i}
(e.g., sampling weights) that satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=n
, and
\pi_{j,i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})
and the linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
as defined in Equations (
2
) and (
3
), respectively.
The penalty function
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B},\lambda,\alpha,\rho)

is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{B},\lambda,\alpha,\rho)=\lambda\bigg(\rho\sum_{p=1}^{P}\big(\alpha|\beta_{p}|+\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)\beta_{p}^{2}\big)+\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{j=-1}^{0}\big(\alpha|\beta_{j,p}|+\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha){\beta_{j,p}^{2}}\big)\bigg),

where
\lambda\geq 0
,
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
\rho\geq 0
play the role of tuning parameters, controlling the degree of regularization toward three possible directions.

First,
\lambda
is a tuning parameter that controls the overall degree of penalization
(
Tibshirani, 1996
, see, e.g.)
.
When
\lambda=0
, the estimation relies solely on the likelihood without any penalization. As
\lambda
increases, the elastic-net penalty contributes more heavily to the estimation, by shrinking both
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
to zero.
Although understanding the role of
\lambda=0
is somewhat didactic, setting it to such a value is not recommended, since the model then suffers from non-identifiability issues due to the double additive structure of the linear predictor as defined in Equation
3
.
Second, the value
\alpha
controls the balance between the
lasso
(
\alpha=0
) and the
ridge
(
\alpha=1
) penalties.
Small values favor models with many parameters exactly shrunk to zero, with the others left to vary. This is a
nice feature when the interest is in selecting variables and reporting a model with few, interpretable effects.
Large values lead to models that are intrinsically less parsimonious,
but still can handle multi-collinearity, leading to more stable estimates where all variables contribute to some extent.
Finally,
\rho
also has the role of calibrating the results between two extremes.
When
\rho=0
, the objective function becomes that of a penalized ordinal model without parallel assumption, while increasing
\rho
shrinks the results more toward the parallel model. As noted by
Wurm et al., 2021
, this is equivalent to the parallel model with regularization parameter
\lambda^{\star}=\lambda\rho
by fixing
\lambda
at a very large value.
A coordinate descent optimization algorithm is used for optimization
(
Wurm et al., 2021
)
. While guarantees of convergence are reported in
Wurm et al., 2021
and references therein, the choice of hyper-parameters depends on the desired properties of the estimates, or can be guided by cross-validation and goodness of fit criteria.
Variability can be evaluated by bootstrapping. Being these aspects application-related, we refer to Section
4
for the details.

4
Application

This section introduces the model specification (Subsection
4.1
), followed by a comparison with alternative approaches (Subsection
4.2
), and concludes with the presentation of the results (Subsection
4.3
).

4.1
Model specification and uncertainty quantification

The model presented in Section
3
defines a general regression framework for analyzing ordinal responses with three ordinal levels, relating the beliefs on the influence of the environment on health—possibly as negative, neutral, or positive—to a vector of explanatory variables with unspecified functional form.

We then define here the linear predictor in Equation (
3
) as a sum of two sources of information:
(i) a linear functional form of the main effects, where the covariate vector
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
is designed for capturing both first-order marginal effects of covariates defined in Table
1
(excluding LHU membership), as well as all their two-way interactions;
(ii) the subject’s membership in one of
L
LHUs, represented by the vector
\boldsymbol{l}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{I}(l_{i}=1)&\ldots&\mathbbm{I}(l_{i}=L)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

More specifically, let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
, with
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{M},j}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{L},j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{m}_{i}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{l}_{i}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

The linear predictor for margin
j
is written as

\displaystyle\eta_{j,i}=c_{j}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{M},j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{L},j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{l}_{i}.

The linear combination
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{M},j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
defines the
national main-effects
component of the model, capturing variability in
y_{i}
through
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
, composed of both individual-level and municipality-level covariates.
This component includes first-order effects (e.g., the marginal impact of having a chronic condition or of being exposed to the average level of municipal pollution) as well as second-order interactions (e.g., the joint effect of chronic disease status and residence in a mountain municipality).
The coefficients in
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{M},j}
are assumed to be homogeneous across the country, capturing the main sources of variability between individuals. Such variability arises both from individual characteristics and contextual factors (captured by information on municipalities).
The inclusion of second-order interactions further accommodates heterogeneity in contextual risk effects.
In particular, variation in municipality-level exposures (e.g., pollution, type of municipality) may yield differential impacts conditional on individual-level attributes (e.g., education, economic status), holding other factors fixed.
These interaction terms quantify subgroup-specific sensitivities to environmental risks and capture systematic effect modification across the population, potentially reflecting differences in vulnerability or awareness.

The linear combination
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{L},j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{l}_{i}=\gamma_{l_{i},j}
is a fixed effect at the LHU level used to capture systematic deviations from the national main-effects component for margin
j
.
The choice of the LHUs as minimal aggregation level is because they represent the geographical units of the sample design
(
Istituto Superiore di Sanità, 2023
, see)
. LHUs can be viewed as a compromise between capturing variability at the micro-territorial scale (municipality, the smallest meaningful unit) and at broader macro-territorial levels (regional or higher).
In the Italian context, LHUs (ASLs, Azienda Sanitaria Locale) are public entities with organizational, managerial, and technical autonomy, responsible for delivering healthcare services within a specific sub-regional territory.
Since Question
2.1
is formulated in general terms, identifying such deviations may point to contextual factors not explicitly included in the model, yet playing a critical role in influencing perception consistently across all LHU territory.
The observed deviations may reflect localized issues to investigate—which can differ in nature across areas of the country—as well as unobserved factors that positively influence well-being and healthy living in the surrounding environment.

We highlight that the reasoning developed here with respect to the coefficients of a generic margin
j
, as their interpretation, applies to both.
As discussed in Section
3
, however, coefficients can also be re-expressed in the
positivity–neutrality
plane.
This transformation preserves their original interpretation in terms of multi-source variability determinants (national main effects and LHU deviations), while providing the advantage of directly isolating factors that specifically influence the two orthogonal dimensions of positivity and neutrality, respectively, rather than their combined effect.

Some aspects remain unaddressed in the proposed methodology, namely the selection of hyperparameters and the quantification of uncertainty in the estimates.
As discussed in Subsection
3.2
, the hyperparameters
\lambda
,
\alpha
, and
\rho
regulate, respectively, the overall degree of penalization, the ridge–lasso trade-off, and the parallel–nonparallel assumption.
While setting
\alpha=0.5
provides a compromise between aggressive and conservative covariate selection, the practical choice of
\lambda
and
\rho
is less straightforward.
We then conduct a grid search over
\log_{10}(\lambda)\in\{-6.00,-5.33,-4.67,-4.00,-3.33,-2.67,-2.00\}
and
\rho\in\{0.50,0.67,0.83,1.00,1.17,1.33,1.50\}
.
This choice permits us to investigate
49
configurations with varying degrees of penalization in a reasonable amount of time.
To assess sensitivity to the choice
\alpha
, we also compare results under
\alpha=0
(Ridge) and
\alpha=1
(Lasso).

To quantify the variability of parameter estimates and performance measures, we implement a bootstrap procedure
(
Rao and Wu, 1988
)
that respects the stratified nature of the sample.
For each replication
r=1,\ldots,R
, and for each stratum
s=1,\ldots,S
with
n_{s}
respondents–defined by the combination of LHU, sex, and age class–we resample
n_{s}
units with replacement to obtain
\mathcal{D}_{r,s}
with
|\mathcal{D}_{r,s}|=n_{s}
. The full bootstrap sample at replication
r
is then given by
\mathcal{D}_{r}=\{\mathcal{D}_{r,1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{r,S}\}
. The model is then estimated with the
ordinalNet

R
package
(
Wurm et al., 2021
)
under the chosen hyperparameters
(\alpha,\rho,\lambda)
, and the regression parameters
\boldsymbol{c}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{r}
are stored.
The bootstrap complements cross-validation by providing an empirical assessment of uncertainty in estimates and predictions.

4.2
Comparison with alternative models

This subsection aims to evaluate the proposed approach against a set of benchmarks.
Model evaluation and comparison with alternative ones are performed using a 5-fold cross-validation procedure.
The sample is randomly divided into five folds of approximately equal size; at each iteration, four folds are used to train the selected models, while the remaining fold is used for validation based on the models’ predictions.
We focus on two metrics: the Ranked Probability Score (RPS) and the Misclassification Error (ME).

Formally, let
y_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}
(
i=1,\ldots,n_{v}
) be the ordinal response for the
n_{v}
respondents of the
v
–th validation set (
v=1,\ldots,5
). Let also
\hat{p}_{mv,ik}
be the predicted probability for unit
i
in category
k
, for model
m
and the
v
–th validation set.
The RPS is defined as

\displaystyle\text{RPS}_{mv}=\frac{1}{n_{v}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{v}}\sum_{k=-1}^{1}(F_{ik}-\hat{F}_{mv,ik})^{2},\quad F_{ik}=\mathbb{I}(y_{i}\leq k),\quad\hat{F}_{mv,ik}=\sum_{j=-1}^{k}\hat{p}_{mv,ik},

while the ME is

\displaystyle\text{ME}_{mv}=\frac{1}{n_{v}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{v}}\mathbb{I}(y_{i}\neq\arg\max_{k\in\{-1,0,1\}}\hat{p}_{mv,ik}).

Both metrics are well known in the literature and generally used for the evaluation of ordinal models
(
Hornung, 2020
)
.
We seek to minimize these metrics as smaller values indicate better performances, with
0
indicating a perfect prediction.
While the first is a well-known metric for the evaluation of probabilistic forecasts of ordinal models, which seeks to evaluate the entire shape of the predicted distribution, the second one aims to assess the accuracy in predicting the most likely outcome.
This procedure represents a standard tool to assess predictive performance and stability across different partitions of the data, helping to mitigate overfitting and providing a robust measure of the ability to predict out-of-sample responses, incorporating, by looking at the distribution in the RPS, an evaluation of uncertainty in out-of-sample predictions.

Regarding the validation procedure, we consider several alternative models. The results are shown in Tables

and

.

As a simple benchmark, we use the marginal class proportions of the response in the training sample (reported as
Marginal mean
in Tables
3
and
4
).
This baseline model ignores stratification variables and any additional covariates, predicting the modal class as the most likely outcome. Its misclassification error is therefore equal to one minus the proportions of observations in the modal class (neutral category) within the validation sets.

The second set of alternatives relies on class proportions of the response in the training sample, stratified according to the combination of the variables LHU, sex, and age class or by region, sex, and age class.
The first (named as
LHU:Sex:AgeClass
in Tables
3
and

) is the stratification of the PASSI sample design
(
Istituto Superiore di Sanità, 2023
)
, whereas the second (named as

in Tables

As competitors, we then consider two ordinal models. The first (reported as

in Tables

and

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
as the proposed model, and an LHU fixed effect. The second (named as

and

These benchmarks provide a basis to directly assess the added value of the proposed approach, which is designed to address the limitations inherent in the alternative methods.

We also compare performance with random forests. These non-parametric methods are generally designed to achieve strong predictive performance and can capture higher-order interactions, but the resulting models are complex black boxes, whose outputs are not immediately interpretable.
We leverage the implementation of the

We note that the most significant gain occurs when moving from a national model to models that use stratification variables, indicating the substantial information they provide.
Interestingly, stratification by region only performs slightly better than stratification by LHU, indicating potential overfitting or limited informativeness when considering smaller aggregation domains, and no other information.

Analysis and discussion of results

\alpha=0
, and considering the best specification of hyperparameters
\lambda
and
\rho
in terms of best average RPS in cross-validation, as explained in subsection

.

R^{2}

\displaystyle R_{j}^{2}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}+\pi^{2}/3},

Therefore, in the first case, one may conclude that the individual and contextual covariates used have limited explanatory power. At the same time, a strong territorial/local component–captured by the fixed effects of LHU–is present.
In the second case, the covariates used can explain a non-negligible part of the explained variability, highlighting how these are effectively related to a strictly negative perception.
This disparity between responses is interesting and potentially explainable by multiple concurrent factors linked both to the nature of the responses and to the selected covariates.
A negative response is a clear and unambiguous indicator of recognizing—and sometimes experiencing—a potential problem for health. On the contrary, modelling
\Pr(y\leq 0)
—alternatively rewritten as
1-\Pr(y=1)
— involves greater ambiguity since neutrality (
y=0

y=1
.
Moreover, some of the selected contextual covariates reflect actual concerns in people’s lives when their levels are high (e.g., pollution—the higher it is, the greater its negative impact on health), while their absence does not necessarily translate into recognized positive aspects in terms of perception.
Identifying exogenous covariates that explain positive perceptions is more challenging, though it would be valuable for enhancing the understanding of subjective and societal well-being and represents a promising direction for future research.

5
Conclusions

A better understanding of people’s perception of the potential environmental effects on personal health is fundamental for better targeting and evaluating public policies.
We believe that this study takes a new step in this direction,
by analyzing the data of the nationally representative PASSI surveillance system to understand the determinants that shape environmental perceptions in Italy.
By integrating subjective survey responses with municipal-level contextual indicators, we highlight key aspects of how Italians perceive the influence of their environment on health, while also illustrating both the explanatory potential and the current limitations of the available data, pointing out even the need for further research.

In the first instance, we observe that a large share of the population exhibits a neutral or indifferent stance, but also that perceptions vary across the territory.
Understanding this indifference is crucial from a policy and communication perspective, as targeted awareness campaigns could increase public engagement, ensuring not only the acceptance of environmental policies but also effective participation in initiatives requiring citizen involvement.
Methodologically, the semi-parallel penalized ordinal regression model is effective in handling complex ordinal data, providing a balance between predictive performance and interpretability.
Representing the regression coefficients along two orthogonal dimensions–positivity and neutrality–offers new lens for interpretation.
Ideally, we would increase positive perception–in response to actual environmental improvements–and reduce the beliefs of neutral attitudes, a key asset for fostering improvements in the surrounding area.
Our analysis reveals clear territorial patterns, including a persistent North–South gradient,
consistent with other observations on the health status and health determinants in Italy
(
Minardi et al., 2011
, see, e.g.)
.
We find that negative perceptions tend to align with contextual risk factors, such as municipal-level PM2.5 concentrations, a high MFI, or densely populated municipalities.
These contextual indicators interact with individual factors that are associated with positive perceptions–such as higher education, good economic status, and living with other people–partially mitigating their positive effects, while also reducing the feeling of neutrality.

By contrast, when examining the results with respect to the response scale, it becomes clear that, beyond the strong territorial pattern, the covariates considered can explain only a small portion of the overall variability.
This becomes particularly evident when examining the determinants of strictly positive responses.
On one side, this highlights the complexity of interpreting these perceptions and suggests that additional social, cultural, or psychological factors may be at play, warranting further investigation.
On the other side, this may also reflect intrinsic limitations of the survey question, where a neutral response signals the absence of recognized problems, which can be challenging to distinguish from a positive response, whereas a negative response clearly indicates recognized problems.

Overall, we believe that our findings advance both substantive and methodological understanding of environmental perceptions in Italy. They not only deepen knowledge of how individuals relate their environment to health but also provide actionable insights for policy by highlighting territorial disparities and the mechanisms that shape perceptions.
Future research should integrate richer data sources, conduct targeted territorial analyses, and extend the methodological framework to accommodate multiple survey items in order to capture the full complexity of environmental perceptions.
