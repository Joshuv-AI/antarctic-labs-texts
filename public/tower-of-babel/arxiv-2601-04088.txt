1.
Introduction

We start by defining the fractional heat content of a domain in Euclidean space. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain and
\alpha\in(0,2]
. Consider the following parabolic equation with Dirichlet boundary condition:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\Delta_{\alpha}\right)u_{\alpha}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{in $(0,\infty)\times\Omega$}

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{in $(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega$}

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(0,x)

\displaystyle=1\quad\mbox{$\forall x\in\Omega$},

where
\Delta_{\alpha}
is the fractional Laplacian defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha}u(x)=C(n,\alpha)\ \mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}dy,\quad C(n,\alpha)=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\frac{n+\alpha}{2})}{\pi^{n/2}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}.

It is known that the solution to above non-local equation is unique.
The fractional heat content of
\Omega
is defined as
Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{\Omega}u_{\alpha}(t,x)dx
. In particular when
\alpha=2
, we recover the classical heat content, and its small-time asymptotics have been of special interest as it depends on the geometry of the domain. The original proof in the diffusion case (
\alpha=2
) is due to van den Berg and Davies
[
28
]
, where the authors showed that any bounded domain with
C^{3}
boundary satisfies

(1.1)

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}_{\Omega}(t)=|\Omega|-\sqrt{\frac{2t}{\pi}}\sigma(\partial\Omega)+\frac{t}{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}H_{\Omega}d\sigma+\mathrm{o}(t),

where
|\Omega|
is the volume of
\Omega
,
\sigma
is the surface measure on
\partial\Omega
, and
H_{\Omega}
denotes the mean curvature of the boundary points. Later, the above asymptotic formula was extended to Riemannian manifolds by van den Berg and Gilkey
[
29
]
, and an asymptotic expansion holds similar to (
1.1
) adapted to Riemannian manifolds.

While the results for diffusion case are known for many years, asymptotics of
Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}
when
\alpha\in(0,2)
were proved quite recently. For the one-dimensional Euclidean case, it was first obtained by Valverde
[
1
]
by means of probabilistic techniques. Subsequently, using distributional properties of isotropic
\alpha
-stable processes, small time asymptotics of the fractional heat content in higher dimensions was proved by Park and Song
[
19
]
for any bounded domain with
C^{1,1}
regular boundary. More precisely, defining the function
\mu_{\alpha}
as

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(t)=\begin{cases}t^{1/\alpha}\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}^{\alpha}(1)]&\mbox{if $1<\alpha\leqslant 2$}\\
\frac{1}{\pi}t\log(1/t)&\mbox{if $\alpha=1$}\\
t&\mbox{if $0<\alpha<1$},\end{cases}

where
\overline{Y}^{\alpha}(t)
denotes the running supremum of one-dimensional symmetric
\alpha
-stable process,
it was shown in
[
19
]
that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}=\begin{cases}|\partial\Omega|&\mbox{if $1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2$}\\
\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(\Omega)&\mbox{ if $0<\alpha<1$},\end{cases}

where
|\partial\Omega|
is the perimeter of
\Omega
, and
\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(\Omega)
denotes the fractional perimeter of
\Omega
when
0<\alpha<1
, see
[
19
, Equation (1.2)]
for details. It is noteworthy that the rate function in the case of
\alpha\in[1,2]
does not depend on the dimension of the space. This is an indication that the multiplicative constants in the limit of (
1.3
) should be universal if one replaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by a different space with different geometry.

In this paper, we study the asymptotic of the fractional heat content for
Carnot groups
. A connected and simply connected
stratified nilpotent Lie group
(\mathbb{G},\star)
is said to be a
Carnot group of step
k
if its Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
admits a
step
k
stratification
, that is,
there exist linear subspaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
such that

(1.4)

{\mathfrak{g}}=V_{1}\oplus...\oplus V_{k},\quad[V_{1},V_{i}]=V_{i+1},\quad V_{k}\neq\{0\},\quad V_{i}=\{0\}\ {\textrm{ if }}i>k,

where
[V_{1},V_{i}]
is the subspace of
{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by
the commutators
[X,Y]
with
X\in V_{1}
and
Y\in V_{i}
. For a good exposition on Carnot groups, we refer to the books
[
11
,
8
]
. Such groups are of special interests as they are equipped with a sub-Riemannian structure, which we are going to introduce now. Set

m_{i}=\dim(V_{i})
, for
i=1,\dots,k
and
n_{i}=m_{1}+\dots+m_{i}
, so that
n_{k}=N
. For the sake of simplicity, we write

n_{0}=0,\ m:=m_{1}
.
We denote by
Q
the
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{G}
, that is, we set

\displaystyle Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\dim(V_{i}).

We choose now a basis
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
of

\mathfrak{g}
adapted to the stratification, that is,

X_{n_{j-1}+1},\dots,X_{n_{j}}
is a basis of
V_{j}
for each
j=1,\dots,k
.
With an abuse of notation, let
X=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
be the family
of left invariant vector fields corresponding to the above basis. The sub-bundle of the tangent bundle
T\mathbb{G}
that is spanned by the
vector fields
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
plays a particularly important
role in the theory, it is called the
horizontal bundle

H\mathbb{G}
; the fibers of
H\mathbb{G}
are

\displaystyle H_{x}\mathbb{G}=\mbox{span}\{X_{1}(x),\dots,X_{m}(x)\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{G}.

We can endow each fiber of
H\mathbb{G}
with an inner
product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
that makes the basis
X_{1}(x),\ldots,X_{m}(x)

an orthonormal basis. Due to the stratification in (
1.4
),
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfies
Hörmander’s condition
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lie}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})=T\mathbb{G}.

Since
\mathbb{G}
is nilpotent, it has a unique (up to multiplicative constants) bi-invariant Haar measure, and
(G,H\mathbb{G})
is a sub-Riemannian manifold equipped with the smooth bi-invariant Haar measure. Once an orthonormal basis
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
of the horizontal bundle is fixed, we
define the horizontal gradient of a smooth function
f:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
, denoted by
\nabla_{H}f
, as,

\nabla_{H}f:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(X_{i}f)X_{i},

and the associated sub-Laplacian by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}^{2}f.

By
[
17
, Theorem 3.6]
it is known that
\mathcal{L}
does not depend on the choice of the orthonormal basis of
H\mathbb{G}
. Moreover, by
[
15
, p. 428]
,
(\mathcal{L},C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G}))
is essentially self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})
, the
L^{2}
spaces weighted by the left-invariant Haar measure. Denoting the unique self-adjoint extension of
(\mathcal{L},C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G}))
by
(\mathcal{L},D(\mathcal{L}))
, we consider the fractional powers defined by means of functional calculus:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{L}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\int_{\sigma(\mathcal{L})}\lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dE_{\lambda},

where
E
is the spectral measure of
\mathcal{L}
. Given any bounded domain
\Omega
, let us consider the Dirichlet fractional sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\alpha}
, which is defined as the closure of
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
restricted to
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Then,

u_{\alpha}(t,x)=e^{-t\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\alpha}}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}(x)
solves the following parabolic problem with Dirichlet boundary condition:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right)u_{\alpha}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{in $(0,\infty)\times\Omega$}

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{in $(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega$}

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(0,x)

\displaystyle=1\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\Omega$}.

The fractional heat content of
\Omega
is defined as

\displaystyle Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{\Omega}u_{\alpha}(t,x)dx.

We also need the following definition of
characteristic points
on the boundary of a domain.

Definition 1.1

(Characteristic points)
.

Let
\Omega
an open subset of
\mathbb{G}
with
C^{1}
boundary. A point
p\in\partial\Omega
is said to be a characteristic point if the projection of the outward unit normal
\nu(p)
onto
H_{p}\mathbb{G}
is zero.

The following is the main result of this article.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded, open subset of
\mathbb{G}
with
C^{2}
boundary having no characteristic points. Then for all
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}=|\partial\Omega|_{H},

where
\mu_{\alpha}(t)
is defined in (
1.2
).

When
\alpha=2
, by a recent work of Rizzi and Rossi
[
21
]
, the asymptotic expansion of
Q^{(2)}_{\Omega}(t)
holds on any sub-Riemannian manifold, and
\Omega
is required to have
C^{\infty}
boundary with no characteristic points. In the aforementioned paper, the authors used Savo’s technique, see
[
24
]
, adapted to the sub-Riemannian setting, and they obtained asymptotic expansion up to order
5
. Moreover, the first two coefficients in the asymptotic expansion are given in terms of the perimeter and horizontal mean curvature of the domain at boundary points. We also refer to
[
12
]
, where the results in
[
21
]
have been generalized to RCD spaces with a local Dirichlet form. However, our framework is different from the aforementioned work as fractional sub-Laplacians are non-local operators.

The main contribution of our work is to propose a novel approach to study small time asymptotic of fractional heat content on Carnot groups by means of probabilistic and analytic techniques. Using standard subordination argument for fractional powers of generators of Markov processes, see
[
3
, Section 1.15.9]
, the fractional heat content can be represented as

\displaystyle Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\mbox{$B^{\alpha}(s)\in\Omega$ for all $0\leqslant s\leqslant t$}\right)dx,

where
B^{\alpha}
is the subordinated horizontal Brownian motion on
\mathbb{G}
, see Section
3
for details. When
\alpha=2
, Tyson and Wang
[
27
]
used the above probabilistic formula to obtain second order asymptotic expansion of the heat content for
3
–dimensional Heisenberg group. Their method relies on the exit distribution of the horizontal Brownian motion on Heisenberg group along the outward horizontal normal direction of the boundary, as intuitively, the process would most likely exit the domain along this direction. To formalize this heuristic, their proofs involve various technical arguments which are difficult to adapt for subordinated processes on general Carnot groups. We provide a simplified argument based on Taylor formula and some refined estimates of exit probabilities of horizontal Brownian motion on Carnot groups, which leads to the small time asymptotics of the fractional heat content for any
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2
.

Let us now briefly describe the main idea behind our method. We consider a functional version of the fractional heat content, namely, for any nonnegative measurable function
f
on
\mathbb{G}
, we define

(1.6)

\displaystyle Q^{(\alpha)}_{f}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{G}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]dx.

This approach is motivated from a recent work
[
22
]
of the author on the spectral heat content of isotropic processes in Euclidean space.
When
f=\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}
, (
1.6
) equals the fractional heat content on
\Omega
. Using the Taylor formula on Carnot groups, thanks to Bonfiglioli
[
9
]
, we prove the asymptotics of (
1.6
) for compactly supported smooth functions
f
, and the lower bound of asymptotic heat content follows by an approximation argument. Our method also shows that the lower bound of the asymptotic holds for any bounded open set with finite horizontal perimeter, see Proposition
5.2
. The regularity conditions on the boundary are needed for proving the upper bound of the limit. We emphasize that our approach mainly requires some estimates of the probabilities of small exit times (see Theorem
3.1
), and a Tyalor type expansion of smooth functions on Carnot groups, indicating that this method can be useful for proving heat content asymptotics on more general spaces.

The assumption of non-characteristic boundary is very crucial in our method, and the reason is somewhat similar to
[
21
,
27
]
. We require
C^{2}
regularity of the signed distance function from the boundary of the domain. Even if the domain has
C^{\infty}
boundary, the signed distance function from the boundary may not be Lipschitz continuous, see
[
2
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we discuss some properties of the horizontal perimeter and the Taylor formula on Carnot groups, which play significant roles in the proofs. We introduce the horizontal Brownian motion, its subordination, and an estimate of the probability of small exit times in Section
3
. Section
4
is devoted to proving the asymptotic of the functional defined in (
1.6
). Finally, Theorem
1.2
is proved in Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries on Carnot group

2.1.
Global coordinates of
\mathbb{G}

Since
\mathbb{G}
is simply connected and nilpotent, the intrinsic exponential map
\mathrm{Exp}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{G}
is a global diffeomorphism. As a result, fixing any basis
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, we can identify
\mathbb{G}
with
\mathbb{R}^{N}
using the global coordinates

\displaystyle(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\mathrm{Exp}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}X_{i}\right).

Due to Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff-Dynkin formula, the group multiplication can be realized on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

\displaystyle x\star y=\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}(x)\star\mathrm{Exp}(y)\right),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

With an abuse of notation, we write
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{N},\star)
. With this identification,
0\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the identity element, and the bi-invariant Haar measure on
\mathbb{G}
coincides with the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Also, there is a natural dilation
\delta_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{G})
defined as

(2.1)

\delta_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=(\lambda\xi_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{k}\xi_{k}),

where
x=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}}\equiv\mathbb{G}
..

Indeed, the global coordinates depend on the choice of the basis of
\mathfrak{g}
. A natural way of constructing a basis is as follows: recall that
\dim(V_{1})=m
, and fix a basis
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
of
V_{1}
which is orthonormal with respect to the left-invariant inner product on
V_{1}
. For any multi-index
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}\}\subset\{1,2,\ldots,m\}^{l}
let us denote the higher order Lie brackets by

\displaystyle X^{J}=[X_{j_{1}}[X_{j_{2}}\cdots]\cdots].

Due to the stratification of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
in (
1.4
),
X^{J}\in V_{l}
whenever
|J|=l
and
X^{J_{1}},X^{J_{2}}
are linearly independent if
|J_{1}|\neq|J_{2}|
. Since
\mathrm{span}\{X^{J}:J\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}\}=\mathfrak{g}
, let
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,m\})
be such that
\{i\}\in\mathcal{J}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\{X^{J}:J\in\mathcal{J}\}

forms a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Throughout the paper, we consider the global coordinates of
\mathbb{G}
with respect to the basis
\mathcal{B}
unless stated otherwise.

2.2.
Homogeneous norms and distances

We call a norm
\|\cdot\|:\mathbb{G}\to[0,\infty)
homogeneous if

(1)

\|\delta_{\lambda}x\|=\lambda\|x\|
for any
\lambda>0
and
x\in\mathbb{G}
.

(2)

\|x\|=0
if and only if
x=0
.

Any homogeneous norm induces a pseudo-metric on
\mathbb{G}
defined by

\displaystyle d(x,y)=\|y^{-1}\star x\|,\quad\mbox{$x,y\in\mathbb{G}$}.

A metric
d
defined above is also homogeneous, that is,
d(\delta_{\lambda}x,\delta_{\lambda}y)=\lambda d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{G}
and
\lambda>0
. Any Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
with a sub-Riemannian structure described in the introduction above can be equipped with a Carnot-Carathéodory metric defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle d_{c}(x,y)=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|_{H}dt:\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y,\ \dot{\gamma}(t)\in H_{\gamma(t)}(\mathbb{G})\right\}.

It is known that
(\mathbb{G},d_{c})
is a path connected metric space, see
[
8
, Chapter 19]
, and
d_{c}
is a homogeneous distance.
By
[
8
, Proposition 5.1.4]
, any two homogeneous norms on
\mathbb{G}
are equivalent. If
d_{\infty}
denotes the homogeneous pseudo-metric on
\mathbb{G}
induced by the homogeneous norm

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|x\|_{\infty}=\sup_{1\leqslant j\leqslant k}\{\varepsilon_{j}|(x_{n_{j-1}+1},\ldots,x_{n_{j}})|^{\frac{1}{j}}\},\quad\varepsilon_{j}>0,

it was proved in
[
16
, Theorem 5.1]
that one can choose
\varepsilon_{1}=1
and
\varepsilon_{j}\in(0,1),j\geqslant 2
, depending on the group
\mathbb{G}
so that
d_{\infty}
becomes a metric. Therefore, any homogeneous metric
d
satisfies

\displaystyle c^{-1}d_{\infty}(x,y)\leqslant d(x,y)\leqslant cd_{\infty}(x,y)\quad\mbox{for all $x,y\in\mathbb{G}$}

for some positive constant
c
independent of
x,y
.
Throughout the paper, we work with an arbitrary homogeneous metric
d
unless stated otherwise.

2.3.
Horizontal perimeter

We start by recalling the definition of functions with bounded variation. For
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{G})
, the
horizontal variation
of
f
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f)=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{G}}fX_{i}\phi_{i}dx:\phi_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G}),\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\phi_{i}|^{2}\leqslant 1\right\}.

f
is said to have bounded variation if
\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f)<\infty
. The space of all functions with bounded variation, denoted by
\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{G})
, is a Banach space with the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{G})}=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{G})}+\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f).

Variation is lower semicontinuous, that is, for any
(f_{n}),f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{G})
with
\|f_{n}-f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{G})}\to 0
implies
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f_{n})\geqslant\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f)
.
A measurable set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
is called
Caccioppoli set
if
\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{G})
. In this case, the
horizontal perimeter
of
\Omega
is defined as

\displaystyle|\partial\Omega|_{H}:=\mathrm{Var}_{H}(\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}).

When
\Omega
is bounded with
C^{1}
boundary,
|\partial\Omega|_{H}<\infty
, and by
[
10
, Equation (3.2)]

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\partial\Omega|_{H}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle X_{i},\nu\rangle^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}d\mathcal{H}^{N-1},

where
\nu
is the Euclidean unit outward normal to the boundary, and
\mathcal{H}^{N-1}
is the
(N-1)
-dimensional Euclidean Hausdorff measure. While (
2.4
) provides an explicit relationship between the perimeter and Euclidean Hausdorff measure, it is also possible to represent the horizontal perimeter in terms of the Hausdorff measure with respect to a homogeneous distance on
\mathbb{G}
.
Let
d_{\infty}
be the homogeneous metric defined in (
2.3
), and let
\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}
denote the
(Q-1)
-dimensional spherical Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{G}
with respect to that metric, that is,

(2.5)

\displaystyle S^{Q-1}_{\infty}(E)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{diam}(D_{x_{i}}(t_{i}))^{Q-1}}{2^{Q-1}}:E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}D_{x_{i}}(t_{i}),\quad t_{i}\leqslant\varepsilon\right\},

where
D_{x_{i}}(t_{i})=\{x\in\mathbb{G}:d_{\infty}(x,x_{i})\leqslant t_{i}\}
and the diameter is with respect to
d_{\infty}
.
Then by
[
18
, Proposition 1.9]
and
[
18
, Theorem 2.5]
, we have the following relationship between the horizontal perimeter and
\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G})
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and
E=\{x\in\Omega:\phi(x)>s\}
. Assume that
\nabla_{H}\phi(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\partial E
, and
|\partial E|_{H}<\infty
. Then there exists a positive constant
c(\mathbb{G})
depending on
\mathbb{G}
such that

\displaystyle|\partial E|_{H}=c(\mathbb{G})S^{Q-1}_{\infty}(\partial E).

Remark 2.2
.

[
18
, Theorem 2.5]
is stated for
H
-differentiable functions and from the discussion after
[
18
, Defintion 1.12]
it follows that any
C^{1}
function is
H
-differentiable.

Another important fact we need in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is the continuity of perimeter of hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{G}
. The following result follows directly from
[
14
, Theorem 9.1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{G})
be such that
E=\{x:\phi(x)>0\}
is pre-compact in
\mathbb{G}
and
|\nabla_{H}\phi|
is bounded away from
0
in a neighborhood of
\partial E
. Assume that there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r\in[-r_{0},r_{0}]
, the family of domains
E_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{G}:\phi(x)>r\}
have
C^{2}
boundary. Then,
\lim_{r\to 0}|\partial E_{r}|_{H}=|\partial E|_{H}
.

As a consequence of Lemma
2.1
and
2.3
, we get continuity of
(Q-1)
-dimensional
d_{\infty}
–spherical Hausdorff measure of
C^{2}
hypersurfaces, that is,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(\partial E_{r})=\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(\partial E),

where
E,E_{r}
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
2.3
.
Lastly, we need the coarea formula on
\mathbb{G}
which is proved in
[
18
, Corollary 3.6]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function, and
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{G})
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{G}}h(x)|\nabla_{H}\phi(x)|dx=c(\mathbb{G})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\phi^{-1}(s)}h(x)d\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(x)ds,

where
c(\mathbb{G})
is same constant as in Lemma
2.1
.

2.4.
Taylor formula

Fix a basis
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. With respect to the global coordinates of
\mathbb{G}
induced by this basis, we have the following Taylor formula due to Bonfiglioli
[
9
, Corollary 1]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

For any
\phi\in C^{n+1}(\mathbb{G})
we have

\displaystyle\phi(x\star h)=\phi(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r=1}^{n}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}=1}^{N}\frac{h_{i_{1}}\cdots h_{i_{r}}}{r!}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{r}}\phi(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n+1}=1}^{N}\frac{h_{i_{1}}\cdots h_{i_{n+1}}}{n!}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{1}(X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{n+1}})\phi\left(x\star\left(sh_{1},sh_{2},\ldots,sh_{N}\right)\right)(1-s)^{n}ds.

We say that
\phi\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{G})
if
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G})
, and there exists
L>0
such that
|X_{i}\phi(x)-X_{i}\phi(y)|\leqslant Ld(x,y)
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
and for some homogeneous metric
d
. Applying Theorem
2.5
we get the following upper bound of the error term in first order Taylor expansion of
C^{1,1}
functions.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
\phi\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{G})
and
d
is a homogeneous metric on
\mathbb{G}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|\phi(x\star h)-\phi(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}X_{i}\phi(x)\right|\leqslant c\sum_{j=1}^{k}d(h,0)^{j+1}

for some constant
c>0
independent of
x,h
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.5
, using the first order Taylor expansion, it suffices to show that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}|h_{i}|\left|\int_{0}^{1}X_{i}\phi(x\star(sh_{1},\ldots,sh_{N}))ds-X_{i}\phi(x)\right|\leqslant c\sum_{j=1}^{k}d(h,0)^{j+1}.

Using Lipschitz regularity of
X_{i}\phi
, there exists a constant
L>0
such that
|X_{i}\phi(x)-X_{i}\phi(y)|_{H}\leqslant Ld(x,y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{G}
and
i=1,\ldots,N
. Therefore, the translation invariance of the distance function and
[
9
, Lemma 3]
yield

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}X_{i}\phi(x\star(sh_{1},\ldots,sh_{N}))ds-X_{i}\phi(x)\right|\leqslant c_{1}d(h,0)

for some constant
c_{1}>0
. On the other hand, by equivalence of homogeneous norms
d
and
d_{\infty}
(see (
2.3
) for definition) on
\mathbb{G}
it follows that
|h_{i}|\leqslant c_{2}d(h,0)^{j}
if
X_{i}\in V_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
for some constant
c_{2}
independent of
h
and
j
. This proves (
2.7
) and completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.
Horizontal Brownian motion and subordination

With the identification
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the horizontal Brownian motion
B=(B(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
on
\mathbb{G}
is the unique solution to the following Stratonovich stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle dB(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}(B(t))\circ dW^{(i)}_{t}

\displaystyle B(0)

\displaystyle=x,

where
W_{t}=(W^{(1)}_{t},\ldots,W^{(m)}_{t})
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The generator of
B(t)
on the set of compactly supported smooth functions coincides with the sub-Laplacian
-\mathcal{L}
. Ben Arous
[
5
]
obtained the exact form of
B(t)
in terms of iterated stochastic integrals of Brownian motion, see
[
5
, Theorem 13]
, which is motivated from the Strichartz formula (see
[
26
]
) for deterministic ordinary differential equations on nilpotent Lie groups. The horizontal Brownian motion on
\mathbb{G}
is
\frac{1}{2}
-self-similar with respect the dilations
(\delta_{\lambda})
defined in (
2.1
), that is, if
B(0)=0
,

\displaystyle\{\delta_{\lambda}B_{t}:t\geqslant 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{B(t\lambda^{2}):t\geqslant 0\}\quad\mbox{for all $\lambda>0$}.

For
\alpha\in(0,2]
, consider the
\alpha/2
-stable subordinator
S^{\alpha}_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}
, see
[
23
, Definition 21.4]
or
[
6
, Chapter III.1, p. 73]
, such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda S^{\alpha}_{t}}\right]=e^{-t\lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\quad\text{for all $\lambda,t>0$}.

Assume that
S^{\alpha}
is independent of the horizontal Brownian motion
B
.
Then, the subordinated process
B^{\alpha}=(B^{\alpha}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
defined by

\displaystyle B^{\alpha}(t):=B(S^{\alpha}_{t})

is a left translation invariant Markov process on
\mathbb{G}
, and its generator on the space of compactly supported smooth functions coincides with
-\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=-\mathcal{L}^{\alpha/2}
. Due to self-similarity of
S^{\alpha}
, see Appendix
A
,
B^{\alpha}
is also self-similar with index
\alpha
, that is,

\{\delta_{\lambda}B^{\alpha}(t):t\geqslant 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{B^{\alpha}(t\lambda^{\alpha}):t\geqslant 0\}

for all
\lambda>0
. When
\alpha=2
, we recover the horizontal Brownian motion. For any bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
, let us denote the exit time of the subordinated process by

\displaystyle\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:B^{\alpha}(t)\notin\Omega\}.

Then the solution to the fractional Dirichlet problem (
1.5
) can be written as

(3.2)

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(x,t)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}>t).

Due to the above probabilistic formula, the small-time analysis of the heat content
Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)
depends on the behavior of the exit probability from the domain as
t\to 0
, which is studied in the following subsection.

3.1.
Small time estimates of the exit probability

Let
d
be any homogeneous metric on
\mathbb{G}
. If
\Omega=D_{R}(0)
, the
d
–ball of radius
R
around
0
, then

\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau^{(2)}_{\Omega}\leqslant t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B(t),0)\geqslant R\right).

The next result provides an upper bound of this exit probability.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exist constants
c,\beta>0
such that for all
R>0
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B(s),0)>R\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{ct}}+\sum_{p=1}^{k}\mathcal{G}_{p}(R,t)+\sum_{p=1}^{k}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{p}\left(R,t\right),

where

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{p}(R,t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{R^{p\beta}}{ct^{\frac{\beta(p+1)}{2}+1}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{R^{p\beta}}{ct^{\frac{p\beta}{2}+1}}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{p}(R,t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{R^{\beta(p+1)+2}}{ct^{\frac{\beta p}{2}+\beta+2}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{R^{\beta p+2}}{ct^{\frac{\beta p}{2}+2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\exp\left(-\frac{R^{\beta(p+1)+2}}{ct^{\frac{\beta(p+1)^{2}+4p+2}{2p}}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{R^{p\beta+2}}{ct^{\frac{p\beta}{2}+2+\frac{1}{p}}}\right).

Remark 3.2
.

We note that by
[
4
, Proposition 3.3]
one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B(s),0)>R\right)\leqslant c_{1}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{KR^{2}}{e^{c_{3}Kt}-1}\right)

for some constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
and
K\geqslant 0
that depends on
\mathbb{G}
. The above theorem provides sharper upper bound for large values of
t
.

Remark 3.3
.

We note that both
\mathcal{G}_{p}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{p}
are sums of
\exp(-R^{\theta_{1}}/ct^{\theta_{2}})
where
\theta_{2}/\theta_{1}>1/2
. This observation will be important for small exit time estimates of the subordinated Brownian motion.

To prove the theorem, we require the following exact formula due to Ben Arous
[
5
]
for the horizontal Brownian motion
B(t)
. For any multi-index
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}^{l}
we write

\displaystyle W^{J}_{t}=\int_{T_{m}(t)}dW^{(j_{1})}_{t_{1}}\cdots dW^{(j_{l})}_{t_{l}},\quad T_{l}(t)=\{0\leqslant t_{1}<\ldots\leqslant t_{l}\leqslant t\}.

For any
l\geqslant 1
, we denote the permutation group of order
l
by
\sigma_{l}
, and for any
\sigma\in\sigma_{l}
let us write

\displaystyle e(\sigma)=\mathrm{card}\{1\leqslant j\leqslant l:\sigma(j)>\sigma(j+1)\}.

By
[
5
, Theorem 13]
there exist real constants
c_{J}
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle B(t)=\exp(\xi(t))(x),\quad\xi(t)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}c_{J}W^{J}_{t}X^{J}

where
X^{J},\mathcal{J}
are defined in Section
2.1
, and

\displaystyle W^{J}_{t}=\sum_{\sigma\in\sigma_{|J|}}\frac{(-1)^{e(\sigma)}}{|J|^{2}\dbinom{|J|-1}{e(\sigma)}}W^{J\circ\sigma^{-1}}_{t}.

Also by
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]
for any
2\leqslant p\leqslant k
, one can write

(3.6)

\displaystyle B(t)=\mathrm{Exp}\left(\xi_{p}(t)\right)(x)+t^{\frac{p}{2}}R_{p}(t),

where
\xi_{p}(t)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{p}}W^{J}_{t}X^{J}
,
\mathcal{J}_{p}=\{J\in\mathcal{J}:|J|\leqslant p\}
, and there exist
\beta,c>0
such that for all
R>c
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|R_{p}(s)|\geqslant R\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{R^{\beta}}{ct}\right).

If we choose global coordinates of
\mathbb{G}
with respect to the basis
\mathcal{B}
as described in Section
2.1
, (

) shows that any
2\leqslant p\leqslant s
,

\displaystyle Y_{p}(t)=t^{\frac{p}{2}}R_{p}(t)-t^{\frac{p-1}{2}}R_{p-1}(t)=\mathrm{Exp}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|=p\\
J\in\mathcal{J}\end{subarray}}c_{J}W^{J}_{t}X^{J}\right).

Notation 3.1
.

We observe that with respect to the global coordinates of
\mathbb{G}
induced by the basis
\mathcal{B}
we have

\displaystyle B_{i}(t)=W^{(i)}_{t}\quad\text{for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant m$.}

We call

the horizontal component of
B(t)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Y_{p}
is
\frac{p}{2}
-self-similar, that is, for any
c>0
,

\displaystyle\{Y_{p}(ct):t\geqslant 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{c^{\frac{p}{2}}Y_{p}(t):t\geqslant 0\}.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of
Y_{p}(t)
and
\frac{1}{2}
-self-similarity of Brownian motion.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that there exist positive functions
\mathcal{G}
and
g
such that for all
R\geqslant g(t)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|Y_{p}(s)|>R^{p}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{G}(R,t).

Then, for all
R>0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|Y_{p}(s)|>R^{p}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{G}(R,t)+\mathcal{G}\left(g(t),\frac{g(t)^{2}t}{R^{2}}\right).

Proof.

For any
R\leqslant g(t)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|Y_{p}(s)|>R^{p}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\frac{g(t)^{p}}{R^{p}}\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|Y_{p}(s)|>g(t)^{p}\right).

By Lemma
3.4
it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{g(t)^{p}}{R^{p}}\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|Y_{p}(s)|\overset{d}{=}\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant\frac{g(t)^{2}t}{R^{2}}}|Y_{p}(s)|.

As a result, for any
R\leqslant g(t)
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|Y_{p}(s)|>R^{p}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{G}\left(g(t),\frac{g(t)^{2}t}{R^{2}}\right).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since any homogeneous metric is equivalent to the homogeneous metric

defined in (

), it is enough to prove (

) with

. Clearly,

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d_{\infty}(B(t),0)>R\right)

4.
Asymptotic of the minimum functional

For any nonnegative
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{G})
, we define the functional of the heat content as

(4.1)

\displaystyle Q^{(\alpha)}_{f}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{G}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]dx.

Note that the above integral is finite as the right hand side is bounded by
\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{G})}
. Also, for any
t\geqslant 0
we have

\displaystyle Q^{(\alpha)}_{\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}}(t)=Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t).

In this section, we study the small time asymptotic behavior of

for nonnegative compactly supported smooth functions
f
. We recall that for any

, we have
\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{G}}|\nabla_{H}f|dx
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G})
be nonnegative. Then for any

,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{f}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}=\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f).

where

is defined in (
1.2
).

The proof of this theorem will use Taylor formula on Carnot groups and several small time estimates of the supremum process
\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B^{\alpha}(s),0)
, which are proved in subsequent lemmas. We start with a straightforward consequence of Theorem
2.5
applied to functions of left translation invariant processes on
\mathbb{G}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix a homogeneous metric

on
\mathbb{G}
. Let

be any left translation invariant process on
\mathbb{G}
with

. For any

and

\displaystyle\left|\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(x\star Z(s))-f(x)-\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\right|

For any

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]-f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}(t)}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant c\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d_{\infty}(B^{\alpha}(s),0)^{1+j}\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}(t)}\right]\sum_{i>m}|X_{i}f(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant c\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d_{\infty}(B^{\alpha}(s),0)^{1+j}\wedge R\right]\sum_{i>m}|X_{i}f(x)|.

Since
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G})
, invoking Lemma
4.5
we arrive at

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}c\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\int_{\mathbb{G}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d_{\infty}(B^{\alpha}(s),0)^{1+j}\wedge R\right]\sum_{i>m}|X_{i}f(x)|dx=0.

We note that

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}(t)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}(t)}\right].

For any
x\in S_{f}
, let us denote

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\inf\{d_{\infty}(x,y):y\notin S_{f}\}.

Then,
A^{x}_{\delta(x)}(t)\subseteq A^{x}(t)\subseteq A^{x}_{R}(t)
for all
t>0
and
x\in S_{f}
. As a result,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}_{\delta(x)}(t)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}(t)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}_{R}(t)}\right].

Since
(B^{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,B^{\alpha}_{m})
is an isotropic process under the probability law
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, and for any orthogonal transformation
U:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the mapping

\displaystyle x=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})\mapsto(U\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{k})

is a
d_{\infty}
–isometry on
\mathbb{G}
, it follows that for any
r>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}_{r}(t)}\right]=|\nabla_{H}f(x)|\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}_{r}(t)}\right].

Now, combining (
4.4
), (
4.5
), and (
4.6
) along with Lemma
4.4
yields

\displaystyle\ \limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{G}}\left(f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]\right)dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}_{r}(t)}\right]\int_{\mathbb{G}}|\nabla_{H}f(x)|dx

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f),

and

\displaystyle\ \liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{G}}\left(f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]\right)dx

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{t\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{G}}|\nabla_{H}f(x)|\frac{1}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{A^{0}_{\delta(x)}(t)}\right]dx

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f),

where the last inequality follows from Fatou’s lemma. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.
Proof of the lower bound

Let
f
be a nonnegative measurable function such that
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{G})
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, define the convolution

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x)=f*\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{G}}f(y^{-1}\star x)\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy,

where
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G})
is a mollifier supported in
D_{\varepsilon}(0)
, the
d
–ball of radius
\varepsilon
around
0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
t>0
and
0<\alpha\leqslant 2
,

\displaystyle Q^{(\alpha)}_{f_{\varepsilon}}(t)\geqslant Q^{(\alpha)}_{f}(t),

where
Q_{f}(t)
is defined in (
1.6
).

Proof.

By translation invariance of
B^{\alpha}
and Fubini’s theorem we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f_{\varepsilon}(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f_{\varepsilon}(x\star B^{\alpha}(s))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\int_{\mathbb{G}}f(y^{-1}\star x\star B^{\alpha}(s))\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{\mathbb{G}}\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(y^{-1}\star x\star B^{\alpha}(s))\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{G}}\mathbb{E}_{y^{-1}\star x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy.

This shows that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{G}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f_{\varepsilon}(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]dx\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{G}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}f(B^{\alpha}(s))\right]dx.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are now ready to prove the lower bound of the spectral heat content asymptotic.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Omega
be any bounded, open subset of
\mathbb{G}
having finite horizontal perimeter. Then,

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\geqslant|\partial\Omega|_{H}.

Remark 5.3
.

We note that the lower bound of the limit holds without any regularity assumption on the boundary of the domain.

Proof.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let us define
f_{\varepsilon}=\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}*\rho_{\varepsilon}
, where
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{G})
is a mollifier supported in
D_{\varepsilon}(0)
. Also,
\int_{\mathbb{G}}f_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=|\Omega|
. Therefore by Lemma
5.1
and Theorem
2.5
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\geqslant\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{G}}f_{\varepsilon}(x)dx-Q^{(\alpha)}_{f_{\varepsilon}}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}=\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f_{\varepsilon}).

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary and
f_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{G})
, by lower semi-continuity of
\mathrm{Var}_{H}
we conclude that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\geqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathrm{Var}_{H}(f_{\varepsilon})=|\partial\Omega|_{H}.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Due to Proposition
5.2
, it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\leqslant|\partial\Omega|_{H}

if
\partial\Omega
is
C^{2}
with no characteristic points. In the next lemma, we show that any bounded domain with
C^{2}
boundary with no characteristic points can be realized as a hypersurface.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with
C^{2}
boundary having no characteristic points. Then there exists
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{G})
such that

(i)

\phi
is bounded and
|\nabla_{H}\phi|=1
in a
d_{c}
–neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
,

(ii)

\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{G}:\phi(x)>0\}
.

Proof.

Consider the signed distance function

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\begin{cases}d_{c}(x,\partial\Omega)&\mbox{if $x\in\Omega$}\\
-d_{c}(x,\partial\Omega)&\mbox{if $x\in\Omega^{c}$}.\end{cases}

Since
\Omega
has
C^{2}
boundary with no characteristic points, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
\delta
is
C^{2}
in
\Omega^{\prime}_{r_{0}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{G}:|\delta(x)|<r_{0}\}
, and
|\nabla_{H}\delta|=1
in
\Omega^{\prime}_{r_{0}}
. Fix
0<r<r_{0}
and define a
C^{2}
function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}x&\mbox{if $|x|<r/2$}\\
r&\mbox{if $x\geqslant r$}\\
-r&\mbox{if $x\leqslant-r$}.\end{cases}

Consider the function
\phi=\varphi\circ\delta
. As
\delta
is
C^{2}
in
\Omega^{\prime}_{r_{0}}
and
\phi
is constant on
\{x:|\delta(x)|\geqslant r\}
,
\phi
is a bounded
C^{2}
function on
\mathbb{G}
. Also,
\phi(x)>0
if and only if
x\in\Omega
. For any
x\in\Omega^{\prime}_{r/2}
,
|\nabla_{H}\phi(x)|=|\varphi^{\prime}(\delta(x))\nabla_{H}\delta(x)|=1
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

From the construction of the function
\phi
in the proof, it follows that
\phi
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
2.3
.

We note that for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tau^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}\leqslant t)dx\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\phi(B^{\alpha}(s))\leqslant 0\right)dx.

For
\varepsilon>0
let us define
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\Omega:\phi(x)<\varepsilon\}
. We will split the integral above into the disjoint sets
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, and will prove that the latter term is negligible when scaled by
\mu_{\alpha}(t)
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\phi(B^{\alpha}(s))\leqslant 0\right)dx=0.

Proof.

Assume that
B^{\alpha}(0)=x
. From Lemma
4.2
we observe that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\left.\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\phi(B^{\alpha}(s))\leqslant 0\right|B^{\alpha}(0)=x\right\}\subseteq\left\{\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}B^{\alpha}_{i}(s)X_{i}\phi(x)\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left.c\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}d(B^{\alpha}(s),e)^{1+j}\sum_{i>m}|X_{i}\phi(x)|\geqslant\phi(x)\right|B^{\alpha}(0)=e\right\}.

From the construction of
\phi
, we have
\|X_{i}\phi\|_{\infty}<\infty
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
. Let

(6.2)

\displaystyle M=\max\{\|\nabla_{H}\phi\|_{\infty},\sum_{i>m}\|X_{i}\phi\|_{\infty}\}.

Since
\phi(x)\geqslant\varepsilon
for any
x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, (
6.1
) implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\phi(B^{\alpha}(s))\leqslant 0\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(M\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(M\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B^{\alpha}(s),e)^{1+j}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{ck}\right)

\displaystyle=:I(t)

Since
\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\leqslant c\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B^{\alpha}(s),0)
for some constant
c>0
independent of
t
,
by the tail probability estimate in Lemma
4.3
,
I(t)=\mathrm{O}(t)
as
t\to 0
. Noting that
|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon}|\leqslant|\Omega|
, proof of the lemma follows as
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2
.
∎

Concluding proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough so that
\phi=\delta
and
|\nabla_{H}\phi|=1
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
. Invoking (
6.1
) once again, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\phi(B^{\alpha}(s))\leqslant 0\right)dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(|\nabla_{H}\phi(x)|\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\geqslant\eta\phi(x)\right)dx

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(M\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B^{\alpha}(s),e)^{1+j}\geqslant(1-\eta)\frac{\phi(x)}{k-1}\right)dx

\displaystyle=:I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t),

where
M
is defined in (
6.2
). Since
|\nabla_{H}\phi|=1
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, by coarea formula in Lemma
2.4
we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=c(\mathbb{G})\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{\phi^{-1}(r)}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\geqslant\eta r\right)d\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(x)dr

\displaystyle\leqslant c(\mathbb{G})\sup_{0\leqslant r\leqslant\varepsilon}\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(\phi^{-1}(r))\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\wedge\varepsilon\right].

As
\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\overline{B}^{\alpha}_{1}(t)\wedge\varepsilon\right]/\mu_{\alpha}(t)=1
, we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{I_{1}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\leqslant\frac{c(\mathbb{G})}{\eta}\sup_{0\leqslant r\leqslant\varepsilon}\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(\phi^{-1}(r)).

On the other hand, using coarea formula once again for
I_{2}(t)
and following same argument as in
I_{1}(t)
we get

\displaystyle I_{2}(t)\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\eta}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[M\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(B^{\alpha}(s),e)^{1+j}\wedge\frac{\varepsilon}{k-1}\right].

By Lemma
4.5
,
\limsup_{t\to 0}I_{2}(t)/\mu_{\alpha}(t)=0
for every
\eta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
, which further implies that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Omega|-Q^{(\alpha)}_{\Omega}(t)}{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}\leqslant\frac{c(\mathbb{G})}{\eta}\sup_{0\leqslant r\leqslant\varepsilon}\mathcal{S}^{Q-1}_{\infty}(\phi^{-1}(r)).

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\eta\to 1
and using (
2.6
), Lemma
2.1
combined with Remark
6.2
, we conclude the proof of the theorem.
∎

Appendix A
Some estimates related to
\alpha/2
-stable subordinators on
\mathbb{R}

Let
S^{\alpha}=(S^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the
\alpha/2
-stable subordinator defined in (
3.1
). Then,
S^{\alpha}
is
\frac{\alpha}{2}
-self-similar, that is,

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}_{t}\overset{d}{=}t^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}S^{\alpha}_{1}\quad\text{for all $t>0$}.

From
[
23
, Proposition 28.3]
it is known that
S^{\alpha}_{t}
is an absolutely continuous random variable for each
t>0
. Denoting the density of
S^{\alpha}_{t}
by
\eta^{\alpha}_{t}
, we know from
[
25
]
that

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha}_{1}(u)\sim 2\pi\Gamma(1+\frac{\alpha}{2})\sin(\frac{\pi\alpha}{4})u^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\quad\text{as }u\to\infty,

and in particular by
[
7
, p. 97]
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha}_{1}(u)\leqslant C\min\{1,u^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\}.

Lemma A.1
.

For any
0<\alpha<2
and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{(S^{\alpha}_{t})^{\theta_{2}}}\right)\right]\leqslant C\min\{1,tr^{-\frac{\alpha\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}}\}.

Proof.

From (
A.1
) we obtain for all
r>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{(S^{\alpha}_{t})^{\theta_{2}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{t^{\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha}}(S^{\alpha}_{1})^{\theta_{2}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{t^{\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha}}u^{\theta_{2}}}\right)u^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}du.

With the change of variable
\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{t^{\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha}}u^{\theta_{2}}}\mapsto v
, the above integral simplifies to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{u^{\theta_{2}}}\right)u^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}du=\frac{tr^{-\frac{\alpha\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}}}{\theta_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-v}v^{\frac{\alpha}{2\theta_{2}}-1}dv.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
1\leqslant\alpha<2
. Then, for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
satisfying
2\theta_{2}>\theta_{1}
, and
R>0
, we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\mu_{\alpha}(t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{(S^{\alpha}_{t})^{\theta_{2}}}\right)\right)dr

\displaystyle=0,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\mu_{\alpha}(t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{R}r^{\kappa}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{S^{\alpha}_{t}}\right)\right]dr

\displaystyle=0

where
\mu_{\alpha}
is defined in (
1.2
).

Proof.

Invoking Lemma
A.1
together with the change of variable
rt^{\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha\theta_{1}}}\mapsto v
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{(S^{\alpha}_{t})^{\theta_{2}}}\right)\right]dr\leqslant Ct^{\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha\theta_{1}}}\int_{0}^{Rt^{-\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha\theta_{1}}}}\min\{1,v^{-\frac{\alpha\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}}\}dr.

The above estimate implies that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{r^{\theta_{1}}}{(S^{\alpha}_{t})^{\theta_{2}}}\right)\right]dr=\begin{cases}\mathrm{O}(t^{\frac{2\theta_{2}}{\alpha\theta_{1}}})&\mbox{if $\frac{\alpha\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}>1$},\\
\mathrm{O}(t\log(1/t))&\mbox{if $\frac{\alpha\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}=1$},\\
\mathrm{O}(t)&\mbox{if $\frac{\alpha\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}<1$}.\end{cases}

This proves (
A.2
) as
2\theta_{2}>\theta_{1}
. Using Lemma
A.1
with
\theta_{1}=2,\theta_{2}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}r^{\kappa}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{S^{\alpha}_{t}}\right)\right]dr

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{0}^{R}r^{\kappa}\min\{1,tr^{-\alpha}\}dr

\displaystyle=Ct^{\frac{\kappa+1}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{Rt^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}v^{\kappa}\min\{1,v^{-\alpha}\}dv

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{O}(t^{\frac{\kappa+1}{\alpha}})&\mbox{if $\kappa-\alpha<-1$},\\
\mathrm{O}(t\log(1/t))&\mbox{if $\kappa-\alpha=-1$},\\
\mathrm{O}(t)&\mbox{if $\kappa-\alpha>-1$}.\end{cases}

Since
\kappa>0
, the above upper bound implies (
A.3
). This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎
