Korea-Japan Biophysics Collaboration: A journey through microbial rhodopsin research
Authors: Jae-Hyun Park
Published: 2025

Introduction

When I first became involved in the collaboration between Korean and Japanese research groups studying chloride ion pumping rhodopsin, I was struck by the challenges of collecting diffraction data from microbial rhodopsin protein crystals, delivering light activation to rhodopsin crystals to observe light-induced structural changes, and interpreting the resulting structural data. These critical problems were successfully resolved through collaborative research with the Japanese and the other international research group. The story I want to share is not merely about two papers published in prestigious journals [1,2]. Rather, it is about how a sustained collaboration between research groups at Yonsei University in Korea and Yokohama City University in Japan along with partners across Asia and America has advanced our understanding of how light-driven microbial rhodopsin, NM-R3, works at the most fundamental level. As a graduate student at the time, I found this collaboration particularly instructive. It demonstrates how geographic proximity can facilitate frequent exchanges, how complementary technical strengths can overcome individual limitations, and how personal relationships forged through science can solve the scientific challenges.

The collaboration story

The collaboration began, as many do, with a shared scientific interest and a fortunate meeting of minds. The Yonsei group, led by Professor Weontae Lee, had been working on the biochemical characterization and crystallization of NM-R3, a newly discovered chloride-pumping rhodopsin from the marine flavobacterium Nonlabens marinus S1-08T [3]. Meanwhile, the Yokohama City University group, under Professor Sam-Yong Park, had deep expertise in X-ray crystallography and structural analysis of membrane proteins. What made this partnership particularly powerful was the division of labor that emerged from each group’s strengths. The Korean team excelled at protein expression, purification, and lipidic cubic phase (LCP) crystallization which is a delicate procedure for forming membrane protein crystals suitable for X-ray analysis (Fig. 1). They also conducted functional assays, the crucial measurements of chloride transport activity that validated what the protein was actually doing in living microbes. The Japanese team brought multiple complementary capabilities that were essential to reveal the light induced activation mechanism of NM-R3. Professor Park’s group provided sophisticated expertise in data collection and structure refinement, with extensive experience at synchrotron facilities, particularly the NW-12A beamline at Photon Factory (Fig. 2a). Their mastery of crystallographic computing and difference density map analysis was critical for extracting maximum information from each dataset and visualizing subtle intermediate states in the photocycle. Equally important was the spectroscopic expertise brought by Professor Naoya Shibayama from Jichi Medical University. His expertise in spectroscopy techniques provided the essential bridge between structure and function (Fig. 2b). Using irradiation of 532 nm green laser pulses, he could directly induce the NM-R3 activation in the single crystal used for X-ray diffraction, verifying that the structural features of intermediate states captured by crystallography corresponded to functional photocycle states. This brilliant spectroscopic approach showing that the protein’s response to light in crystals matched its behavior in solution was essential for confident interpretation of the structural data.

The collaboration extended beyond these core groups. The connection to Marius Schmidt at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee and Haiguang Liu at the Beijing Computational Science Research Center, experts in time-resolved crystallography, free electron lasers, and serial crystallography, proved instrumental when the project evolved to include X-ray free electron laser (XFEL) experiments. The LCLS team at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory became partners in making the femtosecond experiments possible (Fig. 2c and d). Although these collaborations with American partners were essential for the XFEL experiments, the substantial distance requiring about 13 hours flight and significant travel costs limited the frequency of in person meeting. This made the regional Korea-Japan partnership even more valuable. The geographic proximity between Seoul and Yokohama, roughly 1,200 km, about 2 hours flight, facilitated in person meetings even with modest travel budgets. Group members could visit each other’s labs for much longer periods to learn specific techniques or work together on challenging datasets. As a testament to this accessibility, Dr. Ji-Hye Yun and I, from the Korean research team, visited Yokohama City University nearly every month during 2017. These in person meetings built trust and enabled spontaneous scientific discussions that don’t happen as easily through email or online discussions. This created an international yet regionally anchored research team with all the expertise needed to tackle ambitious questions about microbial rhodopsin mechanism.

Conclusion

The Korea-Japan collaboration on NM-R3 research stands as an example of how international partnerships can advance science in ways that would be difficult or impossible for individual groups working alone. By combining complementary expertise, sharing access to sophisticated facilities, and building genuine relationships based on mutual respect, the teams have produced work that is greater than the sum of its parts. As Asia’s prominence in science continues to grow, collaborations like this will become increasingly important. They show what’s possible when neighboring countries choose cooperation over competition, when institutions support rather than hinder international partnerships, and when individual scientists prioritize collective progress over individual credit. I hope this collaboration inspires others. There are countless pairs of laboratories around the world with complementary expertise and shared interests who could benefit from closer partnership. The logistical, financial, and cultural barriers to international collaboration are real but not insuperable. And the rewards, both scientific and personal, make the effort worthwhile.
