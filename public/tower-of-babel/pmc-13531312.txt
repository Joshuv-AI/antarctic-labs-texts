Dispensing of actionable pharmacogenetic drugs among adults receiving cardiovascular disease medications in the Northern Netherlands: A serial cross‐sectional study
Authors: Zhuolin Zhang, Guiling Zhou, Jens H J Bos, Eelko Hak, Talitha Feenstra, Frank Klont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe extent of pharmacogenetic (PGx) drug dispensing among Dutch adults receiving medications for cardiovascular disease (CVD) is unknown. Using the University of Groningen IADB.nl pharmacy database, we performed a serial cross‐sectional study (2019–2023) to estimate the annual prevalence of PGx drug dispensing and annual rates of initiation. We also identified the most dispensed PGx drug classes, most frequently associated genes, and the proportion of individuals with potential opportunities for reuse of PGx testing results. Adults on CVD medication treatment were defined as those with ≥2 prescriptions for the same CVD medication (ATC classes B01, C01, C03, C07–C10) within 180 days in a calendar year. PGx drugs were defined as drugs with actionable drug–gene interactions according to international CPIC or national DPWG guidelines. The annual cohort size ranged from 47,602 in 2019 to 41,846 in 2023 (mean age ~70 years; ~48% male). The prevalence of ≥1 PGx drug dispensing (~90%) and multiple (≥2) PGx drug dispensing (~68%) remained stable across 5 years, both increasing significantly with age. CVD‐related PGx drugs were most common (75%), followed by proton pump inhibitors (55%). In 2023, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and SLCO1B1‐associated drugs had most users (60%, 53%, and 51%). Among those receiving drugs associated with CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and SLCO1B1, 24%, 23%, and 4% would have opportunities for reuse of testing results with single‐gene testing, and 66%, 65%, and 83% with panel testing. In conclusion, PGx drug dispensing was highly prevalent among people receiving CVD medications, warranting further research into the (cost‐)effectiveness of PGx testing.

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) medications are among the most commonly dispensed pharmacogenetic (PGx) drugs, and polypharmacy is common in CVD patients. However, the extent of PGx drug dispensing among adults receiving long‐term medications for CVD in the Netherlands is unknown.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study explored PGx drug dispensing among adults receiving CVD medications in the Northern Netherlands, including the annual prevalence and annual rates of initiation, the most dispensed PGx drug classes, the most frequently associated genes, and the proportion of patients with opportunities for downstream reuse of PGx testing results for single‐gene and panel testing.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

This study provides an overview of real‐world dispensing of PGx drugs among adults receiving long‐term CVD medications. It demonstrates that PGx drug dispensing was highly prevalent, particularly among elderly patients. CVD‐related PGx drugs and proton pump inhibitors represent the most commonly dispensed PGx drug classes. The most frequently involved genes were CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and SLCO1B1. Importantly, the study identifies the proportion of patients who could potentially reuse PGx testing results for future prescriptions when dispensed multiple PGx drugs.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

The study demonstrated widespread real‐world dispensing of actionable PGx drugs among adults receiving long‐term CVD medications. It highlighted that a substantial proportion of patients would potentially reuse PGx testing results. Future studies may build on this to assess the clinical and economic value of PGx testing, while considering multiple PGx drug dispensing and the prevalence of clinically relevant pharmacogenetic variants to better inform decision‐making for CVD management.

Given the substantial clinical and economic burden of cardiovascular disease (CVD), 1 , 2 , 3 effective pharmacotherapy for treatment and prevention is critical. The effectiveness of CVD‐related pharmacotherapy varies across individuals. One possible explanation for this variation is genetic differences among individuals, which can influence both drug metabolism (pharmacokinetics) and drug response (pharmacodynamics), 4 forming the basis of pharmacogenetics (PGx). For example, patients exhibit considerable variability in their response to clopidogrel and warfarin due to genetic variation in drug‐metabolizing enzymes, such as Cytochrome P450 2C19 (CYP2C19) for clopidogrel 5 and Cytochrome P450 2C9 (CYP2C9) for warfarin, as well as in drug targets, including vitamin K epoxide reductase complex 1 (VKORC1) for warfarin. 6 Evidence on drug–gene interactions (DGIs) has been identified through pharmacogenomic studies over the past decades. Several organizations, such as the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) and the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG), 7 have developed guidelines that provide therapeutic recommendations. For brevity, we refer to drugs with actionable DGIs according to the evidence and recommendations presented in PGx guidelines as PGx drugs. 8 , 9

CVD medications themselves are among the most commonly prescribed classes of PGx drugs, 10 and at least 10 drug–gene pairs have been described in established guidelines. 8 , 9 An example is clopidogrel. Among patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, meta‐analyses have shown that carriers of CYP2C19 loss‐of‐function (LOF) alleles treated with clopidogrel have a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) compared with non‐carriers. 11 , 12 CYP2C19 genotype‐guided treatment would involve prescribing an alternative therapy, such as ticagrelor, to individuals identified as CYP2C19 LOF carriers. A meta‐analysis reported that this strategy was associated with an approximately 40% reduction in the risk of MACE among patients with coronary artery disease. 13 This example underscores the importance of PGx testing to guide evidence‐based drug selection.

The potential health and economic benefits of PGx testing may vary depending on the testing strategy. Single‐gene testing focuses on one specific gene relevant to a particular drug, while panel testing simultaneously examines multiple genes that may influence the response to a range of medications. 14 Regardless of the strategy employed, an often‐overlooked health and economic advantage is the downstream benefits of PGx testing. For example, CYP2C19 genetic variants not only affect clopidogrel response but also are relevant for other CYP2C19‐associated drugs, including some proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). 15 Once tested, the healthcare providers could therefore reuse the PGx testing results to guide future prescriptions, possibly offering patients additional benefit when they later on start using any other drug associated with the tested gene(s). This is particularly relevant for CVD patients, who often have multiple comorbidities, 16 leading to polypharmacy 17 and thereby an increased likelihood of receiving multiple PGx drugs. Additionally, such multimorbidity and polypharmacy were associated with a higher likelihood of DGIs, 18 which may result in treatment modifications such as discontinuation, drug switching, dose adjustment, and even MACE. 19 , 20 These findings highlight the importance of understanding the pattern of multiple PGx drug use, including the concurrent and subsequent use of two or more PGx drugs. Real‐world data on PGx drug use patterns is therefore needed to inform decision‐making regarding the implementation of PGx testing.

In this study, we aimed to estimate the annual prevalence of PGx drug dispensing and annual rates of new PGx drug dispensing among adult patients on CVD medications by calendar year in the period 2019–2023. In addition, we described the most dispensed PGx drug classes and the most frequently associated genes. Furthermore, we assessed the potential downstream benefits by estimating the proportion of patients who would have the opportunities for reuse of PGx testing results for single‐gene and panel testing, as they use multiple medications for which PGx testing could be relevant and action could be taken if the patients carry relevant genetic variants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source, study design, and study population

A serial cross‐sectional study was performed using the University of Groningen IADB.nl drug dispensing database from 2019 to 2023. 21 This database contains dispensing data from 1994 onwards and includes detailed patient‐level information such as date of birth, sex, date of the first prescription (entry date), date of the last prescription (exit date), and dispensing records such as the date of dispensing and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes. So far, the database has collected data from 155 community pharmacies in the Northern Netherlands, covering approximately 1.56 million individuals, 22 representing 9% of the Dutch population. Patient information for research is fully anonymized; hence, ethical approval is not required for this study. 23

In this study, we focused on adults (aged 18 years or older) on CVD medication treatment, defined as individuals who received at least two dispensings for the same drug to treat or prevent CVD (ATC classes B01, C01, C03, C07–C10) within 180 days during a calendar year. For this serial cross‐sectional study, patients were eligible for inclusion in any year if they met the study criteria. Therefore, a patient could be included in multiple study years or appear in only one study year.

Definitions of PGx drugs

Actionable PGx drugs were identified based on guidelines from the CPIC and the DPWG, as indexed on the PharmGKB website, accessed on December 20, 2024. 24 PGx drugs without an ATC code (e.g., toluidine blue and methylene blue) and topical ATC codes of drugs with multiple formulations (e.g., gentamicin: S01AA11, S02AA14, S03AA06) were excluded from the analysis. Where applicable, ATC codes for combination products were supplemented. In total, 102 actionable PGx drugs were included in the analysis (see Figure S1 ). PGx drug dispensing was defined as the dispensing of at least one actionable PGx drug, whereas multiple PGx drug dispensing was defined as the dispensing of two or more distinct actionable PGx drugs in a calendar year, either concurrently or at different times. The actionable PGx drug list and corresponding ATC codes are shown in Table S1 .

Outcomes of interest

Prevalence of (multiple) PGx drug dispensing

The annual prevalence of PGx drug dispensing was calculated as the proportion of patients receiving at least one actionable PGx drug among the study population dispensed with CVD medications in the same calendar year. Specifically, the numerator was defined as the number of unique patients dispensed with any actionable PGx drug within a given year, whereas the denominator was the total number of patients receiving at least two dispensings of the same CVD medication within 180 days during that year. Regarding multiple PGx drug dispensing, prevalence was defined as the proportion of patients receiving CVD medications who were dispensed two or more distinct actionable PGx drugs in the same calendar year.

Initiation of (multiple) PGx drug dispensing

For each study year, new PGx drug dispensing was defined as receiving a PGx drug after at least 12 months without any prior PGx drugs. An annual rate was calculated as the number of patients who newly initiated PGx drugs in a calendar year, divided by the total person‐years of CVD medication users in that year. The denominator was the total follow‐up time for each individual within a calendar year, calculated from the later of January 1 or the entry date to the earlier of December 31 or the exit date. For example, for a patient included in 2020 with an entry date of 2018‐06‐06 and an exit date of 2020‐11‐16, the follow‐up time contributed to the year 2020 was calculated as the period from 2020‐01‐01 to 2020‐11‐16. This rate was reported as per 10,000 person‐years. For multiple PGx drug dispensing, the numerator was the number of patients who newly initiated at least two distinct PGx drugs in a single calendar year.

Most dispensed PGx drug class

The most dispensed PGx drug classes were determined by the percentage of patients who were dispensed each PGx drug class in each calendar year. Each patient was counted only once, regardless of multiple prescriptions within the same PGx drug class. Seven therapeutic areas were used to categorize PGx drugs: CVD, gastrointestinal drugs, infectious diseases, analgesics or anesthetics, psychiatry or neurology, oncology, and others. The full list of PGx drugs and their corresponding classifications can be found in Table S1 .

Genes most frequently associated with PGx drug dispensing and potential opportunities for reuse of PGx testing results in 2023

The genes most frequently associated with PGx drug dispensing were determined based on the number of patients dispensed PGx drugs associated with a certain gene in 2023. If a patient was dispensed one or more PGx drugs associated with the same gene, they were counted only once. This analysis was conducted only for the year 2023, since it reflected the most recent and relevant dispensing information. The full list of PGx drugs and corresponding genes is provided in Table S1 .

After identifying the main genes, we calculated the percentage of patients dispensed multiple (≥2) PGx drugs associated with each gene as well as the percentage of patients dispensed multiple PGx drugs across different genes. The numerator was the number of patients dispensed multiple PGx drugs for a given gene or across different genes, respectively. The denominator for both calculations was the number of patients dispensed certain gene‐associated PGx drugs.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by restricting the analysis to PGx drugs with CPIC level A evidence. The CPIC evidence level for each PGx drug is presented in Table S1 . In total, 78 CPIC level A drugs were included in this analysis. However, in addition to the evidence level, specific clinical contexts, such as indications, target populations addressed by guidelines, and drug dosages, may also need to be considered when determining whether the dispensing of a PGx drug should be considered actionable. For example, although nitrofurantoin is classified as a CPIC Level A PGx drug, its clinical relevance as a PGx drug may vary across populations because of variation in the prevalence of G6PD deficiency. 25 , 26 Therefore, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding nitrofurantoin from the CPIC Level A PGx drug list, given that G6PD deficiency is relatively low prevalence in Dutch populations. 27

In a third sensitivity analysis, we applied longer washout periods of 24 months and 60 months to assess the impact of washout period definition on the identification of patients initiating PGx drugs.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed for each calendar year and stratified by sex and age. Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were summarized using proportion. Age was categorized into four groups: under 40 years (<40), 40–64 years, 65–79 years, and 80 years or older (≥80).

Patient counts and annual person‐years were calculated using MySQL, and statistical tests were conducted using R (version 4.4.3). Regarding annual overall prevalence estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Wilson method. For annual overall initiation rate estimates, 95% CIs were calculated based on the Poisson distribution. Log‐binomial regression of the prevalence of (multiple) PGx drug dispensing on calendar year was used to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs). Poisson regression of the number of initiators of (multiple) PGx drugs on calendar year, with a log link and the logarithm of person‐years as an offset, was used to estimate initiation rate ratios (IRRs). To assess changes between the first and last calendar years, PRs and IRRs comparing 2023 with the reference year (2019) were presented. Separate log‐binomial and Poisson regression models were fitted for each study year to evaluate differences by sex and age group, estimating PRs and IRRs, respectively. Sex (male as reference) and age group (<40 years for prevalence and < 65 years for initiation as reference groups) were included as covariates. A P‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study population and PGx drugs identified in IADB.nl

The total number of patients included from 2019 to 2023 was 47,602, 47,220, 46,056, 43,829, and 41,846, respectively, with the corresponding total person‐years for each year varying from 46,000 to 40,000 (Table S2 ).

The mean age (±SD) of the included patients from 2019 to 2023 was 70.3 years with an SD of 13.7 (Table S2 ). The sex distribution remained stable across all years, with 47.9% of patients being male (Table S2 ).

A total of 140 CVD medications were used to define the study population, of which 17 were also classified as PGx drugs (see Table S3 ). Among the 102 PGx drugs which we selected, 33 were consistently missing in the dataset from 2019 to 2023. Most of these are typically used in hospital settings and hence were not captured in this community pharmacy database, which are indicated with asterisks in Table S1 .

Prevalence of (multiple) PGx drug dispensing

The annual prevalence of ≥1 PGx drug dispensing is presented in Figure 1 , which shows a slight decline from 90.3% (95% CI: 90.0–90.5%) in 2019 to 89.4% (95% CI: 89.1–89.6%) in 2023 (Table S4 ). The prevalence of PGx drug dispensing in 2023 was slightly lower than in 2019 (PR = 0.990, 95% CI 0.986–0.994). The magnitude of the change was minimal, and the overall prevalence was stable.

Annual prevalence of (multiple) pharmacogenetic drug dispensing among patients receiving cardiovascular disease medications.

The number of patients receiving multiple PGx drugs (≥2) decreased from 32,994 in 2019 to 28,468 in 2023, and the corresponding prevalence dropped slightly from 69.3% (95% CI: 68.9%–69.7%) to 68.0% (95% CI: 67.6%–68.5%) (Table S4 ). There was a slight reduction in 2023 compared with 2019 (PR = 0.982, 95% CI 0.973–0.990), although the absolute change was small.

Female patients showed slightly higher prevalence of PGx drug dispensing compared with males across all study years, both for at least one PGx drug (Male: 89.5%, Female: 89.8%) and for multiple PGx drugs (Male: 67.6%, Female: 68.8%), but differences were small and not statistically significant in any study year. Detailed information is provided in Tables S5 , S6 and Figure S2 .

The prevalence of ≥1 PGx drug dispensing was 74.1% among patients aged under 40, 85.0% among those aged 40–64, and 91.2% and 93.5% among patients aged 65–79 and ≥80, respectively. This increase with age was statistically significant in all study years. The prevalence of multiple PGx drug dispensing also significantly increased with age in all study years, and was 45.3% among patients under 40, 59.4% among those aged 40–64, 70.7% among those aged 65–79, and 75.6% among those ≥80. The detailed information is presented in Tables S5 , S6 and Figure S3 .

Initiation of (multiple) PGx drug dispensing

The number of patients newly starting one or more PGx drugs was relatively low. Rates of new PGx drug dispensing decreased from 149.1 (95% CI: 138.6–161.2) to 108.9 (95% CI: 98.9–119.6) per 10,000 person‐years. Similarly, the rate of patients initiating multiple PGx drugs decreased from 30.4 (95% CI: 25.6–35.9) to 23.0 (95% CI: 18.6–28.2) per 10,000 person‐years (Table S7 ). Compared with 2019, the initiation of ≥1 PGx drug and multiple PGx drugs decreased in 2023, with IRRs of 0.728 (95% CI: 0.645–0.820) and 0.757 (95% CI: 0.582–0.985), respectively. The annual rates of new PGx drug dispensing are presented in Figure 2 .

Annual rates of new (multiple) pharmacogenetic drug dispensing among patients receiving cardiovascular disease medications.

Male patients showed slightly higher rates of new PGx drug dispensing than female patients, both for ≥1 PGx drug dispensing and for multiple PGx drug dispensing. Statistically significant differences between sexes were observed for ≥1 initial PGx drug dispensing. For multiple PGx drug dispensing, significant differences were found in all years except 2020 (see Tables S8 , S9 ; Figure S4 ).

Younger adults (aged <40 and 40–64 years) had higher rates of initial dispensing of PGx drug than older adults (aged 65–79 and ≥80 years), for both ≥1 PGx drug dispensing and multiple PGx drug dispensing. Statistically significant differences existed in rates of initial dispensing of ≥1 PGx drug among the four age groups (Table S8 ). For multiple initial PGx drug dispensing, no statistically significant differences by age were observed in most years, except in 2019 (≥80 vs. <65) and 2021 (65–79 vs. <65) (Table S9 ). The detailed information is also presented in Figure S5 .

Most dispensed PGx drug class

The top three dispensed PGx drug classes were CVD‐related PGx drugs, gastrointestinal drugs, and psychiatric or neurology medications. The corresponding percentages of patients receiving dispensings in each class remained stable over the 5‐year period, at 74.7%, 54.9%, and 16.6%, respectively. Similar findings were obtained for the top two drug classes for initiation of PGx drugs, being CVD‐related and gastrointestinal PGx drugs. Rates for CVD‐related PGx drugs declined from 78.8 per 10,000 person‐years in 2019 to 59.1 in 2023. Gastrointestinal drugs declined from 51.9 to 36.5 from 2019 to 2023. The remaining drug classes showed lower rates (see Figures 3 and 4 ).

Sensitivity analysis

When the analysis was restricted to PGx drugs with CPIC Level A evidence, the prevalence of PGx drug dispensing was 81.4%, and the prevalence of dispensing of multiple PGx drugs was 52.0% across the study years (Table S11 ). Excluding nitrofurantoin further reduced these estimates to 80.0% and 49.1%, respectively.

When a 24‐month washout period was applied to identify first‐time PGx drug dispensing, the initiation rate of PGx drugs decreased from 136.8 to 99.1 per 10,000 person‐years, while the initiation rate of multiple PGx drugs decreased from 28.2 to 19.8 per 10,000 person‐years from 2019 to 2023. With a 60‐month washout period, the initiation rate of PGx drugs decreased from 114.0 to 79.6 per 10,000 person‐years, and the initiation rate of multiple PGx drugs decreased from 21.9 to 16.5 per 10,000 person‐years over the same period. As expected, the initiation rates were both slightly lower than those obtained using the 12‐month washout period (Figure S6 ). The results were consistent across different washout definitions, suggesting that the findings were robust to the choice of washout period.

DISCUSSION

The vast majority of patients receiving CVD medications from community pharmacies in the Northern Netherlands were dispensed at least one PGx drug, and nearly 70% were dispensed two or more PGx drugs annually between 2019 and 2023. Older individuals showed a higher prevalence of PGx drug dispensing, whereas younger individuals were more likely to initiate PGx drugs, which may inform testing strategies. CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and SLCO1B1 were the most frequently associated genes, suggesting they should be prioritized in PGx testing for CVD management.

CVD‐related PGx drugs were the most frequently dispensed class, which was expected given that our study population consisted of patients receiving CVD medications. More interestingly, gastrointestinal drugs, particularly PPIs, ranked second, with more than half of the population receiving PGx drugs. Although PPIs are classified as CPIC level A drugs, indicating there is sufficient evidence for using genetic information to guide dosing, 15 , 28 the recommendations are primarily driven by efficacy considerations rather than toxicity concerns. For example, CPIC recommends increased doses for CYP2C19 normal, rapid, and ultrarapid metabolizers, who may experience therapeutic failure. However, PPIs generally have a wide therapeutic window, and genetic variation is unlikely to result in significant adverse events. This suggests that the clinical value of PGx testing for PPIs may be limited when considered in isolation. However, the clinical relevance of PGx testing for PPIs should be considered in the broader context of co‐prescribed medications in CVD patients. As shown by our study, many CVD patients used CVD‐related PGx drugs and PPIs in the same year, which would suggest a potential for concomitant use, particularly among patients receiving clopidogrel. Such concomitant use is clinically relevant for these patients. Further studies are needed to evaluate the extent of the actual concomitant use and prevalence of the relevant genetic variants. In clinical practice, CYP2C19 testing is performed primarily for antiplatelet therapy with clopidogrel, but the test result may subsequently be used to inform PPI dosing as a secondary application. Such reuse of PGx testing results may provide comprehensive CVD management, for example, by informing both antiplatelet therapy and PPI use, thereby potentially reducing cardiovascular events and bleeding risk in patients with cardio‐ and cerebrovascular disease.

Our results were consistent with previous research in the Dutch general population, which found higher PGx drug use among females and the elderly. 10 CYP2D6, SLCO1B1, and CYP2C19 were also the top three genes in terms of estimated exposure to relevant PGx drugs among users carrying actionable genotypes in that study. It further showed that CVD‐related PGx drugs, PPIs, and psychiatric/neurological medications were the main dispensed PGx drug classes. These consistent patterns of PGx drug dispensing in patients receiving CVD medications and in the general population are notable, particularly given the multimorbidity and polypharmacy in the CVD population.

While previous studies primarily reported prevalence, 29 , 30 , 31 few studies reported the rate of PGx drug initiation. 32 It is the initiation of PGx drug that offers insight into estimating the potential size of preemptive PGx testing in clinical practice. In our study, overall initiation rates were low and fluctuated over 5 years. One possible explanation for the low initiation rates is that patients in our study population, who were long‐term users of CVD‐related medications, may have already been prescribed the relevant PGx drugs (e.g., statins). The observed decrease in initiation rates is consistent with our methodological approach of focusing on first‐time starters. The decline in initiation rates observed from 2019 to 2020 might be partly explained by disruptions in healthcare use during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Sensitivity analyses using 24‐ and 60‐month washout periods showed a similar decline from 2019 to 2020. This explanation is supported by a previous study in the Netherlands reporting a decline in first‐time dispensing of analgesic drugs during this period. 33

In this serial cross‐sectional design, each calendar year may include both newly treated patients and those continuing treatment from the previous year. A decreasing absolute number of included patients was observed over the years in our study population. This decline was seen over all included ATC groups (Table S12 ). The population size of the IADB.nl dispensing database increased between 2019 and 2023 (by 5%). This increase would be expected to lead to higher, rather than lower, numbers of patients in the study population. One possible explanation is a decline in the prevalence of common cardiovascular conditions in the Netherlands from 2019 to 2023, including hypertension, coronary heart disease, and heart failure, which dropped from 166.3 to 158.5, 47.3 to 44.4, and 14.8 to 14.0 per 1,000 individuals, respectively. 34 , 35 , 36 This may partly be reflected in our observed reduction of patients receiving medication to prevent or treat CVD.

Females had a higher prevalence of PGx drug dispensing, while males showed a higher rate of initiation across the 5‐year study period. Females had a slightly higher mean age than males across all study years (Table S2 ). Previous evidence has shown that increasing age is associated with a modest increase in medication use, which may in turn slightly increase the likelihood of receiving PGx drugs. 18 Therefore, the slightly higher prevalence observed among females may be partially explained by their older average age. Consistently, after adjustment for age group, the observed differences were not statistically significant in any study year, suggesting that age may partly contribute to the observed sex differences.

Our study showed that initiation rates were lower among older compared with younger individuals, who typically have less prior medication use 37 and are therefore more likely to be initiating PGx drugs during the study period. Conversely, among older individuals, the prevalence of PGx drug dispensing and multiple PGx drug dispensing was higher than in young individuals. Elderly individuals are more likely to be exposed to polypharmacy, leading to multiple PGx drug dispensing. Notably, multiple use also increases the risk of drug–drug‐gene interactions, 38 , 39 which can lead to phenoconversion, defined as the conversion of a genotype‐predicted phenotype into a different observed phenotype due to concomitant exposure to CYP enzyme inhibitors or inducers, which modify enzyme activity, or due to comorbidities (e.g., inflammation). A substantial proportion of older adults are exposed to at least one CYP enzyme inhibitor or inducer, highlighting the potential for clinically meaningful phenoconversion among elderly individuals with polypharmacy. 40 Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of PGx testing‐guided therapy in patients with multiple PGx drugs and suggest the need for PGx testing strategies, including panel testing and single‐gene testing. The PREPARE study has demonstrated that panel testing can reduce adverse drug reactions 41 and panel testing has the potential to provide more comprehensive coverage, as patients can have actionable DGIs with more than one gene. 42 Although single‐gene testing may be appropriate when a specific drug‐gene pair is clinically relevant, it provides less comprehensive coverage than panel testing. However, panel testing may also lead to potential overuse of resources, since not all patients will require certain medications. Furthermore, our findings indicate that a portion of patients could have opportunities for reuse of PGx testing results via single‐gene testing of CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and SLCO1B1, respectively. Therefore, real‐world studies on actual drug use are still needed to further evaluate the clinical utility of different PGx testing strategies.

Table 2 shows the proportions of patients dispensed PGx drugs associated with the same and multiple genes among patients receiving CVD medications in 2023. The proportion of patients receiving multiple PGx drugs associated with the same gene may be interpreted as an upper bound for the potential opportunities for reuse of single‐gene testing results. The difference between the two percentages represents the proportion of patients receiving PGx drugs associated with different genes, indicating an upper bound for the potential opportunities for reuse of panel testing results beyond the specified gene of interest. However, these estimates are based on dispensing data and not all patients receiving PGx drugs carry relevant genetic variants. Therefore, the estimated opportunity for reuse of PGx testing results should be interpreted as potential opportunities for reuse of testing results rather than measures of clinical benefit. On the other hand, our analysis was limited to 1 year. Once tested, PGx testing results can be reused over a lifetime, and hence estimated opportunities for reuse of PGx testing may be conservative.

Besides utility, implementing PGx testing strategies also requires consideration of cost, which was one of the concerns identified by patients. 43 Given the large potential target population for CVD prevention and treatment, integrating PGx testing into clinical practice requires careful decision‐making, in which economic evaluation and budget impact analysis could play an important role. A previous systematic review showed that the majority of cost‐effectiveness analyses of PGx testing‐guided treatment for CVD, including CYP2C19 genotype‐guided antiplatelet treatment, concluded that testing was cost‐effective compared with universal pharmacotherapy without genetic information. 44 Regarding panel testing including CYP2C19, SLCO1B1, and VKORC1/CYP2C9, published cost‐effectiveness studies suggested that panel testing, particularly preemptive panel testing, 45 was more cost‐effective than single‐gene CYP2C19 testing. 46 All these studies ignored the potential downstream economic impacts of PGx testing. Such downstream benefits should be considered when estimating the impact of PGx testing, particularly its economic impact, since the one‐time cost of genetic testing can be distributed across multiple treatment decisions if the test results are used repeatedly to guide medication therapy. Incorporating downstream clinical effects may further enhance the cost‐effectiveness of both single‐gene testing and panel testing.

This study has several strengths. This is the first study estimating annual initiation rates of PGx drugs in serial cross‐sectional cohorts of patients receiving medications for treatment or prevention of CVD, including the post‐COVID‐19 period. It employed the large and widely researched University Groningen IADB.nl pharmacy database, which provides real‐world patterns of drug dispensing. By examining both prevalence and new users, our study gave a detailed overview of PGx drug dispensing. Our analysis considered multiple PGx drugs, the main genes, main drug classes, and the proportion of patients with potential opportunities from PGx testing, providing detailed information that is relevant for clinical decision‐making and policy development and may inform future strategies for implementing PGx testing.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the IADB.nl database includes only community pharmacy dispensing records, excluding over‐the‐counter medications or drugs dispensed in hospital settings. Our findings may underestimate the actual use of PGx drugs, as some PGx drugs, such as certain analgesics, may be dispensed in hospital settings rather than in community pharmacies. Notably, 28 of the 33 missing drugs were CPIC Level A, indicating that some clinically highly actionable DGIs may be underestimated. However, most medications used for the chronic management of CVD are dispensed in community pharmacy, and hence the impact of missing intramural dispensings on the estimated number of patients receiving CVD medications is likely small. Yet, the prevalence and initiation rate estimates of PGx drugs in this study are likely to be somewhat underestimated. Second, regarding the PGx drugs identified in this study, specific clinical contexts were not taken into account, which may lead to an overestimation of the actionable PGx drug dispensing. The sensitivity analysis excluding nitrofurantoin resulted only in slightly lower estimates of PGx drug dispensing and is an example of the impact of considering such clinical contexts. Future studies should consider these clinical contexts to provide a more accurate estimate of actionable PGx drug dispensing. Furthermore, evidence levels for drug‐gene pairs may change over time as guidelines are updated. For example, although the IFNL3‐ribavirin, peginterferon alfa‐2a, and peginterferon alfa‐2b drug‐gene pairs were dropped by CPIC in May 2026, they were retained in our analyses because our study period covered 2019–2023. Based on previous CPIC records, these pairs were considered to have Level A evidence. As only around 10 users of these drugs were identified across the study period, their inclusion is unlikely to have affected our findings. Besides, death information was not available in the IADB.nl database. Therefore, patients who were lost to follow‐up were assumed to have exited the database at the time of their last recorded dispensing, although the reason for exit (death or migration) could not be distinguished. However, this is unlikely to materially affect the estimation of person‐years. Moreover, our estimates could only be based on dispensing data, and patients' genotypes were not available. If genotype data were available, these should be carefully combined with detailed dispensing data to account for concomitant medications and clinical characteristic, including comorbidities such as inflammation, on enzyme activity and the potential for phenoconversion. Although the IADB.nl data would allow for the dispensing data, it misses genetic and additional clinical information and therefore phenoconversion was not further analyzed. Future research should therefore focus on exploring datasets that combine genetic data with dispensing records and clinical information to provide a more accurate assessment of drug‐gene interactions and drug–drug‐gene interactions in real‐world settings. Finally, given the large sample size, even minor differences could reach statistical significance. Therefore, statistical significance was interpreted with caution and in conjunction with absolute values, PRs and IRRs. Although some differences reached statistical significance in certain years, the absolute differences were small and are unlikely to be of clinical relevance.

CONCLUSIONS

A large majority of patients on CVD medications were dispensed at least one PGx drug, and the majority were dispensed multiple PGx drugs within a year during the 2019–2023 period, suggesting the potential value for PGx testing‐guided CVD pharmacotherapy. Future studies that evaluate the clinical and economic value of single‐gene and panel testing in CVD management should especially consider the widespread dispensing of multiple PGx drugs and include potential downstream benefits of PGx testing.
