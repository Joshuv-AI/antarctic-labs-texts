The crosstalk of m6A modification and non-coding RNAs in gastric cancer: biomarkers and therapeutic potentials
Authors: Penghui Li, Yuan Xue, Xinyu Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are instrumental in the progression and regulation of gastric cancer (GC), affecting various cellular processes including gene expression, apoptosis, cell proliferation, and metastasis. N6-methyladenosine (m6A), a dynamic and reversible RNA modification, profoundly influences GC by modulating RNA metabolism and function, impacting RNA stability, maturation, and interactions. The crosstalk between non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) and m6A modification is pivotal for the stability, maturation, and functional interactions of ncRNAs. Moreover, m6A-modified ncRNAs are integral in GC by regulating vital pathways involved in tumor growth and metastasis. This interaction is critical for the rapid response of cancer cells to environmental changes, facilitating their adaptation and survival. The differential expression of m6A-modified ncRNAs in GC tissues compared to normal tissues can serve as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, aiding in patient stratification and personalized treatment plans. Targeting m6A modification emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy for GC. Inhibiting m6A “writers” such as METTL3 can diminish the stability of oncogenic ncRNAs, thereby hindering tumor growth and metastasis. Conversely, augmenting m6A modification on tumor-suppressive ncRNAs can enhance their stability and therapeutic efficacy. This review highlights the key roles of m6A modification and ncRNAs in GC, providing valuable insights for future research and potential clinical applications.Keywords: M6A modification, Non-coding RNAs, LncRNA, Gastric cancer, Biomarker

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. It poses a significant health challenge due to its high incidence and mortality rates, particularly in regions such as East Asia, Eastern Europe, and Central Europe [1–4]. Characterized by the malignant proliferation of gastric mucosal cells, GC often advances silently, with symptoms manifesting only in the late stages, complicating early diagnosis. Despite advancements in surgical and targeted therapies, the prognosis for patients with GC remains dismal. The complexity of GC is driven by factors such as diet, Helicobacter pylori infection, and genetic predisposition, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of its molecular mechanisms and treatment strategies [5–7].

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, a reversible chemical alteration commonly found in RNA, plays a critical role in various RNA metabolic processes, including stability, translation, and splicing [8–14]. In cancer, m6A modification influences the RNA lifecycle and cellular signaling pathways essential for tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis [8, 15, 16]. Research indicates that dysregulation of m6A-modifying enzymes (commonly known as “writers,” “erasers,” and “readers”) can promote tumorigenesis and cancer progression [17, 18]. In GC, m6A modification regulates gene expression by affecting RNA splicing, transport, stability, and translation [19–21]. Alterations in m6A sites can lead to changes in the expression of key oncogenes, thereby influencing tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. For example, changes in m6A modification can impact the expression of cell cycle-related genes, affecting the proliferation and apoptosis of GC cells [22, 23]. The m6A modification process is mediated by specific enzyme complexes, including “writers” (such as METTL3), “erasers” (such as FTO), and “readers” (such as YTHDF1). Dysregulation of these enzymes in GC may promote or inhibit tumor progression by altering m6A modification patterns [24–26]. For instance, overexpression of METTL3 in certain patients with GC is associated with poor prognosis, suggesting that m6A modification levels could serve as a biomarker for GC progression [27].

Non-coding RNA (ncRNA) constitutes a class of RNA molecules that do not encode proteins but significantly influence gene expression through mechanisms such as epigenetic regulation, mRNA degradation, and protein activity modulation [28–32]. Key cancer-related ncRNAs include long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), each playing intricate and diverse roles in the initiation, progression, and metastasis of GC [28, 33, 34]. Specifically, lncRNAs and miRNAs have substantial impacts on multiple facets of GC [35–40]. For instance, lncRNA HOTAIR is markedly overexpressed in GC and is linked to increased tumor invasiveness and metastasis by interacting with the miR-1277-5p/COL5A1 axis, thereby promoting tumor development [38]. Conversely, miR-21, a prevalent oncogenic factor, is highly expressed in GC and promotes tumor growth by targeting tumor suppressor genes such as PTEN [39]. The interplay between m6A modification and ncRNAs is particularly essential in GC research [41]. m6A modification influences ncRNA function by modulating their maturation and stability, exemplified by its role in accelerating the maturation of specific miRNAs, thus enhancing their inhibitory effects on target mRNAs. This regulation profoundly affects the expression of genes related to tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis in GC cells. Moreover, m6A modification can modulate the interaction between lncRNAs and RNA-binding proteins, as well as influence the structure and function of circRNAs, thereby impacting the regulation of tumor-related genes. In-depth research into these interactions illuminates the molecular mechanisms underlying GC and offers potential avenues for novel therapeutic strategies. This review aims to elucidate the complex interactions between m6A modification and non-coding RNAs, particularly lncRNAs, miRNAs, and circRNAs, in GC. By examining the dynamic interactions among these molecules, this review seeks to highlight how m6A modification of ncRNAs influences the regulatory mechanisms governing GC pathogenesis and progression.

m6A modification in gastric cancer

Basic mechanisms and functions of M6A modification

The m6A modification, involves the methylation of the nitrogen atom at the 6th position of the adenine base in RNA molecules. This process is catalyzed by a complex methyltransferase assembly, predominantly composed of METTL3 (methyltransferase-like protein 3) and METTL14 (methyltransferase-like protein 14), forming a functional heterodimer [8, 42, 43]. During this methylation, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) serves as the methyl donor, with METTL3 catalyzing the methyl transfer and METTL14 primarily recognizing and binding specific RNA substrates [44]. M6A modification is highly dynamic and reversible, allowing it to swiftly respond to cellular changes and environmental signals, thus regulating the biological functions of RNA. This modification influences various aspects of RNA regulation, including stability, splicing, nuclear export, localization, and translation efficiency [45]. By these mechanisms, m6A impacts the fate and function of RNA, playing a vital role in processes such as cell differentiation, stress responses, circadian rhythm regulation, and disease pathogenesis.

Key enzymes mediating the M6A modification process

The m6A modification process involves three primary types of proteins: writers, erasers, and readers (Fig. 1) [17, 46–48]. Writers, including the core components METTL3 and METTL14, add methyl groups to adenosine residues, forming a stable heterodimer complex that serves as the catalytic core. METTL3 functions as the catalytic unit, while METTL14 is essential for substrate recognition and binding. This complex often interacts with proteins like WTAP (Wilms tumor 1-associated protein), which regulate its localization and activity [49, 50]. Erasers, such as the demethylases FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated protein) and ALKBH5 (AlkB homolog 5), remove methyl groups from m6A-modified RNA, thereby reversing the modification [51]. FTO, the first identified RNA demethylase, is implicated in metabolic processes including energy homeostasis and nucleic acid repair [52, 53]. ALKBH5 influences RNA export and metabolism, significantly impacting RNA stability and gene expression [54, 55]. Readers are proteins that recognize and bind to m6A-modified RNA, facilitating subsequent biological processes. M6A modification exerts widespread effects on various RNAs, including mRNA and ncRNAs, through reader proteins such as the YTH domain family members YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3 [56–58]. These proteins regulate numerous RNA functions: YTHDF1 promotes the translation of m6A-modified RNA, enhancing the expression of proteins essential for cancer cell growth and differentiation, while YTHDF2 governs the stability and degradation of m6A-modified RNA [59–63]. This regulation affects not only mRNA metabolism, protein synthesis, and cellular functions but also extends to ncRNAs, influencing their roles in gene regulation, cell signaling, and intercellular communication.

Overview of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification in non-coding RNA. The m6A RNA modification process involves three main groups of proteins: writers, erasers, and readers. Writers catalyze the addition of a methyl group to adenosine residues on RNA molecules. Erasers remove methyl groups from m6A-modified RNA, reverting them to their unmodified state. Readers recognize and bind to these modifications, influencing RNA splicing, translation, and stability. Non-m6A-modified lncRNA, miRNA, and circRNA undergo transformation into their m6A-modified counterparts, reflecting the dynamic nature of RNA modifications and their regulatory roles in gene expression

This intricate regulatory network ensures that m6A modification can rapidly and precisely respond to cellular demands in various biological processes, including those in rapidly proliferating cancer cells. In GC, m6A modification influences tumor cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and treatment response. Abnormal m6A levels and enzymatic activity can lead to the dysregulation of ncRNA and mRNA expression and function, impacting tumor behavior and clinical outcomes. Therefore, a deeper understanding of m6A modification in gastric cancer not only elucidates the molecular mechanisms of the disease but also provides valuable biomarkers and therapeutic targets for developing new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Biological implications: the role of M6A modification in gastric cancer

The function of M6A writers in gastric cancer

The m6A writers, such as METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP, are essential in RNA modification, significantly influencing various biological processes in GC. Expression levels of METTL3 and WTAP are markedly elevated in GC tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Table 1), whereas METTL14 levels are diminished in GC tissues relative to normal tissues [64–67]. METTL3 expression correlates significantly with AJCC stage, lymph node metastasis, vascular invasion, tumor grade, and TP53 gene mutations [64, 68,65,]. Patients exhibiting high METTL3 expression face an increased risk of recurrence and reduced overall survival (OS). ROC curve analysis reveals that WTAP has high diagnostic accuracy for gastric cancer (AUC = 0.779) [66]. Elevated METTL14 levels are associated with improved survival rates [67]. Functionally, METTL3 and WTAP serve oncogenic roles in GC development. METTL3 enhances GC cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, invasion, and peritoneal metastasis [64]. Conversely, METTL14 overexpression inhibits cell viability, colony formation, and invasion [69]. In vivo studies show that inhibiting METTL3 reduces tumor growth and peritoneal metastasis. Furthermore, METTL3-deficient cells exhibit lower sensitivity to everolimus at two concentrations compared to control cells [64]. WTAP overexpression boosts glucose uptake and lactate production in GC cells while accelerating cell proliferation and extracellular acidification rates (ECAR) [66]. In vivo xenograft experiments confirmed that WTAP silencing decreased GC cell proliferation [67].

Expression and functional roles of m6A regulators in gastric cancer

The function of M6A erasers in gastric cancer

ALKBH5 exhibits reduced expression levels in GC tissues [70]. Its expression is negatively correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including poor histological differentiation, deeper invasion, advanced tumor stage, greater nodal involvement, higher TNM staging, and extensive lymphatic metastasis [70–73]. Consequently, patients with lower ALKBH5 expression often experience poorer clinical outcomes, such as shorter OS, recurrence-free survival (RFS), and disease-free survival (DFS) [72, 73]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis indicates that ALKBH5 has high sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing GC, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.7936, underscoring its potential as a reliable biomarker for GC detection [73]. Functionally, ALKBH5 plays a key role in inhibiting GC cell invasion and migration [70, 73]. In vivo studies demonstrate that ALKBH5 overexpression significantly reduces tumor growth and lung metastasis. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting ALKBH5 in GC treatment, as modulating its expression could impede disease progression.

The function of M6A readers in gastric cancer

HnRNPA2B1, IGF2BP3, and hnRNPA1 are upregulated in GC tissues, leading to poor prognosis [74, 75]. Elevated hnRNPA2B1 expression is linked to adverse clinicopathological features such as lower differentiation grade, higher TNM stage, larger tumor size, microvascular invasion, and neural invasion [74, 76]. Higher hnRNPA2B1 levels correlate with decreased OS, first progression survival (FPS), and post-progression survival (PPS) in patients with GC, particularly those receiving 5-FU adjuvant chemotherapy [74]. Increased IGF2BP3 levels are associated with shorter OS and functionally enhance GC cell proliferation [75]. Upregulated hnRNPA1 expression promotes cell invasion by inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in GC cells [77]. Studies demonstrate that hnRNPA1 overexpression leads to increased cell growth, migration, and invasion, confirmed by in vivo experiments showing hnRNPA1-induced EMT [77]. These findings highlight hnRNPA1 as a critical factor in GC progression and a potential therapeutic target.

YTHDF2, a key reader of m6A modification on RNA, acts as a tumor suppressor in GC. Research indicates that YTHDF2 expression is significantly reduced in GC tissues compared to normal stomach tissues [78, 79]. Lower YTHDF2 levels are associated with larger tumor size, advanced AJCC classification, and poorer prognosis for patients with GC [78]. Functionally, YTHDF2 reduction promotes GC cell growth and migration, while its overexpression suppresses these phenotypes. Mechanistically, YTHDF2 enhances the expression of PPP2CA, a catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A), in an m6A-independent manner [78]. Silencing PPP2CA negates the anti-tumor effects of YTHDF2 overexpression, suggesting YTHDF2 as a valuable biomarker and therapeutic target in GC.

Non-coding RNAs in gastric cancer

Overview of NcRNAs

ncRNAs constitute a large and dynamic group of RNA molecules that, despite not encoding proteins, play pivotal roles in regulating gene expression and maintaining cellular homeostasis [80–83]. These molecules can be broadly categorized into lncRNAs, miRNAs, and circRNAs, each with distinct functions in cellular processes.

LncRNAs, typically longer than 200 nucleotides, function through various mechanisms [84–87]. As guides, they direct chromatin-modifying enzymes to specific genomic locations, influencing gene expression [88]. As scaffolds, they bind multiple proteins to form ribonucleoprotein complexes, coordinating transcriptional regulation. As decoys, they sequester transcription factors or other proteins away from DNA targets, inhibiting their normal function [89]. Additionally, lncRNAs interact with mRNAs to regulate their stability or translation, adding another layer of gene expression control [90]. MmiRNAs are small RNA molecules, approximately 22 nucleotides in length, that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level [91–94]. Processed from primary transcripts (pri-miRNAs) via key enzymes such as Drosha and Dicer, miRNAs primarily function by base-pairing with complementary sequences on target mRNAs, leading to their degradation or translational repression [94]. This fine-tuning of gene expression is crucial, and miRNA dysregulation is linked to various diseases, including cancer, where they can act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors [95–97]. CircRNAs possess a unique covalently closed circular structure, rendering them resistant to exonuclease-mediated degradation and thus more stable than linear RNAs [98–100]. Produced through back-splicing, where the 5’ and 3’ ends of a single exon are joined, circRNAs regulate gene expression through various mechanisms. As miRNA sponges, they sequester miRNAs, preventing them from binding to their target mRNAs [101, 102]. By interacting with RNA-binding proteins, they can influence the function of these proteins. In rare cases, they can even be translated into functional peptides [103, 104]. The role of circRNAs in gene regulation is an emerging field, with their contributions to cellular processes still being elucidated. These ncRNAs are integral components of complex gene regulatory networks, affecting numerous biological pathways and processes. By acting at different levels of gene expression—transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and translational—ncRNAs are essential for the precise control of gene activity required for normal development and cellular function. Their dysregulation is often linked to disease pathogenesis, making them potential targets for therapeutic intervention and valuable biomarkers for various diseases.

The role of NcRNAs in gastric cancer: insights from recent research

NcRNAs are pivotal in the progression and regulation of GC by modulating gene expression, apoptosis, cell proliferation, and metastasis. LncRNAs such as HOTAIR and GAS5 have been extensively investigated; HOTAIR facilitates metastasis by modifying chromatin states and activating oncogenic pathways, while GAS5 functions as a tumor suppressor by interacting with glucocorticoid receptors, thereby regulating apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [105, 106]. MicroRNAs, including miR-21 and miR-335, serve as key regulators at the post-transcriptional level. MiR-21 enhances cell survival and proliferation by targeting tumor suppressor genes such as PTEN, whereas miR-335 inhibits metastasis by targeting genes involved in cell migration and invasion [107–110]. Circular RNAs like circPVT1 operate as miRNA sponges, sequestering miRNAs and preventing their interaction with target mRNAs, thus modulating oncogenic pathways and promoting cancer cell proliferation. For example, circPVT1 sequesters miR-125, resulting in elevated expression of the oncogene E2F2 and subsequent tumor growth [111, 112].

The interplay between NcRNAs and m6A modification in gastric cancer

The interplay between ncRNAs and m6A modifications constitutes an essential aspect of gene regulation in GC, significantly impacting the stability, maturation, and functional interactions of these molecules [113–116]. This dynamic and reversible RNA modification, mediated by writers like METTL3, erasers such as FTO and ALKBH5, and readers like YTHDF proteins, introduces an additional layer of regulatory complexity to the intricate gene expression network [117, 118]. This interaction not only enhances the regulatory capabilities of ncRNAs but also provides a mechanism for rapid adaptation to environmental changes, contributing to cancer progression and therapy resistance. Understanding this interplay can offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms of GC and identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

M6A modification enhances ncRNA stability by inhibiting degradation. For example, m6A-modified circRNAs show greater resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation, enabling effective miRNA sponge function [119, 120]. This stability ensures the persistence of circRNAs within the cell, prolonging their regulatory effects on gene expression. In GC, stable circRNAs continuously sequester tumor-suppressive miRNAs, thereby promoting oncogenic pathways [69, 121, 122]. Additionally, m6A modification influences the biogenesis and maturation of miRNAs. The processing of pri-miRNAs to precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) by the Microprocessor complex (Drosha and DGCR8) is enhanced by m6A marks. This modification facilitates the recognition and cleavage of pri-miRNAs, increasing mature miRNA levels [118, 123]. Consequently, miRNAs can downregulate their target mRNAs more effectively, modulating gene expression to favor cancer progression. Moreover, m6A modification can alter the secondary structure of lncRNAs, affecting their interactions with proteins and other RNAs. This structural change can either promote or inhibit the binding of lncRNAs to their interacting partners, thereby modulating their regulatory functions. For example, m6A-modified lncRNAs may exhibit enhanced affinity for chromatin modifiers or transcription factors, thereby influencing gene expression more effectively [118]. In GC, such modifications can activate or repress pathways critical for tumor growth and metastasis [124, 125]. Furthermore, m6A modification introduces a sophisticated level of regulation, as the modification can dynamically change in response to cellular signals and environmental cues. This flexibility allows ncRNAs to adapt quickly to changing conditions within the tumor microenvironment. For example, under stress conditions such as hypoxia or chemotherapy, the levels of m6A writers, erasers, and readers can change, altering the m6A landscape on ncRNAs. This leads to rapid changes in ncRNA stability, maturation, and interactions, enabling cancer cells to survive and proliferate under adverse conditions [126, 127].

The critical role of NcRNAs in gastric cancer: exploring the interplay with M6A modification

The crosstalk between m6A modification and ncRNAs plays a multifaceted role in regulating the oncogenic landscape of GC. m6A writers, erasers, and readers influence the expression, stability, and function of lncRNAs, thereby affecting proliferation, EMT, metastasis, and therapy resistance. The following subsections integrates the regulatory effects of m6A writers, erasers, and readers on ncRNAs and these cancer hallmarks.

m6A-lncRNA axis in tumorigenesis and proliferation

In GC tissues and cells, lncRNAs such as THAP7-AS1, SNHG3, LINC00958, LINC00240, and AGAP2-AS1 are significantly upregulated compared to non-tumorous gastric tissues (Table 2) [66, 128–131]. Conversely, lnc-PLCB1 expression is markedly lower in Helicobacter pylori (Hp)-positive gastric tissues compared to Hp-negative ones [67]. Clinically, elevated THAP7-AS1 are associated with positive lymph node metastasis (LNM) and higher AJCC stages, while increased LINC00240 expression correlates with advanced TNM stages, greater distant metastasis, and extensive lymph node involvement [128, 131]. Mechanistically, LINC00958 interacts with GLUT1 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner, enhancing the stability of GLUT1 transcripts and thereby positively regulating aerobic glycolysis in GC [130]. Moreover, THAP7-AS1 facilitates the nuclear import of cul4B, activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and promoting the malignant phenotype of GC cells [128]. RNA pull-down assays confirm its direct interaction with METTL3, whose overexpression enhances THAP7-AS1 expression via IGF2BP1-dependent stabilization, whereas METTL3 knockdown reduces THAP7-AS1 levels. Similarly, LINC00958, which undergoes m6A methylation by KIAA1429, binds to and stabilizes GLUT1 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner. This enhances aerobic glycolysis, contributing to tumor growth [130]. Additionally, LINC00240 functions as a sponge for miR-338-5p, and its knockdown decreases METTL3 expression, while miR-338-5p mimics suppress METTL3 levels [131]. Upregulation of LINC00240 in GC cells fosters malignant phenotypes by impairing miR-338-5p activity and maintaining high METTL3 levels [131]. SNHG3, another oncogenic lncRNA, is regulated by METTL3-mediated m6A methylation. Reduced METTL3 levels lower SNHG3 m6A modification and expression, enabling SNHG3 to act as a ceRNA for miR-186-5p, thereby upregulating cyclin D2 and promoting cell proliferation [129]. Moreover, AGAP2-AS1 promotes the assembly of the WTAP/METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex by binding to WTAP, stabilizing STAT3 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner and activating the IL6/STAT3 pathway [66]. In addition, lnc-PLCB1, typically downregulated in Hp-positive GC, is upregulated by METTL14 through YTHDF2-dependent m6A binding. Functionally, lnc-PLCB1 interacts with DEAD-box helicase 21 (DDX21), and its knockdown leads to reduced Slug and CCND1, suggesting a tumor-suppressive role in early-stage GC [67]. Similarly, METTL14-mediated m6A modification upregulates lnc-PLCB1 expression through YTHDF2 interaction [67].

Interactions between m6A regulators and NcRNAs in gastric cancer: Expression, clinical Features, functional Roles, and mechanisms

At the post-transcriptional level, miR-4429 directly targets METTL3, as shown by reduced luciferase activity of wild-type METTL3 constructs and diminished METTL3 expression upon miR-4429 mimic transfection. This leads to decreased levels of Sect. 62, a key m6A-stabilized oncogene, thereby suppressing GC progression [132]. Likewise, miR-338-5p binds to the 3’-UTR of METTL3 and reduces its expression. The METTL3-mediated m6A methylation enhances CDCP1 translation, which promotes GC cell proliferation and invasion, thus positioning miR-338-5p as a critical upstream regulator of m6A function [65]. Beyond its regulation of lncRNAs, METTL3 also facilitates the maturation of pri-miR-17–92. m6A modification promotes DGCR8 binding to this pri-miRNA, enhancing its processing into the oncogenic miR-17–92 cluster [64]. These mature miRNAs activate the AKT/mTOR pathway by targeting PTEN and TMEM127, promoting tumor growth and metastasis. Everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, may therapeutically target this METTL3–miR-17–92 axis (Fig. 2) [64].

Mechanisms of M6A Regulators and miRNA in Gastric Cancer. Overexpression of METTL3 promotes m6A modification and maturation of the miR-17–92 cluster, activating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and suppressing PTEN, thereby enhancing cell proliferation and migration in gastric cancer. miR-4429 targets METTL3, inhibiting its function and consequently reducing the m6A-induced stabilization of Sect. 62, which helps halt GC progression. METTL3 regulates the miR-1184/TRPM2 axis through m6A modification, influencing calcium signaling pathways and contributing to GC progression. miR-338-5p targets METTL3, affecting its m6A methyltransferase activity and enhancing the translation of CDCP1, a protein essential for promoting GC cell proliferation and invasion

Several m6A-modified circRNAs are also implicated in GC progression. For instance, EBV-circRPMS1 is upregulated in EBV-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC) and correlates positively with METTL3 expression and distant metastasis [133]. Functional assays reveal that its knockdown inhibits proliferation, invasion, and migration in EBV-positive YCCEL1 cells, while increasing apoptosis [133]. Mechanistically, EBV-circRPMS1 recruits Sam68 to the METTL3 promoter, enhancing METTL3 expression and driving oncogenesis. CircORC5 and circUGGT2, also upregulated in GC tissues, promote cell proliferation and invasion. Their high expression levels are independently associated with poor prognosis [69, 134]. Silencing circORC5 increases miR-30c-2-3p expression, suppressing tumor growth. Inhibiting miR-30c-2-3p restores proliferation, validating the circORC5/miR-30c-2-3p/AKT1S1 axis [69]. CircUGGT2, enriched in cisplatin-resistant cells, sponges miR-186-3p and upregulates MAP3K9, contributing to chemotherapy resistance and metastasis [134]. In vivo studies confirm its role in promoting tumorigenesis and lung metastasis. In contrast, circFOXP1, which exhibits high expression in GC tissues, is regulated by ALKBH5, a key m6A eraser. ALKBH5 demethylates circFOXP1, enhancing its stability and oncogenic potential [122]. CircFOXP1 acts as a sponge for miR-338-3p, inhibiting its tumor-suppressive role. Since miR-338-3p targets SOX4, this ALKBH5–circFOXP1–miR-338-3p/SOX4 axis supports tumor growth and metastasis. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrates that higher circFOXP1 expression is negatively associated with patient survival rates. Furthermore, Cox multivariate analysis identifies high circFOXP1 expression as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with GC [122]. CircNFATC3 and circARID1A are also m6A-modified circRNAs that functionally interact with the m6A reader IGF2BP3. CircNFATC3 stabilizes CCND1 mRNA by binding IGF2BP3 and preventing its ubiquitination via TRIM25, thus enhancing GC cell proliferation [135].

In GC tissues, circNFATC3 is significantly upregulated compared to adjacent normal tissues and correlates positively with tumor size [135]. Similarly, circARID1A forms a ternary complex with IGF2BP3 and SLC7A5 mRNA, enhancing its stability and promoting AKT/mTOR signaling in GC [76]. In contrast, circ-TNPO3 is downregulated in GC plasma and positively correlates with tumor differentiation. ROC analysis reveals that circ-TNPO3 achieves an AUC of 0.65. Functionally, circNFATC3 competes with IGF2BP3 to block its interaction with oncogenic targets such as MYC and SNAIL, suppressing migration and metastasis [135].

2 m6A-lncRNA axis in EMT and metastasis

The m6A-ncRNA axis plays a crucial role in regulating EMT and metastasis in GC. A number of m6A-regulated lncRNAs, including THAP7-AS1, SNHG3, LINC00958, LINC00240, and AGAP2-AS1, are overexpressed in GC tissues and are negatively associated with patient survival [66, 128–131]. Functionally, silencing any of these lncRNAs significantly inhibits cell migration and invasion, highlighting their roles in promoting metastatic behavior. Specifically, THAP7-AS1 overexpression enhances tumor growth, local invasion, and lung metastasis in vivo [128]. In contrast, lnc-PLCB1 displays tumor-suppressive activity, and its knockdown paradoxically leads to increased GC cell proliferation and lung metastasis, emphasizing the context-specific role of m6A-regulated lncRNAs [67]. Moreover, NEAT1 and LINC00659 are consistently upregulated in both GC tissues and cell lines [72, 73]. Elevated LINC00659 expression correlates with lower DFS, and both lncRNAs enhance proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and tumor growth and metastasis in vivo [72, 73]. Mechanistically, both lncRNAs interact with the m6A eraser enzyme ALKBH5. NEAT1 levels decrease upon ALKBH5 knockdown, suggesting a positive feedback loop [72]. This interaction influences EZH2 expression, a component of the polycomb repressive complex, thereby facilitating GC invasion and metastasis. Additionally, LINC00659 recruits ALKBH5 to JAK1 mRNA, leading to m6A demethylation, enhanced mRNA stability, and upregulation of JAK1, a pro-metastatic factor [136]. Another example, OIP5-AS1, enhances glycolysis, glucose uptake, and lactate production in GC cells—metabolic changes that support EMT and metastasis. Treatment with the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG reverses these effects. In vivo, silencing OIP5-AS1 significantly reduces tumor growth and metastasis, highlighting its oncogenic potential [75].

The m6A modification also modulates miRNAs, which in turn regulate EMT. In GC tissues and cells, miR-4429 and miR-17–92 cluster are significantly downregulated, while miR-1184 is upregulated [64, 68, 132]. Additionally, miR-338-5p levels are notably reduced in the serum of GC patients, correlating with tumor size, nodal metastasis, advanced disease stage, and poor survival [65]. Functionally, both miR-4429 and miR-17–92 suppress GC proliferation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosiss [64, 68]. Interestingly, miR-4429 also enhances glucose consumption and lactate production, indicating dual roles in metabolism and EMT regulation. In contrast, miR-1184 overexpression promotes EMT, proliferation, and migration. Overexpression of miR-1184 also reverses the effects of METTL3 knockdown, suggesting that METTL3 promotes EMT via the miR-1184/TRPM2 axis in an m6A-dependent manner [68]. Moreover, miR-17–92 expression is inversely correlated with GC stage and metastasis. Though typically downregulated in tumors, forced expression of this miRNA cluster enhances in vivo tumor growth, indicating its complex role in m6A-modulated signaling [64].

m6A also regulates circRNAs, many of which promote metastasis and EMT (Fig. 3). METTL14, a core m6A writer, has been shown via epitranscriptomic microarray and Me-RIP to target circORC5 and circUGGT2 [69, 134]. Silencing METTL14 reduces m6A levels on these circRNAs while increasing their mRNA expression, suggesting m6A may act as a repressive mark. CircORC5 functions by sponging miR-30c-2-3p, regulating downstream targets AKT1S1 and EIF4B, thereby promoting invasion and inhibiting apoptosis [69]. CircUGGT2, on the other hand, enhances invasion by regulating MAP3K9 via miR-186-3p sponging [134]. Additional m6A-modified circRNAs, circARID1A and circNFATC3, also regulate metastatic processes [76, 135]. CircARID1A forms a ternary complex with IGF2BP3 and SLC7A5, enhancing SLC7A5 stability and AKT/mTOR pathway activation [76]. While CircNFATC3 binds to IGF2BP3, preventing its ubiquitination by TRIM25, thereby stabilizing CCND1 mRNA and promoting EMT-related proliferation [135]. In contrast, circ-TNPO3 acts as a tumor suppressor. It is downregulated in GC tissues and patient plasma, and positively correlated with tumor differentiation. Functionally, circ-TNPO3 suppresses GC cell migration and proliferation. In vivo, its inhibition leads to tumor growth, abdominal tumor formation, and lung metastasis, suggesting it acts by sequestering IGF2BP3 and modulating MYC and SNAIL signaling [137].

m6A-lncRNA axis in chemoresistance and therapy response

Recent studies have revealed that multiple m6A-regulated ncRNAs contribute to chemoresistance, often through ferroptosis suppression, stemness enhancement, or hypoxia adaptation. CBSLR, OIP5-AS1, and lncRNA NEAT1 are significantly upregulated in GC tissues compared to normal tissues [74, 75, 138]. CBSLR expression increases with prolonged hypoxia [138]. Both NEAT1 and CBSLR negatively correlate with chemotherapy response, while OIP5-AS1 is significantly associated with TNM stage, T stage, and distant metastasis [74, 75, 138]. CBSLR expression increases under prolonged hypoxia, and its silencing under hypoxic conditions reduces GC cell viability and reverses chemoresistance. Importantly, this effect is abrogated by ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), indicating CBSLR’s role in suppressing ferroptosis [138]. Mechanistically, CBSLR interacts with YTHDF2, forming the CBSLR/YTHDF2/CBS axis, which enhances YTHDF2 binding to m6A-modified CBS mRNA, promoting mRNA degradation and reducing CBS levels. CBSLR also inhibits ferroptosis by regulating methylation and polyubiquitination of ACSL4, another ferroptosis-related protein [138]. Additionally, NEAT1 contributes to chemoresistance and stemness via an m6A-dependent interaction with hnRNPA2B1, which stabilizes NEAT1 transcripts [74]. Together, hnRNPA2B1 and NEAT1 activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, promoting GC stemness and resistance to therapeutic stress. OIP5-AS1, another oncogenic lncRNA, is highly expressed in GC and correlates with TNM stage, T stage, and distant metastasis [75]. It is stabilized by IGF2BP3 in an m6A-dependent manner. Knocking down either IGF2BP3 or METTL3 reduces the half-life of OIP5-AS1, confirming this m6A dependence. Furthermore, OIP5-AS1 protects hnRNPA1 from ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, and regulates its interaction with Trim21, thereby enhancing GC progression through dual interactions with IGF2BP3 and hnRNPA1 [75].

In addition to lncRNAs, several circRNAs contribute to cisplatin (DDP) resistance in GC. CircORC5 and circUGGT2 are upregulated in GC tissues, and notably, circUGGT2 is elevated in cisplatin-resistant cell lines compared to sensitive ones [69, 122, 134]. Functional studies show that knockdown of circUGGT2 significantly reduces the IC50 of DDP, increases chemosensitivity, and suppresses tumor growth in vivo [134]. Mechanistically, METTL14-mediated m6A modification of circUGGT2 enhances its oncogenic function. The modified circUGGT2 sponges miR-186-3p, thereby upregulating MAP3K9, a kinase involved in tumor progression and therapy resistance [134].

Clinical implications

Biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis

M6A-modified lncRNAs, miRNAs, and circRNAs exhibit significant potential as biomarkers for diagnosing, prognosing, and therapeutically targeting gastric cancer (GC). Specific m6A modification reflects the molecular heterogeneity and progression of tumors. For instance, the differential expression of m6A-modified ncRNAs in GC tissues compared to normal tissues can serve as diagnostic markers [128, 129, 138]. Elevated levels of m6A-modified lncRNAs, such as circFOXP1 and OIP5-AS1, correlate with advanced TNM stages, increased metastasis, and poorer survival rates, making them valuable prognostic biomarkers [75, 122]. Additionally, m6A-modified lncRNAs like THAP7-AS1 are associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including lymph node metastasis and higher AJCC staging [128]. The expression patterns of m6A-modified miRNAs, such as miR-338-5p, downregulated in GC tissues and serum, can indicate disease stage and patient prognosis [139]. Reduced miR-338-5p levels correlate with larger tumor size, lymph node involvement, and advanced disease stages, thus providing critical information for patient stratification and personalized treatment plans [118]. M6A-modified circRNAs also demonstrate potential as biomarkers. Furthermore, Cox multivariate analysis identifies high circFOXP1 expression as an independent factor associated with poor prognosis in patients with GC [122]. The stability conferred by m6A modification on circRNAs enhances their reliability as indicators of disease state.

Emerging therapeutic approaches

Targeting m6A modification and associated ncRNAs presents promising therapeutic strategies for GC treatment. Modulating m6A modification can alter the stability and function of oncogenic or tumor-suppressive ncRNAs. For instance, inhibiting m6A “writers” like METTL3 can reduce the stability of oncogenic lncRNAs and miRNAs, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis [64, 68, 128, 129, 131, 132]. METTL3 has been shown to enhance the expression of oncogenic lncRNAs such as SNHG3 and LINC00958, facilitating tumor progression through specific pathways [129, 130]. Conversely, enhancing m6A modification on tumor-suppressive ncRNAs can improve their stability and therapeutic efficacy. For example, promoting the m6A modification of lnc-PLCB1, which downregulates CCND1 and Slug expression, could act as a tumor suppressor in GC development [67]. Small molecules targeting the m6A pathway components are being explored for their potential to disrupt m6A-dependent oncogenic pathways. Combining m6A-targeted therapies with existing treatments, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, may enhance therapeutic outcomes by sensitizing cancer cells to these treatments. For instance, targeting m6A modification to reduce the expression of circRNAs that confer drug resistance, like circUGGT2, can improve chemotherapy efficacy [134]. Integrating m6A-modified ncRNAs into personalized medicine approaches could lead to more effective and tailored treatment regimens for patients with GC. By identifying specific m6A-modified ncRNA profiles, clinicians can better predict patient responses to various therapies and develop individualized treatment plans that target these molecular pathways.

Conclusion and future directions

The interaction between m6A modification and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), plays a vital role in regulating gene expression and maintaining cellular homeostasis in gastric cancer (GC). These modifications significantly influence the stability, maturation, and functional interactions of ncRNAs. The dynamic nature of m6A modification allows ncRNAs to adapt swiftly to cellular signals and environmental changes, providing cancer cells with the flexibility to thrive under various conditions. Understanding the complex relationship between m6A modification and ncRNAs is essential for comprehending the molecular mechanisms underlying GC. This knowledge not only enhances our understanding of GC pathogenesis but also opens new avenues for the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Regulating m6A modification presents a promising approach to target oncogenic pathways and improve patient outcomes. Incorporating m6A-modified ncRNAs into personalized treatment regimens can make GC therapy more precise and effective. Despite significant progress, several research gaps remain. The exact mechanisms by which m6A modification regulate the diverse functions of ncRNAs in GC are not fully understood. Additionally, the roles of various m6A “writers,” “erasers,” and “readers” in ncRNA regulation within specific contexts require further exploration. The clinical relevance of these modifications in patient stratification and therapy response necessitates extensive validation. Future research should focus on elucidating the detailed molecular mechanisms of m6A-modified ncRNAs in GC, identifying novel m6A modification sites, and understanding their functional significance. Investigating the interplay between m6A modification and other epigenetic changes could provide a more comprehensive view of gene regulation in GC. Developing robust and sensitive techniques for detecting m6A modification in clinical samples is critical for translating these findings into clinical practice. Technological advancements such as high-throughput sequencing and CRISPR-based epigenome editing could significantly advance this field. Single-cell RNA sequencing combined with m6A profiling can offer insights into the heterogeneity of m6A modification and their impact on ncRNA function at a single-cell level. Advanced bioinformatics tools and machine learning algorithms can aid in predicting m6A modification sites and understanding their regulatory networks. These innovations will be instrumental in advancing our knowledge of m6A modification and ncRNAs in GC, ultimately leading to improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
