General Decidability Results for Systems with Continuous Counters
A. R. Balasubramanian, Matthew Hague, Rupak Majumdar, Ramanathan S. Thinniyam, Georg Zetzsche

Abstract
Counters that hold natural numbers are ubiquitous in modeling and verifying software systems; for example, they model dynamic creation and use of resources in concurrent programs. Unfortunately, such discrete counters often lead to extremely high complexity. Continuous counters are an efficient over-approximation of discrete counters. They are obtained by relaxing the original counters to hold values over the non-negative rational numbers. This work shows that continuous counters are extraordinarily well-behaved in terms of decidability. Our main result is that, despite continuous counters being infinite-state, the language of sequences of counter instructions that can arrive in a given target configuration, is regular. Moreover, a finite automaton for this language can be computed effectively. This implies that a wide variety of transition systems can be equipped with continuous counters, while maintaining decidability of reachability properties. Examples include higher-order recursion schemes, well-structured transition systems, and decidable extensions of discrete counter systems. We also prove a non-elementary lower bound for the size of the resulting finite automaton.



0.1
Introduction

Counters are ubiquitous in modeling and verifying software systems.
They model dynamic creation and use of resources in concurrent systems.
For example, a fundamental type of infinite-state system with counters is a
vector addition system
(VAS).
These consist of finitely many counters that can be incremented and decremented in a coordinated way.
VAS, and related models such as Petri nets, have a long record of modeling resources in concurrent systems,
including manufacturing systems and discrete control
[
35
]
, business workflows
[
57
]
, hardware design
[
58
]
,
and biological pathways
[
40
]
.
In concurrent programming, they can model and analyze dynamic thread creation
[

,
6
,
10
,
24
]
,
numerical data types
[
31
]
, network broadcasts
[
19
]
, and population protocols
[
20
]
.

However, analyzing systems with discrete counters encounters two crucial challenges.
First, when the size of the counters is very large, e.g., modeling packets in a network or individuals in a large population,
individual discrete interactions are too fine-grained.
In such situations, one would like a “fluid limit” that captures the limit behaviors as counters go to infinity.
Second, the complexity of reachability for the simplest model, vector addition systems (VAS), which have
natural-number counters,
is already Ackermann-complete
[
15
,
46
,
44
]
.
As soon as we combine such counters with more expressive
infinite-state data structures, algorithmic tools become extremely rare.
Such combinations are natural in concurrency theory: for example, combining pushdown systems with counters (pushdown VAS) lets us model systems with both recursion and concurrency,
but decidability of reachability remained a longstanding open problem until a very recent breakthrough
[
28
]
.
Reachability becomes undecidable very quickly when we extend systems (that themselves have decidable reachability) even with very weak counters.
For example, lossy channel systems (LCS for short; which model unreliable communication channels) and
higher-order pushdown automata (HOPA; a standard model for programs with higher-order recursion) are
well-known to have decidable reachability, but equipping them even with
very weak kinds of counters makes reachability undecidable (
[

and
[

for HOPA, and
[

Continuous counters

[
18
]
provide a fluid relaxation of counter machines.
In a continuous counter machine, any transition that adds a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to the counters,
can be executed with any
non-zero fraction
: we are allowed to
pick a rational
0<\alpha\leq 1
and add

\alpha\cdot\bm{x}
to the counters.
As a result, the counters may thus assume values in
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
, the non-negative rationals.
This means that the possible behaviors of the relaxation are an overapproximation of the original machine.
This idea of relaxing discrete counters to continuous ones
goes back to David and Alla, who introduced continuous semantics in the context of
studying fluid limits of Petri nets already in the 1980s
[
17
]
.

Just as linear programming is a relaxation of integer linear programming with better algorithmic
complexity, continuous relaxations of counter machines have much better
complexity in many cases.
For example, reachability in Petri nets with continuous semantics can be decided in
polynomial time
[
23
]
; reachability in finite systems with continuous counters
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
12
]
;
reachability in pushdown continuous counter systems is
\mathsf{NEXP}
-complete
[
8
]
,
and there is an almost complete description
of the complexity of systems with affine continuous counters
[
9
]
.

The continuous relaxation has found applications in many fields, such as modeling client-server systems
[
47
]
, chemical reaction networks
[
37
]
and biological networks
[
29
]
. Also, continuous Petri-nets are well-studied in biochemical settings, with a variety of semantics and interpretations
[
33
,
34
]
.

In certain cases, continuous counters not only overapproximate the original system, but even allow an
exact representation of system behavior
. For example,
we consider leaderless rendez-vous protocols, which are a model of distributed systems composed of arbitrarily many identical finite-state agents interacting in pairs.
These interactions can be modeled using a (discrete) counter system where each of the counters is used to keep track of the number of agents in each state.
It turns out that important questions such as reachability and coverability for such protocols can be solved
exactly
by using the continuous relaxation
[
7
]
.
Furthermore, even more involved analysis problems such as the cut-off problem can be solved by combining the continuous relaxation along with the so-called integer relaxation
[
7
]
.
All of these algorithms run in polynomial time, which highlights the importance of the continuous relaxation as an important tool in algorithmic verification.

Finally, continuous relaxations have proven to be useful for the problem of coverability in Petri nets. In
[
21
]
, continuous relaxations were used in an SMT approach to solve (i.e. prove safety of) 94 of 115 instances of Petri net benchmarks.
Maintaining integer counters only helped prove 2 additional instances (with higher runtimes). In contrast, coverability tools only scaled to about 60 instances.
The continuous relaxation has also been used as an optimization in
coverability algorithms for VAS
[
11
]
.
Thus, better analysis tools for continuous counters will yield better algorithms for discrete counter systems in practice.

Given these positive results, it is natural to ask how far one can push the decidability frontier
for continuous counters: Is reachability decidable if we extend well-known
infinite-state models with continuous counters?
This is an important concern in modeling and analysis, because we
often take fluid limits for certain components while maintaining the discrete structure for other components.

Our main results show that indeed, continuous counters have extraordinarily good
decidability properties.
In a nutshell, decidable models remain decidable when continuous counters are added, as long as the models are closed under taking products with finite-state systems.

0.1.1
Outline of Main Results

To make our results precise, we need some terminology.
In a continuous counter machine, a transition that adds a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
can be scaled by any rational
0<\alpha\leq 1
, that is
\alpha\cdot\bm{x}
is added.
We refer to this as
fractional firing
.
A system that has access to
d
continuous counters has transitions which are labeled with vectors from a
finite set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, which represent the set of counter instructions.
If a sequence
w\in\Sigma^{*}
can lead from counter configuration
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}

to
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
via fractional firing, then we write
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{w}\bm{y}
.

Our main result is that for any dimension

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}

with

is an
effectively

.

This is very surprising: The set of configurations attainable between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
is infinite, and involves arbitrarily large numbers. 0.1.2
Implications of the Main Result

Our main result implies a very general decidability result for infinite-state
systems extended with continuous counters: For any class of infinite-state
systems that is closed under taking products with finite-state systems,
reachability is decidable if and only if it is decidable when equipped with
additional continuous counters.
Thus, it follows that, e.g., lossy channel systems and higher-order pushdown automata (even with
the collapse operation
[
32
]
), when extended with continuous counters, still have decidable
reachability.

Our result also applies to the general class of well-structured transition
systems (WSTS)
[
1
,
22
]
.
Here, the state space is ordered by a well-quasi
ordering (WQO)
\leq
that enjoys some compatibility with the transition
relation. Under mild assumptions, many questions are decidable for
WSTS
[
1
,
22
]
.
An example is the
coverability problem
: Given a
configuration
\bm{c}
of a WSTS, can we reach a configuration
\bm{c}^{\prime}
with

\bm{c}\leq\bm{c}^{\prime}
? In this case,
\bm{c}
is said to be
coverable
. Our result implies that
given such a WSTS whose transitions carry labels from some finite
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
one can even decide whether
\bm{c}
is coverable by a run that, working on
d

continuous counters, reaches a given
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
.
This is because, by our
result, the
d
continuous counters can be simulated by finitely many states,
and WSTS are well-known to be closed under taking products with finite-state
systems
[
22
]
.

Our general decidability result starkly contrasts with discrete counters.
There, many infinite-state models (e.g. LCS or HOPA) have undecidable reachability when extended with
weak kinds of counters (see references above).
Until very recently, the only known situation where
combining (discrete) counters with another type of data structure retains decidability is
pushdown systems with reversal-bounded counters
[
31
]
.
The recent breakthrough on PVASS reachability generalizes the result to all counters
[
28
]
.

Our result also makes the vast collection of algorithmic tools for
regular languages
(see, e.g.
[
52
,
53
,
27
,
56
,
3
]
) available to the
languages of continuous counters.
This includes not only
combinatorial methods working with finite automata
[
52
]
and regular expressions
[
53
,
27
]
, but also algebraic tools working with finite
semigroups
[
56
]
, and topological
approaches using topological semigroups
[
3
]
.

0.2
Preliminaries and Main Results

In this section, we recall the basic definitions of our central object of study, namely, continuous vector addition system (CVAS for short). Then we formally state our main results.

0.2.1
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we use
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
to denote the natural numbers, integers, rational numbers, and non-negative rational numbers, respectively.

A
d
-dimensional
Continuous Vector Addition System
(
d
-CVAS or simply CVAS) is a finite set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
of vectors called
transitions
. The operational semantics of a CVAS is given by means of its
configurations
, which we define below.

A
configuration
of
\Sigma
is a tuple
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, which intuitively denotes the current value of each of the
d
continuous counters.
Let
t\in\Sigma
be a transition and let
\alpha\in(0,1]
be a rational number.
A
step
from a configuration
\bm{x}
to
\bm{y}
by means of the pair
(\alpha,t)
(denoted
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha t}\bm{y})
is possible iff
\bm{y}=\bm{x}+\alpha t
. In this case,
\alpha
is called the
firing fraction
of this step.
Since each configuration must be in
\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, a step is only possible if no component of the configuration becomes negative.
If
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha t}\bm{y}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then we also write
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{t}\bm{y}
.

A
run
of
\Sigma
is a finite sequence of steps
\bm{x}_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}t_{1}}\bm{x}_{1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{2}t_{2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n}t_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}
. Here, the sequence
\alpha_{1}t_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}t_{n}
is called a
firing sequence
.
If such a firing sequence exists between
\bm{x}_{0}
and
\bm{x}_{n}
, we say that
\bm{x}_{n}
is
reachable
from
\bm{x}_{0}
(written
\bm{x}_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}t_{1},\alpha_{2}t_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}t_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}
or
\bm{x}_{0}\xrightarrow{*}\bm{x}_{n}
).

For a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, we write
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{w}\bm{y}
if
w=t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
and there exist rational fractions
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in(0,1]
such that
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}t_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}t_{n}}\bm{y}
. We also write
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha w}\bm{y}
where
\alpha=\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
is the sequence of firing fractions. It will always be clear from the context whether
\alpha
is a single firing fraction or a sequence of firing fractions. In the sequel, we often view
\Sigma
as an alphabet and write
letters
to mean transitions.

Finally, for a CVAS
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, we define its transition language as

L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}:=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\bm{x}\xrightarrow{w}\bm{y}\}

Example 0.2.1
.

Let
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
with
a=(1,0,0)
,
b=(-1,1,0)
and
c=(0,-1,1)
.
Let
\bm{x}=\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,0,0\right\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}
and
\bm{y}=\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,1/4,1/4\right\rangle}{\langle 0,1/4,1/4\rangle}{\langle 0,1/4,1/4\rangle}{\langle 0,1/4,1/4\rangle}
. It is easy to see that
abbc\in L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
because of the following run:

\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,0,0\right\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}\xrightarrow{\frac{1}{2}a}\mathchoice{\left\langle\frac{1}{2},0,0\right\rangle}{\langle\frac{1}{2},0,0\rangle}{\langle\frac{1}{2},0,0\rangle}{\langle\frac{1}{2},0,0\rangle}\xrightarrow{\frac{1}{4}b}\mathchoice{\left\langle\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4},0\right\rangle}{\langle\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4},0\rangle}{\langle\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4},0\rangle}{\langle\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4},0\rangle}\xrightarrow{\frac{1}{4}b}\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,\frac{1}{2},0\right\rangle}{\langle 0,\frac{1}{2},0\rangle}{\langle 0,\frac{1}{2},0\rangle}{\langle 0,\frac{1}{2},0\rangle}\xrightarrow{\frac{1}{4}c}\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right\rangle}{\langle 0,\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\rangle}{\langle 0,\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\rangle}{\langle 0,\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\rangle}

On the other hand, note that
bbc\notin L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. Indeed, suppose there exist fractions
\alpha_{1}>0,\alpha_{2}>0,\alpha_{3}>0
such that
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}b,\ \alpha_{2}b,\ \alpha_{3}c}\bm{y}
. Note that the first counter of
\bm{x}
is zero and also that
\alpha_{1}b
decrements the first counter by
\alpha_{1}
. Hence, the first counter becomes negative after firing
\alpha_{1}b
from
\bm{x}
, which is a contradiction to the requirement that configurations contain only non-negative values.

Now that we have all the necessary definitions, we now move on to stating our main result.

0.2.2
Main Result - Effective Regularity

The main result of this paper is the following

Theorem 0.2.2
.

For every CVAS
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, the
language
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is regular. Moreover, given
\Sigma,\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, one can effectively compute an NFA for it.

We now discuss some implications of this result. Theorem
0.2.2
implies
that for any class of labeled transition systems (LTS) that (i) has decidable
reachability and (ii) is closed under taking products with finite-state
systems, the following problem is decidable: Given an LTS
S
over an alphabet

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, CVAS configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, and states
s,t

of
S
, is there a run of
S
from
s
to
t
, such that it can take the CVAS
configuration
\bm{x}
into
\bm{y}
? Indeed, a decision procedure for this problem
is as follows: First, construct the NFA for
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
using
Theorem
0.2.2
, then take the product of this NFA with
S
using (ii), and
then finally check reachability of this product system using (i).

Let us formalize this result in language-theoretic terms. We say that a class

\mathcal{C}
of languages has
decidable regular intersection
if given

L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
from
\mathcal{C}
and a regular language
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, it
is decidable whether
L\cap R=\emptyset
. This is the case, e.g., if
\mathcal{C}
is
the class of languages of a class of LTS with the above properties (i) and
(ii).

Corollary 0.2.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a class of languages with decidable regular intersection.
Then, given a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
in
\mathcal{C}
over some

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
, we can decide
whether
L
intersects
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

Corollary

0.2.3
applies to a wide range of (languages of) infinite-state systems.
For example, it applies
to the large class of well-structured transition systems (WSTS)
[
1
,
22
]
.
They can be viewed as language-accepting devices
[
25
]
(by
considering all labels of runs that arrive in some upward closed set of
configurations), and since they are closed under taking products with
finite-state systems (which are well-structured), their corresponding language
class is closed under intersection with regular languages. In particular, this
means
reachability is decidable in lossy channel-systems equipped with
continuous counters
.

Another application concerns higher-order pushdown automata
[
16
,
48
,
49
]
(even with
the collapse operation
[
32
]
). Their language class is closed under regular
intersection, since their finitely many control states admit a product
construction with a finite automaton. Moreover, their emptiness problem is
decidable: With collapse, they are equivalent to higher-order recursion schemes (HORS)
[
32
, Thm. 4.1]
, and for HORS, even monadic second-order logic is decidable
[
51
, Thm. 16]
. Thus,
Corollary

0.2.3
implies that
in higher-order
pushdown automata (even with collapse) equipped with
continuous counters, reachability is decidable
. This is in
contrast to discrete counters: It is known that already for second-order
pushdown automata (a special case of higher-order pushdown automata), even
adding very simple kinds of discrete counters leads to an undecidable
reachability problem (see
[
59
, Prop. 7]
and
[
39
, Thm. 4]
).

Continuous Petri nets and CVASS

Closely related to CVAS, there are two other formalisms that overapproximate
discrete counter systems with continuous semantics:
continuous Petri
nets
(CPN) and
continuous vector addition systems with states
(CVASS).
The latter are almost the same as CVAS, except that the configurations also
feature a control-state, and each transition can only be taken in a particular
control-state (and updates the control-state). From
Theorem

0.2.2
, it follows
immediately that
languages of CVASS are regular as well
, since the
language of a CVASS is just the language of a CVAS, intersected with a regular
language.

CPN are slightly different: Each transition is not just a vector in

\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, but
two
vectors
\mathsf{pre},\mathsf{post}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. It is
applied by picking a fraction
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then first subtracting

\alpha\cdot\mathsf{pre}
and then adding
\alpha\cdot\mathsf{post}
.
Crucially, after subtracting
\alpha\cdot\mathsf{pre}
, the configuration
vector must be non-negative. While in the discrete setting, Petri nets are
easily translated into VASS, it is not obvious that CPN can be translated into
CVASS. However, Blondin and Haase
[
12
, proof of
Prop. 4.1]
provide a
language-preserving translation from CPN to CVASS. Thus,
languages of
continuous Petri nets are regular as well
.

0.2.3
A Non-Elementary Lower Bound

We now make a remark on the complexity of computing an NFA for
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.
As we shall show in
Section

0.4
, the construction from Theorem
0.2.2

only yields an Ackermannian upper bound for the NFA for
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. Usually, one would expect that if all languages of a class of systems is regular, then there should be an elementary upper bound (just as, e.g. in the region construction for timed automata
[
4
]
). However, as our second result, we show that this is not the case: There is a non-elementary lower bound for the size of NFAs for
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.
More precisely, let
\exp_{h}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be the
h
-fold exponentiation function, i.e.
\exp_{0}(n)=n
and
\exp_{h+1}(n)=2^{\exp_{h}(n)}
.
We define the
size
of a configuration
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
to be the largest absolute value of a numerator or denominator occurring in some component in
\bm{x}
. Hence, a configuration
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
of size
s
can be represented using at most
O(\log(s)\cdot d)
bits.
We then show that

Theorem 0.2.4
.

For each
h\geq 2
, there is a
5h
-dimensional CVAS
\Sigma_{h}
of size

O(h)
with the following property: For each
n
, there are configurations
\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n}
of size
at most
n
, such that any NFA for
L_{\Sigma_{h}}^{\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n}}
requires
at least
\exp_{h}(n)
states.

This theorem shows that CVAS can be extremely succinct in representing regular languages. This is perhaps surprising, given that checking whether a language of a CVAS is non-empty
can be done in polynomial-time
[
23
]
. Note that with these results, we leave a complexity gap between non-elementary
and Ackermannian. In particular, we leave open whether there is a
primitive-recursive construction of an NFA for
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

0.3
Examples and Behaviours of CVAS

Before presenting our proofs, we give examples to show the behaviour of CVAS and motivate our definitions. In this section, let us consider the same CVAS
that we had discussed in
Example

0.2.1
. That is, we consider the following 3-dimensional CVAS with transitions
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
given by

a=(1,0,0),\ b=(-1,1,0),\ c=(0,-1,1)

Let us also set

0.3.3
Limitations on Run Padding

In the examples above, we could add new transitions, but we only added them after the first instance of each transition in the original run. More precisely, in

This is not by accident, as the following argument shows, which limits the kinds of padding that we can perform on runs.

Suppose we consider the word

\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,0,0\right\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}

\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,1/4,1/4\right\rangle}{\langle 0,1/4,1/4\rangle}{\langle 0,1/4,1/4\rangle}{\langle 0,1/4,1/4\rangle}

decrements the first counter and so it would be impossible to choose a positive non-zero fraction to fire

from
\mathchoice{\left\langle 0,0,0\right\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}{\langle 0,0,0\rangle}

By the same argument, we can establish similar claims for

We can now see that a similar limitation also applies to the last appearance of transitions, i.e.,

0.3.4
Gatherings and the Lifting Runs Theorem

So far we have seen that it might be possible to adjust a run by adding additional transitions, as long as we add any new instance of a transition after its first appearance and before its last appearance in the run. This tells us that tracking the first and last appearances of a transition is important. Let us formalize this by means of
first-
and
last-appearance records
.

Let
w
be a word. For each transition
a
that appears in
w
, let
f_{a}\in[1,|w|]
(resp.
\ell_{a}\in[1,|w|]
) be the index
of the first (resp. last) appearance of
a
in
w
. The
first-appearance
(resp.
last-appearance
)
record
of
w
is the unique sequence of transitions
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
(resp.
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
)
such that
f_{a_{1}}<f_{a_{2}}<\ldots<f_{a_{n}}
(resp.
\ell_{b_{1}}<\ell_{b_{2}}<\ldots<\ell_{b_{n}})
.

First- and last-appearance will be used in
Section

0.6
to generalize the
reasoning in
Section

0.3.2
for showing that, under some broad conditions, if we have a run over a word
w
between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, then we also have a run between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
over
any word

w^{\prime}
obtained by inserting transitions into
w
whilst preserving its first- and last-appearance records (
Theorem

0.6.4
).

The first- and last-appearance records of a word are part of the crucial notion of gatherings: Informally, a gathering is a set of words that have a particular first- and last-appearance record and where the first appearances occur to the left of all last appearances. Formally,
a
gathering
is an expression of the form

X_{a_{1}\dots a_{n}}^{b_{1}\ldots b_{n}}

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
are letters such that
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
. Intuitively,
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is the order in which each transition should appear first, and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
is the order in which each transition should appear last.
Additionally, the last instance of
b_{1}
appears after the first appearance of
a_{n}
in the gathering.
In full, a word
w
over the alphabet
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
is said to
match
this gathering if (i) all first appearances of letters are to the left of all last appearances of letters in
w
and (ii) it has the same first- and last-appearance records as the one specified by the gathering. Note that each of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
must appear in the word, and these characters may not necessarily comprise the whole of
\Sigma
. The term gathering comes from the fact that in such a word, “everyone comes before everyone leaves”.

Example 0.3.1
.

Suppose we have the gathering
X^{abc}_{abc}
. Then the words
abcabc,abcbcaabc
match
X^{abc}_{abc}
,
but the words
abcacb,abcabcacb
do not: since the way the letters appear last in

and
abcabcacb
is

.
Moreover, due to condition (i),

is before the first appearance of
c
.
If we have the gathering

, then the word

matches
X^{ba}_{ab}
,
but

and
abab
do not match
X^{ba}_{ab}
.

In
Theorem

0.6.4
, the Lifting Runs Theorem for Gatherings, we see that, by using these ideas, from a gathering that intersects
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
we can find an infinite regular language that is contained in
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. This allows us to make strides towards a regular representation of
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

However, to construct a regular representation of
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
, gatherings alone are too simple. As a next step, we synthesize the ideas presented so far to introduce

. The Lifting Runs Theorem for Gatherings is generalised to path-schemes in
Theorem

0.6.1
(The Lifting Runs Theorem).

0.3.5
Path-Schemes

In
Section

0.3.4
, we saw that it is important to track

in order to characterize the language of a CVAS. In
Section

0.3.1
we also saw that it is sometimes necessary to track languages of the form
w_{0}\Sigma_{1}^{*}w_{1}\Sigma_{2}^{*}\dots\Sigma_{n}^{*}w_{n}

for words
w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
and subsets
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\dots,\Sigma_{n}\subseteq\Sigma
.
We refer to the
\Sigma_{i}^{*}
components as

.
Let us now combine both of these ideas into the notion of a
path-scheme
.

Let us fix a set of transitions
\Sigma
and two configurations
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
.
A
path-scheme
over
\Sigma
defines a regular language of words and is given by an expression

\rho=u_{0}X_{1}u_{1}\ldots X_{n}u_{n}

where each
u_{i}\in\Sigma^{*}
and each
X_{i}
is a
bubble
. A bubble is either,

•

a
star

X_{A}
for a set of characters
A\subseteq\Sigma
, or

•

a
gathering

X_{a_{1}\ldots a_{n}}^{b_{1}\ldots b_{n}}

where

\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}\subseteq\Sigma
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are pairwise distinct (and thus also
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
).

The language of a star
L(X_{A})
is the set of words
A^{*}
.
The language of a gathering
L(X_{a_{1}\ldots a_{n}}^{b_{1}\ldots b_{n}})
is the set of all words that match the gathering, i.e., all
w
such that

w=a_{1}w_{1}\ldots a_{n}w_{n}b_{1}v_{1}\ldots b_{n}v_{n}

where for all
i
we have
w_{i}\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}\}^{*}
and
v_{i}\in(\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\setminus\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{i}\})^{*}
.
For words
w\in L(X_{a_{1}\ldots a_{n}}^{b_{1}\ldots b_{n}})
, we define
\mathsf{center}(w)=w_{n}
.
That is,
\mathsf{center}(w)
is the part of
w
between the first occurrence of
a_{n}
and the last occurrence of
b_{1}
where all characters in the gathering can occur freely.

The language
L(\rho)
of a path-scheme
\rho=u_{0}X_{1}u_{1}\ldots X_{n}u_{n}
is the set of words of the form
u_{0}w_{1}\ldots w_{n}u_{n}
where
w_{i}\in L(X_{i})
for all
i
.
In such a case, we say that
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
is a
\rho

w
.
A path-scheme
\rho
is said to be

if all of its bubbles are gatherings.
\rho
is said to be
perfect
if it is pre-perfect and in addition
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
.

We will prove that the language of a CVAS can be (effectively) represented as a finite union of perfect path-schemes. This will immediately imply that the language of a CVAS is (effectively) regular. In the next section, we introduce the main ideas and give an overview of this proof.

0.4
Key Concepts and Proof Overview

0.4.1
Proof Overview

Our NFA construction for
Theorem

0.2.2
proceeds as follows. It maintains a
list of path-schemes, which initially just consists of a single path-scheme

X_{\Sigma}
, representing
\Sigma^{*}
. As we saw in
Section

0.3.5
, a path-scheme
\rho

gives rise to a set
L(\rho)\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
of transition sequences. Here, it may still be the case that a path-scheme contains no word from
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

One step in our procedure is to decompose each path-scheme into finitely many
perfect path-schemes. By definition, a perfect path-scheme intersects

L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. This is the
first decomposition
procedure.
However, just finding perfect path-schemes using the first decomposition does not yield effective regularity:
Instead, we show in addition that each perfect path-scheme
\rho
not only contains a

single run
, but in fact contains an infinite regular subset

R_{\rho}\subseteq L(\rho)
with
R_{\rho}\subseteq L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

However, the set
R_{\rho}
may not capture all of
L(\rho)
. Therefore, our
proof uses a
second decomposition
to turn a perfect
\rho
into finitely
many (not necessarily perfect) path-schemes
\sigma,\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}

where
\sigma
captures
R_{\rho}
and
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
capture the
remaining transition sequences of
\rho
. This step employs the Lifting Runs Theorem (
Theorem

0.6.1
) to identify
R_{\rho}
, from which
\sigma
and
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
are computed.

Our construction then alternates between these two decomposition steps.
Termination is guaranteed by the fact that each decomposition step yields
path-schemes that are smaller—in some appropriate lexicographical
ordering—than the path-scheme that is decomposed.

Having described our high-level strategy, we now state the precise theorems
corresponding to the first and the second decomposition mentioned above.

0.4.2
The Two Decomposition Steps

We will describe here what our two decomposition steps
achieve. The resulting theorems
—
Theorems

0.4.2
and
0.4.3
—will be
stated here and proven in
Section

0.5
and

Section

0.6
, respectively.

For the rest of this section, let us fix a CVAS with set of transitions
\Sigma
and two configurations

\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
. We now state the two main theorems that we need to prove our main result. In order to state these two theorems, we first need to set up a key definition.

As mentioned before, to prove termination of our construction, we need to impose a
lexicographic ordering on path-schemes . To this end, we define the
weight
of a path-scheme
W(\rho)
as a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{|\Sigma|}
such that the

component of

denotes the number of bubbles in
\rho

with exactly
i
distinct elements in it. More formally, for each
1\leq i\leq|\Sigma|
, we first let
\overline{i}
be the vector that is
1
in the
i
th component and
0
elsewhere. Then, we inductively define weight as

•

W(X_{A})=\overline{|A|}
.

•

W(X_{a_{1}\ldots a_{n}}^{b_{1}\ldots b_{n}})=\overline{n}
.

•

W(u_{0}X_{1}\ldots X_{n}u_{n})=\sum_{i}W(X_{i})
.

Example 0.4.1
.

Let
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}

has weight
(0,1,0)

Similarly, the gathering

(0,0,1)
.
For the path-scheme

(0,1,1)
.

Now, given two path-schemes
\sigma

\rho

W(\sigma)\leq_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)

is

than
W(\rho)

(|\Sigma|-1)^{th}
component of

is strictly smaller than the
(|\Sigma|-1)^{th}
component of

and so on. We say that
W(\sigma)<_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)
if
W(\sigma)
is
strictly lexicographically smaller
than
W(\rho)
, i.e.,
if
W(\sigma)\leq_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)
and
W(\sigma)\neq W(\rho)
.

We now introduce the two main theorems needed to prove our effective regularity result. Both of these results decompose path-schemes into “simpler” path-schemes
whilst still preserving certain properties regarding

The first one states that any path-scheme
\rho

that together preserves the intersection

Theorem 0.4.2

(Path-Scheme Decomposition)
.

Given a path-scheme
\rho
, we can compute a finite set of perfect path-schemes
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
such that
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=(\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i}))\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
and
W(\rho_{i})\leq_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)
for each

.

Note that if
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is empty, then the above theorem guarantees that so is

(\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i}))\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. However, since
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}
are required to be perfect path-schemes, in this case we will have

.

The second theorem states that any perfect path-scheme
\rho
can be decomposed into a finite number of path-schemes such that one of them completely sits inside
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
and all the others
have strictly smaller weight than
\rho
.
This theorem relies on the Lifting Runs Theorem (
Theorem

0.6.1
) to identify the required path-schemes.

Theorem 0.4.3

(Perfect Path-Scheme Decomposition)
.

Given a perfect path-scheme
\rho
,
we can compute a path-scheme
\sigma
and a finite set of path-schemes
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
such that
L(\rho)=L(\sigma)\cup\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i})
and
L(\sigma)\subseteq L^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}_{\Sigma}
and
W(\rho_{i})<_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)
for all
i
.

We shall now see how these two theorems can be used to prove our effective regularity result.

0.4.3
Proof of Effective Regularity

Assuming the above two theorems,
we show that
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is effectively regular. Here (and later in the proof), we employ a result by Blondin and Haase
[
12
, Thm 4.9]
, which allows us to decide, for a given CVAS
\Sigma
and a regular language
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, whether the language
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
intersects
R
.

Proposition 0.4.4

(Blondin & Haase)
.

Given a CVAS
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{d}
and a regular language
R
, the problem of deciding whether
R\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
is decidable (and
\mathsf{NP}
-complete).

This is because in
[
12
, Thm 4.9]
, Blondin and Haase show that reachability in “CVAS with states”, which are CVAS with finitely many control states, is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proposition

0.4.4
implies that given a path-scheme
\rho
, it is decidable whether
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
(since
L(\rho)
is a regular language).

To show effective regularity, using
Proposition

0.4.4
, we first check if
\Sigma^{*}\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
, i.e., we check if
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is non-empty. If it is empty, then we are already done.
Hence, for the rest of this proof, we assume that
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
.

We start with a tree
T_{0}
and then progressively add leaves to construct trees
T_{1},T_{2},\dots
with each tree
T_{i}
satisfying the
following invariants:

C0

Each node of the tree will be labelled by one path-scheme
\rho
such that
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
. This path-scheme will either be marked or unmarked.
If it is at an odd level, then it will be a perfect path-scheme.

C1

For any node
\rho
at an odd level, if its parent is
\rho^{\prime}
, then

W(\rho)\leq_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho^{\prime})
.

C2

For any unmarked node
\rho
at an even level, if its parent is
\rho^{\prime}
,
then
W(\rho)<_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho^{\prime})
.

C3

If a node
\rho
is marked, then
L(\rho)\subseteq L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
and it will not have any children.

C4

The union of the languages at the leaf nodes will be an overapproximation of
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
, i.e., a super-set of
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

We begin by setting
T_{0}
to be the tree consisting of just the unmarked root node labelled with the path-scheme
X_{\Sigma}
. Clearly, the five conditions are satisfied by
T_{0}
(where we take the root node
to be a node at level 0).
Suppose we have already constructed
T_{k}
. If all the leaves of
T_{k}
are marked,
then we stop the construction. Otherwise, let
\rho
be an unmarked leaf of
T_{k}
.
There are two possibilities:

•

\rho
is a leaf at an even level: In this case, we apply the Path-Scheme Decomposition
theorem to
\rho
to get a finite set of perfect path-schemes
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}

such that
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=(\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i}))\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
and
W(\rho_{i})\leq_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)
for each
i
. We then attach each
\rho_{i}
as an unmarked leaf
to
\rho
. Let this new tree be
T_{k+1}
.

By construction, the invariants C0, C1, C2 and C3 are immediately satisfied. For invariant C4, notice that
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=(\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i}))\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.
Since C4 was satisfied by the tree
T_{k}
and since the leaf
\rho
in
T_{k}
is replaced
by the leaves
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}
in
T_{k+1}
, it follows that C4 is also satisfied by the tree
T_{k+1}
.

•

\rho
is a leaf at an odd level: By invariant C0,
\rho
must be a perfect path-scheme.
We apply the Perfect Path-Scheme Decomposition theorem to
\rho
to get a path-scheme
\sigma
and a finite set of path-schemes
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
such that
L(\rho)=L(\sigma)\cup\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i})
and
L(\sigma)\subseteq L^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}_{\Sigma}
and
W(\rho_{i})<_{\mathsf{lex}}W(\rho)
for all
i
. We then attach
\sigma
as a marked leaf. Further, we add each
\rho_{i}
that satisfies
L(\rho_{i})\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
(we check this using
Proposition

0.4.4
) as an unmarked leaf to
\rho
. Let this new tree be
T_{k+1}
.

By construction, the invariants C0, C1, C2 and C3 are once again immediately satisfied.
For invariant C4, note that the only reason we
do not add some
\rho_{i}
as a leaf to
\rho
is if
L(\rho_{i})\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=\emptyset
. Hence, if
\sigma,\rho_{i_{1}},\rho_{i_{2}},\dots,\rho_{i_{q}}
are the path-schemes which
we added as a leaf to
\rho
, then
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=(L(\sigma)\cup\bigcup_{i_{j}}L(\rho_{i_{j}}))\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. Since C4 was satisfied by the tree
T_{k}
and since
the leaf
\rho
in
T_{k}
is replaced by the leaves
\sigma,\rho_{i_{1}},\dots,\rho_{i_{q}}
in
T_{k+1}
,
it follows that C4 is satisfied by
T_{k+1}
as well.

We claim that the construction of
T_{0},T_{1},\ldots
halts after a finite number of steps.
Towards a contradiction, suppose
T_{0},T_{1},\ldots
does not terminate.
Note that each
T_{i}
satisfies all the five invariants, is finitely-branching and is obtained by adding a finite number of leaves to
T_{i-1}
.
Hence, from
T_{0},T_{1},\ldots
we get a finitely-branching infinite tree
T

that also satisfies all of the five invariants.
By König’s lemma,
T
must have an infinite path. By invariant C3, this path has no marked nodes. By C1 and C2, the weight of an unmarked node at an even level
along this path strictly decreases (with respect to the
<_{\mathsf{lex}}
ordering) when compared
with the weight of its grandparent. By the well-ordering of the lexicographic ordering,
this path cannot be infinite, leading to a contradiction.

Hence, the construction halts at some point (say
T_{m}
).
By definition of the construction, this can happen iff there are no unmarked leaves in
T_{m}
.
Let
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{\ell}
be the set of all leaves in
T_{m}
. By invariant C4, we have
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\subseteq\cup_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\ L(\rho_{i})
and by invariant C3, we have

\cup_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\ L(\rho_{i})\subseteq L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. Hence,
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=\cup_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\ L(\rho_{i})
,
and thus
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is effectively regular.

We now conclude with a brief discussion on the complexity of our construction, i.e., the size of the final set of path-schemes that we obtain.

Complexity.
By a straightforward application of length function
theorems for ordinals
[
55
, Theorem 3.5]
, we show in

Section

.10
that the NFA for
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
can be computed
in Ackermannian time. Moreover, if we fix the number
h=|\Sigma|
of
transitions in our CVAS, then we obtain a primitive recursive construction.
This is because termination in our construction is guaranteed by descent
w.r.t. the lexicographical ordering
(\mathbb{N}^{|\Sigma|},\leq_{\mathsf{lex}})
, which is the
ordinal
\omega^{|\Sigma|}
, and each construction of path-schemes runs in
elementary time. This elementary time bound in particular yields an elementary
control function for the application of the length function theorem for ordinals. See

Section

.10
for a detailed argument.

Division of Proof into Sections.

Hence, in order to complete the proof of effective regularity (
Theorem

0.2.2
), it suffices to prove Theorems
0.4.2
and
0.4.3
.
This is what we do now, by using Section
0.5
to prove Theorem
0.4.2
and Section
0.6
to prove Theorem
0.4.3
.

Path-Scheme Decomposition

In this section, we will prove

0.4.2
. We begin by proving a slightly weaker claim for the special case of

Lemma 0.5.1

(Star Decomposition)
.

Given a star
X_{A}
, we can compute a finite set of
pre-perfect path-schemes
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
such that
L(X_{A})=L(\rho_{1})\cup\cdots\cup L(\rho_{m})

Proof.

We proceed inductively w.r.t. the size of

. The base case is when
|A|=0
. Hence

consisting of
just the empty word. It is then easy to see that

.

For the induction step, suppose

A^{*}~~~=~~~\bigcup_{G}L(G)~~\cup~~\bigcup_{\begin{smallmatrix}B\subsetneq A\\

B\subseteq A\setminus\{a\},\\
C\subseteq A\setminus\{a\}\end{smallmatrix}}B^{*}aC^{*},

(3)

G
ranges over all gatherings
X_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}}^{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}}

A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}

The inclusion “

the other direction “

belongs to one of the terms on the right-hand side of the above equation.

Consider a word

. If some letter

occurs less than twice in

, then
w
belongs either (i) to
B^{*}aC^{*}
for some

C\subseteq A\setminus\{a\}
or (ii) to
B^{*}
for some
B\subsetneq A
.
Thus, let us assume that every letter occurs at least twice in

and for each

in

, and
\ell_{i}\in[1,|w|]
be the last occurrence of

in

. We now consider two cases:

(i)

Some first occurrence is to the right of some last occurrence, i.e. there are

(ii)

Every first occurrence is to the left of every last occurrence, i.e.
f_{i}<\ell_{j}
for all

. In this case we claim that there is a gathering
G
such that

. Indeed, let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}

and

be permutations of

. Then, it is easily seen that

belongs to the gathering

and whose last-appearance record is

With (

) at our disposal, in order to prove the induction step

that appears in the right-hand side of (

), we can decompose

as a finite set of pre-perfect path-schemes

such that

is either the language of a gathering

, or a term of the form

or

for some

B^{*}
and

.
From these, we can construct a set of pre-perfect path-schemes for each language of the form

and

that inductively, the pre-perfect path-schemes for

,

have weight

has weight

. Thus, every pre-perfect

. A detailed proof of this case analysis on

is given in

This completes the induction step and therefore concludes the proof of the lemma.

0.6
Perfect Path-Scheme Decomposition

In this section, we will prove Theorem
0.4.3
.
In order to prove this theorem, we first set up some notation and introduce some key definitions.

Recall that a word
w
is said to be less than or equal to another word
w^{\prime}
over the subword ordering (denoted by
w\preceq w^{\prime}
) if
w
can be obtained from
w^{\prime}
by deleting some letters in it.
Now, let
X
be a gathering and let
w\in L(X)
. We define
(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}
to be the set of all words
w^{\prime}\in L(X)
such that
\mathsf{center}(w)\preceq\mathsf{center}(w^{\prime})
with respect to the subword ordering
\preceq
. Hence,
(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}
is the set
of all words in
L(X)
whose center component is larger than or equal to the center
component of
w
.

We extend the above notion to pre-perfect path-schemes. Let
\rho=u_{0}X_{1}\ldots X_{n}u_{n}
be a pre-perfect path-scheme and let
\vec{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
be a
\rho
-factor.
We let
(\vec{w})^{\uparrow}_{\rho}
be the set of words given by
\{u_{0}w_{1}^{\prime}u_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}\dots w_{n}^{\prime}u_{n}:\forall\ 1\leq i\leq n,\ w_{i}^{\prime}\in({w_{i}})^{\uparrow}_{X_{i}}\}
. Intuitively,
(\vec{w})^{\uparrow}_{\rho}
is the set of all words obtained by replacing each
w_{i}
with some
w_{i}^{\prime}\in(w_{i})^{\uparrow}_{X_{i}}
.
We call
(\vec{w})^{\uparrow}_{\rho}
the
\rho
-upward closure of
\vec{w}
.

With these notations set up, we are now ready to state the three main ingredients which will together imply Theorem
0.4.3
. To state these ingredients, we first set up some context.
Recall that to prove Theorem

\rho

into path-schemes

is contained entirely in

and the weight of each
\rho_{i}

\rho

The three ingredients will help us find the path-schemes

The first two are about finding
\sigma

\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}

X=X^{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}}_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}}
is a gathering such that
L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
. Then, we can compute a word
w\in L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
such that

(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}\subseteq L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.

Proof Sketch.

To prove this theorem, we begin with a fact from the proof of
[
12
, Proposition 4.5]
.
It states that if
L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
then we can compute
a word
w\in L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
such that
w=u\cdot\mathsf{center}(w)\cdot v

where
u=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}
,
v=b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n}
, the set of letters that appear in
\mathsf{center}(w)
is exactly
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
and the following property is satisfied:
There exist two vectors
\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime}
and three sequences of firing fractions
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
such that
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha u}\bm{x}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta\mathsf{center}(w)}\bm{y}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\gamma v}\bm{y}
and

•

If a counter is non-zero at any point in the run
r_{1}=\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha u}\bm{x}^{\prime}

then it stays non-zero from that point onwards in
r_{1}
.

•

If a counter is zero at any point in the run
r_{3}=\bm{y}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\gamma v}\bm{y}

then it stays zero from that point onwards in
r_{3}
.

•

If a counter was non-zero at any point in either
r_{1}
or
r_{3}
,
then it stays non-zero along the run
r_{2}=\bm{x}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta\mathsf{center}(w)}\bm{y}^{\prime}
.
5
5

5

Technically, this fact was proven for cyclic reachability in CVAS with finitely many control states, i.e., reachability in CVAS with states with the same starting and the final state. However, the same fact applies in our setting as well, because any gathering
X
over a CVAS
\Sigma
can be converted into a CVAS with states where the starting and the final state are the same.

With these properties at our disposal, we can prove that not only is
w\in L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
but that we have the stronger property
(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}\subseteq L(X)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. To this end suppose
w^{\prime}\in(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}
. By definition,
w^{\prime}\in L(X)

and so it suffices to prove that
w^{\prime}\in L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.
Since
X
is a gathering and since
w^{\prime}\in(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}
, we can split
w^{\prime}
as
w^{\prime}=u^{\prime}\cdot\mathsf{center}(w^{\prime})\cdot v^{\prime}
such that
\mathsf{center}(w)\preceq\mathsf{center}(w^{\prime})
.
Furthermore, since
u=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}
and
v=b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n}
, by definition of a gathering,
we also have that
u\preceq u^{\prime}
and
v\preceq v^{\prime}
.

We can now argue that there exist sequences of firing fractions
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime}

such that
\bm{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha^{\prime}u^{\prime}}\bm{x}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta^{\prime}\mathsf{center}(w^{\prime})}\bm{y}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\gamma^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\bm{y}
. Intuitively, we do this as follows: For each transition
t
, we show that we can take away a small part of its firing fraction from the sequences
\alpha u,\beta\mathsf{center}(w)
and
\gamma v
and redistribute this small part amongst its additional occurrences present in
u^{\prime},\mathsf{center}(w^{\prime})
and
v^{\prime}
respectively. When this redistribution is done naively and arbitrarily, it might happen that some counter value becomes negative at some point.
To prevent this, we use the special properties of
w
to show that
there is a way of redistribution which forces the counters to stay non-negative at each step. The formal details of this redistribution can be found in
Section

.12
.
∎

a_{1}X_{\{a_{1}\}}a_{2}X_{\{a_{1},a_{2}\}}\ldots a_{n}X_{A}c_{1}X_{A}c_{2}\ldots X_{A}c_{m}X_{A}b_{1}X_{A\setminus\{b_{1}\}}b_{2}X_{A\setminus\{b_{1},b_{2}\}}\ldots X_{A\setminus\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}\}}b_{n}

•

For each

•

,

•

And for each

Hence,

iff

and
\mathsf{center}(w^{\prime})\in L(\xi)

Since a word is in

iff it is larger than or equal to

We can now prove the Path-Scheme Theorem for
\rho
-Upward Closures.

Proof of
Theorem

.

Let
\rho=u_{0}X_{1}u_{1}\dots X_{n}u_{n}
be a perfect path-scheme and take some

∎

0.6.3
Proof of the Third Ingredient: Path-Scheme Theorem for Complements

In this subsection, we prove our third ingredient, the Path-Scheme Theorem for Complements (
Theorem

0.6.3
). The strategy is similar to that of
Theorem

0.6.2
: First, prove the theorem for the special case of gatherings and then prove the general case. We begin with the case of gatherings.

Theorem 0.6.7

(Path-Scheme Theorem for Complement over Gatherings)
.

Given a gathering
X=X_{a_{1}\ldots a_{n}}^{b_{1}\ldots b_{n}}
and a word

,
we can compute a finite set of path-schemes
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}
such that

L(X)\setminus(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}=\bigcup_{i}L(\rho_{i})

and
W(\rho_{i})<_{\mathsf{lex}}W(X)
for all
i
.

Proof Sketch.

Let
\mathsf{center}(w)=c_{1}\ldots c_{m}
and
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
.
For any
a\in A
, let
\overline{a}=A\setminus\{a\}
.
Note that a word is not larger than or equal to
\mathsf{center}(w)
(with respect to the subword ordering) iff the largest prefix of
\mathsf{center}(w)
that is a subword is strictly smaller than
\mathsf{center}(w)
.

Hence, for every
i\in[1,m]
, we let
\sigma_{i}
be the path-scheme given by

X_{\overline{c_{1}}}c_{1}X_{\overline{c_{2}}}c_{2}\ldots X_{\overline{c_{i}}}
.
By the above,
\bigcup_{i\in[1,m]}L(\sigma_{i})
is exactly the set of all words
that are not larger than or equal to
\mathsf{center}(w)
.

Now the set of words in
L(X)\setminus(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}
are precisely those words in
L(X)
whose center component
belongs to
\bigcup_{i\in[1,m]}L(\sigma_{i})
. Recall that
\mathsf{flat}(X)
is a path-scheme such that
L(\mathsf{flat}(X))=L(X)
. Furthermore, the central bubble
of
\mathsf{flat}(X)
captures precisely the center of all words in

.
Hence, if we replace the central bubble of
\mathsf{flat}(X)
with
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}

to get path-schemes
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}

L(X)\setminus(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}=\bigcup_{i\in[1,m]}L(\rho_{i})
. Since the central bubble of
\mathsf{flat}(X)
is its

component, it then suffices to set each

to be
\rho_{i}=\mathsf{flat}(X)[\sigma_{i}/n]
.
The formal proof behind the correctness of the equality
L(X)\setminus(w)^{\uparrow}_{X}=\bigcup_{i\in[1,m]}L(\rho_{i})
can be found in
Section

.13
.

For any
i
, notice that any bubble of
\rho_{i}
is either a bubble of
\mathsf{flat}(X)
that is not the central bubble
or a bubble of
\sigma_{i}
. By construction, in either case, it follows that, each bubble of
\rho_{i}
is actually a star of the form

with
|S|<|A|
. It follows then that the weight of each bubble of
\rho_{i}

has only 0 in the

component for any

. Hence, the overall weight of
\rho_{i}
also

j^{th}
component for any

is lexicographically strictly smaller than the weight of

(which has a 1 in the

∎

We can now prove the Path-Schemes Theorem for Complements.

Proof of
Theorem

0.6.3
.

Let
\rho=u_{0}X_{1}u_{1}\dots X_{n}u_{n}
be a perfect path-scheme and also let
\vec{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
be a
\rho
-factor. By
Theorem

0.6.7
,
for each
X_{i}
and
w_{i}
, we can compute a finite number of path-schemes
\sigma_{i}^{1},\dots,\sigma_{i}^{m_{i}}
such that
\bigcup_{j\in[1,m_{i}]}L(\sigma_{i}^{j})=L(X_{i})\setminus(w_{i})^{\uparrow}_{X_{i}}
and
W(\sigma_{i}^{j})<_{\mathsf{lex}}W(X_{i})
.
Now, for each
i\in[1,n]
and each
j\in[1,m_{i}]
, we can create
a path-scheme
\rho_{i}^{j}=\rho[\sigma_{i}^{j}/i]
.

First, we claim that if
w^{\prime}\in L(\rho)\setminus(\vec{w})^{\uparrow}_{\rho}
,
then
w^{\prime}\in L(\rho_{i}^{j})
for some
i,j
. Indeed, if
w^{\prime}\in L(\rho)\setminus(\vec{w})^{\uparrow}_{\rho}

then for any
\rho
-factor
\vec{w^{\prime}}=(w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{n})
there must be some
i
such that

\mathsf{center}(w_{i})\not\preceq\mathsf{center}(w_{i}^{\prime})

.

Second, since each
\rho_{i}^{j}

.

∎

0.7
Comparison with the KLM Decomposition

In the previous three sections, we have presented our effective regularity proof for CVAS languages.
Our construction bears a striking similarity with the KLM-decomposition
6
6

6

The authors of this work discovered the similarity only after obtaining the results of this paper. However, some structural aspects and some terms in the construction (such as “perfect” for path-schemes) have been chosen after this discovery to match the KLM-decomposition.
from the theory of (discrete) vector addition systems
[
41
,
42
,
50
]
.
Since we find it surprising that a KLM-like decomposition (i) can be used for a regularity proof and (ii) applies to the continuous setting, we use this section to point out similarities and differences between our construction and the KLM-decomposition.
Readers who only want to understand the new proofs may safely skip this section.

Vector Addition Systems.

Intuitively, a vector addition system is the same as a CVAS, except that the firing fraction at each step must be exactly equal to 1.
Formally, a
vector addition system
(VAS) is syntactically the same
as a CVAS, and is given by a finite set of transitions
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
A configuration of a VAS is a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. A step from
a configuration
\bm{x}
to
\bm{y}
by means of a transition
t
is possible if and only if
\bm{y}=\bm{x}+t
.
Since each configuration must be an element in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, a step is only possible if no component of the configuration becomes negative. Similar to CVAS, we can define runs and reachability in a VAS.
The
reachability problem for VAS
is to decide, given a set of transitions
\Sigma
and two configurations
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, whether
\bm{x}
can reach
\bm{y}
.

A landmark result in the theory of VAS is that reachability
is decidable and in fact,
Ackermann-complete
[
15
,
46
,
44
]
.
The first known algorithm for reachability in VAS is the so-called
KLM-decomposition
(named after three key contributors
Kosaraju
[
41
]
,
Lambert
[
42
]
, and
Mayr
[
50
]
).

Why is the Similarity Surprising?.
At first glance, it might seem
natural that ideas for VAS also apply to CVAS. However,
compared to other VAS overapproximations like
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS (where the counters
can become negative), it is not obvious that a CVAS can be
translated into a VAS with the same language. For example, consider the
following decision problem: Given a VAS, configurations
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and a
number
\ell
(represented in binary), decide whether there is a run of length

\ell
from
\bm{x}
to
\bm{y}
. In the VAS setting, this problem is

\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete (since all intermediate counter values are at most exponential). However, for CVAS, this problem is

\mathsf{NEXP}
-complete
[
8
, Theorems 1.1 and
6.1]
. Hence, unless
\mathsf{PSPACE}=\mathsf{NEXP}
,
one cannot translate a CVAS into a language-equivalent VAS in polynomial time.
Of course,
Theorem

0.2.2
implies that CVAS languages are also VAS languages
(since the former are regular), but we are not aware of any proof of this fact besides

Theorem

0.2.2
.

An Overview of KLM.

Roughly speaking, the KLM-decomposition works as follows (we adopt the terminology of Leroux and
Schmitz
[
43
]
).
It maintains a list of structures
called
marked witness graph sequences
(MWGS). Each MWGS describes a set
of
pre-runs
, which intuitively are sequences of transitions that are not
necessarily valid VAS runs. In the beginning, the list consists of a single
MWGS—essentially the VAS itself.

A crucial notion for MWGSes is that of a “perfect MWGS”. A key insight in the KLM-decomposition is that a perfect MWGS has a valid run among its pre-runs.
Moreover, whether an MWGS is perfect can be decided using simpler algorithmic
tools: (i) a coverability check, and (ii) feasibility of a system of integer
linear inequalities.

The KLM algorithm first checks perfectness of each of the current MWGS. If one
of them is perfect, it concludes the existence of a valid run. On the other
hand, for every MWGS
\xi
that is
not
perfect, the algorithm can
decompose
\xi
into finitely many MWGS that are smaller w.r.t. some
lexicographical ordering. This implies that after finitely many steps, all
remaining MWGS (if any) must be perfect. Hence, if at the very end, some
perfect MWGS remains, we can conclude the existence of a run; otherwise, we can
conclude that no run exists.

Similarities.

Let us first highlight similarities between our construction and the KLM-decomposition.
Our construction follows a similar overall strategy: While KLM decomposes MWGS, our algorithm decomposes path-schemes.
Moreover perfectness in MWGS has a correspondence in perfectness of path-schemes, since
the perfectness condition in
Section

0.3.5
consists
of a (i) “coverability part” and (ii) a “linear inequalities” part.
Indeed, recall that a path-scheme
\rho
is perfect if it is pre-perfect and
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
. First, pre-perfectness is closely related to coverability (of the
zero vector) in CVAS along a run over a given word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
. This is because
whether some word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
can cover the zero vector (starting
from a given initial vector) depends only on the order of first
appearances of letters in
w

[
8
, Lemma
3.10]
. Similarly, last appearances determine “backwards coverability”.
Furthermore, the proof of
Proposition

0.4.4
(specifically, the
\mathsf{NP}
upper bound in
[
12
, Thm 4.9]
) is based on expressing
L(\rho)\cap L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\neq\emptyset
as solvability of systems of
linear inequalities.

Another similarity is that KLM-based proofs
have a step that constructs a run (or a subset of runs) inside a perfect MWGS.
Examples include Lambert’s Iteration
Lemma
[
42
, Lemma 4.1]
or Leroux and Schmitz’s
discovery that the pre-runs of perfect MWGS actually form an
adherent
ideal
of the set of all pre-runs below valid
runs
[
43
, Lemma VII.2, Theorem VII.5]
. In our proof, the
construction of a regular subset
R_{\rho}
of
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}

in
Theorem

0.6.4
, which is based
on
[
12
, Proposition 4.5]
, is similar
(in spirit and in technique) to these steps in KLM-based proofs.

Differences.

Let us now point out differences between our proof and existing variants of the KLM-decomposition.

First, our decomposition does not stop when all path-schemes are perfect.
Instead, it is possible that the regular sets
R_{\rho}
do not cover the entire
set
L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
: Classical KLM-decompositions only construct over-
and/or underapproximations of the set of runs
7
7

7

Examples of overapproximations are semilinear overapproximations (which are implicit in
the various KLM-based algorithms for reachability, including the new algorithm
for PVASS by Guttenberg, Keskin, and Meyer
[
28
]
), regular
overapproximations
[
30
,
14
]
, or integer VASS
overapproximations
[
38
]
. Underapproximations are given, e.g. by the
Iteration Lemma
[
42
, Lemma 4.1]
and related
facts, see above.
, whereas we need to find an exact representation.
This necessitates the
second decomposition step

(
Theorem

0.4.3
): In addition to constructing regular
sets
R_{\rho}\subseteq L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
of perfect path-schemes, we then
need to decompose the perfect path-schemes further into path-schemes that cover
those runs that have not been caught by the already-built sets
R_{\rho}
.

Second, the steps to achieve perfectness of path-schemes
(
Theorem

0.4.2
) are very different: In KLM, one decomposes
by bounding counters or by bounding the number of transition occurrences (which
is anyway not possible in the continuous semantics). Instead, our construction
relies on a word-combinatorial observation:
Lemma

0.5.1
.

Third, in order for both decomposition steps to work together (and ensure
termination), our proof requires the novel notion of gatherings, which is built
so that it (i) yields subsets of the CVAS language (
Theorem

0.6.4
)
and (ii) can be achieved by decomposing arbitrary path-schemes (
Theorem

0.4.2
). Note that gatherings
are different from just the set of words with a particular first- and last
appearance record (as in

[
8
, Lemma 3.10]
): In a gathering, it is important
that all first appearances come before all last appearances.

Transition Notation.

To make the effects of the transitions easily visible, we introduce some
notation. Recall that our CVAS has
5h
counters (see (
10
)). We use the names of a
counter to indicate the effect of incrementing this counter. For example,
x_{i}

is the vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{5h}
that has
1
in the coordinate for counter
x_{i}
.
This also means, e.g., that
x_{i}+\mathsf{step}_{i}
would be a transition that adds
1

to the counters
x_{i}
and
\mathsf{step}_{i}
, but leaves all others unchanged.
For example, the transition sequence
u
is

\displaystyle u=f_{1}g_{1}\cdots f_{h}g_{h},

\displaystyle f_{j}=-x_{j},

\displaystyle g_{j}=-y_{j},

for every
j=1,\ldots,h
. Thus, if the counters

x_{1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{1},\ldots,y_{h}
carry values
\leq 1
, the sequence
u
allows us to reset
them to zero.

The transition sequences
w_{1},r_{1},\ldots,w_{h},r_{h}

will have the following property:

Property 0.8.6
.

The sequences
w_{i}
and
r_{i}
have no effect on
x_{j},\bar{x}_{j}
,
y_{j}
,
\bar{y}_{j}
,
\mathsf{step}_{j}
for
j<i
.

Before we describe the transition sequences

w_{1},r_{1},\ldots,w_{h},r_{h}
and prove
Lemma

0.8.5
, let us see
how
Lemma

0.8.5
implies

Lemma

0.8.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

0.8.3
.

First, by initially firing a
(1,n)
-maxed out run, then a
(2,n2^{n})
-maxed out
run, etc., and then resetting all
x_{j},y_{j}
(for
j=1,\ldots,h
) using
u
, we obtain a maxed out run from
\bm{x}_{n}
to
\bm{y}_{n}
. Note that here,
u
is able to reset all
x_{j},y_{j}
since their values are
\leq 1
.

For the second statement, take a short run along the transition sequence

w_{1}^{\ell_{1}}r_{1}\cdots w_{h}^{\ell_{h}}r_{h}u
. Then the configuration reached after
the prefix
w_{1}^{\ell_{1}}r_{1}
must agree with
\bm{y}_{n}
in the counters

\mathsf{step}_{1},\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1}
, because the latter are not touched during

w_{2}^{\ell_{2}}r_{2}\cdots w_{h}^{\ell_{h}}r_{h}u
. Hence, the run along

w_{1}^{\ell_{1}}r_{1}
is a short
(1,n)
-run. By
Lemma

0.8.5
,
the run along
w_{1}^{\ell_{1}}r_{1}
is a
(1,n)
-maxed out run. Thus, after

w_{1}^{\ell_{1}}r_{1}
, our run reaches a configuration where

x_{1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{1},\ldots,y_{h}
carry
\tfrac{1}{n2^{n}}
, the counters

\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{h},\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{h}
carry
0
, and

\ell_{1}=n
. By repeating this argument, we get that
w_{i}^{\ell_{i}}r_{i}
is a

(i,M_{i})
-short run, and hence a
(i,M_{i})
-maxed out run, for
M_{1}=n
and

M_{i+1}=M_{i}2^{M_{i}}
for
i\in[1,h-1]
. In particular, we obtain
\ell_{i}=M_{i}

for all
i
. Overall, this implies that our run along
w_{1}^{\ell_{1}}r_{1}\cdots w_{h}^{\ell_{h}}r_{h}u
is a maxed out run.
∎

It thus remains to set up
w_{1},\ldots,w_{h},r_{1},\ldots,r_{h}
so as to satisfy

Lemmas

0.8.5
and
0.8.6
.

The Three Gadgets.

When describing the transition sequences
w_{1},\ldots,w_{h}
and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{h}
,
some more notation will be useful: Since
\bar{x}_{i}
is meant as a complement
counter to
x_{i}
, most transitions will have opposite effect on these two counters
(and likewise with
\bar{y}_{i}
and
y_{i}
). Therefore, we define

\hat{x}_{i}:=x_{i}-\bar{x}_{i}
and
\hat{y}_{i}:=y_{i}-\bar{y}_{i}
. Thus, a
transition with effect
\hat{x}_{i}
adds
1
to
x_{i}
and subtracts
1
from

\bar{x}_{i}
.

Let us now describe the words
w_{1},\ldots,w_{h},r_{1},\ldots,r_{h}
, together with the
(i,k)
-maxed out runs. We will show afterwards that the described
(i,k)
-maxed out run is indeed the only
(i,k)
-short run. First, we have
w_{i}=t_{i,1}\cdots t_{i,6}
, where the first three transitions are:

\displaystyle t_{i,1}=-2\hat{x}_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}-2\sum_{j=i+1}^{h}(\hat{x}_{j}+\hat{y}_{j}),

\displaystyle t_{i,2}=\hat{x}_{i}+\mathsf{step}_{i},

\displaystyle t_{i,3}=-\hat{x}_{i}+\mathsf{step}_{i}.

Thus,
t_{i,1}
subtracts some value
\alpha\in(0,1]
from
y_{i}
, and subtracts
2\alpha
from
x_{i}
and all counters
x_{i+1},y_{i+1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
. In our
(i,k)
-maxed out run, this is applied with
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2k}

x_{i}
and
x_{i+1},y_{i+1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
, but sets
y_{i}
to
\tfrac{1}{2k}
.
Because of opposite effects on the complement counters, this sets
\bar{x}_{i}
and
\bar{x}_{i+1},\bar{y}_{i+1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{h},\bar{y}_{h}
to
\tfrac{1}{k}
. Moreover,
\bar{y}_{i}
becomes
\tfrac{1}{2k}
.

In
t_{i,2}t_{i,3}
, we move mass back and forth between
x_{i}
and
\bar{x}_{i}
, while adding the mass (twice) to
\mathsf{step}_{i}
.
In our
(i,k)
-maxed out run,
t_{i,2}t_{i,3}
moves all the mass
(
\tfrac{1}{k}
) from
\bar{x}_{i}
to
x_{i}
(in
t_{i,2}
), and then back (in

t_{i,3}
); and we add
\tfrac{2}{k}
to
\mathsf{step}_{i}
. Thus, compared to the
configuration after
t_{i,1}
, we only added
\tfrac{2}{k}
to
\mathsf{step}_{i}
.

The second part of
w_{i}
, namely
t_{i,4}t_{i,5}t_{i,6}
, works as follows:

\displaystyle t_{i,4}=\sum_{j=i}^{h}\hat{x}_{j}+\hat{y}_{j}

\displaystyle t_{i,5}=-\hat{y}_{i}+\mathsf{step}_{i}

\displaystyle t_{i,6}=\hat{y}_{i}+\mathsf{step}_{i}

In
t_{i,4}
, we fill the counters
x_{i},y_{i},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
back up, but only as far as the capacity of
y_{i}
(as guaranteed using the complement counter
\bar{y}_{i}
) allows: In our
(i,k)
-maxed out run, before
t_{i,4}
, we have
y_{i}
at
\tfrac{1}{2k}
, and the counters
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1},y_{i+1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
at
0
. Thus, with
t_{i,4}
, we can increase all counters
x_{i},y_{i},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
by
\tfrac{1}{2k}
. This means, we get
\tfrac{1}{2k}
in
x_{i}
and in
x_{i+1},y_{i+1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
. And we get
\tfrac{1}{k}
in
y_{i}
.

Along
t_{i,5}t_{i,6}
, we then have a “back and forth” again, but now between
y_{i}
and
\bar{y}_{i}
. In our
(i,k)
-maxed out run, we move the entire
\tfrac{1}{k}
back and forth. We thus have the same configuration as after
t_{i,4}
, but
\mathsf{step}_{i}
now carries
\tfrac{2}{k}+\tfrac{2}{k}=\tfrac{4}{k}
.

We have now described our
(i,k)
-maxed out run along the first copy of
w_{i}
. Note that overall, it had the same effect on
x_{i}
and on
x_{i+1},y_{i+1},\ldots,x_{h},y_{h}
: It replaced the value
\tfrac{1}{k}
with the value
\tfrac{1}{2k}
. Moreover, the run did not change
y_{i}
. And it added
\tfrac{4}{k}
to
\mathsf{step}_{i}
. To describe the later iterations of
w_{i}

0.9
Conclusion

We have shown that the language of every CVAS is effectively regular (
Theorem

0.2.2
). This clarifies the decidability landscape of reachability in systems with continuous counters (
Corollary

0.2.3
). While the decidability aspects are now understood, we view these results as the starting point for investigating the complexity of reachability in a wide range of systems with continuous counters.

Complexity.
There are many complexity aspects that remain unclear.
For example, what is the exact complexity of computing NFAs for

L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
? We have a non-elementary lower bound, and our
construction yields an Ackermannian upper bound. It would be interesting to investigate whether there is a primitive-recursive construction.

We already know situations where computing an NFA for

L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
(which has non-elementary size) does not yield optimal
complexity for reachability in systems with continuous counters. For example,
in finite-state systems equipped with continuous counters, reachability is

\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
12
, Theorem 4.14]
, and for pushdown
systems with continuous counters, reachability is

\mathsf{NEXP}
-complete
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
. This
suggests that for order-
k
pushdown systems with continuous counters,
reachability should be
k
-
\mathsf{NEXP}
-complete, but our results only yield an
Ackermannian upper bound.

Demystification.
It would also be interesting to provide a
simple, abstract description of how the path-scheme decomposition in our
construction relates to the CVAS. For the KLM decomposition, such a
description was obtained by Leroux and Schmitz
[
43
]
.
Their description—the so-called “demystification”—casts the
decomposition as a computation of ideals. However, this was 30 years after
the KLM decomposition was first described. Hence, this might require deep
conceptual insights.

For example, we can ask if there is a counterpart to the run embedding
introduced by Jančar
[
36
]
and
Leroux
[
45
]
that would make our construction an ideal
decomposition. Also, since our regular language is a finite union of sets that
look similar to upward closures: Is there a well-quasi ordering such that

L_{\Sigma}^{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is upward closed? A potential candidate for these two
questions might be the DFA-defined well-quasi orderings from

[
60
, p. 3]
, where the DFA is induced by a suitable
pre-perfect path-scheme.

On the one hand, such a demystification would conceptually clarify our proof.
On the other hand, it might also deepen our understanding of VAS: Because of
the alternation of decomposition steps, our construction yields a precise
description of firing sequences, whereas the KLM decomposition for VAS only
provides an overapproximation (i.e. an ideal decomposition over the set of
pre-runs). Perhaps the two approaches can be merged into a more general construction
for VAS that lets us understand the set of runs of a VAS as a regular set,
once runs are equipped with appropriate structure — just as context-free
languages are yields of regular sets of trees.

Comments on the lower bound

Here is an exponential run from

to
\bm{y}_{n}
that is of the form in (
9

. This is clearly not a short run (by our definition of short run), but it is of at most exponential length.

Intuition.
Before we explain in detail how this can be done, let us give some intuition.
The reason our lower bound construction enforces short runs to be maxed out is that during the firing of
w_{i}
, the only way to put
4
into
\mathsf{step}_{i}
is to always use the maximal possible firable fractions:
t_{i,1}
needs to empty
x_{1}
completely, and then
t_{i,4}
needs to fill up
y_{1}
completely. This is because in an
(i,k)
-short run, we need the full
\tfrac{1}{k}
fraction during
t_{i,2}t_{i,3}
and during
t_{i,5}t_{i,6}
in order to get
\mathsf{step}_{i}
up to
4
in only
k
iterations of
w_{i}
.

However, if we allow ourselves to iterate
w_{i}
a whole
2k
number of times
(starting from a configuration that is consistent with
(i,k)
-short runs,
i.e.
\langle\tfrac{1}{k};\tfrac{1}{k};0\rangle
) and still make sure that

t_{i,5}
and
t_{i,6}
can fire with fraction at least
\tfrac{1}{k}
, then it
is not necessary for
t_{i,2}
and
t_{i,3}
to fire will the full

\tfrac{1}{k}
fraction: The
2k
occurrences of
t_{i,5}t_{i,6}
can compensate
for whatever

were not able to add to
\mathsf{step}_{i}
. Therefore, we can fire

and
t_{i,4}
so as to only subtract a very small value (we will choose

) from

. This allows us to fire all copies of

and instead of turning

from

(as we must in a

only halves the values in the high-precision counters, instead of applying an exponential function to their denominator.
