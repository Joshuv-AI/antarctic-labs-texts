First record of nymphal larvae of Alofia sp. (Pentastomida: Sebekidae) in Megalechis thoracata from the Western Amazon (Brazil)
Authors: Lucena R Virgilio, Ricardo M Takemoto, Mayara D Passere, Alessandra V de Oliveira, Dionatas U O Meneguetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPentastomids of the family Sebekidae utilize fish as intermediate hosts. In Brazil, records of the genus Alofia Giglioli, 1922 remain scarce and fragmented. This study reports the first occurrence of Alofia sp. nymphs in the armored catfish Megalechis thoracata (Valenciennes, 1840) from the western Amazon, Acre State, Brazil. Of 33 fish examined, 42.42% were parasitized, with a mean intensity of 1.57 parasites per infected host and a mean abundance of 0.66. Nymphs were recovered from the coelomic cavity, associated with the serosa of internal organs. Morphological identification was supported by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), revealing diagnostic characters consistent with the genus. Molecular analysis of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (cox1) gene generated a 647 bp sequence, representing one of the few available molecular records for Alofia in the Neotropical region. The observed genetic divergence highlights the potential presence of an uncharacterized lineage. These findings expand the known host range and geographical distribution of Alofia, while providing novel molecular data that contribute to the systematics and phylogenetic understanding of Neotropical Sebekidae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08691-5.Keywords: Fish parasites, Sebekidae, Molecular phylogeny, cox1, Amazon Basin

Introduction

Pentastomids are highly specialized endoparasites, phylogenetically recovered as a subclass of maxillopod crustaceans, that predominantly infect the respiratory tract of terrestrial vertebrates, especially reptiles (Self 1969; Abele et al. 1989). Within the family Sebekidae, species exhibit a complex heteroxenous life cycle in which fish act as essential intermediate hosts, harboring infective larval stages (nymphs) until they are predated by definitive hosts, such as crocodilians (Winch and Riley 1986; Riley 1994). In the Neotropical region, pentastomid biodiversity is exceptionally high; however, their distribution patterns and host–parasite interactions remain poorly understood, particularly within the complex drainage systems of the Amazon basin (Almeida and Christoffersen 1999).

Despite advances in integrative taxonomy, the molecular characterization of Sebekidae in South America remains limited. Most available data are restricted to a reduced number of taxa and geographic regions, resulting in a fragmented understanding of phylogenetic relationships and lineage diversity within the group, particularly in the Neotropical region (Riley 1994; Christoffersen and Assis 2013; Giesen et al. 2013; Almeida et al. 2020; Montes et al. 2023). This gap becomes even more evident in the Amazon basin, where the high diversity of hosts and ecological complexity suggest the occurrence of a large number of lineages that remain undescribed or molecularly uncharacterized (Poulin 2007; Zuanon et al. 2015; Virgilio et al. 2022).

The genus Alofia Giglioli, 1922 is distinguished by specific morphological traits in the hooks and oral cavity of its nymphs (Riley 1994). Despite its ecological relevance within aquatic trophic chains, records of Alofia in South American freshwater fish are fragmented and often limited to sporadic reports (Giesen et al. 2013; Almeida et al. 2020). The armored catfish Megalechis thoracata (Valenciennes 1840) is a widely distributed Siluriform in the Amazon, playing a central role in energy flow between benthic environments and top predators (Ferreira et al. 2019). Considering that pentastomids may influence host fitness and act as sensitive indicators of environmental conditions, documenting their occurrence in new hosts and localities is fundamental for advancing Neotropical parasitology and understanding ecological dynamics in aquatic systems (Poulin 2007; Rohde 2005).

Historically, pentastomid taxonomy has relied on morphological characters that often show considerable overlap among species, leading to persistent identification challenges (Junker 2002). Consequently, the integration of molecular tools—specifically the analysis of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (cox1) gene—is essential to provide a more robust taxonomic framework and clarify the phylogenetic position of Neotropical lineages (Folmer et al. 1994; Kumar et al. 2016). However, for the genus Alofia, the available molecular data remain extremely limited, being predominantly represented by sequences of the cox1 gene deposited in public databases such as GenBank, while other markers remain poorly explored. This scenario reflects the general scarcity of molecular data for Sebekidae, particularly in the Neotropical region (Junker 2002; Giesen et al. 2013; Almeida et al. 2020; Montes et al. 2023). This context reinforces the use of cox1 as a standard marker for phylogenetic comparisons and molecular identification within the group, while also highlighting the need to expand multilocus datasets to achieve better taxonomic and evolutionary resolution (Giesen et al. 2013; Almeida et al. 2020; Montes et al. 2023).

Thus, this study reports the first occurrence and molecular characterization of Alofia sp. parasitizing M. thoracata in the western Amazon. By combining detailed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with new molecular data, we aim to expand the known geographical distribution of this genus and contribute to the systematic resolution of the family Sebekidae in one of the most biodiverse regions of the world.

Materials and methods

Study area and fish sampling

A total of 33 specimens of Megalechis thoracata (Valenciennes, 1840) were collected from the western Amazon basin, specifically in the Juruá River drainage, Acre State, Brazil. Four rivers in the basin were sampled: Ipixuna River, Juruá River, Crôa River, and Moa River (Fig. 1). Sampling was conducted during the dry season (September 2024), characterized by reduced water levels and increased habitat fragmentation, which favors fish capture and parasite detection (Junk et al. 1989; Lowe-McConnell 1999).

Map of the study area in the western Amazon, Acre State, Brazil, showing the collection sites of Megalechis thoracata Valenciennes 1840

Fish were captured using gillnets and cast nets under a permanent license for the collection of zoological material issued by the Instituto Chico Mendes de Conservação da Biodiversidade (ICMBio; license no. 87064—1/2023). Sampling sites were selected based on the known occurrence of the host and the accessibility of the locations during the sampling period.

After capture, specimens were transported in isothermal boxes to the laboratory, where they were euthanized in accordance with current ethical protocols for the use of animals in scientific research, following guidelines for the use of vertebrates in research (CONCEA 2018). The examined hosts showed total length ranging from 14.0 to 18.5 cm (mean = 16.5 ± 2.0 cm) and body weight ranging from 60.0 to 120.0 g (mean = 85.0 ± 25.0 g).

Parasitological examination and morphological characterization

Each fish was measured (standard length) and weighed prior to necropsy. The coelomic cavity, gills, and internal organs (liver, stomach, and intestines) were examined under a stereomicroscope, following standard protocols in fish parasitology studies (Eiras et al. 2006).

Pentastomid nymphs were carefully removed, washed in saline solution (0.9% NaCl), and fixed in 70% ethanol for light microscopy or in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for scanning electron microscopy (SEM), following standard procedures for helminth preparation (Amato et al. 1991).

Parasitological descriptors, including prevalence, mean intensity, and mean abundance, were calculated according to the standardized definitions proposed by Bush et al. (1997).

A total of eight nymphs were used for morphological analysis and morphometric measurements under light microscopy. Specimens were cleared in Hoyer’s medium or phenol to allow visualization of internal structures and sclerotized hooks, following standard techniques in morphological studies of parasitic invertebrates (Anderson 2000). Measurements were obtained using a light microscope equipped with a digital camera and image analysis software.

For SEM analysis, two specimens were processed following standard protocols. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, dried at the critical point with CO₂, and sputter-coated with gold (20 nm thickness), as described for ultrastructural studies of parasites (Bozzola and Russell 1999). Observations and micrographs were obtained using a scanning electron microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

Taxonomic identification was performed based on diagnostic keys and morphological descriptions available in the specialized literature (Riley 1994; Junker 2002).

Molecular and phylogenetic analyses

Total genomic DNA was extracted from individual nymphs (preserved in 95% ethanol) using a commercial DNA extraction kit (DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany)), following the manufacturer’s instructions. A partial fragment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (cox1) gene was amplified using the universal primers LCO1490 and HCO2198 (Folmer et al. 1994). PCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 23 µL, containing 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase (5 U/µL, Invitrogen), Tris-KCL (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.4 with 50 mM KCl), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM of each dNTP, 0.2 µM of each primer, template DNA (10 ng), and Milli-Q water to complete the final volume. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by five cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 45 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 48 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Amplicons were purified and one specimen from the Moa River was sequenced using an automated sequencer. The resulting sequences were edited in BioEdit (Hall 1999) and aligned using CLUSTAL W (Thompson et al. 1994) in MEGA7 (Kumar et al. 2016). All available cox1 mtDNA sequences for Sebekidae were added to the analyses (Supplementary Material 1), and a sequence from Raillietiella indica (MK208827) was used as an outgroup. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA7, based on the best nucleotide substitution model (HKY + G + I) selected by the same program. Nodal support was estimated using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Genetic distances (p-distance) were calculated in MEGA7 to evaluate interspecific and intraspecific divergence. The final phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL (Letunic and Bork 2021). Access to the genetic heritage was authorized by the National System for the Management of Genetic Heritage and Associated Traditional Knowledge – SisGen No. AD84C4F.

Results and discussion

A total of 33 specimens of Megalechis thoracata (Valenciennes, 1840) were examined, with a mean total length of 16.5 ± 2.0 cm and mean body weight of 85.0 ± 25.0 g. Among these, 14 individuals were infected with pentastomid nymphs, totaling 22 specimens of Alofia sp. The prevalence was 42.42%, with a mean intensity of infection of 1.57 and a mean abundance of 0.66.

The nymphs were found exclusively in the coelomic cavity, frequently associated with the serosa of the liver and intestine and were not observed in other organs. This infection pattern is consistent with the typical heteroxenous life cycle of Sebekidae, in which freshwater fish act as intermediate hosts by accumulating infective larval stages that are subsequently transmitted to definitive hosts through trophic interactions (Winch and Riley 1986; Riley 1994; Christoffersen and Assis 2013; Almeida et al. 2020).

The relatively high prevalence observed in this study, compared to other Amazonian hosts, suggests that M. thoracata may play a relevant role in local transmission dynamics. In parasites with complex life cycles, hosts with higher infection levels tend to contribute disproportionately to the maintenance of the parasitic cycle (Poulin 2007; Rohde 2005; Giesen et al. 2013).

Morphological analysis, based on eight specimens, revealed diagnostic characters consistent with the genus Alofia. The nymphs were whitish and elongated, with body length ranging from 6.5 to 9.5 mm and width from 0.7 to 1.1 mm. The oral cadre presented an anterior opening, with nearly parallel lateral margins, measuring 0.065–0.087 mm in width and 0.130–0.189 mm in length (Figs. 2 and 3). Four robust nymphal hooks surrounded the oral cadre (Fig. 3A), exhibiting a dorsally convex morphology, while the fulcra were elongated, measuring 0.190–0.330 mm (anterior) and 0.165–0.335 mm (posterior). The trunk presented 60–65 annuli bearing annular spines (Figs. 2 and 3B–C), measuring 0.020–0.026 mm, with chloride cell pores irregularly distributed. The posterior extremity was slightly conical, terminating in a central anus (Fig. 3D). These characters correspond to the diagnostic set described for larvae of the genus Alofia, corroborating the taxonomic identification at the generic level (Riley 1994; Junker 2002; Giesen et al. 2013; Almeida et al. 2020). Scanning electron microscopy confirmed these characters, revealing the organization of the oral cadre, hook morphology, and distribution of annular spines (Fig. 3), reinforcing the consistency of the morphological identification. Minor variations observed may reflect intraspecific variability or ontogenetic differences.

Molecular analysis yielded a 647 bp sequence of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (cox1) gene. After editing and alignment with other sequences available in GenBank, the final alignment comprised 516 bp. BLAST analysis (Altschul et al. 1990) revealed that our sequence shares 88.43% similarity with another Alofia sequence available in GenBank (MH484065). However, the high genetic divergence observed between the analyzed Amazonian specimen and known Alofia lineages from other South American basins may indicate reproductive isolation or the existence of a cryptic species complex (Table 1). The molecular data available for the group are still scarce and limited, highlighting the need for additional studies, especially in the Amazon region. In the phylogenetic reconstruction, the specimens formed a monophyletic clade with high bootstrap support, positioned as a sister group to other Neotropical sebekids (Fig. 4).

The occurrence of Alofia sp. in M. thoracata provides new insights into the transmission of pentastomids in Amazonian environments. The benthic habit and omnivorous diet of this species, associated with low-flow environments, favor the incidental ingestion of eggs or infective stages present in the sediment (Ferreira et al. 2019), a pattern widely documented for parasites with trophic transmission (Poulin 2007; Rohde 2005). This pattern has also been observed in other Neotropical fishes, in which feeding behavior directly influences parasite acquisition (Giesen et al. 2013; Montes et al. 2023).

Furthermore, the spatial overlap between the habitat of M. thoracata and areas with high crocodilian density favors the completion of the pentastomid life cycle, as these reptiles act as definitive hosts and become infected through the predation of parasitized fish (Winch and Riley 1986; Riley 1994; Almeida et al. 2020).

From a biogeographical perspective, this record expands the known distribution of Alofia spp. in South America, particularly in the Western Amazon. Previous records in Brazil are mainly concentrated in the Pantanal and isolated localities, indicating that the distribution of the genus is likely broader but still underestimated in Neotropical freshwater systems (Riley 1994; Giesen et al. 2013; Almeida et al. 2020; Montes et al. 2023). The high hydrological connectivity of the Amazon basin favors the dispersal of infective stages over large spatial scales, contributing to lineage diversification and expansion of the host spectrum.

Regarding the taxonomic status, although the molecular divergence may suggest the presence of an undescribed species, we adopt a conservative approach by identifying the specimens as Alofia sp. This decision is consistent with best practices in pentastomid taxonomy, as descriptions based exclusively on larval stages may result in taxonomic instability and later synonymization. Thus, the present study provides a robust baseline for future integrative approaches.

Conclusion

This study reports the first occurrence of Alofia sp. in M. thoracata from the western Amazon, expanding the known host range and distribution of the genus. The combined morphological and molecular evidence supports its identification and suggests the presence of a distinct lineage within Neotropical Sebekidae. These findings highlight the ecological importance of Siluriformes in pentastomid transmission and reinforce the need for integrative approaches to resolve the taxonomy and diversity of the group in the Amazon region.
