False-positive mammogram has a negative impact on quality of life: A cross-sectional study among Jordanian women at King Abdullah University Hospital
Authors: Maha M. Gharaibeh, Eyhab A. Elobeid, Sammah M. Ahmmed, Jehad Z. Fataftah, Ruba A. Khasawneh, Mohammad A. Rawashdeh, SayfAldeen M. El-Heis, Safa I. Gharaibeh, Mwaffaq A. Elheis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women in Jordan. While mammography is an effective tool for early detection, false-positive results can cause significant psychological and physical distress. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of false-positive mammograms on the quality of life among Jordanian women attending King Abdullah University Hospital. This cross-sectional study included 672 women aged 40 to 74 who underwent mammography at King Abdullah University Hospital. Of these, 168 had abnormal mammography findings (cases), and 504 had normal results (controls). Quality of life was assessed using the EuroQol five-dimensional health-related quality-of-life questionnaire-Visual Analog Scale, focusing on 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Statistical analysis included chi-square tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with a linear regression model to evaluate the association between false-positive results and quality of life. Women with false-positive mammograms showed significantly lower quality of life scores in 4 of the 5 EuroQol five-dimensional health-related quality-of-life questionnaire domains compared to controls. Anxiety and problems with usual activities were significantly associated with abnormal mammography findings. Current smokers had a 3.25 times higher likelihood of abnormal findings. No significant differences were found in mobility or breast cancer history between cases and controls. False-positive mammograms negatively impact on the quality of life, particularly in terms of anxiety and daily functioning. These findings underscore the need for careful consideration of the psychological effects of breast cancer screening and the importance of patient-centered care in the screening process.

1. Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequent diagnosed cancer among women in Jordan with estimated 994 new cases of breast cancer in 2012, it accounts for 37.3% of the new cancer cases.[1] Breast cancer death rate decreased by 1.3%/yr in whites and by 1.1% in blacks from 2007 to 2011 due to improvement in early detection, through mammogram, and treatment. Mammogram efficacy has been shown to reduce cancer mortality through clinical trials.[2]

Mammography is an effective procedure to discover breast cancer at early stages, but it may be criticized due to the harm it may cause. Harms of mammography have been defined in some articles to include physical, emotional, social, financial, intergenerational, or psychological.[3] Moreover, the value of a screening tool is determined by the relationship between the nature of the disease being screened and the performance characteristics of the screening tool, namely sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity or what is called true-positive rate, can be calculated by dividing the number of breast cancer cases detected through mammogram by the total number of breast cancer cases in the population tested. Specificity or can be called true-negative rate; is proportion of woman does not have breast cancer among screened women without breast cancer.[4] Health is defined as “A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease and infirmity.”[5] The anxiety level for women who received negative results was compared to the anxiety level of false-positive result in 1 study. The anxiety level was measured on a scale of 0 to 5; where 0 equal to “not at all anxious” and 5 equal to “very anxious.” Based on the measurement scale used and dimensions of the anxiety; the anxiety levels of the women who received false positives ranged from averages of 1.5 to 2 which is about 3 times higher than for women whose mammograms had been negative from the start. but they were still only “moderately anxious” and not “very anxious” (level 5) as much as 8 weeks after the resolution of the false positive.[6] The study was criticized for its small sample size of women who received false-positive results (36 women) and its time. The study was conducted in Switzerland during the introduction of mammography concept where the level of anxiety could be influenced by the unfamiliarity of these participants with screening programs.

The key questions on the effectiveness/benefits side of any intervention include: What should be the type of measure – for example Why use QALYs? How should benefits be described (and by whom)? How should benefits be valued? Whose values should be used? And how should benefits (QALYs) be aggregated across individuals? Patients may have a different perception of false-positive utility value than the physician or health care provider has. In this era where we have patient-centered care and shared decision making, those guidelines must consider the patient preference in weighing the risk versus benefit for any evidence- based recommendation.[7]

Patients were found to overestimate benefits and underestimate harms for around two-thirds of studies interventions, regardless of the type of service, patient population or clinical context.[8] In 1 study that assessed attitudes about different types of cancer screening, respondents who had had a false-positive result described the experience as “very scary” or the “scariest time of my life,” but 98% of them were glad to have had the initial test.[9] In 1 survey, for instance, although >99% of women were aware that mammography can produce false-positive results, only 38% believed that this possibility ought to be considered in decisions about having the test.[10]

The reason behind that could be that we, as human beings, are considered as risk aversions. We would like to accept the anxiety now rather than being regretted later. And when we face a danger, we are more sensitive to the mere possibility of its occurrence than its actual probability.

Quality-adjusted life year (QALY) as defined by Neumann et al “A measure of health outcome which assigns to each period of time a weight, ranging from 0 to 1, corresponding to the health-related quality of life during that period, where a weight of 1 corresponds to optimal health, and a weight of 0 corresponds to health state judged to be equivalent to death.”[11] The utility value assigned to QALY can be derived using different techniques such as time to trade-off (TTO) or standard gamble (SG).

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants and study design

A purposive sample of consecutive women aged 40 to 74 presenting to our mammography clinic at King Abdullah University Hospital (KAUH) for follow-up of abnormal mammograms were selected. For each participant with abnormal findings on screening mammography, another 3 women with normal findings who are screened the same day and at our screening clinic as the woman with the abnormal result will be recruited. Women with abnormal finding meaning that they are classified as Bi-Rad III, IV, or V. Pregnant women were excluded from this project as they may have other factors may contaminate their revealed preference, such as hormonal changes. At our clinic, participants that met the eligibility criteria were identified by clinical staff.

2.2. Ethical approval

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at King Abdullah University Hospital (KAUH) and Jordan University of Science and Technology (JUST) approved the study. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, 1975. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

2.3. Data collection

The original version of EuroQol five-dimensional health-related quality-of-life questionnaire (EQ-5D) was developed by a multidisciplinary research from 7 centers across 5 countries (Euroqol Group 1990) to address the core of domains common to other generic health status questionnaires and which reflects the most important concerns of patients themselves.[12] The current version is a 2 pages questionnaire. The first page is composed of 5 dimensions, each with 3 levels of severity that defines 243 states. The 5 dimensions of EQ-5D are mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. Each health state has a predefined tariff value using regression analysis, elicited from the general population, to estimate a scoring algorithm to value all 243 health states. The second page is composed of Visual Analog Scale (VAS).[13]

Patients will be given the EQ-5D-VAS questionnaire and asked to complete it during routine visits to the clinics, in a quiet room with no help from nursing staff, family, or friends. Participants will be asked to indicate “which statements best describe their own health state today.” Each state is referred to in terms of a 5-digit code. For example, state 11111 indicates no problems on any of the 5 dimensions, while state 23131 indicates some problems with mobility, severe problems with self-care, no problems with performing usual activities, severe pain or discomfort and no anxiety or depression.

Patients will be asked to complete the VAS task with a value that describes the patient’s health status today. This methodology will be able to estimate the decrement in health status associated with false-positive mammogram by converting the harm as measured by EQ-5D into a utility value.

2.4. Statistical analysis

A sample size of 168 was defined assuming power of 0.8, to capture a mean difference of 0.2 with a standard deviation of 0.4. We analyzed the differences in baseline covariates between the 2 screening groups (false positive and normal) using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables. False positive (FP) means that the patient suspected of having some sort of breast cancer (classified as Bi-Rad III, IV, or V) and the biopsy is negative. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to capture mean difference in utility value between both groups. Subgroup analysis was conducted per age specific, abnormal mammogram result versus comparator to make sure that the decrement in health quality of life is due to false-positive mammogram only and not due to other confounders such as age. A linear regression model will be used to evaluate change in EQ-5D score by health status (FP vs no normal), controlling for characteristics variables, as reported at baseline. Statistical significance was set a priori as P < .05. Patients’ characteristics reflecting utility value will be identified.

3. Results

A total of 672 samples were enrolled, of which 168 had abnormal findings on screening mammography and were defined as cases, and 504 had normal mammography findings and were defined as controls. The mean age of participants was 50.0 (7.0) years, with no significant difference between cases and controls. Majority of included participants 93% (n = 623) were married, 37 (5.5%) were divorced of which 36 were from the control group (P-value < .001). Most participants completed graduate education (n = 437, 65%) with no significant difference between cases and controls. The majority of participants were nonsmokers (78%, n = 522). Diabetes mellitus was seen in 31 (4.6%) of patients, 105 (16%) patients had hypertension, and 56 (8.4%) had both diabetes and hypertension. BC history was positive in 187 (28%) of patients. 4. Discussion

Breast cancer cases are rising by the day, but mortality rates are declining thanks to the advancements of science, the increase in the reported breast cancer cases does not necessarily indicate a weakness in the health care system but it is rather a strength. Mammography has proven over the decades its crucial role in early screening, and its effectiveness in detecting tumors before the size of 2 cm.[14] Breast cancer incidences are directly influenced by race, income, and age. In some developing countries, breast cancer cases and mortality rates among women are rapidly increasing, predominated by Asian countries. While in developed countries and primarily linked to specific races like White and Hispanic women, incidences decreased compared to other races like African American women who reported a slightly higher rate of occurrence.[2,15,16] Age is a crucial element in multiple aspects of breast cancer including diagnosis, treatment, and survival rates, they differ in different age groups. McGuire et al stated that diagnosis of breast cancer among young-aged women is directly linked to increased risk of mortality.[17] Kehm et al also reports that young women are at an increased risk of developing breast cancer than older women.[18] In another study conducted by Chen et al he discussed how diagnosis is affected and influenced by age, he reported that women being at 60 years of age, or more was a sole determinant of poor prognosis, young women also displayed similar chances, he added, with middle-aged women having the highest survival rates.[19]

While mammography screening has demonstrated its potential to prevent deaths from breast cancer among women, there is no evidence supporting a reduction in overall mortality. Moreover, guidelines like those in Brazil advised against the use of mammogram screening in women below the age of 50 and nothing against women above 50, the guideline even encouraged them to get screened every 2 years.[20] In the United States, there are 2 guidelines for breast cancer screening: the US Preventive Service Task Force (USPSTF) and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG). For women between the ages of 50 and 74, UPSTF recommends routine mammograms once every 2 years in the absence of symptoms but less routinely for women <50 years of age. No recommendation is made about mammograms in women over the age of 75, as very little research has been performed in this age group. ACOG recommends that mammogram screening to be offered every other year to women beginning at age 40.[21] The USPSTF guidelines claimed that the annual mammogram screening as associated with more false-positive result that may affect the quality of life of the women screened and induce anxiety to her on top of the extra cost associated with annual mammogram test. The ACOG guidelines made their conclusion based on the high incidence of breast cancer in the United States and didn’t give high weight to the false-positive cases as long as the screening test is able to detect most cancer cases at an early stage. The tradeoff between false-positive results and early detection of breast cancer have never been investigated before, as a result different women may be exposed to conflicting mammogram screening test. In 1 article, a disutility value range between 0 and 0.013 QALY’s were calculated for the first and subsequent years after diagnosis of ductal carcinoma in situ or invasive local, regional, or distant breast cancer using the EuroQol-5D values for Swedish women with breast cancer at different stages. The study claimed that mammogram is not cost effective if the disutility value of false-positive mammogram included in the analysis.[22]

Additionally, the screening process contributes to overdiagnosis and numerous false-positive mammograms, negatively impacting the quality of life for many women.[23,24] Kerlikowske et al discussed a similar dilemma, where 90% of positive mammography patients turned out to be cancer free upon confirmational examinations.[25] On the contrary, a systematic review by Otto et al concluded that a routinely mammography screening significantly decreased mortality rates of breast cancer among 55 to 75 aged women.[26] In addition, Ultrasound (US) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) proved to be more effective and accurate in spotting invasive breast cancer, but at the cost of overestimating its magnitude.[27] The EQ-5D stands out as one of the frequently employed generic questionnaires worldwide to evaluate health-related quality of life. Its application extends to assessing health status, serving as an outcome measure in economic evaluations, participating in health surveys targeting the general population, and progressively becoming integrated into routine data collection within clinical and healthcare settings.[28,29] In this study, we investigated the effects of mammography findings and the 5 domains of quality of life from the EQ-5D questionnaire in a population of Jordanian females.

Our study included a total of 672 samples, with 168 identified with abnormal findings on screening mammography, and 504 with normal mammography findings. Digital breast tomosynthesis, extensively utilized in the United States, has been linked to heightened cancer detection and lowered recall rates.[30,31] A study by Conant et al showed that screening with digital breast tomosynthesis could result in a more favorable balance of benefits, involving the early detection of clinically significant breast cancers and, ultimately, a decrease in breast cancer mortality, compared to associated harms such as false-positive results.[32] In a study by Honig et al demonstrated that digital breast tomosynthesis mammography showed a significant reduction in the recall rate when compared to full-field digital mammography.[33] Additionally, prior mammograms and a history of benign breast biopsy were correlated with a decreased incidence of false-positive results.[34]

Demographic characteristics revealed that the mean age of participants was 50.0 years, with no significant difference between cases and controls. Notably, a statistically significant difference was observed in the marital status of participants who were divorced, with a higher prevalence in the control group (P-value < .001). A study by Keen et al showed that the rate of cancer detection exhibits an upward trend from 1.9 per 1000 at the age of 40 to 7.2 per 1000 at the age of 50. The positive predictive value of a screening mammogram experiences an ascent from 1.3% at the age of 40 to 9.8% at the age of 60.[35] This entails that the incidence of breast cancer, the rate of cancer detection, and all additional performance measures in secondary screening mammography experience significant growth with advancing age.[36,37]

Health-related factors such as smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and a history of breast cancer were also considered. Among the participants, 78% were nonsmokers, 4.6% had diabetes mellitus, 16% had hypertension, and 28% had a positive history of breast cancer. The logistic regression model revealed several significant associations between certain variables and the likelihood of abnormal mammography findings. Current smokers had a 3.25 increased likelihood of abnormal mammography findings. In terms of quality of life, problems with usual activities and anxiety were associated with 2.65 and 2.99 increased likelihood in patients with abnormal mammography findings. Pain problems also increased the likelihood by 1.66. However, there was no significant association between mobility problems, breast cancer history, and mammography findings.

5. Conclusion

To conclude, our findings suggest that age and smoking status play significant roles in the likelihood of abnormal mammography findings. Additionally, abnormal mammography findings significantly impact specific aspects of quality of life, such as usual activities, anxiety, and pain. Our study provides important information for healthcare professionals to consider when assessing breast health and underscores the importance of addressing lifestyle factors and quality of life in breast cancer screening and prevention efforts.
