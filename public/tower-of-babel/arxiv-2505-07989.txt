rd2d : Causal Inference in Boundary Discontinuity Designs
Matias D. Cattaneo, Rocio Titiunik, Ruiqi (Rae) Yu

Abstract
Boundary Discontinuity (BD) designs are used in empirical research to learn about causal treatment effects along a continuous assignment boundary defined by a bivariate score. These designs are also known as multi-score regression discontinuity (RD) designs, and include geographic RD designs as a prominent example. This article introduces rd2d , a statistical software package for R , Python , and Stata that implements local polynomial estimation and inference for BD designs using either the bivariate score or a univariate signed distance-to-boundary score. The software covers sharp and fuzzy BD designs, providing automatic bandwidth selection, robust bias-corrected pointwise inference, uniform confidence bands, cluster-robust inference with joint or separate fitting conventions, covariate-adjusted efficiency improvements, mass-point checks, and covariance regularization, among other features. We illustrate the package with an empirical application to Opportunity Zones, where eligibility has a strong first-stage effect on designation but no significant effects on early workplace-job growth.



Keywords
: boundary discontinuity designs, regression discontinuity designs, treatment effects, nonparametric inference, R, Python, Stata.

1
Introduction

Regression Discontinuity (RD) designs are commonly used for treatment effect estimation and causal inference in quantitative sciences
(see
Cattaneo and Titiunik, 2022
, and references therein)
. In their canonical form, each unit
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
is assigned to control (
T_{i}=0
) or treatment (
T_{i}=1
) according to the discontinuous rule
T_{i}=\mathds{1}(X_{i}\geq c)
, where
X_{i}
denotes a scalar score variable,
c
denotes a scalar cutoff, and
\mathds{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function. The key idea underlying all RD designs is that units with a score near the cutoff determining treatment assignment are comparable in terms of all pretreatment observables and unobservable characteristics, the only difference being that some units are assigned to control (
X_{i}<c
) while others are assigned to treatment (
X_{i}\geq c
). Therefore, in the absence of score manipulation, units with scores near but on different sides of the cutoff can be used as counterfactuals for each other to learn about causal treatment effects.

Boundary discontinuity (BD) designs generalize the canonical RD design by allowing treatment assignment to be determined by a multidimensional score. The most common case is a bivariate score
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{1i},X_{2i})^{\top}
and a one-dimensional assignment boundary
\mathcal{B}
that separates treated and control regions. These designs are also called multi-score RD designs
(
Papay et al., 2011
;
Reardon and Robinson, 2012
;
Wong et al., 2013
)
, and include geographic RD designs as a prominent special case
(
Keele and Titiunik, 2015
;
Keele et al., 2015
;
Keele and Titiunik, 2016
;
Keele et al., 2017
;
Galiani et al., 2017
;
Rischard et al., 2021
;
Diaz and Zubizarreta, 2023
)
. Figure
1(a)
illustrates a two-score eligibility rule with a kinked boundary, while Figure
1(b)
illustrates a generic geographic boundary. Empirical examples include education, taxation, political communication, labor markets, and place-based policy applications; see, among others,
Londoño-Vélez et al. (2020)
and
Jardim et al. (2024)
. See
Cattaneo et al. (2020)
;
Cattaneo et al. (2024b)
for a two-part practical introductory monograph.

While classical RD designs based on a scalar score are well understood, BD designs have only recently received systematic methodological treatment. Empirical work has used different reductions of the boundary problem, often without formal guidance about which causal parameter is being estimated, how treatment-effect heterogeneity along the boundary should be summarized, or when inference is valid uniformly over many boundary locations.
Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
and
Cattaneo et al. (2026a)
address this gap by studying two leading approaches:

•

the
location-based approach
, which uses the bivariate score directly and fits local polynomial regressions on the two sides of
\mathcal{B}
; and

•

the
distance-based approach
, which replaces the bivariate score with a scalar signed distance to each point on
\mathcal{B}
and then applies univariate local polynomial methods.

Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
develops location-based identification, estimation, bandwidth selection, and inference results, both pointwise for each point on
\mathcal{B}
and uniformly over
\mathcal{B}
.
Cattaneo et al. (2026a)
develops the corresponding distance-based theory and clarifies when distance-to-boundary analyses recover the same causal targets. A central lesson is that distance-based methods can be useful and interpretable, but their validity depends more directly on the geometry of the boundary: kinks and other irregularities can generate additional bias even when the underlying bivariate conditional expectations are smooth. The location-based approach therefore provides the main benchmark whenever the bivariate score is available, while distance-based methods are valuable as a simpler implementation, a diagnostic, or a necessary alternative when only signed distances can be used.

This article introduces
rd2d
, software for
R
,
Python
, and
Stata
that implements these recent methodological developments for applied work. The package estimates the
Boundary Average Treatment Effect Curve
(BATEC), the
Weighted Boundary Average Treatment Effect
(WBATE), and the
Largest Boundary Average Treatment Effect
(LBATE), together with fuzzy BATEC, fuzzy WBATE, and fuzzy LBATE for settings with imperfect treatment take-up. It provides automatic bandwidth selection, robust bias-corrected pointwise confidence intervals, uniform confidence bands, heteroskedasticity-robust and cluster-robust variance estimators, joint and separate fitting conventions, covariate-adjusted efficiency improvements, mass-point checks, covariance regularization for uniform inference, and derivative estimation for location-based methods. The robust bias-correction and coverage-error ideas build on
Calonico et al. (2014)
,
Calonico et al. (2018)
,
Calonico et al. (2020)
, and
Calonico et al. (2022)
, adapted here to boundary-indexed local polynomial estimators. The
R
implementation centers on
rd2d()
and
rdbw2d()
for location-based analysis, and
rd2d.distance()
and
rdbw2d.distance()
for distance-based analysis; the
Python
and
Stata
implementations follow the same conceptual interface with language-specific syntax.

Two options deserve emphasis. First, the default option
fitmethod = "joint"
uses the treatment-interacted local regression representation when forming degrees-of-freedom adjustments, cluster-robust covariance matrices, and bandwidth-selection variance constants. This convention matters when a cluster, such as a school district, municipality, county, or tract group, can contain observations on both sides of the boundary: the cluster score should aggregate all local influence contributions from that cluster rather than treating the control and treated sides as independent samples. The option
fitmethod = "separate"
is retained for analyses that require the earlier side-specific convention. Second, the option
covs.eff
allows users to supply predetermined covariates for efficiency adjustment. These covariates enter with common coefficients across treatment sides and are propagated through both fixed-bandwidth estimation and automatic bandwidth selection.

The article is organized around the empirical steps in a BD analysis. We first clarify the causal target: a curve of local effects along the boundary, together with weighted and extremal summaries of that curve. We then show how the same targets are estimated using location-based and distance-based methods, how fuzzy designs decompose into reduced-form, first-stage, and Wald-ratio objects, and how software choices such as bandwidth selection, fitting convention, covariate adjustment, covariance storage, and boundary-grid construction affect empirical reporting. Throughout, an Opportunity Zones application provides the notation, code snippets, figures, and numerical output.

1.1
Running Example: Opportunity Zones

We use the Opportunity Zones program as a running empirical example. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 allowed governors to nominate eligible low-income census tracts for Qualified Opportunity Zone designation. Following
Corinth and Feldman (2024)
, we construct a bivariate boundary discontinuity design in which eligibility is determined by two tract-level scores: a poverty-rate threshold and a median-family-income threshold. Actual designation is not mechanically assigned to all eligible tracts, which makes this a fuzzy BD design.

The public analysis dataset contains one observation per census tract. In the notation used below,
Y_{i}
is the 2017–2019 change in
\log(1+\text{workplace jobs})
,
X_{1i}
is the poverty rate minus 20,
X_{2i}
is 80 minus the tract’s median-family-income percentage,
T_{i}
is low-income-community eligibility, and
W_{i}
is Qualified Opportunity Zone designation. The assignment boundary is an L-shaped frontier with a kink at
(0,0)
. We evaluate treatment-effect curves at 31 boundary points: the kink and 15 points on each arm of the boundary, avoiding the far extremes where local effective sample sizes are thin.

Setup

We employ standard potential outcomes notation
(
Hernán and Robins, 2020
, and references therein)
. Suppose that
(Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1),\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top})^{\top}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n
, is a random sample, where
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
denote the scalar potential outcomes for unit
i
under control and treatment assignment, respectively. Units are assigned to control group or treatment group according to their bivariate location score
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{1i},X_{2i})^{\top}
relative to a known one-dimensional boundary
\mathcal{B}
splitting the support of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
in two disjoint regions:
\mathcal{A}_{0}
denotes the control region, and
\mathcal{A}_{1}
denotes the treatment region. Thus,
\mathcal{B}=\setbd(\mathcal{A}_{0})\cap\setbd(\mathcal{A}_{1})
, where
\setbd(\mathcal{A}_{t})
denotes the boundary of the set
\mathcal{A}_{t}
. We use the convention that boundary points belong to the treatment region, so
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{A}_{0}=\emptyset
. The observed response variable is
Y_{i}=\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{0})Y_{i}(0)+\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})Y_{i}(1)
, and
T_{i}=\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})
denotes treatment assignment. Figure
1
gives two graphical examples.

For implementation, the continuous assignment boundary
\mathcal{B}
is first discretized into
J
cutoff points
\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{b}_{J})^{\top}
with
\mathbf{b}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}
for all
j=1,\dots,J
. Then, the empirical analysis is conducted pointwise for each cutoff or uniformly over all cutoffs, employing either the bivariate location score
\mathbf{X}_{i}
directly, or an induced univariate distance to each cutoff point. See
Cattaneo et al. (2024b, Section 5)
for an introductory discussion.

In the Opportunity Zones example,
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{1i},X_{2i})^{\top}
contains the poverty and income scores shown in Figure
2
;
\mathbf{b}
contains the 31 evaluation points on the L-shaped frontier;
T_{i}
records eligibility;
W_{i}
records actual designation; and
Y_{i}
is early workplace-job growth. We use this notation in the code snippets below.

2.1
Sharp BD Designs

In a sharp BD design, assignment to the treatment region determines treatment exposure. The main causal parameter is the
Boundary Average Treatment Effect Curve
(BATEC):

\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)|\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{x}],\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

The BATEC is a curve of local treatment effects indexed by points on the assignment boundary. It is useful when the policy rule creates many local comparisons rather than a single cutoff, because it allows researchers to inspect treatment-effect heterogeneity along the boundary before reporting lower-dimensional summaries.

In
rd2d
, the BATEC is obtained from the main output of a sharp BD design fit. The option
params.cov = "main"
stores the cross-boundary covariance matrix needed for uniform bands and aggregate summaries.

The BATEC captures treatment effect heterogeneity along the assignment boundary, but researchers often want scalar summaries of that heterogeneity. For a weight function
w:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, the
Weighted Boundary Average Treatment Effect
(WBATE) is

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{B}}\tau(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\int_{\mathcal{B}}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}.

Different weights encode different policy or scientific targets. Equal weights over a pre-specified boundary grid produce a simple average of the BATEC values, while application-specific weights can emphasize boundary regions with more policy relevance or more population mass. A related scalar summary is the
Largest Boundary Average Treatment Effect
(LBATE),

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\tau(\mathbf{x}),

which summarizes the largest treatment effect along the boundary.

The WBATE aggregates the BATEC into a weighted average effect, while the LBATE reports the largest value of the curve. Thus, WBATE is an aggregation target and LBATE is an extremal heterogeneity target. In
rd2d
, WBATE estimation and inference are implemented via the optional argument
WBATE = w
in the
summary()
method for
rd2d
and
rd2d.distance
objects, while LBATE summaries are requested with
LBATE = TRUE
. Uniform bands, WBATE, and LBATE require the relevant cross-boundary covariance matrix to be stored.

2.2
Fuzzy BD Designs

In a fuzzy BD design, the assignment rule changes the probability of treatment receipt but does not perfectly determine it. This is the structure of the Opportunity Zones example: eligibility
T_{i}
changes the probability of designation
W_{i}
, but many eligible tracts are not designated and some policy choices are made after eligibility is determined. Let
T_{i}=\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})
denote assignment. Following
Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
, let
W_{i}(t)
denote potential treatment status under assignment
t\in\{0,1\}
, and let
Y_{i}(t,w)
denote the potential outcome under assignment
t
and treatment status
w
. The observed treatment status and outcome are

\displaystyle W_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{0})W_{i}(0)+\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})W_{i}(1),

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{0})Y_{i}(0,W_{i}(0))+\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})Y_{i}(1,W_{i}(1)).

The reduced-form or intention-to-treat BATEC, abbreviated ITT, and the first-stage BATEC, abbreviated FS, are

\displaystyle\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1,W_{i}(1))-Y_{i}(0,W_{i}(0))\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{x}],

\displaystyle\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[W_{i}(1)-W_{i}(0)\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{x}],\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

Under the standard continuity conditions for fuzzy BD designs, these curves are identified by the observed boundary discontinuities

\displaystyle\tau_{A}(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{u}]-\lim_{\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{u}],\qquad A\in\{Y,W\}.

When the first stage is nonzero and the standard fuzzy BD design assumptions hold locally, the fuzzy BATEC is the Wald ratio

\displaystyle\zeta(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})}{\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

We refer to
\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
as the ITT BATEC,
\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
as the FS BATEC, and
\zeta(\mathbf{x})
as the fuzzy BATEC. The ITT curve captures heterogeneity in the effect of assignment on the outcome, the FS curve captures heterogeneity in compliance or take-up induced by assignment, and the fuzzy BATEC rescales the outcome discontinuity by the local first stage. The Wald ratio is a fuzzy BD estimand; interpreting it as a complier-average treatment effect additionally requires local exclusion, monotonicity, and a nonzero first stage near the boundary point.

Fuzzy analogues of WBATE and LBATE replace
\tau(\mathbf{x})
with
\zeta(\mathbf{x})
. For a weight function
w:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, the fuzzy WBATE is

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{B}}\zeta(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\int_{\mathcal{B}}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}},

and the fuzzy LBATE is

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\zeta(\mathbf{x}).

These summaries play the same role as in the sharp BD design: fuzzy WBATE aggregates the fuzzy BATEC over the boundary, while fuzzy LBATE reports the largest value of the fuzzy treatment-effect curve.

reports the FS BATEC, and
output = "main"
reports the fuzzy BATEC. The option
bwparam = "main"
targets automatic bandwidth selection to the fuzzy BATEC, while
bwparam = "itt"
targets the ITT BATEC.

3
Location-Based Methods

Location-based methods use the bivariate score
\mathbf{X}_{i}
directly and estimate side-specific local polynomial approximations at the boundary. In implementation these side-specific fits are represented through a joint treatment-interacted regression, which is equivalent for point estimation and is used for covariance calculations. Under the usual continuity assumptions invoked in canonical RD designs
(
Hahn et al., 2001
)
, the BATEC in a sharp BD design is identified by

\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x})=\mu_{1}(\mathbf{x})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B},

where
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t)|\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{x},T_{i}=t]
,
t\in\{0,1\}
, are smooth functions on their corresponding sides of the boundary. This is the standard continuity argument for RD designs, adapted to a bivariate score and a one-dimensional assignment boundary
(
Papay et al., 2011
;
Reardon and Robinson, 2012
;
Wong et al., 2013
;
Keele and Titiunik, 2015
)
. Identification of WBATE and LBATE follows from identification of
\tau(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathcal{B}
. In fuzzy BD designs, the same continuity argument identifies the reduced-form BATEC
\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
and the first-stage BATEC
\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
; when
\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
is bounded away from zero on the target boundary, the fuzzy BATEC
\zeta(\mathbf{x})=\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})/\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
and its WBATE and LBATE analogues are identified, with the complier-average interpretation requiring the additional conditions discussed in Section
2
. Following
Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
, this section focuses on estimation, bandwidth selection, and uncertainty quantification for location-based methods.

3.1
Treatment Effect Estimation: Methods

For each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}
, the location-based estimator can be written as a single local polynomial regression with treatment-side interactions. Let
\mathbf{r}_{p}(\mathbf{u})
collect all monomials
u_{1}^{a_{1}}u_{2}^{a_{2}}
with
a_{1}+a_{2}\leq p
, starting with the constant term, and let
\mathfrak{p}_{p}=(2+p)(1+p)/2
. With
T_{i}=\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})
, define the augmented basis

\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{u};T)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{p}(\mathbf{u}/h)&T\mathbf{r}_{p}(\mathbf{u}/h)\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

For any multi-index
\boldsymbol{\nu}
with
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|\leq p
, let
\mathbf{e}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
denote the length-
2\mathfrak{p}_{p}
vector that selects the coefficient on the treatment-interaction monomial indexed by
\boldsymbol{\nu}
in
\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{u};T)
; in particular,
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}
selects the treatment-interaction intercept. The local polynomial estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\mathfrak{p}_{p}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}\big)^{2}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}),

where
K_{h}(\mathbf{u})=K(u_{1}/h,u_{2}/h)/h^{2}
. The common scalar bandwidth
h
is used for exposition; in implementation the score coordinates can be standardized, and separate bandwidths can be used by coordinate and by treatment side.
For interpretation, decompose the coefficient vector as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x})=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{0}(\mathbf{x})^{\top},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})^{\top})^{\top}
with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{p}_{p}}
. Then,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
describes the control-side local polynomial, while
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})
describes the effects due to treatment assignment. Thus the BATEC estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

This interaction representation is useful for software implementation because additional terms, such as predetermined covariates, can be added to the same weighted regression while leaving the treatment-side intercept interaction as the treatment-effect parameter.

The leading MSE terms depend on the local design, conditional variance, and curvature of the regression functions on each side of the boundary. Let
\sigma_{t}^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{V}[Y_{i}(t)|\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{x}]
. Using the augmented basis, define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})^{\top}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x})\right],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}

\displaystyle=h^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{1};T_{i})\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{2};T_{i})^{\top}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{1})K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{2})\sigma_{T_{i}}^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right].

Then the variance constant for the BATEC is

V_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}.

Using multi-index notation, the leading bias is

\displaystyle B_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{1}\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=p+1}\frac{\mu_{t}^{(\mathbf{k})}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathbf{k}!}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})\left((\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x})/h\right)^{\mathbf{k}}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x})\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{t})\right].

Under the regularity conditions in
Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
,
\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
is pointwise and uniformly consistent for
\tau(\mathbf{x})
as
h\to 0
and
nh^{2}
grows. The pointwise conditional MSE expansion has the usual bias-variance form,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})-\tau(\mathbf{x}))^{2}|\mathbf{X}]\approx_{\mathbb{P}}h^{2p+2}B_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+\frac{1}{nh^{2}}V_{\mathbf{x}},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B},

with the leading bias and variance constants defined above. Integrating the same expansion over
\mathcal{B}
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})-\tau(\mathbf{x}))^{2}|\mathbf{X}]w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\approx_{\mathbb{P}}h^{2p+2}\int_{\mathcal{B}}B_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{nh^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}V_{\mathbf{x}}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Ignoring higher-order terms gives the infeasible pointwise MSE and integrated MSE bandwidths

\displaystyle h_{\mathtt{MSE},\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{2V_{\mathbf{x}}}{(2p+2)B_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/(2p+4)},\qquad h_{\mathtt{IMSE}}=\left(\frac{2\int_{\mathcal{B}}V_{\mathbf{x}}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{(2p+2)\int_{\mathcal{B}}B_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/(2p+4)}.

The pointwise selector targets a particular boundary location, while the integrated selector targets the full boundary curve or a chosen subset of it.

3.3
Statistical Inference: Methods

For a chosen bandwidth and polynomial order, define the feasible covariance kernel
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}
. For example, in the sharp BD design,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}=\frac{1}{nh^{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}.

Here

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})^{\top}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}),

and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}=\frac{h^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{1};T_{i})\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{2};T_{i})^{\top}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{1})K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{2})\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\big],

where
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is the residual from the augmented local polynomial fit at
\mathbf{x}
. These objects are also what make uniform bands and aggregate standard errors depend on covariance across boundary points, not only on pointwise standard errors.
The pointwise Wald interval is

\displaystyle\widehat{\CI}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\left[\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})-q_{\alpha}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}^{1/2},\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})+q_{\alpha}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}\right],\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

For pointwise inference,
q_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
. For uniform inference over
\mathcal{B}
, the critical value is chosen to approximate the distribution of
\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}|\widehat{Z}(\mathbf{x})|
, where
\widehat{Z}(\mathbf{x})
is a conditionally mean-zero Gaussian process with covariance induced by
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}
. In practice, this process is simulated on the boundary grid
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{J}
.

Because MSE-optimal bandwidths leave non-negligible smoothing bias,
rd2d
implements robust bias-corrected (RBC) inference, following the local-polynomial RD literature
(
Calonico et al., 2014
;
Calonico et al., 2018
;
Calonico et al., 2020
;
Calonico et al., 2022
)
. With a
p
th order polynomial used for point estimation and a
q
th order polynomial, typically
q=p+1
, used for inference, let
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathtt{bc}}(\mathbf{x})
denote the bias-corrected estimator and let
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{bc},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}
denote the corresponding covariance kernel, including the extra variability from estimating the leading bias. The RBC interval is

\displaystyle\widehat{\CI}_{\mathtt{bc},\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\left[\widehat{\tau}_{\mathtt{bc}}(\mathbf{x})-q_{\alpha}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{bc},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}^{1/2},\widehat{\tau}_{\mathtt{bc}}(\mathbf{x})+q_{\alpha}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{bc},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}\right].

The resulting intervals and bands are generally not centered at the original BATEC point estimator
\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
.

3.5
WBATE and LBATE: Estimation and Inference

The WBATE is fit by plugging the estimated BATEC into the boundary integral. With normalized weights
\int_{\mathcal{B}}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=1
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

On a grid, this is approximated by
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_{j}\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{b}_{j})w(\mathbf{b}_{j})
, with quadrature weights
\omega_{j}
normalized so the weighted sum integrates to one. The WBATE covariance is the corresponding double integral of the BATEC covariance kernel,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}w(\mathbf{x}_{1})w(\mathbf{x}_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{2},

or its grid analogue. Aggregation changes the effective variance order: the pointwise BATEC estimator has variance of order
(nh^{2})^{-1}
, while the WBATE has variance of order
(nh)^{-1}
because only boundary locations within an
O(h)
neighborhood have non-negligible covariance. Thus, the WBATE behaves like a one-dimensional nonparametric functional even though each local fit uses a two-dimensional score. Inference uses a normal critical value, with RBC implemented by applying the same plug-in formula to the bias-corrected BATEC and its covariance.

The LBATE is the supremum of the BATEC, with plug-in estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\approx\max_{1\leq j\leq J}\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{b}_{j}).

Because LBATE is an extreme functional,
Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
recommends reporting it together with the full BATEC and WBATE. Its confidence interval is obtained by projecting the uniform confidence band for
\tau(\mathbf{x})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\CI}_{\alpha,\mathtt{LBATE}}=\left[\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})-q_{\alpha}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}\right),\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})+q_{\alpha}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}\right)\right],

using the uniform critical value
q_{\alpha}
. The interval is generally conservative because it inherits simultaneous coverage for the full BATEC.

3.6
Imperfect Compliance

For fuzzy BD designs, the same local polynomial machinery is applied to the outcome
Y_{i}
and the treatment status
W_{i}
. Let
\widehat{\tau}_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
and
\widehat{\tau}_{W}(\mathbf{x})
denote the reduced-form and first-stage BATEC estimators. The fuzzy BATEC estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{Y}(\mathbf{x})}{\widehat{\tau}_{W}(\mathbf{x})}.

Here
\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
is the ITT BATEC and
\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
is the FS BATEC. The ratio
\zeta(\mathbf{x})
is the fuzzy BATEC parameter; it has a local complier-average causal interpretation only under additional fuzzy BD design assumptions such as a local exclusion restriction, monotonicity, and a nonzero first stage. For related discussion of causal identification in fuzzy multidimensional RD designs, see
Choi and Lee (2023)
,
Schwarz et al. (2025)
,
Jiang and Zhu (2026)
, and
Cattaneo et al. (2016)
;
Cattaneo et al. (2021)
; for univariate fuzzy RD designs, see
Arai et al. (2022)
and references therein.

Cattaneo et al. (2026b)
uses the linearization

\displaystyle\widehat{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})-\zeta(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{w}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathfrak{T}(\mathbf{x})+\mathfrak{R}_{\mathtt{F}}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathfrak{w}(\mathbf{x})=\left[\frac{1}{\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})},-\frac{\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})}{\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})^{2}}\right]^{\top},

where
\mathfrak{T}(\mathbf{x})=(\widehat{\tau}_{Y}(\mathbf{x})-\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x}),\widehat{\tau}_{W}(\mathbf{x})-\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x}))^{\top}
. The remainder
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathtt{F}}(\mathbf{x})
is negligible when the first stage is uniformly bounded away from zero and the reduced-form and first-stage estimators are uniformly consistent. Thus, estimation and inference for fuzzy BATEC, fuzzy WBATE, and fuzzy LBATE follow by applying the sharp BD design results to the linearized combination
\mathfrak{w}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathfrak{T}(\mathbf{x})
.

The fuzzy covariance kernel is

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{F},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{w}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\Omega}_{Y,Y,\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}&\widehat{\Omega}_{Y,W,\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}\\
\widehat{\Omega}_{W,Y,\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}&\widehat{\Omega}_{W,W,\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathfrak{w}}(\mathbf{x}_{2}),

where
\widehat{\mathfrak{w}}(\mathbf{x})
replaces
\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
and
\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
by their local polynomial estimates. Fuzzy BATEC intervals and bands use
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{F},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}
in place of the sharp BD design covariance kernel. Fuzzy WBATE uses

\widehat{\zeta}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{F,WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathtt{F},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}w(\mathbf{x}_{1})w(\mathbf{x}_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{2},

with a normal critical value. Fuzzy LBATE uses
\widehat{\zeta}_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})
and projects the fuzzy BATEC uniform band in the same way as for LBATE in a sharp BD design.

, and

. The option

targets the fuzzy BATEC, while

targets the reduced-form BATEC.

3.7
Regularization Strategies and Implementation Choices

The package
rd2d
implements several numerical safeguards and reporting choices that are important in empirical applications. These choices do not change the target parameters; they stabilize the finite-sample implementation of BATEC, WBATE, LBATE, and their fuzzy analogues, and make the resulting diagnostics easier to interpret.

•

Boundary grid and target set
. Uniform bands, WBATE, and LBATE are computed on the user-supplied boundary grid
b
. For a piecewise linear boundary, a natural default is to place grid points approximately equally spaced in arc length, while forcing kinks, corners, or substantively important boundary points to be included. If inference is desired only on a trimmed subset
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, for example to avoid endpoints or poorly supported corners, the grid should represent
\mathcal{B}_{0}
and the reported estimands should be interpreted as applying to that trimmed target set.

•

Grid sensitivity
. Since the theoretical uniform band is indexed by the continuum
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}
but the software simulates the process on a finite grid, the grid should be fine enough that the discretized supremum is stable. A useful diagnostic is to refine the grid and check whether the simulated critical value, the confidence band, and aggregate summaries are essentially unchanged. In applications with heterogeneous effects or irregular boundaries, this check is more informative than relying on a fixed number of grid points.

•

Bandwidth choices along the boundary
. Pointwise selectors target a particular boundary location, whereas integrated selectors target the full boundary curve or a chosen subset. For uniform inference and graphical displays, a common boundary-wide bandwidth often gives smoother covariance estimates and simpler interpretation. Point-specific bandwidths can also be used, but the covariance matrix used for uniform bands and aggregate summaries must be computed using the same bandwidth choices. The default standardization option,
stdvars = TRUE
, helps make scalar bandwidths comparable when the two score coordinates have different units.

•

Joint versus separate fitting
. The option
fitmethod = "joint"
is the default because it matches the treatment-interacted regression used to define the estimator and because it aggregates cluster scores across both sides of the boundary. This distinction matters when the same cluster can contain control and treated units near a boundary point. The option
fitmethod = "separate"
is available when users want side-specific finite-sample adjustments for comparison with earlier implementations.

•

Covariate efficiency adjustment
. The option
covs.eff
supplies predetermined covariates used to improve precision without changing the treatment-effect estimand. The same adjustment is used in automatic bandwidth selection, pointwise inference, and covariance matrices for uniform bands and aggregate summaries. The safeguards
covs.drop
and
covs.tol
control how redundant covariate columns are handled after residualization.

•

Small bias regularization
. Approximate MSE and IMSE bandwidth choices require nonzero leading bias terms. A very small estimated bias can therefore produce an excessively large bandwidth. To avoid this problem, the software adds a regularization term to the estimated bias denominator:

\displaystyle h_{\mathtt{MSE},\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{2\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{x}}}{(2p+2)(\widehat{B}_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+s\cdot\mathbb{V}[\widehat{B}_{\mathbf{x}}])}\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/(2p+4)}.

The analogous IMSE selector regularizes
\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{B}_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
by the integrated variance of the bias estimator. The factor
s
is controlled by
scaleregul
; its default is
3
when bandwidth selection is called through
rd2d()
and
1
in standalone calls to
rdbw2d()
.

•

Minimum sample size
. The option
bwcheck
enlarges the selected or user-provided bandwidth until a minimum number of observations is included in the estimation region on each side of the cutoff. For
rd2d()
, the default is
\text{{bwcheck}}=50+p+1
; standalone calls to
rdbw2d()
use
\text{{bwcheck}}=20
.

•

Mass points in
\mathbf{X}_{i}
. The option
masspoints = "check"
reports the number of unique score values and warns when duplication is substantial. With
masspoints = "adjust"
, bandwidths are regularized so that kernels contain a minimum number of unique observations. With
masspoints = "off"
, the potential presence of mass points is ignored.

•

Covariance regularization for uniform bands
. The estimated covariance matrix is converted to a correlation matrix before Gaussian simulation. In finite samples this matrix can be nearly singular or slightly fail to be positive semidefinite because of numerical precision, closely spaced grid points, or weak local support. The implementation symmetrizes the matrix, truncates eigenvalues below a small tolerance, rescales the diagonal entries to one, and warns when the adjustment is nonnegligible. This eigenvalue regularization follows the same logic as other covariance regularization methods for uniform inference
(
Cattaneo et al., 2024a
)
.

•

Gaussian simulation error
. The option
repp
controls the number of Gaussian simulations used for uniform critical values and LBATE inference. The default is
repp = 1000
in the summary methods. When the critical value is central to the empirical conclusion, increasing
repp
and checking that the reported band is stable is a simple numerical diagnostic.

•

Local support and design stability
. Boundary points with little one-sided support, very unbalanced kernel mass across the two sides, or poorly conditioned local design matrices can lead to unstable BATEC estimates and covariance estimates. The
bwcheck
and
masspoints
options address two common symptoms, but researchers should also inspect the score scatterplot, side-specific local sample sizes, and sensitivity to trimming weakly supported regions of the boundary. These checks matter most near kinks, corners, and endpoints.

•

Reporting practice
. Empirical analyses should report the target boundary set, the evaluation grid, the bandwidth selector, the fitting convention, any clustering or covariate adjustment, whether covariance regularization produced a warning, and the simulation count used for uniform bands. Basic sensitivity checks with alternative grid sizes, bandwidth choices, and, when relevant, boundary trims help separate genuine treatment-effect heterogeneity from discretization, weak support, or numerical instability.

The empirical implementation can be organized as follows. First, construct and plot the boundary grid, making sure that kinks, endpoints, and substantively important locations are represented. Second, estimate the location-based BATEC with covariance storage for the outputs that will be used for uniform bands, WBATE, or LBATE, using
fitmethod = "joint"
unless the goal is a comparison with the earlier convention. Third, add
cluster
and
covs.eff
when the design calls for clustered inference or pre-treatment efficiency adjustment. Fourth, in fuzzy BD designs, inspect the FS BATEC before interpreting the fuzzy Wald curve, because weak or sign-changing first stages make the ratio unstable. Fifth, report pointwise intervals, uniform bands, WBATE, and LBATE for the main target, and use the side-specific effective sample sizes and bandwidth table to flag poorly supported boundary regions. Finally, compare the location-based analysis with distance-based estimates and with modest changes to the grid, bandwidth selector, and trimmed target set.

3.8
Derivative Estimation

Location-based methods can also be used to estimate derivatives of the side-specific regression functions and their discontinuities at the boundary. For a multi-index
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
with
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\leq p
, define the derivative BATEC

\displaystyle\tau^{(\boldsymbol{\nu})}(\mathbf{x})=\mu_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\nu})}(\mathbf{x})-\mu_{0}^{(\boldsymbol{\nu})}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B},

where
\mu_{t}^{(\boldsymbol{\nu})}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the partial derivative of
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x})
of order
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. These derivative targets describe how the discontinuity changes locally with the score coordinates; for example,
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(1,0)
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(0,1)
correspond to the two coordinate-specific slopes of the BATEC at the boundary.

The plug-in estimator uses the same local polynomial coefficients as the level estimator. Using the selector
\mathbf{e}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
introduced above,

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{(\boldsymbol{\nu})}(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\nu}!\,h^{-|\boldsymbol{\nu}|}\mathbf{e}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B},

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}!=\nu_{1}!\nu_{2}!
. Directional derivatives, including derivatives along the boundary, are obtained by applying the corresponding linear combination to the first partial derivatives.

In
rd2d
, derivative targets are requested with
deriv
; for example,
deriv = c(1, 0)
estimates the discontinuity in the first partial derivative with respect to the first score coordinate. Directional derivatives along user-supplied vectors, such as boundary tangent vectors, are requested with
tangvec
, a
J\times 2
matrix with one vector for each boundary evaluation point; when supplied,
tangvec
overrides
deriv
. In fuzzy BD designs, the derivative options also apply to the reduced-form and first-stage output tables. Ratios involving derivative discontinuities should be interpreted separately from the level fuzzy BATEC
\zeta(\mathbf{x})=\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})/\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
.

3.9
Covariate Adjustment

Predetermined covariates can improve precision in boundary discontinuity designs when they explain residual variation in the outcome. The implementation in
rd2d
follows the covariate adjustment logic developed for canonical RD designs by
Calonico et al. (2019)
. Let
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
be a
d_{Z}
-vector of predetermined covariates. We use a tilde to denote covariate-adjusted quantities. The covariate-adjusted fit uses the same augmented local-polynomial basis as above and adds
\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
to the weighted least-squares regression:

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\mathfrak{p}_{p}},\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{p,h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x};T_{i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\big)^{2}K_{h}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}).

The covariate-adjusted BATEC estimator is
\widetilde{\tau}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x})
. Under the usual smoothness and no-manipulation conditions, this adjustment does not change the estimand; it can reduce variance when the covariates are predictive of the outcome.

In the software, covariates are supplied through
covs.eff
. The covariate coefficient is common across treatment sides, while the local polynomial basis remains side-specific. The implementation residualizes the covariates on the local polynomial basis and carries the resulting adjusted influence functions through pointwise inference, uniform bands, WBATE, LBATE, fuzzy ratio inference, and automatic bandwidth selection. Thus, when
h
is omitted, the same covariate adjustment used in estimation is also used by
rdbw2d()
. The options
covs.drop
and
covs.tol
control safeguards for rank-deficient covariate matrices after residualization.

4
Distance-Based Methods

Distance-based methods replace the bivariate score
\mathbf{X}_{i}
by a scalar signed distance score constructed separately for each boundary point. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})^{\top}\in\mathcal{B}
, let

\displaystyle D_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\big(\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1})-\mathds{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{0})\big)\mathcal{d}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathcal{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a distance function such as the Euclidean distance
\mathcal{d}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{x})=\|\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\|
. Thus,
D_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
identifies observations on the treated side of the boundary and
D_{i}(\mathbf{x})<0
identifies observations on the control side. Conditional on
\mathbf{x}
, the problem resembles a univariate RD design with cutoff zero, while varying
\mathbf{x}
over
\mathcal{B}
recovers a curve of local effects. In the Opportunity Zones example, each column of the distance matrix is the signed distance from a tract’s poverty-income score to one of the 31 boundary points shown in Figure
2
.

This construction is attractive when the full bivariate score is unavailable, difficult to release, or inconvenient to smooth directly. It also gives an intuitive set of RD plots and diagnostics for each boundary point. The cost is that distance-based methods use less location information than the location-based procedures in Section
3
; as a result, their interpretation depends more explicitly on the geometry of
\mathcal{B}
and on the chosen distance function.

Distance-based identification requires an additional step because the scalar score averages potential outcomes over level sets of the distance function. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}
, define

\displaystyle\theta_{t,\mathbf{x}}(r)=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}\mid D_{i}(\mathbf{x})=r\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(t)\mid\mathcal{d}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{x})=|r|,\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}\big],\qquad r\in\mathcal{I}_{t},

where
\mathcal{I}_{0}=(-\infty,0)
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}=[0,\infty)
. The induced signed-distance treatment-effect parameter is

\displaystyle\vartheta(\mathbf{x})=\theta_{1,\mathbf{x}}(0)-\theta_{0,\mathbf{x}}(0),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

Cattaneo et al. (2026a, Theorem 1)
gives conditions on the data-generating process, the assignment boundary, the kernel, and the distance function under which

\displaystyle\vartheta(\mathbf{x})=\tau(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

The result is obtained by representing the induced conditional expectations as integrals over shrinking submanifolds around
\mathbf{x}
; without those restrictions on the distribution, the boundary geometry, and the distance function, the distance-based estimand need not coincide with the BATEC.

The same logic identifies aggregate and fuzzy targets. Since
\vartheta(\mathbf{x})=\tau(\mathbf{x})
pointwise along
\mathcal{B}
under the distance-based identification conditions, WBATE and LBATE can be represented by applying the same integral and supremum functionals to
\vartheta(\mathbf{x})
. In fuzzy BD designs, applying the signed-distance construction to
A\in\{Y,W\}
gives

\displaystyle\vartheta_{A}(\mathbf{x})=\theta_{A,1,\mathbf{x}}(0)-\theta_{A,0,\mathbf{x}}(0),\qquad\xi(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\vartheta_{Y}(\mathbf{x})}{\vartheta_{W}(\mathbf{x})}.

Under the fuzzy identification conditions in
Cattaneo et al. (2026a, Section SA-6)
,
\vartheta_{A}(\mathbf{x})=\tau_{A}(\mathbf{x})
for
A\in\{Y,W\}
, and if the first stage is nonzero then
\xi(\mathbf{x})=\zeta(\mathbf{x})
. Thus, aggregating
\xi(\mathbf{x})
or taking its supremum gives distance-based representations of the causal fuzzy WBATE and fuzzy LBATE.

The point estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\vartheta}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B},

where, for
t\in\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{p}(D_{i}(\mathbf{x})/h)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\big)^{2}K_{h}(D_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\mathds{1}(D_{i}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{I}_{t}),

with
\mathbf{e}_{0}
the intercept selector,
\mathbf{r}_{p}(u)=(1,u,u^{2},\ldots,u^{p})^{\top}
the usual univariate polynomial basis,
K_{h}(u)=K(u/h)/h^{2}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{0}=(-\infty,0)
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}=[0,\infty)
. The normalization by
h^{2}
reflects the dimension of the original bivariate score; it does not change the weighted least squares solution because it multiplies all weights by the same positive constant.

Cattaneo et al. (2026a)
establishes pointwise and uniform consistency, distributional approximations, and confidence intervals and bands for
\widehat{\vartheta}(\mathbf{x})
. The main practical distinction relative to location-based methods is the bias behavior near nonsmooth regions of the boundary. If
\mathcal{B}
is sufficiently smooth, the distance-induced regression functions have the usual local-polynomial approximation properties, and robust bias correction proceeds with
q=p+1
by default. If
\mathcal{B}
has a kink or another local irregularity, the induced univariate regression can be only Lipschitz even when the underlying bivariate regression functions are smoother. Then the leading bias is of order
h
uniformly near the irregularity, and increasing
p
does not remove it.

4.2
WBATE and LBATE

The causal WBATE and LBATE remain the functionals of the BATEC defined in Section
2
. Under the distance-based identification conditions,
\tau(\mathbf{x})=\vartheta(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}
, so the same targets can be written using the signed-distance estimand. With normalized weights
\int_{\mathcal{B}}w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=1
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\tau(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\vartheta(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\qquad\widehat{\vartheta}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\vartheta}(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

On the evaluation grid,
\widehat{\vartheta}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}
is implemented as a weighted average of the distance-based BATEC estimates. The covariance is the corresponding double integral of the distance-based covariance kernel,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\Xi}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}}w(\mathbf{x}_{1})w(\mathbf{x}_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{2},

or its grid analogue. As shown in
Cattaneo et al. (2026a, Section SA-4)
, the WBATE has effective variance order
(nh)^{-1}
under local boundary-measure regularity, even though each pointwise distance-based fit has the bivariate variance order
(nh^{2})^{-1}
when
d=2
.

Similarly, under distance-based identification, the causal LBATE can be written as

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\tau(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\vartheta(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\widehat{\vartheta}_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\vartheta}(\mathbf{x}).

Its confidence interval is obtained by projecting the uniform confidence band for
\vartheta(\mathbf{x})
, as in
Cattaneo et al. (2026a, Section SA-5)
. The resulting interval is conservative in general because it inherits simultaneous coverage for the full distance-based BATEC.

summary()
method once the relevant covariance matrix has been stored:

4.3
Imperfect Compliance

Distance-based methods extend to fuzzy BD designs by applying the same signed-distance local polynomial procedure to the outcome and to treatment receipt. For
A\in\{Y,W\}
, let

\displaystyle\widehat{\vartheta}_{A}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A,1}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A,0}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{A,t}(\mathbf{x})
is the local polynomial fit above with
A_{i}
replacing
Y_{i}
. The distance-based fuzzy BATEC estimator is the Wald ratio

\displaystyle\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\widehat{\vartheta}_{Y}(\mathbf{x})}{\widehat{\vartheta}_{W}(\mathbf{x})},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}.

The reduced-form output estimates
\vartheta_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
, the first-stage output estimates
\vartheta_{W}(\mathbf{x})
, and the main fuzzy output estimates
\xi(\mathbf{x})
. Under the identification conditions summarized above, these correspond to
\tau_{Y}(\mathbf{x})
,
\tau_{W}(\mathbf{x})
, and the fuzzy BATEC
\zeta(\mathbf{x})
.

Inference is based on the ratio linearization in
Cattaneo et al. (2026a, Section SA-6)
. With

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}(\mathbf{x})=\left[\frac{1}{\vartheta_{W}(\mathbf{x})},-\frac{\vartheta_{Y}(\mathbf{x})}{\vartheta_{W}(\mathbf{x})^{2}}\right]^{\top},

the first-order component of
\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{x})-\xi(\mathbf{x})
is the linear combination of the reduced-form and first-stage errors indexed by
\mathfrak{w}(\mathbf{x})
. Thus, the fuzzy covariance kernel is obtained by sandwiching the joint covariance kernel of
(\widehat{\vartheta}_{Y}(\mathbf{x}),\widehat{\vartheta}_{W}(\mathbf{x}))^{\top}
with this gradient vector. This delta-method covariance is used for pointwise intervals, uniform confidence bands, fuzzy WBATE inference, and fuzzy LBATE inference.

With the same weight normalization, the causal fuzzy WBATE and fuzzy LBATE remain the functionals of
\zeta(\mathbf{x})
defined in Section
2
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\zeta(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\qquad\zeta_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\zeta(\mathbf{x}).

The corresponding distance-based plug-in estimators replace
\zeta(\mathbf{x})
by
\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{x})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\xi}_{\mathtt{WBATE}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\qquad\widehat{\xi}_{\mathtt{LBATE}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}}\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{x}).

Under fuzzy distance-based identification,
\xi(\mathbf{x})=\zeta(\mathbf{x})
, so these are plug-in estimators of
\zeta_{\mathtt{WBATE}}
and
\zeta_{\mathtt{LBATE}}
. In the Opportunity Zones application, the FS curve measures how eligibility changes the probability of designation, the ITT curve measures how eligibility changes early workplace-job growth, and the fuzzy curve rescales the latter by the former.

In the software, supplying
fuzzy
changes
main
to the fuzzy Wald effect, while
itt
and
fs
report the reduced-form and first-stage BATECs. The option
bwparam = "main"
selects automatic bandwidths for the linearized fuzzy ratio, while
bwparam = "itt"
targets the reduced-form outcome.

5
Empirical Results

We now return to the Opportunity Zones running example introduced in Section
1.1
. The empirical analysis reports the three objects in a fuzzy BD design: the reduced-form BATEC for early workplace-job growth, the first-stage BATEC for Qualified Opportunity Zone designation, and the fuzzy BATEC obtained by dividing the reduced form by the first stage. We report the fuzzy curve as a Wald estimand; a complier-average causal interpretation would require the additional local exclusion, monotonicity, and nonzero-first-stage conditions discussed in Section
2
. For each object, we compare the location-based fit, which uses the bivariate poverty-income score directly, with the distance-based fit, which uses the signed Euclidean distance to each evaluation point on the boundary. Both fits use the package’s automatic default choices, including
fitmethod = "joint"
; the only additional request is to store covariance matrices for uniform bands and aggregate summaries. We do not use
covs.eff
in this illustration, so that the reported results correspond to the unadjusted fuzzy BD estimators.

Figure

gives the main visual comparison. The rows correspond to the reduced form, first stage, and fuzzy effect; the columns correspond to the location-based and distance-based methods. Both methods find a strong first stage along most of the boundary: eligibility sharply increases the probability of Qualified Opportunity Zone designation. In contrast, the reduced-form BATEC for early workplace-job growth is small relative to its uncertainty, and the fuzzy BATEC is correspondingly imprecise. The distance-based first stage is slightly less uniform near some boundary locations, which is consistent with the sensitivity to boundary geometry discussed in Section

Conclusion

This article introduced
rd2d
, software for causal inference in boundary discontinuity designs. The package implements location-based and distance-based local-polynomial methods, robust bias-corrected pointwise inference, uniform confidence bands, BATEC, WBATE, LBATE, fuzzy BATEC, fuzzy WBATE, and fuzzy LBATE. It also includes joint cluster-robust fitting, the earlier separate fitting convention, automatic bandwidth selection, and covariate efficiency adjustment. The current software ecosystem includes
R
,
Python
, and
Stata
implementations with aligned syntax and output conventions. The Opportunity Zones application illustrates how the package can separate the reduced-form BATEC, the first-stage BATEC, and the fuzzy BATEC along a multidimensional assignment boundary. Replication codes and related information are available at
https://rdpackages.github.io/
.
