Introduction

We consider integer linear programs (ILP) in equality form

\min\left\{c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x:Ax=b,x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\right\}

(1)

where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Integer linear programs are a widely used model to describe problems in combinatorial optimization and
the algorithms available for them are used as general purpose optimization suites.
Unlike its continuous counterpart, where
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, integer linear programming is NP-complete even in the case of
m=1
. Hence, theoretical research is mainly focused on identifying structural assumptions, under which ILPs can still be
solved efficiently. Here, two dominant directions can be distinguished.

Based on the geometry of numbers, Lenstra
[
15
]
showed that ILPs of the form
\min\{c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x:Ax\leq b,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}
can be solved in polynomial time for constant
n
, in fact, that they are fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) in parameter
n
.
Note that this form is equivalent to (
1
) by a standard transformation, but this would led to an increase in
parameter
n
.
Subsequent improvements by Kannan
[
13
]

and most recently Reis and Rothvoss
[
20
]
lead to the currently best running time of
(\log n)^{O(n)}\poly(I)
, where
I
is the input size.

The second direction is mainly based on dynamic programming approaches and goes
back to Papadimitriou
[
18
]
. He considered (
1
) in the case when the absolute values of the entries of
A
and
b
are bounded by
\Delta
and designed a pseudopolynomial time algorithm when
m
is fixed. This generalizes the classical pseudopolynomial time algorithm for Knapsack problems
[
1
]
. Later, his algorithm was improved by Eisenbrand and Weismantel
[
6
]
. The current best algorithm is due to Jansen and Rohwedder
[
10
]
with a running time of
O(\sqrt{m}\Delta)^{2m}\poly(I)
.
In the latter two results the bound of
\Delta
is only required on
A
, but not
b
.
Similar approaches were also applied successfully to constraint matrices
A
, where both dimensions
n
and
m
can
be large but satisfy certain structures of their non-zero entries, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Space efficiency of integer linear programs.

NP-completeness tells us that any algorithm that solves general ILPs needs to have an exponential running
time at least in some parameter unless P
=
NP. But not only running time, also space usage can be a limiting factor.
It is well known that for practical ILP solvers based on Branch-and-Bound it is often a bottleneck to store
the list of active nodes, see e.g.
[
21
, Section 7.5.1]
.
This motivates the theoretical question of whether the aforementioned
results can also be achieved in polynomial space or, more generally, what tradeoffs between running
time and space one can achieve.

Frank and Tardos
[
8
]
showed that Lenstra’s algorithm and the improvement by Kannan
with running time
n^{O(n)}\poly(I)
can be implemented in polynomial space.
For the improvement by Reis and Rothvoss this is not known.

Regarding the algorithms based on dynamic programming, the space usage is also pseudopolynomial, which
is inherent in the method. Alternative, space-efficient methods are known for Subset Sum, a very
restricted variant of ILP, where the task is to find a solution to

\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:a^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x=b\}
with
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Note that here
m=1
and there is
no objective function.
For the Subset Sum problem, Lokshtanov and Nederlof
[
16
]
introduced an algebraization technique and designed an
\tilde{O}(n^{3}b\log{b})
time and
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
space algorithm (see also
[
12
,
7
]
). This algorithm was later improved by Bringmann
[
2
]
, and the current best low-space algorithm for Subset Sum is due to Jin et al.
[
11
]
, which runs in
\tilde{O}(nb)
time and
O(\log{n}\log\log{n}+\log{b})
space.
Despite the intensive research on polynomial space algorithms for Subset Sum, little is known for
more general problems.
The seminal result of Lokshtanov and Nederlof
[
16
]
is the only one that considers the more general Knapsack problem, that is,
\max\{c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x:a^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x\leq b,x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
with
c,a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, and
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Nevertheless, their method suffers from pseudopolynomial factor in both
b
and
\lVert c\rVert_{\infty}
in the running time, see
[
16
, Theorem 4.5]
, whereas the classical dynamic programming algorithm only requires pseudopolynomial time in one of the two parameters. A
\poly(n,b)
time and polynomial space algorithm is widely open for the Knapsack problem and Unbounded Knapsack (the variant with
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
)
[
17
]
.

Savitch’s theorem can be used to avoid the high space usage of dynamic programming, but often comes at a significant
cost for the running time.
In the case of (
1
), this method leads to an algorithm in time
(m\Delta)^{O(m^{2}\log(m\Delta))}\poly(I)

and polynomial space, see
Section

2
for details.

Our contribution.

In this paper, we show that integer programming (
1
) can be solved in polynomial space
and a running time that nearly matches the best known running time of the dynamic programming approach by
[
6
,
10
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
suppose that the absolute value of each entry of
A
is bounded by

\Delta>1
. Then, in time

(m\Delta)^{O(m(\log{m}+\log\log{\Delta}))}\cdot\poly(I)

and polynomial space, one can compute a solution
to (
1
) or conclude that such a solution does not exist.
Here,
I
is the encoding size of
A,b
, and
c
.

Compared to the running time in
[
6
,
10
]
, this is an additional factor of

O(\log m+\log\log\Delta)
in the exponent of
m\Delta
, whereas all known techniques lead to an additional factor of at
least
O(m\log\Delta)
, see
Section

2
.

2
Overview of previous and new techniques

Let us briefly introduce some notation and the model of computation.
Throughout this paper, we assume all logarithms are base 2. We write
\tilde{O}(f)=O(f\cdot\polylog(f))
. Further, we make use of the shorthand notation
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, let
\lVert x\rVert_{p}
denote the
\ell_{p}
norm of vector
x
. In the special case of
p=0
, recall that
\lVert x\rVert_{0}=|\supp(x)|
, where
\supp(x)=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq 0\}
are the non-zero coefficients.

For two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, we say that
x
is lexicographically smaller than
y
(
x\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}y
) if there exists an index
i\in[n]
such that
(i)
x_{j}=y_{j}
for every
j<i
, and (ii)
x_{i}<y_{j}
.

As in related works, see
[
11
]
, we work with a random-access model with word length
\Theta(\log{n}+\log{W})
, where
W
is the largest absolute value of an input integer. We measure space complexity in terms of the total number of words in the working memory.
We say that an algorithm runs in polynomial space if the space complexity is bounded by a polynomial in the
input size, that is, in
\poly(I):=\poly(n,m,\log(\Delta),\log(\lVert b\rVert_{\infty}),\log(\lVert c\rVert_{\infty}))
.
Similarly, the running time of an algorithm is measured in the number of arithmetic operations
on words.

Before we explain the ideas behind
Theorem

1
we will briefly review
previous techniques used to achieve polynomial space dependency.

The standard tool to reduce space complexity usage are the ideas in Savitch’s theorem, which transforms
algorithms with non-deterministic space complexity to slightly higher deterministic space complexity.
Here, the key technique is to decide
(s,t)
-reachability in a directed graph with
N
vertices in

N^{O(\log{N})}
time and
O(\log N)
space. This is straight-forward by recursively
guessing the middle vertex of the path from
s
to
t
. The space complexity comes from the
O(\log N)

vertices stored in the stack.
This can easily be adapted to finding the shortest
path in a weighted graph in the same time and space.
One can already apply this to ILP. Namely,
the algorithm of Eisenbrand and Weismantel
[
6
]

constructs a directed acyclic graph with
N=(m\Delta)^{O(m)}\cdot\lVert b\rVert_{\infty}
vertices and
then computes the shortest path between two of the vertices.
With standard polynomial time preprocessing described in
[
10
]
, one can reduce
\lVert b\rVert_{\infty}
to
(m\Delta)^{O(m)}
.
This shortest path query is the main source of the high space usage of
[
6
]
and
using Savitch’s theorem, the ILP can therefore be solved in
N^{O(\log{N})}=(m\Delta)^{O(m^{2}\log(m\Delta))}
time and
O(\log{N})=O(m\log(m\Delta))
space.

A more intricate technique is the algebraization technique
[
16
]
, which relies on the following coefficient test lemma.

Theorem 2

(cf. Lemma 3.2 in
[
11
]
)
.

Consider a polynomial
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{i}x^{i}\in\mathbb{F}[x]
of degree at most
d

and assume there is an algorithm running in time
T
and space
S
for
evaluating
f(a)
for a given
a\in\mathbb{F}
. Then, there
is an algorithm running in
\tilde{O}(dT)
time and
\tilde{O}(S)
space
that for a given
t\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
decides if
\alpha_{t}=0
.

The idea to solve the Subset Sum problem, is to consider the polynomial
p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x^{a_{i}})
and use
Theorem

2
to test whether the coefficient

\alpha_{t}
is nonzero, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
are the input integers,

t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is the target, and
\alpha_{t}
is a coefficient in front of monomial

x^{t}
of polynomial
p
. Note that the polynomial
p(x)
can be evaluated in

\poly(n,\lVert a\rVert_{\infty})
time, which, combined with
Theorem

2
yields the result
of
[
16
]
.

This technique can be applied to check feasibility of (
1
)
by aggregating the
m
constraints of
A
into a single one
a^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x=b^{\prime}
where

\lVert a\rVert_{\infty}=(m\Delta)^{O(m^{2})}
, see e.g.
[
14
]
,
and reducing the non-negative integer variables to binary ones,
which can be done by applying proximity techniques
[
6
]
.
We omit the details here, since
the method has two inherent disadvantages for our application:
it has a higher running time with an additional factor of
O(m)
in the exponent compared to
[

,
10
]
and
to use
Theorem

2
to solve
the optimization variant in (
1

coefficient of the objective function
c
in

, which would yield a pseudopolynomial running time
in
\lVert c\rVert_{\infty}
that is not necessary in
[

,
10
]
.

Another straight-forward approach is based on the notable property of (

For our result we introduce two new ideas.
In

we introduce the following branching algorithm that can be seen as an application of
Savitch’s theorem to the support of the solution. Consider
the lexicographically minimal solution
x^{\ast}
and let
x^{\ast}=x^{\ast}_{\ell}+x^{\ast}_{r}
be such that
x^{\ast}_{\ell}
and
x^{\ast}_{r}
have half the support each.
We will then guess a vector
b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
such
that
Ax^{\ast}_{\ell}=b_{\ell}
and for each such guess, recurs into subproblems
with the right-hand sides
b_{\ell}
and
b-b_{\ell}
and then retrieve both
x_{\ell}^{\ast}

and
x_{r}^{\ast}
. Note, that in each recursive call, the support halves,
so the total depth of the recursion is
O(\log{\Gamma})=O(\log m+\log\log{\Delta})
.
This idea already yields a
(m\Delta)^{O(m^{2}\log\log{\Delta})}\poly(I)
time and
polynomial space algorithm. However, the dependence on the number
of constraints
m
is much higher than what is promised in
Theorem

1
.
The bottleneck of the earlier mentioned idea of guessing the support and applying Lenstra’s algorithm
is that one enumerates
O(n^{\Gamma})
support sets.
In
Section

4
we show that the number of relevant support sets is much
lower and that they can be enumerated using the branching algorithm as a subroutine
on small instances where it runs more efficiently. This is then combined with running
Lenstra’s algorithm on each support set.

4
Candidate support sets and main theorem

A naive enumeration of all support sets of cardinality at most
\Gamma
would require

O(n^{\Gamma})
. In this section we will design a more efficient enumeration and prove
Theorem

1
.

Definition 6

(Candidate support set)
.

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
, we say that a set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
is a
candidate support set
if:

(i)

\lVert x\rVert_{1}\leq\Gamma
for every
x\in\mathcal{C}
, and

(ii)

for every
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that (
1
) is bounded and feasible there exists an optimal solution
x^{\ast}
to (
1
) with
\supp(x^{\ast})\in\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 7
.

For every
A\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
with
\lVert A\rVert_{\infty}\leq\Delta
, there exists a candidate support set of size
(m\Delta)^{O(m)}
. Moreover, this set can be enumerated in time
(m\Delta)^{O(m\log{\Gamma})}\cdot\poly(I)

Lemma 8
.

Let

is the lexicographically minimal optimal solution to
\min\{c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x^{\prime}:Ax^{\prime}=b^{\prime}\}
and (ii)
\supp(x^{\prime})=\supp(x^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
x^{\prime}
be the indicator vector of
\supp(x^{*})
and let
b^{\prime}=Ax^{\prime}
. Clearly, Property (ii) holds. Hence, it remains to prove (i). For the sake of contradiction, assume that
y
is a solution to the integer program with right-hand side
b^{\prime}
and
c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}y<c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x^{\prime}

Consider
z:=y+x^{*}-x^{\prime}
. Then
z
is a solution to
\{Ax=b^{\prime},x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\}
. But
c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}z<c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x^{*}
, which contradicts the optimality of
x^{*}
.
Similarly, if there is a solution
y
with

and

. Then

z=y+x^{*}-x^{\prime}
is an optimal solution to
\min\{c^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x,Ax=b^{\prime},x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\}
that is lexicographically smaller than
x^{*}
.
∎

and uses polynomial space.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we show that an integer linear program can be solved
in time
O(m\Delta)^{O(m(\log m+\log\log{\Delta})}\poly(I)

and polynomial space.
It remains open whether the additional factor of
O(\log m+\log\log\Delta)
in the exponent
compared to the algorithm without space limitation can be avoided.
We note that our algorithm has two bottlenecks that lead to this running time:
(i) the depth of
O(\log m+\log\log{\Delta})
for our branching and
(ii) because we use the algorithm of Lenstra and
Kannan that runs in time
N^{O(N)}\poly(I)
on an instance with
N=O(m\log(m\Delta))
.
Thus, even if the improvement to
(\log{N})^{O(N)}\poly(I)
by Reis and
Rothvoss
[
20
]
could be implemented in polynomial space, this would not immediately
lead to an improvement.
Already for the special case of Unbounded Knapsack it is unclear how to avoid
the exponent of
\log\log\Delta
and achieve a pseudopolynomial running time.

Finally, we want to mention the case of binary integer linear programs, i.e., where
we substitude the domain constraint for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
in (
1
).
Here, the best known algorithm runs in time

(m\Delta)^{O(m^{2})}\poly(I)

[
6
]
and naive application of
Savitch’s theorem yields polynomial
space at significant higher running time of
(m\Delta)^{O(m^{3}\log(m\Delta))}\poly(I)
.
It is unclear how to come close to
an exponent of
O(m^{2})
in polynomial space and the ideas in this paper do not seem to be
applicable, since the solutions need not be sparse.
