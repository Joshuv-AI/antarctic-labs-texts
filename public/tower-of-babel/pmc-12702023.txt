Human neutrophil α-defensin HNP1 interacts with bacterial cell wall components to promote biofilm formation by Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Chongbing Liao, Dan Yao, Zeyu Sun, Bryan Ericksen, Bei Jiang, Yang Zhou, Mingzhu Zhao, Xiaomei Hu, Dan Xu, Yang Wu, Wuyuan Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus is a major opportunistic pathogen whose ability to form biofilms significantly enhances its persistence and antibiotic resistance. We previously found that human neutrophil peptide 1 (HNP1), a member of the α-defensin family, promotes biofilm formation in Acinetobacter baumannii. Here, we demonstrate that HNP1 enhances biofilm formation across a range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, with particularly strong effects in S. aureus, which increases its tolerance to multiple antibiotics. Mechanistically, HNP1 promotes bacterial adhesion and self-aggregation, facilitating the early stages of biofilm development. Transcriptomic analysis revealed minimal activation of canonical biofilm-associated genes, suggesting that HNP1 acts through a non-transcriptional mechanism. Notably, HNP1 was found to interact with S. aureus cell wall components, peptidoglycan and wall teichoic acid, which mainly triggers biofilm formation. These findings expand the role of HNP1 in promoting biofilm formation, which may have implications for understanding infection persistence and antibiotic tolerance.Keywords: HNP1, Human neutrophil peptides, Defensin, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacterial biofilm, Peptidoglycan, Wall teichoic acid

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus, a Gram-positive opportunistic pathogen, usually colonizes human skin as a common commensal, and is carried by about 30 % of individuals in their noses [1,2]. As one of the top six leading pathogens for deaths associated with antimicrobial resistance [3], S. aureus has been identified as a high priority pathogen by the World Health Organization (WHO) [4]. It is responsible for a wide range of both community- and hospital-acquired infections, including soft tissue and bloodstream infections, characterized by high recurrent rates and growing resistance to antibiotics [[5], [6], [7]], with significant contributions by biofilm formation [[8], [9], [10]]. Biofilms are complex microbial communities encased in an extracellular matrix composed mainly of proteinaceous materials, polysaccharides, and extracellular DNA (eDNA) [11,12]. Although numerous studies have elucidated bacterial factors critical for biofilm formation [10], the role of host-derived factors in biofilm dynamics remains relatively underexplored.

Human neutrophil peptides (HNPs), members of the human α-defensin family, are primarily produced and stored by neutrophils, with HNP1-3 being the most abundant isoforms, accounting for 5–7 % of the total cellular protein [13,14]. Beyond their well-studied antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties, emerging evidence suggests that HNP1 also exhibits pleiotropic effects on microbial behavior, including modulation of bacterial virulence, enhancement of biofilm formation, and increasement of bacterial or viral infection [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Along with neutrophils, which are rapidly recruited to the sites of infection and inflammation, large amounts of HNPs can be released through degranulation or cell death [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]], leading to high local concentrations and potential interactions with the colonizing pathogens.

In our previous study, we found that HNP1 remarkably enhances biofilm formation by A. baumannii, suggesting an unexpected role for this host defense peptide in facilitating bacterial persistence [19]. However, whether this phenomenon extends beyond A. baumannii to other clinically relevant pathogens, such as S. aureus, remains unknown. In this study, we investigated the effect of HNP1 on biofilm formation in a panel of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, identifying a particularly strong effect on S. aureus. Therefore, we further characterized the effect of HNP1 on S. aureus, studied the potential mechanisms, and identified peptidoglycan as a bacterial target by HNP1 that is different from previous studies. These findings broaden our understanding of the multifunctional roles of defensins, which offer insights into how host-derived antimicrobial peptides may paradoxically contribute to microbial colonization, biofilm persistence, and antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, cell lines, and peptides

The bacterial strains and cell lines used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. S. aureus strains were cultured at 37 °C in TSB broth (Hopebio, China) with agitation at 220 rpm in an orbital shaker or on TSB agar plates. L. monocytogenes was cultured in BHI broth (Hopebio, China), while the other strains were grown in LB broth. A549, Hep2 and J774a.1 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (Cat #: FSP500, ExCell, China) and 1 % Pen-Strep (Cat #: 15140122, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. All peptides used in this study, including the site-directed HNP1 mutants, were lab stocks, and were synthesized, folded, and purified as previously described [[25], [26], [27], [28]].

Microtiter plate assay for biofilm formation

The bacterial biofilm formation was quantified using CV staining as previously described [29] with minor modifications. Briefly, the overnight bacterial inoculum was diluted with fresh media to an OD600 value of ∼0.1 and added with indicated concentrations of HNP1 (0–32 μM). Then the bacterial suspensions were added to 96-well plates (100 μl/well). Next, the plate was incubated in a humidified chamber to reduce evaporation at 37 °C for 24 h. To stain the biofilms, the plate was washed twice with 200 μl/well Milli-Q water to remove the planktonic bacteria, stained with 125 μl/well 0.1 % crystal violet (Cat #: 548-62-9, Yeasen, China) at room temperature for 15 min, washed again, and dissolved with 125 μl/well 30 % acetic acid. The biofilms were quantified by reading the absorbance at 595 nm using a plate reader (Bio-Rad xMark).

Microfluidic analysis

Real-time bacterial aggregation under flow conditions was evaluated using the BioFlux 1000z microfluidic system (Fluxion Biosciences, USA) with 48-well BioFlux plates with silicone bottoms composed of polydimexylsiloxane. First, 100 μl of TSB, with or without 8 μM HNP1, was introduced into the channels from the outlet to the inlet wells at 2 dyn/cm2 for approximately 5 min. Then, 500 μl of the diluted overnight inoculum (OD600 ≈ 0.01) was added to the outlet well with or without 8 μM HNP1. A sheer force of 0.1 dyn/cm2 was applied to the outlet well, and the bacterial aggregation was monitored at 37 °C using a microscope (200 × magnification) with images captured every 5 min for 2 h. Bacterial aggregation was quantified with grayscale analysis in ImageJ version 1.53t (https://imagej.net/ij/).

Scanning electron microscopy

The overnight inoculum was diluted with fresh media to an OD600 of ∼0.1 and seeded into 48-well plates (300 μl/well) containing 8-mm-diameter silicone pieces, with or without specified HNP1 concentrations. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, samples were washed once with 10 mM phosphate buffer saline (1 × PBS), fixed overnight at 4 °C with 500 μl/well 2 % paraformaldehyde and 2.5 % glutaraldehyde in 1 × PBS, and dehydrated in a gradient ethanol series (50 %,70 %, 100 % × 2, 15 min each). Samples were dried using the critical-point method, coated with 1–3 nm gold, and observed using a HITACHI SU8020 (Japan) or Zeiss GeminiSEM 500 (Germany) scanning electron microscope.

Virtual colony count assay

The viable bacteria were quantified using the previously described virtual colony count (VCC) method [25]. Briefly, ∼1 × 106 CFU/ml bacteria were treated with HNP1 (0–32 μM) in TSB or 10 mM phosphate buffer (PB) in 96-well plates (100 μl/well) at 37 °C for 2 h. Then, 100 μl/well of TSB was added, and bacterial growth was monitored at 37 °C in a plate reader (Molecular Devices, SpectraMax ABS) by reading absorbance at 600 nm every 5 min for 12 h. The time for each well to reach the threshold (OD600 = 0.02) was calculated using a Visual Basic script in Microsoft Excel [25]. The threshold times from the reference wells, containing different initial bacterial concentrations (10°–107 CFU/ml) without HNP1, were used to determine the virtual colony forming units (CFUv) for treated groups. The results were expressed as percentages of virtual survival.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

The MIC assay for HNP1 against S. aureus ATCC 29213 was performed under the same culture condition as the biofilm assays. Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures were diluted (v/v = 1:100) in TSB. Two-fold serial dilutions of HNP1 were prepared in TSB in 96-well plates (50 μl/well), and 50 μl of bacterial suspension was added to each well, resulting in a final volume of 100 μl with HNP1 concentrations ranging from 0 to 128 μM. Vancomycin (0–32 μM) was used as a positive control. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h without shaking. Bacterial growth was assessed visually.

PCR detection of capsule genotype

Capsule genotypes were determined by PCR following Sutter et al. (2011) with minor adaptations [30]. Primer pairs were: gyrA (internal control), gyrA1 5′-AATGAACAAGGTATGACACC-3′ and gyrA2 5′-TACGCGCTTCAGTATAACGC-3′; cap5, cap5-1 5′-GGTTTGCTGAAAAACCAGTC-3′ and cap5-2 5′-CCTCATATGCTCCTACATTT-3′; cap8, cap8-1 5′-GCGCTACAAACATTAAGCAT-3′ and cap8-2 5′-TTCTTAGCCTGCTGGCATC-3′. For colony-boil templates, a single colony from TSB agar was resuspended in 100 μL nuclease-free water, heated at 95 °C for 10 min, then centrifuged at 14800 rpm (21000×g) for 1 min at room temperature; the supernatant served as PCR template. Reactions (20 μl) contained 10 μl 2 × Phanta Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# P515-02-AA), 0.5 μM of each primer, and 1 μl template. Cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 50 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 50 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons were resolved on 1.25 % agarose (TAE buffer) at 80 V for 40 min, stained with Ultra GelRed (Vazyme, Cat# GR501-01).

Antibiotic treatment of biofilms

The overnight inoculum of S. aureus ATCC 29213 was diluted with fresh TSB to an OD600 of ∼0.1 and added into 48-well plates (250 μl/well) with or without 8 μM HNP1. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h to form biofilms, 250 μl/well of TSB containing ampicillin, kanamycin, or tetracycline (0–100 μg/ml for ampicillin and kanamycin, and 0–25 μg/ml for tetracycline) were added, and incubation continued overnight at 37 °C. The viable bacteria in biofilms were quantified using a modified VCC method, which allows bacteria to regrow under well-mixed, planktonic conditions until they reach a defined optical density threshold. Thus, the measured growth lag time correlates with the number of viable units capable of initiating exponential growth, making VCC less sensitive to incomplete disaggregation than CFU-based assays. Briefly, the plates were washed twice with 1 × PBS to remove planktonic bacteria, and the biofilms were resuspended in 1 ml/well Milli-Q water. Subsequently, 100 μl/well of the suspension was mixed with 100 μl/well of 2 × TSB in a 96-well plate, and growth curves were measured at 37 °C using a plate reader (Molecular Devices, SpectraMax ABS) by reading OD600 every 5 min for 12 h. The time to reach the threshold (OD600 = 0.02) was calculated using a Visual Basic script in Microsoft Excel [25]. Reference wells containing serially diluted inocula (101–108 CFU/ml) were used to generate calibration curves for determining the virtual colony-forming units (vCFU) of treated groups. Representative growth and calibration curves are shown in Fig. S3D–E.

To evaluate the effects of additional clinically relevant antibiotics, assays using imipenem, vancomycin, and daptomycin were performed in 96-well plates under identical biofilm formation conditions to minimize peptide consumption. Briefly, 100 μl/well diluted bacterial suspensions were incubated for 24 h, followed by the addition of 100 μl antibiotic solution prepared in TSB (final concentrations 0–100 μg/ml) and further incubation overnight at 37 °C. After treatment, plates were washed twice with 1 × PBS, and biofilms were resuspended in 200 μl/well Milli-Q water. The suspensions were pipetted vigorously using an INTEGRA VIAFLO electronic pipette (maximum speed; 30 repetitions per cycle × 10 cycles) to ensure thorough biofilm dispersion. The remaining steps were identical to those described for 48-well plate assays.

Bacterial adhesion assay

A549, Hep2 and J774A.1 cells were seeded into 48-well plates (∼1 × 105 cells/well) one day prior to the assay. The cells were washed once with pre-warmed (37 °C) DMEM, then supplemented with 200 μl/well DMEM containing 0, 4 or 8 μM HNP1 and S. aureus ATCC 29213 (mid-exponential phase, MOI of 10). The plates were centrifuged at 2000 rpm (700 g) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with DMEM. For CFU enumeration, the cells were lysed with 1 ml/well Milli-Q water, diluted with 1 × PBS, plated on TSB agar, and cultured at 37 °C overnight. The colonies were counted using ProtoCOL 3 HD (Synbiosis, UK). For visualization, the MRSA USA300 strain harboring the pCM29-GFP plasmid was used. After centrifugation and washing, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton-X100/PBS for 5 min, stained with rhodamine-labeled phalloidin (Actin-Tracker Red-Rhodamine, Cat #: C2207S, Beyotime, China), mounted on DAPI Fluoromount-G (Yeasen, China), and observed with a confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris 5, Germany).

Bacterial aggregation assay

The bacterial overnight cultures were diluted with 1 × PBS to an OD600 of ∼1. Then, 100 μl of the suspension was incubated with or without 8 μM HNP1 at room temperature for 2 h, followed by staining with 3.34 mM SYTO9 (Cat #: L7012, Invitrogen, USA) for 15 min. A 10 μl aliquot of the stained bacterial suspension was placed on a slide, covered with a coverslip, and observed using an All-in-One Fluorescence Microscope BZ-X800 (KEYENCE, USA).

Bacterial sedimentation assay

The overnight inocula of the bacteria were diluted with 1 × PBS to an OD600 of 1∼2, mixed with or without 8 μM HNP1, and incubated in a cuvette. The bacterial sedimentation was monitored by measuring OD600 at the upper portion of the cuvette hourly for 7–10 h. The data are normalized to the OD600 at the 0-h time point.

Transposon screening

A previously constructed S. aureus Newman transposon mutant library (stored in 15 % glycerol/TSB at −80 °C) was plated on BHI agar containing 10 μg/ml erythromycin and cultured at 37 °C overnight. Single colonies were randomly selected and subjected to the CV staining assay (as specified in Section 2.2) with 0 or 8 μM HNP1 to assess HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation. Mutants exhibiting less than 25 % of the wild-type biofilm enhancement were regarded as potential hits. The transposon insertion sites of these hits were determined as previously described [31,32] with modifications. Briefly, 100–250 ng of the bacterial chromosomal DNA was digested with FastDigest EcoRⅠ (Cat #: FD0274, Thermo Scientific, USA), self-ligated with T4 DNA Ligase (Cat #: 2011A, Takara, Japan), and transformed into E. coli DH5α (Tsingke, China). Transformants were plated on LB agar containing 100 μg/ml ampicillin and cultured at 37 °C overnight. The plasmid DNA from single colonies was sequenced by Tsingke Biotech Co. (China) using the primer 5′-AGAGAGATGTCACCGTCAAGT-3’. The sequencing results were mapped to the S. aureus subsp. aureus NCTC 8325 reference genome assembly ASM1342v1 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_000013425.1/).

RNA-seq analysis

The overnight inoculum of S. aureus ATCC 29213 was diluted by fresh TSB to an OD600 of ∼0.1 and incubated with 0 or 8 μM HNP1 (n = 3 per group) at 37 °C and 220 rpm in an orbital shaker for 8 h. The bacteria were then collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C till delivered on dry ice for transcriptomic sequencing by BIOZERON Co., Ltd. (China). Total RNA was extracted, and DNA was removed using DNase Ⅰ (TaKara, Japan). 5 μg of total RNA was used for library preparation using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced with Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150 bp).

Raw reads were trimmed and quality-controlled with Trimmomatic v0.36 (http://www.usadellab.org/cms/?page=trimmomatic) with parameters SLIDINGWINDOW 4:15 and MINLEN 75, then aligned to the reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_022870465.1/) using Rockhopper (https://cs.wellesley.edu/∼btjaden/Rockhopper/).

GO and KEGG pathway analyses were performed using Goatools (https://github.com/tanghaibao/Goatools) and KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do), respectively. COG analysis was conducted using EGGNOG-MAPPER (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/). GSEA analysis was performed using the R package “clusterProfiler” and visualized with “GseaVis” in RStudio 2024.12.0 Build 467 (https://rstudio.com/products/rstudio). The underlying code for this study is available in GitHub and can be accessed via this link https://github.com/basilmars/SA_biofilm.

Wall teichoic acid extraction

Wall teichoic acid of S. aureus ATCC 29213 was extracted as described by Kho et al. [33] with minor modifications. Briefly, 100 ml of an overnight inoculum grown in TSB was centrifuged (5000 g, 5 min) and resuspended in 30 ml of Buffer 1 (50 mM MES, pH 6.5), followed by resuspension in 30 ml of Buffer 2 (4 % SDS, 50 mM MES, pH 6.5). The suspension was boiled for 1 h in a water bath and then cooled to room temperature. The bacterial pellet was sequentially washed and centrifuged (12000 g, 3 min) with 10 ml of Buffer 1, 10 ml of Buffer 2, 10 ml of Buffer 3 (2 % NaCl, 50 mM MES, pH 6.5), 10 ml of Buffer 1, and finally 5 ml of Digestion buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.5 % SDS). Proteinase K (10 μl of 20 mg/ml; Adamas life, Cat# P9918-1 mL) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 4 h on a heat block with intermittent shaking. The sample was centrifuged (12000 g, 3 min) and washed with 10 ml of Buffer 3, followed by 10 ml of Milli-Q water. The water wash was repeated 3 times to remove SDS. The pellet was then resuspended in 10 ml of 0.1 M NaOH and incubated at room temperature for 16 h on a tube rotator to release WTA. The suspension was centrifuged (12000 g, 60 min), and the supernatant containing WTA was collected. To precipitate WTA, 0.1 volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH5.1) and 3 vol of ice-cold 95 % ethanol were added, followed by incubation at −20 °C for 4 h and centrifugation at 12000g for 30 min. The resulting pellet was washed 5 times by 2 ml of 95 % ethanol, air-dried to remove residual ethanol, and dissolved in 1 ml of Milli-Q water (clear and colorless). The WTA solution was transferred to a pre-weighted tube, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, lyophilized to yield a white powder, and stored at −20 °C until use.

Indirect detection of HNP1-peptidoglycan binding

Due to the poor solubility of S. aureus peptidoglycan (Cat #: 77140, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in aqueous buffers, conventional protein-binding assays such as surface plasmon resonance or fluorescence polarization were infeasible. Therefore, we developed an indirect method to evaluate HNP1-peptidoglycan binding. Briefly, peptidoglycan suspensions (0–500 μg/mL) in 1 × PBS were mixed with 10 μM HNP1 and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After centrifugation (14800 rpm [21000 g], room temperature, 5 min), 20 μl of the supernatant was analyzed using an LC-TOF system (Agilent HPLC Infinity Ⅱ 1290 coupled with a TOF 6230 detector) with an Agilent SB-C18 RRDH column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm). Residual HNP1 was quantified based on the peak area of HPLC chromatograms at 214 nm absorbance (A214).

Results

HNP1 most effectively promotes biofilm formation by S. aureus among tested bacteria

In our previous studies, we observed that high concentrations (128 μg/ml) of HNP1 induced aggregation of S. aureus in 96-well plates in a virtual colony count (VCC) assay [34] (Fig. S1A), and we demonstrated that HNP1 significantly enhanced biofilm formation by A. baumannii [19]. These findings led us to investigate whether HNP1 generally promotes biofilm formation across different bacterial species, including S. aureus. To this end, we compared the effect of HNP1 on biofilm formation in five Gram-negative and three Gram-positive species using the crystal violet (CV) staining assay.

As shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 4 μM of HNP1 enhanced biofilm formation in almost all tested species. Significant increases were observed in A. baumannii (∼2.7 fold), E. coli (∼3.8 fold), S. Typhimurium (∼23.3 fold), L. monocytogenes (∼2.3 fold), S. aureus (∼10.5 fold), and S. epidermidis (∼8.1 fold). Mild and insignificant increases were noted for K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa (∼1.5 fold each). While S. Typhimurium exhibited the highest fold change, its absolute biofilm level (OD595 ≈ 0.256) was substantially lower than that of S. aureus (OD595 ≈ 1.23), indicating that S. aureus not only responded strongly to HNP1 but also produced the most substantial biofilm among all tested species.

HNP1 dose-dependently enhances S. aureus biofilm formation with moderate bactericidal activities

To further characterize the effect of HNP1 on S. aureus biofilm formation, we treated S. aureus ATCC 29213 with a two-fold serial dilution of HNP1 (0–32 μM) and performed CV staining. As shown in Fig. 1B, biofilm formation dose-dependently increased at concentrations below 8 μM but was attenuated at concentrations above 16 μM, likely due to the moderate bactericidal activity of HNP1, which was remarkably inhibited in TSB broth as shown by the virtual colony count (VCC) assay [25] (Fig. S1B).

To visualize the HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation, we inoculated the ATCC 29213 strain in microfluidic channels on 48-well all-silicone-bottom plates and monitored the bacterial aggregation under flow conditions in the absence or presence of 8 μM HNP1 using the BioFlux 1000z system. Remarkable enhancement of bacterial aggregation was observed in the HNP1-treated group within 2 h post-inoculation (Fig. 1C and D, Supplementary Video 1). We also examined biofilm morphology after 24 h of incubation on silicone membranes using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). S. aureus ATCC 29213 formed dense and structured biofilms in the presence of 8 μM HNP1 (Fig. 1E), as did MRSA 33591 with 4 μM HNP1 (Fig. S1C). Strain 25923, showing a higher basal aggregation tendency, exhibited increased aggregation with HNP1, but lacked the blanket-like architecture seen in the other two strains, suggesting that the 25923 strain responded to HNP1 differently from the other S. aureus strains.

Therefore, we compared ten S. aureus strains, including four reference strains (ATCC 29213, ATCC 25923, XEN36, and Newman), two clinical isolates (14945 and 0912943), two MRSA strains (33591 and USA300), and two MRSA clinical isolates (15098 and 234), using the CV staining assay to evaluate the strain specificity of this effect (Fig. 1F). In most strains, including ATCC 29213, biofilm formation was significantly enhanced by HNP1. Of note, strain 25923 showed no increase in CV staining (Fig. 1F), despite the visible interaction observed with SEM analysis (Fig. 1E). Similar to the ATCC 29213 strain (Fig. S1B), HNP1 exhibited moderate bactericidal activities in TSB against the other strains (Fig. S1D), and the minimal inhibition concentration (MIC) of HNP1 against ATCC 29213 was 64 μM in TSB, while evident bacterial aggregation was observed (Fig. S1E). These findings suggest that while all the tested S. aureus strains may respond to HNP1 treatment, the effect on biofilm formation can vary and may not be fully captured using CV staining alone.

We also examined the presence of cap5 and cap8 genes in the S. aureus strains used in this study by PCR, using the housekeeping gene gyrA as an internal control. As shown in Fig. S2A, all tested strains carried at least one of the two capsule-associated genes, and the 14945 strain contained both. The summarized results are presented in Fig. S2B. No clear correlation was observed between the capsule genotype and the HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation. While PCR-based detection confirms the presence of capsule-related genes, it does not verify actual capsule expression [35], and therefore the true capsule phenotype remains undetermined.

HNP1 promotes antibiotic tolerance of S. aureus in biofilms

Given that biofilm formation contributes to bacterial antibiotic tolerance [9,11,12], we asked whether the HNP1-enhanced biofilms of S. aureus show increased drug tolerance. Using a modified bactericidal assay (Fig. 2A) [19], we pre-formed biofilms of S. aureus ATCC 29213 with or without 8 μM HNP1 and then challenged them with ampicillin, tetracycline, or kanamycin. After 12 h, the viable bacteria in the biofilms were quantified via VCC [25]. As shown in Fig. 2B–D, biofilms formed in the presence of 8 μM HNP1 exhibited significantly increased tolerance to all three tested antibiotics, particularly at intermediate antibiotic concentrations.

HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation increases antibiotic tolerance in S. aureus. Data are mean ± SD of two independent experiments (three biological replicates per experiment). TWO-way ANOVA; ∗, P < 0.05; ∗∗, P < 0.01; ∗∗∗, P < 0.001.

Similar results were obtained when other clinically relevant antibiotics were tested. Biofilms formed in the presence of HNP1 contained significantly higher numbers of surviving bacteria after treatment with imipenem, vancomycin, or daptomycin, particularly at lower antibiotic concentrations (Fig. S3A–C).

HNP1 enhances initial S. aureus adhesion on both biotic and abiotic surfaces

Bacterial adhesion is the first step in biofilm development [9,36,37]. In our prior studies, we found that human α-defensins, including HNP1 and HD5, enhance the adhesion of S. flexneri and A. baumannii to host cells [17,19,38,39]. To determine whether HNP1 similarly promotes S. aureus adhesion, we performed cell adhesion assays using three mammalian cell lines: A549 (lung epithelial cells), Hep2 (laryngeal epithelial cells), and J774A.1 (murine macrophages). Colony-forming unit (CFU) counts revealed significantly enhanced adhesion of S. aureus ATCC 29213 in the HNP1-treated groups across all tested cell lines within 10 min of centrifugation (Fig. 3A–C).

To visualize the enhanced adhesion, GFP-expressing S. aureus USA 300 was co-incubated with Hep2 and J774A.1 cells cultured on glass slips with or without HNP1, followed by fluorescent microscopy analysis. As shown in Fig. 3D, markedly more bacteria were observed on the surfaces of both host cells and glass slips in the presence of HNP1. Quantification of the green bacteria using ImageJ, which reflected the total number of bacteria per microscopic field, including those attached to both cells and the glass substrate, confirmed a significant increase in overall bacterial adhesion (Fig. 3E). To specifically assess cell-associated attachment, bacteria adhering to twelve randomly selected host cells per group were manually counted (Fig. 3F), which likewise demonstrated a significant increase in bacterial attachment to host cells upon HNP1 treatment, supporting its role in promoting both biotic and abiotic surface adhesion.

HNP1 promotes bacterial aggregation to different degrees across various species

We next assessed whether HNP1 promotes bacterial self-aggregation, which precedes surface attachment and biofilm maturation [40]. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, we suspended the bacteria in PBS and treated them with or without 8 μM HNP1 at room temperature (RT) for 2 h. The bacteria were then stained with SYTO9 and visualized using fluorescence microscopy.

HNP1 markedly enhanced the aggregation of all five tested bacterial species (Fig. 4B), albeit to varying degrees. In the presence of HNP1, aggregation potency ranked as: S. aureus ATCC 25923 > S. pneumoniae > S. aureus ATCC 29213 ≈ L. monocytogenes > S. epidermidis; while the baseline aggregation trend without HNP1 was: S. pneumoniae > S. aureus ATCC 25923 > S. aureus ATCC 29213 ≈ L. monocytogenes ≈ S. epidermidis. These observations suggest that while HNP1 effectively promotes bacterial aggregation across different species, the magnitude of this effect is strain- and species-dependent.

HNP1 selectively promotes bacterial sedimentation, independent of its biofilm-enhancing activity

As bacterial aggregation often precedes sedimentation [41], we evaluated HNP1's impact on bacterial sedimentation using a static sedimentation assay (Fig. 5A). The bacterial suspensions (OD600 ≈ 1 in PBS) were incubated with or without 8 μM HNP1, and OD600 of the upper phase was measured over time to monitor bacterial sedimentation kinetics.

HNP1 significantly accelerated sedimentation in S. aureus ATCC 29213, S. aureus ATCC 25923, and S. epidermidis, as reflected by a faster decline in OD600 values compared to the untreated controls (Fig. 5B–D). Besides, S. aureus ATCC 25923 also exhibited a higher baseline sedimentation rate than S. aureus ATCC 29213, consistent with its SEM and aggregation phenotypes (Fig. 1, Fig. 4B). In contrast, HNP1 had no effect on sedimentation of L. monocytogenes and E. coli (Fig. S4A–B), and even slightly inhibited sedimentation in S. pneumoniae and A. baumannii (Fig. S4C–D). These data indicate that the sedimentation-promoting activity of HNP1 is not universal.

To be noted, CV staining assays revealed that HNP1 enhanced biofilm formation in all these species (Fig. 1A), including those that did not exhibit enhanced sedimentation. This disconnect suggests that the ability of HNP1 to promote biofilm formation is not necessarily dependent on sedimentation. While increased aggregation, sedimentation and settling may contribute to biofilm development in strains such as S. aureus, other mechanisms such as improved adhesion, aggregation, or modulation of surface properties, may be responsible for HNP1's biofilm-promoting activity in non-sedimentation-enhancing species.

Transposon screening in S. aureus fails to identify the bacterial target of HNP1

To further explore bacterial factors involved in HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation, we conducted a high-throughput transposon library screen using S. aureus Newman mutants (Fig. S5A). A total of 288 mutants were screened with CV staining in the presence of HNP1.

As shown in the replicate plot (Fig. S5B), 20 potential hits were identified. 10 of these mutants were successfully sequenced, with seven mapped to the saeS gene (Fig. S5C), which encodes a sensor kinase in the SaeRS two-component system known to regulate virulence and surface-associated phenotypes. Other hits included one mutant in a conserved hypothetical protein (hit 6–29), one in a truncated transposase (hit 8–15), and one unmapped mutant (hit 5–12).

To validate whether saeS disruption affects the interaction between S. aureus and HNP1, we compared both aggregation and sedimentation in the saeS mutant (hit 1–33) and the wild-type Newman strain. The fluorescent microscopy shows that HNP1 induced comparable levels of bacterial aggregation in both strains (Fig. S5D–E). Similarly, sedimentation assays demonstrated that HNP1 significantly accelerated sedimentation in both the wild-type and saeS mutant backgrounds (Fig. S5F–G). The saeS mutant behaved like strain ATCC 25923. These results indicate that saeS is not required for HNP1-mediated aggregation or sedimentation and is unlikely to be the direct bacterial target of HNP1.

HNP1 alters S. aureus transcriptional profiles with limited impact on biofilm-associated genes

A variety of pathways are involved in biofilm modulation of S. aureus [[8], [9], [10],42]. Then we wondered whether HNP1 influences gene transcription associated with these pathways. To answer this question, we performed RNA-seq analysis on S. aureus 29213 treated with or without 8 μM HNP1 (n = 3 per group) (Fig. S6A). Reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM) [43] was used to estimate the gene expression levels (Supplementary Data 1).

According to the RPKM density distribution and the boxplot of each sample (Fig. S6B–C), the overall transcription was slightly reduced under treatment with HNP1, possibly due to its moderate bactericidal activity (Fig. S1B). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a distinct separation between the HNP1-treated and the control samples (Fig. 6A), consistent with the clustered heatmap based on sample-to-sample Poisson distances (Fig. 6B), indicating significant transcriptomic changes induced by HNP1.

(A) PCA plot of the RNA-seq data from S. aureus ATCC 29213 treated with or without 8 μM HNP1 (n = 3). (B) Clustered heatmap of the sample-to-sample Poisson distances. (C) Volcano plot of the DEGs. The up-regulated genes are labeled in red, and the down-regulated genes are in blue. (D) COG category distribution of the DEGs. Bars to the right represent the up-regulated genes (orange); bars to the left represent the down-regulated genes (blue). (E) Clustered heatmap showing the transcription of the biofilm-related genes [log2(RPKM+1)], with annotations for FDR and absolute log2 fold change (|logFC|). (For interpretation of the references to color in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the Web version of this article.)

Differential expression analysis identified 93 up-regulated and 329 down-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 6C). Functional annotations of these DEGs using the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification revealed enrichment mainly in genes related to substance transport and metabolism (categories P, E, and G), energy production and conversion (C), translation (J), transcription (K), and cell wall biogenesis (M) (Fig. 6D), aligning with the report that antimicrobial peptides like HNP1 inhibit cell wall synthesis by targeting lipid II of S. aureus [44,45]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses further confirmed enrichment in metabolic regulation, quorum sensing, and two-component systems (Fig. S6D–E).

Although the KEGG pathway “Biofilm formation – Escherichia coli” was enriched (Fig. S6E), driven by icaA, most classical biofilm genes were minimally impacted, as shown in Fig. 6E. Most of these genes remained unchanged; and even among those with statistically significant changes (false discovery rates, FDR <0.05), the fold of change was generally modest (absolute log2 fold change, |logFC| ≤ 2). Specifically, icaA, icaB, cidA, harA, isdA, and sdrD were downregulated. In contrast, genes that were upregulated, e.g. coa, eap, efb, and saeS/R, are also involved in virulence regulation and host interaction. Among these, eap, encoding the extracellular adherence protein related to bacterial adhesion and aggregation, showed the strongest increase (|logFC| > 2).

Besides, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed the lack of coordinated alteration in biofilm-related genes (NES = 1.07, adjusted P = 0.34) (Fig. S6G). Conversely, the clustered heatmap and GSEA plot for the virulence-related genes (NES = 2.69, adjusted P < 0.001) revealed a significant enrichment in the HNP1-treated group (Fig. S6F and S6H), in line with the previous reports that HNP1 upregulated virulence gene expression [16].

These results suggest that despite the significantly altered virulence pathways, HNP1 does not enhance biofilm formation via canonical transcriptional biofilm pathways but may instead act through physical mechanisms such as enhanced adhesion and aggregation.

Interaction with peptidoglycan and wall teichoic acid mediates HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation in S. aureus

Given that transcriptional modulation alone could not explain the robust biofilm induction, we hypothesized that HNP1 might physically interact with a bacterial surface component to promote S. aureus biofilm formation. Based on our previous finding that HNP1 physically targets the outer membrane protein OmpA in A. baumannii [19], we first tested two major components of the S. aureus cell wall, peptidoglycan (PG) and lipoteichoic acid, and used them as competitive inhibitors in CV staining assays.

PG (Sigma, Cat# 77140) and lipoteichoic acid were respectively added at various concentrations to S. aureus cultures in the presence of 8 μM HNP1, and their impact on biofilm formation was evaluated (Fig. 7A and B). The addition of PG significantly suppressed the biofilm-enhancing activity of HNP1 in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas lipoteichoic acid had no observable inhibitory effect. These results suggest that HNP1 likely interacts specifically with PG, rather than with lipoteichoic acid.

HNP1 promotes biofilm formation in S. aureus by interaction with peptidoglycans. (A) Peptidoglycan inhibited HNP1-enhanced S. aureus biofilm formation in a dose-dependent manner. (B) Lipoteichoic acid had no significant effect on the biofilm-enhancing effect of HNP1. Data are mean ± SD of three independent experiments (two biological replicates per experiment). ONE-way ANOVA; ns, no significance; ∗∗, P < 0.01 ∗∗∗, P < 0.001. (C) LC-TOF detection of HNP1 levels in the supernatant after incubation with increasing concentrations of S. aureus peptidoglycan. (D) Quantitative analysis showed that the peptidoglycan dose-dependently depleted free HNP1 from the supernatant, with an estimated IC50 of 101.4 μg/mL. Data are mean ± SD of three biological replicates.

To further confirm that the observed inhibition was due to PG itself rather than potential contaminants, we compared PG preparations from three commercial sources: Sigma PG (Cat# 77140), InvivoGen PGN-SA (Cat# tlrl-pgns2), and InvivoGen PGN-SAndi ultrapure PG (Cat# tlrl-sipgn). All three products, validated for use in TLR2 or NOD2 stimulation assays, are of high purity and suitable for biological applications. Each PG preparation significantly reduced HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation by S. aureus ATCC 29213 (Fig. S7A–C). The inhibitory magnitude varied slightly, with the InvivoGen preparations—particularly the ultrapure PGN-SAndi—showing weaker inhibition (Fig. S7B–C), likely due to poorer dispersibility in aqueous solution.

Given that wall teichoic acid (WTA) is another abundant anionic component of the S. aureus cell wall, we next evaluated whether it could also influence HNP1-mediated biofilm formation. When extracted WTA was added together with 8 μM HNP1, biofilm formation was significantly reduced (Fig. S8A–B). The inhibitory effect of WTA (IC50 = 60.03 μg/mL) was approximately 4-fold weaker than that of PG (Sigma Cat# 77140) (IC50 = 13.88 μg/mL) (Fig. S8C). Considering that PG and WTA exist at comparable levels within the S. aureus cell wall [46,47], these data suggest that both components may interact with HNP1 and mediate its biofilm-promoting activity.

PG is an insoluble, highly cross-linked polymer lacking a defined molecular weight, while WTA is water-soluble but also heterogeneous in chain length and chemical modification. Because neither polymer has a precise molecular weight, their concentrations cannot be expressed in molar units, making quantitative affinity measurements by techniques such as surface plasmon resonance, fluorescence polarization, or isothermal titration calorimetry impractical. We therefore used a sedimentation-based assay to qualitatively assess HNP1-PG binding. The amount of free HNP1 remaining in the supernatant after incubation with increasing concentrations of PG was quantified using LC-TOF (Fig. 7C). A dose-dependent decrease in HNP1 levels was observed, indicating direct binding and sequestration of HNP1 with PG, with an estimated IC50 of 101.4 μg/mL (Fig. 7C and D).

To determine whether HNP1 binding depends on the structural integrity of PG, we treated the ultrapure PGN-SAndi preparation (500 μg/mL in PBS) with 20 μg/ml lysostaphin, which specifically cleaves the pentaglycine bridges of S. aureus PG. The digestion reduced the suspension turbidity (OD600 from 0.523 to 0.262), confirming enzymatic cleavage. Both lysostaphin-treated and untreated PG samples were subsequently diluted and incubated with 10 μM HNP1, and the remaining peptide in the supernatant was quantified with LC-TOF. The amount of free HNP1 did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. S9), indicating that HNP1 interacts with regions of the PG molecule distinct from the lysostaphin-sensitive cross-links.

These findings support a non-genetic mechanism whereby HNP1 promotes S. aureus biofilm formation through interactions with bacterial cell wall components, primarily PG and WTA.

Dimerization and hydrophobicity are essential for HNP1's biofilm-enhancing activity

To identify the structural determinants of HNP1 responsible for its biofilm-enhancing effect, we compared a panel of site-directed HNP1 mutants using the CV staining assay in S. aureus ATCC 29213. As shown in Fig. 8A, two dimerization-deficient mutants, W26A and MeIle-20 [27,48], almost lost their ability to enhance biofilm formation, revealing the critical role of HNP1 dimerization. In addition, reduced biofilm enhancement was observed for the I6A, E13A, and I20A-L25A mutants, indicating that these residues also contribute to HNP1-bacteria interactions. To further evaluate the contribution of hydrophobicity, we gradually restored the sidechain hydrophobicity at residue 26 by substituting Ala with increasingly hydrophobic analogs (Abu, Nva, Nle, Ahp, and Bpa), and partial recovery of biofilm enhancement was observed with these substitutions (Fig. 8C). These results were consistent with previous reports that hydrophobicity at W26 is critical for maintaining HNP1's dimer structure and, consequently, its biological function [27,48,49].

Discussion

In this study, we expanded our previous observations that HNP1 promotes biofilm formation by A. baumannii [19] and demonstrated that this phenomenon is not restricted to a single species but is broadly conserved across both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, particularly effective in S. aureus. Although the enhancement of biofilm appears to be a common effect, the underlying mechanisms by which HNP1 acts vary between bacterial species. For instance, prior research identified OmpA and OmpC as targets of HNP1 in S. flexneri [17,38] and OmpA in A. baumannii [19], whereas our current data indicate that HNP1 interacts with peptidoglycan and WTA in S. aureus. These findings reveal a versatile and species-dependent repertoire of bacterial targets by HNP1, further advancing our understanding of defensins not only as antimicrobial effectors, but also as modulators of microbial colonization and biofilm dynamics, which may have implications for understanding infection persistence and antibiotic tolerance.

S. aureus is a common commensal organism in humans and a frequent cause of infection and inflammation [1,2,[5], [6], [7]], whereas neutrophil infiltration usually occurs and further elevates local HNP1 levels [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. While extracellular levels of HNPs are typically low under physiological conditions, HNP1 is stored at local concentrations of 10–50 mg/ml in neutrophil granules [[50], [51], [52]]. Physiological studies have shown that the nasopharyngeal mucosa of S. aureus carriers can contain ∼2.7 μM HNP1 with local levels rising in response to inflammation [53,54]. We also found that HNP1 can reach to 2.8–16.6 μM in BALF/sputum samples of A. baumannii-infected patients [19], comparable to the concentrations used in our experiments. This supports the plausibility that HNP1 could promote biofilm formation in clinical settings. However, HNP1 concentrations can vary widely depending on anatomical site, individual differences, and the presence of infection or inflammation. Accordingly, the concentrations used in our assays, while within the reported physiological range for certain clinical contexts, may not fully recapitulate all physiological conditions. Besides, the relationship between the HNP1 concentration and biofilm formation was not linear. While low concentrations enhanced biofilm formation, higher concentrations (≥16 μM) attenuated this effect. This dose-dependent biphasic behaviour likely reflects a balance between HNP1's biofilm-promoting activity and its intrinsic antimicrobial properties against S. aureus. In contrast to its membrane-disrupting mechanism in Gram-negative bacteria [[55], [56], [57]], HNP1 targets lipid II in S. aureus, inhibiting cell wall biosynthesis [44]. This bactericidal activity, though reduced in salt-rich media like TSB, remains moderately active to inhibit growth at higher concentrations (Fig. S1B and S1D), which may in turn interfere with biofilm development.

Given that biofilm formation contributes to antibiotic tolerance, we investigated whether HNP1-induced biofilms are more resistant to antimicrobial treatment. Indeed, biofilms formed in the presence of HNP1 exhibited increased survival when exposed to ampicillin, tetracycline, or kanamycin, like our previous study in A. baumannii [19]. This is likely due to the well-known barrier function of biofilms, which restricts antibiotic penetration and efficacy [58,59]. Similar observations have also been reported for the membrane-permeabilizing antimicrobial peptides that lyse the bacteria and form a barrier, preventing further killing against dense bacterial cell populations [60,61]. Nevertheless, additional mechanisms beyond physical shielding may also be involved and merit further exploration.

Regarding the mode of HNP1 action, our data suggest that HNP1 acts primarily during the early stages of biofilm formation, rather than the later stages of matrix production or biofilm maturation. We observed markedly enhanced bacterial adhesion to both biotic (host cells) and abiotic (glass surfaces) substrates, consistent with our previous findings that HNP1 can enhance bacterial adhesion in S. flexneri and A. baumannii [17,19,38]. This notion is further supported by transcriptomic profiling in S. aureus: HNP1 did not broadly upregulate classical biofilm-associated genes. Instead, it triggered moderate transcriptional changes primarily related to metabolism and transport, likely reflecting the bacterial response to HNP1's antimicrobial pressure. In addition, a subset of virulence-related genes was upregulated. These results align with an earlier report that HNP1 can modulate S. aureus virulence expression [16], though the functional consequences of such transcriptional changes remain to be elucidated.

Strain-specific differences further complicated the interpretation of our results. While CV staining showed no enhancement of biofilm formation in S. aureus ATCC 25923, SEM, aggregation, and sedimentation analyses revealed that HNP1 indeed interacted with this strain. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of CV staining as a sole readout to assess biofilm changes. Notably, the ATCC 25923 strain displayed a greater baseline aggregation and sedimentation than strain ATCC 29213, potentially masking additional effects of HNP1. Similar inconsistencies were also observed in our transposon library screen using the Newman mutants. While CV-based screening identified saeS mutants as potential hits, follow-up experiments showed that these mutants responded to HNP1 similarly to the wild-type Newman strain in both aggregation and sedimentation assays. This again suggests that CV staining assays may miss or misclassify HNP1-responsive phenotypes, especially when downstream phenotypes like adhesion are uncoupled from matrix production.

When comparing various bacterial species, we found that HNP1 broadly enhanced bacterial aggregation but had divergent effects on sedimentation. Some species that exhibited enhanced biofilm formation (e.g., L. monocytogenes and S. pneumoniae) did not show increased sedimentation in the presence of HNP1, and in some cases, sedimentation was even suppressed. These findings imply that sedimentation is not a prerequisite for biofilm enhancement and reinforce the hypothesis that HNP1 promotes biofilm primarily through increased adhesion and aggregation.

Our discovery that HNP1 interacts with S. aureus PG and WTA offers a plausible explanation for its pro-biofilm activity. Previous studies have reported that human enteric α-defensin HD5 and the cyclic θ-defensins can covalently bind to carbohydrates or glycoproteins [62,63], suggesting that similar interactions may occur between HNP1 and the carbohydrate backbone of S. aureus PG. In addition, WTA carries abundant phosphate groups, rendering it a highly anionic polymer that likely interacts electrostatically with the cationic HNP1. The possible covalent interaction between PG and HNP1, together with the electrostatic attraction between HNP1 and WTA, may cooperatively facilitate bacterial aggregation and surface colonization, thereby providing a non-transcriptional route for biofilm initiation. While this finding differs from previous reports identifying OmpA or lipid II as primary HNP1 targets [19,38,44], it reflects the multifunctional nature of defensins. Binding to lipid II yields bactericidal activity, whereas interactions with PG and WTA appear to drive pro-biofilm behavior. Nonetheless, the precise molecular mechanism governing these interactions remains to be fully elucidated. It is also plausible that additional, yet unidentified, HNP1 targets contribute to distinct functional outcomes under different physiological conditions.

Two limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the potential contribution of capsule to HNP1-enhanced biofilm formation remains unresolved. Although all strains carried cap5 or cap8 locus, the presence of capsule genes does not guarantee production of capsular exopolysaccharides [35], and our study lacked direct characterization of capsule expression, such as assays using capsule-specific antibodies or genetically engineered capsule-expressing strains. Second, the concentrations of HNP1 used in this study are not necessarily physiologically relevant. We examined a range of 2–32 μM, which may reflect only a subset of in vivo conditions. Nevertheless, the presence of HNP1 at colonization and infection sites, particularly under inflammatory conditions, makes this scenario biologically plausible. Future investigations, including both animal models (e.g., nasal colonization, wound infection, or device-associated infection) and clinical studies, will be essential to determine whether HNP1 indeed facilitates biofilm formation during infection and contributes to persistent colonization, antimicrobial resistance, or treatment failure.
