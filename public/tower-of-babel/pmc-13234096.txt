The link between dental care use and incident diabetes, cancer, and Alzheimer disease among older Americans: the health and retirement study
Authors: Nafise Agheshteh Sabzevar, Farhan Musaie, Ava Ostovar Ravari, Narin Gharehdaghi, Koosha Kianfar, Sina Ahmadi, Shahryar Irannejadrankouhi, Tara Fazel, Sina Sadeghi, Danyal Yarahmadi, Hadis Malekpour, Niloofar Deravi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral health is crucial for overall well-being, especially in older adults at higher risk for chronic diseases. This study examines the link between regular dental care and 10-year incidence of major conditions—Alzheimer’s, dementia, stroke, diabetes, cancer—using data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a representative survey of older Americans. Data from 15,320 dementia‑free participants aged 50 years and older interviewed in 2008 were analyzed. Regular dental visits in the previous 2 years were the primary exposure, and a composite dental condition measure (poor versus good, combining edentulism and self‑rated oral health) was examined in an oral‑health subsample. Incident Alzheimer’s disease, all‑cause dementia, stroke, diabetes, and cancer over approximately 10 years of follow‑up were ascertained from physician diagnoses and cognitive assessments. Multivariable logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), controlling for sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. Regular dental visiting was associated with a substantially lower 10‑year risk of cognitive diseases, stroke, cancer, and diabetes outcomes. In fully adjusted models, regular attenders had markedly reduced odds of incident Alzheimer’s disease and all‑cause dementia compared with non‑attenders, and similar but weaker associations with stroke, diabetes, and cancer were observed. In the oral‑health subsample, poor dental condition was strongly related to higher odds of incident all‑cause dementia, with attenuated but persisting elevation after adjustment, while showing little evidence of association with incident Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, diabetes, or cancer. In this large cohort of older Americans, regular dental care use and favourable baseline dental condition were both associated with a lower 10‑year incidence of dementia, particularly all‑cause dementia, whereas links with vascular and metabolic outcomes were limited. These findings support integrating oral health maintenance into strategies to preserve cognitive health in ageing populations.Keywords: Chronic diseases, Dental care utilization, Difficulty eating, Aging population, HRS

Introduction

The population of the United States (US) is aging rapidly. As Americans live longer, they face an increasing prevalence of chronic conditions. Numerous chronic diseases and poor oral health are linked to common risk factors, including stress, nicotine use, loneliness, and poor diet (Sheiham and Watt 2000, Finlayson et al. 2024). Chronic diseases account for approximately 71% of global deaths annually, with 41 million fatalities reported worldwide, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/noncommunicable-diseases. Consequently, impaired dental health can have long-term effects on overall health and well-being (Holmstrup et al. 2017).

Comprehensive clinical evaluations for various health conditions are costly and time-consuming (Meng et al. 2014). In reality, approximately 70% of seniors lack dental insurance (Manski et al. 2009). To efficiently assess and monitor population health, data are frequently collected through self-reported surveys and inquiries (Meng et al. 2014). In the United States, there are differences in dental care usage and tooth loss across various racial and ethnic groups (Griffin et al. 2019, Kramarow 2017). Older persons who are edentulous are more vulnerable to long-term health issues and are less likely to receive routine dental care (Kramarow 2017, Weintraub et al. 2019).

The US National Institutes of Health (NIH) funds two large-scale, independent datasets that gather self-reported health and oral health data for older adults: the “Health and Retirement Study” (HRS) and the continuing “All of Us (AoU) Research Program” (Sonnega et al. 2014). In 1992, the HRS was introduced by the University of Michigan with assistance from the Social Security Administration, the National Institute on Aging, and the NIH. The project maintains around 20,000 participants at any given moment by recruiting additional birth cohorts on a regular basis (Weintraub et al. 2024).

A study of Korean National Health Insurance claims data from 2002 to 2013 demonstrated correlations between periodontitis and conditions such as cerebral infarction, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction (MI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and rheumatoid arthritis, based on both bivariate and multivariate statistical analyses (Kim et al. 2013). In this study, we aim to reassess the relationship of dental care use and dental condition with incident Alzheimer’s disease, all-cause dementia, stroke, diabetes, and cancer using data from version of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) in the United States.

Methods

Place of research

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative longitudinal cohort conducted in the United States.

Data source and population selection

Data were drawn from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), an ongoing nationally representative cohort of US adults aged ≥ 50 years and their spouses (Sonnega et al. 2014). The HRS conducts biennial interviews that capture health, socioeconomic, and behavioral information. For this investigation, we used the RAND HRS longitudinal files linked to the 2008 core interview (wave 9), which uniquely includes an expanded oral health module and therefore served as the analytic baseline.

From all 2008 respondents, we first selected individuals with available information on dental attendance in the previous two years. We then excluded participants interviewed by proxy and those who met a dementia threshold at baseline on the 27-point cognitive battery, in order to construct a cognitively intact risk cohort. This process yielded the main study population used to examine regular dental visits in relation to subsequent chronic disease.

A nested oral-health subcohort was defined by the presence of key dental status indicators, specifically self-rated oral health and tooth loss measures, allowing derivation of a composite dental condition variable. Participants contributed to disease-specific analyses if they were free of the corresponding condition at baseline and had outcome data during follow-up. Follow-up extended through HRS waves 10–15, providing up to approximately 10 years of observation per individual.

Exposure

The primary exposure was regular dental attendance at baseline, assessed using the 2008 question on whether the respondent had seen a dentist in the previous two years or since the last interview. Responses were coded as a binary variable contrasting regular dental visits with no regular visits.

In the oral-health subsample, we defined a secondary exposure capturing overall dental condition. Poor dental condition was coded when participants were edentulous or rated the condition of their mouth and teeth as fair or poor. Good dental condition required very good or good self-rated oral health and absence of edentulism. This composite was intended to reflect both structural tooth loss and perceived oral health status.

Outcomes

We examined five systemic outcomes over approximately 10 years of follow-up: Alzheimer’s disease, all-cause dementia, stroke, diabetes, and cancer. Incident Alzheimer’s disease and dementia were identified using respondent or proxy reports of a physician diagnosis in waves 10–15, supplemented for cognition score by the telephone interview for cognitive status (TICS)-27 (Crimmins et al. 2011). Participants whose cognitive performance fell at or below the established dementia threshold at a score of ≤ 6 during follow-up were classified as having incident dementia.

Stroke, diabetes, and cancer outcomes were based on biennial reports of ever having received a physician diagnosis of each condition. For every outcome, individuals with evidence of that condition at baseline were excluded from the corresponding analysis, thereby forming outcome-specific risk sets. Participants were followed from the 2008 interview until the first report of the disease of interest or their last completed interview, whichever occurred earlier. This approach allowed construction of consistent 10-year cumulative incidence indicators across conditions while respecting baseline disease status.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were selected a priori based on their potential to confound associations between dental health and systemic disease. Sociodemographic factors included age, sex, race, Hispanic ethnicity, education, and household socioeconomic status derived from total income categorized into zero income plus quartiles among positive income. Health-related behaviors comprised smoking status (never, former, current), typical alcohol consumption (none, light, heavy), and frequency of vigorous and moderate physical activity.

Clinical characteristics included body mass index as a continuous measure, global self-rated health collapsed into three categories, and indicators of prior physician-diagnosed stroke, diabetes, and cancer. All covariates were taken from the 2008 interview to ensure temporal precedence over exposures and outcomes.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2. We first described baseline sociodemographic, lifestyle, systemic health, and oral health characteristics by regular versus no regular dental visits. Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) and were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, whereas categorical variables are shown as counts (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s χ² tests. For longitudinal analyses, we constructed a dementia-free baseline cohort in 2008 and defined 10-year incidence of Alzheimer’s disease, all-cause dementia, stroke, diabetes, and cancer using information from waves 10–15, excluding participants with the relevant condition at baseline for each outcome. Regular dental visiting (yes/no) was the primary exposure, and a composite measure of poor dental condition (edentulism or fair/poor self-rated oral health versus very good/good without edentulism) was examined in a subsample with detailed oral data.

We fitted multivariable logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Models for regular dental visits were adjusted for age, sex, race, Hispanic origin, education, income, smoking, alcohol use, body mass index, physical activity, self-rated health, and baseline comorbidities, while dental-condition models included staged adjustment for age and sex (Model 1) and additionally education, smoking, and race (Model 2).

Results

At baseline, the analytic cohort comprised 15,320 dementia-free HRS participants interviewed in 2008 who had information on dental attendance. Just over one third reported no regular dental visits (n = 5,432), whereas nearly two thirds had seen a dentist within the previous two years (n = 9,888). Individuals without regular visits tended to be slightly older and were more often women. They were also more likely to identify as Black or belong to another non-White racial group, to have lower educational attainment, and to cluster in the lower income categories compared with those who reported regular attendance (all p-values < 0.05). Lifestyle profiles differed in parallel: participants without regular dental visits were more frequently current smokers, less physically active, and more likely to rate their overall health as fair or poor. Baseline stroke and diabetes were also more common in this group, while baseline cancer prevalence was similar across categories (Table 1).

Marked oral health gradients were observed by dental visiting pattern. In the oral-health subsample, participants without regular dental visits showed substantially greater structural tooth loss (all p-values < 0.05): most had lost more than two teeth and around half were edentulous, compared with a small minority among regular attenders. Loss of all upper or all lower jaw teeth followed a similar pattern. Use of dentures was also more frequent in the non-attending group, who were less likely to report having no dentures. Self-rated oral health differed sharply (p-value < 0.001): poor or fair ratings were concentrated among those not visiting a dentist regularly, while very good or good ratings dominated among regular attenders. Consequently, poor dental condition, defined as edentulism or fair/poor oral health, was highly prevalent in the non-attending group and relatively uncommon in the regular-visit group (Table 2).

Regular dental visiting was significantly associated with a lower 10-year risk of systemic disease (Table 3; Fig. 1). After multivariable adjustment for sociodemographic factors, health behaviors, body mass index, physical activity, self-rated health, and baseline comorbidities, regular attendance was linked to a notably reduced odds of Alzheimer’s disease, with an adjusted OR around one-third lower than in non-attenders (OR = 0.68). A similar or slightly stronger association was observed for all-cause dementia, where regular visitors had roughly two-thirds the odds of developing dementia over follow-up compared with those without regular care (OR = 0.65). Associations with vascular and metabolic outcomes were more modest but remained statistically significant: regular visiting was related to lower odds of incident stroke, diabetes, and cancer, with ORs below 1.0 and confidence intervals that did not include the null.

In the oral-health subsample, poor dental condition showed a more selective pattern of association (Table 4; Fig. 2). In age- and sex-adjusted models, poor dental condition was strongly linked to incident all-cause dementia, with an OR approaching three when compared with good dental condition. This association was attenuated but remained substantial after additional adjustment for education, smoking, and race, with the odds of incident dementia still approximately doubled. In contrast, there was no clear evidence that poor dental condition was associated with incident Alzheimer’s disease after adjustment, as confidence intervals were wide and crossed unity.

For the non-cognitive outcomes, point estimates generally suggested higher odds of stroke and diabetes among participants with poor dental condition, but these associations lost precision and statistical significance after multivariable adjustment. Cancer incidence appeared largely unrelated to baseline dental condition across all modelling stages. Taken together, these findings indicate that regular contact with dental services is broadly associated with reduced long-term risk of several major chronic diseases, whereas underlying poor dental status, capturing edentulism and unfavourable self-rated oral health, is most clearly linked to subsequent all-cause dementia in this older population.

Discussion

The present study investigated the association between dental care visits and the incidence of five major chronic conditions in older Americans, including Alzheimer’s disease, all cause dementia, stroke, cancer, and diabetes. The results showed a significant protective association between regular dental care and decreased incidence of major conditions including Alzheimer’s disease, all cause dementia, stroke, cancer, and diabetes. It aligns with “mouth to mind” connection in neurodegenerative disease prevention (Chamut et al. 2024). This points to a potential preventive strategy that could be integrated into prevention frameworks in dementia (Rice 2021).

Our study showed that regular dental care was associated with an approximately 32% lower odds of incident Alzheimer’s disease after adjustment for key demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. The inverse relationship of dental care and Alzheimer’s disease, can be understood through several biological pathways. Regular dental care likely reduces the overall inflammatory burden associated with untreated oral diseases, which has a direct link to increased Alzheimer’s risk (Tiisanoja et al. 2019). It also minimizes harmful bacteria like Porphyromonas gingivalis which have been found in the brains of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (Kanagasingam et al. 2020). A twelve-year cohort prospective study showed that gum treatment for adults aged ≥ 50 years with periodontal symptoms can improve for their cognitive health, and 38% decrease risk of dementia (Qi et al. 2024).

Furthermore, dental providers routinely assess patients and educate them about cardiovascular health, smoking, and other factors that can significantly reduce Alzheimer’s disease risk (Rice 2021).

The association between dental care and reduced Alzheimer’s risk observed in our study is supports the growing evidence suggesting an impact of oral health on decline of cognitive impairment (Chamut et al. 2024, Tiisanoja et al. 2019, Hamza et al. 2021). Individuals with multiple carious teeth face are at elevated risk of developing cognitive impairment (Tiisanoja et al. 2019). Our findings suggest that dental care services, rather than just the absence of oral disease, may extend protective benefits. Due to its preventive effect, the integration of oral health providers into dementia prevention strategies has been discussed recently in the literature (Rice 2021, Qi et al. 2024).

There were also significant associations between dental care visit and all cause dementia, stroke, diabetes, and cancer incidence; whereas these associations were not significant for diabetes, stroke, and cancer incidence in our subgroup analysis based on poor and good dental health. While several studies showed a connection between oral health and systemic disease, no significant association was observed in our analysis regarding poor dental health, except for all-cause dementia.

The absence of a significant association between diabetes and poor dental condition, alongside a significant inverse association between diabetes incidence and dental care visit may suggest a complex bidirectional relationship between these two, where the timing and nature of dental interventions could influence outcomes differently. Additionally, diabetes development may be more linked to specific oral health conditions rather than general dental service utilization. Previous studies that found a relationship between diabetes mellitus and oral health were often investigating periodontal diseases (Păunică et al. 2023, Preshaw et al. 2012).

Similarly, the lack of association between cancer incidence and poor dental condition, alongside the significant inverse association between regular dental care visit and cancer incidence, suggests that dental condition and dental care utilization may capture different aspects of oral and general health. There is a diverse etiological factor in the development of cancer that that may not be significantly influenced by the oral health through routine dental care visits. Similar to diabetes, most studies that concluded oral health can decrease risk of cancers, that were often about oral health, were investigating periodontal diseases (Joseph 2002, Mathur et al. 2019).

The combination of non-significant findings for diabetes and cancer regarding poor dental health and significant reversed associations of mentioned diseases with regular dental visit, in our results, present a more complex picture than some previous studies that have identified oral health-diabetes connections. However, these differences may reveal differences in study populations, follow-up periods, and the specific aspects of oral health or dental care measured across different studies.

The varying relationship between routine dental care and these chronic diseases offers important insights into the relationship between oral health and systemic disease prevention in the elderly American population. This pattern suggests that the mechanisms linking dental care utilization to health outcomes may be condition-specific and may not represent a general health effect.

For clinical practice, these results suggest that regular dental care among older adults may represent an underutilized strategy for supporting cognitive health, including Alzheimer’s disease and all-cause dementia prevention.

Our study benefits from several methodological strengths that promotes the reliability and generalizability of our findings. The use of the Health and Retirement Study provides access to a large nationwide longitudinal dataset that reflects the experiences of elderly Americans across a wide range of demographic and socioeconomic backgrounds. The focus on new cases assists examination of relationships between dental care use and disease development, and helps reduce concerns about reverse nature of cross-sectional analyses.

The analytical approach, which adjusted for key demographic and behavioral covariates including age, gender, race, and smoking status, helps isolate the specific contribution of dental care and disease risk. The extended follow-up period and large sample size provide adequate statistical power to detect associations and reducing influence of random variation.

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Self-reported data used for both dental care utilization and disease diagnosis raises a potential for misclassification bias, that would likely reduce the strength of the observed association rather than strengthening it. Alzheimer’s disease diagnoses in the Health and Retirement Study are based on self- or proxy-reported physician diagnoses and cognitive testing thresholds. As a result, some degree of misclassification between Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias is possible. This potential overlap may partly explain the differential associations observed between dental care utilization and Alzheimer’s disease versus all-cause dementia in the present analyses.

Another limitation is that the present analyses did not incorporate the complex survey sampling weights of the Health and Retirement Study. Although this may limit the generalizability of effect estimates to the broader U.S. older adult population, the internal validity of the observed associations within the analytic cohort remains intact. Similar unweighted approaches have been used in prior HRS-based etiologic analyses when the primary aim is to examine exposure–outcome relationships rather than population-level prevalence.

Classifying dental care use simply as any care versus no care over a two-year period of time, overlooks the importance of differences in the type, frequency, intensity, and quality of dental services received, which may have an important role in health outcomes.

The potential for residual confounding remains despite statistical adjustments, as unmeasured factors such as health consciousness or access to healthcare may influence both dental care utilization patterns and chronic disease risk. Additionally, the focus on older adults limits the generalizability of findings to younger populations who may exhibit different patterns of oral health maintenance and disease development.

The study design does not evaluate dose-response relationships, and does not examine specific types of dental interventions that may be more effective for disease prevention.

Another limitation is that the present analyses did not incorporate survey weights. This may limit the generalizability of our quantitative estimates to the entire older U.S. population, although the internal associations observed are still considered valid for the study sample.

Future research should investigate the specific dental care interventions that contribute most significantly to reducing Alzheimer’s disease and dementia and clarify whether similar mechanisms explain associations with other chronic diseases. Understanding the mechanisms underlying the protective associations could inform the development of interventions that have the best effect on the cognitive status.

Studies with long-term follow-up period are needed to clarify relationships between oral health maintenance and chronic disease development.

Conclusion

The present findings reveal a significant protective association between dental care utilization and the incidence of Alzheimer’s disease, all-cause dementia, stroke, diabetes, and cancer. Subgroup Analyses based on poor versus good dental health showed no significant associations with Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes, stroke, or cancer incidence in the population of older Americans, whereas poor dental health was associated with increased incidence of all-cause dementia. These results emphasize the importance of integrating oral health maintenance into comprehensive care for chronic disease prevention.
