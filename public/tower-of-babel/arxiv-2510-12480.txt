Cyclic and alternating U U -statistics Thanks: Supported by the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation and the Swedish Research Council
Svante Janson

Abstract
. We define cyclic U U -statistics as a variant of U U -statistics based on variables X 1 , … , X n X_{1},\dots,X_{n} that are assumed to be cyclically ordered. We also define alternating U U -statistics where in the definition terms are summed with alternating sings (in three different ways). Only U U -statistics of order 2 are considered. The definitions are inspired by special cases studied by Chebikin (2008) and Even-Zohar (2017) for random permutations. We show limit theorems similar to well-known results for standard U U -statistics, but with some differences between the different versions. In particular, we find both “nondegenerate” normal limits and “degenerate” non-normal limits.



1.
Introduction

U
-statistics were introduced by
Hoeffding 1948
as statistics of the
form

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{n}=\bar{U}_{n}(f)=\bar{U}_{n}(f;X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=\sum f(X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{m}})

(1.1)

where

m
(the
order
of the
U
-statistic) and
n
are integers with

1\leqslant m\leqslant n
,
the sum is over all
(n)_{m}=n!/(n-m)!
different

m
-tuples
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}
of distinct
indices in
\{1,\dots,n\}
,

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
is a sequence of random variables taking values in
some measurable space
{\mathcal{X}}
,
and

f:{\mathcal{X}}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, called the
kernel
of the

U
-statistic.
(
Hoeffding 1948
and many later authors
include in the definition
a normalization factor
1/(n)_{m}
; this is often convenient, but in the
present paper we choose to omit such factors in the definitions.)
The random variables
X_{i}
are usually assumed to be independent and
identically distributed (i.i.d.), and this will be assumed in the
present paper.
We will also assume that the kernel
f
is square integrable
in the sense

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}|f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})|^{2}<\infty
,
which we write as

f\in L^{2}=L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}^{m})
.

In the definition (
1.1
), the order of the variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}

does not matter; in other words, the indices
1,\dots,n
are used for
labelling but their order does not matter and any other labels could be used.
Another definition of
U
-statistics where the order of the variables matters
is obtained by summing only over increasing sequences
i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}
:

\displaystyle U_{n}=U_{n}(f)=U_{n}(f;X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}}f(X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{m}}).

(1.2)

Note that if
f
is symmetric, as is often assumed,
the definitions (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)
differ only by an unimportant factor
m!
. In fact,
\bar{U}_{n}
in
(
1.1
) remains the same if
f
is replaced by its symmetrization;
hence we may as well assume that
f
in (
1.1
) is symmetric, and
therefore (
1.1
) can be seen as a special case of the more general
(
1.2
). (Although many applications use only the symmetric version
(
1.1
), or equivalently (
1.2
) with a symmetric kernel
f
,
there are also many applications that require the asymmetric version
(
1.2
).)
The literature on
U
-statistics and applications of them is enormous, and we
will in the sequel only give a few relevant references.

In the present paper we consider
four modifications of the definitions above,
defined in the following two subsections.
As in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), we may include
f
and the variables
X_{i}

in the notation, but we often omit them when they are clear from the context.
Our main results are theorems on the asymptotic distribution of these
modifications, stated in Sections
4
–
6
and summarized for
convenience in Section
7
.
The results are similar to the well-known results for classical
U
-statistics,
which we state for comparison in Section
3
,
although the details in the limit theorems differ between
the different versions.
In particular, in the classical case
U_{n}(f)
there is a well-known
dichotomy of the kernels
f

into a nondegenerate case with variance of order
n^{3}
and
an asymptotically normal distribution,
and a degenerate case with a variance of order
n^{2}
only
and with a non-normal limit distribution;
all but one of the versions studied here
exhibit the same two cases, but not necessarily for the same kernels;
however, for one version (Theorem
5.1
) there is no “nondegenerate” case.
Furthermore, for some versions there is an exceptional third, futher
degenerate and almost trivial case, with variance of order
n
and again a
normal limit distribution.
In the degenerate case, for all versions,
the limit distribution can be expressed as a
(possibly infinite) linear combination of centred squares of independent
standard normal variables, where the coefficients are the eigenvalues of a
certain integral operator with kernel derived from the kernel
f
of the

U
-statistic, although again the details differ between the differnt versions;
see e.g. (
3.6
).
(From an abstract point of view, the limit distribution is a Wiener chaos of
order 2, see e.g.
[
12
, in particular Theorem 6.1]
.)

Some simple examples are given in Section
8
; this includes applications to
the
writhe
and
alternating inversion number
of a uniformly
random permutation, previously defined and studied in
[
6
]
and

[
2
]
. These two examples were the inspiration of the definitions
and results in the present paper.

Some further results and open problems are given in Section
9
.

Appendix
A
contains the proof of the well-known Theorem
3.1
for
classical
U
-statistics, included for completeness; we also reuse parts of the
appendix in other proofs.
Appendix
B
collects some formulas for cumulants.
Appendix
C
gives further calculations for one example from Section
8
.

As mentioned above, in the degenerate cases, the asymptotic distribution is
described by the eigenvalues of an integral operators.
Consequently, some proofs and most examples require finding such eigenvalues;
this is straightforward in our cases, but we include details
for completeness.

1.1.
Cyclic
U
-statistics

For this version, we
regard the indices
1,\dots,n
as
circularly ordered instead of linearly ordered.
We regard the indices as elements of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
;
we therefore assume again that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables, and
extend the notation to
X_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
by
X_{i}:=X_{j}
if
i\equiv j\pmod{n}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

We consider only the case
m=2
and then define the
cyclic
U
-statistic

\displaystyle U^{\circ}_{n}=U^{\circ}_{n}(f):=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<n/2}f(X_{i},X_{i+j}).

(1.3)

Note that if we regard the elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
as lying on a circle in the
natural way, then for any pair of distinct
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the sum
(
1.3
) contains the term
f(X_{i},X_{j})
if the shortest path from
i
to
j

goes in the positive direction, and the term
f(X_{j},X_{i})
if the shortest path
goes in the negative direction; for even
n
, no terms
f(X_{i},X_{i+n/2})

appears at all.

One example of a cyclic
U
-statistic appears in a paper by
Even-Zohar 2017

where he studies the
writhe
of permutations and framed knots,
see Example
8.5
for details.
In particular,
[
6
]
studies the writhe of a uniformly random
permutation
and finds its asymptotic distribution.
The writhe of a uniformly random permutation can be written as
a cyclic
U
-statistic (
1.3
)
(see Example
8.5
again),
and this example is the motivation for the present paper, where
we study general cyclic
U
-statistics (assuming only that
f\in L^{2}
)
and prove general limit theorems; in particular, this
gives an alternative proof of the limit theorem by
Even-Zohar 2017

(where the theorem is proved by quite different methods).

Remark 1.1
.

If
f(x,y)
is a symmetric function, then we have

\displaystyle U^{\circ}_{n}=\begin{cases}U_{n},&n\text{ is odd}\\
U_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}f(X_{i},X_{i+n/2})&n\text{ is even},\end{cases}

(1.4)

where it is easily seen that the sum appearing for even
n
is
asymptotically negligible.
(If
f\in L^{2}
, this sum has variance
O(n)
since its terms are i.i.d.,
while
U_{n}
except in trivial
cases has variance of order at least
n^{2}
.)
Hence, we do not really obtain anything new for symmetric
f
.
The main interest seems to be in the opposite case, when
f
is
antisymmetric. We will in the sequel
give special attention to the cases of symmetric and antisymmetric kernels.

Note that every
f
may be decomposed as a sum of a symmetric and an
antisymmetric part; hence a
U
-statistic of order
m=2

(of any of the versions studied here)
can be written as a sum of a symmetric and an antisymmetric
U
-statistic;
We will see in Theorem
4.1
and Remark
4.4
that for the cyclic
U
-statistic

U^{\circ}_{n}
, the
two parts are asymptotically independent, and thus can be treated separately.
However, for the other
U
-statistics considered here, including the classical

U_{n}
,
this decomposition is of limited value since the two parts typically are
dependent, also asymptotically.
For an interesting example of this, largely taken from
[
14
]
, see
Example
8.2
.

\triangle

Remark 1.2
.

A variation of the definition (
1.3
) is the more symmetrical

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}^{\circ}_{n}(f):=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<n/2}f(X_{i},X_{i+j})-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<n/2}f(X_{i},X_{i-j}).

(1.5)

However, replacing
i
by
i+j
in the second sum yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}^{\circ}_{n}(f)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<n/2}\bigl(f(X_{i},X_{i+j})-f(X_{i+j},X_{i})\bigr)=U^{\circ}_{n}(g),

(1.6)

where
g(x,y):=f(x,y)-f(y,x)
.
Hence, cyclic
U
-statistics of the form (
1.5
)
are special cases of
U^{\circ}_{n}
, and therefore need not be considered further.
Note that the function
g
arising here always is antisymmetric;
conversely, if
f
is antisymmetric, then (
1.6
) implies

U^{\circ}_{n}(f)=\widetilde{U}^{\circ}_{n}(\frac{1}{2}f)
.
Consequently, the version
\widetilde{U}^{\circ}_{n}
is
equivalent to the special case of
U^{\circ}_{n}
for antisymmetric kernels only.

\triangle

1.2.
Alternating
U
-statistics

One of the results in
[
6
]
is that the writhe of a uniformly random
permutation has the same distribution as the

bi-alternating inversion number
, which is defined in
[
6
]

in analogy to the
alternating inversion number
defined in

[
2
]
, see Example
8.6
;
it is also shown in
[
6
]
that the alternating and
bi-alternating inversion numbers of a uniformly random permutation,
although similarly defined,
have quite different asymptotic distributions.

These numbers are defined as versions of the usual inversion number of a
permutation.
It is well-known that the usual inversion number of a uniformly random
permutation can be written as a
U
-statistic. Again we study
corresponding modifications of general
U
-statistics.
We consider again only the case of order
m=2
, so that (
1.2
) becomes

\displaystyle U_{n}=U_{n}(f):=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}f(X_{i},X_{j}).

(1.7)

We then define the
alternating
U
-statistics

\displaystyle U^{-+}_{n}=U^{-+}_{n}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(-1)^{i+1}f(X_{i},X_{j}),

(1.8)

\displaystyle U^{+-}_{n}=U^{+-}_{n}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(-1)^{j}f(X_{i},X_{j}),

(1.9)

\displaystyle U^{--}_{n}=U^{--}_{n}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(-1)^{i+j}f(X_{i},X_{j}).

(1.10)

We also call
U^{--}_{n}

bi-alternating
.
(As in
[
6
]
for a special case, see Example
8.5
.)

Remark 1.3
.

Define

\displaystyle f^{*}(x,y):=f(y,x).

(1.11)

Then, by replacing
X_{i}
by
X_{n+1-i}
, it follows that

\displaystyle U^{+-}_{n}(f)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(-1)^{j}f(X_{n+1-i},X_{n+1-j})=(-1)^{n}\sum_{1\leqslant k<\ell\leqslant n}(-1)^{k+1}f(X_{\ell},X_{k})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}U^{-+}_{n}(f^{*}).

(1.12)

Consequently, up to a trivial change of sign and replacing
f
by
f^{*}
,

U^{+-}_{n}
is the same as
U^{-+}_{n}
, and thus it suffices to consider the
latter.
On the other hand,
as noted in
[
6
]
for the example in Example
8.6
,
we will see
that
U^{--}
in general is quite different.

\triangle

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Some notation

We assume throughout that
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. random variables with
values in some measurable space
{\mathcal{X}}
,
and let
\nu
be the common distribution of
X_{i}
.
We let
X
denote any random variable with this distribution.
Thus
({\mathcal{X}},\nu)
is a probability space, which we for simplicity also
denote by
{\mathcal{X}}
.

We define
\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}:={\mathcal{X}}\times[0,1]
, where (as always below),
[0,1]
is equipped with the
Lebesgue measure
\ell
.

We assume also that
f:{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function such
that
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}|f(X_{1},X_{2})|^{2}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}|f(x,y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)<\infty.

(2.1)

We define

\displaystyle\mu:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{1},X_{2}).

(2.2)

We will mainly consider real-valued functions, but to apply functional
analysis it will sometimes
be convenient to also consider complex-valued fuctions.
When it is necessary to distinguish them, we use
L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathcal{X}})
for the
complex Hilbert space of complex-valued functions
g
on
{\mathcal{X}}
such that

\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}|g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty
, and

L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{X}})
for the subspace of real-valued functions (this is
a real Hilbert space).

All functions are assumed to be measurable.
We will sometimes omit “a.s.” or “a.e.” when this is obvious.

For a function
g:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{C}
,
let
T_{g}
denote the integral operator on
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}})
defined by

\displaystyle T_{g}\varphi(x):=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}g(x,y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y),\qquad\varphi\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}).

(2.3)

We will only consider the case when

g\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
; it is well-known that then,
for every
h\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}})
,
the integral in (
2.3
) converges for a.e.
x

and that
T_{g}h\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}})
,
so that
T_{g}
is well-defined,
and furthermore that
T_{g}
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator on
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}})
,
and thus in particular compact (and bounded).
(See e.g.
[
3
, Proposition II.4.7 and Exercise IX.2.19]
.)
We will also use the notation (
2.3
) for other measure spaces, in
particular
{\mathcal{X}}^{2}={\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
and

\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}={\mathcal{X}}\times[0,1]
.

Recall that a bounded operator
T
on a Hilbert space
H
is

self-adjoint
(also called
Hermitian
, or
symmetric
)
if
\langle Th,k\rangle=\langle h,Tk\rangle
for all
h,k\in H
. It is easily seen
that if
g
is real-valued and symmetric (i.e.,
g(x,y)=g(y,x)
), then
T_{g}

is self-adjoint.

Eigenvalues of operators are always counted with multiplicities; sets of
eigenvalues are thus in general really multisets.

We will occasionally
also use tensor notation for functions and operators.
If
g
and
h
are (real- or complex-valued) functions defined on measure
spaces
{\mathcal{Y}}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}
, then

g\otimes h
denotes the function
(y,z)\mapsto g(y)h(z)
defined on

{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Z}}
.
It is well known that if
(\varphi_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
and

(\psi_{\beta})_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}

are orthonormal bases in
L^{2}({\mathcal{Y}})
and
L^{2}({\mathcal{Z}})
,
then

(\varphi_{\alpha}\otimes\psi_{\beta})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\beta\in\mathcal{B}}
is an orthonormal basis in

L^{2}({\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Z}})
.
If
g\in L^{2}({\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
, and
h\in L^{2}({\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathcal{Z}})
, then

g\otimes h
can be regarded as a kernel on
{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Z}}
, and it is easily
seen that

\displaystyle T_{g\otimes h}(\varphi\otimes\psi)=(T_{g}\varphi)\otimes(T_{h}\psi),\qquad\varphi\in L^{2}({\mathcal{Y}}),\psi\in L^{2}({\mathcal{Z}}).

(2.4)

We write also
T_{g}\otimes T_{h}:=T_{g\otimes h}
.
(This is a special case of tensor products of linear operators on Hilbert,
or more general, spaces, but we have no need for the general theory.)
It follows from (
2.4
) that
if
\varphi
is an eigenfunction of
T_{g}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
and

\psi
is an eigenfunction of
T_{h}
with eigenvalue
\rho
,
then
\varphi\otimes\psi
is an eigenfunction of

T_{g}\otimes T_{h}=T_{g\otimes h}
with eigenvalue
\lambda\rho
.

For a function
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
, we define
its
symmetric
and
antisymmetric parts
by,
recalling (
1.11
),

\displaystyle{f_{\mathsf{s}}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(f+f^{*})\qquad\text{and}\qquad{f_{\mathsf{a}}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(f-f^{*}).

(2.5)

Thus
f={f_{\mathsf{s}}}+{f_{\mathsf{a}}}
.
We also define (as another form of symmetrization)
the symmetric function
{\widehat{f}}
on
\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}\times\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}
by
(recall that
\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}:={\mathcal{X}}\times[0,1])

\displaystyle{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}f(x,y),&t<u,\\
f(y,x),&t>u.\end{cases}

(2.6)

(For completeness, we may define
{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr):=0
when
t=u
;
this case has measure 0 and is therefore irrelevant.)
Note that, with
\widehat{\nu}:=\nu\times\ell
,

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}}|{\widehat{f}}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{\nu}(\widehat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{\nu}(\widehat{y})

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}|{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}|f(x,y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}|f(x,y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y).

(2.7)

In some cases (when studying
U^{-+}
and
U^{+-}
) we need also the
corresponding antisymmetric function on
\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}\times\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}
defined by

\displaystyle{\widecheck{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}f(x,y),&t<u,\\
-f(y,x),&t>u.\end{cases}

(2.8)

For a real number
x
, we let
\lfloor x\rfloor
be
x
rounded down to the nearest
integer.
The complex unit is denoted
\mathrm{i}
. (This should not be confused with
i
,
often used to denote indices.)

\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{E}\}
denotes the indicator function of an event
\mathcal{E}
.
The sign function
\operatorname{sgn}
is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{sgn}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&x>0,\\
0,&x=0,\\
-1,&x<0.\end{cases}

(2.9)

We let
\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}
,
\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}
, and
\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}
denote convergence of random variables
in distribution, in probability, and almost surely, respectively.

Given a sequence
(a_{n})_{n}
, we let

Y_{n}=O_{L^{2}}(a_{n})
mean that
Y_{n}
are random
variables such that

\lVert Y_{n}\rVert_{L^{2}}:=(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[|Y_{n}|^{2}])^{1/2}=O(a_{n})
.
Similarly,

Y_{n}=o_{L^{2}}(a_{n})
means that

\lVert Y_{n}\rVert_{L^{2}}=o(a_{n})
, in other words that
Y_{n}/a_{n}\to 0
in
L^{2}
.

Unspecified limits are as
{n\to\infty}
.

2.2.
Hoeffding’s decomposition

As said above, we assume
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})
.
The basis of our work (as for many previous results for
U
-statistics)
is the orthogonal decomposition
introduced (in the symmetric case) by

Hoeffding 1948
. In the case
m=2
treated here,
the orthogonal decomposition is:

\displaystyle f(x,y)=f_{\emptyset}+f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(y)+f_{12}(x,y)

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle f_{\emptyset}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{1},X_{2})=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)=\mu,

(2.11)

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(x,X)-f_{\emptyset}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-f_{\emptyset},

(2.12)

\displaystyle f_{2}(y)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X,y)-f_{\emptyset}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-f_{\emptyset},

(2.13)

\displaystyle f_{12}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=f(x,y)-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)-f_{\emptyset}.

(2.14)

Equivalently, (
2.12
)–(
2.14
) can be written

\displaystyle f_{1}(X_{1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{1}]-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{1},X_{2}),

(2.15)

\displaystyle f_{2}(X_{2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{2}]-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{1},X_{2}),

(2.16)

\displaystyle f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})

\displaystyle=f(X_{1},X_{2})-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{1}]-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{2}]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{1},X_{2}).

(2.17)

Of course, (
2.14
) makes (
2.10
) trivial, but the point is that the
four terms in the sum in (
2.10
) are orthogonal in
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})
, which is
easily verified from the definitions (
2.11
)–(
2.17
), which imply

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f_{2}(X)=0,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f_{12}(x,X)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f_{12}(X,y)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})=0.

(2.19)

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(i,j)
and
(k,l)
be two pairs of indices with
i\neq j
,
k\neq l
,
and
\{i,j\}\neq\{k,l\}

(i.e.,

2.3.
Three distributions

The limit distributions below will, apart from normal distributions,
be given by (possibly infinite) linear combinations of
independent copies of the following three random variables.

(i)

If
\zeta\in N(0,1)
, then
\zeta^{2}
has a
\chi^{2}(1)=\Gamma(\frac{1}{2},2)

distribution. We will use the centred variable
\zeta^{2}-1
, which has mean 0,
variance

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\zeta^{2}-1\bigr)=2,

(2.20)

and characteristic function

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\mathrm{i}t(\zeta^{2}-1)}=e^{-\mathrm{i}t}(1-2\mathrm{i}t)^{-1/2},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.21)

(ii)

Lévy’s
stochastic area
, which we denote by
\eta
, is
the stochastic integral

\displaystyle\eta:=\int_{0}^{1}B_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}B_{2}(x)-\int_{0}^{1}B_{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}B_{1}(x)

(2.22)

where
B_{1}(x)
and
B_{2}(x)
are two independent Brownian motions.
See e.g.
[
17
]
,
[
24
]
,

[
21
, Theorem II.43 and its Corollary]
.
For us this background is not important; we only need that
the stochastic area is a random variable
\eta

with the characteristic function

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\mathrm{i}t\eta}=\frac{1}{\cosh(t)},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.23)

The stochastic area has variance, from (
2.22
) or from (
2.23
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\eta=1,

(2.24)

and density
\frac{1}{2\cosh(\pi x/2)}
,
-\infty<x<\infty
.

(iii)

Thirdly, we define
\vartheta
by
\vartheta:=\int_{0}^{1}B_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}B_{2}(x)
,
i.e., “the first half of the stochastic area (
2.22
)”.
This random variable has the characteristic function,
see e.g.
[
24
, (1)]

(or as a consequence of the calculations in Example
8.2
and (
2.31
)
below).

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}=\frac{1}{\cosh^{1/2}(t)},

(2.25)

and hence variance

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\vartheta=\tfrac{1}{2}.

(2.26)

Remark 2.3
.

The characteristic function (
2.23
) of the difference of the two stochastic integrals
in (
2.22
) thus equals the product of their characteristic functions, which both are
(
2.25
); however, the two integrals are
not independent
.
Their joint characteristic function is given by, see
[
24
, (1)]
,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(\mathrm{i}s\int_{0}^{1}B_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}B_{2}(x)+\mathrm{i}t\int_{0}^{1}B_{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}B_{1}(x)\Bigr)\Bigr]\\
=\Bigl(\cosh^{2}\Bigl(\frac{s-t}{2}\Bigr)+\Bigl(\frac{s+t}{s-t}\Bigr)^{2}\sinh^{2}\Bigl(\frac{s-t}{2}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)^{-1/2}.

(2.27)

\triangle

Remark 2.4
.

We will frequently use sums

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1)
where

(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
is a finite or infinite sequence of real numbers with

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{2}<\infty
, and

\zeta_{r}\in N(0,1)
are independent.
Note that this sum converges in
L^{2}
(and a.s.), and is thus well defined
also when

.
Furthermore,
it is easily seen from (

) that
the sum
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1)
has a characteristic function whose square
extends to a meromorphic function in the complex plane with poles
(counted with multiplicity)
at the points
1/(2\mathrm{i}\lambda_{r})
and nowhere else.
Thus the distribution of the sum determines the coefficients
(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}

(up to order).

By the representations in Lemma
2.5
below, the same holds for sums

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\eta_{r}
and
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\vartheta_{r}
.

It follows also that the distribution of the sum is
not
normal,
since its characteristic function is not entire.
This alternatively follows by the Lévy–Cramér theorem
[
4
]
.

\triangle

Obviously, if
\vartheta_{1}
and
\vartheta_{2}
are i.i.d. with the distribution
(
2.25

\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}

–

Lemma 2.5
.

let
\zeta_{k,j}\in N(0,1)
be independent. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\vartheta}:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)\pi}\bigl(\zeta^{2}_{k,1}-1\bigr)\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)\pi}\bigl(\zeta^{2}_{k,1}-\zeta^{2}_{k,2}\bigr)

has the distribution of

in (

), and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)\pi}\bigl(\zeta^{2}_{k,1}+\zeta^{2}_{k,2}-\zeta^{2}_{k,3}-\zeta^{2}_{k,4}\bigr)

has the distribution of the stochastic area

in (

).

Proof.

All sums converge in

, cf. Remark

.
We obtain the second equality (in distribution) in (

) by
combining in the first sum the terms for

and

The difference
\zeta^{2}_{k,1}-\zeta^{2}_{k,2}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\mathrm{i}t(\zeta^{2}_{k,1}-\zeta^{2}_{k,2})}=(1-2\mathrm{i}t)^{-1/2}(1+2\mathrm{i}t)^{-1/2}=(1+4t^{2})^{-1/2},

and consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\mathrm{i}t\widetilde{\vartheta}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1+\frac{4t^{2}}{(2k-1)^{2}\pi^{2}}\Bigr)^{-1/2}=\bigl(\cosh(t)\bigr)^{-1/2},

3.
Background: classical
U
-statistics

As a background, we summarize in the following theorem some
known result on the asymptotic distribution of
U
-statistics of the
standard type (
1.2
)
in the special case of order
m=2
.
The general case with arbitrary (fixed)

m\geqslant 2
is similar with mainly notational complications;
the only essential difference is that degeneracies of higher order may occur
and then the limiting distributions are much more complicated
(although such cases are rarely seen in applications).
We restrict ourselves to
m=2
because this is the case relevant for the
cyclic and alternating
U
-statistics discussed in the present paper,
and also because it may be easier to see the general ideas in this somewhat
simpler case.
(We do not know any reference where all these results are collected and
presented for the case
m=2
.)
For completeness, we give a proof in Appendix
A
.

For further results and for
the general case with arbitrary
m
, we refer to, for example,

[
10
;
11
;
22
;
5
]
for the symmetric case
(
1.1
),
and
[
12
, Chapter 11.1–2]
for the general (asymmetric) case (
1.2
).
For the strong law of large numbers, see Section
9.2
.

In the theorem, note in particular the dichotomy between
the
nondegenerate
case with
\sigma^{2}>0
and then variance of order

n^{3}
and asymptotically normal distribution (see
(ii)
),
and the
degenerate
case in
(iii)
–
(v)
with
\sigma^{2}=0

and then variance of smaller order
n^{2}
and a non-normal limit distribution.

Theorem 3.1
.

With notations and assumptions
as in Section
2
,
the following holds.

(i)

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{n}(f)=\tbinom{n}{2}\mu

(3.1)

and, as
{n\to\infty}
, we have the weak law of large numbers

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}U_{n}(f)\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\mu.

(3.2)

(ii)

As
{n\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle n^{-3}\operatorname{Var}[U_{n}(f)]\to\sigma^{2}:=\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{1}(X)^{2}]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{2}(X)^{2}]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{1}(X)f_{2}(X)]\bigr)

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle n^{-3/2}\left(U_{n}(f)-\tbinom{n}{2}\mu\right)\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma^{2}).

(3.4)

Furthermore,
\sigma^{2}>0
unless
f_{1}(X)=f_{2}(X)=0
a.s.

(iii)

If
f_{1}(X)=f_{2}(X)=0
a.s., and thus
\sigma^{2}=0
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[U_{n}(f)]=\tbinom{n}{2}\operatorname{Var}[f(X_{1},X_{2})]=\tfrac{1}{2}n^{2}\operatorname{Var}[f(X_{1},X_{2})]+O(n).

(3.5)

Moreover, there exists
a finite or infinite sequence of real numbers
(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
such that

\displaystyle n^{-1}\left(U_{n}(f)-\tbinom{n}{2}\mu\right)\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}W:=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{r}(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1),

(3.6)

where
(\zeta_{r})_{1}^{R}
are independent standard normal variables.
The coefficients

(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
are the nonzero eigenvalues (with multiplicities)
of the self-adjoint integral operator
T_{{\widehat{f}}-\mu}

on
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times[0,1],\nu\times\ell)

(where
\ell
is Lebesgue measure)
defined as in (
2.3
) using (
2.6
).
We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}W=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}[f(X_{1},X_{2})]<\infty.

(3.7)

(iv)

In the special case of
(iii)
where
furthermore

f
is symmetric,
the coefficients

(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
in (
3.6
) are the nonzero eigenvalues (with multiplicities)
of the self-adjoint integral operator
T_{f-\mu}

on
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}},\nu)
.

(v)

In the special case of
(iii)
where
furthermore

f
is antisymmetric,
then also

\displaystyle n^{-1}{U_{n}(f)}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}W:=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{+}}\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q}\eta_{q},

(3.8)

where
(\eta_{q})_{1}^{Q_{+}}
are independent random variables with the stochastic area
distribution (
2.23
), and
the coefficients

(\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q})_{1}^{Q_{+}}
are the positive numbers such that the imaginary
number
\mathrm{i}\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q}
is an eigenvalue of the
anti-self-adjoint
operator
T_{f}
on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathcal{X}},\nu)
.
We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}W=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{+}}(\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q})^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}[f(X_{1},X_{2})].

(3.9)

As said above, the proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Remark 3.2
.

As a sanity check, we note that if
f
is symmetric, then the
nonzero eigenvalues of the operators
T_{{\widehat{f}}-\mu}
in
(iii)
and

T_{f-\mu}
in
(iv)
are the same, so the conclusions agree.
In fact, if
f
is symmetric, the (
2.6
) yields

\displaystyle{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)=f(x,y).

(3.10)

Letting
\boldsymbol{1}
denote the function on
[0,1]^{2}
that is constant 1,
we thus have, using the tensor notation in (
2.4
),

{\widehat{f}}=f\otimes\boldsymbol{1}
and consequently

\displaystyle T_{{\widehat{f}}}=T_{f}\otimes T_{\boldsymbol{1}}.

(3.11)

T_{\boldsymbol{1}}
is the integral operator
T_{\boldsymbol{1}}g(t)=\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u
; this is
the projection onto the constant functions and has a single nonzero
eigenvalue 1. Consequently,
T_{{\widehat{f}}}=T_{f}\otimes T_{\boldsymbol{1}}
has the same
nonzero eigenvalues as
T_{f}
.
(In this simple case, this can also easily be seen
directly from (
3.10
); the eigenfunctions of
T_{\widehat{f}}
with nonzero
eigenvalues are the functions of the form
\varphi(x,t)=\varphi_{1}(x)
where
\varphi_{1}

is an eigenfunction of
T_{f}
with the same eigenvalue.)

\triangle

7.
A short summary

Comparing Theorems
3.1
,
4.1
,
5.1
,
6.1
, and
6.3

we see strong similarities but also differences.
The nondegenerate cases are similar, with variances of
order
n^{3}
and normal limits; the proofs show that in these case, the
dominating terms are linear combinations of
f_{1}(X_{i})
and
f_{2}(X_{i})
,
but the details differ because the linear combinations that appear are different
for the different
U
-statistics. For
U^{-+}
and
U^{+-}
this is due to partial
cancellations caused by the alternating signs, and for

this
cancellation is (almost) complete so that the nondegenerate case does not
occur at all.
The asymptotic variances in (
3.3
), (
(ii)

6.3
)
are in general different, but note that in the special case when

is
antisymmetric, and thus
f_{2}=-f_{1}
,
and further

and

(and
U^{-+}_{n}(f)

is of the degenerate type, and has the same asymptotic
distribution as
U^{--}_{n}(f)

For the degenerate cases the general pattern is again similar, but details
differ in more subtle and nonobvious ways.
To see this clearer, we collect in the corollaries below the results for
the degenerate case when
f
is symmetric or antisymmetric; we further
assume for simplicity

,
i.e.,
\mu=0=f_{1}=f_{2}
, since the degenerate cases
always reduce to this case. Note that the variables

are

; hence,
when

,
alternating signs do not change
the variance of the

-statistic and do not

We let
\zeta_{r}\in N(0,1)
,
\eta_{q}
, and
\vartheta_{r}
be independent copies of
the variables in Section
2.3
.

Let the nonzero eigenvalues of

be

Then

\displaystyle n^{-1}{U_{n}(f)}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}W:=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{r}(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1).

(7.1)

The same result holds for
U^{\circ}_{n}
and
U^{--}_{n}
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle n^{-1}{U^{+-}_{n}(f)}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}W:=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\vartheta_{r}.

(7.2)

The same result holds for
U^{-+}_{n}
.

The limit distributions in (

) and (

Suppose that
f

.

with positive imaginary part be
(\mathrm{i}\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q})_{1}^{Q_{+}}

The same result holds for

,

that the fact that two of our

Example 8.6

9.
Further results and open problems

9.1.
Joint convergence

We may also consider joint convergence of the different
U
-statistics

U_{n}(f)
,
U^{\circ}_{n}(f)
,
U^{-+}_{n}(f)
,
U^{+-}_{n}(f)
, and
U^{--}_{n}(f)
for the same
kernel
f
.
In the nondegenerate cases, this is straightforward, since the proofs above
in all cases approximate the
U
-statistic by a linear combination of
f_{1}(X_{i})

and
f_{2}(X_{i})
, and the central limit theorem implies that
these linear combinations converge jointly (after
normalization by
n^{-3/2}
) to some jointly normal limits.

Also in the degenerate cases, or when some
U
-statistics are nondegenerate and
some degenerate (and we thus normalize them differently),
the methods above make it in principle possible to study also
asymptotic joint distributions of
U_{n}
,
U^{-+}
,
U^{+-}
, and
U^{--}
,
see Remark
5.2
for a simple example; furthermore,
it seems possible to include also
U^{\circ}_{n}
by considering

{\widetilde{X}}_{i}:=(X_{2i-1},X_{2i},X_{2i-1+n/2},X_{2i+n/2})

(for
n
divisible by 4).
We leave such extensions to the reader.

Note that in all results above showing convergence in distribution to some
limit,
both in nondegenerate and degenerate cases,
the theorems and proofs also show convergence of first and second moments.
Hence, if
U^{\prime}_{n}
and
U^{\prime\prime}_{n}
denote two of the
U
-statistics in this paper,
and
{\tilde{U}}^{\prime}_{n}
and
{\tilde{U}}^{\prime\prime}_{n}
are the corresponding normalized variables,
then the squares
|{\tilde{U}}^{\prime}_{n}|^{2}
and
|{\tilde{U}}^{\prime\prime}_{n}|^{2}
are uniformly integrable,
see e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 5.5.9]
. It follows, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, that
also the product
{\tilde{U}}^{\prime}_{n}{\tilde{U}}^{\prime\prime}_{n}
is uniformly integrable, and thus
if
{\tilde{U}}_{n}
and
{\tilde{U}}^{\prime\prime}_{n}
have limits in distribution jointly, then the
covariance of their limits is the limit of their covariances.
In particular, when the limits are jointly normal, we can easily find their
joint distribution.

In the case when both
U^{\prime}_{n}
and
U^{\prime\prime}_{n}
have limits of the degenerate type,
and we thus may replace
f
by
f_{12}
(up to negligible terms), it follows
easily from Lemma
2.1
that,
except in the case
(U_{n},U^{\circ}_{n})
,
the two different
U
-statistics have covariance of
order
o(n^{2})
because of cancellations caused by the alternating signs.
Hence, except for
(U_{n},U^{\circ}_{n})
, any joint limits have to be uncorrelated.
(In particular, the two limits cannot be the same random variable, unless
they are 0.) We conjecture that, more strongly, in these cases there is
joint convergence to independent limits, but we leave this as an open problem.
On the other hand,
(U_{n},U^{\circ}_{n})
is different:
if we further assume that
f
is
symmetric, then
U_{n}(f)-U^{\circ}_{n}(f)
is negligible, see Remark
1.1
,
and thus

U_{n}(f)
and
U^{\circ}_{n}(f)
jointly converge, after normalization, to the same
limit.

9.2.
Strong law of large numbers

We stated in (
3.2
) the weak law of large numbers for
classical
U
-statistics. There is also a well-known corresponding
strong law of large numbers,
see

[
11
]
(the symmetric case) and
[
13
]
(the general case):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}U_{n}(f)\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\mu.

(9.1)

This extends to the alternating
U
-statistics in the following, less interesting
form; recall that by (
5.1
) and (
6.2
), the expectations are

O(n)
.

Theorem 9.1
.

We have, as
{n\to\infty}
, for any
f\in L^{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}U^{--}_{n}(f)\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(9.2)

The same holds for
U^{-+}_{n}
and
U^{+-}_{n}
.

Proof.

We treat
U^{+-}_{n}
; the same argument works for
U^{-+}_{n}
and
U^{--}_{n}

with minor modifications (and some simplifications).
We use (
6
) and treat the terms on the right-hand side separately.
As just noted, the expectation
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[U^{+-}_{n}(f)]=O(n)
, so the first term in
(
6
) is
o(n^{2})
. The second term has varianc
O(n)
, and
it follows from Chebyshev’s inequality and the Borel–Cantelli lemma that
it is
o(n^{2})
a.s. For the third term, let

\displaystyle S_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}f_{2}(X_{j}),

(9.3)

and note that by the law of large numbers, applied to even and odd indices
separately,

S_{k}/k\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
, i.e.,
S_{k}=o(k)
a.s.
Then the third term in (
6
) can be written

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(-1)^{j}f_{2}(X_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}(-1)^{j}f_{2}(X_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(S_{n}-S_{i})=o(n^{2})\quad\text{a.s.}

(9.4)

The double sum in (
6
) is
U^{+-}_{n}(f_{12})
.
For even
n
we use (
6
), where
the sum again has variance
O(n)
and thus a.s. is
o(n^{2})
,
and
U_{n}(F)/\binom{n}{2}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
by (
9.1
) since

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[F({\widetilde{X}}_{1},{\widetilde{X}}_{2})]=0
by (
6.19
).
Finally, for odd
n
, the result follows from (
6.18
), where the sum
again has variance
O(n)
.
∎

Note that this argument does not work for
U^{\circ}_{n}
, since the definition
(
4.13
) involves
n
explicitly, and we therefore cannot apply (
9.1
)
to
U_{n}(F)
. Moreover, it is not clear that it is interesting to study
the sequence
(U^{\circ}_{n}(f))_{n=1}^{\infty}
as a stochastic process, since the
point of the definition (
1.3
) of
U^{\circ}_{n}
is that the indices are
regarded as elements of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Nevertheless, out of mathematical
curiosity, we might ask:

Problem 9.2
.

Does (
9.1
) hold for
U^{\circ}_{n}(f)
?

9.3.
Functional limit theorems

As another aspect of regarding
the sequence
(U_{n}(f))_{n=1}^{\infty}
and its variants as stochastic
processes,
we may ask
for functional limit theorems of Donsker-type.
For the classical
U
-statistic
U_{n}(f)
, it is known that,
extending (
3.4
),

n^{-1/2}\bigl(U_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(f)-\frac{n^{2}}{2}t^{2}\mu\bigr)
, regarded as a stochastic
process with continuous parameter
t\geqslant 0
,
converges in
D[0,\infty)
, as
{n\to\infty}
, to a continuous centred Gaussian process;
similarly, in the degenerate case,

n^{-1}\bigl(U_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(f)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[U_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(f)]\bigr)

converges to a continuous process whose
marginals are of the type (
3.6
);
see e.g.
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
9
]
,

[
12
, Remark 11.11]

(the symmetric case);

[
14
, p. 83]
,

[
12
, Remarks 11.11 and 11.25]
,

[
13
, Theorem 3.2]

(the general case).
It seems likely that this too extends to the alternating
U
-statistics by
arguing using (
5.20
) and (
6
), but we have not checked the
details and leave this to the reader.

As in Section
9.2
, and for the same reason,
this argument does not apply to
U^{\circ}_{n}
; moreover,
it seems less interesting to consider functional limit theorems for
U^{\circ}_{n}
.

9.4.
Moment convergence

As noted in Section
9.1
,
in all results above showing convergence in distribution,
we also have convergence of first and second moments.

For higher moments, it is known that for the classical normal limit in
(
3.4
), and any
p\in(2,\infty)
,
all moments and absolute moments of order
\leqslant p
converge provided

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}|f(X_{1},X_{2})|^{p}<\infty
, see
[
13
, Theorem 3.15]
.
It seems likely that this extends to the cyclic and alternating
U
-statistics
considered here, using the methods in the proofs above, but we have not
checked the details and leave this to the reader.

We conjecture that there also is a similar result showing moment convergence
in the degenerate cases, but in this case we are not even aware of a general
result for the classical
U
-statistic and moment convergence in (
3.6
).

3.1
) is immediate from the
definitions (
1.2
) and (
2.2
).

We have, by (

) and (

\displaystyle U_{n}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}f(X_{i},X_{j})

\displaystyle=\tbinom{n}{2}f_{\emptyset}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n-i)f_{1}(X_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j-1)f_{2}(X_{j})+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}f_{12}(X_{i},X_{j})

\displaystyle=:S^{(\emptyset)}_{n}+S^{(1)}_{n}+S^{(2)}_{n}+S^{(12)}_{n}.

(A.1)

Here
S^{(\emptyset)}_{n}=\tbinom{n}{2}\mu=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{n}(f)
. In the sequel, we may replace
f
by
f-\mu
; this does not affect
f_{1}
,

f_{2}
, or
f_{12}
. (Note also that when
f
is antisymmetric,

\mu=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{1},X_{2})=-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}f(X_{2},X_{1})=-\mu

and thus
\mu=0
so
f-\mu=f
is still antisymmetric.)
We may thus without loss of generality assume that
\mu=0
, and
hence
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{n}(f)=0
.

We next study the variances of the sums in (
A
).
The random vectors
\bigl(f_{1}(X_{i}),f_{2}(X_{i})\bigr)
are i.i.d., with mean 0 and finite
second moments. Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[S^{(1)}_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n-i)^{2}\operatorname{Var}[f_{1}(X)]\sim\tfrac{1}{3}n^{3}\operatorname{Var}[f_{1}(X)],

(A.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[S^{(2)}_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j-1)^{2}\operatorname{Var}[f_{2}(X)]\sim\tfrac{1}{3}n^{3}\operatorname{Var}[f_{2}(X)],

(A.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}[S^{(1)}_{n},S^{(2)}_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n-i)(i-1)\operatorname{Cov}[f_{1}(X),f_{2}(X)]

\displaystyle\qquad\sim\tfrac{1}{6}n^{3}\operatorname{Cov}[f_{1}(X),f_{2}(X)].

(A.4)

Consequently, recalling (
3.3
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[S^{(1)}_{n}+S^{(2)}_{n}]

\displaystyle=n^{3}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{3}\operatorname{Var}[f_{1}(X)]+\tfrac{1}{3}\operatorname{Var}[f_{2}(X)]+\tfrac{2}{6}\operatorname{Cov}[f_{1}(X),f_{2}(X)]+o(1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=n^{3}\bigl(\sigma^{2}+o(1)\bigr).

(A.5)

Turning to
S^{(12)}_{n}
, we note that the terms
f_{12}(X_{i},X_{j})
are identically
distributed and have mean 0, and that they
are orthogonal; this follows from (
2.17
), which implies that

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{1}]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{2}]=0
, and thus,
for example,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})f_{12}(X_{1},X_{3})]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})\mid X_{1}]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{12}(X_{1},X_{3})\mid X_{1}]\bigr]

\displaystyle=0.

(A.6)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[S^{(12)}_{n}]=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\operatorname{Var}[f_{12}(X_{i},X_{j})]=\tbinom{n}{2}\operatorname{Var}[f_{12}(X_{1},X_{2})].

(A.7)

The variance of
S^{(12)}_{n}
is thus
O(n^{2})
, while
S^{(1)}_{n}+S^{(2)}_{n}
typically has
a larger variance of order
n^{3}
. Hence, the sum (
A
) is
dominated by
S^{(1)}_{n}+S^{(2)}_{n}
, except in the case that
\sigma^{2}=0
when these terms
vanish (as we will see below), and therefore (
A
) reduces to
S^{(12)}_{n}
.
This is the reason for the two different cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
in
Theorem
3.1
; the generic case
(ii)
,
i.e., assuming
\sigma^{2}>0
, the nondegenerate case,
and the degenerate case
(iii)
with
\sigma^{2}=0
.
We treat these cases separately below, after completing the proof of
(i)
.

(i)
:
We have already shown (
3.1
). Furthermore, (
A
) and
(
A.7
) imply

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[U_{n}(f)]=O(n^{3}),

(A.8)

and thus (
3.2
) follows. (Actually, with convergence in

.)

(ii)
:
We apply the standard central limit theorem for triangular arrays
(see for example
[
8
, Theorem 7.2.4]
or
[
15
, Theorem 5.12]
)
to
S^{(1)}_{n}+S^{(2)}_{n}
.
It is easily verified that the triangular arrays

\bigl(n^{-3/2}(n-i)f_{1}(X_{i})\bigr)_{i\leqslant n}
and

\bigl(n^{-3/2}(i-1)f_{2}(X_{i})\bigr)_{i\leqslant n}

satisfy the Lindeberg condition, and thus so does the summed array

\bigl(n^{-3/2}((n-i)f_{1}(X_{i})+(i-1)f_{2}(X_{i}))\bigr)_{i\leqslant n}
.
Consequently, the central limit theorem yields, using (
A
),

\displaystyle n^{-3/2}(S^{(1)}_{n}+S^{(2)}_{n})\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma^{2}).

(A.9)

(If
\sigma^{2}=0
then (
A.9
) still holds, as a trivial consequence of
(
A
).)
Furthermore, as noted above, (
A.7
) implies that

\operatorname{Var}[n^{-3/2}S^{(12)}_{n}]=n^{-3}\operatorname{Var}[S^{(12)}_{n}]\to 0
, and thus

n^{-3/2}S^{(12)}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}0
. Hence, (
3.4
) follows from (
A.9
) and
(
A
)
by the Cramér–Slutsky theorem
[
8
, Theorem 5.11.4]
.

Finally, (
3.3
) can be written

\displaystyle 3\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[(f_{1}(X)+\tfrac{1}{2}f_{2}(X))^{2}]+\tfrac{3}{4}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f_{2}(X)^{2}],

(A.10)

which implies that
\sigma^{2}=0
if and only if
f_{1}(X)=f_{2}(X)=0
a.s.,
which completes the proof for the nondegenerate case
(ii)
.

We turn to the degenerate case
\sigma^{2}=0
in
(iii)
–
(v)
.
In this case we thus have
f_{1}(X_{i})=f_{2}(X_{i})=0
a.s., and consequently

S^{(1)}_{n}=S^{(2)}_{n}=0
and, by (
A
) again,

\displaystyle U_{n}(f)=S^{(12)}_{n}.

(A.11)

We first treat the symmetric case
(iv)
.

(iv)
:
We apply Lemma
A.1
below,
noting that (
A.29
) holds by (
2.12
) and the assumption that

f_{1}(X)=0
a.s. (and our simplifying assumption
f_{\emptyset}=\mu=0
in this proof).
This shows that
f(x,y)=f_{12}(x,y)
has an orthogonal expansion
(
A.27
),
for some
R\leqslant\infty
and some orthonormal sequence of functions

\varphi_{r}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{X}})
, which furthermore satisfy
(
A.30
), which is equivalent to
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi_{r}(X)=0
.
The orthonormality means that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[\varphi_{r}(X)\varphi_{q}(X)]=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\varphi_{r}(x)\varphi_{q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\delta_{rq}.

(A.12)

In other words,
(\varphi_{r}(X))_{1}^{R}
is a sequence of uncorrelated random
variables with mean 0 and variance 1.

Suppose first that
R<\infty
, so that the sum (
A.27
) is finite. Then
(
1.2
), the symmetry of

, and (
A.27
) yield

\displaystyle 2U_{n}(f)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}f(X_{i},X_{j})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f(X_{i},X_{j})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i},X_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})\varphi_{r}(X_{j})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})\Bigr)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})^{2}\biggr).

(A.13)

Now let
{n\to\infty}
.
By the law of large numbers, for each
r
,

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})^{2}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[\varphi_{r}(X)^{2}]=1.

(A.14)

Furthermore, by the central limit theorem, since
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[\varphi_{r}(X)]=0
and

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[\varphi_{r}(X)^{2}]=1
as remarked above,

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}\zeta_{r}\in N(0,1).

(A.15)

Moreover, since the variables
\varphi_{r}(X)
are uncorrelated, the limit in
(
A.15
) holds jointly for all
r\leqslant R
, with the limits
\zeta_{r}

uncorrelated and thus independent. Combining (
A
)–(
A.15
) yields

\displaystyle 2n^{-1}U_{n}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\biggl(\Bigl(n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})\Bigr)^{2}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r}(X_{i})^{2}\biggr)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\bigl(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1\bigr).

(A.16)

This proves (
3.6
) when
R<\infty
.

If
R=\infty
, let, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle f_{N}(x,y):=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}(x)\varphi_{r}(y).

(A.17)

Then the case just proven applies to
f_{N}
, and thus, for each fixed
N<\infty
,
as
{n\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle 2n^{-1}U_{n}(f_{N})\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\lambda_{r}\bigl(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1\bigr).

(A.18)

As
{N\to\infty}
, the right-hand side converges to

\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\bigl(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1\bigr)

in
L^{2}
and a.s., and in particular in distribution.
Furthermore, by (
A.7
) applied to
U_{n}(f-f_{N})
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[(U_{n}(f)-U_{n}(f_{N}))^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\bigl[U_{n}(f-f_{N})\bigr]=\tbinom{n}{2}\operatorname{Var}\bigl((f-f_{N})(X_{1},X_{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tbinom{n}{2}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{N+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}(x)\varphi_{r}(y)\Bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\tbinom{n}{2}\sum_{N+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}^{2}.

(A.19)

Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[(n^{-1}U_{n}(f)-n^{-1}U_{n}(f_{N}))^{2}\bigr]\leqslant\sum_{N+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{r}^{2}\to 0

(A.20)

as
{N\to\infty}
, uniformly in
n
.
The result (
3.6
) now follows from (
A.18
) and (
A.20
),
see e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Finally,
by (

) and (
2.20
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}W=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{r}\bigr)^{2}\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{2}

(A.21)

and thus
(
3.7
) holds by (
A.28
) in Lemma
A.1
.

(iii)
:
Now consider the general degenerate case, where
f_{1}=f_{2}=0
, and further
as above without loss of generality

, but no symmetry

We use the following trick to reduce to the symmetric case.
(See
[

Let
(Z_{i})_{1}^{\infty}
be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables,

, with each

uniformly distributed on

.
Consider the random variables
\widehat{X}_{i}:=(X_{i},Z_{i})
in

and define the function

by (

).
Note that this definition makes

, and assume as we

Consequently, conditioned on

and hence (

) holds also unconditionally.

applied to

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\int_{X^{2}}{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}u=\mu=0,

\displaystyle{\widehat{f}}_{1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\widehat{{\mathcal{X}}}}{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\,\mathrm{d}u=\begin{cases}f_{1}(x),&t<u\\
f_{2}(x),&t>u\end{cases}\biggr\}=0

and, by symmetry,

.
Furthermore,

) for

,

.

Lemma A.1
.

If
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
is real and symmetric, then

T_{f}
defined by (
2.3
), i.e.,

\displaystyle T_{f}g(x):=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y),

(A.26)

is a self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operator
on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathcal{X}})
.
Hence
T_{f}
is compact and has thus at most countably many nonzero
eigenvalues, each of them real and each having a finite-dimensional eigenspace.
Let
(\lambda_{r})_{r=1}^{R}
(where
0\leqslant R\leqslant\infty
) be an
enumeration of the nonzero eigenvalues (with multiplicities) of
T_{f}
.
It is then possible to find
a corresponding orthonormal sequence of real-valued eigenfunctions

(\varphi_{r})_{1}^{R}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{X}})

such that
T_{f}\varphi_{r}=\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}
for every
r
.

For any such
(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
and
(\varphi_{r})_{1}^{R}
,

f(x,y)
has a (finite or infinite) orthogonal expansion

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}(x)\varphi_{r}(y)

(A.27)

which converges in
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})
because

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{2}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}f(x,y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)<\infty.

(A.28)

Moreover, if

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=0,\qquad\text{for $\nu$-a.e. }y\in{\mathcal{X}},

(A.29)

then

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\varphi_{r}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=0,\qquad\text{for every }r\leqslant R.

(A.30)

Here and below,
r\leqslant R
should be interpreted as
r<\infty
when
R=\infty
.

Proof.

Since
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})
, (
A.26
) defines a bounded and compact linear
operator
T_{f}
on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathcal{X}})
; furthermore,
T_{f}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt
operator.
Since furthermore
f
is real and symmetric,
T_{f}
is a
self-adjoint operator.
By the spectral theorem for compact and self-adjoint linear operators on a
Hilbert space
(see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 28.3]
or
[
23
, Theorem 6.4-B]
),

T_{f}
has a finite or countably infinite set of
nonzero eigenvalues, each with finite multiplicity, so we may
arrange the nonzero
eigenvalues, with multiplicities, in a sequence
(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
with
R\leqslant\infty
;
we denote the index set also by
{\mathcal{R}}:=\{r\in\mathbb{N}:r\leqslant R\}
.
Moreover, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{r}
are real, and
there exists a corresponding orthonormal sequence of
eigenfunctions
(\varphi_{r})_{1}^{R}
, and this may be extended to an orthonormal
basis
(\varphi_{r})_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}\cup\mathcal{N}}
where

\mathcal{N}
is a disjoint (possibly empty) index set
such that
T_{f}(\varphi_{r})=0
for every
r\in\mathcal{N}
, i.e., each
\varphi_{r}
is an
eigenfunction also for
r\in\mathcal{N}
, with eigenvalue
\lambda_{r}:=0
when
r\in\mathcal{N}
.

Furthermore, since
f
is real,
we may choose all
\varphi_{r}
to be in
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{X}})
.
(By
[
23
, Theorem 6.4-B]
applied to
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{X}})
,
or by noting that
since also the eigenvalues are real,
the real and imaginary parts of any eigenfunction of
T_{f}
are also
eigenfunctions for the same eigenvalue; hence each eigenspace is spanned
by the real functions in it.)

Fix any such sequence
(\varphi_{r})_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}}
and extension
(\varphi_{r})_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}\cup\mathcal{N}}
.
Since
(\varphi_{r})_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}\cup\mathcal{N}}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}})
,
it is easily seen (and well-known) that
if we define
g\otimes h(x,y):=g(x)h(y)
for functions
g,h\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}})
, then
the set

\{\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{s}:r,s\in{\mathcal{R}}\cup\mathcal{N}\}
is an orthonormal basis in

L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
. Hence,

\displaystyle f=\sum_{r,s\in{\mathcal{R}}\cup\mathcal{N}}\langle f,\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{s}\rangle\,{\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{s}},

(A.31)

where the sum converges in
L^{2}
.
By Fubini’s theorem and (
A.26
),

\displaystyle\langle f,\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\varphi_{r}(x)\varphi_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\varphi_{r}(x)T_{f}(\varphi_{s})(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\varphi_{r}(x)\lambda_{s}\varphi_{s}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\lambda_{s}\langle\varphi_{r},\varphi_{s}\rangle=\lambda_{s}\delta_{rs}.

(A.32)

This vanishes unless
r=s\in{\mathcal{R}}
, and thus (
A.31
) simplifies to,
using (
A
) again,

\displaystyle f=\sum_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}}\langle f,\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{r}\rangle\,{\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{r}}=\sum_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}}\lambda_{r}\,\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{r}.

(A.33)

This is (
A.27
), and (
A.28
) follows because

\{\varphi_{r}\otimes\varphi_{s}\}
is an orthonormal basis.

Finally, if (
A.29
) holds, then for every
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
,
by (
A.26
) and Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\varphi_{r}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}T_{f}(\varphi_{r})(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\varphi_{r}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\varphi_{y}(y)\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)=0.

(A.34)

Since
\lambda_{r}\neq 0
for
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
, (
A.30
) follows.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
be real and antisymmetric.
The the operator
T_{f}
on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathcal{X}})

is anti-self-adjoint and has purely imaginary eigenvalues.
Let
(\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q})_{q\in{\mathcal{Q}_{+}}}
be an enumeration of the positive real numbers
such that

\mathrm{i}\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q}
is an eigenvalue of
T_{f}
(counted with multiplicities).
Then the multiset of nonzero eigenvalues
(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}

of the self-adjoint operator
T_{{\widehat{f}}}
on

L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times[0,1])
(counted with multiplicities)
equals

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\pm\frac{2}{(2k-1)\pi}\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q}:q\in{\mathcal{Q}_{+}},k\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr\}\quad\text{with each pair $(q,k)$ counted twice.}

(A.35)

As a consequence, if
\zeta_{r}
are i.i.d. standard normal variables and

\eta_{q}
are independent random variables with the stochastic area
distribution (
2.23
), then

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{r}(\zeta_{r}^{2}-1)\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\sum_{q\in{\mathcal{Q}_{+}}}\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q}\eta_{q}.

(A.36)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{2}=2\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{+}}(\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q})^{2}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}f(x,y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)<\infty.

(A.37)

Proof.

In the antisymmetric case,
we can write (
2.6
) as

\displaystyle{\widehat{f}}\bigl((x,t),(y,u)\bigr)=f(x,y)\operatorname{sgn}(u-t)=:f(x,y)h(t,u),

(A.38)

where
\operatorname{sgn}

\displaystyle\{-\lambda^{\mathsf{a}}_{q}\rho_{s}:q\in\mathcal{Q},s\in{\mathcal{S}}\}.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
h(t,u):=\operatorname{sgn}(t-u)
. Then the anti-self-adjoint operator

T_{h}
acting on

L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}[0,1]
has
eigenvalues, all simple,

\displaystyle{\pm\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{(2k-1)\pi},\qquad k=1,2,3,\dots}.

(A.44)

Proof.

Suppose that
\varphi
is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\lambda
.
Then, for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\lambda\varphi(t)=T_{h}\varphi(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{sgn}(u-t)\varphi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{t}^{1}\varphi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

(A.45)

Suppose first that
\lambda\neq 0
.
The right-hand side of (
A.45
) is a continuous function of
t\in[0,1]
, and thus
\varphi

can be assumed to be continuous.
Then (
A.45
) holds for every
t\in[0,1]
, and
the right-hand side of (
A.45
) is continuously differentiable in
(0,1)
;
thus
\varphi
is continuously differentiable on
[0,1]
.
Taking the derivative in (
A.45
) yields

\displaystyle\lambda\varphi^{\prime}(t)=-2\varphi(t)

(A.46)

and thus (for some irrelevant
C\neq 0
)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=Ce^{i\omega t}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\omega=2\mathrm{i}/\lambda.

(A.47)

Taking
t=0
and
1
in (
A.45
) yields

\displaystyle\lambda\varphi(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=-\lambda\varphi(1),

(A.48)

i.e.,
\varphi(1)=-\varphi(0)
. This and (
A.47
) yield

e^{\mathrm{i}\omega}=-1
, and thus

\displaystyle\omega=\pm(2k-1)\pi,\qquad k=1,2,\dots.

(A.49)

Conversely, it is easily checked that each such
\omega
gives an eigenfunction

\varphi
by (
A.47
), satisfying (
A.45
) with eigenvalue

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\omega}=\pm\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{(2k-1)\pi}.

(A.50)

These are thus the nonzero eigenvalues, and we see from (
A.47
) that
they are simple.

Finally, if (
A.45
) holds with
\lambda=0
, then
\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u
is
constant, and thus
\varphi(t)=0
a.e. Hence,
0
is not an eigenvalue.
(Equivalently, the eigenfunctions
e^{\pm(2k-1)\pi\mathrm{i}}
form an orthonormal
basis on
[0,1]
, as is well-known from Fourier analysis.)
∎

Appendix B
Cumulants

The limit distributions in our theorems are, apart form the normal
distribution, given by (possibly infinite) linear combinations of
independent copies of the variables
\zeta^{2}-1
,
\eta
, and
\vartheta
in
Section
2.3
. We give here some simple results on the cumulants of such sums;
we denote the cumulants of a random variable
Y
by
\varkappa_{m}(Y)
, where

m\geqslant 1
.
We write
for convenience
\chi:=\zeta^{2}-1
.

The cumulants
\varkappa_{m}(\chi)
,
\varkappa_{m}(\eta)
, and
\varkappa_{m}(\vartheta)

are (by definition) obtained by
Taylor expansions of the logarithms of the
characteristic functions of
\chi
,
\eta
, and
\vartheta
given in (
2.21
)
(
2.23
), and (
2.25
);
note that these characteristic functions and their logarithms are analytic functions of
t
in a
neighbourhood of
0
. This yields
for
m\geqslant 2
the cumulants, using
[
20
, 4.19.8, 4.28.9, and 24.2.2]

for (
B.2
)–(
B.3
),

\displaystyle\varkappa_{m}(\chi)

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}(m-1)!,

(B.1)

\displaystyle\varkappa_{m}(\eta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+m/2}\frac{2^{m}(2^{m}-1)}{m}B_{m}=\frac{2^{m}(2^{m}-1)}{m}|B_{m}|,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\varkappa_{m}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\varkappa_{m}(\eta)=(-1)^{1+m/2}\frac{2^{m-1}(2^{m}-1)}{m}B_{m}=\frac{2^{m-1}(2^{m}-1)}{m}|B_{m}|,

(B.3)

where
B_{m}
denotes the Bernoulli numbers
[
20
, Chapter 24]
.
For
m=1
, we have
\varkappa(\chi)_{1}=\varkappa(\eta)_{1}=\varkappa(\vartheta)_{1}=0
. (These are
just the means.) Note that thus
\varkappa_{m}(\eta)=\varkappa_{m}(\vartheta)=0
for all odd
m
,
which reflects the fact that
\eta
and
\vartheta
have symmetric
distributions.

Sums of the type

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\chi_{r}
,
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\eta_{r}
, and
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\vartheta_{r}

(where
\chi_{r}
are independent copies of
\chi
, and so on)
appear frequently above; they
have cumulants that can be expressed in terms of the sums

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{m}
,
since the cumulant of a sum of independent variables equals the sum of their
cumulants.
Hence, for any finite or infinite sequence
(\lambda_{r})_{1}^{R}
with

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{2}<\infty
,

\displaystyle\varkappa_{m}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\chi_{r}\Bigr)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}^{m}\cdot\varkappa_{m}(\chi),

(B.4)

with
\varkappa_{m}(\chi)
given by (
B.1
), and similarly for

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\eta_{r}
and
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\vartheta_{r}
.

For example, since
\eta\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}
, we have

\varkappa_{m}(\eta)=2\varkappa_{m}(\vartheta)
for all
m\geqslant 1
, as is seen in
(
B.2
)–(
B.3
).
Similarly, since (
2.28
) can be written

\displaystyle\vartheta\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)\pi}\chi_{k},

(B.5)

we have, for all even
m\geqslant 2
,
using the standard formula
[
20
, 25.6.2]
for

\zeta(m)
,

\displaystyle\varkappa_{m}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{((2k-1)\pi)^{m}}\varkappa_{m}(\chi)=\pi^{-m}2\bigl(1-2^{-m}\bigr)\zeta(m)\varkappa_{m}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{m}\bigl(1-2^{-m}\bigr)}{m!}|B_{m}|\varkappa_{m}(\chi)=\frac{2^{m}\bigl(1-2^{-m}\bigr)}{m!}|B_{m}|2^{m-1}(m-1)!,

(B.6)

which agrees with (and thus gives a proof of) (
B.3
) and (
B.2
).
(Recall that the odd cumulants of
\zeta
and
\eta
are 0.)

For a final example, the limit distribution
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\pi k}\eta_{k}

in (
8.31
) has cumulants, using (
B.2
) and, again,
[
20
, 25.6.2]
,

\displaystyle\varkappa_{m}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\pi k}\eta_{k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\pi k)^{m}}\varkappa_{m}(\eta)=\pi^{-m}\zeta(m)\varkappa_{m}(\eta)=\frac{2^{2m}(2^{m}-1)}{2m\cdot m!}B_{m}^{2},

(B.7)

which agrees with the cumulants given (implicitly) in

[
6
, Corollary 2]
. (Note that the variable
W
there is twice as
big, because of different normalizations.)
