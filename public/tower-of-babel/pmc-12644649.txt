Sideband injection locking of microwave frequency combs in a superconducting optomechanical circuit
Authors: Jinhoon Jeong, Younghun Ryu, Hyojae Jung, Jinwoong Cha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Superconducting optomechanical circuits enable frequency mixing of optical and mechanical modes, facilitating the generation of microwave frequency combs. However, such optomechanical combs suffer from frequency fluctuations, requiring their stabilization for applications in precision sensing and signal processing. Here, we investigate the sideband injection locking of microwave frequency combs in a niobium-based superconducting optomechanical circuit. By strongly driving the device with a blue-detuned pump to induce parametric instability and introducing an additional tone near individual comb peaks, we study how the locking range varies with the power, the frequency position, and the sweep direction of the injection tone. The locking responses show interesting features such as injection hysteresis, which cannot be explained by existing models. Numerical simulations of the classical optomechanical equations implementing a cubic mechanical nonlinearity show that the nonlinearity contributes to broadening the locking range. We also characterize the Allan deviations and phase noise of the injection-locked combs for different injection frequencies, demonstrating enhanced stability performance. Our results lay the foundation for the utilization of optomechanical combs for applications in nanomechanical sensing and cryogenic microwave pulse generation.

Introduction

Frequency combs feature equally spaced spectral lines generated through wave-mixing processes in nonlinear systems. Understanding and controlling their formation and evolution across various physical platforms is crucial for applications such as optical clocks, metrology, spectroscopy1–5, and, most recently, quantum information processing6–13.

Over the past decades, frequency combs in the optical frequency domain, utilizing Kerr nonlinearities or electro-optic effects in various nanophotonic devices, have been of central interest3. More recently, superconducting microwave circuits implementing nonlinear elements such as Josephson junctions11,14–16 have emerged as a promising platform for exploring frequency comb generation in the microwave domain, demonstrating their potential for on-chip microwave sources14–16 and continuous quantum information processing11,16.

Cavity optomechanical systems, which couple an optical cavity to a mechanical resonator, also manifest optical nonlinearities through their parametric interactions, enabling the generation of frequency combs under strong optical pumping above certain thresholds17,18. A key feature of optomechanical frequency combs is their frequency spacing, or repetition rate, which directly reveals the resonant frequency of the mechanical resonator. Various platforms, including microwave optomechanical circuits19–21, optomechanical crystals22,23, and microtoroids24 have been utilized to demonstrate optomechanical frequency combs. Among these, microwave optomechanical circuits serve as a versatile hardware platform for studying cavity optomechanics by leveraging the reliability of microwave instrumentation, which enables precise control and measurement of microwave signals without requiring sophisticated optical alignment and stabilization. In addition, their on-chip architecture allows for engineering of device configurations and interaction paths across multiple degrees of freedom in various ways, offering promising opportunities for both fundamentals and applications.

However, frequency combs are not always ideal, presenting fluctuations, requiring stabilization to achieve their best performance. Injection locking techniques offer an effective solution for comb stabilization. To realize this, a coherent tone is injected into a self-oscillating system, leading to phase synchronization between the injection tone and the signal from the system. This locking effect enables the enhancement of phase stability of signals in various electrical25 and optical26 oscillators. Recently, it has been shown that the repetition rate of a dissipative Kerr comb in a microring resonator can be locked by injecting an additional coherent tone near the sideband signals in the comb spectrum27.

Optomechanical systems are inherently nonlinear due to the intensity dependence of an optical cavity frequency, which is attributed to the motion-induced modulation. This resembles the Kerr nonlinearity in nonlinear photonic systems28, facilitating similar locking effects in optomechanical systems. Recent studies have demonstrated injection locking in optomechanical systems where the injection tone directly locks a mechanical mode29–33, not the full comb spectrum. In this work, we study sideband injection locking of microwave frequency combs in a niobium-based superconducting optomechanical circuit. Our device exhibits anomalous locking behavior, such as hysteresis in locking range, which depends on various injection parameters, such as injection and pump powers, and the frequency positions of an injection tone.

Results

Our device consists of a superconducting cavity composed of a spiral inductor and a free-standing, nanomechanical capacitor with ~120-nm vacuum-gap (Fig. 1a). The two elements form a capacitive coupling, realizing dispersive optomechanical coupling20,34. The sample is mounted on the mixing chamber of a dilution refrigerator, which is maintained at T = 3.9 K. The high critical temperature of niobium (~9 K) enables high-quality microwave resonances even at this temperature. The resonant frequency, fc = ωc/2π ≈ 3.78 GHz, and the total decay rate, κ/2π ≈ 0.97 MHz, of the microwave resonator are obtained from a two-port transmission measurement using a vector network analyzer. The internal and external decay rates are κi/2π ≈ 0.35 MHz and κe/2π ≈ 0.62 MHz, respectively. A weak, red-detuned pump is applied to characterize the resonant frequency, Ωm/2π ≈ 8.04 MHz, and intrinsic damping rate, Γm/2π ≈ 1.34 kHz, of the mechanical resonator. These parameters set the operation of our device into the resolved-sideband regime (κ < Ωm). The single photon optomechanical coupling strength, g0/2π is approximately 2.9 Hz20. To generate microwave frequency combs in our device, we introduce a blue-detuned microwave pump at fd ≈ fc + Ωm/2π, where fd denotes the pump frequency. If the power of the pump signal is larger than a threshold power, the self-oscillation of the mechanical resonator occurs (Fig. 1b). In our experiment, we select fd that maximizes the output power of a comb (Supplementary Information). Assuming a coherent mechanical motion and letting the displacement of the mechanical resonator x(t) = Xmcos(Ωmt), the optomechanical equation for a cavity field a(t) in the frame rotating at 2πfd, da(t)/dt = [iΔ–κ/2]a(t) + i(g0/xzpf)x(t)a(t) + (κe/2)–1/2sin, leads to a(t) = (κe/2)–1/2sinexp[–iβmcos(Ωmt)]Σninαn(βm)exp[inΩmt] with αn(βm) = Jn(βm)/[κ/2+i(nΩm − Δ)] and βm = Xmg0/(xzpf Ωm)35. The solution contains multiple spectral components with an equal spectral distance of Ωm, revealing the existence of a frequency comb. Here, Xm denotes the self-oscillation mechanical amplitude, Δ/2π = fd − fc is the cavity detuning, xzpf is the zero-point fluctuation of the mechanical resonator, sin is the input power, and Jn is the Bessel function of the first kind, nth order.

The threshold power in our experiment is Pd = 22.8 dBm, where Pd denotes the setting value of the radio-frequency (RF) signal generator used in this experiment (Fig. 1b). Above this threshold, the total power emitted from the device suddenly increases, creating multiple, equidistant spectral peaks. Note that the threshold power level is still insufficient to induce notable cavity nonlinearities, and no significant change in the comb’s noise floor due to cavity heating is observed (Supplementary Information). The spectral position of a comb peak in the frequency domain is described by the comb equation, fp = f0 + pfr, where fp is the corresponding peak frequency, f0 is the comb's peak frequency nearest to fc, p is the peak index counted from the comb peak near the cavity resonance, and fr is the repetition rate, which equals to the spacing between neighboring comb lines. When the device is free-running, fr is equal to Ωm/2π.

Injection locking of the frequency comb is realized by introducing an additional microwave signal whose frequency is close to that of one of the comb’s peaks (Fig. 1a), except for near fd. To demonstrate the injection locking effects, we apply an injection tone near f0 and monitor the evolution of a selected comb peak at f0 through an RF spectrum analyzer. We measure the power spectrum near f0 over approximately 6 min with and without the injection tone. The power of the injection tone from the RF signal generator is set to be Pinj = 13 dBm. For the free-running case, the intensity of the comb peak decreases from −20 dBm (single trace) to −40 dBm (200 averages), exhibiting spectral broadening. On the other hand, the comb peak with the injection tone maintains its spectral shape, exhibiting improved frequency stability (Fig. 1c).

To examine the effects of the injection parameters, we characterize the spectral responses of the 0th comb peak while sweeping the injection frequency, finj = fp + δ, both in the forward and backward directions. Here, the range of the frequency sweep, δ, is 20 kHz. We particularly focus on p = 0 (Fig. 2a, b) and p = −1 (Fig. 2c, d). When finj is far from fp, we observe the formation of symmetric sidebands (fside,p,q = fp ± qδ, where q = ±1, ±2, ±3, …) with respect to fp, which arise from the frequency mixing of the injection tone and the comb peaks25,33. As finj is approaching fp, the injection pulling effect occurs, dragging a comb’s peak to the injection frequency. When finj becomes close enough to one of the comb’s peaks, this peak is locked to finj25. It is worth noting that the comb peaks outside the locking ranges exhibit long-term frequency fluctuations. This locking simultaneously affects the other comb peaks, satisfying the comb equation, modifying the repetition rate to a new repetition rate, fr’ = fr + δ/(p − 1) (Supplementary Information). We also observe hysteresis in locking ranges for different sweep directions (forward and backward) of finj. This hysteresis shows the bistable nature of two-tone drives and cannot be explained by either a simple Adler-like theory for optomechanical synchronization30 or DKS comb theory27. When finj ~ f0, sub-comb features are observed in the 0th peak for certain injection frequencies within the locking range for both forward and backward sweep cases. However, these sub-comb features are not observable for finj ~ f-1. There is a difference in the frequency changes of the 0th peak for injection tones locked to the 0th peak and the –1st peak. To further investigate the injection locking effects, we characterize the dependence of the locking range on Pd and Pinj for the injection tones locked to p = 0 (Fig. 2e, f) and p = −1 (Fig. 2g, h) peaks. These data indeed show the Arnold tongues at each comb peak with respect to Pinj. For finj ~ f0 at Pd = 23 dBm (Fig. 2e), the locking range increases with Pinj for both forward and backward sweep cases when Pinj < 7 dBm. While the backward sweep case shows a consistent increase in the locking ranges, those for the forward sweep case decrease above Pinj = 7 dBm. On the other hand, for finj ~ f–1 at Pd = 23 dBm (Fig. 2g), the locking range increases with the injection strength. The locking ranges for both forward and backward sweeps are similar when the injection strengths are low (Pinj < 7 dBm), but they start to show deviations as the injection strength increases. The locking ranges for the forward sweep case are consistently smaller than those for the backward case. This anomalous locking behavior, along with the dependence on locking frequency positions, differs significantly from previously reported optomechanical synchronization, which exhibits a monotonous increase in the locking range with injection strength30.

The pump power dependence of the locking ranges also shows distinct behaviors for the two different locking positions, p = 0 and p = −1. For finj ~ f0 at Pinj = 13 dBm (Fig. 2f), the locking ranges from backward injection sweep measurements show a monotonous decrease with Pd. However, the locking ranges from the forward injection sweep measurements exhibit an increase and reach a peak value at Pd ~ 23.8 dBm and show a decrease above this value. On the other hand, when finj ~ f–1 at Pinj = 13 dBm (Fig. 2h), the locking ranges for both forward and backward sweep measurements increase with Pd. The backward locking ranges are always larger than the forward locking ranges.

A notable feature from our injection locking experiments is the presence of hysteresis in locking ranges. To study this, we perform numerical simulations by integrating equations of motion for a classical optomechanical system implementing a cubic, stiffening nonlinearity in the mechanical equation of motion (Supplementary Information). The presence of this nonlinearity in our device has been reported in the previous study on superconducting optomechanical combs20, which leads to an increase in comb repetition rate with the blue-detuned pump power. We also verify the presence of the stiffening nonlinearity in our device by performing two-tone driving measurements. The forced mechanical responses show the fold-over effect in the resonance spectra as the two-tone driving power increases (Supplementary Information).

The results from the numerical modeling with the mechanical nonlinearity show a good match with experimental data (Fig. 2e, f). To examine whether this nonlinearity affects the locking performance, we also perform numerical simulations without considering the mechanical nonlinearity. The locking ranges estimated from this model are much smaller compared to both experimental results and simulation results with the mechanical nonlinearity (Fig. 2e). This supports that mechanical nonlinearities can be utilized to leverage injection locking performance.

We further investigate the variation in locking ranges for different finj (Fig. 3a). Here, we focus on selected values of finj around each comb’s peak. finj ~ f1 is not studied as f1 ~ fd. Pd and Pinj are 23 dBm and 13 dBm, respectively. The peak dependence of locking ranges has shoulder-like features. For finj ~ f–2, f–1, and f2, the locking range increases as finj is far away from f0. We then observe decreases in the locking ranges when the locking frequencies are finj < f–2 and finj > f2. We also note that red-detuned injection tones lead to larger locking ranges. This trend closely resembles the locking behavior shown in DKS combs27. The non-monotonic locking ranges for different injection positions might be attributed to higher harmonic optical forces exerted on the mechanical resonator33. The blue-detuned pump at fd = fc + Ωm/2π and an injection tone at fp generate a beating tone whose frequency is close to an integer multiple of Ωm/2π, which can directly drive the mechanical resonator via subharmonic excitations. With increasing the frequency separation between the injection tone and the pump tone, the locking becomes less efficient due to the non-resonant nature of the optical force and the reduced optical driving strength. At the same time, a larger injection-pump frequency difference results in a broadened locking range required to achieve the same amount of repetition rate tuning. These two effects compromise, causing the shoulders in the locking range distribution.

Lastly, we characterize the stability of injection-locked optomechanical combs for different finj by measuring their corresponding Allan deviations (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Information). To measure the Allan deviations, we down-convert the comb signals with the pump tone using an RF frequency mixer and send this tone to a frequency counter. A free-running comb obviously exhibits poor long-term stability as revealed in its Allan deviation. If the frequency difference of the pump tone and the injection tone is (p–1)fr’, the fluctuations of fr’ are suppressed by a factor of 1/|p – 1| . As expected, the Allan deviation for finj ~ f-3 shows the improved stability realized by the injection tone, demonstrating a 3-order enhancement at 10-sec averaging time. The Allan deviations of combs for finj ~ f0, and f2 follow those of the injection tone. For larger injection detuning from the pump tone, such as finj ~ f-1, and f3, their Allan deviations exhibit further suppression. In addition to the Allan deviation measurements, we characterize the phase noise of the optomechanical comb for different injection positions using a phase noise analyzer (Fig. 4b). As expected, the phase noise follows that of the injection tone. The locked comb exhibits significantly reduced phase noise below 100 Hz. The phase noise of the free-running comb and an injection-locked comb differs by 30 dBc/Hz at 10 Hz.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrate the sideband injection locking of microwave frequency combs generated in a niobium-based superconducting optomechanical circuit. We study how key properties, including locking ranges, repetition rates, and stability, depend on various injection parameters such as the spectral position and the power of an injection tone, as well as the pump power. The numerical simulations demonstrate the role of stiffening mechanical nonlinearity, which can serve as a potential resource for engineering locking of optomechanical combs. Our device shows enhanced long-term stability of the comb repetition rate as revealed in Allan deviation, exhibiting the three-orders-of-magnitude improvement for averaging times exceeding 10 s. The enhancement of comb stability is supplemented by additional phase noise measurements. Considering that this locking effect can be realized in MEMS and NEMS-based microwave optomechanical systems at both cryogenic and room temperatures, this locking-based optomechanical technique can be utilized for various applications in nanomechanical sensing and RF signal processing with extreme stability.

Furthermore, combined with the high critical temperature and strong magnetic resilience of niobium, our device will provide a platform for cryogenic superconducting electronics and microwave photonic devices with enhanced operating conditions. Potential applications of injection-locked microwave frequency comb include on-chip microwave references and stable microwave pulse sources for cryogenic quantum applications. For example, multiplexed microwave signals can be generated for cryogenic qubit control and readout, and their spectral positions can be tuned by implementing a DC tunable mechanical resonator in the system.
