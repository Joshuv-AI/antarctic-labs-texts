Quantification of diffuse parenchymal lung disease in non‐small cell lung cancer patients with definitive concurrent chemoradiation therapy for predicting radiation pneumonitis
Authors: Ye Chan An, Jong Hoon Kim, Jae Myung Noh, Kyungmi Yang, You Jin Oh, Sung Goo Park, Hong Ryul Pyo, Ho Yun Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe sought to quantify diffuse parenchymal lung disease (DPLD) extent using quantitative computed tomography (CT) analysis and to investigate its association with radiation pneumonitis (RP) development in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients receiving definitive concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CCRT).MethodsA total of 82 NSCLC patients undergoing definitive CCRT were included in this prospective cohort study. Pretreatment CT scans were analyzed using quantitative CT analysis software. Low‐attenuation area (LAA) features based on lung density and texture features reflecting interstitial lung disease (ILD) were extracted from the whole lung. Clinical and dosimetric factors were also evaluated. RP development was assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for grade ≥3 (≥GR3) RP.ResultsRP was identified in 68 patients (73.9%), with nine patients (10.9%) experiencing ≥GR3 RP. Univariable logistic regression analysis identified excess kurtosis and high‐attenuation area (HAA)_volume (cc) as significantly associated with ≥GR3 RP. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that the combined use of imaging features and clinical factors (forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1], forced vital capacity [FVC], and CHEMO regimen) demonstrated the best performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.924) in predicting ≥GR3 RP.ConclusionQuantified imaging features of DPLD obtained from pretreatment CT scans would predict the occurrence of RP in NSCLC patients undergoing definitive CCRT. Combining imaging features with clinical factors could improve the accuracy of the predictive model for severe RP.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, concurrent chemoradiation therapy, interstitial lung disease, predictive model, radiation pneumonitis

INTRODUCTION

Radiotherapy such as definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), is a practical intervention for patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) considered to be inoperable due to factors like advanced age or poor pulmonary function, achieving similar outcomes compared to medical surgery. 1 , 2 , 3 The primary objective of thoracic RT is to deliver a sufficient radiation dose to tumors while minimizing toxicity to the surrounding organs at risk (OARs), including the normal lungs, heart, and esophagus. Despite these precautions, one of the major concerns associated with thoracic RT is the possibility of acute pulmonary toxicity, including radiation pneumonitis (RP), which can be severe and even life‐threatening. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 A previous study reported that symptomatic RP incidence rates range from 15% to 40%. 10

Clinical risk factors that contribute to the likelihood of RP include old age, male sex, tumor location, poor pulmonary function, and the presence of comorbidities. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Especially, RP has been shown to be highly prevalent in patients with diffuse parenchymal lung diseases (DPLDs), including interstitial lung disease (ILD) or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Nevertheless, the management of lung cancer in the presence of ILD is a common clinical scenario, with approximately 5%–10% of lung cancer patients having a concurrent diagnosis of ILD. 20 , 21

Quantitative computed tomography (CT) analysis has been used in a growing number of studies to investigate the role of imaging features in the diagnosis and management of DPLD, 22 , 23 , 24 including the evaluation of changes in lung density and texture over time. 25 , 26 Moreover, recent advances in deep‐learning technology have had a significant impact on the accurate handling of patient data and on reducing the processing time for diagnosing DPLD. 27 Despite continued efforts, the integration of clinical risk factors with quantitative CT measures of DPLD for predicting RP has not yet been effectively explored.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether the imaging features that reflect DPLD extracted through whole‐lung volume analysis can predict the occurrence of RP. Additionally, we tried to find out the predictive potential for RP of our model by integrating these imaging features with clinical risk factors and treatment‐related dosimetric factors.

METHODS

Patients

This study was conducted prospectively with approval from the institutional review board (IRB) (IRB file no. 2020‐12‐060‐001). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before their enrollment in the study. Out of 98 patients total with locally advanced NSCLC who received definitive CCRT between October 2020 and February 2022 at Samsung Medical Center, 82 patients who met the inclusion criteria were eligible for this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows 1 : concurrent delivery of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, 2 use of only photon beams for radiotherapy planning, and 3 availability of completed clinical characteristics. Ten patients who received proton beam therapy, four patients who received both proton and photon therapy concurrently, and two patients with missing pulmonary function test (PFT) data were excluded (Figure 1). All tumors were staged based on the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer tumor staging criteria. Concurrent chemotherapy consisted of platinum‐based agents. Additionally, patients who received durvalumab immunotherapy following definitive CCRT were also included. 28 The baseline characteristics of all 82 patients are summarized in Table 1.

The patients underwent clinical examinations and chest CT imaging in the first month after treatment, followed by follow‐up appointments every 3 months during the first year, and every 6 months during the second year. Regarding confirmation of RP, the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 was used to evaluate the degree of RP, with grade ≥3 RP characterized by the need for supplemental oxygen or hospitalization. 29

CT acquisition and imaging analysis

A multislice CT scanner (64‐channel) from General Electric (GE), Siemens Healthcare, or Canon Medical Systems was used to perform CT imaging before radiotherapy. Patients were placed in a supine position, and the imaging parameters used for all patients were as follows: voltage, 120 kVp; slice thickness, 1–2.5 mm; and matrices of images, X: 512, Y: 512, and Z: 80–142. Standard and bone kernels were used for the GE scanner, while B40f, B45f, or B50f kernels were used for the Siemens Healthcare scanner. An FC04 kernel was used for the Canon Medical System scanner. The images obtained using the bone kernel from the GE scanner and using the B45f and B50f kernels from the Siemens Healthcare scanner were subjected to texture analysis after performing kernel conversion using RouteGAN, a deep learning–based technique. 30

Data‐driven features were extracted from precontrast CT images for each patient by using the AVIEW COPD program (Corelinesoft, Seoul, South Korea) (Figure 2). Initially, the program automatically calculated the volume of the whole lung by excluding pulmonary vessels or airways. Following this, two types of imaging features were considered for our study, including (1) low‐attenuation area (LAA) features and (2) texture features. LAA features based on lung density (threshold value, −950 HU) represent first‐order statistics, such as mean and standard deviation values, variance, skewness, and kurtosis. Texture features reflecting the heterogeneity of ILD patterns were quantified as the distinct textural characteristics, as follows: (1) ground‐glass opacity, (2) reticulation, (3) honeycombing, and (4) consolidation. A thoracic radiologist (H.Y.L.) with 20 years of experience precisely reviewed the automatic segmentation of texture patterns. In cases where the program failed to correctly classify the texture patterns, manual corrections were performed. Detailed descriptions of each imaging feature can be found in Supplemental Material A in Data S1.

For prediction modeling, we considered the average values for the integrated regions, ipsilateral or contralateral lung, which is defined as the inclusion of target locations in RT planning. In the right or left lung, the ipsilateral lung region was defined as the lung area encompassing the target lobe for the RT plan, whereas the contralateral region was defined as the other side of lung not including the target lobe.

Radiotherapy

For treatment planning CT, four‐dimensional simulation CT scans were used. The tumor volume was identified based on all available clinical information and defined as the gross tumor volume (GTV). Then, the internal target volume (ITV) was delineated by combining all GTVs in each respiratory phase, while the clinical target volume (CTV) was generated by expanding the ITV in all directions by 5 mm and then adjusting based on adjacent anatomical structures. Finally, the planning target volume (PTV) was created by expanding the CTVs by 5 mm. The experienced oncologists and radiologists reached a consensus on target volumes and OARs used for treatment. The Pinnacle (Philips Healthcare) treatment planning system was used for volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) treatment planning. 31

The prescribed total dose for the PTV was 59.4–70.4 Gy while ensuring that the normal lung volume receiving ≥20 Gy (V20) did not exceed 35% (V20 ≤ 35%) and the mean lung dose (MLD) did not exceed 20 Gy. Additionally, the maximum dose to the spinal cord was limited to 50 Gy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corporation). The study group was divided into two groups of patients with grade <3 (<GR3) RP and patients with ≥GR3 RP, respectively. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics were compared between these two groups using appropriate statistical tests, such as student's t test for continuous variables and the chi‐squared test for categorical variables. Then, univariable logistic regression analysis was performed to compare clinical variables and RT factors between the two groups, and we considered p < 0.1 to be statistically significant.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to identify the presence of multicollinearity among the imaging features. Any imaging features with a VIF score of >10 points were removed from consideration. Univariable analysis was then performed on the imaging features selected based on the VIF, and we considered p < 0.05 to be statistically significant.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted using the statistically significant features determined in each univariable analysis result as independent risk factors to predict ≥GR3 RP. For the results of the multivariable analysis, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To examine whether imaging or clinical factors affect RP prediction, our study proposed two approaches for model construction: using only imaging features as input variables and using both imaging and patients‐specific clinical factors as input variables, respectively. Furthermore, we calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to assess the performance of the multivariable analysis model in predicting ≥GR3 RP.

RESULTS

Patients

A total of 82 individuals were enrolled, including 68 (82.9%) men. The median age of the patients was 65.0 years (range, 34–84 years). The most frequently observed T stage was T2 (26 patients, 31.6%) and the most common N stage was N3 (52 patients, 63.4%). The median value of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) was 2.5 (range, 1.0–3.8). Among the 82 enrolled patients, 42 (51.2%) had an FEV1 (%) of ≥80%, and 40 patients (48.8%) had an FEV1 (%) of <80%. The median value of forced vital capacity (FVC) was 83.0 (range, 54.0–s115.0). A total of 35 patients (42.7%) had a DLCO (%) of ≥80%, while 47 patients (57.3%) had a DLCO of <80%.

The planning results for the dose–volume histogram (DVH) parameters of the target and OARs are summarized in Supplemental Material B. The median volume of the CTVs was 452.6 cm3 (range, 107.9–1499.7 cm3). The most frequently prescribed dose for definitive CCRT was 66 Gy with 33 fractions (n = 71, 86.6%). The median MLD, V5, and V20 values were 19.61 Gy (range, 8.7–33.9 Gy), 60.75% (range, 17.4–85.6%), and 33.81% (range, 7.2–52.9%), respectively, with most of them being lower than values of the planning criteria (Supplemental Material B, in Data S1).

Radiation pneumonitis

The median follow‐up period after finishing definitive CCRT was 20.0 months (range, 1–30 months). A total of 68 patients (73.9%) were confirmed to have RP, including nine patients (10.9%) with ≥GR3 RP. The median period from the start of RT to the occurrence of RP was 4 months (range, 1–9 months).

All patients with symptoms of RP, such as dyspnea, were given oral steroid treatment, and those with severe symptoms of RP requiring O2 resuscitation were also treated. Among them, one patient was diagnosed with life‐threatening RP grade 4, and one patient expired after exacerbation of RP grade 5.

Prediction model for RP

Univariable analysis was conducted to examine the association between clinical and dosimetric factors and the occurrence of severe (≥GR3) RP (Table 1). In the ≥GR3 RP group, higher age (p = 0.436), a higher proportion of N2 stage (p = 0.071), and lower values of FEV1 (p = 0.076) and FVC (p = 0.070) in PFT were observed. Regarding the usage of chemotherapy regimens, the use of carboplatin in the ≥GR3 RP group was observed to be statistically significant in relation to RP (p = 0.066), while the immunotherapy application status was not statistically significant (p = 0.724). In terms of univariable analysis for RP prediction, FEV1 (odds ratio [OR], 0.31 (0.09–1.16); p = 0.083), FVC (OR, 0.95 (0.9–1.01); p = 0.077) and CHEMO regimen (OR, 0.28 (0.07–1.16); p = 0.079) were the clinical parameters significantly associated with severe ≥GR3 RP. Regarding the RT factors, although values of V5 (p = 0.449) and MLD (p = 0.941) were higher in the ≥GR3 RP group, the differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, the univariable prediction modeling for the dosimetric factors of normal lungs did not yield significant results.

Supplemental Material C in Data S1 presents the results of the multicollinearity analysis for imaging features, resulting in the selection of 35 imaging features. The details of univariable and multivariable prediction modeling for the selected imaging features are summarized in Table 2, revealing that ExcessKurtosis and HAA_Volume(cc) features were statistically significant for the ipsilateral lung and target lobe.

DISCUSSION

In this study we attempted to develop prediction models for RP using quantification of DPLD, which is well known as the predominant high‐risk factor for RP. Through this approach, we demonstrated that the imaging features reflecting DPLD derived from quantitative CT analysis for the whole lung might predict the occurrence of RP. In addition, the integration of clinical factors into the predictive model resulted in improved performance (AUC = 0.924). The findings of this study present a promising approach to assist clinicians in identifying patients at high risk of developing RP and in developing personalized plans to optimize treatment outcomes before radiotherapy.

In recent years, with the widespread adoption of low‐dose CT imaging, high‐resolution CT (HRCT), and artificial intelligence technologies, there has been a substantial increase in newly diagnosed cases of lung cancer, particularly among the elderly population. 32 , 33 Although surgical resection has been the standard treatment for medically operable early‐stage NSCLC, 1 there are some cases where patients are deemed inoperable due to their old age and poor health status. 34 Consequently, patients with unresectable NSCLC often undergo RT, which has also evolved in parallel with surgical advancements. 35 However, RP remains an unresolved issue.

Patient‐specific clinical factors such as old age and thoracic comorbidities have been reported to be correlated with severe RP. 36 Li et al. reported that lung cancer patients with subclinical ILD involving ≥25% of the lung volume have an increased risk of developing ≥GR3 RP. 37 Takeda et al. reported that patients with severe COPD who received stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) experienced relatively mild RP. The study found that heavy smoking, which was strongly correlated with severe COPD, served as a significant negative predictor for severe RP. 38 The authors suggested that additional follow‐up and quantitative analysis of the lungs could be necessary to determine the long‐term tolerability of SBRT. While previous studies have highlighted the significance of DPLD as a significant risk factor for RP, there has been a lack of research focusing on predictive models based on precise quantitative analysis.

Quantitative CT analysis for DPLD in the diagnostic field has been extensively studied. Moon et al. demonstrated that quantitative CT analysis, including whole‐lung evaluation and iodine mapping, combined with physiological parameters, can predict patient survival and provide valuable insights into the characterization and prognosis of ILD. 23 Ye et al. demonstrated that pre‐treatment measurement of %LAA‐860, a quantitative assessment of emphysematous changes on CT scans, provides valuable information for optimizing therapeutic strategies in early‐stage NSCLC patients receiving SBRT. However, it should be noted that the study is limited by the lack of mandatory pulmonary function tests, which could have provided additional valuable data for assessing the relationship between emphysema and RP. 26

On the other hand, we have developed a prediction model using the quantification of DPLD on pretreatment CT images, along with various clinical factors like pulmonary function tests, which ultimately showed improved accuracy compared to previous models for predicting RP. We utilized LAA imaging features, specifically excess kurtosis and HAA_Volume(cc) features for the ipsilateral lung and target lobe, to construct the predictive model. Especially, these imaging features have been shown to reflect COPD, and our findings align with previous research, highlighting the significant association between COPD and the development of RP. 39 Furthermore, we have also conducted multivariable analysis, incorporating clinical factors such as FEV1, FVC, and CHEMO regimen. The inclusion of these clinical factors further contributed to the improved performance of our predictive model.

Several studies have also reported that the correlation between the occurrence of RP and DVH parameters in patients with definitive CCRT. 10 , 40 These studies have indicated that the percentage of lung volume receiving ≥5 Gy (V5) and ≥ 20 Gy (V20), along with the MLD, serve as predictive factors of severe RP. Palma et al. reported that the type of chemotherapy (e.g., carboplatin–paclitaxel) is also identified as a predictive factor for the occurrence of severe RP along with DVH parameters. 10 In the results of our study, the V5, V10, and MLD values were found to be higher in the severe RP group. However, dosimetric values were not included during the modeling process in our study. This failure is attributed to the lack of an integrated spatial analysis that incorporates both the spatial dosimetric map and the distribution of DPLD. In future studies, there is a need to conduct analyses that consider the spatial information of dosimetric maps along with the extent of DPLD within the whole‐lung volume. This integrated approach will provide valuable insights into the relationship between radiation dose distribution and DPLD, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of RP risk prediction.

The current study had several limitations. First, there was an issue of small sample size and imbalanced data, with a ratio of 73:9 for RP grade 3. To validate our results, further research on a larger scale and preferably in a multicenter setting is necessary. Second, the use of CT systems from various vendors in our institution may introduce potential bias in the quantification results of DPLD. Although we attempted to mitigate the impact of different kernel biases by employing advanced deep learning technology—specifically, RouteGAN methods 29 , 41 —the issue of vendor bias remains.

In summary, our study highlights the crucial role of early detection and characterization of DPLD through pretreatment CT imaging. By quantifying DPLD in pretreatment CT scans, we could effectively identify patients at high risk of developing RP. This knowledge allows for the development of tailored radiotherapy plans to mitigate treatment‐related toxicity and the implementation of personalized approaches to optimize treatment outcomes.
