Impact of rotation on the accretion of entropy perturbations in collapsing massive stars
Olzhas Mukazhanov

Abstract
Convection in the innermost shells of massive stars plays an important role in initiating core-collapse supernovae. When these convective motions reach the supernova shock, they create extra turbulence, which helps energize the explosion. In our earlier work, we studied the effect of rotation on the hydrodynamic evolution of convective vortices in collapsing stars. This study focuses on how rotation influences the entropy perturbations, which naturally form in turbulent convection. As these perturbations are carried inward with the collapsing star, they generate both vorticity and sound waves. Using linear perturbation theory, we model entropy waves as small disturbances on top of a steady background flow. Our results show that stellar rotation has little effect on the evolution of entropy perturbations during collapse, prior to encountering the supernova shock. This outcome is consistent with our earlier findings on the limited influence of rotation in the accretion of convective eddies.

Introduction

Core-collapse supernovae mark the violent deaths of massive stars, releasing vast amounts of energy and driving the synthesis of heavy elements
(
Burrows 2013
;
Janka et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. When the stellar core exhausts its nuclear fuel, it becomes unstable and collapses under gravity, forming a neutron star or black hole
(
O’Connor and Ott 2011
;
Kotake et al. 2012
;
Burrows et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. The collapse launches a shock wave that initially stalls, and its subsequent revival through neutrino heating, turbulence, and rotation remains a central open problem in astrophysics
(
Foglizzo and Tagger 2000
;
Müller and Varma 2020
;
Mezzacappa et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. Understanding these explosions is essential for linking stellar evolution with compact object formation, nucleosynthesis, and multimessenger signals such as neutrinos and gravitational waves
(
Nakamura et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
.

One of the effects that may help the explosion is the role of convection in pre-collapse stars. The progenitors of core-collapse supernovae develop strong convection in their nuclear-burning layers prior to iron core collapse
(
Arnett and Meakin 2011
;
Fields and Couch 2021
;
Varma and Müller 2021
, e.g.,)
. This activity is thought to play an important role in shaping the supernova explosions
(
Couch and Ott 2013
;
Couch et al. 2015
;
Müller et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
. After the iron core collapses, the convective shells fall toward the center
(
Lai and Goldreich 2000
;
Takahashi and Yamada 2014
;
Boccioli et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. The supernova shock, formed at core bounce, collides with these perturbations
(
Müller and Janka 2015
;
Müller 2016
, e.g.,)
. This interaction enhances the chaotic, non-radial motions in the region behind the shock
(
Müller et al. 2017
;
Huete and Abdikamalov 2019
)
. As a result, extra pressure is produced that helps the shock expand and makes the conditions for an explosion more favorable
(
Takahashi et al. 2016
;
Nagakura et al. 2019
;
Vartanyan et al. 2022
;
Bollig et al. 2021
)
. Among the different shells, the innermost shells, those undergoing oxygen and (to a lesser extent) silicon burning are, expected to contribute most strongly to shaping the explosion conditions
(
Collins et al. 2018
)
. See, e.g.,
Müller 2020
for a recent review.

Using linear hydrodynamic equations,
Abdikamalov and Foglizzo 2020
investigated how convective vortices evolve during the collapse of non-rotating stars under idealized conditions for non-rotating stars. A simplified framework enables systematic parameter studies and helps to elucidate the key physical mechanisms
(
Fernández and Thompson 2009
;
Foglizzo 2009
, e.g.,)
. They showed that accelerating collapse stretches vortices radially, reducing their velocity, while at the same time distorting density surfaces and inducing pressure fluctuations that generate acoustic waves
(
Kovalenko and Eremin 1998
;
Müller and Janka 2015
)
. When these disturbances reach the shock, velocity perturbations from acoustic waves are expected to dominate over those from the vortices themselves
(
Huete et al. 2018
)
.

Some stars are expected to be rotating
(
Heger et al. 2005
;
Popov and Turolla 2012
;
Mosser et al. 2012
;
Deheuvels et al. 2014
;
Dhanpal et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. In
Abdikamalov et al. 2021
(hereafter Paper I), we investigated the role of rotation in the fluid-dynamical evolution of convective vortices over the course of stellar collapse. Employing first-order linear perturbation theory within a simplified framework, we traced the vortices from convective shells down to the vicinity of the supernova shock. Our main conclusion was that rotation has only a minor effect on their evolution. The reason is that, prior to shock interaction, i.e. when the perturbations are still at large radii, the infalling matter does not spin up sufficiently to alter their dynamics. This finding holds even for the most rapidly rotating progenitors. Building on this, the present work focuses instead on the influence of rotation on entropy perturbations, which are generated by turbulent convection in nuclear-burning shells of massive stars
(
Yadav et al. 2020
;
Yoshida et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.

Entropy perturbations in stellar matter generate vorticity during collapse through the baroclinic effect
(
Foglizzo 2001
)
. In a non-uniform flow with entropy variations, surfaces of constant pressure do not coincide with those of constant density. As a result, the net pressure force on a fluid element does not pass through its center of mass, creating a torque that produces additional vorticity, as explained in more detail below. Both entropy and vorticity waves can also excite acoustic waves as they propagate: vorticity waves, in particular, distort density surfaces and thereby induce pressure perturbations
(
Foglizzo et al. 2006
;
Foglizzo et al. 2007
, e.g.,)
.
In this work, we examine how rotation influences these processes. As shown below, the effect of rotation is found to be weak. The reason is straightforward: before encountering the shock, when the matter is still at large radii, the spin-up during infall is insufficient to alter the dynamics in a significant way.

A clarification is in order. Although stellar matter does not spin up to dynamically important rotation rates before shock crossing, rotation can still play an important role in the post-shock region and give rise to a variety of interesting effects in rapidly rotating stars
(
Takiwaki et al. 2016
;
Summa et al. 2018
;
Piran et al. 2019
;
Shishkin and Soker 2021
)
. For instance, centrifugal deformation at core bounce can produce significant gravitational-wave emission
(
Zwerger and Mueller 1997
;
Fuller et al. 2015
;
Mitra et al. 2023
;
Mitra et al. 2024
;
Abylkairov et al. 2024
)
and lead to non-axisymmetric modes developing after core bounce
(
Shibagaki et al. 2020
, e.g.,)

2
Methdods

As in Paper I, we model entropy perturbations as linear perturbations on top of the mean flow of the collapsing star. The stellar material is described by an ideal gas equation of state with adiabatic index
\gamma=4/3
, a reasonable representation for for stellar matter dominated by radiation
(
Fernández 2015
, e.g.,)
. A rotating transonic Bondi solution is used to model the background flow. It exhibits a sonic radius
r_{\rm s}
, where the flow transitions from the subsonic to the supersonic regime. Assuming constant specific angular momentum radial profile, the solution depends on
\gamma
, the core mass, rotation, and pressure. We take the central mass as
1.4M_{\odot}
, which is in line with the mass of proto neutron stars obtained in modern 3D simulations
(
Nagakura et al. 2020
;
Müller et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Since
r_{\rm s}
depends on the sound speed, and hence on temperature, setting its value fixes the pressure scale. We adopt
r_{\rm s}=1.5\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{km}
, a typical value for the early phase of post-bounce evolution
(
Takahashi and Yamada 2014
, e.g.,)
. This model accounts for the leading-order effects of rotation: the differential rotation caused by angular momentum conservation and the slowdown of collapse due to centrifugal force. Details of the method used to obtain the background solution can be found in Appendix B of Paper I.

We explore ten different values of specific angular momentum
L
, spanning from 0 up to
L_{\mathrm{max}}=3\times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{2}/s}
. This upper value exceeds by a factor of a few the maximum angular momenta expected even in the most rapidly rotating pre-supernova cores, which are estimated to be
{\lesssim}10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{2}/s}

(
Woosley and Heger 2006
, e.g.,)
. We nevertheless adopt it as an extreme case to probe the effect of rotation over a broad range. As shown below, even at this extreme limit, rotation has little influence on the perturbations during collapse, prior to their interaction with the supernova shock.

Following our Paper I, the perturbation dynamics are described by linear hydrodynamic equations, which we express as a 2nd-order ordinary differential equation (cf. Appendix
5
for its derivation),

(1)

where

. To simplify, we neglect poloidal derivatives

)
. The angular dependence is separated by assuming a

We note that our method, though simplified, is deliberately designed to isolate the influence of rotation from other physical processes. While not intended to yield precise quantitative predictions, it effectively highlights the underlying mechanisms and enables systematic parameter studies, thereby complementing large-scale simulations.

3.1
Qualitative Picture

If the entropy variation in a star exhibit a non-radial structure, the surfaces of constant density no longer coincide with those of constant pressure. Consequently, the pressure force acting on a fluid element is not directed through its center of mass. This misalignment gives rise to a baroclinic effect, which exerts a torque and thereby generates vorticity
(
Foglizzo 2001
, e.g.,)
. The mechanism is schematically illustrated in Fig.
1

(see, e.g.,
Thorne and Blandford 2017
, for a pedagogical explanation)
. As this vorticity is advected inward, it perturbs the flow and distorts the iso-density surfaces, which in turn leads to the emission of acoustic waves
(
Müller and Janka 2015
;
Abdikamalov and Foglizzo 2020
)
.

In addition, entropy perturbations can also couple directly to the background flow to produce acoustic waves. Consider a fluid element with mass
m
with entropy variation of
\delta S
. How much the element expands under a change in pressure depends on its entropy. This expansion produces sound waves. If the element moves from a region with average specific enthalpy
h_{1}
to another region with enthalpy
h_{2}
, the energy of the sound waves is
(
Foglizzo and Tagger 2000
)

\delta E\sim(h_{2}-h_{1})\delta m

(2)

where
\delta m
is the change in the mass
m
of a fluid element that keeps the same volume but has an entropy perturbation of
\delta S
,

\delta m=m\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho}=m\frac{\delta S}{\gamma c_{\mathrm{v}}},

(3)

where
c_{\mathrm{v}}
is the specific heat at constant volume. The specific energy carried by the emitted sound waves is therefore
(
Foglizzo and Tagger 2000
)
:

\delta{\cal E}\sim\left(h_{2}-h_{1}\right)\frac{\delta S}{\gamma c_{\mathrm{v}}}.

(4)

So, the energy of the sound waves is directly proportional to the entropy change
\delta S
and the difference in enthalpy
h_{2}-h_{1}
. If the flow is uniform (
h_{2}=h_{1}
), no sound waves are produced.

Assuming that the entropy fluctuations are due to dissipation of turbulent kinetic energy, the specific entropy can be estimated to an order of magnitude as

\delta S\sim\frac{dQ}{T}\sim\frac{\delta\upsilon^{2}}{T}

(5)

The temperature is related to the speed of sound

T=\mu c^{2}/(\gamma R)

\delta S\sim\gamma\frac{R}{\mu}\delta{\cal M}^{2},

(6)

where

\delta{\cal M}\sim 0.1

(
Collins et al. 2018

4
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed how entropy disturbances evolve in massive, rotating stars during collapse. To do this, we used linear hydrodynamic equations on a steady-state background flow. The background flow was described with the rotating, transonic Bondi solution. The equations were then solved in spherical coordinates restricted to the equatorial plane (see Section
2
for details). We study the effects of rotation over a wide range, from non-rotating models to stars rotating at extreme rates.

We investigated vorticity waves generated by the advection of entropy perturbations through baroclinic effects. Our results show that the radial velocity component of these waves is largely unaffected by rotation, whereas the azimuthal component increases as the core spins faster. This behavior arises because collapse stretches vortices in the radial direction, while differential rotation shears and distorts them azimuthally.

At small radii, where the perturbations are expected to impinge upon the supernova shock, we find that radial velocity and pressure perturbations remain largely insensitive to rotation. In contrast, the azimuthal velocity perturbations increase with rotation, especially for intermediate angular modes (
m=3,4
). This is a direct consequence of the spin-up of infalling matter as it moves inward. Despite this growth, the vortex velocities remain below one percent of the local sound speed, suggesting that their contribution to the explosion dynamics is minor (see Section
3
for details).

When comparing the contributions of entropy perturbations with those of the original convective eddies, we find that low-order modes (
m=1,2
) are dominated by convective eddies. These modes are most relevant for the explosion mechanism since they strongly reduce the critical neutrino luminosity required for shock revival. For higher-order modes (
m=3,4
), the contribution of entropy perturbations can become significant (relative to the contribution of the convective eddies), but these modes are expected to have a smaller impact on the explosion outcome
(
Müller et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
.

Our results provide insight into the evolution of entropy perturbations in rotating stellar cores prior to their encounter with the supernova shock. A key long-term objective is to determine how such pre-collapse perturbations influence the explosion dynamics in rotating models. Shock revival is governed by a number of intertwined processes, particularly the interaction of incoming acoustic waves with the shock front
(
Huete et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
and with the post-shock flow
(
Fernández 2015
;
Müller et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. While advanced three-dimensional simulations exist
(
Müller et al. 2017
;
Vartanyan et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, the specific role of rotation in these interactions remains somewhat unexplored, even within simplified setups
(
Buellet et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

It is important to emphasize that our analysis relies on an idealized framework constructed to isolate the influence of rotation from other competing effects. While this setup is not intended to yield precise quantitative predictions, it provides a powerful means of uncovering the fundamental mechanisms at play and enables a systematic parameter study. In this way, our work complements large-scale numerical simulations by offering a clearer physical interpretation of the processes that govern explosion dynamics.

\displaystyle-i\omega^{\prime}\delta\rho+{1\over r^{2}}{\partial\over\partial r}{r^{2}\rho\upsilon_{r}\delta h}+{im\over r}{\rho\delta\upsilon_{\phi}}=0,

(15)

where
\omega^{\prime}
is the Doppler shifted frequency,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\equiv\omega-{mL\over r^{2}}.

(16)

The linearized Euler equation is expressed as:

\displaystyle-i\omega\delta\upsilon_{r}+{L\over r}\delta w_{\theta}+{\partial\delta f\over\partial r}={c^{2}\over\gamma}{\partial\delta S\over\partial r},

(17)

\displaystyle-i\omega\delta\upsilon_{\phi}-\upsilon_{r}\delta w_{\theta}+{im\over r}\delta f={c^{2}\over\gamma}{im\over r}\delta S.

(18)

The entropy perturbation evolves according to

\displaystyle\left({\partial\over\partial r}-{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}\right)\delta S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(19)

The curl of the Euler equation yields the vorticity equation :

\displaystyle{\partial\over\partial r}(r\upsilon_{r}\delta w_{\theta})={i\omega^{\prime}r}\delta w_{\theta}-im{\delta S\over\gamma}{\partial c^{2}\over\partial r},.

(20)

where

\displaystyle\delta w_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over r}{\partial r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}\over\partial r}+{im\over r}\delta\upsilon_{r}.

(21)

is vorticity. The complete system can now be expressed through the following set of differential equations:

\displaystyle{\partial\delta f\over\partial r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\omega\delta\upsilon_{r}+{c^{2}\over\gamma}{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}\delta S-{L\over r}\delta w_{\theta},

(22)

\displaystyle{\partial\delta h\over\partial r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}{\delta\rho\over\rho}-{im\over r\upsilon_{r}}{\delta\upsilon_{\phi}},

(23)

\displaystyle\left({\partial\over\partial r}-{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}\right)\delta K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(24)

\displaystyle\left({\partial\over\partial r}-{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}\right)\delta S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(25)

\displaystyle\omega r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta K}{m}+{m}\delta f,

(26)

\displaystyle\delta f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\omega\over m}r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}+{\delta K\over m^{2}},

(27)

where

\displaystyle\delta K

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-imr\upsilon_{r}\delta w_{\theta}+m^{2}{c^{2}\over\gamma}\delta S.

(28)

Quantities
\delta S
and
\delta K
can easily be integrated to

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta S_{0}{\rm e}^{\int_{r_{0}}{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r},

(29)

\displaystyle\delta K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta K_{0}{\rm e}^{\int_{r_{0}}{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r},

(30)

\displaystyle\delta w_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{i\over r\upsilon_{r}}\left[\frac{\delta K_{0}}{m}-m{c^{2}\over\gamma}\delta S_{0}\right]{\rm e}^{\int_{r_{0}}{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r},

(31)

If we express
\delta f
in terms of
r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\partial\over\partial r}(r\delta\upsilon_{\phi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle im\delta\upsilon_{r}-i{\delta K\over{m\upsilon_{r}}}+im{c^{2}\over\gamma\upsilon_{r}}\delta S,

(32)

\displaystyle{\partial\delta h\over\partial r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}{\delta\rho\over\rho}-{im\over r\upsilon_{r}}{\delta\upsilon_{\phi}}.

(33)

By introducing the new variable
X
, defined via,

\displaystyle{\partial X\over\partial r}\equiv{\upsilon_{r}\over 1-{\cal M}^{2}},

(34)

one can obtain

\displaystyle\left({\partial\over\partial X}+{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}\right)(r\delta\upsilon_{\phi})

\displaystyle=im\delta h-i{\delta K\over{m\upsilon_{r}^{2}}}+{im\over\gamma}\delta S\left({1\over{\cal M}^{2}}+\gamma-1\right),

(35)

\displaystyle\left({\partial\over\partial X}+{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}\right)\delta h

\displaystyle={iW\over m}r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}-{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}^{2}}\left(\delta S-{\delta K\over m^{2}c^{2}}\right),

(36)

The parameter
W
is

\displaystyle W=\frac{\omega^{\prime 2}\mu^{2}}{\upsilon_{r}^{2}c^{2}},

(37)

where

\displaystyle\mu^{2}\equiv 1-{m^{2}c^{2}\over r^{2}\omega^{\prime 2}}(1-{\cal M}^{2}).

(38)

Combining these together, we obtain a single 2nd-order ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\left\{\left({\partial\over\partial X}+{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}\right)^{2}+W\right\}

\displaystyle(r\delta\upsilon_{\phi})={\omega^{\prime}m\over\upsilon_{r}^{2}}\left(\delta S-{\delta K\over m^{2}c^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-im\left({\partial\over\partial X}+{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}\right)\left[{\delta K\over m^{2}\upsilon_{r}^{2}}-{\delta S\over\gamma}\left({1\over{\cal M}^{2}}+\gamma-1\right)\right].

(39)

Using Eq. (
31
), the equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left\{\left({\partial\over\partial X}+{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}\right)^{2}+W\right\}(r\delta\upsilon_{\phi})=-{\partial\over\partial X}\left({r\delta w_{\theta}\over\upsilon_{r}}\right),

(40)

This equation closely resembles that of the corresponding flow without rotation. The role of angular momentum enters only through
\omega^{\prime}

(
Yamasaki and Foglizzo 2008
)
. We now introduce the variable

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi}\equiv\delta\upsilon_{\phi}{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X},

(41)

in terms of which, the system of Eqs. (
35
)–(
36
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle{\partial(r\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi})\over\partial X}

\displaystyle=im\delta\tilde{h}+\left[-i{\delta K\over{m\upsilon_{r}^{2}}}+{im\over\gamma}\delta S\left({1\over{\cal M}^{2}}+\gamma-1\right)\right]{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X},

(42)

\displaystyle{\partial\delta\tilde{h}\over\partial X}

\displaystyle={iW\over m}r\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi}-{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}^{2}}\left(\delta S-{\delta K\over m^{2}c^{2}}\right){\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X}.

(43)

Eq. (
40
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\left\{{\partial^{2}\over\partial X^{2}}+W\right\}(r\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi})=-{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X}{\partial\over\partial X}{r\delta w_{\theta}\over\upsilon_{r}}.

(44)

The in-going and out-going waves, which represent the solution of the homogeneous part of the linearized equations, as well as boundary conditions are obtained following the method outlined in Section C1 of Paper I. Once we know
\delta f
,
\delta h
,
\delta\upsilon_{\phi}
,
\delta K
, and
\delta S
, we can obtain other hydrodynamic variables via expressions

\displaystyle{\delta\upsilon_{r}\over\upsilon_{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 1-{\cal M}^{2}}\left(\delta h+\delta S-{\omega^{\prime}\over mc^{2}}r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}-{\delta K\over m^{2}c^{2}}\right),

(45)

\displaystyle{\delta\rho\over\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 1-{\cal M}^{2}}\left({\cal M}^{2}\delta h+\delta S-{\omega^{\prime}\over mc^{2}}r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}-{\delta K\over m^{2}c^{2}}\right),

(46)

\displaystyle{\delta p\over{\gamma p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 1-{\cal M}^{2}}\left({\cal M}^{2}(\delta h+\delta S)+(1-{\cal M}^{2}){\delta S\over\gamma}-{\omega^{\prime}\over mc^{2}}r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}-{\delta K\over m^{2}c^{2}}\right).

(47)

5.1
Inhomogeneous solution

To obtain the full solution generated by advected entropy perturbations, we linearly superpose two solutions: one with
dS
and another with
\delta K=m^{2}c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}{\delta S}/{\gamma}
, where
c_{\mathrm{shell}}
is the speed of sound at the initial radius of the perturbations. The latter solution takes into account the contribution of vortices generated by advected entropy perturbations. This choice
\delta K=m^{2}c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}{\delta S}/{\gamma}
guarantees vanishing vorticity at the initial radius of the perturbations.

To derive the solution for advected vorticity waves (
\delta S\neq 0
,
\delta K=0
), we begin by rewriting Eq. (
44
) in terms of
\delta\tilde{f}
:

\displaystyle\left\{{\partial^{2}\over\partial X^{2}}+W\right\}\delta\tilde{f}\!=\!-\frac{1\!-\!{\cal M}^{2}}{\upsilon_{r}}{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X}\frac{\omega}{{\cal M}^{2}}\left(i\partial_{r}\!\log{\cal M}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\upsilon_{r}}\right)\frac{\delta S}{\gamma}.

(48)

We solve this equation by generalizing the method used for the non-rotating case
(
Foglizzo 2001
)
to non-uniform frequency
\omega^{\prime}
, as proposed by
Yamasaki and Foglizzo 2008
. Since

{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X}\delta S={\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over c^{2}}{\rm d}X}{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r}\delta S_{R}={\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}^{2}}{\rm d}X}\delta S_{R},

(49)

we can write Eq. (
48
) as

\displaystyle\left\{{\partial^{2}\over\partial X^{2}}+W\right\}\delta\tilde{f}\!=\!-\frac{1\!-\!{\cal M}^{2}}{\upsilon_{r}}{\rm e}^{\int{i\omega^{\prime}\over\upsilon_{r}^{2}}{\rm d}X}\frac{\omega}{{\cal M}^{2}}\left(i\partial_{r}\!\log{\cal M}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\upsilon_{r}}\right)\frac{\delta S_{R}}{\gamma}.

(50)

Assuming regularity at the sonic point and no incoming acoustic waves at infinity, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta f(r>r_{\rm s})=-{\delta S_{R}\over 2\gamma}

\displaystyle\bigg\{\delta f^{-}\int_{r_{\rm s}}^{r}{\delta\tilde{f}_{0}}{\partial\over\partial r}\left({1-{\cal M}^{2}\over{\cal M}^{2}}{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}\right){\rm d}r

\displaystyle-\delta f_{0}\int_{\infty}^{r}{\delta\tilde{f}^{-}}{\partial\over\partial r}\left({1-{\cal M}^{2}\over{\cal M}^{2}}{\rm e}^{i\omega\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}\right){\rm d}r\bigg\},

(51)

where
\delta f^{-}
and
f_{0}
are homogeneous solutions and they are obtained as described in Appendix C1 of Paper I. After an integration by parts, we obtain the following

\displaystyle\delta f(r>r_{\rm s})={\delta S_{R}\over 2\gamma}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\delta f^{-}\int_{r_{\rm s}}^{r}{\partial{\delta\tilde{f}_{0}}\over\partial r}{1-{\cal M}^{2}\over{\cal M}^{2}}{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle-\delta f_{0}\int_{\infty}^{r}{\partial{\delta\tilde{f}^{-}}\over\partial r}{1-{\cal M}^{2}\over{\cal M}^{2}}{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r\bigg\}.

(52)

To simplify this further, we re-write the system (
42
)-(
43
) into a system for homogeneous functions
\delta\tilde{f}
and
\delta h
:

\displaystyle{\partial\delta\tilde{f}\over\partial X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\omega\delta\tilde{h},

(53)

\displaystyle{\partial\delta\tilde{h}\over\partial X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{iW\over\omega}\delta\tilde{f}.

(54)

where we used relation
r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}=(m/\omega)\delta f
for homogeneous solution (cf. Eq.
26
). Since

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}X}=\frac{1-{\cal M}^{2}}{\upsilon_{r}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r},

(55)

the above system can be written as

\displaystyle{\partial\delta\tilde{f}\over\partial r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\omega\upsilon_{r}}{1-{\cal M}^{2}}\delta\tilde{h},

(56)

\displaystyle{\partial\delta\tilde{h}\over\partial r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{iW\over\omega}\frac{\upsilon_{r}}{1-{\cal M}^{2}}\delta\tilde{f}.

(57)

Using this, we can re-write solution (
52
) as

\displaystyle\delta f(r>r_{\rm s})={{i\omega\delta S_{R}}\over 2\gamma}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\delta f^{-}\int_{r_{\rm s}}^{r}{{\delta\tilde{h}_{0}}}{\upsilon_{r}\over{\cal M}^{2}}{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle-\delta f_{0}\int_{\infty}^{r}{\delta\tilde{h}^{-}}{\upsilon_{r}\over{\cal M}^{2}}{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r\bigg\}.

(58)

One more integration by parts yields integrals that converge at infinity:

\displaystyle\delta f(r>r_{\rm s})

\displaystyle={{\delta S_{R}}\over 2\gamma}\bigg\{\frac{2\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}(c^{2}-\upsilon_{r}^{2})e^{\int\frac{i\omega^{\prime}}{\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle-\delta f^{-}\int_{r_{\rm s}}^{r}\partial_{r}\left[\delta\tilde{h}_{0}(c^{2}-\upsilon_{r}^{2})\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\right]{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle+\delta f_{0}\int_{\infty}^{r}\partial_{r}\left[\delta{\tilde{h}}^{-}(c^{2}-\upsilon_{r}^{2})\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\right]{\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}{\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r\bigg\}.

(59)

We write it in a more compact form:

\displaystyle\delta f(r>r_{\rm s})

\displaystyle={{\delta S_{R}}\over 2\gamma}\bigg\{\frac{2\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}(c^{2}-\upsilon_{r}^{2})e^{\int\frac{i\omega^{\prime}}{\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle-\delta f^{-}\int_{r_{\rm s}}^{r}G(r){\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}(1+{\cal M}^{2}){\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle+\delta f_{0}\int_{\infty}^{r}H(r){\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}(1+{\cal M}^{2}){\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r\bigg\},

(60)

where

\displaystyle G(r)=\frac{i\upsilon_{r}c^{2}W}{\omega^{\prime}}\delta f_{0}+\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\bigg[\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial r}-\frac{\partial\upsilon_{r}^{2}}{\partial r}-(c^{2}-\upsilon_{r}^{2})\frac{2mL}{\omega^{\prime}r^{3}}\bigg]\delta h_{0}

(61)

and

\displaystyle H(r)=\frac{i\upsilon_{r}c^{2}W}{\omega^{\prime}}\delta f^{-}+\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\bigg[\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial r}-\frac{\partial\upsilon_{r}^{2}}{\partial r}-(c^{2}-\upsilon_{r}^{2})\frac{2mL}{\omega^{\prime}r^{3}}\bigg]\delta h^{-}.

(62)

One drawback of integration by parts is that it introduces terms
\propto 1/\omega^{\prime}
, which become singular at the corotation point, where the Doppler-shifted frequency
\omega^{\prime}
becomes zero. We avoid this singularity by combining integration with and without integration by parts around that point.

The solution for function
\delta h
is obtained using Eq. (
42
):

\displaystyle\delta h(r>r_{\rm s})={{\delta S_{R}}\over 2\gamma}\bigg\{

\displaystyle-\delta h^{-}\int_{r_{\rm s}}^{r}G(r){\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}(1+{\cal M}^{2}){\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle-\delta h_{0}\int_{\infty}^{r}H(r){\rm e}^{\int_{R}^{r}{{i\omega^{\prime}(1+{\cal M}^{2}){\rm d}r}\over\upsilon_{r}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}}{\rm d}r-2\gamma{\rm e}^{\int\frac{i\omega^{\prime}}{\upsilon_{r}}{\rm d}r}\bigg\},

(63)

where
\delta h^{-}
and
\delta h_{0}
are homogeneous solutions. In the supersonic domain (
r<r_{\rm s}
), the homogeneous solution is constructed using
\delta f
together with the Wronskian
(
Abdikamalov and Foglizzo 2020
)
. The inhomogeneous solution is subsequently derived using an approach analogous to that employed for the subsonic region.

5.2
Generation of vorticity by advected entropy waves

To calculate the vorticity generated by advected entropy perturbations, we linearly superpose two solutions: one with
dS
and another with
\delta K=m^{2}c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}{\delta S}/{\gamma}
, where
c_{\mathrm{shell}}
is the speed of sound at the initial radius of the perturbations. This choice guarantees that the vorticity at the initial radius is zero.

To obtain vortex velocities, we use the incompressibility condition described in Appendix F of
Abdikamalov and Foglizzo 2020
. We rewrite Eq. (
44
) as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X^{2}}+W\right)(r\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi})=A\delta S_{0}{\rm e}^{\int\frac{i\omega^{\prime}dX}{\upsilon_{r}^{2}}},

(64)

where
A\equiv m\omega^{\prime}(1-{\cal M}^{2})\left(c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}-{c^{2}}\right)/{\gamma}{\upsilon_{r}^{4}}
. The solution is

\displaystyle r\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi}={A\over W-\frac{\omega^{\prime 2}}{\upsilon_{r}^{4}}}\delta S_{0}{\rm e}^{\int\frac{i\omega^{\prime}dX}{\upsilon_{r}^{2}}},

(65)

or

\displaystyle r\delta\upsilon_{\phi}=r\delta\tilde{\upsilon}_{\phi}{\rm e}^{-\int\frac{i\omega^{\prime}dX}{c^{2}}}={A\over W-\frac{\omega^{\prime 2}}{\upsilon_{r}^{4}}}\delta S,

(66)

from which we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\upsilon_{\phi}}{\upsilon_{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\omega^{\prime}\upsilon_{r}r}\frac{1-{\cal M}^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}{\cal M}^{2}}\left(c^{2}-c^{2}_{\mathrm{shell}}\right)\frac{\delta S}{\gamma}.

(67)

From Eq. (
32
), we obtain

{\delta\upsilon_{r}\over\upsilon_{r}}=\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{m\upsilon_{r}^{2}}r\delta{\upsilon_{\phi}}+\frac{\delta K}{m^{2}\upsilon_{r}^{2}}-\frac{\delta S}{\gamma{\cal M}^{2}},

(68)

where we replaced the radial derivative with a multiplication by a factor
i\omega^{\prime}/\upsilon_{r}
, which is valid for advected perturbations. After substitution of the value of
\delta\upsilon_{\phi}
and
\delta K=m^{2}c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}{\delta S}/{\gamma}
, we obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\upsilon_{r}}{\upsilon_{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}{\cal M}^{2}}\left(\frac{c^{2}_{\mathrm{shell}}}{c^{2}}-1\right)\frac{\delta S}{\gamma}.

(69)

Using Eqs. (
27
) and (
36
) leads to

\displaystyle\delta f^{S}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1-{\cal M}^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}{\cal M}^{2}}(c^{2}-c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2})+c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}\right]\frac{\delta S}{\gamma},

(70)

\displaystyle\delta h^{S}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\mu^{2}(1-{\cal M}^{2})}{1-\mu^{2}{\cal M}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)+\frac{c_{\mathrm{shell}}^{2}}{c^{2}}-\gamma\right]\frac{\delta S}{\gamma}.

(71)
