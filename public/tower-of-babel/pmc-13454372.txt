Early Improvements in Metabolic Risk Factors and Non‐Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Following One Anastomosis Gastric Bypass Surgery: A 4‐Month Prospective Study
Authors: Najme‐sadat Moosavi, Sina Dadgar, Nasser Bahari, Mohammad Mersad Mansouri Tehrani, Mohammad Talebpour, Fakhroddin Kiani, Mohammad Masih Mansouri‐Tehrani, Omid Ghaemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsObesity is a growing public health concern and major risk factor for conditions including non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), cardiovascular disease, and diabetes. This study aimed to assess the early effects of one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) surgery on metabolic risk factors and liver‐related parameters in patients with morbid obesity.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal study included 41 patients aged over 30 years who had a medical indication for bariatric surgery. All participants underwent one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) surgery and were assessed preoperatively and 4 months postoperatively for anthropometric and biochemical parameters, liver ultrasonographic and elastographic findings, as well as visceral and subcutaneous fat distribution.ResultsFour months postoperatively, mean body weight and BMI decreased significantly to 90.2 ± 18.1 kg (p < 0.001) and 32.6 ± 5.5 kg/m2 (95% CI: 9.5 [8.5–10.6], p < 0.001), respectively. Glycemic indices, lipid profiles, and liver enzymes improved significantly, alongside reductions in visceral and abdominal subcutaneous fat thickness (p < 0.001). Shear wave elastography (SWE) measurements also significantly decreased (95% CI: 0.9 [0.6–1.1], p < 0.001). By follow‐up, 70.7% of patients showed no ultrasonographic evidence of hepatic steatosis, reflecting a 65.8% increase in resolution compared to pre‐surgical status, with 11 classified as grade 1, one as grade 2, and none as grade 3.ConclusionOAGB surgery is associated with significant early improvements in obesity, NAFLD and its related metabolic risk factors in morbidly obese patients. Future studies should assess long‐term outcomes to determine the sustainability of these early benefits.

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health concern worldwide, with its prevalence nearly tripling over the past four decades [1, 2]. Defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as excessive fat accumulation that may impair health, obesity is diagnosed when body mass index (BMI) reaches or exceeds 30 kg/m2 [3, 4]. Globally, approximately 1.5 billion adults are overweight, among whom 500 million are obese [5]. In the United States, over 30% of adults are classified as obese, with 3–5% meeting the criteria for morbid obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2) [6, 7]. The rising prevalence of obesity has contributed to an increase in metabolic syndrome and related conditions, such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [3, 8].

NAFLD is defined by excessive lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, accounting for more than 5% of liver weight, in the absence of alcohol or other known causes of liver disease [9, 10]. It is strongly linked to obesity, affects approximately 70% of obese individuals and up to 95% of those with morbid obesity [11, 12]. The disease presents a spectrum from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, which can progress to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [13, 14]. While weight loss is one of the most effective strategies for preventing and even reversing NAFLD, sustaining it through diet and exercise alone is particularly challenging for morbidly obese individuals [11, 15, 16, 17]. Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), recently redefined under the broader term metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), represents one of the most common chronic liver diseases worldwide. Because the present study was designed and conducted using the NAFLD nomenclature, the term NAFLD is retained throughout this manuscript for consistency with the study protocol and the cited literature.

Compared to lifestyle modifications, bariatric surgery offers a more effective and sustained strategy for weight loss, particularly for individuals with morbid obesity [17, 18, 19, 20]. Among bariatric procedures, Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (RYGB), sleeve gastrectomy (SG), and adjustable gastric banding (AGB) have been shown to facilitate significant weight reduction and mitigate metabolic risk factors associated with NAFLD [2, 11, 12, 21, 22, 23]. However, the effects of one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) also known as mini‐gastric bypass surgery on NAFLD and its associated metabolic improvements has been limitedly explored.

This longitudinal prospective study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of NAFLD among morbidly obese Iranian patients and assess the early effects of OAGB on metabolic risk factors and liver disease. Specifically, changes in glycemic and lipid profiles, as well as liver enzyme levels, were assessed through biochemical analyses before and after surgery. Additionally, ultrasonographic evaluations were performed to assess liver characteristics and visceral fat distribution, along with elastography to examine liver stiffness.

Methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted from July 2024 to January 2025 at Sina Hospital and Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Iran. The study protocol adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, was registered prior to patient enrollment at Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20240105060617N1, Principal investigator: M.M.M.T, Date of registration: 3rd of July, 2024) and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval ID: IR.TUMS.SHARIATI.REC.1401.022). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Eligibility Criteria

The study enrolled patients over 30 years of age who had a medical indication for bariatric surgery. Candidates for surgery included patients with a BMI of 40 kg/m2 or higher with acceptable surgical risks, and those with a BMI of 35 kg/m2 or greater and at least one serious obesity‐related comorbidity, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, NAFLD, gastroesophageal reflux disease, asthma, debilitating arthritis, or markedly reduced quality of life. Patients with a history of type 2 diabetes or criteria‐proven metabolic syndrome were excluded from the study to avoid potential confounding effects on the assessment of NAFLD. Additionally, individuals who did not complete the postoperative follow‐up were also excluded from the analysis.

Preoperative Workup

A multidisciplinary team of surgeons, endocrinologists, gastroenterologists, anesthesiologists, psychiatrists, and specialized nutritionists conducted comprehensive preoperative evaluations of all bariatric surgery candidates. The assessment protocol included a detailed medical history, physical examination, medication review, and standard laboratory testing. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and pulmonary function tests were performed when clinically indicated. The psychiatric evaluation assessed each patient's capacity to adhere to postoperative dietary and lifestyle modifications.

At baseline, 14 patients were receiving antihypertensive medications and 15 patients were receiving statin therapy. These treatments were continued during the postoperative follow‐up period without major modifications. Because patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome were excluded, no participants were receiving GLP‐1 receptor agonists, insulin‐sensitizing agents, or other glucose‐lowering medications.

Surgical Technique

All participants underwent laparoscopic OAGB surgery performed by the same team of experienced surgeons (F.K and M.T), as previously described [24]. Briefly, the procedure began with the dissection of the esophagogastric angle and opening the left gastrophrenic ligament to expose the diaphragmatic crus. A narrow gastric pouch (15–18 cm length, 50–150 mL capacity) was created using sequential stapling. At 200 cm distal to the Treitz ligament, an antecolic, isoperistaltic omega‐loop gastrojejunostomy (25 mm) was constructed. Staple lines were reinforced with continuous sutures, and the Petersen's defect was closed.

Postoperative Diet

Dietary progression followed a structured protocol. During weeks 1–3 post‐surgery, patients consumed only clear liquids and easily digestible fluids (clear broths, apple juice). From weeks 3–8, patients followed a regimen of multiple small meals (approximately matchbox‐sized) at regular intervals, emphasizing protein‐rich, easily digestible foods. Certain carbohydrate‐rich foods such as rice, potato, and bread were restricted for up to 1 year postoperatively.

Anthropometric and Biochemical Assessments

Anthropometric and biochemical parameters were evaluated at baseline (preoperative) and 4‐month follow‐up. Anthropometric measurements included body weight (measured to the nearest kilogram with patients in light clothing without shoes) and height (measured to the nearest centimeter) for BMI (kg/m2) calculation. Following overnight fasting, blood samples were collected to assess fasting blood sugar (FBS), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), serum insulin levels, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol, triglycerides (TG), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), and serum C‐reactive protein (CRP). Insulin resistance was estimated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR), calculated as follows: [fasting glucose (mg/dL) × fasting insulin (μU/mL)]/405 [25]. The FIB‐4 index is a non‐invasive marker used to assess liver fibrosis, calculated using the following formula: (Age [years] × AST [U/L])/(Platelet Count [×109/L] ×√ ALT [U/L]).

Ultrasonographical Evaluations

Ultrasonographic assessments were performed twice: at baseline during preoperative evaluation and at the 4‐month postoperative follow‐up. All examinations were conducted by the same radiologist (N.S.M), who has 10 years of experience in abdominal ultrasonography, at Tehran Heart Centre hospital, using a LOGIQ E9 (General Electric HealthCare, Germany) ultrasound machine after overnight fasting. Parameters evaluated included visceral fat thickness (VFT), abdominal subcutaneous fat thickness (ASCFT), liver stiffness via shear wave elastography (SWE), and hepatic steatosis grading.

VFT was measured using three methods: (1) distance between the internal face of the rectus abdominis muscle sheath and anterior border of liver segment 2 capsule, and (2,3) distance between the internal face of rectus abdominis and the anterior and posterior borders of the abdominal aorta proximal to its bifurcation. ASCFT was quantified as the maximum distance between the skin and the external face of the rectus abdominis muscle sheath, obtained 1–2 cm superior to the umbilicus in the abdominal midline.

SWE was performed via intercostal approach on the right hepatic lobe (segments V, VII, and VIII). A minimum of five valid acquisitions were obtained per patient, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The minimum required sample size was determined using the following formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, 80% statistical power and considering the expected effect size (Cohen's d = 1.13), Based on changes in visceral fat thickness (VFT), which was considered the primary outcome of the previous study by Giannetti et al [27]. To accommodate potential loss to follow‐up in this longitudinal study, the sample size was increased by 20%, yielding a final minimum requirement of 30 participants. Ultimately, 41 patients were included in the study.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired samples t‐test was used for parametric continuous variables and the Wilcoxon test for non‐parametric continuous variables when comparing pre‐ and postoperative measurements. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Post‐hoc power analyses were conducted to assess the statistical power of the study concerning the FIB‐4 and SWE variables. These analyses were performed using G‐Power software (version 3.1.9.4). For the FIB‐4 variable, a t‐test for dependent means was employed, while for the SWE variable, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was utilized.

Results

A total of 45 patients met the inclusion criteria for the study. However, four were excluded after data collection due to missing postoperative data and Comparison of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics revealed no meaningful differences between excluded and included participants. Consequently, 41 patients were evaluated, comprising 6 men (14.6%) and 35 women (85.4%), with a mean age of 39.8 ± 7.7 years. At baseline, Hypertension was present in 14 patients (34%), dyslipidemia in 15 patients (36%), obstructive sleep apnea in 7 patients (17%), and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) in 9 patients (21%) and ultrasonography demonstrated grade 1 steatosis in 11 patients (28.2%), grade 2 steatosis in 15 patients (38.5%), and grade 3 steatosis in 13 patients (33.3%).

Anthropometrical Characteristics

The mean height, body weight, and BMI of patients before surgery were 166.1 ± 10.3 cm, 116.8 ± 22.5 kg, and 42.1 ± 5.8 kg/m2, respectively, classifying them as morbidly obese. Four months postoperatively, both body weight and BMI significantly decreased, with mean body weight reduced to 90.2 ± 18.1 kg (p < 0.001) and mean BMI to 32.6 ± 5.5 kg/m2 (95% CI: 9.5 [8.5 ‐ 10.6], p < 0.001), shifting patients from the morbid obesity category to the obesity range. Biochemical Characteristics

Table 1 presents the preoperative and postoperative biochemical parameters of patients who underwent OAGB surgery. All diabetes‐related biochemical markers, including FBS, HbA1c, serum insulin levels, and HOMA‐IR index showed a significant reduction 4 months postoperatively compared to preoperative values (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). The mean reduction in HOMA‐IR index and serum insulin levels were 71.2% and 65.6%, while FBS and HbA1c decreased by 16.5% and 14.1%, respectively.

Regarding lipid profile changes, total cholesterol, TG, and LDL levels significantly decreased 4 months postoperatively compared to preoperative values (p = 0.05, p < 0.001, and p = 0.009, respectively). In contrast, HDL levels significantly increased following surgery (p < 0.001).

Liver enzyme levels, AST and ALT, were also significantly reduced 4 months postoperatively compared to preoperative levels (p = 0.002 and p = 0.02, respectively), with the decrease in ALT concentration being nearly four times greater than that of AST (21.3% vs. 5.7%). Additionally, serum CRP concentration significantly declined postoperatively, reaching approximately one‐third of its preoperative value (p < 0.001).

A comparison of the FIB‐4 index levels in patients before and after surgery revealed a statistically significant decrease. The mean FIB‐4 level before surgery was 0.76 ± 0.3, which reduced to 0.62 ± 0.2 4 months post‐surgery. This reduction was significant (p = 0.005), with a mean difference of 18.4% (CI95% 0.14 (0.05, 0.24)).

Ultrasonographical Characteristics

Four months postoperatively, VFT was significantly reduced compared to preoperative levels, as assessed by three measurement methods: ALVFT (95% CI: 9.2 [7.9 – 10.3], p < 0.001), AAVFT (95% CI: 32.3 [26.2 – 38.2], p < 0.001), and PAVFT (95% CI: 35.0 [28.9 – 41.1], p < 0.001). The average VFT across these three measurements also showed a significant postoperative reduction (95% CI: 25.5 [21.5 – 29.5], p < 0.001).

Similarly, mean ASCFT significantly decreased following surgery (95% CI: 8.6 [6.6 – 10.6], p < 0.001). Additionally, SWE measurements demonstrated a significant reduction postoperatively compared to preoperative values (95% CI: 0.9 [0.6 – 1.1], p < 0.001). Hepatic Steatosis Evaluation

Before surgery, more than 95% of patients exhibited some degree of hepatic steatosis, with 11 patients (26.8%) classified as grade 1, 15 patients (36.6%) as grade 2, and 13 patients (31.7%) as grade 3. However, 4 months postoperatively, 70.7% of patients showed no signs of hepatic steatosis (65.8% increase compared to pre‐surgery), while 11 patients remained at grade 1, 1 patient at grade 2, and no patients were classified as grade 3. For changes in liver stiffness (Δ SWE), uni‐variable analysis demonstrated that baseline BMI and reduction in visceral fat thickness (Δ Mean VFT) were associated with improvement in liver stiffness (β = 0.268, p = 0.09 and β = 0.390, p = 0.01, respectively). However, in the multivariable model, only reduction in VFT remained an independent predictor of liver stiffness improvement (β = 0.368, 95% CI: 0.005–0.041, p = 0.02), whereas the association with baseline BMI was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.1).

Regarding changes in hepatic steatosis grade (Δ hepatic steatosis Grade), gender (β = 0.209, p = 0.2), baseline BMI (β = 0.235, p = 0.1), and reduction in VFT (β = 0.450, p = 0.003) met the predefined criteria for inclusion in the multivariable model. In the adjusted analysis, only reduction in VFT remained independently associated with improvement in hepatic steatosis grade (β = 0.421, 95% CI: 0.007–0.042, p = 0.009), while gender (p = 0.7) and baseline BMI (p = 0.2) were not independently associated with steatosis improvement.

Overall, these findings suggest that reduction in VFT was the strongest independent factor associated with improvements in liver‐related outcomes following OAGB surgery, and its effect remained significant after adjustment for other clinical variables.

Postoperative Safety Outcomes

During the 4‐month follow‐up period, no mortality or hospital readmissions were observed. One patient developed splenic infarction and required reoperation with splenectomy. No other major postoperative complications were recorded.

Sample Power Analysis

In this study, a post‐hoc power analysis was conducted using G‐Power software (version 3.1.9.4) to evaluate the statistical power of the findings.

The effect size was calculated based on the mean and standard deviation of the FIB‐4 variable before and 4 months after the surgical procedure (preoperative: 0.76 ± 0.3, postoperative: 0.62 ± 0.2), assuming a correlation = 0.373. The resulting effect size was estimated at “0.4641”. Considering a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a sample size of 41 patients, the “critical T‐value” was 1.681, with DF = 40. The computed power of the test was: Power (1 – β err prob) = 0.899. Since the power value exceeded 0.8, it indicates that the sample size of 41 participants provided sufficient statistical power for this study.

A complementary Post‐hoc power analysis was also performed for the SWE variable using the same software and configuration demonstrating statistical power exceeding 80%, suggesting that the sample size was adequate for detecting the observed changes.

Discussion

This prospective longitudinal study evaluated early changes in metabolic risk factors and liver‐related characteristics following OAGB surgery in obese patients without cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome. The results demonstrated significant weight loss and reductions in BMI and visceral fat 4 months postoperatively, accompanied by improvements in metabolic parameters, including glycemic indices and lipid profiles. Additionally, liver enzyme levels significantly decreased during the follow‐up period, aligning with notable improvements in hepatic steatosis grading and liver elastography findings. These findings suggest that OAGB may be associated with favorable hepatic and metabolic changes even in obese patients who have not yet developed overt diabetes or metabolic syndrome. However, because of the study design and absence of a control group, further studies are required before conclusions can be drawn regarding the preventive role of OAGB in the development of future obesity‐related disorders such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.

The substantial reduction in ultrasonographic evidence of hepatic steatosis 4 months after OAGB highlights the procedure's potential beneficial effects on NAFLD‐related liver abnormalities. Notably, 65.8% of patients demonstrated disappearance of ultrasonographic findings consistent with hepatic steatosis, while the remaining individuals exhibited only mild (grade 1) or moderate (grade 2) steatosis. All patients with severe (grade 3) hepatic steatosis preoperatively showed significant improvement, with none remaining in this category postoperatively. A recent study assessing NAFLD modification following laparoscopic SG reported complete resolution in approximately 40% of patients 6 months postoperatively, with the remainder exhibiting grade 1 or 2 steatosis [2]. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta‐analysis indicated significant improvements in the NAFLD activity score among morbidly obese patients following SG and RYGB [21]. These findings suggest that the hepatic benefits of OAGB are comparable to those observed with more established bariatric procedures, such as RYGB and SG.

Rapid weight loss can lead to a significant reduction in hepatic fat; however, it may also increase visceral free fatty acid levels and inflammatory cytokines, potentially facilitating the progression of NAFLD to fibrosis [28]. In the present study, liver stiffness, as measured by SWE with ultrasound and FIB‐4 index calculated from biochemical parameters, significantly decreased 4 months after OAGB surgery. Also, the number of patients with SWE values exceeding 7 kPa decreased from four at baseline to one after surgery, suggesting that the observed reduction in liver stiffness may be clinically relevant in addition to being statistically significant. This finding aligns with a study by Nickel et al., which reported a significant reduction in liver stiffness following RYGB and SG at a median follow‐up of 12 months, as assessed by transient elastography [29]. These results suggest that, similar to other bariatric procedures, OAGB contributes to reduce liver stiffness even in the short term and in the absence of obesity‐related comorbidities.

The significant reductions in liver enzyme levels, including AST and ALT, further support the ultrasonographic findings, indicating reduced hepatocellular injury following OAGB surgery. Notably, the more pronounced decrease in ALT (21.3%) compared to AST (5.7%) underscores ALT's greater specificity for hepatic damage. Similar patterns of enzyme reduction have been reported in previous studies following both short‐ and long‐term follow‐up after RYGB and SG [2, 12, 22, 30]. These biochemical improvements, along with the observed decrease in liver stiffness, suggest that OAGB not only reduces hepatic steatosis but may also alleviate inflammation and potentially slow the progression of early fibrosis.

The metabolic benefits following OAGB were substantial. The marked improvements in glycemic parameters, including FBS, HbA1c, insulin levels, and HOMA‐IR index indicate enhanced insulin sensitivity—a key factor in NAFLD pathogenesis [31]. The observed 71.2% reduction in HOMA‐IR index and 65.6% reduction in insulin levels are particularly notable, suggesting a significant reversal of insulin resistance, a primary driver of hepatic fat accumulation [32]. Although none of the included patients had type 2 diabetes, their preoperative HOMA‐IR index was elevated (5.9 ± 3.2), consistent with severe insulin resistance based on the normal range of 0.63–2.68 reported by Tohidi et al. [33]. Four months after surgery, the HOMA‐IR index decreased to 1.7 ± 0.9, within the normal range. These metabolic improvements suggest that OAGB was associated with significant improvements in glycemic indices, including FBS, HbA1c, insulin levels, and HOMA‑IR.

Improvements in lipid profile, characterized by decreased total cholesterol, TG, and LDL levels, alongside increased HDL, also reflect significant metabolic enhancements that likely contribute to improvement in ultrasonographic hepatic steatosis [1]. These findings, consistent with previous studies [2, 30], indicate that OAGB was associated with significant weight loss and improvements in metabolic parameters in morbidly obese patients. However, given the single‐arm design and absence of a control group, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

The substantial reduction in CRP levels to approximately one‐third of preoperative values suggests a marked decrease in systemic inflammation. This finding is particularly relevant given the role of inflammatory processes in the progression of NAFLD from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis and fibrosis [34]. The observed reduction in inflammation may, in part, be attributed to the significant decrease in VFT across all measurement methods. Visceral adipose tissue is metabolically active and secretes pro‐inflammatory cytokines that contribute to insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis [35]. The concurrent reduction in subcutaneous fat thickness further reflects an overall improvement in adiposity.

The significant decrease in BMI from 42.1 to 32.6 kg/m2 corresponds to a mean excess weight loss of approximately 45%, surpassing the typical weight reduction achieved through lifestyle modifications alone [36, 37]. This substantiates the superior efficacy of bariatric surgery such as OAGB for weight management in morbidly obese individuals and its consequent benefits for obesity‐related comorbidities including NAFLD.

The magnitude and rapidity of these improvements, occurring within just 4 months postoperatively despite patients remaining in the obese category (mean BMI 32.6 kg/m2), suggest that weight‐independent mechanisms may contribute to these improvements; however, this hypothesis requires confirmation in future mechanistic studies. Similar observations have been reported following other bariatric procedures and may involve alterations in gut hormones, bile acid metabolism, and the gut microbiome [2, 38, 39]. The OAGB procedure, which combines restrictive and malabsorptive elements, may induce unique hormonal and metabolic changes that warrant further investigation.

Strengths and Limitations

Compared to other bariatric procedures, OAGB offers several potential advantages, including technical simplicity, shorter operative time, and potentially fewer complications [24]. Our findings suggest that these advantages do not appear to the expense of metabolic efficacy, as the improvements observed were broadly consistent with those reported with more established procedures such as RYGB. Major strengths of the present study include its prospective design, comprehensive metabolic and ultrasonographic assessment, and the simultaneous evaluation of hepatic steatosis, liver stiffness, visceral fat thickness, and metabolic parameters following OAGB. These features enabled a multidimensional assessment of early postoperative changes and provided additional insight into the relationship between visceral fat reduction and improvements in liver‐related outcomes.

While our study demonstrates promising early results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, given the chronic nature of NAFLD, the 4‐month follow‐up period only allows assessment of early postoperative changes. Consequently, the present study cannot determine the long‐term durability of weight loss, persistence of metabolic improvements, histological resolution of NAFLD, or long‐term fibrosis progression/regression. Future studies with longer follow‐up durations and, when feasible, histopathological confirmations are needed to clarify the long‐term hepatic effects of OAGB. Second, our cohort had a predominance of female patients (85.4%); therefore, may have influenced the observed magnitude of metabolic and hepatic improvements following OAGB. Consequently, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to male patients, and future studies with larger and more balanced sex distributions are needed to validate our results across different populations. Third, an important limitation of the present study is the absence of a control group. As a single‑arm prospective study, our findings are limited to within‑patient comparisons before and after surgery. Therefore, the observed improvements cannot be attributed solely to OAGB, as postoperative dietary restrictions, lifestyle modifications, regression to the mean, or natural temporal variation may also have contributed to these changes. Controlled studies with longer follow‑up periods are required to confirm these findings. Fourth, although excluding patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and established metabolic syndrome reduced potential metabolic confounding and allowed evaluation of OAGB‐associated changes in a relatively homogeneous population, this approach may limit the generalizability of the findings. Because many bariatric surgery candidates have diabetes, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, our cohort may not fully represent the broader clinical population undergoing bariatric surgery. Fifth, although the baseline characteristics of patients lost to follow‐up were comparable to those of the analyzed cohort, exclusion of these individuals may still have introduced some degree of selection bias. Sixth, another limitation of this study is that nutritional outcomes and postoperative adverse events were not systematically assessed. Therefore, although favorable metabolic and hepatic changes were observed, the present study does not provide a comprehensive evaluation of the overall risk–benefit profile of OAGB.

Conclusion

In conclusion, OAGB surgery was associated with significant early improvements in obesity‑related metabolic parameters and NAFLD among morbidly obese Iranian patients within 4 months after surgery. The high rate of disappearance of ultrasonographic evidence of hepatic steatosis (65.8%) within just 4 months, alongside substantial improvements in metabolic parameters and ultrasonographical characteristics of liver and abdominal fats, supports this association. However, due to the single‑arm design and the absence of a control group, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further controlled studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‑up are needed to clarify the causal effects of OAGB on metabolic outcomes.
