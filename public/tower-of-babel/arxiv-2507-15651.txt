Data augmentation enables label-specific generation of homologous protein sequences Preprint: APS/123-QED
Lorenzo Rosset, Martin Weigt, Francesco Zamponi

Abstract
Accurately annotating and controlling protein function from sequence data remains a major challenge, particularly within homologous families where annotated sequences are scarce and structural variation is minimal. We present a two-stage approach for semi-supervised functional annotation and conditional sequence generation in protein families using representation learning. First, we demonstrate that protein language models, pretrained on large and diverse sequence datasets and possibly finetuned via contrastive learning, provide embeddings that robustly capture fine-grained functional specificities, even with limited labeled data. Second, we use the inferred annotations to train a generative probabilistic model, an annotation-aware Restricted Boltzmann Machine, capable of producing synthetic sequences with prescribed functional labels. Across several protein families, we show that this approach achieves highly accurate annotation quality and supports the generation of functionally coherent sequences. Our findings underscore the power of combining self-supervised learning with light supervision to overcome data scarcity in protein function prediction and design.



I
Introduction

The sequence-structure-function paradigm in biology posits that a protein’s amino acid sequence determines its structure, which in turn underlies its function. While AlphaFold
Jumper et al. 2021
has largely resolved the sequence-to-
structure
problem, predicting protein
function
remains far more complex. In specific settings like protein-protein interactions, structure-based docking
Dequeker et al. 2022
or sequence-based machine learning methods can infer binding partners and sites
Cong et al. 2019
;
Evans et al. 2021
;
Grassmann et al. 2024
, including the case of interaction specificity
Gueudré et al. 2016
;
Bitbol et al. 2016
;
Meynard-Piganeau et al. 2023
. However, when a function is less directly reflected in structure, as is often the case for enzymes, finding the sequence determinants of protein function remains a major challenge.

In the case of abundant functionally annotated data, supervised or semi-supervised learning with deep protein language model (pLMs) embeddings has proven effective to predict functions such as enzyme commission (EC) numbers
Yu et al. 2023
, catalytic activity
Eom et al. 2025
, gene ontology (GO) annotations
Kabir and Shehu 2022
;
Sanderson et al. 2023
, or protein families
Vitale et al. 2024
.
However, more generally, protein function lacks a clear-cut mathematical definition, and the dependence on context-specific low-throughput experiments, which are typically time and cost-intensive, makes quantitative data rather rare. In the Uniprot database
uni 2025
, out of more than 250 million deposited protein sequences, only about 0.2% contain human annotations, many of which are of structural rather than of functional nature.
Hence, precise functional annotations are, in most cases, scarce; typically, one might expect to have of the order of 100 such annotations in a protein family that might contain
10^{4}-10^{6}
homologous sequences.

To make the most of available data, the aim of this work is to develop methods that link protein sequences (genotypes) to functional phenotypes with minimal supervision.
We focus on a common yet challenging scenario, which manifests in two subsequent tasks:

•

predicting fine-grained specificities within a homologous protein family using only few labeled sequences;

•

using these predictions for the generation of artificial homologs conditioned on a given specificity.

The ability to generate such functionally specific artificial protein sequences is of great interest for protein design, potentially in combination with subsequent refinement by directed evolution
Romero and Arnold 2009
.

However, the first point already presents three major difficulties, which directly impact our ability to succeed in the second. First, the limited number of labeled sequences makes it difficult for traditional supervised methods to generalize in the high-dimensional space of homologous proteins, whose size is typically exponential in the protein length
Trinquier et al. 2021
. Second, because homologous protein sequences share a highly similar three-dimensional structure, predicted folding offers limited additional information.
Third, homologous protein families show complex phylogenetic correlations between sequences that, for example, make it difficult to split the dataset into meaningful test and training subsets
Petti and Eddy 2022
, or to dissect functional similarity from evolutionary relatedness
Vicedomini et al. 2022
.
In this setting, the specificities we aim to infer are subtle, fine-grained features – unlike the broader functional categories of, e.g., GO annotations. As a result, we face a weak labeling signal, phylogenetically correlated sequences, and hardly defined functional distinctions.

Our strategy is to seek sequence embeddings that reflect the underlying functional landscape. A straightforward option, which goes beyond the trivial but widely used one-hot encoding, is to exploit the multiple-sequence alignment (MSA) of a protein family (
\sim 10^{3}
–
10^{6}
sequences) to train a latent-variable model such as a Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM), yielding unsupervised representations that may correlate better with specificity labels
Tubiana et al. 2019
;
Shimagaki and Weigt 2019
;
Decelle et al. 2023
.
A second and, as we will show, more powerful option consists of exploiting the latent representations learned by a pLM encoder, pre-trained across hundreds of millions of proteins belonging to many diverse families. Intuitively, a model that has already learned the “grammar” of the protein language might encode general sequence features and may transfer well to narrower functional tasks, even if these are not explicitly present in the training data.
Whether or not such pre-trained pLMs can help recognize sub-functions in protein families is not a trivial question since those models are trained on the whole proteome, and specific details at the family level might be overlooked.
In an attempt to improve the pLM sequence representation, we therefore fine-tune them using a contrastive learning objective
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2023
, aligning the embedding space with functional labels by drawing together functionally similar sequences and pushing apart dissimilar ones, cf.
Materials and Methods
Sec.
IV.5
for the details. These refined embeddings can then support simple classification models like logistic regression. We chose ProtBERT
Elnaggar et al. 2021
as a representative pLM since its moderate number of parameters (100 M) permits to carry out computations on a single GPU node, but we found similar results when considering ESM2 (150 M) as a backbone model (SI, Sec.
S4
).

We validate our approach on several protein families. The Response Regulator (RR) family (Pfam PF00072
Paysan-Lafosse et al. 2025
), involved in bacterial two-component signaling systems, serves as our main testbed, with
\sim 8\cdot 10^{5}
sequences and 8 specificity classes defined by the proteins’ domain architecture. To test broader applicability, we also consider SH3 domains (PF00018, with orthology group labels
Lian et al. 2024
), the Globin family (PF00042, functional labels from UniProt
Ziegler et al. 2023
), and Chorismate Mutase (CM, PF01817) enzymes, where experimentally tested
in vivo
functionality in
E. coli
defines the labels
Kast et al. 1996
;
Russ et al. 2020
.
Across these different families and labeling schemes, we show that embedding the sequences with pLMs results in more robust label predictions, which is our first major result.

As mentioned before, enriching functional labeling of protein families is not just interesting per se, but can be used to build probabilistic models – here label-aware RBM – for the conditional generation of artificial homologs with given labels. Besides evident biotechnological applications in protein engineering and design, such functionally resolved generative probabilistic models can be used to explore specificity-changing evolutionary trajectories
Frechette and Best 2021
;
Mauri et al. 2023
, and to analyze the role of epistasis in protein-functional evolution
Di Bari et al. 2024
.
Therefore, our second major result is to show that, with sufficient high-quality labels inferred with the aforementioned strategy, generative models can identify residue motifs linked to specificities and design sequences with targeted functions.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Sec.
II.1
, we define the general pipeline and specify the used data embeddings and the definition of the training and test sets, which underlie the entire work. In Sec.
II.2

We compare the label prediction performance of a simple logistic regression model trained on the embeddings of an RBM and a pLM (with and without contrastive fine-tuning) against the predictions obtained on the raw one-hot encoding of the family’s MSA. Finally, in Sec.
II.3
we imagine that we have extended the annotations to unlabeled sequences using the methods above and we study how an RBM model responds to the accuracy and number of annotations provided during the training for the task of generating protein sequences subject to having a chosen specificity.

II
Results

II.1
Pipeline

We consider as input data a set of homologous protein sequences that have been previously organized into either a union of full-length sequences (for pLM embedding) or an MSA (for one-hot encoding and RBM embedding). In the latter case, each sequence is represented as a string of 21 symbols (20 amino acids and one alignment gap), and all sequences have the same length. A typical data set will contain
\sim 10^{4}-10^{6}
sequences, and we assume that a much smaller subset of
\sim 10^{2}
sequences have been annotated for some specific functionality. Annotations are labels assigned to sequences; here we consider categorical labels that can belong to a fixed number of functionality classes, cf.
Methods and Materials
Sec.
IV.1
for more details on the considered families and annotation schemes. As stated in the introduction, the goals of our pipeline are to (i) annotate all the sequences in the input dataset (
data augmentation
), and (ii) generate new, artificial sequences that belong to each of the functionality classes (
label-specific generation
).

Our data augmentation and label-specific generation pipeline (Fig.
1
) is implemented and tested as follows.
We consider a dataset where all sequences are labeled, and we hide the labels of most sequences in the dataset to use them as a test set,
using a proper train/test splitting (Fig.
1
a) to be discussed below. Then, we first train several ‘predictor’ models, based on a logistic regression applied to a given embedding of the data (Fig.
1
b).
Second, we use the predictor to label the entire protein family and we train a ‘generator’ model on the augmented data (Fig.
1
c). We use a simple RBM architecture as a benchmark for the generator.
We study how this data augmentation affects the ability of the RBM to generate sequences conditioned on a required specificity. In particular,
to assess the self-consistency of the pipeline,
sequences generated with a given specificity prescription are re-labeled using the predictor, and we check whether the predicted label is consistent with the one used for generation.

We compare the performance of several embedding schemes (Fig.
1
d) for the predictor, starting from simple one-hot encoded sequences and going to pLM embeddings:

•

MSA
: In the simplest case, we use the one-hot encoding of the MSA as a dummy latent representation that serves as a baseline.

•

: We train an embedding-RBM

•

ProtBERT foundation

•

Note that the first three data representations do not make use of any annotation (unsupervised / zero-shot), whereas contrastive learning relies on label supervision (few-shot), and the results obtained with this type of embedding will thus depend on the training set size. In the following, the training set for the contrastive learning will always be the same used for the training of the predictor (i.e., in practical applications, all annotated sequences). Our main finding is that the ProtBERT foundation embedding already provides an efficient representation of the data. It has the best performance on essentially all datasets we consider, while avoiding the use of any annotation.

Special care should be taken in performing the train/test splitting on the initial data. In fact, homologous protein families are the result of natural evolution, thus they display phylogenetic correlations, i.e. sequences from evolutionarily related species are very similar to each other. Because of that, knowing the label of a given sequence would easily allow one to label the phylogenetically close ones, which can skew the metrics used in assessing the performances of the predictor (see the
Materials and Methods
Sec.
IV.2
for a more detailed discussion).
We then adopt the
cobalt
train/test splitting method, specifically designed in
Petti and Eddy 2022
to address this issue. The method selects test sequences that are distant from the training set and from each other (Fig.
1
a), by imposing a maximum allowed fractional sequence identity
t1
between training and test sequences, and a maximum allowed sequence identity
t2
between test sequences. The training sets of RR is constructed so that each specificity class has the same number of samples in order to get an unbiased measure of the classification quality. For the other datasets, we allowed for a moderate class imbalance to maintain a good number of sequences in the dataset (cf. Figs.
S7
,
S8
and
S9

To better understand why predictions based on the raw MSA are so unsatisfactory compared to those based on pLM embeddings, we studied the distribution of distances from the training set for all sequences with false positive annotations, yielded by the MSA and ProtBert Foundation embeddings. We chose the Foundation representation to show that the embedding properties do not depend on the model’s fine-tuning, but very similar results are obtained when considering the Contrastive representation. In particular, we selected only the wrong predictions for which the classifier gave a high probability to the predicted label (

As shown in Fig.

This indicates that MSA tends to get fooled when two sequences of distinct specificities are relatively similar in terms of sequence identity. II.3
Label-specific generation

Once we have a predictor capable of annotating all sequences in the input dataset, our goal is to investigate how the accuracy of these annotations influences a generative model designed to produce artificial sequences with a desired specificity.
To this end, we consider a modification of the RBM that integrates the label information together with the aligned sequence

After training, we can ask the model to generate artificial sequences conditioned on a chosen specificity and compare the statistical properties of the generated samples with those of the natural ones.

On the other hand, the generator might fail to generate a sequence conditioned on the given label. III
Discussion

The aim of this work was twofold: first, we wanted to develop a pipeline to extend functional annotations to unlabeled sequences, when the label information is scarce and the sequences belong to the same homologous protein family (i.e. functional disctinctions are subtle and not simply encoded in sequence similarity or overall sequence conservation patterns). Second, we wanted to exploit these predicted annotations to augment data for training generative probabilistic models, which enable the generation of synthetic sequences conditioned to any given functional label.

To advance in the first point, we needed to get rid of correlations due to phylogeny and sampling biases in the data. We therefore constructed training sets at small and large distances from the test set and made the test set more uniform by removing sequences that were too close together. This construction allowed us to systematically analyze diverse protein sequence representations. We observed that protein embeddings based on latent variable models integrating unsupervised information are generally better than the naive MSA one-hot representation for the data annotation task. In particular, we found that pLM-based embeddings – pre-trained on diverse sequence datasets and not using any functional annotations – generalize very well, even in the least favorable case with a few labeled training data, and tests perfomed on distant sequences having less than 40% sequence identity with any training sequence. We confirmed our results on three additional protein families (
Materials and Methods
Sec.
IV.1
) other than RR, also considering the ESM2 pLM embeddings for the analysis (SI, Sec.
S4
). Moreover, these embeddings allowed for performances which were much less sensitive to data scarcity than one-hot encoded MSA or RBM-based representations. They are far less confused by sequence similarity, consequently generalizing better to distant sequences. Fine-tuning the pLM embeddings on the training data using contrastive learning seams to improve performances in some cases, but the gain over the zero-shot embedding is quite small due to the limited number of training sequences.
We point out that pLMs are trained using a very broad and diverse library of protein sequences, so the fact that they are able to facilitate recognition of fine-grain family-specific features is quite remarkable.

An apparently similar procedure was used in
Eom et al. 2025
for the annotation problem, with the aim of proposing natural homologs with enhanced catalytic efficiency as compared to a given wildtype. However, in contrast to our work aiming at generalizability even to rather distant homologs, the training and test sequences of
Eom et al. 2025
have a high average pairwise sequence identity (
\simeq 93\%
). Interestingly, the authors find nonetheless that some of the proposed top-ranking homologs show low sequence identity with the training/test set, corroborating our finding that predictions based on pLM embeddings are only weakly affected by sequence similarity.

To address our second objective, we studied how the different methods for data augmentation affect the downstream task of generating new sequences conditioned on a chosen specificity label. To this end, we trained generative models (we chose an RBM with an additional layer taking into account the labels, but in principle other architectures can be used) with label supervision when (a) all true labels are provided, (b) just a few true labels are given, (c) labels are extended using the various predictors. In particular, we focused on a very hard scenario with only 100 training sequences distributed over 8 functional labels, with sequences having at most 40% sequence identity with the test set. We showed that the generative model trained with labels predicted from the pLM embedding is capable of generating sequences conditioned on a chosen specificity at a quality level that approaches the best-case scenario (a), in which the true labels are used. In contrast, informing the model using the predictor trained on the MSA yields pretty poor results. Moreover, we checked that the label prediction on generated sequences has a good level of consistency with the assignment when pLM-based data augmentation is implemented in the pipeline.

In summary, we found that latent embeddings of protein sequences, especially foundational pLMs embeddings, encode unsupervised information about proteins in a way that family-related specificities become more easily recognizable,
and that they provide much more robust representations for downstream classification tasks when the number of training sequences is small. In particular, they proved much more resilient to errors in the classification that are induced by sequence similarity. These findings are particularly remarkable when considering that pLMs are generally trained on very broad datasets of divergent sequences, meaning that no family-specific or homology related information is explicitly given to the model. By combining this pipeline for extending annotations with a simple label-informed generative model, we demonstrated how it is possible to start with a family of unlabeled homologous sequences (
\geq 10^{4}
) and a handful of annotations (
\sim 10^{2}
) and obtain a sequence generator that also respects the desired specificity.

We believe that this pipeline can be extremely useful in particular in connection with current experimental techniques enabling the efficient synthesis and functional assaying of up to
\sim 10^{2}
proteins, while experimental protocols assessing thousands of homologous proteins or more still remain extremely challenging and of uncertain outcome. In this context, our approach can therefore efficiently be combined with experiments to finally generate artificial proteins such as enzymes of desired specificity, which can be in turn tested and optimized experimentally at will. Furthermore, we believe that the label-specific generative model can be used to obtain information on (natural or
in vitro
) evolutionary dynamics, such as inferring the specificity of ancestral sequences to characterized functionality switches that occurred during evolution. Thereby, it can be used to shed light on the evolutionary emergence of new protein functions, a basic question in protein biology.

IV
Materials and Methods

IV.1
Datasets: protein families and functional annotations

IV.1.1
Response Regulators

The Response Regulator Receiver Domain (RR, Pfam accession: PF00072) is among the largest protein families, encompassing over 2 million sequences. Most RR are part of a two-component signal transduction system, where they are activated by a sensor histidine kinase following an environmental stimulus, subsequently triggering a transcriptional response as transcription factors. Specific functions of proteins containing RR domains can be deduced from their Pfam domain architecture, in particular via the presence of different families of DNA binding, sigma factor or histine kinase domains. Here we present only the RR domain, which is common to all these proteins, labeled by the other functional domains. For our study, we selected 855,395 RR domain sequences from 8 abundant domain architectures and aligned them into a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of length
L=112
residues. The abundance of annotated sequences allowed us to generate training and test sets with a maximum of 40% sequence identity, and training set sizes of 100, 500, 1,000, and 2,000 sequences, each balanced across label classes. The test set sizes for
t1
=0.4 and
t1
=0.7 are, respectively, 25,408 and 14,728.

IV.1.2
SH3 domain

Src-homology 3 domains (SH3, Pfam accession: PF00018)
Musacchio et al. 1992
are small protein interaction modules commonly found in a wide range of intracellular and membrane-associated proteins. They play key roles in various cellular processes, including promoting local protein concentration, altering subcellular localization, and facilitating the assembly of large multiprotein complexes. Following the dataset from
Lian et al. 2024
, we compiled 7,861 SH3 domain sequences along with their corresponding multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of length
L=62
. Each sequence is annotated by orthology group, spanning 13 distinct classes. To evaluate model performance, we created training and test sets with sequence similarities capped at 70%, and training sizes of 100, 500, and 1,000 sequences. The size of the test set is fixed to 532. The class distribution across these splits is shown in Fig.
S7
.

IV.1.3

=0.7
, we generated a train/test split consisting of 1,394 training sequences and 403 test sequences across 15 specificity classes (see Fig.
S8
).

IV.2
Train/test splitting

of homologous protein families

Working with homologous protein families poses the problem of finding a sensible splitting of the data in a training and a test set. Because phylogenetic relationships introduce correlations into the data,
any naive random splitting of the dataset yields test sequences which are phylogenetically close to training sequences. Consequently, the model scores similarly on the training and the test set, thereby invalidating any estimate of the model’s generalization capabilities. On top of this, homologous sequences tend to be distributed unevenly across sequence space, resulting in clusters with high sequence identity. This is problematic when we want to assess the model on a test set because some regions of sequence space are overrepresented,
preventing us from getting a global, unbiased measure of the model’s performance.

To address this problem, we use the
cobalt
algorithm introduced in
Petti and Eddy 2022
. The method consists of two stages. In the first stage, a training and a test set are extracted from the initial database so that no sequence in the test set has more than
t1
fractional sequence identity with any other sequence in the training set. This allows us to reduce correlations between the two sets of sequences. In the second stage, the test set is pruned until the fractional pairwise sequence identity between any pair of test sequences does not exceed
t2
. This second part of the algorithm allows us to obtain a homogeneous test set that is not skewed towards any sequence. In our experiments, we considered
t1

\in\{0.4,0.7\}
and
t2
=0.7
.

More precisely, we perform a
cobalt
splitting independently for each annotation class.
For each dataset that we tested, we followed the following train/test splitting pipeline.

•

We split the annotated sequences into several files, one for each annotation class.

•

For each file, we obtain a train/test splitting using
cobalt
with specific values for
t1
and
t2
.

•

We merge the obtained train and test splittings and we randomly extract a subset of the training sequences to be used for fine-tuning the pLM and training the logistic regression.

Note that by first partitioning the sequences into sets of common labeling and then applying
cobalt
to each label, we are not guaranteed that no test sequence is closer than
t1
to the training set in the final split. In fact, our procedure allows sequences with
different
labels to be closer than
t1
. This is, however, a stricter way of testing the pipeline, because a model might mistakenly infer the same label for sequences of different specificities, because of being fooled by their sequence similarity.

IV.3
Embedding Restricted Boltzmann Machine

The RBM is the simplest generative model implementing latent variables
Smolensky 1986
;
Hinton 2002
. In the RBM, the physical (also called
visible
) variables
\boldsymbol{a}
are connected to the latent (or
hidden
) variables
\boldsymbol{x}
on a bipartite graph. When dealing with biological sequences, visible units are vectors of categorical (or Potts) variables representing the aligned sequences
\boldsymbol{a}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{L}\}
with
a_{i}\in\{1,\dots,q\}
where
L
is the size of the alignment and
q=21
is the total number of symbols (20 amino acids plus the alignment gap). In our implementation, the latent variables are binary vectors
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{P}
with
P
being the chosen number of hidden units. The joint probability distribution of visible and hidden variables is a Boltzmann measure

p(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x})}\ ,

(2)

where
Z
is a normalization constant and
\mathcal{H}
is the statistical energy given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x})=&-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\theta_{i}(a_{i})-\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\eta_{\mu}x_{\mu}\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}W_{i\mu}(a_{i})x_{\mu}\ .\end{split}

(3)

In this expression,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are local biases, while
W
is a rank-3 tensor that couples visible and latent variables. The role of visible biases is to model the conservation signal in the MSA, while the hidden bias and the coupling matrix undirectly encode the higher-order epistatic signal between residues
Decelle et al. 2025
.
The model parameters are tuned to maximize the likelihood of the data, which can be achieved via stochastic gradient ascent. The log-likelihood gradient is estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations, and in particular using the Persistent Contrastive Divergence (PCD) scheme
Tieleman 2008
;
Decelle et al. 2021
.
Importantly, the bipartite structure of the RBM interaction graph allows us to sample all at once, alternatively, the visible and the hidden variables from the conditional probability distributions. Such a sampling scheme is called
block Gibbs sampling
.

For each dataset, we trained the RBM for
10^{5}
updates using 10 block Gibbs sampling steps between two gradient updates (PCD-10). We used a learning rate of 0.01, 2000 Markov chains and a latent dimension of 1024.
After training, we encode train and test sequences through the deterministic map
m_{\mu}=p(x_{\mu}|\boldsymbol{a})
, forming the
P=1024
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},...,m_{P})
representing the latent probability profile of the sequence.
We then feed this vector to a logistic regression model to predict the label.
As we show in SI (Fig.
S6
), the predictive performance of the model increases during the training, then it drops a bit in correspondence with a major phase transition of the model and finally it grows again up to reaching a plateau. For all our predictions, we made sure to be well inside the saturating region.

IV.4
Embedding using protein language models

Despite being appealing for its simplicity, the shallowness of the RBM architecture poses a limitation on the model representation capabilities.
Indeed, it is known that many hidden layers are needed in order to encode high-level hierarchical features of the data.
We thus explore the possibility of leveraging the representation capability of deep pre-trained protein language models to discriminate different specificities in homologous sequences.
We consider ProtBERT
Elnaggar et al. 2021
, which consists of the transformer-based BERT architecture
Devlin et al. 2019
trained using masked language modeling on more than 200 million sequences. Despite being trained on a completely different task than recognizing a protein sequence specificity, the pre-trained model is capable of extracting high-level features from the data to produce a meaningful internal representation that can be used to inform downstream tasks like supervised label prediction. Given an input sequence, we consider its representative embedding as the average over the sequence positions of the model hidden representation (
mean-pooling
) at the last hidden layer. In this way, every sequence is mapped in a 1024-dimensional vector that we then feed to a logistic regression model. In this work, we denote as
foundation
(also called
zero-shot
in the literature) the embedding produced by the pre-trained pLM.

IV.5
Contrastive low-rank fine-tuning of the pLM

In this work we also study how the classification performances get affected when fine-tuning the pLM using a contrastive learning strategy
Zhou et al. 2024
.
In this scheme, sequences that share the same annotation are paired together in
positive pairs
, while sequences of different classes are in
negative pairs
.
The contrastive loss function rewards the model when the embeddings of a positive pair are close together and the embeddings of a negative pair are far apart.
In particular, we adopt a Weighted SimCLR training objective
Chen et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2022
that gives higher importance to the negative pairs that are more similar in the embedding space.

Denoting
a_{i}
the
i
-th anchor data point,
a_{i+}
its positive pair and
f(a_{i})
the embedding of the data point
a_{i}
, the loss for
a_{i}
in a batch of
B
data points is

\ell(f(a_{i}))=-\log\frac{\exp(s(f(a_{i}),f(a_{i+}))/\tau)}{\sum_{j\neq i}^{1,B}\alpha_{ij}\exp(s(f(a_{i}),f(a_{j}))/\tau)},

(4)

where
s(\cdot)
is the cosine similarity function and
\alpha_{ij}
is a weighting matrix defined as

\alpha_{ij}=\frac{\exp(s(f(a_{i}),f(a_{j}))/\tau)}{\frac{1}{2B-2}\sum_{k\neq i,i+}\exp(s(f(a_{i}),f(a_{k}))/\tau)}

(5)

with
\alpha_{ii^{+}}=1
.
The weighted contrastive loss for the entire batch is then

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}(\ell(f(a_{i}))+\ell(f(a_{i+})))\ .

(6)

After fine-tuning, the model should be able to produce a meaningful embedding that better aggregates the data points based on their specificity.

Rather than fine-tuning the model by changing the weights of the entire architecture, it is much more computationally convenient to adopt a low-rank update of a subset of the total number of parameters (LoRA)
Hu et al. 2022
. If
W\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is a weight matrix of the model that we wish to fine-tune, this strategy consists of choosing the parameters update as
W\leftarrow W+\Delta W
where
\Delta W=AB
with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
and
d
is a (small) integer number representing the rank of the update. The advantage is that, in the low-rank update, we need to change only
d\cdot(M+N)
instead of
M\cdot N
parameters, enabling the fine-tuning of small-to-medium size pLMs on a single GPU node. We implemented the contrastive fine-tuning with LoRA using the
peft
Python library
Mangrulkar et al. 2022
. In Sec.
IV.6
we discuss the problem of choosing the hyperparameters in this specific setup, and in the SI Sec.
S2
we report the hyperparameters adopted for each training.

IV.6
The problem of choosing the fine-tuning hyperparameters

In the framework in which we work, we are given a set of phylogeny-related sequences for which we have a very limited number of labels. This particular setup poses severe limitations when we apply the contrastive finetuning on pLMs. In particular:

•

We do not have enough annotated sequences to extract a validation set. We can only evaluate our models based on the training score, at the risk of introducing overfitting in our inference.

•

Since training sequences are correlated by homology, any k-fold cross-validation strategy is uneffective.

•

Because we do not have a validation set, we cannot afford performing a grid-search to find the optimal parameters for a given dataset. We can only try with a reasonable set of hyperparameters and assume that it will perform fairly well across different datasets.

For the aforementioned reasons, we chose a simple and consistent method to fine-tune pLMs with contrastive loss. We chose reasonable values for the main hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, weight decay and LoRA parameters, and we run the training until the training loss did not improve for 5 to 8 log records, depending on the dataset. In SI Sec.
S2
we report the detailed list of hyperparameters we adopted.

IV.7
Generative Restricted Boltzmann Machine

With a simple modification in the RBM architecture, it is possible to integrate the information on the label along with the amino acid sequence
Larochelle and Bengio 2008
;
Carbone et al. 2024
;
Agliari et al. 2024
;
Alemanno et al. 2023
.
We introduce the label variable
\ell\in\{1,\dots,K\}
with
K
being the number of annotation classes. The energy function of the model gains an additional term with respect to Eq. (
3
),

\mathcal{H}^{A}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x},\ell)=\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x})+\mathcal{H}^{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\ell)\ ,

(7)

where

\mathcal{H}^{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\ell)=-\varphi(\ell)-\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}L_{\mu}(\ell)x_{\mu}\ .

(8)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
are the biases acting on the labels and
L
is a weight matrix that couples each label with the hidden layer. In Fig.
4
we report a schematic representation of the model’s architecture.

Figure 4:
Schematic representation of the
label-specific generative RBM.

The annotation-aided RBM can be trained both on fully or partially-annotated datasets. In the latter case, it is sufficient to multiply the label bias and coupling matrix by zeros to account for missing annotations. Once the model has been trained, it is possible to generate new sequences conditioned on a chosen specificity. To achieve this, we clamp the label variable to a given value and we run a Monte Carlo simulation using block Gibbs sampling between hidden and visible layers. Fixing the label has the effect of biasing the latent vector toward a label-specific pattern, which in turn constrains the simulation in the desired region of the protein sequence space.

For all the datasets, we trained the model for
3\cdot 10^{4}
gradient updates using PCD with 100 hidden units, learning rate of
10^{-2}
, 10 block Gibbs sampling steps between updates and 5000 Markov chains running in parallel.
