A Matlab code for analysis and topology optimization with Third Medium Contact
Andreas Henrik Frederiksen, Ole Sigmund, Federico Ferrari

Abstract
We present a Matlab code for modelling and topology optimization of hyperelastic structures, including contact modelled by the Third Medium Contact (TMC) approach. By using the so-called HuHu -regularization we penalize the skew distortion of the bilinear finite elements discretizing void regions, thus promoting convergence of the nonlinear solver. First, we show how this method is implemented in a compact code, allowing to simulate contact and force transfer in hyperelastic structures. Then, we solve a topology optimization problem for minimum end-compliance of a structure exhibiting contact. The Matlab scripts that replicate the results are included, and we discuss some possible extensions to more general problems.



keywords
Third Medium Contact, Topology Optimization, Hyperelasticity, Matlab

1
Introduction

This work provides a user-friendly, open-source
Matlab
implementation of the Third Medium Contact (TMC) model, and of its application to density-based Topology Optimization (TO).

Contact simulation by the TMC method was initiated by
43
and
8
; however, convergence issues due to the extreme deformation of finite elements (FE) discretizing the void regions prevented the method from taking hold. Recently, TMC has been revived in the context of topology optimization (TO) by
6
, thanks to the introduction of a void regularization, later coined “
HuHu
-regularization”, which cured the aforementioned convergence issues.

Since then, the synergistic use of TMC within TO has found increasing applications, including the design of self-contacting structures undergoing large deformations
(
20
)
, metamaterials
(
12
)
, thermal switches
(
11
)
, 3D contacting hooks
(
18
)
, pneumatic actuators for robotics
(
30
)
, springs
(
7
)
, and multi-stable lattices showing snap-through
(
4
)
.

The plethora of applications clearly shows the relevance and versatility of TMC for the design of advanced structures and materials taking advantage of contact response. Contrary to classical contact formulations
(
44
;
13
)
, the TMC approach is fully implicit, avoiding the need for explicit tracking of the contact interfaces, and the associated Lagrange multipliers. This allows its seamless integration in optimization frameworks, since the small but finite stiffness of void regions, represented by the so-called
Third Medium
, provide a differentiable contact response. In particular, TMC is well-suited for use within TO, since it allows the contact interface to form naturally within the design domain, thus preserving full design freedom. The only other fully implicit family of contact formulation is based on phase-field methods
(
28
;
29
)
, but has to date not been tested within TO.

The dissemination of TO in academia and industry greatly benefits from educational codes, which facilitate learning of basic TO concepts
(
31
)
, but also offer an assisted introduction to more advanced methods and applications
(
16
;
21
;
40
)
. The number of educational codes, largely written in
Matlab
and addressed at various physics or applications, is steadily growing and we refer to
35
for a thorough list of contributions.

The recent improvement and revival of TMC, and its application to TO offers a timely opportunity for presenting yet another educational code. The present
Matlab
implementation builds on the framework and methods established in
3
and
15
. Thus, the user is supposed to be familiar with those codes and with the basics of density-based TO
(
5
)
.

The code has been written for compactness, readability, and modularity, rather than aiming at optimal efficiency or general robustness. The implementation has been split into five subroutines, such that the advanced user may adapt or extend each of them to handle more general problems, elements and regularizations
(
41
;
42
)
, or heavier computational tasks. Two main scripts are included, which can be used for replicating the results shown in the paper.

We believe that this can be a relevant contribution to the field, showing the inherent simplicity of implementation and effectiveness of TMC for solving complex topology design problems of hyperelastic structures in contact. To date, this is the only code available covering such advanced task. A formulation for TMC for contact analysis (without optimization) is currently available in the
AceFEM
software
(
26
;
41
)
. The recently published work by
34
introduces a
Matlab
code for solving TO problems with contact. However, it is limited to linear elastic response and uses a classical, explicit contact formulation.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section 2
gives an overview of contact modelling by the TMC method and the
HuHu
-regularization. A popular benchmark example, which can be replicated by the script
cshapeTMC.m
, is shown in
Subsection 2.2
.
Section 3
introduces the density-based TO framework used in the code, and
Subsection 3.1
shows an example involving minimum end-compliance design, taking advantage of contact, which can be replicated by the script
topTMC.m
. Finally, in
Section 4
we discuss possible extensions to the code provided.

The complete
Matlab
code is listed in
Section 5
, where we discuss in detail the implementation, its underlying assumptions and input data, and we give some specific hints on extensions for generality or improved efficiency.

2
Contact modelling by TMC

The key idea in TMC modelling is illustrated in
Figure 1
. The void region surrounding the two solids coming into contact is modelled as a nonlinear material by introducing a
fictitious
Third Medium (TM) featuring: (1) very low,
but finite
stiffness at small deformations; (2) exponentially increasing stiffness as the local volume shrinks to zero (see
Figure 1
(b)).

These two features give the following response, as the solids come into contact:

•

a small
but finite
force is transferred between the two solids even at long range, before contact is engaged;

•

the TM acts as a barrier, preventing the solid bodies from penetrating each other as the contact is established.

These features make the TMC very attractive for contact modelling within TO, avoiding the need for methods tracking the solid/void interface and explicit modelling of force transfer by nonlinear constraints, ultimately making the optimization problem non-differentiable
(
44
;
13
)
. Most importantly, without the TMC the topology optimizer would have no means to discover the advantage of establishing contact within the design domain.

The TM is assigned a neo-Hookean material law
(
23
)
with Strain Energy Density (SED)

\displaystyle W(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\ln(|\boldsymbol{F}|)^{2}+

(1)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mu}{2}\left(\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{F})-3\right)-\mu\ln(|\boldsymbol{F}|)

where
\lambda
,
\mu
are the Lamé parameters, and
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{I}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}
is the deformation gradient.

The first term in (
1
), proportional to
\ln(|\boldsymbol{F}|)^{2}
, accounts for volumetric changes, and is pivotal for providing the TM with the extreme stiffening response depicted in
Figure 1
(b). Clearly, the TM can also be modelled by other hyperelastic material laws accommodating large deformations and showing the same exponential stiffening for
|\boldsymbol{F}|\rightarrow 0
, such as those listed in
25
;
12
.

We point out that by using TMC, a small gap between the solid interfaces still exists, even when full contact is considered (see
Figure 1
(a)). For practical applications, the material parameters of the third medium are chosen such that this small gap cannot be visually observed, and has no practical influence on the systems’ response.

For simplicity, and following
18
, we use the same neo-Hookean material for both the solid and the TM, and the SED (
1
) is scaled by the factor
k_{v}\ll 1
in the TM region, making it multiple orders of magnitude softer compared to the solid.

The nonlinear equilibrium equation follows from the first variation of the total potential energy (TPE)

\displaystyle\delta\Pi(\lambda,\boldsymbol{u};\delta\boldsymbol{u})=\mathcal{R}_{\rm int}(\boldsymbol{u};\delta\boldsymbol{u})-\Lambda\ell(\delta\boldsymbol{u})=

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{s}\cup\Omega_{v}}\delta W(\boldsymbol{u};\delta\boldsymbol{u})\>\mathrm{d}\Omega-\Lambda\ell(\delta\boldsymbol{u})=0\quad\forall\>\delta\boldsymbol{u}

where
\mathcal{R}_{\rm int}
are the internal forces, and proportional loading is assumed. The linear form associated with the surface tractions
\bar{t}
, reads

\ell(\delta\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{\Gamma_{t}}\bar{t}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\>\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{t}

(3)

whereas the variation of the SED (
1
) gives

\delta W(\boldsymbol{u};\delta\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{P}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{F}

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{P}=\lambda\ln(|\boldsymbol{F}|)\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}+\mu(\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{F}^{-T})
is the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.

2.1
HuHu
-regularization

As contact is approached some compressed, heavily distorted regions in the TM jeopardize the stability of
Equation 2
, which is discretized by finite elements (FE). Therefore, some form of stabilization is necessary to achieve convergence of the nonlinear equilibrium solver.

The revival of TMC within TO was indeed driven by a regularization method proposed by
6
, and then popularized by
20
;
39
under the name of
HuHu
-regularization.

The idea of
HuHu
-regularization is to penalize specific high order, skew deformations of the FE discretizing the TM, such that these do not become extreme, cause element inversion, and thereby destroy convergence. The bilinear,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
element used in this work allows deformations spanned by the modes shown in
Figure 2
. In this case, the
HuHu
-regularization only targets the skew deformation, allowing linear compression and shear.

The
HuHu
regularization is easily introduced by augmenting the SED (
1
) as

\tilde{W}(\boldsymbol{u})=W(\boldsymbol{u})+\dfrac{k_{r}}{2}\mathbb{H}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbb{H}\boldsymbol{u}

(5)

where
\mathbb{H}\boldsymbol{u}:=\frac{\partial^{2}u_{i}}{\partial X_{i}\partial X_{j}}
is the spatial Hessian of the displacement field.

The scaling factor
k_{r}=\alpha L^{2}(K+\frac{4}{3}G)
, depends on a characteristic domain length,
L
, the coefficient
\alpha>0
, which is set in the range
\alpha\in[10^{-5},10^{-7}]

(
20
;
18
)
and the bulk and shear moduli
(K,G)
.

We note that in previous works
k_{r}
was a function of the bulk modulus only
(
20
;
18
;
6
;
11
)
, which made the regularization essentially insensitive to the value of the shear modulus (
G
). A more robust, and physically sound alternative is to make
\alpha
proportional to the longitudinal modulus
M=K+\frac{4}{3}G

(
23
)
, also known as the
P
-wave modulus, since the TM regions near contact interfaces experience uniaxial compression, rather than pure volumetric change. Thus, using
M
appears more rational than just
K
, with the added educational benefit of keeping the regularization material parameter
>1
also for low values of the Poisson ratio
\nu
(see
Figure 3
).

Details on the discretization of (

We remark that some alternative formulations for the regularization of the TMC approach exist
(
14
;
41
)
, and some may also be more effective than the
HuHu

38

41

HuHu
due to its ease of implementation and acceptable robustness.

For completeness, we also recall the
HuHu-LuLu
-regularization, proposed by
18
. Making use of (
5
), this improved regularization reads

\tilde{\tilde{W}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\tilde{W}(\boldsymbol{u})-\frac{k_{r}}{2{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{I})}\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{u}

(7)

where
\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{u}:=\frac{\partial^{2}u_{i}}{\partial X_{j}\partial X_{j}}
is the Laplacian of the displacement field.

The added term, proportional to
\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{u}
, reduces the penalization of nonlinear compression and bending modes, which carry important physical information in higher-order elements. However, for the low-order
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
element used here we have
\mathbb{L}\boldsymbol{u}=0
, and therefore (
5
) and (
7
) are identical.

3
Topology optimization framework

Following density-based TO
(
5
)
, we consider the design field
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
and the relative density field
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega:=\Omega_{s}\cup\Omega_{v}
. The two fields are linked by the linear PDE-based filter
(
27
)
, and the relaxed Heaviside projection
(
37
)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\rm argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\int_{\Omega}l^{2}_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\varrho}|^{2}+(\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\varrho})^{2}\>{\rm d}\Omega

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tanh(\beta\eta)+\tanh(\beta(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\eta))}{\tanh(\beta\eta)+\tanh(\beta(1-\eta))}

(9)

where
l_{\Omega}\geq 1
is the filter radius,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the intermediate field, and
\eta=[0,1]
,
\beta=[1,\infty)
define the projection threshhold and curvature.

The relative density is used to parametrize the material properties, here through the RAMP interpolation
(
32
)

\gamma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\gamma_{0}+(1-\gamma_{0})\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{1+p(1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x}))}

(10)

where
\gamma_{0}
is the scaling of the material property value over the void, and
p\geq 0
is the penalization factor.

The interpolation (
10
) enters the mechanical equilibrium through the SED

\tilde{W}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{u})=\gamma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})W(\boldsymbol{u})+\frac{k_{r}}{2}\mathbb{H}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbb{H}\boldsymbol{u}

(11)

such that the void region, representing the TM becomes significantly softer than the solid. In the current implementation we interpolate both Lamé parameters according to (
10
), such that
\lambda=\lambda(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x}))
and
\mu=\mu(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{x}))
.

Also, we remark that the
HuHu
regularization term is not scaled by the material interpolation, and thus it depends on
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
only implicitly, through the displacement field
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})
.

3.1
End-compliance minimization with support contact

We consider the task of minimizing the
end-compliance
of a loaded structure subject to a volume constraint
(
9
;
24
)
, which can be formulated as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle c(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})=\Lambda_{\rm end}\ell(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm end})

(12)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rm int}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm end};\delta\boldsymbol{u})-\Lambda_{\rm end}\ell(\delta\boldsymbol{u})=0\quad\forall\>\delta\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle v_{f}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{(d)})\leq\bar{v}_{f}

\displaystyle 0\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega

where
\Lambda_{\rm end}
is the end value of the load multiplier,
\ell(\boldsymbol{u})
is defined in (
3
), and
\bar{v}_{f}
is the allowed maximum volume fraction.

Problem (
12
) is solved by a Nested Analysis and Design approach
(
22
)
. At each re-design step the sensitivity of the end-compliance to the variation of the control variable, say
\delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
reads

\delta c(\delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})=\mathcal{R}_{\rm int}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm end};\delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\boldsymbol{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm end})

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
solves the adjoint system
\mathcal{K}_{T}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm end})\boldsymbol{\theta}=\Lambda_{\rm max}\ell(\boldsymbol{u})
, and
\mathcal{K}_{T}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm end})
is the tangent stiffness operator evaluated for the end-displacement. The sensitivities w.r.t. the design field
\boldsymbol{\rho}
are then obtained by applying the chain rule according to the relationships (
8
).

Upon discretization, these equations assume the well-known form (see, e.g., equations 13-14 in
9
). At each redesign step, the design field
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is updated through the simplified MMA-like approximation
(
33
)
, adapted from
16
and given in the routine
ocUpdate.m
.

To obtain a design with a sharp solid-void boundary we apply continuation to the curvature of the Heaviside projection
\beta
, starting from
\beta=1
and then gradually increasing it up to the value
\beta=15
. To further promote convergence towards
0-1
designs, we impose the volume constraint on a
dilated
design, defined by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{(d)}
, computed by the relaxed Heaviside projection (
8
) for a threshold value
\eta^{(d)}<\eta
. We stress that this does not necessarily amount to using a robust optimization approach, although it is inspired by it; we just further penalize intermediate densities by computing stiffness properties on a design which is slightly eroded (thus weaker) than the one used for computing the volume constraint.

We consider the configuration in
Figure 7
. The square region is clamped along the whole left edge
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{u}(1)}\cup\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{u}(2)}
, and loaded by a downward surface traction at the boundary
\Gamma_{t}
, with length

. With the end-magnitude set to

, we consider

The optimizer will update the design variable field over the design domain
\Omega_{D}

\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
over the void region
\bar{\Omega}_{v}
. We stress that, even if solid material can connect to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{u}}
only for a depth of length

\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{u}(1)}
, which is directly connected to the design domain. Thus, solid material is placed only in a shallow region close to the top edge of
\Omega_{D}
and we see that the structure shows large localized deformations at the clamped region.

We also observe that considering large deformations and hyperelastic response gives only minor changes in the resulting structural configuration, but a 20% improvement in the optimized end-compliance. The
HuHu
-regularization is still very beneficial for this case, avoiding ill-conditioning of the equilibrium system due to the huge mesh distortion happening near the top left corner.

On the other hand, when contact is introduced by the TMC modelling (iii), the optimizer utilizes the advantage of placing material in contact with the left wall, also over the region
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{u}(2)}
. Therefore, a deeper design develops to engage contact at an earlier stage, reducing the deformation near the top-left corner. The force transfer happens through the upper clamped region and the contact point at the bottom. As a result, the SED is almost evenly distributed over the whole domain of the final design, and the compliance is reduced to nearly 1/4 of that of the nonlinear design in case (ii).

4
Discussion and conclusions

We have presented the Third Medium Contact (TMC) model built into a
Matlab
code for mechanical analysis and topology optimization of geometrically nonlinear, hyperelastic structures.

The self-contained implementation shows that the TMC method, together with the
HuHu
-regularization of void regions, can be easily integrated in density-based topology optimization. These methods are demonstrated on two examples, which are readily reproducible with the provided code, involving analysis and topology design of structures exhibiting contact.

The implementation prioritizes clarity and educational value over generality and computational efficiency. However, several extensions are possible with minor changes to the code. For instance:

•

Switching to other material laws
(
25
)
is easily achieved by changing the Piola stress and tangent moduli expressions in the routine
assembleKtFi.m
;

•

Using higher-order finite elements, and the associated
HuHu-LuLu
-regularization, requires rather intuitive changes to the routines
initializeFEA.m
and
assembleKtFi.m
;

•

General and robust path-following methods and nonlinear solvers can be introduced in the
solveIncrIter.m
routine, keeping the tangent operator and residual vector computed by
assembleKtFi.m
;

•

The extension to 3D problem, and use of efficient iterative solvers
(
1
)
should also be straightforward.

We envision that extensions to more advanced applications or physics, such as frictional contact
19
or multi-physics
11
is also possible, albeit with heavier changes to the base code provided here.

5.2
Nonlinear Equilibrium Solver

The incremental-iterative analysis is implemented in the function in
3
, where at each load step
\lambda
, the equilibrium is restored by solving
Equation 2
by the Newton-Raphson method.

In the
k
-th Newton step, the displacement correction
\Delta\mathbf{u}^{(k)}
is computed by solving the FE-discretized algebraic equations

\mathbf{K}_{T}(\mathbf{u}^{(k)})\Delta\mathbf{u}^{(k)}=\lambda\mathbf{f}_{0}-\mathbf{f}_{\rm int}(\mathbf{u}^{(k)})

(14)

until
\|\mathbf{f}_{0}-\mathbf{f}_{\rm int}(\mathbf{u}^{(k)})\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{f}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\tau
. The tangent stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}_{T}
and the internal forces
\mathbf{f}_{\rm int}
are computed by the routine described in the following subsection.

Here we consider a simple load controlled procedure, and a Newton scheme with no damping or line search. Thus, arbitrary changes in the parameters or in the test example may hamper the robustness of the solver, in which case the user may extend the current code with more robust path-following procedures and/or nonlinear solvers.

and internal force vector
\mathbf{f}_{{\rm int}(e)}
are computed and assembled in the global quantities. This is performed by the routine listed in
4
.

The variation of the internal forces, considering the
HuHu
regularization reads (see
Equation 6
)

\delta^{2}\tilde{W}(\boldsymbol{u};\delta\boldsymbol{u})=\delta\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{P}+\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{P}+k_{r}e^{-5|\boldsymbol{F}|}\left(\mathbb{H}\delta\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\>\mathbb{H}\delta\boldsymbol{u}-5|\boldsymbol{F}|^{2}{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\delta\boldsymbol{F})\mathbb{H}\delta\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\>\mathbb{H}\boldsymbol{u}\right)

(15)

Since we consider a Total Lagrangian approach, based on the Green-Lagrange strain measure
2\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{I}
, the stress tensor
\boldsymbol{P}
is converted to the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
\boldsymbol{S}=\lambda\ln|\boldsymbol{F}|\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}+\mu(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{-T})
.

Upon FE discretization, (
15
) gives the tangent stiffness matrix, consisting of material, geometric, and stabilization contributions:

\mathbf{k}_{T(e)}=\int_{\Omega_{e}}\mathbf{B}^{T}_{\gamma}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}+\mathbf{G}^{T}\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{S})\mathbf{G}\>{\rm d}\Omega+k_{r}\int_{\Omega_{e}}e^{-5|\mathbf{F}|}\left(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H}-5|\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{H}\right)\>{\rm d}\Omega

(16)

where
\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{B}_{0}+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{G}
is the nonlinear strain-displacement matrix,
\mathbf{C}
is the material tangent tensor, and
\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{S})
represents the geometric stiffness contribution.

The last term is the contribution from the
HuHu
regularization, depending on the Hessian of the displacement field
\mathbf{H}
. This contribution generally makes the tangent matrix nonsymmetric, which is why some of the fast assembly and solve strategies used in
17
are not used in the present code.

The internal force vector reads

\mathbf{f}_{\rm int(e)}=\int_{\Omega_{e}}\mathbf{B}^{T}_{\gamma}\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{S})\>{\rm d}\Omega+k_{r}\int_{\Omega_{e}}e^{-5|\mathbf{F}|}\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H}\>{\rm d}\Omega

(17)

5.4
Finite Element Analysis initialization

The routine
initializeFEA.m
builds some data and operators which are stored in the data structure
feP
, and used in the finite element analyses.

First, the discretization information are defined (
nNdEl, nElDof, nEl, elNrs, ndNrs, nDof
). Then the arrays defining the connectivity of the mesh are defined (
cDofMat, iK, jK
). We recall that the mesh sorting and connectivity rules follow what given in
15
.

All the FE operators which do not depend neither on the design (
\boldsymbol{\rho}
) nor on the space variable (
\boldsymbol{u}
) can be precomputed once and for all, then used in all the subsequent analyses. These are the operator
\mathbf{G}
, the linear strain displacement matrix
\mathbf{B}_{0}
, and the Hessian matrix
\mathbf{H}
. All these quantities are defined as three-dimensional arrays, since we compute their value on each quadrature point. Therefore
G(i,j,k)=\left.G_{ij}\right|_{(\xi_{i},\eta_{j})}
, whereas the last index is unnecessary as these quantities are identical for all elements on a uniform mesh.

The construction of these operator follows what outlined in
16
, to which we refer for further details. We define the array collecting the shape functions
\mathbf{N}(\xi,\zeta)=[N_{1}(\xi,\zeta),N_{2}(\xi,\zeta),\ldots,N_{k}(\xi,\zeta)]
and their first and second derivatives wrt the logical coordinates
(\xi,\zeta)
,
\nabla_{(\xi,\zeta)}N
, and
\nabla^{2}_{(\xi,\zeta)}N
.

Then, the three operators of interest are computed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla_{(x,y)}N\otimes[1,0]^{T}\\
\nabla_{(x,y)}N\otimes[0,1]^{T}\end{array}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{0}

\displaystyle=L(\nabla_{(x,y)}N\otimes I_{2})

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla^{2}_{(x,y)}H\otimes[1,0]^{T}\\
\nabla^{2}_{(x,y)}H\otimes[0,1]^{T}\end{array}\right]

where
\nabla_{(x,y)}\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{J}^{-1}\nabla_{(\xi,\zeta)}\mathbf{N}
,
\mathbb{H}_{(x,y)}=\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\,\mathbb{H}_{(\epsilon,\eta)}\,\boldsymbol{J}^{-\top}
, and
\mathbf{L}
is the strain-displacement combination matrix and “
\otimes
” denotes the dyadic (Kronecker) product of two arrays.

This transformation ensures that the second derivatives required for the HuHu regularization are correctly computed in the physical domain, even for distorted or non-uniform elements. For
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
elements the mapping is affine and the transformation is straightforward, but for higher-order elements, the full transformation must be applied at each integration point.

Finally, the routine computes the material parameters (
\lambda,\mu,k_{s},k_{r}
) starting from the input data (
E_{0},\nu,\alpha
) following well-known elasticity relationships and equations previously expressed.
