Operationalising readiness for practice in final OSCEs: insights from a mixed-methods study across five Australian medical schools
Authors: Bunmi S Malau-Aduli, Shannon Saad, Richard Hays, Karen D’Souza, Jane Smith, Bradley Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReadiness for practice (RFP) is a critical outcome for medical education, reflecting a graduate’s ability to transition from student to clinician. Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) play a pivotal role in evaluating RFP. This study aimed to investigate the conceptualisation and operationalisation of RFP within the OSCE context and evaluate the contribution of a newly developed RFP-focused rating tool compared to traditional OSCE scoring.MethodsUtilising a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach, this study was conducted across five Australian medical schools. Quantitative data from final-year medical students’ OSCE results were analysed to examine correlations between RFP scores and components of a 5-item Process of Decision-Making (PDM) checklist: safety, reliability, trustworthiness, insight, and an organised/logical approach. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews with OSCE examiners were analysed using inductive thematic analysis to explore their perceptions of RFP and decision-making processes.ResultsQuantitative analyses of 550 medical students’ OSCE performance datasets revealed that global rating had the strongest correlation with RFP scores, followed by safety and reliability components of the PDM checklist, whereas trustworthiness showed a weaker association. Failing students consistently scored poorly across all checklist components and RFP, with approximately half or fewer meeting individual checklist requirements. Qualitative analysis of nine examiners’ interviews produced a schema of What, How, and Why: What assessors viewed as RFP was the integration of clinical competence, safety, scope awareness, reasoning, and team readiness; How RFP was judged was through behaviours such as supervision-seeking, recognising unwell patients, attitudinal maturity, and structured consultations; Why RFP judgments were used included: to assess socio-relational skills, address workplace limitations, and make decisions, particularly on borderline performances.ConclusionsReadiness for practice is a complex, multi-dimensional construct that includes application of knowledge, technical skills, reliability, professional identity, safety and trustworthiness. While this study does not directly evaluate examiner training, understanding how examiners apply RFP frameworks may inform future efforts to enhance training consistency, fairness, and assessment utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09126-6.Keywords: Readiness for practice, OSCE, Medical education, Clinical assessment, Examiner decision-making

Introduction

Graduation from a professional course to clinical practice is a critical transition in the professional development of health professionals. While universities focus on acquisition of knowledge, skills and behaviours that indicate competence in graduate outcomes, clinical colleagues value "readiness for practice” (RFP) which is a multifaceted construct that goes beyond clinical competence to include critical thinking, communication skills, professionalism, and adaptability [1–5]. Ethical decision-making and patient safety are central to readiness for practice (RFP) as healthcare systems become increasingly complex [6], heightening the need to assess this broader construct before graduates enter the clinical workforce [7], and prompting regulatory bodies globally to incorporate RFP-related competencies into accreditation standards and graduate outcome frameworks. However, while most regulatory bodies require observed clinical assessments prior to licensing of graduates, RFP remains loosely defined and variably expressed in clinical assessment marksheets.

Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) are used widely to evaluate observable competencies during clinical assessment across medical education programs [8–11]. Typically, examiners rate performance after brief training and brief observations, using structured checklists and/or rating scales, and are encouraged to provide feedback on observed errors and omissions [12].

Although OSCEs are designed to be objective and standardised, examiner judgments are often shaped by cognitive heuristics, professional experiences, and emotional cues, introducing subjectivity into the process [13–15]. While rater variability and cognitive bias in performance assessments have been well documented [13], the specific cognitive mechanisms underlying examiners’ judgments of RFP remain poorly understood. These judgments are informed not only by observable behaviours but also by implicit mental models and accumulated professional experience [14, 15].

Shared mental models (SMMs) are the tacit understandings individuals develop about roles, tasks, and performance expectations. In clinical education, SMMs shape how examiners interpret observed behaviours and apply assessment criteria, with alignment or misalignment influencing judgment and evaluation outcomes [16]. However, few studies have examined how SMMs shape individual examiners’ constructions of work readiness in solo assessment contexts. Although examiner cognition is recognised as a key influence in clinical assessment, most research has centred on outcomes rather than the cognitive and affective processes that drive them [14, 15].

Our previous research highlighted the complexity of examiners’ judgments, particularly in borderline OSCE performances [17]. In these “grey zones,” examiners were guided by marking criteria but also drew on implicit cues such as demeanour and patient safety. A recurring mental model of the “prototypical intern” emerged, reflecting expectations of how a graduate should perform in a clinical team. Our subsequent research further theorised that examiners draw on two overlapping constructs: the academic construct, based on checklist-driven assessments of knowledge and skills; and the clinical workplace construct, which values attributes like safety, reliability, teamwork, and trust built over time [18].

Examiners form impressions of a “safe” junior doctor or "prototypical intern" based on prior personal experience with graduates [19, 20], often using a heuristic benchmark — “How would this person fit into my clinical team?”—especially in borderline cases or time-limited assessment settings [18]. This representative heuristic integrates professional traits such as sound communication, safe practice, trustworthiness, reliability and insight in addition to observable skills. These findings mirror the widely used clinical shorthand, “Would you trust them to look after your mother?”, and resonate with the rise of Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) in Workplace-Based Assessments (WBAs) [21]. Examiners particularly valued the integration of professional identity and conduct into global judgments, perceiving clinical readiness as greater than the sum of checklist parts.

Emerging research on examiner cognition indicates that judgments are shaped by pattern recognition, emotional cues, and contextual expectations, rather than purely objective observation [22–24]. This challenges traditional assumptions of objectivity in assessment and underscores the significant role of cognitive processes. However, empirical evidence on how these processes operate specifically in the context of RFP remains limited. Variability in how attributes like trustworthiness are interpreted highlights a lack of alignment between formal assessment criteria and examiner expectations. Importantly, examiners sometimes revealed that some candidates, though meeting all checklist items, still elicited concern, often described as “technically competent but not safe.” This raises important questions about the tacit factors influencing examiner decisions, which, if unaddressed, may perpetuate inconsistency in scoring.

To enhance the validity, reliability, efficiency and educational impact of clinical assessments, we must look beyond explicit criteria and explore how examiners construct impressions of RFP. This study investigates examiners’ interpretations of RFP within the context of “final” OSCEs, focusing on the influence of implicit heuristics, prioritisation of key attributes, and the formation of holistic judgments. This study utilises a five-item Process of Decision-Making (PDM) framework (safety, reliability, trustworthiness, insight, and an organised/logical approach), designed to evaluate integrated professional behaviours often associated with readiness for entrustable professional activities. In doing so, the study seeks to deepen theoretical and practical understanding of judgement formation in clinical assessments, informing future OSCE design, examiner calibration, and the broader discourse on workplace readiness in medical education.

Methods

Study context

This study involved exit-level (‘final’) OSCEs conducted in Australian medical school programs that have similar curricula and assessment processes and collaborate on assessment through the Australasian Collaboration for Clinical Assessment in Medicine (ACCLAiM) [25]. Although all participating medical schools use other clinical assessment formats, including Workplace-based Assessment (WBA), OSCEs were selected for this study because of their capacity for increased standardisation across multiple schools. Five schools conducted final OSCEs within the study timeframe and agreed to participate. The study was approved by the James Cook University Human Research Ethics Committee (H6833), adopted by all participating universities.

Procedure

Based on our previous research detailed in the introduction, we developed an RFP focused marking rubric that explicitly includes attributes such as trust, safety, reliability, and organisation and investigated whether including RFP constructs in the scoring tool better aids examiners in making informed judgements about medical students’ performance in exit-level OSCE examinations which is the final clinical assessment prior to entering the workforce. This tool captures nuanced socio-professional attributes often missed by standard checklist or global scores. Specifically, this study aimed to:

analyse the correlation between traditional rating scales used in OSCEs (checklist, global rating—see Fig. 1) and a set of newly developed rating scales based on our previous research results (trustworthiness, safe practice, reliability, insight into limitations, and organisation) and RFP anchors embedded within a selected OSCE station (see Fig. 2).

Choice of OSCE station

A new Gastrointestinal (GI) Handover station was developed by a small team through normal ACCLAiM processes. This station reflected a common junior doctor task that required professional information sharing (clinical handover).

‘Readiness for practice’ OSCE station marking sheet

A standardised marking sheet was developed, adding a 5-point process of decision making (PDM) Likert scale and a 5-point readiness for practice (RFP) Likert scale, as shown in Fig. 2. To evaluate the internal consistency of the developed marking instrument, we calculated Cronbach’s alpha (α) which was 0.89. This high alpha indicates that the items are closely related and measure a common underlying construct.

Study design

A sequential explanatory mixed methods design within a pragmatic research paradigm was chosen, which allowed the researchers to combine quantitative and qualitative data to address a complex issue. This ensured that the findings are directly applicable to real-world educational challenges, enhancing the relevance and impact of the research [26, 27]. The study design is summarised in Fig. 3.

Quantitative phase

Participants and data collection

The five participating medical schools provided de-identified students’ performance data on the selected OSCE station. One school provided incomplete quantitative data which were excluded from the analyses. Examiners scored final-year medical students using both original (see Fig. 1 – checklist, global rating) and newly developed (see Fig. 2—examiner process of decision-making) rating scales embedded within the specifically designed OSCE station (Gastrointestinal (GI) Handover station).

Data analysis

Borderline regression method was used to determine the pass mark for the station. Correlation analysis examined the relationships between various variables, including the Checklist score, Global rating score, Examiner Judgement score, and each of the qualities assessed in the PDM rating scale: trust, safety, reliable team member, insight into limitations, logical approach and RFP. Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess how consistently the assessment items measured readiness for practice, with values above 0.8 indicating greater reliability and high internal consistency. Spearman rank-order correlation for non-parametric tests was used to assess relationships between checklist, global, PDM, and RFP scores; r-values greater than 0.70 were interpreted as strong correlations. Calculations employed standard alpha formulas, providing a conservative estimate of reliability, given known limitations such as the assumption of tau-equivalence. We also calculated a disattenuated correlations (corrected for attenuation) which was used to account for measurement error, providing more accurate estimates of association. The disattenuated correlation is the raw correlation between two variables x and y (denoted as rxy) divided by the square root of the product of the reliability of variable x (denoted as rxx) and the reliability of variable y (denoted as ryy) formula:

Multiple regression analysis was utilised to predict each of the qualities assessed in the examiner process rating scale and RFP using Checklist, Global and Examiner Judgement scores as independent variables. Additionally, number of failing students was calculated based on the station pass mark. Furthermore, the frequency of use of each rating level for each of the qualities assessed in the examiner process rating scale and RFP were calculated. The dataset met the conditions for these statistical analyses.

Qualitative phase

Participants and data collection

In-depth interviews explored examiners’ use of the rating scales and RFP score. Selection of the interview participants was based on a purposive sampling framework. Two categories of volunteer examiners were recruited from the five participating medical schools: experienced OSCE examiners (≥ 5 years) and inexperienced OSCE examiners (< 5 years). All the 15 examiners who were involved in assessing this station across the five medical schools were invited to participate in the interviews: nine consented, representing a balanced mix of experience levels and roles. Data saturation was achieved. All interviewees were provided an information sheet and gave written informed consent which was confirmed verbally at each interview.

Semi-structured interviews focused on enhancing clarification of participants’ understanding of RFP. RBH and BMA, (external to the participating medical schools), conducted all interviews, using the same agreed interview schedule with probing questions (see Supplementary file 1). All interviews commenced with a question exploring the RFP concept. Participants then observed two video-recorded versions of the OSCE that were developed for examiners calibration and asked to discuss how they made judgements about clinical performance using the new rating scales based on practice-readiness concepts – i.e. the new PDM and RFP Scores (see Fig. 2). Further questions explored how the new rating scales contributed to assessments, clarified and expanded upon with follow-up probing questions. One hour was allowed for each interview and all were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently until data saturation was achieved – when subsequent interviews were no longer revealing new information [28].

Data analysis

Participants were de-identified and differentiated by gender and a participant ID. Inductive thematic analysis was performed by two members of the research team (BMA and SS) using the six-phase framework described by Braun and Clark [29]. The analysis process involved familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. To enhance the trustworthiness [30] of the study, the authors employed multiple strategies. The identified codes and themes were verified by two other members of the research team (RBH and KDS). Methodological triangulation was achieved by integrating quantitative and qualitative data, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of how RFP was conceptualised and operationalised. An audit trail was maintained throughout the study to ensure transparency and dependability. To ensure the credibility of the qualitative findings, reflexivity was employed through ongoing critical reflection and discussion within the research team to identify and mitigate potential biases, especially during thematic analysis of the interview data.

Triangulation and interpretation of findings

The findings from the quantitative and qualitative components were integrated to provide a comprehensive understanding of OSCE examiners' evaluations and perceptions. This integration allowed for the identification of convergent and divergent themes, enhancing the study's overall credibility.

Results

Quantitative results

The OSCE results of 550 medical students from four schools were analysed. Based on Borderline regression analysis, the pass mark for the station was determined as 51.4%. The mean percent scores for the elements assessed in the marking rubric ranged from 25–100%. Mean percent scores for the checklist components were 70% for Professional discourse, 71.8% for Diagnosis and synthesis of data, 67.6% for Management plan and 68.4% for Examiner’s comprehensive judgment. Mean global rating score was 72%. Mean PDM scores were 80%, 80.5%, 79.7%, 76.7% and 80.1% for trustworthiness, safety, reliability, insight and logical approach respectively. Mean RFP score was 73.4%.

The overall scale combining all rating components (checklist, global, process domains, and readiness score) achieved a Cronbach’s α of 0.93, indicating excellent consistency across these items in this single-station OSCE. Figure 4 and Supplementary file 2 summarise the correlation analysis between the ‘old’ and ‘new’ rating scales. To interpret the strength of the relationships between variables, correlation values ≥ 0.70 were classified as strong, ≥ 0.40 and < 0.70 was moderate and < 0.40 was classified as weak or poor. The correlation analysis revealed significant relationships between various assessment criteria used in evaluating medical students' RFP. The Checklist score had a strong positive correlation with RFP (r = 0.76), Global rating (r = 0.74) and Overall Examiner Judgement (r = 0.71). The global rating score had the highest correlation with RFP (r = 0.84). It also had strong correlations with Safety (r = 0.70) and Reliability (r = 0.74). Safety and Reliability demonstrated strong correlations with RFP (r = 0.75 and r = 0.70, respectively).

Thematic analysis findings

Thematic analysis of the RFP concept and its application found several factors that could be organised according to a schema of What, How and Why (see Fig. 5).

Schema representing the themes derived from the conceptualisation of RFP by examiners and its influence on decision making processes in observed clinical assessments

The What

Participants described factors that allowed the effective integration and transference of the medical program’s teaching, learning and assessment to the clinical workplace. The concepts described included: safety; clinical competence; awareness of scope of practice; clinical reasoning ability and team-readiness.

Safety: Participants reported the concept of clinical safety as an important feature of their conceptualisation of RFP. The conceptualisation could include considerations of health service context and access to effective supervision.

“I think to me that means that they can operate safely under supervision. So, they’ve still got a direct supervisor that they have to report to. But the decisions that they’ve brought to them should be done in a safe manner that’s up to the standards of whatever health service that they’re working in.” (P4.M.In.Cl)

Clinical competence: The RFP concept included the ability to function in the PGY 1 clinical role of the intern, with an acceptable minimum level of knowledge, skills and behaviours enabling completion of assigned routine clinical tasks.

“I think it’s probably helpful to add in competence, the actual skills and knowledge as part of kind of being ready to practice, and not just being safe - because if you completely do nothing, well, sometimes that’s pretty safe, but that doesn’t make you ready to practice because you don’t know enough.” (P1.F.In.Ac)

Awareness of scope of practice: Participants’ description of RFP included an awareness of and commitment to act within the boundaries of the intern role. This included a cognisance of personal capabilities that could impact how they functioned in that role.

“…they’ve got that self-awareness about their own scope, and where that sits and what to do when the task is pushing them outside of that scope.” (P8.F.Ex.Cl)

Clinical reasoning ability: An ability to synthesise information to facilitate effective clinical work was identified as part of RFP. Self-awareness of this concept – clinical reasoning – was thought to contribute to the reflective practice of the clinician.

“So, I just feel like they need to have an understanding of what they’re doing is the main thing. How they extract information and then how they process it, and how they come to conclusions.” (P3.M.Ex.Cl)

Readiness to work in clinical teams: The ability to integrate functionally into the clinical team environment contributed to participants’ understanding of RFP. This involved not only the ability to collaborate with a clinical team but also integrate patients into the care team.

“… it’s [RFP] safety, willingness to ask for help, and I put that as very strong- not to work on one’s own.” (P7.M.Ex.B)

The How

Participants used the concepts outlined above to identify attributes that infer RFP in students—supervision seeking; recognition of the unwell patient; attitudinal maturity; and robust consultation organisation.

Supervision seeking: RFP concepts such as safety and awareness of scope of practice could be identified when candidates demonstrated seeking supervisor advice.

“I guess … they might say something like ‘I’d like to do this, this, this, but I will confirm this with my ED supervisor or call the … medical registrar who’s admitting the patient’ or something along those lines that lets me know that they are yes, they’re going to double check their decisions.” (P1.F.In.Ac)

Awareness of the unwell patient: Clinical vigilance in seeking and/or recognising indicators of a patient deterioration was used by examiners to gauge the safety of the candidates and suitability to commence internship.

“I suppose I’m looking to make sure they don’t miss red flag conditions…in particular, they can recognise a [very] sick patient from a less sick patient, I suppose... I think that my primary criterion is to make sure that they’ll be safe interns.” (P5.F.Ex.B)

Attitudinal maturity: Participants inferred RFP concepts such as team-readiness, awareness of scope of practice and safety by observing behaviours that demonstrated a maturity of attitude that would enhance function in the clinical workplace by basing care on the patient.

“I would probably be intuiting to some extent based on how they’ve performed in the station. If they are considerate to the patient, if they’re sort of displaying the kind of professionalism we expect of them, if they communicate well with their patient, those are the sorts of things that you would be able to use as a determinant of whether you would like this person on your team.” (P6.F.In.B)

Awareness of scope of practice could be inferred by observing student performing clinical assessments on patients that were potentially unwell, seeking to clarify the acuity of the presentation.

“..if it’s just chest pain that’s associated with…. a cough and they’ve suspected pneumonia, I wouldn’t be expecting them to escalate ….in the first instance.” (P9.M.In.Cl)

While a confident demeanour was perceived as generally positive, examiners were aware of the potential risk that confidence could be over attributed as competence.

“I’m sure we make errors in that we can be conned by people who are confident. In teaching, we generally recognise the class king and the class clown fairly quickly. It doesn’t mean the class king is the best and most confident, sometimes the shy one who sits back and doesn’t say much is actually the most competent of all.” (P7.M.Ex.B)

Robust consultation organisation: The flow and composition of the observed performance was perceived as a marker of clinical competence and clinical reasoning ability by examiners.

“… there wasn’t a lot of time for additional discussion, most of what I would have wanted, she had presented, and the manner in which she presented demonstrated to me that she understood why she was doing the things she was doing.” (P8.F.Ex.Cl)

Physical examination skills that flow and are effortless, purposeful and targeted are also perceived as reflecting clinical reasoning ability and RFP.

“I think performing examination has to be so practiced that it is somewhat second nature, but then not robotic either…the examination would follow on from what information preceded that whether that’s a full history or a little bit of history in the OSCE…Now you’ve got to be concentrating on the findings to be able to do that, so the examination has to be automatic enough that they’re thinking about what they’re finding.”(P3.M.Ex.Cl)

The Why

Examiners reported using these RFP concepts for a variety of purposes. These included: to judge socio-relational behaviour relevant to clinical practice; in response to the educational limitations of the clinical workplace; as legitimate subjectivity; and to facilitate judgements on borderline performances.

Judging socio-relational behaviour relevant to clinical practice: In the clinical workplace, junior doctors are integrated into various healthcare teams where mutual trust enables effective team function. Trustworthiness was perceived as being difficult to judge in an OSCE station, due to the limited timeframes and sampling.

“I find that is hard in a single OSCE station, like I think, how can you tell whether, in …. a 2–3-minute interview, whether someone is trustworthy?” (P2.F.In.Cl)

Faced with this difficulty, participants reported using demonstrated safety behaviours in clinical assessments as one possible way to judge whether a candidate would be trustworthy.

“…because if I think someone’s unsafe then I think they’re untrustworthy. And if I think they’re safe, then I’m more likely to say that they’re trustworthy as well.” (P9.M.In.Cl)

Responding to the educational limitations of the clinical workplace: Some examiners were motivated to use RFP principles when judging final year clinical assessments, as technical skills were easier to teach in the clinical workplace than RFP skills:

“From my point of view, technical skills can be learnt. Whereas humanistic skills - Yes, they can be taught, but not as easily and certainly not… in the pressured on- job situation.” (P8.F.Ex.Cl)

Legitimate subjectivity: Participants expressed an awareness that in their role as examiner, they drew on their own clinical and supervision experience to gauge the RFP of the candidate performance in the station observed.

“I think we [as OSCE examiners], you can’t really separate your clinical self from your examiner self in that way. You’re…. always sort of interpreting things in that way, you know, using your own clinical judgement in a sense.” (P6.F.In.B)

To facilitate judgements around the borderline: Examiners may use RFP concepts when trying to make decisions regarding performances that are of borderline quality, particularly in the time pressured and limited sampling environment of the OSCE.

“That humanistic approach, there is a certain level of importance there. Because you know, you could punch all this stuff into a computer and it could tell you the result but, it’s just not the same to me, and I think it’s important that we do take into consideration when we’re marking people, on that other side of the spectrum [borderline], it’s not all just technical. There’s an art form to it as well, and in order to exceed expectations, I think you have to deliver a little bit of that art, along with all of the technical. And that art, it’s in the delivery and the collection of knowledge, and then how you redistribute that to your colleagues.” (P4.M.In.Cl)

Discussion

Drawing on both quantitative and qualitative analyses enhances understanding of how examiners make judgements in final OSCEs. Quantitative analyses support the use of global rating scales because these strongly internally consistent scores correlate highly with judgments about readiness for practice (RFP), strengthening previous findings [31]. Strong correlations were also observed between RFP and specific elements of the five-item PDM framework, particularly’ safe’ and ‘reliable’, aligning with previous research findings [32, 33]. High inter-item consistency and strong correlations with global and RFP scores suggest that examiners apply relatively uniform mental models from professional norms when judging these domains [34].

RFP remains a multidimensional construct, as is clinical competence more generally [35]. The observation that failing students consistently received low scores across all PDM components and RFP scores suggests a shared underpinning. Safety, reliability, insight, trust, and an organised/logical approach likely interact to form a holistic picture of RFP. Students failing in one component often perform poorly across others, supporting the validity of these constructs as interdependent components of a complex RFP concept, which may be difficult to deconstruct. This begs the question: could there be a single construct that could be assessed that combines the five PDM framework components? However, about half of failing students were judged to meet requirements for some of the five PDM checklist items, suggesting that individual examiners may be seeing different things. There may be a difference between being ready for practice in a general sense as compared with being welcome in ‘my’ team, reflecting socio-relational markers of competence that extend beyond observable skills [14, 31].

The PDM-RFP tool offers a structured approach to assessing socio-cultural dimensions of readiness (such as trustworthiness, insight, and team-readiness) that are often implicit in global ratings. While it correlates strongly with global scores, its strength lies in making these complex, practice-oriented judgments more explicit and transparent, particularly in borderline cases. The tool enhances alignment with EPAs and workplace expectations of new graduates by prompting assessors to articulate socio-professional attributes underpinning safe and effective practice. This facilitates richer feedback, improves decision-making transparency, and enhances the defensibility and educational value of OSCE judgments beyond traditional single-item global scores.

The qualitative analysis provides a robust complementary framework for understanding how examiners translate a candidate’s application of theoretical knowledge and skills competencies into a judgement about RFP, using a three-layered conceptual framework of the ‘What’, the ‘How’ and the ‘Why’. The ‘What’ layer captures what examiners believe constitutes RFP—i.e., the essential characteristics or capabilities that a medical graduate must demonstrate to be considered “ready” for imminent clinical practice. These go beyond technical competence to include socio-cultural aspects such as reliability, scope of practice, professional behaviours and a focus on patient safety. In combination these components inform although cannot provide sufficient evidence of trustworthiness. Examiners think about what are sometimes labelled ‘Day 1 competencies’ [36] that comprise combinations of clinical tasks that recent graduates must perform more independently. These are more like EPAs that are popular at postgraduate medical education levels [37].

The ‘How’ layer addresses how examiners assess RFP in time-constrained OSCE settings—what they look for, how they interpret it, and how it feeds into decision-making. This includes comparison with mental models (heuristics) of how a recent graduate should fit into a clinical team, perhaps including their own team. Issues such as an organised approach to patient encounters, seeking advice when necessary and demonstrating professional identity become more important considerations than the ‘What’ alone. For example, examiners may 'forgive' lack of knowledge if candidates show awareness of this gap and know how to seek appropriate advice. It is also possible for a candidate to satisfactorily meet checklist requirements but achieve a ‘fail’ global rating score. Appearing to take shortcuts and perceptions of over-confidence, even where technical requirements are met, may be interpreted by examiners as risky departures from the candidate’s intended scope of practice. In the workplace ‘How’ may be more important than ‘What’, because candidates need to demonstrate that they can achieve common workplace tasks in a safe manner. The global rating scale score correlated best with these more holistic judgments.

The ‘Why’ layer explains why examiners use this RFP framework, i.e., what motivates their assessment choices, and what functions these judgments serve in both the OSCE and broader clinical education system. Examiners are aware that this clinical assessment may be the last opportunity to detect and correct weaknesses before candidates join the clinical workforce, where clinical teamwork and patient safety assume much greater importance. Humanistic skills become as important as technical skills because of imminent roles as clinical team members in patient care. Examiners are aware of the constraints of brief encounters in forming these judgements but still look for what they regard as important, perhaps picking up subtle cues through comparison with heuristics, or mental models of how junior colleagues should perform [15, 23]. This is similar to Type 1 (fast) thinking used in diagnostic reasoning [38]. Should the performance be borderline, the task is more difficult but there may be insufficient time to switch to Type 2 (slow) thinking and seek further information.

The variations in examiners’ interpretation of the PDM aligns with the concept of ‘meaningful idiosyncrasy’, whereby assessors’ judgements on less concrete constructs are shaped by personal values, clinical experience, or contextual expectations [14, 39]. There may be more variation in interpreting ‘trustworthiness’ than ‘safe practice’, a more concrete concept with a commonly recognised set of behaviours (e.g. following protocol and seeking advice). This phenomenon aligns with descriptions of ‘differential salience’ [34], where different raters implicitly prioritise different aspects of a performance, highlighting the ‘legitimate subjectivity’ of clinical assessments, particularly in borderline cases [40]. The entrustment logic is similar in competency-based education [41] and is consistent with performance-based assessment principles [42], clinical competence frameworks that integrate cognitive, technical, and interpersonal skills [43], and professional identity formation [44, 45]. These implicit differences in interpretation could explain the challenge of identifying a single construct that represents the five-factor PDM framework components.

When designed well, OSCEs offer strengths that include systematic sampling of a wide range of competencies, alignment with the curriculum, and contribution of performance data to inform both learning and progress decisions. As learners advance the ‘What’ changes the most, as new competencies are encountered and mastered. The ‘How’ evolves slowly, because most OSCE stations are relatively brief and focused on a single competency whose importance may become evident in subsequent learning. The ‘Why’ remains relatively unchanged because the next stage is still in closely supervised placements with little or no formal accountability for patient care outcomes, so there is still time to improve.

The results of this study suggest that the purpose and design of OSCEs held just prior to graduation (‘final’ OSCEs) may need careful consideration. This is a more integrated assessment in all three layers of the conceptual framework—the ‘What’, the ‘How’ and the ‘Why’—focused and calibrated here at the level of readiness for imminent practice. The clinical tasks (‘What’) selected for each station should be those expected of recent graduates rather than senior students, more like EPAs [21]. This means that station tasks are usually more integrated and complex. Scoring instruments should reflect the comparison examiners will make with junior members of a clinical team so rating scales that deconstruct both technical and socio-cultural components should be used: safe application becomes key to the ‘How’ and the ‘Why’. Examiner training should include discussion of what satisfactory recent graduate performance looks like with the aim of developing a shared mental model as the comparator heuristic. This should also improve equity and fairness for candidates as well as reducing examiner variability [46, 47]. Ideally, the ‘What’, ‘How’ and ‘Why’ should evolve from junior to senior OSCEs so that candidates and examiners do not face a steep transition in design at the final hurdle.

OSCEs are not the only form of clinical assessment. In particular, they are not the best or only way to assess more complex professional behaviours that inform judgements about reliability, patient safety and trustworthiness. While these aspects should be included where relevant in OSCE station design, it is essential to focus on these aspects in longitudinal workplace-based assessment to produce sufficient information and more robust assessments about RFP [14, 48]. On the other hand, OSCEs may be able to assess competencies underrepresented in WBAs, such as more standardised approaches to managing significant tasks unlikely to be encountered as a student [49]. OSCEs and WBA formats are complementary components of a systematic assessment. Rigorous WBA can improve candidates’ integration of competencies across all curriculum domains which evolve into the clinical tasks that reflect the knowledge, skills, professional behaviours, reliability, trust and impact on patient safety that RFP requires and final OSCE stations can sample [50].

Finally, subjectivity and bias are inherent features of performance-based clinical assessment and cannot be fully eliminated, particularly when judging complex constructs such as readiness for practice, professionalism, and safety [14, 15, 23]. The purpose of the additional RFP tool in this study was not to remove subjectivity, but to make the implicit criteria underlying examiner judgments more explicit. Experienced examiners often draw on accumulated clinical experience and tacit entrustment judgments when deciding whether a candidate appears safe, reliable, and ready to function within a clinical team [20, 21]. Standardised checklists and global ratings may capture technical performance, but they do not always make these broader socio-professional considerations visible.

In this context, the RFP tool complements rather than replaces traditional assessment approaches. Whereas standardised checklists focus on observable components of task performance, the RFP framework prompts examiners to articulate integrated judgments about safety, reliability, insight, organisation, and team-readiness. Similarly, examiner calibration seeks to improve consistency across raters, while the RFP tool provides a clearer conceptual framework for what examiners are being calibrated to judge. By making these constructs explicit, the tool may help foster shared mental models among assessors, support more transparent judgments in borderline cases, and reduce unexamined idiosyncratic bias without denying the role of informed professional judgment [40, 51].

This framing aligns with contemporary views of assessment that recognise the value of legitimate subjectivity when evaluating complex clinical competence. Rather than treating subjectivity as a flaw to be eliminated, the aim is to structure and justify it more clearly. In that sense, the RFP tool offers added value not because it replaces existing scoring systems, but because it helps make visible the broader readiness constructs that examiners may already be using implicitly in high-stakes decisions.

The explicit articulation of readiness constructs may also enhance the trustworthiness of assessment decisions, paralleling principles described in qualitative research methodology. Lincoln and Guba [52] describe credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability as core elements of trustworthy interpretation. When examiners collectively discuss and apply readiness constructs such as safety, reliability, and insight, these processes may help strengthen the credibility of judgments, support consistency across contexts, and reduce idiosyncratic bias among individual assessors.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers a unique contribution to medical education through its large-scale, multi-institutional design involving five Australian medical schools and over 550 final-year medical students, enhancing the generalisability and relevance of findings across diverse training settings. The use of a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach allowed for a robust exploration of both the psychometric performance of a novel RFP rating tool and the nuanced insights from clinical examiners’ perspectives. The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings provided a deeper understanding of how RFP is conceptualised and operationalised in OSCEs. Importantly, the study demonstrated strong methodological rigour to ensure trustworthiness and analytical credibility. By highlighting the socio-relational and workplace-relevant dimensions of RFP, the study addresses a critical gap in performance-based assessment and offers practical implications for enhancing OSCE station design and examiner training.

Nonetheless, there are limitations to be considered. The context is limited to final (exit-level) OSCE station scoring in five Australian medical schools. This is a key career transition point, and it is possible that examiner thinking may be different in scoring OSCE stations for more junior medical students. Data for only one OSCE station was included in both quantitative and qualitative analyses, so replication in other studies is needed to strengthen findings. Also, although our qualitative sample included a diverse range of examiners, the relatively small size may limit transferability of the findings. Additionally, prior exposure to the RFP tool and the OSCE station design may have primed participant responses, leading to earlier thematic saturation. To mitigate this, interviews began with broad, open-ended prompts and incorporated reflexive bracketing during analysis. Further, WBA plays an increasingly important role in medical education and offering opportunities for longitudinal judgements that may be more suited to considering trust, reliability, safety, insight and logical approach that contribute to a judgement on RFP. How these considerations influence decisions in WBA may be different and requires exploration.

Conclusion

This study provides insight into how examiners apply structured and holistic judgement to evaluate readiness for practice at a crucial point of transition from student to novice clinician, particularly through the integration of clinical reasoning, safety, and socio-professional attributes. We have made more explicit the implicit components that contribute to a global rating score, which is readiness for practice, based on safety; clinical competence; awareness of scope of practice; clinical reasoning ability and team-readiness. These components should be more explicit in OSCE station design, including station content and scoring instruments that reflect common workplace tasks performed by new graduates. Examiner training should include explicit discussion of more than knowledge and technical skills, aiming to develop a shared mental model that includes socio-cultural aspects of how a safe, self-aware, ready for practice graduate approaches tasks when they have the requisite knowledge and skills. Trustworthiness is important but may be captured better in longitudinal WBAs. Further research should explore integration of the RFP framework within longitudinal WBAs, where constructs such as trust, reliability, and insight may be more holistically and authentically observed.
