Predicting transcriptional changes induced by molecules with MiTCP
Authors: Kaiyuan Yang, Jiabei Cheng, Shenghao Cao, Xiaoyong Pan, Hong-Bin Shen, Ye Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudying the changes in cellular transcriptional profiles induced by small molecules can significantly advance our understanding of cellular state alterations and response mechanisms under chemical perturbations, which plays a crucial role in drug discovery and screening processes. Considering that experimental measurements need substantial time and cost, we developed a deep learning-based method called Molecule-induced Transcriptional Change Predictor (MiTCP) to predict changes in transcriptional profiles (CTPs) of 978 landmark genes induced by molecules. MiTCP utilizes graph neural network-based approaches to simultaneously model molecular structure representation and gene co-expression relationships, and integrates them for CTP prediction. After training on the L1000 dataset, MiTCP achieves an average Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of 0.482 on the test set and an average PCC of 0.801 for predicting the top 50 differentially expressed genes, which outperforms other existing methods. Furthermore, we used MiTCP to predict CTPs of three cancer drugs, palbociclib, irinotecan and goserelin, and performed gene enrichment analysis on the top differentially expressed genes and found that the enriched pathways and Gene Ontology terms are highly relevant to the corresponding diseases, which reveals the potential of MiTCP in drug development.Keywords: changes of transcriptional profiles, small molecules, graph neural networks

Introduction

Studying cellular responses to small molecule chemical perturbations holds significant importance in biological applications, particularly in the drug design and screening [1, 2]. Drug screening is currently primarily based on target proteins. However, for diseases lacking clear targets [3], understanding cellular responses to drugs can help us identify potentially effective drug molecules.

With the advancement of high-throughput sequencing technologies and artificial intelligence, an increasing number of deep learning-based computational methods have emerged for predicting cellular responses to small molecules [4]. One type of methods involves representing cell lines and small molecules as vectors using corresponding encoders and combining them to predict the IC50 (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) [5]. Multi-omics data is usually used to characterize cell lines [6], employing Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) or Convolutional Neural Networks as cell line encoders [7–9], and small molecules can be encoded using Morgan fingerprints [10], one-dimensional Simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES)-based methods [11–13], or two-dimensional graph-based deep learning methods [14–16]. These methodology transforms the cell response prediction problem into a one-dimensional regression problem, enabling relatively accurate IC50 predictions but lacking the ability to further predict changes in transcriptional profiles (CTPs) at the gene level.

Another type of methods views the prediction of cellular responses to small molecules as a multi-dimensional regression problem, which is predicting the changes of gene expression profiles after perturbation. In comparison to predicting IC50, these approaches allow for a deeper understanding of how cells respond to chemical perturbations, moving from the cellular level to the gene level. In this type of methods, DeepCellState [17] employs an encoder-decoder architecture to predict the CTPs of unknown cell lines based on known cell lines’ CTP after chemical perturbation. Similarly, scGen [18] uses variational autoencoder (VAE) to predict the gene expression of unknown cell lines after perturbation based on expression data from known cell lines in normal and perturbed states. CPA [19] predicts cell responses under different concentrations, cell lines, and perturbation combinations using an encoder-decoder architecture. The problem addressed in this paper is to predict CTPs under molecular perturbation given only the structure of the molecule. The most representative work for this problem is deep learning-based efficacy prediction system (DLEPS) [20], which uses GrammarVAE [21] to encode small molecules and predict changes in gene expression levels using an MLP. However, GrammarVAE views small molecules as one-dimensional sequences, losing the topological structural information of the molecules, resulting in limited effectiveness in molecular representation. Moreover, all the aforementioned methods do not consider the heterogeneity of gene responses to perturbations and do not leverage the interactions between genes.

To address the limitations of the aforementioned methods, in this paper we proposed MiTCP, a Molecule-induced Transcriptional Change Predictor based on the input small molecule structure. MiTCP encodes small molecules utilizing Chemprop [22], which is a directed message passing neural network (D-MPNN)-based molecule encoder and has been experimentally validated for accurate antibiotic discovery [23]. Moreover, MiTCP employs graph convolutional network (GCN) [24] to model co-expression relationships between genes. By combining the structural representation of the small molecule and the gene representation, MiTCP predicts the CTPs of 978 landmark genes. We conducted training and testing on the L1000 dataset [25], which contains CTP data induced by over 20 000 small molecules. Performance comparison with other methods on the L1000 dataset shows that MiTCP can more accurately predict transcriptional changes of small molecule perturbation. We demonstrate that more comprehensive molecular representation and the introduction of gene representations can improve the accuracy of predicting CTPs. Furthermore, we performed enrichment analysis based on MiTCP’s predictions, showing that the predictions of MiTCP can provide valuable insights for drug screening.

RESULTS

Overview of MiTCP

MiTCP is a deep-learning based method used to predict changes in transcriptional profiles (CTP) induced by molecules. Given a molecule as model input, MiTCP can predict transcriptional changes of 978 landmark genes perturbed by the molecule, which is the CTP vector. This CTP prediction task can be viewed as a multi-dimensional regression problem.

The framework of MiTCP is shown as Fig. 1. The input molecules are first encoded with a molecule encoder, Chemprop [22], which views molecules as 2D graphs and calculate molecule embeddings based on a D-MPNN. Then instead of directly predicting CTPs only from molecule embeddings, we introduced a GCN-based gene encoder on a gene co-expression network built from training set to model the unperturbed state of all landmark genes. The unperturbed gene embeddings are combined with the molecule embedding and finally used to predict the molecule-induced transcriptional changes with a gene-specific CTP prediction layer. The introduction of unperturbed gene embeddings enables MiTCP to better capture the heterogeneity of genes’ responses to chemical perturbations.

Schematic of MiTCP framework, which is composed of a molecule encoder based on Chemprop, a molecule encoding method based on D-MPNN, a GNN-based gene co-expression encoder and a gene specific CTP prediction network to predict the transcriptional change induced by input molecules.

Predicting transcriptional change with MiTCP

We trained MiTCP on L1000 dataset [25], which contains ˃20 000 molecule-induced transcriptional changes of 978 landmark genes. After data preprocessing and data splitting, we trained MiTCP on the training set, saved the model with the lowest loss on the validation set and evaluated model performance on the test set. With the predicted CTPs and the true CTPs, two metrics were calculated for evaluation: (i) The average root mean square error (RMSE) of the test set. The lower RMSE indicates the predicted CTPs are closer to the true CTPs, implying higher accuracy of the predictions. (ii) Pearson Correlation Coefficients (PCC). For each predicted CTP—true CTP pair on the test set, we calculated the PCCs of these two vectors, which range from −1 to 1. The higher PCC approaching 1 indicates a stronger correct correlation between predictions and ground truth, implying better model performance.

We compared MiTCP with other two baseline methods, DLEPS [20] and TranSiGen [26]. Both are L1000-based deep learning methods predicting CTPs induced by molecules. The input molecules of DLEPS initially represented by SMILES are parsed to a grammar tree and converted to a one-hot array, then passed to a grammar VAE which is the VAE-based model architecture composed of 1D convolutional layers, to get the molecule embeddings. The molecule embeddings are passed to multiple dense layers to predict the CTP vector. TranSiGen is based on a VAE architecture, integrating molecular representation to learn the mapping from the original transcriptome to the perturbed transcriptome. DLEPS uses 1D SMILES sequence of molecules as input, which lacks molecular topology information and results in limited molecular representation capability. Moreover, DLEPS and TranSiGen predict CTPs only based on molecule embeddings, ignoring the potential influence of gene heterogeneous information in this problem. Improvements were made in MiTCP to address these limitations.

For fair comparison, all models were trained and tested using the same data derived from L1000 dataset after identical preprocessing steps. Additionally, we generated five different train-test splits based on different random seeds, and each method was trained and tested five times, allowing for evaluation of model performance, robustness and the significance of performance differences. The metrics used for evaluation are PCCs and RMSE between the predicted CTPs and ground truths in the test set.

Fig. 2A shows the distribution of PCCs in the test set, where PCCs between MiTCP’s predictions and ground truth are higher and closer to 1 than that of DLEPS on the test set. Fig. 2B and C show the results of five repeated experiments where for each experiment the average PCC and RMSE were calculated for all data in the test set. The average PCC of five experiments is 0.482 for MiTCP, higher than 0.425 for DLEPS and 0.441 for TranSiGen, and the average RMSE is 0.422 for MiTCP, lower than 0.45 for DLEPS and 0.44 for TranSiGen. These results indicate that MiTCP outperforms DLEPS in predicting CTP induced by molecules, and the P-values of the t-test (all ˂10−4) indicate that these performance improvements are statistically significant. Moreover we consider a more challenging task: predicting the CTP of most differentially expressed genes. For each true CTP vector induced by molecules in the test set, we selected the top 50 genes with the highest absolute CTP values as the most differentially expressed genes for the calculation of PCC and RMSE between predictions and ground truth. The average PCC of MiTCP on the test set is 0.801 (0.746 for DLEPS and 0.766 for TranSiGen), and the average RMSE is 1.021 (1.07 for DLEPS and 1.061 for TranSiGen), exhibiting a significant improvement compared to DLEPS and TranSiGen, shown in Fig. 2D–F. We also performed comparison experiments under scaffold splitting, a data splitting strategy to test the model performance on unseen molecule structures. The results in Fig. S3 shown that MiTCP still exhibits a significantly better performance than baseline methods under scaffold splitting. Fig. 2G shows 15 scatter plots of true CTPs, induced by molecules in the test set, versus predictions of MiTCP with top 15 PCCs. More scatter plots are shown in Fig. S1.

To demonstrate the generalizability of MiTCP on external dataset, we conducted validation experiment on sci-Plex3 [27] dataset, which contains CTPs of 187 molecules. We used the MiTCP trained on L1000 to make predictions on sci-Plex3, and for each molecule we counted the number of genes whose direction of gene expression (upregulate or downregulate) are correctly predicted. The results in Fig. S8 show that MiTCP exhibits better generalizability on external dataset. We also performed experiments to test MiTCP's generalizabillity across different cell lines, and the results in Table S1 and S2 show that it exhibits better generalizability across different cell lines than cell line specific models.

Combination of graph-based multi-scale molecule embedding and gene co-expression embedding enables MiTCP to predict CTP precisely

MiTCP is mainly composed of a molecule encoder and a gene encoder, and combines their embeddings for CTP prediction. To demonstrate that these two components of the model enable MiTCP to achieve more accurate predictions compared to DLEPS, we conducted the following experiments.

First, to demonstrate the molecular embeddings carry information about transcriptional changes, we did the experiment as follows: we paired all molecules and calculated structural similarity of each pair. Subsequently, we divided them into Group A (structurally similar molecule pairs) and Group B (structurally dissimilar molecule pairs), using the average structural similarity as threshold. We then calculated the similarity of CTP for the two groups separately. The mean CTP similarity for Group A is 0.234, while for Group B it is 0.128. The p value between the two groups was 1.096 × 10−10, indicating a significant difference in CTP similarity between the two groups. This result shows that molecules with closer embeddings exhibit more similar transcriptional change profiles compared to those with distant embeddings, demonstrating the critical importance of molecular structural information for predicting CTP. Then we tested the influence of different molecule encoders/embeddings on the model performance. We compared four molecule encoders/embeddings: Morgan fingerprints of molecules, grammar-VAE which is used in DLEPS, D-MPNN, and a multi-scale molecule embedding which concatenates D-MPNN embedding with Morgan fingerprints. From the results shown in Fig. 3A and B, we can see that graph-based D-MPNN molecule embeddings enable MiTCP achieve more accurate CTP prediction, with lower RMSE and higher PCC than SMILES-based grammar-VAE and Morgan fingerprints. Compared to one-dimensional SMILES based molecular encoder, graph-based molecule encoding methods can capture the topological structural information of molecules, which is critical for determining molecular properties and subsequently predicting CTP. Compared with Morgan fingerprints, which only indicate the presence of substructures in a molecule, graph-based methods can capture atomic-level features, thereby facilitating more precise predictions. Moreover, we found that better performance can be reached by combining the Morgan fingerprints and D-MPNN embeddings. This indicates that multi-scale molecular representations can provide more comprehensive information for CTP prediction task.

We further explore how MiTCP’s performance is impacted by perturbation of the input molecules, such as modifying atoms. We searched in L1000 for molecule pairs with high structural similarity to simulate perturbation on molecules. We finally found 34 pairs with Tanimoto structural similarity for each pair exceeding 0.75, differing by only one functional group or atom. We used the trained MiTCP model to predict the CTPs of these molecule pairs, and the results show that MiTCP exhibits a good predictive performance (see Supplementary file).

Moreover, we conducted ablation experiments comparing the performance of MiTCP with and without the gene encoder, as shown in Fig. 3C and D. It can be observed that the introduction of gene embeddings improves MiTCP’s performance on CTP prediction, decreasing the RMSE and increasing the PCC, and this effect is statistically significant (P-value <0.05). We also performed ablation experiments of the co-expression information, and the results are shown in Fig. S4.

Enrichment analysis based on predictions of MiTCP

To validate the biological relevance of MiTCP’s predictions and assess its potential in drug development, we selected several drug molecules which were excluded in the training set (their true transcriptional profiles are unknown to the model), and used the trained MiTCP model to predict their CTPs. Then we chose top 100 upregulated or downregulated genes in the predicted results and performed enrichment analysis to examine whether the enriched pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms are associated with the corresponding diseases targeted by the drugs.

We select three cancer drugs, palbociclib, irinotecan, and goserelin for validation. Palbociclib, approved by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2015, is an endocrine-based chemotherapeutic agent to treat HER2-negative and HR-positive advanced or metastatic breast cancer according to drugbank [28]. Palbociclib is a second generation cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor [29] that acts in the cell cycle machinery [30]. It can inhibit cell growth and suppresses DNA replication of cancer cells. We used trained MiTCP model to predict the CTPs of palbociclib and choose top 100 downregulated genes to conduct gene enrichment analysis. We list top 10 KEGG pathways and top five GO terms of biology process (Fig. 4A and B). The top pathways and GO terms are highly associated to cell cycle and cell division, such as ‘cell cycle’, ‘cell division’, ‘G2/M transition of mitotic cell cycle’, ‘mismatch repair’, ‘oocyte meiosis’, and ‘DNA replication’. In research [31], differentially expressed genes in palbociclib-resistant breast cancer cells were identified and also performed enrichment analysis. Our top pathways and terms ‘cell cycle’, ‘oocyte meiosis’, and ‘progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation’ are all in their top four terms.

Enrichment analysis of top 100 upregulated or downregulated genes induced by Palbociclib, irinotecan, and Goserelin, predicted by MiTCP. A, C, E, top 10 enriched KEGG pathways. B, D, F, top five enriched GO terms of biological process (BP).

Irinotecan is a topoisomerase inhibitor which can treat colorectal cancer [32], pancreatic adenocarcinoma [33] and various cancers [34]. It acts in cell cycle and can cause double-stranded breaks in DNA and apoptosis of cancer cells. We chose top 100 upregulated genes according to its CTPs predicted by MiTCP and performed enrichment analysis shown in Fig. 4C and D. Many top enriched pathways and GO terms are related to cancer or the mechanism of irinotecan, such as ‘pathways in cancer’, ‘breast cancer’, ‘gastric cancer’. We also analyzed the top downregulated genes (Fig. S2), and among the top terms are also cancer related, like ‘cell cycle’, ‘cell division’, and ‘cell proliferation’.

Goserelin is a drug to treat breast cancer [35] and prostate cancer [36]. It can inhibit the growth of cancer cells by reducing secretion of gonadotropins from the pituitary [28]. Similar analysis was performed on the top 100 upregulated genes (Fig. 4E and F) and among the top enriched terms ‘mitotic metaphase chromosome alignment’ and ‘negative regulation of cell cycle’ are related to the mechanisms of goserelin.

In addition to cancer drugs, we also conducted enrichment analysis on drugs for two metabolic diseases (obesity and type 2 diabetes), as shown in Fig. S6, also revealing pathways and GO terms associated with the diseases. Furthermore, in Fig. S5, by visualizing the interaction between drug targets and enriched pathway genes in the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, we explain how molecular perturbations lead to transcriptional changes. The above researches and evidence provide strong biological support for the predictions of MiTCP, and reveal the potential value of MiTCP in biological applications such as drug development.

DISCUSSION

In this work we proposed MiTCP, a method predicting changes in transcriptional profiles induced by molecules, which combines the information of molecules and genes. The multi-scale representations of molecular structures using D-MPNN and the introduction of gene co-expression embeddings based on GNN enable MiTCP to achieve more accurate predictions of CTP. Moreover, the enrichment analysis on some drug molecules reveals the potential of MiTCP in biological applications such as drug development, and result in Fig. S7 shows that MiTCP has a good scalability. We believe that MiTCP holds significant application value in phenotype-based drug discovery.

Limitations

MiTCP provides a framework for predicting molecular perturbation-induced CTP. It can be observed that there is still significant room for improvement in the predictive performance of MiTCP. On the one hand, the structural information of molecules and the transcriptional profiles represent two entirely distinct modalities. Learning the mapping relationship between these two modalities is a challenging task. On the other hand, the limitations of the data volume and high noise restrict the model’s performance.

One limitation of MiTCP is the interpretability. Despite demonstrating promising CTP prediction performance, the underlying mechanisms of interactions between molecules and genes in MiTCP remain unknown, making model interpretability a key direction for future research. Moreover, the model training requires more high-quality data. Although L1000 is the largest molecule-induced differential transcriptome database, the number of molecules therein is quite limited, and the inherent noise in the dataset can also influence model predictions.

Future work

In future work, novel molecular and gene embedding methods [37] can be explored to further improve the predictive performance of MiTCP. Furthermore, several strategies can be tried to enhance the interpretability of MiTCP. First, we can introduce attention mechanism, and through attention scores, we can know the interaction strength between molecules and different genes, thereby inferring the pathways affected by the molecules. Also, for molecules with similar perturbation effects, we can observe whether they have similar substructures or scaffolds. This analysis can further guide molecular synthesis efforts.

METHODS

Data processing

The data used for training MiTCP is from LINCS L1000 Project [25], a dataset containing gene expression profiles of 978 landmark genes and 11 350 additional genes under genetic (shRNA, cDNA) and chemical (molecules) perturbations at different time points, doses and cell lines. The expression of 978 landmark genes are experimentally measured while the 11 350 additional genes are computationally inferred through the transformation matrix, hence we only used the data of landmark genes for model training.

The L1000 data is composed of two phases, with each phase provided at five levels of the data processing pipeline. We downloaded data of level 5, differential gene expression profiles represented by z-score, from Gene Expression Omnibus with accession number GSE92742 for phase I and GSE70138 for phase II. For each phase, the same data cleaning was performed as follows: we retained differential gene expression profiles of chemical perturbations, and deleted perturbations (molecules) with less than five replicates. For each molecule we averaged all profiles ignoring time, dose, cell lines and other details. Then we removed duplicate molecules according to their InChI key in case different SMILES correspond to the same molecule. Finally we took union of phase I and II, getting more than 11 000 chemically inducible changes in transcriptional profiles (CTPs) of 978 landmark genes , where is the number of molecules. The goal of MiTCP is to input the structure of small molecules and predict their perturbational effects on the transcriptome, which is represented by a 978-dimensional CTP vector.

Molecule embedding

We used Chemprop [22] as our molecule encoder. Chemprop is a molecule encoding method based on D-MPNN. It viewed molecules as graphs, where atoms are nodes and chemical bonds are edges. Different from traditional MPNN, which performs message passing on nodes (atoms), D-MPNN performs message passing on directed edges to prevent message totters. Besides, the multidimensional edge features enable the learned molecule embeddings contain richer chemical information. Hence Chemprop performs well in multiple molecule property prediction tasks.

The chemprop embeddings can be concatenated with Morgan fingerprints, so that the embeddings can characterize molecules at multiple scales, including both atomic and functional group information. In our experiments, we set the number of message passing steps as 3, and the dimension of molecule embedding as 300.

Training MiTCP

We split the data as training, validation and test set in the ratio of 8:1:1. The validation set is used to monitor the training process to prevent model overfitting. Metrics for performance evaluation

To evaluate the similarity between the predictions of MiTCP and ground truth, the root mean squared error and PCC of molecules in test set are calculated. RMSE primarily evaluates the error of predicted values compared to actual values, while PCC measures the linear correlation between predicted and actual values. Compared with RMSE, PCC is more sensitive to changes in extreme values (Fig. S10). In model performance evaluation, we recommend a comprehensive consideration of both metrics.
