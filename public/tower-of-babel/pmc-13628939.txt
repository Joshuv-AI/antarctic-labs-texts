Multivariate analysis of 110 green super rice (GSR) lines for morpho-physiological diversity attributes
Authors: Muhammad Shahzad Ahmed, Ahmed M. Abdelghany, Abid Majeed, Faiza Siddique, Sobhi F. Lamlom, Rana Arsalan Javaid, Huhammad Jalal Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetic diversity present in rice genotypes plays a crucial role in improving breeding strategies and developing high-quality cultivars. This study evaluated genetic diversity, agronomic performance, and disease resistance across 110 unique rice cultivars over two growing seasons (2021–2022) using an alpha-lattice design. Ten morpho-physiological and yield characteristics were assessed along with resistance to brown leaf spot (BLS) in natural field conditions. The combined analysis of variance indicated highly significant genotypic effects (P < 0.01) across all traits, with broad-sense heritability values varying from 8.83% for days to maturity to 45.56% for plant height. K-means clustering effectively categorized genotypes into five distinct groups. Cluster 4 demonstrated the highest yield at 10.64 t ha⁻¹, characterized by early flowering, whereas Cluster 5 attained significant productivity of 10.2 t ha⁻¹ due to its large grain size. The distribution of BLS resistance demonstrated considerable variation across clusters (χ² = 50.1, P < 0.001). Cluster 1 presented 60% highly resistant genotypes, while Cluster 2 displayed 60% resistant genotypes, with no entries classified as susceptible. Notable performers comprised NGSR.18 with a multi-trait score of 70.77%, GSR1 achieving a yield of 12.76 t ha⁻¹, and GSR34, GSR49, GSR50 exhibiting high resistance to BLS. The correlation analysis demonstrated genetic independence between yield and disease resistance, thereby supporting the feasibility of simultaneous improvement. The findings offer significant genetic resources for the development of high-yielding, disease-resistant rice cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-025-08049-6.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is currently the most significant crop worldwide, and it is a staple food in many countries worldwide [1]. Rice is cultivated in more than 100 countries and about 168 million hectares is under rice cultivation worldwide with the production of 800 million tons FAOSTAT (FAO, 2023). Global consumption of rice is around 502 million tons during 2020-21, whereas it was 437.18 million tons during last 10 years [2]. Major consumption of rice is in Asia where per capita consumption of rice is 45 kg per year [3].

In Pakistan rice is the second main staple food major source foreign exchange earnings [4]. The share of rice in agricultural value addition is 2.5% whereas its contribution in the total GDP of Pakistan is 0.6%. During 2023–2024, area under rice cultivation was 3.6 million hectares, and the total rice production was 9.9 million tons [5]. Pakistan earns US$ 3.36 billion by exporting half of its rice and the rest half is consumed locally. World population is expected to be 10 billion during 2050 and more diverse and climate resilient crops are required to meet the food security challenges. Future predictions suggest that rice demand will increase by up to 40% more than the present world yield by 2025 [6].

Rice production faces multiple challenges under the changing climatic scenario worldwide. In Pakistan rice production faces multiple challenges i.e. drought stress, heat stress, flood & submergence condition in moon soon season in rice growing areas, biotic factors like bacterial leaf blight and brown leaf spot (BLS) at later stages of rice crop combined with a heat wave [7, 8]. BLS caused by fungus Bipolaris oryzae, is a severe threat to rice crop and leading major reduction in yield and quality, some report suggests that BLS caused huge yield losses of up to 90% when the disease severely impacted Bengal in mid-20th century [6]. Genetic variation in local germplasm is less due to a narrow genetic base for both biotic and abiotic factors. Availability of quality is a major issue. More than 70% of the area under Pakistan rice cultivation consists of coarse rice varieties. Among the coarse rice cultivars, hybrid rice is gaining more popularity but getting fresh hybrid seeds every year is cost intensive and beyond the reach of poor farming community. Open pollinated cultivars are best suited for developing countries and production of basic and certified seeds is within the reach of farming community. Among coarse groups of rice, green super rice (GSR) offers its superior climatic resilience and yield attributing traits. It is developed by collective efforts of scientists from China and IRRI, Philippines.

Before introducing into a breeding program for selection or hybridization as parent, germplasm evaluation for variability and morpho-physiological assessment is a pre-requisite. Genetic variation in the rice gene pool has prime importance for the initiation of a breeding program. Variation in the gene pool is of two types, phenotypic variation (PV) and genetic variation (GV), The genotypic variation represents the heritability that is present within population and between populations. Genetic divergence is needed to choose parental lines for hybridization, define heterotic groups, estimate genetic distance and diversity, and forecast gene action in following generations, all of which depend on germplasm genetic variability [9]. There are several ways to determine variability in large populations with multiple traits datasets but multivariate analysis by using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [10–12] and cluster analysis is more common analysis in rice morpho-physiological based variability estimation [13, 14].

In the present study, a set of 110 GSR advanced lines including two check cultivars (KSK-434 and IR-06) were acquired from Plant Genetic Resource Institute of Bio Resource Conservation Institute (PGRI-BCI), National Agricultural Research Center (NARC) Islamabad Pakistan. The objective of this study was to evaluate genetic divergence based on morpho-physiological traits and BLS disease response. Best performing genotypes with stable high yield and disease resilience will be identified for future breeding programs as selection and parental lines for hybridization.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and genetic material

The field experiment was carried out during the 2021 and 2022 growing seasons at the Rice Program, Crop Sciences Institute, National Agricultural Research Center (NARC), located in Islamabad, Pakistan (33°42’N, 73°10’E). The research location is situated on the Potohar Plateau, positioned between the Margalla Hills, with an elevation varying from 460 to 610 m above sea level. The experimental material comprised 110 diverse coarse rice genotypes sourced from two institutions: the Bioresource Conservation Institute-Plant Genetic Resource Institute (BCI-PGRI), NARC, Islamabad, and the National Institute of Genomics and Advanced Biotechnology (NIGAB), NARC, Islamabad. The comprehensive list of rice germplasm utilized in this study is provided in Table S1.

The climatic conditions showed seasonal variations with temperatures ranging from 15.27 to 31.57 °C across both years. Rainfall patterns varied significantly, with July receiving the highest precipitation (5.18–5.64 mm) and November the lowest (0.07–2.15 mm). Monthly rainfall ranged from 1.44 to 6.79 mm during the growing period. These environmental conditions supported rice cultivation and natural BLS disease development throughout both experimental seasons (Table 1).

Experimental design and agronomic management

The 110 rice genotypes were organized in an alpha-lattice design involving two replications, consisting of 10 blocks per replication and 11 genotypes per block. This design enabled effective management of spatial variability while accommodating the extensive range of genotypes in the study.

The nursery establishment utilized plastic trays featuring drainage pores, with dimensions of 45.7 cm in length, 30.5 cm in width, and 5 cm in height. Before sowing, rice seeds underwent treatment with Hombre® pesticide (Bayer) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Each tray was filled with silt soil and inoculated with 100 g of treated seeds, with the surface amended using farmyard manure. Standard agronomic practices were employed for nursery management, incorporating recommended fertilizer application, while manual weed removal was conducted to ensure optimal seedling development for subsequent transplanting.

Seedling transplantation was conducted using the puddled method for land preparation. Rice seedlings, aged one month, were transplanted with two seedlings allocated per hill. The spacing between plants and rows was maintained at 20 cm. The plot dimensions were established at three m in length and two m in width, accommodating a total of 10 rows of plants, resulting in 150 plants per plot. Fertilizer was applied at rates of 120 kg ha− 1 of nitrogen (N), 90 kg ha− 1 of phosphorus (P2O5), and 60 kg ha− 1 of potassium (K2O), utilizing urea, diammonium phosphate, and muriate of potash. The full doses of phosphorus and potassium were applied during land preparation, while one-third of the nitrogen was applied at that time. The remaining nitrogen was applied in two splits at 35 and 55 days post-transplanting. Continuous flooding irrigation was administered for 30 days post-transplantation, followed by weekly irrigation intervals thereafter. Irrigation was administered 20–30 days prior to harvesting. Weeding was performed using conventional manual methods.

Agronomic trait measurements

Agronomic data was recorded on ten randomly selected plants from each genotype for the following traits: Plant height (PH, cm) was measured from ground level to the tip of the tallest panicle at physiological maturity. Days to 50% flowering (DTF) was recorded when 50% of the plants in each plot had emerged panicles with visible anthers. Tillers per plant (TPP) were counted at maturity stage by recording the total number of productive tillers bearing panicles. Panicle length (PL, cm) was measured from the base of the panicle to its tip on the main tiller. Grains per panicle (GPP) were determined by counting the total number of filled grains from five randomly selected panicles per plant.

Thousand grain weight (TGW) was measured by counting and weighing 1000 randomly selected filled grains and expressed in grams. Days to maturity (DM) was recorded when 85% of the grains in each plot reached physiological maturity. Paddy yield (PY) was measured as the total grain weight per plot and converted to kg ha− 1. Normalized Difference Vegetative Index (NDVI) was measured using a handheld NDVI meter during the vegetative growth stage to assess plant vigor. Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) values were recorded using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter to estimate leaf chlorophyll content during the active tillering stage.

Assessment of BLS

The assessment of BLS disease was performed under natural field conditions, employing the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) Standard Evaluation System for Rice [15], without the use of artificial inoculation. Disease assessment utilized a 0–9 scoring scale based on the Standard Evaluation System (SES) for rice [15], determined by the percentage of leaf area affected by BLS lesions. The assessment occurred during the reproductive phase, at which point disease symptoms were most evident. The disease scoring scale and associated resistance categories are presented in Table 2.

Homogeneity of variances between years was confirmed using Levene’s test (P > 0.05) [16], supporting the validity of the combined analysis. Data Preparation and quality control

Prior to statistical analysis, all raw data were examined for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk. Data that deviated significantly from normality (P < 0.05) were transformed using appropriate transformations (log, square root, or Box-Cox transformation) to meet the assumptions of parametric tests. Outliers were identified using the interquartile range (IQR) method, where values exceeding Q3 + 1.5×IQR or below Q1–1.5×IQR were flagged and verified against field records.

Multivariate analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all 11 agronomic traits across 110 genotypes, with data distribution visualized through density plots and violin plots combined with box plots. Phenotypic correlations among traits were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients, with statistical significance tested at P < 0.05 and results displayed as color-coded correlation matrices. Prior to correlation analysis, multicollinearity among traits was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 10 indicating severe multicollinearity. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to identify patterns of variation among genotypes and reduce data dimensionality. Before conducting PCA, the following assumptions were verified: (1) adequacy of sample size (n = 110 genotypes with 11 variables met the minimum ratio of 10:1), (2) suitability of data for factor analysis assessed using Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (KMO > 0.6 considered acceptable) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < 0.05), and (3) linearity among variables examined through scatterplot matrices.

All trait data were standardized (z-score transformation) prior to PCA to ensure variables with different units and scales contributed equally to the analysis. PCA was based on eigenvalue decomposition: (S - λI)v = 0, where S represents the correlation matrix (due to standardization), λ is the eigenvalue indicating variance explained by each component, and v is the corresponding eigenvector. Principal components with eigenvalues > 1 (Kaiser criterion) were retained, collectively explaining a substantial proportion of the total variance.

Multivariate analysis included hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance on standardized trait values and Ward’s linkage method to minimize within-cluster variance, and K-means clustering (k = 5) to identify distinct genetic groups. The optimal number of clusters was determined through elbow method (plotting within-cluster sum of squares against number of clusters) and silhouette analysis (average silhouette width > 0.5 indicating reasonable cluster structure). BLS resistance was evaluated using a 1–9 scale and categorized into six classes (HR, R, MR, MS, S, HS). The relationship between genetic clusters and disease resistance was examined through cross-tabulation and chi-square tests. All statistical analyses were performed with α = 0.05 significance level.

Software and packages

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023) in RStudio version 2023.06.1. Combined analysis of variance for the alpha-lattice design was conducted using “lme4” v1.1–34 for linear mixed models with REML estimation. Data manipulation used “dplyr” v1.1.2, while descriptive statistics and normality tests employed “psych” v2.3.6 and base R functions. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor from “car” v3.1–2. Principal component analysis was performed with “FactoMineR” v2.8, and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin and Bartlett’s tests used “psych” v2.3.6. Hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, Ward’s linkage) and K-means clustering utilized “cluster” v2.1.4, with optimal cluster determination via “factoextra” v1.0.7. Visualizations employed “ggplot2” v3.4.2, “corrplot” v0.92, and “pheatmap” v1.0.12. Statistical tests used “agricolae” v1.3–6 and base R, with α = 0.05 significance level.

Results

Significant genetic variation and genotype × environment interactions detected across rice germplasm

The combined analysis of variance across two growing seasons revealed significant variation for most morpho-physiological and yield-related traits among the 110 rice genotypes evaluated (Table 3). Year effects were highly significant (P < 0.01) for 8 out of 10 traits examined, including PH, DTF, GPP, TGW, DM, PY, NDVI, and SPAD chlorophyll readings, with TPP showing moderate significance (P < 0.05). Only PL demonstrated non-significant year effects, suggesting relatively stable expression of this trait across environmental conditions. Genotypic effects were highly significant (P < 0.01) for all ten traits evaluated. The magnitude of genotypic variance components was considerable across all traits, with mean squares ranging from 10.64 for PL to 2593.90 for GPP, providing the genetic basis for the substantial phenotypic diversity observed in descriptive statistics (Descriptive Statistics and Distribution Analysis) and cluster analysis (Phenotypic clustering reveals distinct genetic groups within the rice germplasm collection). The genotype × year interactions were significant for 7 out of 10 traits, with DTF, GPP, TGW, DM, and PY showing highly significant interactions (P < 0.01), while PH and SPAD readings demonstrated moderate significance (P < 0.05). Non-significant genotype × year interactions were observed for TPP, PL, and NDVI.

Genetic components of variance and heritability estimates

The estimation of genetic parameters revealed substantial variation in the magnitude of variance components and heritability estimates across the ten morpho-physiological and yield-related traits evaluated (Table 4). Genotypic variance components ranged from 0.0014 for NDVI to 2127.00 for GPP, reflecting the diverse genetic architecture underlying different trait categories. Environmental variance components were consistently larger than their corresponding genotypic components for all traits, with ratios of environmental to genotypic variance ranging from 1.19 for PH to 22.84 for DM. Phenotypic variance, representing the total observable variation, exhibited the widest range among variance components, with values spanning from 0.0052 for NDVI to 7383.92 for GPP. The partitioning of phenotypic variance revealed that environmental factors contributed 54.44% to 91.17% of the total phenotypic variation across traits, while genetic factors accounted for 8.83% to 45.56% of the observed variation.

For H2 estimates, the results demonstrated considerable variation among traits, ranging from 8.83% for DM to 45.56% for PH. Based on the magnitude of heritability estimates, traits could be categorized into three distinct groups. High heritability traits (H² > 35%) included PH (45.56%), PL (39.67%), and PY (37.40%), indicating strong genetic control and favorable prospects for direct selection. Moderate heritability traits (H² = 20–35%) comprised DTF (29.44%), GPP (28.81%), and NDVI (26.92%), suggesting moderate genetic influence with reasonable selection potential under controlled conditions. Low heritability traits (H² < 20%) encompassed TGW (20.67%), SPAD value (19.65%), TPP (16.52%), and DM (8.83%), indicating predominant environmental control and limited effectiveness of direct phenotypic selection. Overall, the heritability estimates provided insights for further analyses: traits exhibiting high heritability (PH: 45.56%, PL: 39.67%, PY: 37.40%) demonstrated consistent clustering patterns (Phenotypic clustering reveals distinct genetic groups within the rice germplasm collection). In contrast, traits with low heritability (DM: 8.83%, TPP: 16.52%) exhibited increased within-cluster variation, aligning with the significant G×E interactions previously reported.

Discussion

The noteworthy significant genotype × year interactions observed for seven of ten traits, especially yield-related parameters, underscore the variability of gene expression in response to different environmental conditions. This pattern aligns with recent multi-environment studies that demonstrate significant genotype-environment interaction effects on rice yield traits across various growing seasons [18, 19]. In contrast, the non-significant interactions noted for TPP, PL, and NDVI indicate that these traits demonstrate enhanced phenotypic stability, thereby rendering them potentially more dependable selection targets for breeding programs in varied environments [20]. Contemporary genomic studies have revealed similar patterns, indicating high phenotypic correlations between environments for architectural traits, while correlations for grain yield remain low [21].

Furthermore, H2 estimates observed across the ten evaluated traits reveal distinct genetic architectures that align with the established understanding of quantitative trait inheritance in rice. The H2 estimates for PH (45.56%) and PL (39.67%) correspond with contemporary research performed in highland rice ecosystems [22], suggesting that these morphological traits demonstrate consistent genetic control across diverse environmental conditions. This pattern supports the hypothesis that structural traits exhibit enhanced genetic canalization compared to yield components, which typically show higher sensitivity to environmental factors [23]. For DM, H2 is notably low at 8.83%, which stands in contrast to earlier studies that have documented higher values for phenological traits, exceeding 60% (Poli et al., 2013). This suggests that the GSR germplasm may exhibit unique allelic diversity affecting maturation timing.

The observed H2 values for yield-related traits, specifically PY at 37.40% and GPP at 28.81%, also indicate a multifaceted polygenic structure consistent with the principles of quantitative trait theory. These findings align with contemporary research suggesting a moderate level of genetic progress concerning yield components [24], indicating that the germplasm possesses sufficient additive genetic variance for enhancement through breeding, while also acknowledging the considerable influence of environmental factors on traits related to grain production. That is, the prevalence of the environmental variance component, which varies between 54.44% and 91.17% across different traits, underscores the critical importance of multi-environment evaluation protocols in rice breeding initiatives [25].

The identification of five distinct phenotypic clusters within the germplasm collection illustrates a structured genetic diversity that can be effectively employed for the optimization of breeding programs [26]. The disparities in cluster distribution suggest a dominant genetic background, potentially reflecting historical selection pressures or founder effects linked to the GSR program [27]. Cluster 4 demonstrates early flowering at 96.18 days, a high grain count per panicle of 180.36 grains, and a yield performance of 10.64 t ha− 1, highlighting its considerable breeding potential. This cluster demonstrates that early maturity does not inherently reduce yield potential, challenging traditional notions about the trade-offs between developmental timing and grain production [28]. The genetic framework underlying this combination requires further investigation through genomic methodologies to identify associated quantitative trait loci. The differing strategies identified in Clusters 4 and 5 illustrate unique evolutionary methods for optimizing yield. Cluster 4 achieves higher yields through an increase in grain quantity, while Cluster 5 compensates for reduced tillering by enhancing the weight of individual grains (28.27 g). This contradiction exemplifies the conventional trade-offs described in yield component balance theory and provides breeding programs with diverse strategies for yield enhancement tailored to specific target environments and market requirements [29, 30].

The evaluation of BLS resistance indicates a significant correlation between plant architecture and susceptibility to disease. The observed positive correlations between BLS susceptibility and PL (r = 0.3) as well as PH (r = 0.257) indicate that selecting for increased plant stature could unintentionally elevate disease susceptibility. Recent biochemical studies indicate that the pathogenesis of Bipolaris oryzae is characterized by intricate host-pathogen interactions that are affected by plant morphology and the microenvironment [31]. The relationship between architecture and disease has considerable implications for the development of breeding strategies. Brown spot disease results in approximately 4% yield losses worldwide and is recognized as a re-emerging threat in the context of climate change [32]. The observed correlations indicate that breeding programs should strategically balance the selection of favorable architectural traits with the preservation of disease resistance.

The identification of 38 genotypes (34.5%) demonstrating resistant responses, along with 10 genotypes (9.1%) exhibiting high resistance, establishes a significant genetic foundation for resistance breeding. The low frequency of highly resistant genotypes indicates that strong resistance alleles are likely rare in this population, highlighting the need for careful conservation and strategic deployment of these genetic resources [8].

The phenotypic diversity patterns identified in the GSR collection demonstrate both similarities and differences when compared to other global rice germplasm assessments. The coefficient of variation values, ranging from 3.15% to 23.83%, are consistent with earlier findings from Taiwanese rice collections, which reported comparable variability ranges through the use of molecular markers [33]. The observed distribution of variation among trait categories indicates a distinct genetic architecture within this germplasm. The observed clustering patterns contrast with those documented in pigmented rice germplasm, where molecular markers indicated distinct population structure patterns. This divergence likely indicates the unique breeding history and selection pressures encountered during the development of GSR, leading to distinct allelic frequencies in comparison to landraces and traditional varieties. The correlation structure among traits, notably the significant positive association between PL and PH (r = 0.7), indicates coordinated genetic control mechanisms that are consistent across various rice populations [24]. This pattern corresponds with recent research employing SSR markers to evaluate genetic diversity, affirming the universal characteristics of developmental trait coordination in rice.

Overall, the incorporation of heritability estimates, genotype-environment interaction effects, and phenotypic clustering outcomes offers a definitive strategic framework for the optimization of breeding programs. The identification of traits with high heritability, such as PH, PL, and PY, indicates that direct phenotypic selection is likely to be effective for these characteristics, especially when implemented across diverse environments to address genotype-environment interaction effects [25]. Genomic selection methodologies may demonstrate advantages over phenotypic selection for these traits (Monteverde et al., 2023), as molecular markers can elucidate genetic effects that are masked by environmental variability. The identified disease resistance patterns require a balanced breeding strategy that integrates agronomic performance with pathogen resistance. The creation of selection indices that balance yield potential with disease resistance is essential for breeding varieties adapted to varied production environments characterized by fluctuating BLS pressure [8].

Conclusions

This extensive examination of the GSR germplasm collection revealed genetic diversity across numerous characteristic categories, aiding breeding program optimization and variety development. Strategic genetic improvement for productivity, disease resistance, and adaptation to varied production systems begins with the discovery of superior genotypes, comprehension of genetic factors, and analysis of trait connections.

Genomic profiling of superior genotypes should be done to understand phenotypic variation and find breeding markers. Genomic selection and phenotypic evaluation will improve breeding efficiency and genetic merit prediction across traits and habitats. Detailed physiological examinations of the trait correlations found in this work will provide mechanistic insights into plant development and resource allocation patterns to improve breeding strategies.

This work suggests pathotype-specific evaluations and molecular characterization of resistance genes to investigate disease resistance diversity. Long-term rice production disease management will benefit from resistance gene pyramiding tactics using recognized resistant genotypes. Finally, the genetic clustering patterns imply heterotic group identification and hybrid breeding applications that should be investigated through future systematic crossover and evaluation programs.
