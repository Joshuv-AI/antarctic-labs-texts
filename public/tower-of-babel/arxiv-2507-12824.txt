Invariant subalgebras rigidity for von Neumann algebras of groups arising as certain semidirect products
Tattwamasi Amrutam, Artem Dudko, Yongle Jiang, Adam Skalski

Abstract
. We study the ISR (von Neumann invariant subalgebra rigidity) property for certain discrete groups arising as semidirect products from algebraic actions on certain 2-torsion groups, mostly arising as direct products of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} . We present, in particular, the first example of an amenable group with the ISR property that admits a non-trivial abelian normal subgroup. Several other examples are discussed, notably including an infinite amenable group whose von Neumann algebra admits precisely one invariant von Neumann subalgebra which does not come from a normal subgroup. We also investigate the form of invariant subalgebras of the group von Neumann algebra of the standard lamplighter group.

Primary: 46L10; Secondary 22D25

1.
Introduction

The study of von Neumann algebras of discrete groups dates back to the very origins of operator algebra theory, as these appeared already in the foundational work of Murray and von Neumann. It was understood even then that the group von Neumann algebra
L(G)
of a discrete group
G
is a factor—i.e., it contains no nontrivial weakly closed ideals—if and only if
G
is ICC (i.e., all nontrivial conjugacy classes are infinite). A von Neumann ideal in
L(G)
is automatically an
invariant
subalgebra, in the sense that it is preserved under the natural adjoint action of
G
. In recent years, beginning with the work of Kalantar and Panagopoulos
[
13
]
and its predecessor due to Alekseev and Brugger
[
1
]
, there has been renewed interest in the study of general invariant subalgebras. Notably, maximal abelian invariant subalgebras of
L(G)
serve as prototypical examples of
Cartan subalgebras
, which have long played a central role in the theory of von Neumann algebras (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
18
]
).

It is easy to see that every normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
yields an invariant subalgebra
L(N)\subseteq L(G)
; these should be viewed as the “obvious” ones. In
[
13
]
, Kalantar and Panagopoulos proved that if
G
is a lattice in a higher-rank Lie group, then all invariant subalgebras of
L(G)
arise from normal subgroups. This was taken up in
[
2
]
, where the authors formally introduced the
ISR
(invariant subalgebra rigidity) property for a discrete countable group
G
, understood as the non-existence of “non-obvious” (or “exotic”) invariant subalgebras of
L(G)
. They also showed that noncommutative free groups, and more generally certain “negatively curved” groups and their finite direct products, have the ISR property. The results of
[
2
]
were later partially generalized in
[
4
]
.

It is not difficult to see that an infinite group with the ISR property must be ICC (
[
2
, Proposition 3.1]
). However, in contrast to factoriality, it does not appear to be easy to characterize the ISR property in simple group-theoretic terms. The examples given in
[
13
]
,
[
2
]
, and
[
4
]
mentioned above were all non-amenable; in fact, the first example of an infinite amenable group with the ISR property—namely
S_{\infty}
—was pointed out in
[
12
]
. The proof of the main result in
[
12
]
relied on detailed knowledge of the indecomposable characters of
S_{\infty}
. After that, the so-called character approach was applied in
[
5
]
to prove, among other things, the ISR property for a broad class of approximately finite groups arising as symmetry groups of simple Bratteli diagrams.

In fact, the only apparent obstruction to the ISR property for amenable groups, noted in
[
2
, Example 3.5]
, has been the existence of non-trivial abelian normal subgroups. The construction in
[
2
, Example 3.5]
shows that if
G
contains a normal abelian subgroup with elements of order at least 3, then
G
cannot have the ISR property. This observation serves as the motivation for the study undertaken in this paper, where we construct the first example of an amenable group
G
with a non-trivial normal abelian subgroup such that
G
possesses the ISR property. By the above discussion, the abelian part of such a group must consist entirely of elements of order
2
—that is, it must be a
2
-torsion group. It is then natural to consider wreath-type actions on infinite direct products of 2-torsion groups. A modification of this idea leads to the following result; we refer to the beginning of Section
3
for the detailed definition of the group appearing in the statement.

Theorem A
.

The group
S(2^{\infty})\ltimes\tilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
(which is amenable, ICC, and admits a non-trivial abelian normal subgroup) has the ISR property.

In view of the above, it is natural to ask what happens for semidirect products involving actions on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}:=\bigoplus_{\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. It turns out that we need not obtain the ISR property, but may witness a different interesting behaviour, as exemplified by the next statement, where
\textup{Aut}_{\textup{fin}}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})
denotes the group of all
finitary
automorphisms of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})
.

Theorem B
.

The von Neumann algebra of the group
\textup{Aut}_{\textup{fin}}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
admits a unique invariant subalgebra which does not come from a normal subgroup.

We would like to mention that another case of a group
G
without the ISR property where all the invariant subalgebras of
L(G)
can be determined, namely
G=SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, was treated recently in
[
11
]
.

As noted in
[
2
, Example 3.5]
, the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, with
n\geq 3
, does not possess the ISR property, a failure rooted in the presence of order
n
elements in its normal abelian subgroup. This raises the natural question of whether a complete classification of invariant von Neumann subalgebras is possible when the abelian part consists only of order 2 elements. In this paper, we address this, providing detailed information on invariant subalgebras of the group von Neumann algebra of the standard lamplighter group and showing that the latter does not enjoy the ISR property. Relying on classical ergodic-theoretic tools, including relatively weakly mixing factors of Bernoulli shifts, we establish the following theorem.

Theorem C
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}=(\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
be the classical lamplighter group. Let
A=\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq L(G)
be a
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra. Then the following hold true:

(i)

there exists a normal subgroup
N\lhd G
such that
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}\cap L(A))\vee L(N)
, i.e.
\mathcal{P}
is generated as a von Neumann algebra by
P\cap L(A)
and
L(N)
;

(ii)

\mathcal{P}\cap L(\mathbb{Z})=L(k\mathbb{Z})
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(iii)

if the factor map
\pi:(\widehat{A},\text{Haar})\rightarrow(Y,\nu)
is relatively weakly mixing, where
L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)\cong\mathcal{P}\cap L(A)
, then
\mathcal{P}=L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)\rtimes k\mathbb{Z}
;

(iv)

G
does not have the ISR property.

To understand the role of property (i) above one can mention that its obvious analogue holds also in the case of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}
(
[
11
]
), but not in the case of
\textup{Aut}_{\textup{fin}}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
considered in Theorem
B
.

We also investigate the “most obvious” semidirect product
S_{\infty}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
. This group also does not have the ISR property, which we prove, constructing certain explicit “exotic” invariant subalgebras of its group von Neumann algebra.

The general line of argument for Theorem
A
, Theorem
B
and the results on
S_{\infty}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
is similar. We begin by noting that the semidirect products under consideration have only one non-trivial normal subgroup. We then proceed to analyze—essentially combinatorially—the possible invariant subalgebras of
L(G)
, exploiting properties of conditional expectations, a natural variation of the argument characterising the factoriality via the ICC property and a version of the ‘character method’ developed earlier in
[
5
]
.
As already mentioned, the techniques used to establish Theorem
C
are somewhat different, exploiting notions and methods of ergodic theory.

Organization of the paper

The detailed plan of the article is as follows: after this introduction, in Section
2
we discuss general properties of invariant subalgebras and corresponding conditional expectations. Section
3
treats the ISR example

S(2^{\infty})\ltimes\tilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
; here we prove Theorem
A
. In Section
4
we focus on
\textup{Aut}_{\textup{fin}}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
and establish Theorem
B
. The classical lamplighter case is taken up in Section
5
. It should not come as a surprise, then, that Theorem
C
is proven in this section. Finally, in Section
6
we gather certain observations concerning invariant subalgebras of
L(S_{\infty}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})
and present several examples.

2.
Preliminaries regarding invariant subalgebras

In this short section we gather the conventions and basic facts concerning invariant subalgebras of group von Neumann algebras. We shall follow the following convention throughout the paper.

Notation

•

The scalar products will be linear on the right side.

•

Throughout the paper, we will work with countable discrete groups.

•

If
G
is such a group, we will denote by
e
its unit element.

•

L(G)
is the von Neumann algebra associated to
G
, i.e. the von Neumann algebra generated in
B(\ell^{2}(G))
by the shift operators
u_{g},g\in G
, where
u_{g}\delta_{g^{\prime}}=\delta_{gg^{\prime}}
for every
g,g^{\prime}\in G
.

Recall that the canonical trace
\tau
on
L(G)
is defined via
\tau(x)=\langle\delta_{e},x\delta_{e}\rangle
for any
x\in L(G)
. Moreover, as
\tau
is faithful, the map
L(G)\ni x\mapsto x\delta_{e}\in\ell^{2}(G)
is injective.
For any
x\in L(G)
, we may write
x=\sum_{g\in G}c_{g}u_{g}
for its Fourier expansion (with
(c_{g})_{g\in G}\in\ell^{2}(G)
), which simply means that
x\delta_{e}=\sum_{g\in G}c_{g}\delta_{g}
;
write
\text{supp}(x)=\{g\in G:c_{g}\neq 0\}
for the
support
of
x
. We will often simply write
g
for
u_{g}
, viewing
g
as a unitary in
L(G)
, if no confusion may arise.

A von Neumann subalgebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
is said to be
G
-invariant
if for any
g\in G,m\in\mathcal{M}
we have
u_{g}mu_{g}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
; in other words
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under the natural adjoint action of
G
. In such a case denote by
E:L(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
the unique
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation onto
\mathcal{M}
; the commutant of
\mathcal{M}
is as usual denoted
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Let us recall some properties of
E
, needed to study invariant von Neumann subalgebras of
L(G)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
be a
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra, with the associated
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation
E:L(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
. Then

(1)

\tau(E(g)s)=\tau(E(g)E(s))=\tau(gE(s))
for all
s,g\in G
;

(2)

sE(g)s^{-1}=E(sgs^{-1})
for all
s,g\in G
;

(3)

sE(s^{-1})\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\cap L(G)
for any
s\in G
;

(4)

the formula
g\mapsto\tau(g^{-1}E(g))
defines a character (normalised,
G
-invariant, positive-definite function) on
G
;

(5)

for any
g\in G
we have
E(g)=0
if and only if
\tau(g^{-1}E(g))=0
, and
E(g)=g
if and only if
\tau(g^{-1}E(g))=1
.

Proof.

All the above are well-known; for (4) see for example
[
12
, Proposition 3.2]
.
∎

For any
G
and a set of elements
S\subseteq G
let
C_{G}(S):=\{g\in G:gs=sg\textup{ for all }s\in S\}
denote the
centralizer
of
S
. Given an element
g\in G
let us call an element
h\in G

finitely permuted by the centralizer
of
g
if
\sharp\{t^{-1}ht\mid t\in C_{G}(\{g\})\}<\infty
. Denote the set of such elements by
{\rm fpc}(g)
. The following statement seems well-known. For a proof, see
[
5
, Lemma 4.6]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
be a
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra, with the associated
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation
E:L(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
. Then
E(g)\in L({\rm fpc}(g))
for any
g\in G
.

If
N\subseteq G
is a subgroup, it is easy to see that we can identify in a natural way
L(N)
with a von Neumann subalgebra of
L(G)
(and the
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation
E:L(G)\to L(N)
is a natural extension of the map sending
u_{g}
to itself for
g\in N
and to
0
for
g\in G\setminus N
). If
N
is in addition
normal
,
L(N)
becomes an invariant von Neumann subalgebra of
L(G)
. The following definition is key for what follows.

Definition 2.3

(ISR-property)
.

We say that
G
has the ISR (invariant subalgebra rigidity) property if every invariant von Neumann subalgebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
is of the form
\mathcal{M}=L(N)
for some normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
.

We will occasionally refer to an invariant von Neumann subalgebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
as
exotic
if it is not of the form
L(N)
for
N\trianglelefteq G
. An infinite group
G
with the ISR property must be necessarily ICC by
[
2
, Proposition 3.1]
.
Sometimes, we would like to understand when certain subspaces are preserved under the canonical conditional expectation. The following technical lemma allows us to do so.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{B}
be an inclusion of von Neumann algebras, let
\mathcal{\tau}
be a faithful trace on
\mathcal{B}
and let
E:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{P}
be the
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation. Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}
be a von Neumann subalgebra and let
S\subset\mathcal{B}
be a subspace such that:

•

E(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{A}
;

•

E(S)\subseteq S+\mathcal{A}
;

•

\tau
restricted to
S\mathcal{A}
is equal to zero.

Then
E(S)\subseteq S
.

Proof.

For
s\in S
, let us write
E(s)=t+A
for some
t\in S
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Applying
E
on both sides, we get that

t+A=E(s)=E(t)+E(A).

Therefore,
E(t)=t+(A-E(A))
. Since
E(A)\in\mathcal{A}
,
A_{2}=A-E(A)\in\mathcal{A}
. Now,

E(t)E(t)^{*}=(t+A_{2})(t^{*}+A_{2}^{*})=tt^{*}+tA_{2}^{*}+A_{2}t^{*}+A_{2}A_{2}^{*}.

Since
\tau|_{S\mathcal{A}}=0
, applying
\tau
on both sides, we get that

\tau(tt^{*})+\tau(A_{2}A_{2}^{*})=\tau\left(E(t)E(t)^{*}\right)\leq\tau(E(tt^{*}))=\tau(tt^{*}).

This is turn implies that
\tau(A_{2}A_{2}^{*})=0
, hence,
A-E(A)=A_{2}=0
. Therefore,
E(t)=t
. Note that this implies
A=E(s-t)
. Now, since
\tau|_{S\mathcal{A}}=0
,

\tau(AA^{*})=\tau\left(E((s-t)A^{*})\right)=\tau\left((s-t)A^{*})\right)=0

This shows that
A=0
, establishing the claim.
∎

Note that the conditions of the above lemma imply that
S\cap\mathcal{A}=\{0\}
.

3.
Amenable ISR group with a non-trivial normal abelian subgroup

In this section we construct an example of an amenable ISR group with a non-trivial normal abelian subgroup.

Consider the sequence of symmetric groups
(S(2^{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with inclusions
S(2^{n})\to S(2^{n+1})
given by:
s\to(s,s)
. Denote by
S(2^{\infty})
the inductive limit
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}S(2^{n})
. Observe that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the group
S(2^{n})
acts as a group of permutations on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We let
S(2^{n})
act on
X=\{0,1\}^{\infty}
by:
s((x,y))=(s(x),y)
for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
y\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
. Then
S(2^{\infty})
can be viewed as the group of homeomorphisms of
X
whose action depends only on finitely many coordinates. For
g\in S(2^{\infty})
we will write
{\it supp}(g)=\{x\in X:g(x)\neq x\}
.

Let
C(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})=\{f:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}\;\text{continuous}\}
. Observe that each function in
C(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
can be written as

(3.1)

f_{A}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0\in\mathbb{Z}_{2},&x\in X\setminus A,\\
1\in\mathbb{Z}_{2},&x\in A,\end{array}\right.

where
A\subseteq X
is a clopen set. We will view
C(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
as a group with multiplication given by pointwise addition (modulo 2); note that in terms of elements
f_{A}
, multiplication corresponds to taking the symmetric difference of sets. The group
S(2^{\infty})
acts naturally on
C(X)
. Let
S=\{f_{X},f_{\varnothing}\}
. Then
S
is a subgroup of
C(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
fixed by the action of
S(2^{\infty})
. Let
\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})=C(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})/S
. For a clopen set
A\subseteq X
we will write
\widetilde{f}_{A}=f_{A}+S
for the relevant element of
\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Observe that
\widetilde{f}_{X\setminus A}=\widetilde{f}_{A}
for any clopen
A\subseteq X
; we will say that
A\subseteq X
is
non-trivial
if
A\neq\emptyset,A\neq X
.
Throughout this section, we will set

G=S(2^{\infty})\ltimes\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}).

We begin by analysing normal subgroups of
G
and as it turns out,
G
admits only one non-trivial normal subgroup.

Proposition 3.1
.

The only non-trivial normal subgroup of
G
is
\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
.

If
N\cap S(2^{\infty})\neq\{e\}
then
N\supseteq S(2^{\infty})
, since
S(2^{\infty})
is simple.

If
N\cap\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})\neq\{e\}
then
N\supseteq\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Indeed, let
A\subseteq X
be a non-trivial clopen set such that
\widetilde{f}_{A}\in N
. For any sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a non-trivial subset
A_{n}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
A=\bigcup\limits_{x\in A_{n}}[x],
where

(3.2)

[x]=\{(x,y):y\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}\}\subseteq X

is the cylinder set of level
n
corresponding to
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. In the group
S(2^{n})
of permutations on
\{0,1\}^{n}
there exists a permutation
s
such that
\#\{s(A_{n})\Delta A_{n}\}=2
. Then, for any
y\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
there exists
t\in S(2^{n})
such that
t(s(A_{n})\Delta A_{n})=y\times\{0,1\}
. We obtain that
[y]=t(s(A)\Delta A)
and so
\widetilde{f}_{[y]}\in N
. Since this is true for all sufficiently small cylinders in
X
, we obtain that
N\supseteq\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Now, assume that
N\neq\{e\}
and
N\neq\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Then the last paragraph implies that
g\cdot\widetilde{f}_{A}\in N
for some
g\in S(2^{\infty})\setminus\{e\}
and a (possibly trivial) clopen set
A\subseteq X
.

a)
Assume first that
g(A)\neq A
. One has:

N\ni g\widetilde{f}_{A}\cdot(g^{-1}\cdot g\widetilde{f}_{A}\cdot g)^{-1}=\widetilde{f}_{g(A)\Delta A}.

We obtain that
N\cap\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})\neq\{e\}
. Then, again by the last paragraph,
N\supseteq\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Thus
\widetilde{f}_{A}\in N
and further
g\in N
. By the first lines of the proof,
N\supseteq S(2^{\infty})
. It follows that
N=G
.

b)
Assume now that
g(A)=A
. Either
A\cap{\it supp}(g)\neq\varnothing
or
(X\setminus A)\cap{\it supp}(g)\neq\varnothing
. Take any
h\in S(2^{\infty})
supported inside either
A\cap{\it supp}(g)
or
(X\setminus A)\cap{\it supp}(g)
and such that
hg\neq gh
; it exists, as non-empty clopen sets in
X
are necessarily big enough to support such
h
.
Then

N\ni g\widetilde{f}_{A}\cdot(hg\widetilde{f}_{A}h^{-1})^{-1}=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}.

It follows that
N\cap S(2^{\infty})\neq\{e\}
. By the first paragraph,
N\supseteq S(2^{\infty})
. Take any non-trivial clopen set
B\subseteq X
. There exists
h\in S(2^{\infty})
such that
h(B)\neq B
. Then
\widetilde{f}_{h(B)\Delta B}=h\widetilde{f}_{B}h^{-1}\widetilde{f}_{B}\in N
. By the second paragraph,
N\supseteq\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Therefore,
N=G
.

∎

The next lemma will be used to ‘spread’ subsets of
X
using elements of
S(2^{\infty})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any clopen subsets
A,B\subseteq X
with
B\notin\{\varnothing,X,A,X\setminus A\}
there exists a sequence of elements
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
S(2^{\infty})
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
h_{n}(A)=A
and the sets
h_{n}(B)
are pairwise distinct.

Proof.

Replacing
A
with
X\setminus A
if necessary we may assume that
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
and
A\setminus B\neq\varnothing
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
A
and
B
are unions of cylinders of level
m
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
pick a point
x_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{m+n}
and a point
y_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{m+n}
such that

[x_{n}]\subseteq A\cap B,\;\;[y_{n}]\subseteq A\setminus B.

Let
h_{n}\in S(2^{m+n})
be the transposition of
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
. It is not hard to see that
h_{n}(A)=A
and the sets
h_{n}(B)
are pairwise distinct for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

The next two results compute the sets
fpc(g)
for particular elements
g\in G
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A\subseteq X
be a non-trivial clopen set. Then
{\rm fpc}(\widetilde{f}_{A})=\{e,\widetilde{f}_{A}\}
.

Proof.

Consider first an element
g\tilde{f}_{B}\in G
with
g\in S(2^{\infty}),

g\neq e
,
B\subseteq X
clopen. Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)\in X=\{0,1\}^{\infty}
be such that
gx\neq x
. There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g\in S(2^{m})
and
g((x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}))\neq((x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}))
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
B_{n}=[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})]
(a cylinder set of level
n+m
, see (
3.2
)). Then the elements

\widetilde{f}_{B_{n}}\cdot g\widetilde{f}_{B}\cdot\widetilde{f}_{B_{n}}=\widetilde{f}_{B_{n}\Delta g(B_{n})}\cdot g\widetilde{f}_{B}

are pairwise distinct. Since the elements
\widetilde{f}_{B_{n}}
belong to the centralizer of
\widetilde{f}_{A}
we obtain that
g\widetilde{f}_{B}\notin{\rm fpc}(\widetilde{f}_{A})
.

Consider now an element
\widetilde{f}_{B}
such that
B\notin\{\varnothing,X,A,X\setminus A\}
. Let
h_{n}
be the sequence of elements from Lemma
3.2
. Then
h_{n}\in C_{G}(\widetilde{f}_{A})
and the elements
h_{n}\widetilde{f}_{B}h_{n}^{-1}=\widetilde{f}_{h_{n}(B)}
are pairwise distinct. Thus,
\widetilde{f}_{B}\notin{\rm fpc}(\widetilde{f}_{A})
.
∎

We now focus on computing the fpc(s) for an involution
s
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
s\in S(2^{\infty})\subseteq G
be an involution. Then

{\rm fpc}(s)=\{e,s,\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)},s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}\}.

Proof.

Consider
g\in S(2^{\infty})
and
A\subseteq X
clopen such that
g\widetilde{f}_{A}\notin\{e,s,\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)},s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}\}
. We will construct a sequence of elements
h_{n}\in C_{G}(s)
such that the elements
h_{n}\cdot g\widetilde{f}_{A}\cdot h_{n}^{-1}
are pairwise distinct, which would imply that
g\widetilde{f}_{A}\notin{\rm fpc}(s)
. Take
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g,s\in S(2^{m})
and
A
is a union of cylinders of level
m
. Several cases are possible.

{\bf a})

gs\neq sg
. Then, as
s
is the product of disjoint transpositions, there exists

x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)\in{\it supp}(g)\cap{\it supp}(s)

such that
y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},\ldots):=sx\neq gx
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
h_{n}\in S(2^{n+m})
be the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})
. Then
h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}\in S(2^{n+m})
and

h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}((y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m}))=g((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})).

Let
k>n
and
z\in[(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})]\setminus[(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k+m})]
(difference of cylinders of levels
n+m
and
k+m
around
y
, see (
3.2
)). Observe that by the choice of
x
and
m
the cylinders
[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})]
,
[(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})]
, and
g([(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})])
are pairwise disjoint.
Then

h_{n}^{-1}(z)\in[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})],

h_{k}^{-1}(z)=z
and so

h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}(z)\in g([(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})]),\;\;h_{k}gh_{k}^{-1}(z)\in g([(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})])\cup{\it supp}(h_{k}).

It follows that
h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}(z)\neq h_{k}gh_{k}^{-1}(z)
. We obtain that
h_{n}g\widetilde{f}_{A}h_{n}^{-1}\neq h_{k}g\widetilde{f}_{A}h_{k}^{-1}
, and elements
h_{n}
clearly commute with
s
.

{\bf b)}

gs=sg
and
{\it supp}(g)\setminus{\it supp}(s)\neq\varnothing
. Let
x\in{\it supp}(g)\setminus{\it supp}(s)
. Let
h_{n}\in S(2^{n+m+1})
be the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m},0)
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m},1)
, which clearly commutes with
s
. Then for
k>n
and
z\in[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})]\setminus[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+m})]
one has
h_{k}g(z)=h_{n}g(z)=g(z)
. Indeed, since
g\in S(2^{m})
and
x\in{\it supp}(g)
we have that
g
changes at least one of the first
m
coordinates of
x
. Thus,
g[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})]\cap[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})]=\varnothing
. It follows that
gz\notin[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})]
and does not belong to the support of either
h_{n}
or
h_{k}
. This implies in turn that
h_{k}gh_{k}^{-1}(z)=g(z)\neq gh_{n}(z)=h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}(z)
, since
z
and
h_{n}(z)
have different
(n+m+1)
-st coordinate. Therefore,
h_{n}\widetilde{g}f_{A}h_{n}^{-1}\neq h_{k}\widetilde{g}f_{A}h_{k}^{-1}
.

{\bf c)}

gs=sg
,
{\it supp}(g)\subset{\it supp}(s)
, and
g\notin\{e,s\}
. The following two subcases are possible.

{\bf c1)}

gx\neq sx
for some
x\in{\it supp}(g)
. Let
u_{n}\in S(2^{n+m+1})
denote the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m},0)
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m},1)
. Set
h_{n}=u_{n}su_{n}s^{-1}
. Since

s([(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})])\cap[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})]=\varnothing

we have that
u_{n}
and
su_{n}s^{-1}
commute. Therefore,
h_{n}
commutes with
s
. Arguing in the same way as in the case
b)
, we obtain that the elements
h_{n}g\widetilde{f}_{A}h_{n}^{-1}
are pairwise distinct for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

{\bf c2)}

gx=sx
for all
x\in{\it supp}(g)
. Then, as
g\neq e
,
g\neq s
, there exist
x,y\in X
such that
gx=sx\neq x
,
gy=y\neq sy
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
u_{n}\in S(2^{n+m})
be the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})
. Set
h_{n}=u_{n}\cdot(su_{n}s^{-1})
. Let
z\in[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})]\setminus[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+m})]
. Then
h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}(z)=z\neq s(z)=h_{k}gh_{k}^{-1}(z)
. Once again,
h_{n}\widetilde{g}f_{A}h_{n}^{-1}\neq h_{k}\widetilde{g}f_{A}h_{k}^{-1}
.

{\bf d)}

g\in\{e,s\}
. It is not hard to see that
s\widetilde{f}_{A}\in{\rm fpc}(s)
if and only if
\widetilde{f}_{A}\in{\rm fpc}(s)
. Therefore, without loss of generality in this case, we may assume that
g=e
.

{\bf d1)}

s(A)\neq A
. Let
x\in A\setminus s(A)
. Let
h_{n}\in S(2^{n+m})
be the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})
and
s((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m}))
, commmuting with
s
. Then
h_{n}(A)\setminus A=s[(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})]
.

{\bf d2)}

s(A)=A
,
A\cap{\it supp}(s)\notin\{\varnothing,{\it supp}(s)\}
. Let
x\in A\cap{\it supp}(s)
,
y\in{\it supp}(s)\setminus A
. Let
u_{n}\in S(2^{n+m})
be the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})
. Set
h_{n}=u_{n}\cdot(su_{n}s^{-1})
; one can check that
h_{n}s=sh_{n}
. Then
h_{n}(A)\setminus A=[(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})]\cup s[(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})]
.

{\bf d3)}

A\cap{\it supp}(s)\in\{\varnothing,{\it supp}(s)\}
. Our initial assumptions imply that
A\notin\{\varnothing,X,{\it supp}(s),X\setminus{\it supp}(s)\}
. We obtain that there exist points
x\in A\setminus{\it supp}(s),y\in X\setminus(A\cup{\it supp}(s))
. Let
h_{n}\in S(2^{n+m})
be the transposition of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+m})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})
. Then
h_{n}(A)\setminus A=[(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+m})]
.

In the latter three cases we obtain that the elements
h_{n}\widetilde{f}_{A}h_{n}^{-1}=\widetilde{f}_{h_{n}(A)}
are pairwise distinct for

. This finishes the proof.
∎

In general, computing the set
fpc(s\widetilde{f}_{A})
is rather complicated, as the next two remarks show.

Remark 3.5
.

Let
s\in S(2^{\infty})
be an involution and let
A\subseteq X
be a clopen set such that
s(A)\cap A=\varnothing
. Let
g\in S(2^{\infty})
be such that
g(x)=s(x)
for all
x\in A\cup s(A)
and
g(x)=x
othervise. Then
g\in{\rm fpc}(s\widetilde{f}_{A})
. Thus, in general, it is not true that
{\rm fpc}(s\widetilde{f}_{A})\subseteq s\cdot\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})\cup\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Remark 3.6
.

Let
s\in S(2^{\infty})
be an involution. Then
C_{G}(s)=C_{G}(s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})
. Therefore,
{\rm fpc}(s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})={\rm fpc}(s)
.

Invariant von Neumann subalgebras of
L(S(2^{\infty})\ltimes\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))

In this subsection, we fix an invariant von Neumann subalgebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
, and denote by
E:L(G)\to\mathcal{M}
the unique
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation. In many ways,
\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
governs the algebraic structure of
\mathcal{M}
in the sense that either it has a trivial intersection with
\mathcal{M}
or is completely contained inside
\mathcal{M}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

We have either
\mathcal{M}\cap L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))=\mathbb{C}1
or
\mathcal{M}\supseteq L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}\cap L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))\neq\mathbb{C}1
. Then there exists a non-scalar element
m\in\mathcal{M}\cap L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
. Expand
m
in its Fourier series as

(3.3)

m=\sum_{\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})}m_{\widetilde{f}}\cdot\delta_{\widetilde{f}}.

Subtracting
m_{e}\cdot 1
from
m
we may assume that
m_{e}=0
.
Since
m\notin\mathbb{C}1
, there exists a nontrivial clopen set
A\subsetneq X
such that
m_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}\neq 0
. For convenience, normalize
m
so that
m_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}=1
. Assume that
m\neq\delta_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}
. Choose
\widetilde{f}_{B}\in\text{supp}(m)
with
\widetilde{f}_{B}\neq\widetilde{f}_{A}
such that

|m_{\widetilde{f}_{B}}|=\max\{|m_{\widetilde{f}}|:\widetilde{f}\neq\widetilde{f}_{A}\}.

Using Lemma
3.2
choose a sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset S_{2^{\infty}}
such that

•

h_{n}^{-1}(A)=A
for all
n
,

•

the sets
h_{n}^{-1}(B)
are pairwise distinct (modulo complements).

The Fourier coefficients of
u_{h_{n}}mu_{h_{n}}^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}
satisfy

(u_{h_{n}}mu_{h_{n}}^{-1})_{\widetilde{f}}=m_{h_{n}^{-1}\widetilde{f}h_{n}}.

Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that there exists a limit in the weak operator topology
m^{\prime}=\mathrm{w\text{-}lim}_{n\to\infty}u_{h_{n}}mu_{h_{n}}^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}
. Clearly,
m^{\prime}_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}=m_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}=1
. Since
\{m_{\widetilde{f}}:\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})\}
is square-summable,

m_{\widetilde{f}_{B}}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}m_{\widetilde{f}_{h_{n}^{-1}(B)}}=0.

Fix for the moment the sequence
(h_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Next, let us order all elements
\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
in an arbitrary way:

\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})=\{\widetilde{f}_{1},\widetilde{f}_{2},\widetilde{f}_{3},\ldots\}.

Set
S_{0}=\mathbb{N}
. By induction, we will construct an increasing sequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive integers and a decreasing sequence of infinite subsets
S_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds for each
k\in\mathbb{N}

1)

n_{k}\in S_{k-1}
;

2)

\min S_{k}>n_{k}
;

3)

either
h_{j}^{-1}\widetilde{f}_{k}h_{j}
does not depend on
j
for
j\in S_{k}
or
h_{j}^{-1}\widetilde{f}_{k}h_{j}
are pairwise distinct for
j\in S_{k}
.

The construction of the starting pair,
n_{1}
and
S_{1}
, can proceed as below, so we will not repeat it. Assume that for a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have constructed
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
satisfying the inductive hypothesis. If the number of distinct elements in
\{h_{j}^{-1}\widetilde{f}_{k+1}h_{j}:j\in S_{k}\}
is infinite there exists an infinite subset
S^{\prime}_{k+1}\subseteq S_{k}
such that
h_{j}^{-1}\widetilde{f}_{k+1}h_{j}
are pairwise distinct for
j\in S_{k+1}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, there exists an infinite subset
S_{k+1}^{\prime}\subseteq S_{k}
such that
h_{j}^{-1}\widetilde{f}_{k+1}h_{j}
are all equal for
j\in S_{k+1}^{\prime}
. In both cases, set

n_{k+1}=\min S_{k+1}^{\prime},\;\;S_{k+1}=S^{\prime}_{k+1}\setminus\{n_{k+1}\}.

The inductive hypothesis is satisfied for
k+1
by construction.

Now, replace
h_{n}
with the subsequence
(h_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then for any element
\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
the following is true: either
h_{n}^{-1}\widetilde{f}h_{n}
becomes constant for large
n\in\mathbb{N}
or the elements
\{h_{n}^{-1}\widetilde{f}h_{n}:n\geqslant N\}
are pairwise distinct for
n\geqslant N
for sufficiently large
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that
m^{\prime}
is the weak limit of
u_{h_{n}}mu_{h_{n}}^{*}
. In particular,
m^{\prime}_{\widetilde{f}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{h_{n}^{-1}\widetilde{f}h_{n}}
for any
\widetilde{f}
. We obtain for each
\widetilde{f}
that either
m^{\prime}_{\widetilde{f}}=m_{h_{n}\widetilde{f}h_{n}^{-1}}
for large enough
n
or
m^{\prime}_{\widetilde{f}}=0
.

By performing the operation
m\to m^{\prime}
repeatedly starting with
m
we obtain a sequence of elements
m^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}\cap L(F_{2}^{\infty})
such that
m^{(n)}_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}=m_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}=1
for all
n
and for any
\widetilde{f}\neq\widetilde{f}_{A}
one has
m^{(n)}_{\widetilde{f}}=0
for all sufficiently large
n
. It follows that
m^{(n)}
converges weakly to
\widetilde{f}_{A}
. Therefore,
\widetilde{f}_{A}\in\mathcal{M}
.

Since
\delta_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under conjugation by
G=S(2^{\infty})\ltimes\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, we get:

(3.4)

\delta_{g(\widetilde{f}_{A})}=u_{g}\delta_{\widetilde{f}_{A}}u_{g}^{-1}\in\mathcal{M},\quad\forall g\in S(2^{\infty}).

The set
\{\delta_{\widetilde{f}_{C}}:C\subseteq X\text{ clopen, nontrivial}\}
spans a dense *-subalgebra of
L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
. Hence,

(3.5)

L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))\subseteq\mathcal{M}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Moreover, it follows immediately from Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
3.3
that under the canonical conditional expectation
E
,
L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
remains preserved. The following proposition makes it precise.

Proposition 3.8
.

One has
E(L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})))\subseteq L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
.

We now examine the image of an involution under the canonical conditional expectation
E
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
s\in S(2^{\infty})\subseteq G
be an involution. Then

E(s)\in\{s,\tfrac{1}{2}s({\rm id}+\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}),\tfrac{1}{2}s({\rm id}-\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})\}\cup\mathbb{C}s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}.

Proof.

We may and do assume that
s\neq{\rm id}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}=L(\{{\rm id},\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}\})
and
\mathcal{S}=s\mathcal{A}
. By Lemma
3.3
, one has
E(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. By Proposition
3.4
, Remark
3.6
, and Lemma
2.2
, one has
E(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A}
.
Clearly,
\tau
is equal to zero on
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}
. By Proposition
2.4
,
E(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. It follows that

E(s)=\lambda_{1}s+\lambda_{2}s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}.
We will now consider several cases.

{\bf a)}

\lambda_{2}=0
. Then
either
\lambda_{1}=0
and
E(s)=0
or
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
,
s\in\mathcal{M}
and
E(s)=s
.

{\bf b)}

\lambda_{1}=0
. Then
E(s)=\lambda_{2}s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}
.

{\bf c)}
Assume that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\neq 0
. Then one has:

E(s)E(s)^{*}=E(s)^{2}=(\lambda_{1}{\rm id}+\lambda_{2}\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})^{2}\in L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))\setminus\mathbb{C}{\rm id}.

Using Proposition
3.7
, we obtain that
\mathcal{M}\supseteq L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
. Therefore,

s\cdot(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})=E(s)=E(E(s))=E(s)(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})=s\cdot(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})^{2}.

It follows that
P:=\lambda_{1}{\rm id}+\lambda_{2}\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}
is a projection, which is easily seen to be selfadjoint, from which it is straightforward to obtain that

P\in\{0,{\rm id},\tfrac{1}{2}({\rm id}+\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}),\tfrac{1}{2}({\rm id}-\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})\}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

We are ready to formulate and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.10
.

The group
G=S(2^{\infty})\ltimes\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is an amenable ICC group with the ISR property containing a non-trivial abelian normal subgroup.

Proof.

The only non-trivial statement is the one concerning the ISR property (as the ICC property follows then by
[
2
, Proposition 3.1]
). Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(G)
be an invariant von Neumann subalgebra and denote by
E:L(G)\to\mathcal{M}
the unique
\tau
-preserving conditional expectation.

We will consider several cases.

{\bf 1)}
Assume first that
E(s)=s
for some
s\in S(2^{\infty})\setminus e
. Then, by simplicity of
S(2^{\infty})
, one has
E(g)=g
for all
g\in S(2^{\infty})
. Therefore, for every clopen subset
A\subseteq X
one has
E(g\widetilde{f}_{A})=gE(\widetilde{f}_{A})
. Take a non-trivial clopen subset
A\subseteq X
, and any
g\in S(2^{\infty})
such that
gA\neq A
. We have:

g\widetilde{f}_{A\Delta g^{-1}(A)}=\widetilde{f}_{A}g\widetilde{f}_{A}=\widetilde{f}_{A}E(g)\widetilde{f}_{A}=E(\widetilde{f}_{A}g\widetilde{f}_{A})=E(g\widetilde{f}_{A\Delta g^{-1}(A)})=gE(\widetilde{f}_{A\Delta g^{-1}(A)}).

It follows that
E(\widetilde{f}_{A\Delta g^{-1}(A)})=\widetilde{f}_{A\Delta g^{-1}(A)}
. By Proposition
3.7
,
\mathcal{M}\supseteq L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
. Thus, in this case,
\mathcal{M}=L(G)
.

{\bf 2)}

Now, assume that
E(g)\neq g
for all
g\in S(2^{\infty})\setminus\{e\}
and that
E(s)\in\mathbb{C}s\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)}
for some involution
s\in S(2^{\infty}),s\neq e
, with
{\it supp}(s)\neq X
. Consider the character
\chi(g)=\tau(g^{-1}E(g))
,
g\in G
. Its restriction to
S(2^{\infty})
remains a character,
and indecomposable characters on
S(2^{\infty})
were described in
[
7
]
. They are indexed by the parameter set
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{\infty}:=\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we have

\chi_{k}(g)=\mu({\rm Fix}(g))^{k},\;\;\;g\in S(2^{\infty}),

where
{\rm Fix}(g)=X\setminus{\it supp}(g)
is the set of fixed points of
g
, and
\mu=\{1/2,1/2\}^{\infty}
is the uniform Bernoulli measure on
X
. On the other hand
\chi_{\infty}
is the regular character on
S(2^{\infty})
. It follows from the standard decomposition theory for characters (see
[
24
]
, Chapter IV, Theorem 8.21) that there exists a family of non-negative numbers
(\alpha_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{\infty}}
summing to
1
such that

\chi=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{\infty}}\alpha_{k}\chi_{k}.

Since
\chi(s)=\lambda\tau(\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})=0
and
\mu({\rm Fix}(s))\neq 0
we obtain that
\alpha_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and thus

\tau(g^{-1}E(g))=\chi(g)=\chi_{\infty}(g)=\delta_{g,e}

for
g\in S(2^{\infty})
. Standard arguments show that
E(g)=0
for all
g\in S(2^{\infty})\setminus\{e\}
.

Next, let
g\in S(2^{\infty})\setminus\{e\}
and let
A\subseteq X
be a clopen subset.
Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
g\in S(2^{m})
and
A
is a union of cylinders of level
m
. Take a point
x\in{\it supp}(g)
. Denote for
n\in\mathbb{N}
by
h_{n}\in S(2^{m+n})
the transposition of
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m+n},0)
and
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m+n},1)
.
Then
h_{n}\in S(2^{\infty})
are such that
g_{n}:=h_{n}\cdot g\widetilde{f}_{A}\cdot h_{n}^{-1}
are pairwise distinct and
h_{n}(A)=A
. In particular,
g_{n}g_{k}^{-1}\in S(2^{\infty})\setminus\{e\}
and so
\chi(g_{n}g_{k}^{-1})=0
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\neq k
. Applying
[
5
, Lemma 2.7]
we obtain that
\chi(g\widetilde{f}_{A})=0
. By Proposition
2.1
(5) it follows that
E(g\widetilde{f}_{A})=0
. Thus
E(h)=0
for all
h\notin\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}).
Taking into account Corollary
3.8
and Lemma
3.7
, we obtain that either
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}1
or
\mathcal{M}=L(\widetilde{C}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
.

{\bf 3)}
Assume that neither
1)
nor
2)
holds. By Proposition
3.4
, for all involutions
s\in S(2^{\infty})
with
{\it supp}(s)\notin\{\varnothing,X\}
one has
E(s)=\tfrac{1}{2}s({\rm id}\pm\widetilde{f}_{{\it supp}(s)})
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denote by
(x,y)\in S(2^{n})
the transposition of
x
and
y
. Let
s=(000,100)\in S(2^{3})\subseteq S(2^{\infty})
and
g=(11,10)\subseteq S(2^{2})\subseteq S(2^{\infty})
. Recall that for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n},n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

4.
Finitary automorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
and a group von Neumann algebra with a unique ‘exotic’ invariant subalgebra

This section is devoted to the study of the semidirect product for the action on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
given by all “finitary” automorphisms.

Let
F_{2}^{\infty}:=\bigoplus_{\mathbb{N}}F_{2}
be the vector space of infinite columns of elements of the field
F_{2}
having finitely many nonzero entries. This vector space also can be viewed as a group with multiplication given by addition of vectors. By definition,
GL(\infty,F_{2})
is the group of all infinite invertible matrices
M
over
F_{2}
such that
\#\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:M_{ij}\neq\delta_{i,j}\}<\infty
. This group acts by matrix multiplication from the left on
F_{2}^{\infty}
.
For
v\in F_{2}^{\infty}
and
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2})
we denote by
g(v)
the image of
v
under the action of
g
. Consider the semidirect product
GL(\infty,F_{2})\ltimes F_{2}^{\infty}
. We will write elements of this group as
gv
or
g\cdot v
,
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2}),v\in F_{2}^{\infty}
, where
\cdot
is used to visually separate the terms of a product of elements of a group. We have:

(4.1)

g_{1}v_{1}\cdot g_{2}v_{2}=g_{1}g_{2}\cdot(g_{2}^{-1}(v_{1})+v_{2}),\;\;g_{1},g_{2}\in GL(\infty,F_{2}),\;v_{1},v_{2}\in F_{2}^{\infty}.

One of the motivations to consider the group
GL(\infty,F_{2})
is that it can be viewed as a certain group of automorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
. Let
\textup{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n})
be the group of all automorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. Since
S(n)
acts naturally on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
by permuting coordinates we have
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n})\supseteq S(n)
. Notice that for
n\geqslant 2
the inclusion is strict, as the next example shows.

Example 4.1
.

The map
\varphi:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
given by
\varphi((0,0))=(0,0),\varphi((1,0))=(1,1),\varphi((0,1))=(0,1),\varphi((1,1))=(1,0)
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
, which does not arise from any element of
S_{2}
in the manner described above.

Considering natural inclusions
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\times\{0\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain inclusions
\textup{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n})\subseteq{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+1})\subseteq\textup{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})
. Let
\textup{Aut}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n})<{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})
. Then
\textup{Aut}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})\supseteq S_{\infty}
.

If
\mathcal{I}:F_{2}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}
denotes the tautological map forgetting the multiplication in
F_{2}
, we see that the map
g\to\mathcal{I}(g):=\mathcal{I}\circ g\circ\mathcal{I}^{-1}
provides an isomorphism from
GL(\infty,F_{2})
onto
\textup{Aut}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})
. Therefore, semidirect products
\textup{Aut}_{\rm fin}(Z_{2}^{\infty})\ltimes Z_{2}^{\infty}
and
GL(\infty,F_{2})\ltimes F_{2}^{\infty}
are isomorphic. For the rest of this section set

G=\textup{Aut}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\infty}\cong GL(\infty,F_{2})\ltimes F_{2}^{\infty}.

For
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2})<G
and
v\in F_{2}^{\infty}<G
we have:
g\cdot v\cdot g^{-1}=g(v)
(see (
4.1
)).
This characterization of
G
allows us to conclude that there is only one normal subgroup of
G
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The group
F_{2}^{\infty}
is the only non-trivial normal subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
.

If
v\in N
for some
v\in F_{2}^{\infty}\setminus\{0^{\infty}\}
, then taking into account that
GL(\infty,F_{2})
acts transitively on
F_{2}^{\infty}\setminus\{0^{\infty}\}
we obtain that
F_{2}^{\infty}<N
. Then either
N=F_{2}^{\infty}
or
N
contains an element in
G\setminus F_{2}^{\infty}
.

Assume now that
gv\in N
for some
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2})\setminus\{e\}
,
v\in F_{2}^{\infty}
. Take any
w\in F_{2}^{\infty}
such that
gw\neq w
. Then
(-w)\cdot gv\cdot w\cdot(gv)^{-1}=(g(w)-w)\in N
. By the previous paragraph, we obtain that
F_{2}^{\infty}<N
. Moreover,
g=gv\cdot(-v)\in N
. Using simplicity of
GL(\infty,F_{2})
, which is a consequence of the results in
[
20
, Chapter 8]
we deduce that
GL(\infty,F_{2})<N
. Thus, in this case

, which finishes the proof.
∎

The following statement is straightforward.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any distinct non-zero vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in F_{2}^{\infty}
and any distinct non-zero vectors
w_{1},w_{2}\in F_{2}^{\infty}
there exists
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2})
such that
g(v_{1})=w_{1}
,
g(v_{2})=w_{2}
.

The next lemma concerns the sets
{\rm fpc}(v)
for
v\in F_{2}^{\infty}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any nonzero vector
v\in F_{2}^{\infty}
one has
{\rm fpc}(v)=\{v,e\}
.

Proof.

Let
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2})
,
w\in F_{2}^{\infty}
. Suppose that
g\cdot w\notin\{e,v\}
. Consider first the case
v\neq w
. Lemma
4.3
implies that there exists a sequence of elements
h_{n}\in GL(\infty,F_{2})
such that
h_{n}(v)=v
, but
h_{n}(w)
are pairwise distinct for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From this we conclude that
h_{n}\in C_{H}(\{v\})
, but the elements
h_{n}\cdot gw\cdot h_{n}^{-1}=h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}\cdot h_{n}(w)
are pairwise distinct in
H
. This implies that
g\cdot w\notin{\rm fpc}(v)
.

Let then
g\neq e
,
w=v
and assume that
R(g-I)\neq\{v,0\}
. There exists
u\in F_{2}^{\infty}
such that
g(u)-u\notin\{v,e\}
. Lemma
4.3
implies that there exists a sequence of elements
h_{n}\in GL(\infty,F_{2})
such that
h_{n}(v)=v
(and thus
h_{n}\in C_{H}(\{v\}))
, but
h_{n}(g(u)-u)
are pairwise distinct for

. Observe that
h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}(h_{n}(u))=h_{n}(g(u))
, and thus
h_{n}(g(u)-u)
belongs to the range of
h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}-I
. Since for every element

the matrix

has finite range we conclude that among the elements
h_{n}\cdot g\cdot v\cdot h_{n}^{-1}=h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}\cdot h_{n}(v)=h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}\cdot v
there is an infinite subset consisting of pairwise distinct elements of

. Therefore,
g\cdot v\notin{\rm fpc}(v)
.

Finally, assume that
w=v
and
R(g-I)=\{v,0\}
. Then
g-I=v\cdot r
for some finitely supported row-vector

. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
r_{n}=v_{n}=0
for all

. Take

such that

. Set
h_{n}=I+e_{k,n}
for
n>m
. Here,

denotes the matrix whose only non-zero entry is at the
(k,n)
-th position. Then

h_{n}^{-1}=h_{n},\;h_{n}v=v,\;h_{n}(g-I)=g-I,\;h_{n}gh_{n}^{-1}-I=(g-I)h_{n}^{-1}=v\cdot r\cdot h_{n}=v\cdot(r+\delta_{n}).

In particular,

has

-th column equal to

, and at the same time all

are pairwise distinct for

and so

.
∎

The following factorization lemma will be used later.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
g\in GL(\infty,F_{2})\setminus\{e\}
. Then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
and elements
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\in GL(\infty,F_{2})
such that

•

s_{i}
is conjugate to

in
GL(\infty,F_{2})
,

,

•

s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{k}=g
,

•

R(s_{1}-I)+R(s_{2}-I)+\ldots+R(s_{k}-I)=R(g-I)

Proof.

Set
n=\dim R(g-I)
. Let

be such that

. Replacing

by
hgh^{-1}
without loss of generality we will assume that

. Then

is of the form

\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&m\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}
, where

and

. Assume first that

. Observe that

\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

The latter matrix is conjugate to every matrix of the form

,

. Clearly, there exist

and matrices

of the form

,

,

, such that

.

Since

for

[
20

,

can be written as a product

of elements from

, for some

. We obtain:

g=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&m\\

0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&0\\

If

and

with

By construction,

. Since for every

5.
Invariant Subalgebras of the classical lamplighter group

In this section, we focus our attention on the classical lamplighter group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}=(\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
. We aim to study all
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebras in
L(G)
. First, note that as
G
is a metabelian group with
\mathbb{Z}
as the quotient, we have a short exact sequence of groups

0\rightarrow\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\rightarrow G\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow 0

Thus every subgroup of
G
is isomorphic (abstractly as a group) to
H\rtimes k\mathbb{Z}
for some subgroup
H\subset\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. This does not mean that the abstract isomorphism is compatible with the natural inclusions
H\subset\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\subset G
and
k\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset G
, even if the subgroup in question is normal (see an example below in Remark
5.5
).
For more information on the subgroup structure of
G
, see
[
10
]
.

Given a p.m.p. action
(X,\mu)
and a
\mathbb{Z}
-factor map
\pi:(X,\mu)\rightarrow(Y,\nu)
,
let
\mu=\int_{Y}\mu_{y}d\nu(y)
denotes the measure disintegration w.r.t.
\pi
. Set
X\times_{Y}X=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times X:\pi(x_{1})=\pi(x_{2})\}
and
\theta=\int_{X}\mu_{y}\times\mu_{y}d\nu(y)
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
\pi
is relatively weakly mixing if the action
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(X\times_{Y}X,\theta)
is ergodic.

For more details on relative weak mixing, see
[
9
, § 2.5]
and the references therein.

The following lemma is essentially a simple version of
[
9
, Lemma 3.2]
, which should be compared to Popa’s original
[
19
, Lemma 2.11]
. We decided to include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\langle s\rangle=\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
and
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
be two p.m.p. actions and
\pi:(X,\mu)\rightarrow(Y,\nu)
be a factor map which is relatively weakly mixing. Denote the action of
\langle s\rangle
by
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}\to\textup{Aut}(X,\mu)
. If
v\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
is a unitary element such that
\sigma_{s^{n}}(v)v^{*}\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
v\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma_{s^{n}}(v)v^{*}\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)
, we deduce that

[\sigma_{s^{n}}(v)v^{*}](x_{1})=[\sigma_{s^{n}}(v)v^{*}](x_{2})

for
\theta
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times_{Y}X
, i.e.
v(s^{-n}x_{1})v^{*}(x_{1})=v(s^{-n}x_{2})v^{*}(x_{2})
, or equivalently

\displaystyle v(x_{1})v^{*}(x_{2})=v(s^{-n}x_{1})v^{*}(s^{-n}x_{2})\penalty\ \text{for
$\theta$-a.e. $(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times_{Y}X$}.

Set
\eta(x_{1},x_{2})=v(x_{1})v^{*}(x_{2})
for
\theta
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times_{Y}X
. Then the above implies
\eta
is
\mathbb{Z}
-invariant and hence must be a constant function by the relatively weakly mixing assumption. Since
v
is a unitary, we deduce that
\eta(x_{1},x_{2})=v(x_{1})v^{*}(x_{2})=c\in\mathbb{T}
for
\theta
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times_{Y}X
. By swapping the two coordinates, we also have
c=\eta(x_{2},x_{1})=v(x_{2})v^{*}(x_{1})=\overline{\eta(x_{1},x_{2})}=\overline{c}
. Hence
c=\pm 1
.

If
c=-1
, then for
\theta
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{2},x_{3}),(x_{3},x_{1})\in X\times_{Y}X
we have:

v(x_{1})=-v(x_{2})=-(-v(x_{3}))=v(x_{3})=-v(x_{1}).

Thus
v=0
, a contradiction to the unitary assumption. Thus
c=1
and hence
v(x_{1})=v(x_{2})
for
\theta
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times_{Y}X
; equivalently,
v\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)
. ∎

Let
A=\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Note that
L(A)\cong L^{\infty}((\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}))
. Therefore, one might suspect that every
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra of
L(G)
is of the form
L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)\rtimes k\mathbb{Z}
for some
\mathbb{Z}
-factor
(Y,\nu)
of the Bernoulli shift. It turns out that this is true in the case when the corresponding factor map is relatively weakly mixing, but not in general.

Before we formulate the corresponding result, we need one more lemma. It can be deduced directly from the classical theorem of Ornstein that every non-trivial factor of a Bernoulli shift is still measurably conjugate to a Bernoulli shift (
[
16
]
). We provide below a direct proof.

Lemma 5.3
.

Every factor of the Bernoulli shift
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}^{\mathbb{Z}})
is either trivial or essentially free.

Proof.

First, we prove that any factor
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
is either essentially free or a transitive map on a finite space, i.e.
[\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)]\cong[\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright\mathbb{Z}_{n}]
for some
n\geq 1
. Indeed, assume that
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
is not essentially free. Write
\mathbb{Z}=\langle s\rangle
.
Then for some
n\geq 1
we have
\nu(\text{Fix}(s^{n}))>0
, where
\text{Fix}(s^{n})=\{y\in Y:s^{n}y=y\}
. Note that since
\mathbb{Z}
is abelian,
\text{Fix}(s^{n})
is
\mathbb{Z}
-invariant. As
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}^{\mathbb{Z}})
is the Bernoulli shift, it is ergodic. Since ergodicity passes to factors, we have
\nu(\text{Fix}(s^{n}))=1
, i.e.
s^{n}y=y
for
\nu
-a.e.
y\in Y
.

Note that
(Y,\nu)
has no diffuse part. Indeed, otherwise, we could pick any
Z\subset Y
with
0<\nu(Z)<\frac{1}{n}
. Then
W:=\cup_{i=0}^{n-1}s^{i}Z
would be
\mathbb{Z}
-invariant and such that
0<\nu(W)<1
, which would contradict the ergodicity of
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
. Pick any atom
\{y_{0}\}
in
Y
. Then
Y=\cup_{i=0}^{n-1}\{s^{i}y_{0}\}
and clearly this shows that
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
is measurably conjugate to the transitive action
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Second, Bernoulli shifts are mixing, and this property passes to non-trivial factor maps. Thus if
[\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)]\cong[\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright\mathbb{Z}_{n}]
, then
n=1
and the action is trivial.
∎

The following is the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}=(\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
be the classical lamplighter group.
Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq L(G)
be a
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra. Then the following hold true:

(i)

there exists a normal subgroup
N\lhd G
such that
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}\cap L(A))\vee L(N)
, i.e.
\mathcal{P}
is generated as a von Neumann algebra by
\mathcal{P}\cap L(A)
and
L(N)
;

(ii)

\mathcal{P}\cap L(\mathbb{Z})=L(k\mathbb{Z})
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(iii)

if the factor map
\pi:(\widehat{A},\text{Haar})\rightarrow(Y,\nu)
is relatively weakly mixing, where
L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)\cong\mathcal{P}\cap L(A)
, then
\mathcal{P}=L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)\rtimes k\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

First, note that
\mathcal{P}\cap L(A)
is a
G
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra of

L(A)\cong L^{\infty}\left(\left(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\right),

and as such it corresponds to a factor of the Bernoulli shift
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(\widehat{A},\text{Haar})\cong(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}^{\mathbb{Z}})
. Write
L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)=\mathcal{P}\cap L(A)
and consider the corresponding factor map.

As usual set
E:L(G)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{P}
be the trace-preserving conditional expectation onto
\mathcal{P}
and set
\phi(g):=\tau(g^{-1}E(g))
for all
g\in G
. Set
N=\{g\in G:\phi(g)=1\}
, recalling Proposition
2.1
. In particular,
g\in N
implies
g\in\mathcal{P}
, i.e.
L(N)\subseteq\mathcal{P}
. It is also clear that
N
is a normal subgroup of
G
.

We split the proof into two cases.

Case 1
.
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
is the trivial action.

In this case, we shall prove that
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{C}
,
N=\{e\}
and
k=0
. In fact, we will show that
\phi(g)=0
for all
e\neq g\in G
. First, note that for each
a,b\in A
, we have
bE(a)b^{-1}=E(bab^{-1})=E(b)
and thus
E(a)\in L(A)^{\prime}\cap L(G)=L(A)
(where we use the essential freeness of the action). Hence, actually
E(a)\in L(A)\cap\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{C}
. Thus
E(a)=0
for all
e\neq a\in A
and so
\phi(a)=0
.

Fix now
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
e\neq a\in A
. Take any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
k>\max\{|j-i|:i,j\in\text{supp}(a)\}
. Then note that

\sigma_{s^{km}}(a)\neq a
for all
0\neq m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
t_{m}:=s^{m}(as^{n})s^{-m}=\sigma_{s^{m}}(a)s^{n}
. Then
t_{m}^{-1}t_{m^{\prime}}=s^{-n}\sigma_{s^{m}}(a)^{-1}\sigma_{s^{m^{\prime}}}(a)s^{n}
. Thus
\phi(t_{m}^{-1}t_{m^{\prime}})=\phi(\sigma_{s^{m}}(a)^{-1}\sigma_{s^{m^{\prime}}}(a))=\phi(a^{-1}\sigma_{s^{m^{\prime}-m}}(a))=0
for all
m^{\prime}\neq m\in\mathbb{Z}
since
e\neq a^{-1}\sigma_{s^{m^{\prime}-m}}(a)\in A
. Thus by the well-known lemma on characters, see e.g.
[
5
, Lemma 2.7]
, we deduce that
\phi(as^{n})=\phi(t_{m})=0
for all
m
.

Finally for any
0\neq n\in\mathbb{Z}
note that for any mutually distinct elements
\{a_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset A
, we have
\sigma_{s^{n}}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j})\neq a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N},i\neq j
. Thus
\phi(s^{n})=0
by a similar argument as above by noticing that
a_{i}s^{n}a_{i}^{-1}=a_{i}\sigma_{s^{n}}(a_{i}^{-1})s^{n}
and
[a_{j}\sigma_{s^{n}}(a_{j}^{-1})s^{n}][a_{i}\sigma_{s^{n}}(a_{i}^{-1})s^{n}]^{-1}=(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j})\sigma_{s^{n}}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j})^{-1}
. Therefore,
\phi\equiv\delta_{e}
and hence
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{C}
.

Case 2
.
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
is essentially free.

In this case, we have
L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)^{\prime}\cap L(G)=L(A)\cong L^{\infty}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}^{\mathbb{Z}})
(see e.g.
[
17
, Lemma 1.6]
). We briefly review the proof. Pick any
f\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)^{\prime}\cap L(G)
, with the Fourier type decomposition
f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}s^{n}
, where
f_{n}\in L(A)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\xi f=f\xi
for any
\xi\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)
shows that
f_{n}(\xi-\sigma_{s^{n}}(\xi))=0
, hence also
P(f_{n}^{*}f_{n})(\xi-\sigma_{s^{n}}(\xi))=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
P:L(A)\rightarrow L^{\infty}(Y)
is the (faithful) conditional expectation. Since
\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(Y,\nu)
is essentially free, this implies that
f_{n}=0
for all
n\neq 0
. Indeed, assume that for some
n\neq 0
, we have
f_{n}\neq 0
; equivalently,
P(f_{n}^{*}f_{n})\neq 0
, i.e. there exists some
A\subseteq Y
such that
\nu(A)>0
and
P(f_{n}^{*}f_{n})(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in A
. Then by
[
14
, Proposition 2.4]
, we may find some
B\subseteq A
with
\nu(B)>0
and
s^{n}B\cap B=\emptyset
. Set
\xi=\chi_{B}
, the characteristic function on
B
, then for a.e.
x\in B
, we get
0=P(f_{n}^{*}f_{n})(x)(\xi-\sigma_{s^{n}}(\xi))(x)=P(f_{n}^{*}f_{n})(x)\neq 0
, a contradiction.

Note that for each
g\in G
we have
g^{-1}E(g)\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)^{\prime}\cap L(G)=L(A)
, so that we may write
E(g)=gp_{g}
for some
p_{g}\in L(A)
for all
g\in G
. Hence using the bimodule property, we have that
g^{n}E(g)g^{-n}=E(g)
and hence
p_{g}=\sigma_{g^{n}}(p_{g})
for all
g\in G
. In other words,
p_{g}
is invariant under the action of the cyclic group
\langle g\rangle
. Note that for all
g\in G\setminus A
, as an algebraic action
\langle g\rangle\curvearrowright(\widehat{A},\text{Haar})
is ergodic by
[

Remark 5.5
.

We remark that the relatively weakly mixing assumption could not be dropped. Indeed, take any non-trivial normal subgroup
N\lhd G
such that
N
is not a semidirect product inside
G
.
For example, we may take

to be the normal subgroup generated by
\delta_{0}s
, where

takes value

at coordinate 0 and

at other coordinates.
Then

for any factor
(Y,\nu)
of

and any

. To see this, first note that

. Indeed, suppose that we do have equality above. Then

since

, where

is the natural surjective homomorphism. Next, note that
N\cap A=\{a\in A:\penalty\ \sharp\text{supp}(a)\penalty\ \text{is even}\}
, and on the other hand
\delta_{0}=(\delta_{0}s)s^{-1}\in N\cap A
, which gives us a contradiction. Finally, suppose that

is not relatively weakly mixing for this example.

The theorem above does not make it immediately clear whether the lamplighter group does have the ISR property. Below we show this is not the case.

Proposition 5.6
.

The group
G=(\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
does not have the ISR property.

Proof.

On the one hand, recall that by Sinai’s celebrated theorem in
[

]
, for any number

, there exists a

-factor map

-invariant subgroups; equivalently, there are only countably many normal subgroups of

∎
