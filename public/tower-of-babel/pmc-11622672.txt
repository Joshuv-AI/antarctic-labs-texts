The effect of omega-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid (PUFA) prescription preparations on the prevention of clinical cardiovascular disease: a meta-analysis of RCTs
Authors: Shujie Dong, Yalan Wang, Jialu Bian, Hongsheng Chen, Jie Dong, Jun Zhu, Tongyan Zhang, Qian Du, Rongsheng Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceEvidence from systematic reviews of the cardioprotective effect of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) remains controversial, and interventions including PUFAs dietary supplements or prescription medications cannot accurately reflect the role of PUFA RX in cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention.ObjectiveWe conducted a meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) to evaluate the efficacy of PUFA prescription medication in preventing CVD.MethodsTwo reviewers conducted a literature search of Embase, MEDLINE/PubMed, and the Cochrane Library from their inception to September 2023. The inclusion criteria were RCTs evaluating long-term supplementation (≥ 1 year) with PUFA prescriptions and reporting cardiovascular outcomes. Data were extracted independently by two authors, and the certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE system. Random-effects models were used to estimate the risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The primary outcomes were cardiovascular events. Secondary endpoints included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), cardiac death, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, and revascularization. Subgroup analyses were performed based on PUFA components, dosage, follow-up duration, and risk status.ResultsTwelve RCTs involving 99,830 participants were included. The mean age of participants ranged from 59.4 to 74.0 years, with a follow-up period varying from 1 to 6.2 years. Compared with placebo and statins, PUFA prescription medication was associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular events (8 RCTs, n = 75,929, RR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.81–0.95]; P = 0.0007; I2 = 45%), cardiac death (10 RCTs, n = 95,440, RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P = 0.02; I2 = 23%), myocardial infarction (9 RCTs, n = 94,877, RR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.73–0.96]; P = 0.009; I2 = 62%), and revascularization (9 RCTs, n = 91,242, RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P = 0.02; I2 = 63%).Conclusions and relevancePUFA prescription medication could lower the risks of cardiovascular events, cardiac death, myocardial infarction and revascularization. This research provides insight into the efficacy of PUFA prescription medications in CVD prevention and contributes to the ongoing debate on the role of PUFA products in cardiovascular outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-024-01051-y.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Fatty acids, Omega-3, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) are two omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) that exhibit antitumor [1, 2], antidementia [3], antidepressant [4], and especially cardiovascular protective [5–7] effects. Both EPA and DHA have been shown to lower triglyceride concentrations, inhibit platelet activity, improve vascular function, and reduce heart rate and blood pressure [8]. However, evidence from systematic reviews of RCTs has led to various conclusions [9–11] about the impacts of PUFAs on cardiovascular disease (CVD) events and mortality over the past few years and has been extensively debated. The study by Abdelhamid et al. [9] did not find significant variations in cardiovascular outcomes at any dose level. Rizos et al. [10] found that intake of ≥ 3 PUFA supplement capsules (both EPA-only and EPA + DHA) may reduce the risk of cardiac death. Maki et al. [11] reported that intake of > 1 g/d EPA + DHA may also reduce the risk of cardiac death. The National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence in the UK encourages fish intake but discourages PUFA supplementation for primary or secondary prevention of CVD [12], while the American Heart Association (AHA) suggests that individuals with coronary artery disease may benefit modestly from PUFA supplementation [13].

Of note, PUFA supplementation typically includes both omega-3 nonprescription dietary supplements and prescription (Rx) medications, and the two are radically different. For instance, PUFA Rx is regulated under the rigorous standards for safety, efficacy, and manufacturing of medications by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in US. But dietary supplements are managed as food and regulations are less strict. This discrepancy can result in a range of product problems such as substandard quality, insufficient content, etc. According to the US Pharmacopeia (USP), PUFA Rx products must contain at least 800 mg/g of EPA ethyl esters plus DHA ethyl esters. However, a recent US Department of Agriculture survey found that the most common amounts of dietary supplements were 180 mg of EPA and 120 mg of DHA per dose [13], which is much lower than the content required in PUFA Rx. Since some studies have found that dose may be an important factor affecting the cardiovascular protective effect of PUFA [10, 11], there may be discrepancies in the clinical effects of PUFA Rx and dietary supplements.

Previous systematic reviews of RCTs involved both omega-3 nonprescription dietary supplements and prescription (Rx) medications [9–11], which may bias the results. Current published systematic reviews could not accurately reflect the effect of PUFA Rx on CVD prevention. Therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis of RCTs to date to evaluate the efficacy of PUFA Rx medication in the prevention of CVD.

Materials and methods

Literature search

The current systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [14]. Two independent reviewers (SJ Dong and Q Du) conducted a comprehensive search for relevant published RCTs in Embase, MEDLINE/PubMed, and the Cochrane Library up to September 30, 2023. A third reviewer (RS Zhao) settled any disagreements. The search terms ‘omega-3 fatty acids’ and ‘cardiovascular disease’ were used without imposing any language restrictions. Additionally, manual screening of references from included trials and published meta-analyses was conducted to identify any additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

In the present investigation, only RCTs that evaluated the effect of PUFA Rx on cardiovascular protection with long-term follow-up durations (≥ 1 year) were incorporated. Any omega-3 PUFAs Rx product was eligible.

The assessment criteria for omega-3 PUFA Rx products according to US Pharmacopoeia and European Pharmacopoeia were as follows: (1) the total omega-3 acid ethyl esters content must be no less than 90%, with the combined content of EPA and DHA ethyl esters reaching no less than 80%; the EPA ethyl ester content should be no less than 40% and the DHA content no less than 34%; (2) the combined content of EPA and DHA ethyl esters must range between 800 mg/g and 880 mg/g, with the EPA ethyl ester content falling between 430 mg/g and 495 mg/g, and the DHA ethyl ester content between 347 mg/g and 403 mg/g; and (3) other high-purity EPA or DHA prescription products that comply with the criteria specified in the pharmacopoeia.

Data extraction

For each RCT included in the review, we extracted the baseline characteristics of the study population, intervention and control information, and clinical outcomes related to CVD. Two reviewers (SJ Dong and Q Du) independently extracted the relevant data using a pre-set data collection sheet. Any discrepancies or controversies were resolved by the third independent reviewer (RS Zhao).

Quality assessment

Quality assessment and risk of bias appraisals were performed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool for RCTs [15] which encompassed assessments regarding random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and healthcare personnel, blinded outcome assessment, completeness of outcome data, evidence of selective reporting, and potential biases.

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of cardiovascular events. The secondary outcomes included major cardiovascular events (MACEs), cardiac death, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction (MI), stroke and revascularization.

Due to the clinical variability in population characteristics, PUFA Rx components, dosing of supplements, and follow-up duration, we conducted subgroup analyses based on components (EPA-only or EPA + DHA), EPA + DHA dosage (1 capsule/day or > 1 capsule/day), follow-up duration (< 3 years or ≥ 3 years) and risk status (primary prevention, mixed prevention [primary plus secondary prevention], or secondary prevention).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was employed to compare the cumulative frequencies of preset CVD outcomes between the PUFA Rx and control groups. The relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were pooled using the Mantel–Haenszel method random-effects model in Cochrane Review Manager software (RevMan version 5.3). Heterogeneity was identified between studies and evaluated by visual inspection of forest plots and the I2 statistic, which indicates the percentage of variation across studies due to heterogeneity rather than chance. A funnel plot was utilized to analyze publication bias. We assessed publication bias via funnel plots and sensitivity analysis of CVD outcomes by sequentially removing each RCT. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all tests were two-sided.

Assessment of certainty of evidence

Two independent reviewers, YL Wang and Q Du, utilized the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) framework to assess level of the certainty of the evidence for each outcome in our meta-analysis.

Results

Evidence-based overview

After reviewing 41,922 studies from the databases, 957 were selected for full-text review. Ultimately, 12 RCTs published between 2001 and 2019, encompassing 99,830 participants, were included in the final analysis [16–27] (Fig. 1). The mean age of the participants in the studies ranged from 59.4 to 74.0 years, and the follow-up period varied from 1 to 6.2 years. The intervention drugs were PUFA Rx capsules, with two of them being EPA-only [17, 21] and the remaining ones being EPA + DHA compounds [16, 18–20, 22–27]. Most of the controls were capsules filled with mineral oil or vegetable oil, one [21] was statin (the intervention was PUFA Rx + statin), and one was [22] no supplement. The daily dosage of PUFAs Rx was 1 capsule (1 g) in 8 studies and more than 1 g/d in other 4 studies. The population involved primary prevention in one study [23], secondary prevention with PUFAs Rx used for acute MI in 3 studies [20, 22, 26], and a mix of primary and secondary prevention in other studies [16–19, 21, 24, 25, 27]. Primary outcomes

Cardiovascular events

Eight studies reported cardiovascular events [16–23, 25], comprising 5,140 cardiovascular events among 75,929 participants. One study [16] defined cardiovascular events as cardiac arrest, MI, revascularization, stroke, abdominal aortic aneurysm surgery and peripheral vascular revascularization; two studies [21, 25] limited cardiovascular events to coronary events alone. The remaining five studies' definitions were roughly consistent, encompassing a composite of coronary heart disease (angina, myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization and coronary heart disease death) and other unanticipated cardiovascular events (heart failure, stroke, transient ischaemic accident and other circulatory problems).

Compared to the control group, PUFA Rx demonstrated a 12% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular events (RR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.81–0.95]; P = 0.0007; I2 = 45%; tau2 = 0.00; Fig. 3; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S1). Subgroup analyses (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S8-11) showed a significant difference between PUFA Rx with EPA-only and those with EPA + DHA (P = 0.004). EPA-only reduced cardiovascular event risk by 21%, while EPA + DHA combined decreased it by 8% (EPA only: RR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.73–0.85]; P < 0.00001; I2 = 0; tau2 = 0.00; EPA + DHA: RR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.86–0.99]; P = 0.02; I2 = 0; tau2 = 0.00). Furthermore, long-term utilization of PUFA Rx (more than 3 years) exhibited a more pronounced effect among individuals with shorter duration of use (RR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.80–0.95]; P = 0.001; I2 = 46%; tau2 = 0.00). The reduction in cardiovascular event risk was statistically significant for both primary and secondary prevention (primary: RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.83–1.00]; P = 0.04; I2 = 0; tau2 = 0.00; secondary: RR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.74–0.94]; P = 0.003; I2 = 57%; tau2 = 0.01).

Secondary outcomes

MACEs

A total of 5,793 events occurred among 91,055 participants in eight RCTs [17, 18, 21–26]. In this meta-analysis, MACE outcomes (including fatal or nonfatal stroke, fatal or nonfatal myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death) were extracted for each study. MACEs in one study [21] were defined as major coronary events, i.e., excluding cerebrovascular events (stroke), while another study [25] defined MACEs as all-cause mortality, coronary reinfarction and stroke.

There was no significant difference in PUFA Rx between groups regarding MACEs incidence (RR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.83–1.03]; P = 0.17; I2 = 77%; tau2 = 0.02; Fig. 4; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S2). Notably, Subgroup analyses (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S12-14) revealed that EPA-only PUFA Rx reduced the risk of MACEs by 24% (RR, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.68–0.84]; P < 0.00001; I2 = 0; tau2 = 0.00; Table 2), while EPA + DHA showed no significant difference (RR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.89–1.08]; P = 0.66; I2 = 58%; tau2 = 0.00; Fig. 4). No study evaluated MACEs using EPA + DHA at a dosage > 1 g/day, thus subgroup analysis was not conducted for dosage.

Cardiac death

Ten studies with 4,051 deaths among 95,440 participants were included [16–25]. There was a 9% reduction in the risk of cardiac death (RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P = 0.02; I2 = 23%; tau2 = 0.00; Fig. 4; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S3). In the subgroup analysis (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S15-18), we found that long-term use of PUFA Rx (more than 3 years) may reduce the risk of cardiac death by 9% (RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P = 0.03; I2 = 32%; tau2 = 0.00), while short-term use of PUFA Rx (less than 3 years) may have no effect on cardiac death (RR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.39–2.59]; P = 1; I2 = N/A). The secondary prevention population may experience a 20% reduction in cardiac death risk (RR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.66–0.96]; P = 0.02; I2 = 9%; tau2 = 0.00; Table 2), while there was no significant difference in primary and mixed prevention populations (primary: RR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.79–1.18]; P = 0.72; I2 = 0%; tau2 = 0.00; mixed: RR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.88–1.00]; P = 0.06; I2 = 0%; tau2 = 0.00; Table 2).

All-cause mortality

All twelve studies [16–27] provided data on all-cause mortality for 99,830 participants and 7,842 deaths for any reason. PUFA Rx was not associated with reduced all-cause death (RR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.91–1.04]; P = 0.40; I2 = 45%; tau2 = 0.00; Fig. 4; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S4). No significant differences were detected in any of the subgroup analyses (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S19-22).

MI

Nine RCTs with 2,716 fatal and nonfatal MI events among 94,877 participants were included [17–25]. PUFA Rx, compared to the control, was associated with a reduced MI risk (RR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.73–0.96]; P = 0.009; I2 = 62%; tau2 = 0.02; Fig. 4; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S5). In the subgroup analysis (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S23-26), EPA alone reduced the MI risk by 28% (RR, 0.72 [95% CI, 0.62–0.82]; P < 0.00001; I2 = 0; tau2 = 0.00), while no significant difference was detected for EPA + DHA (RR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.76–1.02]; P < 0.10; I2 = 58%; tau2 = 0.02). The reduction in MI risk was 18% when taking PUFA Rx for more than 3 years (RR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.72–0.94]; P = 0.0008; I2 = 63%; tau2 = 0.02). However, similar results were not found in duration less than 3 years (RR, 1.42 [95% CI, 0.70–2.86]; P = 0.33; I2 = N/A). The primary prevention population benefited from PUFA Rx, but the effect was not seen in the mixed prevention or secondary prevention groups (primary: RR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.61–0.89]; P = 0.002; I2 = 0; mix: RR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.71–1.02]; P = 0.08; I2 = 69%; secondary: RR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.65–1.06]; P = 0.13; I2 = 25%).

Stroke

Eight studies [17–19, 21–25] were included, reporting 2,044 stroke events among 94,577 participants. PUFA Rx showed no effect on stroke prevention (RR, 1.03 [95% CI, 0.91–1.16]; P = 0.68; I2 = 45; tau2 = 0.01; Fig. 4; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S6). Similar results were observed in the subgroup analyses (Table 2).

Revascularization

Nine studies [17, 18, 20–26] reported revascularization for analysis, with 6,250 events among 91,242 participants. There was a 9% reduction in the risk of revascularization by PUFA Rx (RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P = 0.02; I2 = 63%; tau2 = 0.00; Fig. 4; prediction interval shown in the supplementary Figure S7).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis conducted by sequentially removing each study showed nonsignificant results. No significant publication bias was detected for cardiovascular events, cardiovascular death, all-cause death, stroke or revascularization. However, a potential significant publication bias was observed for MACE and MI.

GRADE certainty of evidence

High-certainty evidence indicates a probable reduction in the incidence of cardiovascular events. Moderate-certainty evidence suggests a likely decrease in the risks of cardiac death and myocardial infarction. Low-certainty evidence implies that PUFA Rx may diminish the risk of cardiac death and stroke. The effect of PUFA Rx on reducing the risk of MACE is nebulous, supported by very-low-certainty evidence, as is the potential for a reduction in the risk of revascularization procedures. Discussion

The current synthesis effort pooled 99,830 participants from 12 RCTs that compared PUFA Rx with placebo or statin for the prevention of CVD clinical outcomes. We further analyzed subgroups based on component composition of PUFA Rx capsules, dosage, follow-up duration, and risk status. Our findings indicate that use of PUFA Rx is associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular events, cardiac death, MI and revascularization. Patients with primary or secondary prevention of CVD could both benefit from a reduction in cardiovascular event risk, even at a daily dose of 1 g of PUFA Rx. PUFA Rx containing only EPA or long-term (more than 3 years) treatment provided a greater benefit for cardiovascular events, MACEs and MI. However, no effect was observed from PUFA Rx for all-cause mortality or stroke.

To our knowledge, this is the first and most comprehensive meta-analysis to assess the association between PUFA Rx and CVD risk. The inclusion criteria were specific for PUFA Rx, which distinguishes this study from other meta-analyses [9–11] that included a PUFA supplementation mixture of omega-3 nonprescription dietary supplements and Rx medications, and this discrepancy may lead to distinctive results in our study. Rizos et al. [10] analysed the cardiovascular effect of omega-3 supplementation and found that ≤ 1 capsule/day was not associated with any cardiovascular protective effect. Moreover, it concluded that ≥ 3 omega-3 capsules could reduce the risk of cardiovascular death, but the overall risk ratio was not reported. Abdelhamid et al. [9] included both omega-3 supplementation and dietary intervention and found that fish oil could not reduce the risk of cardiovascular events. Dietary supplements are managed by the FDA as food and distinguished from medications, which are subject to rigorous regulatory standards (Rx or over the counter). Multiple issues specific to omega-3 dietary supplements have been reported, including variable content, labelling inconsistencies, and poor product quality/impurity [28]. Therefore, the previous research based on PUFA supplement products cannot directly reflect the protective effect of PUFA Rx on the cardiovascular system as our study.

The cardiometabolic effects of DHA and EPA vary in terms of their potential benefits for cardiac protection. DHA increased the high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol concentration and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol concentration, as well as particle size, while EPA decreased HDL cholesterol and increased LDL cholesterol [8]. In the current study, we performed a subgroup analysis according to the different components in PUFA Rx and found that EPA-only demonstrated a more substantial benefit for cardiovascular events, MACEs, MI and revascularization. The two RCTs including EPA-only [17, 21] used high dosages (4 g/d or 1.8 g/d), which might explain their superior efficacy. Furthermore, the subgroup analysis suggested that EPA in combination with DHA also lowered the incidence of cardiovascular events and cardiovascular death.

Subgroup analysis was performed to compare the effects of 1 g/day and more than 1 g/day of EPA + DHA capsules on cardiac protection in 10 RCTs [16, 18–20, 22–27]. Although drug instructions recommend 2 g/d EPA + DHA PUFA Rx for lowering triglyceride, 1 g/d was recommended by the American Heart Association for cardioprotection [29]. Most of the RCTs [18, 19, 22–27] in the study used a 1 g/d EPA + DHA intervention, except for two that used > 1 g of EPA + DHA [16, 20]. This dose grouping differs from other systematic reviews and meta-analyses [9–11]. In the subgroup analysis of Abdelhamid et al. [9] that included 6 dose groups, no significant variations in cardiovascular outcomes were detected at any dose level. It might be related to the incorporation of dietary supplements, which render the effect less pronounced. However, increased supplementation of PUFA may decrease the risk of cardiac death when taking ≥ 3 PUFA supplement capsules (both EPA-only and EPA + DHA), as reported by Rizos et al. [10], or > 1 g/d EPA + DHA, as reported by Maki et al. [11]. Notably, systematic reviews and meta-analyses that included PUFA products did not differentiate between dietary supplements and medications. We discovered that EPA + DHA could lower cardiovascular events at 1 g/d PUFA Rx. The effect of > 1 g/d PUFA Rx on any cardiovascular outcomes was not statistically significant. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as only two RCTs with high doses were included, and both had small sample sizes and varied follow-up durations. The present meta-analysis demonstrated that PUFA Rx could benefit cardiovascular events in both primary and secondary CVD prevention, MI in primary prevention, and cardiac death in secondary prevention. The results of MI and cardiac death by risk status were mainly congruent with the studies by Rizos et al. [10] and Maki et al. [11].

According the AHA, there was no consensus on whether current evidence from RCTs suggested that omega-3 PUFA supplementation provides a significant benefit in patients at high risk for CVD [13]. Our study demonstrates that the use of PUFA prescription medications for the prevention of cardiovascular disease can reduce the risk of cardiovascular events, cardiac death, MI, and revascularization procedures. Guidelines from the European management of dyslipidaemias (2019) [30], the American Medical Care in Diabetes (2022) [31], and the Chinese Lipid Management (2023) [32] recommend the use of IcosaPent Ethyl, a prescription medication containing PUFA, for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) patients with elevated triglyceride levels to reduce the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events. In recent years, there has been an increase in the variety of fish oils available on the market. Clinicians should notice the distinction between dietary supplements and prescription drugs and carefully select prescription medications containing omega-3 PUFA to optimize cardiovascular risk reduction. The EPA and DHA content need to be noted when choosing fish oil dietary supplements for patients.

There are some limitations in the present study. First, limited studies [16, 20] have reported on high dose (> 1 g/d) of EPA + DHA and short follow-up duration (< 3 y) for inclusion in the subgroup analysis with small sample sizes, which might lead to an inaccurate prediction of clinical outcomes in these subgroups. Secondly, since some studies [21, 22] were not double-blinded, there exists potentials for bias. In addition, although PUFA prescription drugs are recommended for severe hypertriglyceridemia, our meta-analysis did not analyse the role of triglycerides in the cardioprotective effect of PUFA Rx. This is because the dosage used in most of the included RCTs did not reach the recommended dosage for hypertriglyceridemia and data were unavailable for subgroup analysis. Further studies are needed to evaluate the cardioprotective effect of PUFA Rx in these subgroups of patients.

Conclusion

PUFA Rx medication demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful risk reduction for cardiovascular events, cardiac death, myocardial infarction, and revascularization procedures. This study provides evidence of the efficacy of PUFA prescriptions in reducing the risk of cardiovascular disease and contributes to the ongoing discussion of the role of PUFA products in cardiovascular disease outcomes. Clinicians should carefully differentiate between dietary supplements and prescription drugs among fish oil products, considering the quality and regulatory status of supplements, and choose the appropriate variety of PUFA Rx medication depending on the patient’s condition and treatment goals. Moreover, clinicians should ensure that patients are adequately educated about the appropriate use and potential benefits of PUFA medications to enhance adherence and achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes.
