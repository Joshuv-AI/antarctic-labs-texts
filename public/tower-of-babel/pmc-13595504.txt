Differential expression of Musashi 2 in single and double corpus luteum of hybrid Mongolian sheep
Authors: Hao Qinqin, Li Penghui, Zhu Hefeng, Cao Guifang, Liu Haodong, He Yang, Zhang Zelin, Fan Jiaxin, Yin Junzhe, Li Yongqiang, Xu Dan, Du Chenguang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the differential expression of Musashi 2 (MSI2) in the single and double corpus lutea of Mongolian sheep (Xing’an black sheep) and its role in multiparity.ResultsResults showed two synonymous SNPs identified in the exons of MSI2. MSI2 was co-localized with GDF9/BMP15 in follicles and corpus lutea. MSI2 expression was significantly higher in single corpus luteum, whereas GDF9/BMP15 expression was higher in double corpus luteum. Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed significant negative correlations between mRNA and protein expression of MSI2 and GDF9, BMP15. The double corpus luteum group exhibited higher levels of P4, LH, E2 and lower FSH.ConclusionsThis study preliminarily elucidates the expression pattern of MSI2 in the corpus luteum of Mongolian sheep. It provides a theoretical basis for exploring the molecular regulatory mechanism of prolificacy in Mongolian sheep, and also offers a potential target for improving the reproductive performance of Mongolian sheep via molecular breeding techniques.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-12981-8.Keywords: Mongolian sheep, MSI2, Prolificacy trait, Single/double corpus luteum, Single nucleotide polymorphism

Introduction

A sheep (Ovis aries) provides valuable agricultural products to people, making it an important economic animal. Increasing ewe productivity and reproductive performance generally enhances the economic and biological efficiency of the sheep industry. Many factors affect reproductive performance, including litter size, season, photoperiod, age, nutrition, management, male behavior, and genetics [1]. The Xing’an black sheep is produced by crossbreeding Mongolian and blackhead Suffolk sheep, which have the advantageous characteristics of both parents, such as fast growth rate and good meat quality. However, its singleton trait greatly restricts the potential for economic benefits; therefore, improving the reproductive performance of Mongolian sheep is of great significance. Compared with technologies such as embryo transfer, screening for major prolificacy genes at the genetic level offers advantages such as a short running time, low cost, and simple operation. Musashi 2 (MSI2) is a highly conserved RNA-binding protein that plays a crucial role in mammalian follicular development [2–4]. MSI2 is mainly expressed in mouse ovarian granulosa cells and oocytes, and its expression levels increase as follicles develop, particularly in antral follicle granulosa cells [5]. Ying et al. [6] found that the conditional knockout of MSI2 in KRT14Cre/+:MSI2Flox/Flox mice led to superprolificacy in the third litter, suggesting that MSI2 may be a key regulatory gene of prolificacy. Subsequent research by our laboratory using the same mouse model revealed significantly higher follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels and increased follicle numbers in the late estrus [7], indicating “super-prolificacy.” Zhou et al. [8] reported significantly higher MSI2 expression in single dominant follicles compared with that in double dominant follicles in Mongolian sheep, suggesting that it plays an important role in follicular development and may regulate prolific traits.

Reproductive traits are subject to complex interactions between multiple genes and genetic loci; therefore, molecular genetic markers related to reproductive traits should be screened for in the genetic breeding of Mongolian sheep. A study found that the mutation site g.42,115,010 T > C in the exon region of the growth differentiation factor 9 (GDF9) gene may affect the litter size of sheep [9]. However, no single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites of MSI2 have been reported as candidate genes that affect the reproductive performance of Mongolian sheep.

The formation, degeneration cycle, and hormone secretion pattern of corpus luteum tissues affect the female reproductive cycle and pregnancy [10]. In a reproductive study on small-tailed Han sheep, RFRP-3 (which inhibits estrus) was found to be highly expressed during the luteal phase [11], whereas SMAD4 (involved in follicular development and granulosa cell differentiation) was more abundant in the luteal phase than in the follicular phase [12]. However, differences in MSI2 expression in the Mongolian sheep corpus luteum and its role in ovulation remain unreported. Therefore, in this study, we used single corpus luteum ovaries as the single-lambing control group and double-corpus-luteum ovaries as the twin-lambing experimental group. Through SNP analysis of MSI2 in corpus luteum tissues, we aimed to determine the structural variations and functions of MSI2 in single and double corpus luteum, elucidate its expression and molecular regulatory mechanisms in ovarian corpus luteum, and explore its association with prolificacy traits to provide a theoretical basis for improving reproductive efficiency.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The ovary tissues of Mongolian sheep were sourced from the Haoren Liyongqiang Sheep Farm in Keyouqian Banner, Xing’an League, northeastern Inner Mongolia. The animals were euthanized in accordance with the regulations and ethical guidelines outlined by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University (NND2025006). All experimental procedures were conducted accordingly.

Experimental materials

A total of 172 healthy, one-year-old, non-pregnant Mongolian hybrid sheep (Xing’an black sheep, Supplement 1 A, 1B, 1 C) of similar weight (with a body weight of 50–55 kg) were selected from the Haoren Li Yongqiang mutton farm in Keyouqian Banner, Xing’an League, northeastern Inner Mongolia, in September, during the estrus season of sheep in Inner Mongolia. All sheep were raised under similar standardized conditions, with free access to feed and water. The selection was conducted through rectal ultrasound monitoring, bilateral ovarian laparoscopy, and ram teasing (4 times a day, 30 min each time). After ram teasing, estrous ewes were selected, and laparoscopy was performed to examine the formation status and quantity of corpora lutea, and detect the content of progesterone in the serum to determine that the selected corpus luteum is the pregnancy corpus luteum (Appendix 1, Fig. 1). These ewes were slaughtered within 48 h after ovulation, and their bilateral ovaries were removed (The sheep were intravenously anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (20 mg/kg) and then euthanized via exsanguination, after which experimental samples were collected). The ovaries were collected aseptically within 30 min, washed 3 times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing penicillin-streptomycin, and immediately placed in a 4 °C incubator. The ewes that corresponded with each ovary collected were recorded to ensure sample traceability. In this experiment, corpus-luteum number as a proxy for ovulation rate. Grouping was performed based on the number and distribution of corpora lutea in the ovaries: the unilateral single-corpus-luteum group (only one side of the ovaries had one corpus luteum) served as the putative low-ovulation-rate group, the unilateral double-corpus-luteum group (one side of the ovaries had two corpora lutea), and the bilateral single-corpus-luteum group (each side of the ovaries had one corpus luteum) served as the putative high-ovulation-rate group. 40 ewes with one single-corpus-luteum in the ovary were assigned to the control group, 36 ewes with unilateral double-corpora-lutea and 36 ewes with bilateral single-corpora-lutea were placed in the experimental group. These ovarian samples were placed in centrifuge tubes and shipped to a sequencing company on dry ice. Part of the remaining ovaries was transported to the laboratory on dry ice and stored at -80 °C for subsequent RT‑qPCR and Western blot assays, while another portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence analysis. Concurrently, 10 mL of blood was collected from the jugular vein in a centrifuge tube, allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min at 3000 rpm. The upper serum was transferred into a cryopreservation tube and labeled as the control or experimental group before being stored in liquid nitrogen for subsequent experiments.

The preprocessed ovaries were fixed in a Petri dish lined with filter paper. After observing their surface characteristics under a stereomicroscope, they were cut open along the long axis using dissecting scissors. Incisions were made on the surface of mature follicles, and the follicular tissues were extracted. Subsequently, immature follicles in the cortex were peeled off, and the follicular tissues were separated. These follicular tissues were washed 3 times with PBS, and placed into a centrifuge tube. At the same time, the target corpus luteum tissues were observed and cut; after removing the attached excess tissues, they were washed 3 times with PBS and placed into the corresponding centrifuge tube. After the separation of both follicular tissues and corpus luteum tissues was completed, the outer cortex of the ovaries was cut and separated to obtain the medulla. The medulla sections were washed 3 times with PBS, cut into tissue blocks, and then placed into a centrifuge tube and label them. After all tissues were processed, they were immediately stored at -80 °C.

Extraction of total DNA from single and double corpus luteum

The ovaries were removed to obtain corpus luteum tissues of different groups, single and double corpus luteum DNA was extracted according to the tissue genomic DNA extraction kit (TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit, 4992199, Beijing). DNA concentration and purity were detected using a Biotek Synergy HT microplate reader, and qualified DNA was stored at -20 °C for later use.

Primer design and synthesis

Sequences of sheep MSI2 (GenBank: XM_060394727.1; Chromosome: 11; Exon count: 17), GDF9 (GenBank: NM_001142888.2), BMP15 (GenBank: NM_001114767.2), and β-Actin genes were retrieved from the NCBI database. Primers for these genes were designed using Primer 5.0 software, including all exon sequences of MSI2, and then synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The specific sequences are shown in Table 1 (Supplement 2).

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) amplification and SNP detection

The DNA extracted from single and double corpus luteum tissues was used for PCR amplification. The amplification system was: 1 µL template DNA, 1 µL 2×Reaction Mix (Shanghai Sangon, B500056, Shanghai), 1 µL each of upstream and downstream primers (10 µM), 2.5 µL Taq Buffer (with MgCl2) (Shanghai Sangon, B600017, Shanghai), 0.2 µL Taq enzyme (5 U/µL, Shanghai Sangon, B600001, Shanghai), and ddH2O to a final volume of 25 µL. PCR reaction program: 95 ℃ pre-denaturation for 4 min; 94 ℃ denaturation for 40 s, 59 ℃ annealing for 40 s, 72 ℃ extension for 30 s, 32 cycles; 72 ℃ final extension for 10 min. 5 µL of PCR product was detected by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis (220 V, 30 min), and the target PCR band was excised and recovered according to the SanPrep column DNA gel recovery kit (Shanghai Sangon, B518131, Shanghai), and sent to Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for sequencing. DNAStar software was used to splice the upstream and downstream sequences obtained after sequencing; SnapGene software was used for sequencing peak analysis, and SNPs were screened in combination with sequencing peaks; RNAfold online tool (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) was used to predict the changes in mRNA secondary structure at the mutation site.

Ovarian multiplex immunofluorescence

The six Mongolian sheep ovaries were preserved in 4% polyformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A, China) for 72 h, washed, dehydrated and embedded, and the thickness of continuous slices was 5 μm. After xylene dewaxed, the ovaries were rehydrated with ethanol, EDTA antigen repair (Solarbio, C1034, Beijing) was performed for 25 min, and 3% endogenous peroxidase was added dripping to block. The sections were blocked with 5% donkey serum at room temperature (20–25 ℃) for 15 min, then the corresponding primary antibody was added dripping, and incubated at 4 ℃ for 9 h. In the negative control group, 0.01 M PBS was used instead of the primary antibody; after dripping HRP secondary antibody (ZSGB-Bio, PV-9001, Beijing) and incubating at room temperature in the dark for 50 min, adding TSA-488 (Immunoway, YS0008, USA) staining working solution, incubating at room temperature in the dark for 10 min, incubating the primary and secondary antibody of the second antibody, and the aforementioned incubation procedure was repeated. After that, the TSA-561 (Immunoway, YS0010, USA) working solution was added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min, and finally, 4’,6-DiAmidino-2-PhenylIndole (DAPI) (Solarbio, S2110, Beijing) was used for mounting. The sections were mounted and imaged using a laser confocal microscope (Nikon C2; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The antibody information is shown in Table 2 (Supplement 2).

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from single and double luteal groups (unilateral double and bilateral single luteal) stored in liquid nitrogen, according to the RNA extraction kit’s instructions (TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit, Takara, 9767, Japan). After the concentration is qualified by the nucleic acid analyzer, the RNA was reverse transcribed to synthesize cDNA (PrimeScript™RT reagent Kit, Takara, RR037A, Japan). The 20 µL RT-qPCR reaction system was: SYBR®Green premix Ex TaqTM II 10 µL, upstream and downstream specific PCR primers 0.8 µL, and cDNA 9.2 µL. The reaction procedures were 95 ℃ for 30 s, 60 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 30 s, for 40 cycles (shown in Table 1 for primers). The β-actin gene was employed as the endogenous reference gene in RT-qPCR. Each group had 6 biological replicates and 3 technical replicates.

Western blot

Six samples were remove from each group of single- and double-corpus-luteum groups (unilateral double-corpus-luteum and bilateral single-corpus-luteum) preserved in liquid nitrogen, different groups of single- and double-luteum proteins were extracted according to the instructions of high-performance RIPA lysate (PMSF has been added) (CWBIO, CW2333S, Jiangsu), and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA method (Pierce Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, Cat# 23227, USA). After the concentration was unified, 16 µg was taken for SDS-PAGE (80 V/30 min, 120 V/90 min), transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk solution at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubated with antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. On day 2, the membrane was washed thrice with 1 × Tris Buffered Saline (TBS) buffer, and the secondary antibody was incubated on a shaker at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing with 1 × TBS, the membrane was placed the membrane in a protein scanning imaging system (Li-Cor, Odyssey-CLX, USA) for scanning photos, the fluorescence values of target protein and internal reference are counted. The antibody information is shown in Table 3 (Supplement 2). To verify the specificity of the antibody, we also performed control experiments using MSI2 antibody blocking peptide (Bioss, bs-17853P, Beijing), GDF9 antibody blocking peptide (Bioss, bs-4720P, Beijing), BMP15 antibody blocking peptide (Bioss, bs-6612P, Beijing). All antibody information in this experiment are provided in Supplement 2.

Reproductive hormone assays in serum

The concentrations of P4, E2, LH, and FSH were determined using sheep enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Wuhan Fine Biotech, ESH0086, EU0390, ESH0034, ESH0028, Wuhan). Assays included standard wells, blank wells, and sample wells (with triplicates). After adding reagents, set up standard wells, blank wells and sample wells on the enzyme labeling plate respectively (unilateral double-corpus-luteum group and bilateral single-corpus-luteum group, with 8 biological replicates in each group), set up 3 replicates for each well; add 40 µL of sample diluent and 10 µL of serum to be tested respectively to the sample wells, and gently mix well; except for blank wells, add 100 µL of enzyme-labeled reagent to each well, and mix well; seal the plate with a sealing membrane, incubate at 37 °C for 60 min; discard the liquid, spin dry, and wash the detergent (diluted 20 times with concentrated detergent) repeatedly 5 times, and pat dry; add 50 µL of chromogenic reagent A and chromogenic reagent B to each well in turn, gently mix well, react at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min; add 50 µL of stop solution to each well to terminate the reaction; measure OD450 nm value in microplate reader (Bio-Tek, Synergy HT, Germany). Negative controls (buffer-only) and kit-provided positive controls were included in each run.

Statistical analysis

The genotypes of each polymorphic locus were counted in Excel, and the SHESS online software was used (http://analysis.bio-x.cn/) Calculate the genotype frequency, allele frequency, genotype distribution chi-square value (χ2), observed heterozygosity (He), effective number of alleles (Ne) and polymorphism information content (PIC) of SNP loci; the statistical software GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to perform variance significance and Pearson correlation analysis on the test data. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to verify the normality of data distribution, and the Brown-Forsythe test was used to assess the homogeneity of variances. Based on the results of these tests, a t-test, one-way analyses, and Pearson correlation analyses were employed for data that conformed to a normal distribution and had homogeneous variances, followed by Duncan and Tukey’s multiple test (P < 0.05). Intergroup comparisons were performed using the same type of tissue from different individual ewes. For individuals with unilateral double corpora lutea or bilateral single corpora lutea, the two corpora lutea from one individual were regarded as a single biological sample and not counted repeatedly. The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and correlation coefficient (r). P < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

PCR amplification results

PCR amplification was performed using DNA from single/double luteal tissue DNA of different groups of Mongolian sheep as template. After gel electrophoresis detection, the results are as shown in Supplement 3. The amplification bands of all exons of MSI2 gene are clear, and the size of the target band meets the expectations, indicating that the amplification is successful and met the sequencing requirements.

Analysis of SNP detection results

The sequencing results were compared with SnapGene software. The results showed that Mongolian sheep MSI2 gene had two SNP sites in the exons of the coding region, which were Exon 6 g.7,828,531 A > G and Exon 11 g.8,144,512 C > G (Fig. 1). Analysis of the mutation sequence in Ensembl revealed that the g.7,828,531 A> G mutation caused the codon to change from A to G, i.e. CCA> CCG, but the amino acid remained proline and did not change, which was a synonymous mutation; the g.8,144,512 C> G mutation caused the codon to change from C to G, i.e. GGC> GGG, and the amino acid remained glycine and did not change, which was also a synonymous mutation, indicating that the MSI2 gene has a certain degree of conservatism.

Population genetic characteristics of MSI2 SNPs

The analysis of SNP population genetic characteristics results (Table 4, Supplement 4) shows that there are three genotypes in the exon region of MSI2 gene, Exon 6 g.7,828,531 A > G and Exon 11 g.8,144,512 C > G, namely AA, AG, GG and CC, CG, GG, with genotype frequencies of 0.66, 0.31, 0.03 and 0.64, 0.32, 0.04, respectively. The frequencies of allele A, G and C, G are 0.817, 0.183 and 0.821, 0.179, respectively. Among them, the dominant genes of the two mutation sites are A and C, and the dominant genotypes were AA and CC. The expected heterozygosity and effective allele number of the two mutation sites were 0.701, 1.427 and 0.706, 1.416 respectively. Both A> G and g.8,144,512 C> G sites were low polymorphism (PIC < 0.250) and in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P > 0.05). As shown in Table 5 (Supplement 4), the results of the association analysis between the number of ovarian corpora lutea and two SNP loci respectively indicated that there was no significant association between the two SNP loci of MSI2 and the number of corpora lutea (P > 0.05).

Prediction of MSI2 gene mRNA secondary structure

The RNAfold online tool was used to predict the effects of the Exon 6 g.7,828,531 A > G and the Exon 11 g.8,144,512 C > G site of the Mongolian sheep MSI2 gene on the secondary structure of mRNA. The results are shown in Fig. 2. The mRNA secondary structures of the two sites changed. The minimum free energy of the g.7,828,531 A> G site changed from − 421.20 kcal/mol to -423.20 kcal/mol, and the minimum free energy of the g.8,144,512 C> G site changed from − 369.10 kcal/mol to -372.00 kcal/mol.The minimum free energy after mutation increased, resulting in increased structural stability.

Analysis of MSI2 localization in mongolian sheep ovaries

Immunofluorescence technique was used to analyze the expression of MSI2 at different stages of follicular development and corpus luteum (n = 6). The results showed that MSI2 (red) was expressed in the oocytes of ovarian primordial follicles (Fig. 3A), the oocytes and granulosa cells of primary follicles (Fig. 3B), the granulosa cells, cumulus, and oocytes of antral follicles (Fig. 3C), as well as in the corpus luteum (Fig. 3D). The indicates that MSI2 may play an important role in follicular development, maturation and maintenance of corpus luteum function. At the same time, no positive signal was seen in the negative control (Fig. 3A1-D1), indicating that the results are specific and reliable.

Analysis of MSI2 expression in single/double corpus luteum

RT-qPCR results showed that the expression level of MSI2 mRNA in single-corpus-luteum tissue in double-corpus-luteum group was significantly higher than that in unilateral double-corpus-luteum group (P = 0.0005), the expression level of MSI2 mRNA in single-corpus-luteum group was significantly higher than that in bilateral single-corpus-luteum group (P = 0.0034, Fig. 4A); in follicles, the expression level of MSI2 mRNA in the single-corpus-luteum group was significantly higher than that in the unilateral double-corpus-luteum group of the double-corpus-luteum group (P = 0.0029), the expression level of MSI2 mRNA in follicular tissue of ovary in single-corpus-luteum group was significantly higher than that in bilateral single-corpus-luteum group (P = 0.0209, Fig. 4B); in medullary tissue, the expression level of MSI2 mRNA in medullary tissue of unilateral double-luteum ovaries and bilateral single-luteum ovaries in double-luteum group was not significantly different from that in single-luteum group (P = 0.3346; P = 0.8699, Fig. 4C). In summary, the expression level of MSI2 mRNA in the single-corpus-luteum control group was significantly higher than that in the double-corpus-luteum test group.

Analysis of co-localization of MSI2, GDF9 and BMP15 in ovaries

Multiple immunofluorescence results showed that MSI2 and GDF9 were co-expressed in the oocytes of ovarian primordial follicles (Fig. 5A), the oocytes and granulosa cells of primary follicles (Fig. 5B), the cumulus, oocytes and granulosa cells of antral follicles (Fig. 5C) and the corpus luteum (Fig. 5D). MSI2 and BMP15 were co-expressed in the oocytes of ovarian primordial follicles (Fig. 5E), the oocytes and granulosa cells of primary follicles (Fig. 5F), the cumulus, oocytes and granulosa cells of antral follicles (Fig. 5G) and the corpus luteum (Fig. 5H); at the same time, no specific staining signal was detected in the negative control group (Fig. 5I-L), indicating that the results were reliable. To sum up, it can be inferred that MSI2, GDF9 and BMP15 have dense co-localized zones in the ovary, suggesting that the three may interact with each other to jointly regulate follicular development process, thereby affecting the physiological function of the ovary.

Pearson correlation analysis between MSI2 and GDF9, BMP15

The Pearson correlation analysis was further performed on the data of MSI2 and the gene and protein expression of GDF9 and BMP15 to explore the relationship between MSI2 and the expression of GDF9 and BMP15. The results of gene Pearson correlation analysis showed that in the corpus luteum and follicle tissues, the Pearson correlation coefficients (r) of MSI2 and GDF9 and BMP15 were − 0.8416 (95% CI: -0.9394~-0.6172, P < 0.0001), -0.8957 (95% CI: -0.9608~-0.7371, P < 0.0001) and − 0.9515 (95% CI: -0.9821~-0.8719, P < 0.0001), -0.8850 (95% CI: -0.9566~-0.7125, P < 0.0001), respectively, indicating that there was a significant negative correlation between MSI2 and GDF9 and BMP15 genes in different tissues of the ovary (P < 0.05, Fig. 8A, B, C, D). Analysis of reproductive hormone measurement results

The levels of P4, LH, E2 and FSH in the serum of the one-side double-corpus-luteum (CL) group, the one-side single-CL group and the single-CL group were measured (n = 8). The results showed that the levels of P4 (Fig. 9A), LH (Fig. 9B) and E2 (Fig. 9C) in the one-side double-CL group and the one-side single-CL group were significantly higher than those in the single-CL group (P < 0.05); the FSH level in the one-side double-CL group was significantly lower than that in the single-CL group (P = 0.0003), and there was no difference in the FSH level between the bilateral single-CL group and the single-CL group (P = 0.1976, Fig. 9D), indicating that low expression of MSI2 may promote the secretion of P4, LH and E2 and inhibit the secretion of FSH, thereby regulating the formation of double-luteal bodies in Mongolian sheep.

Discussion

Xing’an black sheep are obtained through the binary hybridization of Mongolian sheep and Blackhead Suffolk sheep, combining the excellent traits from both parents, including fast growth, good meat quality, and prolificacy traits. The formation of a double corpus luteum is closely related to the prolificacy traits of Mongolian sheep and is of great significance for improving their reproductive efficiency. RNA-binding proteins can also influence luteal development and function by regulating the expression of key genes in luteal cells [13]. MSI2 is a highly conserved RNA-binding protein that plays an important role in follicular development. MSI2 may bind to mRNAs, such as gonadotropin-releasing hormone receptor (GnRHR) and follicle-stimulating hormone β subunit (FSH β), affecting gonadotropin secretion and thus regulate luteal development [14]. However, the role of MSI2 in the corpus luteum of hybrid Mongolian sheep has not been reported. This study indicates that MSI2 is expressed in the ovarian corpus luteum of Mongolian sheep and is highly expressed in single corpus luteum ovaries.

SNPs caused by single nucleotide variations have been widely used in animal genetic breeding, such as in marker-assisted selection and the construction of animal genetic maps [15]. Identifying SNPs in genes is important for improving animal production and reproductive performance. Ji et al. [16] found that the c.994 A > G (Fec GA) mutation site in GDF9 was significantly associated with litter size in the Suffolk × Ujimqin hybrid population F1, and the c.31_33CTTinsdel (B1) mutation site in bone-morphogenetic protein 15 (BMP15) was significantly associated with litter size in the Ujimqin sheep population, indicating that these two mutation sites may be potentially effective genetic markers for improving sheep litter size. To explore the relationship between MSI2 and the prolificacy traits of Mongolian sheep, we analyzed the entire genome of MSI2 for SNPs and identified two sites, g.7,828,531 A > G and g.8,144,512 C > G, both of which were low polymorphism sites, indicating low genetic diversity of alleles and genotypes in the population, possibly related to the long-term within-breed selection of Mongolian sheep. The genotype distribution of the g.7,828,531 A > G and g.8,144,512 C > G sites conformed to the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, indicating that their alleles and genotypes can be stably inherited in the population [17], and have not been affected by gene mutation, selection, and genetic drift.

The boundary region between exons and introns strictly follows the GT-AG splicing rule, which is crucial for ensuring accurate mRNA splicing and maturation of mRNA [18]. In this study, sequencing revealed a C-to-G base mutation in the MSI2 gene, where the first base G is exactly the last base of the tenth exon, and the subsequent two bases, GG, are located in the first two bases of the tenth exon. Mutations spanning the exon boundaries are worthy of attention. The integrity of the base sequence at the exon terminus is crucial for the correct recognition of splice donor sites. Disrupting the exon splicing enhancer or introducing a splicing silencer can interfere with the normal splicing process of pre-mRNA, leading to the production of abnormal splicing variants that affect the translation and functional conformation of proteins [19]. The SNPs in the MSI2 gene of Mongolian sheep are synonymous mutations that do not change the amino acid sequence; therefore, the structure and function of the protein encoded by this gene are consistent with the reference sequence, showing a certain degree of conservation. However, increasing evidence has shown that synonymous mutations do not necessarily mean functional “neutrality” and may affect gene expression by changing mRNA stability, secondary structure, splicing process, and translation kinetics [20]. Studies in Escherichia coli, yeast, and fruit flies all support the translation selection theory of dominant codons, and there is a strong correlation between synonymous codon usage bias and gene expression levels. In the human genome, there are significantly more codons ending in GC in constitutive exons than alternative splicing exons, indicating that synonymous codon usage may also involve translation selection [21]. In this study, the prediction analysis of the secondary structure of mRNA before and after the MSI2 mutation site showed that the secondary structure of the mRNA changed before and after the mutation at both sites, and the minimum free energy decreased with increased structural stability. These changes may affect the interactions between MSI2 and RNA-binding proteins, thereby regulating their expression and function. Further studies on the protein levels of MSI2 found that its protein expression in the double corpus luteum group (unilateral double corpus luteum and bilateral single corpus luteum) was significantly lower than that in the single corpus luteum group, consistent with the expression results in single- and double-dominant follicles. It has been speculated that this mutation may lead to low MSI2 expression, thereby affecting the prolificacy traits of Mongolian sheep. In the future, a larger sample size should be used, and more phenotypic data should be combined to further study the linkage between these SNP sites and prolificacy traits.

The prolificacy traits of livestock are complex traits influenced by the micro-effect accumulation of multiple genes and environmental factors [22]. GDF9 and BMP15 belong to the transforming growth factor-β superfamily of secretory signaling molecules, which play important roles in regulating follicle formation, growth and development, ovulation, and luteal function [23]. Currently, GDF9 and BMP15 are the major genes controlling sheep prolificacy. The ratio of GDF9 to BMP15 mRNA expression in oocytes of multi-ovulating animals (such as rats) is significantly higher than that in single-ovulating animals (such as sheep), indicating different expression levels of GDF9 and BMP15 in different mammals [24]. GDF9 and BMP15 exhibit synergistic effects during multiple stages of follicular growth and development [25]. In this study, the multiplex immunofluorescence results showed that MSI2 co-expresses with GDF9 and BMP15 in primordial follicles, primary follicles, cumulus oophorus, oocytes, granulosa cells of antral follicles, and corpus luteum. This is consistent with the findings of Silva et al. [26] and Sun et al. [27], suggesting that MSI2 may interact with GDF9 and BMP15 functionally. Pearson’s correlation analysis further showed negative correlations between MSI2 mRNA and protein expression levels and GDF9/BMP15; however, the specific regulatory mechanism requires further verification.

Sheep reproductive characteristics are cyclical and regulated by various endocrine hormones, and MSI2, as a highly conserved RNA binding protein, can play an important role in various endocrine hormone signaling pathways by regulating mRNA translation, stability, and degradation. The hypothalamus-pituitary-ovary axis is the dominant axis that regulates neuroendocrine function and reproduction in female mammals [28]. To further reveal the effect of reproductive hormones on multiple fertility traits, we tested the changes in reproductive hormones in different luteal types. The results showed that the P4 levels in the unilateral double corpus luteal group and the bilateral single corpus luteal group were significantly higher than those in the single corpus luteal group, which was consistent with the findings of McNatty et al. [29], that is, the number of corpora luteal is positively correlated with the amount of P4 secreted, and the total amount of P4 in sheep with double corpus lutea can be 1.5-2 times that in single corpus luteal groups. In vitro culture studies of follicular GCs have shown that, during the follicular and luteal phases, the level of E2 in the follicular fluid is high, and follicular development is related to the proliferation of GCs and LH-binding sites in the GC and TC layers [30]. In this study, the levels of LH and E2 were significantly higher in the unilateral double corpus luteal group and bilateral single luteal groups than in the single luteal group, and the level changes were consistent. He et al. [31] and Gong et al. [32] reported that FecB mutations increase the sensitivity of granulosa cells to FSH by inhibiting the activity of the BMP/SMAD pathway and indirectly increasing the LH threshold. In this study, the LH level in the double-luteal body group increased, indicating that biconovulating sheep require a higher LH peak to simultaneously trigger multi-follicular ovulation [33]. The E2 levels in the double luteal group were significantly higher than those in the single luteal group [34]. The FSH level in the unilateral double-corpus-luteum group was significantly lower than that in the single-corpus-luteum group, whereas there was no difference between the bilateral and single-corpus-luteum groups. This difference may be due to the selective inhibition of s tatin, resulting in direct inhibition of pituitary FSHβ subunit transcription by the SMAD2/3 pathway [35].

The effect of MSI2 on prolificacy traits in Mongolian sheep still needs to be further validated using a larger sample size. With the application of technologies such as single-cell omics, brain stereotactic injection, gene editing, and epigenomics, future research should explore the interactions between MSI2 and GDF9/BMP15 in different species, developmental stages, and tissues. Additionally, using network biology methods to reveal the complex relationships between MSI2 and other prolificacy-related genes will help improve the MSI2 regulatory network and clarify the genetic mechanisms of prolificacy traits, thereby providing new methods for molecular breeding.

Conclusions

In this study, two SNP sites (g.7828531 A > G and g.8144512 C > G) were identified in the MSI2 gene of Mongolian sheep, which are synonymous mutations that stabilize the secondary structure of MSI2 mRNA and confirm that MSI2 has a certain degree of conservation. MSI2 protein expression levels in the corpus luteum and follicle tissues of the single-corpus-luteum group were significantly higher than those in the double-corpus-luteum group (unilateral double-corpus-luteum and bilateral single-corpus-luteum), and MSI2 was co-expressed with the prolificacy-related genes GDF9 and BMP15 in primordial follicles, primary follicles, antral follicles, and corpora lutea of the ovary, and both the gene and protein expression levels were negatively correlated. Meanwhile, the P4, LH, and E2 levels in the double-corpus-luteum group were significantly higher than those in the single-corpus-luteum group, whereas the FSH levels were significantly lower. These results suggest that MSI2 may affect the prolific traits of Mongolian sheep by regulating luteal function. Future studies should expand the sample size and incorporate more phenotypic data to deeply analyze the linkage between MSI2 gene SNP loci and prolificacy traits; further clarify the molecular regulatory mechanisms of MSI2 with GDF9 and BMP15 respectively, and explore more key regulatory factors, so as to comprehensively and profoundly reveal the functional mechanism of MSI2 in ovulation rate and luteal function in Mongolian sheep.
