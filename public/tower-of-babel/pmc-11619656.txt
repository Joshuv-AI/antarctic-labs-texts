WTAP mediates IL-1β-induced chondrocyte injury by enhancing CA12 mRNA stability depending on m6A modification
Authors: Gang Deng, Yizhou Xu, Zhengnan Li, Guangxuan Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) poses a significant risk to the mobility of patients. Carbonic anhydrase 12 (CA12) can boost apoptosis and inflammation in several cancers, but its role in OA is unknown.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes in OA were analyzed using the GEO database (GSE169077). RT-qPCR and western blot estimated relative mRNA and protein levels of CA12. Cell viability and apoptosis were estimated by cell counting and flow cytometry assays. Oxidative stress (OxS) was determined by detecting with ROS and MDA levels, as well as CAT and SOD activities. Cytokine levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were detected by ELISA. Parameters associated with apoptosis and extracellular matrix (ECM) were detected by western blot. The m6A modification profile was determined by methylated RNA immunoprecipitation assays.ResultsRelative CA12 and wilms’ tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP) mRNA and protein levels were overexpressed in OA articular cartilages and IL-1β-challenged chondrocytes CHON-001. CA12 silencing impaired IL-1β-induced cell apoptosis, inflammation, OxS, and ECM degradation in chondrocytes. Yet, CA12 overexpression exerted an opposing function. WTAP reinforced the stability of CA12 mRNA depending on the m6A modification. Furthermore, WTAP knockdown weakened cell apoptosis, inflammation, OxS, and ECM degradation in chondrocytes induced by IL-1β, but these changes were impaired after CA12 overexpression. In addition, WTAP knockdown mitigates cartilage degeneration in DMM-induced mouse models.ConclusionIL-1β-induced WTAP enhances CA12 mRNA stability depending on m6A modification, thus promoting chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammatory response, OxS, and ECM degradation, providing evidence to support the possibility of WTAP and CA12 as potential targets for OA treatment.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, M6A, CA12, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative disease in the joints caused by the interaction of genetic, metabolic, biochemical and physical factors [1]. The characteristic features of OA are cartilage destruction, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone thickening, and bone capsule formation, with clinical manifestations of swollen and painful joints, temporary stiffness, and functional limitations [2]. An average prevalence of 13.2% was found in people over 55 years of age in Beijing [3]. A gradual increase in the incidence of OA is expected with an aging population and rising obesity rates [4]. For most people with OA, painkillers, anti-inflammatory drugs, and corticosteroids are administered to slow the progression of the disease without curing it [5]. Therefore, deciphering the mechanisms involved in OA progression is urgently needed to provide directions for developing strategies to treat OA.

In recent years, the association between oxidative stress (OxS) and OA has been recognized by a growing number of scholars [6]. For example, OxS contributes to OA by accelerating the telomere shortening and senescence of chondrocytes significantly [7]. Furthermore, OxS accelerates mitochondrial metabolic disorders and increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing cartilage degradation through cleavage of collagen and aggrecan as well as activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) directly, leading to the promotion of OA progression [8].

Carbonic anhydrases (CAs), a class of metalloenzymes, catalyze the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide (CO2 + H2O ⇋ HCO3− + H+) [9]. This reaction regulates the acid–base balance of the organism and participates in numerous physiological processes such as gluconeogenesis and urea production [10]. The available evidence highlights a role for CAs in multiple human diseases, such as in neuropathic pain [11], cerebral ischemia [12], rheumatoid arthritis [13], OxS [14], and cancer [15]. CA12 is a transmembrane protein of the CA family. Upregulated CA12 engages in chemoresistance via interaction with P-glycoprotein in human cancers [16]. Decreased expression of CA12 has been reported in degenerated intervertebral discs, and CA12 inhibition may lead to decreased synthesis of extracellular matrix (ECM), thus aggravating the degenerated intervertebral disc disease [17, 18].

Therefore, this study focuses on the role of CA12 in OA and the mechanism of CA12 dysregulation, which provides new mechanisms to further figure out OA progression.

Materials and methods

Data collection

OA-related datasets were searched from the freely available GEO database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The GSE169077 dataset containing knee cartilage samples from 5 normal subjects and 6 OA patients was gained. Analysis of DEGs was implemented using the GEO2R tool attached to the GEO. A screening of DEGs was conducted based on the criteria with |log2 (FC)|> 1 and p < 0.05.

Clinical articular cartilages

Knee articular cartilages (n = 25) were collected from OA patients who underwent total knee replacement surgery at the Ganzhou People’s Hospital. Control cartilage samples were taken from trauma patients who had never suffered from OA or rheumatoid arthritis, with an age matched to OA patients. After the operation, these knee articular cartilages were stored in liquid nitrogen immediately. All cartilage donors had provided written informed consent. Approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Ganzhou People’s Hospital.

Cell culture

Human chondrocytes CHON-001 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone) and 1% antibiotics (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) specific for targeting CA12 (sh-CA12) or WTAP (sh-WTAP), an overexpression plasmid for CA12 (oeCA12), and their corresponding negative controls (sh-NC, oe-NC) were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transient transduction was undertaken using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the directions provided by the manufacturer. Lentiviral particles were generated using a lentiviral packaging system consisting of psPAX2 (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) and PMD2.G (Addgene). The infection of host cells with lentiviral particles was performed in the presence of 6 µg/mL polybrene (Solarbio). Stable transduced cells were then selected with puromycin (Solarbio) for a fortnight. After transfection, chondrocytes were treated with 10 ng/mL of IL-1β (Solarbio) for 24 h.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

The MolPure Cell/Tissue DNA kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was utilized to extract total RNA from clinical articular cartilages and cultured chondrocytes in line with the operating instructions provided by the manufacturer. After examination of RNA concentration, reverse transcription was undertaken with a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Dalian, China). The complementary DNA was then amplified to detect relative WTAP and CA12 mRNA levels by qPCR using a SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the thermocycler conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, and 60 °C for 30 s. Quantification of fold change was made using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are registered in Table 1.

Antibodies and western blot analysis

Clinical articular cartilages and cultured chondrocytes were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein samples were quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Protein samples (approximately 30 μg) were separated on 8–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were immunoblotted with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Lastly, visualization of the blots was accomplished using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate reagent (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Primary antibodies used: CA12 (ab195233, 1/10000; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), WTAP (ab195380/ab155655, 1/1000; Abcam); anti-Bax (ab32503, 1/2000; Abcam), anti-Cleaved caspase-3 (ab32351, 1/5000; Abcam), and anti-MMP13 (ab51072/ab39012, 1/1000; Abcam), anti-COL2A1 (ab34712, 1/2000; Abcam), anti-aggrecan (ab3778, 1/1000; Abcam), and anti-GAPDH (ab8245, 1/5000; Abcam).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Chondrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates (4 × 103 cells) and cultured overnight, followed by application of IL-1β (10 ng/mL) for 24 h. The CCK-8 reagent (10 µL; Solarbio) was added and incubated for 2 h. Measurement of the OD value at 450 nm was conducted by a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, Vermont, USA).

Flow cytometry assay

After incubation for 48 h, the cells were collected and trypsinized and washed with PBS. Subsequently, cells were suspended and stained with the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime) as per the instructions supplied by the manufacturer. Cell apoptosis was monitored by a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA).

ROS analysis

To measure ROS production, we labeled chondrocytes with a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Solarbio). The chondrocytes were taken for frozen sections, and incubated in 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C. Observation of stained cells was carried out with a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Image J software was used for quantitative fluorescence analysis.

Measurement of OxS-related indicators

The chondrocytes were lysed and centrifuged (4 °C, 3000 rpm) for 15 min, followed by the collection of the supernatants. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) as well as the intracellular malonaldehyde (MDA) were assessed using the corresponding kits (Jiancheng Bio., Nanjing, China; Beyotime) in terms of the manufacturer’s directions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Interleukin (IL)−6 and IL-1β levels released from cultured cells were assessed with specific ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) in according to the kit instructions.

RNA stability analysis

Transfected chondrocytes were treated with IL-1β and actinomycin D (5 μg/mL) for 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 h. After extraction of total RNA, CA12 mRNA levels were analyzed by RT-qPCR.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

Briefly, IL-1β-treated chondrocytes were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime). Cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with an anti-WTAP or anti-IgG antibody at 4 °C overnight. The captured products were obtained for the assessment of CA12 mRNA levels. Execution of the RIP assay was done as per the manufacturer’s instructions using the Magna RIP™ RNA Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA).

Methylated RIP (MeRIP)-qPCR

For the meRIP assay, a methylated RNA immunoprecipitation kit (BersinBio, Guangzhou, China) and an RNA immunoprecipitation kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) were applied. Isolation of total RNA from IL-1β-treated chondrocytes was undertaken, followed by fragmentation to approximately 300 bp using an ultrasonic cell disruptor. Then, the fragmented RNA was co-immunoprecipitated with an anti-m6A antibody at 4 °C for 4 h, followed by incubation with protein A/G beads for 1 h and eluting by proteinase K to acquire the m6A-modified RNA. RNA samples were subjected to RT-qPCR analysis to analyze CA12 abundance.

Experimental animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (8-week-old; Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were maintained at 21 °C ± 2 °C on a 12-h light/dark cycle and with food and water provided ad libitum. All experimental protocols were performed in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ganzhou People´s Hospital. Posttraumatic OA mouse models were constructed by destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery [19]. After anesthesia, mice in the Sham group underwent a sham operation via an incision in the medial capsule of the knee joint (n = 6). Twelve C57BL/6 mice were separated into 2 groups: DMM and DMM + sh-WTAP.

Adeno-associated virus 9 containing sh-WTAP (sh-WTAP) and its control virus (WZ Biosciences, Jinan, China) was administered to C57BL/6 J mice (10 µl, 1 × 1012 vg/ml) by intra-articular injection. Three weeks later, the DMM surgery was performed to establish posttraumatic OA mouse models. The knee joints of the mice were collected for subsequent experiments at the eighth week after surgery.

Histological analysis

The knee joint specimens were fixed in 10% zinc-buffered formalin overnight, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4-μm sections. After deparaffinization with xylene and gradient hydration, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) (Beyotime) or safranin O/fast green (Biosharp, Hefei, China) following the corresponding instructions.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least 3 times and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were processed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparison of the two groups was done using t-test. Comparisons of multiple groups were conducted using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Higher CA12 expression is associated with OA

To comprehend the pathogenesis of OA, we analyzed DEGs in OA patients versus normal controls using data from the GEO database (GSE169077). Among all DEGs, CA12 with a significant upregulation caught our eye (Fig. 1A). Consistent with the database results, CA12 mRNA and protein levels were significantly higher in knee articular cartilages from OA patients than those from non-osteoarthritic controls (Fig. 1B and C). Moreover, chondrocytes showed an overt elevation in CA12 expression in response to IL-1β stimulation (Fig. 1D and E). These findings demonstrated that higher CA12 expression is related to OA.

B and C RT-qPCR (n = 25) and western blot (n = 3) detected CA12 mRNA and protein levels in knee articular cartilages from OA patients and non-osteoarthritic controls. **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001 vs. control; unpaired t-test. CA12 silencing weakens chondrocyte injury urged by IL-1β

To elucidate the role of CA12 in OA pathogenesis, CA12 was stably knocked down in chondrocytes and challenged with IL-1β. We observed that the elevated transcriptional and translational levels of CA12 in IL-1β-challenged chondrocytes were partly reversed after CA12 knockdown (Fig. 2A and B). CA12 knockdown remarkably restrained IL-1β-induced decrease in cell survival and increase in cell apoptosis in chondrocytes (Fig. 2C and D). As expected, CA12 silencing impaired the elevated Cleaved caspase-3 and Bax protein levels in IL-1β-challenged chondrocytes (Fig. 2E). Indicators associated with OxS were also tested. The results showed that IL-1β stimulation facilitated ROS production, elevated CAT and SOD activities, and decreased MDA levels, yet these changes were eroded following CA12 downregulation (Fig. 2F-G). Furthermore, silenced CA12 expression significantly undercut IL-1β-prompted inflammation, accompanied by reduced release of IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 2H). For ECM-associated proteins, downregulation of COL2A1 and aggrecan and upregulation of MMP13 in IL-1β-prompted cells were overturned after knockdown by CA12 (Fig. 2I). Together, these results suggested that CA12 silencing lessens IL-1β-urged chondrocyte injury.

Increased CA12 expression aggravates chondrocyte injury mediated by IL-1β

To further establish the function of CA12, we also explored the effect of CA12 upregulation on IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes. Figure 3A and B showed that CA12 overexpression further strengthened the elevated transcriptional and translational levels of CA12 in IL-1β-challenged cells. Moreover, CA12 upregulation repressed cell viability and facilitated cell apoptosis in IL-1β-urged cells, along with increased Cleaved caspase-3 and Bax protein levels (Fig. 3C-E). As expected, IL-1β stimulation-mediated effects on ROS production, CAT and SOD activities, and MDA levels were further intensified after CA12 elevation (Fig. 3F-G). Also, upregulation of CA12 promoted the release of IL-6 and TNF-α from IL-1β-challenged cells (Fig. 3H). Additionally, overexpression of CA12 elevated COL2A1 and aggrecan protein levels, as well as decreased MMP13 protein levels in IL-1β-induced cells (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these findings manifested that CA12 upregulation contributes to IL-1β-induced chondrocyte injury.

WTAP facilitates CA12 mRNA stability through an m6A-dependent mechanism

To figure out the mechanism of CA12 deregulation, we used the RMBase website to predict the “writer” that may mediate the m6A modification of CA12 mRNA. The results suggested that the m6A modification of CA12 mRNA may be mediated by WTAP. SRAMP predicted the m6A modification site on CA12 with very high confidence (Fig. 4A). Next, we tested the transcriptional and translational levels of WTAP in OA patients' knee cartilages. A significant upregulation of WTAP mRNA and protein levels was observed in OA patients’ knee cartilages versus controls (Fig. 4B and C). Moreover, WTAP mRNA and protein levels were also markedly elevated in IL-1β-challenged chondrocytes, yet these changes were undercut after WTAP knockdown (Fig. 4D and E). However, IL-1β-mediated upregulation of CA12 protein levels in chondrocytes was partially reversed after WTAP silencing (Fig. 4F). Analysis of RNA stability displayed that WTAP knockdown significantly accelerated the decay of CA12 mRNA in IL-1β-stimulated cells (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, the anti-WTAP antibody significantly enriched CA12 mRNA in IL-challenged cells in comparison with the anti-IgG antibody, as detected by RIP (Fig. 4H). MeRIP assays demonstrated that WTAP knockdown reduced the m6A levels of CA12 mRNA in IL-1β-challenged cells (Fig. 4I). Collectively, these findings suggested that WTAP mediates CA12 mRNA stability through an m6A-dependent mechanism.

WTAP regulates chondrocyte injury by regulating CA12 under IL-1β stimulation

To explore the role of the WTAP/CA12 axis, we generated chondrocytes co-transfected with sh-WTAP and oe-CA12. As expected, CA12 overexpression significantly reversed WTAP knockdown-mediated suppressive effects on CA12 mRNA and protein levels in IL-1β-challenged cells (Fig. 5A and B). Moreover, WTAP knockdown elevated cell viability and restrained cell apoptosis in IL-1β-stimulated cells, along with downregulating the protein levels of Cleaved caspase-3 and Bax (Fig. 5C-E). Also, downregulation of WTAP repressed ROS production, elevated CAT and SOD activities, decreased MDA content, and lightened release of IL-6 and TNF-α in chondrocytes with IL-1β stimulation. Nevertheless, overexpression of CA12 reversed these alterations drove by WTAP silencing (Fig. 5F-H). Additionally, CA12 elevation impaired the reduced protein levels of MMP13 and the elevated protein levels of COL2A1 and aggrecan in IL-1β-driven cells propelled by WTAP downregulation (Fig. 5I). Together, these results indicated that WTAP facilitates IL-1β-driven chondrocyte injury by mediating CA12 expression.

WTAP silencing eases cartilage degeneration in DMM-induced mouse models

Based on the above results, we constructed DMM-induced mouse OA models to verify the function of WTAP in vivo. HE staining showed that mice in the Sham group possessed well-arranged chondrocytes with an intact chondrocyte structure, while mice in the DMM group suffered from disorganized chondrocyte arrangement, disrupted cell structure, and extensive cell necrosis. However, WTAP knockdown can improve chondrocyte destruction in DMM mice (Fig. 6A). Safranin O/fast green staining showed that articular cartilages of mice in the DMM group showed less red staining, whereas WTAP knockdown enhanced red staining of articular cartilages in DMM mice, suggesting that WTAP knockdown ameliorated cartilage degeneration (Fig. 6B). In addition, CA12 and MMP13 protein levels were overexpressed in articular cartilages from DMM-induced mice, but these changes were impaired after WTAP silencing (Fig. 6C). All outcomes manifested that WTAP knockdown mitigates cartilage degeneration in DMM-induced mouse models.

Discussion

Articular cartilage is unable to repair itself after an injury due to the absence of lymphatic vessels, nerves, and blood vessels [20]. Currently, therapeutic options for OA can only relieve symptoms but not prevent or reverse the course of the disease [21]. Therefore, elucidating the pathological mechanisms associated with OA progression is particularly important for developing new strategies for the clinical treatment of this disease.

Nutritional sources of articular chondrocytes are mainly dependent on the permeation of joint fluid and the ECM, thus the ECM exerts a vital role in maintaining the homeostasis of articular cartilage [22]. OxS has been established as an element that contributes to OA pathogenesis, which is mainly related to excessive ROS [23]. Regarding ROS, it acts as a cellular messenger in the normal signal transduction and regulates bone tissue metabolism. Yet, disruption of the mitochondrial stability in chondrocytes by excessive ROS alters the mitochondrial membrane potential, causes OxS, and accelerates chondrocyte apoptosis, thereby exacerbating OA [24]. Also, excessive ROS induces matrix-degraded proteins and reduces the synthesis of ECM, leading to an abnormal bone metabolism, causing cartilage damage [25]. In addition, ROS can activate transcription factors to regulate the production of anti-oxidant proteins and pro-inflammatory factors, such as Nrf2 and NF-κB [26]. Thus, exploring the mechanisms that cause ROS hyperproduction, OxS, ECM degradation, and inflammation in articular chondrocytes is critical.

In the current study, we screened an up-regulated DEG CA12 from the GEO database (GSE169077) associated with knee OA. In line with the results in GSE169077, the up-regulation of CA12 at the transcriptional and translational levels was also validated in OA articular cartilage in this study. Interestingly, IL-1β stimulation elevated CA12 expression in chondrocytes, and CA12 silencing impaired apoptosis and ROS production, reduced MDA and inflammation, elevated CAT and SOD activities, as well as improved ECM degradation in chondrocytes urged by IL-1β. Chen et al. disclosed the protection of endplate cartilage from degeneration by CA12, which is mediated by the IGF-1/PI3K/CREB pathway [18]. COL2A1, the main component of the ECM synthesized by chondrocytes, reflects the level of anabolism [27]. MMP13, one of the most important metalloproteinases, degrades COL2A1 and aggrecan in chondrocytes [28]. Yang et al. uncovered that CA12 inhibition significantly reduced the increase in COL2A1 and aggrecan protein levels and decrease in MMP13 protein levels mediated by hydroxysafflor yellow A in tert-Butyl hydroperoxide-induced nucleus pulposus cells [29]. In contrast, the present study demonstrated that CA12 knockdown inhibited IL-1β-mediated changes in MMP13, COL2A1, and aggrecan protein levels in chondrocytes, indicating that CA12 facilitates ECM degradation. Our study preliminarily identified a supportive role for CA12 in IL-1β-induced dysfunction of chondrocytes, and OA animal models need to be constructed to validate the role of CA12 in OA in the future.

Accumulating evidence suggests a potential relationship between OA and epigenetics, including non-coding RNAs, non-histone posttranslational modification, histone modification, m6A, and DNA methylation [30, 31]. m6A, a dynamically reversible epigenetic modification, has a large impact on OA. Abnormal modification of m6A is also strongly associated with inflammation, ECM degradation, and OxS in OA progression [32]. WTAP is a widely expressed nuclear protein that can mediate m6A modification by binding to METTL3 and METTL14 to form WTAP-METTL3-METTL14 complexes [33]. Liu et al. uncovered that WTAP promotes the pyroptosis of fibroblast-like synoviocytes by elevating m6A modification of NLRP3 in rheumatoid arthritis [34]. Alao, WTAP facilitates intervertebral disc degeneration by – mediating m6A modification of lncRNA NORAD [35]. Several studies indicated that WTAP facilitates OA progression by mediating the m6A modification of multiple RNAs, such as miR-92b-5p and FRZB [36, 37]. The websites of SRAMP and RBPsuite predicted that the m6A methylation modification of CA12 may be mediated by WTAP. The current research showed that WTAP was overexpressed in OA samples and IL-1β-challenged chondrocytes, and WTAP reinforced the stability of CA12 mRNA depending on the m6A modification. Furthermore, WTAP knockdown weakened cell apoptosis, inflammation, OxS, and ECM degradation in chondrocytes induced by IL-1β, but these changes were impaired after CA12 overexpression. Animal experiments exhibited that WTAP silencing eased cartilage degeneration in DMM-induced mouse models. All results manifested that IL-1β-induced WTAP enhances CA12 mRNA stability depending on m6A modification, thus promoting chondrocyte dysfunction. Lack of exploration of the detailed mechanism by which WTAP regulates the m6A modification of CA12 is a limitation of this study, and whether the interaction of WTAP with other methyltransferases (like METTL3 or METTL14) to mediate CA12 will be explored in the future.

In summary, WTAP induced by IL-1β boosts CA12 mRNA stability through m6A modification, thereby promoting ECM degradation, inflammation, OxS, and ECM degradation in chondrocytes, emphasizing the potential promising therapeutic targets of WTAP and CA12 for OA.
