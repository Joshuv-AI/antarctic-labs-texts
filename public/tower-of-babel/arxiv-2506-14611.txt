Exploring MLLMs Perception of Network Visualization Principles
\authororcid Jacob Miller*0000-0002-0567-785X, \authororcid Markus Wallinger*0000-0002-2191-4413, \authororcid Ludwig Felder0009-0007-7854-0778, \authororcid Timo Brand 0009-0004-3111-2045, \authororcid Henry Förster0000-0002-1441-4189, \authororcid Johannes Zink0000-0002-7398-718X, \authororcid Chunyang Chen0000-0003-2011-9618, \authororcid Stephen Kobourov0000-0002-0477-2724

Abstract
In this paper, we test whether Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) can match human-subject performance in tasks involving the perception of properties in network layouts. Specifically, we replicate a human-subject experiment about perceiving quality (namely stress) in network layouts using GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5. Our experiments show that giving MLLMs exactly the same study information as trained human participants results in a similar performance to human experts and exceeds the performance of untrained non-experts. Additionally, we show that prompt engineering that deviates from the human-subject experiment can lead to better-than-human performance in some settings. Interestingly, like human subjects, the MLLMs seem to rely on visual proxies rather than computing the actual value of stress, indicating some sense or facsimile of perception. Explanations from the models provide descriptions similar to those used by the human participants (e.g., even distribution of nodes and uniform edge lengths). All the supplemental material and a free copy of our paper are available on OSF: https://osf.io/748mx/ .

Introduction

keywords
Evaluation, Large language models, Explainable AI

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are AI systems capable of understanding and generating content across multiple data types, such as text, images, audio, and video.
They have rapidly become ubiquitous across all fields of science and excel at producing rapid results to general inquiries via text or image prompts.
Several high-quality models have recently become available to the public, such as OpenAI’s GPT-4o
1
1

1

https://platform.openai.com/docs/models/gpt-4o
and Google’s Gemini-2.5
2
2

2

https://ai.google.dev/models
.
These models seem to achieve a high level of understanding of several topics, capable of matching or exceeding human-subject performance in the SAT and LSAT exams, as well as competitive programming challenges
[
16
]
.

With the advance of the vision capabilities of these models, a natural question to ask is what exactly are their capabilities and limitations in understanding visualizations? Recent experiments testing the general visualization understanding of ChatGPT-4 and Gemini, using the Visualization Literacy Assessment Test
[
24
,
5
]
, revealed that both models can handle tasks such as comparing scatterplots or finding correlations fairly well. However, neither model performed consistently well on other tasks, such as reading pie charts or histograms.

What remains unclear is whether these models have a sense of perception to understand and interpret complex visualizations such as node-link diagrams of networks.
Networks represent complex structures and there can be many different layouts of the same underlying data.
Hence, there are many possible features of a network that one might want to emphasize, and many techniques to produce them have been proposed. For example, one can highlight local
[
79
,
28
]
or global
[
19
]
structures, clusters
[
42
]
, or even particular network properties of interest
[
17
]
.
Evaluating or comparing techniques using established quality metrics such as
stress
is common.
Informally, stress measures how well the underlying shortest-path distances between pairs of nodes are captured in the layout of the network.
Lower stress indicates a better representation of the network topology, and recently, an empirical study
[
40
]
determined that human experts can reliably decide which of two diagrams has lower stress and non-experts can be trained to do so, too.
Computing the stress values of node-link diagrams directly
is difficult if only the diagram is available, for example if the underlying data comes from an online image.
As asking human experts to compare diagrams with respect to stress is expensive, it may be useful to delegate this task to an MLLM.

The research landscape is rapidly evolving with regard to MLLMs, and so it is imperative to broaden our collective understanding as a community of how these models might be used to draw conclusions about visualizations, and how valid these conclusions might be. Additionally, MLLMs show promising value to visualization designers and researchers by potentially allowing thorough, inexpensive piloting for human-subject studies if they indeed possess human-like visualization literacy. We aim to begin to address these questions in the context of network visualization, a yet unexplored topic at the time of writing.

This paper evaluates how MLLMs interpret, perceive, and respond to the most common network visualizations – node-link diagrams.
Specifically, we compare the performance of
GPT-4o
GPT-4o
and
Gemini-2.5
Gemini-2.5
by replicating a
human-subject
human-subject
experiment of Mooney et al.
[
40
]
where participants were given two images of node-link diagrams.
The participants had to decide whether the left or right image showed a node-link diagram with lower stress, or whether both had similar stress.
Our results show that by giving the same training information, MLLMs mimic the performance of all three different types of human participants (untrained novices, trained novices, and experts). By fine-tuning the prompts, we obtain better performance with interesting deviations.
We extract and analyze the main topics from the LLMs’ reasonings
and investigate correlations with the underlying properties of the network layouts.
The reasonings of both MLLM models show that, similar to human participants, they rely on visual proxies when judging stress.
In summary, our contributions are

•

the first, to our knowledge, replication of a network visualization human-subject study with MLLM participants,

•

statistical analysis of MLLM performance compared to human-subject performance,

•

analysis of how deviating from the instructions given to human-subjects affects the performance of the models, and

•

an investigation of the reasonings provided by the models for the visualization comparisons performed, and how these responses are affected by network layout properties.

All prompts, code, data, results, and analysis are provided in an open-source OSF repository:
https://osf.io/748mx/
. We also provide a summary video in supplemental material.

1.2
Background

Networks arise naturally as a mathematical model of relational systems, such as biological protein interactions
[
20
]
, power and infrastructure
[
64
]
, and social interactions
[
45
]
. Creating effective network visualizations to understand and interpret such complex systems is a core visualization topic.

By far the most popular choice of idiom for network visualizations is the so-called
node-link diagram
. In this setting, each entity in the network (called node or vertex) is encoded as a mark, such as a circle, while the presence of a relationship (called link or edge) between two nodes is encoded as a line segment between them. Evaluation of the effectiveness of node-link diagrams has historically been grounded in perceptual principles
[
48
]
with studies showing the aesthetic metrics of edge crossings, angular resolution, node uniformity, edge length uniformity, and several others to affect readability and task performance
[
49
]
.

As there are many possible features of a network that one might want to emphasize in a node-link diagram, many techniques to produce them have been proposed.
Some techniques highlight local structures
[
79
,
28
]
, which preserve small neighborhoods around nodes but often distort the large-scale structures of a network.
Other techniques highlight global structures
[
19
]
, which attempt to capture the large-scale structures of a network while allowing local errors. Techniques can also highlight clusters by forcing separation between groups of nodes
[
42
]
, or
to “visually prove” specific network properties, such as the existence of a bridge in the network
[
17
]
.
It is common to evaluate or compare techniques using established quality (faithfulness) metrics
[
41
]
, which express the distortion of a network property represented in the diagram. The most common quality metric of this type is known as
stress
,
the third most reported quantitative evaluation metric in network visualization (behind running time and the number of crossings)
[
12
]
.

Stress is a family of quality metrics that measures the discrepancy between the graph-theoretic distances between nodes in the data (i.e., the number of edges on a shortest path between them), and the realized geometric distances between their coordinates in the diagram. The most often used stress measure is called normalized stress, defined by Gansner et al.
[
19
]
.
The concept of stress dates back to the works of Torgerson
[
62
]
, Kruskal
[
29
]
, Shepard
[
56
]
, and later Sammon
[
53
]
who initially utilize it as a statistical analysis tool. It was first used in network visualization as an optimization function proposed by Kamada and Kawai
[
26
]
. Their technique was later improved with Gansner et al.’s stress majorization
[
19
]
, and Zheng et al.
[
78
]
show that stochastic gradient descent is even more effective at optimizing stress. There are many variants of stress used as optimization functions
[
18
,
39
]
, and many more examples of works which use stress as an evaluation metric despite not directly optimizing it
[
25
,
36
,
65
,
57
,
3
]
.

Stress has been shown to be a good proxy for symmetry
[
74
]
, and it has been shown that people tend to prefer diagrams with lower stress
[
10
]
.
A recent study of Mooney et al. attempts to answer the broad question, “Can people see stress?
[
40
]
”
The study is further detailed in
Section

2.1
, but a key takeaway is that participants could reliably identify diagrams with lower stress, although the feedback received and subsequent interviews conducted indicated that the participants were making this judgement on a perceptual level. In other words, although participants were asked directly about stress, they relied on high-level visual proxies to identify the stress of a diagram. In this work, we primarily ask whether these results extend to MLLMs and to what extent. If asked directly about stress, which has a well-defined mathematical definition, will an MLLM also engage in a similar high-level perceptual processing as human participants indicated?

2.1
Reference experiment

We describe in detail the study design of Mooney et al.
[
40
]
, on which our experiment is based. They investigate whether participants can “see” stress using a paired stimulus experiment; participants are shown a pair of diagrams of the same network and are asked to choose the diagram with the lower stress.

The controlled variable in the experiment is the difference in stress between the two diagrams. In particular, the authors use the Kruskal stress metric (KSM)
[
29
]
which is also known as non-metric stress. KSM is bound to a
[0,1]
range, is scale-invariant, and
is formally defined as follows:

\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i,j}\left(||X_{i}-X_{j}||-\hat{d}_{i,j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i,j}||X_{i}-X_{j}||^{2}}}

where the sum is over all pairs
i,j
of nodes in the network,

X_{i}
is the coordinate position of node
i
, and
\hat{d}_{i,j}
is found from a monotonic regression on the Shepard diagram
[
56
]
of the diagram.

The study contained three groups of participants:
Trained
novices,
Untrained
novices, and
Experts
. The instructions and the training for the different groups varied as follows.
Trained
novices were first introduced to concepts of network visualization (nodes, edges, diagrams, etc.) and were presented with the concept and definition of stress. Next came a round of 9 training questions, and after each answer the participants received feedback (correct or incorrect). Finally, the participants answered 45 questions.
Untrained
participants received the same introduction but were not given any feedback on the training questions. The
Experts
(identified by the authors) were given just the two-sentence instruction – “You will be asked to look at two networks, side by side. You will be asked to indicate which one has more stress.”

Participants in all three settings were shown (in random order), 45 pairs of network diagrams and were asked to identify the diagram with lower stress, with choices of “The drawing on the left has lower stress”, “The drawings have the same stress”, and “The drawing on the right has lower stress.” After each response, participants were asked to report their confidence (either “confident” or “not confident”). For each network size considered in the study (10, 25, and 50 nodes), there were five unique networks with many diagrams at KSM levels between 0.4 to 0.8, with intervals of 0.05. This gives nine unique KSM level differences for each of the five networks. Finally,
the participants were asked about “the overall strategy used to determine which drawing had lower stress” and
some demographic and free-response feedback was collected.

The results of the experiment showed that by and large, all participant groups could reliably identify the network diagram with lower stress in most cases, with overall accuracy being 68%, 75%, and 78% for the
Untrained
,
Trained
, and
Expert
groups, respectively, compared to the baseline of 33% for guessing.
Notably, training helped to improve the performance:
the difference in mean accuracy between
Untrained
and
Trained
participants was statistically significant.

However, the comments in response to the question about “the overall strategy used to determine which drawing had lower stress” indicated that participants were making their decisions based on what “looked right” or “felt right”.
More detailed responses mentioned features of the diagram specific to aesthetic criteria, such as node distribution, edge crossings, and edge length uniformity.
This indicates that participants were not necessarily performing low-level tasks on the data visualization but making a more high-level perceptual judgement of the aesthetic appeal of the two diagrams via visual proxies.

Since human participnats could generally differentiate between low- and high-stress diagrams
without actually computing the stress,
it is a natural question to ask how MLLMs perform in the same test.
Can they reach the similar accuracy levels?
In about a quarter of the instances, human participant judgments were wrong –
do MLLMs possess or gain a better notion of salient differences in low- and high-stress diagrams?
Can MLLMs potentially exceed human participants in terms of accuracy?
In the context of explaining answers given by MLLMs,
we are also interested to see if MLLMs resort to visual proxies or perceptual level judgments, just as the human participants did.

3.2
Tuning the Prompts

While the prompts from
Section

3.1
focus on being as close as possible to the instructions from the
human-subject
human-subject
experiment, they might be suboptimal for an MLLM.
Hence, we focused on improving the performance by tuning a prompt for better performance. We show snippets from the prompt and explain the overall strategy. The full prompt can be found in the supplemental material.

Several strategies and recommendations
[
52
,
43
]
for better prompts have been proposed. Our prompt is based on the “chain of thought” strategy
[
73
]
, where the structure of instructions is fine-grained and provides clear delimiters between sections. After several iterations, we present the
Tuned
prompt with the highest overall accuracy:

•

Provide a concise role for the MLLM and what information it will receive.

•

Carefully explain stress and how it can be visually evaluated.

•

Lay out the evaluation criteria, how they can be inferred, and which visual cues help during the evaluation process. Specifically, we focus on node distribution, edge lengths, and crossings.

•

Detailed instructions on how the evaluation process works.

•

Show the MLLM training examples.

The first two bullet points are aimed to set the context for the MLLM. We find that the models understand the concept of stress well, and it is debatable if it is necessary in this context. However, this might be required in other domains to achieve good results.

From our experience, we find that laying out detailed evaluation criteria, a structured evaluation process, and fitting examples had the most impact on performance.
Our
Tuned
prompt defines the most obvious visual proxies to determine stress and tries to guide the models toward a correct answer.
We provide all prompts open source.

The last major change compared to the prompts in
Section

3.1
is showing the MLLM models a more detailed evaluation process.
This is a typical recommendation
[
73
]

Unsuccesful Attempts

We tested multiple variants with mixed success and report our findings here. Firstly, instead of giving the same examples as in the study, we tried to give specific examples where the models performed poorly. For example, we gave more image pairs that show a network with similar stress. While this improves the accuracy in the ’same stress’ bucket, it results in overall lower accuracy due to the decrease in all other buckets. Hence, we give the models the same examples as in
Section

3.1
.

We also tried to see if forcing the models to compute the stress from the image input is possible. Our preliminary results with the web interface indicated that this is potentially feasible, but we could not evaluate this variant due to the API’s technical limitations. However, extracting the network structure and computing the stress is impossible even with the web interface.
Both models could not accurately extract a network from the image input, even when explaining how a node or edge is visually represented and correcting mistakes.
We conjecture that this capability will be possible in the future.

The bootstrap statistical analysis method takes a data collection and creates many thousands of simulated samples (of the same size as the original) by drawing from the original data collection with replacement. On each of these simulated samples one can compute statistics of interest, typically the mean or median, which creates a distribution of statistics. Given the large number of statistics, the resulting confidence intervals are reliable. The only assumption required of the original data is that it accurately represents the true population it is sampled from.

). The combined model substantially improves predictive strength, achieving a pseudo

between

and

, clearly surpassing the explanatory power of any single-variable model.
Node uniformity and edge length deviation correlate with each other (
\rho\approx 0.73

,

), but each variable independently contributes meaningful explanatory information.

Analyzing both

consistently shows that a combined logistic regression model with all three proxies provides superior predictive capability.
Minor numerical differences between the analyses of the models do not affect this core conclusion.

Discussion

Qualitative answers from the human-subject experiment
[
40
]
indicate that humans rely on visual proxies.
We conjecture that evaluating even a fraction of all possible pairs of nodes requires too much cognitive load, while the alternative visual proxies are more manageable to perceive and process.
For example, evaluating stress requires correctly identifying shortest paths for every pair of nodes (quadratic), while node distribution involves considering a linear number of nodes.

Our statistical analysis suggests that MLLM models use visual proxies similar to humans as they cannot extract network structure and compute the stress.
Even though we observed this effect for all prompts, this is especially evident when considering the
Expert
prompt with the least information, forcing the model to rely on its own context.
One proxy alone lacks the predictive power to explain our experimental results, especially for networks with higher stress differences where both models have perfect or near-perfect accuracy; see
Table

4
.
Still, the fact that all three visual proxies are (strongly) correlated with stress requires us to be careful when drawing conclusions.
Without access to the technical details of the models, we cannot exclude that both models consider other means, besides the visual proxies we analyzed.

5
Discussion and Limitations

Our findings suggest that MLLMs perform similarly to
human subjects
human subjects
on a perceptual task involving node-link diagrams, with relatively small but statistically significant deviations based on the amount of context given. This is in spite of recent work
[
5
,
67
]
which indicates MLLMs may struggle with complex visualizations, but is in line with the general upward trajectory of MLLM performance on increasingly complex tasks. Additionally, the MLLMs give reasonably sound justifications for their choices and seem to use similar visual proxies to
human participants
human participants
, but it remains unclear exactly how they arrive at a conclusion.

Our results have practical implications for design, development, and implementation for future visualization evaluation studies. We recommend care in such experiments,
using attention checks, and considering carefully how participants could make use of MLLMs that may skew study results. In particular, the near-human accuracy of MLLMs suggests that designers must account for the possibility that such models could be participating in human-subjects studies, as pointed out by Agnew et al.
[
1
]
. Thus, as proposed by Hämäläinen et al.
[
23
]
, usage of LLMs and MLLMs, should be limited to pilot experiments.

We emphasize that our experiments do not directly aim to draw new conclusions on the human perception of network visualizations. However, our results may provide early evidence that MLLMs may indeed be applicable in studies on the aesthetics of (relational) data visualizations, with some caveats.
For example, the MLLM models consistently underused the option, “the stress is the same”. The use of the word,
“same”
is a potentially confounding factor as it is slightly misleading; two network diagrams are very unlikely to have the exact same stress values. However, two diagrams are considered to have the
“same”
stress if they are within a small threshold. Had an MLLM been used as a pilot test subject in this study, this wording issue might have been caught.

Our study has several limitations, starting with the use of only two of the many available MLLMs. Further, both
GPT-4o
GPT-4o
and
Gemini-2.5
Gemini-2.5
are closed-source, making study replication difficult as the models are phased out.
We investigate only one high-level perceptual task. While we investigate this task in great detail, it does not tell us much about performance of other specific tasks on complex visualizations. We note that evaluating the stress of a node-link diagram involves several low-level tasks to compare exactly (path following, distance estimation, deriving value, etc.) or high-level perceptual processes (estimating symmetry, distribution of points and lines). Finally, although we attempt to replicate the
human-subject
human-subject
study of Mooney et al.
[
40
]
as precisely as possible with MLLMs, there are small differences in how the data is given and collected (e.g., every request to the MLLM is independent).

There are many interesting avenues for future work involving MLLMs and node-link diagrams.
To this end, all prompts, code, data, results, and analysis are provided in an open-source OSF repository:
https://osf.io/748mx/
.

6
Conclusions

We investigate a topical question in visualization: “Do MLLMs have the perceptual capacity to understand complex visualizations?” We attempt to answer this question by replicating a recent human-subject study on a high-level perception task for node-link diagrams, using MLLMs. Our findings indicate that MLLMs achieve results comparable to human performance, and
by carefully tuning the prompts, MLLMs can outperform humans. Reasoning responses given by MLLMs provide some insight into the underlying decision processes, including evidence that they use visual proxies similar to those used by humans.
