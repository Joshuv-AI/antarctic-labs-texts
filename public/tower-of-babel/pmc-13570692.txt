What Do We Know about The Potential Toxicity of Turmeric and Curcumin Derivatives
Authors: Mohammad Nemati, Reyhaneh Rabiee, Faezeh Ghanbari-Gohari, Negar Mosayeb, Fatemeh Almasi, Maryam Zeydi, Omid Sadeghi, Alireza Milajerdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Turmeric is a well-known compound in traditional medicine and its derivatives, such as curcumin, have been used for years to prevent, treat, and improve symptoms of various diseases due to their beneficial properties. Curcumin is known for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Many studies have investigated the effects of curcumin as a dietary supplement on human health, and positive results have been reported. However, curcumin has low bioavailability, so several new derivatives have been produced to overcome this limitation. Most studies conducted so far have not reported any serious toxic effects from consuming turmeric, curcumin, and its derivatives. However, side effects have been associated with the consumption of these supplements in some human studies. There are also concerns regarding the use of higher doses of curcumin and its long-term use. Our review of the published human studies suggests that consuming turmeric, curcumin, and its derivatives has no major side effects. However, a few adverse effects have been reported in some studies, such as gastrointestinal complications like flatulence, abdominal pain, nausea, and diarrhea. Side effects are more commonly reported in studies conducted on patients. Due to the specific physical conditions of patients and drug treatments, it is better to consult a health expert before consuming turmeric or curcumin. According to studies, it appears that consuming 6000 mg/day of curcumin is safe in the short term. However, further studies, especially on forms of curcumin with improved bioavailability, are needed.

Introduction

Turmeric belongs to the family Zingiberaceae which is obtained from the rhizomes of Curcuma longa, a plant growing in the tropical region of southern Asia.[1] Turmeric is used as a food spice. Moreover, Turmeric is a well-known compound in traditional medicine. Turmeric was used in traditional Chinese and Indian medicine to heal wounds, liver diseases and cancer treatment.[234] In addition, studies have shown the beneficial effects of turmeric for human health.[5] Curcumin, a curcuminoid, is the main bioactive ingredient in turmeric (1.5–3 wt % of turmeric rhizome).[67] Due to the favorable properties of curcumin, the favorable health effects of turmeric are considered to be caused by it. Therefore, nowadays, curcumin is one of the most common herbal and dietary supplements.[8] Curcumin has several pharmacological properties, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimutagenic, antimetastatic, and antimicrobial properties, that resulted in wide use of it for several medical conditions.[910111213] The importance of curcumin in traditional medicine is such that it includes up to 5% of traditional medicine treatments.[14] Today many studies investigate the use of curcumin and its derivatives in health outcomes.[15] Findings of a clinical trial showed that supplementation of curcumin leads to decrease in fasting blood sugar level from 140 to 70 mg/dl so that it may have significant effect on reduction of FBS and diabetes improvement.[16] In addition, the beneficial effect of curcumin was observed in arteriosclerosis, so curcumin supplementation was effective in reducing the serum level of total cholesterol and increasing the HDL level.[17] Moreover, the effectiveness of curcumin in fatty liver disease was observed.[18] Due to the anti-inflammatory properties of curcumin, it is used as a complementary treatment for inflammatory bowel disease. Results of a clinical study showed that supplementation of curcumin can reduce the level of inflammatory biomarkers in Crohn 's disease.[19] Also, effectiveness of curcumin supplementation in improving symptoms and protecting against relapse of ulcerative colitis has been found in some studies.[2021] Additionally, several studies indicate that curcumin has anticancer properties and beneficial effects on certain types of cancer such as colorectal, breast, prostate, and lung cancer.[22232425] Moreover, curcumin is also effective in skin health.[26] Despite several favorable properties of curcumin [Figure 1], this compound has a basic limitation in that it has low bioavailability which is an obstacle to its therapeutic effects. The lipophilic nature of free curcumin has limited its bioavailability in clinical applications.[27] Several alternative strategies have been applied to improve the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of curcumin.[28] For instance, nanoencapsulation is an advanced approach to overcome the limitations of curcumin.[29] Curcumin nanoparticles have improved bioavailability, stability, and maintenance.[303132] Despite the many health benefits of curcumin and its derivatives, there are still concerns about its possible toxicity and effects on health.[13] Past clinical studies mostly indicate the safety of using curcumin and its derivatives and serious toxicity was not reported; however, in some studies, side some associated effects have been reported, which should be taken into consideration. In this study, we aimed to review the side effects of using turmeric, curcumin, and its derivatives that were reported in past studies.

A summary of side effects and health benefits of curcumin and its derivatives

Side effects of curcumin in human studies

Due to the favorable properties of curcumin, many studies have evaluated the role of curcumin in human health. In addition to the effectiveness of curcumin, findings regarding its side effects have also been reported. Some studies were conducted on healthy humans, and other studies examined the use of curcumin on a group of patients.

The finding of a double-blind placebo-controlled study on 59 healthy adults did not show any specific side effects for taking 200 mg of curcumin for 8 weeks.[33] Also, another study in the population of healthy women did not show the toxic effects of consuming 200 mg/day of curcumin for three 10-day periods.[34] In addition, supplementation with 150 mg/day for 8 weeks and 150 mg/day for 4 weeks in other studies conducted on healthy subjects did not show any specific side effects after taking curcumin.[3536] In another study, in order to evaluate the toxic effects of curcumin, doses of 500-12000 mg were orally given to 24 healthy people. Side effects including headache, diarrhea, yellow stool, and skin rash were reported in seven participants.[37] Another study investigated the effects of a single intravenous dose of 120 mg/m2 of curcumin in healthy subjects, the results of which indicated no serious toxicity in the participants, although changes in the morphology of red blood cells were observed.[38]

Some studies have investigated the effects of curcumin in a group of patients and reported side effects. Two clinical studies on Alzheimer‘s disease patients did not reveal any serious adverse effects of taking up to 4000 mg/day of curcumin for 6 months.[3940] Two studies on osteoarthritis patients showed that taking 1500 mg/day curcumin for 6 weeks and 1000 mg/day for 3 months was safe.[4142] However, another study in this group of patients revealed some adverse effects including tachycardia, hypertension, and redness of the tongue after consuming 180 mg/day of curcumin for 8 weeks. Two studies conducted on a group of mental disorders patients, such as depression, did not show any major toxic effects following the consumption of curcumin,[4344] although, in one of them, gastrointestinal side effects including flatulence and stomach pain were reported.[44] In a controlled trial on postsurgical patients, 1200 mg/day of curcumin for 5 days was consumed by 15 patients. Among them, one case complained of mild dizziness on the third day.[45] A clinical trial on 50 patients with advanced colorectal cancer showed that the consumption of 450 to 3600 mg/day of curcumin for up to 4 months caused some digestive problems such as nausea, diarrhea, and abdominal pain, as well as an increase in the serum levels of lactate dehydrogenase and alkaline phosphatase in some patients.[46] In another study on ulcerative colitis patients, with the consumption of 2000 mg/day of curcumin for 4 months, no major toxic effects were seen, but flatulence was reported.[20] In another study 2500 mg/day of curcumin was given to patients with acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), no serious adverse effect was reported and blood tests were normal. Of course among 19 patients, two of them had complained of some gastric disorders.[47] Two studies on individuals with impaired glucose tolerance and diabetes showed the side effects of itching, nausea, and constipation after taking 1500 mg/day of curcumin supplement for 6 months.[4849] In addition, several studies conducted in the group of individuals with metabolic disorders did not report serious toxic effects of curcumin, but side effects such as nausea, diarrhea, and changes in the serum level of liver enzymes were reported.[5051]

In general, by reviewing the published studies, it can be concluded that the use of curcumin does not have serious toxic effects, and the side effects of its use are usually are usually mild not mild. Most of the adverse effects reported are related to gastrointestinal side effects such as flatulence, abdominal pain, nausea and diarrhea. In addition, in some studies headache and dizziness, skin symptoms such as itching and rash and changes in serum factors have also been reported. These side effects were seen more in the patient groups than in healthy individuals [Figure 1]. So it may be concluded that the underlying problems of the patients and the interactions of drugs they are taking with curcumin were also the cause of these adverse events.

Side effects of curcumin derivatives in human studies

Although curcumin is recognized as a safe compound,[1352] the safety of curcumin derivatives, including nanocurcumin, should be further explored.[53] Various types of curcumin nanoparticles have been developed.[54] The toxicity of curcumin-based nanomaterials is influenced by their accumulation and mechanical properties, which in turn depend on their manufacturing and purification methods.[55] Despite the improved efficacy of curcumin, nanoformulations of curcumin may have effects on normal tissues, which hinders their wide application in pharmacology.[56] Microemulsions can be prepared with different types of surfactants, some of which may be toxic.[56] Therefore, surfactant selection is very important in microemulsions.[56] Synthetic polymers are the most popular surfactants in nanoformulations, which improve the pharmacokinetics of curcumin but may also have some negative effects on normal cells.[56] There are concerns about neuroinflammation, allergic reactions, and excitotoxicity when using nanoparticles for drug delivery.[55]

The results of numerous clinical studies on the use of nanocurcumin have not shown any serious side effects.[57585960616263] In a controlled trial study, using 80 mg/day nanocurcumin for 12 months did not cause serious toxic effects; however, muscle fasciculation, itching, and changes in liver function test were observed in some cases.[64] In addition, in another study on schizophrenia patient‘s mild adverse effects, including daytime drowsiness, dry mouth, and nausea, were observed in some patients after using 160 mg/d nanocurcumin for 16 weeks.[65] In another study, taking 150 mg nanoparticle curcumin (named THERACURMIN) resulted in diarrhea in one person. Reported side effects, such as diarrhea, have been reported.[66] Nanocurcumin is considered safe at a dose of 210 mg/day,[66] but long-term studies are needed to monitor the long-term effects of this compound.

Another practical strategy for developing the pharmacokinetics of curcumin is the use of excipients such as piperine.[32] Piperine is an alkaloid compound derived from black pepper[67] that can improve the bioavailability of curcumin by up to 2000%.[68] Numerous clinical studies have investigated the efficacy of curcumin and piperine in the treatment of various diseases, and it has been found that concomitant administration of piperine and curcumin has no major side effects.[69] Due to the chelating property of curcumin and its derivatives, long-term supplementation with this substance may lead to an increase in RDW.[7071] Although no significant side effects have been observed in human studies, high-quality clinical trials are needed to confirm the safety of curcumin and piperine in the clinical setting.[69]

Eventually, although curcumin is considered safe, various curcumin derivatives should also be evaluated for safety. Several clinical studies have been conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these compounds. The studies conducted to date have not shown any serious side effects following the use of curcumin derivatives, but further long-term studies are needed to monitor the long-term effects of curcumin derivatives.

Side effects of turmeric in human studies

Turmeric is a spice with therapeutic uses,[72] there is evidence of the potential toxic effects of this substance. A long-term study in the United States demonstrated 1,697 reports of drug-induced liver injuries, nine of them (0.5%) could be related to turmeric intake. Of these nine cases, turmeric was taken on average 102 days before the onset of damage with a range of 30-425 days. Eight of the nine cases caused hepatocellular injury, five patients required hospitalization, and one died due to injuries.[73] In addition, The French Agency for Food, Environmental, and Occupational Health and Safety (ANSES) showed that over 100 reports of hepatitis were received, 15 reports attributed to turmeric and its derivatives.[74] Furthermore, European Medicine Agency (EMA) considered that side effects of dry mouth, flatulence, and stomach irritation, may occur in patients with digestive disorders who take turmeric.[75] In contrast, another clinical study has shown no side effects in healthy volunteers between 20 and 33 years that consumed 0.6 ml of turmeric oil three times a day for one month and 1 ml three times a day for 2 months.[76] Tang et al.[77] indicated turmeric contains around 2% oxalate. Therefore, high doses of turmeric may lead to kidney stones in predisposed individuals. Moreover, reduce in iron availability by 20-70% was reported when 5-20 mg polyphenols from turmeric were added to the meal.[78] Four case studies indicated acute hepatitis and hepatotoxicity in people ages 52-71 years who consume more than 8 g/day of turmeric supplements without any hepatological history. The duration of consumption is different between 1 and 8 months. Liver function tests were normalized after 3 weeks to 1 year ceased of taking turmeric supplements.[798081] One case study demonstrated a transient complete atrioventricular block in a 38-year-old man taking a turmeric supplement for one month. His disturbance improved after six months of discontinuing turmeric supplements.[82] Turmeric may interfere with some of the medication actions. For example, the anticoagulation effect of turmeric can interfere with blood clotting. Taking turmeric with anticoagulating drugs and blood thinner such as, warfarin, clopidogrel, and aspirin can elevate their effects and may increase bleeding.[83] Moreover, turmeric can increase stomach acid levels, which may inhibit the effectiveness of antacids.[84] Two case studies discussed drug interactions with turmeric. First, a 67-year-old man in chemotherapy treatment due to lung cancer was hospitalized for hepatotoxicity. The probable reason was the pharmacokinetic interaction between medical treatment with paclitaxel (165 mg/m2) and self-treatment with turmeric (15 g/day)[85] through a mechanism turmeric inhibited CYP2C9 and 3A4.[86] Hepatoxicity may occur in chemotherapy treatment, although in this case, the hepatic damage level was more than the norm for this standardized treatment. Second, severe hepatotoxicity and asthenia were reported in a 78-year-old woman who intakes etoricoxib 60 mg/day for osteoarthritis one month before and turmeric consumption for a long time. Her status improved after she discontinued turmeric intake gradually.[87] This case of hepatotoxicity can be explained by the interaction of etoricoxib with long-term turmeric intake; probably because turmeric can influence many genes and molecules, including COX2, that could enhance the toxic effects of etoricoxib.[88] Notably, both cases had no prior hepatological history.

Finally, although the use of turmeric is safe, using excessive doses in the long term may lead to side effects. Therefore, withdrawal periods are recommended. In addition, it is important to consider the potential interaction of turmeric with prescribed medications, then, patients should consume turmeric in consultation with a health professional along with drug therapy. Most studies have investigated the effects of taking curcumin in health, and the evidence regarding the long-term use of turmeric is limited, so further studies in this field are recommended.

Conclusions

So far, most of the human studies that have examined the effects of taking turmeric, curcumin, and its derivatives in humans have not reported major side effects, although a few adverse effects have been reported in some studies, which include mostly gastrointestinal complications. The low bioavailability of curcumin may be a reason for its safety. Of course, in relation to curcumin supplements with improved bioavailability, studies have not reported serious toxic effects, although studies on this form of curcumin are limited, and more studies in the future can help clarify the issue. In most of the published studies, curcumin supplements have been used for a maximum of 6 months, and there is a possibility that longer use may have side effects, which requires further investigation in future studies. In addition, findings about the use of curcumin in pregnancy are limited, and there is a need for more studies.
