Refractory rapidly progressive diffuse in-stent restenosis with left main involvement: a case report
Authors: Xiaoyan Wang, Haichao Yang, Liqiang Qin, Wei Lu, Lijun Wang, Jianjun Wang, Juan Li, Kaibin Deng, Chenguang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug-eluting stents (DES) have significantly reduced the incidence of in-stent restenosis (ISR). However, a subset of patients still develop refractory, rapidly progressive, and diffuse ISR, which poses substantial therapeutic challenges and reflects its complex underlying mechanisms.Case presentationWe report a 46-year-old male who underwent five percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) over five years, with repeated diffuse ISR in the left anterior descending artery (LAD). Initial PCI was followed by DCB therapy after first ISR, achieving temporary symptom relief. Four years later, ISR progressed to chronic total occlusion, requiring further stenting and DCB therapy. Within five months, he developed 99% LM stenosis and recurrent LAD ISR. Despite a SYNTAX score of 36 favoring CABG, the patient refused surgery. Under intra-aortic balloon pump support, a balloon-only PCI (stent-less) strategy was performed. IVUS revealed diffuse fibrous proliferation and neoatherosclerosis. During follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable overall.ConclusionThis case highlights a potential systemic hyperproliferative phenotype, in which repeated vascular injury triggers accelerated neoatherosclerosis and intimal hyperplasia. It challenges the conventional view of ISR as a local mechanical problem and underscores the importance of mechanism-driven risk stratification and individualized management, particularly when standard surgical therapy is declined.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06343-1.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Although drug-eluting stents (DES) have significantly reduced target lesion revascularization, in-stent restenosis (ISR) continues to be an important limitation of percutaneous coronary intervention, with an incidence of approximately 5–10% [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of ISR is multifactorial, including neointimal hyperplasia, neoatherosclerosis, stent underexpansion, vascular calcification, and hypersensitivity reactions [3]. Recurrent and rapidly progressive ISR represents a particularly challenging clinical scenario, often reflecting an unfavorable biological substrate. For patients with left main (LM) disease combined with multivessel involvement, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is generally recommended [4]. Nevertheless, in real-world practice, surgical revascularization is not always feasible or acceptable, necessitating individualized interventional strategies. Here, we report a rare case of five PCI procedures over five years involving the same LAD segment, characterized by recurrent rapidly progressive ISR ultimately extending to the LM. We aim to explore the potential mechanistic basis of this unusual disease course and discuss its therapeutic implications.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old man presented to the emergency department in August 2020 with a 5-hour history of persistent chest pain accompanied by diaphoresis. Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed ST-segment elevation in leads V1–V5, consistent with acute extensive anterior STEMI. The patient had no prior history of hypertension or diabetes mellitus. Coronary angiography (Fig. 1A, Video S1) revealed proximal left anterior descending (LAD) 70% diffuse stenosis, complete occlusion of a diagonal branch, normal left main (LM) and left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary artery (RCA) mid-segment 60% stenosis. LAD was successfully reopened, and three consecutive DES of different brands were implanted the mid-to-proximal LAD (2.5 × 33 mm “Firebird2” at 12 atm for 8 s, 2.75 × 29 mm “AlphaStent” at 14 atm for 8 s, and 3.0 × 21 mm “LePu” at 14 atm for 8 s), with 1–2 mm overlap between adjacent stents. Because this was an emergency PCI, only the 3.0 × 21 mm “LePu” stent balloon was used for post-dilation at the junction of the proximal two stents (14 atm, 8 s). Angiography showed no significant stent under expansion or malapposition. Post-procedure TIMI flow was 3 (Fig. 1a, Video S2). Post-procedurally, the patient was maintained on dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and ticagrelor, along with rosuvastatin.

One year later, the patient was readmitted with recurrent chest discomfort and dyspnea. Coronary angiography revealed severe diffuse ISR of the proximal-to-mid LAD (99%) with TIMI grade 1 flow (Fig. 1B, Video S3), and collateral circulation from the RCA was observed. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) demonstrated marked neointimal hyperplasia (Fig. 2A). Sequential balloon dilatation was performed using a 2.5 × 15 mm “ScoreFlex” balloon (14–16 atm, 8 s), a 3.0 × 12 mm “Gusta NC” non-compliant balloon (16 atm, 8 s), and a 3.5 × 17 mm “Vector plus” balloon (16–18 atm, 8 s) to dilate the mid-to-distal and proximal LAD stents extending to the LAD ostium. Following lesion pre-dilation, drug-coated balloon (DCB) angioplasty (3.5 × 30 mm “Qingzhou”) restored adequate coronary flow (Fig. 1b, Video S4). Platelet function testing excluded resistance to aspirin or ticagrelor. Given obesity (BMI 33.8 kg/m2) and elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C 2.07 mmol/L, above the guideline-recommended target of < 1.4 mmol/L), lipid-lowering therapy was intensified with rosuvastatin in combination with evolocumab.

Four years later (May 2025), the patient presented with a 12-day history of worsening angina. ECG obtained after the initial PCI and during subsequent follow-up consistently demonstrated QS complexes with persistent ST-segment elevation in leads V1–V4 (Supplementary Fig. 1A), without significant dynamic T-wave changes. The only notable variation was an increased ventricular rate during episodes of chest pain. Transthoracic echocardiography showed segmental wall motion abnormalities with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 52%. Coronary angiography demonstrated chronic total occlusion (CTO) of the proximal LAD (Fig. 1C). Attempts were made with Pilot 50, Pilot 150, Gara 2, and Gara 3 guidewires to cross the occlusive lesion. Sequential balloon dilatation was performed using a 1.2 × 12 mm “TREK” balloon, a 2.0 × 15 mm “Yinyi” balloon, a 2.5 × 12 mm “Gusta NC” non-compliant balloon, and a 2.75 × 10 mm “Bomai” scoring balloon to repeatedly dilate the in-stent occlusive lesion, and IVUS confirmed diffuse ISR. A DES (2.5 × 18 mm Xience Alpine, 10 atm, 8 s) was implanted in the distal LAD with 2 mm overlap with the previous stent. However, prolonged procedural time was complicated by acute heart failure, leading to premature termination of the intervention.

One week later, repeat angiography revealed diffuse ISR involving the entire previously stented LAD segment with 80–85% stenosis (Video S5). A fourth PCI was subsequently performed following adequate lesion preparation with cutting and non-compliant balloons (2.75 × 10 mm “Boston Scientific” cutting balloon and 3.0 × 15 mm “Gusta NC” non-compliant balloon). Following this intervention, ECG demonstrated partial recovery of electrical activity, with the previous QS morphology in lead V4 replaced by a small positive R wave (Supplementary Fig. 1B), suggesting limited improvement in myocardial viability and ischemic burden after revascularization. An additional DES (3.0 × 12 mm Medtronic, 14 atm, 8 s) was implanted in the proximal LAD with overlap of previous stents (overlap zone approximately 8 mm) (Fig. 2B), and angiography showed no significant stent underexpansion or malapposition. DCB angioplasty (2.75 × 30 mm “beilang”) (16 atm, 60 s, given the in-stent lesion) was applied to the mid-to-distal segments (Fig. 1c). IVUS demonstrated a minimal lumen area of 4.07 mm2 in the distal stent segment with a plaque burden of 60%, 5.71 mm2 in the mid-stent segment with a plaque burden of 70%, and 6.23 mm2 in the proximal stent segment with a plaque burden of 71%. However, a high plaque burden of 60–71% was noted (Fig. 2C–D), consistent with features suggestive of neoatherosclerosis. At this stage, no significant LM involvement was observed (Fig. 2E).

Five months later (October 2025), the patient developed unstable angina (CCS class IV). Coronary angiography revealed rapid disease progression with severe left main stenosis (99%) and recurrent LAD ISR (Fig. 1D and d, Video S6). A multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion recommended CABG given a SYNTAX score of 36. However, the patient declined surgical treatment due to concerns regarding a scar-prone phenotype (he had a history of keloid formation following appendectomy 20 years ago, with a visible approximately 6-cm surgical scar on the right lower abdominal wall). After detailed discussion and informed consent, percutaneous coronary intervention was performed under intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support via dual femoral access. Sequential balloon dilatations were performed for the LM lesion (Fig. 3A-C), followed by cutting balloon angioplasty for LAD ISR (Fig. 3D-E). Final angiography demonstrated marked improvement in LM stenosis with restoration of antegrade coronary flow (Fig. 3F-G, Video S7). IVUS confirmed the absence of dissection or thrombus in the LM and revealed diffuse fibrotic and partially calcified tissue within the LAD stent segments (Fig. 2F). The minimum lumen area in the LM was 7.7 mm2 with a residual stenosis of 53.16%; the minimum lumen area in the proximal LAD stent was 4.25 mm2 with a plaque burden of 69%. Given the history of recurrent ISR and the high stent burden, no additional stent was implanted. The patient’s symptoms improved significantly, and he was discharged on optimal medical therapy with close follow-up.

During follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable with occasional mild chest discomfort and good adherence to prescribed medications.

Discussion

ISR continues to represent a major limitation of PCI despite advances in DES technology. While most ISR cases are gradual and manageable, this case describes a rare and clinically challenging presentation of recurrent, rapidly progressive diffuse ISR, ultimately extending to involve the left main coronary artery.

The mechanisms underlying ISR in this patient were likely multifactorial and evolved over time. Early ISR within the first year after PCI was mainly consistent with neointimal hyperplasia without features suggestive of lipid accumulation, supporting biological smooth muscle cell proliferation as the dominant mechanism. In contrast, late recurrent ISR showed heterogeneous tissue characteristics, including fibrotic and partially calcified components, suggesting progression toward a neoatherosclerotic phenotype [4, 5]. The timing of ISR may provide additional insights into its underlying biological substrate, as early ISR is predominantly driven by neointimal hyperplasia, whereas late ISR is more frequently associated with neoatherosclerosis, lipid accumulation, and calcification. Recent evidence from the COIL registry further suggested that the timing of stent failure may influence outcomes following calcium-modification therapies, with late ISR demonstrating distinct therapeutic challenges [6].

Because IVUS has limited ability to characterize lipid components and plaque morphology, definitive confirmation of neoatherosclerosis would require higher-resolution imaging such as OCT. Thrombotic mechanisms were considered during episodes of symptom recurrence. Nevertheless, serial angiography, IVUS examinations, continuous dual antiplatelet therapy, and repeated platelet function testing did not provide evidence of definite stent thrombosis.

Mechanical factors also played a crucial role in this case. The initial PCI involved implantation of multiple DES with a total length exceeding 80 mm, followed by repeated stenting procedures, resulting in a cumulative metal burden approaching 100 mm. Previous studies have shown that long stented segments (≥ 60 mm), overlapping stents, and the use of multiple stent platforms are all associated with an increased risk of ISR [7, 8]. Although follow-up IVUS showed acceptable minimal lumen areas (approximately 4.07–6.23 mm2 in LAD segments) without definite severe underexpansion or malapposition, subtle mechanical factors cannot be completely excluded because the index PCI was performed in an emergency STEMI setting without systematic IVUS-guided optimization.

Beyond local mechanical factors, this case suggests an important contribution of patient-specific biological predisposition. The patient had a history of previous abdominal surgery 20 years earlier, with a clinically apparent keloid scar at the incision site, suggesting a possible individual tendency toward exaggerated scar formation. Nevertheless, no definitive systemic fibroproliferative disorder or genetic predisposition was identified. Previous studies have indicated that individuals with hypertrophic scarring may have a higher risk of ISR, possibly due to heightened vascular healing responses [9]. In this patient, repeated vascular injury caused by multiple PCI procedures may have further amplified local reparative responses, potentially contributing to excessive neointimal proliferation and subsequent restenotic remodeling. However, whether a systemic predisposition toward abnormal scar formation directly contributed to ISR development remains speculative and requires further investigation.

The development of severe LM stenosis after repeated LAD interventions represents another important feature of this case. Although retrograde extension from the LAD is one possible explanation, the available data do not establish a direct causal relationship. The LM bifurcation has a unique biomechanical environment characterized by complex flow patterns, hinge motion, and heterogeneous wall shear stress, which may promote endothelial dysfunction and accelerated atherosclerotic remodeling. Previous mechanistic studies have emphasized the importance of altered wall shear stress, vessel geometry, and hinge motion in LM bifurcation regions, which may contribute to plaque progression and stent failure [10]. Factors such as repeated manipulation of the LAD ostium, overlapping stent edges, and altered local hemodynamics may have contributed to plaque progression toward the proximal vessel [11, 12]. Therefore, LM involvement likely resulted from the interaction of possible disease extension, local biomechanical stress, and patient-specific vascular susceptibility rather than a single mechanism.

Calcified ISR represents another clinically relevant aspect of this case. Calcification within stented segments may result from stent underexpansion, peri-stent calcium, progressive calcific neoatherosclerosis, or repeated vascular injury. These mechanisms may require different treatment approaches. Recent evidence from the COIL registry demonstrated favorable procedural and clinical outcomes of intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) in selected patients with stent failure, supporting its potential role in complex calcified ISR [13]. Comparative data have further evaluated rotational atherectomy and IVL for calcified ISR, suggesting that both approaches may provide effective calcium modification depending on lesion characteristics [14]. In addition, excimer laser coronary atherectomy (ELCA), scoring balloons, and cutting balloons remain valuable adjunctive techniques, particularly for fibrotic or resistant ISR lesions [15]. Importantly, plaque modification using cutting and non-compliant balloons not only facilitates mechanical luminal gain but also enhances drug penetration into the vessel wall by disrupting fibrotic barriers and increasing surface area exposure. This biological facilitation of antiproliferative drug uptake has been increasingly recognized as a key determinant of drug-coated balloon efficacy. Recent evidence further supports that lesion preparation strategies significantly enhance drug delivery and therapeutic response [16].

In this case, two different drug-coated balloons (Qingzhou and Beilang) were used during separate interventions. It is increasingly recognized that drug-coated balloons are not a homogeneous therapeutic class. Differences in drug type, excipients, and coating technology may result in variable drug transfer efficiency and tissue retention. Paclitaxel- and sirolimus-based platforms differ substantially in pharmacokinetics and tissue uptake, which may translate into differences in angiographic late lumen loss and long-term clinical outcomes. Recent evidence suggests that such inter-device variability may influence restenosis suppression efficacy [17]. Therefore, variation between DCB platforms may have contributed to differences in treatment response, although this cannot be confirmed from a single case.

The ECG evolution also provided important diagnostic information. The initial ECG demonstrated ST-segment elevation in V1–V5, consistent with acute extensive anterior myocardial infarction. During subsequent admissions, persistent QS complexes with stable ST elevation in V1–V4 reflected chronic infarct remodeling and possible left ventricular apical aneurysm rather than recurrent dynamic ischemia. Although severe LAD disease may occasionally present with Wellens-like patterns, the absence of typical T-wave changes and the presence of persistent QS complexes argued against Wellens syndrome in this patient [18].

In terms of treatment strategy, current guidelines recommend CABG for patients with left main disease and high SYNTAX scores [19]. However, in this case, surgical revascularization was declined, necessitating an alternative approach. Considering the extensive stent burden and repeated ISR recurrence, additional stent implantation was avoided, and a stent-less balloon-based strategy was adopted. IVUS after the final procedure demonstrated restoration of coronary flow, with a LM minimal lumen area of 7.7 mm2 and acceptable LAD lumen dimensions, supporting the feasibility of this approach in selected patients. Recent evidence supports the use of DCB and balloon-only strategies in selected ISR patients, although long-term outcomes remain to be fully established [4].

Finally, aggressive secondary prevention remains essential in patients with severe ISR phenotypes. The recommended LDL-C target for very-high-risk patients is < 1.4 mmol/L; however, this patient had prolonged periods without regular follow-up, and subsequent LDL-C levels remained > 2.0 mmol/L, potentially contributing to disease progression. Long-term aspirin plus ticagrelor therapy was maintained, with no evidence of platelet resistance. Although hs-CRP levels remained normal, IL-6 and other inflammatory markers were not assessed. Colchicine and other anti-inflammatory strategies were considered but not initiated because objective evidence of persistent inflammation was lacking.

Overall, this case highlights that recurrent ISR is not merely a mechanical complication but a complex vascular process involving biological healing responses, mechanical stress, hemodynamic factors, and individual susceptibility. Recognition of different ISR phenotypes and integration of intravascular imaging with individualized treatment strategies may help optimize management of patients with aggressive recurrent ISR.

Conclusion

This case describes a rare phenotype of rapidly progressive recurrent diffuse ISR with subsequent LM involvement after multiple PCI procedures. The disease progression likely reflected a combination of neointimal hyperplasia, neoatherosclerotic transformation, calcific remodeling, extensive stent burden, and patient-specific susceptibility. Mechanism-based assessment and individualized treatment strategies, including careful lesion preparation and avoidance of unnecessary additional metal implantation, may provide valuable guidance for managing complex ISR patients.
