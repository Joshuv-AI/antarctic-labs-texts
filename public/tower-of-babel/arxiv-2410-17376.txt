Lorentz-violating Yukawa theory at finite temperature
D. S. Cabral, L. A. S. Evangelista, J. C. R. de Souza, L. H. A. R. Ferreira, A. F. Santos

Abstract
This paper addresses Yukawa theory, focusing on the scattering between two identical fermions mediated by an intermediate scalar boson, considering the effects of thermal contributions and Lorentz symmetry breaking. Temperature is introduced into the theory through the TFD formalism, while Lorentz violation arises from a background tensor coupled to the kinetic part of the Klein-Gordon Lagrangian. Two important quantities are calculated: the cross-section for the scattering process and the modified Yukawa potential. The main results obtained in this work demonstrate that considering Lorentz symmetry breaking has several implications for changes in symmetries and physical states, while the presence of temperature is strongly related to the strength of the interaction. This interplay between symmetry breaking and temperature effects provides deeper insights into the behavior of the Yukawa theory under different conditions.

II
Thermal Yukawa theory in a Lorentz-violating context

This section presents the Lorentz-violating Yukawa theory. Thermal effects are then introduced using the TFD formalism. Starting from the free scalar theory, we can incorporate a term that breaks Lorentz symmetries by adding a traceless and symmetric background field
\displaystyle b^{\mu\nu}
to the free Klein-Gordon Lagrangian
[
14
,
32
]
, as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Free}}^{(\phi)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(g^{\mu\nu}+b^{\mu\nu}\right)\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}m_{\phi}^{2}\phi^{2},

(1)

which leads to the following modified equation of motion

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(g^{\mu\nu}+b^{\mu\nu}\right)\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\phi+m_{\phi}^{2}\phi=0.

(2)

Therefore, the solutions for the scalar field can be expressed as a sum of plane waves, given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\phi(x)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k_{0}}}\left[a_{k}^{\dagger}e^{-ikx}+a_{k}e^{ikx}\right].

(3)

From Eq. (
2
), the dispersion relation is obtained as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle k^{2}+k\cdot b\cdot k=m_{\phi}^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{or}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\eta^{\mu\nu}k_{\mu}k_{\nu}=m_{\phi}^{2},

(4)

with
\displaystyle\eta^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\nu}+b^{\mu\nu}
. Additionally, another important feature is the modified stress-energy tensor for the Lagrangian (
1
), which is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{\alpha\beta}=\partial^{\alpha}\phi\partial^{\beta}\phi-\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}m_{\phi}^{2}\phi^{2}+b^{\alpha\mu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\beta}\phi-\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}b^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi.

(5)

Note that
\displaystyle b_{\mu\nu}
is a two-rank real constant tensor that selects certain preferred directions in space-time.

On the other hand, for a free Dirac spinor field, the Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Free}}^{(\psi)}=\bar{\psi}(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m)\psi,

(6)

where the solution to this equation is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi(x)=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2p_{0}}}\sum_{s}\left[u^{s}_{p}b^{\dagger}_{s}(p)e^{-ipx}+v^{s}_{p}c_{s}(p)e^{ipx}\right].

(7)

For a scattering process involving both fields (
3
) and (
7
), we can consider them in their asymptotic limits, incorporating a specific interaction Lagrangian. In this way, we start with the following total Lagrangian

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\bar{\psi}\left(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m\right)\psi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{1}{2}b^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}m_{\phi}^{2}\phi^{2}-g\bar{\psi}\phi\psi,

(8)

which represents the Lorentz-violating Yukawa theory, with
\displaystyle g
being the coupling constant. This is analogous to QED theory, where fermions interact through an intermediate particle, but here the intermediate particle is a scalar boson rather than a spin-1 gauge boson. The similarities are quite evident and become even more pronounced when considering a massless particle, i.e.,
\displaystyle m_{\phi}=0
.

The equation of motion for the scalar field described by (
8
) is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\square\phi+m_{\phi}^{2}\phi=-g\bar{\psi}\phi\psi-b^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\phi.

(9)

This is analogous to the
\displaystyle\nu-
th component of the Maxwell equation,
\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=j^{\nu}
. From this, we can see that the Lorentz-violating (LV) term plays a significant role, providing a correction to the “scalar” interaction current. Consequently, the Yukawa theory behaves like a scalar Klein-Gordon field, similar to how gauge QED operates within its gauge field framework.

Significant results also emerge when studying these characteristics at finite temperature. One of the most commonly used formalisms for introducing temperature is the real-time TFD formalism, which will be discussed in the next section.

II.1
Thermo Field Dynamics (TFD) Formalism

The construction of TFD arises from the duplication of the Hilbert space into the usual and tilde spaces
[
35
,
36
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
. In other words, the thermal Hilbert space can be written as
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{T}=\mathbb{S}\otimes\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}
represents the usual Hilbert space and
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
represents the dual Hilbert space. For an arbitrary operator
\displaystyle A
, the doubled notation leads to

\displaystyle\displaystyle A^{a}=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
\chi\tilde{A}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
\displaystyle\chi=1
(
\displaystyle-1
) for bosons (fermions) and
\displaystyle a=1,2
. Another component of this construction is the Bogoliubov transformations, which connect the thermal and zero-temperature operators. They are given by
\displaystyle\phi^{a}=\mathbb{M}^{ab}\phi_{b}(\beta)
and
\displaystyle\psi^{a}=\mathbb{N}^{ab}\psi_{b}(\beta)
, where the transformation matrices
\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{ab}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{ab}
are defined for bosons and fermions, respectively, as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{ab}(\beta,p_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}U^{\prime}(\beta,p_{0})&V^{\prime}(\beta,p_{0})\\
V^{\prime}(\beta,p_{0})&U^{\prime}(\beta,p_{0})\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{N}^{ab}(\beta,p_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}U(\beta,p_{0})&V(\beta,p_{0})\\
-V(\beta,p_{0})&U(\beta,p_{0})\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Here we have
\displaystyle(U^{\prime})^{2}=1+n(\beta,p_{0})
and
\displaystyle(V^{\prime})^{2}=n(\beta,p_{0})
, as well as
\displaystyle U^{2}=1-f(\beta,p_{0})
and
\displaystyle V^{2}=f(\beta,p_{0})
, in addition to

\displaystyle\displaystyle n(\beta,p_{0})=\frac{1}{e^{\beta p_{0}}-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptf(\beta,p_{0})=\frac{1}{e^{\beta p_{0}}+1},

(12)

where these quantities represent the boson and fermion distribution functions, respectively.

With these ingredients, a thermal vacuum state
\displaystyle\ket{0(\beta)}
can be constructed. The thermal average of quantum operators
\displaystyle A
can then be calculated using the following form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle A\rangle=\bra{0(\beta)}A\ket{0(\beta)}.

(13)

Now, let’s briefly discuss the boson propagator and how to derive it within the TFD formalism, including the addition of the Lorentz violation term.

II.2
Boson propagator at finite temperature with Lorentz violation

In the TFD formalism
[
35
,
36
]
, the boson propagator is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{F}^{ab}(x-y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{0(\beta)}\mathcal{T}\left[\phi^{a}(x)\phi^{b}(y)\right]\ket{0(\beta)}

(14)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\bra{0(\beta)}\phi^{a}(x)\phi^{b}(y)\ket{0(\beta)}+\Theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\bra{0(\beta)}\phi^{a}(y)\phi^{b}(x)\ket{0(\beta)},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator,
\displaystyle\Theta(x)
is the step function and the
\displaystyle\phi^{a}(x)
is the free scalar field operator (
3
) in the doubled notation. Let’s calculate each term separately. For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{0(\beta)}\phi^{a}(x)\phi^{b}(y)\ket{0(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}qd^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2q_{0}}\sqrt{2k_{0}}}\biggl[e^{-iqx}e^{iky}\bra{0(\beta)}a^{\dagger a}(q)a^{b}(k)\ket{0(\beta)}

(15)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle e^{iqx}e^{-iky}\bra{0(\beta)}a^{a}(q)a^{\dagger b}(k)\ket{0(\beta)}\biggr].

Using the Bogoliubov transformation we can write

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{0(\beta)}a^{\dagger a}(q)a^{b}(k)\ket{0(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{a2}(\beta,q)\mathbb{M}^{b2}(\beta,q)(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(q-k)

(16)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{0(\beta)}a^{a}(q)a^{\dagger b}(k)\ket{0(\beta)}=\mathbb{M}^{a1}(\beta,q)\mathbb{M}^{b1}(\beta,q)(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(q-k).

(17)

Similar results are obtained for the second term of Eq. (
14
). Thus, the boson propagator becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{ab}_{F}(x-y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2q_{0}}\biggl[\Theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\mathbb{M}^{a1}\mathbb{M}^{b1}+\Theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\mathbb{M}^{a2}\mathbb{M}^{b2}\biggr]e^{-iq(x-y)}

(18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2q_{0}}\biggl[\Theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\mathbb{M}^{a1}\mathbb{M}^{b1}+\Theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\mathbb{M}^{a2}\mathbb{M}^{b2}\biggr]e^{iq(x-y)}.

After performing some calculations and applying the residue theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{F}^{ab}(x-y)=-i\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\biggl[\frac{1}{q\cdot\eta\cdot q-m_{\phi}^{2}}\tau+2\pi in\delta(q\cdot\eta\cdot q-m_{\phi}^{2})\Delta(q_{0})\biggr]e^{-iq(x-y)},

(19)

where the dispersion relation
\displaystyle(q_{0}\pm i\epsilon)^{2}\sim q_{0}^{2}\pm i\epsilon=m_{\phi}^{2}+\vec{q}^{2}-q\cdot b\cdot q\pm i\epsilon
has been used and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta(q_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{\beta q_{0}/2}\\
e^{\beta q_{0}/2}&1\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\tau=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

Additionally, a regularized delta function relation
[
45
]
has been considered, i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\pi i\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}\delta(x)=\left(-\frac{1}{x+i\epsilon}\right)^{n+1}-\left(-\frac{1}{x-i\epsilon}\right)^{n+1}.

(21)

On the other hand, knowing that, as a first approximation, we have the following

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta^{\mu\nu}q_{\mu}q_{\nu}-m_{\phi}^{2}-i\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\eta^{\mu\nu}q_{\mu}q_{\nu}-m_{\phi}^{2}+i\epsilon}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\pi i\delta(q^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})-2\pi ib_{\mu\nu}q^{\mu}q^{\nu}\delta^{\prime}(q^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2}),

(22)

the thermal propagator with Lorentz violation corrections can be written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{F}^{ab}(x-y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-i\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\biggl\{\frac{1}{q\cdot\eta\cdot q-m_{\phi}^{2}}\tau

(23)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\pi i\left[\delta(q^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})-b_{\mu\nu}q^{\mu}q^{\nu}\delta^{\prime}(q^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\right]n\Delta(q_{0})\biggr\}e^{-iq(x-y)}.

Note that as the temperature approaches zero, the Lorentz-violating zero-temperature propagator, as obtained by
[
32
]
, is recovered, that is,

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{F}(x)=-i\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{\eta^{\mu\nu}q_{\mu}q_{\nu}-m_{\phi}^{2}}e^{-iqx}.

(24)

With these tools, the next section will explore the Yukawa scattering process with Lorentz violation at finite temperature.

III
Yukawa scattering

In this section, we will discuss Yukawa scattering with thermal effects and Lorentz violation symmetries. To calculate the cross section, we need to derive the average of the scattering amplitude and define a reference frame. For this study, we will use the Lab frame. Let’s begin by determining the transition amplitude.

III.1
The scattering

This subsection focuses on the interaction between fermions through Yukawa scattering at finite temperature. The Feynman diagrams illustrating this scattering process are shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Feynman diagrams for fermion-fermion Yukawa scattering. Diagrams (a) and (b) represent the t-channel and u-channel, respectively.

In order to calculate the cross section, we need to determine the scattering amplitude
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{fi}^{(\beta)}
, which is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{fi}^{(\beta)}=\bra{f(\beta)}\hat{S}^{(2)}\ket{i(\beta)},

(25)

where the thermal initial and final states are given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{i(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle b^{\dagger}_{p}(\beta)b^{\dagger}_{k}(\beta)\ket{0(\beta)},

(26)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{f(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{0(\beta)}b_{{k}^{\prime}}(\beta)b_{{p}^{\prime}}(\beta).

(27)

Here, we consider
\displaystyle\hat{S}^{(2)}
as the second-order term of the scattering matrix, given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{S}^{(2)}=\frac{(-i)^{2}}{2!}\int d^{4}x\,d^{4}y\mathcal{T}\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(x)\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(y)\right],

(28)

where
\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{I}=\mathcal{L}_{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{I}
is the interaction Lagrangian in the doubled space, and is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{I}=-g\bar{\psi}\phi\psi+g\tilde{\bar{\psi}}\tilde{\phi}\tilde{\psi}.

(29)

While the non-tilde terms represent the conventional Hilbert space, the tilde terms represent the dual space arising from the doubling of this representation, resulting in parameters analogous in structure to those in the standard space. This construction is necessary to introduce thermal effects via TFD formalism
[
35
,
36
]
. The physical quantities are described by the non-tilde operators.
Substituting the interaction Lagrangian, the transition amplitude becomes

i\mathcal{M}_{fi}^{(\beta)}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(\Sigma p)=\frac{(-ig)^{2}}{2!}\int d^{4}x\,d^{4}y\bra{f(\beta)}\mathcal{T}\left[\bar{\psi}_{x}\phi_{x}\psi_{x}\bar{\psi}_{y}\phi_{y}\psi_{y}\right]\ket{i(\beta)},

(30)

where the delta function guarantees that momentum conservation is maintained. Here, only the non-tilde part is considered; however, a similar result holds for the tilde part. The only non-vanishing contributions are from terms where the contractions occur between the field operators and the creation and annihilation operators of the initial and final particles.
Solving Eq. (
30
) we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(\Sigma p)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{2}F(\beta)\int d^{4}x\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,d^{4}y\bar{u}_{{p}^{\prime}}\,u_{p}\bar{u}_{{k}^{\prime}}u_{k}e^{i(p^{\prime}-p)x}e^{i(k^{\prime}-k)y}D_{F}^{11}(q)e^{-iq(x-y)}

(31)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{2}F(\beta)\int d^{4}x\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,d^{4}y\bar{u}_{{p}^{\prime}}\,u_{k}\bar{u}_{{k}^{\prime}}u_{p}e^{i(p^{\prime}-k)x}e^{i(k^{\prime}-p)y}D_{F}^{11}(q)e^{-iq(x-y)}\,,

where
\displaystyle F(\beta)=U_{p^{\prime}}(\beta)U_{p}(\beta)U_{k^{\prime}}(\beta)U_{k}(\beta)
,
\displaystyle D^{11}_{F}(q)
is the
\displaystyle(a,b)=(1,1)
component of the doubled-space bosonic Feynman propagator on the momentum space. Assuming that total momentum is conserved, the thermal transition amplitude can be written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{2}F(\beta)\left[\bar{u}_{p^{\prime}}u_{p}D_{F}^{11}(p^{\prime}-p)\bar{u}_{k^{\prime}}u_{k}-\bar{u}_{p^{\prime}}u_{k}D_{F}^{11}(p^{\prime}-k)\bar{u}_{k^{\prime}}u_{p}\right].

(32)

This result corresponds to the sum of two Feynman diagrams depicted in Figure
1
. It is often denoted as
\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}=i\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(\beta)}+i\mathcal{M}_{b}^{(\beta)}
, where each index refers to diagrams (a) and (b) in the Figure
1
.

In the next subsection, we will calculate the differential cross section for this scattering process.

III.2
Yukawa scattering differential cross-section

In order to obtain the cross section for the Yukawa scattering process, let’s start by rewriting Eq. (
32
) in a more convenient way, such that,

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(\beta)}+i\mathcal{M}_{b}^{(\beta)}.

(33)

In such a way that the helicity average of the probability density is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle|\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{s}|\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}|^{2}=\bigl\langle|\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}_{a}|^{2}\bigr\rangle+\bigl\langle|\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}_{b}|^{2}\bigr\rangle+\bigl\langle 2\Re{\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(\beta)\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{b}^{(\beta)}}\bigr\rangle,

(34)

where
\displaystyle\langle|\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(\beta)}|^{2}\rangle
and
\displaystyle\langle|\mathcal{M}_{b}^{(\beta)}|^{2}\rangle
represent the two Feynman diagrams in Figure
1
, and the third is an interference term between them.

Using the Mandelstam variables defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle p\cdot k=s/2-m^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptp\cdot p^{\prime}=m^{2}-t/2,\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptp\cdot k^{\prime}=m^{2}-u/2,

(35)

we can write the average values of the scattering amplitudes up to the second order of the LV-factor as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle|\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}_{a}|^{2}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{4}F^{2}(\beta)(t-4m^{2})^{2}\biggl\{\frac{1}{(t-m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}\bigg[1-\frac{2f_{b}(q_{1})}{(t-m_{\phi}^{2})}+\frac{3f_{b}^{2}(q_{1})}{(t-m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}\bigg]

(36)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{(e^{\beta E_{CM}}-1)^{2}}\biggl[\delta^{2}(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})-2f_{b}(q_{1})\delta(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\delta^{\prime}(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})+f_{b}^{2}(q_{1})\delta^{\prime 2}(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\biggr]\biggr\},

where
\displaystyle(p^{\prime}-p)^{\mu}=q_{1}^{\mu}
and
\displaystyle f_{b}(q)=b_{\mu\nu}q^{\mu}q^{\nu}
. Similarly, for the other terms,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle|\mathcal{M}^{(\beta)}_{b}|^{2}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{4}F^{2}(\beta)(u-4m^{2})^{2}\biggl\{\frac{1}{(u-m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}\bigg[1-\frac{2f_{b}(q_{2})}{(u-m_{\phi}^{2})}+\frac{3f_{b}^{2}(q_{2})}{(u-m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}\bigg]

(37)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{(e^{\beta E_{CM}}-1)^{2}}\biggl[\delta^{2}(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})-2f_{b}(q_{2})\delta(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\delta^{\prime}(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})+f_{b}^{2}(q_{2})\delta^{\prime 2}(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\biggr]\biggr\},

with
\displaystyle(p^{\prime}-k)^{\mu}=q_{2}^{\mu}
, and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle 2\Re{\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(\beta)\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{b}^{(\beta)}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{g^{4}F^{2}(\beta)}{2}\frac{[16m^{4}+8m^{2}(s-t-u)-s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}]}{(t-m^{2}_{\phi})(u-m^{2}_{\phi})}

(38)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\displaystyle\biggl\{\bigg[1-\frac{f_{b}(q_{1})}{(t-m_{\phi}^{2})}-\frac{f_{b}(q_{2})}{(u-m_{\phi}^{2})}+\frac{f_{b}^{2}(q_{1})}{(t-m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{f^{2}_{b}(q_{2})}{(u-m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{f_{b}(q_{1})f_{b}(q_{2})}{(u-m_{\phi}^{2})(t-m_{\phi}^{2})}\bigg]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{2}(t-m_{\phi})(u-m_{\phi})}{(e^{\beta E_{CM}}-1)^{2}}\biggl[\delta(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\delta(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})-f_{b}(q_{1})\delta^{\prime}(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\delta(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{b}(q_{2})\delta(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\delta^{\prime}(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})+f_{b}(q_{1})f_{b}(q_{2})\delta^{\prime}(q_{1}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\delta^{\prime}(q_{2}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2})\biggr]\biggr\}.

Using the center-of-mass frame of reference, the momenta can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle p=(E,\vec{p}_{i});\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptk=(E,-\vec{p}_{i});\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptp^{\prime}=(E,\vec{p}_{f});\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptk^{\prime}=(E,-\vec{p}_{f}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle p=(m,\vec{p});\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptk=(m,\vec{k});\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptp^{\prime}=(m,\vec{p}^{\prime});\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptk^{\prime}=(m,\vec{k}^{\prime});

In this context, we can directly express the Yukawa potential as

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{T}(r)=-g^{2}F_{NR}(\beta)\biggl\{\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}-b_{ij}q_{i}q_{j}+m_{\phi}^{2}}-\frac{2\pi i}{e^{\beta E_{q}}-1}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta\biggl[\vec{q}^{2}-b_{ij}q_{i}q_{j}+m_{\phi}^{2}\biggr]e^{i\vec{q}\vec{r}}\biggr\},

(61)

with
\displaystyle E_{q}=E_{p}+E_{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\bar{q}

\displaystyle G_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-b_{ij}

\displaystyle d^{3}q\to\frac{d^{3}\bar{q}}{\sqrt{|G|}}

\displaystyle|G|=\det(G_{ij})

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\delta_{ij}-b_{ij})q_{i}q_{j}=G_{ij}q_{i}q_{j}\to\bar{q}\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{and}\hskip 9.24994pt(\delta_{ij}+b_{ij})r_{i}r_{j}=G_{ij}^{-1}r_{i}r_{j}.

(62)

Hence, the position transforms inversely to the momentum, so the product
\displaystyle\vec{r}\vec{q}\to\vec{\bar{r}}\vec{\bar{q}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{T}(r)=-\frac{g^{2}F_{NR}(\beta)}{\sqrt{|G|}}\biggl\{\int\frac{d^{3}\bar{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\vec{\bar{r}}\vec{\bar{q}}}}{(\vec{\bar{q}})^{2}+m_{\phi}^{2}}-\frac{2\pi i}{e^{\beta E_{q}}-1}\int\frac{d^{3}\bar{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta\biggl[(\vec{\bar{q}})^{2}+m_{\phi}^{2}\biggr]e^{i\vec{\bar{r}}\vec{\bar{q}}}\biggr\}.

The main observation is that temperature in Yukawa theory modifies both the cross-section and the potential. The modification of the potential is simpler than that of the cross-section: thermal corrections to the Yukawa potential primarily increase the strength of the interaction between particles. This enhancement is due exclusively to the function
\displaystyle F_{\text{NR}}(\beta)

Regarding Lorentz violation, we observe that it modifies the potential not only by increasing it but also by decreasing and potentially canceling it. These effects are closely dependent on the values of the LV parameters. From the one-dimensional profile described in Figure

\displaystyle b_{ij}

The analysis of the Lorentz-violating thermal potential is straightforward, as it involves combining both features. In other words, Lorentz violation breaks all spatial symmetries, while the presence of thermal effects enhances the potential, as illustrated in Figure

V
Conclusion

In this work, we address the Yukawa theory by considering corrections from both Lorentz symmetry violation and the presence of a heat bath. The Lorentz-violating parameters are introduced into the problem through a kinetic term in the Lagrangian, which modifies the scalar boson propagator. We employed a real-time formalism for thermal field theory, deriving the propagator using the TFD formalism and incorporating temperature dependence through the doubling of the Hilbert space. In this context, the transition amplitude was derived, and the total cross section was calculated under these conditions. The results were analyzed for specific values of temperature and Lorentz-violating coefficients. Additionally, we presented the Yukawa potential, a non-relativistic quantity representing the interaction between two fermions, under the effects of temperature and Lorentz violation. We examined specific cases and discussed the resulting features. Finally, it was observed that considering Lorentz symmetry breakdown has several implications for changes in symmetries and physical states, while the presence of temperature is strongly linked to the strength of the interaction. Understanding the corrections due to both modifications is crucial for determining some characteristics of the early universe, as these factors are intrinsic to the primordial conditions.
