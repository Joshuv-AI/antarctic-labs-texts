Role of LINC00332 in colorectal cancer progression and paclitaxel resistance
Authors: Meysam Moghbeli, Negin Taghehchian, Mahsa Palizkaran Yazdi, Mohammad Reza Abbaszadegan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer and a leading cause of cancer deaths. Standard therapeutic management is faced with challenges like treatment resistance and late-stage diagnosis, highlighting a need for better diagnosis. LncRNAs are crucial in CRC progression and may serve as non-invasive biomarkers. For the first time, this study explores LINC00332 potential as a therapeutic and diagnostic marker in CRC.MethodsEighty fresh normal and tumor tissues were enrolled to evaluate the levels of LINC00332 expression using Real-time PCR method. LINC00332 ectopic expression was also done to evaluate its role in drug resistance and cell migration in HCT116 cells.ResultsEctopic expression of LINC00332 significantly induced NOTCH and WNT pathways in HCT116 cells. LINC00332 promoted EMT process through up regulation of Vimentin, CDH2, Snail, ZEB2, Slug, MMP10, and MMP3 while down regulation of CDH1 and OCLUDIN in HCT116 cells. LINC00332 significantly induced the HCT116 cell migration and paclitaxel (PTX) resistance in HCT116 cells (p < 0.0001). There was significant up regulation of LINC00332 in CRC clinical samples compared to normal margins (p = 0.042). There was significant up regulation of LINC00332 in stage I/II tumors that was located in transverse and left colon in comparison to right colon (p = 0.03). Tumors with perineural invasion had significant LINC00332 up regulation compared with negative ones in CRC patients (p = 0.02).ConclusionsLINC00332 promotes PTX resistance and EMT in CRC via WNT and NOTCH pathways activation. It can also be suggested as a potential diagnostic marker in early-stage CRC patients. Targeting LINC00332 may enhance PTX response, warranting further studies for therapeutic application.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortalities globally, with over 1.9 million new cases and 930,000 deaths annually [1]. Developed nations with Western lifestyles report higher rates due to modifiable risk factors (e.g., obesity, processed meat consumption, smoking), while low- and middle-income countries face a rapidly rising burden linked to urbanization and dietary shifts [2, 3]. Alarmingly, the global CRC burden is projected to surge by 60% by 2030, exceeding 2.25 million new cases [4]. Standard CRC management employs a multimodal approach including surgical resection, chemotherapy (5-FU, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan), radiotherapy, and immunotherapy (cetuximab and pembrolizumab) [5]. Despite recent therapeutic advances, there are still some significant limitations: Stoma-related morbidity, infection, and prolonged recovery impair quality of life. Intrinsic/acquired chemotherapy resistance also leads to relapse in > 50% of metastatic cases [6, 7]. Late-stage diagnosis remains a critical barrier to improving CRC survival. Abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, or changes in bowel habits are common but overlap with benign conditions (e.g., hemorrhoids, IBS). Symptom misinterpretation in primary care and long colonoscopy waiting lists contribute to diagnostic delays, leading to 60% of cases being diagnosed at advanced stages. These factors underpin the dismal 5-year survival for metastatic CRC (< 15%), highlighting the urgent need for non-invasive, cost-effective diagnostic tools [2, 3].

LncRNAs constitute ~ 75% of the human transcriptome and modulate gene expression via interactions with DNA, RNA, and proteins. They are involved in CRC progression as oncogene or tumor suppressor [8, 9]. LncRNAs offer transformative potential as non-invasive biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer patients because of their tissue-specific signatures and stability in blood/plasma [10]. LncRNAs serve as master regulators of WNT and NOTCH signaling in cancer, functioning as scaffolds, decoys, guides, and sponges to amplify oncogenic outputs to underlie stemness, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [11, 12]. Deciphering the lncRNA-WNT-NOTCH interactome will advance precision oncology, particularly for aggressive carcinomas reliant on these pathways. LINC00332 (located on chromosome 13q14.11) has been identified as a significant tumor suppressor in gastric cancer by inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling that reduced EMT process, cell stemness, and cisplatin resistance [13]. However, there is not any report about the role of LINC00332 in CRC. Therefore, in the present study we assessed the role of LINC00332 to suggest that as a novel therapeutic and diagnostic marker in CRC patients.

Study population and LINC00332 expression

The cohort comprised 38 males (47.5%) and 42 females (52.5%), with a mean age of 57.30 ± 13.22 years. Tumors were ranged between 1 and 13 cm with mean size of 4.99 ± 2.25. Clinicopathological annotation included tumor location (Left colon: 66.2% [53/80], right colon: 28.8% [23/80], and transverse colon: 5% [4/80]), histological differentiation grade (I: 46.2% [37/80], II: 52.5% [42/80], and III: 1.3% [1/80]), TNM stage (I/II: 58.8%, III/IV: 41.2%), depth of invasion (T1/2: 20%, T3/4: 80%), lymph node metastasis (negative: 41.2%, positive: 58.8%), and perineural invasion status (positive in 25% [20/80] of CRC patients) (Table 1). LINC00332 over and under expressions were observed in 32.5% (26/80) and 21.2% (17/80) of patients, respectively. Quantitative analysis revealed significant up regulation of LINC00332 in CRC clinical samples compared to normal margins (p = 0.042) (Fig. 6). Females had higher levels of LINC00332 expressions compared with male CRC patients (0.74 ± 0.55 vs 0.25 ± 0.73). There was noticeable higher levels of LINC00332 in primary stages (I/II) compared with advanced stages (III/IV) tumors (0.81 ± 0.64 vs 0.06 ± 0.59). There was significant up regulation of LINC00332 in stage I/II tumors that was located in transverse and left colon in comparison to right colon (1.38 ± 4.5 vs −0.29 ± 4.7) (P = 0.03). There were noticeable higher levels of LINC00332 in grade I compared with grade II tumors (1.07 ± 0.74 vs −0.05 ± 0.54). Tumor with low depth of invasion (T1/2) had higher levels of LINC00332 expressions compared with high depth of invasion (T3/4) (1.33 ± 1.13 vs 0.30 ± 0.48). Lymph node positive tumors had higher levels of LINC00332 expressions compared with negative ones (0.72 ± 0.64 vs 0.19 ± 0.59). Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a global health challenge, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Therefore, there is an urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving CRC pathogenesis. Among the critical signaling cascades implicated in CRC, the WNT and NOTCH pathways stand out as master regulators of intestinal development, stem cell maintenance, and epithelial homeostasis. Deregulation of these evolutionarily conserved pathways contributes fundamentally to CRC initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Recent researches has illuminated the crucial regulatory roles of lncRNAs in fine-tuning WNT and NOTCH signaling, opening new avenues for diagnostic and therapeutic innovation.

WNT signaling pathway comprises multiple branches, including the canonical WNT/β-catenin pathway, the planar cell polarity (WNT/PCP) pathway, and the WNT/Ca2⁺ pathway, each with distinct roles in CRC pathogenesis [15, 16]. The canonical pathway, centered on β-catenin stabilization, is the most extensively characterized and frequently deregulated pathway in CRC [15]. In the absence of WNT ligands, a destruction complex orchestrates the phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation of β-catenin. WNT ligands disrupt the destruction complex that results in β-catenin translocation to the nucleus, where it partners with T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) transcription factors to activate WNT target genes [12]. WNT pathway maintains cancer stem cell (CSC) populations that drive tumor initiation, recurrence, and therapy resistance [17]. Furthermore, WNT signaling promotes EMT, facilitating metastatic dissemination through transcriptional up regulation of EMT-inducing transcription factors [18]. Recent evidence also implicates WNT signaling in chemo resistance, with sustained β-catenin activity conferring resistance to 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin in CRC [19].

Several lncRNAs function as negative regulators of WNT signaling in CRC. ADAMTS9-AS1 inhibited β-catenin nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, reducing expression of cyclin D1 and c-MYC that reduced CRC progression [20]. KIAA0125 suppressed cell proliferation and EMT process through Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC [21]. There was LINC01550 down regulation in CRC tissues that was correlated with advanced stage and metastasis. LINC01550 suppressed CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and EMT by inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [22]. LINC00261 inhibited CRC progression via miR-324-3p sponging and inactivation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway [23].

Numerous lncRNAs drive CRC progression by potentiating WNT/β-catenin signaling. H19 promoted methotrexate resistance in through activating Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC cells [24]. There was SNHG1 up regulation in CRC tissues that was correlated with poor survival. SNHG1 induced WNT/β-catenin pathway through regulating β-catenin, TCF4, CCND1, and MMP9 in CRC cells [25]. LINC01315 increased EMT via activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC cells [26]. In the present study, we also observed that LINC00332 induced WNT pathway in CRC via down regulation of APC and GSK3β, while up regulation of LEF1, TCF7, LRP6, and β-catenin that was associated with increased PTX resistance and cell migration.

NOTCH signaling pathway represents a highly conserved intercellular communication system fundamental to cell fate determination, tissue development, and homeostasis [27]. NOTCH activation initiates through ligand-receptor binding, triggering a cascade of proteolytic cleavages which releases the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). NICD translocates to the nucleus, associates with CSL/MAML complex that induces expression of NOTCH target genes [28, 29]. NOTCH signaling exhibits context-dependent roles in CRC, functioning as both oncogene and tumor suppressor depending on specific receptors, cellular context, and disease stage. NOTCH1 activation demonstrates predominantly oncogenic functions, promoting CRC proliferation, survival, and metastasis [30]. Mechanistically, NOTCH1 drives EMT process through direct transcriptional upregulation of SNAIL and SLUG, repressing E-cadherin expression and enhancing cell motility [31, 32]. FOXD2-AS1 induced CRC progression by regulating EMT and Notch pathway [33]. We also observed that LINC00332 activated the NOTCH pathway and EMT process in CRC cells that can be associated with increased PTX resistance and cell migration. WNT and NOTCH pathways have extensive crosstalk that significantly influences tumor biology. It was shown that NOTCH1 activated Wnt/β-catenin pathway by increased translocation of β-catenin to the nucleus in CRC cells [34]. Simultaneous activation of WNT and NOTCH pathways following the LINC00332 ectopic expression in CRC cells confirms a probable interaction between these signaling pathways during CRC progression that should be assessed by further studies. Since, WNT and NOTCH pathways are the key activators of EMT process [35], LINC00332/WNT & NOTCH/EMT axis can be associated with increased PTX resistance and cell migration in CRC cells. We also confirmed the oncogenic role of LINC00332 in clinical CRC samples, in which there was significant LINC00332 up regulation in tumor samples compared with normal margins. There were significant LINC00332 up regulations in CRC tumors with lymph node metastasis and perineural invasion. However, early-stage and low-grade tumors had higher levels of LINC00332 expressions compared with advanced and high-grade tumors.

There were some limitations in our study. All the functional experiments (ectopic expression, migration, drug resistance) were conducted only in the HCT116 cell line. CRC is a highly heterogeneous disease. The role of LINC00332 may vary across different CRC cell lines (e.g., SW480, LoVo, Caco-2). The conclusions need to be verified in multiple cell lines to ensure they are not unique to HCT116. The study is entirely based on in vitro (cell culture) and human tissue analysis. The critical finding that LINC00332 promotes paclitaxel resistance and metastasis was not tested in an animal model (e.g., mouse xenograft). In vivo experiments are essential to confirm these effects in a complex, living system with a tumor microenvironment, blood vessels, and an immune system.

Conclusions

LINC00332 induced PTX resistance and EMT process in CRC by activation of WNT and NOTCH pathways. There was also significant LINC00332 up regulation in CRC samples compared with normal tissues. It seems that LINC00332 can be involved in CRC aggressiveness in the early stages and grades, while it has a lower oncogenic value in advanced stages. Therefore, LINC00332 can be suggested as a tumor marker for the early-stage diagnosis in CRC patients. Moreover, LINC00332 targeting can improve the PTX response in the early stages of CRC progression. However, further animal studies and clinical trials are required to use LINC00332 as a therapeutic target in CRC. While this report provides exciting and novel evidence that LINC00332 is a potential oncogene in CRC, its limitations highlight that it is an early-stage, discovery-phase study. The findings are within the experimental setup, but require extensive further validation to confirm the mechanism, generalize the results across CRC subtypes, and establish true clinical relevance for diagnosis and therapy.

Methods

Cell culture and transfection

The human CRC cell line HCT116 (obtained from Pasteur Institute, Iran) was cultured in DMEM medium (10% FBS, 37 °C, 5% CO₂). For ectopic expression of LINC00332, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well) and transfected 24 h post-seeding using X-treme GENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, Germany). LINC00332- pcDNA3.1 plasmid was used for transfection, while the pCDH-513b vector (System Biosciences) served as a positive control to check the efficiency of transfection via fluorescence microscopy (Optika, Italy).

Migration assay

Cell migration was evaluated using a scratch/wound-healing assay. HCT116 cells were grown to 90–100% confluency in 6-well plates, transfected with LINC00332- pcDNA3.1, and scratched with a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by PBS washing. Wound closure was monitored at 0, 24, and 48 h using an inverted microscope (Optica, Italy). Images were analyzed with ImageJ software (v1.42, NIH, USA), and the percentage of wound closure was calculated. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Drug resistance assay

Paclitaxel (PTX) resistance was assessed via MTT assay. Briefly, 3 × 104 transfected or non-transfected HCT116 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates, cultured overnight, and treated with PTX (0.75 µM) for 48 h. Post-treatment, 20 µL MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by 4-h incubation. Formazan crystals were solubilized with DMSO, and absorbance was quantified at 570 nm. Assays were conducted in tetraplicate.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from HCT116 cells and CRC tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). RNA integrity was verified via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and purity was quantified spectrophotometrically (A260/A280 ratio > 1.8). cDNA synthesis was performed with a First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Fermentas, Lithuania). qRT-PCR was carried out in duplicate reactions using SYBR Green Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a LightCycler system (Roche, Germany). Ethical approval and patient samples

Fresh tumor tissues and adjacent histologically normal margins were obtained from eighty CRC patients during surgical resection at Imam Reza and Omid hospitals during 2018–2022 (Mashhad, Iran). Specimens were immediately preserved in RNAlater solution (Qiagen, Germany) and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Patients with prior chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or incomplete clinical records were excluded. All methods were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1402.384). Written informed consent was secured from all participants prior to sample collection.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Associations between gene expression and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated using chi-square, Fisher’s exact, ANOVA, and independent t-tests. Linear regression analyzed correlations between gene expression and tumor size. Statistical significance was determined using ANOVA for migration and drug resistance assays. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v8.0 and SPSS (v27.0.1, USA). Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.
