Hearing loss in the working-age population: a systematic analysis from the Global Burden of Disease study 2021
Authors: Qidi Hu, Wen Shen, Lili Kong, Yi Zhou, Ru Chen, Jing Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe working-age population (WAP) refers to individuals aged 15–64, who are the main drivers of production. Among the various factors affecting their productivity, hearing loss plays a significant role. However, epidemiological data on hearing loss in the WAP remain limited. The study analyses the global, regional and national situation of hearing loss in the WAP and predicts the disease burden up to 2040.SettingThis study was based on data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study, covering 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021.ParticipantsThe study population included all individuals aged 15–64 years, consistent with the United Nations definition of the WAP and adopted in the GBD 2021 study.DesignData on the prevalence and years lived with disability (YLDs) due to hearing loss among the WAP were extracted from the GBD database. The disease burden was represented using both absolute numbers and age-standardised rates (ASRs). Trends were analysed with the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). Subgroup analyses on sociodemographic index (SDI), gender, disease severity and causes were performed, and projections for 2040 were estimated using the Nordpred model.ResultsGlobally, from 1990 to 2021, the number of hearing loss cases in the WAP increased from 558.08 million to 1.04 billion, and the number of YLDs rose from 14.45 million to 26.55 million. In 2021, the prevalence in the WAP was 19 607.24 per 100 000, with YLDs at 501.81 per 100 000. The EAPC shows an upward trend: the change in age-standardised prevalence is 0.11 (95% uncertainty interval (UI 0.10, 0.12), and the change in age-standardised YLDs is 0.10 (95% UI 0.08, 0.11). High SDI regions have the lowest burden of hearing loss globally. At the regional level, as SDI increases, the age-standardised prevalence and YLDs of hearing loss show a downward trend. In contrast, the burden is higher in Oceania, Southeast Asia, South Asia, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa and East Asia. The top three countries in terms of prevalence and YLDs are Madagascar, Malawi and Kenya. By 2040, the global prevalence and YLDs of hearing loss in the WAP are projected to be 1.31 billion and 33.30 million, respectively, with ASRs of 19 890.33 and 512.27 per 100 000 population.ConclusionsThe burden of hearing loss in the WAP is gradually increasing, with differences in prevalence and YLDs across regions, countries and SDI levels. Continued attention is needed for this vulnerable group’s hearing loss, along with the implementation of effective measures to reduce future burdens.Keywords: Hearing, PUBLIC HEALTH, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Audiology

Introduction

Hearing loss is the most common hidden disability among adults and the third leading cause of disability.1 2 In 2019, hearing loss affected roughly 1.57 billion individuals worldwide, contributing to an estimated 43.45 million years lived with disability (YLDs).1 The effects of hearing impairment span across all age groups. It can affect language development in children, increase unemployment rates in adults, elevate the risk of dementia and cognitive impairments in the elderly, and cause anxiety in patients.3,6 The high prevalence of hearing loss and its widespread consequences place a significant burden on society.

Among the populations affected by hearing loss, there is limited research focusing on the working-age population (WAP), which refers to individuals aged 15–64, a group that constitutes the majority of the workforce in society.7 According to statistics, over 50% of the worldwide demographic falls within the 15–64 age bracket, a group closely linked to productivity levels and economic growth, and serving as a key factor in the well-being of society.8 Studies have demonstrated that individuals with hearing loss face disadvantages in terms of unemployment rates, income and retirement age.9,11 Previous research has largely treated individuals with hearing loss as a single group, resulting in a lack of epidemiological data for specific subpopulations.12,14 Given the far-reaching impact of hearing loss on unemployment rates and the economy, understanding the trends of hearing loss in the WAP is crucial, as it could provide valuable insights for policy adjustments.

Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 provides a framework for hearing loss research. The objective of this research is to evaluate the prevalence, YLDs of hearing loss and the progression of tendencies spanning 1990–2021 among the WAP, and to explore the subgroup effects of factors such as disease type, severity, country, gender, region and socio-demographic index (SDI).

Methods

Data sources

The GBD provides epidemiological data on hearing loss prevalence and YLDs for the years 1990 and 2021, covering the global, 21 regions and 204 countries. Data were obtained from the GBD Results Tool available at the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) website (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). We restricted all data extraction and analyses to the WAP, defined as individuals aged 15–64 years. Populations outside the working-age range were excluded. Countries or years with missing or implausible data were excluded from trend and stratified analyses. GBD 2021 defines hearing loss as an aggregate impairment that includes disability due to hearing loss exceeding 20 dB in the optimal ear, determined based on calculating the average across four frequencies ranging from 500 to 4000 Hz. Additionally, the database categorises hearing loss into six levels, ranging from mild to complete. It also identifies four main causes: congenital birth defects, otitis media, meningitis and ageing. The Global Health Data Exchange, maintained by IHME, was used to set specific criteria for subgroup analyses (severity, causes, gender and age) based on data obtained from the same platform.

Estimation framework

As this study is a secondary analysis based on the standardised and validated modelling framework of the GBD 2021 study, no additional model selection or sensitivity analyses were performed. The GBD framework computes health metrics based on the detailed methodologies outlined earlier.15 16 The overall tally of instances of hearing loss cases is calculated by adding previously diagnosed cases and newly diagnosed cases. The rate of prevalence is subsequently calculated by splitting the entirety of instances relative to the demographic magnitude, resulting in the prevalence rate per 100 000 people. In this study, YLDs represent the years of life lost due to disability caused by hearing loss.

Sociodemographic index

The SDI is an index that combines factors like income per capita, education level and fertility rates to assess a country or region’s development. The SDI, ranging from 0 to 1, reflects a region’s development level, with higher values indicating more advancement. Quintiles of the SDI group countries and regions into five categories, allowing for easier comparison of development and health outcomes.16

Statistical analysis

R V.4.2.2 software was used for conducting the statistical analysis. Prevalence, YLDs and their 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were used to assess the burden of hearing loss in the WAP. A log-linear regression model was applied to calculate the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) for each indicator, including the numbers and age-standardised rates (ASRs) of prevalence and YLDs, to capture temporal trends in the data from 1990 to 2 0217. Health inequality analysis based on YLDs was conducted using the Concentration Index and Slope Index of Inequality (SII) to quantify the disparities in hearing loss burden across countries, health inequality in hearing loss from 1990 to 2021 is reflected in the SII slope: a flatter slope signifies smaller disparities between countries.18 The Concentration Index ranges from −1 to 1, with values near 0 indicating better balance. Positive and negative values show that the disease burden is concentrated in higher and lower SDI regions, respectively. Decomposition analysis was used to attribute the changes in total YLDs to three contributing factors: population growth, population ageing and epidemiological changes.19 Frontier analysis was used to explore how countries perform at different levels of SDI.20 At each SDI level, countries with the lowest age-standardised YLDs were considered to represent the best performance, showing the minimum burden achievable for that development stage. Full methodological details for both the decomposition and frontier analyses, including definitions and computation steps, are available in prior publications.21 22 The Nordpred model, incorporating the age-period-cohort framework, was used to forecast hearing loss data through 2040.23

Results

The global burden of hearing loss in WAP

Online supplemental table S1 presents the trends in disease prevalence, YLDs and EAPC along with their age-standardised values for both global and regional SDI groups in 2021.

Over the past 30 years, as the global population surged, the number of hearing loss cases in the WAP increased from 558.08 million (95% UI 503.49 million, 617.98 million) to 1.04 billion (95% UI 941.04 million, 1.15 billion), marking an 86.35% rise. Additionally, the prevalence rate of hearing loss increased from 18 848.52 (95% UI 17 008.10, 20 873.21) to 19 607.24 (95% UI 17 683.27, 21 698.87), with an EAPC of 0.11 (95% UI 0.10 to 0.12). YLDs rate increased from 480.15 (95% UI 300.85, 717.83) to 501.81 (95% UI 314.40, 751.24), with an EAPC of 0.10 (95% UI: 0.08, 0.11). Additional information can be found in online supplemental table S1 and figure 1.

Trends in hearing loss prevalence and years lived with disability from 1990 to 2021 at (A) the global level and (B) the regional level.

Gender subgroup analysis revealed that, in both 1990 and 2021, males exhibited higher rates of YLDs and prevalence of hearing loss compared with females (figure 1).

Considerable variation exists in the prevalence and YLDs among diverse SDI regions. The middle SDI region attained the utmost prevalence rate, at 21 237.50 in 2021 (95% UI 19,126.07, 23 593.89). The high SDI region had the lowest prevalence rate, at 13 949.09 in 2021 (95% UI 12 523.94, 15 468.49). Regarding the YLDs rate, the low SDI region had the utmost, at 566.30 in 2021 (95% UI 359.11, 843.39), while the high SDI region exhibited the lowest rates, at 332.38 in 2021 (95% UI 205.81, 505.02). In the high-middle SDI regions, the rising burden of hearing loss is particularly noticeable, with EAPC for the YLDs and prevalence rates being 0.22 (95% UI 0.21, 0.23) and 0.19 (95% UI 0.19, 0.20) respectively.

Subgroup burden analysis based on the degree and causes of hearing loss

In 2021, among the WAP, mild hearing loss was the most widespread, with an age-standardised prevalence rate of 15 517.42 (95% UI 13 826.08, 17 343.04), and a YLDs rate of 212.54 (95% UI 107.35, 371.77). Investigating the origins of hearing loss, age-related and other hearing loss is the leading factor, with an ASR of 461.05 (95% UI 284.44, 697.95) in YLDs and a prevalence rate of 18 251.31 (95% UI 16 258.39, 20 489.23). The regional burden of hearing loss in WAP

Our study found a negative correlation between hearing loss and SDI in terms of prevalence and YLDs. To enhance the visualisation of the data, we further employed graphical representation.

Figure 2A shows that, with the rise of SDI, the overall prevalence first increases and then decreases. In most regions, the trend in prevalence remains relatively stable. The burden is higher in Oceania, South Asia, East Asia, Southeast Asia and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa. In 2021, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest prevalence at 23 555.50 (95% UI 20 799.28, 26 849.55), while Western Europe had the lowest at 12 014.13 (95% UI 10 785.50, 13 331.04). Gender subgroup analysis reveals that the prevalence burden is typically higher in males than in females, but the trend follows the same pattern as the overall trend.

Age-standardised prevalence (A) and age-standardised years lived with disability (B) for hearing loss by sociodemographic index, 2021.

Figure 2B shows that, with the increase in SDI, YLDs generally follow a decreasing trend. In most regions, the trend in YLDs remains relatively stable. The burden is higher in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, South Asia and East Asia. In 2021, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest YLDs at 667.64 (95% UI 419.06, 992.42), while Western Europe had the lowest at 253.29 (95% UI 153.50, 391.26). Analysis of gender subgroups indicates that the burden of YLDs is generally higher among males compared with females, yet this pattern aligns with the general trend.

The national burden of hearing loss in WAP

In 2021, at the national level, Madagascar, Malawi and Kenya reported the highest prevalence rate of hearing loss in this demographic layer, with values of 25 422.13 (95% UI 22 381.71, 28 997.96), 24 805.85 (95% UI 22 030.03, 28 116.56) and 23 896.43 (95% UI 20 995.12, 27 327.60), respectively. Meanwhile, the YLDs for Madagascar, Kenya and Malawi were 810.74 (95% UI 513.80, 1201.91), 780.02 (95% UI 493.66, 1159.61) and 688.95 (95% UI 435.92, 1023.76), respectively, representing the highest values. Health inequality analysis of hearing loss in WAP

Based on YLDs, we conducted a health inequality analysis of hearing loss in the WAP. In 1990, the SII value was −242.50, and in 2021, it was −223.00. The concentration index rose from −0.11 (95% UI −0.12, 0.10) to −0.07 (95% UI −0.09, −0.06) (figure 4). Decomposition analysis

The visual results of the decomposition analysis are shown in figure 5. Globally, population factors, epidemiological changes and ageing accounted for 73.04%, 7.38% and 19.57%, respectively. In each SDI region, population was the primary factor affecting YLDs, with contributions as follows: low SDI (105.43%), low-middle SDI (94.63%), middle SDI (66.53%), high-middle SDI (47.18%) and high SDI (52.35%). The role of ageing across various regions of the SDI is outlined below: low SDI (−2.71%), low-middle SDI (9.99%), middle SDI (31.70%), high-middle SDI (38.22%) and high SDI (42.14%). The contribution of epidemiological changes in each SDI region was as follows: low SDI (−2.72%), low-middle SDI (−4.61%), middle SDI (1.77%), high-middle SDI (14.60%) and high SDI (5.51%). In the gender subgroup analysis, the component contributions across SDI regions were generally consistent with the overall trends.

Projections of hearing loss in WAP

This research forecasts the trend of hearing loss in the WAP from 2022 to 2040 (figure 7). It is anticipated that by 2040, the prevalence and number of YLDs, as well as the ASRs of hearing loss, will continue to rise. The worldwide prevalence of hearing loss within the WAP is projected to reach 1.31 billion by 2040, with an ASR of 19 890.33, the number of YLDs is expected to be 33.30 million, with an ASR of 512.27. Among these, the disease burden for men is expected to be higher than for women.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to analyse the burden of hearing loss among the WAP. Additionally, we have explored this issue from various perspectives, including gender, SDI and the causes and severity of hearing loss. This study fills the gap in current epidemiological data on hearing loss among the WAP.

From 1990 to 2021, the number of cases of hearing loss in the WAP globally increased by 86.35%, which may be attributed to the rapid population growth over the past 30 years. However, the rise in ASRs indicates that the burden of hearing loss in the WAP continues to increase. In terms of gender, males exhibit a heavier burden, which may be linked to differences in occupational exposure and hormonal influences, such as the protective role of oestrogen.24 Fortunately, mild hearing loss is the most prevalent in terms of severity, while complete hearing loss accounts for only a small proportion, emphasising the critical importance of prompt intervention. In terms of aetiology, age-related and other forms of hearing loss dominate in the overall burden.25 Both prevalence and YLDs rates exhibit a decline for hearing loss due to meningitis and otitis media. This change reflects improvements in global healthcare interventions, such as vaccination against pneumococcal and meningococcal infections.26

The marked correlation between the SDI and metrics of disease burden highlights the substantial influence of socioeconomic disparities on the overall burden of disease. In high SDI regions, both prevalence and YLDs are significantly lower than in the other four SDI regions, reflecting regional disparities in hearing loss. These disparities suggest that hearing protection policies should be tailored to the socioeconomic contexts of different populations. As SDI increases, we observe that the prevalence in different regions initially rises and then decreases, while YLDs generally show a declining trend. This pattern reveals an interesting divergence: compared with middle-SDI regions, low-SDI regions show a lower age-standardised prevalence (20 164.73 vs 21 237.5 per 100 000) but higher YLDs (566.30 vs 540.06 per 100 000). This discrepancy may reflect disparities in healthcare access and system capacity. Evidence suggests that many low-income countries have not yet integrated ear and hearing care into their national health systems, and there is a severe shortage of ENT specialists and audiologists.27 28 Such limitations may delay diagnosis and treatment, leading to more severe or prolonged disability and consequently higher YLDs. However, in five regions—Southeast Asia, South Asia, East Asia, Oceania and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa—prevalence poses significant challenges. Regarding YLDs, Southeast Asia, South Asia, East Asia and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa carry a heavy burden. If left uncontrolled, these regions may soon experience a ‘hearing loss epidemic’. In this overall context, certain countries, such as Madagascar, Malawi and Kenya, also exhibit high prevalence and YLDs. This may be partly due to the lag in healthcare development in developing countries in contrast to developed ones. Secondary hearing loss associated with various factors, such as infections and ototoxic drugs, is particularly common, with most patients not receiving hearing healthcare.27 On the other hand, governments often allocate limited healthcare resources to address life-threatening diseases, neglecting the prevention and control of hearing loss. As a result, hearing loss screening remains inadequate.29,32 Fortunately, the SII and concentration index for hearing loss in the WAP have shown a downward trend across different SDI levels, indicating an improvement in health inequality related to hearing loss in the WAP.

To further explore the impact of various factors on YLDs, we conducted a decomposition analysis of YLDs. Overall, the increase in YLDs is primarily driven by population changes. The impact of age and epidemiological changes on hearing loss is generally lower than that of population factors. Against the backdrop of continued global population growth in the future, the group affected by hearing loss in WAP may further expand, highlighting the increasing importance of protecting hearing loss.

To better understand the differences in healthcare efficiency across countries with varying levels of development, frontier analysis was performed using age-standardised YLDs and SDI, drawing on data spanning from 1990 to 2021. In regions with low SDI, such as Haiti, Zimbabwe, Niger, Burkina Faso and Chad, the effective disparities in hearing loss are relatively low. This suggests that, despite limited resources, these countries have managed hearing loss effectively and can offer valuable strategic insights for other low SDI regions. In contrast, some high SDI regions, such as Lithuania, Taiwan (Province of China), Canada, Japan and the USA, exhibit higher effective disparities. This observation suggests that these countries' healthcare systems may face potential inefficiencies, which could stem from lifestyle factors, disparities in healthcare access or other systemic issues.33 The data above indicate significant variability in the effectiveness of hearing loss management across countries. It is essential to learn from the successful strategies of countries with effective management practices and to alert nations with large disparities. Further investigation into the underlying causes of hearing loss in these regions could help reduce the future disease burden.

Based on the existing epidemiological data, we predicted the burden of hearing loss in the WAP from 2022 to 2040. Projections indicate that by 2040, the number and ASRs of hearing loss in the global WAP will further increase. This further highlights the importance of hearing loss prevention strategies.34

Among the populations affected by hearing loss, WAP are a crucial focus at individual, family and societal levels due to their higher risk of lower incomes and employment rates. Hearing loss in this group has significant economic and social implications, potentially contributing to a cycle of poverty and limited opportunities, affecting not only individuals but also their families and communities. Addressing hearing loss in this population is therefore not only a health issue but also an economic and social equity concern. For individuals with mild hearing loss, although they may not face significant challenges with communication, they can experience dissatisfaction due to the decline in independence, emotional health and increasing limitations.35 This subtle frustration can affect their quality of life, leading to feelings of isolation or inadequacy in social or work environments. The lack of obvious symptoms often means that these individuals may not seek help, which can worsen their condition and increase psychological stress. Although mild hearing loss is the most common severity, strategies to address it may differ across SDI regions. In low-SDI and middle-SDI settings, higher prevalence and YLDs may result from limited healthcare resources and delayed interventions. Strengthening healthcare investment, training hearing care professionals, policy support and public education are therefore essential. In contrast, high-SDI countries have a lower overall burden, but mild hearing loss may often be overlooked. Expanding routine screening, integrating hearing checks into primary care and improving awareness could help reduce underdiagnosis and prevent progression. For those with severe hearing loss, the financial burden is heavier, as they require costly medical devices like hearing aids, assistive listening devices and cochlear implants. The cost of these devices, along with maintenance and potential upgrades, can strain individuals and their families.36 37 Additionally, the need for specialised services such as speech therapy or rehabilitation programmes further exacerbates the financial strain. This underscores the need for more affordable healthcare solutions and policies to improve accessibility for individuals with hearing impairments. Moreover, WAP often serve as primary caregivers in their households, supporting elderly family members and children. Their hearing loss not only affects their health but also redistributes caregiving responsibilities within the family.38 On a broader scale, hearing loss in WAP is a significant factor in economic development, as it can force workers to exit the labour market, hindering economic growth and adding pressure on social security systems.39 While current health economics analyses focus on direct disease costs, the indirect costs associated with hearing loss often surpass direct medical expenses. Data suggest the economic cost of hearing loss in the USA exceeds US$100 billion, with healthcare costs alone surpassing US$8 billion.9

Based on the above analysis, low-income and middle-income countries should place greater emphasis on preventive measures and improving overall health awareness. This can be achieved by reducing environmental noise, limiting exposure to toxic environments or by training ear and hearing care professionals to promote hearing loss screening and follow-up, thereby improving the prevalence of the disease.40 41 Proper guidance and effective education could help alleviate the significant burden caused by hearing loss. For high-income countries, with more advanced medical facilities, comprehensive healthcare systems and a well-trained healthcare workforce, these nations can reduce the burden by expanding hearing screenings and improving clinical treatments.42 Additionally, these countries typically have longer life expectancies, and the impact of population ageing on YLDs is similar to that of population size. Therefore, these countries should invest more resources to explore ways to address the impact of ageing on the burden of hearing loss.

This study has several limitations. First, it mainly relies on GBD 2021 estimates, which may limit the representativeness of the underlying epidemiological data. As these estimates are derived from modelling rather than direct observation, deviations from actual disease patterns may occur, especially in regions with limited data. Second, despite international standards for hearing loss diagnosis and grading, variations in definitions and assessment methods across countries may affect data comparability. Third, the GBD database provides only broad aetiological categories, making it difficult to distinguish risk factors such as occupational noise exposure, ototoxic drugs or genetic susceptibility. Future research should integrate GBD data with detailed national and cohort-level information to clarify these causes and support targeted prevention strategies.

To sum up, this study offers comprehensive insights into the evolution of the hearing loss burden within the WAP from 1990 to 2021. The continued increase in prevalence and YLDs, coupled with disparities among countries and SDI regions, highlights the enduring management challenges faced by the WAP. We must continue to focus on this vulnerable group, developing targeted strategies based on the disease characteristics of each country, while improving regional healthcare systems to effectively prevent and control hearing loss in the WAP.
