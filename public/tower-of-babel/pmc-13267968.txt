Comments on the Letter “Bridging the Gap: The Critical Next Steps for Implementing Genetic Risk Scores in Myocardial Infarction Prevention in Saudi Arabia”
Authors: Abdullah Saeed
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Dr. Ahmed Abdulaziz Almohammadi provided an excellent commentary on our recent review, “Genetic Predisposition Associated with Myocardial Infarction in the Saudi Population: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis” [1]. We really appreciate his insights. To clarify our position and keep the conversation moving forward, we want to address the four specific issues he raised.

Construction of a Saudi-based polygenic risk score

A Polygenic Risk Score (PRS) made specifically for the Saudi population is absolutely necessary–we completely agree on this. You cannot just rely on scores built from European groups. The Saudi population has a unique ancestral background, along with high rates of consanguinity, which means we need tools calibrated for this specific group [1,2]. To actually build a PRS that works in the clinic to predict the risk of myocardial infarction (MI), we need large genome-wide association studies (GWAS) that represent different Saudi subpopulations.

Clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of PRS implementation

We need prospective studies to see if using PRS to prevent cardiovascular disease actually makes sense financially. This is especially true for adults sitting right on the borderline of risk. Before we can justify rolling this out everywhere, integrating a PRS into everyday clinical practice in Saudi Arabia requires a solid analysis of the health economics involved. Essentially, any new tool has to align directly with our national priorities for healthcare [3].

Counseling on gene–environment interactions

If you look at our review, you will see that genetic risk for MI changes significantly based on outside factors: smoking, high-fat diets, and obesity are major drivers in the Saudi population [1,4]. Because of this, patient counseling has to cover both the genetics they inherit and the lifestyle choices they can actively change. In fact, doctors need to make one thing very clear–having a genetic risk does not mean a heart attack is inevitable. Changing how you live is still the absolute foundation of preventing heart disease.

Pharmacogenomics and therapeutic guidance

Looking ahead, genetic research in Saudi Arabia should naturally expand into pharmacogenomics. We pointed this out in the review: tailoring treatments based on a patient's genes can improve heart health and cut down on bad side effects. This is particularly clear when you look at using CYP2C19 to guide antiplatelet therapy, or targeting PCSK9 inhibitors in patients who are at high risk [5,6].

Conclusion

Dr. Almohammadi provided exactly the kind of constructive feedback that moves this field forward, and we thank him for it. Basically, Saudi Arabia has a clear opportunity to lead the way in precision medicine for the heart. We just need to keep pushing forward with genomic research tailored to our population, developing accurate scoring systems, and giving doctors clear advice for the clinic. To actually turn these research findings into strategies that prevent illness across the Saudi population, geneticists, cardiologists, and public health experts will have to work closely together.
