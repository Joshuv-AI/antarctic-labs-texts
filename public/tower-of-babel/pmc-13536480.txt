Large Language Models Versus Multidisciplinary Tumor Board Decisions in Thyroid Cancer
Authors: Bayram Barış Büyük, Arzu Or Koca, Felat Toprak, Aslı Ayan, Tuncay Tunçcan, Ayça Arslan, ArzuBetül Duran, Elif Akyol Şen, Ömer Semih Budak, Alptekin Gürsoy, Caner Kılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed as clinical decision‐support tools; however, their agreement with real‐world multidisciplinary tumor board (MDT) decisions remains insufficiently investigated in thyroid oncology. To evaluate the concordance between treatment recommendations generated by ChatGPT 5.2 and Gemini 3.0 and decisions made by a tertiary multidisciplinary thyroid tumor board.MethodsThis study included 59 consecutive patients discussed at a tertiary MDT between August and December 2025. Anonymized clinical data, including demographics, ultrasonographic findings, and Bethesda cytology, were provided to both LLMs using standardized structured prompts. MDT decisions were defined as the reference standard. Agreement was assessed using exact concordance rates and Cohen's kappa (κ) statistics with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsChatGPT 5.2 achieved a concordance rate of 71.2% (42/59), demonstrating substantial agreement (κ = 0.623; 95% CI 0.459–0.771). Gemini 3.0 showed a concordance rate of 64.4% (38/59), reflecting moderate agreement (κ = 0.527; 95% CI 0.359–0.684). Discordance increased in complex scenarios involving lateral neck dissection, radioactive iodine therapy, and active surveillance.ConclusionsWhile LLMs demonstrate promising concordance in standardized thyroid cancer management, they are best positioned as supportive decision aids—such as in MDT preparation and workflow streamlining—rather than replacements for expert multidisciplinary evaluation, particularly in complex clinical scenarios.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

The widespread and routine use of advanced imaging techniques has led thyroid nodules and differentiated thyroid cancers to become among the most commonly encountered endocrine pathologies in contemporary clinical practice [1]. In particular, the marked increase in relatively low‐risk cases such as papillary thyroid carcinoma has necessitated more individualized decision‐making processes in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches [2].

Current treatment paradigms aim to strike a delicate balance between oncologic adequacy and avoidance of overtreatment, encompassing surgical strategies such as thyroidectomy with central and lateral lymph node dissection, radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy, and active surveillance [3]. In this context, Multidisciplinary Tumor Boards (MDTs) are widely accepted as the gold standard in the management of thyroid diseases.

Multidisciplinary Tumor Boards are consensus‐based structures integrating expertise from otolaryngology–head and neck surgery, endocrinology and metabolism, and nuclear medicine, combining cytopathological data, imaging findings, and guideline‐based risk stratification. The literature has demonstrated that the MDT approach improves treatment outcomes while reducing morbidity and unnecessary interventions [4, 5].

In recent years, interest in artificial intelligence (AI) applications in healthcare—particularly large language models (LLMs)—has increased substantially [6]. Advanced LLMs such as ChatGPT and Gemini have demonstrated the ability to analyze unstructured clinical text, provide guideline‐based recommendations, and suggest treatment options for complex clinical scenarios [7, 8].

Several studies focusing on thyroid diseases have shown that LLMs can generate recommendations consistent with the American Thyroid Association (ATA) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, particularly in low‐risk and standardized clinical scenarios [3, 5]. However, evidence remains limited regarding model performance in situations with high clinical uncertainty, such as active surveillance, RAI indication, extent of lymph node dissection, and cases with indeterminate cytology.

A major gap in the existing literature is the lack of studies directly comparing LLM recommendations with real‐world MDT decisions. Most available studies rely on theoretical patient scenarios or guideline concordance alone. In real clinical practice, MDT decisions incorporate numerous implicit factors, including clinician experience, patient‐specific characteristics, and multidisciplinary interaction.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the concordance between treatment recommendations generated by ChatGPT 5.2 and Gemini 3.0 and thyroid MDT decisions at a tertiary referral center. Secondary objectives included analyzing the direction of model errors, decision‐specific discordances, and factors predictive of disagreement. Through this approach, the true potential and limitations of LLMs in thyroid cancer management are explored within the framework of responsible use. Unlike prior studies relying on hypothetical or guideline‐driven case vignettes, the present study evaluates LLM performance against real‐world MDT decisions, which inherently integrate tacit clinical judgment, multidisciplinary interaction, and patient‐specific nuances that are rarely captured in standardized datasets.

Materials and Methods

A total of 59 patients discussed at the thyroid MDT between August 2025 and December 2025 were included in the study. The MDT consisted of specialists in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery, endocrinology, and nuclear medicine. Although the pathologist and radiologist were not permanent members of the council, they were available to be consulted when necessary. MDT treatment recommendations were made by consensus after evaluation of clinical findings, laboratory results, ultrasonographic features, cytological classifications, and relevant imaging studies. MDT decisions were considered the reference standard, as they represent the highest level of integrated clinical judgment in contemporary thyroid cancer management, incorporating guideline recommendations alongside patient‐specific and multidisciplinary considerations. Agreement between MDT and LLM recommendations was evaluated using blinded qualitative assessment and bootstrap‐derived confidence intervals for Cohen's κ.

Considering the up‐to‐date knowledge cutoff of the language models, only cases discussed after the publication of the 2025 ATA guidelines (August 2025 onward) were included [3]. Patients with missing clinical, radiological, or cytological data were excluded. No cases were excluded based on treatment outcome or disease stage.

The study was approved by our institutional ethics committee (Approval No. 2026‐05/110). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because only anonymized patient data were used. All patient data were handled confidentially and anonymized before analysis.

Large Language Models and Prompting Strategy

Two large language models were evaluated: ChatGPT 5.2 and Gemini 3.0. Each case was independently presented to both models using identical structured prompts. Based on the provided clinical information, a single primary treatment recommendation was requested (Figures 2 and 3).

The models were not instructed to use any specific guideline or external clinical knowledge. Follow‐up questions, clarifications, or iterative prompting were not allowed. Each model was queried only once per case to simulate real‐world, single‐use clinical decision‐support scenarios.

Assessment of Prediction Quality

Beyond exact concordance, LLM recommendations were qualitatively classified as follows: Completely correct: The LLM recommendation perfectly matched the MDT decision with appropriate clinical rationale (e.g., advising total thyroidectomy and central neck dissection for a Bethesda VI nodule with ultrasonographically suspicious central nodes). Correct but inadequate: The core treatment matched the MDT decision, but the LLM omitted essential complementary steps or lacked sufficient reasoning (e.g., recommending only a total thyroidectomy while overlooking the indication for central neck dissection despite ultrasound findings). Mixed correct and misleading information: The response contained a partially correct approach but included contradictory, inappropriate, or hallucinated clinical steps (e.g., advising total thyroidectomy but unnecessarily recommending prophylactic lateral neck dissection without radiological evidence). Completely incorrect: The recommendation entirely diverged from the MDT consensus, posing a risk of severe overtreatment or undertreatment (e.g., suggesting active surveillance for a locally invasive, large Bethesda VI carcinoma).

For all responses other than “completely correct,” recommendations were further classified as overtreatment or undertreatment.

This evaluation was performed independently by two head and neck surgeons blinded to each other's assessments. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The classification scheme was based on previously published studies [9].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Agreement between MDT decisions and LLM recommendations was evaluated using exact agreement rates and Cohen's kappa (κ) statistics. Cohen's kappa was selected to account for agreement beyond chance in a multicategorical decision framework. Bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals for κ values. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using standard statistical software.

Results

A total of 59 cases were included. The mean age was 49.18 ± 11.08 years. Females comprised 78% (n = 46) of the cohort. ECOG performance status was ECOG 1 in 54.2%, ECOG 0 in 22%, ECOG 2 in 22%, and ECOG 3 in 1.7%. Smoking was reported in 23.7% of patients and alcohol use in 8.5%. No comorbid disease was present in 42.4% of cases. Previous thyroid‐related treatment had not been administered in 66.1% of patients. At presentation, 40.7% of patients were asymptomatic. The most common symptoms were neck swelling (27.1%) and compressive symptoms (11.9%). On ultrasonography, nodules were bilateral in 50.8% of cases. No pathological lymphadenopathy was detected in 71.2%. According to the Bethesda classification, the most common diagnoses were Bethesda VI (32.2%) and Bethesda II (22%).

According to the confusion matrix analysis, both models showed the highest agreement in standard surgical decision categories such as lobectomy and total thyroidectomy. In lobectomy decisions, the agreement of both models with MDT was 75%. In total thyroidectomy decisions, the agreement rate for ChatGPT and Gemini was 73.1%. Discussion

In this study, the treatment recommendations provided by large language models (LLM) such as ChatGPT 5.2 and Gemini 3.0 were compared with real‐world decisions made by a MDT at a tertiary cancer hospital. Our findings show that both language models demonstrated moderate to high agreement with the multidisciplinary council's decisions; however, ChatGPT exhibited a higher overall agreement rate compared to Gemini. Our findings show that while both language models were successful in treatment recommendations clearly stated in the guidelines, they still lag behind human expertise in confusing issues such as active surveillance and the scope of neck dissection.

Studies examining the relationship between thyroid diseases and large language models are increasing in the literature [9, 10]. Recently, White et al. evaluated the performance of a large language model in thyroid cancer multidisciplinary tumor board (MDT) decision‐making and reported encouraging agreement with MDT recommendations [11]. However, that study was presented as a conference abstract with limited methodological detail. More broadly, most published studies have relied on hypothetical clinical scenarios or guideline concordance rather than consecutive real‐world MDT cases. In contrast, the present study evaluates two contemporary LLMs using consecutive patients discussed at a tertiary thyroid MDT, standardized prompting, predefined qualitative assessment criteria, and agreement analysis using Cohen's κ with bootstrap confidence intervals. By benchmarking LLM recommendations against real‐world multidisciplinary decisions that incorporate patient‐specific characteristics, radiological nuances, and expert clinical judgment, our findings provide a more clinically applicable assessment of the current capabilities and limitations of LLMs in thyroid cancer management.

Our study found that ChatGPT 5.2 (71.2%) showed a higher rate of agreement with MDT decisions compared to Gemini 3.0 (64.4%). This can be explained by the higher density of medical text in ChatGPT's training dataset and its more advanced ability to synthesize complex clinical scenarios [12]. Some recommendations, while categorically consistent with MDT decisions, were downgraded in the qualitative assessment due to insufficient or partially misleading reasoning; this is evidenced by the “exact match” rate, which is higher than the “completely correct” rate.

In terms of surgical planning, the highest concordance rates for both language models were observed in the lobectomy and total thyroidectomy categories. Particularly in scenarios where cytological diagnosis is clear, such as Bethesda VI, and strictly defined in guidelines, the models' algorithmic decision‐making processes align with the tumor board in a manner consistent with the literature [13]. ChatGPT's complete agreement in cases requiring central and lateral neck dissection along with total thyroidectomy suggests that LLMs can recommend appropriately aggressive surgical approaches when high‐risk features are clearly defined. It is important to note that the clearly stated risk factors are similarly included in the guidelines. However, one of the original findings of our study is that the Gemini model struggled with combined surgical procedures, particularly with total thyroidectomy combined with central lymph node dissection, where its agreement with the tumor board dropped to around 50%. Conversely, the model achieved 83.3% concordance in cases involving lateral neck dissection. This discrepancy may be attributed to the routine use of preoperative biopsies for lateral lymph nodes, which provides definitive text‐based data for the LLM. Even when central compartment lymph nodes are not suitable for biopsy, clinicians frequently decide to perform central dissection based on their visual experience with ultrasound features—such as node location, size, echogenicity, and the presence of microcalcifications. Therefore, the inability of text‐based prompts to fully capture the nuanced visual insight provided by radiological imaging remains a critical factor limiting the models' success in these specific scenarios [14].

Both models showed lower and heterogeneous compliance with MDT decisions in categories such as isolated lateral neck dissection, Radioactive Iodine (RAI) therapy, repeat biopsy, and follow‐up. In these categories, high clinical uncertainty due to cytological ambiguities, borderline imaging findings, patient comorbidities, patient preferences, and variable risk classification approaches may have made decision‐making difficult for large language models. It is noteworthy that most of the misclassifications observed in these categories were toward more conservative and alternative treatment approaches. This can be explained by a natural risk‐averse behavior of large language models, reflecting a tendency to lean toward less invasive and monitoring‐focused recommendations in the face of uncertainty. Although language models exhibiting avoidance behavior to avoid incorrect answers have been shown in the literature before, these versions suggest that the “personalized and morbidity‐reducing approach” emphasized in the 2025 ATA guidelines is beginning to be internalized by artificial intelligence models [15]. Clinicians should bear in mind that, in these versions, the models may make recommendations that lead to inadequate treatment in an attempt to prevent errors.

From a clinical perspective, it seems more appropriate to position large language models such as ChatGPT 5.2 and Gemini 3.0 as supportive decision aids rather than autonomous decision‐makers in thyroid cancer management. Here, it would be more appropriate to position them as a decision support mechanism to accelerate the council preparation process and address overlooked details in decisions.

Our study has some limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, retrospective design, and single‐center nature may limit generalizability. Another limitation is that visual data was not provided to the large language models. Furthermore, the data was obtained through one‐time, non‐iterative prompts, whereas in real‐world use, repeated interactive queries may occur. However, these limitations reflect current real‐world constraints of LLM deployment in clinical practice and therefore enhance, rather than diminish, the external validity of our findings. The strengths of our study include the following: the multidisciplinary tumor board, which consists of experienced and permanent members in their respective fields; the performance of neck ultrasonography and lymph node mapping by a single experienced physician; the invitation of patients to the board to ensure their active participation in the decision‐making process; and the use of data based on real patient experiences. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first in the literature to compare treatment recommendations from large language models with MDT decisions based on real‐world data.

Future studies conducted with multicenter and larger patient cohorts, the evaluation of iterative prompting strategies, and the transition to hybrid decision support systems integrated with imaging‐based models may support the development of models more suitable for clinical use.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while large language models show strong potential as valuable decision‐support tools and auxiliary aids for streamlining MDT preparation, they should not replace expert multidisciplinary evaluation. MDTs integrate nuanced clinical intuition, real‐time interdisciplinary interaction, and patient‐centered values that current LLMs cannot replicate. Consequently, LLMs are best utilized as supportive adjuncts to optimize workflow and reduce cognitive workload, while definitive treatment decisions must strictly remain under expert MDT oversight.
