Effects of an Ultrasound‑Guided Femoral, Sciatic, and Genitofemoral Nerve Block with Ropivacaine on EC50 and EC95 for High Saphenous Vein Ligation
Authors: Hui Wang, Jiangtao Chang, Yufei Li, Lina Chen, Changjun Chen, Qiuru Wang, Xiaojun Gao, Fan Yang, Songsong Chen, Yuelan Wang, Jianbo Wu, Guanghan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine the effective concentrations (EC50 and EC95) of ropivacaine for an ultrasound-guided triple nerve block (femoral(FNB), suprapopliteal sciatic(SPSB), and genitofemoral nerve femoral branch(GFNfB)) in patients undergoing high saphenous vein ligation and stripping.MethodsThirty-six patients scheduled for elective unilateral high saphenous vein ligation and stripping were enrolled. Nerve blocks were performed under ultrasound guidance. The effective concentration of ropivacaine was determined using the Dixon–Massey up-and-down sequential allocation method (initial concentration: 0.25%; step size: 0.01%). EC50 and EC95 with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using probit analysis. Hemodynamic parameters (systolic blood pressure(SBP), diastolic blood pressure(DBP), heart rate (HR)) were recorded at four time points. Pain intensity (visual analog scale, VAS) and motor block (modified Bromage score) were assessed at skin incision. Postoperative complications were recorded.ResultsRelative to baseline, hemodynamic parameters showed small but statistically significant decreases at 15 and 30 minutes after block (all P < 0.05), remaining within clinically acceptable limits. At skin incision, the VAS score in the Success group (n = 23) was significantly lower than in the Failure group (n = 13) [median (IQR), 1 (0, 2) vs 5 (4, 5); z = −5.000, P < 0.001], and the modified Bromage score was significantly higher [1 (0, 1) vs 0 (0, 0); z = −3.062, P = 0.002]. The EC50 of ropivacaine was 0.149% (95% CI, 0.118–0.165%) and the EC95 was 0.210% (95% CI, 0.181–0.509%). No patient experienced local anesthetic systemic toxicity or other complications.ConclusionThis ultrasound-guided triple nerve block regimen is safe and effective for high saphenous vein ligation. The EC50 and EC95 of ropivacaine were 0.149% and 0.210%, respectively. An initial concentration of approximately 0.22% is suggested, with subsequent adjustment guided by individual response.

Varicose veins represent a common condition in vascular surgery and are among the most prevalent chronic venous disorders for which surgery remains the primary treatment. According to a recent epidemiological study from Brazil, the annual surgical rate for varicose veins is approximately 4.52 per 10,000 individuals.1 Large-sample epidemiological studies on this surgery are relatively scarce in China. In clinical practice, epidural anesthesia is a commonly used anesthetic technique for such procedures.2 However, this approach has significant limitations, particularly in elderly patients with cardiovascular comorbidities or those on anticoagulant therapy, as it is associated with increased risks of complications such as epidural hematoma, hemodynamic instability, and urinary retention.3

Anatomically, the surgical area of high ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein involves primarily regions innervated by the femoral nerve, the sciatic nerve, and the genitofemoral nerve. The genitofemoral nerve emerges from the medial border of the psoas major muscle, descends retroperitoneally along its surface, and divides into genital and femoral branches above the inguinal ligament.4 The saphenous hiatus area is innervated by the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve.5 Although traditional combined femoral and sciatic nerve blocks cover the main surgical area, they often overlook this critical region,6 frequently necessitating supplemental local infiltration anesthesia or deeper sedation to facilitate surgery, which can compromise anesthetic quality and patient experience.7

In recent years, significant advances in ultrasound-guided nerve blockade have greatly enhanced the precision of analgesia for lower limb surgery. Compared with neuraxial anesthesia, this technique effectively meets intraoperative analgesic requirements while significantly reducing the incidence of related complications, thereby improving perioperative safety. Ropivacaine, with its characteristic sensory‒motor dissociation, has become an ideal agent for lower extremity nerve blocks. However, in elderly patients or those with multiple comorbidities, the risk of neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity from excessive ropivacaine use cannot be overlooked. Therefore, optimizing the concentration of ropivacaine for great saphenous vein surgery is of paramount importance. Nevertheless, high-quality studies on comprehensive nerve block techniques for high ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein are lacking.8 This study innovatively proposes and implements a triple nerve block strategy—femoral nerve block (FNB), supra-popliteal sciatic nerve block (SPSB), and genitofemoral nerve femoral branch block (GFNfB)—for high ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein. The effective concentration of ropivacaine, including the median effective concentration (EC50) and the 95% effective concentration (EC95), was further investigated to achieve complete intraoperative analgesia. A key innovation is the precise inclusion and blockade of the genitofemoral nerve femoral branch, successfully addressing the analgesic gap in the saphenous opening region inherent to traditional blocks. Pharmacologically, this study employs the Dixon–Massey up-and-down sequential method to accurately determine the EC50 and EC95 of ropivacaine for this combined block, providing robust evidence for achieving complete surgical analgesia with low concentrations of ropivacaine.9 This research offers a safer, more precise, and enhanced recovery-oriented anesthetic strategy for patients who have undergone varicose vein surgery, particularly those who require anticoagulation therapy or who are of advanced age.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Design

This prospective, single-center, dose-finding study enrolled patients who were scheduled for elective unilateral high ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University between March and October 2021. Patients were enrolled consecutively on the basis of the surgical schedule. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. [YXLL-KY-2020(065)], and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000039897) after data collection but prior to analysis, in accordance with journal policy. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) a diagnosis of lower extremity varicose veins according to the guidelines for the management of varicose veins of the lower extremities,10 scheduled for first-time surgery; 2) aged 45–75 years; 3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; 4) anticipated surgery duration of 1–3 hours; and 5) voluntary participation with signed informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) known allergy to amide-type local anesthetics; 2) skin infection at the puncture site; 3) severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal insufficiency (eg, NYHA class III–IV heart failure, COPD GOLD stage 3–4, Child‒Pugh class B/C cirrhosis, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <30 mL/min/1.73 m2); 4) long-term use of psychotropic drugs or history of significant psychiatric illness; 5) history of alcohol abuse or drug dependence; 6) history of postoperative cognitive dysfunction, organic brain disease, or cerebrovascular accident; and 7) conditions preventing cooperation with assessments, such as severe visual/hearing impairment, language barriers, severe dementia, or coma.

Anesthesia Protocol

Nerve Block Technique and Ultrasound Guidance

All patients adhered to a standard preoperative fasting protocol. Upon arrival in the operating room, routine monitoring was initiated, including noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), electrocardiography (ECG), and pulse oximetry (SpO2). For patients with significant cardiopulmonary or cerebrovascular comorbidities, invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring was employed. An intravenous line was secured, and Lactated Ringer’s solution was infused at a rate of 7 mL/kg/h. Supplemental oxygen was administered via face mask at 3 L/min. Sedation was provided with intravenous midazolam (0.03 mg/kg) administered five minutes prior to the nerve block procedure.11 All blocks were performed under strict aseptic conditions using a portable ultrasound system equipped with a high-frequency linear transducer (6–13 MHz). Patients were positioned supine, and an in‑plane needle approach was consistently used. All procedures were conducted by a single experienced anaesthesiologist (Dr. Wu Guanghan), who had over five years of expertise in ultrasound‑guided regional anaesthesia. Postoperative assessments, including pain scoring and evaluation of block‑related complications, were carried out by an independent trained anaesthesiologist who remained blinded to both the block procedure and the concentration of local anaesthetic administered.

Nerve Block Procedures

Femoral nerve block (FNB) (Figure 1A and B): The transducer was placed transversely at the level of the inguinal ligament to identify the femoral artery and the lateral, hyperechoic, triangular-to-oval femoral nerve. The needle was advanced laterally to medially, piercing the iliac fascia to reach the vicinity of the femoral nerve. After negative blood aspiration, a total volume of 0.16 mL/kg of the predetermined ropivacaine concentration was injected around the nerve (above and below), ensuring adequate circumferential spread. Suprapopliteal sciatic nerve block (SPSB) (Figure 1C and D): The knee on the operative side was flexed approximately 45°. The transducer was placed 6–8 cm cranial to the popliteal crease to identify the popliteal artery. The sciatic nerve, which appeared as an oval hyperechoic structure, was located 1–2 cm lateral to the artery. The needle was advanced laterally to medially toward the sciatic nerve. After negative aspiration, 0.24 mL/kg of the predetermined ropivacaine concentration was injected.

Genitofemoral Nerve Femoral Branch Block (GFNfB) (Figure 1E and F): Keeping the transducer in the inguinal region, the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve was identified as a small hyperechoic dot or stripe within the superficial connective tissue between the fascia iliaca and fascia lata lateral to the external iliac/femoral artery. If the nerve was not clearly visualized, local anesthetic was deposited into this fascial plane. The needle was advanced laterally to medially to the target. After negative aspiration, 0.08 mL/kg of the predetermined ropivacaine concentration was injected.

Determination of Ropivacaine Effective Concentrations (Dixon–Massey Up-and-Down Method)

The effective concentration of ropivacaine for triple nerve block (FNB + GFNfB + SPSB) was determined using the Dixon–Massey up-and-down sequential method.

Starting concentration: The first patient received 0.25% ropivacaine (FNB: 0.16 mL/kg; GFNfB: 0.08 mL/kg; SPSB: 0.24 mL/kg).

Concentration adjustment rule: Based on the blocking outcome (success/failure) of the previous patient, the ropivacaine concentration for the next patient was adjusted upward or downward by 0.01%. The study continued until approximately seven crossover events (from success to failure or vice versa) were observed, meeting the requirements for Dixon–Massey analysis.

Assessment of Block Efficacy: At 15 minutes after the last nerve block (at the skin incision), analgesic efficacy was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS, 0–10, where 0=no pain and 10=worst pain imaginable). Motor block was assessed simultaneously using the modified Bromage scale (0–3: 0=no block; 1=unable to flex the hip, able to move the knee and ankle; 2=unable to flex the hip and knee, able to move the ankle; 3=unable to move the hip, knee, or ankle).

Response Criteria

Success: VAS score ≤ 3 and modified Bromage score > 0, with satisfactory surgical conditions requiring no supplemental sedation/analgesia. The concentration for the next patient decreased by 0.01%.

Failure (Inadequate Analgesia): VAS score >3 but ≤6 and modified Bromage score > 0 (inadequate analgesia despite motor block). The concentration for the next patient was increased by 0.01%.

Block failure was defined as a VAS score > 6 or a modified Bromage score = 0 (no motor block). This case was considered a technical failure and was excluded from the effective concentration calculation, and the same concentration was repeated for the next patient.

Intraoperative Management: In cases with “Failure (Inadequate Analgesia)” or “Block Failure”, intravenous fentanyl (or other short-acting opioids) and/or propofol were administered as needed to complete the surgery. Hemodynamics were closely monitored. Significant deviations (eg, a decrease in systolic blood pressure >20% from baseline or <90 mmHg and a heart rate >100 bpm or <50 bpm) were managed with a fluid bolus or vasoactive drugs (eg, ephedrine, atropine, and phenylephrine) to maintain stability.

Observation Metrics

Hemodynamic parameters, such as systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate (HR), were recorded at the following time points: T0 (before block, baseline), T1 (immediately after the last block), T2 (15 minutes after the last block, at skin incision), and T3 (30 minutes after the last block).

Block quality indicators (assessed at T2).

Analgesic effect: VAS score.

Motor block degree: Modified Bromage score.

Overall anesthetic efficacy (Intraoperative): Assessed by an anesthesiologist not involved in the block performance or data collection:

Excellent: No pain reported by the patient; no supplemental sedative/analgesic drugs were needed.

Good: mild pain or movement; required small doses of supplemental drugs (eg, fentanyl ≤50 µg or equivalent).

Poor: significant pain or movement; required large doses of supplemental drugs (eg, fentanyl >50 µg or equivalent) or conversion to general anesthesia.

Safety Outcomes

The following safety parameters were monitored: local anesthetic systemic toxicity (signs/symptoms such as tinnitus, perioral numbness, metallic taste, muscle twitching, seizures, and circulatory collapse), postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within 48 hours, postoperative headache, and neurological complications (eg, persistent sensory abnormalities or motor deficits in the blocked area lasting beyond the expected block duration or 24 hours post-operatively).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD and compared with the independent-samples t test; non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are summarized as n (%) and compared using the χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The EC50 and EC95 of ropivacaine and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated by probit regression of the binary block response (Success/Failure) against the natural logarithm of ropivacaine concentration; model adequacy was checked using the Pearson goodness-of-fit test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Determination of the Effective Ropivacaine Concentration

The relationship between ropivacaine concentration and the probability of a successful block was modelled by probit regression: probit(P) = 9.135 + 4.796 × ln(concentration, %). The Pearson goodness-of-fit test was non-significant (P = 0.443), supporting adequate model fit. For the triple nerve block (FNB + GFNfB + SPSB) used during high ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein, the EC50 of ropivacaine was 0.149% (95% CI, 0.118–0.165%) and the EC95 was 0.210% (95% CI, 0.181–0.509%). Safety and Adverse Events

None of the 36 patients experienced local anesthetic systemic toxicity (eg, tinnitus, perioral numbness, seizures, circulatory collapse), postoperative nausea or vomiting within 48 hours, or headache. An immediate postoperative assessment confirmed that motor strength in the operated limb had recovered to a modified Bromage score of ≥ 2 (able to ankle flexion) in all patients. During postoperative follow-up (eg, at 24 hours, 48 hours, or before discharge), no symptoms of persistent neurological injury related to nerve blocks (such as paresthesia or motor deficits) were reported.

Discussion

The surgical area involved in high ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein is primarily innervated by the femoral and sciatic nerves. Ultrasound-guided femoral nerve block (FNB) and sciatic nerve block (SNB) have been widely used for intraoperative analgesia in orthopedic surgery, such as lower limb long bone fractures and total knee arthroplasty.12–14 However, its application in High Ligation and Stripping of the Great Saphenous Vein is rare. Our clinical experience indicates that the conventional FNB combined with SNB approach often leaves a critical analgesic gap—the saphenous opening region, which is the key site for high ligation. This area is mainly innervated by the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve (GFNfB).15 Inadequate blockade of this nerve frequently results in insufficient analgesia during dissection in this region, often necessitating supplemental intravenous analgesia or local infiltration, thereby compromising anesthetic quality and procedural fluency.

This study innovatively proposed and implemented a triple nerve block strategy combining FNB, suprapopliteal sciatic nerve block (SPSB), and GFNfB. Its primary anatomical significance lies in the systematic inclusion of the GFNfB as a target nerve, thereby precisely covering the saphenous opening region often missed by traditional blocks and addressing the analgesic challenge for this critical surgical step at its neuroanatomical source. Ropivacaine was selected because of its favorable sensory‒motor dissociation profile and relatively lower neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity, making it an ideal agent for lower extremity nerve blocks.16 Optimizing its concentration is particularly crucial for elderly patients with multiple comorbidities and reduced physiological reserves to balance effective analgesia and safety margins. Using the well-established Dixon–Massey up-and-down method for dose‒response relationship studies,17 we accurately determined the effective concentration of ropivacaine for this triple block: the EC50 was 0.149% (95% CI: 0.118–0.165%), and the EC95 was 0.210% (95% CI: 0.181–0.509%). These results provide crucial evidence-based guidance for clinical dosing, filling a gap in the literature regarding ropivacaine concentrations for this specific nerve block combination.

Our EC50 and EC95 for ropivacaine in triple-nerve block align closely with prior institutional studies. Xu et al reported EC50 0.113–0.142% for sciatic block alone,18 slightly lower than ours—likely due to broader nerve coverage enabling lower per-nerve concentration. Yao et al found EC50/EC95 of 0.158%/0.198% for femoral-sciatic block and confirmed 0.20% provides optimal analgesia with preserved motor function;19 our EC95 (0.210%) matches this validated threshold. In contrast, single-nerve or low-volume blocks yield higher effective concentrations. Rey Moura et al reported MEC50/MEC90 of 0.360%/0.477% for saphenous block with only 10 mL;20 Xie et al found epidural EC50 0.399–0.487% in elderly patients.21 These differences reflect three key factors: (i) single-nerve blockade requires higher concentration to compensate for unblocked adjacent nerves; (ii) smaller injection volume limits circumferential nerve spread; (iii) epidural delivery needs higher concentrations than peripheral targeting for segmental effect. Together, these data confirm that 0.15–0.21% ropivacaine is both necessary and sufficient for multi-nerve lower-limb blocks. Our study uniquely defines the precise effective concentration when GFNfB is added—enabling full surgical analgesia for great saphenous vein surgery while keeping total dose within safety limits.

This triple block scheme offers distinct advantages over traditional anesthetic techniques, aligning well with the principles of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) and the management of high-risk patients.22 Compared with neuraxial anesthesia, the triple block acts peripherally on the operative side, avoiding extensive sympathetic blockade. Consequently, it causes minimal hemodynamic disturbance (as confirmed by the stable parameters in this study), significantly reducing the risk of circulatory fluctuations in patients with cardiovascular disease. More importantly, it eliminates the risk of epidural hematoma associated with neuraxial techniques—a critical consideration for patients on anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy—and avoids common complications such as post-operative urinary retention and headache.3 Compared with general anesthesia, nerve blocks avoid endotracheal intubation, reduce airway manipulation and associated complications, and lower the risk of postoperative pulmonary issues. They allow for earlier oral intake, avoiding prolonged fasting and reducing the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), thereby increasing patient comfort and recovery speed.23,24 In patients with severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency, this approach circumvents potential circulatory depression from general anesthetics and the need for ICU admission, potentially shortening hospital stays and reducing costs.25,26

Furthermore, this block protocol is highly consistent with ERAS principles. The use of low concentrations of ropivacaine (close to the EC50 and EC95) effectively achieved sensory‒motor dissociation. Postoperative motor function recovered promptly (all patients achieved a modified Bromage score ≥2 immediately after surgery), facilitating early ambulation. This not only improves patient satisfaction but also crucially reduces the risk of deep vein thrombosis, a key concern in venous surgery patients.27 In addition, low effective concentrations minimize the risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity in elderly or frail patients.28 The favorable sensory‒motor separation profile meets surgical analgesic requirements while enabling early limb functional exercise, contributing to satisfactory recovery. Notably, no adverse events, such as local anesthetic systemic toxicity, PONV, headache, or neurological injury, occurred, underscoring the safety of this approach.

This study has several limitations. First, and most importantly, we used the Dixon–Massey up-and-down method, which is designed primarily to estimate the median effective concentration (EC50); estimates of higher quantiles such as the EC95 derived by extrapolation from this design are known to carry substantial bias and wide confidence limits. This is reflected in our EC95 point estimate of 0.210% and its broad 95% CI (0.181–0.509%). The biased-coin design (BCD) combined with isotonic regression, which concentrates allocations near the target quantile, is now generally preferred for directly and more precisely estimating the MEC90/EC95, and our EC95 should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Second, this was a single-center study with a modest sample size (n = 36), which further limits the precision of the higher-quantile estimate and the generalizability of the findings. The wide CI may additionally reflect interindividual pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic variability in an older population, as well as anatomical differences affecting local anesthetic spread. Future adequately powered, multicenter studies employing a BCD with isotonic regression—or population pharmacodynamic modeling—are needed to refine the EC95 and to confirm the recommended starting concentration. Notwithstanding these caveats, the EC50 estimate is robust, and together with the EC95 point estimate it offers a practical reference for selecting an initial concentration and individualizing therapy. The 13 cases classified as “Failure” (defined as a VAS >3 but ≤6 at the incision) were primarily due to the ropivacaine concentration being below the individual’s effective threshold and requiring supplemental medication. Notably, while the slight decreases in HR, SBP, and DBP at T2 and T3 compared with those at T0 were statistically significant, the magnitudes were clinically acceptable and did not require pharmacological intervention. The baseline (T0) values may partly reflect preoperative anxiety, and the subsequent stabilization could be a reasonable physiological response to block onset and mild sedation, further highlighting the minimal circulatory impact of the nerve blocks.

In conclusion, this study determined the EC50 (0.149%) and EC95 (0.210%) of ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided triple nerve block (femoral, sciatic, and genitofemoral nerve femoral branch) in high ligation of the great saphenous vein. These low effective concentrations enable sensory–motor dissociation and hemodynamic stability, offering a safe and practical dosing reference, particularly for elderly patients or those with contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia. Further validation in larger, diverse populations is warranted.
