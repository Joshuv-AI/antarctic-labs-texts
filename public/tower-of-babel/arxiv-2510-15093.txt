Fast spectral separation method for kinetic equation with anisotropic non-stationary collision operator retaining micro-model fidelity
Yue Zhao, Huan Lei

Abstract
In this paper, we present a generalized, data-driven collisional operator for one-component plasmas, learned from molecular dynamics simulations, to extend the collisional kinetic model beyond the weakly coupled regime. The proposed operator features an anisotropic, non-stationary collision kernel that accounts for particle correlations typically neglected in classical Landau formulations. To enable efficient numerical evaluation, we develop a fast spectral separation method that represents the kernel as a low-rank tensor product of univariate basis functions. This formulation admits an 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) \mathcal{O}(N\log N) algorithm via fast Fourier transforms and preserves key physical properties, including discrete conservation laws and the H-theorem, through a structure-preserving central difference discretization. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed model accurately captures plasma dynamics in the moderately coupled regime beyond the standard Landau model while maintaining high computational efficiency and structure-preserving properties.



1
Introduction

Collisional kinetic theory provides an effective computational framework for modeling dynamical processes that bridge microscopic particle interactions with macroscopic transport phenomena in fields such as plasma physics, gas dynamics, and astrophysics. Besides the Vlasov term, the governing kinetic equation includes a collision operator that captures the unresolved particle interactions beyond the mean-field approximation. A widely adopted choice is the Landau collision operator
[
23
]
, which models binary particle collisions under long-range (e.g., Coulomb) interactions through a three-dimensional integro-differential equation. It can be formally derived as a limiting case of more detailed models, such as the five-dimensional Boltzmann collision operator
[
5
,
34
]
in the grazing collision limit, or the seven-dimensional Balescu–Lenard operator
[
27
,
2
]
under the assumption of an isotropic dielectric response. The Landau operator provides an accurate description of plasma kinetics in the weakly coupled regime and strictly preserves conservation laws as well as the H-theorem.

The numerical solution of the Landau equation is challenging due to its intrinsic physical structure, high-dimensional integral formulation, and nonlinear stiffness. Substantial effort has been devoted to developing efficient and accurate numerical methods for this equation. Positivity-preserving schemes have been developed for both the linearized Fokker-Planck equation
[
10
]
and the full nonlinear form
[
24
]
. The entropy-based schemes proposed in
[
6
,
11
]
are designed to rigorously preserve key physical quantities while ensuring non-negative entropy dissipation.
However, direct implementation of these schemes can be computationally expensive due to the high-dimensional integral form of the collision operator. To address this, several fast evaluation techniques have been developed. These include the multigrid algorithms
[
7
]
, multipole expansions
[
25
]
, the Fourier spectral method
[
30
,
15
,
35
]
that exploit the convolution structure of the Landau kernel, the particle-based method
[
8
]
with regularized entropy functional, as well as the approaches
[
9
,
33
]
utilizing the Rosenbluth formulation
[
32
]
via Poisson solvers.
A recent work developed a Hermite spectral method that efficiently evaluates the spectral coefficients for the quadratic collision terms. To handle the stiffness arising from the nonlinear structure of the equation, implicit schemes
[
13
,
9
,
26
,
33
]
and the asymptotic-preserving approaches
[
14
,
19
]
have been proposed to accelerate the convergence and improve stability in regimes with disparate temporal scales.

Despite the significant theoretical and computational advances, the applicability of the Landau collisional operator remains limited to the weakly coupled regime, where the ratio between the kinetic and potential energy is much larger than one. Due to the assumption of small-angle scattering and uncorrelated particle interactions, the operator exhibits limitations in modeling the kinetic processes in regimes where particle correlations become non-negligible. This limitation poses severe challenges for realistic applications such as inertial confinement fusion
[
31
,
1
]
and photon scattering
[
12
]
that span a broader range of physical regimes. To overcome these challenges, we proposed a generalized data-driven collision operator directly learned from the microscale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. In contrast to the Landau form with an isotropic and stationary collision kernel, the proposed collision operator takes a generalized metriplectic structure
[
29
]
with an anisotropic kernel. In particular, the kernel takes a non-stationary form that depends not only on the relative velocity but also on the average velocity of the colliding pair. This generalized formulation strictly preserves the frame-indifference and various physical constraints. Moreover, it enables us to faithfully account for the heterogeneous energy transfer in the plane perpendicular to the relative velocity. This heterogeneity effect arises from the collective interactions between the pair of collision particles and the surrounding environment, which have been broadly overlooked in most existing forms but prove crucial for capturing the plasma kinetics beyond the weakly coupled regime, where the Landau model shows limitations.

While the proposed generalized operator significantly extends the modeling capability of collisional kinetics, one remaining challenge is the efficient evaluation of the new collision operator. Due to the anisotropic and non-stationary form of the kernel, the fast approximation based on the convolution structure or the Rosenbluth formulation can not be directly applied; direct evaluation requires
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
computational complexity, where
N
denotes the number of velocity grid points. In this study, we aim to fill the gap by developing an efficient approach based on the fast spectral separation (SS) method. The main idea is to seek a spectral representation of the anisotropic collision kernel as the low-rank tensor product of a set of univariate basis functions. Each basis function depends on a scalar argument, such as the magnitude of the velocity of one of the colliding particles or their relative velocity.
This tensor representation strictly preserves the symmetry constraints and enables spectral separation of the individual and relative velocity dependencies. By embedding the kernel representation into the bilinear metriplectic form of the collision operator, we demonstrate that each integral term inherits a convolution structure, and therefore achieves
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
computational complexity via the fast Fourier transform (FFT) method. The tensor representation in the form of the univariate basis can be directly learned from the MD data, allowing the collision model to faithfully capture the effects of micro-scale particle correlations beyond the Landau model. Meanwhile, the constructed model strictly satisfies the conservation laws and the H-theorem, which enables the development of structure-preserving numerical schemes for the present collisional kinetic equation following the essential ideas for the Landau model. Numerical results show that the present model improves the Landau equation in comparison with full MD simulation results, and achieves efficient computational complexity with structure-preserving numerical solution.

2
Methods

The collisional Vlasov-Poisson equation takes a general form as

\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(\bm{z},t)+\bm{v}\cdot\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}-\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial\bm{x}}(\bm{x};f)\cdot\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{v}}=\left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right|_{c},

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{6}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the single-particle probability density function in the phase space and
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},\bm{v})
represents a phase space point with position
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
\phi(\bm{x};f)=\int V(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})f(\bm{z}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{z}^{\prime}
is the mean field potential energy with pair potential
V(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
.
The collision term
\left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right|_{c}
represents the unresolved particle interactions beyond the mean field approximation. In this work, we focus on the spatially homogeneous scenario and abusively use
f(\bm{v},t)
to denote the velocity distribution function for the remainder of this work; the kinetic equation reduces to

\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(\bm{v},t)=\left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right|_{c}.

(2)

In principle, the collision term can be derived from the Liouville equations with the BBGKY hierarchy
[
21
,
22
,
4
,
3
]
. However, the derived form is generally unclosed and depends on the higher-order correlation functions. In practice, various empirical closed forms
C[f]
, such as the Landau, BGK, and Boltzmann collision operators, are often introduced based on different heuristic approximations. In this work, we choose a different pathway and aim to construct a generalized collision operator directly from the micro-scale MD data.

2.1
Generalized anisotropic and non-stationary collision operator

To capture the anisotropic nature of energy transfer in the velocity space, we represent the generalized collision operator with the metriplectic structure
[
29
]
in the form of

\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(\bm{v},t)=\nabla\cdot\int\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\nabla f(\bm{v})-f(\bm{v})\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},

(3)

where
\bm{\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{3}_{++}
is a collision kernel representing the unresolved particle interactions. As shown later,
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})
takes a generalized anisotropic non-stationary structure and differs from the stationary form
\bm{\omega}(\bm{u})
where
\bm{u}=\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime}
. As a special case, by choosing
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\propto|\bm{u}|^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{P}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}=(\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{T}/|\bm{u}|^{2})
, the operator recovers the standard Landau model with an isotropic energy transfer (
\propto|\bm{u}|^{-1}
) in the plane perpendicular to
\bm{u}
.

To construct a generalized form, the collision kernel
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})
needs to satisfy certain properties such that the collision operator in the form of Eq. (
3
) satisfies the frame-indifference and physical constraints.

Proposition 1
.

Let the collision kernel
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})
be a symmetric positive semi-definite kernel and satisfy the following properties: permutation invariance, rotational symmetry, and orthogonality to the relative velocity
\bm{u}=\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}(\mathcal{U}\bm{v},\mathcal{U}\bm{v}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathcal{U}^{T},~\mathcal{U}\in{\rm SO}(3)

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}^{\prime},\bm{v})

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})(\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

Then the kinetic model (
3
) strictly conserves the mass, momentum, and energy, preserves the frame indifference, and satisfies the H-theorem.

Proof.

The total mass, momentum and kinetic energy are

m=\int f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},~\bm{p}=\int\bm{v}f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},~K=\frac{1}{2}\int\bm{v}^{2}f\mathrm{d}\bm{v}.

(5)

For any physical quantity
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\phi(\bm{v})f\mathrm{d}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\int\phi(\bm{v})\left(\nabla\cdot\int\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\nabla f(\bm{v})-f(\bm{v})\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{v}

(6)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\iint\left(\nabla_{\bm{v}}\phi(\bm{v})-\nabla_{\bm{v}^{\prime}}\phi(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right)\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\nabla f(\bm{v})-f(\bm{v})\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\bm{v},

so the quantities
(m,\bm{p},K)
are conserved.
The entropy of the system and its evolution are

\displaystyle S(f)

\displaystyle=-\int f\log f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},

(7)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\iint\bm{B}^{T}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\bm{B}f(\bm{v})f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}\geq 0,

where
\bm{B}=\nabla_{\bm{v}}\log f(\bm{v})-\nabla_{\bm{v}^{\prime}}\log f(\bm{v}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, it ensures a non-negative solution and admits the Maxwellian distribution as the equilibrium state. In addition, we can show that the collision kernel needs to satisfy the symmetry condition
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})=\bm{\omega}(-\bm{v},-\bm{v}^{\prime})
.
∎

Based on Proposition
1
, we propose a generalized anisotropic and non-stationary collision kernel satisfying Eq. (
4
) in the form of

\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})=\bm{\mathcal{P}}\left(g_{r}^{2}\widetilde{\bm{r}}\widetilde{\bm{r}}^{T}+g_{s}^{2}\widetilde{\bm{s}}\widetilde{\bm{s}}^{T}\right)\bm{\mathcal{P}},

(8)

where
\bm{r}=\bm{v}+\bm{v}^{\prime}-2\bar{\bm{v}}
,
\bm{s}=\bm{u}\times\bm{r}
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{T}/|\bm{u}|^{2}
.
\bar{\bm{v}}=m^{-1}\bm{p}
is the average velocity. In this work, we assume
\bar{\bm{v}}\equiv 0
for simplicity. Furthermore, we denote
\widetilde{\bm{u}}=\bm{u}/|\bm{u}|
,
\widetilde{\bm{r}}=\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{r}/|\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{r}|
and
\widetilde{\bm{s}}=\bm{s}/|\bm{s}|
as mutually orthogonal unit vectors, satisfying
\widetilde{\bm{u}}\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{T}+\widetilde{\bm{r}}\widetilde{\bm{r}}^{T}+\widetilde{\bm{s}}\widetilde{\bm{s}}^{T}=\bm{I}
. The functions
g_{r}
and
g_{s}
represent the magnitude of the energy transfer along
\widetilde{\bm{r}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{s}}
directions. They should be rotational invariant and therefore constructed as
g_{r}\left(|\bm{u}|,|\bm{r}|,|\bm{s}|\right)
and
g_{s}\left(|\bm{u}|,|\bm{r}|,|\bm{s}|\right)
.

We emphasize that the anisotropic form of the new collision kernel does not violate the frame-indifference constraint. In particular, the solution of the new kinetic model strictly preserves the isotropy of the initial condition.

Corollary 1
.

If
f(\bm{v},0)
is isotropic, the solution
f(\bm{v},t)
of model (
3
) (
8
) remains isotropic for
t>0
.

Proof.

Let us consider two points
\bm{v}_{1}=\mathcal{U}\bm{v}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{T}=\bm{I}
. It is easy to show

\begin{split}C[f](\bm{v}_{1},0)&=\nabla_{\bm{v}_{1}}\cdot\int\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime})\nabla f(\bm{v}_{1})-f(\bm{v}_{1})\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime}\\
&=(\mathcal{U}\nabla_{\bm{v}_{2}})\cdot\int\bm{\omega}(\mathcal{U}\bm{v}_{2},\mathcal{U}\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime})\mathcal{U}\nabla f(\bm{v}_{2})-f(\bm{v}_{2})\mathcal{U}\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime}\\
&=(\mathcal{U}\nabla_{\bm{v}_{2}})\cdot\int\mathcal{U}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{2},\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime})\mathcal{U}^{T}\left[f(\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime})\mathcal{U}\nabla f(\bm{v}_{2})-f(\bm{v}_{2})\mathcal{U}\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{2}^{\prime}\\
&\equiv C[f](\bm{v}_{2},0),\end{split}

∎

As shown in Ref.
[
36
]
, the generalized collision operator (
3
) (
8
) can capture the heterogeneous energy transfer arising from the collective interactions between pairs of collision particles and the environment and improve the Landau collision operator in a significantly broader range of physical conditions. However, the kernel
\omega(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})
in Eq. (

) is non-stationary and therefore does not preserve the convolution structure. The direct numerical evaluation of the collision operator takes

2.2
Spectral separation representation

To address the aforementioned challenge, we seek a new form of the kernel based on the spectral separation (SS) representation. Since
\widetilde{\bm{u}}\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{T}+\widetilde{\bm{r}}\widetilde{\bm{r}}^{T}+\widetilde{\bm{s}}\widetilde{\bm{s}}^{T}=\bm{I}
,
\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{u}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}=\bm{\mathcal{P}}
, the collision kernel
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})
in Eq. (
8
) can be rewritten as

\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})=g_{1}^{2}|\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{r}|^{2}\bm{\mathcal{P}}+(g_{2}^{2}-g_{1}^{2})\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{r}\bm{r}^{T}\bm{\mathcal{P}},

(9)

where the encoder functions
g_{\ast}
are constructed as
g_{\ast}=g_{\ast}(|\bm{u}|,|\bm{v}|,|\bm{v}^{\prime}|)
for
\ast=1,2
. Given
\bm{v}
and
\bm{v}^{\prime}
,
g_{s}^{2}
and
g_{r}^{2}
in Eq. (
8
) can be determined by
g_{s}^{2}=g_{1}^{2}|\mathcal{P}\bm{r}|^{2}
and
g_{r}^{2}=g_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{P}\bm{r}|^{2}

For each encoder function
g_{\ast}(\cdot)
, we seek a low-rank tensor product representation with a set of univariate basis functions to separate the individual and relative velocity dependencies, i.e.,

\displaystyle g_{\ast}(|\bm{u}|,|\bm{v}|,|\bm{v}^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}}^{J^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}(|\bm{u}|)\mathcal{M}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}(|\bm{v}|)\mathcal{N}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}(|\bm{v}^{\prime}|)+\mathcal{L}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}(|\bm{u}|)\mathcal{N}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}(|\bm{v}|)\mathcal{M}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}(|\bm{v}^{\prime}|),

(10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}^{J}L_{\ast}^{j}(|\bm{u}|)M_{\ast}^{j}(|\bm{v}|)N_{\ast}^{j}(|\bm{v}^{\prime}|),

where
\{\mathcal{L}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}},\mathcal{M}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}},\mathcal{N}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}\}_{j^{\prime}=1}^{J^{\prime}}
are univariate basis functions, which are essentially encoders represented by neural networks and will be learned from the MD data as shown in Sec.
2.3
.
J^{\prime}
is the total number of modes used for the low-rank representation. The right-hand-side of Eq. (
10
) preserves the permutation symmetry for
\bm{v}
and
\bm{v}^{\prime}
due to Eq. (
4

L_{\ast}^{2j^{\prime}-1}=L_{\ast}^{2j^{\prime}}=\mathcal{L}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}
,
M_{\ast}^{2j^{\prime}-1}=N_{\ast}^{2j^{\prime}}=\mathcal{M}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}
and
M_{\ast}^{2j^{\prime}}=N_{\ast}^{2j^{\prime}-1}=\mathcal{N}_{\ast}^{j^{\prime}}
, for
j^{\prime}=1,\cdots,J^{\prime}
, and
J=2J^{\prime}
.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\int g_{1}^{2}|\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{r}|^{2}\bm{\mathcal{P}}f(\bm{v})f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\left[\nabla\log f(\bm{v})-\nabla^{\prime}\log f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle:I_{1}+I_{2}-I_{3},

\displaystyle|\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{r}|^{2}

2.3
Data-driven construction from micro-scale MD

To construct the new collision kernel (
9
) (
10
), we establish a data-driven learning of the univariate basis functions
\{L_{\ast}^{j},M_{\ast}^{j},N_{\ast}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
by matching the prediction of the kinetic processes between the kinetic equation (
3
) and the micro-scale full MD data. Let
\left\{\bm{v}_{m}^{n}\right\}_{m=1}^{N_{MD}}
denote a collection of the samples of the MD particle velocities at
t=t_{n}
. However, direct evaluation of Eq. (
3
) involves terms such as
\nabla f(\bm{v})
and
\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})
and therefore
relies on the numerical estimation of the three-dimensional probability density function of the MD velocity
f(\bm{v})
from the empirical distribution
f^{\ast}(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{N_{MD}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{MD}}\delta(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{m})
, which can be computationally expensive. To alleviate this issue, we propose a weak form of the loss function by introducing a set of test basis functions
\left\{\psi_{k}(\bm{v})\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\left(\left.\dfrac{\partial f^{n}}{\partial t}\right|_{MD}-\left.\dfrac{\partial f^{n}}{\partial t}\right|_{c},\psi_{k}(\bm{v})\right)^{2},

(15)

where
(h_{1},h_{2}):=\int h_{1}(\bm{v})h_{2}(\bm{v}){\rm d}\bm{v}
represents the inner product in the velocity space.
K
and
N_{T}
represent the number of test basis functions and time steps, respectively. We refer to
B
for the details on the MD and training setup. For the MD terms of the loss function, we have

\left(\left.\dfrac{\partial f^{n}}{\partial t}\right|_{MD},\psi_{k}(\bm{v})\right)\approx\left(\dfrac{f^{n+1}-f^{n}}{\Delta t},\psi_{k}(\bm{v})\right)=\dfrac{1}{N_{MD}\Delta t}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{MD}}(\psi_{k}(\bm{v}_{m}^{n+1})-\psi_{k}(\bm{v}_{m}^{n})),

(16)

which can be efficiently evaluated by the Monte Carlo method with the empirical distribution
f^{\ast}(\bm{v})
. For the kinetic part, we use the weak form

\begin{split}\left(\left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right|_{c},\psi_{k}(\bm{v})\right)=&\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\left(\dfrac{\partial f(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}}\int\bm{\omega}_{ij}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}\right)-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\left(f(\bm{v})\int\bm{\omega}_{ij}\dfrac{\partial f(\bm{v}^{\prime})}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}\right),\psi_{k}(\bm{v})\right)\\
=&\left(f(\bm{v})\int\bm{\omega}_{ij}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial v_{i}\partial v_{j}}\right)+\left(f(\bm{v})\int\dfrac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{ij}}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\right)\\
&-\left(f(\bm{v})\int\dfrac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{ij}}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}^{\prime}}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\right),\\
\end{split}

(17)

where the individual terms can also be evaluated by the Monte Carlo method with empirical distribution
f^{\ast}(\bm{v})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(f(\bm{v})\int\bm{\omega}_{ij}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}\partial\bm{v}_{j}}\right)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N_{MD}^{2}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}^{N_{MD}}\bm{\omega}_{ij}(\bm{v}_{m},\bm{v}_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime})\dfrac{\partial^{2}\psi_{k}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}\partial\bm{v}_{j}}(\bm{v}_{m}),

(18)

\displaystyle\left(f(\bm{v})\int\dfrac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{ij}}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\right)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N_{MD}^{2}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}^{N_{MD}}\dfrac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{ij}}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}}(\bm{v}_{m},\bm{v}_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime})\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}(\bm{v}_{m}),

\displaystyle\left(f(\bm{v})\int\dfrac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{ij}}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}^{\prime}}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\right)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N_{MD}^{2}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}^{N_{MD}}\dfrac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{ij}}{\partial\bm{v}_{j}^{\prime}}(\bm{v}_{m},\bm{v}_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime})\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}(\bm{v}_{m}).

In practice, the number of MD particles
N_{MD}
can be large (e.g.
N_{MD}\sim O(10^{6})
). The double summation could be computationally expensive. Alternatively, we use the mini-batch method
[
20
,
28
,
17
,
18
]
to accelerate the numerical evaluation, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(f(\bm{v})\int\bm{\omega}_{ij}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\dfrac{\partial\psi_{k}(\bm{v})}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}\partial\bm{v}_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N_{MD}^{2}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}^{N_{MD}}\bm{\omega}_{ij}(\bm{v}_{m},\bm{v}_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime})\dfrac{\partial^{2}\psi_{k}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}\partial\bm{v}_{j}}(\bm{v}_{m})

(19)

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\bm{\omega}_{ij}(\bm{v}_{m(p)},\bm{v}_{m^{\prime}(p)}^{\prime})\dfrac{\partial^{2}\psi_{k}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}\partial\bm{v}_{j}}(\bm{v}_{m(p)}),

with pairs
(\bm{v}_{m(p)},\bm{v}_{m^{\prime}(p)}^{\prime})
randomly selected from the MD samples for
p=1,\cdots,P
.

2.4
Structure-preserving numerical discretization

With the new collision kernel
\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})
(
9
) (
10
) constructed by minimizing the empirical loss function (
15
), we obtain a generalized collision operator that retains the MD fidelity beyond empirical models, and meanwhile, strictly preserves the physical constraints.
This enables us to further develop structure-preserving numerical schemes that inherit the essential physical properties on the discrete level. To pursue the numerical solution, we consider the collisional kinetic equation in a bounded domain
\Omega
, i.e.,

\begin{split}&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(\bm{v},t)=\nabla\cdot\int\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\nabla f(\bm{v})-f(\bm{v})\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime},\quad\bm{v}\in\Omega\\
&\left(\int\bm{\omega}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})\left[f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\nabla f(\bm{v})-f(\bm{v})\nabla^{\prime}f(\bm{v}^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}(\bm{v})=0,~~\quad\forall\bm{v}\in\partial\Omega.\end{split}

(20)

It is straightforward to show that the conservation laws and H-theorem similar to Proposition
1
hold for model (
20
) in the bounded domain
\Omega
.

In this work, we focus on the fast approximation of the generalized collision operator and follow the essential idea of Refs.
[
11
,
6
]
to construct the semi-discrete structure-preserving scheme based on the finite-difference method.
For simplicity, we consider a cubic domain and the distribution function is approximated by a piecewise constant function on an equal-space grid mesh at
\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}=(i_{1}h,i_{2}h,i_{3}h)
with mesh size
h
and
\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.
Denote
f_{\bm{i}}(t)
as the approximated discrete probability at velocity
\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}
and time
t
.
The evolution of the PDF is formulated as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{i}}(t)}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{+}\bm{p}_{\bm{i}},

(21)

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{\bm{i}}=

\displaystyle h^{3}\sum_{\bm{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}},\bm{v}_{\bm{j}})f_{\bm{i}}f_{\bm{j}}\left[\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{j}}\right],

where
\bm{p}_{\bm{i}}
is the probability flux at
\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}
, and
\mathrm{D}^{-}
and
\mathrm{D}^{+}
are dual discrete gradient and divergence operators of central difference discretization defined by

\displaystyle(\mathrm{D}^{-}\psi_{\bm{i}})_{k}

\displaystyle=[\psi(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}+\bm{e}_{k}h)-\psi(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}-\bm{e}_{k}h)]/2h,

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{+}\bm{\xi}_{\bm{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}[\xi_{k}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}+\bm{e}_{k}h)-\xi_{k}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}-\bm{e}_{k}h)]/2h,

where
k=1,2,3
and
\bm{e}_{k}
is the unit vector in the
k
-th dimension.
In all numerical examples in this paper, we use the above central difference scheme for simulation.

Proposition 2
.

The numerical semi-discretization scheme defined above satisfies the conservation law of mass, momentum, and kinetic energy, and the H-theorem in the discrete sense.

Proof.

The definition of any discrete physical quantity on the grids and its evolution can be represented by

\displaystyle\Phi(t)

\displaystyle=h^{3}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}f_{\bm{i}}\phi_{\bm{i}},

(23)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=h^{3}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{i}}}{\partial t}\phi_{\bm{i}}

\displaystyle=h^{6}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\phi_{\bm{i}}\mathrm{D}^{+}\sum_{\bm{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}},\bm{v}_{\bm{j}})f_{\bm{i}}f_{\bm{j}}\left[\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}h^{6}\sum_{\bm{i},\bm{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\left[\mathrm{D}^{-}\phi_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\phi_{\bm{j}}\right]^{T}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}},\bm{v}_{\bm{j}})f_{\bm{i}}f_{\bm{j}}\left[\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{j}}\right].

When we choose
\phi_{\bm{i}}=\{1,~v_{k},~\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}^{2}\}
,

\mathrm{D}^{-}\phi_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\phi_{\bm{j}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&\phi_{\bm{i}}&=1\\
&0,&\phi_{\bm{i}}&=v_{k}\\
&2(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}-\bm{v}_{\bm{j}}),&\phi_{\bm{i}}&=\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}^{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

(24)

Moreover,
\bm{v}_{\bm{i}}-\bm{v}_{\bm{j}}\in\text{Ker}(\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}},\bm{v}_{\bm{j}}))
, so that the conservation law holds.

The discrete entropy and its time derivative are defined as

\displaystyle S(f)

\displaystyle=-h^{3}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}f_{\bm{i}}\log f_{\bm{i}},

(25)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}S(f)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}h^{6}\sum_{\bm{i},\bm{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\left[\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{j}}\right]^{T}\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}},\bm{v}_{\bm{j}})f_{\bm{i}}f_{\bm{j}}\left[\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{j}}\right]\geq 0.

The equality holds if and only if
\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{i}}-\mathrm{D}^{-}\log f_{\bm{j}}\in\text{Ker}(\bm{\omega}(\bm{v}_{\bm{i}},\bm{v}_{\bm{j}}))
, i.e. the equilibrium state is the discrete Maxwellian distribution.
∎

Remark 2
.

The dual discrete gradient and divergence operators can be formed by spatial discretization schemes such as the forward-backward difference or finite volume method.

Remark 3
.

In this work, we use the explicit time discretization with time step size
dt\propto h^{2}
, and the full discrete scheme can only guarantee the preservation of the conservation laws. Although numerical results in Sec.
3
show the nonnegative entropy dissipation, implicit time schemes should be used for the theoretical guarantee of the H-theorem and numerical simulations with large time steps, which will be pursued in future work. Alternatively, the numerical scheme
[
6
]
based on the geometric average of the distribution function might be considered.

4
Summary

This study addresses an essential limitation of classical collisional kinetic model by introducing a generalized collision operator capable of accurately modeling plasma dynamics beyond the weakly coupled regime.
The proposed generalized collision operator incorporates an anisotropic and non-stationary kernel learned directly from MD simulations. This enables the new operator to capture the broadly overlooked anisotropic energy transfer arising from the collective interactions between the pair of colliding particles and the environment, where the traditional Landau formulation shows limitations.

To overcome the computational challenges due to the non-stationary form of the generalized operator, a fast spectral separation method is developed by seeking a low-rank tensor representation of the generalized collision kernel. This enables the efficient evaluation of the collision operator by exploiting the convolution structure of individual integral terms, and thereby reduces the computational complexity from
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
.
The constructed operator strictly preserves the conservation laws and H-theorem, and enables the further development of structure-preserving numerical schemes.

Numerical results demonstrate that the present model improves the Landau operator in the accuracy of the model while maintaining high computational efficiency. Various physical constraints can be numerically preserved at the discrete level.
This work achieves an advance toward accurate and efficient modeling of collisional plasmas over a broader range of physical conditions. Future work will be devoted to developing an implicit numerical scheme with the theoretical guarantee of the H-theorem and constructing the full kinetic model with a generalized collisional operator that further accounts for spatial inhomogeneity.

Appendix A
Physical regime of plasma kinetics

In this work, we investigate the kinetic processes of one-component plasma systems.
Following the standard collisional kinetic theory
[
16
]
, we use the dimensionless coupling parameter
\Gamma
to characterize the degree of coupling between the potential and kinetic energy, i.e.,

\Gamma=\dfrac{q_{e}^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}k_{B}T}(4\pi n/3)^{1/3},

(29)

where
q_{e}
denotes the particle charge,
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the temperature, and
n
is the particle number density.

This parameter serves as a metric for the physical regime of the plasma kinetics. For
\Gamma\ll O(1)
, the plasma is in the weakly coupled regime (e.g., high-temperature, low-density), and the small-angle scattering and binary collisions are the dominant interaction mechanisms. The Landau equation provides a valid approximation that remains valid.
For
\Gamma\sim O(1)
, the plasma is in the moderately coupled regime, and the effects of the particle correlation become non-negligible. The Landau equation is insufficient to characterize the plasma kinetics due to the oversimplification of the collisional interactions in the form of a homogeneous kernel. In this study, we focus on this challenging regime and aim to accurately model the plasma kinetics that accounts for the unresolved particle correlations in the form of a generalized heterogeneous non-stationary collisional kernel (
8
) (
9
).

Appendix B
MD settings

In this study, MD simulations are performed by using
N_{MD}=10^{6}
particles confined in a cubic box of side length
100~\AA
.
The particles interact via an unscreened Coulomb interaction, with each particle carrying a monovalent charge.
Under this condition, the coupling parameter of the system is
\Gamma=2.3
, corresponding to the moderately coupled plasma regime.
The MD simulations are initialized with various velocity distributions and equilibrium configurations.

In order to simplify the processing of neural network training and numerical simulations, we introduce characteristic scales for velocity and time

v_{0}=a\omega_{p}\approx 81700~\text{m/s},\qquad t_{0}=\omega_{p}^{-1}\approx 0.76~\text{fs},

(30)

where
a=\left(3/4\pi n\right)^{1/3}
is the Wigner-Seitz radius and
\omega_{p}=(ne^{2}/m\epsilon_{0})^{1/2}
is the plasma frequency.
Velocity and time are nondimensionalized via
\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{MD}/v_{0}
,
t=t_{MD}/t_{0}
, and the variance of the velocity in
3
-D is
0.433
.
