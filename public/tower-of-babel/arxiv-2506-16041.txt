Transporting a Dirac mass in a mean field planning problem
Pierre Cardaliaguet, Sebastian Munoz, Alessio Porretta

Abstract
We study a mean field planning problem in which the initial density is a Dirac mass. We show that there exists a unique solution which converges to a self-similar profile as time tends to 0 0 . We proceed by studying a continuous rescaling of the solution, and characterizing its behavior near the initial time through an appropriate Lyapunov functional.



Keywords:
optimal transport; Dirac mass; self-similar solutions; degenerate elliptic equations; intrinsic scaling; displacement convexity; Lagrangian coordinates; free boundary; mean field games; Hamilton-Jacobi equations; continuity equation.

MSC:
35R35, 35Q89, 35B65, 35J70.

1
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the mean field planning problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}|u_{x}|^{2}=m^{\theta}\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\\
m_{t}-(mu_{x})_{x}=0\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\\
m(0)=\delta_{0},\;m(T)=m_{T}\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac mass centered at
x=0
, and
m_{T}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is the smooth density of a probability measure, assumed to be compactly supported, and
\theta
is a positive constant.

System (
1.1
) is a sample of mean field game system with a local coupling. It describes Nash equilibria in a differential game played by infinitely many players
[
9
,
10
]
. Here
u
solves a Hamilton-Jacobi equation and is interpreted as the value function of a typical small controller paying a cost made of a kinetic term (leading to the term
\frac{1}{2}|u_{x}|^{2}
) and a congestion term
m^{\theta}
. The map
m
describes the population density which evolves in time according to the optimal feedback of the controllers, given by
-u_{x}
. We consider here the planning problem, in which initial and terminal conditions for the density are prescribed.

The model can also be interpreted as the system of optimality conditions of an optimal transport problem on the Wasserstein space of measures. The goal is to minimize the functional

{{\mathcal{B}}(m,v)}:=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\,|v|^{2}dmdt+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(m)dxdt\quad\text{subject to }\begin{cases}m_{t}-(vm)_{x}=0&\\
m(0)=\delta_{0}\,,m(T)=m_{T}&\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
F(s)=\frac{s^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1}
. This problem is a variant of the dynamic formulation of the mass transport problem à la Benamou-Brenier
[
4
]
, and has been widely investigated in the literature: see for instance
[
2
,
6
,
8
,
11
,
18
]
. In this context, the functions
u(0),u(T)
are the so-called Kantorovich potentials, see
[
1
,
20
,
22
]
. The cost
F
penalizes measures with a high density.

Since P.-L. Lions’ course at the College de France
[
12
]
, it was observed that system (
1.1
) behaves as a time-space elliptic equation in the
u
variable, as least in the region were
m
is positive. This remark was fully developed and extended in a series of papers
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
19
]
, showing in various contexts that the solution to (
1.1
) is smooth whenever the positivity of
m
can be proved to hold.

More recently, we have investigated in
[
5
]
for the first time the case were
m
has a compact support. Working in dimension 1, and assuming that the initial measure
m_{0}
and the terminal measure
m_{T}
are smooth with a compact support, it is proved in
[
5
]
that (
1.1
) has a unique solution, and that this solution remains smooth in the support of
m
. In addition,
m
is globally Hölder continuous; its support is of class
C^{1,1}
and convex in time-space. We also explain in
[
5
]
that there exists a particular self-similar solution to (
1.1
), reminiscent of the Barenblatt solution for the porous medium equation
[
3
,
21
]
: for
\theta>0
, let us set

\alpha=\frac{2}{2+\theta}

and define

\phi(r)=\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha(1-\alpha))^{1/\theta}(R_{\alpha}^{2}-r^{2}\right)_{+}^{1/\theta}

(where
R_{\alpha}>0
is chosen such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi=1
). We actually prove that
\phi
is a self-similar profile for system (
1.1
), meaning that the pair
(u(t,x),t^{-\alpha}\phi(t^{-\alpha}x))
, with
u(t,x)=-\alpha x^{2}/(2t)-Ct^{2\alpha-1}
in
(-R_{\alpha}t^{\alpha},R_{\alpha}t^{\alpha})
for a suitable constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
, is a solution to (
1.1
) for the terminal condition
m_{T}(x)=T^{-\alpha}\phi(T^{-\alpha}x)
. Note that indeed, in the sense of distributions,

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}t^{-\alpha}\phi(t^{-\alpha}x)=\delta_{0}\,.

Let us note for later use that
\theta=2
is a threshold for the behavior of
u
: if
\theta<2
, and thus
\alpha>1/2
, then
u
is continuous up to time
t=0
, at least in the support of
m
. On the contrary, when
\theta>2
, then
u(t,0)
diverges as
t\to 0
.

System
1.1
has seldom been studied in the literature. In the particular case where
m_{0}=m_{T}=\delta_{0}
and
\theta=1
, Lions and Souganidis prove in
[
13
]
the existence of a solution to (
1.1
) by using a vanishing viscosity method. They also give an explicit expression for this solution. Their analysis is motivated by the large deviation principle for the
1+1
KPZ equation.

In this paper we investigate (
1.1
) for general terminal conditions. Our aim is to show two main facts; firstly, that (
1.1
) admits a solution
which behaves like the self similar solution in the right time-space rescaling (i.e. in self-similarity variables), secondly that there is a unique solution satisfying such an asymptotic convergence, in a suitable sense, to the self-similar profile. In a rough version, our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
\theta>0
, and assume that
m_{T}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is supported in an interval
[a,b]
, is smooth in
(a,b)
and satisfies the compatibility condition

C^{-1}\,{\rm dist}(x,\{a,b\})^{1/\theta}\leq m_{T}(x)\leq C\,{\rm dist}(x,\{a,b\})^{1/\theta},\quad x\in[a,b],

(1.3)

for some constant
C>1
. Then there exists a unique solution to (
1.1
) such that the map
x\to t^{\alpha}m(t,t^{\alpha}x)
converges, in a suitable sense, to the self-similar profile
\phi
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

The precise sense in which convergence to the self-similar solution holds will be made precise later, as well as the class of solutions where uniqueness is proved. This is in fact a delicate issue and our uniqueness result will be different, in strength, depending whether
\theta<2
or
\theta\geq 2
.

The basic notion of solution introduced for problem (
1.1
) is explained in Definition
2.11
, where the convergence as
t\to 0
to the Dirac mass is only meant in the weak topology of measures. When
\theta\in(0,2)
, this is enough to lead to uniqueness, only using the global bound of
t^{\frac{\alpha\theta}{\theta+1}}\|m(t)\|_{\theta+1}
, which yields Hölder continuity of
u
up to
t=0
. So for
\theta<2
the convergence to the self-similar profile is just an additional property that we prove, which is not needed in the uniqueness argument. By contrast, when
\theta\geq 2
,
u
turns out to be unbounded and the picture becomes more difficult; but we can still prove uniqueness in a class of solutions that suitably converge to the self-similar profile in the continuous rescaling time-space reference frame.
In relation to the main text, the aforementioned cases correspond to Theorem
2.13
(
\theta<2
), Theorem
3.6
(
\theta=2
), and Thm
3.12
(
\theta>2
).

Our starting point for the proofs is the paper
[
17
]
, where the second author explores the long-time behavior of the MFG planning system proving that, for smooth initial and terminal conditions, the solution—after a suitable scaling—converges to the self-similar profile as the time horizon
T
tends to infinity. For this, the author introduces a Lyapunov function which forces the (rescaled) solution to behave as the self-similar one. We use here the same Lyapunov function (for a different scaling) to prove the behavior near
t=0
of the solution with initial condition
m_{0}=\delta_{0}
.

We now describe more precisely the content of the paper and its organization. In Section
2
, we build a solution to (
1.1
) starting from a solution
(u^{\epsilon},m^{\epsilon})
to (
1.1
) with smooth initial densities
m_{0}^{\epsilon}
and
m_{T}
. We first obtain estimates - uniform in
\epsilon
- of the measure
m^{\epsilon}
near
t=0
(Subsection
2.1
), exploiting the representation of
m^{\epsilon}
in terms of the flow of the optimal trajectories. Then we focus on
u^{\epsilon}
obtaining oscillation estimates with the optimal time scale (Subsection
2.2
). This eventually leads to the existence of a solution (Proposition
2.12
). The behavior near
t=0
of this solution, in the scale of similarity variables, is discussed in Section
3
: the main result of this section, Theorem
3.1
, shows the convergence to the self-similar profile for any
\theta>0
.
Finally, we prove the uniqueness of solutions; as mentioned before, here the situation is different according to whether
\theta<2
or
\theta\geq 2
. When
\theta<2
, the map
u
remains bounded: the uniqueness of the bounded solution follows easily (Theorem
2.13
). The case
\theta\geq 2
is singular and more difficult to analyze: indeed, the map
u
blows up as
t\to 0
. The limiting case
\theta=2
is treated in Proposition
3.6
. When
\theta>2
, we first quantify the convergence rate to the self-similar profile of the solutions previously built by approximation. Then we obtain both existence and uniqueness in a class of solutions with a suitable behavior at
t=0
(Proposition
3.12
).

We stress that our analysis is truly localized in time near the singularity; in particular, the same approach applies to the case that the final target
m_{T}
is itself a Dirac mass, see also Remark
3.13
. Let us finally underline that we only discuss here the case of the planning problem, because in the more standard case of a mean field game system with a terminal payoff depending on
m
, the solution enjoys some symmetry and convexity property, making the problem much easier to analyze (see Section 5.2 in
[
5
]
) .

2
Existence of a solution

In this section, we construct a solution to (
1.1
), for any value of
\theta>0
. We assume that
m_{T}
is supported in an interval
[a,b]
, is smooth in
(a,b)
and satisfies (
1.3
).

As a starting point, we
approximate the initial condition
\delta_{0}
by

m^{\varepsilon}_{0}(x):=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\phi(\varepsilon^{-\alpha}x),

and we consider the associated problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-u^{\varepsilon}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}|u^{\varepsilon}_{x}|^{2}=(m^{\varepsilon})^{\theta}\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\\
m^{\varepsilon}_{t}-(m^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{x})_{x}=0\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\\
m^{\varepsilon}(0)=m^{\varepsilon}_{0},\;m^{\varepsilon}(T)=m_{T}\,.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

The existence of a solution
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
of (
2.1
) is given by
[
5
, Thm. 4.3]
. We also recall from
[
5
, Thm. 1.4, Thm. 1.5]
that the map
u^{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1}
(and can be constructed as globally Lipschitz even outside the support of
m
), while
m^{\varepsilon}
is globally Hölder continuous, with
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
smooth in
\{m^{\varepsilon}>0\}
.

2.1
Estimates on the flow and the density

We begin by establishing estimates on the flow of optimal curves, which yield estimates on the density. For this purpose, we let
\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
denote the Lagrangian flow associated to
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
. That is,
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot,x)=-u^{\varepsilon}_{x}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot,x)),\\
\gamma(0,x)=x.\end{cases}

(2.2)

We recall from
[
5
, Thm. 4.10]
that

\{m^{\varepsilon}>0\}=\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\,:\,\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha})<x<\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha})\},

(2.3)

\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t,x)=\frac{m^{\varepsilon}_{0}(x)}{m(t,\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,x))},

(2.4)

and
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
solves the elliptic equation

\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{tt}+\frac{\theta(m_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{\theta}}{(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{x})^{2+\theta}}\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{xx}=\frac{((m_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{\theta})_{x}}{(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{x})^{\theta+1}}\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha},R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha}).

It will be convenient to rescale the
x
variable and to introduce the blown-up functions

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,y)=\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,\varepsilon^{\alpha}y)\,,\qquad y\in[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}].

We note that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(0,y)=\varepsilon^{\alpha}y
in
[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}]
and that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
solves

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t,y)=\frac{\phi(y)}{m(t,\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,y))},

(2.5)

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{tt}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2+\theta}}\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{yy}=\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{\theta+1}}\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}).

(2.6)

We will require below the following estimate of
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,\cdot)
, which is the main application of Assumption (
1.3
).

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a constant
C
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

C^{-1}\leq\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,\cdot)\leq C.

Proof.

By conservation of mass, we have, for any
y\in[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}]
,

\int_{\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y)}^{\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,R_{\alpha})}m_{T}(x)dx=\int_{\varepsilon^{\alpha}y}^{\varepsilon^{\alpha}R_{\alpha}}m_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x)dx.

Recalling our assumption on
m_{T}
in (
1.3
), the definition of
\phi
and the fact that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,R_{\alpha})=b
, we get, assuming that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y)\geq(a+b)/2
to fix the ideas,

(1+1/\theta)^{-1}C(b-\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y))^{1+1/\theta}\geq\int_{y}^{R_{\alpha}}\phi(s)ds\geq C^{-1}(R_{\alpha}-y)^{1+1/\theta}.

(2.7)

Thus, using again assumption (
1.3
),

\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,y)=\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{x}(T,\varepsilon^{\alpha}y)=\frac{m_{0}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\alpha}y)}{m_{T}(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y))}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\phi(y)}{C^{-1}(b-\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y))^{1/\theta}}\leq C\varepsilon^{-\alpha}.

This gives an upper bound on
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,\cdot)
.

We can show in the same way that, for
y\in[0,R_{\alpha}]
such that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y)\geq(a+b)/2
,

(1+1/\theta)^{-1}C^{-1}(b-\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y))^{1+1/\theta}\leq\int_{y}^{R_{\alpha}}\phi(s)ds\leq C(R_{\alpha}-y)^{1+1/\theta}.

Hence, using (
1.3
) once more, we get

\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,y)=\frac{m_{0}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\alpha}y)}{m_{T}(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y))}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{-\alpha}C^{-1}(R_{\alpha}-y)^{1/\theta}}{C(b-\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,y))^{1/\theta}}\geq C\varepsilon^{-\alpha},

which gives the lower bound.

∎

We now establish sharp upper bounds on
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t,y)\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\qquad\forall(t,y)\in[0,T]\times[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}].

Proof.

Differentiating (
2.6
), we see that
v=\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}
solves

\displaystyle v_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{v^{2+\theta}}v_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{v^{2+\theta}}v_{yy}-(2+\theta)\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{v^{3+\theta}}(v_{y})^{2}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{v^{\theta+1}}=0.

We consider the map
w=w(t,y)
defined by

w(t,y)=k(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha},

where
\delta
is any (sufficiently small) positive number, whereas
k>0
and
\xi:(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})\to(0,\infty)
are to be chosen below. We have

w_{t}=k\alpha(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-1},\qquad w_{tt}=k\alpha(\alpha-1)(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}

while

w_{y}=k\alpha\delta(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-1}\xi^{\prime},\qquad w_{yy}=k\alpha\delta(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}\left((\alpha-1)\delta(\xi^{\prime})^{2}+(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)\xi^{\prime\prime}\right).

Then

\displaystyle w_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{w^{2+\theta}}w_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{w^{2+\theta}}w_{yy}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{w^{\theta+1}}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\leq(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}\left(k\alpha(\alpha-1)-(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}k^{-1-\theta}\right)+k^{-1-\theta}\alpha\delta(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-3}\left((2\theta+1)(\phi^{\theta})_{y}\xi^{\prime}+\theta\phi^{\theta}\xi^{\prime\prime}\right)

where we used the fact that
\alpha(\theta+1)=-(\alpha-2)
to put together the terms involving
w_{tt}
and
\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{w^{\theta+1}}
and where we have omitted the first term involving
w_{yy}
which is non-positive. We choose
\xi
as a smooth, convex, positive, and even map, with a derivative given by
\xi^{\prime}=\phi^{-(2\theta+1)}
on
[R_{\alpha}/2,R_{\alpha})
(which is possible since
\phi^{-(2\theta+1)}
is increasing on
[0,R_{\alpha})
). Note then that
\xi(y)\to\infty
as
y\to R_{\alpha}^{-}
. With this choice, the last term in the right-hand side of (
2.1
) vanishes on
[R_{\alpha}/2,R_{\alpha})
. Thus, there exists a constant
C
(independent of
\varepsilon
,
k
and
\delta
), such that

\left|k^{-1-\theta}\alpha\delta(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-3}\left((2\theta+1)(\phi^{\theta})_{y}\xi^{\prime}+\theta\phi^{\theta}\xi^{\prime\prime}\right)\right|\leq Ck^{-1-\theta}(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}.

So we can choose
k>0
large enough such that the right-hand side of (
2.1
) is negative for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Namely, we have

\begin{split}&w_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{w^{2+\theta}}w_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{w^{2+\theta}}w_{yy}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{w^{\theta+1}}\\
&\leq(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}\left(k\alpha(\alpha-1)-(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}k^{-1-\theta}\right)+Ck^{-1-\theta}(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}<0\,.\end{split}

(2.9)

In addition, if we choose
k
large enough (again independently of
\varepsilon,\delta
), we also have
v(0,y)=\varepsilon^{\alpha}<w(0,y)
and
v(T,y)=\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,y)<w(T,y)
(because
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,y)
is bounded by a constant independent of
\varepsilon
, by Lemma
2.1
).

Given this choice of
k
, we now claim that
v\leq w
. Assume the contrary, and define

k^{*}:=\inf\{\lambda>1\,:\,v\leq\lambda\,w\quad\hbox{in $(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})$}\}\,.

We note that the set above is non-empty, since
v
is bounded, so
k^{*}<\infty
. If it happens that
k^{*}>1
, this implies the existence of some contact point in the interior, since
v<w
at the parabolic boundary; indeed, by optimality of
k^{*}
there should exist some
(t_{0},y_{0})\in(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})
such that
v\leq k^{*}w
and
\max(v-k^{*}w)=0
is attained at
(t_{0},y_{0})
. Therefore, at
(t_{0},y_{0})
, we have

(v_{t},v_{y})=k^{*}(w_{t},w_{y}),\qquad v_{tt}\leq k^{*}\,w_{tt},\qquad v_{yy}\leq k^{*}\,w_{yy}.

Hence, at
(t_{0},y_{0})
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=v_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{v^{2+\theta}}v_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{v^{2+\theta}}v_{yy}-(2+\theta)\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{v^{3+\theta}}(v_{y})^{2}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{v^{\theta+1}}

\displaystyle\leq k^{*}w_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{v^{2+\theta}}k^{*}w_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{v^{2+\theta}}k^{*}w_{yy}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{v^{\theta+1}}

\displaystyle=k^{*}w_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(k^{*}w)^{2+\theta}}k^{*}w_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{(k^{*}w)^{2+\theta}}k^{*}w_{yy}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{(k^{*}w)^{\theta+1}}\;<\;0,

where the last equality comes from the fact that
v=k^{*}w
at
(t_{0},y_{0})
. However, exactly as in (
2.1
)–(
2.9
), for any
k^{*}>1
we have

\displaystyle(k^{*}w)_{tt}+(2\theta+1)\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(k^{*}w)^{2+\theta}}(k^{*}w)_{y}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{(k^{*}w)^{2+\theta}}(k^{*}w)_{yy}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}}{(k^{*}w)^{\theta+1}}

\displaystyle\leq(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}\left(k^{*}k\alpha(\alpha-1)-(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}(k^{*}k)^{-1-\theta}\right)+C(k^{*}k)^{-1-\theta}(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha-2}<0

whenever
k^{*}>1
.
Hence there is a contradiction, which proves that
k^{*}=1
, namely
v\leq w
. This means
v\leq k(t+\varepsilon+\delta\xi)^{\alpha}
, and then letting
\delta\to 0
, we get

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t,y)\leq k(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\,.

∎

We deduce next an important estimate on the support of
m^{\varepsilon}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

There exists
C>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

\left|\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,y)\right|\leq C(\varepsilon+t)^{\alpha}\qquad\forall(t,y)\in[0,T]\times[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}].

(2.10)

Proof.

We recall from
[
5
, Thm. 4.14]
that the free boundary curves
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,-R_{\alpha})
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,R_{\alpha})
are, respectively, convex and concave. By Lemma
2.2
, we have

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,R_{\alpha})-\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,-R_{\alpha})\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha},

so that, using the convexity of
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,-R_{\alpha})
and the fact that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(0,-R_{\alpha})=-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha}
and

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(T,-R_{\alpha})=a
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,R_{\alpha})\leq\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,-R_{\alpha})+C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\leq-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha}\left(\frac{T-t}{T}\right)+a\,\frac{t}{T}+C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\leq C(\varepsilon+t)^{\alpha}.

In the same way, one can prove that

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,-R_{\alpha})\geq-C(\varepsilon+t)^{\alpha}.

Hence, for any
y\in[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}]
,

|\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,y)|\leq\max\{|\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,-R_{\alpha})|,|\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,R_{\alpha})|\}\leq C(\varepsilon+t)^{\alpha}.

∎

The next step is to prove sharp lower bounds for
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t,y)\geq C^{-1}(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\qquad\text{in}\;(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}).

Proof.

Let us set
v^{\varepsilon}(t,y)=(m^{\varepsilon})^{\theta}(t,\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,y))=\frac{\phi^{\theta}(y)}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t,y))^{\theta}}
. Following
[
5
]
we have

-v^{\varepsilon}_{tt}-\frac{\theta v^{\varepsilon}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}v^{\varepsilon}_{yy}\leq-\frac{v^{\varepsilon}_{y}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}\left(\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{\theta}}-v^{\varepsilon}_{y}\right).

(2.11)

We claim that, for
k
large enough (but independent of
\varepsilon
),
v^{\varepsilon}(t,y)\leq k\phi^{\theta}(y)(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}
. To prove this, we argue by contradiction and assume that the map
w(t,y):=v^{\varepsilon}(t,y)-k\phi^{\theta}(y)(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}
has a positive maximum. Let
(t_{0},y_{0})
be a maximum point. Note that the maximum cannot be reached at
t_{0}=0
for
k\geq 1
, nor for
y_{0}=\pm R_{\alpha}
. It cannot be reached for
t_{0}=T
for
k
large enough thanks to the lower bound on
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(T,\cdot)
in Lemma
2.1
.
Thus
(t_{0},y_{0})
is an interior maximum and we have at
(t_{0},y_{0})

v^{\varepsilon}_{tt}\leq k\phi^{\theta}\alpha\theta(\alpha\theta+1)(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta-2},\;\,v^{\varepsilon}_{y}=k(\phi^{\theta})_{y}(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta},\;\,v^{\varepsilon}_{yy}\leq-k\alpha(1-\alpha)(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}\,,

where we used
(\phi^{\theta})_{yy}
in the last inequality.
Thus

-v^{\varepsilon}_{tt}-\frac{\theta v^{\varepsilon}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}v^{\varepsilon}_{yy}\geq-k\phi^{\theta}\alpha\theta(\alpha\theta+1)(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta-2}+\frac{\theta v^{\varepsilon}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}k\alpha(1-\alpha)(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}

and using the fact that
v^{\varepsilon}(t_{0},y_{0})>k\phi^{\theta}(y_{0})(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}
, the bound
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha}
, and the equality
-\alpha\theta-2=-2\alpha\theta-2\alpha
, we get

\begin{split}-v^{\varepsilon}_{tt}-\frac{\theta v^{\varepsilon}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}v^{\varepsilon}_{yy}&\geq k(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta-2}\phi^{\theta}\left(-\alpha\theta(\alpha\theta+1)+C^{-1}k\alpha(1-\alpha)\right)\;>\;0,\end{split}

(2.12)

for
k
large enough (depending on
\alpha,\theta
and
C
from Lemma
2.2
).
On the other hand,

\displaystyle-\frac{v^{\varepsilon}_{y}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}\left(\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{\theta}}-v^{\varepsilon}_{y}\right)=-\frac{k((\phi^{\theta})_{y})^{2}(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}\left(\frac{v^{\varepsilon}}{\phi^{\theta}}-k(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}\right)

where we used that
(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t_{0},y_{0}))^{\theta}\,v^{\varepsilon}(t_{0},y_{0})=\phi^{\theta}(y_{0})
. Since
v^{\varepsilon}(t_{0},y_{0})>k\phi^{\theta}(y_{0})(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}
, the right-hand side of the above equality is negative so (
2.11
) yields

-v^{\varepsilon}_{tt}-\frac{\theta v^{\varepsilon}}{(\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y})^{2}}v^{\varepsilon}_{yy}<0

which contradicts (
2.12
). So we have proved that
v^{\varepsilon}(t,y)\leq k\phi^{\theta}(y)(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}
, which implies the result because
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}(t,y)=\frac{\phi(y)}{v(t,y))^{1/\theta}}
.
∎

We observe now that upper bounds on
m
follow from the previous result.

Lemma 2.5
.

The sequence
m^{\varepsilon}
is locally bounded and there exists
C>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that, for
t\in[0,T]
,

\|m^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}

(2.13)

and

\|m^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{\theta+1}^{\theta+1}\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}\,.

(2.14)

In addition,
m^{\varepsilon}
is Hölder continuous, locally in time, uniformly in
\varepsilon
. Namely, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
\alpha=\alpha(\delta^{-1})
such that
m\in C^{\alpha}([\delta,T-\delta]\times\mathbb{R})
, and

\|m^{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{\alpha}([\delta,T-\delta]\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C_{\delta}.

(2.15)

Proof.

We proved in Lemma
2.4
that
m^{\theta}(t,\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t,y))\leq k\phi^{\theta}(y)(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha\theta}
, and since
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
is a bijection between the support of
m(t)
and
m_{0}
, this readily yields
\|m^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq k^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\,\|\phi\|_{\infty}(t+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}
, hence (
2.13
). Then (
2.14
) obviously follows by interpolation.
As for the Hölder continuity, in view of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
, the result follows from the interior regularity estimate of
[
5
, Thm. 4.21]
.
∎

We can also now bound the first two derivatives of the free boundary curves.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\gamma_{L}(t)=\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,-R_{\alpha})
and
\gamma_{R}(t)=\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t,R_{\alpha})
be the left and right free boundary curves, respectively. Then there exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that, for
t\in[0,T]

|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t)|,|\dot{\gamma}_{R}(t)|\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1},

(2.16)

\frac{1}{C}(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-2}\leq\ddot{\gamma}_{L}(t)\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-2},\quad\frac{1}{C}(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-2}\leq-\ddot{\gamma}_{R}(t)\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-2}.

(2.17)

Proof.

The proof follows
[
17
, Prop. 4.5]
. It is enough to treat
\gamma_{L}
. The main point is that from
[
5
, Thm. 4.14]
we have the second derivative estimate

0<((m_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{\theta})_{x}(-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha})\|\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}^{-(1+\theta)}\leq\ddot{\gamma}_{L}(t)\leq((m_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{\theta})_{x}(-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha})\|(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t,\cdot))^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{1+\theta}.

Thus, in view of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
, and the definition of
m^{\varepsilon}_{0}
, we get sharp second derivative estimates for
\ddot{\gamma}_{L}
:

\frac{1}{C}(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-2}\leq\ddot{\gamma}_{L}(t)\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-2}.

The bound on
\dot{\gamma}_{L}
then follow by basic interpolation thanks to Corollary
2.3
.

∎

2.2
Estimates on
u^{\varepsilon}

As a third step, we deduce estimates on
u^{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every
t_{0}\in[0,(T-\varepsilon)/2],

\underset{\{t\in[t_{0},2t_{0}+\varepsilon],m(t)>0\}}{\emph{osc}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t))\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{2\alpha-1}.

(2.18)

Moreover, if
\theta\in(0,2)
, then

\underset{\{t\in[0,T],m(t)>0\}}{\emph{osc}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t))\leq C,

(2.19)

if
\theta>2
, then

\underset{\{t\in[t_{0},T],m(t)>0\}}{\emph{osc}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t)),\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{2\alpha-1}

(2.20)

and if
\theta=2
, then

\underset{\{t\in[t_{0},T],m(t)>0\}}{\emph{osc}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t))\leq C(1+|\ln(t_{0})|).

(2.21)

Proof.

The proof follows
[
17
, Prop. 4.7]
. For simplicity, we write
(u,m):=(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
. The main difference is that here the constant
C
is uniform as
t_{0}\to 0
thanks to the sharp bound of Corollary
2.3
.
Writing
S=\{t\in[t_{0},2t_{0}+\varepsilon],m(t)>0\}
, we claim that

\sup_{S}u=\sup_{S\cap\{t=t_{0}\}}u,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\inf_{S}u=\inf_{S\cap\{t=2t_{0}+\varepsilon\}}u.

(2.22)

To see this, we switch to Lagrangian coordinates, setting
\overline{u}(t,x)=u(t,\gamma(t,x))
. In these coordinates, the set
S
becomes the rectangle
[t_{0},2t_{0}+\varepsilon]\times(-R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha},R_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha}),
and

\overline{u}_{t}(t,x)=u_{t}(t,\gamma(t,x))+\gamma_{t}(t,x)u_{x}(t,\gamma(t,x))=u_{t}-u_{x}^{2}=-m^{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}\leq 0,

which implies (
2.22
). Now, given
(t_{0},x)\in S\cap\{t=t_{0}\}
and
(2t_{0}+\varepsilon,y)\in S\cap\{t=2t_{0}+\varepsilon\}
, we let
\beta:[t_{0},2t_{0}+\varepsilon]\to\mathbb{R}
be the linear function joining
(t_{0},x)
and
(2t_{0}+\varepsilon,y)
. As a result of Corollary
2.3
, we have

|\dot{\beta}|=\frac{|y-x|}{t_{0}+\varepsilon}\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1}.

From Lemma
2.5
, using
\beta
as a competitor in the dynamic programming principle, we infer that, up to increasing the value of
C
,

u(t_{0},x)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{2t_{0}+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}(s)|^{2}+m(s,\beta(s))^{\theta}\right)ds+u(2t_{0}+\varepsilon,y)\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{2\alpha-1}+u(2t_{0}+\varepsilon,y).

Thus, (
2.18
) follows from (
2.22
).
The remaining inequalities readily follow from the same idea, while taking into account the sign of
2\alpha-1
. For instance, when
\theta\in(0,2)
,
2\alpha-1>0
, and we may repeat the above argument on the interval
[0,T]
, taking
\beta:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
to be the linear function joining
(0,x)
and
(T,y)
, and letting
S=\{m>0\}
. Then (
2.22
) still holds, and

u(0,x)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}(s)|^{2}+m(s,\beta(s))^{\theta}\right)ds+u(T,y)\leq C(T+\varepsilon)^{2\alpha-1}+u(T,y),

which then implies (
2.19
).

∎

We now obtain a local in time gradient estimate which is sharp as
t\to 0
, by using the well-known displacement convexity formula (compare with
[
17
, Prop. 4.10]
, which gives the sharp rate as
t\to\infty
).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\delta\in(0,T/2)
. For every
t_{0}\in(0,T-\varepsilon-\delta],

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t_{0}+\varepsilon,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\{m(t_{0}+\varepsilon)>0\})}\leq C_{\delta}(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1}

(2.23)

Proof.

Assume first that
t_{0}\in[0,T/2-\varepsilon]
. Let
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,T)
, with
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
,
\zeta\equiv 1
in
[(t_{0}+\varepsilon)/2,3(t_{0}+\varepsilon)/2]
,
\zeta\equiv 0
outside of
((t_{0}+\varepsilon)/4,7(t_{0}+\varepsilon)/4)
, and

|\ddot{\zeta}(t)|\leq\frac{C}{(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{2}},\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.24)

Our starting point is
[
17
, Lem 2.5]
, which yields

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{xx}^{2}\zeta(t)dxdt\leq 4\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}\ddot{\zeta}(t)dxdt\right|.

In view of (
2.13
) and Corollary
2.3
, we obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}\ddot{\zeta}(t)dxdt\right|\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha/2+\alpha-1}.

Combining the last two equations, we then infer that

\int_{t_{0}/2+\varepsilon/2}^{T-t_{0}/2-\varepsilon/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{xx}^{2}dxdt\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha/2-1}.

(2.25)

In particular, there exist
t_{1}\in[(t_{0}+\varepsilon)/2,t_{0}+\varepsilon]
and
t_{2}\in[t_{0}+\varepsilon,3(t_{0}+\varepsilon)/2]
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{xx}^{2}(t_{1},x)dx\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha/2-2},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{xx}^{2}(t_{2},x)dx\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha/2-2}.

(2.26)

We now use the well-known fact that
u
satisfies, in the set
\{m>0\}
, a space-time elliptic equation of the form

-\text{tr}(A(u_{t},u_{x})D^{2}_{tx}u)=0.

(2.27)

In particular, differentiating this equation with respect to
x
, it follows that
u_{x}
satisfies the maximum and minimum principle on the domain
D=\{\text{supp}(m)\}\cap([t_{1},t_{2}]\times\mathbb{R})
. If the maximum of
|u_{x}|
is attained when
x=\gamma_{L}(t)
or
x=\gamma_{R}(t)
, the estimate follows from (
2.2
) and Lemma
2.6
. So it remains to estimate
|u_{x}|
when
t=t_{1}
and
t=t_{2}
. We have

\osc_{[\gamma_{L}(t_{1}),\gamma_{R}(t_{1})]}u_{x}(t_{1},\cdot)\leq\int_{\gamma_{L}(t_{1})}^{\gamma_{R}(t_{1})}|u_{xx}(t_{1},\cdot)|\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{xx}^{2}(t_{1},\cdot)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\gamma_{L}(t_{1})}^{\gamma_{R}(t_{1})}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t_{1},\cdot)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.28)

On the other hand, recalling that,
\tilde{\gamma_{y}}(t,y)=\phi(y)/m(t,\tilde{\gamma}(t,y))
, we obtain from Lemma
2.2
that

\int_{\gamma_{L}(t_{1})}^{\gamma_{R}(t_{1})}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t_{1},\cdot)=\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}(m(t_{1},\tilde{\gamma}(t_{1},y))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\gamma}_{y}(t_{1},y)dy\\
=\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}\phi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\gamma}_{y}(t_{1},x)^{1+\frac{1}{2}}dy\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{3\alpha/2}.

(2.29)

Thus, combining (
2.26
), (
2.28
) and (
2.29
),
we obtain

\osc_{[\gamma_{L}(t_{1}),\gamma_{R}(t_{1})]}u_{x}(t_{1},\cdot)\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1},

(2.30)

so that, from (
2.2
) and Lemma
2.6
,

\|u_{x}(t_{1},\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\{m(t_{1})>0\})}\leq C(t_{0}+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1}.

(2.31)

The same reasoning allows us to estimate
\|u_{x}(t_{2},\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\{m(t_{0}+\varepsilon)>0\})},
which concludes the proof in the case that
t_{0}\in(0,T/2-\varepsilon]
.

Now, if
t_{0}\in(T/2-\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon-\delta]
, we may simply repeat the above argument, using instead a test function
\zeta
such that
\zeta\equiv 1
in
[T/2,t_{0}+\varepsilon+\delta/2]
and
\zeta\equiv 0
outside of
(T/4,t_{0}+\varepsilon+\delta)
, which satisfies

|\ddot{\zeta}|\leq\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}.

This yields a constant
C_{\delta}
such that

\|u_{x}(t_{0}+\varepsilon,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\{m(t_{0}+\varepsilon)>0\})}\leq C_{\delta}.

Up to increasing the value of
C_{\delta}
, this implies (
2.23
).
∎

In general, the function
u^{\varepsilon}
is not expected to be unique (even up to a constant) outside of the support of
m
. We now show that there always exists a sufficiently well-behaved choice for the non-unique values of
u^{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

One may redefine
u^{\varepsilon}
outside of the set
\{m^{\varepsilon}>0\}
in such a way that
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
remains a solution, and

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\{m^{\varepsilon}(t)=0\}})}\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1}.

(2.32)

Proof.

This follows
[
17
, Prop. 6.2]
. By symmetry, it is sufficient to explain how to construct the values of
u(t,x)
in the region
\Omega=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]:x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)\}
.
For concreteness, we assume first that there exists
t^{*}
such that
\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t^{*})=0
. In this case,
t^{*}
is the unique global minimum point of the convex function
\gamma_{L}
. The region
\Omega
is then subdivided into four subregions with non-overlapping interiors:

\Omega_{1}=\{0\leq t\leq t^{*},\,\gamma_{L}(t^{*})\leq x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)\},\quad\Omega_{2}=\{0\leq t\leq t^{*},\,\,x\leq\gamma_{L}(t^{*})\},

(2.33)

\Omega_{3}=\{t^{*}\leq t\leq T,\,\gamma_{L}(t^{*})\leq x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)\},\quad\Omega_{4}=\{t^{*}\leq t\leq T,\,\,x\leq\gamma_{L}(t^{*})\}.

(2.34)

We extend
u
piece by piece from
\text{supp}(m)
to
\text{supp}(m)\cup\Omega
, starting with
\Omega_{1}
. Consider the family
\{L_{t}\}
of line segments

L_{t}(s):=(s,l_{t}(s)):=(s,\gamma_{L}(t)+(s-t)\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t)),\quad s\in[0,t].

(2.35)

Then, by the convexity and monotonicity of
\gamma_{L}
, it follows that
\Omega_{1}
is the disjoint union of these line segments. Recalling that
\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t)=-u_{x}(\gamma_{L}(t),t),
it follows that, through the method of characteristics, one may define the values of
u
on
\Omega_{1}
along the line segments
L_{t}(\cdot)
in terms of the values of
u(t,\gamma_{L}(t))
and
u_{x}(\gamma_{L}(t))
. Note that the regions
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
overlap on the horizontal segment
\{(t,\gamma_{L}(t^{*})):t\in[0,t^{*}]\}
, where
u_{x}\equiv u_{t}\equiv\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t^{*})=0
. Hence, extending
u
continuously to be constant on
\Omega_{2}
produces a
C^{1}
solution to the HJ equation on
\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
. Performing the same construction on
\Omega_{3}
, and then extending
u
to be constant on
\Omega_{4}
, we get a
C^{1}
extension of
u
to all of
\text{supp}(m)\cup\Omega
. The key feature of this solution is that, by construction, at any
(t,x)\in\Omega_{1}
, we have
u_{x}(t,x)=u_{x}(t_{1},\gamma_{L}(t_{1}))=-\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t_{1})
for some
t_{1}\in[t,t^{*}]
and, therefore, using the convexity and monotonicity of
\gamma_{L}
on
[0,t^{*}]
,

|u_{x}(t,x)|\leq|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t_{1})|\leq|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t)|\leq C(t+\varepsilon)^{\alpha-1}.

(2.36)

In the alternate case that
\dot{\gamma}_{L}
never vanishes, the region
\Omega
is instead subdivided into two sub-regions with non-overlapping interiors:

\Omega_{1}=\{0\leq t\leq T,\,l_{T}(t)\leq x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)\},\quad\Omega_{2}=\{0\leq t\leq T,\,\,x\leq l_{T}(t)\}.

(2.37)

In the region
\Omega_{1}
, we define
u(t,x)
through the method of characteristics exactly as before. In the region
\Omega_{2}
, we define
u(t,x)
as a linear function in
x
with slope
u_{x}(t,l_{T}(t))=-\gamma^{\prime}(T)
, namely

u(t,x)=(l_{T}(t)-x)\gamma^{\prime}(T)+u(t,l_{T}(t)).

(2.38)

By construction, this is a
C^{1}
extension of
u
, and it solves the HJ equation classically. Indeed, differentiating (
2.38
) for
(t,x)\in\Omega_{2}
,

u_{t}(t,x)=l_{T}^{\prime}(t)\gamma^{\prime}(T)+u_{t}(t,l_{T}(t))+u_{x}(t,l_{T}(t))l_{T}^{\prime}(t)=\gamma^{\prime}(T)^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}(T)^{2}/2-\gamma^{\prime}(T)^{2}=\gamma^{\prime}(T)^{2}/2=\frac{1}{2}u_{x}(t,x)^{2}.

∎

We end by observing that, locally in time,
Du^{\varepsilon}
is Hölder continuous, uniformly as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\delta>0
, and assume that
0<\varepsilon<\delta
. There exist
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
depending on
\delta
, but not on
\varepsilon
, such that

[Du^{\varepsilon}]_{C^{\alpha}([\delta,T-\delta]\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C.

(2.39)

Proof.

In view of (
2.23
) and (
2.32
),
|u^{\varepsilon}_{x}|
is uniformly bounded on
[\delta,T-\delta]\times\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, from (
2.15
), there exists
\alpha^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that the quantity
[(m^{\varepsilon})^{\theta}]_{C^{\alpha^{\prime}}}
is bounded on
[\delta,T-\delta]\times\mathbb{R}
, uniformly in
\varepsilon
. The result then follows from
[
5
, Thm. 4.23]
.

∎

2.3
Existence of a solution

Let us start by making precise the notion of solution to (
1.1
) that will be used.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that
(u,m)\in C^{1}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})\times C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))

is a solution to (
1.1
)
if

(i)

u
is a classical solution to the HJ equation

-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}=m^{\theta}\,\,\,\,\,\,(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},

(ii)

m
satisfies the continuity equation

m_{t}-(mu_{x})_{x}=0\,\,\,\,\,\,(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},

in the distributional sense, with
m(0,\cdot)=\delta_{0}
and
m(T,\cdot)=m_{T}
.

Having obtained basic estimates on the sequence
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
, we are now ready to prove the existence result by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
be the solution to (
2.1
), with initial condition
m^{\varepsilon}_{0}
, which satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\varepsilon}(T)m_{T}=0
. Up to a subsequence, the functions
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
converge locally uniformly to a solution
(u,m)
to (
1.1
). Furthermore, there exists
\tilde{\gamma}:(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}\to\tilde{\gamma}
locally uniformly,

\tilde{\gamma}_{y}(t,y)=\frac{\phi(y)}{m(t,\tilde{\gamma}(t,y))},

(2.40)

m(t)=\tilde{\gamma}(t,\cdot)_{\sharp}\phi,\qquad\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(t,y)=-u_{x}(t,\tilde{\gamma}(t,y)),

(2.41)

and

\tilde{\gamma}_{tt}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{(\tilde{\gamma}_{y})^{2+\theta}}\tilde{\gamma}_{yy}=\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(\tilde{\gamma}_{y})^{\theta+1}}\qquad\text{in}\quad(0,T)\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}).

(2.42)

Proof.

Since
u^{\varepsilon}
is only unique in
\{m^{\varepsilon}>0\}
, we may choose it in such a way that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\varepsilon}(T)m_{T}=0
. Furthermore, thanks to Lemma
2.9
, we may also assume that (
2.32
) holds. From Lemma
2.5
,
m^{\varepsilon}
is bounded and uniformly Hölder continuous, locally in time. On the other hand, from Lemmas
2.7
and
2.10
the function
u^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded, locally in time, and its space-time gradient is uniformly Hölder continuous, locally in time. It follows readily from the Arzelá-Ascoli theorem that a subsequence of
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
converges to some limit
(u,m)\in C^{1}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})\times C((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})
which solves (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.11
. The convergence is locally uniform in time and space, but
m^{\varepsilon}(t)
also converges in
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R})
uniformly in time, which justifies the initial and final traces assumed by
m
.

In view of (
2.2
) and Lemma
2.2
, the space-time gradient of
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
is locally bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Therefore, since
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{y}
is also bounded below and
\tilde{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
satisfies the elliptic equation (

), it follows that

is locally bounded in

, for any

. Thus, up to a subsequence, (

), (

), and (
2.42
) follow by letting

in (

), (

) and (

).

∎

Since the solution
(u,m)
obtained in Proposition

is the subsequential limit of

, we may also let

in the estimates of the previous subsections. In particular, we have the bounds

\underset{\{t\in[t_{0},2t_{0}],m(t)>0\}}{\text{osc}}(u)\leq Ct_{0}^{2\alpha-1}.

(2.43)

\|u_{x}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C_{\delta}t^{\alpha-1},\quad t\in(0,T-\delta],

(2.44)

|\tilde{\gamma}(t,y)|\leq Ct^{\alpha},\qquad C^{-1}t^{\alpha}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{y}(t,y)\leq Ct^{\alpha},\qquad|\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(t,y)|\leq Ct^{\alpha-1},

(2.45)

\|m(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq Ct^{-\alpha}.

(2.46)

We now show that if

, then the solution

can be shown to be unique by the standard Lasry-Lions argument.
Note that the case
\theta=1
considered in

]

Theorem 2.13
.

Assume that
\theta\in(0,2).
There exists a solution
(u,m)
to (
1.1
) such that

is globally bounded and

\|m(t)\|_{\theta+1}^{\theta+1}\leq Ct^{-\alpha\theta},\quad t\in(0,T].

(2.47)

Furthermore, under these conditions,
m
is uniquely determined, and
u
is unique in the set
\{m>0\}
up to a constant.

Proof.

We note first that the existence follows from Proposition
2.12
and Lemma
2.7
. Indeed,
u^{\varepsilon}
satisfies

and is thus globally bounded as

due to (

) follows from (

).

Note that, in view of (

), the right hand side of the HJ equation is bounded in

The uniqueness result then readily follows from the standard Lasry-Lions argument, in view of the boundedness and continuity of

∎

3
Local behavior near the singularity

The aim of this section is now to understand the behavior, as
t\to 0
, of the solution
m(t)
built previously. First, we consider the general case (
\theta>0
) and we prove the convergence to the self-similar profile, as well as uniqueness in the critical case
\theta=2
. Next, we will focus on the case
\theta>2
: we quantify the rate of convergence and we use this property to infer the uniqueness result, in a suitable class of solutions, for this super-critical range.

3.1
Convergence to the self-similar profile

Throughout this section, we consider the solution
(u,m)
which was built in Proposition
2.12
as a subsequential limit of solutions
(u^{\varepsilon},m^{\varepsilon})
of (
2.1
). Our first goal will be to show the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

As
t\to 0^{+}
, the map
x\to t^{\alpha}m(t,t^{\alpha}x)
converges, in the
L^{\infty}-
weak-
*
sense, to the self-similar profile
\phi
.

The proof will be achieved after several steps. Following
[
17
]
we use a continuous rescaling by setting

t=e^{\tau},\;x=t^{\alpha}\eta,

making the change of variables

\mu(\tau,\eta)=t^{\alpha}m(t,x),\;\quad v(\tau,\eta)=\begin{cases}t^{1-2\alpha}u(t,x),&\hbox{if $\theta\in(0,2)$}\cr t^{1-2\alpha}(u(t,x)-u(0,0))&\hbox{if $\theta\geq 2$},\end{cases}

and defining

w(\tau,\eta)=v(\tau,\eta)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\eta^{2}.

Then
(w,\mu)
solves

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-w_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}|w_{\eta}|^{2}=\mu^{\theta}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}+(2\alpha-1)w&\qquad\text{in}\;(-\infty,\ln(T))\times\mathbb{R}\\
\displaystyle\mu_{\tau}-(\mu w_{\eta})_{\eta}=0&\qquad\text{in}\;(-\infty,\ln(T))\times\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(3.1)

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R>0
. There exists a constant
C_{R}>0
such that, for
\tau\in(-\infty,\ln(T)]
,

\osc_{[-R,R]}(w(\tau))\leq C_{R}.

(3.2)

Proof.

We write
t=e^{\tau}
. From (
2.43
), we have

\osc_{m(t)>0}(u(t))\leq Ct^{2\alpha-1}.

(3.3)

But then, from (
2.45
) and (
2.32
), we get

\osc_{[-Rt^{\alpha},Rt^{\alpha}]}(u(t))\leq C(1+R)t^{2\alpha-1},

(3.4)

which implies the result.
∎

Following
[
17
]
, we now introduce the functional

\mathcal{H}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu(\tau)|w_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}-\left(\frac{\mu^{\theta+1}(\tau)}{\theta+1}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\mu(\tau)\right)\right)d\eta-\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\theta+1}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}R_{\alpha}^{2}.

(3.5)

We note for later use that, as
\phi^{\theta}(\eta)=\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(R_{\alpha}^{2}-\eta^{2})_{+}
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu(\tau)|w_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}-\right.&\left.\left(\frac{\mu^{\theta+1}(\tau)}{\theta+1}-\frac{\phi^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1}-\phi^{\theta}(\eta)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right)\right)d\eta\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{|\eta|\geq R_{\alpha}}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(R_{\alpha}^{2}-\eta^{2})\mu(\tau)d\eta.\end{split}

(3.6)

Hence
\mathcal{H}(\tau)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}\mu(\tau)|w_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}
.
Moreover (this is exactly the computation in
[
17
, Lem. 3.7]
),

\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathcal{H}(\tau)=-(2\alpha-1)\int\mu|w_{\eta}|^{2}\,d\eta\,,

(3.7)

so that
t\to\mathcal{H}(t)
is monotone. We now show that
\mathcal{H}
is bounded. By Corollary
2.3
and (
2.13
), the term

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta^{2}\mu(\tau,\eta)d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{-2\alpha}x^{2}m(t,x)dx

is bounded. We also have

\int\mu^{\theta+1}d\eta=t^{\alpha(\theta+1)}\int m^{\theta+1}(t,t^{\alpha}\eta)d\eta=t^{\alpha\theta}\int m^{\theta+1}(t,x)dx

which is bounded by (
2.14
). In the same way

\displaystyle\int\mu|w_{\eta}|^{2}d\eta

\displaystyle=\int\mu(\tau,\eta)\left|t^{1-\alpha}u_{x}(t,t^{\alpha}\eta)+\alpha\eta\right|^{2}d\eta

\displaystyle\leq 2t^{2-2\alpha}\int\mu(\tau,\eta)|u_{x}(t,t^{\alpha}\eta)|^{2}d\eta+2\alpha^{2}\int\eta^{2}\mu(\tau,\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle\leq 2t^{2-2\alpha}\int m(t,x)|u_{x}(t,x)|^{2}dx+2\alpha^{2}\int\eta^{2}\mu(\tau,\eta)d\eta

which is bounded as well (using (
2.44
) for the first term).
Thus
\mathcal{H}
, being monotone and bounded, has a limit as
\tau\to-\infty
.

Assume that
\theta\neq 2
. Then,
2\alpha-1\neq 0
, so that, from (
3.7
), we obtain

\lim_{\tau_{0}\to-\infty}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu|w_{\eta}|^{2}d\eta=0.

(3.8)

Let
\tau_{n}\to-\infty
, and set
\mu_{n}(\tau,\eta):=\mu(\tau+\tau_{n},\eta)
. We know that
\mu_{n}
is bounded.
As a consequence,
\mu_{n}
converges in
L^{\infty}-
weak-
*
to some
\bar{\mu}
along a subsequence that we denote in the same way and that we fix from now on. Our goal is to check that
\bar{\mu}
equals
\phi
, which will prove the result. Note that, by the equation satisfied by
\mu_{n}
and (
3.8
),
\bar{\mu}
is constant in time.

We come back to the optimal trajectories and we set

\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)=t^{-\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}(t,y),

so that

\tilde{\gamma}(t,y)=t^{\alpha}\hat{\gamma}(\ln(t),y).

Hence

\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(t,y)=\alpha t^{\alpha-1}\hat{\gamma}(\ln(t),y)+t^{\alpha-1}\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\ln(t),y)

and

\tilde{\gamma}_{tt}(t,y)=\alpha(\alpha-1)t^{\alpha-2}\hat{\gamma}(\ln(t),y)+(2\alpha-1)t^{\alpha-2}\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\ln(t),y)+t^{\alpha-2}\hat{\gamma}_{\tau\tau}(\ln(t),y)

Recalling the equation (
2.42
) satisfied by
\tilde{\gamma}
, we get:

\alpha(\alpha-1)\hat{\gamma}+(2\alpha-1)\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}+\hat{\gamma}_{\tau\tau}+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{(\hat{\gamma}_{y})^{2+\theta}}\hat{\gamma}_{yy}=\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(\hat{\gamma}_{y})^{\theta+1}}\qquad\text{in}\;(-\infty,\ln(T))\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}),

(3.9)

because
\alpha-2=-(\theta+1)\alpha
. Note that
\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)=y
is a solution. The goal is now to show that
\hat{\gamma}(\tau_{n},\cdot)
converges to
y
as
\tau\to-\infty
. Note that, by (
2.45
), we have

|\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)|\leq C,\qquad C^{-1}\leq\hat{\gamma}_{y}(\tau,y)\leq C.

(3.10)

Next we claim:

Lemma 3.3
.

We have

\lim_{\tau_{0}\to-\infty}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+1}\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}|\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\tau,y)|^{2}\phi(y)dyd\tau=0.

Proof.

Recalling that
\gamma_{t}(t,x)=-u_{x}(t,\gamma(t,x))
, we get
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(t,y)=-u_{x}(t,\tilde{\gamma}(t,y))
. On the other hand

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(t,y)

\displaystyle=\alpha t^{\alpha-1}\hat{\gamma}(\ln(t),y)+t^{\alpha-1}\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\ln(t),y)

\displaystyle=t^{\alpha-1}\left(\alpha\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)+\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\tau,y)\right)

while

u_{x}(t,x)=t^{2\alpha-1}(v(\ln(t),t^{-\alpha}x)))_{x}=t^{\alpha-1}v_{\eta}(\ln(t),t^{-\alpha}x)

and thus

u_{x}(t,\tilde{\gamma}(t,y))=t^{\alpha-1}v_{\eta}(\ln(t),t^{-\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}(t,y))=t^{\alpha-1}v_{\eta}(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)).

Hence we deduce that

\alpha\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)+\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\tau,y)=-v_{\eta}(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)),

and thus

\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\tau,y)=-w_{\eta}(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)).

Therefore, recalling (
2.40
) and the definition of
\hat{\gamma}
, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau_{0}\to-\infty}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\eta}|^{2}\mu=\lim_{\tau_{0}\to-\infty}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+1}\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}|w_{\eta}(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))|^{2}\phi(y)dyd\tau

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau_{0}\to-\infty}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+1}\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}|\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\tau,y)|^{2}\phi(y)dyd\tau.

∎

As
\hat{\gamma}(\tau_{n},\cdot)
is increasing and bounded, it converges up to a subsequence (denoted in the same way) in
L^{1}_{loc}((-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}))
and a.e. to some limit
\xi
which is also increasing.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have the equality
\bar{\mu}=\xi_{\sharp}\phi
and
\xi
is a classical solution to

\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2}(\xi^{2})_{y}-\left(\frac{\phi^{\theta}}{(\xi_{y})^{\theta}}\right)_{y}=0\qquad\text{in}\;(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}),

(3.11)

Proof.

For any test function
f\in C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\eta)\bar{\mu}(\eta)d\eta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\eta)\mu_{n}(\tau,\eta)d\eta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}f(\hat{\gamma}(\tau_{n},y))\phi(y)dy=\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}f(\xi(y))\phi(y)dy.

As we are in 1D and
\xi
is increasing, the relation
\bar{\mu}=\xi_{\sharp}\phi
implies that
\xi
is the unique optimal transport from
\phi
to
\bar{\mu}
(see e.g.
[
20
, Theorem 2.5]
).
Therefore the whole limit
\hat{\gamma}_{n}:=\hat{\gamma}(\tau_{n}+\cdot,\cdot)
converges locally uniformly to
\xi
. Note that, for any
\delta>0
,
\hat{\gamma}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}
are bounded, and
\hat{\gamma}_{y}
is bounded from above and below in
(-\infty,\ln(T-\delta))\times(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})
. Thus by the uniformly elliptic equation satisfied by
\hat{\gamma}
, we have
C^{2+\beta}
bounds for
\hat{\gamma}
, these bounds being local in space but global in time. Therefore
(\hat{\gamma}_{n})_{\tau}
and
(\hat{\gamma}_{n})_{\tau\tau}
tend locally uniformly to
0
and, recalling (
3.9
), the limit
\xi
of
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
is a classical solution to

0=\alpha(\alpha-1)\xi+\frac{\theta\phi^{\theta}}{(\xi_{y})^{2+\theta}}\xi_{yy}-\frac{(\phi^{\theta})_{y}}{(\xi_{y})^{\theta+1}}=\alpha(\alpha-1)\xi-\frac{1}{\xi_{y}}\left(\frac{\phi^{\theta}}{(\xi_{y})^{\theta}}\right)_{y}\qquad\text{in}\;(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}),

which can be rewritten as (
3.11
).
∎

We now prove the key statement:

Lemma 3.5
.

We have
\xi(y)=y
in
[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}]
.

Proof.

By (
3.11
) there is a positive constant
c_{1}
such that

\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2}\xi^{2}(y)=\frac{\phi^{\theta}(y)}{(\xi_{y}(y))^{\theta}}+c_{1}\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)R_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}.

This can be rewritten as

\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)c_{1}}{2}\left(R_{\alpha}^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{c_{1}}\right)=\frac{\phi^{\theta}(y)}{(\xi_{y}(y))^{\theta}}\,.

This equality implies that
|\xi|\leq R_{\alpha}c_{1}^{1/2}
and, by definition of
\phi
, it reads as

(c_{1})^{1/\theta}\phi(c_{1}^{-1/2}\xi(y))=\frac{\phi(y)}{\xi_{y}(y)}.

(3.12)

By the bound below in (
3.10
), we have that
\xi_{y}
is bounded below. Hence we can take
y=\pm R_{\alpha}
in the equality above to infer that
\phi(c_{1}^{-1/2}\xi(\pm R_{\alpha}))=0
. This implies, as
\xi
is increasing:

c_{1}^{-1/2}\xi(R_{\alpha})=R_{\alpha},\qquad c_{1}^{-1/2}\xi(-R_{\alpha})=-R_{\alpha}.

Let
\Phi
be the antiderivative of
\phi
which vanishes at
0
. Then integrating Equation (
3.12
) yields the existence of a constant
c_{2}
such that

(c_{1})^{1/\theta+1/2}\Phi(c_{1}^{-1/2}\xi(y))=\Phi(y)+c_{2}.

Then, as
\Phi(R_{\alpha})=-\Phi(-R_{\alpha})=1/2
, we get

\frac{1}{2}(c_{1})^{1/\theta+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}+c_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\frac{1}{2}(c_{1})^{1/\theta+1/2}=-\frac{1}{2}+c_{2}

so that
c_{2}=0
and
c_{1}=1
. Hence
\xi(y)=y
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

In the case
\theta\neq 2
, we have proved that, along the subsequence
\tau_{n}\to-\infty
, the map
\mu(\tau_{n}+\cdot,\cdot)
converges in
L^{\infty}-
weak-
*
to
\xi_{\sharp}\phi=\phi
. As the subsequence is arbitrary, this shows the whole convergence of
\mu
to
\phi
as
\tau\to-\infty
.

We now treat the critical case
\theta=2
, namely when
2\alpha-1=0
. For
\tau\in(-\infty,\ln(T))
, we set

f(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu-\phi)w.

(3.13)

In view of (
2.46
), (
3.2
), and (
3.10
),
f
is bounded. The Lasry-Lions monotonicity argument, applied to (
3.1
), gives

f^{\prime}(\tau)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mu+\phi)w_{\eta}^{2}+\left(\mu^{2}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(R_{\alpha}^{2}-\eta^{2})\right)(\mu-\phi)\right)\,.

(3.14)

Recalling the definition of
\phi
, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mu^{2}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(R_{\alpha}^{2}-\eta^{2})\right)(\mu-\phi)\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mu^{2}-\phi^{2}\right)(\mu-\phi)\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu+\phi)(\mu-\phi)^{2}.

(3.15)

In particular,
f
is non-increasing. Thus,
f(\tau)
has a limit as
\tau\to-\infty
. We then have, for every
\tau_{0}\in(-\infty,\ln(T))
,

\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\int_{\tau+\tau_{0}-1}^{\tau+\tau_{0}}\left(\mu^{2}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(R_{\alpha}^{2}-\eta^{2})\right)(\mu-\phi)=0.

(3.16)

As in the case
\theta\neq 2
, this characterizes the limit as
\overline{\mu}=\phi
(and in fact, as a pointwise almost everywhere limit).

∎

We can now show the well-posedness result for the critical case
\theta=2
. In the following Theorem,
d_{1}
denotes the standard Wasserstein 1-distance in the space of probability measures (see
[

, Sec. 3.1]
).

Theorem 3.6
.

When
\theta=2
, there exists a unique solution such that (
2.44
) and (
2.46
) hold, and the convergence of Theorem
3.1
holds in the
d_{1}
sense.

Proof.

Existence was already shown in Proposition
2.12
. Indeed, in view of (
2.45
), the functions

have a uniformly bounded support, so the
L^{\infty}
weak-* convergence of Theorem
3.1
implies
d_{1}
convergence. To show uniqueness, let
(w,\mu)
and
(\tilde{w},\tilde{\mu})
be two solutions satisfying the assumptions of the theorem. By Lasry-Lions’ argument, we have, for
\tau\in(-\infty,\ln(T))
,

-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu(\tau,\cdot)-\tilde{\mu}(\tau,\cdot))(w(\tau,\cdot)-\tilde{w}(\tau,\cdot))+\int_{\tau}^{\ln(T)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mu+\tilde{\mu})|w_{\eta}-\tilde{w}_{\eta}|^{2}+(\mu^{\theta}-\tilde{\mu}^{\theta})(\mu-\tilde{\mu})\right)=0.

(3.17)

To conclude, it is enough to show that
\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}f(\tau)=0
, where

f(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu(\tau,\cdot)-\tilde{\mu}(\tau,\cdot))(w(\tau,\cdot)-\tilde{w}(\tau,\cdot)).

(3.18)

Indeed, we have, by (
2.44
), (
2.46
), and the convergence of
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
,

|f(\tau)|\leq d_{1}(\mu(\tau,\cdot),\tilde{\mu}(\tau,\cdot))(\|w_{\eta}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{w}_{\eta}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{\infty})\to 0

(3.19)

as
\tau\to-\infty
.
∎

3.2
Convergence rate in the case
\theta>2

From now on we focus on the case
\theta>2
. As in the previous section, we still assume here that
(u,m)
is the particular solution constructed in Proposition
2.12
. The goal is to find a convergence rate as
t\to 0^{+}
for the solution built in Section
2
. This will allow us to define a suitable class of solutions where both existence and uniqueness for (
1.1
) can be obtained. We first estimate the growth rate for the Lyapunov functional
\mathcal{H}(\tau)
defined in (
3.5
).

Lemma 3.7
.

Set
\kappa=1-2\alpha>0
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for
\tau\leq 0
,

0\leq\mathcal{H}(\tau)\leq Ce^{2\kappa\tau}.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{H}(\tau)\leq(1/2)\int|w_{\eta}|^{2}\mu(\tau)
and, by (
3.7
),

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(\tau)=\kappa\int|w_{\eta}|^{2}\mu(\tau)
, we get
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(\tau)\geq 2\kappa\mathcal{H}(\tau),

and thus
\mathcal{H}(\tau)\leq e^{2\kappa\tau}\mathcal{H}(0).

On the other hand, by (
3.6
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\tau)

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\mu^{\theta+1}(\tau)}{\theta+1}-\frac{\phi^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1}-\phi^{\theta}(\eta)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right)d\eta-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(\eta^{2}-R_{\alpha}^{2})_{+}\mu(\tau)d\eta,

where
\mu(\tau)
converges to
\phi
. Hence

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\mathcal{H}(\tau)

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(\eta^{2}-R_{\alpha}^{2})_{+}\phi\,d\eta=0.

Thus
\mathcal{H}\geq 0
.
∎

Next we estimate the convergence rate in the
2-
Wasserstein distance (see
[
20
, Chap. 5]
).

Lemma 3.8
.

We have

{\bf d}_{2}(\mu(\tau),\phi)\leq Ce^{\kappa\tau}.

Proof.

For any
r\in(0,2\kappa)
and any
\tau_{0}<\tau
, we have

\displaystyle{\bf d}_{2}^{2}(\mu(\tau),\mu(\tau_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)-\hat{\gamma}(\tau_{0},y)|^{2}\phi(y)dy\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\sigma,y)d\sigma\right|^{2}\phi(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{r\sigma}d\sigma\right)\left(\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{-r\sigma}|\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\sigma,y)|^{2}d\sigma\right)\phi(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{r\tau}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-r\sigma}|w_{\eta}(\sigma,\hat{\gamma}_{\tau}(\sigma,y))|^{2}\phi(y)dyd\sigma

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{r\tau}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{-r\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\eta}(\sigma,\eta)|^{2}\mu(\sigma,\eta)d\eta d\sigma.

Recalling (
3.7
) we obtain

\displaystyle{\bf d}_{2}^{2}(\mu(\tau),\mu(\tau_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{r\tau}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{-r\sigma}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(\sigma)d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{r\tau}\left(e^{-r\tau}\mathcal{H}(\tau)-e^{-r\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}(\tau_{0})+r\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{-r\sigma}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)d\sigma\right).

Using Lemma
3.7
, and the fact that
\mathcal{H}\geq 0
, we get therefore

\displaystyle{\bf d}_{2}^{2}(\mu(\tau),\mu(\tau_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{r\tau}\left(e^{(2\kappa-r)\tau}-e^{-r\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}(\tau_{0})+r\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{(2\kappa-r)\sigma}d\sigma\right)\leq Ce^{2\kappa\tau}.

We obtain the result by letting
\tau_{0}\to-\infty
.
∎

Let
f
be a locally Lipschitz map. We now use the
d_{2}
–bound to estimate
\int f(\mu(\tau)-\phi)
in function of
\int|f_{\eta}|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\phi)
.

Lemma 3.9
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
f
, such that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right|\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{\eta}|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\phi)\right)^{1/2}e^{\kappa\tau}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))-f(\tau,y))\phi(y)dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\eta}(\tau,(1-\lambda)y+\lambda\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))(\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)-y)\phi(y)d\lambda dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\eta}(\tau,(1-\lambda)y+\lambda\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))^{2}\phi(y)d\lambda dy\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)-y)^{2}\phi(y)dy\right)^{1/2}

As
\hat{\gamma}(\tau,\cdot)
is the unique optimal transport from
\phi
to
\mu(\tau)
, the last term in the right-hand side is just
{\bf d}_{2}(\mu(\tau),\phi)
. We claim that there exists
\tau_{0}
such that, for any
\tau<\tau_{0}
and any non-negative map
g
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g((1-\lambda)y+\lambda\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))\phi(y)dy\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\mu(\tau)+\phi),

(3.20)

where
C
is independent of
\lambda
,
\tau
and
g
. Note that this implies that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\phi)\right)^{1/2}{\bf d}_{2}(\mu(\tau),\phi)\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\phi)\right)^{1/2}e^{\kappa\tau},

where we used Lemma
3.8
in the last inequality. This completes the proof of the lemma.

We now check that (
3.20
) holds. Let us choose
\tau_{0}
so small that
|\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)-y|\leq R_{\alpha}/4
for any
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
and
y\in[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}]
. Let us set
T_{\lambda}(y)=(1-\lambda)y+\lambda\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)
. Note that
T_{\lambda}
is bi-Lipschitz from
(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})
to its image. Hence

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g((1-\lambda)y+\lambda\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))\phi(y)dy\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\phi(T^{-1}_{\lambda}(x))dy.

We now check that

\phi(T^{-1}_{\lambda}(x))\leq C\max\{\phi(x),\mu(\tau,x)\},

where
C
is independent of
\lambda
and
x
. For proving that claim, we note that, by (
3.10
) and (
2.41
), for any
y\in(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})
,

\mu(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))\geq C^{-1}\phi(y),

so that

\mu(\tau,x)\geq C^{-1}\phi(\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,x))

(3.21)

where
\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,\cdot)
is the inverse of
\hat{\gamma}(\tau,\cdot)
with respect to the last variable. Hence we just have to prove that

\phi(T^{-1}_{\lambda}(x))\leq C\max\{\phi(x),\phi(\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,x))\}.

(3.22)

Set
y=T^{-1}_{\lambda}(x)
and assume (to fix the ideas) that
y<x
. If
x\leq 0
, then, as
\phi
is nondecreasing on
(-\infty,0]
,
\phi(y)\leq\phi(x)
. If now
x\in[0,R_{\alpha}/2]
, then, as
\phi
is nonincreasing on
[0,\infty)
and
\phi(R_{\alpha}/2)>0
,

\phi(x)\geq\phi(R_{\alpha}/2)\geq\phi(R_{\alpha}/2)\frac{\phi(y)}{\|\phi\|_{\infty}},

so that

\phi(y)\leq\frac{\|\phi\|_{\infty}}{\phi(R_{\alpha}/2)}\phi(x).

Finally, let us assume that
x\geq R_{\alpha}/2
. Note that, by the definition of
\tau_{0}
,
|\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,x)-x|\leq R_{\alpha}/4
and thus
\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,x)\geq R_{\alpha}/4
. On the other hand, as by assumption
x>y
, we have
x=(1-\lambda)y+\lambda\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)>y,

so that
\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y)>y
. Hence
x\in(y,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))
. This implies that
\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,x)<y
. As
\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,y)\geq 0
and
\phi
is nonincreasing on
[0,\infty)
, we obtain that
\phi(\hat{\gamma}^{-1}(\tau,x))\geq\phi(y)
, which concludes the proof of (
3.22
). Hence (
3.20
) is proved.
∎

With the above result, we can now obtain a precise estimate of the convergence rate for a key term that appears in the Lasry-Lions uniqueness argument.

Lemma 3.10
.

We have

-Ce^{2\kappa\tau}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\tau)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\leq 0.

(3.23)

In particular,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\tau)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)=O(e^{2\kappa\tau}).

(3.24)

Proof.

By Lasry-Lions’ argument applied to system (
3.1
), we have

-\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(e^{-\kappa\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\tau)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right)\\
=e^{-\kappa\tau}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|w_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\phi)+\int_{\phi>0}(\mu^{\theta}(\tau)-\phi^{\theta})(\mu(\tau)-\phi)+\int_{\phi=0}\mu\left(\mu^{\theta}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}(\eta^{2}-R_{\alpha}^{2})\right)\right)\\
\geq e^{-\kappa\tau}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu+\phi}{2}w_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu-\phi)(\mu^{\theta}-\phi^{\theta})\right).

(3.25)

By Lemma
3.9
the right-hand side is bounded below by
C^{-1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\tau)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right|^{2}e^{-3\kappa\tau}
. Let us set

\xi(\tau):=e^{-\kappa\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\tau)(\mu(\tau)-\phi)

and note that
\xi
is nonincreasing. Then
\xi
satisfies the ODE

-\xi^{\prime}(\tau)\geq C^{-1}(\xi(\tau))^{2}e^{-\kappa\tau}.

Integrating this equation between
\tau<0
and
0
, we get

\frac{1}{\xi(0)}-\frac{1}{\xi(\tau)}\geq C^{-1}(e^{-\kappa\tau}-1).

This implies that
\xi(\tau)\to 0^{-}
as
\tau\to-\infty
and, as
\xi
is nonincreasing, that
\xi
is negative. Hence

-Ce^{\kappa\tau}\leq\xi(\tau)\leq 0.

We obtain the lemma by the definition of
\xi
.
∎

The last estimate needed to characterize the solutions is the following non-degeneracy integral estimate for
\mu
in its support.

Proposition 3.11
.

For each
\tau\in(-\infty,\ln(T))
, let
[a(\tau),b(\tau)]
be the smallest closed interval containing
\{\mu(\tau)>0\}.
Then, for some constant
C
independent of
\tau
,

\int_{a(\tau)}^{b(\tau)}\frac{1}{\mu^{\theta-1}(\tau,\eta)}d\eta\leq C.

(3.26)

Proof.

This is a consequence of (
3.10
), together with the fact that, given that
\theta>2
,
\phi
satisfies

\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{\phi^{\theta-1}(y)}dy<\infty.

(3.27)

Indeed, note that, from (
2.40
), we have

\hat{\gamma}_{y}(\tau,y)=\frac{\phi(y)}{\mu(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))}.

(3.28)

Note that
a(\tau)=\hat{\gamma}(\tau,-R_{\alpha})
, and
b(\tau)=\hat{\gamma}(\tau,R_{\alpha})
. Thus, switching to Lagrangian coordinates, and using (
3.10
), we obtain

\int_{a(\tau)}^{b(\tau)}\frac{1}{\mu(\tau,\eta)^{\theta-1}}d\eta=\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{y}(\tau,y)}{\mu(\tau,\hat{\gamma}(\tau,y))^{\theta-1}}dy\leq C\int_{-R_{\alpha}}^{R_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{\phi(y)^{\theta-1}}dy<\infty.

(3.29)

∎

3.3
Uniqueness in the case
\theta>2

As it turns out, conditions (
3.2
), (
3.24
), and (
3.26
), as well as a weaker version of (
2.10
), are sufficient to guarantee uniqueness. In terms of the original solution
(u,m)
, they can be rewritten as

\osc_{[-Rt^{\alpha},Rt^{\alpha}]}(u(t))\leq C_{R}t^{2\alpha-1},\,\,R>0,

(3.30)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u(x,t)+\alpha x^{2}/t)(m(x,t)-t^{-\alpha}\phi(t^{-\alpha}x))dx=O(t^{\kappa}),

(3.31)

\int_{\overline{a}(t)}^{\overline{b}(t)}\frac{1}{m^{\theta-1}(x,t)}dx\leq Ct^{2-2\alpha},\quad and\quad|\overline{a}(t)|+|\overline{b}(t)|\leq Ct^{\alpha}

(3.32)

where
[\overline{a}(t),\overline{b}(t)]
is the smallest closed interval containing
\{m(t)>0\}
.

We prove now that there exists a unique solution satisfying the above conditions.

Theorem 3.12
.

When
\theta>2
, there exists a unique solution to (
1.1
) satisfying (
3.30
), (
3.31
), and (
3.32
).

Proof.

The existence follows from Proposition
2.12
and the analysis of Subsection
3.2
, so we focus on uniqueness. Note that the condition on
|\overline{a}(t)|+|\overline{b}(t)|
simply means that the support of
\mu(\tau)
is uniformly bounded as
\tau\to-\infty.
Let
(w,\mu),(w_{1},\mu_{1})
be two solutions satisfying the assumptions.
Integrating (
3.25
), we have, for each
\tau_{0}<0
,

\int_{\tau_{0}-1}^{\tau_{0}}e^{-\kappa\tau}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\phi)+(\mu^{\theta}(\tau)-\phi^{\theta})(\mu(\tau)-\phi)\right)d\tau=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{0}}).

(3.33)

The same computation is valid for
(w_{1},\mu_{1})
. Thus, there exists a sequence
\tau_{n}\to-\infty
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}|w_{\eta}(\tau_{n})|^{2}(\mu(\tau_{n})+\phi)+(\mu^{\theta}(\tau_{n})-\phi^{\theta})(\mu(\tau_{n})-\phi)\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}|(w_{1})_{\eta}(\tau_{n})|^{2}(\mu_{1}(\tau_{n})+\phi)+(\mu_{1}^{\theta}(\tau_{n})-\phi^{\theta})(\mu_{1}(\tau_{n})-\phi)=O(e^{2\kappa\tau_{n}}).

(3.34)

Let the intervals
[a(\tau),b(\tau)]
and
[a_{1}(\tau),b_{1}(\tau)]
be defined according to Proposition
3.11
, corresponding to
\mu
and
\mu_{1}
, respectively. In view of (
3.26
) and (
3.27
), together with the fact that
\theta-1>1
, we have

\int_{[a(\tau),b(\tau)]\cup[a_{1}(\tau),b_{1}(\tau)]}\frac{1}{\mu(\tau)+\mu_{1}(\tau)}+\int_{[a(\tau),b(\tau)]\cup[-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha}]}\frac{1}{\mu(\tau)^{\theta-1}+\phi^{\theta-1}}=O(1).

(3.35)

Thus, (
3.34
) yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu(\tau_{n})-\phi|\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu(\tau_{n})^{\theta-1}+\phi^{\theta-1})(\mu(\tau_{n})-\phi)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}}),

(3.36)

and, similarly,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu_{1}(\tau_{n})-\phi|=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}}).

(3.37)

We thus have, in view of (
3.2
) and the bounded support of
\mu
,
\mu_{1}
and
\phi
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(w(\tau_{n})-w_{1}(\tau_{n}))(\mu(\tau_{n})-\mu_{1}(\tau_{n}))=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}}).

(3.38)

Thus, by Lasry-Lions argument,

\int_{\tau_{n}-1}^{\tau_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\eta}(\tau)-(w_{1})_{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\mu_{1}(\tau))e^{-\kappa\tau}d\tau=O(1)

(3.39)

Noting that (
3.33
) holds for
\tau_{0}=\tau_{n}
, we may in fact replace
\tau_{n}
by a new sequence, not relabeled, such that (
3.34
) still holds, and simultaneously, thanks to (
3.39
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\eta}(\tau_{n})-(w_{1})_{\eta}(\tau_{n})|^{2}(\mu(\tau_{n})+\mu_{1}(\tau_{n}))=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}}).

(3.40)

Let

S(\tau)=[a(\tau),b(\tau)]\cup[a_{1}(\tau),b_{1}(\tau)].

(3.41)

Recall that
\phi
is continuous, and strictly positive in
(-R_{\alpha},R_{\alpha})
. Thus, in view of (
3.36
) and (
3.37
), we must have
0\in[a(\tau_{n}),b(\tau_{n})]\cap[a_{1}(\tau_{n}),b_{1}(\tau_{n})]
for
n
sufficiently large. In particular, we deduce that
S(\tau_{n})
is an interval for
n
sufficiently large. As a result, (
3.35
) and (
3.40
) imply that, for some constant
C
only depending on the size of the support of
\mu,\mu_{1}
,

\osc_{S(\tau_{n})}(w(\tau_{n})-w_{1}(\tau_{n}))\leq C\int_{S(\tau_{n})}|(w(\tau_{n})-w_{1}(\tau_{n}))_{\eta}|\\
\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\eta}(\tau_{n})-(w_{1})_{\eta}(\tau_{n})|^{2}(\mu(\tau_{n})+\mu_{1}(\tau_{n}))\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{S(\tau_{n})}\frac{1}{\mu(\tau_{n})+\mu_{1}(\tau_{n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}/2}).

(3.42)

Then, again by Lasry-Lions argument, we obtain from (
3.36
), (
3.37
), and (
3.42
) that

\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\ln(T)}e^{-\kappa\tau}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|w_{\eta}(\tau)-(w_{1})_{\eta}|^{2}(\mu(\tau)+\mu_{1}(\tau))/2+(\mu^{\theta}(\tau)-\mu_{1}(\tau)^{\theta})(\mu(\tau)-\mu_{1}(\tau))\right)d\tau\\
\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(w(\tau_{n})-w_{1}(\tau_{n}))(\mu(\tau_{n})-\mu_{1}(\tau_{n}))e^{-\kappa\tau_{n}}\right|\leq O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}/2})\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu-\mu_{1}|e^{-\kappa\tau_{n}}=O(e^{\kappa\tau_{n}/2}).

(3.43)

Letting
n\to\infty
, it follows that
\mu=\mu_{1}
and
w_{\eta}=(w_{1})_{\eta}
. So
m
is unique, and
u
is unique in
\{m>0\}
modulo a constant.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

In the above analysis, we have characterized the optimal transport (with congestion cost of power type) of a Dirac mass towards a final target
m_{T}
, supposed to be a bump-like function satisfying (
1.3
). Of course, we could use a similar approximation as in Section
2
in order to build a trajectory transporting in time
T
a Dirac mass
\delta_{0}
onto another Dirac mass
\delta_{x_{1}}
. Namely, by taking
m^{\varepsilon}_{T}(x):=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\phi(\varepsilon^{-\alpha}(x-x_{1}))
in (
1.1
), the same analysis as in Section
2
would lead to a (unique) solution with both initial and final measures given by Dirac masses.
