Computational approach to identify novel genomic features conferring high fitness in Bacillus atrophaeus CNY01 and Bacillus velezensis AK-0 associated with plant growth promotion (PGP) in apple
Authors: Vandana Apurva Das, Budhayash Gautam, Pramod Kumar Yadav, Pritish Kumar Varadwaj, Gulshan Wadhwa, Satendra Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA comparative genomic analysis approach provides valuable information about genetic variations and evolutionary relationships among microorganisms, aiding not only in the identification of functional genes responsible for traits such as pathogenicity, antibiotic resistance, and metabolic capabilities but also in enhancing our understanding of microbial genomic diversity and their ecological roles, such as supporting plant growth promotion, thereby enabling the development of sustainable strategies for agriculture. We used two strains from different Bacillus species, Bacillus velezensis AK-0 and Bacillus atrophaeus CNY01, which have previously been reported to have PGP activity in apple, and performed comparative genomic analysis to understand their evolutionary process and obtain a mechanistic understanding of their plant growth-promoting activity. We identified genomic features such as mobile genetic elements (MGEs) that encode key proteins involved in the survival, adaptation and growth of these bacterial strains. The presence of genomic islands and intact prophage DNA in Bacillus atrophaeus CNY01 and Bacillus velezensis AK-0 suggests that horizontal gene transfer has contributed to their diversification and acquisition of adaptive traits, enhancing their evolutionary advantage. Background

Apples are among the most important and widely consumed fruits worldwide. They are the most widely grown fruit trees in temperate regions, thriving in nearly every temperate climate region across both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. Additionally, because apple trees are perennial and their soil nutrient levels are low, these crops are frequently affected by various phytopathogens [1]. Bitter rot and leaf spot are major apple diseases prevalent in regions with high rainfall, especially in countries with increased apple production in recent years. These diseases can cause yield losses of up to 50% [2]. Bacterial treatments led to a decrease in fruit set of 12% for B. subtilis, and it was determined that certain plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) strains can cause flower thinning in apples when used in excessive doses [3]; thus, PGPR can improve fruit set and quality when applied once at the appropriate concentration. Therefore, these bacterial treatments can be considered biofertilizers for sustainable and ecological fruit production and may be useful for thinning fruit trees with excessive flower loads [4, 5].

Most research on PGPR as helpful agents starts by testing how well individual PGPR strains can fight plant diseases in a controlled laboratory environment [6]. However, in real-world agricultural settings, crops often face multiple types of nutrient stress and disease simultaneously [7, 8]. Additionally, plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits can vary on the basis of soil management practices, niches, and bacterial genera [9–11]. This finding suggests that the phenotype of the PGPR microbial community changes to adapt to changing environmental conditions, thus making it important for microbial strains to have increased fitness. Thus, there is a need for a better understanding of the mechanisms employed by these strains to improve their adaptability, which will aid in identifying PGPR strains that can effectively combat various plant diseases under live conditions [12].

Understanding the genomic landscape of bacterial genomes is a potential way to understand how bacterial strains evolve and develop plant growth-promoting phenotypes [13–15]. This mechanistic understanding can not only shed light on plant‒microbe interactions, leading to a better understanding of the niche and its beneficial impact on plant growth and bacterial survival but also be useful for developing innovative agricultural practices, biotechnological applications, and sustainable solutions for enhancing crop productivity and resilience against environmental stresses.

This study addresses the research gap in understanding the genomic basis and evolutionary mechanisms underlying plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits in Bacillus species by comparing Bacillus velezensis AK-0 and Bacillus atrophaeus CNY01. Despite belonging to different Bacillus species, these strains exhibit similar PGP activities, making them ideal candidates for comparative genomic analysis. This study aims to identify shared and unique genes associated with PGP traits, uncover the role of horizontal gene transfer in functional diversification, and reveal species-specific adaptations. By elucidating these genomic features, this research provides valuable insights for selecting and engineering optimal Bacillus strains for sustainable agricultural applications [16]. Both of these strains have been reported to support the PGPR phenotype in apples [17, 18].

We investigated the plant growth-promoting phenotype of the aforementioned two Bacillus strains by examining the genomic landscape of these two Bacillus species to identify unique genomic features that result in increased genomic diversification and evolutionary fitness, enabling Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 and Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 to acquire traits that give them distinct advantages when growing in habitats that are challenging and changing, as will be the case in agricultural settings.

Methodology

Genome retrieval

For this work, the genomes of Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 (NCBI reference sequence: NZ_CP073265.1) and Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 (NCBI reference sequence: NZ_CP047119.1) were retrieved from the genome database of the National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore). After the DNA was retrieved, various tools were used for in-depth comparative genomic analysis of the two chosen bacillus strains.

The methodology and various bioinformatic tools used for this work are listed in Fig. 1.

Flowchart depicting the methodology and tools used in this study

Comparative genomic analysis

Evaluation of general genomic features

For this work, we used the GC content calculator tool from Vector Builder (https://en.vectorbuilder.com/tool/gc-content-calculator.html). The genomic alignment was performed using the progressiveMauve [19] genomic alignment tool present in the database of the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC) [20, 21]. To determine genomic similarity via the ANI value, we used the ANI calculator [22].

Identification of special genomic features

To identify the genomic islands present within Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 and Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0, we used a web server-based bioinformatic tool called IslandViewer4 [23]. Islandviewer4 uses 3 methodologies for the identification of genomic islands (GIs). These include the following: (1) IslandPick: This methodology relies on comparative genomics and identifies new genomic islands on the basis of genomic sequence similarity to known GIs; (2) SIGI-HMM: This methodology uses sequence composition analysis-based identification of atypical base composition patterns to predict potential GIs; and (3) IslandPath-DIMOB: This methodology looks for atypical sequences and mobility-related genes that are often associated with GIs.

Prophage DNA within the bacterial genome was identified using the bioinformatic tool PHASTEST, which is a web-based tool [24].

Identification of novel genomic features

To elucidate the hidden patterns within the genomic landscape, we identified novel DNA motifs in both Bacillus strains via a bioinformatics tool called MEME version 5.5.5 [25] and examined the identified DNA motifs for their relationships with biological regulatory mechanisms via tools such as Gene Ontology for Motifs (GOMo) [26]. Both MEME and GOMo are part of the MEME Suite software toolbox [27].

Finding association between novel motifs and gene ontology terms

GoMo (Gene Ontology for Motifs) identifies associations between DNA motifs and GO (Gene Ontology) terms by analyzing the occurrence of motifs in the upstream regulatory regions of genes with known GO annotations. The process involves the following steps:

The first step is motif scanning: GoMo scans the promoter regions of genes for the presence of the input motifs via a position weight matrix (PWM) that defines the motif sequence pattern. The next step is gene mapping: for each detected motif, GoMo then maps the motif to the genes in which it occurs, creating a list of motif-associated genes. The next step involves GO term analysis: GoMo then compares the list of motif-associated genes to a reference list of genes with known GO annotations. It evaluates the frequency of each GO term (biological process, molecular function, or cellular component) among the motif-associated genes. The final two steps include statistical enrichment, where GoMo uses statistical tests (e.g., hypergeometric tests) to determine whether certain GO terms are significantly overrepresented among the motif-associated genes compared with what would be expected by random chance. Finally, Significance and Reporting: The GO terms that are significantly enriched are associated with the input motif, indicating that the motif may regulate genes involved in those specific biological functions or processes.

Results

Comparative genomic analysis of the two chosen bacillus strains

We investigated the general genomic information of the two selected Bacillus strains. The genome size was 4.1 M for Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01, with a 43.5% GC content, whereas Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 had a slightly smaller genome, with 3.9 M bases and a GC content of 46.5%. Additionally, it had a slightly greater number of TRNAs and RRNAs (Fig. 2).

To further investigate the genetic relationships highlighting genetic features such as gene rearrangements and gene inversions between these two genomes, these genomes were aligned using progressive Mauve. This algorithm is a powerful tool designed to address complex genome alignment situations where the order of genes has been shuffled owing to rearrangements such as insertions, deletions, and inversions.

The Mauve alignment visualizes each genome as a horizontal line. The blocks coloured along these lines represent distinct gene coding regions. Importantly, the same-colored blocks appearing across different lines pinpoint regions of similarity between the two different genomes.

The interesting features visualized in the mauve alignment in Fig. 3 highlight the lengths of the sequenced contigs (DNA fragments) within the Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 genome, which has undergone genomic inversions. We also estimated the closeness of the two genomes by calculating the ANI scores via the ANI calculator, which estimates the average nucleotide identity between two genomic datasets via both best hits (one-way ANI) and reciprocal best hits (two-way ANI), following the methodology described by Goris et al. [22].

The ANI for these two genomes was calculated to be 79.82% (mean) and 79.04% (median) (Fig. 4), thus providing a quantitative measure of genetic relatedness. Identifying key genomic features

Genomic islands (GIs)

We next asked whether specific genomic features, such as certain sequences, play critical roles in adaptation and in the development of a plant growth-promoting phenotype. For this purpose, we followed a strategy as detailed in Fig. 5, where we wanted to investigate multiple such features.

One such genomic feature known to play an important role in bacterial adaptation is genomic islands (GIs). Genomic islands (GIs) are discrete DNA segments that are often acquired through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and are often associated with providing bacteria with new capabilities, influencing factors such as virulence and antibiotic resistance [28]. For this study, we used Islandviewer 4 to identify genomic islands (GIs) present in Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 and Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0.

Additionally, we also identified genes that play crucial roles in bacterial growth, adaptation and survival, such as tyrosine-type recombinase/integrase, site-specific integrase and IS3 family transposase. These genes increase genome plasticity and recombination rates, leading to genome rearrangements, deletions, duplications, or inversions [29–32]. This flexibility allows bacteria to rapidly adapt to environmental changes. Other important genes, such as the ImmA/IrrE family metallo-endopeptidase [33], which are known to play crucial roles in DNA repair and aid in the survival of bacteria, were also identified in the Genomic Island of Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01.

A complete list of all the genes identified in all the genomic islands (GIs) predicted by Isladviewer4 is available in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

In Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 GI (predicted by Islandpick), ranging from 662,118 to 748,482, a 7.2 KB region repeat was detected; upon closer examination of the proteins encoded by this region, these proteins were found to be phage-related proteins. This arrangement of phage-related proteins prompted us to question whether this and other regions within the bacterial genome had integrated phage DNA. To test this hypothesis, we used PHASTEST [24], a web-based tool that can scan the bacterial genome and predict prophage regions.

The results obtained from PHASTEST predicted the presence of one prophage in Bacillus velezensis AK-0 (Fig. 7A) and three prophages in Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, the prophage DNA identified in Bacillus velezensis AK-0 was not present in the predicted genomic island regions, whereas the prophage regions identified in Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 at (i) 660,151–705,361, (ii) 702,017–757,790, and (iii) 1,402,514–1,434,451 were identified as genomic regions by IslandPath-DIMOB and Islandpick. The presence of prophage DNA within bacterial genomes can lead to genome diversification, thereby enabling better adaptation and survival of bacteria [34]. We identified one of the phage DNAs encoding acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase, which is known to play a crucial role in bacterial cell division and in biofilm formation in other bacterial types [35].

Identification of phage DNA in the predicted genomic islands of (A) Bacillus velezensis AK-0 and (B) Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01

We next asked whether there were differences in information content coded within the bacterial genome and whether such information encodes variation between the genomes of the identified bacterial chromosomes and genomic islands (GIs). For this part of the work, we focused our analysis on the genomic island (GI) regions that were predicted by more than one algorithm. For Bacillus velezensis AK-0, these were (1) in the genomic region of 4,81,885-5,06,034, as predicted by IslandPath-DIMOB and SIGI-HMM, referred to as GI AK-I; (2) in the genomic region of 12,58,279 − 12,81,069, as predicted by IslandPath-DIMOB and IslandPick, referred to as GI AK-II; and (3) in the genomic region of 24,00,505 − 24,37,570, as predicted by IslandPath-DIMOB and SIGI-HMM, referred to as GI AK-III. Similarly, for Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01, we focused our analysis on the genomic island (GI) regions that were predicted by more than one algorithm. This was in the genomic region of 6,59,178-7,54,991, as predicted by IslandPath-DIMOB and IslandPick, referred to as GI CNY-1.

Thus, to determine whether these variations are reflected distinctly in the genomic properties of bacterial chromosomes and genetic features such as genomic islands (GIs), we examined the GC content within the predicted genomic islands (GIs) of these strains and compared it to the overall GC content. We found that the three genomic islands (GIs) identified in the Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 had lower %GC contents for each of the genomic islands (GIs), with two of the genomic islands (GIs) having lower %GC values by nearly 9% and 10% (Fig. 8), respectively, than the overall %GC content of 46.5% (as shown earlier in Fig. 2). Similarly, the %GC content in the genomic island of Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY-01 was also lower at 41.2% than the overall content of 43.46% (Fig. 2).

For this evaluation, we used the sequences of those genomic islands (GIs) that were predicted by more than one algorithm and then used the sequence of the larger genomic island that was predicted (Fig. 9). Discussion

In this study, we analysed two Bacillus strains known to have a positive impact on the growth of apples, focusing on comparing their genomic features to understand the genetic framework of these two strains and decipher the underlying genetic role that is responsible for growth, survival and special phenotypes, such as plant growth promotion in these bacteria.

We started by investigating the general genomic landscape of the two selected strains by examining properties such as the percent GC content and the distribution of TRNA and RNA, as shown in Fig. 2. The genome of the Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 was slightly smaller (3.9 M) than that of the Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 (4.1 M). Progressive Mauve-based alignment revealed that regions encoding the same proteins, called locally collinear blocks (LCBs) (Fig. 3), were differentially arranged for both of these strains, suggesting that evolutionary differences might have resulted in these kinds of genomic arrangements. Interestingly, many regions between the two strains were inverted, probably accounting for evolutionary pressures [36]. One of the methods used to assess genome-wide similarity that has become more popular and widely used is average nucleotide identity (ANI). This technique is considered one of the most effective methods for determining species boundaries and confirming identification [37]. Thus, we decided to quantitatively look at the similarity of two genomes via average nucleotide identity (ANI) scores. Our ANI of ~ 80 suggested that both genomes were similar but still distant enough to originate from different species (Fig. 4) [38].

We next examined the presence of genomic islands (GIs) within these strains, as these are discrete segments of DNA within a bacterial genome that have been acquired through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [39] during the evolutionary journey of the microbes. They often contain clusters of genes that can provide advantageous traits to the host bacterium and play a critical role in the diversification and adaptability of the bacteria [28, 40–42]. HGT enables Bacillus to quickly acquire genes for antibiotic resistance, stress tolerance, and metabolic versatility, increasing their ability to thrive in diverse environments while expanding their ecological roles in terms of plant growth promotion, biofilm formation, and pathogenicity. We identified multiple genomic islands (GIs) in the genomes of the Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 and Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 (Fig. 6; Tables 2 and 3). We examined which genes encoding proteins are present in these genomic islands, focusing on those that play a role in bacterial growth and adaptation and may play a role in plant growth promotion. We identified multiple important proteins that can play crucial roles in plant growth promotion through different pathways (summarized in Table 4), such as lantibiotic dehydratase, which is a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of antimicrobial compounds [43, 44], the MarR and MerR families of transcriptional activators that play a role in antibiotic stress resistance [45, 46] and heavy metal stress resistance [47, 48]. These results highlight the importance of mobile genetic elements such as the identified genomic islands in lending genomic diversity to the bacterial genome with an added role in providing pathways for stress resistance, thus improving the chances of bacterial survival.

While analysing the different proteins that were present on the genomic islands, we observed an intriguing arrangement of phage genes in one of the genomic islands. This prompted us to question whether these bacterial strains harbor phage DNA in their genomes. We identified 3 intact prophage DNAs in the predicted genomic islands of Bacillus atrophaeus strain CNY01 (Fig. 7). We also identified one prophage DNA sequence in the Bacillus velezensis strain AK-0 (Fig. 7). The presence of prophages can contribute to horizontal gene transfer and bacterial evolution [49]. They often carry genes that can enhance the fitness or virulence of their bacterial hosts [50, 51].

The discovery of Prophage DNA in genetic islands intrigued us, and thus, we questioned whether there are differences that can be identified in the way the information is encoded in these genomic islands (GIs) compared with the main genome. Previous studies have shown that genomic islands (GIs) can have an atypical GC content [52, 53]. Approximately 86% of prokaryotic DNA encodes proteins [54], making trinucleotide (codon) frequencies a significant source of genetic information. The GC is known to be correlated with codon distributions that can influence these trinucleotide frequencies [55, 56]. Therefore, these distributions can shed light on additional genomic properties. For both strains identified, we found that Genomic Islands (GIs) had a lower %GC content than the overall %GC content of the bacterial DNA (Fig. 8). This was consistent with what was observed for genomic islands (GIs) in another study by Zhang et al. [57, 58]. Differences in GC/AT content can be indicators of genomic regions being subjected to different environmental and selective pressures. We then questioned whether certain DNA patterns on these genomic islands play critical roles in the survival, growth and adaptation of these bacteria. For this purpose, we looked for DNA motif sequences unique to the genomic islands, as shown in Fig. 9. DNA motifs are often associated with functional elements in the genome, such as transcription factor-binding sites, promoter regions, splice sites, and other regulatory elements [59–61]. Therefore, using our motif sequences, we examined whether any of the motifs were significantly associated with genes linked to one or more Genome Ontology (GO) terms. We obtained one hit for each of the strains. AK-IIM1 had an association with GO:0022836. This GO term is related to Gated channel activity, which can regulate the passage of ions or molecules in response to various stimuli, thus playing a crucial role in many important physiological processes in addition to processes involving plant growth promotion (PGP) activity. For CNY-1M3, the GO term associated with this gene was GO:0046395, which is associated with carboxylic acid biosynthetic processes that are fundamental to many biosynthetic pathways and are critical for energy production and other vital functions.

Conclusion

This study aimed to compare two bacillus strains known to support PGPR activity. We performed an in-depth comparison of the two genomes and examined the genomic features of these two genomes, which can shed light on their evolutionary journey and help us understand the impact of genomic features on the bacterial phenotype. We identified genomic islands that contain key genes that can play key roles in the survival and adaptation of bacteria in different habitats and niches, including close vicinities of plant roots, where they can support PGPR activity. We also reported the presence of Prophage DNA in the bacterial genomes of both of these strains, which might be responsible for the genomic diversity and fitness of these two strains. Finally, we identified new DNA motifs that were found to be associated with key cellular processes. The findings from our study help in understanding the genomic basis of the PGP phenotype. This understanding might be helpful for the use of these strains and other similar bacterial strains to develop and market crop-specific microbial consortia that incorporate Bacillus velezensis AK-0, Bacillus atrophaeus CNY01 and other related strains for targeting high-value crops such as fruits, vegetables, or cereals. Additionally, these strains can also be incorporated as part of Integrated Pest and Nutrient Management (IPNM) programs to reduce overall chemical inputs, such as fertilizers and pesticides, thereby promoting sustainable and eco-friendly farming practices.
