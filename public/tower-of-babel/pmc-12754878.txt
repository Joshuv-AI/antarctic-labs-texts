Outcomes of team based learning in teaching medical ethics: a systematic review
Authors: Maryam Alizadeh, Shirin Bahrami, Zeinabe Saeedi, Loghman Khaninezhad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe integration of Team Based Learning (TBL) into medical ethics education has gained attention as a means of promoting active engagement, ethical reasoning, and professional development. This systematic review evaluates the characteristics, outcomes, challenges and quality of studies on TBL in teaching medical ethics across healthcare education contexts.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guideline, a systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar from 2000 to March 31, 2025. Only peer-reviewed empirical studies published in English and focusing on TBL in ethics education were included. Data were extracted on study characteristics, TBL interventions, measurement tools and outcomes. The identified outcomes were then analyzed and categorized using the Kirkpatrick framework to evaluate their hierarchical levels. Methodological quality was assessed using the MERSQI instrument. Due to heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was conducted.ResultsThe 15 studies, involving 2,134 participants, demonstrated TBL’s effectiveness in ethics education across diverse global settings. Studies originated from the United States (6), Turkey (2), Korea (2), a joint Bahrain/Ireland study (1), and single studies from the United Arab Emirates, Malaysia, Oman, and Nepal. Asia contributed 9 studies, North America 6, and Europe 1. Most studies were published between 2019 and 2025. When evaluated through the Kirkpatrick model, TBL demonstrated positive outcomes across multiple levels. Learner Reaction (Level 1) was highly favorable, with consistent reports of high satisfaction and engagement. In terms of Learning (Level 2), the intervention enhanced knowledge retention, ethical reasoning, and moral sensitivity. Furthermore, there was evidence of Behavioral change (Level 3), as students transferred learning to practice by applying ethical principles more effectively, demonstrating deeper cultural awareness, and engaging in more robust team-based decision-making. The fostering of a stronger professional identity suggests a foundational shift aligned with long-term Results (Level 4). Challenges included low pre-reading compliance, resource intensity, and ambiguous assessments. MERSQI scores ranged from 7.5 to 13.0, with an average of 10.2, indicating moderate methodological quality overall.ConclusionsTBL is an effective pedagogical approach for medical ethics education, fostering critical thinking, ethical behavior, and engagement in diverse global settings. Its interactive, learner-centered design promotes cultural and moral awareness and team-based decision-making, with high student satisfaction. Despite implementation challenges, the evidence supports its broader adoption in ethics teaching. Future research should prioritize rigorous study designs, validated assessment tools, and long-term outcome evaluation.

Background

Medical ethics is one of the fundamental pillars of Health Professions Education, having emerged in the 1970 s as part of medical curricula and gradually evolving into a central focus of professional training [1, 2].This field assists physicians, nurses, and other healthcare professionals in navigating ethical complexities and ambiguities in clinical practice, enabling them to make responsible decisions grounded in professional principles [3]. Through the cultivation of ethical competencies—such as moral sensitivity, knowledge, critical thinking, and decision-making and action skills—medical ethics education prepares professionals and students to confront everyday ethical challenges [4–6].

Healthcare professionals and medical students frequently encounter various ethical dilemmas in clinical practice, which may present as moral conflicts, value-based tensions, or difficult decisions under critical conditions [7]. These challenges can impact the quality of patient care and the mental well-being of healthcare workers [8, 9]. For instance, decisions regarding the continuation or withdrawal of treatment in critically ill patients, or managing conflicts between patient preferences and professional standards, exemplify such dilemmas. Effective medical ethics education can equip professionals with the necessary skills to analyze and resolve these issues [4, 10].

However, traditional methods of ethics instruction, such as lectures and theoretical seminars, often fail to fully address the practical needs of students [11]. Medical students seek active and student-centered learning approaches that allow them to practice ethical thinking and behavior in more realistic settings [11]. In this regard, Team-Based Learning (TBL) has emerged as a learner-centered approach that promotes active learning through teamwork and problem solving [12, 13]. While traditional ethics teaching methods, such as small group discussions or lectures, remain common [14], TBL offers an interactive and practical environment that helps students develop the necessary skills to analyze complex ethical issues in simulated settings [15].

TBL was originally developed by Larry Michaelsen in the late 1970 s, when he was a professor of Business at the University of Oklahoma. Confronted with rapidly growing class sizes and concerned about the limited effectiveness of traditional lectures, Michaelsen designed TBL as a structured, active learning strategy that promotes accountability and engagement through team-based problem solving [16]. During the 1990 s, TBL gained recognition in undergraduate business schools across the United States and later spread to other disciplines [17]. A major milestone occurred in 2001, when the U.S. Department of Education’s Fund for the Improvement of Postsecondary Education supported Baylor College of Medicine to promote TBL in health professions education through faculty workshops, symposia, and scholarly initiatives [18].

TBL is a small-group instructional strategy that encourages students to apply their knowledge through structured activities, including pre-class preparation, individual and team testing, immediate feedback, and collaborative resolution of clinical or ethical scenarios [19]. By allocating more time to critical thinking and problem-solving, this method fosters deeper and more lasting understanding of concepts [20]. The TBL process involves six steps: (1) pre-class preparation, (2) individual readiness assurance test (iRAT), (3) team test and team discussion (tRAT), (4) immediate feedback and clarification (tFAT), (5) team-based resolution of clinical or ethical problems (Team Application Exercise or tAPP as the core of TBL), and (6) Reflection on the process and content [13, 19]. By enhancing communication and teamwork skills, this approach simulates real healthcare teams and prepares students to face ethical dilemmas in real-world situations [15, 20].

Traditional lecture-based ethics education often fails to foster practical decision-making. While problem based learning (PBL) and case based learning (CBL) promote active learning, they may lack TBL’s structured feedback and team accountability, which are critical for ethics education [21]. This review addresses the underexplored role of TBL in enhancing ethical reasoning and collaboration [22, 23].

Ethical dilemmas in healthcare arise from various factors, including value differences, resource constraints, or clinical complexities, and may sometimes compromise professional values [7, 9]. Therefore, healthcare professionals and medical students must be prepared to identify and respond appropriately to such situations. Given the potential of TBL to enhance ethical competencies and collaboration skills, the aim of this systematic review is to examine the impact of this instructional method on improving medical ethics education.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a systematic review to evaluate the effectiveness of TBL in teaching medical ethics. The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure a transparent and comprehensive approach to synthesizing evidence [24].

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify studies assessing the use of TBL in medical ethics teaching. Searches were performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar search engine. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text terms related to (“Team-Based Learning” OR “TBL” OR “team based learning” OR “Collaborative learning”) AND (“Ethics” OR “Moral” OR “Medical ethics” OR “Bioethics”). Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were used to refine the search, and truncation symbols were applied to capture word variations. The search was limited to studies published in English from 2000 to March 31, 2025 (Table 1).

Study selection

The study selection process was conducted in several stages. First, all records were imported into EndNote version 20 [25] to remove duplicates. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Potentially eligible articles were selected for full-text review. Disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers, and, if necessary, a third reviewer was consulted for the final decision. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient to ensure the reliability of the screening process. The study selection process was documented in a PRISMA flow diagram, indicating the number of records screened, included, and excluded at each stage (Fig. 1).

Two reviewers independently extracted the data, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer to ensure accuracy.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the studies was assessed using the Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument (MERSQI) [26]. This tool evaluates features such as clarity, appropriateness of study design, accuracy in data collection and analysis, and risk of bias. Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers, and scores were categorized into low quality (score < 7), moderate quality (7–12), and high quality (>12). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Study quality was considered in interpreting the findings; however, no study was excluded solely based on low quality to maintain comprehensiveness of the review.

Data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and measurement tools, narrative synthesis was employed to integrate the findings. For each outcome, quantitative data (e.g., test scores, p-values, effect sizes) were summarized descriptively, and qualitative findings were synthesized thematically to highlight patterns and divergences. Where possible, effect sizes and statistical significance (e.g., p < 0.001) were reported to quantify the impact of TBL. The identified outcomes were then analyzed and categorized using the Kirkpatrick framework to evaluate their hierarchical levels. Although peer evaluation is commonly included in TBL, this data point was not consistently reported in the included studies and therefore was not analyzed in this review.

Results

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across 3 major databases—PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar—to identify studies examining the integration of TBL with ethics-related education. The searches were carried out on March 31, 2025, using database-specific search strategies designed to capture relevant terminology related to both pedagogical methods and ethical frameworks. In total, 1,008 records were retrieved from all sources. After deduplication and title/abstract screening, 969 records were assessed. From these, 106 full-text reports were sought for retrieval, and 22 were assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 15 studies met all eligibility requirements and were included in the final review. (Fig. 1)

Q1. What are the geographical distributions and methodological characteristics (study design, sample size, duration) of studies examining TBL in ethics education?

Fifteen studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing 2,134 participants. Studies were conducted in diverse regions: United States (6 studies), Turkey (2 studies), Korea (2 studies), a joint study conducted in Bahrain and Ireland (1 study), and single studies from the United Arab Emirates, Malaysia, Oman, and Nepal (1 study each). Most focused on undergraduate education in medicine (7 studies) or nursing (3 studies), with sample sizes ranging from 11 to 700 participants. Mixed methods were the predominant study design (10 studies), followed by qualitative (2), quantitative (2), and descriptive (1) approaches. Intervention durations varied from a single session to 16 sessions over multiple years. Team formation was random in six studies, pre-assigned in two, and unspecified in others. Ethics topics included medical ethics (7 studies), professional ethics (2 studies), bioethics (1 study), medical professionalism (1 study), pharmacy ethics (1 study), applied ethics (1 study), human subjects research ethics (1 study), responsible conduct of research (RCR) ethics (1 study), and research and publication ethics (1 study). Q2. How does TBL influence key outcomes in ethics education

The outcomes are analyzed and presented below according to the Kirkpatrick framework for evaluating training programs, progressing from learner satisfaction to demonstrated behavioral changes.

Level 1: reaction

Student engagement and satisfaction in ethics learning

TBL was widely well-received by students. Chung (2009) reported that 79.5% of 160 students rated TBL positively (mean scores 3.59–4.26/5) for topics like assent/consent and pediatric palliative care, citing its engaging and interactive nature [27]. Spencer (2022) observed high resident satisfaction (mean 4.42/5) across 10 TBL modules on bioethics and professionalism, with residents valuing case discussions and multimodal formats like videos and role-play [28]. Subedi (2022) reported satisfaction scores of 3.59–4.66/5, with students describing TBL as “fun and fruitful.” [29]. Simon (2020) noted a mean TBL-SAI score of 116.85 (neutral: 99), reflecting high satisfaction, with 69.95% of students enjoying the experience and 66.1% reporting better understanding of ethical concepts [30]. Ozgonul (2017) reported higher satisfaction for TBL (4.72/5, median = 5) compared to lectures (4.11/5, median = 4) for topics like informed consent and patient rights [31]. Razali (2024) found that 92.9% of students valued Quasi-TBL lectures for foundational knowledge on medical negligence, with gamified quizzes enhancing engagement [32]. Hasan (2011) identified qualitative themes of enhanced group interaction and interest in applying TBL to other ethics topics [33]. Madigosky (2019) and Lewis (2019) reported significant improvements (p < 0.001) in self-reported preparedness for interprofessional collaboration and engagement in research ethics, respectively [34, 35]. Guraya (2023) highlighted strong engagement in e-TBL sessions on cross-cultural communication and professionalism [36].

Level 2: learning

Knowledge acquisition and retention of ethical concepts

TBL effectively promoted knowledge acquisition and retention. Madigosky (2019) reported individual readiness assurance test (iRAT) scores of 81–89.6 and final exam scores of 85–91.2 for ethics-related domains [35]. Spencer (2022) observed significant knowledge gains in bioethics principles, with iRAT scores improving from 72% to 89% in group readiness assurance tests (gRATs) (p < 0.001) [28]. Subedi (2022) found median gRAT scores significantly higher than iRAT scores (p = 0.006 and p = 0.001) [29]. Chung (2009) and Hasan (2011) confirmed higher gRAT scores compared to iRAT scores (p < 0.000) [27, 33]. Ozgonul (2017) reported no significant difference in end-of-clerkship exam scores between TBL (7.48/8) and lecture groups (7.11/8, p = 0.257), but TBL groups outperformed lecture groups in follow-up exams at 1 year (8.38/10 vs. 7.13/10) and 2 years (8.16/10 vs. 6.62/10, p < 0.001), indicating superior retention [31]. Hickman (2013) and Lewis (2019) reported improved mastery of clinical and research ethics concepts, respectively, with themes of new understanding (n = 109) and accurate use of ethical terminology (n = 118) [34, 37].

Level 3: behavior

Ethical reasoning, problem-solving skills and moral sensitivity

TBL significantly improved students’ abilities in ethical reasoning and problem-solving. Alp (2025) reported that students successfully identified ethical violations in case scenarios, such as withholding a cancer diagnosis (violating autonomy), age-based discrimination in hospital admissions (breaching equality), unauthorized disclosure of a Hepatitis B diagnosis (violating confidentiality), and inappropriate language toward an overweight patient (violating dignity). Students proposed evidence-based solutions grounded in principles of autonomy, beneficence, and justice, although participation in decision-making was limited due to external pressures, such as instructor or peer influence [38]. Lewis (2019) found that students (n = 76) effectively linked ethical principles, such as justice and respect for human dignity, to clinical cases like the Tuskegee and Willowbrook studies, with 93 participants referencing case details to demonstrate critical thinking [34]. Hickman (2013) noted improved quality of case analysis papers following TBL adoption, with students applying the Four-Box Method (evaluating medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, and contextual features) to construct sophisticated ethical arguments [37]. Guraya (2023) reported that electronic TBL (e-TBL) fostered ethical reasoning and cultural competence through transformative case discussions, shifting students from paternalistic attitudes to a blame-free culture [36]. McCormack (2014) observed significant improvements in metacognitive reasoning strategies, including considering others’ perspectives (p = 0.0036), anticipating consequences (p = 0.018), managing emotions (p < 0.0001), and analyzing personal motivations (p < 0.0001), alongside enhanced social-behavioral responses, such as involving others in ethical decisions (p < 0.0001) [39]. Simon (2020) and Spencer (2022) confirmed enhanced analytical thinking and application of ethical concepts in complex scenarios involving autonomy, consent, and confidentiality [28, 30]. Subedi (2022) reported that 64% of students correctly answered application exercises on medical ethics principles in the first TBL session, improving to 100% in the second session [29]. Razali (2024) noted improved quiz performance on medical negligence, with scores rising from 41% to 88% post-discussion, reflecting enhanced critical thinking in legal and ethical contexts [32].

TBL significantly enhanced students’ moral sensitivity. Students adeptly identified complex ethical issues, such as age discrimination, privacy breaches, and information disclosure, proposing solutions aligned with ethical principles [38, 40]. Group discussions deepened understanding of concepts like justice, beneficence, and autonomy [30, 33, 37]. Furthermore, TBL fostered appreciation for diverse perspectives, cultivating cultural sensitivity and empathy [34, 36]. Lewis (2019) reported a shift in students’ ethical perspectives from a rule-based framework to a patient-centered focus, indicating a more nuanced ethical outlook [34].

Level 4: results

Professional identity and ethical professionalism

TBL played a pivotal role in shaping professional identity and promoting ethical behavior. The approach enhanced critical thinking, respect for diverse viewpoints, and the development of a robust professional identity [36, 38]. Madigosky (2019) found that TBL improved understanding of interprofessional roles and responsibilities, fostering ethical professionalism in team settings [35]. Hickman (2013) and McCormack (2014) reported that TBL encouraged ethical behaviors during educational activities and advocacy for ethical beliefs (d = 2.16, p < 0.0001) [37, 39]. Additionally, TBL reduced self-centeredness and increased students’ sense of professional responsibility [36, 39]. These changes in self-concept and professional alignment are critical precursors to the long-term ethical conduct and performance expected at Kirkpatrick Level 4.

Q3. What barriers hinder the effective implementation of TBL in ethics education?

Implementing TBL in ethics education faced several challenges. Low pre-reading completion rates (27.6%) were reported, potentially limiting learning outcomes [28, 40]. Students found educational resources challenging and preparation time-consuming [29, 31, 33]. Ambiguities in multiple-choice questions and the need for clearer guidelines during group discussions were noted [32, 40]. Certain modules, such as data management, were perceived as less engaging [39].

Q4. How is TBL implemented in medical ethics education, including variations in process and lessons learned?

The 15 included studies provided insights into the implementation of Team-Based Learning (TBL) in medical ethics education, detailing processes, variations, and lessons learned. Across studies, TBL generally followed a six-step structure: pre-class preparation through assigned readings or ethical guidelines (e.g., BMA Ethics Toolkit), individual readiness assurance tests (iRAT), team readiness assurance tests (tRAT/gRAT), immediate feedback (tFAT), team application exercises (tAPP) using frameworks such as the Four-Box Method, and reflection through evaluations or discussions [30, 34, 37].

Implementation varied across studies. Nine studies were conducted in-person [35, 37], three used online platforms (e-TBL, e.g., Zoom;), and three employed hybrid formats combining face-to-face and online sessions. Session duration ranged from a single 2-hour workshop [41] to 16 sessions over multiple semesters [35], typically lasting 1–3 h [33, 40]. Teams were formed randomly in six studies [40], pre-assigned based on diversity in two studies [37], and unspecified in others.

The studies covered a variety of ethics topics, including medical ethics (e.g., autonomy, consent;) [30], bioethics [28], professional ethics [36], pharmacy ethics [33], and research ethics [34]. Assessment tools included iRAT/tRAT with MCQs or short-answer questions (Ozgonul, 2017), and tAPP exercises such as case analyses, role-plays, or gamified quizzes (e.g., Quizizz in Razali, 2024). Feedback was collected via surveys (e.g., TBL-SAI) [30] or reflective exercises [34].

Overall, TBL demonstrated flexibility in delivery, structure, and assessment while maintaining its core components, providing practical lessons for the effective teaching of medical ethics across diverse educational contexts.

Q5. What are the strengths and limitations of existing studies on TBL for ethics education, as assessed by methodological quality (e.g., MERSQI scores)?

Table 3 summarizes the MERSQI scores for 15 medical education studies, evaluating their quality across six domains: Study Design, Sampling, Type of Data, Instrument Validity, Data Analysis, and Outcomes. Scores range from 7.5 [38, 40] to 13.0 [29], with an average of approximately 10.2, which is typical for the field. Most studies use pre-post designs (score: 1.5) and single-institution sampling, limiting generalizability. Discussion

This systematic review of 15 studies underscores the efficacy of TBL in enhancing medical ethics education, with significant improvements in ethical reasoning, moral sensitivity, professional identity, student engagement, and knowledge acquisition and retention. These findings align with and extend existing literature, offering insights into TBL’s role in fostering ethically competent healthcare professionals.

This systematic review demonstrates that TBL exerts a multi-faceted and positive influence on medical ethics education. By evaluating the outcomes through the Kirkpatrick framework, a hierarchical model for assessing training efficacy, we can systematically appreciate its impact from immediate learner reactions to foundational changes in professional conduct.

At the most fundamental level, Kirkpatrick Level 1 (Reaction), this analysis found consistently high levels of student engagement and satisfaction with TBL. Students rated the method as more engaging and effective than traditional lectures, a finding corroborated by Sterpu et al. (2024) in their review of TBL in medical education [42]. The interactive, gamified elements of TBL—such as case discussions and readiness assurance tests—are likely key drivers of this positive reception, fostering an appealing and dynamic learning environment.

Progressing to Kirkpatrick Level 2 (Learning), the evidence confirms that TBL significantly enhances knowledge acquisition and, crucially, long-term retention of ethical concepts. Our findings align with established literature; for instance, Emke et al. (2016) and Vasan et al. (2011) attributed similar gains in test performance and retention to TBL’s structured cycles of active recall and application [43, 44]. This is further supported by the umbrella review of Alizadeh et al. (2024), which confirmed TBL’s robust impact on learning outcomes [45]. The iterative engagement with content inherent to TBL appears to effectively move knowledge from short-term memory into long-term mastery.

Beyond knowledge retention, this review provides compelling evidence for outcomes at Kirkpatrick Level 3 (Behavior), where learning is translated into demonstrable skills and attitudes. TBL significantly enhanced students’ ethical reasoning and problem-solving skills, enabling them to deconstruct complex dilemmas and apply ethical principles. This aligns with Alizadeh et al. (2024), who identified the strengthening of critical thinking as a hallmark of TBL [45]. Furthermore, TBL cultivated a heightened moral sensitivity, allowing students to better identify and navigate nuanced issues like discrimination and confidentiality breaches. This development of a more patient-centered ethical perspective, as noted by Joshi et al. (2021), is likely a direct result of the collaborative perspective-taking and discussion that TBL mandates [46].

Finally, the findings indicate that TBL fosters development at the level of Kirkpatrick Level 4a (Antecedents to Results), by shaping a robust professional identity and ethical professionalism. The reviewed studies reported increased awareness of interprofessional roles, a strengthened sense of professional responsibility, and a reduction in self-centeredness. These outcomes represent a transformative shift in learners’ professional self-concept, which is a critical precursor to long-term organizational impact. This aligns with the work of Dearnley et al. (2018), who positioned TBL as a catalyst for transformative learning and professional identity formation [47]. By simulating collaborative, team-based decision-making, TBL effectively prepares students for the ethical and interpersonal complexities of real-world clinical practice.

Beyond medical ethics, TBL’s benefits extend to other disciplines, such as public health and general medical education. Fatmi et al. (2013) found TBL effective in improving academic performance across healthcare disciplines, including public health [48]. Mohebi et al. (2018) demonstrated improved student perceptions and performance in public health education, highlighting TBL’s adaptability [49]. This versatility underscores TBL’s potential as a flexible pedagogical approach across diverse educational contexts.

Limitations and future directions

This review has several limitations. The restriction to English-language studies may have introduced language bias, potentially excluding relevant non-English research. Heterogeneity in study designs, outcome measures, and methodological quality precluded meta-analysis, limiting quantitative synthesis.

TBL offers a robust framework for medical ethics education, with significant implications for curriculum design. Educators should integrate TBL with real-world clinical scenarios, and provide clear discussion guidelines to optimize outcomes. Compared to other active learning methods like PBL, TBL’s structured approach may offer advantages in fostering collaboration and immediate feedback, though direct comparisons are needed.

Conclusion

The consistent positive outcomes across all four levels of the Kirkpatrick framework position TBL as a highly effective pedagogy for ethics education. Its proven effectiveness in enhancing knowledge retention, student satisfaction, and adaptability to various formats positions it as a valuable tool for medical educators. While challenges such as low pre-reading rates and the need for faculty training exist, they are surmountable with strategic interventions and institutional support. As medical education evolves to meet the demands of modern healthcare, TBL can play a pivotal role in preparing ethically competent healthcare professionals capable of navigating complex moral landscapes. By addressing research gaps and leveraging TBL’s strengths, educators can further enhance its impact on the next generation of medical professionals.
