Managing Dissociation and Somatic Symptoms in Complex PTSD Using a Structural Dissociation-informed Approach: A Case Report
Authors: Sanya Kapur, Megha Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic interpersonal trauma can lead to complex clinical presentations characterized by dissociation, somatic symptoms, and disturbances in self-organization, particularly when psychosocial stressors remain active. This case report describes a 32-year-old female with prolonged interpersonal trauma who presented with dissociative episodes, somatic symptoms including fainting and jaw locking, recurrent suicidal ideation, and significant functional impairment. Symptoms intensified amid ongoing interpersonal conflict and financial stress. Assessment findings supported a diagnosis of complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD) with prominent dissociative and somatic features. Treatment followed a phase-oriented approach informed by the structural dissociation model developed by Onno van der Hart, Ellert Nijenhuis, and Kathy Steele. Intervention progressed through stabilization, trauma-focused work, and integration, incorporating psychoeducation, grounding, body-based interventions, affect regulation, and bottom-up approaches including titration and pendulation. Over the course of therapy, improvements were observed in awareness of dissociative experiences, emotional regulation, sleep, and daily functioning. Anxiety, depressive, and dissociative symptoms showed partial reduction, and suicidal behaviors decreased, although suicidal ideation remained intermittently present. Somatic dissociative symptoms, including fainting episodes, persisted but became more predictable and manageable. Given the concurrent psychotropic medication and single-case design, these changes cannot be attributed to psychotherapy alone. This case illustrates the application of a phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed approach in CPTSD with prominent dissociative and somatic symptoms, while highlighting the challenges of treatment amid ongoing psychosocial adversity.

Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), as described in ICD-11, 1 develops following prolonged or repeated interpersonal trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible. In addition to core post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, CPTSD is characterized by disturbances in self-organization, including affect dysregulation, a persistently negative self-concept, and interpersonal difficulties. Individuals with CPTSD frequently present with dissociation, somatic symptoms, and significant functional impairment, making assessment and treatment more complex. 2

Dissociation may involve disruptions in awareness, memory, identity, or perception, and can manifest through somatic symptoms such as fainting episodes, motor disturbances, and sensory changes.3,4 These symptoms may be mistaken for manifestations of an underlying medical condition, particularly when their psychological and dissociative context is not recognized, potentially contributing to delays in appropriate psychological assessment and intervention. 4 The structural dissociation model 5 proposed by van der Hart, Nijenhuis, and Steele provides a useful framework for understanding such presentations by conceptualizing trauma-related psychopathology as involving divisions between daily functioning and trauma-related responses. This framework informs phase-oriented treatment approaches that emphasize stabilization, trauma processing, and integration. 6 Recent evidence syntheses have further examined the assessment and clinical conceptualization of CPTSD, highlighting the multidimensional nature of the disorder and the need to consider both core PTSD symptoms and disturbances in self-organization. 7

In individuals with significant dissociation and ongoing psychosocial stressors, treatment often prioritizes safety, emotional regulation, and functional improvement before trauma-focused work. 6 Increasing attention has also been given to bottom-up interventions targeting physiological arousal and bodily experience, particularly when distress is expressed somatically. 8

This case report describes a 32-year-old woman with CPTSD who presented with dissociative symptoms, somatic manifestations of distress, and suicidal ideation. The case illustrates the application of a phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed approach in the context of ongoing psychosocial adversity.

Case Context and Method

Therapy followed a phase-oriented approach informed by the structural dissociation model developed by van der Hart, Nijenhuis, and Steele. Treatment was delivered over more than 20 psychotherapy sessions, initially conducted twice weekly for approximately 60 min. Intervention progressed through three overlapping phases: Stabilization, trauma-focused work, and integration, with movement between phases determined by the patient’s level of emotional regulation and dissociative symptoms.

The structural dissociation framework was used to understand the patient’s fainting episodes, experiences of unreality, and emotional overwhelm as trauma-related protective responses that emerged when distress exceeded her capacity for regulation. Given the prominence of dissociation, somatic symptoms, and ongoing psychosocial stressors, treatment emphasized safety, grounding, and regulation before engaging with trauma-related material.

Stabilization focused on psychoeducation, grounding strategies, and body-based interventions to increase present-moment awareness and reduce dissociative responses. Trauma-focused work was introduced gradually through bottom-up approaches that emphasized awareness of bodily sensations and tolerance of emotional and physiological activation. This included identifying early bodily signs of dissociation and distress, maintaining present-moment awareness, and gradually increasing tolerance of trauma-related activation through titration and pendulation.6,8 The integration phase focused on strengthening coping strategies, improving daily functioning, and supporting adaptation to ongoing stressors.

This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines, and informed consent for assessment, intervention, and the publication of anonymized information was obtained from the patient.

Case Description

Ms S, a 32-year-old separated woman employed as a housekeeping worker with no formal education, was referred from the Department of Neurology for psychological assessment and intervention following neurological evaluation of her recurrent fainting episodes and jaw locking symptoms. The relevant neurological investigation details were available with the Neurology Department at the time of referral. The fainting episodes were typically preceded by jaw stiffness and occurred during emotionally distressing situations, resulting in temporary loss of awareness.

She presented with persistent headaches, dizziness, fainting episodes, jaw locking, dissociative experiences, and recurrent suicidal ideation. Although these symptoms had been present for several years, they had worsened over the preceding 4 months following significant interpersonal and financial stress related to her husband’s fraudulent activities.

The fainting episodes were typically preceded by jaw stiffness and occurred during emotionally distressing situations, resulting in temporary loss of awareness. She also reported episodes of derealization, describing feelings of disconnection from her surroundings. Additional symptoms included low mood, hopelessness, worthlessness, disturbed sleep with trauma-related nightmares, reduced appetite, and impaired occupational and caregiving functioning.

Her history was characterized by prolonged interpersonal trauma, including childhood neglect, a forced marriage at age 15, emotional and physical abuse, and repeated experiences of abandonment. Despite separating from her husband, she continued to experience conflict, financial exploitation, and limited family support. Premorbidly, she described herself as socially withdrawn, with low self-esteem, and avoidant coping patterns.

On mental status examination, she was cooperative, appropriately groomed, and fully oriented. Psychomotor activity was increased, with visible restlessness and tearfulness. Speech was coherent but reduced in output. Affect was anxious and congruent with thought content, which was marked by depressive cognitions and suicidal ideation. Dissociative experiences were reported, attention was impaired, and insight was partial.

Psychological assessment included the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A), 9 a clinician-administered 14-item instrument that assesses the severity of psychic and somatic anxiety symptoms and has demonstrated good reliability and validity in clinical populations. The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS), 10 a clinician-rated measure widely regarded as a standard instrument for assessing the severity of depressive symptoms, was used to evaluate depression and has established reliability and validity across diverse clinical settings. Dissociative symptoms were assessed using the Dissociative Experiences Scale (DES-II), 11 a 28-item self-report instrument that measures the frequency of dissociative experiences and has demonstrated excellent internal consistency, good test-retest reliability, and strong construct validity. Symptoms of PTSD and CPTSD were assessed using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ), 12 an ICD-11-based self-report measure with strong internal consistency, good factorial validity, and demonstrated cross-cultural validity for distinguishing PTSD from CPTSD. Assessment findings indicated clinically significant anxiety (HAM-A = 29), depression (HDRS = 22), and dissociation (DES = 30.35). The ITQ supported a diagnosis of CPTSD, with symptoms across all CPTSD domains. Cognitive assessment revealed difficulties in attention, concentration, recent memory, and visual retention, while personality assessment suggested prominent anxiety, depression, somatic concerns, dependency, social withdrawal, and mistrust in relationships. During assessment, she required repeated instructions, demonstrated marked emotional distress, and experienced fainting episodes. At the time of psychological assessment and throughout the reported course of psychotherapy, the patient was receiving concurrent psychotropic medication comprising quetiapine 25 mg once daily, Nexito 10 mg once daily, and Petril 0.5 mg once daily.

Taken together, the clinical interview and psychological assessment findings were consistent with a diagnosis of CPTSD as per ICD-11 code 6B41. The clinical formulation was based on the presence of core PTSD symptoms, including trauma-related re-experiencing in the form of recurrent trauma-related nightmares and distress associated with trauma reminders, avoidance of trauma-related material, and a persistent sense of current threat, together with disturbances in self-organization characterized by affect dysregulation, a negative self-concept, and interpersonal difficulties. The ITQ was used to assess PTSD and CPTSD symptomatology systematically and supported the clinical formulation. A more severe underlying dissociative disorder was considered during the clinical workup; however, detailed clinical history and interview did not reveal distinct identity states, significant identity discontinuity, recurrent unexplained amnesia, or other characteristic features suggestive of a separate dissociative disorder. The dissociative experiences were therefore understood within the context of CPTSD and trauma-related responses.

Case Formulation and Treatment Plan

Case Formulation and Treatment Approach

The patient’s presentation was understood within a structural dissociation framework, where prolonged and repeated interpersonal trauma had led to a fragmentation in functioning between states oriented toward daily life and those linked to traumatic experiences. Early experiences of neglect, forced marriage, abuse, and repeated abandonment appeared to have created a persistent sense of threat, which remained active due to ongoing interpersonal and financial stressors.

The prominence of somatic manifestations, particularly their occurrence in the context of emotional distress, suggested that trauma-related distress was frequently expressed through physiological and bodily experiences. Dissociative symptoms, including fainting episodes, jaw locking, and experiences of unreality, were understood as responses that emerged when distress became overwhelming and appeared to allow temporary disengagement from intolerable emotional states, particularly during situations involving perceived threat or interpersonal conflict. This formulation did not imply an absence of verbal or cognitive processing; rather, it reflected the prominent somatic and dissociative expressions of distress observed in the clinical presentation. At the same time, persistent hypervigilance, avoidance of public spaces, and reliance on others reflected an ongoing lack of felt safety.

Difficulties in self-organization were evident in her inability to regulate emotions, a deeply negative self-view, and disrupted interpersonal functioning. Beliefs related to helplessness and worthlessness, along with longstanding patterns of avoidance, appeared to maintain her distress. Both dissociation and avoidance provided short-term relief but reduced opportunities for adaptive coping and engagement.

Several factors contributed to the persistence of symptoms, including ongoing environmental stress, limited social support, and cognitive difficulties affecting attention and processing. Dissociation further interfered with emotional processing, making it difficult to engage in purely cognitive interventions.

Based on this understanding, treatment was planned in a phase-oriented manner. Initial goals focused on safety, reducing immediate distress, and improving the patient’s ability to recognize and manage dissociative states. This was followed by gradual work on trauma-related responses using bottom-up approaches, including attention to bodily sensations, identification of physiological signs of emotional and dissociative activation, grounding, and gradual tolerance of emotional activation. Titration and pendulation were used to regulate the intensity of trauma-related activation and allow engagement with distressing material within the patient’s window of tolerance. This involved careful pacing to avoid overwhelming the patient. The later phase focused on improving daily functioning, strengthening coping strategies, and supporting the patient in managing ongoing stressors rather than aiming for complete symptom resolution.

Treatment Plan and Goals

Treatment goals were developed in response to the patient’s CPTSD, dissociative symptoms, somatic complaints, suicidal ideation, and functional impairment. Given the severity of symptoms and ongoing psychosocial stressors, initial priorities were safety, stabilization, and reduction of immediate distress.

Early goals focused on improving awareness of dissociative experiences, strengthening grounding skills, enhancing affect regulation, and increasing the patient’s sense of safety. Psychoeducation regarding trauma, dissociation, and the relationship between emotional and somatic distress was provided to reduce self-blame and improve understanding of symptoms.

As stability increased, goals expanded to include gradual engagement with trauma-related responses, reduction of avoidance, and improved tolerance of emotional and physiological activation. The final phase focused on consolidating gains, strengthening coping strategies, improving daily functioning, and supporting adaptation to ongoing stressors. Given the chronic nature of her difficulties, treatment emphasized improved regulation, safety, and functioning rather than complete symptom remission.

Therapist and Relational Factors

Therapy was influenced by the patient’s history of neglect, abuse, and repeated interpersonal betrayal, which appeared to affect her ability to establish trust and experience the therapeutic relationship as safe. Early in treatment, emotionally charged discussions could lead to withdrawal, fainting, or marked dissociative responses, particularly when the patient experienced the interaction as emotionally threatening or overwhelming. These responses were understood within the therapeutic relationship as reflecting difficulties with trust, vulnerability, and affective closeness rather than being viewed simply as resistance to treatment.

The therapeutic relationship therefore required a consistent, non-judgmental, and predictable stance. When the patient became emotionally or physiologically overwhelmed, the therapist slowed the pace of the session and shifted attention toward grounding and stabilization rather than continuing with trauma-focused material. This allowed the patient to remain engaged without exceeding her capacity for emotional regulation. Repeated experiences of being able to disclose distress without criticism or rejection appeared to gradually increase her trust in the therapeutic relationship and willingness to engage with difficult material.

The therapist also monitored their own responses to the patient’s episodes of withdrawal, fainting, and persistent suicidal ideation to avoid responding with excessive reassurance, urgency, or pressure to progress with trauma-focused work. Maintaining a regulated and consistent therapeutic stance was particularly important given the patient’s history of interpersonal betrayal and ongoing psychosocial stressors. The therapeutic relationship consequently became an important context for supporting safety, coping, and gradual engagement with trauma-related material.

The Course of Treatment and Monitoring of Treatment Progress

Therapy was conducted over more than 20 sessions, initially held twice weekly, and lasting approximately 60 min each, with frequency adjusted according to clinical need. Progress was monitored through clinical observation, review of dissociative symptoms, suicidal ideation, sleep, daily functioning, and the patient’s ability to use coping strategies outside therapy.

Initial Phase (Stabilization)

The initial phase focused on safety, psychoeducation, and symptom stabilization. 6 The patient was helped to understand the relationship between trauma, dissociation, and somatic symptoms, which appeared to reduce self-blame and improve engagement. Grounding strategies, including the 5-4-3-2-1 sensory technique, paced breathing, sensory touch exercises, and a personalized “safe box,” were introduced and practiced regularly to support present-moment orientation during periods of distress and dissociation. Body-oriented interventions such as attention to bodily sensations, breathing exercises, and relaxation techniques were also used to improve emotional regulation and present-moment awareness. Risk assessment and safety planning were reviewed throughout this phase. Over time, the patient became more aware of early signs of dissociation and was occasionally able to use grounding strategies before losing awareness.

Intermediate Phase (Trauma-focused Work)

As stability improved, therapy gradually addressed trauma-related responses through bottom-up approaches. 13 Direct exploration of traumatic experiences was avoided initially due to the patient’s tendency to dissociate when overwhelmed. Instead, treatment focused on increasing tolerance for emotional and physiological activation. Titration and pendulation techniques6,14 were used to help the patient engage with distress in manageable amounts while maintaining present-moment awareness. Although dissociative symptoms persisted, she became increasingly able to recognize early signs of distress and attempt self-regulation. Suicidal behaviors reduced during this phase, though suicidal ideation continued to fluctuate in response to ongoing stressors.

Final Phase (Integration and Rehabilitation)

The final phase focused on consolidating gains and strengthening the application of coping skills in daily life. Emphasis was placed on improving routine functioning, emotional stability, and management of ongoing interpersonal and financial stressors. The patient reported improved sleep, greater engagement in daily responsibilities, and more independent use of grounding strategies. Fainting episodes continued during periods of significant stress but became more predictable and manageable. Overall, the patient demonstrated modest but clinically meaningful improvements in awareness, emotional regulation, safety, and functioning despite persistent external stressors.

Treatment Outcome

Treatment outcomes were evaluated through clinical observation, functional assessment, and repeat administration of selected measures. Improvements were observed in emotional regulation, awareness of dissociative experiences, and day-to-day functioning.

The patient developed a clearer understanding of the relationship between trauma, dissociation, and somatic symptoms, and demonstrated greater ability to recognize early warning signs of dissociative episodes. She reported increased use of grounding and self-regulation strategies and was able to remain oriented in situations that had previously led to disengagement.

Repeat assessment indicated reductions in anxiety (HAM-A: 29 to 20), depression (HDRS: 22 to 15), and dissociation (DES: 30.35 to 23.4). Although symptoms remained clinically significant, these changes suggested improved emotional stability and coping capacity.

No further suicide attempts were reported during treatment, although suicidal ideation remained intermittently present, particularly during periods of increased stress. Functional improvements included better sleep, increased engagement in daily activities, and greater confidence in managing distress. Fainting episodes persisted but became more predictable and were associated with improved awareness of preceding emotional and physiological cues.

Overall, treatment resulted in partial but clinically meaningful improvement, particularly in awareness, emotional regulation, safety, and daily functioning. Given the concurrent use of psychotropic medication, these changes cannot be attributed to psychotherapy alone.

Discussion and Limitations

This case illustrates the challenges of treating CPTSD in the context of prolonged interpersonal trauma and ongoing psychosocial adversity. In addition to core CPTSD symptoms, the patient presented with prominent dissociative experiences, somatic manifestations of distress, suicidal ideation, and significant functional impairment, highlighting the broad impact of chronic trauma on emotional, cognitive, interpersonal, and physiological functioning.

The structural dissociation model provided a useful framework for understanding the patient’s presentation and guiding treatment. Fainting episodes, experiences of unreality, and emotional overwhelm were conceptualized as trauma-related protective responses that emerged when distress exceeded her capacity for regulation. This understanding supported a phase-oriented approach that prioritized safety, stabilization, and the gradual development of self-regulatory skills before engaging with trauma-related material.

The patient demonstrated modest but clinically meaningful improvements in awareness of dissociative experiences, use of grounding strategies, sleep, daily functioning, and suicidal behavior during the course of treatment. These changes occurred within a phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed therapeutic approach; however, given the single-case design and the absence of a control condition, the improvements cannot be attributed specifically to this approach. Nonspecific therapeutic factors, including the therapeutic alliance and supportive aspects of treatment, natural fluctuation in symptoms, concurrent psychotropic medication, and changes in the intensity of ongoing psychosocial stressors, may also have contributed to the observed changes.

Strengths

The case provides a detailed description of the assessment, formulation, and treatment of CPTSD with prominent dissociative and somatic features using a phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed approach. Standardized psychological measures were used to support clinical assessment and monitor treatment outcomes. The report also illustrates the application of evidence-based psychotherapeutic strategies amid ongoing psychosocial adversity, reflecting the complexities of routine clinical practice. Furthermore, treatment outcomes are presented using both clinical observations and repeat symptom measures, providing a comprehensive account of the patient’s progress.

Limitations

As a single-case report, the findings cannot be generalized to broader populations. Treatment outcomes were evaluated primarily through clinical observation, patient report, and selected standardized measures, and no long-term follow-up was available to assess the maintenance of therapeutic gains. In addition, ongoing interpersonal and financial stressors may have influenced treatment response and limited the extent of symptom improvement. These findings should therefore be interpreted within the context of this individual case.

Implications and Recommendations

The present case highlights several implications for clinical practice and future research regarding the assessment and treatment of CPTSD with dissociative and somatic features. These implications are outlined below.

Implications for Clinical Practice and Theory

This case highlights the importance of assessing dissociative processes in individuals with CPTSD who present with somatic symptoms. Symptoms such as fainting episodes and experiences of unreality may reflect trauma-related dissociative responses rather than solely medical conditions. The findings support the clinical utility of a phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed approach that prioritizes stabilization, grounding, and emotional regulation when significant dissociation and ongoing stressors are present. The case also underscores the potential value of body-oriented and bottom-up interventions for individuals whose distress is expressed primarily through physiological and somatic channels.

Implications for Research

Further research is needed to evaluate phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed interventions in larger samples of individuals with CPTSD and prominent dissociative symptoms. Longitudinal studies incorporating standardized outcome measures and follow-up assessments may help clarify treatment effectiveness and identify factors associated with response amid ongoing psychosocial stressors.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the clinical utility of a phase-oriented, structural dissociation-informed approach for the treatment of CPTSD with prominent dissociative and somatic features. Although significant psychosocial stressors remained unresolved throughout treatment, meaningful improvements were observed in emotional regulation, awareness of dissociative experiences, safety, and daily functioning. The case underscores the importance of integrating stabilization, body-oriented interventions, and gradual trauma-focused work when treating individuals with complex trauma and ongoing adversity.
