How parental educational anxiety fuels adolescent depression: the mediating chain of moral disengagement and negative coping, and the buffering role of psychological resilience
Authors: Yu Lu, Haohan Zhao, Xingchen Zhu, Wencan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the context of academically competitive cultures, particularly in China, parental educational anxiety has emerged as a salient psychosocial stressor affecting adolescent mental health. While growing attention has been given to this phenomenon, the psychological pathways through which parental educational anxiety relates to adolescent depression remain insufficiently delineated. This study investigates the mediating roles of moral disengagement and negative coping style, as well as the moderating function of psychological resilience in this relationship.MethodsA total of 1,979 parent-adolescent dyads were recruited from six schools across three provinces in China (Liaoning, Shandong, and Shanghai). Standardized questionnaires were administered separately to both parents and their adolescent children. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was employed using Mplus 8.3 to test the hypothesized chain mediation pathways, while moderation analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0 to examine whether psychological resilience buffers the direct association between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression.ResultsParental educational anxiety was significantly and positively associated with adolescent depression (β = 0.304, t = 14.929, p < 0.001). Moreover, a significant sequential pathway was identified: moral disengagement was positively associated with negative coping style (β = 0.270, t = 13.548, p < 0.001), and both variables jointly mediated the association between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression. Additionally, psychological resilience moderated this association such that the relationship was attenuated among adolescents with higher resilience (p < 0.001).ConclusionThese findings highlight a complex chain of cognitive and emotional mechanisms linking parental educational anxiety to adolescent depressive symptoms. Moral disengagement and negative coping style operate as sequential mediators, while psychological resilience serves as a buffering factor. Although causal interpretations are limited by the cross-sectional design, the results underscore the importance of addressing both family-based pressures and adolescents’ internal coping resources in interventions aimed at improving youth mental health in high-pressure educational contexts.Keywords: Parental educational anxiety, Moral disengagement, Negative coping style, Adolescent depression, Psychological resilience

Introduction

The prevalence of depression among adolescents has become an increasingly serious global public health concern, with evidence indicating a rising trend in both incidence and severity over the past decade [1]. In the context of academic-oriented cultures, particularly in East Asian societies, growing attention has been directed toward the influence of family-related factors on adolescent mental health. Among these, parental educational anxiety -- characterized by excessive concern over children’s academic performance and future competitiveness -- has emerged as a salient psychological stressor [2]. While parental involvement is traditionally viewed as a protective factor [3], an overly anxious orientation may paradoxically exert a deleterious effect on youth psychological adjustment.

This issue is particularly pronounced in China, where cultural and institutional factors create a uniquely intensive educational environment. The Gaokao (National College Entrance Examination) serves as the primary determinant of university admission and future social mobility, fostering intense academic competition from an early age [4]. Traditional Confucian values of filial piety obligate children to honor parents through academic success [5], while the concept of “face” links children’s academic performance to family honor and social reputation [6]. The intersection of these high-stakes examinations, filial obligations, and collective family honor amplifies parental educational anxiety in China, making it essential to examine how this anxiety affects adolescent mental health within this cultural context.

Despite growing recognition of parental educational anxiety’s significance, several critical gaps remain in our understanding of its impact on adolescent mental health. First, while previous studies have documented correlations between parental anxiety and adolescent depression [7, 8], the underlying psychological mechanisms through which this relationship operates remain inadequately explored. Second, although moral disengagement and negative coping strategies have been independently linked to psychological distress [9, 10], their potential mediating roles in the relationship between parental educational anxiety and depressive outcomes have not been systematically investigated.

Moreover, within contemporary achievement-driven educational contexts, individual differences in psychological resilience may further shape how adolescents respond to parental educational anxiety. Resilient individuals are better able to regulate emotions and maintain adaptive functioning under stress [11]. However, few studies have examined resilience as a moderator in the specific context of parental academic anxiety and adolescent depression. Existing research has largely overlooked how resilience may buffer the psychological impact of family-based academic stress, representing a critical gap that this study seeks to address.

Understanding these complex relationships is crucial for both theoretical advancement and practical intervention development. By elucidating the mediating mechanisms and moderating factors involved, researchers and clinicians can develop more targeted and effective approaches to preventing and treating adolescent depression in contexts where academic pressure is prominent. This study therefore aims to examine the intricate pathways linking parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression, with particular focus on the roles of moral disengagement, negative coping strategies, and psychological resilience in these relationships.

Literature review

Parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression

Parental educational anxiety encompasses the excessive worries, stress, and apprehensions that parents experience regarding their children’s academic achievement and future educational prospects [12]. While such anxiety may stem from aspirational motives, it often manifests as psychological pressure within the family environment, potentially undermining adolescents’ emotional well-being [13]. The Intergenerational Transmission of Anxiety framework [14] provides a domain-specific theoretical lens for understanding how parental anxiety is transmitted to offspring through anxiety-specific pathways. First, parents verbally convey catastrophic interpretations and threat-laden narratives about academic outcomes, directly shaping adolescents’ cognitive appraisals of academic situations as existential threats [15]. Second, parents engage in anxiety-contingent parenting practices such as hypervigilant monitoring, overprotective interference, and excessive reassurance-seeking, which communicate environmental threat and undermine adolescent autonomy and confidence [16]. Third, through repeated anxiety-driven interactions, adolescents observe and internalize their parents’ catastrophizing and intolerance of uncertainty, developing heightened threat perception and reduced confidence in managing academic challenges, ultimately contributing to depressive symptoms [17].

Empirical evidence supports this framework. Research has shown that adolescents exposed to high levels of parental academic pressure report significantly more depressive symptoms, regardless of their actual academic performance [18]. Moreover, parental anxiety has been linked to greater emotional withdrawal, lower perceived support, and heightened conflict [19, 20] -- all of which are associated with adolescent depression. Despite these findings, parental educational anxiety is still often examined as a peripheral component of broader parenting constructs, and its unique contribution to adolescent depression remains insufficiently explored.

To address this gap, the present study conceptualizes parental educational anxiety as a distinct psychological construct and investigates its direct association with adolescent depression, guided by the Intergenerational Transmission of Anxiety framework. Our first hypothesis (H1) is that parental educational anxiety is positively associated with adolescent depression, such that higher levels of parental anxiety predict greater depressive symptoms in adolescents.

Moral disengagement as a mediator

Moral disengagement refers to a set of cognitive mechanisms through which individuals justify maladaptive or unethical behavior while circumventing guilt and self-sanction [21]. These mechanisms -- such as minimizing consequences, displacing responsibility, or morally justifying one’s actions -- temporarily suspend internal moral standards, enabling individuals to maintain a positive self-image even when acting in ways that contradict their values [22].

From a theoretical perspective, moral disengagement may serve as a key cognitive mediator linking parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression. According to Social Cognitive Theory [23], moral self-regulation is shaped by both individual agency and environmental influences [24]. Adolescents living under conditions of heightened parental educational anxiety may experience ongoing pressure to meet unrealistic standards, leading to emotional dissonance when they fall short [25]. To reconcile this conflict without damaging their self-image, they may adopt moral disengagement strategies -- blaming external circumstances, minimizing personal responsibility, or reframing failure in self-protective terms [26]. While these cognitive shifts may temporarily reduce anxiety, they can also undermine emotional resilience, distort self-evaluations, and contribute to internalizing symptoms over time [27]. In this way, moral disengagement may represent a maladaptive coping mechanism that links external family stress to internal psychological distress.

Empirical evidence converges on this mediational pathway. In achievement-oriented family contexts, parental pressure and education-related stress are linked to autonomy suppression and a persistently critical evaluative climate, conditions under which adolescents increasingly adopt moral disengagement strategies such as moral justification and displacement of responsibility [28, 29]. In turn, moral disengagement is consistently associated with internalizing problems, particularly depressive symptoms, and its use to buffer perceived failure is especially predictive of elevated depression [30]. Convergent findings further indicate that moral disengagement undermines adaptive self-regulation and heightens susceptibility to negative affect, thereby increasing risk for the development of depressive symptomatology [31, 32].

While prior studies have linked moral disengagement to internalizing symptoms, few have explicitly examined it as a mediating mechanism between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression. This limits our understanding of how family-based academic stress is cognitively internalized and transformed into emotional distress.

Taken together, theoretical reasoning and empirical findings support the view that moral disengagement may operate as a cognitive conduit linking external academic pressure to internal emotional distress. It is therefore reasonable to hypothesize (H2) that moral disengagement mediates the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression.

Negative coping style as a mediator

Negative coping styles refer to maladaptive strategies that individuals use to manage psychological stress, such as avoidance, denial, self-blame, or emotional suppression [33]. These strategies are often ineffective in resolving stressors and may instead exacerbate psychological distress over time [34]. In adolescence -- a developmental period characterized by heightened emotional sensitivity and evolving self-regulatory capacity– coping styles play a central role in determining mental health outcomes [35, 36].

The Tripartite Model of the Impact of the Family on Children’s Emotion Regulation and Adjustment [37] offers a suitable framework for understanding how parental educational anxiety influences adolescent coping patterns. This model proposes that parental characteristics affect children’s emotional and behavioral regulation through three pathways: observational learning/modeling, parenting practices, and the emotional climate of parent-child interactions [38]. In the context of educational anxiety, anxious parents model dysregulated emotional responses to academic challenges and demonstrate ineffective coping strategies such as catastrophizing and rumination, which adolescents observe and internalize [39]. Moreover, these parents’ anxiety-driven behaviors create an emotionally charged family environment that undermines adolescents’ development of autonomous coping skills, instead fostering reliance on avoidance, emotional suppression, and other maladaptive strategies that increase vulnerability to depression [40].

Empirical research supports this theoretical pathway in a synthesized manner. Parental academic pressure is associated with adolescents’ greater reliance on negative coping, with students reporting more avoidance and passive resignation when they perceive heightened parental anxiety about grades [41]. Qualitative work further shows that avoidant coping tends to predominate under such pressure and provides only short-lived relief, indicating a maladaptive regulatory pattern rather than problem solving [42]. Converging evidence then links negative coping to internalizing difficulties: among Chinese middle and high school students, negative coping correlates with higher depressive and anxious symptoms, whereas positive coping is protective [43]. Longitudinal data also document a positive between-person association between negative coping and anxiety, consistent with a broader vulnerability profile in youth who habitually disengage from stressors [44]. Taken together, these findings indicate that parental academic pressure is tied to adolescents’ adoption of negative coping, and that this coping pattern is implicated in depressive risk via heightened cognitive vulnerability and emotional dysregulation [45].

Despite these findings, few studies have explicitly tested negative coping style as a mediating mechanism linking parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression. Existing work often treats coping as a moderator or a general correlate of distress, without examining how specific coping tendencies develop in response to family-based academic pressure. This gap limits our understanding of the psychological processes through which parental anxiety affects adolescent emotional outcomes.

Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis (H3): Negative coping style mediates the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression.

The chain mediating role of moral disengagement and negative coping style

General Strain Theory [46] offers a compelling framework for understanding how adolescents respond to persistent psychological stress, particularly in the context of family-based academic pressure. According to the theory, when individuals experience strain and lack effective coping resources, they are likely to engage in cognitive and behavioral adaptations aimed at relieving negative emotions, even if those adaptations are ultimately maladaptive [47]. This framework suggests a sequential process whereby moral disengagement functions as a cognitive distortion in response to strain, which subsequently increases the likelihood of negative coping behaviors.

Empirical research supports the plausibility of this pathway. Adolescents who habitually employ moral disengagement to justify or minimize failure show a greater propensity to avoid responsibility and suppress emotion [48]. Consistent with this pattern, moral disengagement predicts reliance on avoidant and emotion-focused coping [49], and cognitive detachment from personal accountability is associated with increased self-blame and withdrawal-based coping [50]. Taken together, these findings indicate that when adolescents rationalize shortcomings under pressure, they preferentially adopt maladaptive coping that maintains rather than resolves distress, aligning with evidence linking such strategies to heightened internalizing risk [34].

Nevertheless, few empirical studies have directly tested a chain mediation model in which moral disengagement precedes negative coping in the transmission of parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression. Most existing models treat these psychological processes in isolation, failing to capture how adolescents’ cognitive and behavioral responses interact dynamically under sustained academic stress. This gap limits our understanding of how externally imposed strain becomes internalized and behaviorally enacted in ways that undermine psychological well-being.

Taken together, these insights suggest a cumulative process through which parental educational anxiety may influence adolescent mental health. Moral disengagement, as a cognitive detachment from self-regulatory norms, may predispose adolescents to adopt negative coping responses that amplify emotional vulnerability. Recognizing this sequential pathway, it is justifiable to propose the following hypothesis (H4): Moral disengagement and negative coping style sequentially mediate the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression.

Psychological resilience as a moderator

Psychological resilience refers to an individual’s capacity to maintain or quickly regain psychological well-being in the face of adversity, stress, or trauma [51]. It reflects a dynamic process encompassing emotional regulation, cognitive flexibility, and adaptive coping, which together buffer individuals against the harmful effects of environmental stressors [52].

In the context of parental educational anxiety, resilience may play a critical moderating role. According to the Diathesis-Stress Model [53], psychological disorders such as depression result not only from exposure to stress but also from individual vulnerabilities or protective factors [54]. Adolescents high in resilience are more likely to interpret academic pressure as manageable and to mobilize internal resources to preserve emotional stability [55]. In contrast, those with low resilience may lack the capacity to regulate distress, increasing their susceptibility to depressive symptoms in response to family-based stress [56].

Empirical research supports this buffering effect. Converging findings indicate that resilience mitigates the mental-health impact of interpersonal stressors in adolescence [57]. In academically demanding contexts, resilience moderates the association between academic pressure and emotional distress, with attenuated slopes among higher-resilience youth [58]. Parallel evidence shows that resilience weakens the link between parental psychological control and depressive symptoms, underscoring a protective role in high-demand family settings [59].

Despite these findings, few studies have specifically examined whether psychological resilience moderates the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression. Most existing work focuses on resilience in response to general life stress or peer conflict, rather than education-specific familial pressure. This limits our understanding of how adolescents’ internal strengths interact with external academic stressors in shaping emotional outcomes.

Considering these theoretical and empirical insights, we propose that psychological resilience moderates the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression, such that this association is weaker among adolescents with higher levels of resilience (H5).

The present study

Although growing evidence suggests that parental educational anxiety plays a role in shaping adolescent mental health, existing research has often examined this relationship through isolated theoretical lenses. As a result, critical mechanisms -- particularly those involving internalized cognition, maladaptive coping, and psychological resilience -- remain conceptually fragmented and empirically underexplored. This study addresses this gap by advancing a theoretically integrated model that explains not only whether, but how and under what conditions, parental educational anxiety contributes to adolescent depression.

Rather than relying on a single theoretical framework, this study integrates five complementary perspectives to elucidate the complex pathways linking parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression. The Intergenerational Transmission of Anxiety framework conceptualizes how parental educational anxiety is transmitted to offspring through modeling, anxiety-specific parenting behaviors, and threat information transfer. Social Cognitive Theory explains how moral disengagement emerges as a self-protective cognitive mechanism when adolescents face sustained pressure to meet unrealistic academic standards. The Tripartite Model of the Impact of the Family on Children’s Emotion Regulation and Adjustment illuminates how parental anxiety shapes adolescent coping through observational learning, parenting practices, and the emotional climate of family interactions. General Strain Theory provides a sequential account of how moral disengagement and negative coping operate as cascading maladaptive responses to academic strain. Finally, the Diathesis-Stress Model positions psychological resilience as a critical moderator that buffers against the depressogenic effects of parental anxiety. This theoretical integration advances beyond isolated mechanism testing to provide a comprehensive understanding of the cognitive, behavioral, and protective processes through which family-based educational anxiety influences adolescent mental health.

Integrating these perspectives allows for the examination of both cognitive and behavioral mediators (i.e., moral disengagement and negative coping style) as well as the moderating role of psychological resilience. This comprehensive framework is especially relevant in achievement-oriented, high-pressure environments, where family expectations often intersect with adolescents’ developing emotional regulation and self-concept.

Materials and methods

Participants

Sampling design and recruitment

Participants were recruited from six schools across three provinces in China: Liaoning (n = 2 schools; 1 middle, 1 high), Shandong (n = 2 schools; 1 middle, 1 high), and Shanghai (n = 2 schools; 1 middle, 1 high). These sites were purposefully selected to reflect regional diversity in economic development (industrial base, agricultural/manufacturing, metropolitan hub), geography (northeast, east, eastern coast), and educational culture. Within each province, we sampled one urban and one rural school, and included both key schools (academically competitive institutions with rigorous admissions and strong university placement records) and non-key schools (institutions with more inclusive admissions and fewer resources). This stratified approach maximized heterogeneity in school environments, family socioeconomic backgrounds, and academic pressure levels.

School sizes ranged from approximately 800 to 2,500 students. All participating schools followed an exam-centric academic model emphasizing standardized assessments and preparation for the Gaokao (National College Entrance Examination), with student rankings based on semester-end examinations commonly used as performance benchmarks. Within each school, we employed a grade-stratified convenience sampling approach, randomly selecting two grade levels (one from grades 7–9 for middle schools; one from grades 10–12 for high schools) and inviting all students from these grades to participate. A total of 2,121 parent-adolescent dyads were approached for participation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) adolescents aged 13–18 years enrolled in regular education programs; (2) intact two-parent families with both biological parents residing in the same household; (3) absence of diagnosed severe mental illness or cognitive impairment in either the adolescent or parents that would preclude questionnaire completion; (4) sufficient literacy in Chinese to complete questionnaires independently; and (5) voluntary informed consent from both the adolescent and at least one parent.

Sample size determination

The target sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1 [60] for small-to-medium incremental effects in multiple regression at and power . Because mediation and moderation effects are typically smaller than main effects, we additionally verified power for the indirect paths via a Monte Carlo approach (5,000 replications) assuming small path coefficients () [61]. These analyses indicated that approximately 1,800–2,000 parent–adolescent dyads would achieve ≥ 0.80 power to detect the hypothesized indirect effects. Given the multi-school sampling, we also accounted for clustering by applying a design-effect adjustment based on plausible intraclass correlations (ICC ≈ 0.01–0.03) and average cluster size [62]; the effective sample size remained adequate. Because interaction effects generally require larger samples due to lower statistical power [63, 64], we targeted ≈ 2,000 dyads to ensure sufficient power for subgroup and moderation tests. The final sample of 1,979 dyads met this target and provided adequate power for all planned mediation and moderation analyses.

Data collection procedures

Data collection commenced in March 2025 following ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee and coordination with school administrators. Informed consent was obtained from all student participants and their legal guardians prior to participation. Given the minimal-risk nature of the study, and to preserve participant anonymity, the Ethics Committee approved consent procedures that omitted personal identifiers such as names and signatures. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without penalty.

To ensure response independence and minimize shared method variance, students and parents completed their questionnaires separately. A unique numerical code was assigned to each family unit to enable accurate dyadic matching while maintaining anonymity. Trained research assistants distributed and collected the paper-and-pencil surveys during school hours. Students completed their questionnaires under supervision in classrooms, while parents completed theirs simultaneously in a separate location (e.g., school auditorium or meeting room). All completed materials were sealed in individual envelopes and securely processed.

Small incentive gifts were provided to participating families as tokens of appreciation. The high completion rate was further facilitated by administrative support from school principals and teachers, multiple opportunities for participation during scheduled school events, and the availability of research assistants to answer questions and provide clarification.

Final sample characteristics

Of the 2,121 dyads initially approached, 142 (6.7%) were excluded due to incomplete responses, failure to meet inclusion criteria (e.g., single-parent families, missing consent), or invalid questionnaire data. The final analytic sample comprised 1,979 intact parent-adolescent dyads, yielding a retention rate of 93.3%. This high retention rate suggests minimal selection bias, and the complete-case approach supports the assumptions of dyadic analyses while providing robust parameter estimates.

Adolescent participants ranged in age from 13 to 18 years (M = 15.570, SD = 1.678), with a balanced gender distribution (50.6% male, n = 1,002; 49.4% female, n = 977). In terms of residential background, 59.5% (n = 1,177) lived in urban areas and 40.5% (n = 802) in rural areas. The majority of participating parents who completed the parental educational anxiety questionnaire were mothers (78.2%, n = 1,547), with fathers comprising 21.8% (n = 432). All parental reports were included in the analyses regardless of parent gender. This approach reflects the naturalistic family structure in which either parent may serve as the primary respondent regarding educational concerns. Parental ages ranged from 35 to 52 years (M = 42.347, SD = 4.728). Regarding educational attainment, 15.2% (n = 301) had completed junior high school or below, 38.5% (n = 762) had senior high school or vocational training, 31.8% (n = 629) held bachelor’s degrees, and 14.5% (n = 287) held graduate degrees or higher. Family monthly income was distributed as follows: <¥5,000 RMB (22.1%, n = 437), ¥5,000–10,000 (41.3%, n = 817), ¥10,000–20,000 (26.4%, n = 522), and >¥20,000 (10.2%, n = 203).

Measurement

All measurements were administered separately to parents and adolescents to minimize shared method variance. Specifically, the Parental Anxiety about Children’s Education Questionnaire was completed by parents (typically the primary caregiver, most commonly mothers), while all other instruments—including the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), Moral Disengagement Scale, Coping Style Scale for Middle School Students (negative coping subscale), and Resilience Scale—were completed by adolescent participants. This multi-informant approach enhanced the validity of our findings by reducing potential biases associated with single-source data collection.

Parental educational anxiety

Parental educational anxiety was measured using the Parental Anxiety about Children’s Education Questionnaire developed by Cheng [65], a psychometrically validated instrument that has demonstrated good reliability in samples of Chinese students [66]. The scale encompasses three key dimension -- achievement anxiety, ability anxiety, and health anxiety -- each assessed through four items, resulting in a total of 12 questions. A representative item includes: “I feel anxious and distressed when I think about my child possibly not being admitted to a good school.” Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), indicating the frequency with which they experienced the described sentiments. This multidimensional structure allows for a comprehensive assessment of parents’ academic-related concerns across different domains of anxiety. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) demonstrated an acceptable fit for a three-factor model of parental educational anxiety (χ²/df = 2.859; Comparative Fit Index [CFI] = 0.947; Tucker–Lewis Index [TLI] = 0.935; Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMSEA] = 0.041; Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR] = 0.035). A composite score reflecting overall parental educational anxiety was calculated as the mean of all item responses, where higher values indicated greater intensity of anxiety symptoms. The internal consistency of the scale was high, as indicated by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.914 for the current sample.

Depression

The present study employed the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [67], a well-validated instrument designed to assess depressive affect with a primary focus on emotional experience, while deliberately downplaying somatic symptomatology [68]. This 20-item measure has demonstrated robust psychometric properties across diverse populations, including Chinese adolescents, and is widely adopted in both empirical research and clinical settings [69]. Sample items include statements such as “I feel that my life is a failure,” with four items (Items 4, 8, 12, and 16) reverse-coded to control for response bias -- for example, “I feel that there is hope for the future.” Participants responded using a 4-point Likert scale, indicating how frequently they had experienced each feeling in the past week: 0 = “rarely or none of the time (less than 1 day),” 1 = “some or a little of the time (1–2 days),” 2 = “occasionally or a moderate amount of time (3–4 days),” and 3 = “most or all of the time (5–7 days).” After reverse-coding positive items, higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. CFA yielded evidence of a well-fitting unidimensional model for the construct of depression, with the following fit statistics: χ²/df = 2.872, CFI = 0.946, TLI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.046, and SRMR = 0.039. The reliability analysis demonstrated good internal consistency for the measure in this sample, as reflected by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.808.

Moral disengagement

The Chinese adaptation of the Moral Disengagement Scale, originally developed by Bandura et al. [70] and subsequently revised by Wang and Yang [71], was utilized to evaluate participants’ tendencies toward moral disengagement. The instrument contains 26 items representing eight cognitive mechanisms: moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison, diffusion of responsibility, displacement of responsibility, distortion of consequences, dehumanization, and attribution of blame. An item such as “Compared with those who cheat on exams, not studying hard is not a big deal” exemplifies the content of the scale. Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with elevated scores reflecting stronger moral disengagement. Previous research has confirmed the scale’s robust construct validity within Chinese student populations [72]. In the present study, the instrument demonstrated excellent reliability, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.939. Moreover, confirmatory factor analysis indicated an adequate model fit, with indices meeting recommended thresholds (CFI = 0.968, TLI = 0.962, RMSEA = 0.033), thereby substantiating the structural validity of the measure in this sample.

Negative coping style

This research employed the negative coping subscale from the Coping Style Scale for Middle School Students (CSSMSS), developed by Huang et al. [73], which drew upon the theoretical framework proposed by Schwarzer and Schwarzer [74]. This scale has demonstrated reliable psychometric properties in prior studies involving Chinese student populations [75]. The CSSMSS consists of 15 items measuring two dimensions: positive coping (problem-solving and help-seeking) and negative coping (withdrawal, fantasy, venting, and tolerance). For the purposes of this study, only the negative coping dimension was utilized. Participants were instructed to respond to the prompt: “How do you usually cope with setbacks and troubles in your studies? Please answer the following questions based on your actual situation”. An illustrative item includes, “I lose my temper and act against the people or things that cause my frustration”. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). Higher item scores denoted stronger tendencies toward negative coping behaviors. The unidimensional construct of the scale was supported by CFA, with satisfactory fit indices observed (χ²/df = 2.983; CFI = 0.958; TLI = 0.942; RMSEA = 0.047; SRMR = 0.036). Internal consistency was found to be acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.784 in the present dataset.

Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience was evaluated using the 10-item Resilience Scale developed by Campbell-Sills and Stein [76], which measures individual adaptation to stress and adversity. Respondents rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Not at all true) to 4 (Completely true), with higher scores reflecting greater resilience. This instrument has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in studies with Chinese samples [77, 78]. The structural validity of the instrument was examined further using CFA, yielding adequate model fit indices (χ²/df = 2.927; CFI = 0.962; TLI = 0.957; RMSEA = 0.043; SRMR = 0.036). The scale also demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.886).

To ensure the linguistic fidelity, psychometric reliability, and cultural appropriateness of the translated instruments, a rigorous multi-phase translation and validation protocol was employed. The initial forward translation was independently conducted by two bilingual experts with advanced proficiency in both English and Chinese, and with specialized training in educational psychology. This stage prioritized semantic accuracy and conceptual consistency, particularly within the sociocultural context of Chinese secondary education. Subsequently, a blind back-translation was performed by two additional bilingual professionals who had no prior exposure to the original English versions. The back-translated items were then systematically compared with the original source instruments to detect discrepancies in meaning or phrasing. Any identified inconsistencies were addressed through consensus discussions involving all translators and members of the research team. To further strengthen the validity of the translated measures, a panel of subject matter experts -- including educational psychologists and applied linguists -- was assembled to review the instruments. Their evaluation focused on the conceptual alignment, item clarity, and contextual appropriateness of the translated content. Revisions were implemented where necessary to improve cultural sensitivity and measurement precision. A pilot study was subsequently conducted with a convenience sample of 60 middle and high school students in China. Participants were invited to provide evaluative feedback on the clarity, readability, and interpretability of each item. In addition, cognitive interviews were conducted with a subset of respondents to probe their understanding of selected items and ensure alignment with intended meanings. Based on these findings, minor lexical adjustments were made to enhance item comprehension and linguistic fluency, ensuring that the final instruments were both contextually valid and psychometrically sound for use in the target population.

Sociodemographic and contextual variables

To reduce potential confounding and enhance the validity of the findings, all regression and structural equation models included the following demographic and contextual covariates as control variables: (1) adolescent age (in years), (2) adolescent gender (coded as 0 = male, 1 = female), (3) grade level (coded as 1 = middle school, 2 = high school), (4) residential background (coded as 0 = rural, 1 = urban), (5) school type (coded as 0 = non-key school, 1 = key school), (6) parent gender (coded as 0 = father, 1 = mother), (7) parental age (in years), (8) parental educational attainment (coded as 1 = junior high school or below, 2 = senior high school/vocational, 3 = bachelor’s degree, 4 = graduate degree or higher), and (9) family monthly income (coded as 1 = <¥5,000 RMB, 2 = ¥5,000–10,000, 3 = ¥10,000–20,000, 4 = >¥20,000). These variables were selected based on prior literature indicating their associations with parental educational anxiety, adolescent coping and cognitive processes, and depressive symptoms [7].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS Version 23.0 and Mplus Version 8.3. As an initial step, Harman’s single-factor test was employed to assess the potential presence of common method bias, which could arise from the use of uniform data collection procedures across variables. This diagnostic procedure aimed to verify that a single factor did not account for the majority of variance, thereby confirming that measurement artifacts did not compromise the integrity of the results. Subsequently, descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the central tendencies and dispersion of key variables, providing foundational insights into the overall characteristics of the dataset. To ensure the suitability of parametric testing, relevant assumptions were examined. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots for all continuous variables, and the results indicated that all key variables approximated normal distributions, supporting the application of parametric procedures. Bivariate Pearson correlations were then computed to examine the direction and magnitude of linear associations among the core constructs, offering preliminary evidence of potential relationships for further modelling. To evaluate the hypothesized chain mediation model, structural equation modelling (SEM) was conducted using Mplus. This analytic approach enabled the simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect pathways from parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression, with moral disengagement and negative coping style specified as sequential mediators. Model fit was assessed using standard indices, and mediation effects were considered statistically significant if the 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals for indirect effects excluded zero. To enhance the robustness of the mediation analysis, non-parametric bootstrapping procedures with 5000 resamples were employed. This method is particularly advantageous for testing indirect effects in models that may involve non-normal distributions, as it produces more accurate estimates of standard errors and confidence intervals. Lastly, moderated regression analyses were carried out using the PROCESS macro version 4.0 for SPSS to test the hypothesized moderating role of psychological resilience. Interaction terms were created to examine whether the strength of the association between parental educational anxiety and depression varied across different levels of resilience.

Results

Common method bias test

To evaluate potential common method variance, an exploratory factor analysis applying Harman’s one-factor criterion was conducted [79]. The exploratory factor analysis identified 13 factors, each with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first factor accounted for 32.742% of the total variance, which was below the commonly cited 40% threshold that would indicate significant common method bias.

Second, we examined variance inflation factors (VIF) to assess the degree of multicollinearity among predictor variables, as excessive collinearity may signal inflated correlations due to shared method variance [80]. VIF values for all predictors ranged from 1.091 to 1.324, substantially below the conservative threshold of 3.0, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious concern [81]. The low VIF values provide converging evidence that common method variance did not artificially inflate relationships among study variables.

Third, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis incorporating an unmeasured latent method factor (ULMF) to further assess potential method bias [82]. A common method factor was added to the structural equation model, with all adolescent-reported indicators loading on both their substantive factors and the shared method factor. The method factor was constrained to be orthogonal to all substantive constructs. Results showed that all originally significant relationships remained statistically significant after including the method factor. The average variance explained by the method factor across adolescent-reported items was 19.0%, indicating that method variance accounted for a relatively small proportion of the total variance [83]. Model fit remained excellent with the method factor included (CFI = 0.995, TLI = 0.994, RMSEA = 0.011). These findings indicate that common method variance does not meaningfully distort the substantive relationships observed in this study.

Therefore, these multiple lines of evidence -- Harman’s test, VIF diagnostics, and ULMF analysis -- converge to indicate that common method bias is unlikely to be a substantial concern in the current study.

Discussion

Parental educational anxiety positively predicts adolescent depression

Results from the current study indicate a significant positive association between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression, thus supporting Hypothesis H1. This finding strengthens the empirical case that adolescents are not only affected by what their parents do [84], but also by what their parents feel [85] -- particularly when those feelings revolve around academic success [86]. In high-pressure educational contexts, where performance often becomes entangled with self-worth, parental anxiety can inadvertently function as a relational stressor, shaping adolescents’ affective development in subtle but significant ways.

This result is consistent with prior research demonstrating a link between academic-related parenting stress and negative adolescent emotional outcomes [87]. However, the current study contributes novel insight by isolating parental educational anxiety as an independent psychological construct, rather than collapsing it into broader parenting dimensions such as over-control or psychological pressure. By doing so, it captures a more nuanced picture of how internalized parental fear -- rather than overt behavior -- may be the more salient determinant of adolescent emotional risk [88]. This refinement helps explain the inconsistencies observed in earlier literature, where parental involvement has sometimes been found to protect against, rather than predict, adolescent depression [89]. Such inconsistencies may reflect a failure to account for the motivational or emotional quality of involvement: when parental involvement is perceived as autonomy-supportive, it may foster resilience, but when it is anxiety-driven, it may produce the opposite effect [90, 91].

Critically, this study reveals that the emotional tone of the parent-child relationship, even in academically engaged families, can become pathogenic when it is infused with parental distress. Adolescents exposed to persistent academic worry may internalize these cues as signals of conditional acceptance or anticipated failure [92]. Over time, this may promote chronic self-monitoring, academic rumination, and fear of disappointing others -- hallmark features of depressive cognition in adolescence [93]. These mechanisms likely operate below conscious awareness, making them more difficult to detect through behavioral observation alone and thus under-addressed in both research and intervention.

Moreover, our findings provide empirical support for the Intergenerational Transmission of Anxiety framework [14], which posits that parental anxiety is transmitted through modeling, anxiety-specific parenting behaviors, and threat information transfer [15, 17, 94]. However, the current results also suggest that this model may benefit from further elaboration: it is not simply generalized stress, but goal-specific anxiety, especially in educational domains, that appears most psychologically corrosive for youth. In this way, our study invites a more domain-specific refinement of family-based stress models.

The observed association between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression invites consideration of how cultural context may shape this relationship. In collectivist societies characterized by strong family interdependence, adolescents may have less psychological distance from parental emotional states compared to their counterparts in individualistic cultures [95]. This reduced separation may limit adolescents’ ability to buffer themselves against parental anxiety, making them more vulnerable to internalizing their parents’ emotional distress as their own [96]. Furthermore, in cultural contexts where academic success carries social significance beyond individual achievement -- serving as a marker of family standing within the community [97] -- the psychological stakes of meeting or failing to meet expectations may be amplified [98]. These cultural dynamics suggest that parental educational anxiety operates not merely as an individual stressor but as a phenomenon embedded within broader systems of family obligation and social evaluation, which may intensify its impact on adolescent mental health in Chinese contexts.

In sum, the present study reinforces and extends existing literature by demonstrating that parental educational anxiety -- when internalized by adolescents -- can become a powerful and underrecognized driver of depressive symptoms.

The Mediating Effect of Moral Disengagement and Negative Coping Style Between Parental Educational Anxiety and Adolescent Depression

The results offer compelling support for Hypothesis H2 by revealing that moral disengagement plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression. This finding moves beyond documenting a simple association between family pressure and psychological distress, and instead emphasizes the cognitive transformation through which externally imposed anxiety becomes internalized emotional suffering [99].

Notably, the identification of moral disengagement as a mediating pathway contributes to a growing re-evaluation of its psychological function. Although traditionally conceptualized in relation to externalizing behaviors such as aggression or academic dishonesty [100, 101], the current findings suggest that moral disengagement may also function inwardly -- as a self-protective device adolescents use to distance themselves from internalized standards they fear they cannot meet [102]. Rather than merely facilitating behavioral transgression, these cognitive mechanisms may also shield the self from overwhelming guilt or perceived failure [103]. Ironically, however, this same process may impair self-integrity and erode emotional coherence over time, thereby contributing to depressive symptomatology.

In this light, moral disengagement can be interpreted as a maladaptive cognitive buffer -- temporarily insulating adolescents from the full weight of parental expectations, yet gradually undermining their emotional resilience and sense of self-efficacy. This interpretation is consistent with recent research showing that adolescents who habitually externalize responsibility or rationalize failure are more prone to depression [104, 105], particularly when these strategies interfere with authentic self-reflection and growth.

Importantly, this study also addresses a critical oversight in previous literature: the neglect of cognitive intermediaries that might explain how and why parental academic anxiety leads to internalizing symptoms. Most prior models treat adolescent responses to parental pressure as direct or emotionally driven [106, 107], overlooking the possibility that belief systems and self-regulatory schemas serve as crucial psychological conduits. By identifying moral disengagement as such a conduit, the present findings provide a more complete picture of adolescent vulnerability under conditions of high academic demand.

Furthermore, the results may help explain individual variability in adolescents’ responses to similar family environments. Not all adolescents exposed to anxious or overinvolved parents develop depression -- those who do may be more likely to adopt disengagement strategies that suppress self-awareness in the short term but generate emotional fragmentation over time [108, 109]. This highlights the need to differentiate between adolescents’ external academic behavior and their internal cognitive processing -- a distinction that has often been obscured in educational and psychological research.

Consistent with Hypothesis H3, the results demonstrate that negative coping style significantly mediates the link between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression. This finding reinforces the notion that adolescents are not passive recipients of environmental stressors; rather, they engage in active psychological processing that determines the emotional trajectory of their experiences [110]. Specifically, under conditions of persistent parental anxiety regarding academic achievement, adolescents may come to appraise challenges as insurmountable or threatening, which in turn predisposes them to rely on maladaptive coping strategies such as avoidance, withdrawal, or self-blame [111, 112].

What is particularly noteworthy is how this mediating effect sheds light on the role of coping not merely as a reaction to stress, but as a mechanism that actively shapes emotional vulnerability. While prior studies have identified negative coping as a correlate of adolescent depression [113, 114], the current findings position it within a more explanatory framework -- suggesting that it functions as a key intermediary that translates external pressure into internalized affective disturbance.

Moreover, the results offer insight into the processual dynamics of coping under academic stress. Adolescents confronted with high parental expectations may lack sufficient autonomy or emotional support to engage in problem-solving or constructive reframing [115]. As a result, they may turn to coping responses that are short-term palliatives rather than long-term solutions. Although these strategies may offer momentary relief, they also reinforce cognitive distortions [116] and emotional dysregulation [117] -- two well-established antecedents of depressive symptomatology.

Importantly, this study advances the literature by emphasizing coping style as a developmentally contingent and context-sensitive construct. Rather than treating negative coping as a fixed trait or stable predictor of psychopathology, the findings suggest that these tendencies may be socially elicited and contextually reinforced -- particularly within families where academic performance is heavily scrutinized [118]. This moves beyond generic stress models and underscores the importance of understanding how specific types of stress (i.e., parental educational anxiety) influence the formation and habitual use of maladaptive coping mechanisms.

These insights may also account for inconsistencies in previous research regarding the protective or harmful effects of parental involvement. In cases where adolescents possess sufficient psychological autonomy, parental guidance may foster adaptive coping and motivation [119]. However, when parental involvement is saturated with anxiety and conditional regard, adolescents may instead develop defensive, emotion-focused coping responses that undermine emotional health [120]. This distinction highlights the need for a qualitative shift in how adolescent coping is conceptualized -- not just as a reaction, but as a learned, dynamic strategy shaped by family emotional climates.

The findings provide empirical support for Hypothesis H4, indicating that moral disengagement and negative coping style jointly serve as sequential mediators in the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression. This result adds conceptual nuance to prior models by showing that emotional distress is not the product of a single mediating mechanism, but rather emerges from a cumulative, psychologically layered process. Specifically, adolescents exposed to high levels of parental educational anxiety may first disengage morally to cope cognitively with unrealistic expectations, which subsequently leads them to adopt maladaptive coping strategies when confronted with academic or emotional failure -- thus setting the stage for depressive outcomes.

This sequential pathway enriches our understanding of how family-based academic stress becomes internalized through distinct psychological stages [121, 122]. While moral disengagement functions as a cognitive reinterpretation mechanism that alleviates initial guilt or self-blame, it may simultaneously erode personal accountability and problem-solving motivation [102]. In turn, this cognitive detachment fosters a greater reliance on emotion-focused or avoidant coping, which fails to resolve the source of distress and instead deepens adolescents’ vulnerability to depression [95]. The data thus support a cascading mechanism in which cognitive distortion precedes and shapes behavioral maladaptation, ultimately compromising emotional well-being [123].

Importantly, this pattern helps to reconcile seemingly contradictory findings in the literature. Some studies have suggested that moral disengagement can buffer stress by preserving self-worth in the short term [124], while others highlight its detrimental effects on long-term adjustment [125]. The current model suggests that the protective function of disengagement is fragile and unsustainable, as it indirectly fosters behavioral patterns -- such as emotional suppression or resignation -- that undermine psychological resilience [126]. In other words, what begins as a defense against failure becomes a gateway to emotional impairment.

This sequential model also provides a compelling account for why certain adolescents appear more susceptible to depressive outcomes in high-pressure environments [127]. Rather than attributing vulnerability solely to external stress or static personality traits, the findings underscore how interacting psychological processes -- first cognitive [128], then behavioral [129] -- jointly contribute to mental health trajectories. Adolescents who simultaneously disengage from internal moral standards and fail to adopt adaptive coping are particularly at risk, suggesting that interventions targeting only one pathway may fall short.

Furthermore, the observed chain mediation model invites a rethinking of intervention priorities. Programs that aim to reduce depression in academically pressured adolescents may benefit from a dual-pronged approach: first, by challenging distorted cognitive appraisals (e.g., self-justifying failure), and second, by fostering more adaptive behavioral coping strategies. Educational systems and families should also be encouraged to create environments where academic difficulties are framed as opportunities for growth, rather than as threats to identity or parental approval.

The sequential mediation observed in this study -- from parental educational anxiety through moral disengagement and negative coping to depression -- may be particularly pronounced in the Chinese cultural context. When academic expectations are framed as familial obligations rather than personal goals [130], adolescents who fail to meet these expectations may experience not only achievement-related stress but also moral conflict regarding filial duties [131]. This conflict may explain why moral disengagement emerges as a salient cognitive mechanism: it allows adolescents to temporarily relieve guilt associated with perceived failure to honor parents [132]. Subsequently, in cultures where open emotional expression or help-seeking may threaten family harmony [133], adolescents may preferentially adopt avoidant or suppressive coping strategies that, while culturally normative, prove maladaptive for mental health [134]. These cultural dynamics help contextualize why both cognitive (moral disengagement) and behavioral (negative coping) pathways operate sequentially and powerfully in linking parental anxiety to adolescent depression within Chinese families.

Taken together, the findings not only validate the sequential mediation hypothesis but also underscore the interdependence between how adolescents think and how they cope, particularly under the strain of parental academic anxiety. This layered mechanism opens new avenues for research and practice focused on disrupting the chain of risk that links external pressure to internal psychological harm.

The moderating effect of psychological resilience between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression

The results offer robust support for Hypothesis H5, indicating that psychological resilience moderates the relationship between parental educational anxiety and adolescent depression, such that the association is significantly attenuated among adolescents with higher levels of resilience. This finding not only affirms resilience as a protective factor but also situates it within a more nuanced stress-response framework, in which individual capacity for adaptation shapes the emotional consequences of environmental pressure [135].

While parental educational anxiety constitutes a salient psychosocial stressor -- especially in cultures that prize academic success -- [136] resilience functions as a psychological shield, enabling adolescents to maintain emotional stability and meaning-making under strain [137]. In this study, the depressive impact of parental anxiety was not uniform across the sample, but varied systematically according to adolescents’ resilience profiles, suggesting that resilience modifies the very pathway by which stress translates into symptoms [53].

What is particularly noteworthy is that the buffering effect of resilience did not simply reduce depressive symptoms overall, but specifically weakened the predictive strength of parental anxiety. This implies that resilient adolescents may not only experience less distress but also process parental expectations differently [138] -- possibly by reframing academic pressure as challenge rather than threat, maintaining self-worth independent of external evaluation [139], or mobilizing internal regulatory resources such as optimism, flexibility, and problem-focused coping [140].

These findings are consistent with a growing body of research showing that resilience mitigates the emotional fallout of family-based stress [141, 142]. However, the present study extends this literature by focusing on education-specific stressors, which are often normalized or even valorized in high-achieving contexts. By highlighting the moderating role of resilience in this specific relational context, the findings offer a corrective to research that treats academic pressure as uniformly harmful, without attending to individual-level variation in stress reactivity.

Moreover, this moderation effect helps explain why some adolescents thrive -- even flourish -- under high expectations [143], while others develop significant emotional dysfunction [144]. It underscores the need to move beyond generic assumptions about “pressure” and toward a differentiated understanding of stress appraisal and regulation. In doing so, the results suggest important implications for both prevention and intervention: resilience-building should not be treated as an optional add-on but as a core objective of adolescent development programs, particularly in academically competitive societies.

Despite these strengths, the interaction pattern also invites further inquiry. While resilience moderated the direct link between parental anxiety and depression, it remains unclear whether it similarly buffers the indirect effects via cognitive and behavioral mediators (e.g., moral disengagement, coping). Future research might explore moderated mediation models to test whether resilience not only dampens direct stress effects but also interrupts maladaptive pathways, offering a more comprehensive understanding of its protective scope.

The protective function of resilience observed in this study may also have culture-specific dimensions. In Chinese contexts, resilient adolescents may be those who can navigate the tension between meeting high parental expectations and maintaining personal well-being [145]. Rather than rejecting academic pressure entirely, resilient Chinese youth may draw upon culturally valued narratives of perseverance and effort (such as “eating bitterness” or chiku) that help them reframe challenges as opportunities for growth [146]. This suggests that resilience interventions in Chinese contexts should consider incorporating culturally congruent coping resources rather than solely adopting frameworks developed in Western individualistic settings.

In sum, the findings provide empirical validation for the buffering role of resilience and highlight its critical importance in educational and family psychology. Promoting psychological resilience in adolescents -- through school-based curricula, family systems interventions, and community programs -- may offer a powerful strategy for mitigating the mental health risks posed by escalating parental educational anxiety.

Theoretical contribution and implications for the practice

This study offers several meaningful contributions to the theoretical understanding of adolescent depression in academic contexts, particularly within achievement-oriented family environments. First, by positioning parental educational anxiety as a distinct and measurable psychological construct rather than a subsidiary aspect of general parenting stress, the study extends the Intergenerational Transmission of Anxiety framework into the educational domain. This reconceptualization allows for a more precise understanding of how performance-related parental concerns function as chronic stressors in adolescents’ emotional development.

Second, the proposed sequential mediation model -- wherein moral disengagement and negative coping style jointly transmit the effects of parental anxiety to depressive symptoms -- adds important cognitive and behavioral depth to existing explanatory frameworks. Prior research has often examined these mechanisms in isolation; by integrating them, this study advances a more dynamic, process-oriented account of internalizing symptom development. The identification of moral disengagement as a cognitive entry point leading to maladaptive coping underscores the need to view adolescent psychopathology not merely as an outcome of stress exposure but as a result of evolving internal processes that translate stress into psychological harm.

Third, the moderating role of psychological resilience enriches our understanding of individual variability in stress reactivity, building upon but also refining the diathesis–stress perspective. Rather than treating resilience as a uniform buffer, this study situates it within a conditional framework -- showing that its protective influence is most evident when adolescents are exposed to elevated external demands. This finding invites more theoretically informed discussions about for whom and under what conditions parental academic pressure becomes harmful.

From a practical standpoint, the findings carry several implications. First, they underscore the importance of parent-focused interventions aimed at reducing excessive academic anxiety and promoting healthier forms of parental involvement. Educators, counselors, and policymakers in high-pressure educational systems should develop programs that encourage parents to adopt supportive rather than controlling strategies, particularly in cultures where academic success is closely tied to family status or future security.

Second, the identification of moral disengagement and negative coping as mediating mechanisms points to adolescent-focused preventive strategies that target these internal processes. Interventions that promote moral responsibility, emotional regulation, and problem-focused coping may serve as effective buffers against depression, especially for students navigating parental expectations. School-based programs incorporating cognitive-behavioral skills training, peer discussion, and self-reflective activities may be particularly effective in interrupting maladaptive psychological pathways.

Finally, the demonstrated role of resilience suggests that fostering psychological adaptability should be a central goal in adolescent development programs. This includes not only individual-level training (e.g., mindfulness, reframing, self-efficacy building) but also system-level initiatives that create emotionally safe, autonomy-supportive school and family environments. Rather than eliminating all forms of pressure, such efforts would empower adolescents to engage with challenges in ways that support growth rather than trigger vulnerability.

In sum, this study bridges conceptual, empirical, and applied domains, offering a nuanced understanding of how parental academic pressure interacts with adolescent cognition and coping to shape emotional well-being. These insights provide a fertile foundation for future theory building and intervention design in both clinical and educational psychology, particularly in academically competitive East Asian contexts.

Limitations and suggestions

This study provides novel insights into the mechanisms linking parental educational anxiety to adolescent depression; however, several limitations should be acknowledged to guide interpretation and future research.

First, the dataset was collected exclusively from three provinces in China -- Liaoning, Shandong, and Shanghai -- which, while diverse in economic development and educational culture, may not fully capture the broader heterogeneity of the Chinese adolescent population. Educational systems, parental expectations, and adolescent mental health literacy can vary widely across regions. To enhance external validity, future research should consider nationally representative samples encompassing a wider array of geographical regions, urban and rural settings, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Such expansion would enable cross-regional comparisons and offer more generalizable insights into how parental educational anxiety operates across different sociocultural contexts.

Second, although the study employed multi-source data -- collecting parental educational anxiety from parent reports and other psychological variables from adolescent self-reports -- potential cross-informant discrepancies remain. Parents’ self-reported anxiety may not fully correspond to adolescents’ lived experiences or interpretations of academic pressure. Likewise, adolescents’ perceptions of their coping responses or psychological distress may be shaped by momentary emotional states or social desirability. While this approach reduces common method variance, future research should incorporate more integrative designs, such as parent-child dyadic analyses or observational methods, to capture the bidirectional and interactive dynamics of family stress and adolescent adjustment.

Third, the cross-sectional nature of the research design limits the ability to draw causal inferences or confirm developmental pathways. Although the hypothesized mediation and moderation effects are supported by theoretical rationale and statistical evidence, longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal ordering and to explore how psychological responses to parental educational anxiety unfold across developmental stages.

Fourth, while the study focused on moral disengagement, negative coping, and resilience as central mechanisms, other potentially salient variables -- such as academic self-efficacy, perfectionism, peer dynamics, or school-level stressors -- were not included. The interplay between family-based pressure and adolescent mental health likely involves a broader network of influences, suggesting that future studies should adopt more comprehensive, ecologically informed models.

Fifth, although this study posits a sequential pathway from moral disengagement to negative coping, it is equally plausible that the process operates in reverse: adolescents with preexisting negative coping tendencies may encounter repeated failures and subsequently adopt moral disengagement as a defensive rationalization. Reciprocal relationships between these mechanisms cannot be ruled out. Future research should employ longitudinal or intervention-based designs to disentangle temporal ordering and test whether targeting one mechanism (e.g., reducing moral disengagement) causally influences the other (e.g., coping strategies). Such evidence would clarify whether these pathways are unidirectional, bidirectional, or context-dependent.

Sixth, although psychological resilience was modeled as a moderator, it was treated as a stable individual difference variable. However, current theoretical frameworks increasingly conceptualize resilience as a dynamic, context-sensitive process that evolves over time. Future research may benefit from repeated measures or experience sampling methods that capture the fluctuating nature of resilience in adolescents navigating academic pressure.

Finally, while the current study situates its findings within the Chinese cultural context by discussing filial piety, face consciousness, and collective achievement norms, a more comprehensive examination of these cultural factors could have strengthened our interpretations. Although we acknowledge these cultural dimensions in our discussion, deeper integration of culturally grounded theories throughout the manuscript, such as indigenous Chinese psychology frameworks, would have provided a more nuanced understanding of how these factors specifically shape the observed pathways. Future research would benefit from both cross-cultural quantitative comparisons to test the cultural specificity of these mechanisms, and qualitative approaches such as in-depth interviews to explore how families from different backgrounds navigate cultural expectations in daily life. Such mixed-methods designs integrating cultural theory with lived experience would enhance both theoretical depth and practical applicability.

Conclusions

This study contributes to the growing literature on family-based academic stress by elucidating how parental educational anxiety influences adolescent depression through distinct psychological pathways. Drawing on a multi-theoretical framework, the research identifies moral disengagement and negative coping style as sequential mediators, while also highlighting the moderating role of psychological resilience. These findings offer empirical support for a nuanced, process-oriented model of adolescent emotional vulnerability, in which parental educational anxiety is cognitively and behaviorally internalized in ways that may undermine mental health.

Importantly, this study advances the conceptualization of parental educational anxiety as a unique and impactful stressor in achievement-oriented societies, distinct from general parenting stress or academic pressure. The results underscore the importance of addressing not only overt parental behaviors but also adolescents’ interpretations and coping responses to these dynamics. Furthermore, the identification of psychological resilience as a protective factor provides a hopeful avenue for prevention and intervention, emphasizing the value of cultivating adaptive capacities in youth as they navigate complex familial and academic expectations.

Taken together, these findings call for more contextually sensitive, developmentally informed, and culturally grounded approaches to understanding and supporting adolescent mental health in high-pressure educational environments. Future research and practice must work in tandem to design holistic strategies that alleviate harmful aspects of parental anxiety while empowering adolescents to manage stress in constructive and resilient ways.

All procedures involving human participants adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent revisions, as well as the institutional guidelines established by the university’s ethics board. Before initiating data collection, all participating students and their legal guardians were provided with detailed written information outlining the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, voluntary nature of participation, and assurances of confidentiality, in strict adherence to institutional ethical standards. Informed consent was formally obtained from both groups. To uphold participants’ anonymity and safeguard their privacy, identifying information -- such as names or signatures -- was not required on the consent forms. Participants were clearly informed that they could discontinue their involvement at any point without any negative repercussions. In cases where matched data from students and parents were collected, individualized consent was sought from each party separately, with participation proceeding only when consent was granted by both the adolescent and their guardian.
