Association between depression symptoms at the early stage of ART treatment and the likelihood of becoming pregnant: a prospective cohort study
Authors: July Khin Maung Soe, Stuart Gilmour, Makiko Sampei, Kazuki Saito, Kevin Y. Urayama, Tsuguhiko Kato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePrevious studies have shown that receiving Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) treatment can affect women’s mental health negatively. Using prospective cohort data of women undergoing ART treatment in Japan, we investigated the associations of depression symptoms at the early stage of ART treatment with (1) the likelihood of achieving pregnancy and (2) depression symptoms at the time of reporting pregnancy during the follow-up period.MethodsOur analysis included 338 women. Our exposure variable was depressive symptoms at the early stage of ART treatment, assessed with Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self report. We examined the associations with two outcomes: (1) archiving pregnancy and (2) depression symptoms, assessed with Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, at the time of reporting pregnancy. We conducted Poisson regression analysis to examine the associations, controlling for potential confounders.ResultsWe observed null association between depressive symptoms at the early stage of the treatment and the likelihood of achieving pregnancy. However, women with mild or more severe depressive symptoms were more likely to exhibit depressive symptoms at the time of reporting their pregnancy [Incidence Rate Ratio = 3.11 (95% confidence intervals: 1.66–5.84) with the mild symptom group].ConclusionNull association of depressive symptoms at the early stage of treatment with the likelihood of achieving pregnancy may provide reassurance to women receiving ART treatment, alleviating some of the possible mental distress. However, a greater risk of showing depressive symptoms at the time of reporting pregnancy highlights the importance of early detection and support for women’s mental health during the ART treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08130-w.

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines infertility as the inability to conceive following a year of regular unprotected sexual intercourse [1–4]. Globally, approximately one out of every six individuals are affected, with prevalence of 18% in high-income nations and 17% in middle to low-income countries [2–4]. Despite its widespread occurrence, addressing infertility remains challenging due to financial constraints and limited accessibility of treatments like Assisted Reproductive Techniques (ART), such as In-Vitro Fertilization (IVF). As per the Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology’s 2021 report, there was a cumulative total of 498,140 ART procedures. Of 88,362 were IVF, 170,350 were Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI), and 239,428 were Frozen Embryo Transfer (FET) treatments. Consequently, there were 2,268 IVF births, 2,850 ICSI births, and 64,679 FET births [5] in 2021. Since the 1800 s, Japan’s fertility rate has been declining, yet it has consistently retained its status as one of the countries with the highest number of ART treatment centers.

ART treatments serve as a pivotal resource in addressing infertility; nevertheless, they are not free from challenges, particularly concerning the mental well-being of both the women and men involved. Research indicates that various stages of ART treatment can induce psychological distress [6]. Prior to commencing treatment, women often grapple with the persistent stress induced by infertility, viewing ART as a final opportunity for biological conception. As treatment progresses, emotional complexities such as fear, uncertainty, and ethical considerations surrounding the procedure intensify [6]. Additionally, the waiting periods for pregnancy results, encountering negative test outcomes, awaiting updates on fertilization, and enduring breaks between IVF cycles constitute highly stressful junctures once treatment is underway [7]. A meta-analysis reported that there is little evidence of association between stress or distress and lower pregnancy rates with ART, as the number of studies was limited and results varied across studies [8, 9]. Though a possibility that stress and anxiety among women undergoing ART treatment reduce the chance of achieving pregnancy seems plausible, no consensus has been reached. Consequently, there is a demand for further research to explore the relationship between mental health and the outcomes of ART treatments. To our knowledge, there has been no study conducted in Japan about the association between mental health status during ART treatment and pregnancy.

Utilizing a prospective cohort study data [9], our primary research question explores the relationship of baseline mental health status with the likelihood of achieving pregnancy during the study period. Additionally, as our second research question, we explored whether or not baseline mental health status influenced mental health status at the time of pregnancy among participants who achieved pregnancy during the study period.

Materials and methods

Study setting and population

We conducted secondary data analysis using data from a prospective cohort study involving women undergoing ART treatment in Japan. The specific criteria for eligibility can be found within the original study [9]. A total of 513 participants provided their informed consent and completed the first survey. In this study, for our first analysis, we restricted our analytical sample to women with oocytes retrieval less than or equal to 2 who could be considered as being at the early stage of ART treatment. This group may be more sensitive to the psychological burden of ART, making it more of a relevant population for studying how mental health status might influence pregnancy outcomes. We excluded 25 women who had undergone three or more oocyte retrievals, leaving 488 women with two or fewer retrievals. We also restricted our analysis to women with no child as having one or more children may alleviate the pressure of wanting a child through the ART treatment. We excluded 56 women who had at least one child. During the study, 94 participants dropped out. After applying these exclusion criteria, we retained 338 participants with no children and the experience of less than or equal to 2 oocyte retrievals who reported pregnancy or did not report pregnancy. This group formed the analytic sample for our first research question. Among the 338 participants, 203 reported pregnancy during the study period. This group was used to examine our second research question. The process of selecting the analytical sample is shown in Fig. 1. Inclusion and Exclusion criteria

We used the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self report (QIDS) to assess depressive symptoms across the entire sample. QIDS is a self-assessment scale with 16 questions to assess depressive symptoms [10]. We categorized the total score into the following: no depression (0–5 points), mild depression [6–10], moderate depression [11–15], severe depression [16–20], and very severe depression [21–27]. In our analysis, we combined the categories of “severe depression” and “very severe depression” because of the small proportion in the very severe category. While QIDS is not developed specifically for ART population, it captures core symptoms of major depressive disorder and have been adapted by many studies to assess depression symptoms [10]. We used Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) to assess depressive symptoms among women who reported pregnancy. EPDS is originally designed for postpartum population [11] and has been validated for use during pregnancy in the Japanese population [12]. We considered the total score of 9 points or above as at risk and the score of 8 or below as not at risk [13]. Both scales have been widely used and validated as screening tools for assessing depressive symptoms.

This research was conducted in full adherence to the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects and received ethical approval from the committee at the National Center for Child Health and Development (Approval No. 1993).

Follow-up period

Participants were followed-up for 11 months during which they filled out self-administered questionnaires every two months. We refer to the first survey as the baseline questionnaire, and the subsequent six surveys as follow-up questionnaires. EPDS was administered to those who reported pregnancy during the follow-up.

Main outcomes

The primary outcome of this study is the reporting of a pregnancy during the study period. The secondary outcome is depression symptoms among women who have reported pregnancy.

Socio-demographic, clinical, and cofounding variables

We collected a wide range of socio-demographic data from our study participants, covering various variables including age, educational background, income, BMI, and employment status. Employment status was classified into four distinct categories: full-time, part-time, self-employed, and unemployed. In the ‘unemployed’ category, we included students and housewives, while ‘part-time’ includes contractors as well. Educational levels were also classified into three categories: high school graduate or lower, vocational school or 2-year college, and 4-year college or graduate university. The ‘vocational schools’ category includes both high school-level and 2-year college-level vocational schools. Participants’ annual income in Japanese Yen (JPY) were categorized into three categories: less than four million JPY annually, four to eight million JPY annually, and more than eight million JPY. Additionally, we used clinical information, such as treatment duration, reasons for infertility, and occurrence of stillbirths in the past.

Statistical analyses

We first conducted descriptive analysis of socio-demographic and clinical information as well as QIDS and EPDS results. Next, we conducted Poisson regression analysis to examine the association between depressive symptoms at the baseline and the chance of pregnancy during the study period, controlling for potential confounders. We included age, education, duration of treatment, reasons for infertility, number of oocyte retrievals, and experience of still birth as potential confounders. We then estimated the risk of depressive symptoms in women who reported pregnancy. In our study sample, 60% of the women reported pregnancy during the study period, and 28% of those who reported pregnancy were categorized as showing depressive symptoms at the time of reporting pregnancy. Because of the high occurrence in these two outcomes, we chose to conduct Poisson regression analysis, as odds ratio estimates can provide a biased estimate of relative risk in cohort studies with common events [14]. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with STATA 17.0 BE (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Discussion

Our study on the mental health of women undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) in Japan using the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology found that 33% of our sample reported mild, 17% reported moderate, and 7% reported severe or very severe depressive symptoms at the baseline. We observed null association between depressive symptoms and the chance of reporting pregnancy. However, women with depressive symptoms at baseline showed a higher chance to show depressive symptoms, assessed with EPDS, at the reporting time of pregnancy: incidence rate ratio of 3.11 for mild depressive symptom, 4.08 for moderate symptom, and 4.03 for sever or very severe symptom. To our knowledge, our study is the first in Japan examining the association of depressive symptoms in women undergoing ART treatment and the chance of pregnancy using cohort study data

Comparison with previous findings

A comprehensive meta-analysis of prospective psychosocial studies conducted in the United Kingdom has revealed that emotional distress may not compromise the likelihood of pregnancy [15]. This extensive review consisted of 14 studies involving 3583 infertile women undergoing fertility treatment. Another prospective study found no evidence linking psychological stress to the outcomes of IVF treatment in a sample of 166 individuals undergoing IVF procedures, assessed using Psychological General Well-Being index [16]. Collectively, these findings indicated that while infertility treatments can be emotionally taxing, psychological distress may not impede the chances of successful conception. This growing body of evidence could inform and reassure both patients and practitioners within reproductive medicine. One comprehensive meta-analysis reviewed the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions for infertile women but presented inconclusive findings due to several methodological limitations present in the reviewed studies [17]. Our study could provide one improvement upon previous studies with its prospective cohort study design as we could identify the temporal sequence of events. Our findings on the null association with pregnancy outcome aligns with several studies from Western populations. Meta-analyses [8, 15] reported no significant impact of emotional distress on ART success. More recently a prospective study [18] similarly found that depression, anxiety, and stress before treatment initiation did not reduce chances of conception via ART, and another systematic review shows how the outcomes of IVF influence emotional adjustment among women [19]. By replicating these findings in a Japanese cohort, our study provides cross-cultural validation of this conclusion, suggesting that pre-treatment psychological status may not influence physiological outcomes of ART.

While previous studies have examined psychological distress after ART failure or during pregnancy conceived via ART [20, 21], few have investigated whether depressive symptoms present before ART would be associated with emotional outcomes after successful conception. Our study also explores this association by showing that mild to severe depressive symptoms at the early stage of ART treatment may be predictive of depressive symptoms during pregnancy. This finding suggests mental health care during and throughout ART treatment is crucial. Most existing research on ART and mental health is based on Western populations, where mental health stigma, access to care, and fertility expectations differ from those in Japan. By focusing on a Japanese cohort, our study provides context-specific data relevant for clinicians and policymakers in Asia, responds to calls for diversifying evidence in reproductive mental health research and highlights how even mild depressive symptoms, often dismissed or underreported in Asian cultures, may have long-term emotional impacts. This makes our study a valuable addition to existing literature.

Implications and relevance of our findings to public health

Our results showed that women showing mild to very severe depressive symptoms at the early stage of the ART treatment were at much higher risk of depressive symptoms at the time of reporting pregnancy compared to those with no symptom.

The relative risks estimated with IRRs ranged from 3.11 to 4.08. Our findings suggest that adopting approaches similar to those of the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) should be considered to meet social and emotional needs of the women and couples who receive ART treatment in Japan. ESHRE recommends routine psychosocial care for infertility and medically assisted reproduction treatments. Providing psychosocial support ensures comprehensive care, reducing psychological burdens and potentially improving treatment outcomes such as psychosocial outcomes [22]. Studies show women receiving psychological support during infertility treatments experience better emotional adjustment and higher satisfaction [22, 23]. Implementing psychosocial interventions with ART treatments can lower adverse birth outcomes [23]. ESHRE emphasizes addressing relational, social, emotional, and cognitive needs, supporting socially isolated patients, involving both partners, and enhancing clinic information accessibility [24]. Addressing mental health in ART treatment can significantly reduce the risk of negative birth outcomes, reported by existing literature. An intervention study has demonstrated that patient education and care programs significantly enhance patient satisfaction, thereby contributing to improved treatment environment scores on the FertiQol assessment among women undergoing non-ART fertility treatments [25]. Another intervention study found that the implementation of a partnership support program effectively reduced psychological distress in women receiving infertility treatment [26]. Currently, there are a couple intervention programs in Japan aim to support the mental health of women undergoing infertility treatment [27]. Nevertheless, further initiatives and research are necessary to adequately address this issue.

It is essential to detect these symptoms early to prevent them from developing into more serious conditions that can impact both the mother and the baby. A meta-analysis reported that evidence suggests that perinatal depression and anxiety in mothers are adversely associated with offspring development such as social-emotional, cognitive, language and motor [28]. A study in Denmark also noted on the significant impact of postpartum depression on mothers, which can persist well beyond the initial postpartum period [29]. Research conducted in South Italy that depressive episodes in new mothers can during pregnancy or after childbirth, with different triggers and manifestations in each phase [30]. Additional meta-analysis study revelated maternal depressive symptoms are related to lower cognitive scores among children aged </56 months [31]. Another systematic review supports this evidence by stating that prenatal distress can have an adverse effect on cognitive, behavioral and psychomotor development [32]. A meta-analysis conducted in the United States found that depression during pregnancy was significantly associated with preterm birth and low birth rate [33].

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of few cohort studies in Japan that investigated depression symptoms of women undergoing ART treatment. Our study allows us to explore the association with the likelihood of pregnancy as well as the presence or absence of depressive symptoms at the time of reporting pregnancy. Our research, centered on a Japanese population, brings insight to reproductive mental health, an area largely shaped by Western data. Our findings also show significance on even low-level depressive symptoms, frequently minimized or unspoken in many Asian societies can have lasting emotional effects. This perspective helps bridge an important gap in the global understanding of depressive symptoms and likelihood of pregnancy of women undergoing ART treatment.

This study has several limitations. Due to the absence of random sampling, our findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader population of individuals undergoing infertility treatment in Japan. National-level data on the socio-demographic profiles of women receiving ART are currently lacking, which further limits the representativeness of our sample. The Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology lacks detailed records of sociodemographic data; therefore, we are unable to make a comparison reference between our study sample and the general population [5].

Although we utilized well-established scales such as the QIDS and EPDS, these were not specifically developed for populations undergoing infertility treatments. Scales specifically developed for the ART population such as COMPI-FPSS and SCREENIVF have been widely used in European settings to assess psychosocial aspects of infertility treatment. SCREENIVF has been translated into Japanese in 2023 [34]. New studies may be able to use culturally and contextually appropriate assessment tools such as the Japanese version of SCREENIVF. Additionally, we lack information on participants’ mental health status prior to initiating ART or biomedical information to control for in the analysis. Our inability to control for these potential confounders due to the absence of data may cause some bias in the results. We did not examine factors such as social support, treatment adherence, and partner involvement as potential mediators in this study. Further studies incorporating these variables could help clarify the pathways linking baseline depressive symptoms with mental health during pregnancy in ART populations. Despite these limitations, the scarcity of epidemiological data on ART patients in Japan underscores the importance of our findings. Future research should aim to include more representative samples through random sampling and national-level data to enhance generalizability.

Conclusion

The aim of our study was to examine the association of depression symptoms at the early stage of ART treatment with the likelihood of achieving pregnancy, and our results show that depression symptoms were not associated with the likelihood of achieving pregnancy. However, women undergoing ART in Japan who already exhibit mild to very severe depressive symptoms could be at risk of great depression symptoms at the time of pregnancy. Our findings highlight the importance of early detection and support for depressive symptoms as our study found association of mental health at baseline and mental health at the time of pregnancy. It is crucial to maintain the quality of life for women undergoing ART treatment, as it can be beneficial for their overall well-being, particularly if they become pregnant.
