Relationship of ABO blood groups with metabolic syndrome and its metabolic abnormalities in a population in North Lahore
Authors: Mohammad Perwaiz Iqbal, Hijab Sarfraz Ahmad, Saleem Perwaiz Iqbal, Muhammad Khurram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:To examine the relationship of ABO blood groups with metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its metabolic abnormalities in an urban population in Lahore.Methodology:A cross-sectional study was carried out at the Department of Life Sciences, University of Management and Technology, Lahore from August 2022 to August 2024 on 890 healthy subjects recruited from localities in Northern Lahore with informed consent. Blood samples were analyzed for ABO blood groups and three metabolic abnormalities, namely: fasting hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol. In addition, central obesity and blood pressure were determined. A brief questionnaire covering demographic characteristics was also administered. Chi-square test and logistic regression were used for determining the association between ABO blood groups and MetS and its metabolic abnormalities.Results:Adults from 18 years to 75 years had a Mean ± SD age of 40.87±11.84 years. Percentages of A, B, AB and O blood groups were 22%, 41%, 11% and 26%, respectively. No association was found between ABO blood groups and MetS (p-value=0.88), however the adjusted odds of having hypertriglyceridemia in blood group A subjects was 1.8 times higher compared to subjects with blood group AB [AOR: 1.80; 95% CI: 1.02-3.18].Conclusion:There was a lack of association between ABO blood groups and MetS. Blood group A individuals appear more vulnerable to having hypertriglyceridemia in this population.KEYWORDS: ABO blood groups, Hypertension, Hypertriglyceridemia, Hyperglycemia, Low HDL Cholesterol, Metabolic Syndrome, Obesity, Urban Population

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a chronic metabolic condition/disorder that has affected nearly a quarter of the adult population in the whole world.1 Those suffering from this condition have increased risk of developing cardiovascular diseases and Type-2 diabetes mellitus.2 The five components of MetS include: hyperglycemia, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL cholesterol and abdominal obesity.2 Since ABO blood groups are genetically predetermined, it is likely that they could be associated with MetS or some of its components which also have a genetic inheritance. Although there have been a few studies carried out in South Asia and the Middle East on the association of ABO blood groups with MetS, there has been hardly any study carried out in Pakistan to explore this relationship in the Pakistani population.3-6

All the published reports have shown conflicting results with associations of ABO blood groups with some of the metabolic components but not with MetS.3,4 Therefore, the objective of the study was to explore the relationship of ABO blood groups with MetS and its metabolic abnormalities in an urban population in the North of Lahore.

METHODOLOGY

It was a cross-sectional study, which involved recruitment of 890 healthy adults, who were recruited from the urban Northern areas of Lahore from August 2022 to August 2024, with informed consent. Their fasting blood samples were obtained for determining the levels of serum glucose, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol.

Ethical Approval:

The study was approved by the Biosafety and Bioethics Committee, University of Management and Technology, Lahore (Ref: sbbc-2023-09, dated August 7, 2023).

Inclusion criteria:

Adults in age range 18-75 years with no chronic ailment (except medically managed diabetes and hypertension) such as cancer, liver disease, kidney disease and malabsorption syndrome.

Exclusion criteria:

It included pregnant females and those suffering from any of the above-mentioned chronic diseases.

ABO blood groups were determined by using a commercially available kit based on direct hemagglutination using anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D monoclonal antibodies (Diagast, France). A brief questionnaire comprising demographic information such as age, gender, marital status, education, and average monthly household income was also administered. Waist circumference was determined using a measuring tape, while blood pressure was monitored using a sphygmomanometer. MetS was defined according to the Harmonized Criteria as described by Alberti et al.7 According to these criteria, the cut-off values of the five components of MetS (i.e. metabolic abnormalities) are as follows: Waist circumference > 90 cm in males or > 80 cm in females, Triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL (or its equivalent), HDL-C < 40 mg/dL (or its equivalent) in males or < 50 mg/dL (or its equivalent) in females, Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg, Fasting serum glucose ≥ 125 mg/dL (or its equivalent). MetS was defined as having at least three of the five above-mentioned components. For waist circumference, WHO criteria for abdominal obesity were used.8 Biochemical markers such as serum glucose, triglycerides and HDL cholesterol were quantitated using commercially available kits as per manufacturers’ instructions as described in a previous publication.9

Statistical analysis:

Statistical analysis was done using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0. Mean ± SD values were compared using independent sample t-test. Chi-square test was applied for comparing percentages of various parameters, while logistic regression was used to find out the association of blood groups with MetS and its metabolic components. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Mean ± SD age of the recruited subjects was 40.87 ± 11.84 years. There were more females compared to males (58% vs. 42%). A greater proportion of subjects were married (Table-I). Nearly 77% of them belonged to the lower socioeconomic strata with average household income below PKR 50,000 per month. The percentages of blood groups A, B, AB and O were 22%, 41%, 11% and 26%, respectively. The proportions of MetS and its five components have been listed in Table-II. The percentage of MetS in this population was 73%, while the percentage value of abdominal obesity was 79%. Hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol were highly prevalent. Hyperglycemia appeared to be less prevalent (47%).

Table-IV depicts the adjusted odds ratios and the 95% confidence intervals (AORs, 95% CI) for association of ABO blood groups with MetS and its five components with respect to demographic variables. With one year increase in age, the adjusted odds of MetS, abdominal obesity, high blood pressure, high fasting serum glucose levels, high concentrations of serum triglycerides increase by 1.11, 1.07, 1.11, 1.08 and 1.03, respectively. DISCUSSION

We found no association of ABO blood groups with MetS. These results are in line with those reported in four studies carried out on South Asian and Middle Eastern populations.3-6 This could be due to the fact that in most of these studies sample size was small in those former studies. However, some of these studies have reported an association of ABO blood groups with some of the metabolic abnormalities associated with MetS. For example, Mashahit et al. found no association of ABO blood groups with MetS but did find an association of blood groups O and AB with BMI in an Egyptian population.4 Similarly, Shaikh et al. reported an association of ABO blood groups with lipid profile in young adults of Hyderabad, Sindh, documenting increased serum levels of triglycerides and cholesterol in B and O blood groups.10 In the present study, we found a lack of association of ABO blood groups with MetS but found an association of blood group A with hypertriglyceridemia in the population in Lahore with 1.8 times adjusted odds of having hypertriglyceridemia compared to subjects with blood group AB.

The overall prevalence of hypertriglyceridemia in this study was found to be 75% (Table-II) and was the highest metabolic abnormality after abdominal obesity. This is further supported by a very comprehensive study comprising 522,719 subjects from healthcare centers in four provinces of Pakistan showing severe hypertriglyceridemia in this country, which is an established risk factor for cardiovascular disease in the Pakistani population.11 A study carried out in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Pakistan by Hussain et al. found hypertriglyceridemia’s prevalence to be 72.09% and a major risk factor for coronary artery disease in this population.12 Ijaz et al have also shown similar association of hypertriglyceridemia with cardiovascular disease in a younger Pakistani population.13

All these reports indicate that hypertriglyceridemia is one of the most common metabolic abnormalities in Pakistan and a risk factor for both cardiovascular disease and MetS. Chen et al in a systematic review and meta-analysis have shown an association of blood Group-A with coronary artery disease [OR: 1.14,95% CI (1.03-1.26)].14 This alludes to the fact that blood Group-A subjects are more likely to have metabolic abnormalities linked to the development of cardiovascular disease. This is supported by a study from Wazirali et al. who have reported an association of blood group A with increased risk of coronary heart disease in the Pakistani Population.15

In a population-based study, triglycerides were reported to have a causal role in coronary artery disease.16 Similarly, Tune et al. have reported that cardiovascular events are consequences of MetS.17 Our study shows an association of blood Group-A with hypertriglyceridemia which is very common in the Pakistani population, therefore it can be conjectured that blood Group-A subjects are more vulnerable to having this metabolic abnormality. Thus, blood Group-A subjects in this population are candidates for change in dietary habits to reduce the risk of hypertriglyceridemia. These dietary modifications include avoidance of oily and fried foods, sugary drinks, red meat and butter, and requirement of the use of Omega-3 fatty acids.18 Those having very high levels of triglycerides will be candidates for drug therapy to reduce serum triglycerides.19,20

Strength

A high sample size is a strength of this study.

Limitations

The recruitment was done by a convenient sample design, therefore some element of bias cannot be ruled out. Secondly, the recruitment was from localities in Northern Lahore, and the results cannot be representative of the whole of Lahore. A multicentric study, representing people from all the four provinces of Pakistan, will be needed to obtain conclusive evidence regarding the relationship of ABO blood groups with metabolic abnormalities linked to MetS and cardiovascular disease.

CONCLUSION

There is a lack of association of ABO blood groups with MetS in a population in Lahore, however blood group A subjects had higher odds of having hypertriglyceridemia as compared to subjects with blood group AB. Dietary modifications are recommended to reduce hypertriglyceridemia in this population, especially in blood group A subjects, to decrease their risk of developing cardiovascular disease.
