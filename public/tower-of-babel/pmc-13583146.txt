Ecological stability of zooplankton communities in changing arctic lakes over three decades
Authors: Casey A. Pennock, Gary P. Thiede, Phaedra Budy, Anne Giblin, Chris Luecke, George W. Kling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the Arctic, rapid warming is altering disturbance regimes, creating an urgent need to assess the ecological stability of these relatively pristine ecosystems. The Arctic landscape has thousands of lakes that are experiencing both gradual, persistent disturbances (e.g., warming) and increased frequency and intensity of pulse disturbances (e.g., land‐surface failures). To assess the ecological stability of lake communities in a changing environment, we analyzed long‐term data (14–39 years) on zooplankton communities and environmental variables from 25 lakes in arctic Alaska. We characterized long‐term change and evaluated the resistance and recovery of communities to natural or experimental disturbances. We hypothesized that reference lakes would demonstrate resistance to background environmental change, represented by variable but consistent relative abundances over time, while manipulated or disturbed lakes would show recovery after the disturbance ceased, as represented by directional changes in relative abundance with return to pre‐disturbance conditions. Environmental variables including water conductivity, chlorophyll‐a, NO3 −, pH, dissolved oxygen, light extinction, and soluble reactive phosphorus changed directionally through time, suggesting press effects due to climate change. Zooplankton communities were resistant to environmental change in undisturbed lakes, but, despite monitoring lakes for up to 28 years after disturbance, few lakes showed evidence of recovery to pre‐disturbance conditions. This lack of recovery may indicate shifting baselines due to environmental change. The variation we did observe was largely due to shifts in species relative abundance rather than in species turnover, and this variability was related to lake depth. Zooplankton communities and environmental variables displayed more interannual variation in shallow (depth ≤6 m) lakes than in deep lakes. Overall, our results demonstrate that arctic zooplankton communities have high resistance to the effects of climate change but potentially low recovery or long recovery times to pulse disturbances.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding the causes and controls of ecological stability, the ability of a system to maintain function and structure in the face of perturbations, is a major goal of ecology (Connell & Sousa, 1983; Van Meerbeek et al., 2021; Vellend, 2016). Ecological stability encompasses multiple properties including resistance (the ability to withstand change) and recovery (the ability to fully return to pre‐disturbance conditions), which jointly determine the net community response to environmental change (Van Meerbeek et al., 2021). Achieving this goal has become increasingly important in a world experiencing rapid environmental change.

Communities can respond to disturbances in various ways including by resisting disturbance, recovering to pre‐disturbance conditions after some amount of time, or displaying low resistance or recovery and even switching to alternative or novel states (Daufresne et al., 2007; Lewontin, 1969; Rodriguez et al., 2003). A meta‐analysis of 508 pulse‐disturbance experiments in freshwater, marine, and terrestrial ecosystems showed that organism abundance recovered to control levels in 94% of studies; community diversity recovered least, resistance and resilience (recovery) were inversely correlated, and very few systems had trajectories toward novel stable states (Hillebrand & Kunze, 2020). Similarly, in a series of experiments Urrutia‐Cordero et al. (2021) found that top‐down pulse disturbances most strongly affected food webs. Community composition changed in highly variable ways, and recovery to initial composition following disturbance was common (Urrutia‐Cordero et al., 2021). These findings align with Matthews et al. (2013), who described six hypothetical trajectories of community or ecosystem change in multivariate space, based on the magnitude and directionality of change over time (Appendix S1: Figure S1). Changes can be gradual or sudden (saltatorial), and trajectories can be nondirectional or directional. Gradual changes indicate slight variations between samples, while saltatorial changes represent substantial shifts. Directionality describes the overall trajectory of samples over time, with close samples indicating consistent community composition and distant samples indicating greater differences. A gradual, nondirectional trajectory suggests natural variation, whereas saltatorial responses indicate relatively large changes such as those following a pulse disturbance (Lake, 2000; Lamothe et al., 2019). Communities with high resistance show shorter distances shifted in multivariate space with nondirectional trajectories, while systems with low resistance show directional trajectories over time. Communities displaying recovery by returning to their previous composition after a disturbance would show a directional trajectory away from the initial composition with eventual return over time.

Zooplankton are important components of lake ecosystems and are key indicators of food web dynamics and environmental disturbance. Zooplankton regulate phytoplankton dynamics through bottom‐up (nutrient recycling) and top‐down (grazing) processes and serve as important prey sources for fish (Lomartire et al., 2021). Understanding how zooplankton communities respond to changing conditions through time can therefore provide important information on the function and health of aquatic ecosystems. Zooplankton communities can have substantial responses to both press and pulse disturbances (Lamothe et al., 2018; O'Donnell et al., 2023; Urrutia‐Cordero et al., 2021). In naturally fishless alpine lakes, zooplankton communities showed recovery from fish stocking and subsequent removal but required long recovery times (20–30 years; Stuparyk & Vinebrooke, 2024). For many studies, the period of record and especially the monitoring after pulse disturbances is relatively short (months to years), whereas at least in some alpine (Stuparyk & Vinebrooke, 2024) and arctic lakes (Budy et al., 2022), the recovery of communities after disturbance can take decades. Such long recovery times require long‐term data to characterize temporal changes in the communities. Despite their importance to aquatic food webs, relatively few studies have assessed zooplankton community responses to disturbance in natural environments (e.g., Esroy et al., 2019; McGann & Strecker, 2022; Quintana, 2002), and those that have often lack long‐term data (but see Lamothe et al., 2018).

Climate change is altering disturbance regimes, particularly in the Arctic where warming is nearly 4× faster than the global average (i.e., arctic amplification; Rantanen et al., 2022). These climate changes lead to novel press (also known as ramp) disturbances and more frequent and intense pulse disturbances that can alter community dynamics. This rapid warming is leading to more tundra fires (Hu et al., 2010, 2015; Mack et al., 2011) and land‐surface failures (thermokarst events) due to melting ground ice (Bowden et al., 2008; Budy et al., 2025; Schuur et al., 2009), mobilizing soil, vegetation, and nutrients into surface waters and affecting aquatic communities. Arctic lakes are relatively pristine (Hobbie & Kling, 2014) with low productivity (oligotrophic to ultra‐oligotrophic), low species diversity, and a short ice‐free season with continuous daylight (Christoffersen et al., 2008; Kling, 2009; Vincent & Hobbie, 2000). Lakes are sensitive to global climate change (e.g., Adrian et al., 2009), and arctic lake communities may be particularly sensitive to changing disturbance regimes (Gough et al., 2016; Hobbie et al., 1999; Hobbie & Kling, 2014). Arctic lakes are expected to show increases in zooplankton biomass in response to increased temperature and phytoplankton productivity (de Senerpont Domis et al., 2013; Klobucar et al., 2018; O'Brien et al., 2005), although high latitude lakes have shown relative stability in interannual species turnover (Shurin et al., 2007). Despite a rapidly changing environment, it is unclear how arctic lake communities will respond to varying physical and biogeochemical characteristics and to changes in higher trophic level energetic demands. Given low productivity, short growing seasons, cold temperatures, and low diversity, we expect arctic lake communities to have low ecological stability (Downing et al., 2014; Ives & Carpenter, 2007).

To assess ecological stability in a rapidly changing environment, we evaluated long‐term trajectories (14–39 years) of zooplankton communities and environmental variables from 25 lakes in arctic Alaska. These lakes represent both pristine or minimally disturbed reference lakes (“reference” hereafter) and lakes that were experimentally manipulated or affected by natural pulse disturbances, such as thermokarst failures (“disturbed” hereafter). These lakes also represent a range of environmental and biological conditions largely driven by differences in lake depth and surface area, surface‐water connectivity, and glacial history, which also control fish presence/absence and community composition (Hershey et al., 1999). We hypothesized that zooplankton community trajectories over time would differ among reference and disturbed lakes. Because communities often recover from pulse disturbance in terms of both abundance and species composition (Hillebrand & Kunze, 2020; Urrutia‐Cordero et al., 2021), we expected communities in disturbed lakes would display directional changes with recovery after disturbance and communities in reference lakes to display gradual, nondirectional trajectories over time. Because the Arctic is experiencing rapid environmental change, we also characterized temporal trajectories of environmental variables to determine whether lakes are experiencing directional environmental change indicative of climate‐driven press disturbances (e.g., Harris et al., 2018). Such press disturbances could lead to directional change in reference lake communities and limit recovery in disturbed lakes by creating shifting baselines (Urrutia‐Cordero et al., 2021). We also expected communities in shallow lakes to exhibit greater interannual variability than those in deep lakes. Shallow lakes freeze to the bottom annually, eliminating fish predators, and are less buffered from environmental variation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area

We used zooplankton and limnological data collected from 1975 to 2022 in 25 lakes near the Toolik Field Station in the northern foothills of the Brooks Range mountains, Alaska (Table 1). These data were collected as part of efforts associated with the National Science Foundation's Arctic Long‐Term Ecological Research site based out of Toolik Field Station (https://arc-lter.lamont.columbia.edu/). The study lakes are surrounded by continuous permafrost soils with summer active layers (thawed soil) less than 1 m thick. Mean annual air temperature is −7°C and mean July temperature is 12°C (Hobbie & Kling, 2014). Several lakes near Toolik Field Station have been used for manipulative experiments including whole‐lake fertilization, fish removals, fish additions, and intentional warming (Table 1). These experiments (reviewed by Budy et al., 2022; Luecke et al., 2014) provide an opportunity to test the resistance and recovery of zooplankton communities to disturbance. For lakes I8 and Toolik near the Dalton Highway, angling by the public started when the highway was completed in 1974. However, we lack quantitative data on the effects of angling on fish populations for these and all study lakes, so we grouped these with reference lakes.

Zooplankton and limnological sampling

The interannual sampling frequency varied among lakes due to logistical constraints and changes in which lakes were prioritized for sampling and processing over time (Table 1). Lakes were generally sampled multiple times throughout the summer (June–August) each year. However, some lakes were only sampled once a year during the ice‐free season. Because we were interested in long‐term, interannual changes, we averaged zooplankton densities and physical and chemical variables across samples collected during the summer ice‐free season (June–August) each year.

We collected and analyzed seven large‐bodied zooplankton species common in the study lakes: the cladocerans Bosmina longirostris, Daphnia longiremis, Da. middendorffiana, and Holopedium gibberum, and the copepods Cyclops scutifer, Diaptomus pribilofensis, and Heterocope septentrionalis. Zooplankton were collected via vertical tows from ~1 m above the sediment to the lake surface. Prior to 2009, zooplankton were sampled with a 30 cm diameter, 335‐μm mesh net. After and including 2009, zooplankton were sampled with a 30‐cm, 243‐μm mesh net. Both mesh sizes effectively retain adults of all taxa in our analysis. Additionally, we found no evidence of changes in community structure or relative abundance between 2008 and 2009, indicating this change in net mesh did not bias our results. We collected zooplankton via two replicate vertical tows from the bottom to the surface at each lake on each sampling date, preserved them with sugar‐buffered Lugol's solution, and identified zooplankton to species with a dissecting scope in the laboratory. The samples were counted by taking a subsample of 1 or 2 mL and enumerating all zooplankton by species using a stereomicroscope. If less than 10 individuals of any taxon were recorded in the subsample, the entire sample was enumerated for that taxon.

We measured a common suite of physical and chemical variables (environmental variables hereafter) that may influence zooplankton communities and are sensitive to climate forcing (Gray et al., 2021; Saros et al., 2022). We measured environmental variables throughout the water column using standard limnological techniques (e.g., Wetzel & Likens, 2000). For deep lakes (max. depth >6 m), temperature and dissolved oxygen averaged across the entire water column and values averaged across only the top 6 m were highly correlated (Appendix S1: Figure S2). Because zooplankton were collected across the entire water column, we used average environmental variables across the water column for our analysis. Additional sampling and processing details have been described elsewhere (Budy et al., 2025; Kling et al., 1992, 2000; Luecke et al., 2014). Sampling for zooplankton and measurements of environmental variables were performed at the deepest part of each lake. We retained nine environmental variables for further analyses including temperature (in degrees Celsius), conductivity (in microsiemens per centimeter), total chlorophyll‐a (Chl a, in micrograms per liter), ammonium (NH4 +, in micromolar), soluble reactive phosphorus (SRP, in micromolar), nitrate+nitrite (NO3 −, in micromolar), pH, maximum depth, and light extinction coefficients (K z ). Sample sizes for zooplankton and environmental variables differed because not all variables were measured every year in each lake. We removed Polyphemus pedunculus before conducting the zooplankton assemblage analysis because this species was relatively rare (occurred in <3% of samples).

Because some lakes were only sampled once in some years, we analyzed data including all years and excluding years with only a single sample from a lake time series. Nonetheless, our results were generally similar with and without excluding single‐sample years (Appendix S1: Table S1), and we discussed subtle differences in trajectories in the Appendix S1. We report results including all years in the main text (Appendix S1: Table S2). In the special case of a lake divided into control and fertilized sections (lake N2; O'Brien et al., 2005), we treated each section as a separate lake; however, data from the pre‐fertilization years are shared among time series.

Analyses

We conducted all analyses in the R software (R Core Team, 2023). To visualize zooplankton communities, we used principal components analysis (PCA) following the analytical framework described by Lamothe et al. (2017, 2018). We calculated a PCA on the covariance matrix of Hellinger distances of square root‐transformed annual‐average zooplankton densities for all lakes and years. Square root‐transformation prior to calculation of Hellinger distances was necessary to reduce variation in taxa‐specific densities and avoid an arch effect. Hellinger transformation of abundance data is appropriate for use with linear approaches, such as PCA, and does not give undue weight to rare species that might be captured sporadically (Legendre & Gallagher, 2001). To visualize environmental variables among disturbed and reference lakes and assess whether reference lakes experienced directional environmental change over time, we conducted analyses on environmental variables using mixed effects models and PCA. Details of these analyses and results are in (Appendix S1: Tables S3 and S4, Figures S3 and S5). We determined the number of principal components to be retained using a permutation approach with 999 permutations (Peres‐Neto et al., 2003, 2005).

To assess relationships between zooplankton communities and environmental variables, we used a redundancy analysis (RDA) on Hellinger‐transformed data using the vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2022). We again calculated Hellinger distances of square root‐transformed zooplankton densities. We screened predictor variables for strong collinearity and excluded pH and maximum depth as these were strongly correlated (Pearson's r > 0.75) with conductivity and temperature, respectively. We created a global model that included temperature, conductivity, Chl a, NH4 +, NO3 −, SRP, and K z as predictor variables. We further assessed collinearity among predictor variables using variance inflation factors, calculated using the vif.cca function. All variables were retained in the global model as all had a variance inflation factor <2. We used forward selection to identify which environmental variables best explained variation in zooplankton community structure. To test the statistical significance of the overall RDA model, the individual constrained axes, and the predictor variables, we used permutational ANOVA with 999 permutations.

Characterizing multivariate trajectories

We used time lag regression to characterize the rate and nature of community change in individual lakes (Collins et al., 2000) and relate trajectories back to hypothetical scenarios proposed by Matthews et al. (2013); Appendix S1: Figure S1). We calculated Hellinger (zooplankton) and Euclidean (environmental variables) distance between each pair of observations for an individual lake. We then regressed distances against the number of years separating each pair of observations for each lake (time lags; Collins et al., 2000). A statistically significant linear relationship between distances and time steps indicates a directional change in community structure or environmental conditions over time. A relationship where the slope is not statistically different from zero indicates no directional change through time, consistent with stable or randomly variable community structure or environmental conditions. A negative slope indicates a community returning towards some historical condition. For each lake, we fit three models: an intercept‐only model (high resistance), a linear relationship between distance and time lags (low resistance), and a second‐order polynomial (high recovery) of distance versus time lags (Lamothe et al., 2018). We accounted for nonindependence among pairwise distances by modeling them as a function of the time lag between observations (e.g., Hoff, 2005), including random effects for the sample IDs involved in the distance comparison since each sample i gets compared to each other sample j ≠ i, creating a possible lack of independence among distances (d(i, j)) that involve the same sample i (e.g., d(i, j) and d(i, j′). We compared models using Akaike information criterion corrected (AICc) for small sample size calculated using the MuMIn package (Barton, 2022). We chose the best model based on the lowest AICc value or the simplest model when multiple models were equivalent (i.e., ΔAICc < 2).

To determine if trajectories changed gradually or abruptly (saltatory) from year‐to‐year, we used Tukey's Fences in the lares package (Lares, 2024). We characterized lakes with at least one outlier as having had a saltatory change; lakes with no outliers were considered as undergoing gradual changes over time (Lamothe et al., 2018). We assessed whether distances among sequential samples could be considered “far” outliers by comparing values to 3× the interquartile range (75th–25th quartiles; IQR). An extreme value of distance among sequential annual samples would indicate a year where the change in the community or environment was substantially larger than changes among other consecutive years. We also calculated Tukey's Fences using 1.5× the IQR (“regular” outliers) and used generalized extreme Studentized deviate tests in the EnvStats package (Rosner, 1983). The “regular” outlier approach detected the most outliers and the ESD test the least, so we report “far” outliers as a conservative metric.

Interannual variability of zooplankton communities

To compare interannual variability in zooplankton communities and environmental variables among disturbed and reference lakes, we used an analysis of multivariate homogeneity of group dispersions (Anderson, 2006). This procedure is the multivariate equivalent of Levene's test for homogeneity of variances and compares the average distance of samples to the group centroid in multivariate space. Because this test only allows inclusion of one factor at a time, we created a new factor representing the interaction between disturbance history (disturbed or reference) and lake depth (shallow [<6 m] or deep). We tested whether interannual variation differed across disturbance history and lake depth using a permutation test with 999 permutations. We also calculated temporal stability for all zooplankton communities using the codyn package (Hallett et al., 2020). Here, temporal stability is defined as the ratio of mean species abundance to the SD of species abundance for each lake where larger values indicate greater temporal stability (Tilman, 1999). We used generalized least squares to test the effect of disturbance history (disturbed or reference), maximum lake depth, and their interactive effect on community temporal stability. Because the interaction term was nonsignificant (likelihood ratio χ2 1 = 0.32, p = 0.574), we refit the model with only main effects. We used residual plots to confirm that assumptions of normality and heteroscedasticity were reasonably met. We modeled variance separately for reference and manipulated lakes because there was evidence of heteroscedasticity in residual plots.

Zooplankton communities and environmental variables displayed differences in interannual variability across disturbance history and depth. DISCUSSION

We characterized changes in zooplankton communities over time in arctic lakes in the face of rapidly changing environmental conditions. Evidence for resistance of zooplankton communities to changing environmental conditions was generally high, but mixed, even in relatively pristine habitats. Fewer communities in reference lakes were changing directionally despite widespread changing physicochemical conditions, suggesting minimally disturbed arctic lake communities are somewhat resistant to changing conditions resulting from climate change observed thus far. While the slope for water temperature over time was not statistically significant, the slope for conductivity was statistically significant and positive. A similar pattern was previously reported from the Toolik region whereby the trend in air temperature over time was not statistically significant, but deep permafrost temperatures were significantly increasing over 25 years (Hobbie et al., 2017). We observed changes over time in several other environmental variables, such as increased conductivity (1.5%/year), Chl a (1.1%/year), and NO3 − (2.3%/year) and declining SRP (3.7%/year). These patterns suggest that lake hydrology and chemistry are changing, likely because of increased groundwater intrusion or deeper flow paths into lakes from thawing permafrost due to climate change (Kling et al., 2014; Luecke et al., 2014). Taken together, the simultaneous changes in conductivity, nutrients, and other physicochemical conditions suggest that while many lakes were resistant to change, ongoing and cumulative environmental shifts are likely to favor more tolerant zooplankton taxa. Such shifts are expected to gradually alter community structure and potentially reduce more sensitive species (e.g., Ho. gibberum; O'Brien et al., 2004).

Our hypothesis that disturbed lakes would display directional changes with recovery after disturbances ceased was not supported. Changing environmental conditions might cause a shifting baseline, limiting zooplankton community recovery from pulse disturbances (e.g., Ingeman et al., 2019; Pauly, 1995; Soga & Gaston, 2018). Alternatively (but not exclusively), zooplankton in arctic lakes might have low or weak recovery to disturbance, characterized by no recovery or long recovery times (e.g., >10 years; e.g., Budy et al., 2022). For example, experimental fertilization of lakes E5 (deep) and E6 (shallow) started in 2001, and a 12‐year, low‐level nutrient addition was used to mimic deeper thaw caused by climate warming and the associated increased nutrient inputs (see Budy et al., 2022; Hobbie et al., 1999). By 2017, the zooplankton biomass from July samples still had not recovered to pre‐disturbance conditions in either lake (Budy et al., 2022). Long recovery times (7–30 years) have been documented in zooplankton communities elsewhere following cessation of both top‐down and bottom‐up disturbances (e.g., McCrackin et al., 2017; Stuparyk & Vinebrooke, 2024). Potential mechanisms for long recovery times of zooplankton communities include dispersal limitation, especially for sexually reproducing species, and biotic interference from persistent tolerant species (Stuparyk & Vinebrooke, 2024). Zooplankton communities are also influenced by hydrological connectivity, which can aid recolonization following disturbance (Blackburn‐Desbiens et al., 2023). Most of the disturbed lakes in our study are isolated, headwater lakes or otherwise have low connectivity to other lakes, which may have contributed to the observed low recovery or potentially long recovery times.

Theory suggests that food webs with many weak interactions should have higher community stability (Hughes & Roughgarden, 2000; Lehman & Tilman, 2000; MacArthur, 1955). Additionally, community stability is affected by predators, habitat complexity, habitat size, and species diversity. Both predators and species diversity can impart stabilizing effects on communities in complex ways (e.g., Jiang et al., 2009), and these effects can be mediated by habitat complexity (e.g., Mykrä et al., 2011). Communities also tend to be more stable in larger habitats (Greig et al., 2022). We observed a significant, positive relationship between community temporal stability and lake depth that was similar for disturbed and reference lakes. This pattern could be driven by the effects of fish predation, small increases in species diversity, or some combination including interactions with environmental conditions or habitat complexity. In the Toolik region, fish only occur year‐round in deeper lakes (Hershey et al., 1999), and zooplankton species richness is higher in deeper, higher surface area lakes with more habitat complexity (O'Brien et al., 2004). Predation by planktivorous fishes can alter community composition and size structure of zooplankton communities (Brooks & Dodson, 1965; O'Brien, 1975; Urrutia‐Cordero et al., 2021). However, zooplankton communities in lakes of the Toolik region might not fit typical patterns described in temperate North American lakes where fish consistently exclude large zooplankton by selective predation (O'Brien et al., 1979). This suggests potential weak interactions between fish and zooplankton in these lakes (Klobucar & Budy, 2020; O'Brien et al., 2005), which could ultimately contribute to increased zooplankton community stability over time. Additionally, predation by predatory zooplankton, such as He. septentrionalis, in shallow lakes can have strong effects on zooplankton communities (O'Brien, 2001).

Understanding how communities change over time and whether community dynamics are governed by equilibrium, non‐equilibrium, or other processes has not only theoretical implications, but also practical implications by understanding natural temporal variability and informing recovery times following disturbance (Hopper et al., 2024; Lamothe et al., 2018; Stuparyk & Vinebrooke, 2024). Community dynamics can be complex and include equilibrium, non‐equilibrium, cyclical, and chaotic patterns (DeAngelis & Waterhouse, 1987; Mallmin et al., 2024; Vellend, 2016). Historically, communities were assumed to exist in a stable equilibrium or in a transient, non‐equilibrium state (Kéfi et al., 2019). An intermediate viewpoint is that communities exhibit stochastic change around a “loose” or “dynamic” equilibrium whereby communities vary temporally within a bounded space as a function of environmental variability but have no explicit state to which they return after displacement (Collins, 2000; DeAngelis et al., 1985; May, 1973). Species abundance reordering rather than species replacements over time and in response to disturbance has been demonstrated in a wide variety of communities including terrestrial plants (Collins et al., 2020; Jones et al., 2017), insects and small mammals (Jones et al., 2017), birds (Jones et al., 2017; Tinoco et al., 2021), marine ecosystems (Nagelkerken & Connell, 2022), freshwater fish (Pennock & Gido, 2021; Říha et al., 2009), and zooplankton (O'Donnell et al., 2023; this study). The importance of species reordering versus turnover in temporal community change has gained increasing research attention (e.g., Collins et al., 2020; Jones et al., 2017; Tinoco et al., 2021). The reordering of species abundance over time was the driving process of change in the arctic zooplankton communities we studied. Zooplankton communities varied over time, but this variation was not driven by changes in species richness or replacement because all species present at the beginning of the time series were generally present throughout the time series. While we did not observe evidence of species gains or losses as a driver of change in arctic zooplankton communities, it is possible that such changes could eventually occur if lakes become colonized by species (gains) from lower latitudes.

The relatively small amount of variation in zooplankton communities explained by environmental variables is consistent with previous research in both arctic and temperate lakes (Kling et al., 1992; Lamothe et al., 2018; O'Brien et al., 2004). The relatively weak concordance between zooplankton and environmental variables could suggest zooplankton dynamics are influenced more by biotic conditions, such as food availability or predation, than by environmental conditions directly, at least seasonally. For instance, we summarized zooplankton communities across the summer growing season when biotic interactions might be more important than abiotic conditions in shaping zooplankton dynamics relative to other times of the year (Sommer et al., 1986). The low correlation between zooplankton communities and environmental variables could also be due to how we summarized the data. We averaged zooplankton densities and environmental variables across the summer growing season to focus on broader interannual patterns. By doing so, we might have obscured finer scale temporal relationships by masking short‐term dynamics or otherwise reducing the variability in the data. Additionally, our sampling only occurred in one place in each lake, and this likely underrepresents the amount of spatial variation within lakes, both in terms of zooplankton communities and environmental conditions.

Zooplankton play a central role in lake food webs by serving as prey for fish and controlling phytoplankton via both bottom‐up (i.e., nutrient recycling; Johnson et al., 2010) and top‐down processes (i.e., grazing; Ger et al., 2016). Thus, understanding zooplankton community dynamics in response to changing environmental conditions is critical for determining how lake ecosystems respond to global climate change and other stressors (Baily & Hood, 2024; Carter & Schindler, 2012). Despite ongoing environmental change, most communities showed relatively stable composition over decades, reflecting high resistance to perturbations. However, recovery from disturbances was limited, suggesting that recovery in these systems may be constrained by intrinsic factors such as low productivity, dispersal limitation, or long recovery times. Temporal stability was generally higher in deeper lakes, indicating that habitat structure associated with lake depth can buffer communities against environmental variability. Changes over time were driven primarily by shifts in species relative abundances rather than species turnover, highlighting the importance of reordering in community dynamics under changing conditions. Overall, arctic zooplankton communities appear to be relatively resistant to changing environmental conditions, but when disturbed they demonstrate long recovery times.
