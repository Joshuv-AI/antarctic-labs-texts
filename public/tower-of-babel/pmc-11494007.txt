The effects of Carpinus betulus expansion on soil properties under changing climatic conditions
Authors: Karol Sokołowski, Janusz Czerepko, Radosław Gawryś
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarpinus betulus (CB) is becoming increasingly important in the forests of Central Europe and is significantly increasing its proportion in various habitat types. We have analysed how the increase in CB affects soil properties. The study was conducted in the Białowieża Forest (BF). We monitored the changes in CB cover between 1962 and 2013 on 56 permanent plots and collected samples of the litter sub-horizons and the content of the underlying A horizons for laboratory analyses in 2014. During the study period, there was a significant increase in CB cover in the second tree layer by an average of 17.4 % (±1.8) and in the shrub layer by 3.8 % (±1.0), as well as an increase in species diversity from 41 % in the past to 59 % currently. The C/N ratio of the Oi sub-horizon was 32.0 (±0.9), and NH4+ compounds predominated over NO3-, with all nitrogen forms most closely associated with the C/N ratio. In the Oea sub-horizon, the C/N ratio was 23.4 (±0.4), and the sum of mineralised nitrogen reached 8%, with a clear predominance of NO3-. The characteristic most strongly associated with the increase in CB coverage was the NO3- content in the Oea sub-horizon. The MID analysis confirmed that the change in CB cover was not only significant, but also ecologically important. We suggest that there is a feedback loop in which the generally observed climate changes lead to trees occupying new habitats that supply the forest floor with high quality litter, which in turn affects the soil and promotes the persistence of changes in the species composition of the forest.Keywords: Soil organic carbon, Nitrification, Exchangeable cations, Litter, Acidity, Białowieża forestSubject terms: Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The distribution of tree species in temperate regions is largely determined by temperature, nitrogen availability, soil acidity and the air–water ratio1. The most important species of European temperate deciduous forests are European beech (Fagus sylvatica L.), European hornbeam (Carpinus betulus L.) and pedunculate oak (Quercus robur L.), although under current environmental conditions only European hornbeam and European beech have a continuous regeneration mode that gives them a major competitive advantage both under the canopy and in gaps2–6. Tree population studies in Central and Eastern Europe, in the Białowieża Forest (BF) in the years 1936–1992 confirm a significant change in the species composition of forests and a quantitative increase in species capable of late succession: Tilia cordata (Mill.) and Carpinus betulus, and to a lesser extent Fraxinus excelsior (L.)7. At the same time, these studies indicated a decline in the proportion of Picea abies (L.) and Pinus sylvestris (L.). Similar conclusions were reached by Le Due et al.8, who analysed the competition between Quercus petraea (Matt. Liebl.) and Carpinus betulus in an old forest in England. They pointed out that the invasion of Carpinus betulus in the canopy layer leads to too little light reaching the forest floor, so that the seedlings of Quercus petraea lose the battle for growth. Other studies confirm that shade-tolerant species (hornbeam, beech, ash) survive better in the stand regeneration phase than others (e.g. oaks) and their height growth is greater9. Research also suggests that hornbeam contributes to a greater extent than beech to improving forest soil quality, increasing soil fertility and supporting the development of more active microbial populations10.

On another page, Sobrino et al.11 pointed out that Holocene tree succession associated with climate warming led to a significant decline in the proportion of hornbeam in the period from ca. 60,000 and ca. 9,000 cal. yr BP in southwestern Europe. Other climate change modelling studies suggest that Carpinus betulus will not be able to migrate fast enough into newly emerging suitable areas in Europe, and at altitudes below 1,600 m above sea level the decline in populations could be more than 25% by 210012. From the above studies, it can be concluded that the northward expansion of Carpinus betulus and Fagus sylvatica is progressing, with range expansion is observed at the expense of habitat loss at the southern edge of the range in the future climate13. In the context of changes in the species composition of temperate forests, important observations were made by Brzeziecki et al.14. Their study in BF showed that there has been a strong downward trend in the population density of several tree species with different successional status and light requirements over the last 8 decades. Hornbeam showed the opposite trend, namely increasing recruitment and growth rates and decreasing mortality. Such changes, especially in quasi-equilibrium or quasi-steady-state forests, have an impact on the soil. CB increases the N content in the litter and has the greatest effect on the mineral soil, where it increases pH, N content and N sequestration compared to other tree stands. This indicates that hornbeam tree stands produce the best soil fertility15. Harijani et al.16, who investigated the effects of hornbeam on the soil in lowland deciduous forests, were also able to show that nitrogen accumulates in the litter layer in pure hornbeam forests, while the pH value in the mineral soil is almost neutral. This leads to high microbiological activity, which is directly reflected in improved soil fertility. Earlier studies have shown that hornbeam litter also leads to an increase in nitrogen and phosphorus and a significant decrease in C/N in mixed forests17.

Climate change can have indirect effects on the soil—via vegetation—but also direct effects. For example, Kupka et al.18 have experimentally shown that the pH value in the litter increases as a result of rising temperatures, which can be attributed to disturbances in the cycle of hydrogen (H+) and aluminium ions (Al3+) in the forest ecosystem. In previous studies, Kupka et al.19 observed increased pH values in litter and in the topsoil layer and found that soil warming causes an increase in dissolved forms of carbon and nitrogen as well as ammonium compounds (NH4–N). A moderate increase in soil temperature can significantly increase the decomposition rate of litter20. This is confirmed by the research results of Fu et al.21, who experimentally showed that the warming of the 0–10 cm soil layer had a significant effect on nitrate nitrogen (NO3−N), and the warming of the 10–20 cm layer significantly increased the content of organic carbon (SOC) and total nitrogen (NT). Global warming accelerates the decomposition of organic matter by soil microorganisms, which increases the release of carbon into the atmosphere22. Soil organisms incorporate less C and N into their biomass (and necromass), resulting in less C and N being stored in the soil and more inorganic C and N being released into the environment23. In addition, the results of the study by Elrys et al.24 indicate that precipitation and temperature influence nitrification by changing the C/N ratio and/or total nitrogen in the soil, and that net N mineralisation is significantly correlated with litter quality25.

Decomposition of litter is the main route by which nutrients return to the soil26,27. Mineralised nitrogen (NO3-, NH4+) accounts for less than 2% of total nitrogen in soil, and its cyclic transformations and the soil-plant cycle are also well described28,29. Significant correlations have been found between nitrogen content in litter and temperature and precipitation. On the Eurasian continent, nitrogen concentrations in litter increase with increasing temperature and precipitation in deciduous and coniferous forests30. Kooch et al.31 pointed out that deciduous forests improve litter quality (higher nitrogen concentration with lower C content and C/N ratio) and soil fertility (greater amounts of total nitrogen and available nutrients, including phosphorus, potassium, calcium and magnesium) and accelerate biological activity. From this, it can be concluded that various soil properties will change—directly or indirectly—to varying degrees as a result of climate change32–34.Despite numerous studies on the effects of climate change on plants and soils, little attention has been paid to the long-term effects of changes in the species composition of forest and the response of soil to these changes, especially in areas with little human influence. It appears that herbaceous plants are the first to respond to climate change, followed by trees and shrubs, and finally the soil. However, there is a lack of comprehensive studies investigating the relationships between plants and soils in the context of climate change and succession over time. We hypothesise that natural temperate forests have experienced an expansion of Carpinus betulus in the last half century and that these changes affect the soil through high quality litterfall. To this end, we analysed the changes in forest cover and the frequency of Carpinus betulus considering the following layers: the upper tree layer [A1], the lower tree layer [A2] and the understorey layer [B] observed during 1962–2013. Samples of litter [Oi, Oea sub-horizons] and underlying soil [A horizon] taken in 2014 were also analysed and their physicochemical properties were determined.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The study was carried out in BF on permanent plots established mainly in the 1960s and 1970s by Sokołowski A. W.35,36 (Table 1). The 28 plots were represented by fertile deciduous forest sites where occured follow plant associations from class Querco-Fagetea (Br.-Bl. et Vlieg. 1937): oak-lime-hornbeam forests Tilio cordatae–Carpinetum betuli (Tracz. 1962) and thermophilous oak forest Potentillo albae–Quercetum (Libbert 1933). A further 28 plots were located on infertile and moderately fertile coniferous and mixed forest sites represented by plant associations from the class Vaccinio–Piceetea (Br.–Bl. 1939): fresh pine–red bilberry forest Vaccinio vitis–idaea–Pinetum (Sokoł. 1981), wet pine–bilberry forest Vaccinio myrtylli–Pinetum (Kobendza 1930 em. Sokoł. 1977), bilberry–spruce forest Vaccinio myrtylli–Piceetum (Sokoł. 1981), fresh reed grass–spruce forest Calamagrostio arundinaceae–Piceetum (Sokoł. 1968) and oak–spruce forest Querco roboris–Pinetum (W.Mat. 1981; J.Mat. 1988). The BF is one of the largest primaeval forests in Europe37 with an area of 1500 km2 , 42% of which is in Poland and the rest in Belarus. The forest community is located on 95% of the BF, with the most common habitat in the BF being oak–lime–hornbeam forests (Tilio–Carpinetum) on Luvisols and Cambisols36,38. The history of the BF and the ecological processes taking place there are also well documented, taking into account the expected changes in the biodiversity, structure and dynamics of the forest39. As in Central Europe, the climatic conditions in BF also show a warming trend according to the measurements of recent decades. The average air temperature measured at the Białowieża meteorological station rose by 2.3 °C between 1951 and 2019. The growing season has been extended by 16 days. Snow cover has been occurred less frequently in the last 68 years (decrease of 34 days). Annual precipitation was high in the period 1967–1981, when the first vegetation survey was conducted, with an average of 733.6 mm, while in the most recent period (2009–2019), when the vegetation survey was repeated, it was relatively dry with 678.7 mm of precipitation40.

Reference areas and nature reserves account for about one third of the BF area, and if set–aside forests and the Białowieża National Park (BNP) established in 1921 are added, they account for almost half of the BF area. In the last three decades, the pressure on forestry in BF has decreased due to the expansion of protected areas41. The European Union regulations on the designation of sites of European Union importance42 and the launch of the Natura 2000 network—PLC200004, under which areas for the special protection of birds 43 and special areas for the conservation of habitats44 were designated, have resulted in BF being characterised by a large mosaic of forms of nature conservation and commercial forests with an emphasis on passive protection.

Soil physiochemical properties analysis

The soil samples were taken from two organic sub–horizons (Oi – leaf litter, Oea – partially and highly decomposed organic matter) and from the underlying A horizon. A total of 168 samples were taken. For the A horizon, the granulometric fractions of sand (grain diameter 2.0–0.063 mm), silt (grain diameter 0.063 – 0.002 mm) and clay (grain diameter <0.002 mm) were determined in accordance with PN-ISO 11277:2005. The thickness [TH] was measured for all horizons. In all samples were determined: [pHKCl] by the potentiometric method according to PN-ISO 10390:1997, soil organic carbon [SOC] by the dry combustion method (elemental analysis) according to PN-ISO 10694:2002, total nitrogen [N] by the dry combustion method (elemental analysis) according to PN-ISO 13878:2002, nitrate [NO3-] and ammonium [NH4+] ions by the automatic segmented flow analysis (CFA) in soil extracts in 0.03M acetic acid according to PB-10 ed. 2 of 1 July 2014 and PB–14 ed. 4. Marks the content of exchangeable acid [Hex], hydrolytic acid [Hh], exchangeable aluminium [Al3+] (PB-12ed. 3 of 01/07/2014), the content of exchangeable cations (magnesium [Mg2+], potassium [K+], sodium [Na+] and calcium [Ca2+]) (PB-05ed. 3 of 01/07/2014). The content of phosphorus [P], calcium [Ca], potassium [K], sulphur [S], magnesium [Mg], manganese [Mn], sodium [Na], iron [Fe], aluminium [Al], copper [Cu], lead [Pb], zinc [Zn] and nickel [Ni] was determined using the extraction method according to the PN–ISO 11466:2002 standard and the inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP–OES) method. The sum of exchangeable cations [EC], the cation exchange capacity [CEC], the cation saturation [CS]45 and the C/N ratio were calculated on the basis of the results obtained. The competence to perform the above tests is confirmed by the functioning of the Environmental Chemistry Laboratory (in Forest Research Institute) within the framework of a management system that meets the requirements of the current PN–EN ISO–IEC 17025 standard "General requirements for the competence of research and calibration laboratories".

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed in R46. The conformity of the distribution of the analysed variables with the normal distribution was checked using the q–q plot. The significance of the differences between the mean values of the cover of Carpinus betulus in the compared periods (no normal distribution of variables) was determined using the Wilcoxon signed rank test (package “stats”). The mean ecological indicator values (EIV), which determine the light (L), moisture (W), trophic (Tr) and soil acidification (R) conditions, were calculated based on the ground vegetation. The EIVs were determined based on the study by Zarzycki et al.47. The mean values of the EIV for the plots were calculated as average values weighted according to the percentage cover of the plant species in 201348. The significance of the differences between the average EIVs in the periods compared (no normal distribution of the variables) was determined using the using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. The significance of the differences in the analysed variables between the horizons (Oi, Oea, A) was determined using the Friedman ANOVA (no normal distribution of variables) and the Nemenyi post–hoc test from the “PMCMRplus” package49. The correlation between the analysed variables was determined using Spearman’s rank correlation and the visualisation of the results in the “corrplot” package50. The diversity of soil horizons against the background of selected soil and environmental variables was visualised using PCA analysis in the “vegan” package51.

To determine the practical significance of the observed changes in the EIV and changes in Carpinus betulus cover, the Minimum Important Differences (MID) method was applied. Specifically, we used the SD criterion52–54. In this study, the MID is a measure of the smallest difference between the analysed characteristics that is not only statistically significant but also ecologically important. In this way, we identified the results that are most important for nature55. To calculate the pooled SD, we used Cohen’s56 formula, i.e. SDpooled = √([(SDpast2) + (SDpresent2]/2, where the two symbols represent the SDs of two independent ones.

As part of the sensitivity analysis, the partial (Cohen’s) f2 was calculated (package “sensemakr”57). According to Cohen56, there are small (f2 ≥ 0.02), medium (f2 ≥ 0.15) and large (f2 ≥ 0.35) effect sizes.

Results

In the period 1962–2013, there was a significant increase in the CB cover in the second layer of tree (A2) by an average of 17.4% (±1.8) and in the understorey layer (B) by 3.8% (±1.0). Changes in the frequency of this species were observed: A1 showed a decrease from 25% to 16%, while A2 and B layers showed an increase from 41% to 59% and from 63% to 77%, respectively. The analysis of changes in ground vegetation showed a significant increase in the values of moisture (W) and trophic (Tr) indicators in the studied habitats. The MID analysis showed that the coverage changes in layer A2 were both statistically and practically significant. In addition, the MID analysis confirmed the usefulness of the W and Tr indices to describe the condition of the habitats studied (Table 2).

The correlation analyses showed a relationship between the changes in the CB stand, some properties of the soils studied and ecological indicators (Fig. 1). A directly proportional correlation was found between the changes in the CB stand and the pH, P and EC content in the organic horizons. The inversely proportional correlation between CB and C/N increased with depth. In the Oi horizon, the C/N ratio showed a inversely proportional correlation with soil acidity (Alex, Hex), but in the Oea and A horizons the correlation was directly proportional, also between C/N and Hh. NO3- was directly proportional correlated with P and EC at all horizons, but showed a strong inversely proportional correlation with soil acidity in the Oea horizons. The trophic of EIV was related to soil properties in the Oi and A horizons, while the humidity of EIV described the relationships in the Oea horizon well.

The PCA analysis showed a significant influence of the change in CB cover on the nutrient content of the topsoil layers. The feature most strongly associated with changes in CB cover was the NO3- content of the Oea sub–horizon. (Fig. 2). The analysis confirmed that the trophic and humidity of EIV describe the state of the habitat in such a way that an increase in CB cover leads to an increase in indicator values towards more fertile and moist habitats.

The LM analysis showed that in the Oi horizon all nitrogen forms are most dependent on C/N, and in the Oea sub–horizon pH is the main factor influencing the total nitrogen content. The analysis also showed that the NO3- content in the Oea sub–horizon is similarly controlled by changes in CB cover, sum of exchangeable cations and the C/N ratio. In the mineral soil horizon A, the NO3- content is also influenced by Al3+ ions in addition to the pH value and the C/N ratio. Discussion

Our study confirmed the hypothesis that between 1962 and 2013 the expansion of Carpinus betulus increased in BF forests and showed that the increase in cover by this species affects soil properties, especially pH, nitrogen forms, C/N ratio and sum of exchangeable cations (EC). The study also showed that the hornbeam succession period mentioned above is more important for the organic horizon than for the mineral soil.

The decomposition of litter is the most important process that exchanges minerals, especially carbon and nitrogen, between the soil and the plant58. The decomposition processes occurring in the individual soil horizons are independent of each other and have different effects on the dynamics of litter and soil mineral nutrients59. Obtained results indicate that a significant increase in Carpinus betulus cover provides the forest soil with high–quality leaf litter, which is poorly decomposed in the top organic horizon (Oi). The high C/N ratio = 32.0 (±0.9) indicates low microbiological activity in this horizon, which is also indicated by the relatively low amount of mineralised nitrogen, where NH4+ compounds predominate over NO3-. In our studies, litter had no direct influence on the total N pool, but it proved to be a reservoir of total phosphorus (P) and nutrients (mainly Ca2+ and Mg2+) for the Oi sub–horizon. Soil nutrient content is related to litter quality, so higher soil nutrient content is generally associated with good litter quality60, but the mineralisation and nitrification rate of organic matter varies depending on the forest type and tree species61. For example, Bai et al.62 experimentally demonstrated that high–quality deciduous tree litter alters the composition and function of microbial communities associated with litter decomposition, which promotes the acceleration of litter decomposition. However, the duration of conifer litter decomposition is quite long, and its slow decomposition depends on various climatic factors and site–specific conditions63. Our analyses showed that with increasing depth, the negative correlation between the changes in Carpinus betulus stands and the C/N ratio became stronger. At the Oea sub–horizon, litter decomposition processes intensified, the C/N ratio = 23.4 (±0.4) decreased significantly and NO3- compounds became the dominant form of mineral nitrogen. In this respect, our study supports the view that ammonium and nitrate compounds have different effects on organic matter dynamics and should be distinguished to accurately predict SOC sequestration responses to N deposition64. The LM analysis showed that the NO3- content in the Oi and Oea sub–horizons is mainly dependent on the change in Carpinus betulus cover in addition to the C/N and EC ratios. The f2 coefficient for NO3- in the Oea sub–horizon was similar for these three properties CB, EC and C/N (0.08; 0.10; 0.11), indicating that nitrogen transformation in the soil is advanced and stable. This result is important because the change in CB cover is not a soil property, but has an equally strong influence on the processes taking place there. A significant increase in inorganic nitrogen (NH4+, NO3-) to almost 8 % with a simultaneous decrease in the SOC content in the Oea sub–horizon indicates a considerable acceleration of nitrification processes due to the supply of high-quality litter to the forest floor. This is most likely due to the fact that forest litter, especially in deciduous forests, serves as a nutrient sink for NO3- during the non-growth period and as a source of N, mainly NH4+, during the growth period65. The studies we conducted only on acidic forest soils (median pHKCl of Oi and Oea is 4.5 and 4.1, respectively) suggest that the observed changes in the cover of Carpinus betulus significantly affect nitrogen transformation through the continuous supply of high-quality litter. Previous research has suggested that the low nitrification rate in acidic forest soils is due to factors related to pH66—the lower the pH, the slower the nitrification, but evidence for high nitrification rates in acidic soils is now well documented67. Our analyses have shown that at the Oi sub-horizon, all forms of nitrogen are most strongly controlled by the C/N ratio. This is the result of the influx of litter to the forest floor, which has tremendous potential to alter soil C and N content in forest ecosystems, and this influx is a direct consequence of environmental changes68. However, these changes have certain spatial limits in the soil profile. The results of the study by Vesterdal et al.69 indicate that tree species affect mineral soil only in some soil layers down to -30 cm, but there is no significant difference between tree species in C and N content in the whole soil profile. Therefore, the expansion of CB we observed is in the initial phase of influencing the soil and now plays an important role in the biochemical processes in the litter, especially in the Oea.

At the local scale, nitrogen decomposition rates and dynamics are more strongly influenced by litter quality than by habitat or light conditions70, and the chemical composition of plant litter influences the nitrogen cycle71. Increased litter deposition increases soil nitrogen resources, including total nitrogen and dissolved nitrogen, NH4+ and NO3-, and significantly increases net nitrogen mineralisation, with mineralisation being stronger in deciduous forests than in coniferous forests72. In our studies, we found an increase in the cover of Carpinus betulus in the second layer of the A2 stand by an average of 17.36% (±1.8) with varying intensity. With the MID analysis, we have shown that not all changes in environmental parameters are useful for describing the state of the habitat and for further analyses. By using four thresholds (from the liberal 0.2 to the restrictive 0.5) for statistically significant differences, we were able to additionally indicate whether the difference is only marginally significant from an ecological perspective or has a greater significance. By using multiple MID thresholds, we were also able to test how stable the results are. The above difference in CB coverage is significantly larger than all MID values, including the highest threshold of 0.5 SD. This means that this difference is both statistically and practically highly significant. Therefore, we included CB in the further analyses, especially in the LM model used.It is well known that tree species can strongly influence the nitrogen cycle in forest ecosystems, which appears to be mediated by soil organic matter quality73. However, analysing the condition of all studied habitats described by EIV showed that it is not the type of habitat resulting from soil properties but the change in Carpinus betulus cover that determines some soil properties. The MID analysis also showed that among the statistically significant EIVs, only the difference of 0.25 in moisture (W) exceeded all MID thresholds, including the highest threshold of 0.5 SD (0.16). This means that beyond statistical significance, this change also has practical significance for the environment.

The estimated annual litter production (November) in fertile deciduous forests (Tilio-Carpinetum and Fagetum Carpaticum) is 3.8 and 3.4 tonnes/ha, respectively. The leaves make up 91% and 80% of the litter, respectively, and the decomposition of the litter takes between 18 months and 3 years74. Recent studies show that in hornbeam-oak-pine forests, the annual mass of fallen litter is 5.8–6.6 tonnes/ha, and is greater in middle-aged stands than in mature stands75. The observed increase in CB only in the A2 layer indicates that the CB are currently in an expansion phase and the production of high-quality litter is approaching or has already reached its maximum. It should be noted that the C/N ratio becomes narrower for all types of litter as decomposition progresses, and this change evens out earlier and at a lower horizon the higher the decomposition rate76. Earlier long-term observations (1935–1983) of a deciduous forest indicate that over time the general tendency to lower the pH in the top soil horizon slows down as the quality of the leaf litter improves77. Our analyses show that the pH value of the litter between the Oi and Oea sub-horizons has decreased by 0.4 units and in the A horizon by a further 0.8 units to 3.3. However, the soil reaction was most strongly associated with the Oea sub-horizon and affected the content of total N, but not the content of mineral N (NO3-, NH4+). Tao et al.78 pointed out that lower soil pH does not necessarily lead to lower litter decomposition rate in general and that a direct effect of soil pH on litter decomposition rate in forest ecosystems with a wide range of species in terms of functional traits seems unlikely. Our research therefore suggests that such a scenario could also apply to native broadleaf species that change their range and invade other habitats, thereby altering existing soil properties. Despite the very acidic response of the soils we studied, we confirmed that nitrogen mineralisation processes occur in organic horizon, which is consistent with the studies of Tietema et al.79, who showed that nitrates are only no longer released at pH values below 3.45. This would explain the small amounts of mineralised nitrogen in mineral soil horizon A, in which Al3+ ions play an important role in addition to the pH value and the C/N ratio. This view was also confirmed in the studies of Kunito et al.80, which showed that Al and soil acidity inhibit the activity of soil enzymes, leading to inhibition of microbial nutrient cycling, and that Al toxicity and reduced organic matter availability due to Al and Fe binding can protect a significant pool of SOC from microbial degradation in acidic forest soils.

It is in the nature of things that higher quality litter occurs in fertile deciduous forests on soils with a higher proportion of exchangeable cations and total phosphorus and a lower C/N ratio81. Kang et al.82 pointed out that the total nitrogen content in the soils of deciduous forests is significantly higher than in the soils of coniferous forests. As already mentioned, in our research we have ruled out the possibility that by studying only fertile sites where the presence of Carpinus betulus can be attributed to soil properties, we merely obtain confirmation of a simple relationship between plant, litter and soil. Therefore, considering the habitats where Carpinus betulus occurred as a previously unrecorded species or was previously removed by extensive forestry and agricultural management, we have shown that the contribution of a particular species to the species composition of the forest seems to be of crucial importance for the biochemical processes occurring in the soil, perhaps even more important than tree species diversity itself83. Recent research by Fernández-Alonso et al.84 on changes in the chemical composition of litter related to global forest succession under the influence of climate change indicates that the evolution of forest soils is moving towards higher nutrient availability, which in turn may have a positive effect on plant growth. In general, our research shows that a certain feedback loop is taking place in temperate forests. The generally observed global climate changes (precipitation and temperature) create space for vegetation to appear in new areas, and this vegetation in turn influences the physicochemical properties of the soil. Each year, as the growing season progresses in temperate latitudes, organic matter is added to the forest floor and most of the nitrogen is retained in the soil. The retained nitrogen is redistributed through the soil to aboveground components and plant roots85. The effect of this coupling is a change in the species composition of the forest that persists over time, which is confirmed in our research by analyses of changes in forest ground cover and the frequency of Carpinus betulus.

Although our study does not directly address the effects of climate change on soil or even on BF vegetation, LM and MID analyses clearly indicate a link between the increase in CB cover and some soil properties, in particular NO3- compounds. A likely explanation for this is the change in competitive relationships caused by complex environmental changes such as climate warming, nitrogen deposition, natural and human disturbances and ungulate herbivory over the last 80 years86. Hornbeam and lime are favoured over spruce, pine, oak and birch. The most visible processes of CB succession were observed in the thermophilic oak forests in BF after antropopresure. Hornbeam regenerates quickly in the understorey, where it shades the forest floor and prevents the growth of other tree species, and eventually takes over the canopy, displacing other heliophilous and even sensitive shade-tolerant species7,87. Our research shows that the succession of a particular species—even a native one—significantly affects the soil, the complexity of biochemical processes at the plant-soil interface requires that we predict the future fate of BF with some distance. The change in the proportion of Carpinus betulus is the reason that more and more high quality litter is ending up on the forest floor, which we believe has a significant impact on the C/N ratio, soil acidity and enrichment of the upper soil horizons with nitrates and exchangeable cations. However, it should be noted that there are other factors that can also play an important role in the life of the forest. For example, some studies suggest that the age of the forest is an important factor that contributes significantly to the overall variability of litter quality characteristics88, and that intraspecific variation in litter quality in turn has profound effects on belowground processes89. Nevertheless, our long-term studies, which are consistent with the findings of Kartalaei et al.90, provide reliable evidence that Carpinus betulus improves litter properties, soil organic matter content, nitrogen forms and available nutrients. Our research also indirectly confirms the hypothesis that global warming will increase the rate of succession towards deciduous forests in the near future91.

Conclusion

The changes in forest vegetation, which are most likely caused by climate change, influence the processes in the upper soil horizons. We observed a strong correlation between the increase in Carpinus betulus cover in 1962–2013 and nitrogen transformation, pH, exchangeable cation content and C/N ratio. Our studies indicate that the biochemical processes that progress from the Oi to the Oea sub-horizon and then to the A horizon are largely determined by processes in the tree stand. By observing the state of the forest at two points in time and analysing the current soil properties, we were able to prove the existence of a feedback loop: The widely observed climate changes mean that plants can now grow in places where they did not occur before, which in turn affects the biochemical properties of the soils. Our results contribute to a better understanding of the complexity of environmental processes by clearly showing that an environmental feature that has nothing directly to do with soil properties (CB cover) can be crucial for biogeochemical processes in the soil. In addition, the use of MID analyses in the natural sciences allowed for better assessment of the significance of differences, more accurate interpretation of variability, the ability to detect subtle effects and greater confidence in the results. We believe that the proportion of deciduous tree species, especially Carpinus betulus, in temperate forests will continue to increase in the foreseeable future.
