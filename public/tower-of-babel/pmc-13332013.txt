Mitigating water stress in Medicago sativa L. through irrigation system modification: a field study in a semi-arid region
Authors: Vahdat Barkhordari, Hossein Shamsi Mahmodabadi, Esmaeil Moghbeli Dameneh, Gholamreza Zarei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn arid and semi-arid regions, severe water scarcity critically threatens agricultural sustainability and food security. Enhancing crop resilience and water productivity is therefore an urgent priority. This study addresses this imperative by evaluating advanced irrigation strategies for alfalfa (Medicago sativa), a vital forage crop, to optimize yield and quality under water-limited conditions. This study evaluated irrigation strategies to enhance water use efficiency (WUE) and resilience in alfalfa. A two-year field experiment in Jiroft, Iran, compared three irrigation systems—Flooding Irrigation (FI), Subsurface Drip-Tape Irrigation (SDTI), and Subsurface Drip Pipe Irrigation (SDPI)—under four irrigation levels (100%, 90%, 80%, and 70% of crop water requirement). Results showed that the SDPI system achieved the highest water use efficiency (WUED and WUEF). However, maximum dry and fresh forage yields (DFY, FFY) were obtained with FI at full irrigation (100%). Under irrigation levels (70%), SDPI improved forage quality by increasing CP, soluble carbohydrates, proline, and antioxidant enzyme activity, while reducing fiber content. Irrigation at 70% generally enhanced WUE and these biochemical indicators across all systems. The findings demonstrate that the SDPI system, particularly under irrigation levels (70% requirement), effectively improves the nutritional value and water productivity of alfalfa, supporting its adoption for sustainable production in water-limited environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40417-6.Keywords: Alfalfa, Drought, Flooding irrigation, Stress, SDPI, SDTISubject terms: Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

In semi-arid and arid regions, water stress is the primary environmental factor restricting plant growth, development, and yield, ultimately leading to oxidative stress1. Thus, optimizing water consumption is a critical priority for achieving sustainable agriculture, especially for crops with high water demands2. A central goal of sustainable agriculture is to optimize water management while maintaining acceptable product quality. Agricultural research has clearly demonstrated that both the choice of irrigation system and the level of water application significantly influence crop production and quality3–5. This is particularly critical for high-water-demand strategic crops—such as sugarcane, alfalfa, potato, and sugar beet—whose cultivation is often severely constrained in arid and semi-arid regions. Consequently, achieving an appropriate balance among crop yield, water use, and product quality is essential for expanding the cultivation of such water-intensive crops in water-limited environments.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is one of the most important forage plants, playing a crucial role in animal nutrition due to its production of high-quality forage6,7. Alfalfa has a high water requirement, yet its extensive and deep root system contributes to its ability to withstand drought stress8,9. Given the scarcity of natural water resources, farm management must prioritize the optimal use of each unit of water. Alfalfa also offers superior nutritional value compared to many other forage sources10. These traits—combined with its inherent drought tolerance—make alfalfa an ideal forage crop for production under water-limited conditions in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide.

The aim of limited irrigation management is to maximize WUE in crop production under water-scarce conditions11. It is well established that irrigation method significantly influences WUE4. In this context, subsurface drip irrigation (SDI)—including systems such as SDTI and SDPI—holds particular promise. SDI allows direct application of water and fertilizer, minimizes water losses, and offers the highest irrigation efficiency among common methods12,13,3. Studies report that SDI requires substantially less water for alfalfa than FI or sprinklers14. Although previous research has shown that alfalfa fresh and dry yields decline as water supply decreases, WUE consistently increases under irrigation levels. Nevertheless, long-term operation of SDI systems can encounter challenges such as emitter clogging by roots, which occurs as roots grow toward water-release points15,16.

However, long-term operation of subsurface irrigation systems can lead to practical challenges, notably the clogging of emitters by plant roots as they grow toward water-release points17.

Biochemical changes represent a common adaptive response in plants to abiotic stresses such as water deficit. Key biochemical parameters—including soluble carbohydrates, total protein, proline content, and the activity of antioxidant enzymes like catalase and peroxidase—are often modulated under stress conditions18–20.

The objectives of this study were to (1) assess the effects of irrigation system and water level on alfalfa productivity and water-use efficiency, and (2) characterize the crop’s biochemical responses to water-deficit stress.

However, the long-term use of such subsurface irrigation system causes problems such as clogging of drippers by plant roots, as a result of the movement of roots towards pipe outlet17. Changes in the biochemical are general adaptive responses in plants to abiotic stresses, such as water deficit stress. The biochemical parameters analyzed included soluble carbohydrates, total protein, proline content, and the activity of enzymes such as catalase and peroxidase18–20. The aims of this study were to evaluate the effects of irrigation type and level on alfalfa production performance and to determine the crop’s biochemical responses to water stress.

Materials and methods

Plant collection

The alfalfa plant material was collected (South Kerman Agricultural and Natural Resources Research and Education Center) and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines. The material was identified by the Seed and Seedling Breeding and Production Research Institute, Iran (Herbarium number: A545789345- Alfalfa) and authenticated by Dr. Ahmad Ahmadpour, a botanist at the South Kerman Agricultural and Natural Resources Research and Education Center, AREEO, Jiroft, Iran.

Site description and planting

The two-year field experiment (2022–2023) was conducted at the Agricultural Research Center of southern Kerman, Jiroft, Iran (28°46′N, 57°44′E; elevation 650 m a.s.l.). A strip-split plot design was used within a randomized complete block arrangement, replicated three times. Treatments comprised three irrigation systems—Flooding Irrigation (FI), SDTI, and SDPI—and four irrigation levels: 100% (I100), 90% (I90), 80% (I80), and 70% (I70) of weekly crop water requirement. Plots measured 4 × 2 m (8 m2) and contained eight crop rows spaced 25 cm apart. Seedbed preparation involved deep ploughing in autumn followed by light ploughing in spring of each year. The seedbeds were ready on 1 March 2022 and 1 March 2023 for the first and second experimental years, respectively.

Determination of crop water requirements (ETc)

The actual crop water requirement for alfalfa was determined as crop evapotranspiration (ETc), which was estimated from the reference evapotranspiration (ETo) and the appropriate crop coefficient (Kc) using the standard equation:

Evapotranspiration (ETo): Daily ETo was calculated using the FAO Penman-Monteith method21, which is the internationally accepted standard. This calculation was based on daily climate data—including maximum and minimum air temperature, relative humidity, solar radiation, and wind speed—obtained from the nearest synoptic weather station in Kerman, Iran.

Crop Coefficient (Kc): The Kc values for alfalfa were adopted from FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper No. 5621. These standard coefficients vary with the crop’s growth stage (initial, mid-season, and late season). The specific monthly Kc values applied in this study were 0.40 (initial), 1.15 (mid-season), and 0.50 (late season), which were adjusted for the local climatic conditions of the study region as recommended.

Irrigation Water Supply and Measurement: Irrigation water was supplied from wells near the experimental site. A central control station managed the water flow from a main 75 mm tube, which branched into three separate 63 mm lines. Each 63 mm line delivered one of the four designated irrigation levels (100%, 90%, 80%, or 70% of ETc). At the beginning of each 63 mm line, a calibrated volumetric water meter was installed to ensure accurate measurement and precise control of the applied irrigation water.

Yield measurement

Fresh forage yield (FFY) was measured at the 30% flowering stage by harvesting plants from a 10 cm stubble height. The harvested material was weighed immediately using a digital scale (accuracy ± 0.001 kg) and expressed as tons per hectare.

For dry forage yield (DFY), a representative subsample of approximately 500 g of fresh forage was oven-dried at 68 °C for 72 h to constant weight. The dry weight was then used to calculate DFY, also reported in tons per hectare.

Determination of crude protein (CP), ash

Crude protein (CP) content was determined using the Kjeldahl method as described by Hemke and Sparks (1996). Ash content was measured according to the procedure outlined by Adebooye (1996)22.

Determination of water soluble carbohydrates (WSC), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF) and CF

The concentrations of key forage quality components WSC, NDF, ADF, and CF—were analyzed. Forage samples were first oven-dried at 65 °C to a constant weight and then ground to pass through a 1-mm screen. The measurements were performed using a Near Infrared (NIR) spectrometer (NIRFlex N-500). Spectral data were interpreted, and concentrations were calculated using calibration equations developed according to the standard wet chemistry methods described by Van Soest et al. (1991). All results for WSC, NDF, ADF, and CF are expressed on a dry matter basis as a percentage (%). Each analysis was performed with three technical replications per treatment sample to ensure precision23.

Determination of proline

Proline content was evaluated using the acid-ninhydrin method as described by Bates et al.24, Proline content was evaluated using the acid-ninhydrin method as described by Bates et al. (1973)24. Briefly, approximately 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of 3% (w/v) aqueous sulfosalicylic acid. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was reacted with acid-ninhydrin reagent. Absorbance of the developed chromophore was measured at 520 nm using a spectrophotometer. Proline concentration was quantified against a standard calibration curve prepared with L-proline. The final proline content is expressed on a fresh weight basis as micromoles per gram (µmol g⁻¹ FW). The analysis was performed with three biological replicates per treatment, each with two technical replicates.

Determination of antioxidant enzymes

The activity of peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) in M. sativa L. leaf tissues were assayed according to Aebi25and Polle et al.26 methods, respectively. The activities of peroxidase (POD; EC 1.11.1.7) and catalase (CAT; EC 1.11.1.6) were assayed. For enzyme extraction, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1 mM EDTA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. POD activity was determined by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm due to guaiacol oxidation (extinction coefficient: 26.6 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹). CAT activity was assayed by tracking the decomposition of H₂O₂ at 240 nm (extinction coefficient: 39.4 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹). Enzyme activity is expressed as units per milligram of protein (U mg⁻¹ protein) or on a fresh weight basis (µmol min⁻¹ g⁻¹ FW), where one unit is defined as the amount of enzyme required to transform 1 µmol of substrate per minute under the assay conditions.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA). A two-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the effects of irrigation system and irrigation level, as well as their interaction, on all measured traits. Correlation patterns among parameters were further explored using principal component analysis (PCA) in R software (version 4.3.19). Treatment means were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) test at the 1% probability level. All statistical analyses were conducted with SAS software (version 9.2).

The concentrations of CP and WSC exhibited a consistent and significant increase under irrigation levels across all three irrigation systems (Table 6). This shared response highlights a conserved biochemical adaptation to water stress, where alfalfa accumulates osmolytes and stress-related proteins. The most pronounced improvements in forage quality were achieved under the most severe water deficit (I70), with the SDPI system consistently yielding the highest absolute values for both CP and WSC. This suggests SDPI is particularly effective at enhancing the nutritional density of alfalfa under water-limited conditions. Statistically, CP and WSC showed a strong negative correlation with fresh and dry forage yields (DFY) (R2 = 0.68–0.79), illustrating the classic trade-off between biomass production and the concentration of quality components. Conversely, both parameters were strongly positively correlated with WUEF, WUED (R2 = 0.98–0.99) and with each other (R2 = 0.98). These relationships underscore that the improvement in water productivity under irrigation levels is intrinsically linked to a metabolic shift toward the accumulation of osmoprotectants (WSC) and proteins (CP), which collectively support cellular tolerance to drought.

NDF, ADF, CF, and ash

Analysis of variance (Table 6) indicated that the three-way interaction (year × irrigation system × irrigation level) was not significant for neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), crude fiber (CF), or ash content. However, the two-way interaction between irrigation system and level was significant (p < 0.05) for all parameters except CF, which was influenced only by the main effect of irrigation level.

Irrigation levels significantly reduced fiber content across all systems. All reduced irrigation levels (I90, I80, I70) resulted in lower NDF, ADF, and CF compared to their respective full irrigation (I100) controls within the same system (Table 6). The greatest reductions in fiber components were observed in the SDTI system.

In contrast, ash content increased progressively with water deficit. The highest ash content was recorded under the most severe deficit (I70) in the SDPI system, whereas the SDPI system overall exhibited the smallest proportional increase in ash content, indicating greater compositional stability under stress.

Ash content exhibited a strong negative correlation with forage yield (FFY, DFY) and fiber components (NDF, ADF, CF) (R2 = 0.67–0.99). Conversely, it was strongly positively correlated with WUEF, WUED and the quality parameters water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) and crude protein (CP) (R2 = 0.98–0.99) (Fig. 1).

Proline, peroxidase enzyme and catalase enzyme

Analysis of variance (Table 7) for proline content, peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) activity revealed distinct patterns of significance. The three-way interaction between year, irrigation system, and irrigation level was non-significant (p > 0.05) for all three biochemical parameters, indicating that the combined effect of these factors did not differ across the two experimental years. For proline and POD activity, the two-way interaction between irrigation system and irrigation level was statistically significant (p < 0.01). This indicates that the effect of reducing irrigation water depended on the type of system used. In contrast, for CAT activity, this two-way interaction was non-significant (p > 0.05). Instead, CAT activity was influenced independently by the main effects of both irrigation system and irrigation level (p < 0.01 for both). No other simple or interaction effects significantly altered CAT activity.

Discussion

Arid and semi-arid regions face harsh environmental conditions—low rainfall, high solar radiation, and elevated evaporation. These areas cover approximately 41% of Earth’s land surface and support more than one-third of the global population27. Iran lies within such a climatic zone, with precipitation well below the global average and a negative water balance. Consequently, effective water management is essential for sustainable agriculture in these regions.

Under water deficit, crop growth is primarily limited by reduced photosynthesis. Stomatal closure restricts CO₂ uptake28, inhibiting photosynthesis and cell expansion29. Therefore, improving WUE is a critical goal, and irrigation levels can be an effective strategy.

In this study, principal component analysis (PCA) and analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed that irrigation system and level significantly affected all measured variables. WUE increased as water supply was reduced to 70% of crop demand, although forage yield declined correspondingly. These findings align with previous work showing that irrigation levels enhances WUE in alfalfa, often at the expense of biomass30. For instance, Ismail and Almarshadi31observed significantly higher WUE when irrigating at 70% of field capacity. Conversely, large reductions in irrigation amount can sharply reduce alfalfa yield32. However, studies such as Lamm et al.3 have reported that irrigation at 70% of the requirement can increase WUE without significantly affecting yield, emphasizing the importance of system-specific management.

Other research33,16 also supports our results. Zafari and Ebadi34 and Riasat35 reported a 27% forage loss in alfalfa under water deficit. The higher WUE under limited irrigation may be attributed to alfalfa’s adaptability to drought, supported by genetic, morphological, and physiological mechanisms. Known responses include reduced plant height, chlorophyll a/b ratio, aboveground biomass, leaf water potential, and relative water content, alongside increased root–shoot ratio, osmolyte accumulation, total chlorophyll content, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant activity levels30,36.

Irrigation method also significantly influenced WUE. The SDPI system markedly improved WUE, consistent with earlier reports for alfalfa and other forage crops. For example, Behbahani and Saberi37 noted increased alfalfa dry matter production with one-third less water compared to full irrigation. In California’s arid regions, Montazar et al.38 found that subsurface drip irrigation (SDI) enhanced WUE and mitigated the negative effects of irrigation levels, maintaining 80% of the yield achieved under full irrigation. The superiority of SDI over flood irrigation in improving WUE and crop yield is well documented for various crops39,3,4,40.

SDI systems (including SDTI and SDPI) are advantageous due to direct fertilizer application, minimal water loss, and high irrigation efficiency. In our study, irrigation method and level also affected biochemical traits in alfalfa. Reducing irrigation to 70% under SDPI increased crude protein, water-soluble carbohydrates, proline, and antioxidant enzyme activities while decreasing NDF, ADF, ash, and crude fiber. In general, biochemical quality declined as irrigation increased to 100%.

The use of SDI systems (SDTI and SDPI) has a special place due to the possibility of applying plant irrigation fertilizer directly, the lowest water loss and the highest irrigation efficiency among other irrigation methods. In this study, irrigation method and level affected some biochemical in the alfalfa. The decreased irrigation levels up to 70% and SDPI system led to higher CP, WSC, proline, peroxidase and catalase activities, while it reduced NDF, ADF, ASH and crude fiber content compared to other treatments. In overall, biochemical showed decreasing trend by increasing of irrigation level up to 100%.

It is well established that plants accumulate osmolytes such as proline to cope with abiotic stresses like water deficit20. Proline accumulates in the cytosol, enhancing water uptake, stabilizing proteins and membranes, and activating antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase41,18,42–44. Antioxidant enzymes help maintain membrane integrity under drought-induced oxidative stress. Similarly, accumulation of proteins and soluble carbohydrates aids in osmotic adjustment under water shortage45.

Our results are consistent with known biochemical adaptations of alfalfa to drought stress. Accumulation of proline46,47, water-soluble carbohydrates48, proteins49,50,48, and antioxidant responses51,52 are key mechanisms by which alfalfa withstands water deficit52. The elevated levels of CP, WSC, proline, and antioxidant enzymes observed here likely represent adaptive responses to water limitation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a clear trade-off in alfalfa production under different irrigation regimes. While full flood irrigation (FI) maximized biomass yield, it resulted in the lowest WUE. Adopting a irrigation levels strategy at 70% of crop water requirement significantly improved WUE across all systems. The SDPI system under 70% RDI emerged as the optimal strategy, achieving the highest WUE while enhancing forage quality. This treatment increased crude protein, soluble carbohydrates, proline, and antioxidant activity while reducing fiber content—indicating a beneficial stress-adaptive response. In contrast, SDTI system improved WUE but not forage quality, and FI produced high biomass with poor water efficiency and inferior quality under deficit. We recommend SDPI combined with 70% RDI for sustainable alfalfa production in semi-arid regions, as it optimally balances water conservation with high-quality forage output. Future studies should assess the long-term economic and operational viability of this approach.
