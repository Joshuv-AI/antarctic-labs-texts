Symptom experiences of cancer-related cognitive impairment in women with ovarian cancer
Authors: Liying Wang, Yan Ding, Yi Zhang, Mei Xue, Zhenqi Lu, Xiaoju Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) is one of the most prevalent and persistent symptoms reported by women with ovarian cancer. This study aims to comprehensively explore the symptom experience of CRCI in people/women with ovarian cancer.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with eligible people/women with ovarian cancer in Cancer Center of Fudan University, Shanghai, China. The interview guide and data coding were developed based on the symptom management theory. Qualitative content analysis was utilized to identify and refine key themes and sub-themes.ResultsThe study included 31 participants with a mean age of 51.38 years (range: 25–65 years) and a median time since diagnosis of one year (range: 0.35–14.2 years). Four themes and ten sub-themes were identified: Theme 1 CRCI Perception, including two subthemes: ‘Experiencing subtle cognitive decline’ and ‘No feeling or no opportunity to feel the changes in cognitive function’. Theme 2 CRCI Attribution, including three subthemes: ‘social disengagement and values reconstruction’ ‘symptom entanglement’ and ‘surgical anesthesia and chemotherapy’. Theme 3 CRCI Impacts: ‘contained life disruption’ and ‘eroded self-efficacy’. Theme 4 Adaptive responses: ‘cognitive load reduction’ ‘Compensatory strategies’ and ‘Passive acceptance’.ConclusionsThe majority of women with ovarian cancer in this study reported experiencing CRCI, primarily characterized by memory and attention deficits. Sociocultural context and individual cancer copying style played an important role on the CRCI symptom experience, underscoring the need for culturally sensitive, patient-centered interventions to address CRCI in ovarian cancer care.Keywords: Cognition, Cognitive impairment, Ovarian cancer, Symptom experience, Quantitative research

Introduction

A growing body of evidence indicates that non-central nervous system(non-CNS) cancers and their treatments can induce cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI), affecting 35–75% of patients [1]. CRCI manifests as deficits in memory, attention, executive function, language processing, and information processing speed, significantly impairing daily functioning, interpersonal relationships, psychological well-being, as well as participation in medical decision-making and adherence to treatment during and after treatment [2]. While, extensive research has focused on breast cancer patients, other vulnerable groups- particularly women with ovarian cancer- remain understudied despite their unique clinical profile [3, 4].

Ovarian cancer, abbreviated as OC, a leading gynecologic malignancy, has exhibited a steadily rising global incidence over the past two decades, including in China [5].Compared to other non-CNS cancers, women with OC are exposed to a unique constellation of risk factors for CRCI. These include a high frequency of advanced-stage disease requiring extensive surgical resection, exposure to multiple cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy, a high rate of disease recurrence, and a substantial burden of comorbid symptoms such as fatigue, anxiety, depression, menopausal symptoms, and sleep disturbances [3, 6–8]. Emerging yet inconsistent evidence suggests that 28–92% of these patients report or exhibit measurable cognitive deficits [9–11], primarily affecting attention, memory, verbal fluency, and executive function, with downstream consequences for quality of life [9, 10]. However, existing studies predominantly rely on quantitative assessments and narrowly focus on chemotherapy-induced cognitive dysfunction, leaving the lived experience of neurocognitive deficits in this population poorly characterized.

Qualitative approaches have proven valuable in elucidating CRCI experiences among breast cancer patients, particularly in domains such as work performance, symptom burden, adaptive strategies, and unmet supportive care needs [12–14]. As Pergolizzi [12]emphasized, understanding patient-reported cognitive changes is critical for developing non-pharmacological interventions. To address the knowledge gap in ovarian cancer, this study employs a descriptive qualitative design to systematically explore the symptom experiences of CRCI in women with OC, aiming to inform patient-centered care strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This descriptive qualitative study employed Sandelowski’s Qualitative Description framework [15], utilizing semi-structured interviews to explore symptom experiences through the lens of Symptom Management Theory [16, 17].The theory conceptualizes symptoms as multidimensional constructs comprising: Perceptions-awareness of physiological or behavioural deviations from baseline, Evaluation-interpretation of symptom etiology, severity, consequences, and controllability and Response-adaptive strategies encompassing physiological, psychological, and sociocultural dimensions. These iterative, interdependent dimensions informed interview design and data analysis [16, 17].

Participants were purposively recruited from the Gynecologic Oncology Clinic and Outpatient Chemotherapy Center at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center between March and July 2021. Inclusion criteria required histologically confirmed primary OC, age 18–65 years [2], stable clinical status (no acute complications), and mandarin proficiency for unaided communication. Exclusion criteria encompassed documented brain metastases (confirmed via imaging), and diagnosed psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, dementia). Sample size followed the principle of thematic saturation, determined through iterative data collection and analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (#2103232-25).

Data collection

Eligibility screening and recruitment were conducted by a researcher trained in qualitative oncology studies. Consented participants completed a demographic survey (age, disease stage, treatment history) in a private consultation room prior to face-to-face interviews. A semi-structured interview guide (Table 1), developed through literature synthesis and pilot-tested with two patients, was administered by an experienced qualitative researcher (WLY, PhD, with 12 years of oncology nursing practice). Interviews lasted 30–45 min, audio-recorded, and iteratively refined to probe emerging themes. Field notes captured non-verbal cues.

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h. Transcripts underwent inductive thematic framework analysis [18]through five stages, as outlined in Table 2. Two researchers independently coded all transcripts. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions with a third investigator.

Theme 1: CRCI perception

Sub-theme 1.1 experiencing subtle cognitive decline

Participants universally reported cognitive deficits, predominantly affecting memory systems including both short and long-term memory, particularly following task interruption. ‘Being forgetful,’ ‘I could not find things,’ and ‘I was unable to remember the words/names’ were their most frequent complaints.

A subset of participants reported other mild deficits across other cognitive domains, including impaired verbal fluency (e.g., word-finding difficulties during conversations), reduced attentional focus (particularly when processing multistep instructions), and challenges in spatial orientation—symptoms most pronounced during novel skill acquisition or socially demanding interactions requiring rapid information synthesis.

Sub-theme 1.2 no feeling or no opportunity to feel the changes in cognitive function

A few respondents indicated that they did not feel any decline in their cognitive abilities such as memory, but among them, some emphasized that it might be due to their current living conditions that they had no opportunity to perceive any changes in their cognitive functions.

Theme 2: CRCI attribution

Sub-theme 2.1 social disengagement and values reconstruction

Most participants consistently described post-diagnostic lifestyle reorganization characterized by environmental monotony, restricted social roles and reduced occupational demands. They were well cared for or protected by family, friends or colleagues, and had little connection with the outside world. They had changed their priorities in life. Thus, it was unnecessary for them to remember/think/talk/concentrate as much as before. Based on the saying ‘use it or lose it’, in the long run, their brains would deteriorate.

Sub-theme 2.2 symptom entanglement

A few patients expressed that they felt like they were trapped in a vicious cycle with several symptoms including fatigue, sleep disturbances, emotional distress and cognitive changes, which all influenced each other.

Sub-theme 2.3 surgical anaesthesia and chemotherapy

Eight patients speculated that their cognitive decline might be related to the use of anaesthetics and chemotherapy, especially those who had undergone multiple operations.

Theme 3: CRCI impacts

Subtheme 3.1: contained life disruption

Most patients reported that the decline in memory, comprehension, and other cognitive abilities would have some impact on their daily lives and work; however, the extent of this influence was not deemed significant.

Sub-theme 7 eroded self-efficacy

A small number of patients were troubled by cognitive decline due to having encountered safety-critical lapses, such as forgetting to turn off the stove-nearly caused a fire, neglecting to lock the door, or got lost in neighbourhood. And these experiences precipitated anxiety about progressive decline.

Theme 4: adaptive responses

Subtheme 4.1: cognitive load reduction

Actively or passively letting oneself ‘slow down’ or ‘offload’ things, most patients allow themselves to be cared for, slow down the pace of their lives, avoid accomplishing multiple tasks at the same time, and actively seek help from family members, which has become a ‘natural’ adaptation due to their cognitive decline. Many patients intentionally start turning a blind eye to many things.

Subtheme 4.2: compensatory strategies

Mobile phone ‘memos,’ shopping lists, post-it notes, alarm clocks, or making notes on time were the most common compensation strategies for many patients. In addition, to prevent mistakes, some patients would, in daily life, repeatedly check or confirm things, such as whether the door was locked, the gas turned off, or the keys had been taken with me.

Subtheme 4.3: passive acceptance

Changes were viewed as inevitable treatment trade-offs for some patients. For certain patients, a slight decline in cognitive function does not affect their ability to lead a comfortable and uncomplicated life. Consequently, these individuals adopted fatalistic stances and remain untroubled by this change and do not take any measures in response.

Discussion

Summary of main results

This study provides the first in-depth exploration of CRCI experiences in women with OC, contextualized through the Symptom Management Theory framework, revealing four interconnected themes: perception, attribution, impact, and adaptive responses. Most respondents reported slight declines in cognitive function after OC diagnosis, particularly in memory and attention function. Patients’ ‘comfortable,’ ‘monotonous,’ ‘isolation’ and ‘narrow focus’ life attributed CRCI. Most participants did not particularly affect so much by the mild cognitive decline. In response to the symptom, many participants mainly chose not to burden the brain, actively or passively letting themselves ‘slow down’ or ‘offload’ burden.

Results in the context of published literature

Notably, while participants universally reported memory and attentional deficits—consistent with breast [19]and cervical cancer [20] populations—their minimized appraisal of symptom severity diverges from Western cohorts documenting profound quality-of-life disruptions. This discrepancy may stem from cultural scripts governing illness disclosure in Asian contexts, where cognitive decline carries familial stigma [21, 22], compounded by post-diagnostic role transitions that reduce cognitive demands. By integrating qualitative findings with cultural and clinical perspectives, we illuminate how cognitive symptom experiences are shaped by both biological and sociocultural forces—a critical advancement beyond prior quantitative approaches in this population.

Cultural embeddedness of symptom experience

The predominant normalization of CRCI symptoms starkly contrasts with Western studies emphasizing profound quality-of-life disruptions [23]. This divergence may reflect Confucianist values of stoicism and Taoist acceptance of impermanence [24], where cognitive struggles are reframed as natural adaptations rather than pathologies. According to the socio-cultural health belief model, culture plays an important role in shaping individuals’ perceptions, attitudes and behaviors relating to various dimensions of health [21]. Such cultural narratives intersect with the “loss of face” stigma surrounding mental health in Asian societies, creating a dual burden: patients mask symptoms to preserve familial honor while internalizing cognitive decline as an inevitable treatment trade-off [22, 25]. Clinically, this underscores the need for non-stigmatized screening tools that disentangle cultural resilience from unmet supportive care needs. As shown by Zeng et al., most Chinese cervical cancer patients’ response to cognitive impairment appears to be ‘doing nothing,’ which reflects Chinese Taoist philosophy [26], asserting that the natural order of the universe is one of functional harmony [24].

Neuroplasticity paradox: isolation as both buffer and risk

After a diagnosis of OC, patients experienced a rapid decline in their family roles and social functions, whether actively or passively. This adjustment towards a more relaxed life style let some patients to report an inability to recognize changes in cognitive function. In terms of symptom evaluation, although the majority of patients express that they perceive a “deterioration” in their cognitive abilities, they believe that this decline has minimal impact on their daily lives or job. Regarding coping strategies of CRCI, certain patients adopt a “let it be” attitude towards their cognitive decline because there is no necessity for maintaining normal cognitive function within this lifestyle context. Karen et al. showed that in Asian cultures, cancer patients and their partners were more likely to adopt protective coping strategies, such as the spouse taking on the patient’s initial domestic roles (e.g., cooking, housework, shopping) [27]. Moreover, for the patients in the study, rebalancing, changing priorities and family roles (from caregivers to those being cared for) were their chief ways of reconciling the fact that they had cancer. This result is consistent with the work of Le Boutillier et al. [28]. For some patients who returned to work, adjusting their job roles, reducing tasks and work hours, lowering their work expectations and accepting care from colleagues were their central coping strategies. This outcome is similar to the work of Zomkowski et al. [14]

Post-diagnosis social disengagement—initially served as a pragmatic coping mechanism, reducing cognitive demands through role delegation. Besides, most patients expressed that their communication with the outside world had decreased a lot, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic [29]. This kind of pandemic-accelerated Isolation exacerbated pre-existing cognitive disengagement, potentially creating synergistic neuroinflammatory risks through loneliness pathways [30, 31]. Clinicians should recognize such culturally-mediated coping not as passivity, but as an active renegotiation of identity within collectivist frameworks.

However, prolonged environmental simplification may accelerate cognitive deconditioning via reduced neuroplastic stimulation, akin to the “use it or lose it” principle observed in aging populations. This paradox highlights an urgent need for interventions that balance cultural respect for familial care hierarchies with evidence-based “brain health” strategies—for instance, collaborative goal-setting with occupational therapists to maintain safe cognitive challenges. such as, for the five patients who had returned to work reported that their job gave them a sense of fulfilment and improved their quality of life, which is also consistent with the findings of the systematic evaluation by Stone et al. [32]

Symptoms and surgical neurotoxicity: an underrecognized contributor

The theme of “symptom entanglement” highlights a core, patient-centered challenge in CRCI. It is also relevant to note that the broader treatment context—such as being in active treatment versus survivorship—is known to shape symptom profiles. While acknowledging this established framework, our findings on the pervasive and mutually reinforcing nature of the fatigue-sleep-distress-cognition cycle offer a nuanced insight that may cut across these different phases.

Notably, some participants attributed CRCI to surgical anesthesia, a novel finding absent in breast cancer literature [19]. While chemotherapy neurotoxicity dominates current CRCI paradigms [2], emerging preclinical models suggest anesthesia-induced microglial activation may prime neural networks for subsequent cognitive decline. This aligns with participant reports of post-hysterectomy “brain haze”, particularly among those undergoing multiple procedures. Future biomarker studies should quantify the cumulative effects of surgical exposures, especially in OC’s high-recurrence context. In addition, attribution of CRCI to surgical anesthesia aligns with emerging evidence on post-operative neuroinflammation, yet contrasts with literature emphasizing chemotherapy neurotoxicity [2]. This highlights the need for pre-surgical cognitive counselling.

Limitations and future directions

This study, while providing the first in-depth profile of CRCI in ovarian cancer (OC), has several limitations that must be considered. Firstly, the transferability of the results may be limited. The participants were recruited from a single center under specific criteria (e.g., Mandarin-speaking, no major psychiatric comorbidities), and the sample predominantly included patients within a short median diagnosis interval (1 year) and undergoing active treatment (20 of 31). This specific context means that the rich, in-depth experiences captured here may not fully represent the diversity of all OC survivors, particularly those in long-term remission or from different cultural and clinical settings. Secondly, the co-occurrence of active treatment for many participants means that their reported cognitive experiences are intricately linked with the acute effects of therapy (e.g., fatigue, emotional distress). While this provides a snapshot of CRCI during a critical phase, it challenges the disentanglement of treatment-induced symptoms from potential longer-term cognitive deficits. Future research should seek to validate these findings in broader, longitudinal qualitative cohorts that strategically include patients at various disease phases (newly diagnosed, on-treatment, off-treatment, long-term survivors). Employing triangulation with neuropsychological test data or caregiver interviews could enhance the credibility and robustness of future findings. The insights generated here lay the necessary groundwork for developing and evaluating patient-centered cognitive rehabilitation strategies, such as those incorporating Taoist mindfulness [26].

Implications for practice and future research

The majority of OC patients complained of memory and attention problems, which should be fully considered when implementing OC patient care. Individualized cognition compensatory strategies can be provided to OC patients, including personalized reminders, doing one thing at a time, asking others to minimize interruptions during communication or working, slowing down the pace of life, seeking and receiving help from others [33]. When managing CRCI, socio-cultural factors, cancer copying style, surgical history, and other cancer-related symptoms such as fatigue and anxiety need to be taken into account. Implement routine cognitive assessments using non-stigmatizing language (e.g., “chemo-fog” vs. “dementia”). Deliver lifestyle prescription, by collaborating with occupational therapists to design “brain-healthy” social role maintenance plans. Patient education should be carried out repeatedly in various forms and in a step-by-step manner.

Conclusion

The majority of OC patients experienced symptoms of CRCI, predominantly characterized by memory and attention problems; however, most patients stated that CRCI did not affect their lives so much. Socio-cultural factors, cancer coping style, and other symptoms played an important role in the participants’ symptom perceptions, evaluations, and responses regarding CRCI.
