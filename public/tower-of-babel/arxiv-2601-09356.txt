Determination of active forces in actomyosin systems as inverse source problems for the Stokes equation
Emily Klass, Tram Thi Ngoc Nguyen, Nilay Cicek, Yoav G. Pollack, Sarah Köster, Andreas Janshoff, Anne Wald

Abstract
The identification of forces and stresses is a central task in biophysics research: Knowledge on forces is key to understanding dynamic processes in active biological systems that are able to self-organize and display emergent properties by converting energy into mechanical work. The aim of this paper is to identify forces generated by a filament-motor network of F-actin and myosin – actomyosin – and exerted on the surrounding fluid, therefore causing a fluid flow. In particular, we evaluate optical microscopy data stemming from two different physical settings, confined and non-confined active gels. As a theoretical model, we use the Stokes equation together with an incompressibility condition and suitable boundary conditions reflecting the physical settings. The problem of determining the forces from knowledge on the fluid flow is formulated as an inverse source problem. Due to experimental limitations, only incomplete data are available. We provide a rigorous analysis of the forward problems and the impact of missing data, derive the adjoints of the forward operators needed for regularization, and demonstrate our methods on both synthetic and experimentally measured data.



Keywords:

inverse problems, source identification, Stokes equation, active matter, regularization

1
Introduction

Active systems are collections of subunits that continuously convert chemical energy into directed motion or internal stresses, leading to sustained nonequilibrium dynamics and emergent collective behavior. At scales from single molecules to tissues, this persistent energy consumption breaks detailed balance and gives rise to phenomena such as spontaneous flows, pattern formation, and large‑scale organization that cannot be captured by equilibrium statistical mechanics
[
52
,
40
,
7
,
19
,
65
]
. Understanding how microscopic activity translates into mesoscopic and macroscopic mechanics is therefore a central goal of active matter research, with implications for soft (smart) materials, synthetic biology and cell biology. In principle, precise knowledge of the subunits’ positions and momenta (forces) is required, which, however, can be rather intricate to determine considering the complex nature of biological systems and the restrictions imposed by the quality of the experimental data.

A particularly tractable and biologically relevant class of active materials are actomyosin gels
[
28
,
50
,
13
]
. The cytoskeleton of eukaryotic cells contains actin filaments and myosin motors whose ATP‑driven interactions generate contractile stresses and reorganize filament networks. Reconstituted actomyosin systems and dense cellular cortices both display rich mechanical responses: they are viscoelastic, heterogeneous, and capable of producing sustained internal flows and force generation through motor activity
[
1
,
38
,
37
,
54
,
62
,
69
]
. The biological implications are immense as these processes enable cells to divide, migrate and organize into larger structures – tissues – that themselves show emergent out-of-equilibrium behavior but on a different length scale.

These properties render actomyosin gels a fundamental, yet minimal and controllable platform for investigating how local biochemical activity produces forces and eventually collective motion.
When confined to finite compartments or embedded in a surrounding fluid, actomyosin networks drive internal, non‑equilibrium flows whose spatial structure reflects the distribution and orientation of active stresses
[
37
,
38
,
62
,
67
]
. In droplets or other confined geometries, internal circulations, vortical patterns and symmetry‑breaking flows can emerge; in bulk, active fluids, contractile or extensile activity can trigger spontaneous flows and hydrodynamic instabilities
[
6
,
39
]
. Importantly, the forces and stresses driving these flows are not directly accessible in most experiments, as probe‑based force measurements are often invasive, can perturb the dynamics under study, and are too local to capture the full spatial distribution of forces.

To gain information on these forces, we adopt an inverse approach. In our experiments, we observe steady, laminar flows using optical microscopy, which is followed by particle image velocimetry (PIV) analysis
[
2
,
51
]
to extract the velocity fields. The relation between these data and the underlying forces that must act within the active medium is described with low‑Reynolds‑number hydrodynamic models that account for spatial heterogeneity in viscosity. This formulation casts the problem as a source identification task for Stokes‑type operators, including Stokes–Brinkman variants when porous or network drag is relevant (see also
[
33
]
). These turn out to be affine linear inverse problems with incomplete data. To account for measurement noise when solving problems that are typically ill-posed
[
21
,
25
]
, regularization methods
[
8
,
15
,
60
]
are used, relying on our rigorous mathematical analysis of the inverse problems.

We use two experimental model systems, one based on contractile actomyosin networks with free boundaries (bulk) and one where the gel is reconstituted in water-in-oil droplets (confined). The latter serves a minimal cell model, in which emergent phenomena such as self-propulsion are feasible.

From a mathematical point of view, the two experimental settings considered in this article (confined and bulk) require two distinct mathematical models as detailed in Section
2
. We present the respective function space settings and give a rigorous analysis, including proofs of the well-posedness of the forward problems in Section
3
and a discussion on the impact of data limitations resulting from the experiment as described in Section
4
. In view of numerical regularization, we derive the adjoints in Section
5
and present results for both simulated and experimental data in Section
6
as a proof-of-concept.

2
The Stokes equation as a forward model

In this section, we introduce our notation as well as the underlying physics to give a mathematical description of the relation between the forces representing the activity, in short: active forces, exerted by the actomyosin network, and the resulting flow field, which can be measured experimentally.
We do not aim at describing the protein network structure, but instead only the surrounding fluid. Hence the elastic effects on the fluid are neglected and the flow immediately adjusts to changes in the force
\mathbf{f}
, allowing a time-independent model for each time-instance.

For simplicity and to reflect the available data, we consider a two-dimensional setting, neglecting flow outside the image plane (i.e., the focus plane of the microscope). The model is, however, also valid in three dimensions.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2
, be a bounded Lipschitz domain with its boundary denoted by
\partial\Omega
.
We assume that the fluid has, in general, a spatially varying viscosity
\eta
to provide an additional degree of freedom, reflecting possible inhomogeneities. The active force
\mathbf{f}
, the velocity
\mathbf{v}
, and the pressure
p
are all spatial quantities, depending on the spatial variable
\mathbf{x}
.
Their relation can be modeled using the Stokes equation

(1)

-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\big)+\nabla p=\mathbf{f},\ \ D(\mathbf{v}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v}+(\nabla\mathbf{v})^{T}\right),

where
p
is the pressure inside of the droplet and
D(\mathbf{v})
is the stress tensor.

The differential operator
\nabla
is applied componentwise, i.e.,

\nabla\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial v_{d}}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial x_{d}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial v_{d}}{\partial x_{d}}\end{pmatrix}.

The viscosity
0<\eta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a strictly positive function and
\eta D(\mathbf{v})
is thus a spatial function with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Its divergence is the function
\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\big):\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We assume that the active fluid is incompressible, which is modeled by additionally postulating that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0
.
This incompressibility condition results in
\nabla\cdot(\nabla\mathbf{v})^{T}
being equal to
0
in every component.
We note that, if
\eta
is a constant function with
\eta(\mathbf{x})\equiv\hat{\eta}>0
for all
x\in\Omega
, then
\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\big)=\hat{\eta}\Delta\mathbf{v}
in the distributional sense, where
\Delta
is the Laplace operator and is applied component-wise.

Notation

The normal and tangential components of a vector
\mathbf{v}
are given as
\mathbf{v}_{\perp}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\parallel}
with the outer normal vector being
\mathbf{n}
. The notation of vector spaces is simplified, so that for example
{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}:=H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}:=L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Additionally, “:” denotes the element-wise or Hadamard product.

The simplest and therefore most commonly used boundary conditions in connection to the Stokes equation are homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and they are discussed often in literature such as
[
11
,
64
,
27
]
. Alternatively, the Stokes operator is also sometimes formulated with Neumann boundary conditions
[
41
]
.

In this paper, we introduce Robin boundary conditions and inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, that are also discussed in, e.g.,
[
53
]
and
[
4
]
, respectively, and will be the main focus of the upcoming sections.

2.1
Fluid flow in actomyosin droplets with Robin boundary conditions

Figure 1
:
Illustration of an actomyosin droplet with domain
\Omega

The droplet is assumed to have a fixed size and shape given by the domain
\Omega
as a two dimensional disk with boundary
\partial\Omega
. We denote the outward normal of the boundary with
\mathbf{n}
and split
\mathbf{v}
and the co-normal derivative
D(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}
into a tangential and a normal component, indicated by
\parallel
and
\bot
respectively.
Since no fluid leaves or enters the droplet, there is no normal component of the flow field on the boundary, i.e.,

\mathbf{v}_{\bot}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

For the tangential part, we postulate Robin-type boundary conditions (slip conditions)

\big(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}\big)_{\parallel}=-\lambda\mathbf{v}_{\parallel}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the slip length at a rigid boundary and thus a material parameter, which we assume to be known here. The choice of this condition is motivated by the physical setting: According to
[
31
]
, an actomyosin droplet is expected to be able to move or swim through a surrounding fluid, driven by internal active forces. An interaction between the outside fluid and the droplet’s fluid is thus expected, but since we do not describe the outside fluid in this simplified model, the use of a slip condition with an appropriate parameter
\lambda
serves as a suitable replacement.

The function space for the flow field
\mathbf{v}
is then given by

H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}:=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\,:\,\mathbf{v}_{\bot}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}.

For the pressure
p
, we use the function space
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega):=\left\{p\in L^{2}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathrm{d}x=0\right\}

to ensure well-definedness of the problem (see Section
3
for more details).

Altogether we use the Stokes problem

(2)

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\big)+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}&=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\mathbf{v}_{\bot}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\\
\big(\eta(\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}\big)_{\parallel}&=-\lambda\mathbf{v}_{\parallel}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{split}

as a model to describe the relation between the material parameter
\eta
, the pressure
p
, the forces
\mathbf{f}
and the velocity field
\mathbf{v}
of the incompressible fluid.

Before establishing the formulation of our inverse problem, we discuss a different setting motivated by experimental data.

2.2
A bulk model for actomyosin-driven flow using Dirichlet boundary conditions

Figure 2
:
Illustration of an actomyosin network with domain
\Omega

From an experimental point of view it can be useful to measure the velocity field of a network when it is not encapsulated in a droplet or the encapsulation is not visible in the data. Since encapsulating the network into droplets is time-consuming, evaluating the flow of a freely suspended acto-myosin network can be of use to test the network structure and make amendments to the composition of the network and surrounding fluid.

For this version of the problem
\Omega
is in the shape of a square for
d=2
or cube for
d=3
. We formulate a Stokes problem with an inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition. That is, we want to find

(\mathbf{v},p)\in({H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega))
given
\mathbf{f}\in{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
solving

(3)

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot(\eta D(\mathbf{v}))+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f}\text{ in}\ \Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}&=0\text{ in}\ \Omega\\
\mathbf{v}&=\mathbf{g}\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{split}

for given viscosity
0<\eta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{g}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
.

While we expect a rotational flow pattern in the droplets, here it is likely that the rotation-free component is a more prominent part of the overall force. The decomposition into rotation- and divergence-free components of the force will be discussed in section
4.1
.

3
Well-posedness of the forward problems

With the proposed model equations, we now examine unique existence of a velocity and pressure field
(\mathbf{v},p)
given any force
\mathbf{f}
. This guarantees well-definedness for the forward map for the inverse problems in Section
5
.
We shall discuss both versions of the Stokes models.

3.1
Well-posedness for the Stokes problem with Robin boundary conditions

Our analysis is based on weak formulation via the bilinear forms

a:H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\times H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\eta D(\mathbf{u}):D(\mathbf{v})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}^{T}\lambda\mathbf{v}_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

and

b:L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\ b(p,\mathbf{u}):=-\int_{\Omega}p(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

as well as the linear form

F:H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\ F(\mathbf{u}):=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{f}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

In our setting, we assume
\eta
to be known and consider the force-to-solution or Stokes operator

(4)

\mathcal{S}_{R}:L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\to\left(H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)\right),\quad\mathbf{f}\mapsto(\mathbf{v},p),

that maps the force
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
to the pair of velocity and pressure fields
(\mathbf{v},p)\in H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
, which solves the variational formulation

(5)

\begin{split}a(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})+b(p,\mathbf{u})&=F(\mathbf{u})\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d},\\
b(q,\mathbf{v})&=0\quad\text{for all }q\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega).\end{split}

The subscript
R
of the operator refers to the Robin boundary condition.

We now provide a well-definedness result for
{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
. While our proof is based on the celebrated inf-sup condition as in
[
11
,
20
,
33
]
, it is necessary to adapt the strategy to the specific boundary condition we are postulating here.

Theorem 3.1

(Well-posedness of
\mathcal{S}_{R}
)
.

The Stokes operator
\mathcal{S}_{R}:L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\ni\mathbf{f}\mapsto(\mathbf{v},p)\in H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
satisfying the variational form

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\eta D(\mathbf{u}):D(\mathbf{v})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}^{T}\mathbf{v}_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\int_{\Omega}p\nabla(\cdot\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{f}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d},\forall v\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)

is well-defined. Moreover, we have the stability result

(7)

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}+\|p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

with some positive constant
C
.

Proof.

We define the solution space as
H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
, where
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}^{2}:=\|D(\mathbf{v})\|_{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{d\times d}}^{2}+\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)^{d}}
is an equivalent norm to the standard
H^{1}
-norm. Indeed, there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}}\leq c\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}\leq\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}}
, where the lower bound follows from Korn’s inequality
[
33
, Lemma A.2(c)]
, and the upper bound is an application of the trace theorem on Sobolev spaces.

With this equivalent norm and the positivity of
\eta
and
\lambda
, we obtain coercivity of the bilinear form
a
as

(8)

\begin{split}\left\lvert a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})\right\rvert&=\left\lvert\left(\int_{\Omega}\eta D(\mathbf{u}):D(\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)\right\rvert\geq\min\{\eta,\lambda\}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}=:\alpha\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}.\end{split}

For any
q\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists
\mathbf{u}\in{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}
such that
q=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{H_{\parallel}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}
, thus

(9)

\beta\leq\inf_{\left\|q\right\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}=1}\ \sup_{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}=1}\frac{\left\|q\right\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}}=\inf_{\left\|q\right\|_{L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}=1}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}=1}b(\mathbf{u},q)

with constant
\beta>0
holding for all
q\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
. Boundedness of
a
and
b
is also clear by similar estimation; see (
11
).
Boundedness together with the inf-sup condition enables us to claim unique existence of
(\mathbf{v},p)\in H^{1}_{\parallel}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
.

As
\mathbf{v}
is the solution to the Stokes equation it is incompressible, leading to to
b(p,\mathbf{v})=0
, hence

(10)

\alpha\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq|a(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})|=|F(\mathbf{v})|\leq\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}.

Similarly, we estimate the pressure
p
using (
5
), (
9
) and (
10
) as

(11)

\begin{split}\|p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}&\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sup_{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}=1}|b(\mathbf{u},p)|=\frac{1}{\beta}\sup_{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{V}=1}|F(\mathbf{u})-a(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sup_{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}=1}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}+\max\{\eta,\lambda\}\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\|\mathbf{f}\|_{{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}}+\frac{\max\{\eta,\lambda\}}{\alpha}\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\right)\end{split}

The stability result (
7
) is then achieved with constant
C:=\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}(1+\frac{\max\{\lambda,\eta\}}{\alpha})>0
by adding the estimates (
10
) and (
11
).
∎

We note that well-posedness for the Stokes problems for incompressible as well as compressible fluids can also be obtained via the T-coercivity concept introduced by
[
14
]
; see also
[
22
]
.

3.2
Well-posedness for the Stokes problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions

We now address the Stokes model with inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary. We begin by introducing a vector function
\mathbf{v}_{g}\in H^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, such that
\mathbf{v}_{g}=\mathbf{g}
on
\partial\Omega
and that
\mathbf{v}_{g}
is incompressible.
Due to the divergence theorem, such
\mathbf{v}_{g}
exists if the boundary term
g
satisfies the
compatibility condition

(12)

\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{g}^{T}\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0.

Setting
\mathbf{v}_{0}:=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{g}
leads to
\mathbf{v}_{0}
satisfying the Stokes equation with homogeneous boundary

(13)

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\eta D(\mathbf{v}_{0})+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f}+\nabla\cdot\eta D(\mathbf{v}_{g})\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}_{0}&=0\text{ in}\ \Omega\\
\mathbf{v}_{0}&=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

This allows us to define the space for the velocity field
\mathbf{v}_{0}
as

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}:\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}
, then the bilinear forms

a:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v_{0}}):=\int_{\Omega}\eta D(\mathbf{u}):D(\mathbf{v_{0}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

and

b:L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\ b(p,\mathbf{u}):=-\int_{\Omega}p(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

as well as the linear form

F:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad F(\mathbf{u})=(\mathbf{f}+\nabla\cdot\eta D(\mathbf{v}_{g}),\mathbf{u}),

forming weak formulation

\begin{split}a(\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{u})+b(p,\mathbf{u})&=F(\mathbf{u})\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d},\\
b(q,\mathbf{v}_{0})&=0\quad\text{for all }q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\end{split}

We define the source-to-state operator as

\mathcal{S}_{D}:L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\to\left({H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)\right),\quad\mathbf{f}\mapsto(\mathbf{v},p),

where
(\mathbf{v}_{0},p)
is the solution to the variational problem (
13
).

Theorem 3.2

(Well-posedness of
\mathcal{S}_{D}
)
.

The Stokes operator
\mathcal{S}_{D}:L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\ni\mathbf{f}\mapsto(\mathbf{v},p)\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)

is well-defined. Moreover, we have the stability result

(14)

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}+\|p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
3.1
, adopting the solution space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\times L_{0}(\Omega)^{d}
for
(\mathbf{v}_{0},p)
and the shifting
\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{g}
.
∎

4
Data acquisition and analysis

In the following section we describe the workflow how we obtain the data from our experimental setup.

Figure 3
:
Experiment-to-reconstruction pipeline for actomyosin droplets

Figure 4
:
Fluorescence image of actin-myosin networks encapsulated in water-in-oil droplets. Actin is fluorescently labelled.

We prepared actomyosin networks following the protocol described previously
[
48
]
. Fluorescently labeled actin filaments and myosin motors were combined to form an entangled, contractile, three‑dimensional network in aqueous buffer solution containing ATP as the chemical energy source. The samples were imaged by time-lapse fluorescence microscopy and the resulting image sequences were analyzed using particle image velocimetry (PIV) to extract the two‑dimensional velocity field of the network (input data). The measured velocity field therefore reports the mesoscale flow of the actomyosin network driven by myosin motor activity in the ATP‑containing buffer, rather than the motion of the solvent alone.
Besides these bulk samples we also encapsulates the contractile networks into water-in-oil droplets to create PIV-data corresponding to the Stokes operator with Robin boundary conditions
\mathcal{S_{R}}
.

The sample was imaged at 5‑minute intervals beginning immediately after preparation using a confocal laser‑scanning microscope fitted with a 60× water‑immersion objective. Time‑lapse videos were recorded with the Galvano scanner operating in one‑way mode. Comparable experimental setups have been reported previously
[
70
,
63
,
49
]
, and a detailed description of materials and methods is provided in the Supplementary Information.

Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) as implemented in the python package
OpenPIV

[
36
]
was used to extract the 2-dimensional vector flow field.
For preliminary work, the implementation in the software
PIVlab

[
66
]
was used. PIV deduces the flow field by finding pixel correlations between subsequent frames. This method has previously been used, e.g., for tracking similar active networks in
[
55
]
. The measured velocity field is used to convert to physical meaningful units depending on the dimensions of the measured area and frame rate.
Since the current study is concerned with a stable steady-state flow, and since the flow in the experiment indeed does not noticeably change throughout the duration of imaging, the entire image stack was considered at once (as an ensemble) producing a single flow field measurement, rather than a time series of flow fields for each frame pair.
While the actin bundle flow provides a reasonable estimate of the velocity field of the underlying fluid, it does not necessarily adhere to the same fluid dynamics constraints. Particularly, in contrast to the flow of the liquid solution, the flow of the filament network does not need to be incompressible.
However, since the Landweber iteration minimizes the difference between the measured velocity field and the velocity field from the reconstructed force, the method finds a force that produces a flow field of the fluid that is closest of the measured flow field of the actin filaments while still complying to the fluid dynamic constraints such as the incompressibility condition.

The procedure to obtain the reconstructed force
\mathbf{f}^{*}
from the sample containing droplets is sketched in Figure
3
.

4.1
Helmholtz decomposition: divergence-free and curl-free force components

While data of the velocity flow field can be obtained in the experimental setting described in section
6.2
, we do not have data of the pressure field
p
.
This section therefore discusses, which components of the force
\mathbf{f}
can be reconstructed using only measured data of the velocity field
\mathbf{v}
for Robin boundary conditions corresponding to the forward operator
S_{R}
.
We construct two different decompositions of the force
f
: the Helmholtz decomposition into a divergence- and rotation-free component and a decomposition into components dependent on the velocity and pressure terms. We will then see under which assumptions these decompositions coincide.

Let us now assume that the viscosity
\eta
is constant in
\Omega
and define the vector spaces

V_{\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}:=\{\mathbf{w}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{w})=0,\ \mathbf{w}\cdot n=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\text{ in the sense of traces}\}

and

V_{\operatorname{curl}}(\Omega)^{d}:=\{\mathbf{w}\in{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\ :\ \exists\,r\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times d}\ \text{with}\ \mathbf{w}=-\nabla r\}

with
\left(V_{\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}\right)^{\perp}=V_{\operatorname{curl}}(\Omega)^{d}
in
{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
.
There exists an
r\in{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
, so that the decomposition via the Helmholtz operator
P_{\mathrm{helm}}:{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\rightarrow V_{\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}
is given by

\mathbf{f}=P_{\operatorname{div}}\mathbf{f}-\nabla r,

where
P_{\operatorname{div}}\mathbf{f}\in V_{\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}
is the divergence-free (or solenoidal) and
-\nabla r\in V_{\operatorname{curl}}(\Omega)^{d}
rotation-free (or curl-free) component of the force.
Using the coercivity of
a(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})
shown in (
8
) and the incompressibility condition of
\mathbf{v}
we have

\begin{split}\alpha\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}&\leq|a(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})|=|F(\mathbf{v})|\leq\|P_{\operatorname{div}}\mathbf{f}\|_{{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{{H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}},\end{split}

where
(\nabla r,\mathbf{v})=0
since
\mathbf{v}\in\left({H^{1}_{\|}(\Omega)^{d}}\cap V_{\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}\right)
.
This provides the continuous dependence of
\mathbf{v}
on the divergence-free component of the force.

We now want to check if the divergence-free component of the force is equal to the term containing
\mathbf{v}
and the irrotational component is equal to the term containing
p
.
The Stokes equation is decomposed into terms, one depending on the velocity and one depending on the pressure, so that
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}^{({\mathbf{v}})}+\mathbf{f}^{({p})}
, where

\mathbf{f}^{(\mathbf{v})}:=-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\big)\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathbf{f}^{(p)}:=\nabla p.

Since the rotation of a gradient-field is always zero, it follows directly that

\nabla\times\mathbf{f}^{(p)}=\nabla\times\left(\nabla p\right)=0
.
In contrast, the divergence of the velocity term can be rewritten, that is

(15)

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}^{(\mathbf{v})}=-\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\left(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\right)\right)=-\left(2\nabla\eta\cdot\Delta\mathbf{v}+D(\mathbf{v}):\nabla(\nabla\eta)\right).

A detailed calculation is provided in Lemma
A.1
.

Equation (
15
) is zero, if
\nabla\eta=0
, e.g. if the viscosity
\eta
is constant.
Then,
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}^{(\mathbf{v})}
is zero and the separation of the velocity and pressure dependent terms of the Stokes equation coincides with the Helmholtz decomposition, so that
\mathbf{f}^{(\mathbf{v})}
and
\mathbf{f}^{(p)}
are the divergence-free and irrotational components respectively. If considering solely irrotational components, one can use a stream function representation resulting in a fourth-order equation for
\mathbf{v}
, e.g.
[
45
]
. We do not proceed in this direction, but instead will establish an inversion framework based on the original Stokes equations with incomplete data.

In the model of the actin network in the experiment’s droplet, the gradient of the viscosity corresponds to changes in filament density. In biological cells the density of actin filaments is highly dependent on the cell type and function. However, a typical actin concentration in the experimentally observed droplets is only
12\mu M
and the volume it takes up is negligible compared to the surrounding fluid.
We can therefore assume for the experimental data considered here that
\nabla\eta\approx 0
.

If the pressure
p
is given, it suffices to formulate a reconstruction method for
\mathbf{f}^{(\mathbf{v})}
from measured data of the velocity field
\mathbf{v}^{\operatorname{meas}}
.
It is then possible to give a full reconstruction of
\mathbf{f}
using the gradient of
p
to recover
\mathbf{f}^{(p)}
.
Especially in the case of actomyosin droplets, i.e., when a confined fluid is considered, it is valid to assume that the divergence-free component is dominant and allows the determination of the most prominent part of the force
\mathbf{f}
.

Remark 4.1

(Source inversion with incomplete data)
.

With the forward problems established, we now address the inverse source problem with incomplete data, referring to the fact that we try to reconstruct the force using only measured velocity and have no information about the pressure. In general, an incomplete data scenario can also arise in the context of statistical data
[
16
]
, imaging with partial measurement
[
42
]
, limited observation in inverse scattering
[
18
]
, radial source problems
[
26
]

as well as in limited angle tomography
[
12
,
17
]
.

5
Active force identification as an inverse source problem with incomplete data

We here consider both versions of the Stokes problem in parallel and define a general Stokes operator

\mathcal{S}\in\{\mathcal{S}_{R},\mathcal{S}_{D}\}.

Due to unavoidable measurement noise in experimental data, it is not a realistic assumption on the measured velocity field
\mathbf{v}^{\text{meas}}
to lie in
{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}
and we rather assume that it is set in
{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
. Additionally, we assume, that the force
\mathbf{f}
is in
{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}
to allow reconstructions on the boundary.

We define the embedding operator

E:\left({H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\to\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right)

and an operator as a composition of the Stokes operator and the embedding, that is

(16)

S:{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\rightarrow\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right),\quad\mathbf{f}\mapsto(E\circ\mathcal{S})(\mathbf{f}),

with

S_{R}:=E\circ\mathcal{S}_{R}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{D}:=E\circ\mathcal{S}_{D},

where
\mathcal{S}
is well-defined as we have shown in section
3
.
We will refer to the operator
S
as the
embedded Stokes operator
.

We define a linear bounded observation operator that only measures the velocity field

M:\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},\quad(\mathbf{v},p)\mapsto\mathbf{v}

and define the forward operator for the inverse problem

(17)

A:{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},\quad\mathbf{f}\mapsto\left(M\circ S\right)(\mathbf{f}),

with

(18)

A_{R}:=M\circ S_{R}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{D}:=M\circ S_{D}.

Lemma 5.1

(Well posedness of
A
)
.

The operator
A:{H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
that maps the force
\mathbf{f}
to the velocity component
v
of the Stokes problem defined in (
17
) is well-posed.

Proof.

The well-posedness of
\mathcal{S_{R}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{D}
are shown in Theorem
3.1
and
3.2
respectively.
Since the measurement operator
M
and the embedding operator

are linear, bounded,
A
as the composition of these is well-posed.
∎

5.1
Differentiability of the forward maps

We now derive the analytical tools that allow us to solve the inverse source problem in a stable manner, using a gradient-based regularization methods such as, in our case, the Landweber iteration. The core of these regularized reconstruction methods is the derivation of the derivative and its adjoint.
This section focuses on derivatives of the embedded Stokes operators
S_{R}
and
S_{D}
following by their adjoint derivation in Section
5.2
.
We note that this process was discussed for general elliptic parameter identification problems in
[
24
]
that can also be applied to the Stokes equation.

Lemma 5.2

(Fréchet derivative of
S_{R}
)
.

The mapping
S_{R}
is Fréchet differentiable. The derivative
{S_{R}}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{f}
is given by

S_{R}^{\prime}(\mathbf{f}):\mathcal{D}(S_{R})\to\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right),\quad\mathbf{h}\mapsto(\mathbf{\mathbf{w}},w_{p}),

where
\left(\mathbf{\mathbf{w}},w_{p}\right)
is the unique weak solution of the boundary value problem

(19)

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{w})\big)+\nabla w_{p}&=\mathbf{h}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}&=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\mathbf{w}_{\bot}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\\
\big(\eta(\nabla\mathbf{w})\mathbf{n}\big)_{\parallel}&=-\lambda\mathbf{w}_{\parallel}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

Proof.

Since
S_{R}
is linear and bounded, the linearzied Stokes equation (
19
) takes the same form as the original problem; also the Gâteaux and the Fréchet derivatives coincide.

∎

While the derivative
S_{R}^{\prime}
is identical to
S_{R}
for the Robin problem,
S_{D}
in the Dirichlet problem is affine-linear. We discuss it in the following.

Lemma 5.3

(Fréchet derivative of
S_{D}
)
.

The mapping
S_{D}
is Fréchet differentiable. The Fréchet derivative
S_{D}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{f}
is given by

(20)

S_{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{f}):\mathcal{D}(S_{D})\to\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right),\quad\mathbf{h}\mapsto(\mathbf{w},w_{p}),

where
\left(\mathbf{\mathbf{w}},w_{p}\right)
is the unique weak solution of the boundary value problem

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\mathbf{w})\big)+\nabla w_{p}&=\mathbf{h}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}&=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\mathbf{w}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

Proof.

The map
S_{D}
is affine-linear and bounded. The quantity
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\varphi_{p}):=S_{D}(\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{h})-S_{D}(\mathbf{f})-S_{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{f})\mathbf{h}
with
S_{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{f})
as defined in (
20
) solves

(21)

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\big)+\nabla\varphi_{p}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}&=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\boldsymbol{\varphi}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

Since (
21
) has a trivial solution,
S_{D}
is Fréchet differentiable with derivative as in (
20
).
∎

5.2
Adjoint operators

Now in the last step, we construct the adjoint operator for the forward maps
A_{R}
,
A_{D}
via that of
S_{R}
,
S_{D}
and the measurement operator
M
.
We begin with the Robin problem, in which the Fréchet derivative
S_{R}^{\prime}(\mathbf{f})
is identical to
S_{R}
.

Lemma 5.4

(Banach space adjoint of
S_{R}
)
.

The Banach adjoint operator of
S_{R}
is given by

S_{R}^{\#}:\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},\quad(\mathbf{w},w_{p})\mapsto\boldsymbol{\varphi},

where
\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\varphi_{p}\right)\in\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
solves the adjoint equation

(22)

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\big)-\nabla\varphi_{p}&=\mathbf{w}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}&=w_{p}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\bot}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\\
\big(\eta(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\mathbf{n}\big)_{\parallel}&=-\lambda\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\parallel}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

Proof.

Take any
(\mathbf{w},w_{p})\in{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and any
\mathbf{h}\in{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
.
Let
(\mathbf{v},p):=S_{R}(\mathbf{h})
and
\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\varphi_{p}\right)
as in (
22
).
Our aim is to show that the following identity holds

(23)

\langle(\mathbf{w},w_{p}),S_{R}(\mathbf{h})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}}+\langle w_{p},p\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{h}\rangle=\langle S_{R}^{\#}(\mathbf{w},w_{p}),\mathbf{h}\rangle_{{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}}.

We proceed by rewriting the right-hand side of (
23
). Firstly, integration-by-part yields

(24)

\begin{split}\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},-\nabla\cdot(\eta D(\mathbf{v}))\rangle&=\int_{\Omega}\eta\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}:D(\mathbf{v})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{T}(\eta D(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\eta D(\boldsymbol{\varphi}):D(\mathbf{v})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{||}^{T}\mathbf{v}_{||}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
&=\langle\mathbf{v},-\nabla\cdot(\eta D(\boldsymbol{\varphi}))\rangle,\end{split}

where the second equality is obtained with symmetries of
D(\mathbf{v})
and
D(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
.
Secondly, using the divergence theorem we have

(25)

\begin{split}\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\nabla p\rangle=-\int_{\Omega}p(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{T}(p\mathbf{n})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\langle-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi},p\rangle,\end{split}

where
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{T}(p\mathbf{n})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0
as
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\bot}=\mathbf{0}
.
The incompressibility of the velocity
\mathbf{v}
allows us to introduce
\varphi_{p}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and together with the divergence theorem gives

(26)

0=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v},\varphi_{p})=-(\mathbf{v},\nabla\varphi_{p}).

Summing up (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
) leads to

(27)

\begin{split}\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{h}\rangle&=\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},-\nabla\cdot(\eta D(\mathbf{v}))-\nabla p\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v},-\nabla\cdot(\eta D(\boldsymbol{\varphi}))-\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle+\langle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi},p\rangle\\
&=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle+\langle p,w_{p}\rangle.\end{split}

which is the left-hand side of (
23
), and the proof is complete.
∎

For the version of the Stokes equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions
S_{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{f})
, the calculation of the Banach adjoint is analogous to Lemma
5.4
and we omit the proof.

where
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\varphi_{p})\in\left({L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\times L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
solves the adjoint equation

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot\big(\eta D(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\big)-\nabla\varphi_{p}&=\mathbf{w}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}&=w_{p}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\

We can now relate the Banach adjoints to the Hilbert space adjoints via the Riesz isomorphism:

Lemma 5.6

(Hilbert space adjoint of
S{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{f})

The Hilbert space adjoint

meaning that

Proof.

Similarity to

∎

6
Numerical results

Finally we apply our results to evaluate synthetic data from simulations as well as measured data from experiments for both actomyosin systems.

The simulations were conducted using the Finite Element library
NGSolve

[
58
,
59
]
. The discretization method used is based on a H(div)-conforming finite element space and a Hybrid Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) formulation of the viscous forces using a method proposed in
[
34
]
and
[
35
]
.
The force was reconstructed from the velocity data using the Landweber iteration method introduced by Landweber in
[
32
]
for the linear and Hanke, Neubauer and Scherzer in
[
23
]
for the non-linear case, which in our setting reads as

(31)

\mathbf{f}_{k+1}=\mathbf{f}_{k}-sA^{*}\left(A(\mathbf{f}_{k})-\mathbf{v}^{\operatorname{meas}}\right),\ \ k=1,2,3,\dots

with constant step size
s>0
.
This is the regularized reconstruction based on the reduced formulation of the forward operator; alternatively one can consider an all-at-one setting
[
29
,
46
]
or a bi-level framework
[
47
,
43
]
, and apply faster regularization techniques such as, e.g., the Nesterov-Landweber method
[
44
]
, or sequential subspace optimization
[
10
,
68
]
. We will denote the reconstructed force as
\mathbf{f}^{*}
.

To ensure stability under noise known as regularization guarantee, the iteration was terminated a posteriori using the discrepancy principle as in
[
23
]
. More precisely, we stop the iteration at the first time that the residual
r_{k}:=\left\|A(\mathbf{f}_{k})-\mathbf{v}^{\text{meas}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
fulfills
r_{k}\leq\tau\delta
with tolerance
\tau>1
and noise level
\delta>0
.
Regarding an initial guess, we use the direct evaluation of the velocity term in the Stokes equation with

(32)

\mathbf{f}_{0}:=-\nabla\cdot\eta D(\mathbf{v}^{\text{meas}}).

We directly evaluate the velocity term of the Stokes equation and
\mathbf{f}_{0}
therefore acts as a naive reconstruction.
As described in section
4
, the measured velocity
\mathbf{v}^{\operatorname{meas}}
is obtained from the raw video data using PIV.
Here, the flow is obtained from averaging over the length of the microscopy image series instead of only comparing a small subset of subsequent frames. While this leads to smoother input data
\mathbf{v}^{\operatorname{meas}}
, the naive reconstruction
\mathbf{f}_{0}
can still be relatively poor depending on the numerical stability of the used differentiation algorithm as we will see in the next section.

The constant step-size

was determined prior to the iteration using backtracking line search (bls).
The full algorithm is shown in detail in Algorithm

.

7
Discussion and outlook

The aim of this work is to provide mathematical tools to identify forces exerted by an active actomyosin network in a fluid.
We used the Stokes equation together with an incompressibility condition and suitable boundary value conditions to reflect the physical setting to describe the fluid flow, which is driven by the activity of the actomyosin network. The respective force appears as a source term in the Stokes equation.
To this end, we formulated a mathematical framework based on the assumption that the fluid can be considered Newtonian. The analysis reveals that only the divergence-free part of the force field
\mathbf{f}
can be reconstructed from the knowledge of
\mathbf{v}
for a known viscosity, which was also demonstrated in numerical experiments for synthetic data with a known ground truth. Furthermore, we have been able to extend our regularization algorithm to evaluate experimental data.

Our findings thus complement a class of applied inverse source problems that aim at identifying forces, for example traction force microscopy, see, e.g.,
[
3
,
61
]
,
where the forces that a cell exerts on an elastic substrate are to be reconstructed to obtain insights into cell mechanics. The underlying mathematical problem is an inverse source problem for the elastic substrate, see
[
57
]
. Inverse source problems for force identification also arise in applications besides cell physics, e.g., in defect localization in composites
[
9
]
or in elastodynamics in seismology
[
5
]
.

In the future, to analyze actual three-dimensional droplets and to understand the development of active forces when the actomyosin network evolves, we will extend our model to incorporate the inherent time-dependence of actomyosin systems, and apply suitable regularization techniques. In addition, our framework will be adapted to the respective data acquisition process that is typically limited to imaging the system only within the focal plain, leading to even more restrictions in data availability.
The full reconstruction of the active force
\mathbf{f}
inside of an actual cell requires knowledge of the viscosity
\eta
and the pressure
p
. In further studies the aim is to substitute these quantities with approximations using experimental data and thus reconstruct the rotation-free part as an additional inverse problem.
