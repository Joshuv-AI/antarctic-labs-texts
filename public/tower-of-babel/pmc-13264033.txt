Scalp cooling outcomes in patients receiving trastuzumab deruxtecan for metastatic breast cancer☆
Authors: E Salehi, X Chu, EL Mayer, S Singer, G Augdahl, M Stalteri, T Li, KE Dibble, N Sinclair, M Faggen, J Walsh, S Sammons, K Sendrick, S Hanna, A Mostaghimi, N Tayob, NR LeBoeuf, SM Tolaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) has improved outcomes for many patients with metastatic breast cancer (MBC). T-DXd is associated with alopecia, yet limited data exist describing the efficacy of scalp cooling (SC) in preventing alopecia among patients receiving T-DXd.Patients and methodsThis prospective, phase II trial evaluated the efficacy of SC among patients with MBC receiving T-DXd. Conducted at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, the trial enrolled patients with MBC without alopecia at baseline, initiating treatment with T-DXd. Participants elected either the SC arm, using the Paxman Scalp Cooling System, or the non-SC arm. The primary endpoint was hair loss rate, defined as locally assessed Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0) grade ≥1 alopecia occurring at cycle 3, day 1; cycle 5, day 1; or end of treatment, whichever occurred first.ResultsAmong 40 enrolled evaluable patients, 33 (82.5%) patients [18 (90%) in the SC arm and 15 (75%) in the non-SC arm] experienced grade ≥1 alopecia. The difference in rates between arms was not statistically significant (P = 0.41). Grade 2 alopecia rates were the same in both arms (55%). Centralized image review revealed no significant differences in grade ≥1 (P = 1.00) or grade 2 alopecia (P = 0.33) between treatment arms. Grade 2 alopecia was the primary reason for SC discontinuation (45%). The median time to grade 2 alopecia was 2.76 months [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.64-NA] in the SC arm; 4.60 months (95% CI, 2.53-NA) in the non-SC arm (P = 0.7).ConclusionsSC with T-DXd did not reduce alopecia rates. Small sample size and lack of randomization may have limited the interpretation. Further studies are crucial to improve SC’s efficacy with antibody-drug conjugate therapy.Key words: alopecia, antibody-drug conjugates, metastatic breast cancer, scalp cooling, trastuzumab deruxtecan

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced alopecia is one of the most common and psychologically devastating adverse events experienced by patients being treated for breast cancer.1, 2, 3, 4 In addition to traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy, other anticancer agents can cause alopecia, including the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-directed antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd). In the DESTINY-Breast series of clinical trials, T-DXd-induced alopecia of grade 1 or higher was reported in approximately 29%-48% of patients with metastatic breast cancer (MBC) who received T-DXd.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Thus, it is important to identify interventions that can reduce the incidence of T-DXd-induced alopecia to help patients select and adhere to this highly effective therapy.

Scalp cooling (SC) is often used to prevent or reduce the severity of chemotherapy-induced alopecia.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 The efficacy varies by type of systemic therapy, with better results observed among patients receiving taxane versus anthracycline-based chemotherapy. In the randomized SCALP trial in patients with early-stage breast cancer, SC achieved hair preservation in 59% of patients receiving taxane-based chemotherapy versus 16% receiving anthracycline-based chemotherapy.16 Similarly, in the Dutch Scalp Cooling Registry, SC was more effective among patients who received taxanes (78%) compared with those who received anthracyclines (40%) or anthracyclines and taxanes (45%).17 To date, SC has not been evaluated in patients receiving T-DXd, which consists of the anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody trastuzumab bound to the topoisomerase I inhibitor deruxtecan (DXd) via a cleavable tetrapeptide-based linker with a drug-to-antibody ratio of 8:1.18

We conducted a prospective, open-label phase II trial of SC versus no SC among patients with MBC receiving T-DXd at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This was a prospective, open-label, phase II trial (clinical trial registration number: NCT04986579) that enrolled patients with MBC who did not have alopecia at baseline and who were initiating treatment with T-DXd at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI). Patients with known scalp metastases were not eligible. Additional exclusion criteria included any contraindications to SC, such as cold agglutinin disease, cold urticaria, or lupus. Participants consented in writing to participate in either the SC arm or the non-SC arm. The DFCI Institutional Review Board approved the trial (21-169).

SC

Participants in the SC arm underwent SC using the Paxman Scalp Cooling System with each dose of T-DXd. Regardless of the specific device, the post-cooling time is highly variable, depending on the institution, drug, and manufacturer. Due to the long half-life of T-DXd, we elected a 90-minute post-cooling time.

The participants underwent SC until the development of grade 2 alopecia, until they stopped receiving T-DXd (due to toxicity or disease progression), until provider or patient withdrawal of consent, or until they reached 6 months on the trial, whichever occurred first. Participants who experienced grade 2 alopecia could opt to continue to undergo SC using the Paxman Scalp Cooling System off-study.

Endpoints and statistical considerations

The primary endpoint was hair loss rate, which was defined as locally assessed Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5.0) grade ≥1 alopecia occurring at cycle 3, day 1 (C3D1), cycle 5, day 1 (C5D1), or end of treatment (EOT), whichever occurred first. Secondary outcomes included the impact of alopecia on quality of life, assessed using the Chemotherapy-Induced Alopecia Distress Scale (CADS)19 and Body Image Scale (BIS)20 at baseline, C3D1, C5D1, and EOT.

The study aimed to enroll 20 participants per arm to provide at least 80% power to detect a 28% decrease in the rate of alopecia between the SC and the non-SC arms (8% versus 36%) using an exact difference in proportions test (one-sided type I error 10%).

Central assessment of alopecia

To ensure consistent and unbiased assessment of treatment-induced alopecia, a centralized image review process was implemented. High-resolution scalp photographs were collected at predefined study time points, including baseline, C3D1, C5D1, and EOT. A panel of two independent, board-certified dermatologists (with a third reviewer to break ties), blinded to treatment arms, assessed each image set and estimated the percent loss of hair from baseline. Hair loss was graded using this part of the CTCAE version 5.0 alopecia definition, where >50% loss is considered grade 2. Patient-reported components of CTCAE were not included in this assessment.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographic and clinical characteristics by treatment arm (SC versus non-SC). Differences in characteristics between arms were evaluated using Fisher’s exact tests for categorical and Wilcoxon rank-sum continuous variables.

For efficacy analysis, alopecia grade per CTCAE criteria was tabulated by treatment arm, and the proportion of patients within each grade was reported. The median duration of chemotherapy treatment (in months and cycles) was calculated overall, by grade, and treatment arm. For patients in the SC arm, the median number of chemotherapy cycles received while undergoing SC was also calculated by alopecia grade. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the alopecia grades between the two arms.

Reasons for discontinuation of SC were summarized in the SC arm, with proportions reported by category. SC-related toxicities were also tabulated descriptively. Time-to-event analysis was conducted to assess the time to onset of alopecia (defined as the first occurrence of grade ≥1 or grade 2 alopecia). Median time to alopecia and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated, and between-arm comparisons were made using the log-rank test. In the SC arm, the total duration of SC treatment was calculated in months. Additionally, for the centralized review result, the first occurrence of grades ≥1 and 2 alopecia was summarized by treatment arm and time point. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess between-arm differences across time points.

Quality of life was assessed using two validated instruments: the CADS score and the BIS score. Both scores were analyzed using the same statistical approach. The distribution of scores was summarized at multiple time points—baseline, C3D1, C5D1, and EOT—for each treatment arm. Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, standard deviation, and range, were reported. Between-arm differences at each time point were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Within each treatment arm, changes in scores over time were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Additional analyses were conducted based on prior chemotherapy exposures, prior endocrine therapy (ET), prior SC use, and prior cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitor use. Between-arm comparisons within these groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test at each time point. For individual questionnaire items, response rates for ‘quite a bit’ or ‘very much’ categories were tabulated at each time point by treatment arm, with corresponding 95% CI calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Patient characteristics

Forty patients were enrolled, including 20 in the SC arm and 20 in the non-SC arm. The median age at enrollment was 55 in the SC arm and 63 in the non-SC arm (P < 0.001). There were no other significant differences between arms in terms of baseline demographic or disease characteristics. Participants had received a variety of prior treatments in the metastatic setting, including ET (67.5%), chemotherapy (87.5%), and CDK4/6 inhibitors (70.0%). Notably, 17.5% of participants had received prior SC, including six participants in the SC arm (30.0%) and one in the non-SC arm (5.0%; P = 0.09; Table 1).

Efficacy of SC

Alopecia of grade ≥1 was experienced by 33 participants, including 18 (90%) in the SC arm and 15 (75%) in the non-SC arm (P = 0.41). In the SC arm, 7 patients (35.0%) experienced grade 1 alopecia and 11 (55.0%) experienced grade 2 alopecia. In the non-SC arm, 4 patients (20.0%) experienced grade 1 alopecia and 11 (55.0%) experienced grade 2 alopecia (Table 2). Centralized image review revealed no significant differences in grade ≥1 alopecia (P = 1.00) or grade 2 alopecia (P = 0.33) between the SC and non-SC arms (Table 3; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769). Grade 2 alopecia was the primary reason for SC discontinuation (n = 9/20 patients; 45%; Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769). The median time to grade 2 alopecia was 2.76 months (95% CI, 1.64-NA) in the SC arm and 4.60 months (95% CI, 2.53-NA) in the non-SC arm (P = 0.70; Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769).

Quality of life

The median CADS scores trended upward from baseline to EOT in the SC arm (baseline: 3.50; C3D1: 7.22; C5D1: 9.00; EOT: 11.5) and were variable in the non-SC arm (baseline: 3.00; C3D1: 5.00; C5D1: 2.56; EOT: 6.50). There were no statistically significant differences in CADS scores between treatment arms at any timepoint (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769). Among patients in the SC arm, the distribution of CADS scores was higher during cycle 5 (median score, 3.50 versus 9.00; P = 0.03) and at EOT (median score, 3.00 versus 11.5; P = 0.02) compared with baseline. Among patients in the non-SC arm, the distribution of CADS scores was higher during Cycle 3 than at baseline (median score, 2.59 versus 5.00; P = 0.04; Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769).

The median BIS scores trended upward in both the SC arm (baseline: 3.00; C3D1: 8.00; C5D1: 9.00; EOT: 11.5) and the non-SC arm (baseline: 3.00; C3D1: 5.00; C5D1: 5.00; EOT: 9.00). There were no statistically significant differences in BIS scores between treatment arms at any timepoint (Supplementary Table S6 and Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769). Among patients in the SC arm, the distribution of BIS scores was higher during cycle 5 (median score, 3.00 versus 9.00; P = 0.01) and at EOT (median score, 1.00 versus 11.50; P = 0.03) compared with baseline and during cycle 5 compared with cycle 3 (median score, 8.00 versus 9.00; P = 0.02; Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769).

Among all patients, there were no differences in the distributions of CADS or BIS scores based on receipt of prior chemotherapy (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769), receipt of prior ET (Supplementary Tables S10 and S11, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769), or prior SC use (Supplementary Tables S12 and S13, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769) at any time point. There were no differences in the distributions of CADS scores based on receipt of prior CDK4/6 inhibitors at any time point (Supplementary Table S14, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769). The distribution of BIS scores was significantly lower at EOT among patients who received a prior CDK4/6 inhibitor (n = 29) compared with those who did not receive a prior CDK4/6 inhibitor (n = 11; median score 4.00 versus 12.50, P = 0.03; Supplementary Table S15, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769). Responses to CADS and BIS instruments at each time point and for each treatment group are included in Supplementary Tables S16-S23, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107769.

Discussion

To date, SC is the only intervention that has been shown to reduce the rate of chemotherapy-induced alopecia in patients with cancer.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 SC induces vasoconstriction, which decreases the amount of chemotherapy taken up into follicular cells, thus reducing hair loss. Colder temperatures also reduce the metabolic activity of follicular cells, which lessens the effect of cytotoxic chemotherapy on hair.21,22

In this prospective, open-label trial, we found that SC using the Paxman Scalp Cooling System did not prevent the development of grade ≥1 alopecia among patients with MBC receiving T-DXd. Grade 1 alopecia affected 35% and 20% of patients on the SC and non-SC arms, respectively, and grade 2 alopecia affected 55% of patients on each arm. Moreover, there were no significant differences in either the primary endpoint (grade ≥1 alopecia) or grade 2 alopecia between the SC and non-SC arms observed on central review. Notably, we observed higher rates of alopecia than those seen in the DESTINY-Breast02 trial in MBC, in which 27% of participants on the T-DXd arm experienced grade 1 alopecia and 10% experienced grade 2 alopecia, highlighting the need for further study of factors contributing to chemotherapy-induced alopecia development.6 To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the efficacy of SC for the prevention of alopecia among patients with MBC receiving T-DXd.

In the randomized SCALP trial, 142 patients receiving neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy for early-stage breast cancer were randomized 2:1 to receive SC (n = 119) using the Paxman Scalp Cooling System or no SC (n = 63). Among the total study population, 51 (36%) received anthracycline-based chemotherapy, and 91 (64%) received taxane-based chemotherapy. The primary outcome was successful hair preservation, which was defined as CTCAE v4.0 alopecia of grade 0-1. The rate of hair preservation was 50.5% in the SC group and 0% in the no SC group (P < 0.001). SC was more effective among patients receiving taxane-based chemotherapy compared with anthracycline-based chemotherapy (59% versus 16%). Notably, there were no significant differences in patient-reported quality of life measures between the SC and non-SC groups at any time point.16 The efficacy of SC was also evaluated in the Dutch Scalp Cooling Registry, which found that SC was more effective among patients who received taxanes (78%) compared with those who received anthracyclines (40%) or anthracyclines and taxanes (45%).17

The variable efficacy of SC for preventing alopecia may be affected by the mechanisms of action of different systemic therapies. T-DXd and anthracyclines induce cell death by inhibiting the functions of topoisomerase I and II, respectively. DXd, the payload of T-DXd, is a topoisomerase I (TOP1) inhibitor that stabilizes DNA topoisomerase I cleavable complexes.23,24 Anthracyclines inhibit the catalytic function of topoisomerase II (TOP2) by intercalating into DNA and stabilizing the TOP2 covalent complexes, thus trapping the enzyme and preventing religation of TOP2-mediated DNA double-strand breaks.25,26 In contrast, taxanes inhibit cell division by binding to tubulin and preventing microtubule disassembly.27 SC reduces the metabolic activity of follicular cells, thus reducing the rate of cell division. This mechanism would, in turn, lessen the impact of taxanes on follicular cells but possibly have less effect on the impact of a TOP1/2 inhibitor. Moreover, differences in the half-life of systemic therapies could affect the efficacy of SC. The terminal half-life of doxorubicin is 20-48 hours28 compared with 5.5-6 days for T-DXd.29 Accordingly, there is a much longer duration of tissue exposure to T-DXd compared with doxorubicin after the end of each SC session. This extended exposure raises concerns about the potential impact of SC if the exposure is much shorter than the half-life of the agent.

In the present study, patient-reported quality of life measures using the CADS and BIS did not differ between the SC and non-SC groups at any time point. This was similar to the results observed in the randomized SCALP study, even though SC was effective in preventing alopecia.16 Systemic therapies, including chemotherapy and ADCs like T-DXd, can cause a wide variety of side effects in addition to alopecia. Cancer can also cause pain and other physical symptoms, and a diagnosis of cancer can be psychologically devastating. The findings from our study suggest that mitigation of alopecia may not be sufficient to improve the quality of life among MBC patients receiving systemic therapy.

In our study, the SC arm had a greater representation of patients who had undergone prior SC compared with the non-SC arm (30% versus 5%), though this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.09). This implies that patients in this study who had undergone prior SC had either achieved some benefit or at least felt comfortable enough with the strategy to try it again.

Our study has several limitations, including a small sample size and the lack of randomization between the SC and non-SC arms, which may limit the generalizability of results.

Larger studies would be crucial to further investigate both in preclinical and clinical settings and improve the efficacy of SC in the prevention of ADC-induced alopecia. Overall, substantial progress has been made in alleviating the systemic burden of chemotherapy in MBC, but more studies are needed to continue this journey.
