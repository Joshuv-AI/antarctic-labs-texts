Circuit-based chatacterization of finite-temperature quantum phases and self-correcting quantum memory
Ruochen Ma, Vedika Khemani, Shengqi Sang

Abstract
Quantum phases at zero temperature can be characterized as equivalence classes under local unitary transformations: two ground states within a gapped phase can be transformed into each other via a local unitary circuit. We generalize this circuit-based characterization of phases to systems at finite-temperature thermal equilibrium described by Gibbs states. We construct a channel circuit that approximately transforms one Gibbs state into another provided the two are connected by a path in parameter space along which a certain correlation-decay condition holds. For finite-dimensional systems of linear size L L and approximation error ϵ \epsilon , the locality of the circuit is Polylog ⁡ ( Poly ⁡ ( L ) / ϵ ) {\rm Polylog}({\rm Poly}(L)/\epsilon) . The correlation-decay condition, which we specify, is expected to be satisfied in the interior of many noncritical thermal phases, including those displaying discrete symmetry breaking and topological order. As an application, we show that any system in the same thermal phase as a zero-temperature topological code coherently preserves quantum information for a macroscopically long time, establishing self-correction as a universal property of thermal phases. As part of the proof, we provide explicit encoding and decoding channel circuits to encode information into, and decode it from, a system in thermal equilibrium.



I
Introduction

Over the last several decades, significant progress has been made in understanding zero-temperature quantum phases of gapped local Hamiltonians. This progress covers both the usual symmetry-breaking phases in Landau’s picture and many exotic phases with topological or fracton order
McGreevy 2023
;
Wen 2017
;
Nandkishore and Hermele 2019
. In this context, a central role is played by a circuit-based definition of phases inspired by quantum information theory: two pure states are in the same phase if there exists a shallow-depth local unitary circuit connecting them
Chen et al. 2010
. This definition underlies most discussions of the classification and characterization of zero-temperature quantum phases.

Recently, thanks to improved experimental control of devices and other platforms
Preskill 2018
, the study of quantum phases has shifted to open systems described by mixed states. Following the paradigm set by ground-state quantum phase studies, an analogous circuit-based definition has been proposed: two mixed states are in the same phase if they are related by a pair of local quantum channel circuits
Coser and Pérez-García 2019
;
Ma and Wang 2023
;
Hastings 2011
, hereafter referred to as two-way connectivity. Based on this, several classification schemes have been proposed
Ma and Wang 2023
;
Rakovszky et al. 2024
;
Sohal and Prem 2025
;
Ellison and Cheng 2025
;
Chen and Grover 2024a
;
Chen and Grover 2024b
, and concrete examples of nontrivial phases have been constructed
de Groot et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Lessa et al. 2025a
;
Lessa et al. 2025b
;
Bao et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Lu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Sala et al. 2025
;
Lessa et al. 2025c
.

Gibbs states describing systems in thermal equilibrium are among the most ubiquitous mixed states in nature. It nevertheless remains unclear whether the circuit-based characterization is applicable to Gibbs states and the thermal phases formed by them, where the latter is traditionally defined as a region of parameter space in which the free energy is analytic. To recap, for ground states, the circuit connecting states within a phase is furnished by quasi-adiabatic continuation
Hastings and Wen 2005
;
Hastings 2010
, which exists whenever the energy gap does not close. For Gibbs states, an analogous circuit construction that transforms Gibbs states within the same thermal phase is lacking, and it is unclear what the counterpart of energy gap should be.

In this work, we present a general construction of local channel circuits that transform Gibbs states within the same thermal phase into one another (See Fig.
2
for an illustration). More specifically, for a system of linear size
L
, we construct a
{\rm Polylog}({\rm Poly}L/\epsilon)
-local channel circuit that approximately transforms one Gibbs state into another with error
\epsilon
, provided that they are connected by a path in the parameter space along which a correlation-decay condition, dubbed
stable clustering
(briefly described below and formally defined in Def.
2
), is satisfied. Under an additional mild assumption on the density of states at low energy, the construction continues to work in the zero-temperature limit.

Colloquially speaking, a Gibbs state
\rho\propto e^{-H}
satisfies stable clustering if its neighboring Gibbs states of the form
\rho^{\prime}\propto e^{-H-\delta H}
all display clustering of correlations when
\delta H
is a small perturbation that is a sum of local terms (temperature is absorbed into
H
and
\delta H
). Here, by ‘clustering of correlations’ we mean that the connected correlation function between any local observable
O_{1}
, supported in region
A
in Fig.
2
, and any other, possibly nonlocal observable
O_{2}
, supported in region
C
, decays exponentially with the distance between their spatial supports. If the Hamiltonians under consideration satisfy a global (0-form) symmetry, then
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
range only over operators respecting the symmetry.
Based on intuition from statistical mechanics, stable clustering is expected to hold in the interiors of a broad class of thermal phases, including those displaying discrete symmetry-breaking order

and quantum topological order. Violations of the condition are expected near finite- and zero-temperature phase-transition points due to the presence of long-range correlations.

A notable feature of our construction is that the channel circuit satisfies
local reversibility
(formally defined in Def.
3
), which intuitively means that each channel gate’s action does not disturb long-distance correlations in the state at that time, and therefore can be reversed locally. Locally reversible channel circuits were proposed in Ref.
Sang et al. 2025
as a generalization of local unitary circuits to open quantum systems. Among other consequences, this implies the existence of a reversal circuit that transforms the final state back to the initial state, thereby connecting to the two-way connectivity definition of mixed-state phases. It also guarantees that the circuit preserves sharp features of the state, including symmetries and their anomalies
Sang et al. 2025
.

As an application of the construction, we prove a connection between self-correcting quantum memory and quantum thermal phases. A Hamiltonian system is called a self-correcting quantum memory if it is capable of preserving quantum information for a macroscopically long time when coupled to a low-temperature bath. Prominent examples include toric codes in
D\geq 4
dimensions
Dennis et al. 2002
;
Alicki et al. 2008
. At first sight, this property relies on both the system’s Hamiltonian and the specific form of the system–environment interaction. Therefore, it is
a priori
unclear whether (a) self-correction can be inferred from the steady state being a low-temperature Gibbs state alone, and (b) if it is a universal property shared by other systems in the same thermal phase. We answer both questions affirmatively. For systems in the same thermal phase as a zero-temperature topological code, we show that any such system acts as a self-correcting quantum memory with an
\exp(L^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)})
memory time (Theorem
3
). As part of the proof, we construct channels that encode and decode quantum information into and from the system at finite-temperature equilibrium.

For Hamiltonians composed of commuting local terms, we can relax the stable clustering condition to a clustering condition only and obtain an improved construction: if two Gibbs states are connected by a path along which clustering (rather than stable clustering) holds, then there exists an
{\mathcal{O}}({\rm Polylog}({\rm Poly}L/\epsilon))
-local Lindbladian evolution that approximately transforms one state into the other (Theorem
4
). We expect a similar relaxation to be possible for the noncommuting case as well and leave it for future work.

We note that our results build on several recent advances in quantum information theory. In particular, we use the result, proved in
Chen and Rouzé 2025
, that the Gibbs state of a local Hamiltonian is approximately Markovian, and that, under clustering of correlations, a local perturbation of the Hamiltonian affects only a nearby region of a Gibbs state. Work on Glauber dynamics for the classical Ising model
Gheissari and Sinclair 2021
, although not phrased explicitly in this manner, also suggests that the symmetry-broken phase of the Ising model possesses some form of circuit connectivity. We further note that a recent work on self-correcting quantum memories studies related questions in the context of stabilizer Hamiltonians, particularly the 4D toric code
Bergamaschi et al. 2025
.

II
Setup and Definitions

We consider a system on a
D
-dimensional regular lattice, where
\Lambda
denotes the set of all sites. For any subset
X\subset\Lambda
, let
|X|
denote the number of sites in
X
. For two disjoint subsets
X
and
Y
, we define the distance
d(X,Y)
as the length of the shortest path between them. For an operator
O
,
\left\|{O}\right\|
and
\left\|{O}\right\|_{1}
denote its operator norm and trace norm, respectively.

We focus on systems with local interactions in thermal equilibrium, described by the Gibbs states:

\rho_{\bm{\beta}}:=\frac{e^{-H_{\bm{\beta}}}}{{\rm Tr}(e^{-H_{{\bm{\beta}}}})},\quad\quad H_{{\bm{\beta}}}:={\bm{\beta}}\cdot{\bf h}=\sum_{Z}\beta_{Z}h_{Z}

(1)

where
{\bf h}=\{h_{Z}\}
is a list of local interaction terms with
\Omega(L^{D})
elements. Each
h_{Z}
acts on a region
Z\subset\Lambda
with
\operatorname{diam}(Z)\leq R
and
\|h_{X}\|\leq 1
. The inverse temperature of
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
is defined as
|{\bm{\beta}}|_{\infty}:=\sup_{Z}|\beta_{Z}|
, in agreement with the literature (e.g.,
Chen and Rouzé 2025
).

We henceforth assume a fixed set of interactions
{\bf h}
, and use
{\mathcal{A}}
to denote the operator algebra generated by
{\bf h}
.
{\mathcal{A}}
specifies all the nonspatial symmetries of the system: an operator
g
is a symmetry if and only if
g
commutes with all elements of
{\bf h}
(and
{\mathcal{A}}
). For a subregion
X\subseteq\Lambda
, we use
{\mathcal{A}}_{X}
to denote the algebra generated by elements of
{\rm Tr}_{\bar{X}}{\mathcal{A}}
. We observe that
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}^{X}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{X}
for any
{\bm{\beta}}
.

We now formally define the clustering of correlations condition used in this work. For a given operator algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and a state
\rho\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we define the covariance of two disjoint subsets
X,Y\subset\Lambda
as:

{\rm{Cov}}^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rho}(X,Y):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}O_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{X}:\|O_{1}\|=1\\
O_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{Y}:\|O_{2}\|=1\end{subarray}}|\braket{O_{1}O_{2}}_{\rho}-\braket{O_{1}}_{\rho}\braket{O_{2}}_{\rho}|,

(2)

where
\braket{O}_{\rho}={\rm Tr}(O\rho)
.

Definition 1

(Clustering of correlations)
.

Let
\xi\geq 0
be a constant and
f(x)
be a function that grows at most polynomially with
x
. We say a state
\rho\in{\mathcal{A}}
exhibits
(\xi,f)
clustering if, for every annulus tripartition in Fig.
2
, the following holds:

{\rm{Cov}}^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rho}(A,C)\leq f(L)e^{-d(A,C)/\xi},

(3)

We remark that this clustering condition is weaker than more common versions in the literature (e.g.,
Capel et al. 2025
) due to the restriction to operators from
{\mathcal{A}}_{A}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{C}
, as well as the particular choice of the A-B-C geometry illustrated in Fig.
2
. This makes it applicable to a large class of states with nontrivial orders. For instance, the Ising model below the critical temperature satisfies our definition of clustering, since local operators in
{\mathcal{A}}_{A/C}
respect the Ising symmetry and display exponentially decaying correlations, even though nonsymmetric ones have long-range correlations. As another example, topologically ordered ground states on a torus satisfy our notion of clustering, even though they have long-range correlations for non-annulus partitions (see, e.g.,
König and Pastawski 2014
, which shows that noncontractible loop operators display long-range correlations).

For Gibbs states, we introduce another concept dubbed
stable clustering
:

Definition 2

(Stable clustering)
.

A Gibbs state
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
(as defined in Eq.(
1
)) satisfies
(\delta,\xi,f)
stable clustering if, for any
\Delta{\bm{\beta}}
with
|\Delta{\bm{\beta}}|_{\infty}\leq\delta
, the Gibbs state
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}}
exhibits
(\xi,f)
clustering.

Henceforth, when we use the term ‘stable clustering’ without specifying
(\delta,\xi,f)
, it is assumed that
\delta,\xi={\mathcal{O}}(1)
and
f={\rm Poly}L
.

We expect stable clustering to be satisfied in the interior of certain classes of thermal phases. Here, ‘certain classes’ include topologically ordered phases, spontaneous symmetry-breaking (SSB) phases of discrete global symmetries (if
{\bf h}
includes only symmetric terms), and the high-temperature trivial phase.
Exceptions should include phases displaying SSB of continuous global symmetries or spatial symmetries; for the former, due to the presence of Goldstone modes leading to power-law-decaying correlations, and for the latter, since the perturbation
\Delta{\bm{\beta}}
can explicitly break spatial symmetry. Stable clustering is also expected to be violated at phase transitions, including both discontinuous (first order) and continuous (higher order) ones.

In open systems, local channel circuits are natural generalizations of unitary circuits. On a lattice
\Lambda
, a local channel circuit of depth
T
is a quantum channel of the form:

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}_{T}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\quad{\rm where}\quad{\mathcal{C}}_{t}={\prod}_{x}{\mathcal{E}}_{t,x}

(4)

where each
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
is called a layer of the circuit, and each
{\mathcal{E}}_{t,x}
is called a gate in layer
t
. Different gates within a layer have non-overlapping spatial support. The range of
{\mathcal{C}}
, denoted by
{\rm rng\,}{\mathcal{C}}
, is defined as
T
times the largest diameter of the supports of the
{\mathcal{E}}_{t,x}
.

The channel circuits we construct in later sections satisfy a strong property called local reversibility
Sang et al. 2025
, formally defined as:

Definition 3

(Local reversibility (LR))
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a channel circuit as defined above.
{\mathcal{C}}
is
\epsilon_{LR}
-locally reversible with respect to an input state
\rho
if, for each channel gate
{\mathcal{E}}_{t,x}
, there exists another channel
\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{t,x}
with the same spatial support as
{\mathcal{E}}_{t,x}
such that

\left\|{\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{t,x}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{t,x}\circ{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}[\rho]-{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}[\rho]}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{LR}

(5)

where
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}\cdots{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
represents the first
(t-1)
layers of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Intuitively, local reversibility means each channel gate’s action does not disturb long-range correlations of the state at that time and can therefore be reversed locally. This gate-by-gate reversibility, among other consequences, implies the existence of a reversal circuit:

\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}=\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{T-1}\circ\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{T}\quad{\rm where}\quad\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{t}={\prod}_{x}\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{t,x},

(6)

that reverses
{\mathcal{C}}
’s action on
\rho
:

\left\|{\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}\circ{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]-\rho}\right\|_{1}\leq|{\mathcal{C}}|\cdot\epsilon_{LR}.

(7)

Here
|{\mathcal{C}}|
denotes the total number of gates within
{\mathcal{C}}
. A proof of Eq.(
7
) can be found in App.
I
.

III
Constructing channel circuits connecting Gibbs states

We now state our first result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}
and
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}}
be two Gibbs states connected by a path in
{\bm{\beta}}
-space along which stable clustering is satisfied. Then there exists a
2\epsilon_{\mathcal{C}}
-locally reversible channel circuit
\mathcal{C}
with range
{\rm rng\,}{\mathcal{C}}={\rm Polylog}({\rm Poly}(L)/\epsilon_{\mathcal{C}})
, such that
\left\|{\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{C}}[\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}]}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{\mathcal{C}}
. Here
L
is the linear system size.

We remark that our construction and the error bound continue to work when one of (or both of)
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}
and
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}}
is (are) zero-temperature gapped ground state(s), if we impose some sparsity conditions on the density of states near ground states, similar to the condition introduced in
Hastings 2007
. We discuss this point in detail in Sec.
III.3
.

Before proving the theorem in the next two subsections, we review several results on which our proof is based.

The first result relates clustering to the property of
local perturbation perturbs locally
(LPPL). As its name suggests, LPPL concerns the extent to which a local change in the Hamiltonian modifies the expectation value of distant observables
evaluated in
the Gibbs state. The theorem is formally stated below. The proof of this result, which closely follows the proof of Theorem 25 in Ref.
Capel et al. 2025
, is included in App.
A
.

Lemma 1

(Clustering implies LPPL. Theorem 25 in Ref.
Capel et al. 2025
, modified)
.

Let
\rho(s)
be the Gibbs state corresponding to Hamiltonian
H(s)=H(0)+s\cdot h_{A}
, with
h_{A}
supported on a local region
A
. Then, for any region
C
non-overlapping with
A
and any
l<d(A,C)
, one has:

\begin{split}&\left\|{\rho^{C}(0)-\rho^{C}(1)}\right\|_{1}\\
\leq&\left\|{h_{A}}\right\|\cdot\left(\sup_{s\in[0,1]}{\rm{Cov}}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho(s)}(A_{+l};C)+6|A|e^{-\frac{1}{1+v/\pi}l}\right).\end{split}

(8)

where
A_{+l}
denotes the set of sites whose distance to
A
is no larger than
l
, and
v
is the maximum Lieb-Robinson velocity of
\{H(s)\}_{s\in[0,1]}
.

The second result states that Gibbs states of local Hamiltonians satisfy so-called approximate Markov property. The property is defined via quantum conditional mutual information (CMI). For any state defined on a tripartite system
ABC
, CMI is defined as:

I(A:C|B)_{\rho}:=I(A:BC)_{\rho}-I(A:B)_{\rho}

(9)

where
I(X:Y)_{\rho}
is the quantum mutual information, a measure of correlations between two parties. For Gibbs states on finite-dimensional lattices, we consider the annulus tripartition (Fig.
2
):
A
is a small simply connected region,
B
is a buffer region surrounding
A
, and
C
is the rest of the system. In this context, CMI measures the locality of correlations: small
I(A:C|B)
means that
A
’s correlations with the rest of the system can be well captured by the buffer
B
—a property called the Markov property. We remark that clustering and the Markov property are not directly related: there are systems displaying clustering but not the Markov property, and vice versa. The following theorem states that all Gibbs states satisfy an approximate Markov property locally:

Lemma 2

(Gibbs states are locally Markovian. Ref.
Chen and Rouzé 2025
)
.

For a Gibbs state
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
and any annular-shaped tripartition depicted in Fig.
2
, the CMI is bounded as:

\begin{split}I(A:C|B)_{\rho_{\bm{\beta}}}\leq|A|\cdot|C|e^{\kappa+\mu|A|-d(A,C)/\lambda},\end{split}

(10)

where
\kappa
,
\mu
, and
\lambda
are system-size-independent constants growing at most polynomially with
|{\bm{\beta}}|_{\infty}
.

We note that for any set of
{\bm{\beta}}
with
\max|{\bm{\beta}}|_{\infty}
bounded above, one can choose
\kappa,\mu,\lambda={\rm Poly}(\max|{\bm{\beta}}|_{\infty})
such that Eq. (
10
) holds uniformly for all
{\bm{\beta}}
in the set. For commuting local Hamiltonians a stronger claim holds:

I(A:C|B)_{\rho_{\bm{\beta}}}=0\quad{\rm when}\quad{\rm dist}(A,C)>R

(11)

which is the content of the quantum Hammersley–Clifford theorem
Leifer and Poulin 2008
;
Brown and Poulin 2012
.

The importance of the Markov property to our construction lies in its connection to recoverability. This is stated precisely as follows:

Lemma 3

(Markov property implies recoverability. Ref.
Junge et al. 2015
, modified)
.

For a state
\rho
defined on a tripartite system
A\cup B\cup C
, there exists a quantum channel
{\mathcal{R}}^{\rho}_{B\to AB}
acting from
B
to
A\cup B
such that:

\left\|{\rho-{\mathcal{R}}^{\rho}_{B\to AB}\circ{\rm Tr}_{A}(\rho)}\right\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{4\ln 2\cdot I(A:C|B)_{\rho}}.

(12)

An explicit construction of
{\mathcal{R}}
is given by the twirled Petz recovery map (see App.
B
for an explicit expression and a proof of the lemma using the main theorem of Ref.
Junge et al. 2015
).

III.1
Local Interaction Variation

We start by constructing a channel that transforms between two Gibbs states whose Hamiltonians differ only in a local region
A
[see Fig.
3
]. Let
{\bm{\beta}}
satisfy
(\delta,\xi,f)
-stable clustering. We aim to find a channel that changes
\rho=\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}
into
\widetilde{\rho}=\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}_{A}}
for some simply connected region
A
and an interaction variation
\Delta{\bm{\beta}}_{A}
such that
\Delta{\bm{\beta}}_{A}\cdot{\bf h}
is supported strictly within
A
and
|\Delta{\bm{\beta}}_{A}|_{\infty}<\delta
.

Figure 3:
Geometry for local interaction variation.

Let
B=B_{1}\cup B_{2}
be an annulus-shaped region surrounding
A
, and
C
be the rest of the system [Fig.
3
]. We use
r_{a},r_{1},r_{2}
to denote the radii of
A,B_{1},B_{2}
, respectively.

We consider the following channel acting on
AB
:

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{R}}^{\widetilde{\rho}}_{B_{2}\rightarrow AB}\circ{\rm Tr}_{AB_{1}}

(13)

where
{\mathcal{R}}_{B_{2}\rightarrow AB}^{\widetilde{\rho}}
is the twirled Petz recovery map using
\widetilde{\rho}
as a reference state (an explicit form of
{\mathcal{R}}
can be found in App.
B
). The intuition behind Eq.(
13
) is to first erase
AB_{1}
from
\rho
; by LPPL, the remaining state on
B_{2}C
is similar to
\widetilde{\rho}^{B_{2}C}
. One then locally resamples the state within
AB_{1}
according to
\widetilde{\rho}
using the recovery map in Eq.(
12
).

As we show,
{\mathcal{M}}
approximately transforms
\rho
into
\widetilde{\rho}
, with an error bounded by:

\begin{split}&\left\|{\widetilde{\rho}-{\mathcal{M}}(\rho)}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}={\rm Poly}(L)(e^{\kappa+\mu|AB_{1}|-r_{2}/\lambda}+e^{-r_{1}/\xi^{\prime}}),\end{split}

(14)

where
\xi^{\prime}=\xi+1+v/\pi
, with
v
being the maximum Lieb–Robinson velocity
Capel et al. 2025
of the Hamiltonians in
\{H_{{\bm{\beta}}+s\Delta{\bm{\beta}}_{A}}\}_{s\in[0,1]}
. The other constants
\kappa
,
\mu
, and
\lambda
are as in Eq.(
10
).

The error bound Eq. (
14
) is derived by invoking Lemmas 1–3. Using the triangle inequality (sub- and superscripts of the recovery map
{\mathcal{R}}
are omitted):

\begin{split}&\left\|{\widetilde{\rho}-{\mathcal{M}}[\rho]}\right\|_{1}\\
\leq&\left\|{\widetilde{\rho}-{\mathcal{R}}[\widetilde{\rho}^{B_{2}C}]}\right\|_{1}+\left\|{{\mathcal{R}}[\widetilde{\rho}^{B_{2}C}]-{\mathcal{R}}[\rho^{B_{2}C}]}\right\|_{1}.\end{split}

(15)

The approximate Markov property of Gibbs states (Lemmas 2 and 3) guarantees that the first term is bounded by the
e^{\kappa+\mu|AB_{1}|-r_{2}/\lambda}
term. Further, since
\rho
satisfies stable clustering, Lemma 1 implies
\rho
and
\widetilde{\rho}
are approximately indistinguishable on
B_{2}C
:

\begin{split}&\left\|{\widetilde{\rho}^{B_{2}C}-\rho^{B_{2}C}}\right\|_{1}\\
\leq~&\delta\cdot(f(L)e^{-\frac{1}{\xi}(r_{1}-l)}+6|A|e^{-\frac{1}{1+v/\pi}l})\quad\forall l<r_{1}\\
=~&{\rm Poly}(L)e^{-r_{1}/\xi^{\prime}},\end{split}

(16)

where the last equality is obtained by letting
l=\left(1+\frac{\xi}{1+v/\pi}\right)^{-1}r_{1}
, which makes the two exponential factors equal. The corresponding
\xi^{\prime}
is
\xi^{\prime}=\xi+1+v/\pi
. This finishes the proof of Eq.(
14
).

We now consider the trade-off between
{\mathcal{M}}
’s locality and the approximation error. To ensure
\left\|{{\mathcal{M}}[\rho]-\widetilde{\rho}}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}
, it suffices to choose

\begin{split}&r_{1}=r_{a}\geq\xi^{\prime}\ln({{\rm Poly}(L)}/{\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}})\\
&r_{2}\geq\kappa\lambda+\mu\lambda(4r_{1})^{D}+\lambda\ln({{\rm Poly}(L)}/{\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}}).\end{split}

(17)

If we further assume the inverse temperature
|{\bm{\beta}}|_{\infty}
is at most
\log({\rm Poly}(L)/\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}})
, we conclude that

r_{a},r_{1},r_{2}={\rm Polylog}({\rm Poly}L/\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}).

(18)

Before proceeding, we note that the channel
{\mathcal{M}}
’s action on
\rho
can be approximately reversed by

\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}:={\mathcal{R}}^{\rho}_{B_{2}\rightarrow AB}\circ{\rm Tr}_{AB_{1}},

(19)

which is of the same form as
{\mathcal{M}}
but replaces
\widetilde{\rho}
by
\rho
in the recovery map
{\mathcal{R}}
. Following the same proof, we have
\left\|{\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}[\widetilde{\rho}]-\rho}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}
, and therefore

\left\|{\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}\circ{\mathcal{M}}[\rho]-\rho}\right\|_{1}\leq 2\epsilon_{\mathcal{M}}.

(20)

III.2
Global Interaction Variation

Building on local interaction variation, we now describe a layered channel circuit that maps between two Gibbs states
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}
and

IV
Thermal phases of self-correcting quantum memories

As an application of Theorem
1
, in this section we show that quantum self-correction is a universal property shared by all states within the same thermal phase as a zero-temperature topological quantum code. More specifically, if
{\mathcal{C}}
is the constructed circuit (following the recipe in the previous section) connecting a quantum code to another Gibbs state
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
, we prove that
{\mathcal{C}}
encodes a pure codeword state into a mixed state that is locally indistinguishable (in the sense of Def.
4
) but globally distinguishable from
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}

\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
, the encoded state is long-lived under any quasi-local dynamics with
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
as a steady state.

We start by formulating the self-correcting property formally. We model the environment’s influence on the system, as well as the system’s internal evolution, jointly by a quasi-local Lindbladian super-operator of the form:

{\mathcal{L}}={\sum}_{X}{\mathcal{L}}_{X}

(26)

such that each
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
satisfies
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\|_{1\to 1}\leq 1
. Quasi-locality of each
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
means there exists an approximating family of diameter-
l
-support super-operators
\{{\mathcal{L}}_{X}^{l}\}
, such that
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{X}-{\mathcal{L}}_{X}^{l}\|_{1\to 1}\leq e^{-\alpha l}
with a constant
\alpha>0
.

3
3

3

We consider quasi-local rather than strictly local Lindbladians since, for any Gibbs state
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}
of local Hamiltonians, there exists a quasi-local Lindbladian
{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{X}{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
having
\rho_{\bm{\beta}}
as a frustration-free steady state, i.e.,
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}[\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}}]=0
for each

Chen et al. 2023

Since the system’s Gibbs state

. Here we assume the stronger property

is a frustration-free steady state of
{\mathcal{L}}

\left\|{{\rm De}\circ e^{\tau{\mathcal{L}}}\circ{\rm En}(\omega)-\omega}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon\quad\forall\omega\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{C}^{K}),\ \tau\leq t.

(27)

We call a local Hamiltonian’s zero-temperature Gibbs state

\Pi O\Pi\propto\Pi

As a crucial ingredient of the proof that self-correction is a universal property, the following theorem shows that whether a local dynamics can preserve quantum information may be inferred from the properties of its steady state.

Suppose a state

In this section, we show that for the special case of commuting Hamiltonians (i.e., terms in

Theorem 4

Suppose terms in
{\bf h}=\{h_{Z}\}_{Z}

\{{\bm{\beta}}(s),s\in[0,1]\}

Z

s

\left\|{\partial_{s}\rho_{s}-{\mathcal{L}}_{s}[\rho_{s}]}\right\|_{1}\leq{\sum}_{Z}{\rm{Cov}}^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rho_{s}}(Z,\overline{Z_{+r}})

where

According to the theorem, we notice that if
(\xi,f)

1

\rho_{s}
along the path

\rho_{0}
into
\rho_{1}

\epsilon_{\mathcal{L}}:=\left\|{\rho_{1}-\mathcal{T}e^{\int{\mathcal{L}}ds}[\rho_{0}]}\right\|_{1}\leq{\rm Poly}(L)e^{-r/\xi}

r\leq\xi\cdot\log\left(\frac{{\rm Poly}(L)}{\epsilon_{{\mathcal{L}}}}\right).

We now prove Theorem
4

\begin{split}\partial_{s}\rho_{s}&={\sum}_{Z}(\partial_{s}\beta_{s,Z})\cdot\partial_{\beta_{Z}}\rho_{s}\\

(37)

We make the following key observation: each term of the derivative—e.g., the one corresponding to

\begin{split}&\rho_{s}\cdot(\braket{h_{Z}}_{s}-h_{Z})=(\lambda_{Z}-\braket{h_{Z}}_{s})\cdot\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{s,Z}-\rho_{s}\right)\\

(38)

Here

This observation motivates defining the Lindbladian as:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{s}&={\sum}_{Z}{\mathcal{L}}_{s,Z}\quad{\rm where}\\
{\mathcal{L}}_{s,Z}&:=(\partial_{s}\beta_{s,Z})\cdot(\lambda_{Z}-\braket{h_{Z}}_{s})\cdot({\mathcal{M}}_{s,Z}-\mathcal{I})\end{split}

\left\|{{\mathcal{L}}_{s,Z}}\right\|_{1\to 1}\leq 2\left\|{{\mathcal{M}}_{s,Z}-\mathcal{I}}\right\|_{1\to 1}\leq 4

\begin{split}&\left\|{\partial_{s}\rho_{s}-{\mathcal{L}}_{s}[\rho_{s}]}\right\|_{1}\\

\leq&{\sum}_{Z}|\lambda_{Z}-\braket{h_{Z}}_{s}|\cdot\left\|{\widetilde{\rho}_{s,Z}-{\mathcal{M}}_{s,Z}[\rho_{s}]}\right\|_{1}.\end{split}

Thus it remains to choose

into

VI
Summary and outlook

To summarize, in this work we provide a circuit-based characterization of Gibbs states within a thermal phase. We formulate a correlation-decay condition dubbed stable clustering (Def.
2
), which is expected to be satisfied for Gibbs states within a vast class of thermal phases. We prove that there exists a local quantum channel circuit that transforms one Gibbs state into another, provided they are connected by a path that satisfies stable clustering. For the case of commuting local Hamiltonians, we are able to relax stable clustering to a weaker condition (Def.
1
) and improve the circuit to a continuous-time evolution. As an application of the construction, we establish that if a system is in the same thermal phase as a zero-temperature topological code, then it can coherently store quantum information for a macroscopically long time while in contact with the thermal bath.

We end this work with some discussions and open questions:

•

Our constructions have room for potential improvements. In light of Theorem
4
, it is natural to ask whether one can relax the stability part of the clustering condition for noncommuting Hamiltonians as well, and obtain a continuous-time Lindbladian. In addition, we also ask whether the transforming circuit/Lindbladian can be made exact (
\epsilon_{\mathcal{C}}=0
) while still preserving locality. The latter question is motivated by the exact quasi-adiabatic continuation for gapped ground states (see e.g.,
Hastings 2010
), which exactly transforms one ground state into another while having a transient Hamiltonian with superpolynomially decaying tails.

•

Rigorously establishing the relationship between (stable) clustering and other characterizations of thermal phases, e.g., analyticity of the free energy, remains an open problem. Ref.
Harrow et al. 2019
shows that analyticity of the free energy at high temperature implies decay of correlations for local observables in quantum Gibbs states. Extending this connection to low temperatures and to correlations of nonlocal observables (as in the annulus tripartition) remains open. It is also desirable to obtain (stable) clustering for important models, especially at low temperatures. In App.
G
, we provide a physical argument suggesting that stable clustering fails for the 2D toric code but holds for the 4D toric code near the zero-temperature limit. Turning this into a rigorous proof is desirable.

•

The circuit construction under additional symmetry constraints deserves further study. When the Hamiltonian has a global (
0
-form) symmetry, a natural question is whether the circuit can be made strongly symmetric—our current construction is not, because a partial trace appears in the local interaction variation [Eq.(
13
)]. In App.
C
we provide evidence that, for onsite
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, local interaction variation can be made strongly symmetric. Making this statement rigorous, and extending it to continuous symmetries, is desirable. Furthermore, when a continuous symmetry is present and spontaneously broken at low temperatures, Goldstone modes produce algebraic spatial correlations, so stable (exponential) clustering fails. Whether two Gibbs states in the ordered regime can still be connected by a circuit—even without requiring strong symmetry—and how its range scales with the system size remain open questions.

•

In this work we take a state-centric point of view to study Gibbs states. Alternatively, one can take a dynamics-centric point of view by formulating and studying phases of thermalizing dynamics where Gibbs states emerge as steady states. As exemplified by our result on self-correcting quantum memories (Theorem.
2
), many properties of the dynamics can already be inferred from its steady states. Nevertheless, there are also properties of the dynamics that are not reflected sharply in the state, with a notable example in finite dimensions being the domain-wall roughening transition of the 3D Ising model
Hasenbusch et al. 1996
. The transition, which occurs at a critical temperature within the Ising model’s ordered phase, is a sharp change in the lifetime of a large, traversing domain wall. Interestingly, the transition is not accompanied by any singular behavior in the Gibbs state, and we expect that Gibbs states below and above the critical temperature can be connected using the channel circuits we constructed. More drastic examples, including ones displaying multiple dynamical phase transitions without any thermodynamic transitions, can be found in systems on expander graphs
Eggarter 1974
;
Montanari and Semerjian 2006
;
Hong et al. 2025
;
Placke et al. 2025
;
Placke et al. 2024
. Characterizing these dynamical phases and transitions in a unified approach remains a challenging open problem.

Gapped Ground States

Ref.

,
then by the same argument as in Sec.
F
, all stabilizers supported in
\bar{A}
have eigenvalue
1
up to an error
\epsilon
, so

{\rm Tr}\bigl(\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}}O_{1}O_{2}\bigr)\overset{{\epsilon}}{=}{\rm Tr}\bigl(\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}}O_{1}\bigr)\overset{{\epsilon}}{=}{\rm Tr}\bigl(\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}}O_{1}\bigr)\,{\rm Tr}\bigl(\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}}O_{2}\bigr).

(77)

where
O_{1}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{A}
and
O_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\bar{A}}
, both of operator norm
1
, indicating that the annular-shaped covariance vanishes in the state
\rho_{{\bm{\beta}}+\Delta{\bm{\beta}}}
.

We emphasize that the latter part does not provide a direct proof that stable clustering holds for the 4D toric code in the low-temperature phase; rather, it offers physical understanding into the differences between the 2D and 4D cases.

Appendix H
Local indistinguishability of quantum codes

Suppose
\Pi
is a code space projector of a diameter-
\ell
quantum code, with codespace dimension
2^{k}
. We introduce a reference system
R
and have it maximally entangled with the code space, such that the total state is

\ket{\Psi_{RQ}}=2^{-k/2}\sum_{a=1}^{2^{k}}\ket{a}_{R}\otimes\ket{\widetilde{a}}_{Q},

(78)

where
\{\ket{a}\}
is a basis of
R
, and
\{\ket{\widetilde{a}}\}
is a basis of the code space
\Pi
. We have used
Q
to denote the physical qubits supporting the code space
\Pi
. Let
A\subset Q
be a region with diameter less than
\ell
. Then, by the definition of topological codes, the code states
\{\ket{\widetilde{a}}_{Q}\}
are indistinguishable on
A
, leading to:

I_{\ket{\Psi}}(R:A)=0

(79)

Following the theorem in Ref.
Hayden et al. 2004
, the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{A}}
admits a decomposition
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{A}}=(\mathcal{H}_{X}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{Y})\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
under which the state
\ket{\Psi_{RQ}}
takes the form

\ket{\Psi_{RQ}}=2^{-k/2}\sum_{a=1}^{2^{k}}\ket{a}_{R}\otimes\ket{\widetilde{a}^{\prime}}_{RX}\otimes\ket{\omega_{0}}_{YA}

(80)

such that
\ket{\omega_{0}}_{YA}
is a fixed state on
Y\cup A
independent of
a
. Now, for any two code states
\rho_{Q}
and
\sigma_{Q}
within the codespace
\Pi
, under the decomposition above, they must be of the form

\rho_{Q}=\rho^{\prime}_{X}\otimes\ket{\omega_{0}}\bra{\omega_{0}}_{YA}\quad\quad\sigma_{Q}=\sigma^{\prime}_{X}\otimes\ket{\omega_{0}}\bra{\omega_{0}}_{YA}.

(81)

Letting
{\mathcal{D}}[\cdot]={\rm Tr}_{X}(\cdot)\rho^{\prime}_{X}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}[\cdot]={\rm Tr}_{X}(\cdot)\sigma^{\prime}_{X}
, we have
{\mathcal{D}}[\sigma]=\rho
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}[\rho]=\sigma
. Since
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
are local operations supported on
\overline{A}
,
\rho
and
\sigma
are
(\ell,0)
-LI.

Appendix I
Local reversibility and local indistinguishability

A few notations for this appendix:
\rho\overset{{\delta}}{=}\sigma
means
\left\|{\rho-\sigma}\right\|_{1}\leq\delta
.
{\mathcal{G}}=\prod_{X}{\mathcal{E}}_{X}
is used to denote one layer of a channel circuit. The gate
{\mathcal{E}}_{X}
acts within a simply connected region
X
. Different
X
s do not overlap.
{\rm rng\,}({\mathcal{G}}):=\sup_{X}{\rm diam}(X)
.
|{\mathcal{G}}|
means the number of gates in
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Def:
A layer
{\mathcal{G}}=\prod_{X}{\mathcal{E}}_{X}
is locally reversible (L. R.) with respect to
\rho
if, for each
X
, there exists a channel gate
\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{X}
supported within
X
such that
\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{X}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{X}[\rho]\overset{{\delta}}{=}\rho
.

Claim:
If
{\mathcal{G}}
is
\delta
-L.R. with respect to
\rho
, then
\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}\circ{\mathcal{G}}[\rho]\overset{{|{\mathcal{G}}|\cdot\delta}}{=}\rho
.
The proof is straightforward.

Def:

\rho
and
\sigma
are
(r,\delta)
locally indistinguishable (L.I.) if, for any simply connected
A
with
{\rm diam}(A)<r
, there exist channels
{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
supported within
\overline{A}
such that
{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]\overset{{\delta}}{=}\sigma,\ {\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}[\sigma]\overset{{\delta}}{=}\rho
.

For instance, any two codeword states
\sigma,\rho
of the toric code on an
L\times L
torus are
(L/2,0)
-L.I.

Claim:
If
\rho
and
\sigma
are
(r,\delta_{LI})
-L.I., and
{\mathcal{G}}
is
\delta_{LR}
-L.R. with respect to
\sigma
, then:

1.

{\mathcal{G}}
is
(2\delta_{LI}+\delta_{LR})
-L.R. with respect to
\rho
.

2.

{\mathcal{G}}[\rho]
and
{\mathcal{G}}[\sigma]
are
(r-2{\rm rng\,}{\mathcal{G}},(2|{\mathcal{G}}|+1)\delta_{LI}+|{\mathcal{G}}|\delta_{LR})
-L.I.

Proof of the first claim is straightforward. For each
X
,

\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{X}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{X}[\rho]\overset{{\delta_{LI}}}{=}\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{X}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{X}\circ{\mathcal{D}}[\sigma]={\mathcal{D}}\circ\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{X}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{X}[\sigma]\overset{{\delta_{LR}}}{=}{\mathcal{D}}[\sigma]\overset{{\delta_{LI}}}{=}\rho

(82)

where
{\mathcal{D}}
is chosen to be within
\overline{X}
.

To prove the second claim, let
A
be any region with
{\rm diam}(A)\leq r-2{\rm rng\,}({\mathcal{G}})
. Let
B
be a width-
{\rm rng\,}{\mathcal{G}}
buffer around
A
, and let
C=\overline{AB}
. Since
{\rm diam}(AB)\leq r
, there exists
{\mathcal{D}}
in
C
such that
{\mathcal{D}}[\rho]\overset{{\delta_{LI}}}{=}\sigma
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}
where
{\mathcal{G}}_{C}
contains all gates having overlap with
C
, and
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}
contains the remaining gates. Then
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}
is supported within
AB
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathcal{G}}:={\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{D}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{C}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime{\mathcal{G}}}:={\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{C}
, both acting on
BC
. Then

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime{\mathcal{G}}}\circ{\mathcal{G}}[\sigma]={\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{C}{\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}[\sigma]\overset{{|{\mathcal{G}}_{C}|\cdot\delta_{LR}}}{=}{\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}[\sigma]\overset{{\delta_{LI}}}{=}{\mathcal{G}}[\rho]\\
&{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathcal{G}}\circ{\mathcal{G}}[\rho]={\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{D}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{C}{\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}[\rho]\overset{{|{\mathcal{G}}_{C}|\cdot(2\delta_{LI}+\delta_{LR})}}{=}{\mathcal{G}}_{C}{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{C}}[\rho]\overset{{\delta_{LI}}}{=}{\mathcal{G}}[\sigma],\end{split}

(83)

which completes the proof.

We now generalize the result to circuits with multiple layers. Consider a circuit
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{G}}_{T}{\mathcal{G}}_{T-1}...{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
such that each layer
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
is
\delta
-LR with respect to
{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}[\sigma]
, where
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}:={\mathcal{G}}_{t}{\mathcal{G}}_{t-1}\cdots{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
(i.e., the first
t
layers of
{\mathcal{C}}
). Further, let
\rho
and
\sigma
be
(r,0)
-LI. Consider any region
A
with
{\rm diam}(A)\leq r-2{\rm rng\,}{{\mathcal{C}}}
, and let
B
be a width-
{\rm rng\,}{{\mathcal{C}}}
buffer region that shields
A
from
C=\overline{AB}
. Analogously, define channels
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}:={\mathcal{C}}_{t,C}{\mathcal{D}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{t,C}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}:={\mathcal{C}}_{t,C}{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{t,C}
(
t=0,...,T
), where
{\mathcal{C}}_{t,C}
contains all gates in
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
that overlap with the
light cone
of region
C
, with
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{t,C}
defined analogously so that it consists of the local inverses of the gates in
{\mathcal{C}}_{t,C}
. Since
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
are supported in region
C
, for
t\leq T
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}
have support outside region
A
. Since
{\mathcal{C}}
is
\delta
-LR with respect to
\sigma
, it follows that for
t\leq T
,

{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{t}[\sigma]\overset{{|{\mathcal{C}}_{t,C}|\delta}}{=}{\mathcal{C}}_{t}\circ{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}[\sigma]={\mathcal{C}}_{t}[\rho].

(84)

As a result, a gate
{\mathcal{E}}_{t,X}
in
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
that is supported on
A
is
(2|{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1,C}|+1)\delta
-L.R. with respect to
{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}[\rho]
:

\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{t,X}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{t,X}\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}[\rho]\overset{{|{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1,C}|\delta}}{=}\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{t,X}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{t,X}\circ{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}^{\prime}\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}[\sigma]\overset{{\delta}}{=}{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}^{\prime}\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}[\sigma]\overset{{|{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1,C}|\delta}}{=}{\mathcal{C}}_{t-1}[\rho].

(85)

Consequently,

{\mathcal{D}}_{T}\circ C_{T}[\rho]\overset{{\epsilon}}{=}C_{T}[\sigma]\quad\quad\epsilon=|{\mathcal{C}}_{T,C}|\cdot(2|{\mathcal{C}}_{T,C}|+1)

(86)

which implies that
{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
and
{\mathcal{C}}[\sigma]
are
(r-2{\rm rng\,}{\mathcal{C}},2|{\mathcal{C}}|^{2}+|{\mathcal{C}}|)
-L.I.. This completes the proof of Lemma
4
.

Here we prove the following claim: if a Lindbladian

, then
\left\|{e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}[\rho]-\rho}\right\|_{1}\leq t\epsilon
. For any
\tau

\displaystyle\left\|{e^{\tau\mathcal{L}(\rho)}-\rho}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{L}^{n}[\mathcal{L}(\rho)]}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{n}}{n!}\left\|{\mathcal{L}^{n}[\mathcal{L}(\rho)]}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{n}}{n!}\left\|{\mathcal{L}}\right\|_{1}^{n-1}\cdot\epsilon

(88)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{k=m-1}\left\|{e^{\tau{\mathcal{L}}}[\rho]-\rho}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(e^{t\left\|{\mathcal{L}}\right\|_{1}/m}-1\right)\frac{m\cdot\epsilon}{\left\|{\mathcal{L}}\right\|_{1}}

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}=
