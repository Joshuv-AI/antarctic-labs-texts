SimAQ: Mitigating Experimental Artifacts in Soft X-Ray Tomography using Simulated Acquisitions
Jacob Egebjerg, Daniel Wüstner

Abstract
Soft X-ray tomography (SXT) provides detailed structural insight into whole cells but is hindered by experimental artifacts such as the missing wedge and by limited availability of annotated datasets. We present SimAQ, a simulation pipeline that generates realistic cellular phantoms and applies synthetic artifacts to produce paired noisy volumes, sinograms, and reconstructions. We validate our approach by training a neural network primarily on synthetic data and demonstrate effective few-shot and zero-shot transfer learning on real SXT tomograms. Our model delivers accurate segmentations, enabling quantitative analysis of noisy tomograms without relying on large labeled datasets or complex reconstruction methods.



1
Introduction

Soft X-ray tomography (SXT) is a powerful imaging modality capable of resolving the ultrastructure of intact, cryo-preserved cells in three dimensions with very high spatial resolution of 30-50 nm. SXT provides intrinsic contrast between water and carbon-rich biological material without the need for exogenous staining
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. This enables quantitative imaging of cellular morphology through the linear absorption coefficient (LAC), which reflects local molecular density and composition
[
6
]
.

To recover the spatial distribution of LACs, a tomographic reconstruction is performed on tilt series of projection images acquired at successive angular increments. While full-rotation datasets can be acquired using capillary-mounted samples, many specimens of biological interest are prepared on flat grids. This geometry restricts the achievable tilt range and gives rise to the well-known "missing wedge" artifact
[
7
]
. Limited-angle acquisition introduces severe reconstruction artifacts, including elongation, directional blurring, and bias in intensity distribution, all of which compromise the accuracy of quantitative and morphological analyses
[
8
]
.

In addition to geometric limitations, a range of experimental noise sources further degrade reconstruction quality. These include various sources of noise, ice cracks and voids, diffraction from impurities, non-uniform absorption due to variable ice thickness, flat-field correction artifacts, and errors stemming from missing or misaligned projections
[
9
]
. Although the Filtered Back Projection (FBP) reconstruction method is commonly used due to its speed and fidelity in high-SNR regions, it is notably sensitive to such imperfections. Iterative reconstruction methods, such as algebraic reconstruction techniques (ART) and their regularized extensions, offer improved robustness but often introduce over-smoothing and require careful hyperparameter tuning
[
10
]
.

Recent advances in deep learning have introduced promising strategies for artifact correction, including supervised denoising, sinogram inpainting, and direct semantic segmentation
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. These methods typically rely on supervised learning with large annotated datasets, which are time consuming to acquire and difficult to annotate, particularly when only artifact-corrupted data is available. Some approaches instead adopt self-supervised learning, for example, by rotating volumes to higher confidence angles or masking sinograms
[
11
,
12
,
15
]
. However, the resulting training signal can be unreliable due to residual artifacts (which may be anisotropic in nature), and evaluating model performance on a complete tomogram is not feasible for data acquired on a limited-angle setup.

Here, we introduce
SimAQ
, a hybrid learning framework that leverages realistic 3D simulations of the SXT acquisition process to train a model for severe artifact correction in reconstructions and/or segmentation. We validate our approach with a neural network trained on a mix of synthetic data and sparsely annotated real SXT data. This strategy allows the model to adapt to the noise characteristics of real acquisitions while preserving the structural priors learned from simulation. In addition to improving artifact correction, the method reduces the amount of annotated data required for segmentation, even in high-quality full-range tomograms. We demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach on tomograms of
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
(baker’s yeast), obtaining accurate segmentations with minimal post-processing. The code for synthetic data generation and all experiments is publicly available at
https://github.com/Wuestner-Lab/SimAQ
.

2
Background

2.1
Principles of Soft X-ray Tomography

Soft X-ray tomography (SXT) enables high-resolution, label-free, and quantitative imaging of cryo-preserved cells and tissues without the need for staining or physical sectioning
[
16
]
. It operates within the soft X-ray water window, a spectral region between the K-absorption edges of carbon (284 eV) and oxygen (543 eV), where water is relatively transparent to X-rays and carbon-rich biomolecules absorb strongly
[
1
]
. This contrast mechanism arises naturally from the intrinsic differences in X-ray attenuation between water and organic matter, allowing subcellular structures to be visualized in their near-native state.

Image formation in SXT follows the Beer–Lambert law, which describes the attenuation of X-ray intensity as a function of material composition and thickness. The measured intensity
I
after transmission through a sample is given by

I=I_{0}\exp\left(-\int\mu(x)\,dx\right)

(1)

where
I_{0}
is the incident intensity and
\mu(x)
is the spatially varying linear absorption coefficient (LAC). The LAC provides a quantitative, physically meaningful measure of local molecular density and composition at each voxel in the reconstructed volume, allowing for discriminating various subcellular organelles, such as lipid droplets, the vacuole or mitochondria in yeast cells
[
17
,
18
]
.

Full-range SXT typically achieves isotropic resolutions on the order of 50 nm and supports the imaging of whole, intact cells under cryogenic conditions. As a result, SXT is increasingly used in structural cell biology, particularly in studies where preserving the native ultrastructure is critical
[
5
,
6
]
. Typical applications include investigations of organelle organization and interaction
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
, virus infections
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
, and morphological changes in response to environmental perturbations or genetic diseases
[
28
,
29
,
22
,
30
]
. The widespread use of SXT is hampered by the fact that in most set-ups, its application requires synchrotron radiation as illumination source, for which available beamtime is a limiting factor. Furthermore, the throughput is much lower than that of light microscopy, as typical acquisition times per tomogram are between 30 to 60 min, and the need for searching the grid for regions, which are suitable for imaging can easily add another hour per sample. Accordingly, the number of obtainable tomograms per imaging session is low.

2.2
Tomographic Acquisition Constraints

The limited availability of synchrotron beamtime for carrying out SXT of cellular samples and the comparably long acquisition and grid-search time make, that biologically meaningful conclusions must be drawn from relatively scarce data sets. Therefore, it is of uttermost importance to reduce any imaging artifacts, which can occur in SXT of cells. In the following, we discuss such artifacts starting with those, inherent to the imaging process. Ideal tomographic reconstructions require projection data spanning 180°. However, in many biological imaging contexts, including SXT of grid-mounted samples, the achievable tilt range is restricted due to mechanical limitations or sample geometry. This leads to incomplete coverage of Fourier space and a characteristic missing wedge of information
[
7
,
31
]
. The missing wedge exacerbates the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem and results in anisotropic resolution, systematic elongation artifacts, and reduced contrast in the affected directions
[
7
]
.

2.3
Experimental Artifacts

In addition to geometric limitations, SXT reconstructions are subject to a variety of experimental artifact and noise sources, several of which are often overlooked by the tomography community.

•

Instrumental noise:
mostly arising at the detector (e.g. readout and amplification noise);

•

Ice damage and cracks:
which introduce structural discontinuities and scattering;

•

Anisotropic absorption:
especially pronounced at high tilt angles due to increased ice attenuation;

•

Diffraction artifacts:
caused by impurities such as ice crystals or the fiducial particles, often gold beads used for alignment;

•

Flat-field mismatches:
in SXT, the sample is illuminated with a focused spot, which is ’scanned’ by moving the ellipsoidal mirror in a circular pattern. This generates a flickering effect and is only partially corrected for by taking a flat-field image (i.e. an image of the grid in regions without the sample)
[
4
]
. This correction for uneven illumination is typically carried out once but not for each projection angle, which results in systematic intensity errors across the detector.

•

Misalignment and missing projections:
instabilities of the sample holder can cause motion blur, and those projections have to be removed. This, together with imperfect alignment and slight tilting of the rotation axis of the sample compared to an idealized geometry introduces inconsistencies in the reconstruction.

These artifacts manifest non-uniformly across the volume and degrade both spatial resolution and quantitative accuracy
[
17
,
32
,
16
]
.

2.4
Tomographic Reconstruction

In soft X-ray tomography, the goal is to recover a three-dimensional map of the linear absorption coefficient (LAC)
\mu(x,y,z)
from a series of two-dimensional projection images acquired at different viewing angles. These projections are governed by (
1
). Taking the negative logarithm of the transmission yields the line integral of
\mu
:

p=-\ln\left(\frac{I}{I_{0}}\right)=\int_{\ell}\mu(s)\,ds

(2)

This integral defines the
Radon transform

\mathcal{R}\{\mu\}
of the object,

p_{\theta}(t)=\iint\mu(x,y)\,\delta\big(t-x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta\big)\,dx\,dy,

(3)

where
p_{\theta}(t)
is the projection of
\mu(x,y)
acquired at view angle
\theta
and detector coordinate
t
, corresponding to the line integral of
\mu
along the path
x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta=t
.

The most widely used analytical reconstruction method is
Filtered Back Projection
(FBP), which approximates the inverse Radon transform. FBP involves two steps:

1.

Filtering:
Each projection is convolved with a high-pass filter (e.g., Ram-Lak or Hamming) in the frequency domain to compensate for the loss of high-frequency information inherent in the projection process.

2.

Backprojection:
The filtered projections are backprojected along the corresponding projection angles to reconstruct the spatial distribution of
\mu(x,y)
.

Mathematically, the reconstruction can be expressed as:

\mu(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{|\omega|\cdot\mathcal{F}\{p_{\theta}(t)\}\right\}\right]_{t=x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta}d\theta

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denote the Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms, respectively, and
|\omega|
is the selected filter, although in practice it is of course discrete. The Hamming filter is commonly used for
|\omega|
and is default in the popular Tomo3D package
[
33
]
.

While FBP is efficient and widely used, it assumes complete and uniformly sampled projection data over 180°, which may not be available in practice due to limitations in acquisition geometry. In such cases, iterative methods offer greater flexibility and robustness.

Let the discrete forward model be represented as:

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{A}\bm{\mu},

(5)

where
\mathbf{p}
is the vector of measured projections,
\bm{\mu}
is the unknown vectorized volume, and
\mathbf{A}
is the system matrix that models the projection operator.

Algebraic Reconstruction Technique (ART):
ART solves the system iteratively by updating
\bm{\mu}
in a row-by-row fashion. At each iteration, the current estimate is corrected to reduce the residual between the measured and predicted projections.

Conjugate Gradient (CG):
CG methods solve the least-squares formulation:

\min_{\bm{\mu}}\|\mathbf{A}\bm{\mu}-\mathbf{p}\|_{2}^{2}

(6)

This approach can incorporate regularization (e.g., Tikhonov, total variation) to stabilize the inversion, especially under limited or noisy measurements. CG is efficient for large, sparse systems and often converges faster than basic algebraic methods.

These reconstruction strategies form the basis of many modern tomographic pipelines and enable the recovery of high-quality 3D volumes under a range of acquisition constraints.

While iterative methods are valuable in dealing with incomplete or noisy datasets, they often involve trade-offs between resolution, stability, and computational cost. In contrast, FBP remains attractive due to its analytical formulation, computational efficiency, and lack of implicit priors and hyperparameters. Unlike many iterative approaches that incorporate smoothing or regularization, FBP preserves all frequency components present in the measured data and introduces no learned bias, hallucination or smoothing. As a result, it offers a reproducible and interpretable reconstruction that is especially valuable as a reference or ground truth for evaluating downstream processing methods.

2.5
Learning-Based Artifact Correction

Deep learning has become a powerful tool for correcting tomographic artifacts. Approaches such as sinogram inpainting restore missing or corrupted projections
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
34
]
, while post-reconstruction networks map artifact-laden volumes to clean counterparts
[
15
]
. Encoder-decoder models, especially U-Net variants, have shown strong performance in biomedical imaging, including cryo-electron and soft X-ray tomography
[
35
,
22
,
36
,
37
]
. However, these methods often depend on fully supervised training with large labeled datasets, which are difficult to obtain, or on self-supervised strategies such as masking sinograms or rotating tomograms. These alternatives often fail to capture the full noise profile and often provide an unreliable training signal, especially when the noise and artifacts are anisotropic.

To mitigate this limitation, semi-supervised and domain adaptation strategies have been proposed. These methods exploit synthetic data for pretraining and use limited experimental data to refine the model’s representation of real-world noise and variability. However, successfully transferring learning from synthetic to experimental domains remains a key challenge, particularly in preserving the statistical properties of noise and artifacts unique to the acquisition system.

2.6
Related work

Chen
et al.
propose a segmentation pipeline for yeast cells using a 3D U-Net trained to segment membrane, vacuole, lipid droplet, and nucleus compartments, optimizing a cross-entropy loss function
[
35
]
. Starting from manual annotations of seven cells, they iteratively expand the training set by correcting the model’s predictions on new data. Their dataset is acquired using soft X-ray tomography (SXT) under full angular coverage with relatively low levels of noise and artifact. This also gives them access to precise LAC measurements, allowing the model to incorporate the pixel values in the decision making. Despite these favorable conditions, their model exhibits uncertainty in delineating lipid droplet boundaries, requiring post-processing refinements.

Martínez-Sánchez
et al.
address the scarcity of reliable ground truth in cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) by introducing a simulation framework for generating biologically realistic synthetic tomograms
[
38
]
. Although most cryo-ET simulators focus on reproducing imaging and reconstruction artifacts, their approach prioritizes the generation of low-order cellular structures, such as membranes, macromolecular complexes, and cytoskeletal elements. The framework employs stochastic geometric models to simulate a wide variety of crowded intracellular environments. Notably, a model trained exclusively on their synthetic data was able to generalize well to real tomograms.

Yao
et al.
introduce UsiNet, a deep learning–based method for sinogram inpainting that does not require paired ground truth data for training
[
34
]
. Operating directly in sinogram space, UsiNet uses a single U-Net architecture to impute missing angular projections in an unsupervised manner, learning solely from the structure of the observed tilt series. Once completed, standard FBP is used to reconstruct a volume with significantly reduced elongation and streaking artifacts, even under large missing wedges of up to 45°. This makes UsiNet particularly suitable for nanoparticle imaging and other materials science applications where acquisition protocols are consistent and isotropic. However, its applicability to low-contrast biological imaging remains uncertain, and the method has not been evaluated under highly anisotropic acquisition conditions typical in limited-angle SXT.

Liu
et al.
propose a related self-supervised approach that operates in the reconstructed volume domain rather than sinogram space
[
15
]
. Their method rotates tomograms to regions with sufficient Fourier coverage and simulates missing data by masking cones in Fourier space. This synthetic pairing allows the model to learn from known artifacts without external ground truth. They demonstrate improved reconstruction quality on several biological cryo-EM datasets, though their method also favors isotropic sampling which may limit performance in strongly anisotropic acquisition scenarios.

3
Method

3.1
Simulation

3.1.1
Phantom generation

Yeast phantoms are simulated using ellipsoids with radii sampled from Gaussian distributions, subject to constraints that prevent organelle overlap and ensure enclosure within the cell membrane. Each phantom includes a membrane, a variable number of vacuoles, and lipid droplets. To reflect vacuole fragmentation observed in real S. cerevisiae samples
[
22
]
, some vacuoles are replaced by clusters of smaller ones generated via an Apollonian packing algorithm
[
39
]
.

For multi-cell volumes, initial cell positions are sampled from a standard normal distribution and adjusted by minimizing a loss function that reduces overlap and promotes spatial dispersion:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}f(r_{i}+r_{j}-\lVert\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}\rVert+\varepsilon)+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{p}_{i}\rVert

(7)

with

f(x)=\begin{cases}x^{2},&\text{if }x>1\\
\max(x,0),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the position of cell
i
,
r_{i}
its effective radius,
n
the number of cells, and
\varepsilon
a small constant that represents the desired gap between cells and
\lambda=0.1
.
During optimization, cell positions are restricted to the XY plane, and Z-coordinates are sampled afterward from a normal distribution. This procedure consistently yields realistic spatial arrangements. Organelles are voxelized at the target resolution, and their intrinsic intensities are sampled from Gaussian distributions based on reported LAC values
[
35
]
. To mitigate geometric bias introduced by idealized shapes, random elastic deformations are applied to introduce realistic morphological variability.

3.1.2
Phantom Processing

Artifacts are introduced to the ground truth phantom through a series of controlled augmentations. Ice cracks are added using pre-generated
128\times 128\times 128
volumes modulated by low-frequency Perlin noise to simulate non-uniform thickness and increase variability
[
40
]
. These volumes are precomputed to mitigate the
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
complexity of the generation algorithm, where
N
is the number of voxels. The crack generation algorithm performs a seeded random walk across a 3D volume, where regions expand outward based on local weights and propagation costs. Boundaries between competing regions are extracted as crack surfaces, producing naturalistic, branching fracture patterns. Ice volumes are assembled from randomly selected precomputed samples, with the orientation of the three axes randomized independently for each sample. These are then masked to remain outside the cell membrane. Discontinuities between crack segments are not discernible in the final reconstruction.

Additional high-frequency Perlin noise is added outside the membrane to simulate scattering and impurities in the surrounding ice. Fiducial markers, modeled as spheres with radii and LACs sampled from distributions matched to experimental gold beads, are inserted at random positions outside the cell membrane. The result is a noisy volume paired with a clean ground truth phantom that preserves both morphological structure and intrinsic LACs of cellular components.

3.1.3
Projection and reconstruction

To simulate realistic acquisition variability, the angular range is sampled from a uniform distribution (e.g.
\theta\in[70\degree,130\degree]
for the experiments in Section
4
), after which a subset of angles is randomly omitted. This models common experimental scenarios where mechanical limitations or motion artifacts render certain projections unusable. The list of retained angles is stored for use during backprojection. Gaussian additive noise is added to the angles for the forward projection to simulate error and uncertainty that may arise under experimental conditions.

Off-axis misalignment can be modeled by rolling pixel values and padding projections accordingly, though this feature was not used in our current experiments.

Flat-field correction mismatch is approximated by adding low-frequency Perlin noise, scaled by the mean intensity of each projection and a tunable flicker-effect hyperparameter.

To account for the influence of varying ice thickness surrounding the sample, which extends well beyond the voxelized specimen, we introduce additive Gaussian noise accounting for the estimated thickness of penetrated ice. The ice thickness
h
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution, and the effective penetration depth is computed as
p(\theta)=\frac{h}{\cos(|\theta|)}
, where
\theta
denotes the projection angle.

Although Poisson noise can simulate low-exposure conditions, we omit it in this work due to the high photon count of our experimental acquisitions, which renders such noise negligible.

The sinogram is reconstructed using FBP with Ram-Lak and Hamming filter. The output is clipped to the inscribed circle where reconstruction is theoretically valid. Final normalization is performed using quantile normalization against a standard Gaussian distribution.

The hyperparameters governing the random variables in the simulation must be tuned to ensure that the resulting synthetic projections resemble those observed in real experimental data. As illustrated in Figure

, the simulated projection closely replicates the structural features and noise characteristics of the real acquisition.

3.1.4
Implementation details

To enable efficient on-the-fly data generation for training, we prioritize computational speed without compromising realism. Although simulating the full cryo-preserved grid and surrounding ice would be more physically accurate, the associated memory requirements are prohibitive. Instead, we approximate these effects in projection space.

Voxelization is performed per ellipsoid using local bounding boxes, leveraging GPU-accelerated PyTorch tensors to evaluate the inequality
\frac{(x-x_{c})^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{(y-y_{c})^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{(z-z_{c})^{2}}{c^{2}}\leq 1
without wasted computation. This localized approach substantially reduces computation time, after which the result is inserted into the global volume.

Forward and inverse projections are computed using the GPU-accelerated
torch-radon
library
[
41
]
. To improve data efficiency during training, we generate synthetic reconstructions along all three principal axes, with independent sampling of acquisition parameters for each orientation.

On consumer-grade hardware, generating a typical volume takes approximately 15 seconds, with the exact runtime depending on the number of cells. Each run yields around 350 triplets, consisting of a phantom, its segmentation, and a noisy reconstruction. Hardware specifications are listed in Appendix
C
, and Table
1

Phantom generation scales more than linearly with the number of cells due to the organization step, which requires solving the optimization problem in (
7
). Voxelization time depends on the number of ellipsoids to be added. Noise injection in phantom space is relatively slow, primarily due to I/O overhead from loading precomputed ice crack and Perlin noise volumes. However, this overhead can be reduced when sampling many phantoms in succession, as the volumes are cached in memory. The runtime of these operations scales with the resolution of the data, which is kept constant throughout. The reconstruction stage, which includes forward projection, noise and artifact injection, and backprojection, scales with the number of projections. The final normalization step scales with the total data size.

3.2
Modeling

Figure 3
:
Training scheme: The encoder encodes the noisy reconstructed phantom, and the decoder outputs predictions for the original phantom and its masks.

Using the simulation scheme described above, we generate a virtually unlimited distribution of paired phantoms, segmentations, and noisy reconstructions. To mitigate overfitting to the specific noise characteristics induced by simulation, we complement this synthetic dataset with a small set of partially annotated real samples (e.g. partial segmentations and no clean phantoms). Our objective is to train a neural network to recover clean phantoms, corresponding segmentations, or both from noisy reconstructions, as illustrated in Figure
3
.

For the results presented in Section
4
, we employ an encoder–decoder architecture tailored for artifact correction, with an emphasis on compactness rather than full generative capacity. The encoder performs moderate downsampling, yielding a latent representation at 1/8 the original spatial resolution with 16 channels, which is sufficient for capturing the underlying structural features. This is implemented using 8, 6, and 4 ResNet blocks at successive levels, with 128, 256, and 512 filters, respectively. The decoder mirrors this structure symmetrically. Although the model is not optimized for high-fidelity generation, its latent space remains compatible with more expressive priors (e.g., normalizing flows or latent diffusion models), which could be integrated in future work to enable generative modeling and density estimation.

We also explored a variety of architectural alternatives, including probabilistic models such as standard and hierarchical VAEs, as well as U-Net variants with both segmentation and reconstruction heads. In our experiments, we found that skip connections made it harder for the network to learn the correct undistorted shape, likely due to shortcut learning
[
42
]
. Better results were achieved by omitting skip connections entirely. We also observed that disabling the reconstruction head improved regularization, as it allowed for more aggressive data augmentations. Nonetheless, the reconstruction head can be beneficial when incorporating real data—for example, simulating missing-wedge artifacts on rotated tomograms, as done in
[
15
]
.

3.3
Transfer Learning

Experimental sample preparation and biological variability lead to highly heterogeneous data, which requires strong regularization during training to ensure that learned features generalize beyond the training distribution. To adapt to real data, we perform light fine-tuning using rough, partially annotated samples. This helps the model avoid learning spurious patterns from unpredictable artifacts introduced by uncharacterized debris or experimental artifacts.

For probabilistic models, we recommend freezing the decoder during fine-tuning in order to preserve the semantics of the learned prior. This allows only the encoder to adapt, focusing updates on the posterior. As a result, the model can better align with the real data distribution while maintaining the structure and consistency of the latent space.

Although not used in this work, several techniques could further reduce the domain gap. For example, unpaired image-to-image translation using CycleGAN
[
43
]
could align real and synthetic domains without requiring additional annotations. A real-to-synthetic generator could act as a preprocessing step for segmentation, while the reverse mapping may be more appropriate when supplementing a large annotated real dataset with synthetic examples.

4
Experiments

4.1
Dataset

We base our experiments on a soft X-ray tomography dataset of
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
(baker’s yeast), acquired at the BESSY II beamline. Although the instrument nominally supports a tilt range of
\pm 65\degree
, the final 15 to 30
\degree
are often excluded due to alignment challenges or low signal-to-noise ratios. As a result, the effective angular coverage is typically 90 to 110
\degree
. Projections are aligned using fiducial markers, padded with the per-projection mean, and reconstructed using FBP via Tomo3D with default parameters, including full-frequency Ram-Lak and Hamming filters. We refer to
[
22
]
for more details.

We partially annotated seven tomograms from the dataset, focusing primarily on background regions where missing wedge artifacts are minimal, along with a few high-confidence pixels within cells. One volume is fully annotated across all slices, while the others contain sparse labels with a focus on the higher-variance background.

4.2
Training

The model described in Section
3.2
is trained using a mixture of synthetic and real data, with real data making up
5\%
of samples. For synthetic samples, the tilt range is drawn uniformly from
[70^{\circ},130^{\circ}]
, and the number of cells is sampled from
\max(1,\mathcal{N}(2,1))
. We optimize the model using the Adam optimizer with L2 weight regularization and maintain an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with a decay rate of
0.999
, which helps reduce overfitting to the limited examples stored in the synthetic data buffer. To address border artifacts and contextual ambiguity near volume boundaries, each input slice is padded with the four preceding and four following slices.

We optimize a Dice-Focal loss function, which combines the advantages of the Dice coefficient for class imbalance and the Focal loss for hard example emphasis. The loss is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\Bigg[\lambda_{\text{Dice}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{2\sum_{i}p_{i,c}g_{i,c}+\epsilon}{\sum_{i}p_{i,c}+\sum_{i}g_{i,c}+\epsilon}\right)+

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Focal}}\cdot\left(-\sum_{i}g_{i,c}(1-p_{i,c})^{\gamma}\log(p_{i,c}+\epsilon)\right)\Bigg]

(8)

where
p_{i,c}
and
g_{i,c}
denote the predicted and ground truth probabilities for voxel
i
and class
c
, respectively. Each voxel’s ground truth label is represented as a one-hot encoded vector across the
C
classes. The terms
\lambda_{\text{Dice}}=1
and
\lambda_{\text{Focal}}=1
control the relative weighting of the Dice and Focal components,
\gamma=2
is the focusing parameter, and
\epsilon=10^{-6}
is a small constant for numerical stability.

4.3
Evaluation

We evaluate our model on 3,000 synthetic slices (including contextual padding) sampled from approximately 5–7 phantoms reconstructed with an
80\degree
projection span. We consider one, two, and three-cell configurations and compute the Intersection over Union (IoU) for each semantic channel.

The results, shown in Figure
5
, indicate that most predictions achieve an IoU close to 0.9 or higher. Lower scores are primarily observed in channels corresponding to vacuole boundaries and lipid droplets, where low contrast and missing wedge artifacts make segmentation more challenging. Figure
4
highlights selected slices that explain some of the lower IoU values. The wider range of intensity of the lipid droplets occasionally leads to extreme values, causing false negatives. Small false positives can also substantially reduce IoU, especially in slices with little or no true positives.

4.4
Inference

We assess the effectiveness of transfer learning by applying our model to three real tomograms exhibiting diverse phenotypes, each with varying levels of annotation. These include: (1) partial annotations across all slices, (2) sparse annotations on a few slices, (3) coarse background-only annotations, and (4) unseen tomogram from a different experimental and instrumental conditions. 5
Discussion

A key obstacle in tomographic imaging is the lack of ground truth, which makes it difficult to assess the accuracy of reconstruction and segmentation methods. In SXT, this challenge is amplified by the inability to obtain precise annotations for cellular structures under missing wedge conditions. Our method addresses this limitation by generating realistic simulations of organelles, producing paired noisy sinograms and corresponding ground-truth volumes. This enables systematic benchmarking of algorithms under well-controlled conditions.

While we demonstrated the approach using FBP, the framework is not limited to this method. The
torch_radon
library employed for reconstruction also supports techniques such as Landweber iteration, a common implementation of the Simultaneous Iterative Reconstruction Technique (SIRT), as well as Conjugate Gradient methods. In addition, the pipeline can operate directly in sinogram space, making it suitable for integration into sinogram inpainting workflows.

The results show that models trained primarily on synthetic data can generalize effectively to real tomograms when suitable training regularization is applied. This is achieved even in zero-shot and few-shot learning scenarios, highlighting the utility of the method for cases where annotation resources are limited. Although the experiments here focused on transfer learning, the same pipeline can support training on larger real datasets to enhance model robustness.

Although we present results using a model trained on (several) 2D slices, the data generation process is inherently three-dimensional, which makes it compatible with volumetric neural network architectures. Using volumetric processing could further improve structural consistency and segmentation accuracy in future applications.

5.1
Limitations

In real data, projections are often affected by absorption and scattering from material outside the reconstruction circle or cylinder, an effect not modeled in our current synthetic pipeline. Our current implementation also uses yeast phantoms tailored to our experimental setup; for other setups, the phantoms must be updated accordingly. This limitation is evident in the left panel of Figure
7
, where an unseen tomogram from a different experimental setup shows mitochondria misclassified as lipid droplets. Also, our pipeline is optimized for relatively small specimen, for which the limited depth of field of the soft X-ray microscopy is negligible
[
44
]
. For larger specimen, the simulations have to be extended to account for out-of-focus contributions in the tomograms.

5.2
Conclusion

We have introduced SimAQ, a fully three-dimensional synthetic data generation framework for tomographic reconstruction and segmentation. SimAQ produces realistic noisy sinograms paired with ground truth volumes and labels, enabling controlled experiments in scenarios where ground truth is otherwise unavailable. The framework is flexible, supporting multiple reconstruction algorithms and data domains. By accurately modeling cellular ultrastructure and key experimental artifacts, SimAQ facilitates the development of models that generalize from synthetic to real data, even in low-annotation settings. Our experiments show that models trained on SimAQ data achieve accurate zero- and few-shot transfer to real tomograms, enabling quantitative analysis without extensive manual labeling or complex reconstruction procedures. When adapted to a target specimen, SimAQ has the potential to substantially advance the development of robust and generalizable methods for SXT.
