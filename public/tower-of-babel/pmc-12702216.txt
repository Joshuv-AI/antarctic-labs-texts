Dataset for classification of forming tool types for aircraft parts based on neural network models using CAD
Authors: Lee DongGyu, Lee HyunSup, Shin JaeHo, Shin MinSeok, Kang JiWon, Kim YoungSoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study presents a data collection using CAD image-based deep learning and machine learning models to classify forming tool types for aircraft parts. Focusing on sheet metal components i.e. which constitute a substantial portion of aircraft structures and are often produced via fluid-cell hydroforming, this dataset uses visual information embedded in CAD images, particularly flange geometries, to classify tool types without requiring physical prototypes or specialized sensors. A dataset was built from publicly available CAD models (sourced from Zenodo) and additional models generated in CATIA, resulting in 12,432 images across three visualization modes (Normal, Hidden Line, and Wireframe) and multiple orientations. Images were categorized into six tool types based on the flange configuration. Several convolutional neural network architectures and machine learning models were evaluated to validate their classification performance on the dataset. ResNeXt achieved the highest accuracy of 96% on Normal View with Wireframe View. ResNeXt was evaluated with accuracy and F1-score to address class imbalance.. This scalable, cost-effective, and readily applicable CAD image-based deep learning and machine learning model for classifying aircraft parts will significantly help people who want to propose similar solutions for small-batch, multi-variety manufacturing environments.

Background

Enhancing the competitiveness of the manufacturing industry through digitization and smart factory integration is being pursued worldwide. Similar efforts are being made to strengthen the competitiveness of the aerospace industry [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

One of the factors that can strengthen competitiveness is cost estimation. Various cost estimation approaches have been studied and applied under limited technical information [5,[7], [8]].

Sheet metal forming under small-batch, multi-variety production is widely used in the aerospace industry. The forming process should consequently offer both flexible operation and low tooling costs. Fluid-cell forming processes meet these requirements [6].

In a small-batch, multi-variety production system, the cost estimation of the forming tool is an important factor. Cost estimation based on limited information inherently entails errors. Classification features are extracted from CAD images of aircraft parts, based on the observation that the flange shapes of the parts influence the external shape of forming tools. These features are valuable for predicting the cost of forming tools.

Using CAD data, it is anticipated that manufacturing and artificial intelligence can be effectively integrated without the need for additional investment in data collection devices or mechanical system components.

To construct the dataset, some CAD data of aircraft parts were obtained from a publicly available dataset from Zenodo platform [9]. Additional CAD data were generated in CATIA [10] in accordance with aircraft design guideline expand the dataset. The CAD data were captured using three different methods, with various angles and orientations, and the images were classified into six forming-tool classes. The dataset was then partitioned into training, validation, and testing of deep learning models.

Data Description

Data structure

The dataset was systematically constructed to reflect the geometric characteristic real-world aircraft structural components' geometric characteristics and parts manufactured via the fluid-cell hydroforming process. To ensure diversity and representativeness, the dataset comprises both publicly available CAD models of aerospace sheet-metal parts (sourced from the Zenodo platform) and additional models generated in CATIA in accordance with established aircraft design guidelines [11]. The primary part categories include stiffeners, ribs, frames, floor panels, and other components commonly produced through fluid cell hydroforming in aircraft structures [12]. This approach ensures that the dataset encompasses a wide range of geometries and flange configurations relevant to practical manufacturing scenarios.

Data designed features

The design features of aerospace sheet-metal parts are categorized into three main groups: web features, trim features, and deformation features. Rather than classifying parts by function or application, we focus on their geometric characteristics [13].

Web features refer to boundaries formed by either deformation or trimming processes. In Fig. 1 solid lines indicate deformation boundaries and dashed lines represent trimmed edges.Fig. 1Illustration of the web.Fig 1

Illustration of the web.

Deformation features include flanges, lips, joggles, twin joggles, deformed flanges, deformed webs, beads, and lightening cutouts and holes.

The deformation features are described as follows: A flange is typically a deformed edge of sheet-metal, which can be either planar or curved (Fig. 2). A lip is a subtype of flange due to its similar geometry (Fig. 2(f)). A joggle is an offset bend in a web or flange (Figs. 2(a), (d), (f)). A twin joggle is formed when combining two joggles to create a recessed surface (Fig. 2(c)). Deformed flanges and deformed webs are offset deformations of a flange or web, respectively (Figs. 2(a), (c), (d), (f)). A bead appears as a protrusion within the web (Fig. 2(a)). Finally, a lightening hole and lightening cutout refer to the deformed edge around a hole or cutout (Figs. 2(a), (b)).Fig. 2Illustration of deformation features (a∼f).Fig 2

Illustration of deformation features (a∼f).

Trim features, as illustrated in Fig. 3, are generated by trimming processes and encompass cutouts, holes, stringer cutouts, bend reliefs, and corners.Fig. 3Illustration of trim features.Fig 3

The dataset is created by converting 3D part models into 2D images in CATIA program to facilitate classification modeling. However, this conversion from 3D to 2D images can lead to geometric information loss. For example, as shown in Fig. 4, the left image clearly displays flange shapes, but in the right image, after a 180-degree rotation, a specific flange becomes less visible. This loss of information could impact the performance of deep learning models. To minimize the loss of feature information when converting 3D shapes into 2D images, we also captured CAD images using isometric projection. Isometric projection is a technique where the three axes (X, Y, and Z), are angled at 120 degrees to each other, allowing each object's surface to be represented without distortion. This method is commonly used when creating 2D drawings to represent 3D shapes on a 2D plane effectively.

Image type

The tool types for fluid-cell hydroforming were classified primarily by the aircraft part's flange geometry, as the flange's forming surface typically defines the tool's outer contour. In cases where multiple flanges are bent in different directions, multiple forming tools may be required for a single part. Based on these considerations, six tool types (Type A–F) were defined, each representing a combination of flange-bending direction and pattern, as shown in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Types of aircraft part images.Fig 6

Types of aircraft part images.

The six tool types are as follows:

Type A refers to parts with zero or one flange-forming surfaces; if present, the flange is bent once in a single direction. This is the simplest configuration, typically involving a single bend in one direction. Type B refers to parts with two or more flange-forming surfaces, each bent only once. All flanges are bent in the same direction, and each is bent a single time. Type C refers to parts with two or more flanges, where the flanges are bent in different directions. This type is characterized by the presence of flanges oriented in multiple directions, with each flange bent only once. Type D refers to parts with at least one flange bent twice in different directions. This configuration introduces additional complexity, as a single flange undergoes multiple bends in distinct orientations. Type E is similar to Type C, but at least one flange is bent twice in the same direction, resulting in a stepped or offset geometry along a single axis. Type F is an extension of Type B, where at least one flange is bent twice in the same direction. This type combines the characteristics of multiple flanges (as in Type B) with repeated bending in the same orientation for at least one flange. Each CAD model was assigned to one of these six types based on its flange configuration. A total of 12,432 images were generated by applying 16 different orientations and three capture methods (Normal, Hidden-Line, and Wireframe) to all six types. Artificial intelligence models for image classification

For image classification, the neural network models ResNeXt, and EfficientNet were considered. EfficientNet is an image classification and object detection model using compound scaling, which uniformly scales the model's depth, width, and resolution – important factors for performance improvement. Width refers to the number of channels in the feature map, depth refers to the number of layers, and resolution refers to the resolution of the input image and feature map [15]. The ResNeXt model for image classification and object detection addresses the vanishing gradients problem by adding the original input value to the output. ResNeXt compensates for performance degradation caused by vanishing or exploding gradients, which can occur when stacking a network with great width or depth [16].

And we considered two machine learning models, XGBoost and Support Vector Machine(SVM). XGBoost is a machine learning methodology that utilizes gradient boosting to prevent overfitting in data provided across various domains. XGBoost is an algorithm that sequentially trains multiple decision trees and averages the predictions of each tree to generate the final prediction [17].

Meanwhile, SVM is a supervised learning model for data analysis, primarily used for classification and regression analysis. Given a set of data belonging to one of two categories, the SVM algorithm creates a non-probabilistic, binary, linear classification model that determines which category a new data point belongs to based on the provided dataset [18]. Those models were anticipated to be able to classify the dataset used in the research with high accuracy.

For the experiment a total of 12,432 images were divided according to View and image type. Then, 80% of the images were designated as training data and the remaining 20% were utilized as test data to evaluate the model's generalization performance. The input image size was set to 384 × 384. Models were then applied to find the optimal classification conditions.

Evaluation metrics

Performance evaluation metrics are necessary to verify how to evaluate model was trained. In this case, Accuracy and F1-score, which are evaluation metrics for classification, were considered.

Accuracy is the most basic evaluation metric representing the proportion of samples that the model correctly predicted. It calculates the ratio of correctly classified predictions among all predictions. However, its reliability as a model evaluation metric can be misleading under class imbalance. To address this issue, precision and recall are used. Precision is the proportion of predicted positives that are truly positive. Recall is the proportion of actual positives correctly identified. This metric signifies how well the model identifies the positives out of all actual positives. F1-score is used to consider a classification model's precision and recall performance simultaneously. F1-score is defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and the closer it is to 1, the better the classification performance is. XGBoost achieved an image classification accuracy of 25.0% and an F1-Score of 0.25. The SVM model yielded an image classification accuracy of 23.7% and an F1-score of 0.24. The performance of the two models is similar, but neither demonstrated high accuracy in image classification.

In Normal view, the EfficientNet model achieved an accuracy of 90.35% and an F1-score of 0.9771. In Normal view with Hidden-Line, the EfficientNet model showed an accuracy of 90.71% and an F1-score of 0.9738. In Wireframe View, it recorded the best results, with an accuracy of 94.08% and an F1-score of 0.9843. We also considered the combination of different views to evaluate performance. First, with the Normal View and Normal View with Hidden Line, the EfficientNet model achieved an accuracy of 91.56% and an F1-score of 0.9855. For the Normal View and Wireframe View, the EfficientNet model achieved an accuracy of 79.73% and an F1-score of 0.8759. With the Normal View with Hidden Line and Wireframe View, the EfficientNet model achieved an accuracy of 95.05% and an F1-score of 0.8051. Finally, with all views combined, the EfficientNet model achieved an accuracy of 68.85% and an F1-score of 0.6895.

We also evaluated the ResNeXt model. In Normal View, the ResNeXt model achieved an accuracy of 87.93% and an F1-score of 0.9408. In Normal View with Hidden Line, the ResNeXt model showed an accuracy of 92.76% and an F1-score of 0.9794. In Wireframe View, it recorded the best results, with an accuracy of 93.96% and an F1-score of 0.9867. And, with the Normal view and Normal view with Hidden Line, the ResNeXt model achieved an accuracy of 91.43% and an F1-score of 0.7336. For the Normal View and Wireframe View, the ResNeXt model achieved an accuracy of 96.26% and an F1-score of 0.7308. With the Normal View with Hidden Line and Wireframe View, the ResNeXt model achieved an accuracy of 79.85% and an F1-score of 0.5642. Finally, with all views combined, the ResNeXt model achieved an accuracy of 85.04% and an F1-score of 0.4474.

Based on these results, our current dataset analysis shows that the ResNeXt model using the Normal View and Wireframe View combination yielded the best accuracy. ResNeXt model using the Wireframe view combination yielded the best performance to predict F1-score. We expect to achieve even higher accuracy and performance in the future by modifying the training methods with the existing dataset compared to machine learning. Especially, the deep learning method using ResNeXt demonstrated high performance while maintaining a good balance in prediction between classes across the three capture methods for aircraft part image classification.

Unlike manufacturing digitization methods that rely on specialized data acquisition systems and complex technologies, the classification of forming tool types for aircraft parts using deep learning models can be more readily applied to on-site settings than machine learning models. The proposed method is expected to be further applied to process prediction or utilized in similar applications in the future. This dataset can be used to analyze aircraft component development using deep learning models and will be useful for researchers working on image-based aerospace component development, an area of growing interest. Furthermore, it can be used to reduce the time and cost associated with high-mix, low-volume, aircraft tool forming., which involves high-mix low-volume production, and enable faster processing of tasks.

Limitations

The CAD data used for image capture were obtained from a study of aerospace sheet-metal parts, and additional CAD data were generated in accordance with relevant design guidelines. Although the CAD data were created to reflect the geometric features of actual aircraft parts, a limitation of this dataset is the limited number of available CAD models/datasets due to research constraints. In practical applications, class-imbalance risk requires attention, as the distribution of classification types may vary. Additionally, further validation and experimentation with actual CAD data of aircraft parts under diverse environmental conditions are necessary to ensure the reliability and applicability of the approach. This dataset is based on the idea that the outer shape of the forming tool influences the flange shape. Therefore, the expert judgment of engineers with domain knowledge plays a crucial role in labeling and defining classifications.
