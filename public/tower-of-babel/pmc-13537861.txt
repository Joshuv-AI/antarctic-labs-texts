Artificial Intelligence-Derived CT Fractional Flow Reserve: Implementation Challenges Beyond Accuracy in Resource-Constrained Healthcare Systems
Authors: Andrea Carolina Munive, Juan Camilo Mora Vergara, Daniela Otalvaro
Published: 2026

We read with considerable interest the editorial by Kim et al. [1], in which the authors discuss the practical barriers to the real-world implementation of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven computed tomography-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR). They appropriately highlight threshold calibration, automation failure, reimbursement, and workflow integration as key determinants of successful adoption. We believe their analysis also raises a broader issue that extends beyond CT-FFR and has important implications for the future implementation of AI-enabled cardiovascular imaging.

Historically, the development and validation of AI tools have focused primarily on diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. However, as demonstrated by experience with CT-FFR, excellent diagnostic accuracy does not necessarily translate into widespread clinical adoption [2,3]. The barriers to implementation described by Kim et al. [1] indicate that healthcare systems may be entering a new phase of AI evaluation, in which operational feasibility becomes as important as algorithmic performance. Indeed, accumulating evidence suggests that the clinical impact of AI depends not only on technical validity but also on its successful integration into existing healthcare workflows and clinical decision-making pathways [2,3,4,5].

Particularly noteworthy in this regard is the distinction between diagnostic failure and automation failure, as emphasized by the authors. The reported challenges relating to coronary calcification, stent artifacts, and segmentation errors do not necessarily reflect limitations of the physiological model but rather vulnerabilities within the imaging-to-analysis workflow [1]. This observation highlights the need to move beyond traditional validation frameworks by incorporating measures of workflow robustness, automation success rates, processing time, user interaction requirements, and resource utilization into future assessments of AI-based technologies. Recent studies on the real-world monitoring of AI systems have likewise emphasized the importance of continuous performance evaluation, operational monitoring, and implementation-focused metrics after deployment [4]. Such implementation-oriented metrics may ultimately prove more relevant than incremental improvements in diagnostic accuracy when determining real-world values [3,4].

The discussion regarding institution-specific threshold calibration is also highly relevant. Although local calibration may improve clinical utility within individual healthcare systems, it raises important questions regarding the generalizability of AI-derived physiological biomarkers. As healthcare organizations increasingly seek scalable AI solutions, balancing local optimization with standardization will become progressively more essential. However, excessive reliance on institution-specific calibration could inadvertently introduce variability in clinical decision-making across institutions and regions, potentially limiting broader adoption and interoperability [1].

A further important consideration is the economic and infrastructural dimension of implementation. Although CT-FFR has shown the potential to reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, the costs associated with software acquisition, maintenance, regulatory compliance, technical support, and workflow adaptation remain substantial [1]. Consequently, future real-world studies should evaluate not only clinical outcomes but also implementation-related outcomes, including accessibility, scalability, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness.

From a Latin American perspective, the barriers identified by Kim et al. [1] may represent only the visible tip of a broader implementation challenge. Discussions surrounding AI-based CT-FFR often assume the existence of an advanced cardiac CT infrastructure, integrated digital ecosystems, dedicated post-processing capabilities, and stable reimbursement pathways. However, in low- and middle-income countries, these prerequisites remain inconsistently available among many healthcare systems. In such settings, implementation readiness may become a more fundamental determinant of adoption than algorithmic performance. As Mollura et al. [6] have noted, the promise of AI in global radiology will not be realized without parallel investments in infrastructure, workforce development, and sustainable deployment models. Similarly, Marey et al. [7] have recently emphasized that technical validation alone is insufficient to ensure successful implementation in resource-constrained environments. Consequently, whereas the challenges described by Kim et al. [1] focus on optimizing the use of CT-FFR within established healthcare systems, many institutions in Latin America continue to face the more fundamental challenge of building the technological and organizational foundations necessary to facilitate the initial deployment of such tools.

Ultimately, the experience with AI-based CT-FFR may serve as a valuable model for the broader translation of AI into clinical practice. Future research should move beyond diagnostic accuracy and place greater emphasis on implementation science, including workflow integration, economic sustainability, automation robustness, and healthcare-system readiness. Without addressing these dimensions, even highly accurate technologies may remain confined to technically capable centers rather than achieving a meaningful population-level impact. As Kim et al. [1] elegantly demonstrate, the future success of AI in medical imaging will depend not only on algorithm performance but also on whether healthcare systems have the infrastructure, resources, and implementation readiness to translate innovation into clinical impact.
