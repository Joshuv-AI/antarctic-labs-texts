High-dimensional learning of narrow neural networks
Hugo Cui

Abstract
Recent years have been marked with the fast-pace diversification and increasing ubiquity of machine learning applications. Yet, a firm theoretical understanding of the surprising efficiency of neural networks to learn from high-dimensional data still proves largely elusive. In this endeavour, analyses inspired by statistical physics have proven instrumental, enabling the tight asymptotic characterization of the learning of neural networks in high dimensions, for a broad class of solvable models. This manuscript reviews the tools and ideas underlying recent progress in this line of work. We introduce a generic model – the sequence multi-index model–, which encompasses numerous previously studied models as special instances. This unified framework covers a broad class of machine learning architectures with a finite number of hidden units – including multi-layer perceptrons, autoencoders, attention mechanisms–, and tasks –(un)supervised learning, denoising, contrastive learning–, in the limit of large data dimension, and comparably large number of samples. We explicate in full detail the analysis of the learning of sequence multi-index models, using statistical physics techniques such as the replica method and approximate message-passing algorithms. This manuscript thus provides a unified presentation of analyses reported in several previous works, and a detailed overview of central techniques in the field of statistical physics of machine learning. This review should be a useful primer for machine learning theoreticians curious of statistical physics approaches; it should also be of value to statistical physicists interested in the transfer of such ideas to the study of neural networks.

1
Basic concepts in machine learning

1.1
Why machine learning theory?

ML is, above all, a collection of techniques and tools in the statistical processing of large quantities of data. It thus constitutes a branch of engineering that has, as such, historically progressed empirically, by trial and error. So why theory for ML?
As Leo Breiman
[
30
]
summarizes : theory is desirable for "comfort (We knew it worked, but it’s nice to have a proof); insight: (Aha! So that’s why it works!); innovation: (At last, a mathematically proven idea that applies to data); suggestion: (Something like this might work with data)." As such,
machine learning theory
is a valuable companion field to machine learning practice. It is also a very much essential guard-rail for the safe development of the field.
With the unremitting pace of practical advances and the increasing ubiquity of ML tools in all fields – including sensitive ones such as health – a trial-and-error approach is no longer sustainable, and a more principled basis for the development of ML is largely called for. A thorough mathematical theory of ML is, to that end, a requisite – both to ensure a safe use of current tools, and to guide and inspire the development of novel methods.

Section
1.2
provides a concise introduction to ML as a
mathematical object
of study, highlighting in particular how it can be formulated as a random optimization problem in high dimensions.
It is primarily addressed to a physics audience seeking a simple and concise introduction to basic ideas in ML, and may be skipped by readers with a higher degree of familiarity with the field.
Section
2
and
3
then illustrates, on a simple case study, how such problems can be analyzed using ideas borrowed from statistical physics. This analysis is finally pedagogically presented in section

1.2
The machine learning pipeline

ML is a discipline concerned with
automating complex tasks
– by which it is understood tasks that admit no direct simple mathematical formulation –, through the statistical processing of
data
. In its barest form, the ML pipeline consists of approximating a mapping
f_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
from the
input data

\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
to its
target value
(label)
f_{\star}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{Y}
. In practice, for example, the input/label pair
\bm{x},f_{\star}(\bm{x})
can be a sentence and its translation, an image and its resolution-enhanced version, an image and its caption, or a pair of physical attributes.
Because
f_{\star}
admits in most settings no known closed-form mathematical or algorithmic formulation which can be readily coded and implemented by a computer, the ML pipeline rather aims at
approximately
implementing the target function
f_{\star}
. To that end, ML techniques seek to leverage an informative representation (
feature map
)
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
, which transforms and processes the original data point
\bm{x}
into a more informative set of
features

\varphi(\bm{x})
, and subsequently approximating the target
f_{\star}
typically as a linear combination
f_{w}=\bm{w}^{\top}\varphi(\bm{x}),

with the
weights vector

\bm{w}
collecting the corresponding coefficients. On an intuitive level, the features
\varphi(\bm{x})
should thus regroup the important characteristics and attributes of the data point
\bm{x}
in the context of the task.

The choice of the feature transformation
\varphi
is, for some applications, natural. Consider for instance the case of images, for which wavelet transforms offer a concise and informative representation, capturing the information encoded at multiple scales
[
119
]
. In practice, however, there could exist more efficient feature extraction maps
\varphi
; further, in some other cases, as for natural language data, there exists no natural off-the-shelf transform candidate. It thus proves convenient to also seek an efficient representation
\varphi
inside a
parametric family

\{\varphi_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
. Allowing the feature extractor
\varphi_{\theta}
to be
trainable
(
learnable
) is the driving paradigm behind modern successes of ML techniques. The learning problem thus consists in finding the best function
f_{\hat{w},\hat{\theta}}
among the parametric family
\{f_{w,\theta}=\bm{w}^{\top}\varphi_{\theta}(\cdot)\}_{w,\theta}
. In the context of ML, the vector
\bm{w}
is referred to as the
readout weights
, and we shall sometimes refer to the feature map parameters
\theta
as the
internal weights
of the model. The selected values of these parameters
\hat{\bm{w}},\hat{\theta}
are usually called the
trained
–or
learnt
– weights.

To find satisfactory weights, ML techniques first seek to find a good approximation of the target
f_{\star}
on a set of points
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{x}^{\mu},f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
for which the target value is known. The set
\mathcal{D}
is called the
training set
, and is used as an empirical proxy for the true data distribution
P_{x}
. Mathematically, this procedure boils down to minimizing a notion of distance between the parametric function
f_{w,\theta}
and the target
f_{\star}
at all points of
\mathcal{D}
, which is referred to as the
empirical

risk
(or
loss
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w},\theta)=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\ell\left(f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu}),f_{\bm{w},\theta}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\right)+g\left(\bm{w},\theta\right).

(1)

\ell(\cdot)
is a function that generically increases when its two arguments are dissimilar;
g(\cdot)
is a –typically convex–
regularizer
, which penalizes too large weight parameters. Satisfactory values for the model weights can then be selected as minimizers of the risk (
1
)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{w}},\hat{\theta}=\underset{\bm{w},\theta}{\mathrm{argmin}}~\mathcal{R}(\bm{w},\theta).

(2)

In practice, this Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) is numerically carried out using first-order Gradient-Descent (GD) based methods. The quality of the minimization (
2
) is measured by the
training error

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\ell_{\mathrm{tr.}}\left(f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu}),f_{\hat{w},\hat{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\right),

(3)

for some metric
\ell_{\mathrm{tr.}}(\cdot)
, which quantifies the distance between the target
f_{\star}
and the fitted parametric function
f_{\hat{\bm{w}},\hat{\theta}}
on the points of the train set
\mathcal{D}
. Typically, in
regression
settings where the target
f_{\star}
takes continuous values – e.g.
\mathcal{Y}={\mathbb{R}}
–, a popular choice is the squared error
\ell_{\mathrm{tr.}}(y,z)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(y-z)^{2}
. In classification settings – e.g.
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,+1\}
–, a natural choice is the misclassification error
\ell_{\mathrm{tr.}}(y,z)=1-\delta_{y,\text{sign}(z)}
, which measures the fraction of misclassified training samples. At the end of the training, the statistician thus has access to an approximate implementation
f_{\hat{w},\hat{\theta}}
of the target map
f_{\star}
, which can be in turn readily applied to fresh data. The discrepancy between the true target and its learnt approximation is quantified by the
test error

\displaystyle\epsilon_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{x}}\left[\ell_{\mathrm{ts.}}\left(f_{\star}(\bm{x}),f_{\hat{w},\hat{\theta}}(\bm{x})\right)\right],

(4)

for some choice of metric
\ell_{\mathrm{ts.}}
. The test error (
4
) measures the discrepancy between the target
f_{\star}
and the fitted parametric function
f_{\hat{\bm{w}},\hat{\theta}}
on fresh, unseen
test data
, which do not belong to the training data
\mathcal{D}
. It thus constitutes a central metric in ML to evaluate the
generalization ability
of the learnt models.

1.3
Some machine learning models

Choosing – and parametrizing– a suitable feature transformation
\varphi
is thus the centerpiece of the ML pipeline.
The development of the field has thus unsurprisingly gone hand-in-hand with a swift expansion of the zoology of possible feature map options.
In this subsection, we offer a selected digest of some standard choices, starting first from off-the-shelf fixed transforms, and secondly tunable NN feature maps.

1.3.1
Off-the-shelf feature maps

No feature map–

The simplest case is when the input
\bm{x}
is used as is, with no further transformation, namely
\varphi(\bm{x})=\bm{x}
. The corresponding models
f_{w}(\bm{x})=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{x}
are commonly regrouped under the umbrella of
(generalized) linear models
(GLMs) in ML, and include canonical algorithms such as

•

Ridge regression
\ell(y,z)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(y-z)^{2},g(\bm{w})=\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}\lVert\bm{w}\lVert^{2}
,

•

Logistic regression
\ell(y,z)=\ln(1+e^{-yz})
,

•

Hinge regression
\ell(y,z)=(1-yz)_{+}
.

Because of their simplicity and their convexity, linear methods are very easy to train. A key limitation however lies in that they can only implement linear functions of the data, and thus suffer from poor
expressivity
. In other words, they do not provide a versatile enough framework to approximate complex targets
f_{\star}
.

Kernel feature maps–

Kernel methods constitute another centerpiece of traditional ML. Given a kernel
K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the Moore–Aronszajn theorem ensures that the bilinear operation it defines can be rewritten in scalar product form

\displaystyle K(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=\langle\varphi(\bm{x}_{1}),\varphi(\bm{x}_{2})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(5)

where the
kernel feature map

\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
maps the data in non-linear fashion to a typically large (or infinite) dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Kernel learning then simply corresponds to learning using the thus defined feature map
\varphi
. Despite their relative simplicity, kernels thus provide a versatile framework to learn using non-linear features, while remaining in the realm of convex optimization. Furthermore, representer theorems such as
[
99
]
imply that kernel methods can be efficiently trained, even as they tap into the expressivity of an infinite feature space.

Random Features (RF) –

The closely related class of RF models were first introduced in
[
157
]
as an efficient way to approximate kernel methods. They can alternatively be seen as NNs at initialization. Mathematically, a depth
L
RF feature map is defined as the composition of maps

\displaystyle\varphi=\psi_{L}\circ\dots\circ\psi_{1}

(6)

where the
\ell-
th layer

\psi_{\ell}
is the map

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell}(\bm{x}_{\ell-1})=\sigma_{\ell}(W_{\ell}\bm{x}_{\ell-1}).

(7)

In (
7
),
\sigma_{\ell}(\cdot)
is a non-linear function, and
W_{\ell}
, referred to as the
\ell-th
layer weights, is a
fixed
–not trained– random matrix. The first dimension of
W_{\ell}
defines the
width
of the
\ell-
th hidden layer. Aside from their connection to kernel methods, RF models provide stylized proxies for NNs, and thus afford an ideal theoretical sandbox to analytically probe some properties of the latter.

1.3.2
Tunable feature maps

While ready-to-use feature maps provide computationally efficient pathways to enhance the model expressivity, the features they extract are not tailored to the data and task, and may prove sub-optimal. On the other hand, trainable maps such as Neural Network (NN) feature maps are themselves parametric and thus allow for additional tunability and versatility.

Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)–

Historically, the first instance of NN is provided by the work of
[
168
]
on the
perceptron
– namely, a single-layer neural network in modern nomenclature. NNs
[
105
]
build expressive feature maps by essentially stacking perceptron neurons into layers, and subsequently stacking several layers to construct deep
architectures
. A depth
L
MLP is thus defined as the composition of maps

\displaystyle\varphi_{W_{1},...,W_{L}}=\psi^{(L)}_{W_{L}}\circ\dots\circ\psi^{(1)}_{W_{1}},

(8)

where the
\ell-
th layer
\psi^{(\ell)}_{W_{\ell}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\psi^{(\ell)}_{W_{\ell}}(\bm{x}_{\ell-1})=\sigma_{\ell}(W_{\ell}\bm{x}_{\ell-1}).

(9)

Here, the weights
W_{1},...,W_{L}
are importantly
learnable
parameters. While MLPs are typically well suited to process vector data
\mathcal{X}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, convolutional architectures
[
66
]
are specifically designed to extract features from image data, leveraging convolutional and downsampling layers to take into account invariances inherent to this type of data.

Autoencoders (AE) –

AEs represent specific instances of NNs designed for self-supervised and denoising tasks, typically when the target and input spaces coincide
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}
. In its simplest two-layer instance, an AE
f_{w,\theta}(\bm{x})=\bm{w}\varphi_{\theta}(\bm{x})
is the succession of an
encoder
feature map
\varphi_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{b}
and a
decoder
layer with weights
\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times b}
. Standard AEs display a
bottleneck
structure, with the hidden layer width
b
being small. This enforces that the encoder learns a concise low-dimensional representation of the data, which can subsequently be mapped back into the original space by the decoder. Because AEs learn compact, thus a priori robust, latent representations, they are popular choices in denoising applications
[
198
]
. AEs, and related modern denoiser architectures such as U-nets
[
167
]
, have further enjoyed a recent regain in interest as they find themselves at the heart of diffusion-based generative models
[
184
,
85
]
.

Transformers –

Transformers
[
197
]
offer an efficient way of extracting features from sequential data – such as language. Given an input
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
of length
L
, a transformer feature map usually corresponds to a composition of a MLP and an
attention layer
, defined as the map

\displaystyle\varphi_{W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}}=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\bm{x}W_{Q}W_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}\right)\bm{x}W_{V},

(10)

parametrized by the three trainable matrices
W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}
. As for MLPs, the value matrix
W_{V}
acts at the level of the token representations to build more informative features therefrom. Simultaneously, the
L\times L
attention score matrix contextualizes each token, by mixing the sequence in an input-aware fashion. Crucially, transformer architectures are scalable, requiring less training time than recurrent network architectures such as long-short term memory networks
[
10
]
.

1.4
Challenges and open questions

Despite the evident successes of day-to-day ML empirics, the field arguably still lacks a solid theoretical foundation. In fact, fundamental questions regarding the unreasonable effectiveness of statistical models such as NNs in approximating complex, typically high-dimensional functions have remained unanswered for decades. The interrogations raised by
[
30
]
in the
90
s –
Why don’t heavily parametrized neural networks overfit the data? […] Why doesn’t backpropagation head for poor local minima?
– still remain of striking relevance in modern ML theory. On a technical level, prominent hurdles in answering those interrorgations lie in the need to analyze
high-dimensional
,
non-linear
,
non-convex
optimization problems. On a conceptual level, learning in NNs arises from the collective effect of very large numbers of variables – the constitutive neurons– in interaction, a complexity that makes the characterization thereof difficult on several levels. These challenges, on the other hand, directly fall under the orbit of statistical physics techniques.

2
Statistical physics of neural networks

2.1
Statistical physics in the ML researchscape

The birth of statistical physics can be traced back to the seminal works of Maxwell, Gibbs and Boltzmann in the
19
th century, which were concerned with the study of emergent collective behaviours arising from the microscopic interaction of large assemblies of particles. More formally, the statistical physicist aims at a concise description of high-dimensional probability measures arising from large systems in interaction, in terms of a compact set of
macroscopic
observables. Connecting to the previous section, learning in NNs also results from the complex interactions between a large number of variables –the eponymous neurons– as they are jointly optimized to minimize the ERM loss. Perhaps then unsurprisingly, ML, as a field concerned with random optimization problems in high dimensions, naturally falls in the orbit of statistical physics techniques.

Statistical physics ideas were first successfully applied to sharply characterize the learning curve of perceptron models by Gardner
[

,

[

(see
[

These seminal advances were accompanied by the development of powerful analysis techniques, based on the replica method

These early works made it patent that the worst case, Probably Almost Correct (PAC) viewpoint on ML

As a part of theoretical physics, statistical physics analyses of ML are almost always model-driven, taking as a triple starting point:

(a)

A specific data distribution;

(b)

A specific target function generating the labels, if the task is supervised;

(c)

A specific learning model and training procedure;

and aiming at

Such

While much remains to be understood, recent years have been marked by a growing realization of the need to analyze tasks and NN architectures beyond the supervised MLP exemplar, dictated by the need to mirror the rapid diversification of learning paradigms and models in ML practice – with new architectures (e.g. transformers), learning paradigms (e.g. diffusion-based models) or data types (e.g. language). In other words, the portfolio of analyzable exactly solvable models represented in Fig.

3.1
Model definition

The sequence multi-index model provides a simplified, yet rich, model for a neural network
f_{\bm{w}}
with
r
hidden units, parametrized by
\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times r}
, learning from a train set
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{x}^{\mu},y^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
. The latter is assumed to comprise
n
i.i.d. samples
\bm{x}^{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
, and the corresponding labels
y^{\mu}
, if a supervised setting is considered. We sequentially describe the distribution of the data
\bm{x}
, and the training procedure of the network weights
\bm{w}
.

Data model –

The sequence multi-index model is a model of NNs learning from sequential data. Each sample
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
is a
L\times d
matrix, viewed as a length-
L
sequence of
d-
dimensional
tokens
(rows)
\{\bm{x}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. For example, in the context of language data,
L
would correspond to the length of the sentence, while
d
represents the dimension of the space in which its constitutive words are embedded. As we will expound below, we will focus in this review on the limit of large embedding dimension
d\to\infty
, while keeping the sequence length finite, namely
L=\Theta_{d}(1)
. In order to design a simple model, we assume that each token
\bm{x}_{\ell}
is drawn from a Gaussian mixture distribution of
K_{\ell}
clusters

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{\ell},c_{\ell}\sim\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\rho_{\ell,k}\delta_{c_{\ell},k}\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\ell,k},\Sigma_{\ell,k}),

(11)

where
\rho_{\ell,k}\in[0,1]
indicates the relative probability of each cluster, and the companion random variable
c_{\ell}\in\llbracket 1,K_{\ell}\rrbracket
indicates which cluster from the mixture a particular sample is drawn from. In particular, the law of
c_{\ell}
is specified by
\rho_{\ell,1},..,\rho_{\ell,K_{\ell}}
. This distribution affords a simple model for a large class of practical setups. For example, the different clusters may correspond to different grammatical classes – verb, pronoun, adjective – if
\bm{x}_{\ell}
is thought of as a word; it can model different types of objects if
\bm{x}_{\ell}
represents a part of a composite picture. Therefore, on a conceptual level, each token is drawn from a vocabulary of
K_{\ell}
classes, while the randomness and variability within a given class further model the idiosyncrasies of the token (e.g. which exact adjective among the class of all adjectives). Finally, we assume that the class assignments
c=\{c_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
of the
L
different tokens
\{\bm{x}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
are correlated, and follow a joint probability distribution
\rho_{c}
over
\mathcal{K}\equiv\llbracket 1,K_{1}\rrbracket\times\dots\times\llbracket 1,K_{L}\rrbracket
. We denoted
c\in\mathcal{K}
the collection of the
L
cluster assignments
c=\{c_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. For instance, in sentences, two consecutive words are seldom from the same grammatical class. Arbitrarily complex class interactions and statistics – e.g. hierarchical, random – can thus be encoded in
\rho_{c}
.

Finally, while the analysis is detailed for a finite number of classes
K_{\ell}
for ease of presentation, the model – and its analysis– seamlessly generalizes to the infinite cluster limit
K_{\ell}\to\infty
. In this limit, the variable
c_{\ell}
gains a continuous support
(0,\infty)
. This limit is of particular interest, since particular classes of elliptic heavy-tailed distribution
[
18
]
can be constructed as infinite superposition of Gaussian clusters, as for example leveraged in
[
2
]
. The data distribution (
11
) thus also includes heavy-tailed (rather than Gaussian) token distributions as a special limiting case. As we further explicate in subsection
3.2
, the data distribution (
11
) encompasses as special cases a large class of data models considered in previous works, including : isotropic unimodal Gaussians
[
41
,
8
,
7
]
, structured unimodal Gaussians
[
111
,
54
,
176
]
, (colored) Gaussian mixtures
[
128
,
113
,
50
]
, sequences of Gaussian tokens
[
43
,
112
]
, and heavy-tailed distributions
[
2
,
1
]
.

Empirical Risk Minimization –

Given the training set
\mathcal{D}
, we are interested in training a neural network parametrized by a set of weights
\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times r}
– with
d
the dimension of the tokens
\bm{x}_{\ell}
and the width
r
representing the number of hidden units of the network – to complete a given task. As is standard in ML, this can be enforced by an ERM of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{w}}=\underset{\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times r}}{\mathrm{argmin}}~\mathcal{R}(\bm{w}).

(12)

The sequence multi-index model corresponds to empirical risks of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\ell\left(\frac{\bm{x}^{\mu}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\bm{x}^{\mu}\bm{w}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{w}}{d},c^{\mu}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}\lVert^{2}.

(13)

We introduced a loss function
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times t}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times r}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}\times\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. This loss function takes as an argument, in particular, the cluster assignment
c^{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
of the
\mu-
th sample, and is allowed to depend on the self-product
\nicefrac{{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{w}}}{{d}}
of the weights
\bm{w}
. This latter feature shall prove convenient, as we shall cover in the following, for the analysis of AE architectures. Aside from the product
\nicefrac{{\bm{x}\bm{w}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
between the data
\bm{x}
and the weights
\bm{w}
, the loss function
\ell
also takes as an argument the product
\nicefrac{{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
. In (
13
), the
target weights

\bm{w}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times t}
represent the parameters of the target function
f_{\star}
(see also (
1
)), when the latter is parametric, and intervenes when considering supervised tasks. In particular, in settings where the target function is itself of NN form – sometimes called
teacher-student
settings in statistical physics
[
205
,
67
]
–, the matrix
\bm{w}_{\star}
represent the weights of the target network. Finally, the last term of (
13
) corresponds to a
\ell_{2}
regularization term. We shall discuss in subsection
3.2
how many problems explored in previous works can be rewritten in the generic form (
13
), and thus fall under the umbrella of the sequence multi-index model .

As in previous sections, the performance of the learnt model is characterized by two particular metrics of interest, namely the training loss

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}=\mathcal{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}),

(14)

and the generalization error

\displaystyle\epsilon_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\ell_{\mathrm{tst.}}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\bm{x}\hat{\bm{w}}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\hat{\bm{w}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{w}}}{d},c\right).

(15)

We note that the expressions (
13
) and (
15
) are admittedly fairly abstract in form. In particular, all the details on the architecture, activations, loss function, form of the target function are subsumed and hidden inside the functions
\ell,\ell_{\mathrm{tst.}}
. In exchange, this rather terse formulation allows for a compact and unique expression that captures a very broad class of settings as special cases, as further discussed in subsection
3.2
. In the following subsection, we detail many setups of special interest which fall under the umbrella of the formulation (
13
). Before doing so, however, we complete the formal statement of the model by specifying the high-dimensional scaling in which the learning problem (
13
) shall be tightly characterized.

\alpha=\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}=\Theta_{d}(1)

are set. Furthermore, we assume that for any

and

, the mean of the

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\prod\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\delta\left(\text{\char 21\relax}_{i}^{\ell,k}-\gamma_{\ell,k}\right)\delta\left(\sqrt{d}\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{\ell,k}-\tau_{\ell,k}\right)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{t}\delta\left(\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{w}_{\star})_{j}-\pi_{j}\right)\xrightarrow{d\to\infty}\nu\left(\gamma,\tau,\pi\right).

Model –

(

with

the encoder (resp. decoder) weights and

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{R}}(b,\bm{w}_{e},\bm{w}_{d})=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\left\lVert f_{b,\bm{w}_{e},\bm{w}_{d}}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})-{\bm{z}}^{\mu}\right\lVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{e}\lVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{d}\lVert^{2}.

(19)

(

Mapping to a sequence multi-index model –

Let us now show how the data and network model specified in (
17
), (
18
) and (
19
) fall in the framework of the sequence multi-index model , starting from the data model. Let us fix
L=2
and define
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times d}
as the stacked input with rows
\bm{x}_{1}={\bm{z}}
and
\bm{x}_{2}={\bm{\xi}}
. It is immediate to see that the distribution of
\bm{x}
is a particular case of that defined around (
11
), with in particular
K_{1}=K,K_{2}=1
, and the cluster assignment variables
c_{1}
and

being uncorrelated. Bringing (
19
) into the form (
13
) requires slightly more work. Expanding the square loss, one reaches

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{R}}(b,\bm{w}_{e},\bm{w}_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}(1-b)^{2}\lVert{\bm{z}}^{\mu}\lVert^{2}+b^{2}\lVert{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu}\lVert^{2}-b(1-b){\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\left[\sigma\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{e}^{\top}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\top}\frac{\bm{w}_{d}^{\top}\bm{w}_{d}}{d}\sigma\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{e}^{\top}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)-2\frac{((1-b){\bm{z}}^{\mu}-b{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})^{\top}\bm{w}_{d}}{\sqrt{d}}\sigma\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{e}^{\top}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{e}\lVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{d}\lVert^{2}.

(20)

Note that the first line regroups terms scaling like
\Theta_{d}(d^{2})
and
\Theta_{d}(d^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}})
, and furthermore only involves

as the sole trainable parameter, while the terms regrouped in the second and third lines only are
\Theta_{d}(1)
. Therefore, the minimization problem for

is dominated by the first term, which by the law of large numbers is to leading order asymptotically equal to

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{R}}(b,\bm{w})\asymp nd\left((1-b)^{2}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}\int d\nu(\gamma,\tau,\pi)\gamma_{k}+\Delta b^{2}\right),

(21)

thus imposing that the minimizer

of the denoising risk (

\displaystyle\hat{b}=1-\frac{\Delta}{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}\int d\nu(\gamma,\tau,\pi)\gamma_{k}+\Delta}.

(22)

The minimization of
\bm{w}
then results from the

terms in (

\bm{w}_{e},\bm{w}_{d}
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w}_{e},\bm{w}_{d})=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\left[\sigma\!\!\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{e}^{\top}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\top}\!\!\!\!\!\frac{\bm{w}_{d}^{\top}\bm{w}_{d}}{d}\sigma\!\!\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{e}^{\top}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\!\!-2\frac{((1-\hat{b}){\bm{z}}^{\mu}-\hat{b}{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu}))^{\top}\bm{w}_{d}}{\sqrt{d}}\sigma\!\!\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{e}^{\top}({\bm{z}}^{\mu}+{\bm{\xi}}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{e}\lVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{d}\lVert^{2}.

(23)

To finish the mapping, let us introduce the stacked weight variable

\displaystyle\ell(h_{\star},h,q,c)

\displaystyle=\ell(h,q)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(h_{11}+h_{21}\right)^{\top}q_{22}\sigma\left(h_{11}+h_{21}\right)-2((1-\hat{b})h_{12}-\hat{b}h_{22})^{\top}\sigma\left(h_{11}+h_{21}\right).

(24)

We viewed the argument

(resp

) of

(resp.

\displaystyle\ell(h_{\star},h,q,c)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\left(h,c\right).

(30)

Model–

(
reproduced from
[
111
]
) Consider coupled Gaussian samples
\bm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p},\bm{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as

\displaystyle(\bm{u},\bm{v})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0_{2d},\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Psi&\Phi\\
\Phi^{\top}&\Omega\end{array}\right)\right).

The target function acts on the covariates
\bm{u}
as

\displaystyle f_{\star}(\bm{u})=\phi\left(\frac{\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}}^{\top}\bm{u}}{\sqrt{p}}\right),

(33)

for a given function
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and is parametrized by a set of weights
\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. The neural network, on the other hand, only has access to the covariates
\bm{v}
– rather than the covariates
\bm{u}
utilized in the target function (
33
)– in order to learn the target. Given a train set
\mathcal{D}=\{f_{\star}(\bm{u}^{\mu}),\bm{v}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
, its training can be carried out by minimizing the empirical risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}\left(f_{\star}(\bm{u}^{\mu}),\frac{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\mu}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}\lVert^{2},

(34)

for a set of trainable weights
\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In the special instance of RFs,
\bm{v}
correspond to the eponymous random features. Finally, the dimension
p
is supposed to tend to infinity jointy with

, with a fixed ratio
\gamma=\nicefrac{{p}}{{d}}=\Theta_{d}(1)
.

Mapping to a sequence multi-index model –

Following
[
38
]
, let us first observe that the random variable
\bm{u}
, following the law (
3.2.3
), can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\Phi\Omega^{-1}\bm{v}+(\Psi-\Phi\Omega^{-1}\Phi^{\top})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\zeta},

(35)

where
\bm{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
, assuming
\Omega
is invertible. Therefore, the target function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle f_{\star}(\bm{v})=\phi\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}\bm{v}}{\sqrt{d}}+z\right),

(36)

where we introduced the effective target weights
\bm{w}_{\star}\equiv\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\gamma}}}\Omega^{-1}\Phi^{\top}\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}}^{\top}(\Psi-\Phi\Omega^{-1}\Phi^{\top})\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}})
. Note that one can always rewrite
z=\nicefrac{{\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}\bm{\xi}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
, provided the variance of the newly introduced isotropic Gaussian noise
\bm{\xi}
is matched as
\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\nicefrac{{\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}}^{\top}(\Psi-\Phi\Omega^{-1}\Phi^{\top})\tilde{\bm{w}_{\star}}}}{{\gamma\lVert\bm{w}_{\star}\lVert^{2}}}\mathbb{I}_{d})
. With all these steps, the ERM problem can finally be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}\left(\phi\left(\frac{\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}(\bm{v}^{\mu}+\bm{\xi}^{\mu})}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\frac{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\mu}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}\lVert^{2}.

(37)

The last step of the mapping consists in introducing, the stacked data
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times d}
, with rows
\bm{x}_{1}=\bm{v},\bm{x}_{2}=\bm{\xi}
. This corresponds to the data distribution (
11
) with sequence length
L=2
, and
K_{1}=K_{2}=1
clusters. Finally, the ERM problem (
37

13
) with effective loss

\displaystyle\ell(h_{\star},h,q,c)

\displaystyle=\ell(h_{\star},h)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\left(\phi((h_{\star})_{1}+(h_{\star})_{2}),h\right).

(38)

3.2.4
Dot-product attention
[

[

Model–

(
reproduced from
[
43
]
) Consider sequential data
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
, where each token
\bm{x}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is sampled, independently from the others, from a Gaussian density
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\ell},\Sigma_{\ell})
.
[

f_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
, parametrized by
\bm{w}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times t}
, and given by

\displaystyle f_{\star}(\bm{x})=\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\bm{x},

(39)

where
\mathcal{T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times t}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}
is a matrix-to-matrix mapping. On a conceptual level,
\mathcal{T}\left(\nicefrac{{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\right)
represents the
target attention matrix
, which a student attention layer is tasked to learn. We consider the learning of a dot-product attention layer

\displaystyle f_{\bm{w}_{Q},\bm{w}_{K}}(\bm{x})=\mathrm{softmax}\left[\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right]\bm{x}.

(40)

\bm{w}_{Q},\bm{w}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times r}
are trainable query and key weights. The model is trained by minimizing the quadratic loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w}_{Q},\bm{w}_{K})=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\left\lVert f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu})-f_{\bm{w}_{Q},\bm{w}_{K}}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\right\lVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{Q}\lVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{K}\lVert^{2}.

(41)

The mean
\bm{\mu}_{\ell}
of the
\ell-
the token corresponds in practice to

, which are used to feed to the model positional information about the ordering of the tokens inside a sentence. Note that in the original work
[
43
]
, these encodings are not present in the target function. We choose here to include them for ease of presentation, at the price of a very minimal deviation from the original analysis
[
43
]
.

Mapping to a sequence multi-index model –

Quite straightforwardly, the data distribution corresponds to that of a sequence multi-index model (
11

L

K_{\ell}=1
for

\ell

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu})-f_{\bm{w}_{Q},\bm{w}_{K}}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\right\lVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr[\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\frac{\bm{x}^{\mu}(\bm{x}^{\mu})^{\top}}{d}\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\top}]+\Tr[\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right)\frac{\bm{x}^{\mu}(\bm{x}^{\mu})^{\top}}{d}\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right)^{\top}]

\displaystyle~~~~-2\Tr[\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right)\frac{\bm{x}^{\mu}(\bm{x}^{\mu})^{\top}}{d}\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\top}]

\displaystyle\asymp\Tr[\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\Lambda\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\top}]+\Tr[\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right)\Lambda\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right)^{\top}]

\displaystyle~~~~-2\Tr[\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{Q}\bm{w}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\top}}{d}\right)\Lambda\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\top}].

(42)

We denoted
\Lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}
the diagonal matrix with diagonal elements

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\ell\ell}=\int d\nu(\gamma,\tau,\pi)\gamma_{\ell}.

(43)

Finally, let us introduce the effective total weight matrix
\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times(r+r)}
, defined as the horizontal stack of
\bm{w}_{Q}
and
\bm{w}_{K}
. The sequence multi-index loss function for the dot-product attention model can then be read from (

\displaystyle\ell(h_{\star},h,q,c)

\displaystyle=\ell(h_{\star},h)

\displaystyle=\Tr[\mathrm{softmax}\left(h_{1}h_{2}^{\top}\right)\Lambda\mathrm{softmax}\left(h_{1}h_{2}^{\top}\right)^{\top}]-2\Tr[\mathrm{softmax}\left(h_{1}h_{2}^{\top}\right)\Lambda\mathcal{T}\left(h_{\star}\right)^{\top}].

(44)

We viewed the argument

as a block matrix with two horizontally concatenated blocks

\bm{w}

3.2.5
Siamese networks

Siamese networks
[
31
]
are employed to learn to discriminate positive (similar) pairs of inputs from negative (dissimilar) pairs of inputs, by training two neural networks sharing the same weights – the eponymous siamese networks. For example, positive pairs of inputs can be two images of signatures from the same person, while a negative pair can be an image of a signature, and an imitation thereof by a third party.

Model –

As a simple model for positive and negative input pairs, let us consider samples
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})
, stacked in
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times d}
. We will assume that both inputs
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}
are independently sampled from a binary Gaussian mixture
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\mathbb{I}_{d})+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\mathcal{N}(-\bm{\mu},\mathbb{I}_{d})
, and that positive (negative) pairs correspond to pairs where
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}
were drawn from the same (different) clusters:

\displaystyle f_{\star}(\bm{x})=\delta_{c_{1},c_{2}},

(45)

where we remind that
c_{1},c_{2}\in\{1,2\}
are the cluster assignment. One can train two siamese perceptrons (or committee machines)
f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{1})=\phi(\nicefrac{{\bm{w}^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}}),\phi(\nicefrac{{\bm{w}^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})
, sharing the same weights
\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
but acting on different tokens, to discriminate between positive and negative pairs
\bm{x}
. A standard loss to enforce the training is the
contrastive loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\left[\delta_{f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu}),1}(f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{1}^{\mu})-f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{2}^{\mu}))^{2}+\delta_{f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu}),0}\max(0,\kappa-(f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{1}^{\mu})-f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{2}^{\mu}))^{2})\right]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}\lVert^{2}.

(46)

The contrastive loss (
46
) enforces that the output of the siamese networks should be close for positive pairs, but larger than a margin
\kappa>0
for negative pairs.

Mapping to a sequence multi-index model

The data model is, by construction, of the form (
11
). The sequence multi-index loss function reads

\displaystyle\ell(h_{\star},h,q,c)

\displaystyle=\ell(h,c)

\displaystyle=\delta_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\phi(h_{1})-\phi(h_{2}))^{2}+(1-\delta_{c_{1},c_{2}})\max(0,\kappa-(\phi(h_{1})-\phi(h_{2}))^{2}).

(47)

Again, we denoted by
h_{1},h_{2}
the components of the argument
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

4.1
The replica method

The replica method
[
150
,
151
,
127
]
(see also
[
126
]
for a review) starts from the simple observation that for any test function (observable)
\phi(\hat{\bm{w}})
of the trained weights
\hat{\bm{w}}
– such as the test error
\epsilon_{g}
(
15
) or the training loss
\epsilon_{t}
(
14
)–, one can write its average over the draw of the training set
\mathcal{D}
as the integral

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\phi(\hat{w})=\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{1}{Z}\int d\bm{w}e^{-\beta\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})}\phi(\bm{w}),

(48)

where we introduced the
partition function
(normalization factor)

\displaystyle Z=\int d\bm{w}e^{-\beta\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})}.

(49)

Characterizing the average
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\phi(\hat{w})
is thus tantamount to studying the family of
\beta-
parametrized measures
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}(\bm{w})=\nicefrac{{e^{-\beta\mathcal{R}(\bm{w})}}}{{Z}}
, for different values of the
inverse temperature

\beta>0
. To that end, it is natural to focus on studying the cumulant-generating function

\displaystyle f=-\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\frac{1}{\beta d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\ln Z.

(50)

In statistical physics,
f
is called the
free energy
. Analytically evaluating the logarithm of a random variable
Z
is, however, usually a complex enterprise. The
replica method
builds on the simplifying identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\ln Z=\underset{s\to 0}{\lim}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}Z^{s}-1}{s}

(51)

to map it back to the simpler computation of the moment
Z^{s}
, for a parameter
s\to 0
. Note that
Z^{s}
corresponds to the partition function (normalization) of the product measure of
s
copies (the eponymous
replicas
) of the original problem. The centerpiece of the replica method then lies in the computation of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}Z^{s}
, which we detail below.

The replicated partition function
Z^{s}
reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}Z^{s}

\displaystyle=\int\prod\limits_{a=1}^{s}d\bm{w}_{a}e^{-\beta\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{a}\lVert^{2}}\prod\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}e^{-\beta\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\ell\left(\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\bm{x}\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\lVert\bm{w}_{a}\lVert^{2}}{d},c\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\prod\limits_{a=1}^{s}d\bm{w}_{a}e^{-\beta\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{a}\lVert^{2}}\prod\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{c}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}|c}e^{-\beta\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\ell\left(\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{c})\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{c})\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{w}_{a}}{d},c\right)}\right]

(52)

In the last line, we used the law of total expectation to decompose
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{c}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}|c}
.
We denoted
\bm{\mu}_{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
the matrix with rows
\bm{\mu}_{\ell,c_{\ell}}
.
Observe how all the technically challenging terms – the non-linear loss
\ell
, and the high-dimensional average over the data
\bm{x}
– appear in the bracketed expression in (
52
), which we shall consequently discuss in priority. To simplify the bracketed expression, let us explicitly name the random variables appearing therein as

\displaystyle h^{a}\equiv\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{c})\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times r},

\displaystyle h^{\star}\equiv\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{c})\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times t}.

(53)

We denoted
\mu_{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
the vector of means, with rows
\bm{\mu}_{\ell,c_{\ell}}
. Note that since, conditional on
c
,
\bm{x}
follows Gaussian statistics (
11
), so do
h^{a},h^{\star}
. The rows
\{h^{a}_{\ell},h^{\star}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
of the variables (
53
) are thus fully characterized by their joint Gaussian statistics

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x|c}[h^{a}_{\ell}]=\mathbb{E}_{x|c}[h^{\star}_{\ell}]=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x|c}[h_{\ell}^{a}(h_{\kappa}^{b})^{\top}]=\delta_{\ell\kappa}\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\bm{w}_{b}}{d}\equiv q^{\ell,c_{\ell}}_{ab}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r},

(54)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x|c}[h_{\ell}^{\star}(h_{\kappa}^{\star})^{\top}]=\delta_{\ell\kappa}\frac{\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}\Sigma_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\bm{w}_{\star}}{d}\equiv\rho_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{t\times t},

(55)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x|c}[h_{\ell}^{a}(h_{\kappa}^{\star})^{\top}]=\delta_{\ell\kappa}\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\bm{w}_{\star}}{d}\equiv\theta^{\ell,c_{\ell}}_{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times t}.

(56)

There are three more parameters that appear in the bracketed expression of (
52
) yet to examine, namely
\nicefrac{{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{w}_{a}}}{{d}}
,
\nicefrac{{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{a}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
,
\nicefrac{{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{\star}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
. These parameters do not depend on the data
\bm{x}
, and we shall treat them as order parameters. Let us rename them as

\displaystyle v_{a}=\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{w}_{a}}{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r},

\displaystyle m_{a}^{c}\equiv\frac{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times r},

\displaystyle m_{\star}^{c}\equiv\frac{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times t}.

(57)

We shall further denote the rows of
m^{c}_{a}
(resp.
m^{c}_{\star}
) as
m^{\ell,c_{\ell}}_{a}=\nicefrac{{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\bm{w}_{a}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
(resp.
m^{\ell,c_{\ell}}_{\star}=\nicefrac{{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\bm{w}_{\star}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
). Using all the notations (
53
)(
54
)(
57
) thus defined, the bracketed expression in (
52
) can be rewritten more compactly as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{c}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x|c}e^{-\beta\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\ell\left(\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{c})\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{\star}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{c})\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\bm{\mu}_{c}\bm{w}_{a}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{w}_{a}}{d},c\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{c}\mathbb{E}_{h^{\star},\{h_{a}\}_{a=1}^{s}|c}e^{-\beta\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\ell\left(h_{\star}+m_{\star}^{c},h^{a}+m^{c}_{a},v_{a},c\right)}.

(58)

We thus introduced a
finite
set of
low-dimensional
order parameters
\{q^{\ell,k}_{ab},\rho_{\ell,k},\theta^{\ell,k}_{a},v_{a},m_{a}^{\ell,k},m_{\star}^{\ell,k}\}_{1\leq a,b\leq s,\ell\in[L],k\in[K_{\ell}]}
, that fully characterize the non-trivial bracketed term in the expression of the replicated partition function
Z^{s}
(
52
). These parameters describe some simple statistics of the weights
\bm{w}_{a}
, and, as we shall later discuss, completely characterize
Z^{s}
. To see this, the next natural step is to shift the integration from the high-dimensional weights
\bm{w}_{a}
(
52
) to the low-dimensional parameters
\{q^{\ell,k}_{ab},\rho_{\ell,k},\theta^{\ell,k}_{a},v_{a},m_{a}^{\ell,k},m_{\star}^{\ell,k}\}
. This agenda can be carried out by inserting in (
52
) (Fourier-transformed) Dirac functions enforcing the definitions (
54
) and (

4.1.1
Replica-Symmetric ansatz

We have thus rephrased the analysis of the high-dimensional average (

\displaystyle q^{\ell,k}_{ab}=(r_{\ell,k}-q_{\ell,k})\delta_{ab}+q_{\ell,k},

(61)

\displaystyle m^{\ell,k}_{a}=m_{\ell,k},

(62)

\displaystyle\theta^{\ell,k}_{a}=\theta_{\ell,k},

(63)

\displaystyle v_{a}=v,

\displaystyle\hat{q}^{\ell,k}_{ab}=-\left(\nicefrac{{\hat{r}_{\ell,k}}}{{2}}+\hat{q}_{\ell,k}\right)\delta_{ab}+\hat{q}_{\ell,k},

(65)

\displaystyle\hat{m}^{\ell,k}_{a}=\hat{m}_{\ell,k},

(66)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\ell,k}_{a}=\hat{\theta}_{\ell,k},

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{a}=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{v}.

(68)

The RS ansatz implies the extremization problem associated with the evaluation of (

) now bears over

order parameters, allowing to greatly simplify the remainder of the computation.
Crucially, motivated by the definition of these quantities, we assume that

are symmetric matrices.
In words,
the RS ansatz assumes that the overlaps between any two distinct replicas are identical, and that all replicas further share the same overlap with the target weights. The RS ansatz (

, but remains otherwise an assumption. In addition, the RS ansatz has been bolstered by compelling numerical support in a large majority of settings explored in the literature

where the last equality results from first taking the

characterized by the SP equations (
90

\hat{\bm{w}}
of the ERM problem (
13
), in terms of a set of summary statistics. In practice, the SP equations (

\hline\cr m^{\star}_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\end{pmatrix},\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}q_{\ell,c_{\ell}}&\theta_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\\

4.1.8
Training loss

We finally turn to the other metric of interest, namely the training loss
\epsilon_{t}
(
14

\epsilon_{g}
(

(
90

f
at zero temperature
\beta\to\infty

\epsilon_{t}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}=-\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\partial_{\beta}\underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\ln Z(\beta)}_{\Phi(\beta)}=-\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\partial_{\beta}\left[\Psi_{w}(\beta)+\Psi_{t}(\beta)+\Psi_{y}(\beta)\right],

(99)

where
\Phi(\beta)
is the free entropy at finite temperature, and we emphasized the
\beta-
dependence of the potentials
\Psi_{t},\Psi_{w},\Psi_{y}
. The trace potential
\Psi_{t}
bears no explicit dependence on
\beta
. On the other hand, we remind that the entropic potential reads

\displaystyle\beta\Psi_{w}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2d}\ln\det\left[\beta\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}+\check{V}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2d}\Tr[(\beta\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}+\check{V})^{-1}\odot\left(\left(\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\sqrt{d}\hat{m}_{\ell,k}\bm{\mu}_{\ell,k}^{\top}+\hat{\theta}_{\ell,k}\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}\Sigma_{\ell,k}\right)^{\otimes 2}+\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\hat{q}_{\ell,k}\otimes\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell,k}\right)],

(100)

and thus explicitly depends on
\beta
. Its derivative is straightforwardly given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}(\beta\Psi_{w})=

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda}{2d}\Tr\left[\beta\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}+\check{V}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda}{2d}\Tr[\left(\beta\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}\odot\mathbb{I}_{d}+\check{V}\right)^{-2}\!\!\!\!\odot\left(\left(\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\sqrt{d}\hat{m}_{\ell,k}\bm{\mu}_{\ell,k}^{\top}+\hat{\theta}_{\ell,k}\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}\Sigma_{\ell,k}\right)^{\otimes 2}+\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\hat{q}_{\ell,k}\otimes\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell,k}\right)].

(101)

Finally, going through the same rescaling steps to take the
\beta\to\infty

\displaystyle\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\partial_{\beta}(\beta\Psi_{w})=\!\!-\frac{\lambda}{2d}\!\!\Tr[\left(\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}\odot\mathbb{I}_{d}+\check{V}\right)^{-2}\!\!\!\!\!\!\odot\left(\left(\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\sqrt{d}\hat{m}_{\ell,k}\bm{\mu}_{\ell,k}^{\top}\!\!+\hat{\theta}_{\ell,k}\bm{w}_{\star}^{\top}\Sigma_{\ell,k}\right)^{\otimes 2}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!+\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\hat{q}_{\ell,k}\otimes\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell,k}\right)].

(102)

By the same token, it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\partial_{\beta}(\alpha\beta\Psi_{y})

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{c,Y,\Xi}\left[\mathcal{M}(c,Y,\Xi)-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\Tr[V_{\ell,c_{\ell}}^{-1}\left(\mathrm{prox}^{c}_{\ell}-q_{\ell,c_{\ell}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\xi_{\ell}-m_{\ell,c_{\ell}}\right)^{\otimes 2}]\right]

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{c,Y,\Xi}\mathcal{M}(c,Y,\Xi)+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\Tr[\hat{q}_{\ell,k}V_{\ell,k}].

(103)

We used the self-consistent equations (
90

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle=\underset{\beta\to\infty}{\lim}\partial_{\beta}\Psi(\beta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\!\!\int d\nu(\gamma,\tau,\pi)\Tr[\left(\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}+\hat{v}+\sum\limits_{\kappa}\sum\limits_{k}\gamma_{\kappa,k}\hat{V}_{\kappa,k}\right)^{-2}\left[\left(\sum\limits_{\kappa}\sum\limits_{k}\hat{m}_{\kappa,k}\tau_{\kappa,k}+\gamma_{\kappa,k}\hat{\theta}_{\kappa,k}\pi\right)^{\otimes 2}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!+\sum\limits_{\kappa}\sum\limits_{k}\gamma_{\kappa,k}\hat{q}_{\kappa,k}\right]]

\displaystyle\qquad-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{c,Y,\Xi}\mathcal{M}(c,Y,\Xi)+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\Tr[\hat{q}_{\ell,k}V_{\ell,k}].

(104)

This constitutes a sharp asymptotic characterization of the training loss
\epsilon_{t}
. For completeness, we finally explicit the connection between
\epsilon_{t}
and the negative free entropy (i.e. the

in statistical physics), thus validating the physical intuition that the two quantities should coincide. We go back to massage the expression for the free entropy (
88

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Tr[q_{\ell,k}\hat{V}_{\ell,k}^{\top}-V_{\ell,k}\hat{q}_{\ell,k}^{\top}]-\Tr[\theta_{\ell,k}\hat{\theta}_{\ell,k}^{\top}]-\hat{m}_{\ell,k}^{\top}m_{\ell,k}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Tr[v\hat{v}]-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{c,Y,\Xi}\mathcal{M}(c,Y,\Xi)

(105)

Algorithm 1

rBP

Inputs

\forall 1\leq\mu\leq n,1\leq i\leq d,~~A^{t}_{i\to\mu}=-\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}(x^{\nu}_{\ell i})(x^{\nu}_{\kappa i})g_{\ell\kappa,\nu\to i}^{t}

\forall 1\leq\mu\leq n,1\leq i\leq d,~~b^{t}_{i\to\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}x^{\nu}_{\ell i}f_{\ell,\nu\to i}^{t}

for

4.2.2
State evolution

We provide in this subsection a detailed derivation of the connection between the SP equations (
90
) derived in subsection
4.1.1
and the just introduced GAMP algorithm (
2
). Before doing so, let us sketch in broad strokes the elements of this connection.
As we shall see, all GAMP variables (
2
) are asymptotically either deterministic, or Gaussian-distributed, with respect to the randomness of the train set
\mathcal{D}
. Their dynamics is thus entirely tracked by that of their limiting values or second-order statistics. These summary statistics in fact identically coincide with the RS order parameters (
61
) of subsection
4.1.1
. Further even : the evolution of these summary statistics is given by the (time-indexed version of) the SP equations (
90
) – implying in particular that the latter also describe fixed points of GAMP.

To build these connections, it is convenient to take as a starting point the equivalent rBP equations (
1
), rather than the GAMP equations (
2
).
In the following, we examine each of the variables
V^{t}_{\mu\to i}
,
\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i}
,
f^{t}_{\mu\to i}
,
g^{t}_{\mu\to i}
,
A^{t}_{i\to\mu}
,
b^{t}_{i\to\mu}
,
\hat{w}^{t}_{i\to\mu}
,
\hat{c}^{t}_{i\to\mu}
involved in the rBP iterations, and ascertain their probability distribution.
As a convention, we note
\circ_{\mu}
the version of a variable
\circ_{\mu\to i}
where the summation also encompasses the index
i
, and
\circ_{i}
the version of a variable
\circ_{i\to\mu}
where the summation also encompasses the index
\mu
. Note that in all cases above the two variables
\circ_{\mu},\circ_{\mu\to i}
or
\circ_{i},\circ_{i\to\mu}
differ by at most
\Theta_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})
. Further, note that under the assumption of section
3
that all covariances are jointly diagonalizable, one can assume without loss of generality all covariances
\Sigma_{\ell,k}
to be diagonal. Let us make one final important remark. Given an index
1\leq i\leq d
(resp.
1\leq\mu\leq n
), it is reasonable to expect from the scaling of equations (
1
) that all incoming variables
\{\circ_{\nu\to i}\}_{1\leq\nu\leq n}
(resp
\{\circ_{j\to\mu}\}_{1\leq j\leq d}
) are asymptotically weakly correlated, between themselves and with the data elements
\{(x^{\nu}_{\ell})_{i}\}_{\ell,\nu}
(resp
\{(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{j}\}_{\ell,j}
). These groups of variables can thus be assumed to be independent. This is in fact a standard assumption in the derivation and analysis of GAMP equations, see e.g.
[
205

Concentration of
(V_{\mu\to i}^{t})_{\ell\kappa}
,
(\Gamma_{\mu\to i}^{t})_{\ell\kappa}

We first show that the variables
V_{\mu\to i}^{t}
concentrate to a deterministic value. Let us first massage its expression as

\displaystyle(V_{\mu\to i}^{t})_{\ell\kappa}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}(x^{\mu}_{\ell j})(x^{\mu}_{\kappa j})\hat{c}^{t}_{j\to\mu}

where we denoted
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\ell}=\bm{x}_{\ell}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell}

\displaystyle(V_{\mu\to i}^{t})_{\ell\kappa}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\ell\kappa}\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}(\Sigma_{\ell,c_{\ell}^{\mu}})_{jj}\hat{c}_{j}^{t}\equiv\delta_{\ell\kappa}V^{t}_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}}.

(109)

We introduced the summary statistic
V^{t}_{\ell,c_{\ell}}

concentrates to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu\to i}^{t}=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{i}\hat{w}^{t}_{i}(\hat{w}^{t}_{i})^{\top}\equiv v^{t},

(110)

where we introduced the summary statistic
v^{t}

Distribution of
\omega_{\ell,\mu\to i}^{t}

We now move to examine the probability distribution of
\omega_{\ell,\mu\to i}^{t}

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{\mu,\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{i}(\tilde{x}^{\mu}_{\ell})_{i}w^{\star}_{i}=y_{\mu\ell}-m^{\star}_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}},

(111)

which correspond to the centered label. As a sum of asymptotically independent variables,

(CLT).
We can now ascertain the joint distribution of
\tilde{y}_{\mu,\ell},\omega_{\ell,\mu\to i}^{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\omega_{\ell,\mu\to i}^{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{w}}^{t}}{\sqrt{d}}\equiv m_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}}^{t},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{y}_{\mu,\ell}]=0,

(112)

and respective variance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\omega_{\ell,\mu\to i}^{t}-m_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}}^{t})(\omega_{\kappa,\nu\to j}^{t}-m_{\kappa,c^{\mu}_{\kappa}}^{t})^{\top}]=\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\ell\kappa}\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{i,j}\hat{w}^{t}_{i}(\Sigma_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}})_{ij}(\hat{w}^{t}_{j})^{\top}\equiv\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\ell\kappa}q_{\ell,c^{\mu}_{\ell}}^{t},

(113)

(114)

(115)

We introduced the summary statistics
q^{t}_{\ell,k},\rho_{\ell,k},\theta_{\ell,k}^{t},m^{t}_{\ell,k}

Distribution of

Let us now study the distribution of

the data component

\displaystyle b_{i\to\mu}^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}(x^{\nu}_{\ell})_{i}f^{t}_{\ell,\nu\to i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}((\tilde{x}^{\nu}_{\ell})_{i}+(\mu_{\ell,c^{\nu}_{\ell}})_{i})\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\gamma}(\tilde{x}^{\nu}_{\gamma})_{i}(w^{\star}_{i}\cdot\nabla_{y_{\gamma}})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left[(V_{\nu\to i}^{t})^{-1}\left(\mathrm{prox}(y_{\nu\to i},\omega^{t}_{\nu\to i},V^{t}_{\nu\to i},\Gamma^{t}_{\nu\to i},c_{\nu})-\omega_{\nu\to i}^{t}\right)\right]_{\ell}

(116)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{i\to\mu}^{t}]=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{k}(\sqrt{d}\mu_{\ell,k})_{i}\underbrace{\alpha\mathbb{E}_{c}\delta_{c_{\ell},k}\mathbb{E}_{y^{c},\Xi^{c}}(V^{t}_{\ell,k})^{-1}\left[\mathrm{prox}(y^{c},m_{c}^{t}+\Xi^{c},V^{t}_{c},v^{t},c)_{\ell}-(m^{t}_{\ell,k}+\Xi^{c}_{\ell})\right]}_{\equiv\hat{m}^{t}_{\ell,k}}

GLMs – approaching Bayes optimality, and the role of regularization

We start this section by briefly reviewing some insights garnered for single-layer networks. For a perceptron target function,
[
7
]
find that a GLM trained with optimally-regularized logistic loss achieves a performance quantitatively very close to the Bayes-optimal performance. This conclusion is shared by
[
41
]
in the more generic setting of multiclass classification with a cross-entropy loss. In the related context of classifying Gaussian mixture data,
[
128

show that for a balanced binary mixture, the optimal regularization corresponds to the limit of infinite
\ell_{2}
regularization
\lambda\to\infty

K\geq 2

[

also find that the Bayes-optimal error is approached for limit of infinite
\ell_{2}

\lambda\to\infty

AEs– architecture and phase diagram

Consider a denoising AE (DAE) (see also
3.2.1
). This already rather complex architecture comprises two components, namely a skip connection, and a bottleneck network. What respective roles do they play in the full architecture ?
[
50
]
explore this question by analyzing the learning of these components when they are trained in isolation, as standalone denoiser networks. Surprisingly, the bottleneck network then learns to perform a simple linear algorithm – namely Principal Component Analysis –. Thus, both components of an AE architecture learn linear algorithms when trained in isolation, but when jointly trained as components of the full architecture, they result in a non-linear, and better performing, model.
[
90
]
leverage a very similar replica analysis as the one presented in section
4
– although their model does not strictly fall under the sequence multi-index model of section
3

Attention – emergence of semantic learning

To complete a supervised regression task,
[
43
]
show that a single attention layer can learn to implement two qualitatively different algorithmic mechanisms – positional attention (with tokens attending to each other based on their respective positions) at small sample complexities, and semantic attention (with tokens attending to each other based on their meanings) past a critical sample complexity
\alpha_{c}

\alpha_{c}

Conclusions

The present manuscript reviews recent progress in the tight asymptotic analysis of neural networks trained with empirical risk minimization, in the limit of comparably large data dimension
d
and number of training samples
n
, and finite number of hidden units
r=\Theta_{d}(1)
. Introducing and using a versatile class of models – the sequence multi-index model – as a case study, we unify and present the analyses reported in many previous works. This allows us to review central techniques in the field of statistical physics of ML, such as the replica method and approximate message passing algorithms. We finally present an overview of the current state of the field, and of the adjoining researchscape, outlining connex topics and modern challenges. This review should be a useful primer for machine learning theoreticians curious of statistical physics approaches; it should also be of value to statistical physicists interested in the transfer of such ideas to the study of neural networks.

Simplification of
\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}

Le us first consider the factor beliefs
\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}
. The starting point is to realize that, under the usual BP assumption that all incoming messages
m_{j\to\mu}^{t}
are statistically independent, the variable
z=\nicefrac{{x^{\mu}\bm{w}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times r}
is Gaussian from the CLT. Each of its rows
z_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
has mean and joint covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[z_{\ell}|w_{i}]=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{1\leq j\neq i\leq d}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{j}\hat{w}_{j\to\mu}^{t}}_{\equiv(\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i})_{\ell}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{i}w_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[z_{\ell},z_{\kappa}|w_{i}]=\underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{1\leq j\neq i\leq d}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{j}(x^{\mu}_{\kappa})_{j}\hat{c}_{j\to\mu}^{t}}_{\equiv(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})_{\ell\kappa}}.

(139)

The expectation bears over the variables
w_{j}\sim m^{t}_{j\to\mu}
, for
1\leq j\neq i\leq d
.
We introduced the rBP variables
\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times r}
and
V^{t}_{\mu\to i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Lr\times Lr}
(viewed as a
L\times L
block matrix with blocks
(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})_{\ell\kappa}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
. We further defined
\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu}
and
\hat{c}^{t}_{j\to\mu}
as the mean and variance of the message
m^{t}_{j\to\mu}
, namely

\displaystyle\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu}=\int dwm^{t}_{j\to\mu}(w)w,

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{t}_{j\to\mu}=\int dwm^{t}_{j\to\mu}(w)w^{2}-(\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu})^{2}.

(140)

Let us further observe that the variable
\nicefrac{{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{w}}}{{d}}
concentrates to

\displaystyle\nicefrac{{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{w}}}{{d}}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{1\leq j\neq i\leq d}(\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu})(\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu})^{\top}}_{\equiv\Gamma^{t}_{\mu\to i}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{1\leq j\neq i\leq d}\hat{c}^{t}_{j\to\mu}}_{\equiv\tilde{\Gamma}^{t}_{\mu\to i}}+\frac{1}{d}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}.

(141)

In order to reach a compact expression, let us introduce the shorthand
\tau(w_{i})\equiv\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times r}
with rows
\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}=(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{i}w_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
. The message
\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}
can be now compactly rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}(w_{i})\propto\int dze^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{1\leq\ell,\kappa\leq L}(z-\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\tau(w_{i}))_{\ell}^{\top}(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}(z-\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\tau(w_{i}))_{\kappa}}e^{-\beta\ell(y^{\mu},z,\Gamma^{t}_{\mu\to i}+\tilde{\Gamma}^{t}_{\mu\to i}+\nicefrac{{w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}}}{{d}},c^{\mu})}.

(142)

For the sake of conciseness, we did not explicitly write the normalization factor, subsumed in the proportionality symbol
\propto
. Let us introduce the density associated to the partition function

\displaystyle Z_{\beta}(y,\omega,V,\Gamma,c)\equiv\int dz\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi V)}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{1\leq\ell,\kappa\leq L}(z-\omega)_{\ell}^{\top}V^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}(z-\omega)_{\kappa}}e^{-\beta\ell(y,z,\Gamma,c)},

(143)

and by brackets
\langle\cdot\rangle
the expectation with respect to
Z_{\beta}(y^{\mu},\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i},V^{t}_{\mu\to i},\Gamma^{t}_{\mu\to i}+\tilde{\Gamma}^{t}_{\mu\to i},c^{\mu})
. Then, expanding the exponent of
\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}(w_{i})
(
142
),

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}(w_{i})\propto

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\ell\kappa}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}^{\top}\underbrace{(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}\langle(z-\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i})_{\kappa}\rangle}_{\equiv f^{t}_{\kappa,\mu\to i}}+\frac{1}{2d}\left(\sum\limits_{\ell\kappa}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}^{\top}(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}\langle(z-\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i})_{\kappa}\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2d}\sum\limits_{\ell\kappa}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}^{\top}(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}\tau(w_{i})_{\kappa}-\beta\Tr[\underbrace{\langle\partial_{3}\ell(y^{\mu},z,\Gamma^{t}_{\mu\to i}+\tilde{\Gamma}^{t}_{\mu\to i},c^{\mu})\rangle}_{\equiv\eta_{\mu\to i}^{t}}\frac{w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}}{d}].

(144)

We introduced the rBP variables
f^{t}_{\mu\to i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times r}
and
\eta^{t}_{\mu\to i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
. Let us further introduce the variance
g^{t}_{\mu\to i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Lr\times Lr}
, seen as a
L\times L
block matrix with components

\displaystyle g^{t}_{\ell\kappa,\mu\to i}\equiv\nabla_{\omega_{\kappa}}f^{t}_{\ell,\mu\to i}=-(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}-f^{t}_{\ell,\mu\to i}(f^{t}_{\kappa,\mu\to i})^{\top}+\left\langle\sum\limits_{\rho\sigma}(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\ell\rho}(z-\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i})_{\rho}(z-\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{\top}_{\sigma}(V^{t}_{\mu\to i})^{-1}_{\sigma\kappa}\right\rangle,

(145)

so that (
144
) can be rewritten in more compact fashion as

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\mu\to i}^{t}(w_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}f^{t}_{\ell,\mu\to i}+\frac{1}{2d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}^{\top}g^{t}_{\ell\kappa,\mu\to i}\tau(w_{i})_{\kappa}+\frac{1}{2d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa}\tau(w_{i})^{\top}_{\ell}f^{t}_{\ell,\mu\to i}(f^{t}_{\kappa,\mu\to i})^{\top}\tau(w_{i})_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\qquad-\beta\Tr[\eta^{t}_{\mu\to i}\frac{w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}}{d}]

\displaystyle\propto e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}f^{t}_{\ell\mu\to i}+\frac{1}{2d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa}\tau(w_{i})_{\ell}^{\top}g^{t}_{\ell\kappa,\mu\to i}\tau(w_{i})_{\kappa}-\beta\Tr[\eta^{t}_{\mu\to i}\frac{w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}}{d}]}

\displaystyle\propto e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{i}w_{i}^{\top}f^{t}_{\ell,\mu\to i}+\frac{1}{2d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{i}(x^{\mu}_{\kappa})_{i}w_{i}^{\top}g^{t}_{\ell\kappa,\mu\to i}w_{i}-\frac{\beta}{d}w_{i}^{\top}\eta^{t}_{\mu\to i}w_{i}}.

(146)

Simplication of
m^{t}_{i\to\mu}

We now turn to examine the variable messages
m^{t}_{i\to\mu}
. Again keeping the normalization implicit:

\displaystyle m^{t}_{\mu\to i}(w_{i})

\displaystyle\propto e^{w_{i}^{\top}\left(\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}\sum\limits_{\ell}(x^{\nu}_{\ell})_{i}f^{t}_{\ell,\nu\to i}}_{\equiv b^{t}_{i\to\mu}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}w_{i}^{\top}\left(\underbrace{-\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa}(x^{\nu}_{\ell})_{i}(x^{\nu}_{\kappa})_{i}g^{t}_{\ell\kappa,\nu\to i}}_{\equiv A^{t}_{i\to\mu}}+\underbrace{\frac{2}{d}\sum\limits_{\nu\neq\mu}\beta\eta^{t}_{\nu\to i}}_{\equiv C^{t}_{i\to\mu}}\right)w_{i}-\frac{\beta\lambda}{2}w_{i}^{\top}w_{i}}.

(147)

in other words,
m^{t}_{\mu\to i}
is a Gaussian dstribution.
One is now in a position to close the equations on

and

(

\displaystyle\hat{w}^{t}_{i\to\mu}=\left(\beta\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}+A^{t}_{i\to\mu}+C^{t}_{i\to\mu}\right)^{-1}b^{t}_{i\to\mu},

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{t}_{i\to\mu}=\left(\beta\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}+A^{t}_{i\to\mu}+C^{t}_{i\to\mu}\right)^{-1}

(148)

A.3
Equivalence rBP/GAMP

The previous subsection established the asymptotic equivalence of BP (
3
) and rBP
1
equations. We now further demonstrate that rBP
1
is also asymptotically equivalent to GAMP
2
, which has affords a simpler algorithm with less variables, and further bears connections to the fixed points of GD, see main text. The basis of this derivation is the observation that variables
\circ_{i\to\mu}
(resp.
\circ_{\mu\to i}
) have a weak dependence on the target node
\mu
(resp.
i
). Therefore,
V^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx V^{t}_{\mu},\Gamma^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx\Gamma^{t}_{\mu},\omega^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx\omega^{t}_{\mu},f^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx f^{t}_{\mu},g^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx g^{t}_{\mu},\eta^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx\eta^{t}_{\mu},A^{t}_{i\to\mu}\approx A^{t}_{i},C^{t}_{i\to\mu}\approx C^{t}_{i},b^{t}_{i\to\mu}\approx b^{t}_{i},\hat{w}^{t}_{i\to\mu}\approx\hat{w}_{i},\hat{c}^{t}_{i\to\mu}\approx\hat{c}^{t}_{i}
, where

\displaystyle(V_{\mu}^{t})_{\ell\kappa}=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}(x^{\mu}_{\ell j})(x^{\mu}_{\kappa j})\hat{c}^{t}_{j\to\mu},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}^{t}=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu}(\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu})^{\top},

\displaystyle\omega_{\ell,\mu}^{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{j}x^{\mu}_{\ell,j}\hat{w}_{j\to\mu}^{t}

\displaystyle A^{t}_{i}=-\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{\nu}(x^{\nu}_{\ell i})(x^{\nu}_{\kappa i})g_{\ell\kappa,\nu\to i}^{t},

\displaystyle C^{t}_{i}=\frac{2}{d}\sum\limits_{\nu}\eta_{\nu\to i}^{t},

\displaystyle b^{t}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{\nu}x^{\nu}_{\ell i}f_{\ell,\nu\to i}^{t},

\displaystyle\hat{w}^{t+1}_{i}=(\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}+C^{t}_{i}+A^{t}_{i})^{-1}b_{i}^{t},

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{t+1}_{i}=(\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}+C^{t}_{i}+A^{t}_{i})^{-1},

\displaystyle f^{t}_{\ell,\mu}=\sum\limits_{\kappa}\scriptstyle(V_{\mu\to i}^{t})^{-1}_{\ell\kappa}\left(\mathrm{prox}(y_{\mu},\omega^{t}_{\mu},V^{t}_{\mu},\Gamma^{t}_{\mu},c^{\mu})-\omega_{\mu}^{t}\right)_{\kappa},

\displaystyle g_{\ell\kappa,\mu}^{t}=\nabla_{\omega_{\kappa}}f_{\ell,\mu}^{t},

\displaystyle\eta^{t}_{\mu}=\partial_{3}\ell\left(\scriptstyle y_{\mu},\mathrm{prox}(y_{\mu},\omega^{t}_{\mu},V^{t}_{\mu},\Gamma^{t}_{\mu},c^{\mu}),\Gamma^{t}_{\mu},c^{\mu}\right).

(152)

This constitutes a smaller set of variables. We note, however, that the equations (
4.2
) are not yet closed, as they still involve
\circ_{i\to\mu},\circ_{\mu\to i}
variables. In the following, we thus focus on obtaining a closed set of iteration equations, which will result in the GAMP algorithm
2
.

Simplifying
V^{t}_{\mu},\Gamma^{t}_{\mu},A^{t}_{i},C^{t}_{i}

Let us first examine the variable
V^{t}_{\mu\to i}\approx V^{t}_{\mu}
. We can further simplify
V^{t}_{\mu}
as

\displaystyle(V^{t}_{\mu})_{\ell\kappa}=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{j}(x^{\mu}_{\kappa})_{j}\hat{c}_{j\to\mu}^{t}=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}(x^{\mu}_{\ell})_{j}(x^{\mu}_{\kappa})_{j}\hat{c}_{j}^{t}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}).

(153)

By the same token,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{t}_{i\to\mu}=\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}\hat{w}_{j}^{t}(\hat{w}_{j}^{t})^{\top}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}).

(154)

and

\displaystyle A^{t}_{i}=-\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa=1}^{L}\sum\limits_{\nu}(x^{\nu}_{\ell i})(x^{\nu}_{\kappa i})g_{\ell\kappa,\nu}^{t}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}),

(155)

and finally

\displaystyle C^{t}_{i}=\frac{2}{d}\sum\limits_{\nu}\eta_{\nu}^{t}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}).

(156)

Onsager terms

We now turn our attention to
\omega^{t}_{\ell,\mu}
. In this term, one may not straightforwardly replace
\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu}
by
\hat{w}^{t}_{j}
, as the neglected term is correlated with
x^{\mu}_{\ell,j}
and results in a
\Theta_{d}(1)
contribution when summed over. To make progress, let us more carefully control
\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu}
as

\displaystyle\hat{w}^{t}_{j\to\mu}=\hat{w}^{t}_{j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}x^{\mu}_{\ell j}(\lambda\mathbb{I}_{r}+C^{t}_{j}+A^{t}_{j})^{-1}f^{t}_{\ell,\mu}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}).

(157)

Thus,

\displaystyle\omega^{t}_{\ell,\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{j}x^{\mu}_{\ell,j}\hat{w}^{t}_{j}-\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{j}\sum\limits_{\kappa}x^{\mu}_{\ell,j}x^{\mu}_{\kappa,j}\hat{c}^{t}_{j}f^{t}_{\kappa,\mu}+o_{d}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{j}x^{\mu}_{\ell,j}\hat{w}^{t}_{j}-\sum\limits_{\kappa}(V^{t}_{\mu})_{\ell,\kappa}f^{t}_{\kappa,\mu}.

(158)

The last expression to close, bearing on
b^{t}_{i}
, can be treated in the same fashion. Carefully expanding
f^{t}_{\ell,\nu\to i}
as

\displaystyle f^{t}_{\ell,\nu\to i}=f^{t}_{\ell,\nu}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\kappa}(g^{t}_{\nu})_{\ell\kappa}x^{\mu}_{\kappa,i}\hat{w}^{t}_{i}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}),

(159)

we reach

\displaystyle b_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{\nu}x^{\nu}_{\ell,i}f^{t}_{\ell,\nu}-\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\ell,\kappa}\sum\limits_{\nu}x^{\nu}_{\ell,i}x^{\nu}_{\kappa,i}(g^{t}_{\nu})_{\ell,\kappa}\hat{w}_{i}+o_{d}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell}\sum\limits_{\nu}x^{\nu}_{\ell,i}f^{t}_{\ell,\nu}+A^{t}_{i}\hat{w}_{i}^{t}.

(160)

Summary of GAMP equations

We have now closed the set of equations
152
. Summarizing, we reach the iterative scheme
