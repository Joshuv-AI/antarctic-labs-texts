Margin Quality Analysis and Risk Factors Associated With Insufficient Margins Following Segmentectomy for Small‐Sized Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Chen Zhao, Kaisen Liu, Zhihua Li, Wenzheng Xu, Yanhu Wu, Liang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSegmentectomy has been recognized as non‐inferior to lobectomy for the treatment of small‐sized non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). This study aimed to identify factors associated with the margin quality of segmentectomy.MethodsPatients with small‐sized NSCLC (≤ 2 cm) who underwent segmentectomy between January 2019 and December 2023 were screened. A sufficient surgical margin was defined as ≥ the maximum tumor diameter. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with insufficient margins following segmentectomy.ResultsA total of 667 patients were included. Of them, sufficient surgical margins were achieved in 577 patients (86.5%). Compared to patients with sufficient margins, those with insufficient margins had larger tumor sizes, longer tumor‐to‐pleura distances, shorter tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distances, and higher percentages of central tumors and intersegmental tumors. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that tumor size (OR = 2.97, p = 0.002), tumor‐to‐pleura distance (OR = 1.26, p = 0.045), tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance (OR = 0.76, p = 0.011), and tumor subsegmental attribution (intersegmental vs. intrasegmental tumors, OR = 2.13, p = 0.003) were significantly associated with the margin quality of segmentectomy. The optimal cutoff values to stratify the risk of insufficient margins were 1.1 cm for tumor size, 3.0 cm for tumor‐to‐pleura distance, and 5.2 cm for tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance, respectively. Notably, 98.9% (89/90) of patients with insufficient margins exhibited at least one of these identified risk factors.ConclusionsTumor size, tumor‐to‐pleura distance, tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance, and nodule subsegmental attribution might be correlated with insufficient margins during segmentectomy. Surgeons should exercise extra caution when performing segmentectomy for patients with tumors harboring multiple such high‐risk features.Keywords: logistic regression, non‐small cell lung cancer, segmentectomy, surgical margin

Introduction

Lobectomy has been regarded as the gold standard surgical procedure for early‐staged non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) for decades [1]. With the widespread use of high‐resolution computed tomography (CT), an increasing number of small‐sized NSCLCs have been identified. Over the past few years, numerous studies have demonstrated that segmentectomy can achieve non‐inferior long‐term outcomes to lobectomy and have favorable effects on pulmonary function [2, 3, 4]. Consequently, multiple clinical guidelines and expert consensus have recommended segmentectomy for peripheral small‐sized NSCLC [5, 6, 7].

According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines, segmentectomy should ensure a sufficient surgical margin: ≥ 2 cm or the maximum tumor diameter [5]. However, in clinical practice, segmentectomy does not always achieve sufficient surgical margins. Akamine et al. reported that the probability of achieving sufficient surgical margins in segmentectomy for clinical stage IA NSCLC was 71.4% [8]. Similarly, a supplementary analysis of the JCOG0802 study indicated that 81.5% of patients undergoing segmentectomy achieved a sufficient margin [9]. Recently, In Ha Kim and colleagues found that sufficient margins were achieved in 69.5% of patients with clinical stage IA1‐IA2 lung adenocarcinoma who underwent segmentectomy [10]. All these findings demonstrated that a substantial portion of patients who underwent segmentectomy failed to yield sufficient surgical margins.

Numerous studies have proved that insufficient surgical margins were significantly associated with a higher risk of tumor locoregional recurrence [9, 11, 12]. However, the factors associated with the quality of the surgical margins in segmentectomy remain largely unclear. In this study, we systematically screened patients with small‐sized NSCLC who underwent segmentectomy in our department between January 2019 and December 2023. The clinicopathological characteristics of patients with sufficient margins and those with insufficient margins were compared. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with the margin quality of segmentectomy.

Methods

Study Subjects

Patients with small‐sized pulmonary nodules who underwent segmentectomy in our department between January 2019 and December 2023 were retrospectively screened. Patients who met the following criteria were initially included in this study: (1) maximum nodule diameter ≤ 2 cm; (2) single nodule; (3) intentional resection; (4) histopathologically confirmed primary NSCLC. Furthermore, patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) nodules located in the right middle lobe; (2) a history of neoadjuvant therapy before surgery; (3) poor CT image quality or lack of primary data in DICOM format. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Individual consent was waived for this retrospective analysis.

Radiological Measurement

Maximum tumor diameter, solid components, and depth ratio were measured as previously described [13]. Tumors with a depth ratio exceeding 33.3% were defined as central tumors. The tumor‐to‐pleura nearest distance was measured from the center of the tumor to the nearest pleura, while the tumor‐to‐pleura distance was defined as the length from the center of the tumor to the costal pleura. The Lewis classification was used to assess the difficulty of resection based on anatomic location [14]. In general, lesions in the lingula, apex, and lung edges are easily approached (Lewis A type), whereas lesions in the base of the lung, deep in the fissure, or on the large ovoid surface are difficult to excise (Lewis B type). In this study, tumors were further classified into four types based on their relationship with adjacent vessels and bronchi: (1) Type A (subsegmental central type): nodules located in the center of the target subsegment; (2) Type B (intrasegmental subsegmental marginal type): nodules located in the marginal region of the target subsegment but within the central region of the target segment; (3) Type C (intersegmental type): nodules located between two different segments; (4) Type D (multi‐intersegmental type): nodules involving three or more segments [15]. Thus, Type A and B nodules were categorized as intrasegmental nodules, while Type C and D nodules were classified as intersegmental nodules.

Preoperative 3D‐CTBA Reconstruction and Surgical Procedures

For peripheral small‐sized nodules, wedge resection was preferentially considered. When wedge resection was less likely to achieve an adequate surgical margin, the feasibility of segmentectomy was evaluated using preoperative 3D‐CTBA (three‐dimensional computed tomography bronchography and angiography) reconstruction with a 2 cm virtual margin. If segmentectomy was considered infeasible (for instance, the virtual margin involved the target segmental bronchus, or the planned resection extent was extensive and the remaining lobe was too small), lobectomy was adopted instead. The surgical extent was designed according to the 3D‐CTBA.

All surgical approaches were jointly determined by at least three experienced surgeons. As we previously reported, the intersegmental plane was identified using the collateral ventilation method, also known as the modified inflation‐deflation method [16]. Intraoperative frozen‐section analysis of the parenchymal margin was not routinely performed. During the operation, the surgical margin was initially assessed by the surgeons in the removed pulmonary tissues before pathological examination. If the surgical margin was considered insufficient, additional resection would be performed. According to the original surgical records, 5 out of 667 patients (0.75%) underwent intraoperative additional resection. Lymphadenectomy depended on the surgeon's decision. Generally, for nodules considered to be minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, selective lymph node sampling was performed. For part‐solid or solid nodules that were considered invasive tumors, routine selective lymph node sampling or dissection was performed. If lymph node metastasis was identified by frozen pathology, systematic lymph node dissection would be conducted. The detailed surgical procedures have been described previously [15].

In this study, patients were classified into four groups according to the surgical type: mono‐subsegmentectomy (MSS), single‐segmentectomy (SS), combined‐subsegmentectomy (CSS), and combined‐segmentectomy (CS). MSS refers to anatomical resection confined to a single pulmonary subsegment; SS is defined as anatomical resection confined to a single pulmonary segment; CSS refers to combined resection involving subsegments, including combined resection of two or more subsegments, and combined segmental and subsegmental resection (e.g., RS1 + S2a); CS is defined as combined resection of two or more segments.

Surgical Margin Measurement

The surgical margin was defined as the minimum distance from the resection margin to the tumor edge, as detected in the removed non‐inflated specimen. All surgical margins were derived from the pathological report, which was measured by a pathologist prior to formalin fixation and then reviewed by another pathologist before release. Multiple clinical guidelines recommend that sublobar resection should ensure a surgical margin of either 2 cm or the maximum tumor diameter for peripheral T1ab N0 NSCLC (5). In this study, all patients had NSCLC measuring ≤ 2 cm. Therefore, this study defined a sufficient margin as equal to or larger than the maximum tumor diameter.

Statistical Analysis

For continuous variables meeting the normal distribution, Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA was used. For non‐normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. The chi‐squared χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test was applied for categorical variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to evaluate the associations between various variables and the margin quality of segmentectomy. The optimal cutoff values for tumor size, tumor‐to‐pleura distance, and tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance to stratify the risk of insufficient surgical margins were determined using the “CatPredi” function [17]. Disease‐free survival (DFS) was defined as the duration from the date of surgery to the date of first tumor recurrence or death of any cause. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the duration between surgery and death from any cause. DFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Log‐rank test was applied for prognostic comparison. All analyses were performed using R (4.2.1), and p < 0.05 (two‐sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Patients With Sufficient and Insufficient Margins

In total, 667 patients with small‐sized NSCLC who underwent segmentectomy were included in this study (Table 1). The detailed surgical types were shown in Table S1. All patients underwent thoracoscopic segmentectomy, and 103 patients (15.4%) underwent extended resection. Five out of 667 patients (5/667, 0.75%) received intraoperative additional resection due to insufficient surgical margins. In general, 577 patients (86.5%, 577/667) achieved sufficient surgical margins, while the other patients (13.5%, 90/667) had insufficient margins. Compared to patients with sufficient margins, those with insufficient margins exhibited larger tumors (1.38 cm vs. 1.26 cm, p = 0.004), longer tumor‐to‐pleura distances (3.12 cm vs. 2.33 cm, p < 0.001), and shorter tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distances (4.45 cm vs. 5.31 cm, p < 0.001). Additionally, patients who underwent insufficient segmentectomy had a higher prevalence of central tumors (55.6% vs. 39.5%, p = 0.006) and intersegmental tumors (C or D type, 71.1% vs. 46.8%, p < 0.001) than those undergoing sufficient segmentectomy. As expected, more pulmonary parenchyma was removed in patients with sufficient margins (65.0 cm3 vs. 49.7 cm3, p = 0.012). The median surgical margin was 2.5 cm in the sufficient segmentectomy group, significantly larger than the 1.0 cm observed in the insufficient segmentectomy group. Similarly, the median margin‐to‐tumor (M/T) ratio was 2.06 and 0.74 in patients with sufficient and insufficient surgical margins, respectively. Among patients with insufficient surgical margins, 19 had margins smaller than the solid size of the tumors, and 39 had margins < 1.00 cm. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding other factors, including age, gender, smoking, history of pulmonary surgery, preoperative localization, number of operating ports, surgical categories, extended resection, and so on.

Characteristics and Margin Quality According to Surgical Categories

Considering the clinical significance of surgical categories, we further compared the characteristics and margin quality according to surgical categories. As shown in Table S2, 84, 212, 236, and 135 patients underwent MSS, CSS, SS, and CS, respectively. The CS group had the largest tumor size, followed by the SS, CSS, and MSS groups (p < 0.001). The lobar distribution significantly differed among the four groups (p < 0.001). The CS group exhibited the largest tumor‐to‐pleura distance, followed by the CSS and SS groups, while the MSS group had the shortest distance to the pleura (p < 0.001). In contrast, the MSS group had the largest tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance, while the CS group had the shortest. Combined resection groups had higher proportions of central nodules and intersegmental nodules than MSS or SS groups (p < 0.05). The CS group had the highest number of removed subsegments, followed by the CSS, SS, and MSS groups. As expected, the CS group achieved the largest median surgical margin (2.70 cm), while the MSS group had the shortest (2.00 cm). However, no significant differences were observed between the four groups in terms of the margin‐to‐tumor ratio (p = 0.937) or the likelihood of achieving sufficient margins (p = 0.920). Notably, the CS group was more likely to achieve a surgical margin ≥ 2.0 cm (76.3%) than the SS (68.6%), CSS (67.9%), and MSS (54.8%) groups (p = 0.011).

Univariate and Multivariate Logistic Regression Analyses

Further, univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between various factors and the risk of insufficient surgical margin. As shown in Table 2, tumor size (OR = 2.52, 95% CI: 1.34–4.74, p = 0.004), tumor‐to‐pleura distance (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.17–1.58, p < 0.001), tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.63–0.86, p < 0.001), nodule subsegmental attribution (intersegmental vs. intrasegmental nodules, OR = 2.80, 95% CI: 1.72–4.54, p < 0.001), and nodule depth (central vs. peripheral: OR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.22–2.99, p = 0.005) were significantly associated with the risk of insufficient surgical margins following segmentectomy. In contrast, factors such as age, gender, smoking, BMI, pulmonary surgery history, preoperative localization, the number of operative ports, surgical categories, and removed subsegment number showed no significant association with the quality of surgical margins in segmentectomy.

Furthermore, variables with a p value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Given the clinical importance of surgical categories and the number of removed subsegments, these two factors were also included in the multivariate analysis. As a result, tumor size (OR = 4.25, 95% CI: 2.01–8.98, p < 0.001), tumor‐to‐pleura distance (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.09–1.73, p = 0.007), tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.60–0.92, p = 0.008), nodule subsegmental attribution (intersegmental vs. intrasegmental, OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.62–5.65, p < 0.001) were identified as independent factors associated with the margin quality of segmentectomy. Notably, nodule depth (central vs. peripheral) was no longer significant after adjusting for other factors (p = 0.304).

The Optimal Cutoff Values of Tumor Size, Tumor‐To‐Pleura Distance, and Tumor‐To‐Segmental Bronchus Distance

Given that tumor size, tumor‐to‐pleura distance, and tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance were continuous variables, we used the “CatPredi” package to determine their optimal cutoff values for stratifying the risk of insufficient margins during segmentectomy. As shown in Figure 1A, with the increase in tumor size, the risk of achieving insufficient margins during segmentectomy significantly increased. The optimal cutoff point for tumor size was 1.1 cm. Multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that the risk of achieving insufficient margins for tumors ≥ 1.1 cm was 2.99 times (OR = 2.99, 95% CI: 1.65–5.42, p < 0.001) that for tumors smaller than 1.1 cm after adjusting for other factors (Table 3). Figure 1B showed that the optimal cutoff point for tumor‐to‐pleura distance was 3.0 cm. Compared to tumors located close to the pleura (< 3.0 cm), those located away from the pleura (≥ 3.0 cm) had a 3.34 times higher risk of insufficient margins (OR = 4.34, 95% CI: 1.99–9.43, p < 0.001, Table 3). As shown in Figure 1C, the risk of achieving insufficient margins significantly declined as tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance increased, with an optimal cutoff point value of 5.2 cm. Tumors < 5.2 cm from the segmental bronchus were associated with a higher risk of insufficient margins following segmentectomy compared with tumors ≥ 5.2 cm from the bronchus (OR = 3.47, 95% CI: 1.94–6.22, p < 0.001, Table 3).

Risk Stratification Based on the Identified Factors

To evaluate the efficacy of risk stratification based on the identified factors, we further compared the distribution of these factors between patients with sufficient and insufficient margins. As shown in Figure 2A, the insufficient segmentectomy group exhibited a higher proportion of tumors ≥ 1.1 cm than the sufficient segmentectomy group (82.2% vs. 63.4%, p < 0.001). Similarly, 54.4% of tumors in the insufficient segmentectomy group had a tumor‐to‐pleura distance ≥ 3.0 cm, which was significantly higher than the 28.4% observed in the sufficient segmentectomy group (p < 0.001, Figure 2B). The proportion of tumors with a tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance < 5.2 cm was also significantly higher in the insufficient segmentectomy group compared with the sufficient segmentectomy group (73.3% vs. 45.6%, p < 0.001, Figure 2C). As previously described, the insufficient segmentectomy group had more intersegmental tumors than the sufficient segmentectomy group (Figure 2D).

Furthermore, patients were stratified based on the number of risk factors they harbored. As shown in Figure 3, 55 patients had none of these risk factors (G0 group), among whom only one patient (1.8%, 1/55) had insufficient margins. There were 196, 197, and 150 patients who exhibited one (G1 group), two (G2 group), and three (G3 group) risk factors, respectively. The incidence of insufficient margins was 6.1% (12/196) in the G1 group, 8.1% (16/197) in the G2 group, and 23.3% (35/150) in the G3 group. Additionally, 69 patients had all four risk factors (G4 group), and 37.7% (26/69) of them failed to achieve sufficient margins. Overall, the incidence of insufficient margins significantly increased as the number of risk factors increased. Notably, 98.9% (89/90) of patients with insufficient segmentectomy had at least one of the above risk factors. Despite the higher incidence of insufficient margins observed in the G1 and G2 groups compared to the G0 group, the results of the logistic regression analysis indicated that these differences were not statistically significant. In contrast, the G3 (OR = 16.43, 95% CI: 2.19–123.11, p = 0.006) and G4 groups (OR = 32.65, 95% CI: 4.26–250.32, p < 0.001) exhibited a significantly higher risk of achieving insufficient margins in comparison to the G1 group.

Prognostic Evaluation

The median follow‐up time was 35 months. During this period, one patient died due to cardiovascular disease, and one patient experienced tumor bone metastasis 22 months after surgery. The detailed characteristics of these two patients were summarized in Table S3. Overall, the 5‐year disease‐free survival and 5‐year overall survival were 99.6% and 99.8%, respectively (Figure S1). The limited number of tumor recurrence events restricted further analysis of the association between surgical margins and tumor recurrences.

Discussion

Segmentectomy has been recommended for peripheral small‐sized NSCLC. However, a considerable portion of patients undergoing segmentectomy failed to achieve a sufficient surgical margin. Numerous studies have demonstrated that insufficient surgical margins contributed to a higher rate of locoregional recurrence [11, 12]. Recently, a supplementary analysis of the JCOG0802 study also reported that a margin distance less than the tumor size was significantly associated with an increased risk of locoregional recurrence, with an OR of 2.68 [9]. Therefore, identifying factors associated with the margin quality of segmentectomy is of great clinical significance. The present study found that tumor size, tumor‐to‐pleura distance, tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance, and nodule subsegmental attribution were significantly associated with the quality of the surgical margins following segmentectomy.

In the present study, 86.5% of patients undergoing segmentectomy had sufficient surgical margins, while the other 13.5% had insufficient surgical margins. This percentage was similar to that reported in the JCOG0802 study [9]. It is well known that tumor size is a fundamental factor determining the surgical procedures. Here, we observed that with the increase in tumor size, the likelihood of achieving sufficient surgical margins in segmentectomy significantly declined. Similar findings were reported by Akamine et al. [8]. Zheng and colleagues found that as tumor size increased, the likelihood of achieving sufficient margins during wedge resection also markedly decreased [18]. The optimal cutoff value for tumor size to stratify the risk of insufficient margins was 1.1 cm. Specifically, patients with tumors measuring 1.1–2.0 cm were 2.99 times more likely to have inadequate margins than those with tumors < 1.1 cm. However, this does not mean that segmentectomy is inappropriate for NSCLC larger than 1.1 cm. The feasibility of segmentectomy should be comprehensively evaluated.

Nowadays, most clinical guidelines and expert consensus recommend segmentectomy only for peripheral small‐sized NSCLC. With the application of 3D‐CTBA, a few studies have demonstrated that segmentectomy is feasible and can achieve long‐term outcomes comparable to lobectomy for selected central small‐sized NSCLC [19, 20, 21]. However, segmentectomy for central tumors may yield shorter surgical margins than for peripheral tumors [22]. In the current study, central tumors accounted for 55.6% of cases in the inadequate segmentectomy group, significantly higher than the 39.5% observed in the adequate segmentectomy group. Additionally, nodules in the inadequate segmentectomy group had a longer distance to the pleura but a shorter distance to the segmental bronchus compared to those in the adequate segmentectomy arm. Notably, in the multivariate regression analysis, nodule depth location (central vs. peripheral) was no longer significant. Instead, tumor‐to‐pleura distance and tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance were independently associated with the risk of insufficient surgical margins. These findings suggested that these specific distances were more critical than the tumor depth ratio in determining surgical margin quality. Zheng and colleagues found that the distance between the tumor and the pleura also influenced the margin quality in wedge resection [18]. Further analysis in our study showed that the optimal cutoff values for stratifying the risk of insufficient surgical margins in segmentectomy were 3.0 cm (distance to the pleura) and 5.2 cm (distance to the segmental bronchus). When tumors were located too close to the segmental bronchus, it became challenging to perform a segmentectomy with an adequate margin. In this study, 73.3% of patients with inadequate segmentectomy had nodules close to the segmental bronchus. Therefore, surgeons should be careful when lesions are adjacent to the segmental bronchus.

In addition to the above‐mentioned factors, we found that the subsegmental attribution of tumors also significantly impacted the margin quality in segmentectomy. In 2021, we proposed a novel classification based on the relationship between pulmonary nodules, vessels, and bronchi [15]. According to this classification, Type A and B nodules were categorized as intrasegmental nodules, while Type C and D nodules were classified as intersegmental nodules. Unlike intrasegmental nodules, intersegmental nodules involve two or more segments and typically require combined segmentectomy or extended resection [23, 24]. Our study revealed that 71.1% of patients with inadequate segmentectomy harbored intersegmental or multi‐intersegmental nodules. The risk of insufficient surgical margins for intersegmental lesions was 2.13 times greater than that for intrasegmental nodules. Therefore, segmentectomy for intersegmental nodules, especially for multi‐intersegmental nodules, should be well designed.

In summary, the current study identified four key factors that significantly influenced the quality of surgical margins in segmentectomy. Among patients with inadequate segmentectomy, only one had none of these risk factors. With the increase in the number of risk factors, the incidence of insufficient margins significantly increased. Strikingly, patients harboring three or four risk factors had a higher risk of insufficient margins during segmentectomy compared to those with no risk factors. The proportion of patients with insufficient margins was 23.3% and 37.7% in patients harboring three and four risk factors, respectively. Thus, surgeons should be extra careful when performing segmentectomy for tumors with three or more risk features.

Apart from these factors, other factors, including preoperative localization and the number of operating ports, did not significantly affect the surgical margins in segmentectomy. Consistent with our findings, Zhu et al. also found that surgical margins between segmentectomy with and without preoperative localization were comparable [25]. On the contrary, preoperative localization has been reported to contribute to sufficient surgical margins in wedge resection [18]. Previous studies reported comparable perioperative outcomes and long‐term efficacy between uniport, two‐port, and three‐port VATS segmentectomy [26, 27]. The present study showed that the number of operating ports had no significant impact on surgical margins in segmentectomy. In addition, we found that combined‐segmentectomy achieved larger median surgical margins than single‐segmentectomy, combined‐subsegmentectomy, and mono‐subsegmentectomy. However, the likelihood of achieving a sufficient surgical margin was comparable across the different surgical categories. This may be largely attributed to the precise surgical design guided by preoperative 3D‐CTBA. Further studies are needed to validate these findings.

Insufficient surgical margins have been reported to be significantly associated with an increased risk of locoregional recurrence of NSCLC [9, 12]. However, the optimal cutoff value for surgical margins remains controversial. Recently, a supplementary analysis of the JCOG0802 indicated that a margin distance less than the tumor size was a risk factor of locoregional relapse following segmentectomy [9]. Present studies also suggest that the margin criteria may differ for NSCLC with different tumor sizes, radiological appearances, and histopathological subtypes [28, 29, 30]. In the current study, only one patient encountered postoperative tumor recurrence, which limited the possibility of analyzing the associations between surgical margin and tumor recurrence. Therefore, more studies are warranted to demonstrate the relationships between surgical margin and tumor recurrence with consideration of radiological and histopathological subtypes.

This study comprehensively analyzed the factors influencing the quality of surgical margins in segmentectomy. However, it has several limitations. First, this is a single‐center retrospective study, and subjective selection bias is inevitable. Second, although the current study included dozens of factors, other factors could also influence the margin quality in segmentectomy. Third, the relationship between surgical margins and the prognosis of NSCLC patients was not assessed due to the limited follow‐up duration.

Conclusions

In summary, tumor size, tumor‐to‐pleura distance, tumor‐to‐segmental bronchus distance, and tumor subsegmental attribution were significantly associated with the quality of surgical margins in segmentectomy. Surgeons should be extra careful when performing segmentectomy for patients with tumors harboring multiple such risk factors.
