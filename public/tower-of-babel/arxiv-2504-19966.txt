Quantum circuit lower bounds in the magic hierarchy
Natalie Parham

Abstract
We introduce the magic hierarchy , a quantum circuit model that alternates between arbitrary-sized Clifford circuits and constant-depth circuits with two-qubit gates ( QNC 0 \textsf{QNC}^{0} ). This model unifies existing circuit models, such as 𝖰𝖠𝖢 f 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f} and models with adaptive intermediate measurements. Despite its generality, we are able to prove nontrivial lower bounds. We prove new lower bounds in the first level of the hierarchy, showing that certain explicit quantum states cannot be approximately prepared by circuits consisting of a Clifford circuit followed by QNC 0 \textsf{QNC}^{0} . These states include ground states of some topologically ordered Hamiltonians and nonstabilizer quantum codes. Our techniques exploit the rigid structure of stabilizer codes and introduce an infectiousness property: if even a single state in a high distance code can be approximately prepared by one of these circuits, then the entire subspace must lie close to a perturbed stabilizer code. We also show that proving state preparation lower bounds beyond a certain level of the hierarchy would imply classical circuit lower bounds beyond the reach of current techniques in complexity theory. More broadly, our techniques go beyond lightcone-based methods and highlight how the magic hierarchy provides a natural framework for connecting circuit complexity, condensed matter, and Hamiltonian complexity.

Introduction

Understanding the limitations of quantum circuits is a central question at the intersection of quantum computing and many-body physics. A
quantum circuit lower bound
means proving that a certain class of circuits cannot perform a specific task— such as preparing a target state
\ket{\psi}
or implementing a unitary
U
.
Classical circuit lower bounds have led to breakthroughs across theoretical computer science, establishing deep connections to pseudorandomness
[
RR94
,
Bra08
]
and learning theory
[
CIKK16
,
OS16
,
LMN93
]
. Furthermore, quantum circuit lower bounds have interesting implications for fundamental questions in quantum matter.

In condensed matter physics, circuit depth provides a notion of equivalence between quantum phases: states in the same phase can be connected by shallow circuits, while lower bounds indicate phase transitions or topological order
[
CGW10
,
CPG19
]
. In Hamiltonian complexity, if no low energy states of a Hamiltonian can be prepared within a certain circuit class, it suggests long-range entanglement beyond the circuit’s expressive power, pointing to exotic quantum phases that persist even at higher temperatures
[
Nir22
]
.

A key challenge is developing techniques beyond the
lightcone argument
. This principle originates as Lieb-Robinson bounds in many-body physics
[
LR72
]
, which formalizes the idea that quantum information propagates at a limited speed through local interactions, reaching only regions within its
lightcone
.

Constant-depth quantum circuits with local gates (
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
circuits) have lightcones of constant size, severely limiting their computational power. Any two regions with nonintersecting backwards lightcones can not be more entangled than their backwards lightcones on the input state. Thus globally entangled states such as the
n
-qubit CAT state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0^{n}}+\ket{1^{n}})
cannot be prepared by constant depth circuits starting from the unentangled
\ket{0^{n}}
state.

In contrast, nonlocal circuits — such as those with logarithmic-depth, or many-qubit gates— have unbounded lightcones, making the argument ineffective for proving lower bounds.

The lightcone argument can also be used to prove lower bounds for
classical
circuits with local gates, so long as the depth is below
\log(n)
. Any output bit of the circuit can only depend on the inputs in its backwards lightcone. Again, once the circuit depth is logarithmic, the lightcone argument is no longer useful. In fact, proving lower bounds against
O(\log n)
-depth classical circuits,
\mathsf{NC}^{1}

However, in the classical world, we have been able to push beyond the lightcone argument with circuits that have unbounded lightcones but are still more restricted than
\mathsf{NC}^{1}

\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits— boolean circuits of constant depth with AND and OR gates that are allowed arbitrarily many input bits, or “unbounded fan-in”. Thus the outputs of the circuit can depend on all inputs, rendering the lightcone argument useless. Despite this, the structure of the model has enabled the development of powerful lower bound techniques, such as random restrictions
[
FSS84
,
Hås86
]
, Fourier analysis
[
LMN93
]
, and combinatorial methods
[
Smo87
]
. These tools have yielded strong lower bounds and insights across complexity theory
[

,

]

[

,

,

]

[

,
EIS22
,
KKMS08
]
, quantum-classical separations
[
RT22
]
, and more.

The success in going beyond lightcone arguments in classical complexity theory can be attributed to three key aspects of the
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
model:
it is
useful
, as lower bounds are actually provable;

In the quantum setting, there has been some effort towards adapting techniques that were successful for proving classical
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
lower bounds. In 1999 Moore proposed a quantum analogue,
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, constant depth quantum circuits with arbitrary single qubit gates and the multi-qubit quantum AND gate
\ket{x}\to(-1)^{\textsf{AND}(x)}\ket{x}

]
. While it resembles

,

Recent work has adapted Fourier analytic methods to quantum circuits

]
, mirroring

To date, lower bounds for

]

[

,

The difficulty of proving lower bounds for
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
suggests the need for a circuit model that’s expressive— capable, for example, of generating global entanglement — but is more tractable to analyze.

In this work, we introduce the magic hierarchy, a quantum circuit model consisting of circuits that alternate between arbitrary-size Clifford circuits and constant-depth circuits with arbitrary two-qubit gates (
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
). We define the
k
th level of the magic hierarchy as circuits alternating
k

\mathsf{QNC}^{0}

The level in the hierarchy reflects increasing complexity of
long-range magic
—that is, nonstabilizerness that cannot be removed with a low-depth circuit
[
WL25
]
— hence the name

In this paper we aim to demonstrate that, just like the classical
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
model, the magic hierarchy is interesting, useful, and natural:

Useful:
We demonstrate the usefulness of the magic hierarchy by proving lower bounds at its first level, pushing beyond existing lower bounds. Our techniques exploit the rigid structure of both Clifford and
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
circuits. While each of these circuit models is individually well-understood, their respective analysis tools—such as stabilizer methods for Clifford circuits and lightcone arguments for
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
— do not extend naturally to the other.
This tension necessitates the development of novel approaches to establish lower bounds.

Interesting:
We highlight interesting connections between the magic hierarchy and condensed matter physics, Hamiltonian complexity, and important open problems in classical complexity. Additionally, we show that the magic hierarchy unifies other well studied models of quantum computation such as constant-depth circuits with multi-qubit Fanout gates and models of quantum circuits with adaptive intermediate measurements.

Natural:
Clifford circuits and
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
circuits are ubiquitous in quantum computing, both in theory and in practice. These circuits are used in quantum algorithms, error-correction, nonlocal games and more. They also align with constraints in noisy quantum hardware, where the accumulation of noise limits the circuit depth. In most fault-tolerant architectures, Clifford operations are inexpensive, while non-Clifford gates are more costly.

This suggests the magic hierarchy as a natural setting for proving quantum circuit lower bounds beyond constant-depth models.

1.1
Formally defining the magic hierarchy

We propose the study of quantum circuits that alternate between arbitrary-size circuits with Clifford gates, and constant-depth quantum circuits with arbitrary two-qubit gates, also known as
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}

This choice simplifies the model while preserving the essential structural limitation of constant-depth circuits—namely, bounded lightcones. Furthermore, since Clifford circuits can permute qubits, enforcing geometric locality only increases the number of alternations by a constant factor.

Now that we have defined the magic hierarchy, we show that it unifies several previously studied circuit models. In particular, the level of the magic hierarchy is a complexity measure that captures the use of nonlocal operations such as fanout gates, or adaptive intermediate measurements.

Fanout gates

The multi-qubit Fanout gate

4.

1.3
Lower bounds in the first level of the magic hierarchy

As a first step to characterizing the computational power of the magic hierarchy, we develop techniques to prove limitations of circuits in the first level. In particular, we consider circuits consisting of first an arbitrary-size Clifford circuit followed by a constant-depth circuit (
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}
). We use two different techniques for proving that explicit state families can not be approximately prepared by
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}

1.6
Summary and future directions

In this work, we introduced the
magic hierarchy
, a quantum circuit model that captures and unifies several previously studied models, interpolating between circuits where lower bounds are well understood (
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
and Clifford circuits) and the more powerful model
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
, for which proving lower bounds remains a major open problem with significant implications for classical complexity theory. Our main technical results establish lower bounds at the first level of the hierarchy. We developed novel techniques to show that certain explicit quantum states—such as ground states of topologically ordered Hamiltonians and nonstabilizer quantum codes—cannot be approximately prepared by a Clifford circuit followed by a
\textsf{QNC}^{0}
circuit. More broadly, the magic hierarchy provides a natural framework for developing stronger circuit lower bounds and for understanding exotic quantum phases through the lens of circuit complexity.

We now highlight several directions for future exploration.

Condensed matter

In condensed matter systems, circuit complexity plays a fundamental role in characterizing quantum phases, as two states belong to the same phase if and only if a constant-depth circuit with geometrically-local gates transforms one into the other
[
CGW10
]
. Consequently, distinguishing quantum phases can establish circuit lower bounds in settings with local gates on a finite-dimensional lattice.

A recent conjecture in
[
TVV23b
]
suggests that intermediate measurements create a hierarchy of topologically ordered phases for preparing quantum double states.
[
SBS25
]
further show how these states can be constructed using constant-depth circuits with fanout gates (
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}_{f}
). This suggests that the computational power is strictly increasing with the level of the magic hierarchy. Furthermore,
[
TVV23b
]
conjectures that the Fibonacci anyon code and the quantum double model for nonsolvable groups lie beyond this hierarchy, implying that it is not in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
. While these ideas remain conjectural, they suggest promising avenues for leveraging the magic hierarchy to study topological phases and circuit lower bounds.

Strengthening lower bounds in the magic hierarchy

Much remains unknown about the structure of the magic hierarchy. We highlight several open problems related to proving stronger circuit lower bounds.

Open Problem 4
.

Prove a lower bound against
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{QC}
or a higher level of the magic hierarchy.

Since
\mathsf{MH}=\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
the magic hierarchy provides a pathway of increasing complexity starting from
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
all the way to
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
.
Is this hierarchy strict? Does each additional alternation increase computational power?

Open Problem 5

(Magic state hierarchy)
.

Does there exist an explicit sequence of families of quantum states
\{\ket{\psi_{n}^{1}}\},\{\ket{\psi_{n}^{2}}\},\dots
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the family of states
\{\ket{\psi_{n}^{k}}\}
can be prepared with
k
alternations between Clifford and
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
circuits, but can not be approximately prepared with only
k-1
alternations?

We note that, even proving this conjecture for a specific sequence would still not establish a lower bound on
\mathsf{MH}
or
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
since all such states still lie within the hierarchy.

Another interesting question concerns the relationship between the two classes
\mathsf{A}_{k}\mathsf{CQ}
and
\mathsf{A}_{k}\mathsf{QC}
. In
Theorem

1.2
we show that
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}
and
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{QC}

Open Problem 6
.

Are
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}
and
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{QC}

Upper bounds

In this work we only proved lower bounds for preparing certain states within the magic hierarchy. Are there matching upper bounds?

Quantum advantage

Constant depth quantum circuits already exhibit quantum advantage for sampling tasks under standard complexity assumptions
[
TD02
,
Aar05
]
. However, they offer no advantage for decision problems, since observables on small subsets of qubits can be efficiently computed classically.

Open Problem 7
.

Does there exist a level
k\in\mathbb{N}
of the magic hierarchy that can solve a decision problem that can not be solved in polynomial time classically? If so, at which level does this occur?

In the case where there exists a specific level
k
at which we can achieve quantum advantage, this is excellent news for achieving quantum advantage on near-term quantum hardware. There would only need to be a constant number of “difficult-to-implement” layers of the circuit: whether as non-Clifford gates on a fault-tolerant device, or fanout gates in a constant depth circuit.

On the other hand, if there is no level that achieves quantum advantage, then this implies a separation between the magic hierarchy and
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
from
BQP
, assuming that polynomial-time quantum computation is more powerful than classical
\textsf{BQP}\neq\textsf{BPP}
.

While this paper was in preparation, two independent works —

[

Like us, they exploit the fact that stabilizer states have integer-valued mutual information, show that low depth circuits cannot change the mutual information, and apply the Fannes-Audenaert inequality to make their bounds robust. 2.3
Entropy and correlation measures

Definition 2.5

(von Neumann entropy / entanglement entropy)
.

\displaystyle S(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-\tr\rho\log\rho.

Definition 2.6

(Quantum mutual information)
.

\displaystyle\textbf{I}_{{\rho}}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{AB})

For an
n
-qubit state
\rho
, the quantum mutual information between two regions takes a real value in
[0,n]
.

The quantum mutual information between
A
and
B
is a measure of the shared information, or correlations between regions
A
and
B
. We note that this includes classical correlations. The state
\frac{1}{2}(\ket{00}\!\bra{00}+\ket{11}\!\bra{11})
is a classical mixture of product states
\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}
, yet the quantum mutual information between the two qubits is maximal
\textbf{I}{\left({1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{2}\right)}=1
.
Since von Neumann entropy is invariant under unitary transformation, we have that the quantum mutual information
\textbf{I}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)}
is also invariant under local unitary transformations on
A
and
B
in tensor product.

\displaystyle\textbf{I}_{{\rho}}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)}=\textbf{I}_{{U\rho U^{\dagger}}}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)},

\displaystyle\text{for }U=U_{A}\otimes U_{B}\otimes I_{(A\cup B)^{c}}

(5)

Furthermore, quantum mutual information is nonincreasing under partial trace
[
Wat18
]
. So for subsets of qubits
A\subseteq A^{\prime},B\subseteq B^{\prime}
of quantum state
\rho
, we have
\textbf{I}_{{\rho}}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)}\leq\textbf{I}_{{\rho}}{\left({A^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B^{\prime}}\right)}
.

2.4
Stabilizer states

Definition 2.7

(Pauli group)
.

The
n
-qubit Pauli group, denoted
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, contains elements that are a tensor product of
n
single-qubit Pauli matrices.

\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left\{i^{b}\sigma_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}b\in\{0,1,2,3\},\ \sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\right\}}

Here,
I
is the identity and
X,Y,Z
are the single-qubit Pauli matrices.

\displaystyle I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

Definition 2.8

(stabilizer group)
.

A stabilizer group
is an abelian subgroup of the Pauli group
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. That is
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a stabilizer group if all elements of
\mathcal{S}
commute with each other. A
generating set
of
\mathcal{S}
is a subset
G\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that each element of
\mathcal{S}
can be written as a product of elements in
G
. The dimension of
\mathcal{S}
, denoted as
|\mathcal{S}|\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
is the size of a minimal generating set. The total number of elements in
\mathcal{S}
is
2^{|\mathcal{S}|}
.

Definition 2.9

(stabilizer state)
.

For a stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
, the corresponding
stabilizer state
is
\rho(\mathcal{S})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}\sigma
.

Note that this definition of stabilizer states allows for mixed quantum states. The dimension of the stabilizer group is indicative of the purity of the state. In the case where
|\mathcal{S}|=n
, the state
\rho(\mathcal{S})
is pure, that is
\rho=\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}
for some pure state
\ket{\psi}
. More generally, we have that

\displaystyle S(\rho(\mathcal{S}))=n-|\mathcal{S}|

After tracing out a subsystem
A^{c}\subset[n]
of a stabilizer state, the remaining state is

\displaystyle\rho(\mathcal{S})_{A}=\tr_{A^{c}}\rho(\mathcal{S})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}\tr_{A^{c}}\sigma=\frac{1}{2^{|A|}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}

(6)

Where
\mathcal{S}_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left\{\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{P}_{|A|}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\sigma_{A}\otimes I^{\otimes|A^{c}|}\in\mathcal{S}\right\}}
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{A}
is also an abelian subgroup of
\mathcal{P}_{|A|}
and so it is also a stabilizer group, and
\rho(\mathcal{S})_{A}
a stabilizer state.

Fact 2.10
.

For any two stabilizer states
\rho,\rho
, their overlap
\tr(\rho\sigma)
is a power of
\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be the stabilizer groups of
\rho
and
\rho
respectively.

\displaystyle\tr(\rho\sigma)=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{S},\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\tr(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}{\left|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\right|}

Since
\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is also an abelian subgroup of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
{\left|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\right|}=2^{k}
for some
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
∎

The proof of

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\psi=U\psi^{\prime}U^{\dagger}
and
{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}_{U}({\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}_{U}(i))\cap{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}_{U}({\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}_{U}(j))=\emptyset

\displaystyle\textbf{I}_{{\psi}}{\left({i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{j}\right)}\leq\ \textbf{I}_{{\psi^{\prime}}}{\left({{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(i)}\right)}\leq\ \textbf{I}_{{\psi}}{\left({{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}({\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(i))}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}({\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(i))}\right)}.

(7)

Proof.

Since
{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(i)\cap{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(j)
, the sub-circuit
{U}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}({i,j})}
induced by the backwards lightcone of

has a tensor product structure

\displaystyle{U}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}({i,j})}={U}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}({i})}\otimes{U}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}({i})}

(8)

By
2.2
we have that.

\displaystyle\psi_{i,j}=\tr_{\{i,j\}^{c}}U\psi^{\prime}U^{\dagger}=\tr_{\{i,j\}^{c}}V\psi^{\prime}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}(i,j)}V^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\text{for }V={U}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}({i})}\otimes{U}_{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}({i})}\otimes I_{({\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(i)\cup{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\leftarrow$}}\vss}}}(j))^{c}}

(9)

So the mutual information can be written as follows.

\displaystyle\textbf{I}_{{\psi}}{\left({i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{j}\right)}=\textbf{I}_{{U\psi^{\prime}U^{\dagger}}}{\left({i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{j}\right)}=\textbf{I}_{{V\psi^{\prime}V^{\dagger}}}{\left({i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{j}\right)}

Now, using the fact that quantum mutual information is non-increasing under partial trace, and that quantum mutual information is invariant under local unitaries (

\displaystyle\leq\textbf{I}_{{V\psi^{\prime}V^{\dagger}}}{\left({{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}(j)}\right)}=\textbf{I}_{{\psi^{\prime}}}{\left({{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{{\mathop{\mathsf{L}}\limits^{\vbox to-0.4pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\text{\tiny$\rightarrow$}}\vss}}}(j)}\right)}.

(11)

We have shown the first inequality in our

. The second inequality follows from the same argument.
∎

The second key observation is that the class of stabilizer states are
discrete
, and so is their mutual information.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
n
-qubit stabilizer state
\varphi
, and each pair of disjoint subsets of qubits
A,B\subseteq[n]
, if
A\cap B=\emptyset
, then
\textbf{I}_{{\varphi}}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)}\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the stabilizer group for
\varphi
. As mentioned in
[
FCY+04
]
, we can write the entanglement of the state
\varphi
as
S(\varphi)=n-|\mathcal{S}|
. Here
|\mathcal{S}|
is the rank of the stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
, the size of the minimal generating set. We first prove this fact for completeness. We can write
\varphi
as a linear combination of its stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
,
\varphi=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}s
. This is proportional to the projector
\Pi
onto the
+1
eigenspace of
\mathcal{S}
.
\varphi=2^{|\mathcal{S}|-n}\Pi
, where
|\mathcal{S}|
is the rank of the stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
, the size of its minimal generating set. Therefore the entropy of
\varphi
is
S(\varphi)=-\tr(\rho\log\rho)=-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}2^{|\mathcal{S}|-n}\log 2^{|\mathcal{S}|-n}=n-|\mathcal{S}|
.

We now consider the reduced state on qubits in
A
. We can write

\displaystyle\varphi_{A}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\tr_{A^{c}}s=\frac{1}{2^{|A|}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}s.

(12)

Where
\mathcal{S}_{A}={\left\{\tr_{A^{c}}s\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}s\in\mathcal{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\tr(s)\neq 0\right\}}
. Thus
\varphi_{A}
is also a stabilizer state with stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}_{A}
. So
S(\varphi_{A})=|A|-|\mathcal{S}_{A}|
. Overall, the mutual information is

\displaystyle\textbf{I}_{{\varphi}}{\left({A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{B}\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=S(\varphi_{A})+S(\varphi_{B})-S(\varphi_{AB})=|A|-|\mathcal{S}_{A}|+|B|-|\mathcal{S}_{B}|-(|B|-|\mathcal{S}_{AB}|)=|\mathcal{S}_{AB}|-|\mathcal{S}_{A}|-|\mathcal{S}_{B}|

(13)

which is an integer, as desired. Finally, the fact that mutual information is non-negative completes the proof.

∎

Already we can sketch a proof for lower bounding the circuit depth required to exactly prepare
\psi
. Combining these two

, we have that if
\psi=U\phi^{S}U^{\dagger}
for some stabilizer state
\phi^{S}

4
Lower bounds for quantum error correcting codes

In this section we identify some subspaces such that
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}

any
of the states in the subspace, even approximately. In particular, we consider
locally-defined subspaces
which we can interpret as codespaces of codes with local-checks, or groundspaces of local Hamiltonians with topological order.

We show that any local codespace that is not approximately a perturbed stabilizer code — a stabilizer code rotated by some constant depth (
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
) circuit — cannot be prepared by
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}
circuits. This allows us to prove lower bounds for the Feynman-Kitaev history state for preparing the CAT state, and for states in a codespace with dimension that is not a power of 2.

Our key ingredient is proving an infectiousness property which we state informally below.

(Robust) infectiousness of
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}
in codes:

For each local code
\mathcal{C}
with good distance, if
{\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}}
can (approximately) prepare a single state in
\mathcal{C}
then
\mathcal{C}
is (approximately) a perturbed stabilizer state.

We provide two separate proofs to show the
exact
infectiousness property and the
robust
infectiousness property. The proof to show exact infectiousness is more simple, but can only be used to prove lower bounds for exact state preparation. We need the robust infectiousness property in order to prove robust lower bounds, meaning lower bounds against even
approximately
preparing a state. The proof of the exact version is in
Section

4.2
and the robust version is in
Section

4.3
. We note that in independent concurrent work,
[
WL25
]
also proved the infectiousness property.

4.2
Proof of exact infectiousness

In the following theorem we show that for a local code that’s defined as the groundspace of a local Hamiltonian with sufficiently large distance, if one of the codewords can be prepared exactly in low depth starting from a stabilizer state, then the code is actually a local stabilizer code that is perturbed by a low-depth circuit.

4.4

Corollary

4.16
tells us that codespaces that are not approximately perturbed Hamiltonians cannot be approximately prepared by
\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{CQ}

]
with a different approach.

Adaptive intermediate measurements:

Adaptive intermediate measurements are another surprisingly powerful nonlocal operation that is used to supplement the power of constant depth circuits.
We consider constant-depth quantum circuits (
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
)that are allowed mid-circuit measurements where classical processing is done on the measurement outcomes and fed back into the next stage of the circuit. See
Section

1.2
for our more formal definition of this model.

We consider the case that the classical circuits are restricted to be polynomials-sized
parity circuits
, meaning that they allow only gates computing the parity of a subset of the bits
\textsf{parity}(x)=\sum_{i}x_{i}\mod 2
. Furthermore, as is typical for classical circuits, the output of the parity gate has unbounded fan-out and can be copied many times.
In the case where the classical computation allows for only parity gates, the number of required intermediate measurement rounds,
\textsc{Measurement}[\oplus]\textsc{rounds}

See
1.7

Using gate teleportation any Clifford circuit can be implemented with
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}

BKP11
]
. Therefore
\textsc{Measurement}[\oplus]\textsc{rounds}\leq\textsc{Clifford-rounds}
. On the other hand, we show that a Clifford circuit can also simulate one round of intermediate measurements when the classical processing only uses parity gates. First, for each qubit that is measured, we copy the bit (in the standard basis) into an ancillary register set to
\ket{0}
by applying a
CNOT
gate. This ancillary qubit is left alone for the rest of the computation. By the principal of delayed measurement this is equivalent to measuring the qubit in the standard basis. Then, since Clifford circuits can compute the Parity function
\textsf{parity}(x)=\sum_{i}x_{i}\mod 2
and the Fanout gate, they can simulate any classical circuit with parity gates. Therefore,

\displaystyle\textsc{Measurement}[\oplus]\textsc{rounds}=\textsc{Clifford-rounds}

(72)

Using

∎

Magic and T-depth

Magic
in quantum computation refers to the presence of non-Clifford gates in a circuit. A widely used measure of magic is the T-count—–the number of
T
gates required in a Clifford+
T

[

]
, adding any non-Clifford gate enables universal quantum computation. Importantly, circuits with low

T

[
BG16
]
.

The number of alternations between
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
and
\mathsf{Cliff}
can be viewed as another measure of magic that is gate-set agnostic. Since
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
circuits allow arbitrary two-qubit operations, the required number of
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}

T
-depth
—–the number of circuit layers that contain at least one
T
gate in a Clifford+
T
circuit
[
AMMR13
]
.
See
1.8

T
-depth may be particularly relevant in fault-tolerant architectures, where Clifford gates can typically be implemented transversally, while non-Clifford gates like

are more costly to realize.

Whether the number of
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
layers and T-depth are equivalent remains an open question. The Solovay-Kitaev theorem allows for the approximation of arbitrary universal gate sets with
\log(1/\varepsilon)
overhead, and it is unclear whether this can be parallelized with the use of a “free” Clifford circuit.

Since
