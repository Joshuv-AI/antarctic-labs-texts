Is a subxiphoid approach equivalent to midline sternotomy for extended thymectomy for achieving disease remission in patients with myasthenia gravis?
Authors: Alison Zhu, Ursula Kemp
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryA best evidence topic in cardiac surgery was written according to a structured protocol. The question addressed was ‘In patients with myasthenia gravis undergoing extended thymectomy, is a subxiphoid approach equivalent to midline sternotomy for achieving disease remission?’ Altogether 106 papers were found using the reported search, of which 5 represented the best evidence to answer the clinical question. The authors, journal, date and country of publication, patient group studied, study type, relevant outcomes and results of these papers are tabulated. Five studies of a total of 803 patients with myasthenia gravis (MG) reported their experience with thymectomy by subxiphoid approach versus thymectomy by midline sternotomy. These studies demonstrated shorter operation duration, reduced intraoperative blood loss, reduced hospital length of stay and lower postoperative pain scores in the subxiphoid group. There was no significant difference in rates of postoperative MG crisis between the 2 groups, and similar rates of improvement in quantitative myasthenia gravis scores. We conclude that while further high-quality research is required, thymectomy by subxiphoid approach for MG may be considered in preference to midline sternotomy due to improved short-term postoperative outcomes and similar disease remission rates.Keywords: Review, Myasthenia gravis, Thymectomy, Thymoma, Subxiphoid

INTRODUCTION

A best evidence topic was constructed according to a structured protocol. This is fully described in the ICVTS [1].

THREE-PART QUESTION

In [patients with myasthenia gravis undergoing extended thymectomy], is a [subxiphoid approach] equivalent to [midline sternotomy] for [disease remission].

CLINICAL SCENARIO

A 32-year-old female with myasthenia gravis (MG) is referred for management of an anterior mediastinal mass, of 4 cm in diameter, likely to be a thymoma. After discussion with the neurologist, the consensus was for extended thymectomy to improve the chance of complete remission. While a midline sternotomy would allow for the removal of ectopic thymic tissue from the neck and mediastinum, the patient is concerned about the cosmesis of a large scar. While you are aware of the benefits of a minimally invasive uniportal subxiphoid approach with carbon dioxide insufflation, including improved cosmetic outcome, reduced postoperative pain and reduced hospital length of stay, you are uncertain if it offers similar disease remission rates, particularly given the size of the mass. You resolve to check the literature for evidence.

SEARCH STRATEGY

The literature search was performed in Medline from 1946 to October 2023 using the Ovid interface using the search terms: [Myasthenia gravis/OR thymectomy/] AND [thoracoscopic OR mediastinoscopic] AND [subxiphoid OR subcostal OR infrasternal]. A review of references of all papers was performed.

SEARCH OUTCOME

A total of 106 papers were found using the reported search. Inclusion criteria were full texts of randomized control trials or cohort studies comparing subxiphoid approach with midline sternotomy for thymectomy in patients with MG. Studies with no comparison between approaches were excluded. Nine papers were excluded as they did not address the surgical approach to thymectomy for patients with MG. Two papers were excluded as they were conference abstracts, 12 papers were excluded as they were reviews without presentation of patient data, 8 papers were excluded as they presented surgical technique only and 25 papers were case reports or case series. Twenty-one papers compared subxiphoid approach with lateral intercostal approach to thymectomy, and 24 papers reported on subxiphoid approach with no comparison. From the above search, 5 papers were identified that provided the best evidence to answer the question. These papers are presented in Table 1.

RESULTS

Yin et al. [2] present a retrospective cohort study of 502 patients with thymoma and MG undergoing extended thymectomy, of which 424 underwent subxiphoid resection (SX) and 78 underwent median sternotomy (MS). Due to significant differences in age and body mass index between the 2 groups, the authors performed propensity-matched analysis to achieve covariate balance. This resulted in 78 patients in the SX group (47 males, 33 females) to achieve covariate balance with non-significant difference for all variables. Patients included Masaoka stage I to IIIB, and similar mean tumour diameter between the 2 groups (3.5 ± 2.7 cm in the MS group and 3.8 ± 1.9 cm in the SX group, P = 0.423). The authors found that the SX group had a shorter operation time (P < 0.001), reduced intraoperative bleeding (P < 0.001) and shorter postoperative hospital stay (P = 0.003). Postoperative myasthenia relief was reported as 72 in the SX group and 69 in the MS group (P = 0.588), although it is unclear how this was measured. There were fewer patients who had postoperative myasthenia crisis (n = 3 compared to n = 8 in the MS group), although it is unclear if this was statistically significant. They conclude that subxiphoid resection is a less invasive procedure with favourable safety, with no difference in rates of myasthenia relief for thymoma in MG.

Zhu et al. [3] present 89 patients with thymomatous MG (Masaoka stage I and stage II) who underwent extended thymectomy by subxiphoid (SX) approach (n = 44) or trans-sternal (TS) approach (n = 45). Mean maximal tumour size was not different between the SX and TS approaches (P = 0.162). There was no statistically significance difference in postoperative MG crisis between the 2 groups (P = 0.677). While the mean quantitative myasthenia gravis (QMG) score was similar between the 2 groups preoperatively, the mean QMG score was significantly lower in the SX group at 1 month (P < 0.001) and 3 months (P = 0.011) postoperatively. However, this was not the case at 6 or 12 months. Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated that the median time to achieve improved clinical status was 3 months (95% confidence interval 2.15–3.85) in the SX approach, compared to 6 months (95% confidence interval 5.54–6.46) for MS. Univariate and multivariate analyses identified the subxiphoid group to show a faster improvement in clinical status, defined as a 2.3 point reduction in QMG score. Overall, subxiphoid approach is associated with more rapidly improved QMG score by 3 months indicating a faster improvement in clinical status, for patients with Masaoka stage I and II thymoma. However, Masaoka stage I and stage II thymoma are on the most part not macroscopically invasive and, thus, may be less associated with myasthenia symptoms, limiting the generalizability of this study to more advanced stage thymoma.

Shiomi et al. [4] present 33 patients with thymomatous and non-thymomatous MG who underwent extended thymectomy by modified trans-xiphoid approach (MTXA) (n = 13) and MS (n = 20). Masaoko stage III to IV thymoma and thymoma of >5 cm in diameter were excluded. The MTXA group had lower operative blood loss (P < 0.0001) and shorter postoperative hospital stay (P = 0.004). There were no patients with postoperative MG crisis in the MTXA group, compared to 4 patients in the sternotomy group. No statistical significance is reported for this difference. There was no significant difference between MTXA and sternotomy for neurological or functional outcomes [Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living scale and/or QMG score (P = 0.425), anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody level (P = 0.431) and dose of oral prednisone (P = 0.676)]. Although the small sample size did not allow for propensity score analysis, and follow-up duration was only 3 months, this study suggests that MTXA for extended thymectomy is not inferior to that via sternotomy.

Zhang et al. [5] present 155 patients who underwent thymectomy for non-thymomatous MG, of which 57 patients were by TS approach and 47 patients were by thoracoscopic subxiphoid (TSX) approach. An additional 51 patients were included in this study who had right unilateral thoracoscopic approach. Disease remission was categorized as complete remission, partial remission, unchanging, or progression of disease, based on consensus between surgeon and neurologist. The TSX approach patients had lower intraoperative blood loss (P < 0.001) and reduced length of hospital stay (P = 0.039). There was no significant difference in postoperative myasthenic crisis (P = 0.563) or remission rates (P = 0.667). Univariate logistic regression demonstrated higher efficiency of ectopic thymic tissue removal in the TS group (P = 0.011) and the TSX group (P = 0.033). However, only the TS group, and not the TSX group, retained this result with multivariate logistic regression. Overall, TSX approach has benefits over TS approach in terms of short-term postoperative recovery. Limitations include potential bias given operative approach was determined by surgeon preference. Operative technique was additionally not described in detail; thus, it remains unclear the standardization of approach. The authors conclude that while TSX approach is a potential alternative to extended thymectomy, TS approach remains the superior approach for efficient removal of thymic tissue.

Murai et al. [6] present 24 patients with non-thymomatous MG undergoing infrasternal mediastinoscopic thymectomy (IMT, n = 14) and transsternal thymectomy (TT, n = 10). No surgical-specific outcomes, such as operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay or mortality, were reported. Both groups demonstrated significant decreases in QMG scores (P < 0.001) and in anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody titres (P = 0.027 for IMT, P = 0.020 for TT) from preoperatively to >5 years after surgery. Twenty-one percent (n = 3) of IMT patients and 20% (n = 2) of TT patients were in complete stable remission at >5 years after surgery (P = 0.932). The authors concluded that the long-term outcomes and immunological effects of IMT are similar to TT, although the small sample limits generalizability of findings.

CLINICAL BOTTOM LINE

In MG patients with thymomatous and non-thymomatous disease, thymectomy by subxiphoid approach allows for a smaller incision, ensuring improved cosmesis, reduced intraoperative blood loss, reduced postoperative pain and shorter hospital length of stay. There is no difference in postoperative MG crisis, and similar rates of improvement in QMG scores. One study reported similar complete stable remission rates between subxiphoid and transsternal groups. Limitations of studies include small sample sizes, variation in reported MG neurological outcomes and lack of long-term follow-up. These studies present a heterogenous patient population, composed of both non-thymomatous and thymomatous MG, limiting generalizability to the wider MG population. In conclusion, thymectomy by subxiphoid approach for myasthenia gravis may be considered in preference to midline sternotomy due to improved short-term postoperative outcomes and similar disease remission rates, although further high-quality research is required.
