SUN5 interacts with TRIM28, enhancing IκBα ubiquitination to promote glycolysis in colorectal cancer cells
Authors: Jingyuan Chen, Youbo Yang, Gang Liu, Lihua Huang, Yunfei Zhang, Yufeng Wang, Xiuwen Xu, Xiaowei Xing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glycolysis provides the main energy source for the rapid proliferation and migration of colorectal cancer (CRC) cells. In our previous studies, we reported that SUN5, a nuclear membrane protein, promotes proliferation and migration. However, whether SUN5 is involved in the process of glycolysis is unclear. Here, we demonstrate that overexpression of SUN5 enhances glucose uptake and lactate production in CRC cells, whereas the opposite results are observed in SUN5-knockdown cells. Mechanistically, SUN5 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, which can be inhibited by the IKK inhibitor BAY11-7082. Further studies reveal that SUN5 interacts with TRIM28 to increase IκB α ubiquitination, leading to the nuclear translocation of phosphorylated P65 (phos-P65) and subsequent increases in the transcription of GLUT1 and LDHA, accelerating glycolysis. Moreover, xenograft transplantation experiments reveal that the knockdown of SUN5 inhibits glycolysis and tumorigenesis in vivo. Taken together, these findings indicate that SUN5 enhances the glycolysis and tumorigenesis of CRC cells via interaction with TRIM28, which provides a potential target for the diagnosis and treatment of CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignant tumor and the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1]. In China, 608,000 new cases of CRC occur, and 202,000 deaths occur every year [2]. The molecular mechanism underlying the development of CRC is not fully understood. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving CRC progression is crucial for identifying novel and effective therapeutic targets for CRC patients.

Cancer cells prefer glycolysis, which is characterized by elevated glucose consumption and lactate production even with adequate oxygen [3]. This phenomenon is known as the Warburg effect [ 4, 5], which regulates various biological processes in CRC, including cell viability [6], invasive migration [ 7, 8], chemotherapy resistance [9] and stemness [10]. The regulation of the Warburg effect is associated with abnormal activation of multiple signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt/mTOR [11], Wnt/β-catenin [12], Hippo/YAP [13] and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways [ 14 – 16]. The NF-κB complex consists of five members, including RelA (P65), RelB, c-Rel, NF-κB1 (P50), and NF-κB2 (P52). The P50/P65 complex is sequestered in the cytoplasm by the IκB protein complex in the quiescent state. A canonical mechanism of NF-κB activation involves IκBα phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and subsequent proteasome-mediated degradation [17]; thus, the P65/P50 dimer translocate to the nucleus to induce the expression of related genes, including glucose transporter 1 ( GLUT1) [ 14, 16] and hexokinase 2 ( HK2) [18]. NF-κB signaling pathway also regulates the promoter activity and expression of hypoxia-induced factor-1α ( HIF-1α) [19], contributing to metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells by activating the expression of glycolytic enzymes, including GLUT1/3, pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) [20] and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) [ 21, 22]. Investigations into the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway may facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of glycolysis in CRC.

Recently, a novel family of nuclear membrane proteins, the Sad1/UNC84 domain (SUN) proteins, was shown to be involved in the glucose metabolism of tumors. The structures of the SUN family members tend to be conserved, with a transmembrane (TM) region at the N-terminus, a helical convoluted structural domain in the middle, and the SUN structural domain at the C-terminus. To date, five SUN proteins have been identified, namely, SUN1, SUN2, SUN3, SUN4 (SPAG4), and SUN5 (SPAG4L, TSARG4) [ 23– 27]. SUN1 and SUN2 are downregulated in cancer, whereas SUN4 and SUN5 are enriched in cancer cells [ 24, 27– 29]. In oral cancer [28] and lung cancer [25], SUN2 exerts tumor suppressor functions by inhibiting the Warburg effect via SIRT5. SUN4 is known as a tumor marker in different cancers, including lung cancer [30], renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [31], hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [32] and glioblastoma [33]. SUN4 enhances mitochondrial respiration and aerobic glycolysis in CRC cells by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [34].

SUN5, the fifth member of the SUN gene family, was first identified by our group and subsequently submitted to GenBank (GenBank accession AF401350). SUN5 was identified as a testis-specific gene. The protein encoded by SUN5 has typical characteristics of the SUN domain protein family. SUN5 deficiency disrupts sperm head-tail junctions [ 35, 36]. SUN5 is also involved in mRNA export during spermatogenesis [37]. In a previous study, we reported that SUN5 was highly expressed in CRC tissues and cells. SUN5 overexpression promoted tumor proliferation and migration via the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway in LoVo and DLD-1 cells, whereas SUN5 knockdown yielded the opposite results. The inhibition of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway by PD0325901 could be partly rescued by SUN5 overexpression [29]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that SUN5 and SUN4 are highly homologous, indicating that they probably originated from a common gene ancestor [23] and that SUN4 facilitates glucose metabolism in cancer cells [36]. Thus, we speculate that SUN5 may be involved in glycolysis.

The tripartite motif (TRIM) family is a large family of proteins that usually contain four typical motifs: a RING domain in the N-terminus, a B-box domain, a coiled-coil domain, and a variable C-terminal domain. TRIMs play important roles by regulating protein ubiquitination in various cellular processes, such as immunity, autophagy and carcinogenesis [38]. Some members of the TRIM protein family are involved in regulating the stability of proteins, including IκB α. For example, TRIM22 and TRIM27 activate the NF-κB signaling pathway by promoting the ubiquitination of IκB α [ 39, 40]. The TRIM28 protein is widely studied in cancer. TRIM28 protects TRIM24 from ubiquitination and degradation to promote tumor growth in prostate cancer [41]. In breast cancer, TRIM28 interacts with HIF-1 and DNA-PK to release paused RNA polymerase II and activate target transcription in response to hypoxia [42]. TRIM28 is also a suppressor of anticancer immunity [ 43, 44]. As TRIM22 and TRIM27 can facilitate the ubiquitination of IκBα in GBM and RCC, we propose that TRIM28 could play a crucial role in the regulation of IκBα ubiquitination.

In the present study, we hypothesized that SUN5 interacts with TRIM28 to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway and investigated the function of SUN5 in glycolysis in CRC and its underlying mechanisms in vitro and in vivo. This study helps elucidate the function of SUN5 in glycolysis, providing a new target for CRC diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and Methods

Culture of CRC cell lines

All human CRC cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). SW620, SW480 and LoVo cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Procell, Wuhan, China). DLD-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS. All the cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO 2.

Generation of SUN5 OE or KD stable cells

The full-length coding sequence of SUN5 with a Flag tag sequence was cloned and inserted into pLVX-IRES-Puro lentivirus vector to generate a pLVX-IRES-Puro-SUN5-Flag fusion expression plasmid. The recombinant and control plasmids were packaged into lentiviruses by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China) as described in our previous study [29]. A short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence that interferes with SUN5 gene expression was designed and inserted into the pLV3ltr-ZsGreen-Puro-U6 vector. The target sequence for interfering SUN5 was 5′-GATGACAGCATAAATGGTCCA-3′ and the control sequence was 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′. The cells were transfected with SUN5 overexpression/knockdown or control lentiviruses (Tsingke Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and treated with puromycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 2 weeks to generate stable SUN5-OE or SUN5-KD CRC cells. The efficiencies of SUN5 overexpression or knockdown were verified by western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis

In general, total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1% PMSF (Biosharp, Hefei, China), a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (GLPBIO, Montclair, USA) for 20 min on ice, and then centrifuged at 13,500 g at 4°C for 20 min. Protein samples were separated by 8%–10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, USA). The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or 5% bovine serum albumin for 2 h at room temperature, incubated with the indicated primary antibodies including SUN5 (1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), α-Tubulin (1:1000; Beyotime), GLUT1 (1:1500; Beyotime) and LDHA (1:1000; Beyotime) at 4°C overnight, and washed with TBST three times for 10 min each. The membranes were then incubated with the HRP-coupled mouse or rabbit secondary antibodies (1:10,000; Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature and washed three times with TBST (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 5 min each. Finally, signals were obtained from the membrane which was treated with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Biosharp) and quantified with the VisionWorks system (AnalytikJena AG, Jena, Germany). Information about the antibodies and other reagents used in this study is listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Measurement of glucose and 2-NBDG uptake and lactate production

The glucose and lactate concentrations in the culture medium were measured via a glucose assay kit (Beyotime) and a lactate assay kit (SolarBio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the measurement of 2-NBDG uptake, The cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 1 × 10 5 cells per well and exposed to 100 μM 2-(N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl)amino)-2-deoxyglucose (2-NBDG; GLPBIO) in glucose-free DMEM. The plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO 2 for 2 h. Images were obtained via identical acquisition settings on a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). The mean fluorescence intensity was analyzed via ImageJ software. The experiment was independently repeated at least three times.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from SUN5-knockdown and control SW620 cells via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, some of the samples were sent to BGI-Wuhan (Wuhan, China) for eukaryotic transcriptome sequencing. The cDNA libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq instrument (Illumina, San Diego, USA), and could be queried at BGI website ( http://biosys.bgi.com). GO ( http://www.geneontology.org/) and KEGG ( https://www.kegg.jp/) enrichment analyses of the DEGs were performed via the hypergeometric test implemented in Phyper, with a threshold of a Q value ≤ 0.05. Genes meeting these criteria were considered significantly enriched in the candidate gene set.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted via AG RNAex Pro RNA Reagent (AG21102; Accurate Biology, Changsha, China). The first cDNA strand was synthesized according to the protocol for HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed via specific primers with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix-Q711-02 (Vazyme) on a qPCR instrument (LightCycler 480; Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The steps for real-time PCR were pre-denaturation at 95°C for 2 min 30 s, and 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing and extension at 60°C for 30 s, and melting curves were obtained for quality control. The results were quantified using the 2 –∆∆Ct method. The sequences of RT-qPCR primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. All procedures were performed at least three times.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates or 3.5-cm dishes with glass slides. After the cells attached to the slides, they were washed with PBS three times, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Beyotime) for 10 min, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies including Flag (1:500; Proteintech), P65 (1:100; Proteintech), phos-P65 (1:50; Santa Cruz, Dallas, USA) and TRIM28 (1:50; Beyotime) at 4°C overnight. The cells were subsequently washed three times and incubated with the Cy3 (1:200; Beyotime) or Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:100; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime). The slides were photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

We performed two rescue experiments by treating cells with BAY11-7082, a selective inhibitor of the NF-κB signaling pathway. CCK-8 assay was used to clarify the changes in cell proliferation ability. In experiment 1, the cells were divided into three groups: (1) the control group (LoVo and DLD-1 cells transfected with control lentivirus and then treated with DMSO); (2) the SUN5-OE group (treated with DMSO); and (3) SUN5-OE cells treated with BAY11-7082 (HY-13453, MCE, Monmouth Junction, USA). In experiment 2, the cells were classified into three groups: (1) the control group (LoVo and DLD-1 cells were treated with DMSO and then transfected with the blank vector); (2) the cells were treated with BAY11-7082 for 8 h and then transfected with the blank vector; and (3) the cells were treated with BAY11-7082 for 8 h and then transfected with the SUN5 overexpression plasmid. In the two rescue experiments, approximately 4000 cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates, with six replicates for each group. CCK-8 reagents (GK10001; GLPBIO) were added at 0, 24, and 48 h and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm via an EnVision microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA).

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

Cells seeded into 6-well plates were treated with serum-free medium containing 10 μM CHX (GC17198; GLPBIO) and harvested at 0, 3, and 6 h posttreatment. The stability of the IκB α protein was determined by western blot analysis.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis and ubiquitination assay

The cells were seeded in two or three 10-cm dishes. Then, the cells were washed with PBS three times and centrifuged at 100 g for 5 min. Total proteins were extracted and lysed with IP lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1% PMSF (Biosharp) and a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (GLPBIO) on ice for 10 min and then centrifuged at 13,500 g at 4°C for 15 min. The supernatants were removed, and the indicated antibodies were added for incubation with shaking at 4°C overnight. Then, the protein samples were incubated with antibody-conjugated magnetic beads with rotation at room temperature for 1 h according to the instructions of the Co-IP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and western blot analysis was subsequently conducted on the purified protein. For the ubiquitination assay, cells were seeded in three 10-cm dishes per group and treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (GLPBIO) at a concentration of 10 μM for 8 h. Then, the cells were harvested and washed with PBS three times. IκBα was immunoprecipitated from cell lysates via an IκBα antibody (Proteintech). Western blot analysis was performed to detect IκBα ubiquitination by using a polyclonal ubiquitin antibody (Proteintech).

TRIM28 overexpression or knockdown

The TRIM28 overexpression plasmid (pLV4ltr-PGK-ZsGreen(2A)Puro-CMV-c3×FLAG-TRIM28) and its control plasmid (pLV4ltr-PGK-ZsGreen(2A)Puro-CMV-c3×FLAG) were purchased from Corues Biotechnology (Nanjing, China). siRNAs targeting human TRIM28 (gene id: 10155) were designed and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The cells were seeded in 6-well plates or 10-cm dishes at approximately 70% confluence. Before transfection, the cells were treated with serum-free medium for 1 h and then transfected with the indicated plasmids, siRNAs or siRNA-control via Lipofectamine 8000 (Beyotime). The sequences of the siRNAs and the control siRNA used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S4. For the TRIM28 rescue experiment, SW620 cells were divided into three groups: (i) control cells transfected with the empty vector; (ii) SUN5-knockdown cells transfected with the empty vector; and (iii) SUN5-knockdown cells transfected with the TRIM28 overexpression plasmid. Glucose consumption, lactate production, and phos-P65 and IκBα ubiquitination were detected in these groups.

Xenograft tumor model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old, 18–20 g) were obtained from the Department of Laboratory Animals of Central South University and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All operations in this study were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Management of Laboratory Animals and the Guide for the Welfare and Use of Animals in Cancer Research. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Laboratory Animals, Central South University (CSU-2024-0188). All the mice were randomly divided into two groups, and then, 1.5 × 10 6 SW620 SUN5-knockdown and control cells were subcutaneously injected into the left underarm of the nude mice ( n = 6 per group). The length and width of the tumors were recorded daily via a Vernier caliper. At 18 days after injection, the mice were sacrificed by an overdose of pentobarbital (250 mg/kg; intraperitoneal injection), and the tumor weight data were recorded. The tumor volumes were calculated via the following formula: volume (mm 3) = (length × width × width)/2. Then, the tumor tissues were further subjected to western blot analysis and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis.

IHC

Mouse tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Immunostaining was performed according to methods described previously [45]. The indicated primary antibodies against SUN5 and Ki-67 were used to detect their corresponding antigens. Images were captured via a light microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Bioinformatics analysis

The amino acid sequences of SUN5, TRIM28 and IκBα were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The crystal structures of the proteins were constructed via SWISS-MODEL ( http://swissmodel.expasy.org ) [46]. GRAMM ( https://gramm.compbio.ku.edu ) [47] was used for protein-protein docking, and the results were visualized via PyMOL 3.1 ( https://pymol.org/). The expression of TRIM28, SLC2A1 (GLUT1) and LDHA in CRC primary tissues as well as normal tissues was investigated via the UALCAN database ( https://ualcan.path.uab.edu) [48]. The correlations among TRIM28, GLUT1 and LDHA in CRC were analyzed via the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database ( http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) [49]. For survival analysis, datasets of CRC patients (GSE39084, GSE39582, and GSE72970) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Subsequently, BEST ( https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/) was used to analyze the GEO datasets of CRC patients [50], and CRC patients were further stratified into low and high SUN5 expression groups on the basis of the expression level for survival analysis via the Kaplan-Meier method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, USA). The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed via unpaired Student’s t test for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multigroup comparisons. Differences in survival were calculated via the log-rank Mantel-Cox test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the experiments were carried out in triplicate, with independent replicates.

Results

SUN5 promotes glycolysis in CRC cells

CRC cells derive approximately 90% of their energy supply through glycolysis even under normoxic conditions [51], contributing to accelerated proliferation, migration, etc. Our previous studies demonstrated that SUN5 promotes cell proliferation and migration in CRC. However, the role of SUN5 in glycolysis remains elusive. To clarify its function in glycolysis, we established stable SUN5-overexpressing (SUN5-OE) and SUN5-knockdown ( SUN5-KD) CRC cell lines. Western blot analysis was performed to verify the efficiency of OE or KD ( Figure 1A,B). We subsequently measured the glucose concentration in the culture medium, and the results demonstrated that the level of glucose uptake was greater in SUN5-OE cells than in control cells ( P < 0.05, Figure 1C), whereas the opposite result was observed in SUN5-KD cells ( P < 0.01, Figure 1C). Furthermore, the results of 2-NBDG uptake assay revealed that, compared with that in the control group, the intensity of the green fluorescence signal was markedly greater in the SUN5-OE cells (LoVo and DLD-1) ( P < 0.01, Figure 1E). Owing to spontaneous green fluorescence, the 2-NBDG uptake assay was not performed in SUN5-KD cells. These data indicate that SUN5 enhances glucose consumption in CRC cells. In addition, we evaluated the effect of SUN5 on lactate production, and the results revealed that, compared with that in the control groups, the concentration of lactate in the culture medium was significantly greater in SUN5-OE cells and lower in SUN5-KD cells ( P < 0.05, Figure 1D). Moreover, the glycolysis biomarkers GLUT1 and LDHA were detected via western blot analysis. The expression of GLUT1 and LDHA was upregulated in the SUN5-OE group and downregulated in the SUN5-KD group ( P < 0.05, Figure 1F). GLUT1 facilitates glucose uptake in CRC cells, and elevated GLUT1 expression is correlated with increased cellular glucose uptake. LDHA converts pyruvate to lactate to support glycolysis, and increased LDHA level accelerates lactate production. Collectively, these data indicate that SUN5 promotes glycolysis in CRC cells.

SUN5 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway in CRC cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which SUN5 is involved in glycolysis, RNA sequencing analysis was performed in SUN5-KD and control SW620 cells. A total of 490 DEGs were obtained, with 182 genes upregulated (log 2FC ≥ 1) and 308 genes downregulated (log 2FC ≤ –1) ( Figure 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed that SUN5 is involved in regulating biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, etc. ( Figure 2B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that many of the proteins encoded by the DEGs participate in multiple signaling pathways, including the NF-κB signaling pathway and central carbon metabolism ( Figure 2 C). In the present study, we focused on the NF-κB signaling pathway. Western blot analysis was performed to analyze the changes in phosphorylated P65 (phos-P65) and P65 in SUN5-OE and SUN5-KD cells. The results revealed that the phos-P65 level was significantly increased in SUN5-OE cells ( P < 0.01, Figure 2D) and decreased in SUN5-KD cells ( P < 0.01, Figure 2D). However, the total level of P65 did not change obviously. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining results demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity of phos-P65 (labeled with red fluorescence) was significantly greater in the nuclei of SUN5-OE LoVo cells than in those of control cells ( Figure 2E), suggesting that the translocation of phos-P65 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus was promoted by SUN5-OE. No significant alteration in the fluorescence intensity of total P65 (labeled with green fluorescence) was observed ( Figure 2E). The nuclear translocation of phos-P65 promotes the transcription of GLUT1 [ 14, 16] and LDHA [ 21, 22]. These results indicated that SUN5 activated the NF-κB signaling pathway to accelerate glycolysis.

SUN5 partially reverses the effect of NF-κB inhibition

To verify whether SUN5 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway to promote glycolysis, we designed two rescue experiments. In experiment 1, the cells were divided into three groups: (i) the control group (LoVo and DLD-1 cells transfected with control lentivirus and then treated with DMSO); (ii) the SUN5-OE group; and (iii) SUN5-OE cells treated with 10 μM BAY11-7082. Cell proliferation, glucose uptake and lactate production were increased by the overexpression of SUN5 in LoVo and DLD-1 cells, but these effects were attenuated by BAY11-7082, a selective inhibitor of the NF-κB signaling pathway ( P < 0.05, Figure 3A–C). Moreover, biomarkers (phos-P65, P65, GLUT1 and LDHA) were detected by western blot analysis, and the results demonstrated that SUN5-OE elevated the protein levels of phos-P65, GLUT1 and LDHA, but this effect could be suppressed by BAY11-7082 ( Figure 3D). No obvious change in the protein level of P65 was observed among the groups. These data indicated that SUN5 promoted cell proliferation and glycolysis, which could be impaired by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. In experiment 2, the cells were classified into three groups: (i) the control group (LoVo and DLD-1 cells treated with DMSO and then transfected with blank vector); (ii) the group of cells treated with BAY11-7082 for 8 h and then transfected with the blank vector; and (iii) the group of cells treated with BAY11-7082 for 8 h and then transfected with the SUN5-OE plasmid. Further detection of biomarkers of NF-κB and glycolysis by western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of phos-P65, GLUT1 and LDHA could be suppressed by BAY11-7082 ( Figure 3H). Then, BAY11-7082-treated cells were transfected with the SUN5-OE plasmid or blank vector. The results showed that after SUN5-OE plasmid transfection, cell proliferation, glycolysis and the levels of biomarkers (phos-P65, GLUT1 and LDHA) were partially restored, while transfection with the blank vector did not reverse the inhibitory effects of BAY11-7082 ( Figure 3E–H). These findings indicated that the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway could be partially rescued via SUN5 overexpression.

These two experiments demonstrated that SUN5 could activate the NF-κB signaling pathway to promote the expression of GLUT1 and LDHA, which contributed to increased glucose consumption and lactate production.

SUN5 promotes IκBα ubiquitination

The NF-κB signaling pathway is vital for the proliferation and glycolysis of CRC cells. However, how SUN5 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway is still unclear. In the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, IκBα binds to the P50/P65 complex, keeping it located in the cytoplasm. After being phosphorylated by the IkB kinase complex (IKK), IκBα is degraded via the ubiquitin-mediated pathway, contributing to the nuclear translocation of P65/P50 [ 17, 52]. Our previous study indicated that SUN5 is involved in ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis [29], and we observed that overexpression of SUN5 increased the nuclear translocation of phos-P65. Thus, we speculated that SUN5 regulates the protein level and ubiquitination of IκBα. RT-qPCR and western blot analysis were employed to evaluate the mRNA and protein levels of IκBα in SUN5-OE/KD cells. The results revealed that there was no significant change in the mRNA level of IκBα ( Figure 4A). However, the protein level of IκBα was downregulated in SUN5-OE cells and upregulated in SUN5-KD cells ( P < 0.05, Figure 4B), indicating that the protein level of IκBα is regulated by SUN5. Subsequently, cycloheximide (CHX), a protein synthesis inhibitor, was utilized to evaluate the influence of SUN5 on the protein stability of IκBα. As shown in Figure 4C,D, the results of the CHX chase assay demonstrated that SUN5 overexpression markedly attenuated the stability of the IκBα protein, whereas the opposite results were observed in SUN5-KD cells ( P < 0.05, Figure 4C,D), suggesting that SUN5 regulates the stability of the IκBα protein. Then, the cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, the total protein from the cells was immunoprecipitated with an anti-IκBα antibody, and western blot analysis was performed to detect IκBα ubiquitination via a polyclonal ubiquitin antibody. The results revealed that the level of IκBα ubiquitination was increased in SUN5-OE cells and decreased in SUN5-KD cells ( Figure 4E). These data indicate that SUN5 can promote the ubiquitination of IκBα. SUN5 interacts with TRIM28 to ubiquitinate IκBα

SUN5 is a nuclear membrane protein that does not exhibit ubiquitin ligase activity. Thus, we propose that SUN5 interacts with ubiquitin ligases to ubiquitinate IκBα. Immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP/MS) was used to detect the potential interacting proteins of SUN5. A total of 549 interacting proteins were identified, including tripartite motif-containing 28 (TRIM28), an E3 ubiquitin ligase.

To validate the interaction between SUN5 and TRIM28, we performed protein-protein docking by using website tool GRAMM and PyMOL 3.1. The results demonstrated that SUN5 and TRIM28 form a stable protein docking model ( Figure 5A), and the hydrogen bonds between the two proteins are shown in Supplemental Figure S1A. The proteins of SUN5-OE cells were subsequently subjected to Co-IP using anti-Flag and anti-TRIM28 antibodies. Both TRIM28 and SUN5 were detected in the immunoprecipitants of the anti-TRIM28 antibody or anti-Flag antibody ( Figure 5B). Further immunofluorescence staining revealed that SUN5, which was labeled with green fluorescence, and TRIM28, which was labeled with red fluorescence, were colocalized in the perinuclear cytoplasm of SUN5-OE LoVo cells ( Figure 5C). These data indicate that SUN5 interacts with TRIM28 to form the SUN5/TRIM28 complex. TRIM28 is involved in the NF-κB signaling pathway and glycolysis

To investigate the importance of TRIM28 in the SUN5/TRIM28/IκBα axis, we conducted TRIM28 silencing experiments and TRIM28 rescue experiments. For the TRIM28 silencing experiment, we synthesized three siRNAs targeting TRIM28 and a control siRNA and transfected them into SUN5-OE LoVo cells. The results of western blot analysis revealed that TRIM28 was downregulated in all three siRNA groups. Moreover, decreased phos-P65 levels and IκBα ubiquitination were detected ( Figure 6C,D) with the knockdown of TRIM28, while the total level of P65 was not altered. Furthermore, we observed that glucose uptake and lactate production were decreased in TRIM28-KD cells ( P < 0.05, Figure 6E). These results indicated that silencing of TRIM28 impaired NF-κB signaling pathway activation and inhibited glycolysis.

In the TRIM28 rescue experiment, western blot analysis revealed that after transfection with the TRIM28-OE plasmid, the phos-P65 level and IκBα ubiquitination were partially restored, but the total level of P65 was not altered ( Figure 6F,G). Moreover, TRIM28 overexpression reversed the markedly reduced glucose consumption and lactate production in SUN5-KD cells ( P​< 0.01, Figure 6H). However, transfection with the blank vector did not reverse the inhibitory effects of SUN5 knockdown. Taken together, the results of the TRIM28 rescue experiment indicated that the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway and glycolysis caused by SUN5 knockdown could be partially rescued via TRIM28 overexpression.

In addition, the relationships among TRIM28, SLC2A1 (GLUT1) and LDHA were analyzed in CRC tissues via the UALCAN database, and the results revealed that TRIM28, GLUT1 and LDHA expression was significantly greater in COAD (colon adenocarcinoma) and READ (rectum adenocarcinoma) tissues than in adjacent normal tissues ( P​< 0.01, Supplementary Figure S2A–C). Results of analyzing the GEPIA database ( http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) revealed that TRIM28 is positively correlated with GLUT1 ( r = 0.36, P < 0.001) and LDHA ( r = 0.37, P < 0.001) ( Supplementary Figure S2D,E), suggesting that TRIM28 is associated with glycolysis. In summary, SUN5 interacts with TRIM28 to ubiquitinate IκBα, contributing to the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and accelerating glycolysis.

SUN5 knockdown inhibits glycolysis and tumorigenesis in vivo

To further explore the function of SUN5 in vivo, we injected SUN5-KD and control SW620 cells into nude mice. After 18 days, we observed the development of tumors at the injection sites in all the mice, and the average volume and weight of the tumors in the SUN5-KD group were significantly lower than those in the control group ( P < 0.05, Figure 7A,B). Subsequently, the tumor sections were subjected to IHC staining. The results demonstrated that the positive signals of SUN5 and Ki-67 in the control group were more obvious than those in the SUN5-KD group, suggesting that cell proliferation was inhibited ( Figure 7C). Moreover, western blot analysis was used to evaluate the levels of phospho-P65 and the glycolytic biomarkers GLUT1 and LDHA in tumor tissues, and the results revealed that the levels of SUN5, phos-P65, GLUT1 and LDHA were significantly decreased in the SUN5-KD group ( P < 0.001, Figure 7D). Taken together, these findings indicate that the suppression of SUN5 results in decreased glycolysis and tumorigenesis in vivo. Upregulated SUN5 is associated with adverse prognosis

To determine whether the expression of SUN5 is correlated with patient survival, we downloaded three datasets (GSE39804, GSE72970, and GSE39582) from the GEO database ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed by using the website tool BEST ( https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/) [50]. The survival curves of all these datasets indicated that high expression of SUN5 corresponds to a shorter overall survival time ( Figure 8A–C). Since SUN5 accelerates cell glycolysis and proliferation, patients with high expression of SUN5 demonstrate rapid progression and a shortened lifespan. Discussion

Tumor cells obtain most of their energy through glycolysis to support rapid growth and proliferation even when the level of oxygen is normal. This phenomenon is termed aerobic glycolysis or the Warburg effect [ 3, 53], which is associated with the malignant progression of CRC [4]. Here, we first demonstrated that SUN5, a nuclear membrane protein, promotes glycolysis in CRC cells. Mechanistically, SUN5 interacts with TRIM28 to ubiquitinate IκBα, leading to the nuclear translocation of phos-P65 and the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, along with elevated expression of glycolytic genes such as GLUT1 and LDHA, to increase glycolysis ( Figure 8D). Further xenograft transplantation experiments revealed that knockdown of SUN5 suppressed glycolysis and tumorigenesis in vivo. These data indicate that SUN5 may be a candidate target for CRC diagnosis and therapy.

SUN5 is the fifth member of the SUN domain protein family and is involved in tumor progression. Accumulating evidence has indicated that the functions of SUN domain protein family members vary across different tumors [ 23– 29]. SUN1 and SUN2 are expressed in almost all tissues and are downregulated in multiple cancers, such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and oral cancer. SUN2 suppresses the Warburg effect by inhibiting the expression of GLUT1 and LDHA, which are regulated by SIRT5, in lung cancer and oral cancer [ 24 , 25, 28]. Unlike SUN1 and SUN2, SUN4 and SUN5 are enriched in the testes and upregulated in cancers [ 27, 29]. SUN4 enhances cell proliferation, migration and SREBP1-mediated lipid metabolism in different cancers [ 30– 32]. In addition, Zhou et al. [34] reported that SUN4 enhances mitochondrial respiration and aerobic glycolysis in CRC cells by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway.

SUN5 was first identified by our group. In a previous study, we established Sun5-knockout mice and reported that Sun5 deficiency disrupted sperm head‒tail connections [ 35, 36]. Recently, we reported that SUN5 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and promotes cell proliferation and migration by activating the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway [29]. However, whether SUN5 is involved in glycolysis is elusive. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that SUN4 and SUN5 are highly homologous and may share a common gene ancestor [23]. Thus, we speculated that SUN5 regulates glycolysis. In this study, we demonstrated that SUN5 overexpression activated the NF-κB signaling pathway in CRC cells to promote glycolysis, whereas SUN5 knockdown yielded the opposite results. We subsequently conducted two rescue experiments. The results of experiment 1 demonstrated that the overexpression of SUN5 enhanced the proliferation and glycolysis of CRC cells, which could be suppressed by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. In experiment 2, suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway decreased cell proliferation and glycolysis in LoVo and DLD-1 cells, and these effects were reversed by transfection with the SUN5 plasmid. These data indicate that the NF-κB signaling pathway plays a crucial role in promoting SUN5-mediated glycolysis.

The NF-κB signaling pathway is vital for cell proliferation, metastasis, metabolism and drug resistance in CRC and other cancers. NF-κB P65/P50 dimers are retained in the cytoplasm through interactions with IκBα proteins. Upon stimulation, IκBα is phosphorylated, leading to its ubiquitin-mediated degradation [ 17, 52]. Therefore, the transcriptionally active P65/P50 heterodimers translocate into the nucleus and regulate the expression of downstream genes. In this study, we observed that upregulated SUN5 promoted the ubiquitination of IκBα, and we investigated the phosphorylation of NF-κB P65 at serine 536 via western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining. The results demonstrated that SUN5 increased the protein level and nuclear translocation of phosphorylated P65 (Ser536). P65 binds to the promoter region of GLUT1 to activate its transcription in different cancers, such as glioblastoma [15], lung cancer [54] and CRC [55]. Zhu et al. [22] demonstrated that P65 could also regulate the expression of HIF-1α, contributing to the elevation of LDHA expression in acute myeloid leukemia. Phosphorylation of the NF-κB P65 subunit at Ser536 increases its transcriptional potential [56]; thus, the expression of GLUT1 and LDHA is elevated, thereby accelerating glycolysis.

The ubiquitination of IκBα requires a ubiquitin ligase. Ubiquitin ligases, such as ABLIM1 [57], TRIM27 [40] and HSPB1 [58], are involved in the NF-κB signaling pathway via interactions with IκBα for proteasome-mediated degradation. Ji et al . [39] demonstrated that TRIM22 bound to IκBα to promote its ubiquitination and degradation, leading to accelerated cell proliferation in glioblastoma. In this study, SUN5 was shown to interact with the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM28, and we observed for the first time that TRIM28 interacted with IκBα in CRC cells. Moreover, upregulated SUN5 increased the expression of TRIM28 to promote IκBα ubiquitination, leading to the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and increased glycolysis, whereas downregulated SUN5 yielded the opposite results. These results indicate that the SUN5/TRIM28/IκBα axis is essential for regulating glycolysis in CRC. However, how SUN5 regulates the transcription of TRIM28 requires further exploration in future studies.

Moreover, the results of further xenograft transplantation experiments revealed that the knockdown of SUN5 suppressed tumor growth and the expression of the glycolytic biomarkers GLUT1 and LDHA, indicating that SUN5 KD inhibited glycolysis and tumorigenesis in vivo. Data from xenograft transplantation studies indicate that SUN5 can be an effective target for CRC therapy. Moreover, we downloaded data from the GEO database to perform survival analysis, and the results revealed that CRC patients with high SUN5 expression had shorter overall survival times. This might be attributed to the role of SUN5 in promoting the early stage of glycolysis. Therefore, cancer cells utilize glucose more effectively to support rapid proliferation and migration, resulting in the malignant progression of tumors and a shortened survival time for patients. Our data suggest that SUN5 may be a potential biomarker for predicting the prognosis of CRC patients and that inhibiting SUN5 expression is a promising strategy for CRC treatment in the future.

In summary, we demonstrated that SUN5, a nuclear membrane protein, interacts with TRIM28 to ubiquitinylate IκBα and promotes the nuclear translocation of phospho-P65, which upregulates the expression of GLUT1 and LDHA to increase glycolysis. These findings provide the basis for target selection for further treatment of CRC.
