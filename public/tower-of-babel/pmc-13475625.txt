Knowledge–Behavior and Adherence Gap in Protective Eyewear Use Among Outdoor Workers with High Ocular Surface Disease Burden in a Desert Environment
Authors: Saeed Aljohani, Mohammed Suliman Alomari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOutdoor workers’ ocular health in harsh environments is often overlooked, despite their heightened risk of eye disease. This study aimed to examine knowledge of sun-related ocular hazards and adherence to protective eyewear use among outdoor workers in a desert environment, and to contextualize these behaviors against objective ocular surface findings.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 133 male outdoor workers engaged in cultivation, cleaning, maintenance, and construction at Qassim University, Saudi Arabia, a region characterized by intense solar ultraviolet radiation, high ambient temperatures, low relative humidity, and frequent dust storms. Participants underwent comprehensive ocular surface evaluation, including slit-lamp biomicroscopy and objective tear film assessment using the Keratograph 5M. Knowledge of UV-related ocular hazards was assessed using items adapted from a validated UV knowledge instrument, and self-reported protective eyewear adherence during outdoor work was recorded.ResultsProtective eyewear adherence was low: 59.4% of workers reported never using eye protection during outdoor work, while only 8.3% reported consistent use. Lower knowledge of sun-related ocular health risks was observed in 63.2% of participants. Objective clinical signs consistent with dry eye disease were observed in 54.1%, and pinguecula was observed in 45.9%. Knowledge level and protective eyewear use were not significantly associated with DED or pinguecula.ConclusionThese findings show a clear gap between what workers know about eye protection and how often they actually use it. The combination of low protective eyewear use and a high observed burden of ocular surface disease suggests that education alone may not be enough. Although workplace factors were not directly measured, workplace-level support, easier access to suitable protective eyewear, and practical eye-health education tailored to workers’ needs represent plausible, testable directions for improving protection in desert outdoor work settings.

Introduction

The ocular surface is essential for maintaining clear vision and overall eye health.1 It is made up of delicate but highly specialized structures, including the conjunctiva, a transparent mucous membrane that lines the eyelids and covers the sclera, and the cornea, the transparent front of the eye responsible for most of its refractive power.2 These tissues are protected by the tear film, a thin, multilayered fluid composed of lipid, aqueous, and mucin layers, though contemporary models characterize it more simply as lipid and muco-aqueous layers. This film not only keeps the eye lubricated but also nourishes it and guards against infection. When this system is disrupted, the result can be discomfort, inflammation, and reduced visual performance.1

Environmental conditions exert a strong influence on ocular surface health. High temperatures, low humidity, wind, dust, and intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation can destabilize the tear film, increasing evaporation and triggering oxidative stress and inflammation.3,4 Recent evidence has further characterized dry eye disease as a multifactorial condition in which environmental and occupational exposures play a key role, even among relatively young and otherwise healthy individuals.5 Contemporary large-scale and mechanistic studies increasingly suggest that climatic and protective behaviors remain under-recognized contributors to dry eye disease burden, underscoring the need for context-specific epidemiological data in exposed worker populations.4,6

In Saudi Arabia, and particularly in arid regions such as Qassim, these challenges are amplified. The climate in the Qassim region is marked by scorching summers, cold winters, year-round low humidity, and frequent dust storms.7 Outdoor workers, such as those maintaining university grounds, face daily exposure to these elements. Research from similar climates indicates that such exposure is associated with an increased prevalence of dry eye disease (DED), pterygium, pinguecula, and certain types of cataract.8–10

The burden of these conditions is substantial. Dry eye disease has been reported to affect approximately one-third to one-half of adults in Saudi-based studies, depending on diagnostic criteria.6,11 Pterygium and pinguecula are strongly linked to cumulative UV exposure, wind, and dust hazards that are largely unavoidable during prolonged outdoor work.9,12 Cataract formation, particularly cortical cataract, has also been associated with long-term solar exposure, with the World Health Organization estimating that approximately 10% of global cataract-related blindness is attributable to UV radiation.10,13

A recent study by Alghamdi et al conducted in central Saudi Arabia demonstrated adverse effects of outdoor environmental exposure, including increased tear film instability (shorter non-invasive keratograph break-up time), higher lipid layer thickness, and greater conjunctival redness among outdoor workers compared with indoor counterparts.14 These findings, from a geographically and climatically similar region, provide a strong rationale for further examining ocular surface health among outdoor workers in the Qassim region, particularly in relation to knowledge of eye health risks and preventive practices.

While ocular surface disease prevalence has been reported in arid regions and Middle Eastern populations, most previous studies have focused either on clinical findings or environmental exposure in isolation. The present study therefore describes the coexistence of high ocular surface burden, limited knowledge of sun-related ocular hazards, and low adherence to protective eyewear use among outdoor workers in a desert environment. Rather than attempting to isolate the causal contribution of UV exposure from other environmental stressors, the study provides clinical and behavioral context to inform workplace eye-health interventions.

In the context of climate change, arid regions are expected to experience increasing temperatures, prolonged heat exposure, and intensified environmental stress.15 For outdoor workers, this may translate into greater cumulative exposure to solar radiation and dry conditions over time. Protective eyewear is a simple and low-cost preventive measure, yet its uptake may be limited by knowledge, comfort, workplace culture, perceived risk, and employer enforcement. Understanding this knowledge–behavior gap is essential for designing acceptable and sustainable eye-health interventions. Viewing protective eyewear use as a preventive-adherence behavior rather than a simple knowledge outcome is consistent with behavioral frameworks such as the Theoretical Domains Framework, and with prior work documenting comparable knowledge–behavior gaps in UV-related ocular protection among outdoor and agricultural populations.16,17

Materials and Methods

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Optometry Clinics of Qassim University to assess ocular surface findings, knowledge of ocular health risks, and protective eyewear adherence among outdoor workers. Data collection took place over a period of 10 months. Purposive sampling was used to recruit workers with sustained outdoor environmental exposure, an approach commonly employed in occupational health research where random sampling may be impractical.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Committee of Research Ethics, Deanship of Graduate Studies and Scientific Research, Qassim University (Approval No. 25–41-04). This research was conducted in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association, including participant consent, confidentiality, and voluntary participation. All participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study, and no identifiable personal information was disclosed.

Participants were male outdoor workers employed at Qassim University, including individuals responsible for grounds maintenance and other outdoor services. Eligible participants were aged 18–55 years and had been engaged in outdoor work for at least six months prior to recruitment. Workers were excluded if they were employed in indoor roles, or had a history of ocular surgery, known ocular pathology, or systemic diseases and medications known to affect ocular surface health.

All participants underwent a comprehensive eye examination conducted by licensed optometrists. The assessment included a detailed medical and occupational history, documenting any ocular or systemic diseases, current medications, duration of outdoor work, and nature of occupational exposure. Years of occupational exposure in the Saudi desert climate were recorded for each participant (range 1–25 years) and considered an indicator of cumulative environmental exposure. Visual acuity was measured at both near and distance, with and without correction, followed by objective and subjective refraction to determine refractive status. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy was used to examine the anterior segment for signs of ocular surface disease, including conjunctival and corneal changes. Pinguecula was defined as a yellowish, elevated conjunctival lesion adjacent to the limbus that did not invade the cornea. Pterygium was defined as a fibrovascular conjunctival growth extending onto the corneal surface. Conjunctivitis was identified by conjunctival hyperemia with associated inflammatory signs. Trichiasis was defined as misdirected eyelashes contacting the ocular surface. Cortical cataract was identified by characteristic peripheral cortical opacities observed on slit-lamp examination. In addition, objective tear film and ocular surface parameters were assessed using the Keratograph 5M (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), a non-contact ocular surface imaging system. Measurements included non-invasive keratograph break-up time (NIKBUT), tear meniscus height (TMH), conjunctival redness, and meibomian gland morphology. Tear film instability was defined as NIKBUT <10 seconds, with values <5 seconds indicating marked instability. For descriptive reporting of NIKBUT severity distribution, values were additionally classified using the four-tier scale generated by the Keratograph 5M’s JENVIS PRO Dry Eye Screening report (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany, developed with JENVIS Research, Ernst-Abbe University of Applied Sciences, Jena, Germany): very short (<7 seconds), short (7–11 seconds), normal (11–15 seconds), and above average (≥15 seconds). This device-generated classification is consistent with the broader clinical convention that non-invasive tear film stability below 10 seconds is indicative of dry eye disease. Tear meniscus height <0.2 mm was considered reduced tear volume. Conjunctival redness was graded using the device’s automated scale (0–4), with higher scores indicating greater hyperemia. Participants were classified according to the overall dry eye disease severity stage (normal, mild, moderate, severe) generated by the JENVIS PRO Dry Eye Screening report (Keratograph 5M, OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany, developed with JENVIS Research, Ernst-Abbe University of Applied Sciences, Jena, Germany), which integrates NIKBUT, tear meniscus height, and conjunctival redness into a composite severity classification, interpreted alongside slit-lamp findings.

To describe workers’ knowledge of UV-related ocular health risks, four items were adapted from the knowledge subscale of a previously published and psychometrically validated UV ocular hazard questionnaire.16 The original instrument demonstrated good test-retest reliability in a population from Jordan, a high-UV, arid-climate country with environmental conditions broadly comparable to those of Qassim. Formal re-validation of the adapted knowledge items in this population was not feasible, as no validated UV ocular knowledge instrument currently exists in Bengali, Hindi, or Urdu, the primary languages of the participants. The questionnaire was therefore administered in Arabic, the shared occupational language of the worksite, with interpreter assistance where needed. The four items addressed: (1) whether prolonged sunlight exposure can affect eye health; (2) whether sunlight exposure can cause eye dryness or irritation; (3) whether prolonged sunlight exposure increases the risk of eye diseases such as cataract or pterygium; and (4) whether ultraviolet radiation from sunlight harms the eyes, and were reviewed by two clinicians with expertise in ocular surface disease to confirm relevance and appropriateness. Given the descriptive and contextualizing role of this component, this approach represents a methodologically proportionate choice for an exploratory cross-sectional screen. Responses were recorded as “yes”, “no”, or “do not know”. Each item was scored as correct (1) or incorrect/ “do not know” (0), yielding a total score range of 0–4, categorized as poor (0–1), neutral (2), good (3), or excellent (4). For analysis, knowledge was dichotomized as higher knowledge (excellent/good) versus lower knowledge (neutral/poor). Protective eyewear adherence was assessed with a single item (“How often do you wear protective eyewear, eg, sunglasses, while working outdoors?”), rated as always, sometimes, rarely, or never, without a defined recall period. Protective eyewear use was treated as a behavioral adherence variable; consistent adherence was defined as reporting “always” using eye protection during outdoor work, while all other responses were classified as non-consistent use. This conservative definition was selected because intermittent use may not provide reliable protection in a setting characterized by prolonged daily environmental exposure. Pinguecula was coded as present if detected in either eye.

Statistical Analysis

All collected data were entered into and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Independent samples t-test (Welch correction where appropriate) was used to compare mean duration between groups; results were consistent with non-parametric testing. For inferential analysis, categorical variables with multiple levels were collapsed into clinically meaningful groups to ensure adequate cell counts and meet statistical assumptions. Associations between ocular surface outcomes (dry eye disease and pinguecula) and knowledge level, protective eyewear use, and occupational category were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test when assumptions were met. Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were less than five. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 133 outdoor workers were included in the study, representing 41.3% of the total outdoor workforce (n = 322) at Qassim University. The mean age was 30.2 ± 8.3 years (range: 18–54 years), with the majority in their twenties and thirties. Occupational distribution showed that nearly half of the participants were employed in cultivation (65; 48.9%), followed by cleaning staff (33; 24.8%), maintenance workers (25; 18.8%), and construction workers (10; 7.5%). In terms of nationality, the largest group of workers originated from Bangladesh (87; 65.4%), while India and Pakistan each accounted for 23 participants (17.3% each). Working hours were uniform across the sample, with most participants reporting 8 hours per day (128; 96.2%). The mean self-reported duration of outdoor occupational exposure was 6.43 ± 5.18 years. Duration did not differ significantly between workers with and without objective clinical signs consistent with dry eye disease (6.67 ± 5.10 vs 6.15 ± 5.30 years, p = 0.57).

Visual acuity assessment showed that most participants had good baseline vision, with mean unaided visual acuity (VA) of 0.05 ± 0.12 LogMAR in the right eye and 0.05 ± 0.13 LogMAR in the left eye, improving to 0.00 ± 0.00 LogMAR and 0.01 ± 0.06 LogMAR respectively after correction. Refractive error classification using spherical equivalent values indicated that most workers were emmetropic, with 106 participants (79.7%) showing no significant refractive error. Myopia was identified in 13 participants (9.8%; 8 with myopia alone and 5 with myopic astigmatism), while 7 (5.3%) were hyperopic (4 with hyperopia alone and 3 with hyperopic astigmatism). Isolated astigmatism was found in 5 participants (3.8%), and an additional 2 workers (1.5%) met the criteria for amblyopia. Adherence to protective eyewear was uncommon: 79 participants (59.4%) reported never using eye protection during outdoor work, whereas only 11 participants (8.3%) reported consistent (always) use (Figure 1). With respect to self-reported ocular symptoms, 35 participants (26.3%) indicated experiencing eye-related problems during outdoor work, while 98 participants (73.7%) reported no such symptoms. In terms of perceived occupational impact, 30 workers (22.6%) reported that the Qassim climate affected their work performance, whereas 103 (77.4%) reported no perceived effect. Additionally, 29 participants (21.8%) believed their eye problems were related to outdoor work exposure, while 104 (78.2%) did not report this perception.

Under slit-lamp examination, ocular abnormalities were frequently observed among outdoor workers at Qassim University. The most prevalent anterior segment abnormality was pinguecula, detected in 61 participants in the right eye (45.9%) and 60 in the left eye (45.1%). Conjunctivitis was also relatively frequent, affecting 23 participants in the right eye (17.3%) and 18 in the left eye (13.5%). Trichiasis was identified in 12 participants in the right eye (9.0%) and 3 in the left eye (2.3%). Cortical cataract was noted in 7 participants in the right eye (5.3%) and 5 in the left eye (3.8%). Meanwhile, pterygium was the least common finding, observed in 4 participants in the right eye (3.0%) and 3 in the left eye (2.3%) (Figure 2). Keratograph assessments revealed notable abnormalities in tear film stability and ocular surface health among participants. Non-invasive Keratograph Break-Up Time (NIKBUT) was markedly reduced in many cases, with 55 workers (41.4%) showing very short values (<7 seconds), and another 24 (18.0%) classified as short (7–11 seconds). Only 30 participants (22.6%) had above-average NIKBUT (>15 seconds), while 24 (18.0%) fell within the normal range (11–15 seconds). Conjunctival redness was predominantly mild, observed in 88 workers (66.2%), whereas 16 (12.0%) showed moderate redness, and only a single case (0.8%) was classified as severe; 28 participants (21.1%) had normal conjunctival appearance. Tear meniscus (TM) measurements indicated that nearly half of the sample (59; 44.4%) had normal tear volume, though reductions were common: 50 participants (37.6%) showed slightly reduced TM and 18 (13.5%) had low TM, while only 6 workers (4.5%) presented with very high tear volume (Figure 3). Discussion

The principal finding of this study is the coexistence of low adherence to protective eyewear use and a high burden of objective ocular surface abnormalities among outdoor workers in a desert environment. Although clinical signs consistent with dry eye disease (54.1%) and pinguecula (45.9%) were common, only a small proportion of workers reported consistent use of protective eyewear. This mismatch between knowledge and behavior highlights an important occupational knowledge–behavior gap in eye protection. While knowledge–behavior gaps have been described in other occupational settings, the distinctive contribution of this study is the pairing of objective, device-based ocular surface staging with behavioral adherence data in a desert outdoor workforce, a combination that has not been widely reported in this climatic and occupational context.

Although no statistically significant associations were observed between knowledge level, protective eyewear adherence, and ocular surface outcomes, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The absence of association may reflect limited statistical power, simplified categorization of behavioral variables, or relatively uniform environmental exposure across participants. Importantly, these results should not be interpreted as evidence that protective eyewear is ineffective. Instead, they highlight that consistent adherence to protective behaviors was low (8.3%) in this cohort, suggesting that factors beyond knowledge alone may influence whether workers routinely use eye protection. The odds ratios for these associations (Table 2) ranged from approximately 0.4 to 1.4, with 95% confidence intervals that were wide and included both protective and non-protective directions of effect; this pattern is consistent with an imprecisely estimated association rather than with confirmed independence between adherence and ocular surface outcomes.

Several factors may contribute to low adherence to protective eyewear use in this setting. These may include discomfort associated with wearing eyewear in hot and dusty conditions, reduced visual clarity due to fogging or environmental particles, limited availability of suitable or well-fitted protective eyewear, lack of workplace enforcement, and low perceived susceptibility to ocular disease.18,19 Qualitative and review-level evidence from other occupational cohorts similarly identifies discomfort, fit, and workplace enforcement as recurring barriers to protective eyewear acceptance, reinforcing the plausibility of these mechanisms in the present cohort.20,21 In addition, language and cultural differences among workers may influence how occupational health information is understood and applied. Although these factors were not directly assessed in the present study, they represent important targets for future research and for the design of practical, acceptable interventions.

The ocular surface findings in this cohort provide important clinical context for interpreting the observed patterns of protective behavior and adherence. The prevalence of dry eye disease and pinguecula in this study is broadly consistent with findings from other arid and Middle Eastern settings,22 particularly among populations with sustained outdoor exposure. For example, a recent study in central Saudi Arabia using similar keratograph-based assessment demonstrated reduced tear film stability and increased ocular surface abnormalities among outdoor workers compared with indoor counterparts.14 An earlier Saudi pilot by Fagehi et al, although limited to 24 employees, similarly reported significantly shorter fluorescein TBUT in outdoor compared with indoor workers.23 Among studies that report DED prevalence in occupational cohorts from the broader region, Alkhaldi et al in 2023 estimated prevalence at 49.5% across 4066 adults surveyed in five Saudi regions.11 Together, these studies provide contextual support for the elevated ocular surface burden observed in the present cohort and highlight the importance of preventive workplace eye-health strategies in outdoor occupational settings. Despite these environmental risks and clinical findings, consistent adherence to protective eyewear use remained low in the present study.

The 45.9% prevalence of pinguecula observed in this cohort is broadly consistent with rates reported in other sun-exposed populations across the region. In western Turkey, Dundar and Kocasaraç reported a prevalence of 52.5%, with outdoor workers at substantially higher risk than indoor workers.24 Similarly, Rezvan et al (2012) reported an overall prevalence of 61.0% in Iran, rising to 76.9% among outdoor workers.12 More recently, the Tehran Geriatric Eye Study documented a prevalence of 55.6% in older adults exposed to chronic environmental risk factors.25 Together, these findings support the elevated ocular surface burden observed in outdoor occupational settings with sustained sun and environmental exposure. Despite these risks, consistent adherence to protective eyewear use remained low in the present cohort.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits interpretation of temporal relationships between knowledge, behavior, and ocular outcomes. The assessment of knowledge and protective practices relied on a brief, exploratory questionnaire that was not formally validated; although the items addressed fundamental aspects of UV-related ocular health, the lack of psychometric validation and the simplified categorization of responses may have limited sensitivity in detecting nuanced differences in knowledge. The small number of consistent (“always”) protective eyewear users (n=11, 8.3%) limited statistical power to detect associations between adherence and ocular surface outcomes; the corresponding confidence intervals were wide, and a true association cannot be excluded on the basis of these data alone. Formal education level and health literacy were not assessed and may act as unmeasured confounders in the observed knowledge–behavior relationship. Similarly, institutional factors such as employer-level protective eyewear policy, supply and enforcement, and workplace safety culture were not captured and may contribute to the low adherence observed. Multivariable adjustment for potential confounders (age, duration of occupational exposure, occupational category) was not performed given the limited size of key subgroups; associations reported here should therefore be interpreted as unadjusted. Finally, the use of purposive sampling within a single institution and the inclusion of male workers only limits the generalizability of findings beyond similar occupational settings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful insight into ocular surface health and protective behavior among outdoor workers exposed to desert environmental conditions. The combination of a substantial ocular surface disease burden and low adherence to protective eyewear use highlights the need for practical workplace prevention strategies, regular ocular surface screening, and targeted occupational eye-health education. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies using validated behavioral instruments are needed to better understand barriers to adherence and to identify effective approaches for improving consistent protective eyewear use in high-risk outdoor settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a high burden of ocular surface abnormalities, particularly dry eye disease and pinguecula, among outdoor workers in a desert environment in Saudi Arabia, alongside low adherence to protective eyewear use. The results highlight an important gap between occupational risk and consistent protective behavior, suggesting that knowledge alone may not be sufficient to support regular eye protection in high-exposure settings. The specific drivers of this gap (comfort, availability, enforcement) were not directly measured in this study. Given the single-site, male-only design, these findings and the practical recommendations outlined above are most directly applicable to comparable outdoor occupational settings.
