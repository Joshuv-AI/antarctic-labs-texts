Risk factors for cervical kyphotic deformity following laminoplasty in degenerative cervical myelopathy: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Negin Safari Dehnavi, Sadegh Bagherzadeh, Mohammad Sina Mirjani, Faramarz Roohollahi, Mahmoud Shehni, Abolfazl Paeenmahali, Mark Greenberg, Jay Kumar, Patrick Kim, Puya Alikhani, Mohsen Rostami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostoperative cervical kyphotic deformity (KD) is a recognized complication after laminoplasty for degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM). This review identified predictors of KD to improve preoperative risk stratification, pooling studies of DCM patients using a random-effects model, with sensitivity and publication bias analyses. Thirty-nine studies (4423 patients) were included, with KD in 20.9% of cases. Age and sex were not significantly associated with KD. Higher body mass index and smoking history showed modest but significant associations. 5-level laminoplasty constructs were associated with increased KD risk, whereas operative time, blood loss, and most construct-related variables were not predictive. Radiographic parameters reflecting sagittal balance and alignment demand demonstrated the strongest associations with KD. Increased T1 slope, greater T1S–cervical lordosis (TS–CL) mismatch, and larger sagittal vertical axis (SVA) measurements (Center of Gravity of Head–C7 SVA and C2–C7 SVA) were significantly associated with KD. Dynamic motion analysis revealed that reduced extension reserve and increased flexion excursion, rather than total range of motion (ROM), characterized higher-risk patients. Magnetic resonance imaging-related factors and thoracolumbar spinopelvic parameters were not significantly associated with KD. Threshold analysis identified CGH–C7 SVA >30.6 mm and extension ROM <11.4° as the most discriminative predictors. Postoperative KD after laminoplasty is primarily determined by preoperative sagittal alignment demand and dynamic motion reserve rather than by general demographics or most comorbidities. Incorporating radiographic and functional parameters – particularly TS–CL mismatch, anterior head translation, and extension reserve – into preoperative evaluation may enhance risk stratification in patients undergoing laminoplasty for DCM.

INTRODUCTION

Laminoplasty is widely used for posterior decompression of the cervical spine, particularly in patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL). The technique allows adequate canal expansion while preserving segmental motion, making it an attractive alternative to laminectomy and fusion. However, a well-recognized concern following laminoplasty is the postoperative loss of cervical lordosis (CL) and progression to kyphotic deformity (KD).[1] Beyond being a radiographic finding, KD alters cervical biomechanics, diminishes the expected posterior drift of the spinal cord, and may adversely affect neurological recovery despite technically adequate decompression.[23] These considerations underscore the importance of accurate preoperative risk assessment.

Several studies have identified preoperative sagittal imbalance as a key factor associated with postoperative kyphotic change, even in patients without baseline kyphosis.[45] Dynamic alignment patterns, including flexion-dominant motion behavior, have also been linked to increased KD risk after laminoplasty.[26] In addition, threshold-based approaches using preoperative CL have been proposed to stratify the likelihood of postoperative kyphotic progression.[7] Other radiographic predictors include parameters reflecting cervicothoracic alignment demand, such as T1 slope (T1S), the T1S–CL mismatch, and sagittal vertical axis (SVA) measurements.[489] Moreover, factors related to posterior soft tissues and foraminal stenosis have been suggested as potential contributors in selected cohorts.[1011]

Despite these observations, the current literature remains fragmented. Variability in KD definitions, follow-up durations, underlying pathology, and the specific sagittal metrics analyzed has limited direct comparison across studies and has hindered the development of unified predictive models. A recent systematic review emphasized the need to integrate existing predictors and improve methodological compatibility across studies.[1]

Accordingly, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aims to provide an updated and comprehensive synthesis of risk factors for postoperative cervical KD following laminoplasty in patients with degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM). We specifically focus on clinically measurable and actionable preoperative predictors, with the goal of refining surgical selection, improving patient counseling, and ultimately optimizing postoperative alignment and functional outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.[12] The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database.

Search strategy, databases, eligibility criteria, and selection process

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception to 4 November 2025. Detailed search strategies and yields for each database are provided in the Supplement [Supplementary Table 1]. All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan, where duplicate entries were removed before screening. Title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review, were conducted independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a senior reviewer.

Eligible studies included adult patients with DCM undergoing cervical laminoplasty and reporting extractable numerical data evaluating associations between candidate predictors and postoperative cervical KD. Nonoriginal publications, case reports, conference abstracts, animal or laboratory studies, inaccessible full texts, and studies lacking sufficient quantitative data were excluded from the study. Studies primarily assessing postoperative sagittal imbalance rather than KD were retained for qualitative synthesis but excluded from quantitative meta-analysis.

Handling of overlapping populations

To avoid double-counting, potential overlapping cohorts were evaluated by comparing institutions, author lists, recruitment periods, and ethics approval identifiers when available. When duplicate cohorts were clearly identified, only one report was included. In cases of suspected overlap with differing sample sizes or variable reporting, the study with the larger cohort and broader variable coverage was prioritized. Predictor-specific data were extracted from smaller overlapping reports only when the relevant variable was unavailable in the primary dataset, ensuring that overlapping cohorts were never pooled simultaneously in the same analysis.

Data extraction and outcomes

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using a standardized data sheet, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Extracted variables were categorized into: study identification (author, year, country, design), population characteristics (pathology, eligibility criteria including baseline kyphosis exclusion, sample size, demographics, and follow-up), surgical factors (technique, operated levels, facet violation, implant type, postoperative immobilization), outcome characteristics (KD definition, timing of diagnosis, imaging modality and patient positioning), and candidate predictors, including demographic and clinical factors, operative variables, and radiographic parameters encompassing both static alignment and dynamic functional metrics such as flexion and extension range of motion (ROM).

Risk of bias assessment and sensitivity analysis

Methodological quality was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS),[13] with disagreements adjudicated by a third reviewer. Domain-specific and total scores were tabulated in the Supplement [Supplementary Table 2] and visually summarized in a traffic-light plot. Prespecified NOS categories were defined as low risk (scores 7–9), moderate risk (scores 5–6), and high risk (scores ≤4).

To evaluate robustness of pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses were conducted using influence diagnostics. A two-layer synthesis approach was applied: initial pooling of all eligible studies for each predictor, followed by re-pooling after removal of statistical outliers. This framework allowed identification of associations that were consistent across heterogeneous datasets and those sensitive to influential studies.

Statistical methods

Quantitative synthesis was performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 4.0, while GraphPad Prism, version 8, was used for data visualization and figure generation. Meta-analysis was conducted only for predictors reported in at least two studies. Continuous variables were summarized using standardized mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and dichotomous variables were analyzed using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. When composite parameters such as TS–CL mismatch were not directly reported, they were calculated from available primary variables using established statistical approaches.

Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity among included cohorts, pooled estimates were generated using a random-effects model. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic,[14] and higher I2 values prompted cautious interpretation and further evaluation through the predefined sensitivity framework.

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test[15] when at least three studies were available for a given predictor. Recognizing the limited statistical power of this test when fewer than ten studies contribute to an analysis, findings were interpreted cautiously.[1617] When asymmetry was suggested, trim-and-fill correction was applied to evaluate the potential impact of small-study effects on pooled estimates, and adjusted results were compared with primary analyses.

RESULTS

Study selection and characteristics

The literature search identified 4495 records (PubMed: 778; Embase: 1157; Scopus: 1333; Web of Science: 1227). After removal of 2423 duplicates, 2072 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 1,895 were excluded from the study. Full texts were sought for 177 articles; seven were not retrievable, leaving 170 studies assessed for eligibility. A total of 131 studies were excluded, primarily due to absence of a comparison group (n = 86), inappropriate outcome definition (n = 18), incorrect study population (n = 9), lack of extractable numerical data (n = 9), conference abstract status (n = 8), or overlapping patient populations (n = 1). Ultimately, 39 studies met inclusion criteria for qualitative synthesis, and 32 of these were eligible for meta-analysis [Figure 1]. The included studies were published between 2007 and 2025 and comprised a total of 4,423 patients, among whom postoperative KD developed in 926 (20.9%). Demographic and baseline characteristics

Sex distribution was reported in 37 cohorts including 4,192 patients, demonstrating a male predominance (65.3% male vs. 34.7% female). Age was reported in 37 studies (n = 4,107), with a pooled mean of 63.7 ± 10.8 years. Body mass index (BMI) was available in 17 studies (n = 1,894), with a mean value of 25.6 ± 3.8 kg/m2.

Regarding underlying pathology, 15 studies (n = 1,883) included patients with CSM only, six studies (n = 507) focused exclusively on OPLL, and 18 studies (n = 2,033) reported mixed populations. Overall, the baseline characteristics reflected a typical DCM cohort undergoing posterior decompression via laminoplasty.

Operative characteristics

Open-door laminoplasty was the most commonly used technique (27 studies), followed by double-door (French-door) laminoplasty (8 studies), whereas four studies reported mixed techniques. Operated levels were specified in 30 studies, most frequently involving C3–C7 (46.7%) or C2–C7 (30.0%). Shorter constructs (e.g., C3–C6, C4–C6, or C2–C6) were less commonly reported and were mainly observed in U.S. cohorts.

Implant or fixation methods were described in 24 studies and included titanium miniplates, plate fixation systems, hydroxyapatite spacers, allograft bone spacers, buttress plates, and suture anchors. Postoperative immobilization protocols were heterogeneous, ranging from no collar use to rigid collars worn for up to six weeks. Facet joint violation was explicitly reported in only five cohorts (12.8%) and happened in four of them.

Definitions and radiographic assessment of kyphotic deformity

Definitions of postoperative KD varied considerably across studies. The most common definition (53.8%) was based on a change in C2–C7 Cobb angle (ΔCA) relative to baseline, typically using thresholds of ΔCA <−10° or <−5°. Less stringent criteria included any loss of lordosis (ΔCA <0°). Thirteen studies (33.3%) defined KD according to an absolute postoperative Cobb angle, most often <0°, with a few using <−5°. A minority of studies defined KD using SVA criteria (ΔSVA ≥0 or ≥10 mm); these cohorts were excluded from meta-analysis due to outcome heterogeneity. All studies assessed alignment using lateral cervical radiographs. Upright or standing positioning was specified in 56.4% of cohorts, while neutral positioning was reported in 69.2%. Dynamic flexion–extension radiographs were obtained in 64.1% of studies. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography were variably used as adjunctive modalities rather than primary tools for alignment measurement.

KD was most frequently diagnosed at 12 months postoperatively (71.8%), followed by 24 months (20.5%) and 6 months (5.1%). Mean follow-up duration ranged from 12 to 54 months (mean 23.7 ± 10.0 months). Baseline kyphosis was explicitly excluded in 48.7% of studies, while the remainder did not report such exclusion.

Threshold risk stratification

Threshold analyses based on significant predictors identified CGH–C7 SVA >30.6 mm as the most discriminative marker of KD risk, demonstrating non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals and categorized as a very strong indicator. Reduced (e-ROM <11.4°) was classified as a strong predictor. TS–CL mismatch >14.6° and f-ROM >24° were considered moderate indicators, while T1S >26.2° and C2–C7 SVA >22 mm were weaker predictors. Risk of bias, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias

Methodological quality assessment demonstrated generally favorable study quality: 31 studies (79.5%) were rated as low risk and eight (20.5%) as moderate risk according to the NOS, with no studies classified as high risk [Figure 3]. Sensitivity analyses using influence diagnostics and trim-and-fill adjustments confirmed the robustness of the main findings.

Newcastle-Ottawa Scale risk of bias assessment for included cohort studies

Egger’s test suggested limited small-study effects, primarily for C2–C7 surgical involvement (P = 0.042) and C2–C7 SVA among radiographic predictors (P = 0.041). However, these did not materially alter pooled effect estimates, supporting the stability and reliability of the overall results.

DISCUSSION

The present systematic review and meta-analysis sought to identify preoperative constitutional, radiographic, and biomechanical factors associated with postoperative KD following cervical laminoplasty for DCM. The principal finding is that KD is primarily driven by preoperative sagittal alignment demand and dynamic motion reserve rather than by general demographic characteristics or most comorbidities. In other words, postoperative kyphotic progression appears to reflect an imbalance between biomechanical demand and compensatory capacity that already exists before surgery.

Clinical profile: Modifiable host and loading factors

In our pooled analysis, traditional demographic variables such as age and sex were not associated with KD, and follow-up duration did not explain the occurrence of deformity. These findings suggest that postoperative sagittal deterioration is not simply a time-dependent phenomenon but rather a consequence of underlying biomechanical predisposition. Among clinical variables, higher BMI and smoking history demonstrated significant associations with KD, whereas diabetes, hypertension, and baseline neurologic or functional scores were not predictive. These results support a loading-based model of postoperative alignment change. Increased body mass may impose greater mechanical demand on the cervical extensor mechanism after posterior element disruption, whereas smoking may impair soft-tissue healing and muscle function. Together, these modifiable factors may reduce the biologic and mechanical reserve required to maintain lordotic alignment following laminoplasty. Importantly, the absence of association with baseline neurologic severity or symptom duration indicates that postoperative alignment deterioration should not be considered merely a surrogate of disease burden.

Static alignment demand: Cervicothoracic geometry and head–neck balance

Radiographic parameters reflecting preoperative sagittal alignment demand emerged as the most consistent predictors of KD. Higher T1S and greater TS–CL mismatch were significantly associated with postoperative kyphotic progression, reinforcing the concept that cervical alignment must satisfy a lordotic requirement imposed by the thoracic inlet. When this requirement is unmet preoperatively, disruption of posterior tension-band structures during laminoplasty may exceed the patient’s compensatory capacity, leading to sagittal collapse.[8918]

Similarly, anterior translation of the head, represented by increased CGH–C7 SVA and C2–C7 SVA, demonstrated strong predictive value. These parameters increase the flexion moment arm acting on the cervical spine and demand greater posterior extensor and ligamentous resistance to maintain alignment. Our findings align with prior reports identifying preoperative sagittal imbalance as a key determinant of kyphotic progression even in patients without baseline kyphosis.[45] Taken together, these observations emphasize that laminoplasty outcomes are strongly influenced by the preoperative balance between alignment demand and structural support.

Dynamic reserve: Motion distribution rather than total mobility

An important insight from the present analysis is that dynamic motion distribution, rather than total cervical ROM, differentiates patients at higher risk of KD. Patients who developed postoperative deformity exhibited reduced extension reserve (lower e-ROM) and greater flexion excursion (higher f-ROM), whereas total C2–C7 ROM did not differ significantly between groups. This suggests that the relative balance between flexion and extension capability is more relevant than overall mobility.

Reduced extension reserve likely reflects diminished capacity of the posterior extensor mechanism to restore lordosis after posterior element weakening. Conversely, greater flexion excursion may indicate a motion pattern predisposed to anterior collapse once posterior restraint is compromised. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that extension capacity and flexion–extension balance indices predict postoperative sagittal deterioration following laminoplasty.[61929] Thus, dynamic functional assessment may provide valuable information beyond static alignment measurements.

Surgical extent and tissue reserve

Among operative variables, most factors (including operative time, blood loss, and inclusion of C7) did not significantly influence KD risk. However, five-level laminoplasty constructs were associated with increased risk, suggesting that more extensive posterior decompression may compromise the integrity of the posterior tension band and extensor musculature to a greater extent. This observation aligns with biomechanical and clinical studies highlighting the importance of preserving the semispinalis cervicis and other extensor muscles in maintaining postoperative lordosis.[10] Recent works have also emphasized the role of tissue reserve, including muscle quality and bone mineral density, in modulating postoperative alignment changes.[233842] Although such parameters were not consistently analyzable in pooled form, they likely interact with alignment demand and dynamic reserve to determine individual susceptibility to KD. Future studies incorporating quantitative muscle and bone quality metrics may further refine predictive models.

Non-significant predictors and their clinical implication

Several variables did not demonstrate consistent pooled associations with KD, including diabetes, hypertension, symptom duration, and baseline neurologic severity. Likewise, MRI-related findings such as cord signal change and the presence of OPLL were not predictive. Thoracolumbar spinopelvic parameters (PI, PT, LL, TK, and C7 PL SVA) also failed to show meaningful associations. These results suggest that postoperative cervical alignment change is primarily governed by regional cervical biomechanics rather than by systemic comorbidities or global spinopelvic alignment. Clinically, this implies that preoperative counseling and risk stratification should prioritize reproducible sagittal balance and dynamic motion parameters rather than less consistent or indirect predictors. Such an approach may allow surgeons to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from closer postoperative monitoring or alternative surgical strategies.

Integrative biomechanical perspective

Taken together, the findings of this review support a unified biomechanical model of postoperative KD following laminoplasty. Patients with high sagittal alignment demand (steep T1S, large TS–CL mismatch, anterior head translation) and limited compensatory reserve (reduced e-ROM, altered flexion–extension balance) are most vulnerable to sagittal deterioration once the posterior tension band is weakened. In contrast, demographic characteristics and many comorbidities appear to play a secondary role. This framework provides a clinically intuitive explanation for why some patients maintain alignment after laminoplasty while others develop progressive kyphosis despite technically adequate decompression. It also underscores the importance of individualized surgical planning that accounts for both static alignment and dynamic functional reserve.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the majority of included studies were retrospective, introducing potential selection bias and limiting causal inference. Second, definitions of KD, radiographic protocols, and timing of postoperative assessment varied considerably across studies, contributing to heterogeneity. Third, the predominance of studies from East Asia may limit generalizability to other populations. Finally, pooled analyses were based on study-level data and could not fully adjust for interactions among alignment demand, muscle quality, surgical technique, and rehabilitation protocols. Although publication bias assessments were largely reassuring, small-study effects cannot be entirely excluded, particularly for predictors evaluated in a limited number of studies.

CONCLUSION

Postoperative KD after cervical laminoplasty is primarily determined by preoperative sagittal balance and dynamic motion reserve. Higher T1S and TS–CL mismatch, increased anterior head translation (especially CGH–C7 SVA and C2–C7 SVA), reduced extension reserve, and greater flexion excursion consistently characterize higher-risk patients. Incorporating these measurable parameters into routine preoperative evaluation may improve patient selection, refine surgical decision-making, and enable targeted postoperative surveillance for individuals at elevated risk of sagittal deterioration.
