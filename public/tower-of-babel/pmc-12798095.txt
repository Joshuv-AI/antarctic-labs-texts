Application of VIBE sequences for visualization and assessing cartilaginous endplate damage in low back pain patients
Authors: Haifeng Zhao, Xiangbo Zhao, Xuan Zhang, Wenjuan Du, Tianmin Zhang, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDamage to the cartilage endplate (CEP) plays a crucial role in the progression of intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration. This study aims to evaluate the application value of MRI Volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) sequences in visualizing and assessing CEP damage in low back pain (LBP) patients, and to explore the correlation between CEP damage and IVD degeneration.MethodsWe recruited 96 LBP patients (35 males and 61 females) and performed sagittal T2-weighted imaging (T2-WI) TSE, T1-weighted imaging (T1-WI) TSE, conventional VIBE, and VIBE e-dixon sequence scans. The Pfirrmann grade for IVDs, endplate defect grades for CEPs, and the total endplate score (TEPS) were evaluated. Likert scales were employed to assess the image quality of the two VIBE sequences. Inter-observer consistency for image quality and TEPS of both VIBE sequences was tested using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). TEPS results showing higher correlation were chosen to analyze the relationship between TEPS and age. Partial correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between TEPS and Pfirrmann grading while controlling for influencing factors. Subgroup analysis was performed for patients aged ≤50 years and > 50 years.ResultsThe conventional VIBE sequence provided superior image quality for CEP clarity compared to VIBE e-dixon sequences (3.29±0.70 vs 2.58 ± 0.61, p < 0.001). The conventional VIBE sequence also demonstrated higher interobserver consistency in image quality (ICC: 0.668–0.767 vs 0.078–0.404, p < 0.001) and TEPS assessment (ICC: 0.685 vs 0.443, p < 0.001). TEPS from conventional VIBE sequences correlated significantly with Pfirrmann grades across all disc levels (r = 0.675, p < 0.001) and with age (r = 0.557, p < 0.001). After controlling for age, the partial correlation between TEPS and Pfirrmann grading remained significant (r = 0.664, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis showed a stronger correlation between TEPS and IVD degeneration in younger patients (≤50 years, r = 0.650, p < 0.001) compared to older patients ( > 50 years, r = 0.610, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe conventional VIBE sequence generates high-contrast images of the CEP and demonstrates significant potential in assessing CEP damage in LBP patients and its relationship with lumbar IVD degeneration. Its stability and accuracy support its feasibility for clinical application, potentially offering a new, non-invasive method for CEP evaluation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is a leading cause of disability and productivity loss worldwide [1]. Most individuals experience at least one episode of LBP during their lifetime, with a significant proportion progressing to chronic pain [2]. This chronic pain severely impacts patients’ quality of life and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden. Intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration is recognized as one of the most common causes of LBP, particularly in individuals under the age of 50 [3]。

The IVD comprises the centrally located nucleus pulposus (NP), the annulus fibrosus (AF) surrounding it, and the cartilaginous endplates (CEP) above and below. The CEP not only provides mechanical stabilization between the IVD and adjacent vertebrae but also serves as a crucial pathway for the transport of nutrients to IVD cells and the removal of metabolic waste [4]. Studies have demonstrated that structural and compositional changes in the CEP, such as thinning and cartilage calcification, can inhibit nutrient entry into the IVD, leading to its degeneration [5]. Moreover, damage to the CEP area is a potential contributor to LBP [4]. Therefore, evaluating the CEP is essential in IVD research.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with its advantages of non-invasive and high soft tissue contrast, is the most commonly used method for IVD evaluation in clinical practice. Conventional T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences have long echo times, making it difficult to capture the rapidly decaying ultrashort T2 relaxation time signals, thus posing challenges in distinguishing the CEP from the bony endplate (BEP) [6]. Ultrashort echo time (UTE) sequence, with echo times typically ranging from 0.008 to 0.50 ms [7], have shown great potential in the visualization and evaluation of the CEP [8]. Recent studies have confirmed that a series of quantitative and semi-quantitative biomarkers based on UTE sequences are associated with IVD degeneration [9–11]. However, these UTE sequences generally have long scanning times, which presents challenges for their application in clinical practice, especially in LBP patients. Furthermore, the UTE sequence currently lack commercial availability, meaning not all MRI suppliers offer clinically applicable UTE sequences. This limitation restricts the widespread use of the UTE sequence in clinical practice.

Some researchers have explored using sequences that are easier to obtain and have shorter scan times on conventional clinical MRI equipment to detect CEP signals, such as fast low-angle shot (FLASH) sequence [12] and 3D spoiled gradient-recalled echo (SPGR) sequence [13]. These studies have confirmed the potential of FLASH and SPGR sequences in high-contrast CEP imaging, offering new solutions to overcome the limitations of existing CEP imaging technologies. The volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) sequence, a fast, high-resolution MRI sequence, is widely used in clinical practice and has a TE smaller than the CEP T2* value [14]. It offers a shorter scan time compared to FLASH and SPGR sequences, which helps improve the tolerance rate and scan success rate in LBP patients. Additionally, it provides a balance between image quality and scanning efficiency, making it more suitable for routine clinical use. However, whether it can overcome the limitations of existing technologies and its clinical application value in LBP patients requires further evaluation. Based on this, our study aims to preliminarily assess the technical feasibility of optimized VIBE sequences for high-contrast CEP imaging and explore their clinical application value in LBP patients by analyzing their correlation with recognized IVD degeneration evaluation standards (Pfirrmann grading).

subjects

This study enrolled 96 patients (35 males and 61 females, aged 48.6 ± 13.16 years) from January 2024 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) adults aged 20 to 75 years in overall good health; (2) diagnosed with chronic low back pain according to the NIH-reported definition [15], which includes experiencing pain on at least half the days in the past six months (accumulating to at least three months of pain days), with or without leg pain. The exclusion criteria were: (1) individuals unable to tolerate long scans due to severe low back pain or claustrophobia, or those with contraindications to MRI such as metallic implants; (2) individuals with congenital spinal deformities, a history of lumbar surgery, radiation therapy, or various spinal diseases such as inflammation, trauma, or tumors (benign or malignant); (3) individuals with systemic diseases such as hematological disorders or metabolic bone diseases, or those currently taking medications that may affect IVD metabolism (e.g., glucocorticoids).

MRI sequence protocol

All MRI scans were performed using a 3T MRI scanner (Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a Siemens Tim coil. The imaging protocol included the following sequences:

Sagittal T2-weighted 2D fat-saturated (FS) dixon sequence: Field of view (FOV) of 300 mm × 300 mm, echo time (TE) = 92 ms, repetition time (TR) = 3480 ms, matrix = 320 × 224, slice thickness = 4.0 mm, number of slices = 11, averages = 2, bandwidth = 340 Hz, scan time 2 m 23 s.

Sagittal T1-weighted 2D turbo spin echo (TSE) sequence: FOV of 300 mm × 300 mm, TE = 8.6 ms, TR = 747 ms, matrix = 320 × 320, slice thickness = 4.0 mm, number of slices = 11, averages = 1, bandwidth = 252 Hz, scan time 2 m 18 s.

Sagittal conventional VIBE sequence: FOV of 380 mm × 380 mm, TR = 4.02 ms, TE = 1.84 ms, slice thickness = 3.0 mm, matrix = 384 × 288, number of slices = 72, averages = 1, with five flip angles of 2°, 5°, 7°, 10°, and 15°, bandwidth = 540 Hz, parallel acquisition factor = 2, single scan time 19 s, and total scan time 1 m 37 s.

Sagittal VIBE e-dixon sequence: FOV of 380 mm × 380 mm, TR = 4.17 ms, TE = 1.23 ms, slice thickness = 3.0 mm, matrix = 192 × 144, number of slices = 40, number of acquisitions = 3, with five flip angles of 2°, 5°, 7°, 10°, and 15°, bandwidth = 1040 Hz, parallel acquisition factor = 4, single scan time 10 s, and total scan time 52 s.

Image analysis

All acquired images were analyzed on a diagnostic screen independently by two radiologists (Z.X and Z.X.B, with 2 and 3 years of musculoskeletal radiology experience, respectively), who were blinded to the scan and patient information. Observers were allowed to adjust the window width and level during the evaluation process.

Initially, the radiologists assessed endplate Modic changes and IVD Pfirrmann grades on T1-weighted and T2-weighted images. Subsequently, the radiologists independently evaluated endplate damage and image quality on the conventional VIBE sequence. After a minimum interval of 2 weeks to reduce memory bias, they reassessed endplate damage and image quality on the VIBE e-dixon sequence. To ensure accuracy of the results, a third experienced radiologist (Z.H.F, with 10 years of musculoskeletal radiology experience) was consulted in cases of disagreement, and consensus was reached through discussion among the three radiologists.

Evaluation of endplate Modic changes and IVD pfirrmann grading

Endplate modic changes

Endplate Modic changes were independently evaluated by two radiologists (Z.X and Z.X.B) on sagittal T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences, based on bone marrow signal alterations above and below the endplate, which categorizes changes into three types: Type 1 (hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images), type 2 (hyperintense on both T1- and T2-weighted images), and type 3 (hypointense on both T1- and T2-weighted images). If none of these changes were present, it was classified as having no Modic changes.

IVD degeneration grading

The severity of IVD degeneration was also evaluated independently by the same two radiologists on sagittal T2-WI images using the widely adopted Pfirrmann grading system [16]. The criteria for grading are as follows:

Grade 1: The nucleus pulposus (NP) and annulus fibrosus (AF) are clearly distinguishable. The IVD structure is homogeneous and bright white, exhibiting hyperintense signal, with normal disc height. Grade 2: The NP and AF are clearly distinguishable. The IVD structure is inhomogeneous, with or without horizontal bands, exhibiting hyperintense signal, with normal disc height. Grade 3: The NP and AF are not clearly distinguishable. The IVD structure is inhomogeneous and gray, exhibiting intermediate signal, with normal to slightly reduced disc height. Grade 4: There is no clear distinction between the NP and AF. The IVD structure is inhomogeneous and gray to black, exhibiting intermediate to hypointense signal, with normal to moderately reduced disc height. Grade 5: There is no clear distinction between the NP and AF. The IVD structure is inhomogeneous and black, exhibiting hypointense signal, with collapsed disc space.

Endplate defect grading and total endplate score (TEPS) calculation

Due to the uncertainty of VIBE sequences in the preliminary assessment of the cartilage endplates, quantitative analyses such as layer thickness and T1 values were not attempted. Instead, we employed a modified six-level grading system proposed by Ji et al. [11]. This semi-quantitative analysis system builds upon the Rajasekaran grading system for endplate lesions [17], adding criteria for diagnosing CEP and bony endplate (BEP) damage.

The specific grades are as follows: Grade I: Both CEP and BEP are normal, with the CEP being continuous and uniformly thin. Grade II: BEP is normal, but the CEP is partially or wholly thinned, though it remains continuous without interruptions or defects. Grade III: BEP is normal, while the CEP is significantly thinned locally but still continuous. Grade IV: BEP shows Modic changes, and the CEP exhibits defects, but these defects do not exceed 25% of the total CEP area. Grade V: CEP damage covers 25% to 50% of the total area, accompanied by BEP Modic changes. Grade VI: CEP damage exceeds 50% of the total area, potentially involving the entire CEP, accompanied by BEP Modic changes. Using this grading system, each IVD's rostral and caudal endplates were independently evaluated for defects. The endplate defect scores from the rostral and caudal endplates were then summed to calculate the total endplate score (TEPS) for each IVD.

Image quality assessment

To ensure the reliability and repeatability of image evaluations, we established stringent image quality assessment standards, focusing on three key aspects: CEP clarity, image noise, and image artifacts. Each aspect was evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale to ensure consistency and objectivity in the assessment process. To ensure consistency and reliability, both radiologists underwent unified training before the assessment. CEP clarity

CEP clarity was assessed for endplates with defect grades of 3 or below, based on the distinguishability of the boundary between the CEP and adjacent structures (including the bony endplate and intervertebral disc). Clear boundaries indicate the ability to accurately outline the CEP and measure its morphological parameters. A 5-point Likert scale was used for scoring, with higher scores representing better clarity. The specific scoring criteria are as follows: 5 point: Excellent, with sharp boundaries between the CEP and surrounding tissues, allowing clear delineation; 4 point: Good, with slightly blurred boundaries between the CEP and surrounding tissues, but still discernible; 3 point: Fair, with blurred boundaries between the CEP and surrounding tissues, but still discernible for endplate defect grading; 2 point: Poor, with significantly blurred boundaries affecting endplate defect grading; 1 point: Very Poor, with the CEP not identifiable.

Image noise

Image noise refers to random signal fluctuations in the image, manifesting as graininess or snow-like artifacts that reduce image clarity and detail resolution. A 5-point Likert scale was used for scoring, with higher scores indicating lower noise levels and better image quality. The specific scoring criteria are as follows: 5 point: The image background is very clean with almost no noise; 4 point: The image background has slight noise but does not affect the observation of the CEP; 3 point: The image background noise is noticeable and may affect the detail observation of the CEP; 2 point: The image background noise is significant, clearly affecting the observation of the CEP; 1 point: Severe background noise, making it difficult to discern the CEP.

Image artifacts

Image artifacts are non-physiological signals such as motion artifacts that interfere with the accurate interpretation of the image. A 5-point Likert scale was used for scoring, with higher scores indicating fewer artifacts and better image quality. The specific scoring criteria are as follows: 5 point: No significant artifacts present in the image; 4 point: Minor artifacts present but do not affect the observation of the CEP area; 3 point: Moderate artifacts present that may affect the observation of the CEP area; 2 point: Significant artifacts present, affecting the observation of the CEP area; 1 point: Severe artifacts making it difficult to observe the CEP.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0.0; IBM Corp). Paired sample t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (for non-normally distributed data) were used to analyze differences in Total Endplate Scores (TEPS) and ratings of CEP clarity, image noise, and image artifacts between the two VIBE sequences. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used to quantify inter-observer consistency for the two VIBE sequences. Spearman’s rank correlation tests were conducted to analyze the relationship between TEPS and IVD degeneration for both VIBE sequences. TEPS results with higher correlations were selected for further analysis. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between TEPS scores and age, as well as sex. Partial correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between TEPS scores and Pfirrmann grading after controlling for influencing factors. Finally, subgroup comparisons of TEPS scores and Pfirrmann grading were made using age as a grouping variable (≤50 years and > 50 years) to determine differences between the subgroups, and to analyze the relationship between TEPS scores and Pfirrmann grading within each subgroup. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demography and IVD degeneration analysis

We evaluated a total of 479 intervertebral discs (IVDs) and corresponding 958 endplates from 96 patients (one disc was excluded due to difficulties in assessment with both VIBE sequences). Among the 96 LBP patients, 35 were male (36.46%) and 61 were female (63.54%), with ages ranging from 20 to 73 (48.6±13.16)years. Of the 479 IVDs assessed, the distribution according to Pfirrmann grading was as follows: 3 discs were grade I (0.6%), 180 discs were grade II (37.6%), 225 discs were grade III (47.0%), 58 discs were grade IV (12.1%), and 13 discs were grade V (2.7%).

Endplate damage assessment and consistency analysis

Consistency analysis of endplate damage assessment

Two observers assessed 958 endplates independently using the modified Rajasekaran grading system on both the conventional VIBE and VIBE e-dixon sequences, and calculated the TEPS for 479 IVDs. The CEP damage assessment on the conventional VIBE sequence showed good consistency with an ICC value of 0.685 (95% CI 0.634–0.730, p < 0.001), while the VIBE e-dixon sequence showed a lower ICC value of 0.443 (95% CI 0.368–0.512, p < 0.001). Further validation at the individual disc level indicated that the consistency of the conventional VIBE sequence was superior to that of the VIBE e-dixon sequence (Table 1).

Endplate damage assessment

Following a thorough discussion and the establishment of a consensus, a total of 958 endplates were assessed. The assessment using the conventional VIBE sequence included 402 grade I, 312 grade II, 84 grade III, 73 grade IV, 41 grade V, and 46 grade VI endplates. In comparison, the VIBE e-dixon sequence assessment included 400 grade I, 306 grade II, 107 grade III, 62 grade IV, 44 grade V, and 39 grade VI endplates.

By summing the scores of the rostral and caudal endplates for each functional spinal unit, we calculated the TEPS for each IVD. For the conventional VIBE sequence, the TEPS of 479 IVDs were: 114 IVDs with 2 scores, 124 with 3 scores, 77 with 4 scores, 50 with 5 scores, 34 with 6 scores, 33 with 7 scores, 16 with 8 scores, 7 with 9 scores, 7 with 10 scores, 7 with 11 scores, and 10 with 12 scores. For the VIBE e-dixon sequence, the TEPS of 479 IVDs were: 139 IVDs with 2 scores, 93 with 3 scores, 97 with 4 scores, 39 with 5 scores, 34 with 6 scores, 20 with 7 scores, 15 with 8 scores, 17 with 9 scores, 7 with 10 scores, 9 with 11 scores, and 9 with 12 scores. There were no statistically significant differences in the overall TEPS scores or the individual disc TEPS scores between the two sequences (Table 2).

Correlation between endplate damage and IVD degeneration, and demography

We employed Spearman’s rank correlation analysis to evaluate the relationship between TEPS and IVD degeneration. The results indicated that TEPS derived from both the conventional VIBE sequences and VIBE e-dixon sequences were significantly correlated with the Pfirrmann grading of IVD degeneration, with correlation coefficients of 0.675 (p < 0.001) and 0.371 (p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 2a and b). Further analysis at the individual disc level revealed that TEPS from the conventional VIBE sequence was significantly correlated with IVD degeneration at every disc level. Comparison of the correlation between TEPS and IVD degeneration in conventional VIBE sequences and VIBE e-dixon sequences

Due to the more robust performance of TEPS from the conventional VIBE sequence, we used TEPS based on the conventional VIBE sequence to explore its correlation with demography. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant correlation between TEPS and age, with a coefficient of 0.557 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). However, the correlation coefficient between TEPS and Pfirrmann grading were nearly equal across genders. (Figs. 2d and 1f).

Subsequently, we further investigated the impact of age on the correlation between TEPS and IVD degeneration. Partial correlation analysis showed that after controlling for the confounding factor of age, TEPS remained significantly correlated with IVD degeneration, with a coefficient of 0.646 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2i). Additionally, we divided the patients into two groups: a younger group (≤50 years) and an older group ( > 50 years). Significant differences were observed between the younger and older groups in both TEPS and Pfirrmann grading (Fig. 2h). TEPS was significantly correlated with IVD degeneration in both the younger and older groups, with coefficients of 0.650 and 0.610, respectively (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2f and g).

Discussion

This study successfully visualized the morphology of the CEP in vivo using MRI VIBE imaging technology and preliminarily explored its application value in assessing CEP damage in LBP patients. The results indicated that the conventional VIBE sequence clearly displays CEP morphology and can be used to evaluate the extent of CEP damage. Moreover, we found a significant correlation between the degree of CEP damage assessed by the VIBE sequence and the degree of IVD degeneration. This supports the potential of MRI VIBE imaging in evaluating CEP damage in LBP patients, which may provide a new and convenient imaging method for CEP assessment and aid in early diagnosis and personalized treatment of IVD degeneration. One important clinical implication of the TEPS is its potential to guide treatment decisions. Previous studies have indicated that a TEPS score based on conventional MRI of 6 or above is associated with extensive CEP damage, which may render patients unsuitable for biological regeneration treatments such as biologics or stem cell therapies; Conversely, these patients would probably require more invasive procedures such as arthroplasty or fusion [17]. By accurately assessing the TEPS using VIBE sequences, clinicians could better determine the appropriate treatment strategy for each patient based on the severity of their CEP damage.

Numerous studies have shown that changes in the structure and composition of the CEP are key pathogenic factors in the occurrence and progression of IVD degeneration [9, 18, 19]. However, due to the small size of CEP and its relatively short T2/T2* tissue characteristics, the clinically routine fast spin echo (FSE) sequence neither clearly delineates the complete morphology of the CEP nor accurately distinguishes it from the BEP [4]. The UTE sequence, which has been extensively used in recent CEP studies, also faces significant challenges in widespread clinical application due to its prolonged scanning time and lack of commercial availability [20]. In contrast, the VIBE sequence offers advantages such as rapid acquisition, high resolution, and ease of clinical implementation, making it increasingly popular in musculoskeletal imaging [21, 22]. Therefore, this study utilized the VIBE sequence for CEP imaging.

We explored two different scanning methods: the conventional VIBE sequence and the VIBE e-dixon sequence. The former, based on 3D spoiled gradient echo imaging, improves scan efficiency with techniques like volume interpolation, thereby facilitating rapid imaging with high resolution, and is suitable for detailed anatomical observations. The latter combines VIBE and Dixon technologies, achieving water-fat separation imaging while improving scanning efficiency. Additionally, we optimized the sequences by employing multiple flip angles for multi-contrast imaging of the cartilaginous endplate (CEP) and nucleus pulposus (NP), aiming to maintain a reasonable signal intensity for the CEP (Figs. 3a and 3b). The conventional VIBE sequence significantly outperformed the VIBE e-dixon sequence in terms of CEP clarity and image noise. While the conventional VIBE sequence was slightly better in terms of image artifacts, the difference was not statistically significant. This indicates that the image quality of the conventional VIBE sequence is suitable for assessing CEP damage, while the VIBE e-dixon sequence is not suitable for this purpose. This may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the conventional VIBE sequence inherently employs a larger acquisition matrix, which enhances spatial resolution and consequently improves the clarity of CEP visualization. In contrast, while the VIBE e-Dixon sequence provides the advantage of water-fat separation imaging, its relatively lower spatial resolution results in insufficient CEP delineation, thereby compromising diagnostic utility. Furthermore, the excessively short scan time (10 s) and an overly large bandwidth (1040 Hz) in the VIBE e-Dixon protocol likely exacerbated noise levels, further degrading CEP conspicuity. Therefore, compared to the VIBE e-Dixon sequence, which prioritizes scanning efficiency at the expense of image quality, the conventional VIBE sequence achieves an optimal balance between spatial resolution and acquisition speed, rendering it a more robust choice for CEP assessment in clinical practice.

Additionally, the subjective image quality of the conventional VIBE sequence demonstrated higher inter-observer consistency, indicating its greater reliability. In assessing the consistency of CEP damage evaluation between observers, the conventional VIBE sequence also performed better. This suggests that the conventional VIBE sequence, with its superior image quality, offers higher stability in assessing CEP damage. Further verification at each disc level showed that the consistency of the conventional VIBE sequence was superior to that of the VIBE e-dixon sequence. We speculate that this may be related to the varying degrees of cartilage damage between different discs. Discrepancies in grading, such as between grades II and III, as well as grades IV and V, are relatively minor compared to grades I and VI, potentially leading to discrepancies in grading opinions. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed in the future to further verify the reliability of CEP damage assessment at different disc levels and to explore strategies for improving assessment consistency.

More notably, we observed that some CEPs appeared as low signal on conventional VIBE and intermediate signal on VIBE e-dixon (Fig. 3d), which we speculate may be related to endplate calcification, leading to discrepancies in our scoring process. CEP calcification is a common phenomenon in the process of IVD degeneration. Studies have shown that CEP calcification can inhibit the transport of nutrients within the disc and lower the pH within the disc [23], playing a significant role in the occurrence and progression of IVD degeneration. However, the modified Rajasekaran grading proposed by Ji et al. did not include calcification in its criteria. Consequently, future research could focus on further optimizing VIBE sequence parameters to enhance its ability to detect CEP calcification and explore the feasibility of incorporating the degree of CEP calcification into the IVD degeneration assessment system.

The conventional VIBE sequence used in this study had a smaller flip angle than the optimal flip angle of 20° for CEP imaging calculated by Moon et al. [12] based on MRI pulse sequence equations. Consequently, Our preliminary attempt did not employ quantitative assessments that demand high image quality, such as thickness or T1 value measurements, for CEP evaluation. Instead, we utilized semi-quantitative evaluation methods that are less dependent on image quality. During the experiment, we set multiple flip angles to lay the groundwork for future T1 value measurements of CEP using the VIBE sequences. Fortunately, the semi-quantitative assessment results were promising. Both the conventional VIBE and VIBE e-dixon sequences showed significant correlations between TEPS and the Pfirrmann grade of IVD, but the TEPS derived from the conventional VIBE sequence was significantly higher than that from the VIBE e-dixon sequence (correlation coefficients 0.675 vs 0.371, both p < 0.001). We further validated this correlation at each IVD level, and the results indicated that the correlation between TEPS based on the conventional VIBE sequence and the Pfirrmann grade of IVD was superior at all disc levels compared to the VIBE e-dixon sequence. Our findings indicate that CEP damage is associated with IVD degeneration, consistent with previous studies [24], supporting a close pathophysiological link between CEP damage and IVD degeneration. These findings demonstrate that the conventional VIBE sequence not only provides clear visualization of CEP damage but also shows a significant correlation with IVD degeneration. Critically, this approach directly targets a fundamental limitation in LBP imaging: poor patient tolerance during prolonged scans. The conventional VIBE sequence achieves a revolutionary single-scan duration of merely 19 seconds—representing a > 5-fold reduction versus FLASH (145 s) and > 3-fold reduction versus SPGR (96 s) sequences documented in current research [13, 25]. This unprecedented temporal efficiency is transformative for the LBP cohort, wherein extended immobilization invariably exacerbates pain, inducing motion artifacts and examination failure. By virtually eliminating prolonged scanning as a primary failure mechanism, the conventional VIBE sequence dramatically reduces motion-related artifacts and failed acquisitions, thereby fulfilling stringent clinical operational requirements. This capability establishes VIBE as a clinically viable paradigm for routine CEP visualization. Consequently, the core innovation and impact of this work transcend the mere application of VIBE; it resides in irrefutably demonstrating VIBE's capacity to overcome a critical barrier in LBP diagnostics. The sequence’s exCEPtional speed redefines the feasibility of visualized CEP assessment in routine practice, effectively solving the previously intractable trade-off between scan quality and patient tolerance inherent to earlier sequences. When combined with its diagnostic-grade image quality and operational simplicity, the conventional VIBE sequence emerges not merely as an incremental improvement, but as a promising methodology poised to become the preferred standard for CEP visualized assessment in clinical LBP management.

We observed a significant correlation between TEPS based on the conventional VIBE sequence and age. After controlling for age as a confounding factor, TEPS based on the conventional VIBE sequence remained significantly correlated with the Pfirrmann grade of IVD. This result aligns with the findings of Rade et al., who noted that when TEPS is included as a predictor in a multivariable model of IVD degeneration, covariates such as age and BMI lose significance [26]. Additionally, We also examined the correlation between TEPS and IVD degeneration across different age subgroups (≤50 years vs > 50 years), with significant correlations in both younger and older groups (0.650 vs 0.610, both p < 0.001). This indicates that TEPS damage is more closely associated with IVD degeneration in younger individuals. The CEP is essential for nutrient supply and metabolic waste exchange in IVDs. CEP degeneration disrupts IVD energy metabolism, potentially triggering or accelerating degenerative processes [27]. In contrast, elderly patients often have comorbidities, and their degenerative changes are more complex than those in younger individuals, which may confound the relationship between CEP injury and IVD degeneration. Therefore, larger sample sizes and more detailed subgroup analyses are needed to further investigate these confounding factors, providing crucial theoretical support for the necessity of CEP evaluation and early intervention in clinical practice.

This study also has certain limitations. Firstly, the sample size is relatively small, and all included patients suffer from chronic LBP, which might limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes, more diverse patient cohort and multi-center prospective designs are needed to comprehensively evaluate the performance of the VIBE sequence in different degrees and types of CEP damage and IVD degeneration. Secondly, the image quality assessment in this study primarily relied on the subjective judgments of two radiologists. Although we introduced quantitative metrics to assist in the analysis, subjective factors could not be entirely eliminated. Future work could involve developing AI-based automated assessment methods to enhance objectivity and efficiency. Additionally, due to medical ethics and other constraints, it was not possible to obtain histological analyses of NP and CEP, leading to the reliance on Pfirrmann grading as the relative gold standard for IVD degeneration and the inability to further refine the TEPS grading of CEP. Future studies could address these limitations by utilizing spine specimens or animal models to obtain histological and molecular-level data, which would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the structural and biological changes associated with IVD degeneration. Such approaches could also help refine the TEPS grading system and provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying CEP changes and their clinical implications. Furthermore, the study lacks histological evidence and did not consider other potential factors influencing CEP damage and IVD degeneration, such as genetic factors, environmental factors, occupation, and lifestyle habits [28]. Future research should aim for a more comprehensive analysis of the complex relationship between CEP damage and IVD degeneration and explore more effective methods for early diagnosis and treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the VIBE sequence is capable of generating high-contrast images of the CEP, showing great promise in assessing CEP damage in LBP patients and its relationship with lumbar IVD degeneration. Its stability and accuracy support its feasibility for clinical application, potentially offering a new method for non-invasive assessment of the CEP in clinical practice.
