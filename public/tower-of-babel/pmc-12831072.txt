The relationship between preoperative relative second metatarsal length and postoperative transfer metatarsalgia following hallux valgus surgery
Authors: Bekir Karagoz, Hunkar Cagdas Bayrak, Durmuş Ekin Dincer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to investigate the potential association between transfer metatarsalgia, a complication that may arise postoperatively following hallux valgus surgery, and the preoperative relative second metatarsal length measured on weight-bearing radiographs. Additionally, it was sought to evaluate the predictive value of this radiographic parameter.Methods:A total of 126 patients who underwent primary hallux valgus surgery between January 2022 and December 2023 and had a minimum follow-up of 12 months were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the presence or absence of transfer metatarsalgia during the postoperative follow-up. Relative second metatarsal length was measured as the primary radiographic variable using preoperative weight-bearing anteroposterior foot radiographs. In addition, the hallux valgus angle (HVA) and the intermetatarsal angle (IMA) were evaluated preoperatively. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society Hallux Metatarsophalangeal–Interphalangeal (AOFAS Hallux MTP-IP) score at the 12-month follow-up. Demographic and surgical variables, such as age, sex, surgical technique, and implant type, were also recorded. Group comparisons were performed, and multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to identify variables predictive of transfer metatarsalgia.Results:Patients who developed transfer metatarsalgia had significantly higher preoperative relative second metatarsal length values (P < .001). Among the variables included in the multivariate logistic regression model, only relative second metatarsal length was found to be a statistically significant predictor of transfer metatarsalgia (odds ratio: 5.176; 95% CI: 2.813-9.493). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified a threshold value of 2.15 mm, which was adopted as 2 mm for clinical applicability, with an area under the curve of 0.832, sensitivity of 79.4%, and specificity of 78.2%. In contrast, other parameters such as age, sex, surgical technique, implant type, preoperative HVA, IMA, and postoperative AOFAS Hallux MTP-IP scores were not significantly associated with the development of transfer metatarsalgia.Conclusion:Relative second metatarsal length was found to be significantly associated with the development of postoperative transfer metatarsalgia, and ROC analysis identified 2 mm as a clinically applicable cutoff value. Preoperative measurement of relative second metatarsal length can help identify patients at higher risk of this complication, allowing for improved patient counseling and more informed surgical planning.Level of Evidence:Level III, Prognostic study.

Introduction

Hallux valgus is one of the most common forefoot deformities, characterized by lateral deviation of the great toe at the first metatarsophalangeal joint, and occurs more frequently in adult women.1,2 It can cause pain, gait disturbance, footwear incompatibility, and cosmetic concerns, making it a significant clinical problem.3-5 Surgical treatment is indicated in patients with progressive deformity and persistent symptoms despite conservative management, and various first metatarsal osteotomy techniques are commonly used.2-4 Although surgery typically achieves deformity correction and pain relief, some patients develop postoperative complications such as transfer metatarsalgia, which can negatively affect satisfaction and functional outcomes.6-8

One such complication is transfer metatarsalgia, which presents as plantar pain typically localized to the second or third metatarsal head and is aggravated by weight-bearing.9-12 Transfer metatarsalgia is believed to result from altered forefoot load distribution and can negatively affect patient satisfaction after surgery.13 Therefore, identifying factors that contribute to the development of transfer metatarsalgia and improving its predictability is of clinical importance.

Several studies in the literature have investigated potential associations between the development of transfer metatarsalgia and factors such as discrepancies in metatarsal lengths, surgical technique, and first metatarsal shortening.14,15 However, this issue remains controversial, and there is currently no definitive criterion to predict which patients are at risk of developing this complication. In particular, there is a need for studies focusing on the identification of at-risk patients in the preoperative period through radiographic assessment.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential relationship between postoperative transfer metatarsalgia and the relative second metatarsal length measured radiographically in the preoperative period among patients undergoing hallux valgus surgery. It was hypothesized that the preoperatively measured relative second metatarsal length may be associated with the development of postoperative transfer metatarsalgia and could serve as a useful parameter for identifying high-risk patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective observational cohort analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Eskişehir City Hospital Institutional Review Board (Date: 25 June 2025; Approval No.: ESH/BAEK 2025/196). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized and retrieved from the hospital information management system and digital archives.

Patients who underwent surgical treatment for primary hallux valgus between January 2022 and December 2023 and were followed postoperatively for at least 12 months were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria were undergoing surgical correction for hallux valgus, availability of preoperative weight-bearing anteroposterior foot radiographs, and the presence of clinical and radiological follow-up data for a minimum of 12 months. Exclusion criteria included presence of preoperative transfer metatarsalgia (n = 11), concurrent second toe surgery (n = 4), diabetic neuropathy (n = 6), rheumatologic disease (n = 4), previous foot surgery (n = 4), and insufficient follow-up or radiological data (n = 7). After excluding 36 patients based on these criteria, a total of 126 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Patient demographics and surgical data, including age, sex, smoking status, follow-up duration, operating surgeon, and type of osteotomy, were recorded. Patients were then divided into 2 groups according to the development of transfer metatarsalgia during postoperative follow-up: patients who developed transfer metatarsalgia were classified as Group A, and those who did not were classified as Group B. Group A consisted of 34 patients, and Group B included 92 patients. All patients had a minimum postoperative follow-up of 12 months, and functional outcomes were evaluated at the 12-month visit.

Surgical technique and postoperative rehabilitation

All surgical procedures were planned and performed by orthopedic surgeons with a minimum of 5 years of clinical experience in the field of orthopedics and traumatology. Surgeries were conducted under spinal or general anesthesia, without the use of a tourniquet, and with the patient in the supine position. A medial longitudinal incision was used as the surgical approach, followed by subcutaneous dissection and longitudinal capsulotomy. The medial eminence of the first metatarsal was resected. Depending on the degree of deformity and the surgeon’s preference, either a Chevron or a Scarf osteotomy was performed (Figure 2). The Chevron osteotomy involved a classic V-shaped cut at the distal metatarsal head, with lateral displacement of the distal fragment.16 The Scarf osteotomy was performed using biplanar Z-shaped cuts along the metatarsal shaft, allowing lateral translation of the distal fragment with additional derotation or elevation when necessary.9 In selected cases, an Akin osteotomy was added to the procedure.17 All osteotomies were stabilized using headless cannulated screws or mini plate-screw systems, depending on the type of osteotomy and the surgeon‘s discretion.

All patients underwent a standardized postoperative care and rehabilitation protocol. Starting from the first postoperative day, all patients were mobilized using a Barouk shoe that allowed heel-weight-bearing. For the first 3 weeks, mobilization was limited to heel contact only. Gradual forefoot loading was permitted after this period based on clinical and radiological healing. Full weight-bearing was typically achieved by the sixth week. No patients were immobilized with a cast, and early mobilization principles were strictly followed.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical assessments were conducted in accordance with a standardized follow-up protocol at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively. The diagnosis of transfer metatarsalgia was based on patient-reported symptoms, including plantar pain localized to the second or third metatarsal head, exacerbated by weight-bearing, tenderness on palpation, and intolerance to footwear.18 Diagnosis was supported by a systematic physical examination performed by 2 orthopedic surgeons experienced in forefoot pathologies. Examiners were blinded to the patients‘ surgical techniques and radiographic data to minimize potential observer bias. At the 12-month follow-up, functional status was assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society Metatarsophalangeal–Interphalangeal (AOFAS Hallux MTP-IP) score, a 100-point scale that objectively evaluates pain, function, and range of motion of the hallux.19

Radiological evaluation

All radiological assessments were performed using preoperative and postoperative weight-bearing anteroposterior foot radiographs. High-resolution digital images were retrieved from the hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS) and analyzed using digital measurement tools. All radiographs were obtained with patients standing, the X-ray beam centered on the midfoot, and a fixed source-to-image distance of 100 cm to minimize magnification errors. The PACS software automatically applies geometric calibration based on known pixel size and acquisition parameters, which improves measurement accuracy; however, a small degree of magnification may still occur due to object-to-image distance. Using a normalized ratio helps to minimize the potential effect of such magnification across patients. No additional radiopaque calibration marker was used; however, this standardized acquisition protocol allows reproducible and reliable measurements across patients. Relative second metatarsal length was specifically measured on preoperative weight-bearing dorsoplantar radiographs and defined as the perpendicular distance from the apex of the second metatarsal head to an imaginary line connecting the distal margins of the first and third metatarsal heads (Figure 3).20,21 In addition to assessing the relative second metatarsal length, a relative parameter was also calculated by expressing this value as a percentage of the first metatarsal length. This parameter was chosen as the primary variable to assess potential biomechanical effects on forefoot load distribution. Additionally, preoperative hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA) were measured according to definitions in the literature.22 All measurements were independently performed by 2 orthopedic surgeons using the PACS system under blinded conditions, with no access to group allocation, surgical techniques, or clinical outcomes. Interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a 2-way random-effects model with absolute agreement.23

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software version 27.0 IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. To identify independent predictors of transfer metatarsalgia, Firth’s penalized logistic regression analysis was performed in R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) instead of conventional logistic regression, as the number of events per variable (EPV ≈ 4.8) was below the recommended threshold of 10. This approach reduces small-sample bias and provides more stable coefficient estimates. Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples, and bias-corrected CIs were reported. Model performance was evaluated in terms of both discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, while calibration was evaluated through the calibration intercept, slope, and Brier score. To account for model optimism, all metrics were adjusted using bootstrap validation, and the optimism-corrected area under the curve (AUC) was reported. Calibration was assessed by comparing predicted and observed probabilities. The relative second metatarsal length was measured in millimeters, and odds ratios were expressed per 1-millimeter increase. For the normalized ratio between the second and first metatarsal lengths, the effect was expressed per 0.10 increase to facilitate interpretation. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was also used to determine the optimal cutoff value (based on Youden’s index) and to calculate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 126 patients were included in the study, with a mean age of 38.0 ± 11.6 years (range: 18-65 years). The cohort consisted of 105 females (83%) and 21 males (17%). The 2 groups were compared in terms of age, sex, HVA, IMA, surgical technique, implant type, and AOFAS Hallux MTP-IP scores measured at the 12-month postoperative follow-up. The mean AOFAS Hallux MTP-IP score was 61.5 ± 8.3 in Group A and 62.1 ± 7.9 in Group B, with no statistically significant difference between the groups. The results are presented in Table 1. None of the other variables showed a statistically significant difference between the groups. In contrast, the relative second metatarsal length was found to be significantly higher in Group A (P < .001).

The ICC for relative second metatarsal length was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88-0.95), for HVA it was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84-0.93), and for IMA it was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.80-0.91). These values indicate a high level of interobserver agreement for the measurements performed.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis using Firth’s penalized maximum likelihood estimation was performed to identify variables associated with the development of transfer metatarsalgia. To improve model stability given the low number of events per variable, internal validation was conducted using 1000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected CIs were reported. The model included age, sex, HVA, IMA, surgical technique, implant type, and relative second metatarsal length (Table 2). Among these variables, only relative second metatarsal length remained a statistically significant predictor of transfer metatarsalgia (P < .001). Each 1-millimeter increase in this measurement was associated with an approximately 5-fold increase in the risk of developing transfer metatarsalgia (OR: 5.176; 95% CI: 2.813-9.493).

The model demonstrated excellent discrimination (optimism-adjusted AUC = 0.832) and good calibration, with a calibration intercept of 0.04 and slope of 0.97. The Brier score was 0.118, indicating adequate overall model performance. The calibration plot showed close agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Figure 4).

In addition, the relative second metatarsal length to the first metatarsal length ratio was evaluated, and this ratio also remained a significant predictor in Firth’s regression analysis (P < .001). The odds of developing transfer metatarsalgia increased in parallel with higher ratio values, supporting the robustness of the association across different foot sizes (Table 3).

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of the relative second metatarsal length (Figure 4). The AUC was 0.832 (95% CI: 0.74-0.90) and statistically significant (P < .001). The optimal cutoff value was 2.15 mm, with a sensitivity of 79.4%, specificity of 78.2%, PPV of 57.4%, and NPV of 91.1%. For ease of use in clinical practice, this value was adopted as 2 mm.

Discussion

This study is among the few to evaluate the predictive role of relative second metatarsal length for postoperative transfer metatarsalgia using both multivariate logistic regression and ROC curve analysis, providing a comprehensive assessment of its clinical relevance. The relationship between postoperative transfer metatarsalgia and relative second metatarsal length measured on preoperative radiographs was investigated in patients who underwent hallux valgus surgery. The findings showed that relative second metatarsal length was significantly greater in patients who developed transfer metatarsalgia. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, this was the only variable found to be statistically significant, and increased values were associated with a higher risk of developing this complication. Receiver operating characteristic analysis also confirmed the discriminative power of relative second metatarsal length. However, given the retrospective and observational design of this study, these results should be interpreted as predictive and associative rather than causal.

The parameter of interest in this study, relative second metatarsal length, quantifies the distal projection of the second metatarsal head relative to a line connecting the distal margins of the first and third metatarsals.18 Digital measurement on weight-bearing radiographs standardizes this process and improves interobserver reliability. Several descriptions of the metatarsal parabola have been reported, including the Hardy and Clapham parabola, but these methods are often limited by subjective interpretation.24 Compared with Hardy–Clapham or Maestro methods, relative second metatarsal length offers a simple linear measurement that can be performed quickly and reproducibly on standard weight-bearing radiographs, making it more practical for both clinical use and research. Although differences in metatarsal length have been described, most previous studies have focused on first metatarsal shortening or the postoperative position of the first metatarsal.9,14,15,21,25 The specific biomechanical implications of a more distally positioned second metatarsal head, particularly its effect on plantar load distribution, have not been thoroughly investigated. Gait analysis and pedobarographic studies have demonstrated that a distalized second metatarsal head increases plantar pressure and prolongs loading during the stance phase, resulting in higher stress on soft tissues.26,27 These effects become more pronounced when the metatarsal parabola is not adequately restored after surgery, leading to chronic irritation and pain beneath the second metatarsal head.18,20,28 Despite these observations, systematic approaches that quantitatively link relative second metatarsal length to plantar pressure distribution remain scarce in the current literature.12,28

Although several parameters such as first metatarsal shortening, metatarsal index, and postoperative load distribution have been examined in relation to transfer metatarsalgia, most of these are either measured postoperatively or based on subjective assessment.9,10,14,15 In contrast, relative second metatarsal length is a quantitative parameter that can be measured preoperatively and has rarely been investigated systematically.18,20 This study demonstrated that relative second metatarsal length was significantly greater in patients who developed transfer metatarsalgia (P < .001). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, it was the only variable that remained statistically significant, showing a stronger association than demographic factors, surgical technique, or implant type. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis confirmed the discriminative power of relative second metatarsal length (AUC = 0.832) and identified an optimal cutoff value of 2.15 mm. To facilitate clinical applicability and account for possible measurement variability, this threshold was rounded to 2 mm, which is recommended as a practical reference value for preoperative risk stratification.

Several studies investigating the impact of surgical technique on the development of transfer metatarsalgia have reported a higher incidence after Scarf osteotomy, potentially due to inadequate restoration of the metatarsal parabola or elevation of the first metatarsal.9,13 In this study, however, there was no statistically significant difference between surgical techniques with respect to transfer metatarsalgia occurrence. This finding may be explained by factors such as the relatively limited sample size, the standardization of surgical procedures, and the surgeons’ technical expertise. Furthermore, the use of a uniform postoperative rehabilitation protocol for all patients may have minimized variability in clinical outcomes between techniques.

Based on these results, relative second metatarsal length should be carefully assessed during preoperative planning. Values exceeding 2 mm were associated with a significantly increased risk of transfer metatarsalgia. Because this measurement can be obtained from routine preoperative radiographs regardless of the osteotomy type, it may serve as an objective and practical screening tool. In patients with relative second metatarsal length greater than 2 mm, limiting excessive lateral translation during osteotomy and verifying restoration of the metatarsal parabola under intraoperative fluoroscopy may help reduce second ray overload. Early postoperative use of orthoses, metatarsal pads, or customized insoles to support the second metatarsal head may also be beneficial. Furthermore, preoperative counseling of high-risk patients can help set realistic expectations regarding postoperative symptoms and improve satisfaction. These strategies aim not to fundamentally alter the surgical technique but to optimize forefoot biomechanics, prevent complications, and improve clinical outcomes. Incorporating relative second metatarsal length measurement into routine preoperative assessment can also serve as a useful counseling tool, allowing surgeons to discuss individualized risk profiles with patients and to plan preventive strategies such as careful intraoperative parabola restoration, orthotic support, and realistic expectation setting.

This study has several limitations. First, it was designed as a retrospective analysis, and thus standardized control over patient follow-up and data recording could not be ensured. The diagnosis of transfer metatarsalgia was based primarily on clinical symptoms and patient-reported complaints, which introduces a degree of subjectivity and increases the risk of diagnostic variability. Objective diagnostic tools such as plantar pressure analysis or pedobarographic evaluation were not used, and postoperative measurements of the relative second metatarsal length were not performed, limiting the ability to evaluate dynamic and postoperative changes in forefoot load distribution. Future prospective studies incorporating both pre- and postoperative measurements of the relative second metatarsal length are warranted to clarify whether surgical restoration or maintenance of the first metatarsal length can mitigate the risk of postoperative transfer metatarsalgia and to identify intraoperative strategies aimed at preventing this complication. Additionally, other potentially relevant radiographic parameters such as metatarsal parabola, Distal Metatarsal Articular Angle, sesamoid position, and postoperative variables including first-ray shortening or elevation and sesamoid reduction were not assessed, which might have provided additional insights into forefoot biomechanics and could act as potential confounders. Although patient records and clinical notes were carefully reviewed to exclude cases with preoperative transfer metatarsalgia, the way symptoms were documented may have varied between physicians, potentially introducing bias. Nevertheless, this study also has notable strengths. All radiographic measurements were performed digitally using the hospital‘s PACS system and were independently conducted by 2 orthopedic surgeons. This approach minimized observer-dependent errors, and interobserver consistency was assessed using the ICC. Furthermore, the use of multivariate statistical analyses to evaluate a quantitative parameter such as relative second metatarsal length, along with ROC curve analysis to determine a cutoff value, enhanced the statistical reliability of the findings. These strengths support the potential of this study to provide preliminary data for future prospective research with larger sample sizes.

In conclusion, this study investigated the association between postoperative transfer metatarsalgia and preoperative relative second metatarsal length in patients undergoing hallux valgus surgery. The results demonstrated that an increased relative second metatarsal length was significantly associated with the development of transfer metatarsalgia. In particular, values exceeding 2 mm may represent a notable risk factor for this complication. Incorporating this measurement into preoperative assessment may allow for early identification of high-risk patients and facilitate more cautious, individualized surgical planning. Future prospective studies using larger sample sizes and objective biomechanical assessment tools are warranted to further validate these findings.
