Global author collaborations in lupus research and publications: a meta-research and network analysis
Authors: Laurent Arnaud, Francois Chasset, Thierry Martin, Guillermo J Pons-Estel, Matteo Piga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal collaboration is a key driver of advances in complex diseases such as lupus, yet its distribution and structure remain insufficiently characterised. This study aimed to analyse global authorship, productivity and collaboration patterns in lupus publications over the last 10 years.MethodsA bibliometric analysis of lupus-related publications indexed in PubMed between April 2016 and 2026 was conducted. Author affiliations were disambiguated and mapped to countries. Productivity indicators (number of authors and publications), population-standardised metrics and collaboration measures, including coauthorship patterns and the International Collaborative Index (ICI), were calculated. Network visualisation was performed using VOSviewer.ResultsA total of 22 813 publications involving 76 536 authors across 142 countries were identified, with a 74% increase in annual output over the study period. China and the USA had the highest numbers of authors and publications, while smaller countries such as Iceland and Greece ranked highest when adjusted per capita. Most publications (83.1%) were single-country studies. The USA was the central hub of international collaboration, with the highest number of links (n=115) and total link strength. The most frequent collaborations involved the USA, particularly with the UK and China. Countries such as Iceland and Uruguay showed high ICI values, whereas China and Japan had lower international collaboration levels. Network analysis revealed a core-periphery structure dominated by North America and Western Europe.ConclusionsLupus research is expanding but remains concentrated within a limited number of countries. International collaboration is uneven, highlighting the need for more inclusive global research partnerships.

Introduction

Lupus erythematosus is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by heterogeneous clinical presentations, variable disease trajectories and significant morbidity and mortality.1 Despite substantial progress in understanding its immunopathogenesis2 and in the development of targeted therapies,3 important gaps remain in disease mechanisms, biomarker validation and the translation of research findings into consistent clinical benefit across populations.4 5 Addressing these challenges requires sustained and coordinated scientific efforts that extend beyond individual research groups and institutions.

Collaboration is a defining feature of contemporary biomedical research and plays a critical role in advancing knowledge in complex diseases such as SLE. At the institutional level, collaboration enables the integration of complementary expertise spanning clinical practice, immunology, epidemiology and computational sciences. At broader scales, interinstitutional and national collaborations support the development of larger cohorts,6 improve methodological robustness7 and enhance the external validity of study findings. In particular, international collaboration is essential in SLE research, where disease heterogeneity and relatively low prevalence in many settings make single-centre studies insufficient to capture the full spectrum of disease characteristics.8

Collaborative structures in scientific research are not uniform and may vary considerably in form and organisation. Coauthorship networks can exhibit highly centralised configurations, in which a limited number of investigators or groups serve as major connectors, or more distributed architectures characterised by widespread partnerships across multiple connecting nodes. Alternatively, fragmented patterns may emerge, reflecting limited interaction between research clusters.9 These structural differences are not merely descriptive; they may influence knowledge flow, research productivity, and the dissemination of scientific findings within the field.

Bibliometric and network analysis methodologies provide a framework to systematically examine these collaborative patterns.10 By mapping coauthorship relationships, these approaches allow for the quantification of network structure and the identification of key actors and geographical regions contributing to the literature.

Although previous studies have described publication trends in SLE, a detailed assessment focusing specifically on international author collaborations and their structural organisation remains limited. The present study therefore aimed to analyse international collaborations among authors publishing in the field of lupus. Using a meta-research approach combined with network analysis techniques, we identified major contributing countries and central actors, and described the typologies of international collaborations within this research field. This analysis provides a framework for understanding how collaborative networks are organised in SLE research and how they may shape the production and dissemination of scientific knowledge.

Methods

This study was conducted as a bibliometric and meta-research analysis aimed at characterising global collaboration patterns in international lupus research.

Study design and data source

Bibliographic records were retrieved from PubMed/MEDLINE, using the export function available on the official PubMed interface. Reference world populations were extracted from the United Nations open-data platform.11

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

The following search strategy was used to maximise specificity for lupus-related publications while minimising retrieval of unrelated entities associated with the Latin term lupus: (“lupus”(title) OR “SLE”(title) OR “CLE”(title)) NOT (“lupus tuberculosis” OR “lupus vulgaris” OR “lupus miliaris” OR “lupus pernio” OR “lupus exedens” OR “canis lupus”). This approach allowed for the inclusion of SLE, cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) and related terminology in the title, while excluding historical dermatological entities and references to the grey wolf (Canis lupus). All publication types indexed in PubMed during the study period were considered eligible.

Data extraction and preprocessing

Data were extracted from PubMed on 24 April 2026, back from 24 April 2016. This 10-year time window was applied to capture recent developments in the field while ensuring sufficient data density for robust network construction. All retrieved records were exported from PubMed with full bibliographic metadata, including author names, institutional affiliations, publication year, and journal information, in PubMed text format.

Affiliations and countries

The country of origin was extracted from the affiliation field using a dedicated parsing computer programme developed for this study by the main author. In most cases, affiliation strings end with either the country name or the corresponding ISO 3166 alpha-3 code, which was used for standardised identification. When affiliation data were incomplete, non-standardised or ambiguous, systematic manual verification was performed. Several common sources of error were identified through targeted screening, including systematic searches for known ambiguous cases. These included, for example, Georgia being misclassified as the country rather than the US state, the Beth Israel Center being incorrectly attributed to Israel, Taiyuan being wrongly assigned to Taiwan despite being located in Shanxi Province, China, and cities such as Córdoba being confused between Spain and Argentina. In addition to these predefined cases, any newly encountered ambiguous locations were systematically screened for and adjudicated across the full database during the data preprocessing. Records for which no reliable country attribution could be made (562 of 161 363, 0.35%) were classified as ‘unknown’.

Author name disambiguation

Author name disambiguation was performed using a custom-built computer programme developed by the main author for this study. The programme was designed as an aid to the manual curation process rather than as a fully automated disambiguation tool. It scanned the bibliographic database to identify likely variants in spelling, initials, name ordering and affiliations, thereby improving the efficiency and consistency of author matching. Potential duplicate records were automatically flagged and subsequently reviewed manually (n=13 256). Following this process, the entire dataset was manually screened line by line to identify any remaining inconsistencies or additional duplicate records. Despite this careful curation, complete disambiguation was not always possible, particularly in cases of full homonyms or when only abbreviated first names were available (see online supplemental table 1 for representative examples).

Network construction

Main coauthorship networks were constructed at the country level. A full fractionalised counting approach was applied, whereby each country appearing in a given article was assigned a proportional credit of the total number of countries involved in that publication. International collaboration was defined as co-occurrence of at least two distinct countries within the same manuscript. The primary analytical focus was placed on intercountry collaboration patterns rather than individual author-level relationships, but author-level network analyses were also performed (see online supplemental document).

Network visualisation and analysis

Network analyses and visualisations were performed using VOSviewer software (V.1.6, Leiden University, The Netherlands). The software applied the association strength normalisation method to account for differences in publication output across countries. Clustering analysis was conducted using the VOS clustering algorithm, which optimises modularity to identify groups of countries with stronger internal collaboration relative to external links. Visual maps were generated to depict the structure of global collaboration networks, represented countries as nodes, with links indicating coauthorship relationships between them. Link strength was determined by the number of shared publications between country pairs.

International collaboration index

The International Collaboration Index (ICI) is a bibliometric indicator designed to capture the extent to which a country’s scientific output is integrated into global research networks. It is defined as the proportion of a country’s publications that include at least one coauthor affiliated with an institution in a different country, relative to the total number of publications attributed to that country over a given period.12 Higher ICI values suggest stronger participation in global knowledge networks, while lower values indicate a more domestically oriented research system.

Other statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise publication and authorship characteristics. Network metrics derived from VOSviewer were used for structural interpretation of collaboration patterns. No formal inferential statistical testing was performed, as the objective of the analysis was descriptive and exploratory in nature. All descriptive analyses were performed using the software JMP13 (SAS Institute, USA). According to French law, no ethical approval was needed, as this research did not involve the direct participation of human subjects. Authors mentioned in the detailed author network analysis (see online supplemental document) gave explicit consent to be mentioned in the graphical representation of the network. Data processing was performed in accordance with the European General Data Protection Regulation.

Results

The PubMed search yielded a total of 22 813 publications for the 10-year study period, increasing from 1701 publications in 2017 to 2967 in 2025 (+74% increase). The initial list of lupus authors was disambiguated to 76 536 individuals across 142 countries. The five countries with the highest number of lupus authors (see table 1 and online supplemental table 2) during the study period were China (n=16 614), USA (n=14 255), Japan (n=4708), India (n=2840) and Italy (n=2704), while the countries with the lowest number of authors were Angola, Chad, Comores, Maldives, Niger and St Vincent & Grenadines (one each). However, when standardised to the 2023 United Nations reference populations, countries with the highest numbers of lupus authors per inhabitant (table 1 and online supplemental table 2) were Grenada (11.1/100 000), Iceland (9.11/100 000), Greece (7.22/100 000), Portugal (6.67/100 000), Denmark (6.57/100 000) and Sweden (6.46/100 000).

Across all lupus publications, countries with the highest number of international collaborations (see online supplemental table 3) were the USA (with 115 other countries), the UK (n=84), India (n=83), Canada, Italy and France (n=79, each) and Mexico (n=78). Considering only the 83 countries having published at least 10 papers during the study period, the highest ICIs were for Grenada and Dominican Republic (ICI=100%, for each), Iceland (96.3%), Cuba (96.0%) and Uruguay (95.2%) while the lowest were for Iran (20.2%), Japan (19.2%), Tunisia (14.7%) and China (12.2). Of note, the ICI for the UK was 66.2% (18th position) and for the USA was 36.8 (64th position).

The network analysis (figure 3 and online supplemental table 3) revealed a highly structured international collaboration network characterised by a core-periphery configuration. The USA emerged as the most central actor in the network, exhibiting the highest total link strength (TLS=4505) and the greatest number of collaborative links (n=115). Other highly influential countries included the UK (TLS=2505), Canada (TLS=1723), Italy (TLS=1573), Spain (TLS=1545) and China (TLS=1493). These countries form the backbone of global scientific collaboration in lupus and are primarily located in North America and Western Europe.

Network analysis of international collaborations in lupus publication. The size of the nodes denote the number of international collaborations while the thickness of the connectors denotes the strength of the publication link between the two connected countries.

Cluster analysis identified several distinct collaboration groups. The largest cluster comprised high-income countries such as the USA, UK, Germany, France, Italy and Canada, representing the core of the global research system. A second cluster was dominated by Latin American countries, including Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Chile and Colombia, indicating strong intraregional collaboration alongside connections to the global core. A third cluster included Asia-Pacific countries such as China, Japan, South Korea and Australia, highlighting the increasing importance of this region in global research output. Additional clusters represented the Middle East (eg, Saudi Arabia, Iran, UAE), South Asia (eg, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh) and African countries (eg, Nigeria and Ghana), which generally exhibited moderate to low TLS values. These regions appear to be more dependent on collaborations with core countries rather than forming equally dense internal networks.

While some countries such as India demonstrated a high number of collaborative links (n=83), their TLS was comparatively lower (TLS=579), suggesting broad but less intensive collaboration patterns. In contrast, countries in the global core exhibited both high connectivity and strong collaboration intensity. At the periphery of the network, several countries showed minimal participation, with very low link counts and TLS values, indicating limited integration into the global research collaboration system.

Finally, analysis of author co-publication network (online supplemental figure, panels A, B & C) confirmed the central role of authors from high-income countries such as the USA, UK, Germany, France, Italy and Canada, while the core of the network was almost exclusively populated by authors from the USA and UK.

Discussion

This bibliometric analysis provides a detailed overview of global research activity and collaboration patterns in lupus over the past decade. Several key findings emerge, highlighting both the expansion of the field and persistent structural imbalances in authorship, productivity, and international collaboration.

First, the steady increase in publications (+74% over the study period) reflects sustained growth in lupus research and publications. However, this growth has not been uniformly distributed. A small number of countries, notably China, the USA, Japan and the UK account for a substantial proportion of both authors and publications, underscoring their central role in shaping the research agenda. However, when adjusting for reference population size, a different picture emerges with smaller countries such as Iceland, Greece and Denmark ranking among the most productive ones. This suggests that research intensity is not dependent on population size but may instead reflect targeted investment in biomedical research, strong academic networks and efficient integration into international collaborations. Of note, ratios derived from countries with small publication or author counts should be interpreted with caution.

The analysis of authorship patterns further highlights the predominance of national research activity, with nearly three-quarters of publications involving authors from a single country. Although international collaboration is widely regarded as a driver of scientific quality and visibility, its overall proportion remains modest in this field. Notably, collaborations involving four or more countries accounted for only a small fraction of total lupus publications, suggesting that large, multinational consortia, common in other areas of biomedical research, are still relatively limited in lupus.

Despite this, certain countries play a disproportionate role in facilitating international collaboration. The USA emerges as the principal hub, with the highest number of collaborative links and by far the greatest TLS. It is also involved in nearly all of the most frequent bilateral collaborations, including those with the UK, China and Canada. This central positioning suggests that the USA functions as a key broker in the global lupus research network, connecting otherwise disparate regions. In contrast, collaborations that do not involve the USA or the UK are comparatively rare, highlighting a degree of dependency on these countries for international connectivity. Another potential explanation for the observed core–periphery structure may relate to historical academic mobility and mentorship networks. In lupus research, many investigators from emerging research systems have historically received fellowship or research training in highly connected countries such as the USA, the UK, France or other major academic hubs. These formative experiences may generate long-standing collaborative relationships that persist after investigators return to their home institutions, contributing to sustained international coauthorship patterns. Thus, global lupus collaboration networks may not solely reflect institutional productivity or funding capacity, but also enduring academic lineage structures shaped by training opportunities, mentorship, and scientific mobility.

The ICI revealed that countries such as Iceland and Uruguay exhibit very high ICI values, indicating that most of their research output is internationally coauthored. This likely reflects limited domestic research capacity, necessitating collaboration. Conversely, large research-producing countries such as China and Japan show relatively low ICI values, suggesting a greater reliance on domestic research networks. The relatively moderate ICI observed for the UK and the lower value for the USA further illustrate that high productivity does not necessarily correlate with high levels of internationalisation.

The network analysis reinforces these observations, revealing a clear core–periphery structure. High-income countries in North America and Western Europe form a densely interconnected core, characterised by both high connectivity and strong collaboration intensity. In contrast, countries in Latin America, Asia the Middle East, and Africa tend to cluster more regionally and exhibit relatively lower TLS, indicating less intensive collaboration. While some of these regions, notably East Asia, are increasingly prominent in terms of output, their integration into the global collaboration network remains uneven. Large collaborative registries and federated networks may also represent part of the solution to some of the disparities observed in global lupus research. Experiences such as GLADEL 2.0 in Latin America, the Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration and LupusNet show that harmonised multinational cohorts and federated data platforms can generate high-quality collaborative research while incorporating populations that are often under-represented in traditional research structures. This is particularly relevant in lupus, where ethnicity, socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access and other sociodemographic factors are known to influence not only disease phenotype and activity, but also access to treatment, disease management and ultimately long-term outcomes and prognosis. These initiatives may therefore help move the field toward a more inclusive and globally representative lupus research model. Finally, analysis of the co-publication network (online supplemental figure, panel A, B & C) confirmed the central role of authors from high-income countries such as the USA, UK, Germany, France, Italy and Canada.

These findings have several implications. The concentration of research activity within a limited number of countries may contribute to disparities in the evidence base, particularly given known geographical and ethnic variations in lupus epidemiology and outcomes. Under-representation of low- and middle-income countries could limit the generalisability of research findings and hinder the development of context-specific interventions. Strengthening international collaboration, particularly involving under-represented regions, should therefore be a priority for the field.

This study has some limitations; bibliometric analyses rely on indexed publications and may underestimate contributions from countries with lower representation in PubMed. Although PubMed provides broad coverage of the biomedical literature, relevant publications indexed exclusively in other databases, such as EMBASE, were not included. Consequently, some authors, institutions or international collaborations may have been missed, which could have slightly influenced the observed collaboration networks. Despite the combined use of algorithmic detection and manual validation, author name disambiguation remained constrained by the occasional use of initials instead of full given names, high-frequency surnames (eg, Gonzalez, Gupta, Huang, Li, Wang), variability and incompleteness of affiliation data and more importantly the typical absence of unique author identifiers (eg, Open Researcher and Contributor ID, ORCID). As a result, only high-confidence matches were retained, and ambiguous cases were conservatively left unresolved to minimise false-positive linkage. Additionally, the use of coauthorship as a proxy for collaboration does not capture informal or non-publishing partnerships. In parallel, future bibliometric studies should also address the integration of regions with substantial amounts of non-indexed or grey scientific literature, particularly in Latin America, Africa and parts of Asia, where relevant clinical and epidemiological knowledge may remain under-represented in conventional databases.

In conclusion, lupus research has expanded substantially over the past decade, but remains characterised by marked geographical inequalities and a highly centralised collaboration network. Efforts to promote more inclusive and balanced international partnerships will be essential to ensure that future research reflects the global burden of disease and supports equitable advances in patient care.
