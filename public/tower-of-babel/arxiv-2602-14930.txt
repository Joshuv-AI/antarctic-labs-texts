A robust and efficient method to calculate electromagnetic modes on a cylindrical step-index nanofibre
Sebastian Golat, Francisco J. Rodríguez-Fortuño

Abstract
The accurate calculation of guided electromagnetic modes in optical nanofibres is critical for applications in nanophotonics, from quantum interfaces to vectorial light sensing. Standard textbook methods rely on solving a 4 × 4 4\times 4 matrix eigenvalue problem to find the modal fields. While widely used, this approach has a subtle but significant flaw: the final determination of the field amplitudes requires finding the numerical null space of a theoretically singular matrix, an ill-conditioned problem that introduces large relative errors in the small but physically crucial longitudinal field components. In this work, we introduce a fundamentally more robust and efficient semi-analytical method. By starting from the foundational symmetries of the cylindrical waveguide and employing a judicious normalisation of the field amplitudes, we demonstrate that the problem can be analytically reduced to a much simpler 2 × 2 2\times 2 system. This reformulation yields two decisive advantages: the dispersion relation is obtained numerically from a simple and well-behaved transcendental equation, and more importantly, the modal field amplitudes are subsequently determined analytically . Our approach completely bypasses the numerical null space calculation, thereby ensuring the accuracy of the full vectorial field structure. This method provides a powerful and reliable tool for the design and analysis of nanofibre-based devices, particularly for applications in chiral quantum optics and nanophotonics where precise knowledge of field polarisation and specifically of the longitudinal components is paramount.



I
Introduction

Nanofibres are ultrathin cylindrical waveguides whose diameters are comparable to, or smaller than, the wavelength of the guided light. Because a substantial fraction of every guided mode resides in the evanescent field outside the glass, nanofibres serve as versatile nanophotonic interfaces. They have enabled optical trapping, propulsion, and rotation of colloids, bacteria, and other microparticles
7
, ultra-sensitive surface and volume biosensing of molecules and quantum dots
9
, and the stable trapping and interrogation of laser-cooled atoms only a few hundred nanometres from the fibre surface—crucial for quantum memories and chiral quantum-optics experiments
12
;
11
. Nanofibre sensors are industry-proven
13
;
8
, known for superior performance, low sample volumes, and strong analyte interactions—driving the fastest-growing segment of the optical-sensor market
5
.

All of these applications require an accurate description of the electromagnetic modes supported by the nanofibre. The conventional approach starts by solving Maxwell’s equations in the core and the cladding and then enforcing continuity of the tangential fields
E_{z},H_{z},E_{\phi},H_{\phi}
at the boundary. Writing the fields (electric and magnetic fields, both in the core and cladding regions) in terms of four constants
(A,B,C,D)
multiplied by Bessel or modified Bessel functions leads to a homogeneous linear system of four equations. This is a standard procedure described in both traditional textbooks and recent research papers
1
;
10
. Setting the determinant of the resulting
4\times 4
matrix to zero yields the dispersion relation for the propagation constant
k_{z}
; a second numerical step then finds the null space to determine
(A,B,C,D)
and hence the modal field distributions and polarisation.

Although it is standard, this two-step procedure suffers from numerical instabilities: the longitudinal field components—responsible for chiral and other vectorial light–matter effects—are much smaller than the transverse fields, so small numerical errors in the null space translate into large relative errors in the quantities of interest. In this work, we realised that, with a convenient normalisation of the field amplitudes, the textbook
4\times 4
system can be shown to be equivalent to:

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1&0\\
0&1&0&-1\\
\frac{\ell n_{\text{e}}}{u^{2}}&\frac{\mathrm{i}{\mu_{1}}}{u}\frac{\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{\prime}}{\operatorname{J}_{\ell}}&\frac{\ell n_{\text{e}}}{w^{2}}&\frac{\mathrm{i}{\mu_{2}}}{w}\frac{\operatorname{K}_{\ell}^{\prime}}{\operatorname{K}_{\ell}}\\
{\frac{{\varepsilon_{1}}}{\mathrm{i}u}\frac{\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{\prime}}{\operatorname{J}_{\ell}}}&\frac{\ell n_{\text{e}}}{u^{2}}&\frac{{\varepsilon_{2}}}{\mathrm{i}w}\frac{\operatorname{K}_{\ell}^{\prime}}{\operatorname{K}_{\ell}}&\frac{\ell n_{\text{e}}}{w^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\!\!\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\\
C\\
D\end{pmatrix}\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(1)

whose first two rows immediately give
A=C
and
B=D
. All the remaining symbols will be thoroughly defined later. The physical problem therefore effectively reduces to a
2\times 2
system that we will concisely derive from first principles. This reformulation, being the main novelty of this work, offers two decisive advantages:

1.

The dispersion relation is obtained by equating the determinant of a
2\times 2
matrix to zero, greatly simplifying numerical root-finding.

2.

Once the propagation constant
k_{z}
and frequency
\omega
are known, the field amplitudes
A
and
B
follow
analytically
from the remaining two equations—thereby eliminating the ill-conditioned null space calculation and preserving the accuracy of the longitudinal components.

The remainder of the paper derives this reduced system from first principles, starting from symmetry arguments alone, benchmarks its accuracy, and illustrates its benefits.

The method described here is implemented in our open-source Python package
Anafibre

II
Cylindrical symmetry and mode expansion

The method to find the field solutions of a nanofibre is well-known, but in order to arrive at a robust, simplified method, it is worth re-deriving the procedure from first principles. We will consider a step-index cylindrical waveguide, described by relative permittivity and permeability that abruptly change at the boundary. If
\rho
is the radial coordinate, then:

\alpha_{\text{r}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{1},&\text{ for }\rho<\rho_{0}\\
\alpha_{2},&\text{ for }\rho>\rho_{0}\end{array}\quad\mbox{where}\quad\alpha_{\text{r}}\in\{\varepsilon_{\text{r}},\mu_{\text{r}}\}\right..

In this work, we restrict attention to monochromatic fields, which ensures time-translational symmetry of the problem, but the generalisation is straightforward. Time translations are transformations generated by the Hamiltonian energy operator
\hat{H}=\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial_{t}
.
This waveguide exhibits cylindrical symmetry characterised by invariance under translations along the fibre axis (
z
-axis) and rotations around it. Translations along the
z
-axis are generated by the linear momentum operator
\hat{p}_{z}=-\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial_{z}
, while rotations about this axis are generated by the angular momentum operator
\hat{J}_{z}=\hat{L}_{z}+\hat{S}_{z}
, with orbital angular momentum
\hat{L}_{z}=-\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial_{\varphi}
. The spin angular momentum operator
\hat{S}_{z}=\mathrm{i}\hbar\,\hat{{\bf\it z}}\times
accounts for rotations of the field itself, provided the field is vectorial rather than scalar. Since both translations and rotations correspond to symmetries of the waveguide, the fields can be most conveniently expressed using a mode expansion into eigenvectors
{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}
(either
{\bf\it E}_{\ell m}
or
{\bf\it H}_{\ell m}
) that simultaneously diagonalise these symmetry operators.
This means that these eigenvectors are separable functions
f(t,\rho,\varphi,z)=T(t)R(\rho)\Phi(\varphi)Z(z)
:

{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}(t,{\bf\it r})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\sum_{s=-1}^{1}F_{\ell m}^{(s)}(\rho)\,\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{s}\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\ell-s)\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}\,,

(2)

where
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{0}=\hat{{\bf\it z}}
and
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\pm 1}=(\hat{{\bf\it x}}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}})/\sqrt{2}
are eigenvectors of the spin operator
\hat{S}_{z}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{s}=\hbar s\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{s}
,
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\ell-s)\varphi}
are eigenvectors of the orbital angular momentum, such that:

\hat{J}_{z}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}={(\hat{L}_{z}+\hat{S}_{z})}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}=\hbar\ell{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}\,,

(3)

where
\ell\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
is the azimuthal order, and
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}
is an eigenvector of the linear momentum:

\hat{p}_{z}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}=-\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial_{z}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}=\hbar(k_{z})_{\ell m}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}\,,

(4)

where the propagation constant
(k_{z})_{\ell m}
has to be determined from the dispersion relation, which for each
\ell
has more than one solution labelled by
m\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
. Finally the
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}
is an eigenvector of energy:

\hat{H}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}=\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial_{t}{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}=\hbar\omega{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}\,.

(5)

The physical fields are then
{\bf\it\mathcal{F}}_{\ell m}(t,{\bf\it r})=\real\quantity[ \vc{F}_{\ell m}(t,\vc{r})]
(either
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{\ell m}
or
{\bf\it\mathcal{H}}_{\ell m}
).
By plugging the ansatz

(6)

where

, this equation is solved by the linear combination of Bessel functions

), but the equations admit arbitrary complex values in

and
\kappa_{2}

\tilde{F}_{\ell m}^{(s)}\;\dfrac{\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{\ell-s}(\kappa_{2}\rho)}{\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\kappa_{2}\rho_{0})}&\text{if}\quad\rho>\rho_{0}\end{cases},

(7)

where
\tilde{F}_{\ell m}^{(s)}
(either
\tilde{E}_{\ell m}^{(s)}
,
\tilde{H}_{\ell m}^{(s)}

Notice that we normalise our radial dependence by the Bessel/Hankel function at the boundary with
s=0

III
Boundary conditions, dispersion relation and mode amplitudes

The boundary conditions require that fields parallel to the interface (in this case, longitudinal and azimuthal) are continuous. The longitudinal components are already continuous thanks to
eq.

8
. The azimuthal components lead to the boundary conditions of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\ell n_{\text{e}}&\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\ell}^{\mu}\\
-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}&\ell n_{\text{e}}\end{pmatrix}\!\!\begin{pmatrix}A_{\ell m}\\
B_{\ell m}\end{pmatrix}\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\!.

(10)

with
u=\kappa_{1}\rho_{0}=k_{0}\rho_{0}(n_{1}^{2}-n_{\mathrm{e}}^{2})^{1/2}
,
w=-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{2}\rho_{0}=-\mathrm{i}k_{0}\rho_{0}(n_{2}^{2}-n_{\mathrm{e}}^{2})^{1/2}
being real (in the lossless case) dimensionless parameters satisfying
V^{2}=u^{2}+w^{2}=k_{0}^{2}\rho_{0}^{2}(n_{1}^{2}-n_{2}^{2})
, with
V
being called the normalised frequency.
We have also defined an analytical function:

\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(n_{\text{e}},V)=\quantity(\frac{uw}{V})^{2}\quantity(\frac{\alpha_1}{u} \frac{\bessel{\ell}^{\prime}(u)}{\bessel{\ell}(u)} + \frac{\alpha_2}{w} \frac{\macdonald{\ell}^{\prime}(w)}{\macdonald{\ell}(w)}),

(11)

which also depends on the core and cladding material parameters
\alpha_{1,2}\in\{\varepsilon_{1,2},\mu_{1,2}\}
.

The dispersion relation comes from the condition under which
eq.

10
has non-trivial solutions, which is precisely when the determinant of the matrix is zero:
2
2

2

For
\ell\neq 0

eq.

12
reduces to
the relationship
\varepsilon_{\text{e}}\mu_{\text{e}}=n_{\text{e}}^{2}\,,

with effective permittivity
\varepsilon_{\text{e}}=\Phi_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}/\ell
and effective permeability
\mu_{\text{e}}=\Phi_{\ell}^{\mu}/\ell
. Interestingly, these effective material properties may be negative.

{\Phi_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}\,\Phi_{\ell}^{\mu}}=({\ell}n_{\text{e}})^{2}\,,

(12)

which is a transcendental equation with more than one solution (labelled by
m\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
) that has to be solved numerically.
This can be done by finding the roots of the function:

F_{\ell}(n_{\text{e}},V)=[{\Phi_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}\,\Phi_{\ell}^{\mu}}-({\ell}n_{\text{e}})^{2}]\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{2}(u),

(13)

which is regular everywhere—the

is added to avoid singularities of
eq.

11

fig.

1

(n_{\text{e}})_{\ell m}(V)
, where for each azimuthal order
\ell
the index
m
labels the roots from high to low value of
n_{\mathrm{e}}
.

For implementation, it is more convenient to define
F_{\ell}(b,V)
as a function of normalised propagation constant
b=(n_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}-n_{2}^{2})/(n_{1}^{2}-n_{2}^{2})\in[0,1]
, which can be understood as quantifying how strongly the mode is confined to the core of the fibre. The effective refractive index is then:

n_{\text{e}}(b)=\sqrt{bn_{1}^{2}+(1-b)n_{2}^{2}}\,,

(14)

while

, and
u=V\sqrt{1-b}
. The advantage is that the search interval is then a fixed range

The amplitudes for a single eigenmode

\begin{pmatrix}A_{\ell m}\\

\nu_{\ell m}\end{pmatrix},

(15)

where
N_{\ell m}
is an arbitrary constant and the only degree of freedom, which we may choose to be such that the field has unit power (see
appendix

),
and the parameter

\nu_{\ell m}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\ell m}^{\varepsilon}}{\ell n_{\text{e}}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\ell n_{\text{e}}}{\Phi_{\ell m}^{\mu}}\,,\!\!\quad\nu_{0m}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad\Phi_{0m}^{\varepsilon}=0\\
\mathrm{i}\infty&\text{if}\quad\Phi_{0m}^{\mu}=0\end{cases},

(16)

which represents the relationship between the longitudinal magnetic and electric fields,
is fixed by the dispersion relation, where the subscript
m
in

emphasises that it is evaluated for a particular solution to
eq.

12

IV
Naming of the modes and degeneracies

In the scalar case where
\ell=0
, the transcendental
eq.

12
can be separated into two distinct equations:

\!\!{\Phi_{0n}^{\mu}}=0=E_{z}\;(\text{TE}_{0n}),\!\quad\text{or}\quad\!\Phi_{0n}^{\varepsilon}=0=H_{z}\;(\text{TM}_{0n})\,,\!\!

and depending on which is satisfied, the mode is either transverse electric or transverse magnetic. The
n\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
is the radial order, which not only labels the roots but is also related to the number of radial nodes of the longitudinal part
n-1
.
Since these modes will have no
\varphi
dependency, the fields have to be symmetric under rotations along
z
. As a consequence, the
{\bf\it E}
(or
{\bf\it H}
) field in the TE (or TM) mode has to be azimuthally polarised, while the
{\bf\it H}
(or
{\bf\it E}
) field will be transverse-spinning (see
appendix

C
).
These modes will be non-degenerate (they will have different
n_{\text{e}}
), unless
\Phi_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{0}^{\mu}
, which would happen only if both the core and cladding are made of a material with
\varepsilon_{\text{r}}=\mu_{\text{r}}
.

When
\ell\neq 0
, the fields vary with
\varphi
and cannot be purely TE or TM. Looking at
eq.

12
, we can notice that the dispersion relation is invariant under the sign change
\ell\mapsto-\ell
. From that, we can expect that the eigenmodes
{\bf\it F}_{\pm|\ell|m}
will be degenerate. These modes have a right(left)-handed quasi-circular polarisation respectively, but since they are degenerate, we can linearly combine them to get any polarisation:

{\bf\it F}^{(\textsc{hem})}_{\ell m}=a_{+|\ell|m}{\bf\it F}_{|\ell|m}+a_{-|\ell|m}{\bf\it F}_{-|\ell|m}\,,

(17)

which spans a Poincaré sphere with Stokes parameters:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=|a_{+}|^{2}+|a_{-}|^{2},

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\imaginary(a_{+}a_{-}^{*})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=2\real(a_{+}a_{-}^{*})\,,

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{S}_{3}

\displaystyle=|a_{+}|^{2}-|a_{-}|^{2}\,,

such that if
\mathcal{S}_{3}=0
we have a quasi-linear polarisation. These combinations are called hybrid modes and should be labelled
\text{HEM}_{\ell m}
according to IEEE standards (formerly IRE)
6
. Notice that if
\varepsilon_{r}=\mu_{r}
for both the core and cladding, TE
0n
and TM
0n
are degenerate,
and it is possible to linearly combine them into HEM
0n
modes
(hybrid because their linear combination will necessarily have
E_{z}
and
H_{z}
nonzero).
In the same dual-symmetric case, one also has
|E_{z}|=\eta_{0}|H_{z}|
for all HEM
ℓm
with
\ell\neq 0
. But outside of such a special scenario, it is common to adopt an alternative naming scheme based on longitudinal components:

\!\!|E_{z}|>\eta_{0}|H_{z}|\;(\text{HE}_{\ell n}),\!\quad\text{or}\quad\!|E_{z}|<\eta_{0}|H_{z}|\;(\text{EH}_{\ell n})\,,\!\!

where the radial order is now given by
n=\frac{m}{2}+\frac{1-(-1)^{m}}{4}
such that the
m
index in
\text{HEM}_{\ell m}
keeps alternating between
\text{HE}_{\ell n}
(
\text{HEM}_{\ell(2n-1)}
) and
\text{EH}_{\ell n}
(
\text{HEM}_{\ell(2n)}
) modes. In general (for example, if
\mu_{\text{r}}\neq 1
), the magnitude of longitudinal components can no longer be used to distinguish between the two mode families. We believe one should look instead at the relative phase between the
E_{z}
and
H_{z}
encoded by
\imaginary(E_{z}H_{z}^{*})(\rho_{0})=\imaginary(AB^{*})=|A||B|\sin(\arg{A}-\arg{B})
:

\imaginary(AB^{*})>0\;(\text{HE}_{\ell n}),\!\quad\text{or}\quad\!\imaginary(AB^{*})<0\;(\text{EH}_{\ell n})\,,\!\!

which is a more general definition of the two families that is directly related to the transverse field patterns
eq.

.

V
Summary of the calculation

Given a cylindrical fibre with a core radius
\rho_{0}
and material relative permittivity and permeability in the core
(\varepsilon_{1},\mu_{1})
and in the cladding
(\varepsilon_{2},\mu_{2})
, with corresponding refractive indices
n_{1}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\mu_{1}}
and
n_{2}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}\mu_{2}}
, we wish to find the dispersion relation of each supported mode. Modal electromagnetic fields have a phase
k_{z}z-\omega t
evolving in space
z
and time
t
in accordance with the dispersion relation that links the propagation constant
k_{z}
and the angular frequency
\omega
. These two variables can be expressed in dimensionless form as the effective index
n_{\text{e}}=k_{z}/k_{0}
, which is further normalised to the unit interval
b\in[0,1]
via
b=(n_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}-n_{2}^{2})/(n_{1}^{2}-n_{2}^{2})
, and the normalised frequency
V=k_{0}\rho_{0}\sqrt{n_{1}^{2}-n_{2}^{2}}
, where
k_{0}=\omega/c_{0}=2\pi/\lambda_{0}
. Once a mode is identified by a specific pair
(b,V)
, equivalently
(k_{z},\omega)
, we also aim to compute the electric and magnetic vector field distributions inside and outside the fibre. The steps below are implemented in the open-source Python package

4
, which reproduces the full workflow from root-finding to field reconstruction.

This section outlines how to implement the proposed method in a simple program in five simple steps:

Step 1
:

Define parameters and functions:

•

Specify the core radius
\rho_{0}
and the core and cladding material parameters
(\varepsilon_{\text{r}},\mu_{\text{r}})
, which can optionally be functions of wavelength (dispersive) and complex-valued (lossy).

•

Implement functions

for

in terms of the normalised propagation constant

using
eq.

11
with
u=V\sqrt{1-b}
,
w=V\sqrt{b}
.

•

is a regular function of
x
, however, evaluating
\operatorname{K}_{\ell}(x)
and its derivative first will lead to a numerical divergence at

.

:

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we have presented a robust and efficient semi-analytical method for calculating the guided electromagnetic modes of cylindrical step-index nanofibres. By exploiting the underlying symmetries of the problem and introducing a convenient normalisation of the field amplitudes, the standard textbook
4\times 4
boundary-matching system was shown to be analytically reducible to an equivalent
2\times 2
formulation. This reduction removes the need for numerical null space calculations and allows the modal amplitudes to be obtained analytically once the dispersion relation is solved.

The resulting dispersion equation is simple, well behaved, and particularly well suited for numerical root finding, while the analytical determination of the full vectorial fields ensures accurate evaluation of the longitudinal components that are crucial for chiral, vectorial, and near-field light–matter interactions. The method is general, applies to arbitrary material parameters (including dispersive and lossy media), and retains full compatibility with standard mode classifications.

Beyond its conceptual clarity, this reformulation provides a practical advantage for numerical implementations and device modelling, offering a reliable foundation for applications in nanophotonics, chiral quantum optics, and fibre-based sensing where precise control and characterisation of guided modes is essential.

Appendix B
Determining spin components from Maxwell’s equations

For the spin basis
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\pm 1}=(\hat{{\bf\it x}}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}})/\sqrt{2}
we can define partial derivatives
\partial_{\pm 1}=(\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\pm 1}^{*}\dotproduct\gradient)=(\partial_{x}\mp\mathrm{i}\partial_{y})/\sqrt{2}
, then the curl can be written in this frame as follows:

\begin{split}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\pm 1}^{*}\dotproduct(\curl{\bf\it F})&=\pm\mathrm{i}\big[\partial_{\pm 1}F^{(0)}-\partial_{z}F^{(\pm 1)}\big]\,.\end{split}

(19)

Maxwell’s equations will be (
{\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}{\bf\it E}
,
{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}}=\sqrt{\mu_{0}}{\bf\it H}
):

\curl{\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}=\mathrm{i}k_{0}\mu_{\text{r}}{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}}\,,\quad\curl{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}}=-\mathrm{i}k_{0}\varepsilon_{\text{r}}{\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}\,,

(20)

and their longitudinal spin components will be:

\begin{split}k_{0}\varepsilon_{\text{r}}{F}^{(\pm 1)}_{\text{e}}&=\mp\big[\partial_{\pm 1}F_{\text{m}}^{(0)}-\partial_{z}F_{\text{m}}^{(\pm 1)}\big]\,,\\
k_{0}\mu_{\text{r}}{F}^{(\pm 1)}_{\text{m}}&=\pm\big[\partial_{\pm 1}F_{\text{e}}^{(0)}-\partial_{z}F_{\text{e}}^{(\pm 1)}\big]\,,\ \end{split}

(21)

and we can combine them such that everything depends only on the scalar components and use
\partial_{z}F=\mathrm{i}k_{z}F
:

\begin{split}(k^{2}-k_{z}^{2}){F}^{(\pm 1)}_{\text{e}}&=\partial_{\pm 1}(\mp k_{0}\mu_{\text{r}}F_{\text{m}}^{(0)}+\mathrm{i}k_{z}F_{\text{e}}^{(0)})\,,\\
(k^{2}-k_{z}^{2}){F}^{(\pm 1)}_{\text{m}}&=\partial_{\pm 1}(\pm k_{0}\varepsilon_{\text{r}}F_{\text{e}}^{(0)}+\mathrm{i}k_{z}F_{\text{m}}^{(0)})\,,\ \end{split}

(22)

where operators
\partial_{\pm 1}
act as ladder operators for so-called spin-weighted cylindrical harmonics (Bessel/Hankel functions with a phase factor):

\begin{split}\partial_{\pm 1}[\operatorname{J}_{\ell}(\kappa\rho)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\ell\varphi}]&=\pm\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{J}_{\ell\mp 1}(\kappa\rho)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\ell\mp 1)\varphi}\,,\\
\partial_{\pm 1}[\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\kappa\rho)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\ell\varphi}]&=\pm\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{\ell\mp 1}(\kappa\rho)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\ell\mp 1)\varphi}\,,\end{split}

(23)

and same for the other functions, which leads to
eq.

9

Appendix C
TE/TM mode fields

While
eq.

2
is universal for every
\ell
, the scalar mode case with
\ell=0
has extra symmetries. Notice that in that case, the only
\phi
dependence is through:

\mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathrm{i}\varphi}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\pm 1}=(\hat{{\bf\it\rho}}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it\varphi}})/\sqrt{2}\,,

(24)

which means that in cylindrical coordinates, the fields have no azimuthal dependence. Not only that, but the TE (TM) mode will have
A=0
(
B=0
) as well as the radial-variation part of the solution:

R_{s}(\rho)=\begin{cases}{\operatorname{J}_{-s}(\kappa_{1}\rho)}/{\operatorname{J}_{0}(\kappa_{1}\rho_{0})}&\text{if}\quad\rho<\rho_{0}\\
{\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{-s}(\kappa_{2}\rho)}/{\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{0}(\kappa_{2}\rho_{0})}&\text{if}\quad\rho>\rho_{0}\end{cases},

(25)

satisfying
R_{1}=-R_{-1}
leading to the following TE fields:

\begin{split}{\bf\it E}^{(\textsc{te})}_{0n}&=\quantity(\frac{\mu_\text{r}R_{1}}{\kappa_\text{r}\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}}\uv{\varphi})|N_{0n}|\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,,\\
{\bf\it H}^{(\textsc{te})}_{0n}&=\quantity(\frac{\ii R_{0}}{\sqrt{\mu_0}}\uv{z}-\frac{n_\text{e}R_{1}}{\kappa_\text{r}\sqrt{\mu_0}}\uv{\rho})|N_{0n}|\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,,\end{split}

(26)

where we assume
B=\mathrm{i}|N_{0n}|
. The TM fields can be obtained similarly or via dual transformation, which is an equivalent of
A=-\mathrm{i}|N_{0n}|
:

\begin{split}{\bf\it H}^{(\textsc{tm})}_{0n}&=\quantity(\frac{\varepsilon_\text{r}R_{1}}{\kappa_\text{r}\sqrt{\mu_0}}\uv{\varphi})|N_{0n}|\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,,\\
{\bf\it E}^{(\textsc{tm})}_{0n}&=\quantity(\frac{n_\text{e} R_{1}}{\kappa_\text{r}\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}}\uv{\rho}-\frac{\ii R_{0}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}}\uv{z})|N_{0n}|\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,.\end{split}

(27)

From this, it is clear that the
{\bf\it E}^{(\textsc{te})}_{0n}
and
{\bf\it H}^{(\textsc{tm})}_{0n}
fields are azimuthally polarised, while the
{\bf\it H}^{(\textsc{te})}_{0n}
and
{\bf\it E}^{(\textsc{tm})}_{0n}
have an elliptical polarisation with the planes of ellipses laying in the

plane, which gives them transverse spin.

Appendix D
Power and normalisation

The following power normalisation derivation works in the case of lossless material. It is sensible to normalise our eigenvectors
{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}
such that the total power flow through the cross-section is unity. This power can be defined via the z-directed time-averaged Poynting vector, integrated over the cross-sectional area:

P=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}[\real({\bf\it E}\crossproduct{\bf\it H}^{*})\dotproduct\hat{{\bf\it z}}]\rho\differential{\rho}\differential{\varphi}\,.

(28)

The integrand depends only on the transverse fields:

\begin{split}\real({\bf\it E}\crossproduct{\bf\it H}^{*})\dotproduct\hat{{\bf\it z}}&=\real[{E}_{x}{H_{y}^{*}}-{E_{y}}{H_{x}^{*}}]\\
&=\imaginary[{E}_{+}{H_{+}^{*}}-{E_{-}}{H_{-}^{*}}]\,,\end{split}

where
F_{\pm}=\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\pm 1}^{*}\dotproduct{\bf\it F}
and we used
F_{x}=(F_{+}+F_{-})/\sqrt{2}
and
F_{y}=\mathrm{i}(F_{+}-F_{-})/\sqrt{2}
. Since the phase factors of
E_{\pm}
and
H_{\pm}
are identical, the product
{E}_{\pm}{H_{\pm}^{*}}={E}^{(\pm 1)}{H}^{(\pm 1)*}
, which are precisely the components that are piecewise defined in
eq.

7
.
After lengthy algebra, the power can be split into a sum of four integrals

\begin{split}\!\!P&={c_{0}{|N_{\ell m}|^{2}}}\sigma_{\ell m},\quad\mbox{with:}\quad\\
\sigma_{\ell m}&=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\pi\rho_{0}^{2}\quantity(\frac{k_zk_0}{{|\kappa_{i}|^2}}\alpha_{\ell m}^+I^{(i)}_++\frac{k_z^2+k_i^2}{{|\kappa_{i}|^2}}\alpha_{\ell m}^-I^{(i)}_-)\,,\!\!\end{split}

(29)

where we have packaged the core radial integrals into

I_{\pm}^{(1)}=\int_{0}^{u}\frac{\operatorname{J}_{\ell-1}^{2}(v)\pm\operatorname{J}_{\ell+1}^{2}(v)}{2\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{2}(u)}\frac{v\differential{v}}{u^{2}}\,,

(30)

and the cladding integrals into

I_{\pm}^{(2)}=\int^{\infty}_{w}\frac{\operatorname{K}_{\ell-1}^{2}(v)\pm\operatorname{K}_{\ell+1}^{2}(v)}{2\operatorname{K}_{\ell}^{2}(w)}\frac{v\differential{v}}{w^{2}}\,,

(31)

and finally, the
\alpha
coefficients are:

\vskip 2.5pt\alpha_{\ell m}^{+}=\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+\mu_{i}|\nu_{\ell m}|^{2}}{1+|\nu_{\ell m}|^{2}}\,,\quad\alpha_{\ell m}^{-}=\frac{\imaginary(\nu_{\ell m}^{*})}{1+|\nu_{\ell m}|^{2}}\,.

Despite their intimidating appearance, these integrals are analytical and do not require numerical integration. The core integrals have the following form:

I_{+}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}-\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{2u^{2}}+\dfrac{\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{\prime}(u)}{u\operatorname{J}_{\ell}(u)}+\dfrac{\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{\prime 2}(u)}{2\operatorname{J}_{\ell}^{2}(u)},\quad I_{-}^{(1)}=\dfrac{\ell}{u^{2}}\,,

where, as in the main text,
u=\kappa_{1}\rho_{0}
.
The cladding counterparts of these expressions can be written in terms of
w=-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{2}\rho_{0}
as:

\begin{split}\!\!I_{+}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}+\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{2w^{2}}-\dfrac{\operatorname{K}_{\ell}^{\prime}(w)}{w\operatorname{K}_{\ell}(w)}-\dfrac{\operatorname{K}_{\ell}^{\prime 2}(w)}{2\operatorname{K}_{\ell}^{2}(w)},\quad\!\!\!I_{-}^{(2)}=-\dfrac{\ell}{w^{2}}\,.\!\!\end{split}

Using these analytical expressions further reduces numerical noise, as we no longer need to perform numerical integration, and in particular, we don’t need to worry about the infinite limit in
eq.

31
.
Finally, if we choose the power carried by the mode to be exactly unity (
1\,\text{W}
), then the normalisation constant has to be:

\begin{split}|N_{\ell m}|=\sqrt{\frac{1\,\text{W}}{c_{0}\,\sigma_{\ell m}}}\,,\end{split}

(32)

where
\sigma_{\ell m}
is an effective cross section defined in
eq.

29
.
We can also further write
|\kappa_{1}|^{2}=u^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}
and
|\kappa_{2}|^{2}=w^{2}/\rho_{0}^{2}
such that we can minimise the number of variables.

Appendix E
Analytical Jacobians of the fields

Several near-field light–matter observables depend not only on the modal fields themselves but also on their spatial derivatives.
An example of such observables is dipolar optical forces, which depend on field gradients (Jacobians)
\gradient\otimes{\bf\it E}
and
\gradient\otimes{\bf\it H}
, as well as quantities such as canonical momenta
2
;
3
.
Analytical expressions for the Jacobians are therefore useful both for physical interpretation and for robust numerical implementation, since they avoid finite-difference noise in regions where the fields vary rapidly (notably near the fibre surface).

Starting from the modal expansion in the spin basis
eq.

2
, and noting that the spin basis vectors
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{s}
are spatially constant (being defined in the Cartesian frame), the gradient acts only on the scalar factor

F_{\ell m}^{(s)}(\rho)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\ell-s)\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}
.
Writing the gradient in cylindrical coordinates
\gradient=\hat{{\bf\it\rho}}\,\partial_{\rho}+\hat{{\bf\it\varphi}}\,\rho^{-1}\partial_{\varphi}+\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,\partial_{z}
one finds the compact outer-product form:

\gradient\otimes{\bf\it F}_{\ell m}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\sum_{s=-1}^{1}(\mathrm{i}{\bf\it k}_{\ell ms})\otimes\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{s}\;F_{\ell m}^{(s)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\ell-s)\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}\,,

where
{\bf\it k}_{\ell ms}
is an effective local wavevector for each spin component,

{\bf\it k}_{\ell ms}=({k_{z}})_{\ell m}\hat{{\bf\it z}}+\frac{\ell-s}{\rho}\hat{{\bf\it\varphi}}-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{1,2}\frac{\operatorname{Z}_{\ell-s}^{\prime}(\rho)}{\operatorname{Z}_{\ell-s}(\rho)}\hat{{\bf\it\rho}}\,.

Here
\operatorname{Z}_{\nu}
denotes the radial cylindrical harmonic used in the given region (e.g.
\operatorname{Z}_{\nu}(\rho)=\operatorname{J}_{\nu}(\kappa_{1}\rho)
in the core and
\operatorname{Z}_{\nu}(\rho)=\operatorname{H}^{(1)}_{\nu}(\kappa_{2}\rho)
in the cladding; equivalently, one may use
\operatorname{K}_{\nu}(-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{2}\rho)
for guided lossless cladding), and
\kappa_{1,2}
is the corresponding radial wavenumber.
