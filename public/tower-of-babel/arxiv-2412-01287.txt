Optimal linear subdivision rules for noisy data
Sergio López-Ureña, Dionisio F. Yáñez

Abstract
Subdivision schemes are iterative processes that recursively refine data by applying subdivision rules. This paper introduces linear subdivision rules tailored to handle noisy data. A key innovation lies in determining the rule coefficients by solving an optimization problem aimed at minimizing the noise variance. The study addresses the general case, allowing for noise correlation among data with a non-uniform distribution. In fact, we show that the subdivision rules, proposed in [S. López-Ureña and D. F. Yáñez, J. Sci. Comput. , 100(1) (2024)], are optimal for uncorrelated noise with non-uniform variance. Numerical experiments are provided to demonstrate the effectiveness of these optimal rules compared to other subdivision rules designed for noisy data.



1
Introduction

Subdivision schemes are widely recognized as a powerful tool for designing functions, including curves and surfaces, due to their simplicity and computational efficiency (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
). These methods have found applications in various fields, such as computer graphics, image processing, and others (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
7
]
).

The process involves iteratively refining an initial dataset by applying a predefined set of subdivision rules, generating a sequence that may converge to a continuous—or even smooth—function. Subdivision theory focuses on analyzing the properties of the limit function derived from the given subdivision rules
[
2
]
.

In certain applications, noisy data are managed, requiring the use of specialized subdivision schemes. To address this, Dyn et al.
[
3
]
proposed a novel scheme based on solving least squares problems. While this approach effectively reduces noise, it exhibits limited capability in approximating smooth functions. To mitigate this limitation, the authors of
[
6
]
introduced a weight function within the least squares formulation, achieving a more balanced trade-off between noise reduction and approximation performance.

The goal of this paper is to derive rules that maximize noise reduction while preserving a specified approximation order. Let
\bm{f}=(f_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
represent a dataset associated with the grid
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathds{R}^{N}
,
x_{i}<x_{i+1}
where
h=\max\{x_{i+1}-x_{i}\ :\ i=1,\ldots,N-1\}
. We assume the data is composed of two components: noise and samples of a smooth function
g:\mathds{R}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}
. Specifically,
\bm{f}=g|_{\bm{x}}+\bm{e}\in\mathds{R}^{N}
, where
g|_{\bm{x}}:=(g(x_{i}))_{i=1}^{N}
and the noise
\bm{e}\sim\bm{E},
being
\bm{E}
a vector of probability distributions with zero mean,
\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{E}\right)=0.
Our objective is to determine coefficients
\bm{a}\in\mathds{R}^{N}
for a linear rule,
\Psi(\bm{f}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}f_{i}
, such that
\Psi(\bm{f})\approx g(t_{0})
for a given
t_{0}\in[x_{1},x_{N}]
, achieving a specified accuracy while minimizing the influence of
\bm{e}
.

We begin by presenting the solution to the proposed problem and establishing its connection to prior research. Then some experiments are performed to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed rules in comparison to other subdivision rules for noisy data.

2
Designing linear rules to minimize noise under prescribed approximation capability

Since
\Psi
is linear,
\Psi(g|_{\bm{x}}+\bm{e})=\Psi(g|_{\bm{x}})+\Psi(\bm{e})
. We propose to split the problem into two parts. One takes care of approximating the smooth function and the other one tries to reduce the noise as much as possible.
On the one hand, we may solve the
smooth problem

\Psi(g|_{\bm{x}})=g(t_{0})+O(h^{d})

that is achieved by imposing to
\Psi
the reproduction of polynomials up to
d-1
degree,
\Pi_{d-1}
, as well known in subdivision theory. For this, we introduce the following definitions.

Definition 1
.

A rule
\Psi:\mathds{R}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}
reproduces
\Pi_{d}
with the setting
(\bm{x},t_{0})
if
\Psi(g|_{\bm{x}})=g(t_{0}),\,\,\forall g\in\Pi_{d}.

Definition 2
.

A rule
\Psi:\mathds{R}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}
has approximation order
d
with the setting
(\bm{x},t_{0})
with
h=\max\{x_{i+1}-x_{i}\ :\ i=1,\ldots,N-1\}
if
\Psi(g|_{\bm{x}})=g(t_{0})+O(h^{d})
is fulfilled for any function
g\in\mathcal{C}^{d}
. That is, if for each
g\in\mathcal{C}^{d}
there exists
C_{g},h_{g}>0
such that
|\Psi(g|_{\bm{x}})-g(t_{0})|\leq C_{g}h^{d}
for all
h\in(0,h_{g})
.

We will omit
(\bm{x},t_{0})
if it is clear by the context. These concepts are related with the next result.

Lemma 3
.

If
\Psi
reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
, then it has approximation order
d
.

On the other hand, for the
noisy problem
, that consists in minimizing the noisy contribution
\Psi(\bm{e})
, we can carry out an easy statistical treatment.

Lemma 4
.

The expectancy and variance of
\Psi(\bm{E})
are

\mathbb{E}\left(\Psi(\bm{E})\right)=0,\qquad\mathbb{V}\left(\Psi(\bm{E})\right)=\|\Omega\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2},

(1)

where
\Omega
is some upper triangular matrix.

Proof.

The probability distribution of
\Psi(\bm{e})
is
\Psi(\bm{E})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}E_{i}=\bm{a}^{T}\bm{E}
, denoting vectors as column matrices. The expectancy and variance are

\mathbb{E}\left(\Psi(\bm{E})\right)=\bm{a}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{E}\right)=0,\qquad\mathbb{V}\left(\Psi(\bm{E})\right)=\bm{a}^{T}\hat{\Omega}\bm{a},

where

\hat{\Omega}
is the covariance
N\times N
matrix of the random distributions
\bm{E}
,

\hat{\Omega}_{i_{1},i_{2}}=\text{Cov}(E_{i_{1}},E_{i_{2}}),\qquad i_{1},i_{2}=1,\ldots,N.

Since
\hat{\Omega}
is a symmetric and positive semidefinite matrix, it admits a Cholesky-like decomposition, say
\hat{\Omega}=\Omega^{T}\Omega
,
being
\Omega
an upper triangular matrix which is regular, provided that the covariance matrix
\hat{\Omega}
is positive definite.
Now we can write

\mathbb{V}\left(\Psi(\bm{E})\right)=\bm{a}^{T}\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}=(\Omega\bm{a})^{T}(\Omega\bm{a})=\|\Omega\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}.

∎

Our aim is to make the noise as small as possible, we propose to reduce the variance of
\Psi(\bm{e})
, or equivalently, to minimize
\|\Omega\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Remark 5
.

From now, we assume that
\Omega
is regular. That is, the covariance matrix of the noise is positive-definite.
Further simplifications can be made, which we do not consider. For instance, if all the variables
\bm{E}
are
uncorrelated
, then
\Omega
is just a diagonal matrix. In addition, if all the variables
\bm{E}
have equal variance, then
\Omega
is a constant diagonal matrix and it could be assumed without loss of generality that it is the identity matrix. These assumptions are very common in practice, as in the numerical experiments of
[
1
,
3
,
6
]
.

Now it is revealed that we may solve the next optimization problem to address both the smooth and noisy problems:

\begin{cases}\min\|\Omega\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}\\
\text{s.t. $\bm{a}\in\mathds{R}^{N}$ such that $\Psi(\bm{f})=\bm{a}^{T}\bm{f}$ reproduces $\Pi_{d-1}$ with the setting $(\bm{x},t_{0})$.}\end{cases}

(2)

Definition 6
.

We say that a linear rule
\Psi
is optimal for a set of random variables
\bm{E}
and for the choice of
t_{0},\bm{x},d
if its
\bm{a}
is a solution of the optimization problem (
2
).

In the following, we compute the optimal rules for any arbitrary choice of
t_{0},d,\bm{x}
and
\bm{E}
, but supposing that the covariance matrix is positive definite, because it guarantees the unicity of the solution of (
2
).

Lemma 7
.

\Psi
reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
if, and only if,
\bm{a}
is a solution of the
d\times N
linear system

\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}x_{i}^{s}=t_{0}^{s},\qquad s=0,1,\ldots,d-1.

(3)

The system can be solved if
N\geq d
, and the solution is unique if
N=d
.

As a consequence, the set of rules able to reproduce
\Pi_{d-1}
is an affine space of dimension
N-d
.

Proof.

Equation (
3
) is obtained from

\Psi(P|_{\bm{x}})=P(t_{0})
for
P\in\{1,x,\ldots,x^{d-1}\}
, which is a basis of
\Pi_{d-1}
.

Since the matrix of the system is a Vandermonde matrix, the system is compatible with
N-d
degrees of freedom. Clearly, the solutions of a linear system form an affine space.
∎

Now we see that this affine space can be easily parameterized. Let us denote
\Pi_{d}|_{\bm{x}}=\{P|_{\bm{x}}\ :\ P\in\Pi_{d}\}
which is a linear subspace of
\mathds{R}^{N}
of dimension
d+1
. Then, we can consider a full-rank linear operator
\nabla_{d}:\mathds{R}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}^{N-d}
projecting any vector in
\mathds{R}^{N}
to the orthogonal complement of
\Pi_{d-1}|_{\bm{x}}
, which is isomorph to
\mathds{R}^{N-d}
. By definition,
\nabla_{d}
is such that

\Pi_{d-1}|_{\bm{x}}=\ker(\nabla_{d}).

(4)

According to
[
5
,
7
]
,
\nabla_{d}
is an
annihilation operator
of
\Pi_{d-1}
. We denote it by
\nabla_{d}
because, for equal-spaced grids
\bm{x}
, the finite difference operator of order
d
can be taken. In that case, its matrix representation is a Toeplitz matrix whose non-zero entries are the combinatorial numbers
(-1)^{i}\binom{d}{i}
,
0\leq i\leq d
.

Proposition 8
.

The affine space of rules reproducing
\Pi_{d-1}
is

\left\{\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta}:\bm{\beta}\in\mathds{R}^{N-d}\right\}.

(5)

where
\bm{a}^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{N}
are the coefficients of some rule reproducing
\Pi_{d-1}
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
7
, this affine space has dimension
N-d
. Since (
5
) is also affine of such dimensionality, we only have to check that one is contained in the other one. In particular, we check that any rule
\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta}
reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
:

\left(\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta}\right)^{T}P|_{\bm{x}}=(\bm{a}^{0})^{T}P|_{\bm{x}}+\beta^{T}\underbrace{\nabla_{d}P|_{\bm{x}}}_{0}=P(t_{0}).

∎

Using the parametrization (
5
), the optimization problem (
2
) can be formulated without constraints as in (
6
).

Theorem 9
.

The problem

\min\left\{\|\Omega(\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta})\|_{2}^{2}:\bm{\beta}\in\mathds{R}^{N-d}\right\}

(6)

has a unique solution,
\bm{\beta}^{*}
.
The optimal rule is

\bm{a}^{*}=\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta}^{*}=(I_{N\times N}-\nabla_{d}^{T}(\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\nabla_{d}^{T})^{-1}\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega})\bm{a}^{0},

being
I_{N\times N}
the identity matrix and
\hat{\Omega}=\Omega^{T}\Omega
.

Proof.

Due to Proposition
8
,
\Psi
reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
if and only if
\Psi
belongs to (
5
). Then, (
2
) is equivalent to (
6
). For solving the last one, the Moore–Penrose inverse can be used, which leads to the solution

\bm{\beta}^{*}=-(\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\nabla_{d}^{T})^{-1}\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{0}.

Observe that the solution exists and is unique since
\nabla_{d}
is full-rank and
\hat{\Omega}
is positive definite (thus, regular).
∎

Remark 10
.

According to the last result,
\bm{a}^{*}
is unique. Thus, its expression is independent of the choice of
\bm{a}^{0}
, but it depends on the noise distribution through
\hat{\Omega}
, the polynomial degree
d
, the grid
\bm{x}
and the evaluation point
t_{0}
.

An interesting property of
\bm{a}^{*}
is that its values are related to the point evaluations of some
\Pi_{d-1}
polynomial, since

\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{*}=(\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}-\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\nabla_{d}^{T}(\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\nabla_{d}^{T})^{-1}\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega})\bm{a}^{0}=(\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}-\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega})\bm{a}^{0}=0.

(7)

Therefore, we can introduce the following result:

Corollary 11
.

There exists
Q\in\Pi_{d-1}
such that
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{*}=Q|_{\bm{x}}
.
If the noise is uniform and uncorrelated, so that
\hat{\Omega}
is a constant diagonal matrix, then it holds true that
\bm{a}^{*}=Q|_{\bm{x}}
.

Proof.

By (
4
) and (
7
),
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{*}\in\ker(\nabla_{d})=\Pi_{d}|_{\bm{x}}
. Then, there exists
Q\in\Pi_{d-1}
such that
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{*}=Q|_{\bm{x}}
.
∎

We saw that if
\bm{a}^{*}
is optimal, then
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{*}\in\ker(\nabla_{d})
. The opposite is also true: if
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}\in\ker(\nabla_{d})
and
\bm{a}
reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
, then
\bm{a}
is optimal. We state this in the next result.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\Psi
be a rule, with coefficients,
\bm{a}
that reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
. It is optimal if, and only if,
\exists Q\in\Pi_{d-1}
such that
\bm{a}=\hat{\Omega}^{-1}Q|_{\bm{x}}
.

Proof.

If it is optimal, Corollary
11
implies that
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}\in\ker(\nabla_{d})
. Let us proof the opposite. Let
\bm{\beta}
be such that
\bm{a}=\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta}
. If
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}\in\ker(\nabla_{d})
, then
\bm{\beta}
fulfils the linear system
\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}(\bm{a}^{0}+\nabla_{d}^{T}\bm{\beta})=0.
The system has a unique solution, since
\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\nabla_{d}^{T}
is regular, as observe in the proof of Theorem
9
. The solution is
\bm{\beta}=-(\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\nabla_{d}^{T})^{-1}\nabla_{d}\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{0}=\bm{\beta}^{*}
. Then,
\bm{a}=\bm{a}^{*}
is optimal.
∎

This result suggests a form of characterization: consider an arbitrary rule that reproduces
\Pi_{d-1}
. If there exists a positive definite matrix
\hat{\Omega}
such that
\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}
corresponds to point evaluations of a polynomial of degree at most, then the rule is optimal for refining noisy data whose noise distribution has
\hat{\Omega}
as covariance matrix. Note that the choice of
\hat{\Omega}
is not unique, implying that a rule
\bm{a}
can be optimal for multiple noise covariance matrices. A simple example is that
\bm{a}
remains optimal for
\lambda\hat{\Omega}
, for any
\lambda\in\mathds{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Finding the optimal rule from Theorem
12
requires solving a
N\times N
linear system, and
N
may be arbitrarily large in practice. An alternative approach is suggested by Theorem
12
: Find the
d
coefficients of some
Q\in\Pi_{d-1}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{\Omega}Q|_{\bm{x}})_{i}x_{i}^{s}=t_{0}^{s},\qquad s=0,1,\ldots,d-1.

This is a
d\times d
system of equations, and
d
is usually small in practice.

According to (
1
), a smaller value of
\|\Omega\bm{a}\|_{2}
corresponds to greater noise reduction. Given the approach adopted in this paper, which focuses on solving a minimization problem, the following statement can be easily proven.

Theorem 13
.

Let
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathds{N}
with
d\leq d^{\prime}
, and let
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}
be two grids of lengths
N,N^{\prime}\in\mathds{N}
such that
\bm{x}^{\prime}\subset\bm{x}
. Suppose
t_{0}\in[x_{1},x_{N}]
, and let
\hat{\Omega}=\Omega^{T}\Omega
represent the covariance matrix associated with the noise on the larger grid
\bm{x}
. Denote
\bm{a}^{d,\bm{x}}
,
\bm{a}^{d^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime}}\in\mathds{R}^{N}
the optimal rules for their respective grids and polynomial degrees, where
\bm{a}^{d^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime}}
is zero-padded at the points
\bm{x}\setminus\bm{x}^{\prime}
.
It follows that the rule with coefficients
\bm{a}^{d,\bm{x}}
achieves superior denoising performance compared to that with coefficients
\bm{a}^{d^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime}}
, satisfying the inequality

\|\Omega\bm{a}^{d,\bm{x}}\|_{2}\leq\|\Omega\bm{a}^{d^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime}}\|_{2}.

(8)

Proof.

Since
\bm{a}^{d,\bm{x}}
solves the minimization problem in (
2
), and
\bm{a}^{d^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime}}
satisfies the constraints defined in (
2
), the inequality in (
8
) follows.
∎

Corollary 14
.

For any rule (with coefficients
\bm{a}
) reproducing
\Pi_{0}
, its denoising capability is lower bounded by
\|\Omega\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}\geq(\mathbf{1}^{T}\hat{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
,
where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in\mathds{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

As a consequence Theorem
13
, the best denoising capability is obtained for
d=0
. By Theorem
12
,
\bm{a}^{0,\bm{x}}=\hat{\Omega}^{-1}(c\mathbf{1})
for some
c\in\mathds{R}
. By the reproduction of
\Pi_{0}
,
\mathbf{1}^{T}\bm{a}^{0,\bm{x}}=1
, which implies that
c=(\mathbf{1}^{T}\hat{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-1}
. Then
\|\Omega\bm{a}^{0,\bm{x}}\|^{2}_{2}=(\bm{a}^{0,\bm{x}})^{T}\hat{\Omega}\bm{a}^{0,\bm{x}}=(\bm{a}^{0,\bm{x}})^{T}(c\mathbf{1})=c
.
∎

2.1
Connection with previous research

Here, we demonstrate that our subdivision rules extend those presented in
[
3
]
and, more broadly, in
[
1
,
6
]
. The subdivision rules in these studies are derived by solving a weighted regression problem using
\Pi_{d-1}
polynomials, which, by construction, reproduce
\Pi_{d-1}
. According to Theorem 3.4 in
[
6
]
, the coefficients of these rules are expressed as
\bm{a}=WQ|{\bm{x}}
, where
Q\in\Pi_{d-1}
,
\bm{x}
represents a centered equispaced grid, and
W
is a positive diagonal matrix. As established in Theorem
12
, these rules are optimal for refining noisy data with noise characterized by the covariance matrix
\hat{\Omega}=W^{-1}
.

The diagonal structure of
W^{-1}
implies that the authors in
[
1
,
3
,
6
]
(unintentionally) focused on subdivision rules optimal for non-uniform, uncorrelated noise. By contrast, the rules we propose here generalize this framework to optimally handle correlated noise.

In
[
6
]
, it is shown that the choice of
W
significantly affects the approximation capability of the subdivision scheme. Specifically, the schemes defined by rules with
t_{0}=0,\frac{1}{2}
were proven to be convergent for
d=0,1
for any positive diagonal matrix
W
, and for
d=2,3
under specific choices of
W
.

3
Numerical experiments

In this section, we demonstrate the advantages of employing optimal rules for refining noisy data. To this end, we conduct three experiments: In the first, we consider uncorrelated noise with varying variances. In the second, we analyze correlated noise with equal variances. The key distinction between these experiments lies in the covariance matrix, denoted as
\hat{\Omega}
. In both cases, we compare the new rules with the optimal rule for uniform uncorrelated noise, characterized by coefficients
a^{0}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}
, which was studied in
[
3
,
6
]
. In the final experiment, we provide a visual comparison of these rules on noisy star-shaped data.

For the first two experiments,
\bm{x}=(-7,\ldots,7,8)\in\mathds{R}^{16}
. We analyze the rules derived for
t=0,1/4,1/2
(commonly used in binary subdivision) and polynomial degrees
d^{\prime}:=d-1=0,1,2,3
. Let
\bm{a}^{t,d,\hat{\Omega}}
denote the optimal rule for given parameters
t,d,\hat{\Omega}
. To evaluate performance, we use the ratio
\rho^{t,d,\hat{\Omega}}:=\|\Omega\bm{a}^{t,d,\hat{\Omega}}\|_{2}^{2}/\|\Omega\bm{a}^{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1
, which quantifies the variance reduction achieved by the optimal rule compared to
\bm{a}^{0}
. The results, presented in Figure
1
, demonstrate that the same conclusions hold across all tested values of
t
and
d
. Specifically, the optimal rule consistently outperforms
\bm{a}^{0}
, and the performance ratio
\rho^{t,d,\hat{\Omega}}
can be arbitrarily small.

First Experiment: Uncorrelated Noise with Varying Variances

Given
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, let
\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}\in\mathds{R}^{16\times 16}
be a diagonal matrix with entries
\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}_{i,i}=\epsilon
for
x_{i}\leq 0
and
\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}_{i,i}=1
otherwise. Here, the noise is uncorrelated (
\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}
is diagonal), and the noise variance is almost zero in the first half of the data. Figure
1
-left illustrates that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\rho^{t,d,\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}}=0
, showing that the optimal rule leverages the data regions with negligible noise to achieve better performance. This highlights the effectiveness of the optimal rule in scenarios where the noise levels vary significantly across the data.

Second Experiment: Correlated Noise with Uniform Variance

For
\epsilon\in(0,0.1)
, let
\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}\in\mathds{R}^{16\times 16}
be a block-diagonal matrix with blocks of the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1+\epsilon&0&0\\
-1+\epsilon&1&-\epsilon&0\\
0&-\epsilon&1&-\epsilon\\
0&0&-\epsilon&1\end{pmatrix}.

In this case, the noise variance equals 1 across all data points, but there are correlations within the grid. As shown in Figure
1
-right,
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\rho^{t,d,\hat{\Omega}^{\epsilon}}=0
. This result demonstrates that the optimal rule effectively exploits noise correlation to enhance denoising performance.

Figure 1:

Graph of
\rho
as a function of
\epsilon
for the first experiment (left) and the second experiment (right). Several values of
d
and
t
are considered. Different line styles are used for
t=0,1/4,1/2
(continuous, dashed, and dotted, respectively) and different colors for
d=0,1,2,3
(blue, red, green, black, respectively).

Third Experiment: Noisy Star-Shaped Curve

Similarly to
[
3
,
6
]
, we sample
F(t)=(4\cos(t)+\cos(4t),4\sin(t)-\sin(4t))
, at
t_{i}=\frac{2\pi i}{320}
,
i=0,\ldots,319
. Noise is added to the two coordinates, with a covariance matrix that is block diagonal. Each block is given by
\frac{1}{2}\hat{\Omega}^{10^{-10}}
of either the first or the second experiment. We consider a moving evaluation point
t_{i}
and stencil
\bm{x}=(t_{i+j})_{j=-7}^{8}
, taking into account the periodicity of the data, to compute and apply the optimal rule (as well as
\bm{a}^{0}
, for comparison) and
d^{\prime}=1
. The results are shown in Figure
2
, where the optimal rule outperforms
\bm{a}^{0}
in both cases.

4
Conclusions and future work

The presented experiments illustrate the effectiveness of linear rules optimized for noisy data with a known covariance matrix in academic experiments. Such rules seem particularly advantageous in scenarios where noise exhibits spatially varying magnitudes or significant correlations. In fact, the performance of an optimal rule can be arbitrarily superior to that of the rule designed for uniform uncorrelated noise.

Further experimentation is convenient to evaluate the performance of optimal rules in practical applications. Future work will focus on studying the subdivision schemes derived from optimal rules and their performance in denoising when combined with multi-resolution analysis.
