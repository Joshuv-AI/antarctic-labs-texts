On thermal transpiration and thermomolecular pressure difference
I-Kun Chen, Kai-Li Wang

Abstract
In this article, we demonstrate the phenomenon of thermal transpiration in a bounded convex domain. We employ the stationary Boltzmann equation with a cutoff potential. For boundary condition, we partition the boundary into diffuse reflection and incoming regions. We establish the existence of solution in a weighted L ∞ L^{\infty} space. Furthermore, we consider a convex domain with diffuse reflection boundary condition in the middle and incoming boundary condition at the two ends. We first consider Maxwellians with the same pressure but different temperatures at the two ends. We prove that the total flux U ⁡ ( x ) U(x) is directed toward the hot end. Furthermore, we derive an estimate for the total flux: U ⁡ ( x ) ≥ C ⁡ ( 1 − 1 T 2 ) . \displaystyle U(x)\geq C\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{2}}}\right). (1) In addition, we show that when the pressures and temperatures on the two ends satisfy the relation P 1 P 2 = T 1 T 2 , \displaystyle\frac{P_{1}}{P_{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{T_{1}}{T_{2}}}, (2) the total flux of the solution is of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 κ ) \mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\kappa}) . This result is consistent with Knudsen’s finding of thermomolecular pressure difference in 1909.

The

The thermal transpiration was first observed by Reynolds in 1879

[
29

. Moreover, when considering two chambers connected by a tube, Knudsen showed that in the limit of infinitely small tube, the pressure between the two chambers satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{P_{1}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}=\frac{P_{2}}{\sqrt{T_{2}}}

(3)

[

[

In kinetic theory, the stationary Boltzmann equation serves as a fundamental mathematical model for describing the behavior of rarefied gas. It reads

\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla_{x}F(x,v)=\frac{1}{\kappa}Q(F,F)(x,v),\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(4)

Here,
\kappa

F
represents the velocity distribution function of the gas molecules. The velocity distribution function is used to describe the number of molecules
\mathrm{d}N
in a six-dimensional phase-space element

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}
. That is,

\mathrm{d}N=F(\mathbf{X},\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

The collision operator
Q

\displaystyle Q(F,G)(x,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{S^{2}}\left[F(v^{\prime})G(u^{\prime})+F(u^{\prime})G(v^{\prime})-F(v)G(u)-F(u)G(v)\right]B(|v-u|,\omega)d\omega du,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v+((u-v)\cdot\omega)\omega\quad\text{and}\quad u^{\prime}=u-((u-v)\cdot\omega)\omega.

(5)

Note that

can also be expressed in the equivalent form

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=\frac{v+u}{2}+\frac{|v-u|}{2}\sigma\quad\text{and}\quad u^{\prime}=\frac{v+u}{2}-\frac{|v-u|}{2}\sigma,

(6)

where
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{2}

Consequently, stationary Boltzmann equation has been extensively applied to describe the gas distribution in the domains involving thermal transpiration. However, in engineering applications, obtaining numerical solutions requires substantial computational efforts, either

Although there are many results on the numerical analysis of thermal transpiration using the linearized Boltzmann equation, there are only a few mathematical results.
In 2007, Chiun-Chuan Chen, I-Kun Chen, Tai-Ping Liu and Yoshio Sone solved this problem for gas between two plates or within an infinite long pipe under diffuse reflection boundary conditions. They showed that when the Knudsen number

&=-\frac{1}{\kappa}Q_{+}(F,F)(x-sv)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\nu_{F}(x-sv)G(s),&&\text{ in }s\in[-\tau_{-}(x,-v),\tau_{-}(x,v)].\\
G(0)&=F(x,v).\end{aligned}\right.

(172)

Note that the solution of (
172
) is unique and
G
has two representations. One of them is

G(s)=g(x,v)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{s}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}Q(F,F)(x-tv)dt.

The other is

G(s)=e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{\kappa}\nu_{F}(x-sv,v)ds}g(x,v)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{s}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\kappa}\nu_{F}(x-sv,v)ds}Q_{+}(F,F)(x-tv)dt.

By uniqueness, the proof is complete.
∎

Now, we consider the following boundary value problem.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} v\cdot\nabla_{x}F&=G,&&\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
F&=F_{in},&&\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega^{-}_{1},\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(173)

We pay attention on the following question

”Does the existence of a mild solution of implies
v\cdot\nabla_{x}F
exists in distribution sense.”

The mild solution of (
173
) is

\displaystyle F(x,v)=F_{in}(q(x,v),v)+\int_{0}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}G(x-sv,v)ds.

Let
\varphi
be a smooth function with compact support. We show the following statement

Theorem 8.2
.

If
F
is a mild solution of (
173
) and
F,G\in L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
for some
p\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(x,v)v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi dxdv=-\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(x,v)\varphi dxdv

for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle F(x+hv,v)-F(x,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau_{-}(x+hv,v)}G(x+hv-sv,v)ds-\int_{0}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}G(x-sv,v)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)+h}G(x-(s-h)v,v)ds-\int_{0}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}G(x-sv,v)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{-h}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}G(x-sv,v)ds-\int_{0}^{\tau_{-}(x,v)}G(x-sv,v)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}G(x+sv,v)ds

for any
h>0
. Let
y=x+hv
. Since
\Omega
is open and
\varphi
has a compact support, we yield

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}G(x+sv,v)ds\varphi dxdv

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{F(x+hv,v)-F(x,v)}{h}\varphi dxdv

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\varphi(x-hv,v)-\varphi(x,v)}{h}F(x,v)dxdv

\displaystyle+\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}(x-hv)-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}(x)}{h}\varphi(x-hv,v)F(x,v)dxdv

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(x,v)v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi dxdv.

Moreover, we have

\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}G(x+sv,v)ds\varphi dxdv=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(x,v)\varphi dxdv.

Hence the proof is complete.
∎
