How 4D TEE Guided Thrombolysis for Mechanical Mitral Valve Obstruction

2D = 2 dimensional; 4D = 4 dimensional; INR = international normalized ratio; LV = left ventricle; MVR = mitral valve replacement; PND = paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography.

Obstructive dysfunction of a mechanical mitral prosthesis is a life-threatening complication that requires prompt diagnosis and immediate treatment selection.1, 2, 3 The central clinical challenge is differentiating thrombus from pannus, because thrombus may respond to intensified anticoagulation or fibrinolysis, whereas obstructive pannus usually requires surgery. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and cine-fluoroscopy are essential first-line tests, but they may not fully characterize the mechanism of obstruction.4, 5, 6 We describe how 4-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography (4D TEE) was used in a stepwise fashion to identify thrombus-predominant obstruction despite coexisting pannus and thereby support successful thrombolytic therapy.

Case Summary

A 24-year-old man with rheumatic heart disease had undergone mechanical mitral valve replacement with a 25-mm St Jude prosthesis 7 years earlier. He presented with progressive exertional dyspnea corresponding to NYHA functional class III, nocturnal dry cough, and recurrent paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea for 15 days. He had previously experienced a stuck prosthetic mitral valve that had responded to streptokinase and had been prescribed warfarin 5 mg once daily.

On admission, his pulse rate was 120 beats/min and regular, blood pressure was 90/60 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation was 91% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed reduced intensity of the prosthetic click at the apex, and bibasilar crepitations suggested pulmonary congestion. Routine laboratory studies were unremarkable except for a markedly subtherapeutic international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.2.

TTE demonstrated severe prosthetic obstruction, with peak and mean transmitral gradients of 43 and 32 mm Hg, respectively, and suggested reduced mobility of 1 prosthetic leaflet (Figures 1A and 1B) (Video 1). Cine-fluoroscopy confirmed restricted single-leaflet motion (Figure 2A) (Video 2). TEE established prosthetic valve obstruction with gradients of 47/37 mm Hg (Figures 3A and 3C) (Video 3), but 4D TEE provided the key mechanistic insight by directly showing a large mobile echogenic thrombus across the prosthetic valve together with pannus arising from the left ventricular (LV) side (Figure 2C) (Video 4). Because the patient was symptomatic, hemodynamically compromised, and had imaging-confirmed thrombus-predominant obstruction, urgent thrombolysis was undertaken with streptokinase 250,000 units over 30 minutes followed by 100,000 units/h for 24 hours under close monitoring for bleeding. Repeat imaging on the following day showed substantial improvement. Peak and mean transmitral gradients fell to 22 and 15 mm Hg at a heart rate of 106 beats/min (Figure 3D). Fluoroscopy (Figure 2B) and repeat TEE demonstrated restoration of bileaflet mobility (Figure 3B) (Video 5, Video 6), and repeat 4D TEE showed a normally functioning mechanical mitral prosthesis with residual nonobstructive pannus on the LV side (Figure 2D) (Video 7). The patient improved clinically, underwent heparin-warfarin overlap until therapeutic anticoagulation was achieved, and was discharged with an INR of 2.7. At 3-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with an INR of 2.9, and TTE showed a well-functioning prosthetic mitral valve with gradients of 15/8 mm Hg at a heart rate of 86 beats/min.

Procedural Steps

Suspect prosthetic valve obstruction early

In any patient with a mechanical mitral prosthesis, worsening dyspnea, pulmonary congestion, diminished prosthetic click intensity, or embolic symptoms—especially in the setting of subtherapeutic anticoagulation—should prompt urgent evaluation for prosthetic valve dysfunction.

Confirm hemodynamic obstruction with TTE

TTE should be the first imaging test to quantify transprosthetic gradients and assess overall prosthetic function (Video 1). In this case, markedly elevated peak and mean gradients established severe obstruction but did not define its mechanism.

Assess leaflet motion with cine-fluoroscopy

Cine-fluoroscopy serves as a useful adjunct for evaluating mechanical mitral valve dysfunction by directly assessing leaflet/disc motion and opening-closing angles (Video 2). Optimal assessment requires acquisition of a true “profile” view, achieved by aligning the x-ray beam perpendicular to the prosthetic valve plane using individualized left anterior oblique (LAO)/right anterior oblique (RAO) with cranial/caudal angulation; commonly, mitral prostheses are best visualized in RAO with cranial tilt or LAO with caudal tilt, whereas aortic prostheses are often optimally profiled in LAO with cranial tilt or RAO with caudal tilt. Diagnostic reliability is highest for bileaflet mechanical prostheses, where both leaflets are radiopaque and allow accurate angle measurement; it is somewhat lower for single tilting-disc valves and limited for ball-and-cage or bioprosthetic valves. However, its inability to differentiate pannus from thrombus remains a key limitation, underscoring the complementary role of computed tomography when available. Here, it confirmed single-leaflet restriction and strengthened the diagnosis of mechanical prosthetic dysfunction.

Use TEE to evaluate the prosthesis directly

TEE provides better structural and functional assessment than TTE when acoustic shadowing limits transthoracic imaging (Video 3). In this patient, TEE confirmed obstructive prosthetic dysfunction.

Use 4D TEE to define the mechanism of obstruction

This was the pivotal step (Video 4). Enface volumetric imaging demonstrated a large mobile thrombus together with coexisting LV-side pannus. The mobility and bulk of the thrombotic component, coupled with the acute presentation and markedly subtherapeutic INR, indicated thrombus-predominant obstruction rather than fixed pannus alone.

Choose therapy according to mechanism and clinical status

Once thrombus-predominant obstruction had been established, thrombolytic therapy was selected because the obstructive lesion appeared potentially reversible and the patient was symptomatic and hemodynamically compromised.

Reassess with multimodality imaging after treatment

Repeat Doppler echocardiography, fluoroscopy, and 4D TEE documented reduced gradients, restoration of bileaflet mobility, and persistence only of nonobstructive pannus (Videos 5 to 7). Posttreatment imaging is essential to confirm therapeutic success and identify any residual fixed tissue.

Reinforce long-term anticoagulation and surveillance

Following acute treatment, heparin bridging and restoration of therapeutic warfarin anticoagulation are critical. Strict INR monitoring and follow-up echocardiography are necessary to minimize recurrence.

Potential Pitfalls

A major pitfall is assuming that elevated prosthetic gradients alone identify the cause of obstruction. Doppler confirms severity, but not lesion type. Similarly, cine-fluoroscopy may show restricted leaflet motion without distinguishing thrombus from pannus. Another common problem is failure to recognize mixed pathology.7 Thrombus and pannus may coexist, and the clinically relevant question is often which component is predominantly responsible for the obstruction. The ECG-gated cardiac computed tomography, which is considered useful for differentiating pannus from thrombus based on attenuation characteristics and morphology, was not performed and may be viewed as a limitation.8 However, the diagnosis was supported by comprehensive multimodality imaging, particularly, 4D TEE prevented misclassification of the lesion as fixed pannus alone and supported a successful nonsurgical strategy.9,10

Imaging-based differentiation is not always equivalent to histopathologic confirmation. Because this patient improved with thrombolysis and did not undergo surgery, histopathologic confirmation of pannus was unavailable. Nevertheless, the residual nonobstructive LV-side tissue seen after thrombolysis strongly supported coexisting pannus. This limitation should be acknowledged whenever imaging drives treatment decisions in prosthetic valve obstruction.

Conclusions

The 4D TEE can provide decisive mechanistic information in mechanical mitral valve obstruction by differentiating thrombus from pannus and identifying mixed pathology. In this case, 4D TEE demonstrated thrombus-predominant obstruction despite coexisting pannus and directly supported the decision to proceed with thrombolytic therapy. Multimodality imaging before and after treatment was central to diagnosis, treatment selection, and confirmation of procedural success.
