Hydrodynamic flutter and free-surface breakup of a retracting elastic sheet
Authors: Cheolgyun Jung, Jian-Tao Zhang, Seyoung Joung, Daegyoum Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe discuss the tension-induced dynamics of an elastic sheet coupled with an interfacial flow. Flutter at the free end of the retracting sheet is initiated by shear-driven instability on the air–water interface, leading to uniformly spaced liquid ligaments. Subsequently, wrinkles induced by inertial buckling instability develop across the sheet. Based on high-speed imaging experiments, a low-order model is established to derive a unified scaling relation for flutter and wrinkle wavelengths in terms of experimental parameters. This work provides fundamental insights into understanding how the flutter and wrinkling of a highly deformable elastic structure, which share a common physical origin, interact with a surrounding flow under high-speed motion.

To ensure the efficient storage and transport of liquid-filled elastic structures such as water balloons and capsules, it is crucial to understand severe deformations that structures may exhibit and their consequences (1–3). A representative and visually striking example of such deformation is the bursting of a water balloon (Fig. 1A). This familiar phenomenon involves the complex physical process initiated by the dynamic fracture of the soft material (4). Specifically, the rapid retraction of the expanded elastic membrane induces shear stress on the water contained by the membrane, resulting in the breakup of the free surface into droplets (5). However, the fluid–structure interaction process of this phenomenon remains elusive due to the complex three-dimensional geometry and dynamic fracture of the bursting balloon. To elucidate the interaction between a retracting elastic structure and a surrounding liquid, we introduce a simplified experimental setup in which an initially stretched elastic sheet floats on a free surface (Fig. 1B and C). When one end of the stretched sheet is released, the sheet becomes unstable, leading to the formation of wrinkles and liquid ligaments.

Previous research on the quasi-static wrinkling of compressed elastic structures has primarily focused on the effects of the elastic modulus and substrate conditions on the controllability of wrinkle patterns, establishing corresponding theoretical frameworks (6–10). For liquid substrates, the effects of capillarity (11–16) and viscosity (17–19), and fluid inertia under rapid boundary retraction (20) on wrinkling dynamics have been addressed under compression from a zero-strain condition. However, the physical mechanisms of wrinkling and the accompanying flow phenomena caused by the unsteady hydrodynamic interaction of a retracting elastic structure with a free surface remain unclear. In this study, we highlight unique features of the fluid–structure interaction for a thin elastic sheet under high-speed retraction. By changing the geometric and initial tensile conditions of the sheet, the coupling between the retracting sheet and the free-surface flow is experimentally and theoretically analyzed.

Experiments were conducted in a water tank with dimensions of 120 cm (length) × 60 cm (width) × 30 cm (water level) (Fig. 1C). A silicone rubber sheet with elastic modulus E=2×106 N m−2, density ρs=1.28×103 kg m−3, and Poisson’s ratio ν=0.49 was placed on the water surface. One end was attached to an acrylic plate using adhesive, and the other end was clamped to an aluminum profile by a gripper. The elastic sheet was uniformly stretched by moving the profile to create an initial tensile condition. Afterward, the gripper was quickly released, allowing the sheet to retract instantaneously. The width w of the sheet was fixed at 3 cm, which ensured that the sheet deformation was dominantly two-dimensional when viewed from the side. The bending rigidity B (=Ewh3/12(1−ν2)), original length L0 before stretching, and initial strain ϵi (=Li/L0−1) of the sheet were varied, where h (= 0.2 to 0.5 mm) and Li are the sheet thickness and initial stretched length, respectively; B=0.05 to 0.82 μN m2, L0=6 to 24 cm, and ϵi=0.33 to 1.45 (Fig. 1B).

The vertical deflection of the elastic sheet is considerably smaller than its entire length. Thus, to obtain sequential deformation profiles, we filmed the laser sheet projected onto the deforming sheet from above using a high-speed camera at 10,000 fps. The curved laser lines on the sheet were extracted from the images, and converted to the actual deformation profiles of the sheet after calibration (see Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for details). In addition, the free-surface flow was captured at 20,000 fps using the high-speed camera.

We first elaborate the overall high-speed retraction process of the elastic sheet. A spatiotemporal diagram of the deformation profiles is exemplified in Fig. 2A. In the diagram, the profiles are stacked in sequence along the y-axis (representing time), enabling both the spatial variation and the temporal evolution of the sheet deformation to be identified. The elastic sheet retracts rapidly on the free surface over the first few milliseconds, with a maximum sheet tip speed of O(101) m s−1. During the early stage of retraction, the tip of the sheet flutters up and down in a regular cycle (red boxes at t=2 and 4 ms in Fig. 2B). Because points closer to the free end retract with a higher horizontal speed, some wrinkles form in the region close to the free end, although the instantaneous length of the sheet is still greater than its original length L0 during this tip flutter stage. The tip flutter can be identified distinctly in the enlarged side-view image (Fig. 2D). By contrast, the tip flutter and wrinkling phenomena are not clear when the retracting sheet is fully immersed into a single fluid, e.g., air (21–24) or water (22).

The rapid retraction of the sheet generates a significant difference in shear stress between the air above and the water below the sheet. The shear-driven instability breaks the planar symmetry of the sheet motion, triggering the initial vertical deflection of the sheet tip. This mechanism is similar to the instability observed in a bursting soap film, where the interaction between the retracting soap film and the surrounding air induces flag-like instability (25, 26). The initial perturbation by shear-stress imbalance is then amplified into the flutter by the interaction between the elastic restoring force of the sheet and the resistive force by the inertial reaction of the surrounding water. During the retraction, the stored elastic energy is continuously converted to sustain the flutter.

During most of the early stage, the underside of the sheet remains mostly in contact with the free surface. The tip of the sheet flutters up and down periodically on the undulating free surface, displacing water below the sheet upward, which results in significant deformation of the free surface (Fig. 3A). Consequently, the water surface elongates and breaks into regularly spaced ligaments, as shown in Fig. 3B and Movie S2. Visualization from the Top view (Fig. 3D and Movie S3) reveals that the formation of discrete ligaments actually involves the sequential ejection of liquid sheets in the direction of the elastic sheet retraction, each spanning in the lateral direction along the z-axis, as illustrated in Fig. 3C. As each of these lateral liquid sheets propagates upward and forward from the free surface, its inertia-driven propagation rapidly stretches the liquid sheet, causing it to become thinner (Fig. 3A, iii, and iv). Concurrently, to minimize its surface area, surface tension drives the formation of rims along the free edges of the liquid sheet. As the sheet propagates and narrows, the rims along the two edges converge toward the longitudinal centerline of the elastic sheet (z=0), leading to their eventual collision (Fig. 3C). This collision of the rims results in subsequent evolution of the liquid sheet into a single long ligament; the long ligament is then divided into multiple droplets (26–28).

To clarify the role of tip flutter in the formation of regularly spaced ligaments, we compare the flow patterns with those produced when a rigid plate pad of length 3 cm and the same width as the sheet is attached to the end of the elastic sheet and released together (Fig. 3E and Movie S4). The rigid plate pad is sufficiently light that the difference in retraction speed is negligible between the cases with and without the pad. In the presence of the pad, the breakup into multiple ligaments does not occur because the pad rapidly slides on the free surface without fluttering. As the pad drags a volume of water in its moving direction, a thin liquid sheet rises above the free surface without any particular ligament patterns. Although the shear condition is the same as the case of the retracting elastic sheet and a significant upward displacement of water is observed, the breakup into multiple ligaments regularly spaced does not occur. This suggests that while the shear instability initiates the vertical motion of the free surface, the specific periodic pattern of the ligaments is intrinsically coupled to the dynamics of the elastic sheet itself. Therefore, we propose a mechanism wherein the shear-driven instability is amplified by the subsequent hydrodynamic flutter of the sheet tip. Admittedly, numerous works about wrinkle formation of elastic sheets on liquid substrates (10, 17, 29–32) or solid materials over a wide range of the elastic modulus (10, 33) have been reported. However, the tip flutter and the dramatic deformation of the free surface are distinct phenomena observed for an elastic sheet retracting on a free surface.

After the tip flutter stage, once the instantaneous length of the retracting sheet becomes smaller than its original length L0, the sheet undergoes contraction along the horizontal direction. The formation of wrinkles initiates near the fixed end of the sheet (light blue box at 5 ms in Fig. 2C) and then merge with those propagating from the free end, causing wrinkles to appear near the center of the sheet. The initiation of wrinkling from the fixed end is analogous to the behavior of retracting rubber bands, which is caused by the compressive wave front that rebounds from the fixed end of the rubber band once the initial tension is fully released (22). Eventually, the wrinkle wavelengths become almost uniform across the entire area of the sheet for a short time during the wrinkle development stage (purple box at t=7 ms in Fig. 2C). This rapid formation of the short wavelength wrinkling pattern can be interpreted as inertial buckling instability, which causes the sheet to form multiple short-wavelength wrinkles. Under rapid compression, the inertia of the structure resists the global, long-wavelength deformation of quasi-static Euler buckling and leads to the rapid growth of short-wavelength patterns (34, 35). Thereafter, wrinkle coarsening occurs as individual wrinkles merge, eventually leading to large-amplitude folding near the fixed end.

The flutter wavelength λf is employed to represent the dynamic behaviors of the elastic sheet during the tip flutter stage (Fig. 2B). The tip of the sheet has a wave-like trajectory during this stage. The flutter wavelength λf is determined by averaging several distances between successive peaks in the trajectory of the tip. As the bending rigidity B of the sheet increases and its resistance to bending becomes greater, fewer flutters occur as the sheet retracts to a given length, thereby increasing λf (Fig. 4A). For a greater original sheet length L0, λf increases for a given initial tensile strain ϵi, which aligns with the general fact that the wavelengths of harmonic modes are proportional to the sheet length (Fig. 4B). This suggests that, as the length of the deformed region at the sheet tip increases, the tip carries more fluid and travels farther during each flutter cycle, thereby further increasing λf. By contrast, an increase in ϵi for a given L0 yields a smaller λf. Rapid retraction under a stronger initial tension causes tip flutter to occur more frequently during the same period, which leads to a reduction in λf (Fig. 4C).

The characteristics of the motion of an extensible elastic sheet can be captured by an unsteady force-balance equation (Fig. 4D), based on the work of Luhar and Nepf (36):[1]−T+iV+e−iθ∫sL(ff+ifdH(−θ)+ifa)eiθ−ifsds∗=e−iθ∫sLfids∗.

Here, s is a curvilinear coordinate along the sheet from its fixed end, and θ(s,t) denotes the deflection angle from the x-axis, as shown in Fig. 4D. The position of the sheet in Cartesian coordinates is represented as z=∫0seiθ(s∗,t)ds∗=x+iy. The internal bending shear force V and tensile force T of the sheet are defined as V=B∂2θ/∂s2 and T=Ewhϵi. The fluid substrate force per unit length in the downward direction is fs=ρfgwy, where ρf (= 998 kg m−3) is the density of water, g is the gravitational acceleration, and y is the vertical displacement. The inertial force fi of the sheet per unit length is ρswhz¨.

Simplified forms of the flow-induced forces are adopted from previous studies (36–38). The flow-induced forces per unit length are composed of the quasi-steady drag force fd=12ρfCdwIm(z˙e−iθ)Im(z˙e−iθ) in the normal direction, skin-friction force ff=12ρfCfwRe(z˙e−iθ)Re(z˙e−iθ) in the tangential direction, and added-mass force fa=−π4ρfCaw2cos(2θ)Im(z¨e−iθ) in the normal direction, which accounts for the inclination of a flat plate (39). When θ>0, the advancing side of the sheet is in air and the receding side is in water, so the drag force is approximated as zero. Conversely, when θ<0, the conditions are reversed, producing a nonzero drag force. Here, Cd, Cf, and Ca are empirically set to 1.0, 0.001, and 1.0, respectively; see SI Appendix for the details and validity of the low-order model.

A scaling analysis based on Eq. 1 is conducted to reveal the parametric relations of wavelengths. In addition to the tip flutter wavelength λf during the tip flutter stage, the wavelength λw of the wrinkles that form almost uniformly over the entire sheet is considered during the wrinkle development stage (purple box in Fig. 2C). The value of λw is obtained by averaging the wrinkle wavelengths at the instant when the wrinkles begin to form almost uniformly over the entire sheet (at xtip/L0≈0.95 where xtip is the x-position of the free end). For a deforming sheet, λ (either λf or λw) is considered as a characteristic length determining the oscillatory behaviors, and thus spatial derivatives with respect to the curvilinear coordinate scale as ∂/∂s∼1/λ and ∂2/∂s2∼1/λ2.

To build a predictive scaling model from the force balance in Eq. 1, the dominant forces that govern the fluttering motion should be identified. As mentioned above, the fluttering motion is strongly resisted by the inertial reaction of the surrounding water. This inertial reaction is physically described by the added-mass force, which represents the inertial reaction force exerted by the surrounding fluid on an accelerating body. As the sheet tip flutters, it undergoes rapid vertical acceleration and deceleration, transferring kinetic energy to the surrounding water. In such a highly unsteady circumstance, this acceleration-dependent force is expected to be the dominant component of the hydrodynamic load. Our experimental results confirm this dominance quantitatively. We compared the magnitudes of the three flow-induced forces per unit length (ff, fd, and fa). Generally, ff and fd are much smaller than fa. For example, the magnitudes of these forces are O(10−3), O(10−1), and O(100) N m−1, respectively, for the time-averaged value at the free end of the sheet under B=0.82 μN m2, L0=12 cm, and ϵi=1.0. The transverse (y-directional) deflection of the retracting sheet is mainly driven by the added-mass force applied in the normal direction to the sheet.

Two distinct motions, retraction in the tangential direction and tip flutter/wrinkling in the normal direction, are dominated by different forces. For the retraction motion, the tensile force is the driving force, inducing the inertial force in the tangential direction. The tensile force per unit length, expressed as ∂(Ewhϵ)/∂s, scales as Ewhϵi/λ. The inertial force per unit length, ρswhz¨, scales as ρswhLi/τ2, where τ is the characteristic time scale of the retraction. Balancing these forces, we obtain τ∼(ρsLiλ/Eϵi)1/2. Regarding the tip flutter/wrinkling motion, the added-mass force is dominant and should be balanced by the bending force to sustain the motion in the normal direction. The bending force per unit length, ∂(B∂2θ/∂s2)/∂s, scales as Bθ/λ3, while the added-mass force per unit length, ρfCaw2z¨, scales as ρfCaw2λθ/τ2; because the vertical deflections are small, we assume that z∼λθ. Balancing these forces gives λ4∼Bτ2/ρfCaw2. From the substitution of τ∼(ρsLiλ/Eϵi)1/2, λ3∼BρsLi/ρfCaw2Eϵi. A scaling relation for the characteristic wavelength is then derived as[2]λ∼ρsh3L0ρfCaw1+1ϵi1/3.

Relation [2] successfully characterizes the trends in λf and λw in terms of various experimental parameters, as depicted in Fig. 4E. Both λf and λw are proportional to the cubic root of the product of h3 and L0, and inversely related to the cubic root of ϵi. The collapse of both datasets onto a single scaling relation indicates that the tip flutter and wrinkling, which might have been considered as independent phenomena, are actually correlated through a single fluid–structure interaction process. The tip flutter wavelength λf is consistently larger than the wrinkle wavelength λw, with their ratio clustering around an average value of approximately 1.7 over a wide range of experimental parameters (Inset of Fig. 4E). Although more comprehensive analysis is required, one plausible explanation for this consistent ratio is that both the tip flutter and wrinkling originate from the same process that exhibits strongly linear behaviors because the transverse deformation of the sheet is much smaller than its length. The wavelength λf results from the tip flutter that occurs during the early stage of retraction, while the wavelength λw comes from the wrinkling caused by compression after the sheet has retracted to less than its original length L0. The difference in magnitude between λf and λw can be attributed to distinct environmental conditions. While the flutter at the free end of the sheet is constrained only by its own elasticity and the resistance from the surrounding fluid, the inertial wrinkling is strongly constrained by the fixed end and subjected to in-plane compression, which suppresses large deformation and yields the patterns of shorter wavelength as discussed earlier; that is, different boundary conditions determine the resulting deformation mode and wavelength magnitude.

In Fig. 4E, there are slight deviations from the linear scaling for the data points corresponding to high initial strain (ϵi>1.0), which are located within the central region of the collapsed data near ρsh3L0(1+1/ϵi)/ρfCaw1/3=0.1 cm. For such large initial strain, the silicone rubber sheet exhibits hyperelastic response and stress softening (i.e., the Mullins effect) (40, 41), which are not accounted for by the linear elastic framework used to derive the scaling relation [2]. While a more complex constitutive model is required to capture this nonlinear regime, the present scaling analysis successfully identifies the dominant physical balance, as validated by the strong agreement across the most range of our experimental parameters. The analysis of the nonlinear effects could be a subject for future investigation.

In Fig. 5A, the multiple tip flutter wavelengths λf,1–λf,3 generated before the sheet reaches its original length are similar to the corresponding distances d1–d3 between the ligaments formed by the breakup of the free surface prior to this instant. Although both λf,i and di gradually increase as the sheet retracts (i.e., as i increases), the variations among the three values are minor. The similarity between λf and d averaged over multiple values persists for other experimental parameters (Fig. 5B). This linear relationship clearly indicates that the formation of ligaments can be attributed to the tip flutter of the sheet and that their trajectories in air are determined by the geometric and initial tensile conditions of the sheet. For example, as ϵi increases, the fast retraction and flutter of the sheet cause the ligaments to protrude faster and farther from the free surface (Fig. 5C). The elastic sheet stores the elastic energy corresponding to its initial deformation. As the sheet retracts, this stored strain energy is converted not only into the kinetic and strain energy of the sheet but also transferred to the fluid, significantly affecting the kinematics of free-surface flow.

We have investigated the complex fluid–structure interaction involved in the retraction of an elastic sheet on a free surface. Along with the strong shear stress exerted on the free surface by the rapid retraction, the hydrodynamic flutter of the tip, a characteristic phenomenon in this study, directly affects the breakup of the free surface into regularly spaced ligaments. By deriving a governing equation from the unsteady force balance and conducting a scaling analysis based on the dominant forces in the normal and tangential directions of the sheet, we established parametric relationships for the characteristic wavelengths, which consistently depend on the same geometric and material parameters. This fundamental study offers physical insights into the instability mechanisms that are inherent in the coupling of a deforming solid and an interfacial flow under highly transient inertia-driven motion.

Materials and Methods

Tracking of Sheet Deformation Profiles.

To track the sequential deformation profiles of the elastic sheet, the centerline of the sheet was illuminated by a continuous laser (10 W, MGL-W-532A, CNI Co., Ltd.) and captured using a high-speed camera (FASTCAM SA-Z, Photron, Inc.) from above at 10,000 fps, following the method of Andreotti et al. (42) (Fig. 1B). The laser sheet parallel to the free surface was reflected by a mirror and projected onto the sheet at a certain angle (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Thus, the vertical deflection of the retracting sheet induced a curved laser line on the sheet (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). The amplitude of the curved laser line was amplified by adjusting the tilt angle of a mirror. Although the actual vertical deflection of the sheet was much smaller than its length, the resolution required for quantitative measurements of the sheet deflection could be easily achieved by the amplified curve of the laser line. The profiles of the curved laser line were extracted from the images using a MATLAB code (The Mathworks, Inc.). The finite-thickness profile was converted to a single line following the procedure depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S1C.
