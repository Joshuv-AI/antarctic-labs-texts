The “up-or-out” system and early-career faculty mental health: a three-year longitudinal study of the paradox of research performance
Authors: Feilong Wu, Qun Yang, Juan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe “up-or-out” system is designed to determine faculty retention based on research performance; however, its potential impact on the mental health of early-career faculty remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of the “up-or-out” system on the mental health of early-career faculty and to explore the underlying mechanisms.MethodsA three-year longitudinal study was conducted among faculty members from 39 universities who had received job offers but had not yet signed employment contracts. Generalized estimating equation models, mediation analysis, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and attrition analysis were used to examine the effects of research performance indicators (frequency of project applications, funding level, and number of published papers) on the primary outcome (PHQ-9) and secondary outcomes (GAD-7 and PSS-10).ResultsA total of 2,632 early-career faculty were included in the analysis. The study found significant interaction effects of research performance on both primary and secondary outcomes (P < 0.05). For the primary outcome, faculty who published an average of ≥ 3 papers per year had a significantly higher depression score (MD [95% CI] = 5.67 [4.14 to 5.98]) than those who published an average of ≤ 2 papers per year (MD [95% CI] = 4.41 [3.06 to 4.86]). Similarly, faculty with ≥ 3 grant applications showed a significantly higher level of depression (MD [95% CI] = 6.06 [5.46, 6.69]) than those with < 3 applications (MD [95% CI] = 3.32 [2.95 to 4.02]). Compared with faculty who received projects lower level, those who obtained national-level funding exhibited a significant reduction in depression scores (MD [95% CI] = − 2.31 [–3.18 to − 1.44]), whereas provincial-level funding was associated with a non-significant change (P > 0.05). Regarding secondary outcomes, stress and anxiety scores increased as the periodic evaluation approached (P < 0.05). Obtaining project funding and publishing more papers exerted a buffering effect on stress (P < 0.05), whereas a higher frequency of grant applications increased the risk of anxiety (P < 0.05). Mediation analyses showed that the frequency of grant applications had a significant positive indirect effect on depression and anxiety (P < 0.05), while project funding level (for stress and anxiety) and number of published papers (for stress) exhibited significant negative indirect effects (P < 0.05). Gender differences showed a convergent trend over time (P > 0.05). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings, and attrition analysis indicated no selective bias, and Harman’s single-factor test did not reveal serious common method bias.ConclusionThe up-or-out system is associated with the high-output–high-depression paradox among early-career faculty. Therefore, optimizing the evaluation system could focus on balancing research performance and mental health, for example, by extending the evaluation cycle, increasing funding coverage, and establishing psychological support mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28756-5.Keywords: Up-or-out, Contract assessment system, Stress, Anxiety, Depression

Introduction

In the context of the global knowledge economy of the 21st century, scientific research and innovation capacity have become cornerstones of national competitiveness [1, 2]. To incentivize research productivity, top Chinese universities have widely adopted the “up-or-out” system (known as the tenure, tenure-track, or tenure model in Western contexts), drawing on western models [3]. This system employs a structured probationary period for talent selection and, in theory, aims to establish a career development pathway oriented toward academic excellence [4]. However, a growing body of research indicates that early-career university faculty, in particular, experience significantly higher levels of occupational stress and anxiety than the general population [5, 6]. Preliminary evidence suggests that this trend is especially pronounced among young Chinese faculty working under the “up-or-out” system [7]. The underlying roots lie in the logic of the system itself: uncertainty about career prospects fuels persistent anxiety [8]; performance-based quantitative assessment transforms academic exploration into a “publish or perish” survival contest, eroding intrinsic academic motivation [9]; and a highly competitive, “siloed” academic environment dismantles peer support networks, leaving individuals isolated under accumulating pressure [10]. This raises a fundamental question: does the system enhance performance through positive incentives, or does it drive productivity by generating stress and anxiety? The precise magnitude of its psychological costs, as well as the underlying mechanisms, remains largely unexplored.

To systematically explain how the above institutional features affect mental health, this study draws on three classical theoretical frameworks. From cognitive appraisal theory, career uncertainty under the “up-or-out” system may induce chronic hypervigilance toward uncontrollable threats, continuously depleting psychological resources [11]. From self-determination theory, research performance metrics (grant applications, grant level, and core papers) may externalize academic exploration, eroding autonomy and intrinsic motivation and undermining the sense of meaning in academic work [12]. From conservation of resources theory, a highly competitive academic environment dismantles peer support networks, leaving individuals lacking necessary resources when stress accumulates, thereby accelerating resource depletion [13]. These three theories collectively illuminate the pathways of institutional mental health erosion—threat appraisal, motivational alienation, and resource loss—and converge on a core outcome: depression. Unlike stress and anxiety, depression is not merely an accumulation of short-term stress but a deeper manifestation of lost meaning and prolonged frustration of autonomy [14]. Notably, in addressing this question, some researchers have turned their attention to research performance—the core vehicle through which the system operates [15]. During the probationary period, the number of grant applications, grant level, and number of published papers serve not only as key indicators for evaluating academic output but also as potential critical variables influencing faculty mental health. However, existing studies largely rely on cross-sectional surveys or qualitative case analyses [16]. Although such approaches can provide static snapshots, they are ill-equipped to reveal the trajectory of how institutional pressure dynamically affects mental health over time, let alone clarify the true role of research performance in this process. Disentangling this complex mechanism therefore calls for longitudinal data to provide robust empirical support.

Therefore, the present study conducted a three-year longitudinal study of faculty members from 39 universities who had received job offers but had not yet signed employment contracts, using depression as the primary outcome and work stress and anxiety as secondary outcomes. Generalized estimating equation models, mediation analysis, and subgroup analysis were employed to systematically test the following hypotheses:

This study is the first to systematically reveal, using longitudinal data, the complex mechanisms through which the “up-or-out” evaluation system affects mental health. It provides key empirical evidence on the hidden psychological costs of this system and offers a scientific basis for optimizing faculty evaluation and management systems in higher education institutions.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling procedure

According to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenyang Sport University (Approval No.: SYSU20220330, March 30, 2022). Informed consent was obtained from all participants after detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and their voluntary involvement. All data were collected and presented ensuring confidentiality and anonymity of the participants. Throughout the recruitment and interview stages, participants were clearly advised that their participation was voluntary, all provided information would remain confidential, and that they retained the right to withdraw from the study at any time.

This study employed a three-year longitudinal design, collecting survey data from the same cohort of early-career scholars over three consecutive academic years, from the start of their employment in September 2022 through the end of the 2025 academic year. The timing of data collection was carefully chosen based on the assessment cycle of the “up-or-out” system, aiming to capture dynamic changes in the mental health status of scholars at different stages—from initial employment to the mid-probationary evaluation. A cluster sampling method was adopted. Based on the university list published by the Ministry of Education and the geographical distribution of higher education institutions across provinces, 39 universities that explicitly implement the “up-or-out” system were selected as sampling units. Paper-based questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site. Prior to each survey wave, researchers explained the study objectives, methods, and privacy protection procedures to the university human resources departments and the participating scholars. Participants were then organized through internal university channels to complete the questionnaires collectively. All completed questionnaires were sealed and collected on-site by the researchers immediately after completion.

Questionnaire collection

The baseline survey was conducted from September 2 to 10, 2022, immediately before the faculty members formally signed the “up-or-out” employment contract, to assess their initial mental health status prior to the probationary period. The first follow-up survey (T1) took place at the end of the first academic year (September 2023). A total of 3,200 questionnaires were distributed, and 2,945 were returned. After screening, 99 questionnaires were excluded due to regular responding patterns or excessive missing data, yielding 2,846 valid questionnaires, with an effective response rate of 88.9%. The second follow-up survey (T2) was conducted from September 2023 to September 2024. Of the 2,846 participants followed up, 2,782 questionnaires were returned. After excluding 67 invalid questionnaires, 2,715 valid questionnaires were obtained, resulting in a follow-up success rate of 95.4%. The third follow-up survey (T3) was conducted from September 2024 to September 2025. Of the 2,715 participants followed up, 2,695 questionnaires were returned. After excluding 63 invalid questionnaires, 2,632 valid questionnaires were obtained, yielding a follow-up success rate of 96.9%. Ultimately, 2,632 participants provided complete data across all three waves, with an overall follow-up success rate of 92.5% (2,632/2,846).

Inclusion criteria

Participants were included if they met the following criteria: ① full-time faculty members at a university who were currently within the “up-or-out” probationary period; ② newly recruited faculty members in September 2022; and ③ voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and provided written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: ① had a history of severe mental illness (diagnosed according to DSM-5 criteria) [17] or were undergoing psychiatric treatment at the time of the study; ② were on long-term sick leave, maternity leave, or overseas academic visits lasting more than three months during the survey period; ③ exhibited regular response patterns on the questionnaire (e.g., selecting the same response option for all items, such as all “0” or all “3” on a 4-point Likert scale, or following a simple cyclical pattern) or excessive missing data (> 20% of items missing), and according to previous research recommendations, such patterns were considered invalid because they indicate non-engagement or careless responding [18, 19]; ④ answered a lie-detection question incorrectly in the survey.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) developed by Kroenke et al. (2001) [20], the validated Chinese version of which was used in this study [21]. The PHQ-9 consists of nine items that measure the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced over the past two weeks. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). The total score ranges from 0 to 27. Scores of 0–4 represent no depression, 5–9 mild depression, 10–14 moderate depression, 15–19 moderately severe depression, and ≥ 20 severe depression, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Study variables and measurement

All research performance variables (frequency of grant applications, funding level, and number of published papers) were measured prospectively at the end of each academic year as the cumulative values for that academic year. Participants reported for each academic year: (1) the number of grant applications submitted as principal investigator; (2) the level of grant obtained; and (3) the number of first-author core journal articles published.

Frequency of project applications

This variable captured the total number of research grant applications submitted by each participant as the principal investigator. To enhance the robustness of the analysis, it was dichotomized based on the sample median into two groups: the low-frequency group (≤ 3 applications) and the high-frequency group (> 3 applications). While dichotomization results in some loss of statistical precision, this categorization aligns with common understandings in academic personnel management. To examine robustness, sensitivity analyses using the raw application counts were also performed (Supplementary Material).

Funding level

Based on the administrative level of the funding agency that approved the research grant, grants were classified into five categories: 1 = no grant; 2 = institutional level (university-funded); 3 = municipal level (including prefecture/city level); 4 = provincial level (including provincial, ministerial, and equivalent agencies); 5 = national level (e.g., National Natural Science Foundation of China, National Social Science Fund of China). This classification follows the research grant management system in China and ensures clear hierarchical distinctions among categories.

Number of published papers

Participants reported the number of papers for which they were the first author and that were published in core journals (e.g., SCI, SSCI) during the probationary period. To enhance model robustness and in light of the common requirement of ≤ 2 core papers stipulated by “up-or-out” evaluation notices in Chinese universities, this continuous variable was dichotomized as follows: low productivity: ≤2 core papers (coded as 1); high productivity: ≥3 core papers (coded as 2). This dichotomization was based on the fact that many universities’ “up-or-out” evaluation guidelines include clear, tiered requirements for the number of core journal papers. For example, publishing two papers per year (with an impact factor > 3.0 and in CAS Region 2) is often regarded as an important threshold or “passing” line. While dichotomization results in some loss of statistical precision, this categorization (≤ 2 vs. ≥3 papers) aligns with common understandings in academic personnel management, as the typical probationary requirement in many Chinese universities is ≤ 2 core papers. To test the robustness of the findings, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis based on the original counts of published papers (Supplementary Material).

Mapping between theoretical framework and performance indicators

①Mapping of grant application frequency to cognitive appraisal theory: Cognitive appraisal theory posits that individuals’ evaluation of events profoundly shapes their stress responses [11]. In the up-or-out context, the frequent process of grant applications is accompanied by outcome uncertainty. Therefore, the frequency of grant applications is regarded as an indicator of individuals’ exposure to potentially evaluative challenges.

②Mapping of funding level to conservation of resources theory: Conservation of resources theory suggests that acquiring key resources helps individuals maintain psychological stability [26]. In an academic career, obtaining a grant not only implies financial support but is also often endowed with symbolic resources such as reputation and professional legitimacy. Accordingly, funding level reflects the extent to which faculty members obtain critical protective resources, with greater resources contributing more strongly to psychological stability.

Statistical analysis

This study adhered to the intention-to-treat principle, including all participants from the first survey wave (N = 2846) in the analyses. Multiple imputation was used to handle missing data generated during longitudinal follow-up. To assess the robustness of the findings, complete-case analysis was also performed as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Material). In addition, sensitivity analyses were conducted using median splits for the number of grant applications and by varying the categorization of application frequency (Supplementary Material). Internal consistency of the PHQ-9, PSS-10, and GAD-7 at each wave was assessed using Cronbach’s α. Following George and Mallery [28], α ≥ 0.9 was considered excellent, 0.8 ≤ α < 0.9 good, 0.7 ≤ α < 0.8 acceptable, and α < 0.7 poor. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a robust covariance matrix estimator and a first-order autoregressive (AR1) working correlation structure were used. Time was treated as a continuous independent variable (coded as 0, 1, 2, 3 for T0, T1, T2, T3). Interaction terms (time × each research performance indicator: grant application frequency group, funding level, and publication group) were included to test whether the rate of change in mental health outcomes differed by performance level. Covariates included baseline mental health scores (depression, anxiety, stress at T0), age, gender, place of birth, pre-entry project experience, and disciplinary background. Because participants were recruited from 39 universities, we specified university as the cluster variable in the GEE model to adjust for non-independence within the same university and to avoid underestimation of standard errors. Mediation analysis was performed using the mediation analysis module in JASP to examine the mediating role of research performance in the relationship between time in the probationary period and mental health. Time was treated as a continuous ordinal variable (1 = Wave 1, 2 = Wave 2, 3 = Wave 3) representing the progression of the probationary evaluation period, research performance was entered as the mediator, and mental health indicators served as outcome variables. Baseline mental health was included as a covariate to control for initial mental health status. Bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was used to estimate the 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. Standardized estimates for total, direct, indirect, and total indirect effects are reported. Subgroup analyses used independent-samples t-tests with Welch’s correction to compare differences in stress, anxiety, and depression scores between male and female participants at each of the three time points, with Cohen’s d effect sizes calculated. Regarding sample attrition, independent-samples t-tests compared completers and dropouts at the overall level; stratified analyses by gender were then conducted to further explore differences (Supplementary Material). To test for common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was performed on all variables, using a 40% variance explained threshold for evaluation [29] (Supplementary Material). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 2,846 early-career faculty were included in the baseline analysis. Among them, 1,644 (57.77%) were male and 1,202 (42.23%) were female; mean age was 32.36 ± 2.34 years. Regarding place of birth, 862 (30.29%) were from urban areas and 1,984 (69.71%) from rural areas. In terms of disciplinary background, 1,812 (63.67%) were from natural sciences and 1,034 (36.33%) from social sciences. Regarding pre-entry project experience, 2,243 (78.81%) had prior project participation (as team members, not as principal investigators) and 603 (21.19%) had none. At baseline, the mean PHQ-9 depression score was 3.19 ± 1.69, falling within the no-depression range. The mean GAD-7 anxiety score was 3.27 ± 1.49, which also lies in the minimal anxiety range. The mean PSS-10 stress score was 7.22 ± 1.45 (Table 1).

Reliability analysis

The PHQ-9 showed Cronbach’s α of 0.77, 0.85, and 0.89 across the three waves. The PSS-10 demonstrated α values of 0.89, 0.78, and 0.91. The GAD-7 yielded α values of 0.91, 0.86, and 0.89, indicating good reliability.

Generalized estimating equation analyses (GEE)

GEE analysis (Table 2) revealed significant interaction effects for both primary and secondary outcomes (P < 0.05). Compared with the group with < 3 grant applications, the group with ≥ 3 applications showed significantly higher scores for depression, stress, and anxiety at T3 (depression: MD [95% CI] = 6.06 [5.46 to 6.69]; stress: MD [95% CI] = 13.21 [12.38 to 13.94]; anxiety: MD [95% CI] = 5.14 [4.45 to 6.02]; P < 0.05). As the periodic evaluation (i.e., the annual performance review conducted by the university at the end of each academic year; the final up-or-out decision, whether to grant tenure or to dismiss, is made after the full probationary period based on cumulative performance) approached, the between-group differences gradually became significant (P < 0.05). These findings are consistent with research hypothesis H1.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis (Table 6; Fig. 1) showed that research performance partially mediated the relationships between the evaluation deadline and depression, stress, and anxiety. The total indirect effects further indicated that research performance had a significant impact on stress (P < 0.01) but not on anxiety or depression (P > 0.05). These findings are consistent with research hypothesis H4.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis (Table 7; Fig. 2) revealed a clear temporal trend in gender differences in mental health indicators over the three-year follow-up period. In the first year, stress (male vs. female: 14.14 ± 7.69 vs. 12.87 ± 6.62, P < 0.01) and depression (male vs. female: 5.53 ± 3.40 vs. 4.67 ± 2.72, P < 0.01) were significantly higher in males than in females, whereas anxiety (female vs. male: 6.83 ± 4.77 vs. 5.66 ± 4.60, P < 0.01) was significantly higher in females. In the third year, there were no significant gender differences in stress, anxiety, or depression (P > 0.05). These findings are consistent with research hypothesis H5.

Discussion

Drawing on three-year longitudinal data, this study explored the impact of research performance on the mental health of early-career faculty under the up-or-out system. The findings indicate that, under this system, new faculty members’ levels of stress, anxiety, and depression continuously increase as the evaluation deadline approaches. This trend likely reflects the cumulative effect of institutional pressure rather than the independent effect of time itself. Counterintuitively, although publishing more papers alleviated stress, it was simultaneously accompanied by a “high output–high depression” paradox. Obtaining provincial-level or higher funding significantly improved anxiety and depression rather than increasing stress. Gender differences completely disappeared over the evaluation period. These findings challenge the intuitive notion that research performance is the primary source of mental health problems, suggesting instead that the up-or-out system may constitute a significant psychological risk factor. Sensitivity analyses and attrition analyses support the robustness of the results.

Notably, in the context of the “up-or-out” system, elevated levels of stress and anxiety are prominent; concurrently, differences in resource acquisition co-occur with the differentiation of depression risk; and when academic output is driven by external institutional discipline, the sense of meaning inherent in the output shows a declining trend, with high producers exhibiting more pronounced depression. This finding suggests that the impact of institutional pressure under the “up-or-out” system on mental health may follow a progressive deepening from external threat perception, through resource differentiation, to meaning loss. When the countdown to the performance evaluation begins, faculty members perceive the approaching evaluation date as an uncontrollable evaluative threat, leading to a continuous depletion of psychological resources—a pattern consistent with the core tenets of cognitive appraisal theory [30, 31]. However, this threat perception alone does not sufficiently explain why depression risk becomes differentiated across faculty groups. Differences in resource acquisition offer a clue: only holders of provincial-level or higher research grants obtain key resources such as career security and academic reputation, which co-occur with reduced depression risk; in contrast, institutional-level grants do not provide such substantive protection and show no significant association with depression risk [32, 33]. From the perspective of conservation of resources theory, this differentiation effect reflects the role of resource disparities in psychological adaptation. Yet resource differences still fail to explain another observation—that high producers do not benefit from their output in terms of mental health, but instead exhibit more severe depression, which is consistent with previous research [34]. Despite their extraordinary efforts, high producers experience a gradual decline in the sense of meaning derived from their output, leading to imbalance and more pronounced depressive symptoms, a pattern that aligns with earlier findings [35, 36]. The emphasis of self-determination theory on the loss of autonomy and intrinsic meaning offers a plausible explanation for this phenomenon [37–39]. The mediation analysis further corroborates this interpretation, showing that the effect of the probationary period on depression is associated with the sustained erosion of meaning, rather than with the sheer number of papers produced.

Subgroup analysis by gender further corroborated the above findings from a different perspective. Cognitive appraisal theory emphasizes the universality of uncontrollable threat, conservation of resources theory highlights the universality of resource loss pressure, and self-determination theory points to the universality of meaning erosion through institutional discipline. The gradual disappearance of gender differences is consistent with the universality of institutional pressure commonly identified by these three theories: when the pressure is sufficiently strong and covers all faculty members, individual-level initial differences tend to recede into the background. As the evaluation deadline approaches, the fading of gender differences aligns with the overall trend of individual differences diminishing under institutional pressure, which is consistent with previous research [40]. Specifically, in the first year, stress was significantly higher in males than in females, whereas anxiety was significantly higher in females than in males; by the third year, all gender differences had disappeared. This “convergence effect” suggests that, as the evaluation deadline draws near, initial gender differences tend to merge with the cumulative effects of institutional pressure, rendering the psychological risk profiles of faculty across genders increasingly homogeneous [41, 42]. The differences observed in the first year are consistent with explanations in the existing literature regarding traditional role expectations (academic achievement pressure for males, work–family conflict for females) [43]. The disappearance of differences by the third year is also in line with previous findings that stressful environments may be associated with the attenuation of gender disparities [44]. Of note, depression showed only a brief gender difference in the second year (slightly higher in males than in females), further corroborating its distinctiveness as a “deep psychological indicator” [45, 46].

In summary, the three theories, along a progressive trajectory from external threat perception to resource maintenance and further to meaning maintenance, jointly delineate the intrinsic logic through which the up-or-out system affects the mental health of pre-tenure faculty. This integration suggests that the impact of institutional pressure on mental health operates not through a single mechanism, but rather through the cumulative layering of threat perception, resource differentiation, and meaning erosion.

Limitations

First, measurement bias may have affected the results. Research performance was based on faculty self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially reducing the precision of the effect estimates. Although Harman’s single-factor test and attrition analysis did not indicate severe bias, the possibility that faculty who left academia entirely due to extreme distress were not included in follow-up surveys cannot be ruled out. This may have introduced sample selection bias, leading to underestimation of the true effects. Future studies should integrate data from research management systems and institutional personnel records to minimize self-report bias. Second, causal inference is limited by the observational design. This study adopted an observational design; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal evidence. Although a longitudinal design was used and baseline levels were controlled for, reverse causation (e.g., deteriorating mental health leading to reduced research performance) and unmeasured confounders (e.g., personality traits) may have influenced the results. It is worth noting that the unidirectional temporal order of time as an independent variable ensures the robustness of the main effect of time; however, reverse causality primarily concerns the mediating pathways involving research performance. Future research could employ cross-lagged panel models or ecological momentary assessment to further clarify the direction of these relationships. Third, generalizability is constrained by contextual factors. Evaluation systems, academic and campus cultures vary considerably across countries, disciplines (e.g., natural sciences and humanities and social sciences), and types of institutions. Therefore, generalization of the present findings to other countries, disciplines, or institutional types should be made with caution. Future studies could adopt growth mixture modeling to explore heterogeneous developmental trajectories and the moderating roles of individual differences. Fourth, this study did not collect participants’ annual income or socioeconomic status (SES). In our communications with university human resources departments during the preliminary phase, we learned that, even though the survey was anonymous, income and SES are still considered highly sensitive personal information. Therefore, these variables were not included in the questionnaire. Future studies may attempt to collect such information or use proxy indicators (e.g., family background) for alternative analyses.

Conclusions

This study reveals that, under the up-or-out system, the mental health of early-career faculty is adversely affected by the approaching evaluation deadline. This temporal trend likely reflects the cumulative effect of institutional pressure rather than the influence of time per se. Counterintuitively, although a greater number of published papers alleviated stress, it was accompanied by a “high productivity, high depression” paradox. Obtaining provincial-level or higher grants significantly improved anxiety and depression, whereas gender differences completely disappeared over the assessment period. Mediation analysis further showed that grant level and number of published papers buffered stress, whereas the frequency of grant applications exacerbated the risk of anxiety and depression. Based on these findings, it is recommended that universities consider implementing psychological support mechanisms for faculty who have repeatedly failed in grant applications. However, it should be emphasized that these suggestions are based on observational data and cannot support causal conclusions, pending validation by future interventional studies.
