Rosa damascena extract mediated synthesis of silver nanoparticles with promising antibacterial synergy in combination with CM11 peptide against multidrug resistant and colistin resistant bacteria
Authors: Majid Taati Moghadam, Mehrdad Moosazadeh Moghaddam, Mahdi Fasihi-Ramandi, Reza Mirnejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rise of pathogens exhibiting significant antibiotic resistance has severely limited treatment options, with multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains showing resistance even to last-resort antibiotics such as colistin. This growing resistance has compounded treatment challenges and intensified the need for alternative therapeutic strategies. This study aimed to synthesize silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using Rosa damascena extract and to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of this biogenic compound—both alone and in combination with the CM11 peptide—against antibiotic-resistant pathogens. The green-synthesized AgNPs were characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS), X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM). The antibacterial activities of both the AgNPs and the synthesized cationic CM11 peptide were determined using the broth microdilution assay. A checkerboard assay was then conducted to assess their combined effects. The AgNPs were successfully synthesized and confirmed using standard analytical techniques. Both the green-synthesized AgNPs and the CM11 peptide demonstrated inhibitory activity against MDR and colistin-resistant bacteria. The checkerboard results revealed that combining AgNPs with CM11 produced synergistic and partial synergistic effects against MDR Escherichia coli and Acinetobacter baumannii, respectively, and an additive effect against colistin-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and MDR Staphylococcus aureus. Our findings demonstrate that the green-synthesized AgNPs and CM11 peptide, whether applied independently or in combination, exhibit strong antibacterial activity against MDR and colistin-resistant isolates. These results suggest that such biogenic compounds could serve as promising alternatives for treating infections caused by resistant bacteria in the future.

Introduction

The misuse and overuse of antibiotics have triggered a rapid surge in bacterial resistance, posing a serious global health concern. This has led to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) bacterial infections [1–3]. The impact of infections resistant to high-level antibiotics extends beyond the challenges faced in clinical care, contributing to prolonged hospitalizations, increased patient mortality, and higher healthcare costs [4–6]. Without effective countermeasures, antibiotic resistance can lead to life-threatening conditions and impede the success of essential medical and surgical procedures—echoing the pre-antibiotic era [5].

In recent years, clinicians have increasingly relied on potent antibiotics such as colistin to treat infections caused by MDR and XDR bacteria. However, a concerning rise in resistance to these last-resort antibiotics has been observed. This resistance has diminished incentives to invest in developing new antimicrobial agents [7–9]. Antibiotic resistance therefore represents a severe threat to public health and the effective management of infectious diseases. To overcome this challenge, it is crucial to develop innovative strategies that exploit the vulnerabilities of resistant bacteria. The discovery of alternative antimicrobial agents and novel therapeutic approaches is essential to combat antibiotic resistance and safeguard global health [5, 10, 11].

Among emerging antimicrobial strategies, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have gained considerable attention due to their unique physicochemical properties and therapeutic potential. Green synthesis methods—eco-friendly and scalable—are particularly attractive for large-scale AgNP production [12, 13]. These methods are considered optimal for generating nanoparticles intended for biological applications because they utilize phytochemicals as both reducing and stabilizing agents, eliminating the need for post-synthesis modification [7, 14–17]. Moreover, environmentally friendly approaches yield nanoparticles with lower toxicity than those produced by conventional chemical or physical techniques [18–23]. Rosa damascena, a small species within the Rosaceae family, exhibits various pharmacological properties, including antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, and immunomodulatory activities. The therapeutic potential of its leaves and petals is largely attributed to a rich diversity of bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, polysaccharides, and phenolic acids. These molecules possess antioxidant, anti-aging, and hepatoprotective properties and contribute to managing cardiovascular complications associated with hyperglycemia. Phytochemical analyses before and after synthesis have shown that phenolic compounds present in R. damascena extract act as both reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle formation [24]. Rosa damascena has attracted increasing attention in green nanotechnology because of its rich phytochemical composition and broad geographical availability, particularly in regions such as Iran [25].

In certain studies, AgNPs have been combined with antibiotics or other antimicrobial agents—such as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs)—to enhance efficacy. AMPs represent a promising class of bioactive molecules with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and significant potential as novel therapeutics. They are key components of the innate immune defense system in eukaryotes, effectively targeting bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and viruses. One such cationic AMP, the CM11 peptide, is an 11-residue sequence (WKLFKKILKVL-NH2) derived from two parent peptides, cecropin A (residues 2–8) and melittin (residues 6–9). CM11 was designed to enhance the stability and antimicrobial potency of its parental sequences and has demonstrated strong activity against a variety of antibiotic-resistant Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens [26–28]. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the antimicrobial properties of green-synthesized AgNPs and the CM11 peptide, both individually and in combination, against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial pathogens exhibiting high levels of antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

All reagents and solvents used in this study were of analytical grade. Silver nitrate (AgNO3) was purchased from Merck (Germany). 2-(4-Iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-phenyl tetrazolium chloride (INT) and antibiotics were obtained from Gibco (Life Sciences Inc., USA). Mueller–Hinton agar and Mueller–Hinton broth media were procured from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Bacterial samples collection

This study utilized 58 multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacterial isolates, including 26 Escherichia coli, 18 Klebsiella pneumoniae, and 14 Acinetobacter baumannii strains, as well as one Staphylococcus aureus isolate. These bacterial strains were obtained from the microbial repository of Baqiyatallah School of Medical Sciences, originally isolated from clinical samples collected at Baqiyatallah Hospital in Tehran, Iran [29–31]. Colistin resistance was evaluated for all MDR Gram-negative isolates. Four representative isolates—three Gram-negative (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and A. baumannii) and one Gram-positive (S. aureus)—were selected for further analysis.

Preparation of Rosa damascena extract

Fresh flowers of R. damascena were collected from a local garden in Kerman, Iran (56° 58′ 24″ N, 52° 30′ 15″ E) in May 2023. The petals were washed thoroughly with distilled water and shade-dried at room temperature. The specimen was deposited at the Herbarium of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (Kerman, Iran) under the accession number KF16467. For extract preparation, 1 g of dried petals was mixed with 100 mL of deionized water in a 250 mL flask. The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 60 min in a water bath, then filtered using filter paper and centrifuged at 8500 rpm for 10 min. The clear filtrate was collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis [10].

Preparation of Ag NPs

To initiate AgNP synthesis, 90 mL of 1 mM AgNO₃ solution was added to 10 mL of R. damascena extract in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The reaction mixture underwent five microwave heating cycles (850 W, 150 s per cycle, Samsung M2330DN) with 2 min intervals between cycles to reduce Ag+ ions. The change in color from nearly colorless to brown indicated the formation of AgNPs. The resulting nanoparticles were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 20 min), washed sequentially with chloroform, ethanol, and distilled water to remove extract residues, sterilized by autoclaving, and stored at 4 °C [32].

Characterization of AgNPs

The synthesized AgNPs were analyzed using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer within a wavelength range of 200–800 nm. The same analysis was performed for Ag+ ions and R. damascena extract to confirm nanoparticle formation [32].

Particle size and size distribution of the AgNPs were determined using a Nano-ZS90 Malvern Zetasizer. Samples (1 mg/mL) were sonicated for 10 min in deionized water to ensure complete dispersion, filtered to remove undissolved particles, and then transferred to quartz cuvettes for analysis [33].

Approximately 500 mg of dried AgNP powder was subjected to XRD analysis using a BRUKER D8 Advance diffractometer (Germany) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The scan range (2θ) was 0.5°–130° with an increment of 0.034°. Operating conditions included 40 kV voltage, 40 mA current, and 0.5 s per step counting time. Data were processed with XPert HighScore Plus version 3 software. CM11 antimicrobial peptide

The CM11 peptide (sequence: WKLFKKILKVL-NH₂) was synthesized by GenScript (USA) with a molecular weight of 1527.50 Da and 98% purity (HPLC verified). The peptide was soluble in double-distilled water and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), but insoluble in 1 × Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (pH 7.1 ± 0.1). It was stored at − 20 °C until use [36].

Investigating the MIC and MBC

The antimicrobial activity of AgNPs and CM11 was determined by the standard broth microdilution method. The CM11 peptide was tested at concentrations ranging from 2 to 128 µg/mL, and AgNPs from 50 to 12,800 µg/mL, against four drug-resistant isolates. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values were determined according to CLSI guidelines. Colistin MICs were also measured for all MDR Gram-negative isolates following EUCAST recommendations [37]. Colistin-resistant isolates were defined as those with MIC ≥ 4 µg/mL. Each test was performed in triplicate.

MICs combination assay

The synergistic interaction between AgNPs and CM11 peptide was assessed using the checkerboard microdilution method [38]. Serial twofold dilutions of both agents were prepared in Mueller–Hinton broth. After incubation (37 °C, 18 h, shaking), optical density was measured at 600 nm. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Differences among treatments were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data and independent-samples t-tests for parametric comparisons. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Bacterial samples

Among the 58 Gram-negative bacterial isolates, only one K. pneumoniae isolate (1.7%) exhibited resistance to colistin, with an MIC of 16 µg/mL. Four isolates were selected for further study, including three Gram-negative bacteria (multidrug-resistant E. coli, colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae, and multidrug-resistant A. baumannii) and one multidrug-resistant S. aureus. The K. pneumoniae isolate was resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, imipenem, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, norfloxacin, ciprofloxacin, kanamycin, chloramphenicol, ceftriaxone, gentamicin, fosfomycin, minocycline, and colistin. The S. aureus isolate showed resistance to norfloxacin, rifampin, penicillin, gentamicin, oxacillin, and ciprofloxacin. The E. coli isolate was resistant to kanamycin, ceftazidime, gentamicin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, imipenem, norfloxacin, and cefotaxime. Acinetobacter baumannii demonstrated resistance to ceftazidime, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, imipenem, cefotaxime, and gentamicin.

AgNPs were synthesized via a green biosynthesis method, employing microwave irradiation of an AgNO3 solution with R. damascena extract as the reducing agent. The solution changed from colorless to brown after microwave irradiation (Fig. 1), indicating successful formation of AgNPs. The green synthesis involved positively charged Ag+ ions, which interacted with functional groups of plant phytochemicals, including alcohols, flavonoids, phenolics, terpenoids, glycosides, and tannins. These compounds acted as reducing agents, converting Ag+ ions to Ag0. FTIR analysis identified functional groups in the plant extract responsible for the reduction and stabilization of AgNPs. Peaks at 3733, 3647, and 3421 cm−1 indicated O–H stretching of alcohols or phenols; peaks at 2918 and 2851 cm−1 corresponded to C–H stretching in alkanes. Peaks at 1734, 1718, and 1700 cm−1 reflected carbonyl groups in esters, ketones, and aldehydes. Amide (NH) groups were suggested by bands at 1636 and 1541 cm−1, while peaks at 1653, 1559, and 1457 cm−1 were attributed to C=C, C=O, and CH2/CH3 groups, respectively (Fig. 3C and D) [40–43].

Comparison of FTIR spectra between AgNPs and R. damascena extract revealed shifts and intensity changes, confirming Ag⁺ ion reduction and nanoparticle capping by plant biomolecules. Reduced intensity and slight shifts in O–H and C=O bands indicated their involvement in reduction and stabilization, while persistent bands post-synthesis confirmed the presence of surface-bound phytochemicals, potentially influencing nanoparticle stability and activity.

FICI for combination of AgNPs/CM11

The FICI analysis indicated that AgNPs combined with CM11 exhibited a synergistic effect against MDR E. coli, an additive effect against colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae and MDR S. aureus, and a partial synergistic effect against MDR A. baumannii. The Kruskal–Wallis test revealed significant differences among treatments for all four bacteria (p = 0.0002). Mann–Whitney tests showed significant differences between AgNPs and CM11 (p = 0.027), AgNPs/CM11 and CM11 (p = 0.028), and AgNPs/CM11 and AgNPs (p = 0.010).

Discussion

The green synthesis of AgNPs using plant extracts has emerged as an innovative and environmentally friendly approach for nanoparticle production. This method is non-toxic, cost-effective, and sustainable, minimizing harmful residues in soil and water. Various plant parts—including fruits, leaves, flowers, rhizomes, and roots—have been successfully employed in the green synthesis of AgNPs [33, 44, 45]. In the present study, R. damascena extract was used as the reducing and stabilizing agent. The color change of the AgNO3 solution from colorless to dark brown confirmed AgNP formation, which is attributed to the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) phenomenon [46]. The successful synthesis and biological activity of the AgNPs observed in this study may be attributed, at least in part, to the phytochemical composition of R. damascena extract. Previous studies have demonstrated that phenolic acids, flavonoids, tannins, and other antioxidant metabolites present in R. damascena can act as effective reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle biosynthesis. These compounds facilitate the conversion of Ag + ions into metallic silver while simultaneously providing surface stabilization to the synthesized nanoparticles. Furthermore, residual phytochemicals adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface may enhance biological interactions and contribute to the antibacterial activity of the biosynthesized AgNPs against MDR and colistin-resistant pathogens [24, 46].

The synthesized AgNPs were predominantly spherical, with sizes ranging from 20 to 29 nm, in agreement with previous reports that described similar morphology and size distributions [7, 46]. The minor differences observed between particle size values obtained by FESEM and DLS could be explained by the polydispersity index of the samples. In DLS measurements, larger particles or aggregates scatter more light, producing a higher apparent size compared with electron microscopy [47]. UV–Vis spectroscopy revealed a characteristic SPR peak at λmax 406 nm, confirming nanoparticle formation; similar peaks have been documented in previous studies, supporting our findings [48, 49]. EDX analysis further verified the elemental composition of the nanoparticles, showing strong silver signals indicative of zero-valent AgNPs. Comparable results were reported in other studies, where clear silver peaks in the EDX spectrum confirmed efficient reduction of Ag+ ions by plant extracts [46, 50].

Our XRD analysis revealed characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes of the face-centered cubic lattice of metallic silver, consistent with previous reports [51]. Peak broadening in XRD patterns typically reflects the nanoscale dimensions of particles and is influenced by nucleation rate, reaction conditions, and growth kinetics [52]. FTIR spectra provided insight into the functional groups involved in nanoparticle formation. A prominent peak at 3421 cm−1 represented O–H stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of hydroxyl and phenolic groups responsible for reducing Ag+ ions to Ag0. Similar findings were reported by Muralidharan et al., who identified an absorption band at ~ 3300 cm−1 related to O–H stretching in phenolic compounds [51]. Another study also attributed strong peaks around 3382 cm−1 to O–H or N–H stretching, suggesting that polyphenols, alcohols, and amine groups in the plant extract played key roles in reduction and stabilization of AgNPs. The antimicrobial assays demonstrated that green-synthesized AgNPs exhibited potent activity against MDR and colistin-resistant Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. This finding aligns with prior studies confirming the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of plant-mediated AgNPs. Ghani et al. [53–56] and Yassin et al. [57]. reported strong antimicrobial effects of green-synthesized AgNPs against antibiotic-resistant K. pneumoniae and other MDR bacteria, while other researchers observed similar activity against P. aeruginosa strains with intermediate colistin resistance [54]. Variations in MIC values across studies may result from differences in the phytochemical composition of plant extracts, synthesis parameters, nanoparticle physicochemical properties, and the bacterial strains tested. Despite these variations, the AgNPs synthesized in the present study demonstrated effective antibacterial activity against clinically isolated MDR and colistin-resistant pathogens, which represent some of the most challenging bacteria encountered in clinical settings. These findings suggest that the biosynthesized AgNPs may serve not only as direct antibacterial agents but also as potentiators of antimicrobial peptides against highly resistant pathogens.

The antibacterial mechanism of AgNPs is multifactorial and synergistic, which helps explain their high efficacy against MDR pathogens. AgNPs adhere to bacterial cell membranes through electrostatic attraction, leading to increased membrane permeability and structural disruption. Once internalized, they release Ag⁺ ions that interact with thiol (–SH) groups in enzymes and proteins, impairing essential metabolic pathways. AgNPs also generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which cause oxidative stress and damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, ultimately resulting in cell death [7, 14–17, 55]. Furthermore, their small size allows close interaction with bacterial membranes, leading to pit formation, cytoplasmic leakage, and cell lysis. The observed activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria in our study supports these mechanisms, as AgNPs can penetrate both the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria and the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria.

The CM11 peptide also demonstrated strong antibacterial activity against MDR and colistin-resistant E. coli, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and S. aureus. This result is consistent with prior studies showing that CM11 possesses broad-spectrum activity against pathogenic and drug-resistant bacteria [27, 36]. The peptide’s antimicrobial effect is primarily based on a “carpet-like” mechanism, in which CM11 molecules align parallel to the bacterial membrane, leading to increased permeability, membrane thinning, and formation of transient toroidal pores. At higher concentrations, membrane disintegration occurs, resulting in micelle formation and cell lysis [27]. This mechanism is rapid, nonspecific, and less prone to resistance development, making CM11 a promising antimicrobial candidate.

The combination of AgNPs with CM11 produced synergistic or partially synergistic effects against MDR E. coli and A. baumannii, respectively, and additive effects against colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae and MDR S. aureus. Synergism between AgNPs and antimicrobial peptides likely results from their complementary mechanisms: AgNPs increase cell membrane permeability and disrupt cell walls, thereby facilitating peptide entry, while CM11 damages internal structures, accelerating bacterial death. Similar synergistic interactions between CM11 and antibiotics have been reported, enhancing antibacterial efficacy and reducing required doses [26, 58]. Amani et al. demonstrated that CM11 exhibited synergism with penicillin, ceftazidime, and norfloxacin against several bacterial pathogens [26]. while the peptide incorporated into sulfate hydrogels also showed enhanced killing of S. aureus [58]. Our findings further support the concept that combining AgNPs with antimicrobial peptides can potentiate antibacterial effects and help overcome resistance.

The present study had several limitations. Reference or ATCC standard strains were unavailable for some assays, and financial constraints limited cytotoxicity testing of synthesized nanoparticles. Additionally, time-kill kinetic assays for AgNPs, CM11, and their combination were not performed; such analyses would provide valuable insights into their bactericidal dynamics. The absence of a standard antibiotic as a positivecontrol restricted direct comparison of AgNP and CM11 efficacy with established antimicrobial agents. Future research should therefore include cytotoxicity evaluation, kinetic studies, and standardized antibiotic controls to better define the therapeutic potential of AgNPs and CM11 combinations. Although the antibacterial activity of AgNPs is commonly associated with Ag + ion release, oxidative stress induction, and membrane damage, these mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study. Therefore, the proposed antibacterial mechanisms are based on previously reported findings for silver nanoparticles. Future studies employing X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, silver ion release measurements, and Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) quantification would provide deeper insight into the relationship between nanoparticle surface chemistry and biological activity.

Conclusion

In this study, both of AgNPs and the CM11 peptide compounds individually exhibited antimicrobial activity against MDR and colistin-resistant Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens. Furthermore, the combination of green-synthesized AgNPs with the CM11 peptide demonstrated synergistic, partially synergistic, and additive effects against pathogens. These results highlight the potential of these compounds as alternatives to conventional antibiotics, capable of eliminating antibiotic-resistant bacteria through mechanisms distinct from those of traditional antibiotics. While these findings demonstrate promising antibacterial activity and synergistic potential against multidrug-resistant and colistin-resistant pathogens, additional studies including cytotoxicity assessments, mechanistic investigations, and in vivo evaluations are required to further validate the safety and therapeutic applicability of this combination strategy.
