An Atypical Hip Pain in a Recreational Athlete: A Case Report
Authors: Qvick Milan, Steyaert Adelheid, Watteyne Karel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOsteochondromas are the most common benign bone tumors, but pelvic involvement is rare. We present a case of an iliac osteochondroma manifesting with atypical hip pain, adding to the limited literature on pelvic osteochondromas and highlighting the diagnostic and management challenges of such cases in an athletic population. A 28‐year‐old male presented with progressive right greater trochanteric area pain, predominantly nocturnal and exacerbated by running and football but absent during cycling. Physical examination and ultrasound revealed no abnormalities. CT and MRI revealed a pedunculated osseous mass arising from the anterosuperior ilium with cortical and medullary continuity, consistent with osteochondroma. MRI demonstrated a thin cartilage cap (< 2 cm) and local irritation of the iliopsoas. Analgesic therapy, sacroiliac joint injection, and physiotherapy failed to provide relief. After discussion in a multidisciplinary oncology board, the patient underwent en bloc excision. Prognosis was considered favorable given the absence of aggressive imaging features. This case emphasizes that nocturnal hip pain and activity‐dependent symptom modulation in a recreational athlete are atypical for the far more common musculotendinous causes of hip pain and should prompt clinicians to consider underlying osseous pathology. Further, it illustrates the importance of multimodality imaging in osteochondromas for accurate characterization and supports the role of multidisciplinary tumor boards in guiding management of rare benign bone tumors.Keywords: athlete, case report, interdisciplinary, osteochondroma, pelvis

Introduction

Osteochondroma is the most common benign bone tumor, defined as a cartilage‐capped osseous outgrowth with cortical and medullary continuity with the parent bone. Although most osteochondromas are considered developmental lesions arising from aberrant physeal growth, occasional cases have been associated with prior trauma or radiation exposure as potential etiological factors. Growth typically occurs during childhood and ceases after physeal closure. Most lesions are solitary (≈85%), but they may also occur in the context of hereditary multiple exostoses (HME), an autosomal dominant disorder. Lesions become symptomatic during the second or third decade of life in the case of solitary exostoses, or earlier in the case of HME [1, 2, 3, 4].

Anatomically, osteochondromas most frequently arise in the metaphyses of long bones, whereas involvement of flat bones such as the scapula, ribs, and pelvis is rare, with pelvic cases representing only about 5% of all osteochondromas [1, 2, 4]. Most lesions remain asymptomatic and can be managed conservatively. However, complications may result from mass effect, fracture, deformity, or, rarely, malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma. In such circumstances, en bloc excision with free margins is the treatment of choice [5, 6].

Given the rarity of pelvic osteochondromas and the predominance of soft‐tissue injuries in athletes presenting with hip pain, recognition of atypical presentations may be challenging. We report a case of an iliac osteochondroma presenting with atypical hip pain in a recreational athlete. This case adds to the limited literature on pelvic osteochondromas and highlights important diagnostic considerations for clinicians evaluating hip pain in athletic populations.

Case History/Examination

A 28‐year‐old male presented with a 5‐month history of progressive right greater trochanteric area pain. The pain was sharp in nature, predominantly nocturnal, and aggravated by running and playing football but absent during cycling. Symptoms had an insidious onset and progressively worsened. The patient frequently woke during the second half of the night because of pain but reported no involvement of other joints. The patient is a recreational athlete participating in amateur‐level cycling and football without any history of elite or competitive training load. Aside from the nonspecific nocturnal right hip pain, the patient reported no additional symptoms. There were no associated medical comorbidities and no family history of similar complaints.

On physical examination, gait was normal and no palpable mass was detected. Bilateral hip motion was unrestricted, and there was no local tenderness. Specific testing for femoroacetabular impingement was negative. Examination of the lumbar spine revealed no abnormalities. Neurological and vascular tests of the right lower limb were unremarkable. Laboratory tests, including inflammatory markers and rheumatologic screening, were within normal limits.

Investigations

Pelvic and hip ultrasound showed no signs of pathology. Standard radiographs of the pelvis and hip revealed preserved joint alignment with no evidence of CAM or pincer impingement. Subtle calcifications were noted lateral to the right L5 transverse process (Figure 1). Given these subtle calcifications and the suspicion of an osseous lesion, a computed tomography (CT) scan was performed.

Anteroposterior radiograph of the pelvis and hips demonstrating preserved joint alignment. A subtle calcified density is seen adjacent to the right L5 transverse process (arrow).

CT of the pelvis demonstrated a pedunculated osseous mass arising from the anterosuperior margin of the right ilium at the level of the sacroiliac joint, measuring approximately 4.4 cm (Figure 2). The lesion was continuous with the cortex and medulla of the parent bone, consistent with an osteochondroma (Figure 2B). Three‐dimensional reconstruction confirmed its exophytic morphology (Figure 2A). Following confirmation of an osteochondroma on CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast was performed for staging purposes.

(A) Three‐dimensional reconstruction highlighting the exophytic morphology. (B) Axial bone‐window image demonstrating cortical and medullary continuity with the ilium. (C) Axial soft‐tissue window showing the lesion's relationship to the adjacent iliopsoas musculature. (D) Coronal bone‐window view.

MRI revealed a heterogeneous osseous lesion with a very thin cartilage cap (Figure 3A,B). T2‐weighted sequences showed surrounding hyperintensity with irritation of the iliacus and iliopsoas muscles (Figure 3C). The mass was in proximity to the exiting right L4 nerve root, without evidence of direct compression or invasion (Figure 3D). No additional pelvic or spinal lesions were identified.

(B) Coronal T1‐weighted fat‐saturated post‐contrast image, confirming no septal enhancement. (C) Axial proton density fat‐saturated pre‐contrast sequence, showing oedema of the iliopsoas muscle. (D) Coronal T2‐weighted fat‐saturated pre‐contrast image, demonstrating the close relationship of the osteochondroma to the exiting right L4 nerve root (red arrow).

Normal physical examination, negative impingement testing, unremarkable laboratory findings, and absence of soft‐tissue pathology on ultrasound made inflammatory, intra‐articular, and musculotendinous causes less likely. The identification of subtle calcifications on radiography prompted further evaluation with CT and MRI, which confirmed the diagnosis of a benign iliac osteochondroma.

Results/Outcome and Follow‐Up

Conservative management with painkillers, a sacroiliac joint infiltration and physiotherapy had provided no benefit. Given the unusual location and rarity of this diagnosis, the case was discussed at the multidisciplinary bone and soft tissue tumor board at Ghent University Hospital, which included orthopedic oncology, radiology, interventional radiology, pathology, oncology, radiotherapy and abdominal surgery specialists. In view of the patient's ongoing functional impairment and intolerable pain, the board reached consensus to proceed with surgical en bloc excision of the osteochondroma.

Preoperative CT angiography (CTA) of the abdominal aorta and iliac arteries was performed to delineate the vascular supply of the iliacus and psoas muscles and to guide surgical planning. Ten months after symptom onset, the patient underwent en bloc resection of the iliac osteochondroma via a midline laparotomy. The open retroperitoneal approach was selected based on the lesion's anatomical location and its relationship to adjacent neurovascular structures, as demonstrated on MRI and CTA.

Postoperatively, physical activity was limited to minimal activities of daily living (e.g., rest, walking, stairs, standing or sitting for long periods of time) for the first two weeks to reduce the risk of complications. Subsequently, a structured, physiotherapist‐guided and progressive rehabilitation program was initiated. The patient achieved a full return to play within 6 to 8 weeks following surgery.

Discussion

Our patient initially presented with atypical hip pain, exacerbated by running and playing football, along with nocturnal symptoms. The initial differential diagnosis was broad and included musculotendinous disorders, peri‐articular or intra‐articular hip pathology, referred pain from the lumbar spine or sacroiliac region, early inflammatory or rheumatologic disease, and less common causes such as benign bone lesions or tumors that may present with nocturnal pain. However, the normal physical examination, absence of focal tenderness, negative impingement tests, and unremarkable laboratory results made soft‐tissue, joint‐related, and inflammatory causes less likely. As the work‐up progressed, attention shifted toward an underlying osseous process. Subsequent cross‐sectional imaging with CT and MRI narrowed the diagnosis and ultimately confirmed the presence of a benign osteochondroma.

Osteochondromas are the most common benign bone tumors, although pelvic involvement accounts for only a small minority of cases and often presents atypically [1, 2, 4]. Symptoms typically arise from mechanical irritation or mass effect rather than from the lesion itself, which may explain the nocturnal pain and activity‐related discomfort observed in our patient [1, 2, 7]. Diagnosis relies on demonstrating cortical and medullary continuity, best visualized on CT, while MRI is essential for assessing cartilage cap thickness and excluding malignant transformation [1, 7]. In our case, CT confirmed the characteristic osseous continuity, and MRI demonstrated a thin, benign‐appearing cartilage cap without aggressive features. Management depends on symptom burden and imaging findings, with en bloc excision recommended for painful, function‐limiting, or anatomically complex pelvic lesions [5, 6]. Given our patient's persistent symptoms and functional impairment, surgical excision was considered appropriate. Postsurgical prognosis is generally excellent when the entire cartilage cap is removed, and recurrence is rare after complete resection [5, 6].

The most plausible explanation for the patient's hip pain was mechanical irritation of the iliopsoas muscle adjacent to the lesion, as suggested by the perilesional edema observed on MRI and the exacerbation of symptoms during hip‐flexion–dominant activities such as running and football. The iliopsoas, formed by the convergence of the psoas major and iliacus, is the primary hip flexor and plays a critical role in gait and athletic activities [8]. During running, it functions eccentrically to decelerate hip extension in late stance and concentrically to accelerate hip flexion in swing, thereby contributing to energy transfer across the hip joint [9]. Weakness or irritation of the muscle can impair hip flexion velocity and alter gait mechanics. In our patient, irritation of the iliopsoas adjacent to the iliac osteochondroma may therefore have contributed to the sharp pain during activities with high hip flexion demand, such as running and football, while cycling remained less symptomatic because it involves a smaller and more controlled range of hip flexion compared to running or football. Nevertheless, given the lesion's proximity to the sacroiliac joint and the exiting L4 nerve root, a multifactorial mechanism of pain cannot be entirely excluded. Local mechanical irritation combined with subtle neural irritation may also have contributed to the patient's symptoms.

In this case, a stepwise multimodality work‐up was performed. Initial radiographs were non‐diagnostic, revealing only subtle calcifications lateral to the right L5 transverse process. CT confirmed the pathognomonic cortical and medullary continuity with the parent ilium and allowed precise delineation of the lesion in relation to adjacent pelvic structures [1, 2]. Ultrasound was of limited diagnostic value due to the location, while MRI clearly depicted the hyaline cartilage cap, which measured < 2 cm and demonstrated the typical high T2/low T1 signal with peripheral but not septal enhancement. These findings were consistent with a benign osteochondroma.

The thickness of the cartilage cap remains the most reliable discriminator for malignant transformation, with a cutoff of ≥ 2 cm in adults generally used as the threshold of concern [7]. Specifically for pelvic lesions, recent data suggest that the risk of malignancy increases markedly when cartilage cap thickness exceeds approximately 3 cm [5]. Other features raising suspicion for secondary chondrosarcoma include progressive pain after skeletal maturity, interval growth, irregular or lobulated margins, scattered calcifications, internal lytic areas, and erosion of adjacent bone [1, 3, 7]. Importantly, the risk of malignant transformation is higher in patients with hereditary multiple exostoses (HME) compared to solitary lesions [2, 10].

For solitary pelvic osteochondromas, the overall prognosis is favorable when managed appropriately. Asymptomatic lesions may be observed, but surgical excision is warranted in symptomatic cases due to mass effect, mechanical irritation, or suspicious imaging findings. En bloc excision with removal of the entire cartilage cap and perichondrium remains the preferred surgical approach and is associated with low recurrence rates [5, 6]. In our patient, the combination of a thin cartilage cap, absence of aggressive features, and correlation of symptoms with local irritation supported a benign diagnosis and an excellent outlook following complete resection.

The decision to proceed with surgical excision was made after discussion in the multidisciplinary bone and soft tissue tumor board at Ghent University Hospital. Based on the imaging findings, including cortical and medullary continuity and a thin cartilage cap without aggressive features, the board considered the lesion most consistent with a benign osteochondroma and judged that a preoperative biopsy was not indicated because of the potential risk of sampling error and diagnostic misinterpretation. En bloc resection with free margins was therefore recommended to both confirm the diagnosis and alleviate the patient's symptoms. For surgical planning, preoperative CT angiography of the abdominal aorta and iliac arteries was additionally performed to delineate the vascular supply of the iliacus and psoas muscles and to guide the selection of the most appropriate surgical approach.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare presentation of an iliac osteochondroma, manifesting with atypical hip pain and nocturnal symptoms likely related to local irritation of the iliopsoas muscle. Multimodality imaging confirmed the diagnosis and excluded malignant transformation, while multidisciplinary discussion guided the decision for surgical excision. This report underscores the importance of considering osteochondroma in the differential diagnosis of unexplained nocturnal hip pain and activity‐dependent symptoms. Further, we highlight the role of advanced imaging in assessing malignant potential and emphasize the value of multidisciplinary collaboration in the management of rare pelvic bone tumors.
