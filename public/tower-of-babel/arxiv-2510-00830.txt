Structure groups and second homology groups of linear Alexander quandles
Adrien Clément

Abstract
. Quandles are self-distributive algebraic structures known as sources of strong knots invariants, but also appearing in other contexts. From any quandle, one can construct two invariants: the structure group and the second quandle homology group. These groups are useful in applications, but hard to compute. In this paper, we focus on Alexander quandles over a cyclic group ℤ n {\mathbb{Z}}_{n} . By using explicit rewriting techniques, we show that the structure group of such a quandle injects into ℤ m ⋉ ℤ n {\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{n} if m m is its number of orbits. This allows us to compute its second quandle homology group, and find that the torsion part depends only on m m and n n .

1.
Introduction

Quandles were independently introduced in 1982 by Joyce
[
Joy82
]
and Matveev
[
Mat82
]
as classifying invariants of knots.
A
quandle
is a non-empty set
X
equipped with a binary operation
\lhd:X\times X\to X
that satisfies the following axioms for all
a,b,c\in X
:

(1)

idempotence:
a\lhd a=a
;

(2)

bijectivity of the right translation
\tau_{c}\colon X\to X,a\mapsto a\lhd c
;

(3)

self-distributivity:
(a\lhd b)\lhd c=(a\lhd c)\lhd(b\lhd c)
.

These axioms encode the compatibility between colorings of knot diagrams and the three Reidemeister moves, so that quandles can provide coloring invariants of knots (see
[
EN15
,
Kam02
]
on quandles invariants).
Self-distributivity also plays a crucial role in the study of the Yang-Baxter equation
[
Bri88
]
and the classification of Hopf algebras
[
AG03
]
.

Starting from a group, one may construct different quandle structures.

•

Conjugation quandles

\Conj(G)
are groups
G
with the conjugation operation

g\lhd h=h^{-1}gh.

•

Core quandles

\Core(G)
are groups
G
with

g\lhd h=hg^{-1}h.

•

Alexander quandles

\Al(M,T)
are abelian groups
M
equipped with a group automorphism
T
, with

a\lhd b=Ta+(1-T)b.

We say that
\Al(M,T)
is
linear
if
M={\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
for an integer
n
.

A well-known example of an Alexander quandle is the Takasaki quandle (also called dihedral), where
M={\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
and
T=-1
.

In the opposite direction, from a quandle
X
one can construct several groups.
In this paper, we will focus on the
structure group
(also called
associated
,
envelopping
, or
adjoint group
) of a quandle
X
, which is defined as follows:

\As(X)=\left<e_{a},a\in X\mid e_{a}e_{b}=e_{b}e_{a\lhd b},a,b\in X\right>.

The structure group acts on
X
by right translations:

a\cdot e_{b}:=a\lhd b.

The orbits of
X
under this action are simply called the
orbits of
X
.
A quandle
X
is said to be
connected
if the action of
\As(X)
on
X
is transitive.

The quadratic presentation of
\As(X)
could suggest one to study the action of the braid group
B_{k}\curvearrowright X^{k}
, given by

\sigma_{i}\cdot(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},a_{i}\lhd a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{k}),

in order to get a normal form for elements of the structure group.
In practice, we don’t know much about the image of
B_{k}\to\Bij(X^{k})
, and so on the rewriting in the structure group.

The quandle (co)homology was introduced in 2003
[
CJK+03
]
, based on the rack homology developed in 1995
[
FRS95
]
.
Most of the applications of quandles depend on effective computation of the
second quandle homology group

H_{2}^{Q}(X)
, and more precisely of its torsion.
The free part of
H_{2}^{Q}(X)
is already known since 2003 for an arbitrary quandle
[
EG03
,
LN03
]
.
However, the torsion part is not known in most cases.
The following exceptions use quite different methods which are very difficult to generalize: odd order Takasaki quandle
[
NP11
]
, connected quandles of order
p^{2}

[
IV17
]
, connected Alexander quandle
[
Cla10
,
BIM+20
]
, finite abelian quandles
[
LM21
]
, conjugation quandles of
\overline{C}
-groups
[
Leb24
]
.
The structure group provides group-theoretic tools to progress in computing
H_{2}^{Q}(X)
through the following formula.

Theorem 1

(Eisermann
[
Eis14
]
)
.

Let
X
be a quandle.
Denote by
\Orb(X)
the set of the orbits of
X
, and fix a family of representatives
\{a_{\mathcal{O}}\}_{\mathcal{O}\in\Orb(X)}
. Then

(1)

H_{2}^{Q}(X)\cong\bigoplus_{\mathcal{O}\in\Orb(X)}\left[\Stab_{\As(X)}(a_{\mathcal{O}})\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)\right]_{Ab},

where
\varepsilon\colon\As(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}
is the
degree
morphism, sending each generator
e_{a}
to
1
.

In this paper, we focus on Alexander quandles.
Section
2
is devoted to constructing a surjective group morphism
f\colon\As(\Al(M,T))\to{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
. This map can be identified with
f=(\varepsilon,\omega)
where
\varepsilon
is the degree morphism and
\omega
the weight map which we will define explicitly.
We also give some properties of the semi-direct product and recall several results on Alexander quandles.
In Section
3
, we explore properties of the
2
-cocycle related to the central group extension defined by
f
.
Then, in Section
4
, we prove our main theorem by using explicit rewriting techniques:

Theorem 2
.

The following map is an injective group morphism:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}u\colon&\As(\Al({\mathbb{Z}}_{n},T))&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{n},\\
&g&\longmapsto&(Ab(g),\varepsilon(g)),\end{array}

where
m
is the number of orbits,
Ab:\As(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
the abelianization and
\omega
the weight map.

It provides a normal form for the elements of the structure group, and finally allows us to compute the second quandle homology group of a linear Alexander quandle:

Theorem 3
.

Let
X=\Al({\mathbb{Z}}_{n},T)
be a linear Alexander quandle with
m
orbits. The second quandle homology group of
X
is given by

H_{2}^{Q}(X)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{(m-1)m}\oplus\left[\gcd\left(m,\frac{n}{m}\right){\mathbb{Z}}_{n}\right]^{m}.

2.
Structure group of an Alexander quandle

Let
M
be an abelian group and
T\in\Aut_{Grp}(M)
.
We denote by
X:=\Al(M,T)
the Alexander quandle
(M,\lhd)
, defined by
a\lhd b=Ta+(1-T)b
.

2.1.
Semi-direct product

We define a right group-action of
{\mathbb{Z}}
on
M
by

a\cdot 1=Ta.

Let us consider the corresponding semi-direct product group-structure
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
, given by

(k,a)(m,b)=(k+m,T^{m}a+b).

Lemma 4
.

Let
\alpha=(k,a),\beta=(m,b)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
. Then

•

The identity of
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
is
(0,0)
.

•

The inverse of
\alpha
is given by
\alpha^{-1}=(-k,-T^{-k}a)
.

•

The conjugate of
\alpha
by
\beta
is given by
\beta^{-1}\alpha\beta=(k,T^{m}a+(1-T^{k})b)
.

Proof.

We check

(k,a)(-k,-T^{-k}a)=(k-k,T^{-k}a-T^{-k}a)=(0,0).

For the conjugate, we get

\beta^{-1}\alpha\beta=\left(-m,-T^{-m}b\right)\left(k+m,T^{m}a+b\right)=\left(k,T^{m}a+(1-T^{k})b\right).\qed

2.2.
Structure group seen as an extension

We recall that the structure group of a quandle
(X,\lhd)
is defined by

\As(X)=\left<e_{a},a\in X\mid e_{a}e_{b}=e_{b}e_{a\lhd b},a,b\in X\right>.

Proposition 5
.

The following defines a surjective group morphism:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}f:&\As(\Al(M,T))&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M,\\
&e_{a}&\longmapsto&(1,a).\end{array}

Proof.

Begin with showing
f(e_{a})f(e_{b})=f(e_{b})f(e_{a\lhd b})
.
For all
a,b\in M
,

\displaystyle f(e_{a})f(e_{b})

\displaystyle=(1,a)(1,b)

\displaystyle=(2,Ta+b).

We get

\displaystyle f(e_{b})f(e_{a\lhd b})

\displaystyle=(2,Tb+a\lhd b)

\displaystyle=(2,Ta+b),

so
f
defines a group morphism.

Moreover, for all
(k,a)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
, we have

f(e_{0}^{k-1}e_{a})=(k,a)

hence
f
is surjective.
∎

We denote by
\omega:\As(X)\to X
the composition of
f
with the projection on the second coordinate.
For
g\in\As(X)
, the quantity
\omega(g)
will be called the
weight
of
g
.
For instance,

\omega(e_{a}e_{b})=Ta+b=\omega(e_{b}e_{a\lhd b}).

More generally,

\omega(e_{a_{1}}\ldots e_{a_{k}})=T^{k-1}a_{1}+\ldots+Ta_{k-1}+a_{k}.

Lemma 6
.

For all
g,h\in\As(X)
, we have

(2)

\omega(gh)=T^{\varepsilon(h)}\omega(g)+\omega(h),

which can be thought of as a
1
-cocycle condition.

Corollary 7
.

Alexander quandles are injective, which means that the map

e_{\text{\textunderscore}}:\begin{array}[]{ccc}X&\to&\As(X),\\
x&\mapsto&e_{x}\end{array}

is injective.

Proof.

The composition of
e_{\text{\textunderscore}}
with
\omega
gives the identity map of
X
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

For any quandle
X
,
e_{x}g=ge_{x\cdot g}
for all
x\in X
and
g\in\As(X)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
and
g\in\As(X)
.
There exists
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in X
and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}
such that
g=e_{y_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\ldots e_{y_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}
.
Since for all
a\in X
,

e_{a}e_{y_{i}}^{\varepsilon_{i}}=e_{y_{i}}^{\varepsilon_{i}}e_{a\cdot e_{y_{i}}^{\varepsilon_{i}}},

then we have

e_{x}g=ge_{x\cdot g}.

∎

Corollary 9
.

If
X
is an Alexander quandle, then

\bigcap_{x\in X}\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)=Z(\As(X)).

Proof.

If
g\in\bigcap\limits_{x\in X}\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)
, then by
8
we have
g\in Z(\As(X))
.

If
g\in Z(\As(X))
, then
\forall x\in X,e_{x\cdot g}=g^{-1}e_{x}g=e_{x}
.
By
7
, we get
x\cdot g=x
, and hence
g\in\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)
.
∎

Remark 10
.

The composition of
f
with the projection on the first coordinate gives the degree morphism
\varepsilon
, so we can write
f=\varepsilon\times\omega
.

If we denote the kernel of
f
by
K(X):=\Ker(f)
, we get a group extension

(E)

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}1&\longrightarrow&K(X)&\longrightarrow&\As(X)&\overset{f}{\longrightarrow}&{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M&\longrightarrow&1\\
&&&&g&\longmapsto&\left(\varepsilon(g),\omega(g)\right)&&\end{array}.

2.3.
Second homology group and centrality of the extension

Recall that the structure group
\As(X)
acts on
X
by the right translations:

a\cdot e_{b}=a\lhd b,\quad a,b\in X.

Moreover, if
a\in X
and
g\in\As(X)
, we have

e_{a}g=ge_{a\cdot g}.

The action of
g\in\As(X)
is fully determined by the image of
g
by
f
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
X=\Al(M,T)
.
For all
x\in X
and
g\in\As(X)
,

x\cdot g=T^{\varepsilon(g)}x+(1-T)\omega(g).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the length of the word
e_{a_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\ldots e_{a_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}
.
If
n=1
, then

x\cdot e_{a_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}=T^{\varepsilon_{1}}x+(1-T)\omega\left(e_{a_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\right).

Now, take
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then, using
eq.

2
, we get

\displaystyle x\cdot e_{a_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\ldots e_{a_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}e_{a_{n+1}}^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=T^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}\left[T^{\varepsilon_{1}+\ldots\varepsilon_{n}}x+(1-T)\omega\left(e_{a_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\ldots e_{a_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)\right]+(1-T)\omega\left(e_{a_{n+1}}^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=T^{\varepsilon_{1}+\ldots+\varepsilon_{n}+\varepsilon_{n+1}}x+(1-T)\omega\left(e_{a_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\ldots e_{a_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}e_{a_{n+1}}^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}\right).\qed

Proposition 12
.

The extension (
E
) is central.

Proof.

Let
g\in K(X)
.
Then,
\varepsilon(g)=0
and
\omega(g)=0
.
Then, for all
x\in X
,

e_{x}g=ge_{T^{\varepsilon(g)}x+(1-T)\omega(g)}=ge_{x}.

and hence
g
is central.
∎

Proposition 13
.

Let
X=\Al(M,T)
.
For all
x_{0}\in X
,
\Stab_{\As(X)}(x_{0})\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)
is the abelian group
\omega^{-1}(\Ker(1-T))\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)
, and in particular does not depend on the choice of
x_{0}
.

Proof.

For all
g\in\As(X)
and
x\in X
, we have

x\cdot g=T^{\varepsilon(g)}x+(1-T)\omega(g).

If
g\in\Stab_{\As(X)}(x_{0})\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)
, then

x_{0}=x_{0}\cdot g=x_{0}+(1-T)\omega(g).

We deduce that
\omega(g)\in\Ker(1-T)
, and hence for all
x\in X
,
g\in\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)
.
Thus,

g\in\left(\bigcap\limits_{x\in X}\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)\right)\bigcap\Ker(\varepsilon),

so

\Stab_{\As(X)}(x_{0})\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)=\left(\bigcap\limits_{x\in X}\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)\right)\bigcap\Ker(\varepsilon).

Since
\bigcap\limits_{x\in X}\Stab_{\As(X)}(x)=Z(\As(X))
, we get that
\Stab_{\As(X)}(x_{0})\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)
is abelian.
∎

The previous proposition allows us to simplify Eisermann’s formula (
1
) to get:

Corollary 14
.

Let
X
be an Alexander quandle.
If
m
denotes the number of orbits of
X
, then

H_{2}^{Q}(X)\cong\left(\Stab_{\As(X)}(0)\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)\right)^{m}.

In the connected case, the following gives an interpretation of the kernel
K(X)
.

Lemma 15
.

The orbits of an Alexander quandle are the cosets of
\im(1-T)
.

Corollary 16
.

Let
X=\Al(M,T)
.
We have the following equivalence:

X\text{ connected }\iff(1-T):M\to M\text{ is surjective.}

Proposition 17
.

Let
X
be a connected Alexander quandle. Then

K(X)\cong H_{2}^{Q}(X)\cong\Stab_{\As(X)}(0)\cap\Ker(\varepsilon).

Proof.

We have

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\Stab_{\As(X)}(0)\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)&\overset{Prop.\ref{stabker}}{=}&\omega^{-1}(\Ker(1-T))\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)\\
&=&\omega^{-1}(0)\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)\\
&=&\Ker(\omega)\cap\Ker(\varepsilon)\\
&=&\Ker(f)\\
&=&K(X),\end{array}

hence the result.
∎

3.
2-cocycle associated to the extension

3.1.
Properties of the 2-cocycle

Let
X=\Al(M,T)
.
We can define a map of sets, which is a section of
f
, by

\begin{array}[]{cccc}s\colon&{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M&\longrightarrow&\As(X),\\
&(k,a)&\longmapsto&e_{0}^{k-1}e_{a}.\end{array}

One studies the defect of “morphicity” of a section by defining a new map as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\varphi\colon&({\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M)\times({\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M)&\longrightarrow&K(X),\\
&\left((k,a),(m,b)\right)&\longmapsto&s(k,a)s(m,b)s((k,a)(m,b))^{-1}.\end{array}

We can give an expression in terms of the generators of
\As(X)
:

(3)

\varphi((k,a),(m,b))=\underbrace{e_{0}^{k-1}e_{a}}_{s(k,a)}\underbrace{e_{0}^{m-1}e_{b}}_{s(m,b)}\underbrace{e^{-1}_{T^{m}a+b}e_{0}^{1-k-m}}_{s((k,a)(m,b))^{-1}}.

This map turns out to be a group
2
-cocycle, that satisfies properties of symmetry and equivariance.

Theorem 18
.

Let
X=\Al(M,T)
.
The map
\varphi
satisfies the following properties for all
\alpha=(k,a)
,
\beta=(m,b)
and
\gamma=(p,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
:

(4)

\displaystyle\varphi(\beta,\gamma)-\varphi(\alpha\beta,\gamma)+\varphi(\alpha,\beta\gamma)-\varphi(\alpha,\beta)=0,

(5)

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha,(m,0))=0,

(6)

\displaystyle\varphi((k,0),\beta)=0,

(7)

\displaystyle\varphi((k,a),(m,b))=\varphi\left((k,Ta),(m,Tb)\right),

(8)

\displaystyle\varphi\left((k,a),(m,b)\right)=\varphi\left((1,a),(1,T^{1-m}b)\right),

(9)

\displaystyle\varphi\left((k,a),(m,b)\right)=\varphi\left((m,T^{1-k}b),(k,T^{m}a+(1-T)b)\right).

Definition 19
.

The term

will refer to the rewriting relations
e_{a}e_{b}=e_{b}e_{a\lhd b}
for all
a,b\in X
.

Convention 20
.

When it is possible, we use the additive notation when working with elements of
K(X)
.

Proof.

We use the centrality of
\varphi(\beta,\gamma)
in
\As(X)
:

\displaystyle\varphi(\beta,\gamma)-\varphi(\alpha\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle+\varphi(\alpha,\beta\gamma)-\varphi(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\varphi(\beta,\gamma)+\varphi(\alpha,\beta\gamma)-\varphi(\alpha\beta,\gamma)-\varphi(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\varphi(\beta,\gamma)+s(\alpha)s(\beta\gamma)s(\alpha\beta\gamma)^{-1}s(\alpha\beta\gamma)s(\gamma)^{-1}s(\alpha\beta)^{-1}-\varphi(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\varphi(\beta,\gamma)+s(\alpha)s(\beta\gamma)s(\gamma)^{-1}s(\alpha\beta)^{-1}s(\alpha\beta)s(\beta)^{-1}s(\alpha)^{-1}

\displaystyle=s(\alpha)\varphi(\beta,\gamma)s(\beta\gamma)s(\gamma)^{-1}s(\beta)^{-1}s(\alpha)^{-1}

\displaystyle=s(\alpha)s(\beta)s(\gamma)s(\beta\gamma)s(\beta\gamma)^{-1}s(\gamma)^{-1}s(\beta)^{-1}s(\alpha)^{-1}

\displaystyle=0.

The normalisation conditions (
5
) and (

) are proved by applying braidings involving the generators
e_{0}
.
For (

), we have:

\varphi((k,a),(m,0))=e_{0}^{k-1}e_{0}^{m}e_{T^{m}a}e_{T^{m}a}^{-1}e_{0}^{1-k-m}=0.

The invariance by multiplication by

(

) uses the centrality of
\varphi(\alpha,\beta)
in
\As(X)
and braidings of the generators:

\displaystyle\varphi((k,a),(m,b))

\displaystyle=e_{0}^{-1}e_{0}\varphi((k,a),(m,b))

\displaystyle=e_{0}^{-1}\varphi((k,a),(m,b))e_{0}

centrality

\displaystyle=\varphi((k,Ta),(m,Tb))

\displaystyle s(k,a)e_{0}=e_{0}s(k,Ta)

The reduction to degree

follows from the next lemma.

Since
\varphi((1,a),(1,b))=\varphi((1,b),(1,a\lhd b)
, we get the last point.

Lemma 21
.

For all
(k,a),(m,b)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
, we have

\varphi((k,a),(m,b))=\varphi((1,T^{1-k}a),(m,T^{1-k}b))=\varphi((1,a),(m,b))

) every generator

The group
2
-cocycle
\varphi
generates the whole kernel of the extension:

Proposition 22
.

We have

K(\Al(M,T))=\left<\im(\varphi)\right>.

Proof.

Let
g\in K(\Al(M,T))
.
Since
\varepsilon(g)=0
, after applying the braidings, one can write

g=\underbrace{e_{x_{1}}\ldots e_{x_{k}}}_{g^{+}}(\underbrace{e_{y_{1}}\ldots e_{y_{k}}}_{g^{-}})^{-1}.

We will apply the following relations:

\forall a,b\in M,\qquad e_{a}e_{b}=e_{0}e_{Ta+b}\varphi((1,a),(1,b)).

Denote

\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}):=&\varphi((1,x_{1}),(1,x_{2}))\varphi((1,Tx_{1}+x_{2}),(1,x_{3}))\ldots\\
&\ldots\varphi((1,\omega(e_{x_{1}}\ldots e_{x_{k-1}})),(1,x_{k}))\in\left<\im(\varphi)\right>.\end{array}

We get

Since
\varphi(\alpha,\beta)
is central for all

, we have

g=e_{0}^{k-1}e_{\omega(g^{+})}e^{-1}_{\omega(g^{-})}e_{0}^{1-k}\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\phi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})^{-1}.

But
\omega(g)=0
, so from the last equality,
\omega(g^{+})=\omega(g^{-})
.
Thus,

g=\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\phi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})^{-1}\in\left<\im(\varphi)\right>.\qed

Lemma 23
.

For
\alpha=(k,a),\gamma=(p,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
, we have

s\left(\gamma^{-1}\alpha\gamma\right)=s\left(k,T^{p}a+(1-T^{k})c\right).

In degree
0

, this leads to:

•

If

Regarding this compatibility with the conjugation, the section

Lemma 24
.

Let
\alpha=(1,a),\gamma=(p,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes M
.
Then,

s\left(\gamma^{-1}\alpha\gamma\right)=s\left(\gamma\right)^{-1}s(\alpha)s(\gamma).

Proof.

We check

\displaystyle s\left(\gamma\right)^{-1}s(\alpha)s(\gamma)

\displaystyle=e_{c}^{-1}e_{0}^{1-p}e_{a}e_{0}^{p-1}e_{c}

\displaystyle=e_{c}^{-1}e_{T^{p-1}a}e_{c}

\displaystyle=e_{T^{p-1}a\lhd c}

4.
Linear Alexander quandles

Definition 28
.

Quandles of the form
\Al({\mathbb{Z}}_{n},T)
are called
linear Alexander quandles
.

In this section, let
X=\Al({\mathbb{Z}}_{n},T)
.
It is known that the orbits of an Alexander quandle
X
are the cosets of
\im(1-T)
.
Since
\im(1-T)
is a subgroup of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
, it is cyclic and hence there exists a unique divisor
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
of
n
such that
\im(1-T)=q{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
.
But
\left.\raisebox{1.99997pt}{${\mathbb{Z}}_{n}$}\middle/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{$q{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}$}\right.\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
, so we get
q=m
: the number of orbits.

Denote by

\begin{array}[]{cccc}Ab:&\As(X)&\to&\As(X)_{Ab}\\
&e_{x}&\mapsto&[e_{x}]\end{array}

the abelianization.
For
x\in X
and
g\in\As(X)
, the relation
g^{-1}e_{x}g=e_{x\cdot g}
implies
[e_{x}]=[e_{x\cdot g}]
, and
\As(X)_{Ab}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.

4.1.
Normal form

We now prove our main theorem.
It will give us a normal form for the elements of the structure group of a linear Alexander quandle.

Theorem 29
.

Let
X
be a linear Alexander quandle over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
.
The following map is an injective group morphism:

u\colon\begin{array}[]{ccc}\As(X)&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}\\
g&\longmapsto&(Ab(g),\omega(g))\end{array},

where

is the number of orbits of

,

the abelianization and
\omega
the weight map.

Since we have a surjective homomorphism

{\mathbb{Z}}
on

gives us an action of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}

a\cdot[g]=a\cdot\varepsilon(g)=T^{\varepsilon(g)}a.

In the proof, we wil need the following technical result:

Lemma 30

For all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in X=\Al({\mathbb{Z}}_{n},T)

(18)

e_{\alpha}e_{\beta}=e_{\alpha-(1-T)\gamma}e_{\beta+(1-T)T\gamma}

Proof.

By using the bi-additivity of

.
Then,
\varphi_{0}((1-T)a,b)=\lambda(a,b)=0
.
Remember that the cocycle condition for
\varphi_{0}

\varphi_{0}(y,z)+\varphi_{0}(x,y+z)=\varphi_{0}(x+y,z)+\varphi_{0}(x,y).

By replacing

,

and

, we get

\underbrace{\varphi_{0}(-(1-T)c,b+(1-T)c)}_{=0}+\varphi_{0}(a,b)=\varphi_{0}(a-(1-T)c,b+(1-T)c)+\underbrace{\varphi_{0}(a,-(1-T)c)}_{=0}

and hence

\varphi_{0}(a,b)=\varphi_{0}(a-(1-T)c,b+(1-T)c).

The expression of

in terms of the generators of the structure group gives

e_{0}^{-2}e_{T^{-1}a}e_{b}=e_{0}^{-2}e_{T^{-1}a-(1-T)T^{-1}c}e_{b+(1-T)c}.

We finally get, for all

,

e_{\alpha}e_{\beta}=e_{\alpha-(1-T)\gamma}e_{\beta+(1-T)T\gamma}.\qed

We will use the following result in the rewriting process:
