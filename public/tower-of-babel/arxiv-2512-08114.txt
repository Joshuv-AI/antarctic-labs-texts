On the existence of large subspaces of C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) that perform stable phase retrieval
Enrique García-Sánchez, David de Hevia, Mitchell A. Taylor

Abstract
. The purpose of this article is to address an open problem posed by Freeman-Oikhberg-Pineau-T. ( Math. Ann. 2024) regarding the existence of large subspaces of C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) that perform stable phase retrieval (SPR). We begin by proving that for both the real and complex fields, the space C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) admits an infinite-dimensional SPR subspace if and only if the second Cantor-Bendixson derivative K ′′ K{{}^{\prime\prime}} is nonempty. We then show how to construct “large” SPR subspaces of C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) , where the size of the subspace depends quantitatively on the number of non-trivial Cantor-Bendixson derivatives that the compact Hausdorff space K K possesses.

1.
Introduction

In many areas of physics and engineering, one is tasked with recovering a signal
f\in H
from a phaseless measurement
|Tf|\in X
where
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\to X
is a linear operator mapping a signal space
H
to a function space
X
. Such problems appear prominently in crystallography, as detectors can only record the modulus of the Fourier transform of the desired signal. This leads to the mathematical task of recovering the true signal
f
from the distorted image
|\mathcal{F}f|
, under the constraint that
f
lies in a known subspace
H
of
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of functions relevant to the experiment. Note that for any unimodular scalar
\lambda
, the signals
f,\lambda f\in H
give rise to the same phaseless Fourier measurements. Thus, the objective of
Fourier phase retrieval
is to stably recover
f\in H
from
|\mathcal{F}f|\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
up to global phase ambiguity.

Although Fourier phase retrieval is the prototypical example of a phase retrieval problem, many other interesting variants arise from different physical models. Given
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\to X
as above, we say that
T
does
phase retrieval
if for all
f,g\in H
we have
|Tf|=|Tg|
if and only if
f=\lambda g
for some unimodular scalar
\lambda
.
Fourier phase retrieval is thus the special case where
T=\mathcal{F}|_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\subseteq L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
Below, we list several other important situations where phase information cannot be directly measured and needs to be mathematically recovered. For a more comprehensive discussion, we refer the reader to the surveys
[
23
,
24
]
.

(1)

(Ptychography).
In modern imaging applications, it is common to insert a window
g
in front of the crystal, so that the detector records
|\mathcal{F}(fg)|
. Translating the window
g
and then repeating the experiment, one gains access to the phaseless short-time Fourier transform (STFT)
|V_{g}f|
. Thus, in this case, the measurement operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
is the STFT with window
g
.

(2)

(Pauli problem).
In quantum mechanics, the famous
Pauli problem
asks whether one can recover a wavefunction
\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\psi\|=1
from
|\psi|^{2}
and
|\mathcal{F}\psi|^{2}
. Here, we note that
|\psi|^{2}
and
|\mathcal{F}\psi|^{2}
represent the probability densities of position and momentum, respectively. In the above language, the Pauli problem asks whether the operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\subseteq L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
T\psi=(\psi,\mathcal{F}\psi)
, does phase retrieval. In general, it is known that when
H=L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, this is not the case. Nevertheless, there are “large” subspaces of
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
where Pauli phase retrieval can be performed in a stable manner
[
16
,
21
]
.

(3)

(Schrödinger flows).
Pauli’s problem asks whether it is possible to recover a wave function from its position and momentum densities at a single time (or equivalently, from the position density of both its initial state and its rescaled asymptotic profile). A more reasonable but equally relevant question is whether the data
\{|\psi(t)|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\in I\}
determines
\psi
uniquely, where
I
is a set of time measurements and
\psi
is a solution to the Schrödinger equation. This leads to an interesting family of phase retrieval problems, modeled by the operators
T_{I}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C(I;L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
T_{I}\psi_{0}=\psi|_{I}
.

In physical applications, it is imperative that phase retrieval not only be possible but also be stable. To quantify this, we say that a linear operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\to X
does
stable phase retrieval
if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that for all
f,g\in H
we have

(1.1)

\inf_{|\lambda|=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\leq C\||Tf|-|Tg|\|.

The primary objective of this article is to develop techniques to address the stability of the phase retrieval problem. However, before we do this, it will be convenient to reformulate the problem in a way that decouples the operator
T
from the nonlinear inequality.

1.1.
A reformulation of the phase retrieval problem

Although the above examples model very different physical phenomena, they can all be unified into a single mathematical problem. Indeed, let us say that a subspace
E
of a function space
X
does
phase retrieval
if for all
f,g\in E
we have
|f|=|g|
if and only if
f=\lambda g
for some unimodular scalar
\lambda
. Since any operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\to X
which does phase retrieval must be injective, it is easy to see that
T
does phase retrieval if and only if the subspace
E\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=T(H)\subseteq X
does phase retrieval. In other words, the operator
T
determines the subspace of
X
that we wish to do phase retrieval but otherwise does not play a role in the nonlinear recovery problem.

When considering the stability of any nonlinear inverse problem, it is natural to assume that the forward problem is well-posed, which in our case means that the mapping
f\mapsto|Tf|
is Lipschitz. In view of (
1.1
), it follows that any operator that performs stable phase retrieval must satisfy the relation

\inf_{|\lambda|=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\sim\inf_{|\lambda|=1}\|Tf-\lambda Tg\|.

Hence, again, we may relabel
f\leftrightarrow Tf
to suppress the operator
T
in the inequality (
1.1
). More formally, we say that a subspace
E
of a function space
X
does
stable phase retrieval
if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that for all
f,g\in E
we have

(1.2)

\inf_{|\lambda|=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\leq C\||f|-|g|\|.

With the above discussion in mind, it is easy to see that an operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\to X
does stable phase retrieval if and only if the subspace
E\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=T(H)
does stable phase retrieval as a subspace of
X
. As we shall soon see, this reformulation of the phase retrieval problem will clarify many aspects of the theory. In particular, it will soon become evident that all subspaces satisfying (
1.2
) share interesting structural features that do not depend on the specific physical models that they arise from.

In the vast majority of phase retrieval papers, the function space
X
is assumed to be
L_{2}(\mu)
. However, to study the phase retrieval problem for Schrödinger flows and other time-dependent PDEs, one must consider more general function spaces. A unified perspective on phase retrieval in function spaces was pioneered in the articles
[
14
,
16
,
21
]
, which showed that a remarkable structural theory for phase retrieval problems could be developed in
L_{p}
-spaces or even general Banach lattices (i.e., spaces equipped with a compatible set of linear, norm, and modulus operations that make the forward problem for phase retrieval well-posed). In particular, this study revealed clear connections with many diverse areas of mathematics, including the
\Lambda(p)
-set theory of sparse Fourier series and the local and isometric theories of Banach spaces.

In this article, we will focus on the stability of the phase retrieval problem when
X
is a space
C(K)
of continuous function on a compact Hausdorff space
K
. In particular, we will answer an open problem from
[
21
]
and develop important mathematical tools that will allow us to establish stability results outside of the realm of Hilbert spaces. Notably, our results will have direct consequences for phase retrieval in vector-valued function spaces such as
C(I;L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, which appear in the Schrödinger phase retrieval problem (
3
).

1.2.
Summary of the results and an outline of the proof

We now proceed to define the concepts studied in this paper and to motivate our main results. We begin by formally defining stable phase retrieval.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice and fix
C\geq 1
. A subspace
E
of
X
does
C
-stable phase retrieval
(
C
-SPR
, for short) if for all
f,g\in E
we have the inequality

(1.3)

\inf_{|\lambda|=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\leq C\||f|-|g|\|.

We remark that the choice of scalar field
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
is extremely important for the phase retrieval problem. If the scalar field is
\mathbb{R}
, then there are only two phases
\{\pm 1\}
, so that the left-hand side of (
1.3
) reduces to
\min\{\|f-g\|,\|f+g\|\}
. However, if the scalar field is
\mathbb{C}
, the infimum in (
1.3
) must be taken over the entire unit circle.

Remark 1.2
.

The theory of Banach lattices provides a natural functional setting for studying many important inverse problems, including phase retrieval, declipping and ReLU recovery
[
1
,
20
,
21
]
. For this reason, we have stated
1.1
(as well as a handful of results below) in this language. However, since all of the new results in this article are specific to
C(K)
, we encourage any reader not familiar with the theory of Banach lattices to assume throughout the paper that
X
is
C(K)
,
L_{p}(\mu)
or
C(K;L_{p}(\mu))
for some compact Hausdorff space
K
and measure
\mu
.

1.2.1.
Summary of the results

Our first main result is a complete characterization of compact Hausdorff spaces

1.2.2.
Obstructions to phase retrieval and an outline of the proof

Our construction utilizes two recent characterizations of stable phase retrieval that clearly isolate the main instability mechanisms. The most obvious obstruction to phase retrieval is the existence of pairs of disjoint vectors in
E
, that is, non-zero functions
f,g\in E
such that
|f|\wedge|g|=0
, or equivalently,
fg=0
. To see that such vectors give rise to an instability in phase recovery, simply note that the functions
h_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f+g,h_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f-g
lie in
E
, are not multiples of each other, and yet satisfy
|h_{+}|=|h_{-}|
. Remarkably, when the scalar field is
\mathbb{R}
, this is the
only
source of instability in the phase retrieval problem. In fact, we have the following complete characterization of SPR in the setting of
C(K)
-spaces (see also
[
21
, Theorem 3.4]
and
[
11
, Proposition 3.4]
for more general results).

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
E
be a subspace of a real
C(K)
-space. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

E
fails SPR.

(2)

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there are
f,g\in S_{E}
such that
\||f|\wedge|g|\|<\varepsilon
.

(3)

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there are
f,g\in S_{E}
such that
\|fg\|<\varepsilon
.

Moreover,
E
does
C
-SPR if and only if all
f,g\in S_{E}
satisfy
\||f|\wedge|g|\|\geq\frac{1}{C}
.

Remark 1.7
.

One interesting feature of
Theorem

1.6
is that it reformulates the phase retrieval problem as a problem in three distinct fields. Indeed, statement (
1
) views phase retrieval as a nonlinear inverse problem, statement (
2
) views phase retrieval as a Banach lattice problem and statement (
3
) views phase retrieval as a Banach algebra problem. Since the lattice, isometric and algebra structures of
C(K)
all uniquely determine
K
, this result (together with Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
) can be viewed not only as a contribution to the study of phase retrieval in function spaces, but also to the classical program initiated in
[
19
]
of characterizing “interesting” Banach space/lattice/algebra properties of
C(K)
in terms of “interesting” topological properties of
K
(see
[
33
, p. 132]
for a summary of such properties).

Remark 1.8
.

Although the equivalence of statements (
1
) and (
2
) and the moreover statement in
Theorem

1.6
are valid in any Banach lattice, they are particularly useful in
C(K)
. Indeed, unlike in
L_{p}
-spaces where one has to quantify the relative mass on the domain where
f
and
g
overlap, in
C(K)
the condition
\||f|\wedge|g|\|\geq\frac{1}{C}
simply states that one may find a single point

where
|f(t)|,|g(t)|\geq\frac{1}{C}
. This will allow us to use “combinatorial” arguments in our constructions of SPR subspaces of
C(K)
, as we now only have to construct

so that for each
f,g\in S_{E}
there is a point
t\in K
where
f(t)
and
g(t)
both have magnitude above a given threshold.

Recall that the existence of almost disjoint pairs implies the failure of complex SPR
[
15
, Proposition 2.5]
, but the converse is not true
[
21
, Example 3.7]
. To characterize complex phase retrieval, the non-existence of disjoint pairs has to be replaced with the non-existence of linearly independent

such that
{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(f\overline{g}\right)=0
(see
[

, Proposition 2.2]

in the domain we have

, or equivalently, that

and

are perpendicular in

(endowed with the Euclidean product of

, resulting in the following useful reformulation of complex SPR (which we again state in

-spaces although it is valid more generally).

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
E
be a subspace of a complex

-space. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

E
fails SPR.

(2)

There exists
\delta>0
satisfying that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there are
f,g\in S_{E}
such that
\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\geq\delta
and
\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(f\overline{g}\right)\|<\varepsilon
.

Theorem

1.9
will play a central role in the proofs of both
Theorem

1.3
and

1.5

1.9
is that it transforms SPR in complex

-spaces (which is initially formulated as a nonlinear inverse problem) into a Banach

-algebra problem. Moreover, it does so in a way that is amenable to techniques from infinite combinatorics. We remark that although we are only interested in the commutative case, non-commutative variants of this criterion have relevance in cryo-electron microscopy

.

We now sketch the ideas in the proofs of our two main results:

and

The proof of

by extending the ideas from

Throughout
Section

3
we will make extensive use of
ordinal numbers
, so let us briefly recall some basic facts about these sets – we refer the reader to standard textbooks such as
[
25
]
for a detailed discussion of the topic.
Loosely speaking, ordinal numbers are a class of sets that can be linearly ordered by inclusion in such a way that every set of the class is the collection of all the sets that precede it; this family has a
smallest
element – the empty set – which will be denoted by
0
. More specifically, ordinal numbers satisfy that
\alpha<\beta
if and only if
\alpha
is an element of
\beta
, and every ordinal can be expressed as
\alpha=\{\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\beta<\alpha\}
. Given an ordinal
\alpha
, we can define its
successor ordinal
as
\alpha+1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha\cup\{\alpha\}
. If
\alpha\neq 0
is
not
a successor ordinal, then it is called a
limit ordinal
, and it can be written as
\alpha=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\beta
. Ordinal numbers admit arithmetical operations, such as addition, multiplication, and exponentiation. To be more concrete, starting at
0
, which is the empty set, we can first generate every natural number using the successor operation. Once they have been created, we may introduce the first infinite ordinal,
\omega=\mathbb{N}
, which is a limit ordinal. After
\omega
comes
\omega+1,\omega+2,\ldots,\omega+n,\ldots,\omega\cdot 2,\omega\cdot 2+1,\ldots,\omega\cdot n,\ldots,\omega^{2},\omega^{2}+1,\ldots,\omega^{2}+\omega,\ldots,\omega^{2}\cdot 2,\ldots,\omega^{n},\ldots,\omega^{\omega}
, and the list keeps going (see
[
17
, Chapter 10]
for a light introduction to the topic). The first uncountable ordinal is denoted by
\omega_{1}
. In this paper, we will only work with countable ordinals, so we will not be using ordinals beyond
\omega_{1}
. Every ordinal
\alpha>0
can be represented uniquely as

(1.4)

\alpha=w^{\beta_{1}}\cdot k_{1}+\ldots+\omega^{\beta_{n}}\cdot k_{n},

where
n\geq 1
,
\alpha\geq\beta_{1}>\ldots>\beta_{n}\geq 0
and
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}
are non-zero natural numbers. This representation is known as
Cantor’s normal form
. We can associate to every ordinal
\alpha
a totally disconnected compact Hausdorff topological space called the
ordinal interval

[0,\alpha]
, which can be described as the set
[0,\alpha]=\{\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0\leq\beta\leq\alpha\}
endowed with the topology generated by the base of open sets
\{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0\leq\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}\leq\alpha\}
, where
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]=\{\beta\in[0,\alpha]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\beta_{1}<\beta\leq\beta_{2}\}
. Note that the interval
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]
is a clopen set in this topology for every
\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}
. It should also be observed that
[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
is homeomorphic to
[0,\omega^{\alpha}]
whenever
\alpha\geq 1
. Thus,
C[0,\omega^{\alpha}]
and
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
are lattice isometric. It will be convenient for us to write
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
(and we will do so throughout the text) as it allows us to obtain a slightly simpler notation when using Cantor’s normal forms.

2.
Existence of SPR subspaces in complex
C(K)
-spaces

In this section, we extend two remarkable results from
[
21
]
concerning the existence of real SPR subspaces of
C(K)
to the complex setting:

\circ

C(K)
contains an infinite-dimensional SPR subspace if and only if
K^{\prime}
is infinite if and only if
C(K)
contains a subspace isometric to
c_{0}
that performs SPR (
Theorem

2.5
).

\circ

C(K)
contains an isomorphic SPR copy of every separable Banach space if and only if
K
contains a perfect set (
Theorem

2.10
).

The real counterpart of the above results can be inferred from the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 6.1]
. Therefore, our approach in this section will be to dissect that proof into different parts that we will adapt appropriately to the complex case. Unless stated otherwise, throughout this section, all the spaces considered will be complex Banach spaces or lattices.

2.1.
C[0,1]
is a universal SPR container of all separable Banach spaces

Let us recall that the well-known Banach-Mazur theorem establishes that
C[0,1]
contains every separable Banach space isometrically. Our aim is to show that these isometric embeddings can be made to do SPR. This was already proven in
[
21
, Proposition 6.2]
for real scalars. We begin by proving a separation lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
E
be a Banach space (real or complex) and
0<\delta<2
. Then for every
x\in S_{E}
and
y\in B_{E}
satisfying
\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|x-\lambda y\|\geq\delta
there exists
x^{*}\in S_{E^{*}}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(x)\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(y)\right\rvert}\right\rvert}\geq\frac{\delta}{3}
.

Proof.

We distinguish two cases. If
\|y\|\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{3}
, then we choose
x^{*}\in S_{E^{*}}
to be a norming functional for
x
, so that
x^{*}(x)=1
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(y)\right\rvert}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{*}\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{3}
. Otherwise, if
\|y\|>1-\frac{\delta}{3}
, then the distance of
y
to
\mathbb{F}x
(the span of
x
with
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
) must be bounded from below. Indeed, given
\mu\in\mathbb{F}
, we have one of the following three possibilities:

\circ

If
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}>1+\frac{\delta}{3}
, then we have

\|y-\mu x\|\geq\|\mu x\|-\|y\|\geq\frac{\delta}{3}.

\circ

If
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}<1-\frac{2\delta}{3}
, then we have

\|y-\mu x\|\geq\|y\|-\|\mu x\|\geq\frac{\delta}{3}.

\circ

If
1-\frac{2\delta}{3}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{3}
, then we have
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{2\delta}{3}
and

\|y-\mu x\|\geq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y-\frac{\mu}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}}x\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\left(\frac{\mu}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}}-\mu\right)x\right\rVert}\geq\delta-\frac{2\delta}{3}=\frac{\delta}{3}.

Thus,
d(y,\mathbb{F}x)\geq\frac{\delta}{3}
. Using the Hahn–Banach Theorem, we can find a functional
x^{*}\in S_{E^{*}}
such that
x^{*}(x)=0
and
x^{*}(y)=d(y,\mathbb{F}x)
, so the proof is concluded.
∎

Our next result generalizes
[
21
, Lemma 4.4]
to the complex setting.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any infinite cardinal
\kappa
there is a
12
-SPR isometric embedding of
\ell_{\infty}(\kappa)
into itself.

Proof.

Let us denote by
E=\ell_{\infty}(\kappa)
and
E_{*}=\ell_{1}(\kappa)
. Let
(z_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
be a dense set in
S_{E_{*}}
with
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert}=\kappa
, and define the operator
J\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E\rightarrow\ell_{\infty}(\Gamma)
by
Jx=(x(z_{\gamma}))_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
. By the density of
(z_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
it can be checked that
J
is an isometric embedding. Let us show that
J(E)
does SPR in
\ell_{\infty}(\Gamma)
, that is, there is some
C>0
such that for every
x,y\in E
,

\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|Jx-\lambda Jy\|\leq C\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jy\right\rvert}\|.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x\in S_{E}
,
y\in B_{E}
, and
\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|x-\lambda y\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Indeed, suppose that the inequality above is established (with some constant
C^{\prime}
) for all
x
and
y
that satisfy the aforementioned conditions. Then, given any
f,g\in E
we can construct vectors
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}
satisfying the properties listed in the statement of
[
21
, Theorem 3.10]
. In particular, denoting
x=\frac{f^{\prime}}{\|f^{\prime}\|}
and
y=\frac{g^{\prime}}{\|f^{\prime}\|}
, we get that

\displaystyle\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|Jf-\lambda Jg\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|Jf^{\prime}-\lambda Jg^{\prime}\|=\sqrt{2}\|f^{\prime}\|\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|Jx-\lambda Jy\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\|f^{\prime}\|C^{\prime}\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jy\right\rvert}\|=\sqrt{2}C^{\prime}\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jf^{\prime}\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jg^{\prime}\right\rvert}\|\leq\sqrt{2}C^{\prime}\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jf\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jg\right\rvert}\|,

so
J(E)
does SPR with constant
C=\sqrt{2}C^{\prime}
.

Now, if
x\in S_{E}
,
y\in B_{E}
and
\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|x-\lambda y\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, we can apply
Lemma

2.1
to obtain
x^{*}\in E^{*}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(x)\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(y)\right\rvert}\right\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}
. Using Goldstine’s theorem and the density of
(z_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
in
E_{*}
, we may find, for every
\varepsilon>0
, some
z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(x)-x(z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\right\rvert}<\varepsilon
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x^{*}(y)-y(z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\right\rvert}<\varepsilon
. Thus,
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x(z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y(z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\right\rvert}\right\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}-2\varepsilon
. It follows that

\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jy\right\rvert}\|\geq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x(z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y(z_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\right\rvert}\right\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}-2\varepsilon

for every
\varepsilon>0
, so that

6\sqrt{2}\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Jy\right\rvert}\|\geq 2\geq\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|Jx-\lambda Jy\|.

We can therefore take
C^{\prime}=6\sqrt{2}
and
C=12
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The same argument can be used to show that any Banach space
E
embeds isometrically into
C(B_{E^{*}})
as a
12
-SPR subspace by means of the canonical embedding that sends every
x\in E
to the evaluation function
x^{*}\mapsto x^{*}(x)
.

We now proceed to show that
C[0,1]
is a universal container for every (complex) separable Banach space.

Proposition 2.4
.

Every separable Banach space embeds isometrically into
C(\Delta)
as well as
C[0,1]
as a
12
-SPR subspace (here
\Delta
is the Cantor set).

Proof.

Given a separable Banach space
E
, let
K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(B_{E^{*}},w^{*})
and let
j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E\rightarrow C(K)
be the isometric embedding introduced in
2.3
. Recall that
\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert jx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert jy\right\rvert}\|\geq\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}
for every
x\in S_{E}
,
y\in B_{E}
such that
\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|x-\lambda y\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Since
E
is separable,
K
is a metrizable compact space. Hence,
[
26
, Theorem 4.18]
produces a continuous surjection
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Delta\rightarrow K
that defines a composition operator
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C(K)\rightarrow C(\Delta)
. In particular,
H
is a lattice isometric embedding. Now, since
\Delta\subseteq[0,1]
,
[
4
, Theorem 4.4.4]
provides an extension operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C(\Delta)\rightarrow C[0,1]
such that
\mathinner{Tf\rvert}_{\Delta}=f
,
T\mathds{1}=\mathds{1}
,
\|T\|=1
and
Tf\geq 0
for every
f\geq 0
(it is straightforward to check that this result is also valid in the complex setting). In particular,
T
is an isometric embedding. Moreover, given
x\in S_{E}
,
y\in B_{E}
such that
\min_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}=1}\|x-\lambda y\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
,
Lemma

2.1
together with the surjectivity of
h
implies that there is some
t\in\Delta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert jx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert jy\right\rvert}\|=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert jx(h(t))\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert jy(h(t))\right\rvert}\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Hjx(t)\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert Hjy(t)\right\rvert}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert THjx(t)\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert THjy(t)\right\rvert}\right\rvert}\leq\|\mathinner{\!\left\lvert THjx\right\rvert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert THjy\right\rvert}\|.

Repeating the argument used in
Lemma

2.2
we conclude that
THj(E)
does
12
-SPR in
C[0,1]
, and similarly
C(\Delta)
contains a
12
-SPR isometric copy of
E
.
∎

2.2.
Theorem

1.3
in the complex setting

We now turn our attention to seeking conditions on a compact Hausdorff space
K
that ensure the existence of (infinite-dimensional) SPR subspaces in
C(K)
. Our objective is to extend to the complex setting the following result proved in
[
21
]
for real scalars:
K^{\prime}
is infinite if and only if
C(K)
contains an SPR subspace. More precisely, we will show the following theorem announced in the introduction (
Theorem

1.3
).

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space. The following statements are equivalent over both the real and complex fields:

(i)

K^{\prime}
, the set of non-isolated points of
K
, is infinite.

(ii)

c_{0}
embeds isometrically into
C(K)
as an SPR subspace.

(iii)

C(K)
contains an infinite-dimensional SPR subspace.

We will present the proof of the above result at the end of this subsection, as a direct consequence of several partial results developed throughout the section. One of the most critical of these will be the next technical lemma, which clearly illustrates the additional difficulty inherent in studying
complex
SPR.

Inspired by the proof scheme in
[
21
, Theorem 6.1]
, where one embeds
c_{0}
into a
C(K)
-space (with
K^{\prime}
infinite) by creating overlapping points for any pair of natural numbers, we use the characterization of complex SPR from
Theorem

1.9
to prove that whenever an embedding between complex
C(K)
-spaces satisfies a certain overlapping property, then it will perform complex SPR.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
K,L
be compact Hausdorff spaces, and assume that the field of scalars is
\mathbb{C}
. Suppose that
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C(L)\rightarrow C(K)
is a contraction so that for every pair
(s,t)\in L\times L
there are
r_{s,t},\widetilde{r}_{s,t}\in K
such that
Tf(r_{s,t})=\frac{1}{2}(f(s)+f(t))
and
Tf(\widetilde{r}_{s,t})=\frac{1}{2}(f(s)+if(t))
. Then
T
is an SPR embedding.

Proof.

The fact that
T
is an isometry follows from the fact that
T
is contractive and
Tf(r_{s,s})=f(s)
for every
s\in L
. We claim that
T(C(L))
does complex SPR. By
Theorem

1.9
, this is equivalent to showing that there exists some
\delta>0
such that if
f,g\in S_{C(L)}
satisfy
\min_{|\lambda|=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\geq\frac{1}{5}
, then
\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq\delta
. Fix
f
and
g
as above and put
f(s)=A_{s}e^{i\theta_{s}}
and
g(s)=B_{s}e^{i\sigma_{s}}
for every
s\in L
. Let
0<c\ll 1
be a small parameter whose value will be established later. It will be useful for the rest of the proof to record the explicit values of
{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)
at the points
r_{s,t}
and
\widetilde{r}_{s,t}
for
s,t\in L
:

\displaystyle{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{s,t})=\frac{1}{4}[

\displaystyle A_{s}B_{s}\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})+A_{t}B_{t}\cos(\theta_{t}-\sigma_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle A_{t}B_{s}\cos(\theta_{t}-\sigma_{s})+A_{s}B_{t}\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{t})],

\displaystyle{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(\widetilde{r}_{s,t})=\frac{1}{4}[

\displaystyle A_{s}B_{s}\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})+A_{t}B_{t}\cos(\theta_{t}-\sigma_{t})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle A_{t}B_{s}\sin(\theta_{t}-\sigma_{s})+A_{s}B_{t}\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{t})].

Since both
f
and
g
have norm one, there exist some
s,t\in L
such that
A_{s}=1=B_{t}
. We distinguish two main cases:

Case A:

A_{t}>\frac{9}{10}
or
B_{s}>\frac{9}{10}
(where without loss of generality, we assume the latter).

\circ

If
|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})|\geq c
, then

\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{s,s})|=A_{s}B_{s}|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})|\geq\frac{9}{10}c.

\circ

If
|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})|<c
, then we use the fact that
\min_{|\lambda|=1}\|f-\lambda g\|\geq\frac{1}{5}
to find some
u\in L
such that

(2.1)

\frac{1}{5}\leq|f(u)-e^{i(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})}g(u)|\leq A_{u}+B_{u}.

Note that this
u
must be different from
s
, since
|f(s)-e^{i(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})}g(s)|=|A_{s}-B_{s}|=|1-B_{s}|<\frac{1}{10}
.

\square

If
B_{u}<c
, then necessarily
A_{u}\geq\frac{1}{5}-c
. Since either
|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|
or
|\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|
is greater or equal than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, evaluating at
r_{s,u}
or
\widetilde{r}_{s,u}
, respectively, and applying the triangle inequality, we get that

4\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq\frac{9}{10\sqrt{2}}A_{u}-3c\geq\frac{9}{50\sqrt{2}}-\mathinner{\Bigl(3+\frac{9}{10\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)}c>0.

\square

If
A_{u}<c
, we proceed analogously.

\square

If
A_{u},B_{u}\geq c
, we distinguish two cases:

\bullet

If
|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u})|\geq c
, then

\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{u,u})|=A_{u}B_{u}|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u})|\geq c^{3}.

\bullet

Otherwise, we are in a situation where
A_{u},B_{u}\geq c
and both
|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})|,|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u})|<c
. This case requires a more careful analysis than the previous ones, so we treat it separately.

Let
\Delta=(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})+(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u})=(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})+(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})
. Using standard trigonometric relations, it is clear that
|\cos\Delta|\geq 1-2c^{2}
and
|\sin\Delta|\leq 2c
.
Moreover, we can write

\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})=\cos\Delta\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})+\sin\Delta\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})

and

\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})=\sin\Delta\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})-\cos\Delta\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s}).

When
\cos\Delta>0
, we have
0\leq 1-\cos\Delta\leq 2c^{2}
, so that

(2.2)

\displaystyle|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})-\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle\leq 4c,

\displaystyle|\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})+\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle\leq 4c.

When
\cos\Delta<0
, it follows that
0\leq 1+\cos\Delta\leq 2c^{2}
, so instead we get

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})+\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle\leq 4c,

\displaystyle|\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})-\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle\leq 4c.

According to this dichotomy, we have the following conclusions:

\circ

If
\cos\Delta>0
, then evaluating at
r_{s,u}
, using the equations (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and invoking the assumption
B_{s}>\frac{9}{10}
we obtain that

\displaystyle 4|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{s,u})|

\displaystyle\geq|B_{u}\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})+A_{u}B_{s}\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq(A_{u}+B_{u})|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-A_{u}(1-B_{s})|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle-A_{u}|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})-\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{5}|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-\frac{1}{10}-6c,

while at
\widetilde{r}_{s,u}
we get

\displaystyle 4|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(\widetilde{r}_{s,u})|

\displaystyle\geq|B_{u}\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})-A_{u}B_{s}\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq(A_{u}+B_{u})|\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-A_{u}(1-B_{s})|\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle-A_{u}|\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})+\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{5}|\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-\frac{1}{10}-6c.

Therefore,

4\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq\frac{1}{5}\mathinner{\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)}-6c>0.

\circ

If
\cos\Delta<0
, then again by standard trigonometric relations it follows that

(2.4)

\cos((\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})-(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u}))=\cos(\Delta-2(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u}))\geq 1-6c^{2}.

We now split one last time into two cases:

\square

If
|A_{u}-B_{u}|\geq\frac{\sqrt{24}}{25}
, we can evaluate at
r_{s,u}
and
\widetilde{r}_{s,u}
and apply (
2.3
) to obtain

\displaystyle 4|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{s,u})|

\displaystyle\geq|B_{u}\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})+A_{u}B_{s}\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq|A_{u}-B_{u}||\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-A_{u}(1-B_{s})|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle-A_{u}|\cos(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})+\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{24}}{25}|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-\frac{1}{10}-6c,

and

\displaystyle 4|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(\widetilde{r}_{s,u})

\displaystyle|\geq|B_{u}\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})-A_{u}B_{s}\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq|A_{u}-B_{u}||\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-A_{u}(1-B_{s})|\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})|

\displaystyle-A_{u}|\sin(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{s})-\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-2c

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{24}}{25}|\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{u})|-\frac{1}{10}-6c,

respectively. Again, this implies that

4\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq\frac{1}{5}\mathinner{\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{12}}{5}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)}-6c>0.

\square

On the contrary, if
|A_{u}-B_{u}|<\frac{\sqrt{24}}{25}
, by (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{25}

\displaystyle\leq|A_{u}-B_{u}e^{i((\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})-(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u}))}|^{2}

\displaystyle=A_{u}^{2}+B_{u}^{2}-2A_{u}B_{u}\cos((\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})-(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u}))

\displaystyle=|A_{u}-B_{u}|^{2}+2A_{u}B_{u}[1-\cos((\theta_{s}-\sigma_{s})-(\theta_{u}-\sigma_{u}))]

\displaystyle<\frac{24}{25^{2}}+12c^{2}<\frac{1}{25},

which becomes a contradiction as long as we choose
c<\frac{1}{25\sqrt{12}}
. Therefore, this situation is not possible, and we have exhausted all of the possibilities within Case A.

Note that by choosing
0<c<\frac{1}{30}(\frac{\sqrt{12}}{5}-\frac{1}{2})
and
0<\delta\leq\min\{c^{3},\frac{1}{4}(\frac{1}{5}(\frac{\sqrt{12}}{5}-\frac{1}{2})-6c)\}
, all the conditions that have appeared in Case A are satisfied.

Case B:

A_{t}<\frac{9}{10}
and
B_{s}<\frac{9}{10}
(in particular,
s\neq t
).

\circ

If
B_{s}<c
, we examine
A_{t}
:

\square

If
A_{t}<c
, then

4|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{s,t})|\geq|\cos(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{t})|-3c

and

4|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(\widetilde{r}_{s,t})|\geq|\sin(\theta_{s}-\sigma_{t})|-3c,

so

4\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-3c>0.

\square

If
A_{t}\geq c
, we split again:

\bullet

If
|\cos(\theta_{t}-\sigma_{t})|\geq c
, then

\|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)\|\geq|{\,\mathfrak{Re}}\left(Tf\overline{Tg}\right)(r_{t,t})|\geq c^{2}.

\bullet

If
|\cos(\theta_{t}-\sigma_{t})|<c
, then evaluating again at either
r_{s,t}
or
\widetilde{r}_{s,t}
we obtain that

Lemma 2.9
.

Let

be a scattered compact Hausdorff space. If

is infinite, then

embeds lattice isometrically into

Proof.

Reproducing the argument from
[
21

it is clear that
K^{\prime}\setminus K^{\prime\prime}

K\setminus K^{\prime}

is a regular space, we can find a sequence of distinct points
(t_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq K^{\prime}\setminus K^{\prime\prime}

(U_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of

(V_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}

and

. Indeed, the sequence of partial sums
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(f(\omega\cdot(k-1)+j)-f(\omega\cdot k)\bigr)\chi_{\{s_{j,k}\}}
is Cauchy, so it converges. Moreover, each of the summands

F_{k}=f(\omega\cdot k)g_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(f(\omega\cdot(k-1)+j)-f(\omega\cdot k)\bigr)\chi_{\{s_{j,k}\}}

to what we have called

Remark 2.11
.

Let

be a Banach lattice. An interesting consequence of

may admit infinite-dimensional SPR subspaces even when the individual components

and

do not. Indeed, one may take

3.1.
A significant reduction of the problem

It was established in

for every

It is clear that the above condition guarantees the preservation of overlaps between any pair of functions. Thus, it can be easily verified that if

does

-SPR in

does

-SPR in

Let

In view of

We now extend the previously mentioned

Proposition 3.3

Let
\alpha<\omega_{1}
be a countable ordinal and

, there exists a positive linear isometry
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]\to C(K)

, and has the property (*), i.e., for every

there is
s_{t}\in K
such that

for every

Proof.

For the moment, we only consider
real scalars
, and at the end of the proof we will indicate how to handle the complex case. We will start by showing by transfinite induction the following claim:

Claim.

Let
\alpha<\omega_{1}
and let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space. If
V
is an open subset of
K
such that there is
s_{0}\in V\cap K^{(\alpha)}
, then there exists a positive linear isometry
S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}[1,\omega^{\alpha})\to C_{0}(V)
with the property (*), i.e., for every
t\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
there exists
s_{t}\in V
such that
Sf(s_{t})=f(t)
for every
f\in C_{0}[1,\omega^{\alpha})
. Moreover,
Sf(s_{0})=0
for every
f\in C_{0}[1,\omega^{\alpha})
.

Proof of the claim.

We will only prove the
limit case
, as the zero case and the successor case are already done in
[
22
, Proposition 3.4]
. Let
\alpha<\omega_{1}
be a limit ordinal and assume that the inductive hypothesis holds for every
\beta<\alpha
. Since
\alpha
is a countable limit ordinal, it has cofinality
\omega
, that is, there exists a sequence of strictly increasing ordinals
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\omega}
such that
\alpha=\sup_{n}\alpha_{n}
. In particular, writing
\omega^{\alpha_{0}}=0
we can decompose

[1,\omega^{\alpha})=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{n}}],

where each
(\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{n}}]
is a clopen set homeomorphic to
[1,\omega^{\alpha_{n}}]
. Similarly, we have
K^{(\alpha)}=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}K^{(\beta)}=\bigcap_{n\in\omega}K^{(\alpha_{n})}
. Now, let
V
be an open set of
K
that intersects
K^{(\alpha)}
, and fix
s_{0}\in V\cap K^{(\alpha)}
. We can choose for every
n
a point
s_{n}\in V\cap K^{(\alpha_{n})}
different from
s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}
. Using the normality of
K
, we can find pairwise disjoint open sets
V_{n}
such that
s_{n}\in V_{n}\subseteq V
and
s_{0}\notin V_{n}
. By the regularity of
K

\overline{V_{n}}\subseteq V
and select open neighborhoods
U_{n}
such that
s_{n}\in U_{n}\subseteq\overline{U_{n}}\subseteq V_{n}
. By the inductive hypothesis, there exists a positive isometry
S_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}(\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{n}})\rightarrow C_{0}(U_{n})
with the property (*); let
\widetilde{S_{n}}
be its extension by zero to
C_{0}(V)
. By Urysohn’s lemma, we can find
g_{n}\in C(K)
such that
0\leq g_{n}\leq 1
,
\left.g_{n}\right|_{\overline{U_{n}}}=1
and
\left.g_{n}\right|_{K\backslash V_{n}}=0
.
Observe that
\left.g_{n}\right|_{V}\in C_{0}(V)
and hence, for simplicity, we will also denote this restriction by
g_{n}
. For every

we can define

Sf\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(f(\omega^{\alpha_{n}})g_{n}+\widetilde{S_{n}}\bigl(\left.f\right|_{(\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{n}})}-f(\omega^{\alpha_{n}})\chi_{(\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{n}})}\bigr)\right).

Proceeding as in
[
22
, Proposition 3.5]
, it can be checked that
S
is a well-defined positive isometry with the property (*). Hence, the inductive hypothesis holds for every countable ordinal
\alpha
.
∎

Now, suppose that
K
is a compact Hausdorff space such that
K^{(\alpha)}\neq\varnothing
for some countable ordinal
\alpha\geq 1
(if
\alpha=0
, the proof of the proposition is trivial). We have seen that, given
s_{0}\in K^{(\alpha)}
, there is a positive linear isometry
S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}[1,\omega^{\alpha})\to C(K)
with the property (*) and such that
Tf(s_{0})=0
for every
f\in C_{0}[1,\omega^{\alpha})
. Thus, the operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]\to C(K)
defined by

Tf\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f(\omega^{\alpha})\mathds{1}_{K}+S\bigl(f|_{[1,\omega^{\alpha})}-f(\omega^{\alpha})\chi_{[1,\omega^{\alpha})}\bigr),\qquad f\in C[1,\omega^{\alpha}],

is a positive linear isometric embedding of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
into
C(K)
which preserves the unit and satisfies the property (*).

Finally, we show how to extend this proof from the real case to
complex scalars
. Let
\alpha<\omega_{1}
be a countable ordinal and suppose that
K^{(\alpha)}\neq\varnothing
. We have shown that there is a positive isometric embedding
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]\to C(K)
such that
T\mathds{1}=\mathds{1}
and
T
has the property (*). We claim that
T_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C([1,\omega^{\alpha}];\mathbb{C})\to C(K;\mathbb{C})
defined by
T_{\mathbb{C}}(f+ig)=Tf+iTg
, for
f,g\in C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
is a positive isometric
\mathbb{C}
-linear embedding with the desired properties. Recall that since
T
is positive, we have
\|T_{\mathbb{C}}\|=\|T\|=1
(see, for instance,
[
2
, Corollary 3.23]
). It is clear that
T_{\mathbb{C}}
satisfies the property (*) and from this we deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in K}|T_{\mathbb{C}}(f+ig)|(s)

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in K}\sqrt{|Tf(s)|^{2}+|Tg(s)|^{2}}\geq\sup_{t\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]}\sqrt{|Tf(s_{t})|^{2}+|Tg(s_{t})|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]}\sqrt{|f(t)|^{2}+|g(t)|^{2}}=\sup_{t\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]}|f+ig|(t)=\|f+ig\|,

and this shows that
T_{\mathbb{C}}
is norm-preserving. It is also immediate to check that
T_{\mathbb{C}}
preserves the unit.
∎

3.2.
A solution to the Question of Freeman et al. for countable ordinals

Cantor’s normal form allows us to express uniquely every ordinal
0<\alpha<\omega_{1}
as a finite combination of ordinals of the form
\omega^{\beta}
with non-zero natural coefficients. Since
\omega^{\beta}\cdot 0=0
for every
\beta
, the representation (
1.4
) can be understood as a bijection from
\omega_{1}
into
c_{00}(\omega_{1},\mathbb{Z}_{+})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigl\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\omega_{1}\to\mathbb{Z}_{+}\>\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\>|\{\gamma\>\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\>x(\gamma)\neq 0\}|<\aleph_{0}\bigr\}
, that is, the set of sequences of non-negative integers indexed in
\omega_{1}
with finite support. Observe that, in general, the coordinate-wise sum in the set
c_{00}(\omega_{1},\mathbb{Z}_{+})
does not coincide with the usual sum of ordinal numbers. Given
\alpha,\beta\in\omega_{1}
, we denote by
\alpha\oplus\beta
the ordinal obtained by adding the normal forms of
\alpha
and
\beta
in
c_{00}(\omega_{1},\mathbb{Z}_{+})
. This operation
\oplus\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\omega_{1}\times\omega_{1}\rightarrow\omega_{1}
is associative, symmetric (in contrast to the usual sum of ordinals) and compatible with the order (if
\alpha\leq\beta
, then
\alpha\oplus\gamma\leq\beta\oplus\gamma
for every
\gamma\in\omega_{1}
). Moreover, it satisfies that
\alpha\oplus 1=\alpha+1
.

We will use this operation to show that for every countable ordinal
\alpha
,
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
embeds isometrically into
C[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]
doing SPR in the real setting, and
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
embeds isometrically into
C[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}\cdot 2]
doing SPR in the complex setting, where
\alpha\odot 2=\alpha\oplus\alpha
, with a constant independent of
\alpha
. Our first step is the following construction:

Lemma 3.4
.

For every pair of ordinals
\alpha,\beta\in\omega_{1}
there is a map
U_{\alpha,\beta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]\times C[1,\omega^{\beta}]\rightarrow C[1,\omega^{\alpha\oplus\beta}]
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

for every
f_{1},f_{2}\in C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
and
g_{1},g_{2}\in C[1,\omega^{\beta}]
,

U_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{1}+f_{2},g_{1}+g_{2})=U_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{1},g_{1})+U_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{2},g_{2});

(ii)

for every
f\in C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
,
g\in C[1,\omega^{\beta}]
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

U_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda f,\lambda g)=\lambda U_{\alpha,\beta}(f,g);

(iii)

for every
f\in C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
and
g\in C[1,\omega^{\beta}]
,

\|U_{\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\|f\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\bigr);

(iv)

for every
s\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
and
t\in[1,\omega^{\beta}]
there exists
r_{s,t}\in[1,\omega^{\alpha\oplus\beta}]
such that

[U_{\alpha,\beta}(f,g)](r_{s,t})=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f(s)+g(t)\bigr).

(v)

for every
r\in[1,\omega^{\alpha\oplus\beta}]
there exists
s_{r}\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
and
t_{r}\in[1,\omega^{\beta}]
such that

[U_{\alpha,\beta}(f,g)](r)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f(s_{r})+g(t_{r})\bigr).

Proof.

First of all, we observe that for any pair
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\omega_{1}
, the construction of the map
U_{\beta,\alpha}
is automatic once the existence of the map
U_{\alpha,\beta}
is established. Indeed, for
f\in C[1,\omega^{\beta}]
and
g\in C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
we define

U_{\beta,\alpha}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=U_{\alpha,\beta}(g,f).

The existence of the map
U_{\alpha,\beta}
for every
\alpha\leq\beta
is proved by means of two nested transfinite induction arguments, one for
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
and another one for
\beta\geq\alpha
:

Inductive Hypothesis (IH):
Given
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
, if
U_{\gamma,\delta}
is defined for every
\gamma<\alpha
and
\delta\geq\gamma
, then
U_{\alpha,\beta}
exists for every
\beta\geq\alpha
.

1.

Base case (
\alpha=0
):
Given
\beta\geq 0
, it is straightforward to check that

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}{U_{0,\beta}}&\colon&{C(\{1\})\times C[1,\omega^{\beta}]}&\longrightarrow&{C[1,\omega^{\beta}]}\\
\mbox{}&\mbox{}&{(f,g)}&\longmapsto&{\frac{1}{2}(f(1)+g)}\end{array}

is well-defined and satisfies the properties of the statement.

2.

Inductive step (
\alpha\to\alpha+1
):
Assume that (IH) has been established for
\alpha

\alpha+1
by a transfinite induction argument on
\beta\geq\alpha+1

Inductive Hypothesis (IH-I):
Fix
\alpha\in\omega_{1}

2.1)

Base case (

We construct

\displaystyle U_{\alpha+1,\alpha+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle|_{(\omega^{\alpha\odot 2+1}\cdot(2n-2),\omega^{\alpha\odot 2+1}\cdot(2n-1)]}=\,U_{\alpha+1,\alpha}\bigl(f|_{(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha+1}]},g|_{(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n]}\bigr),n\in\mathbb{N};

\displaystyle U_{\alpha+1,\alpha+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle|_{(\omega^{\alpha\odot 2+1}\cdot(2n-1),\omega^{\alpha\odot 2+1}\cdot 2n]}=\,U_{\alpha,\alpha+1}\bigl(f|_{(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n]},g|_{(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha+1}]}\bigr),n\in\mathbb{N};

\displaystyle|_{\{\omega^{(\alpha+1)\odot 2}\}}=\,\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f(\omega^{\alpha+1})+g(\omega^{\alpha+1})\bigr).

Clearly, the function defined above satisfies conditions (i), (ii), (iii) and (v). Let us check that it is continuous on
[1,\omega^{(\alpha+1)\odot 2}]

(\omega^{\alpha\odot 2+1}\cdot(m-1),\omega^{\alpha\odot 2+1}\cdot m]
with

there exists

\left|[U_{\alpha+1,\alpha+1}(f,g)](\omega^{(\alpha+1)\odot 2})-[U_{\alpha+1,\alpha+1}(f,g)](r)\right|<\varepsilon.

In other words,

is continuous on

because of property (v) and the fact that for every

. Finally, property (iv) follows from the fact that

and

also satisfy (iv), and

can be decomposed as

\displaystyle[1,\omega^{(\alpha+1)\odot 2}]^{2}=\mathinner{\biggl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha+1}]\times(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n]\biggr)}

\displaystyle\cup\mathinner{\biggl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n]\times(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha+1}]\biggr)}\cup\{(\omega^{(\alpha+1)\odot 2},\omega^{(\alpha+1)\odot 2})\}.

The continuity of the function

and properties (i) to (v) are checked in a similar way to the rest of the steps, so we will omit the details.

2.2)

Inductive step (

):
Assuming that

\displaystyle U_{\alpha+1,\beta+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle|_{(\omega^{\alpha\oplus\beta+1}\cdot(2n-2),\omega^{\alpha\oplus\beta+1}\cdot(2n-1)]}=\,U_{\alpha+1,\beta}\bigl(f|_{(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\alpha+1}]},g|_{(\omega^{\beta}\cdot(n-1),\omega^{\beta}\cdot n]}\bigr),n\in\mathbb{N};

\displaystyle U_{\alpha+1,\beta+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle|_{\{\omega^{(\alpha+1)\oplus(\beta+1)}\}}=\,\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f(\omega^{\alpha+1})+g(\omega^{\beta+1})\bigr).

Limit case (
\beta

Now that we have built an overlapping map for every ordinal
\alpha
, we can use it to obtain the following isometric and isomorphic results in the real setting.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space,
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
a countable ordinal and assume that the field of scalars is
\mathbb{R}
. If
K^{(\alpha\odot 2)}
is non-empty, then there exists an isometric
3
-SPR embedding of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
into
C(K)
.

Proof.

By
3.3
,
C(K)
contains an isometric copy of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]
that preserves SPR subspaces, so without loss of generality we can assume that
K=[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]
. By
Lemma

3.4
, there is a map
U_{\alpha,\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]\times C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]\rightarrow C[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]
satisfying properties (i) to (v). Let

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}{T}&\colon&{C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]}&\longrightarrow&{C[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]}\\
\mbox{}&\mbox{}&{f}&\longmapsto&{Tf=U_{\alpha,\alpha}(f,f).}\end{array}

Properties (i) and (ii) imply that
T
is a linear map. Moreover, (iii) implies that
T
is contractive, and (iv) yields that for every
s\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
there exists
r_{s,s}\in[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]
such that
Tf(r_{s,s})=f(s)
, so
T
is an isometric embedding. It remains to show that
T(C[1,\omega^{\alpha}])
does
3
-SPR in
C[1,\omega^{\alpha\odot 2}]
. Let
f
and
g
be normalized functions in
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
, and find
s,t\in[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
such that
|f(s)|=|g(t)|=1
.

\circ

If
s=t
, then
|Tf(r_{s,s})|\wedge|Tg(r_{s,s})|=|f(s)|\wedge|g(s)|=1
.

\circ

If
s\neq t
, we distinguish three cases:

\square

If

, then

|f(t)|\geq|f(s)|-|f(s)+f(t)|=1-2|Tf(r_{s,t})|>\frac{1}{3},

|Tf(r_{t,t})|\wedge|Tg(r_{t,t})|=|f(t)|\wedge|g(t)|\geq\frac{1}{3}
.

\square

If
|Tg(r_{s,t})|<\frac{1}{3}
, then

so
|Tf(r_{s,s})|\wedge|Tg(r_{s,s})|=|f(s)|\wedge|g(s)|\geq\frac{1}{3}
.

\square

Otherwise,
|Tf(r_{s,t})|\wedge|Tg(r_{s,t})|\geq\frac{1}{3}
.

Hence, there is always a point

such that
\||Tf|\wedge|Tg|\|_{\infty}\geq|Tf(r)|\wedge|Tg(r)|\geq\frac{1}{3}
, and we conclude that
T(C[1,\omega^{\alpha}])
does not contain normalized
\frac{1}{3}
-almost disjoint pairs, so, by
[
11
, Proposition 3.4]
, it does
3
-SPR.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
0<\alpha<\omega_{1}
be a countable ordinal,
K
a compact Hausdorff space with
K^{(\alpha)}\neq\varnothing
, and assume the field of scalars is
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\beta\in\omega_{1}
such that
\omega^{\beta}\leq\alpha<\omega^{\beta+1}
. Then,
C(K)
contains an isometric SPR copy of
C[1,\omega^{\gamma}]
for every
\gamma<\omega^{\beta}
. Moreover, if
\omega^{\beta}\cdot 2\leq\alpha
, then
C(K)
contains an isomorphic SPR copy of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
.

Proof.

Using Cantor’s normal form (
1.4
) it can be easily checked that for every
\gamma<\omega^{\beta}
, we have
\gamma\odot 2<\omega^{\beta}\leq\alpha
, so using
Theorem

3.5
we obtain a
3
-SPR isometric embedding of
C[1,\omega^{\gamma}]
into
C[1,\omega^{\gamma\odot 2}]
, which in turn is a sublattice of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
. By
3.3
,
C(K)
contains a
3
-SPR isometric copy of
C[1,\omega^{\gamma}]
as well.

Next, assume that
\omega^{\beta}\cdot 2\leq\alpha
. It was established in
[
10
, Theorem 1]
that for ordinals
\omega\leq\lambda\leq\mu<\omega_{1}
,
C[1,\lambda]
is isomorphic (as a Banach space) to
C[1,\mu]
if and only if
\mu<\lambda^{\omega}
. In particular, it can be checked that
\omega^{\alpha}<(\omega^{\omega^{\beta}})^{\omega}
, so
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
and
C[1,\omega^{\omega^{\beta}}]
are linearly isomorphic. By
Theorem

3.5
, we know that
C[1,\omega^{\omega^{\beta}\cdot 2}]
, which is a sublattice of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
, contains an SPR copy of
C[1,\omega^{\omega^{\beta}}]
, and hence, of
C[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
.
3.3
yields the result.
∎
