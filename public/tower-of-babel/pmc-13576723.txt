Accelerated Volume-Staged Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Large Cerebral Arteriovenous Malformations: A Single Institutional Experience
Authors: Ramesh Keerthi Gadam, Timothy E Stepp, Hongyu Jiang, Adip Bhargav, Megan Smith, Rajeev Badkul, Jeremy Peterson, Michael Abraham, Ernest Madarang, Alan Reeves, Ying Cao, Koji Ebersole, Ronny L Rotondo, Dima Soultan, Fen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLarge cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) pose significant management challenges, particularly in achieving complete nidus obliteration without increasing the risk of symptomatic necrosis. Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is an established, noninvasive treatment for inoperable AVMs. However, for large AVMs, single-stage SRS is complicated by larger volumes of irradiated normal brain and increased radiation-induced toxicity. Here, we describe our institution’s accelerated volume-staged SRS (AVS-SRS) strategy for large cerebral AVMs and report long-term clinical outcomes.Methods and MaterialsA retrospective analysis was performed on 19 patients with cerebral AVMs (≥6 cm3) treated with AVS-SRS at the University of Kansas Medical Center between 2009 and 2024. AVMs were divided into 2 to 3 subtargets, which were treated sequentially using an AVS-SRS approach at 2- to 3-week intervals. The prescription dose to the entire nidus ranged from 16 Gy to 24 Gy. Treatments were delivered on a stereotactic-dedicated linear accelerator platform. Annual follow-up included magnetic resonance imaging/magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and a confirmatory cerebral arteriogram if MRA showed resolution of the nidus. One patient was excluded from outcome analysis due to a lack of follow-up evaluation. Outcomes included nidus obliteration, symptomatic radiation necrosis, posttreatment hemorrhage, and permanent neurological deficits.ResultsThe median patient age was 38.5 years, with a median follow-up of 44.5 months. The median treated AVM volume was 9.6 ± 8.4 cm3. Angiographic- and MRA-confirmed complete resolution was achieved in 5 patients (27.7%), MRA-confirmed resolution was achieved in 2 patients (11.1%), and a partial response was achieved in 11 (61.1%) patients; 1 patient was not evaluated after treatment due to comorbidities. Three patients developed symptomatic radiation necrosis (16.6%); 1 developed acute neurological symptoms after the first stage, and 2 had radiographic necrosis, both resolving completely with corticosteroids. There were no post-AVS-SRS hemorrhage sequelae.ConclusionsAVS-SRS with a 2- to 3-week interstage interval represents a novel treatment approach for large cerebral AVMs. It demonstrated feasible obliteration outcomes with acceptable toxicity, including a low risk of symptomatic necrosis and no post-AVS-SRS hemorrhage, in this small retrospective cohort.

Introduction

Cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are complex vascular anomalies that present significant management challenges due to their potential for intracranial hemorrhage and the morbidity associated with available therapeutic interventions. Large AVMs—commonly defined as those exceeding 10 cm3 in volume—carry an elevated hemorrhagic risk, largely due to their size and frequent involvement of eloquent or deep brain structures.1, 2, 3, 4 Invasive treatments, including microsurgical resection and endovascular embolization, are often limited by high procedural and postoperative morbidity.

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), which delivers a high dose of focused radiation in a single session, has emerged as an effective noninvasive treatment for small- to moderate-volume AVMs, demonstrating high obliteration rates and reduced hemorrhagic risk compared with invasive modalities.4, 5, 6

However, management of large AVMs with single-session SRS remains challenging, as achieving complete nidus obliteration typically requires higher doses that increase the risk of radiation-induced neurologic toxicity, including symptomatic radiation necrosis (S-RN).6,7 The irradiated brain volume receiving 12 Gy (V12 Gy) has been directly correlated with the risk of developing S-RN.8

To minimize this risk, staged SRS techniques have been introduced to allow delivery of therapeutic doses while limiting radiation exposure to normal brain tissue. Two primary approaches are described in the literature: volume-staged SRS (VS-SRS) and dose-staged SRS (DS-SRS). In VS-SRS, the AVM nidus is divided into 2 or 3 subtargets, each treated independently with full SRS dose prescriptions, typically separated by 2 to 6 months between stages.9,10

Conversely, DS-SRS delivers lower fractional doses to the entire nidus over a small number of sessions.11

Here, we describe a novel approach—accelerated volume-staged SRS (AVS-SRS)—developed primarily for the treatment of large-volume cerebral AVMs and secondarily for AVMs in critical locations for high bleeding risk and high risk of neurological sequelae, in which the nidus is divided into 2 or 3 subtargets treated sequentially with intervals of only 2 to 3 weeks. This approach aims to achieve effective nidus obliteration while minimizing normal brain dose and treatment-related morbidity. We report radiographic and clinical outcomes, including nidus resolution, posttreatment hemorrhage, neurological sequelae, and incidence of S-RN.

Methods and Materials

Study design

This retrospective study (STUDY00141133) received a waiver from the University of Kansas Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB00000161) to evaluate treatment outcomes in patients with large-volume cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) treated with accelerated volume-staged stereotactic radiosurgery (AVS-SRS) between 2009 and 2024.

Patient selection and clinical history

Nineteen patients with large-volume AVMs were treated with AVS-SRS. Selection criteria were:

1.AVM nidus volume ≥6 cm3;

AVM nidus volume ≥6 cm3;

2.Not surgical candidates due to Spetzler-Martin grade III to V AVMs; and

Not surgical candidates due to Spetzler-Martin grade III to V AVMs; and

3.AVMs adjacent to critical structures where single-session SRS posed excessive risk.

AVMs adjacent to critical structures where single-session SRS posed excessive risk.

Ten patients (52.6%) did not undergo prior surgical intervention, embolization, or clipping before AVS-SRS, including 1 patient in whom embolization was attempted but aborted due to a high rupture risk. Two patients (10.5%) underwent aneurysm clipping before AVS-SRS. Seven patients (36.8%) had prior embolization, resulting in no change in 1 case and a reduction in AVM nidus size ranging from 0.2 to 0.7 cm3 in the remaining cases. Two patients (10.5%) required external ventricular drain placement, and 1 patient had a longstanding ventriculoperitoneal shunt (Table 1).

Treatment planning and delivery

Patients were immobilized with an SRS-dedicated thermoplastic mask and simulated on a Philips Large Bore CT simulator (Philips Healthcare) with intravenous contrast. Simulation computed tomography angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and cerebral arteriogram were coregistered within the Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS) (Varian Medical Systems) and Brainlab Angio Element software (Brainlab SE, Munich, Germany) to facilitate treatment planning.

The AVM nidus was delineated collaboratively by a radiation oncologist and neurosurgeon using fused multimodal imaging.11, 12, 13 Large-volume AVMs were divided into 2 to 3 subtargets, respecting each nidus’s unique angioarchitecture and surrounding critical structures (Fig. 1). Each subtarget, typically measuring 3 to 4 cm3, was treated sequentially in an accelerated volume-staged approach at 2- to 3-week intervals. The cumulative prescription dose to the entire nidus ranged from 16 Gy to 24 Gy (Fig. 2).

Before mid-2020, 9 patients were planned using intensity modulated radiation therapy in the iPlan dedicated SRS TPS (Brainlab SE, Munich, Germany). After mid-2020, we moved to volumetric modulated arc therapy, where 10 patients were planned using the Eclipse TPS. The planning approach and goals were the same for both TPS platforms. In each stage, optimization prioritized minimizing dose spillage to adjacent prior or subsequent subtargets while achieving acceptable coverage of the individual subtarget and full cumulative coverage of the entire nidus, with the goal of maximizing the conformity index (CI) and minimizing the gradient index (GI).

Both individual and composite plans were evaluated for target coverage, CI, GI, and brain V12 Gy. Organ-at-risk (OAR) tolerances were respected, and all plans were peer-reviewed and approved by the radiation oncologist before treatment delivery.

All treatments were delivered on a linear accelerator (LINAC) equipped with a high-definition multileaf collimator, a 6-degree-of-freedom robotic couch, and image guided radiation therapy (IGRT) with cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), ExacTrac System (Brainlab SE, Munich, Germany) on Novalis Tx (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA), and later surface-guided radiation therapy Identify 3.0 System on Edge Truebeam (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA). Each AVS-SRS session was delivered with both the radiation oncologist and neurosurgeon present at the LINAC.10

Clinical and radiographic follow-up

After AVS-SRS, patients underwent close clinical follow-up at 6 months and annually thereafter. During each follow-up visit, patients were evaluated for symptoms of radiation-induced neurologic toxicity or S-RN. Patients underwent annual MRA after AVS-SRS. Follow-up MRAs were reviewed for evidence of AVM nidus response or radiation-induced changes.

Patients demonstrating complete nidus obliteration on MRA were referred for a confirmatory cerebral arteriogram to verify complete resolution (CR) of the AVM.

Treatment outcome measures

The primary outcome was complete AVM obliteration (CR) confirmed by MRA and cerebral arteriogram. Secondary outcomes included partial response (≥50% nidus reduction), S-RN, posttreatment hemorrhage, neurological sequelae, and need for repeat SRS or incomplete nidus obliteration.

CR on cerebral arteriogram was defined as complete angiographic disappearance of the nidus with no evidence of early venous drainage.

Patients who developed S-RN were treated with steroids and Bevacizumab if the steroid treatment did not stop the necrosis. Subsequent improvement in S-RN symptoms, and the presence of any persistent neurological deficit secondary to S-RN were evaluated and recorded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze patient and treatment characteristics in this small cohort. Continuous variables were reported as the mean and SD or the median and range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as counts (n) and percentages (%). Fisher’s exact test and Wilcoxon rank sum test for S-RN occurrence, comparing AVM volume, CI, GI, and V12 indices, were performed, and a probability (P) value <.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Patients’ clinical characteristics

Nineteen patients with a median age of 38.5 ± 14 years, 6 (31.6%) male patients, and 13 (68.4%) female patients were included in this study. Median follow-up was 44.5 months. Median AVM nidus volume was 9.6 ± 8.4 cm3.

AVMs were predominantly located in noneloquent regions (94.7%), most commonly multilobar supratentorial locations (36.8%). One AVM (5.2%) was in the left basal ganglia.

The most common initial presentations of neurological symptoms were focal neurological deficits (68.4%), headaches (47.4%), intracranial hemorrhage (36.8%), and seizures (36.8%). Hemiparesis and syncope were each present in 2 patients (10.5%). Overall, 18 patients (94.7%) presented with 1 or more neurological symptoms (Table 1).

Dosimetric results

Thirteen patients (68.4%) underwent treatment in 2 stages, and 6 (31.6%) in 3 stages. The median subtarget volumes were 4.3 ± 2.8 cm3, 4 ± 2.7 cm3, and 4.7 ± 3.6 cm3 for the first, second, and third subtargets, respectively.

The AVS-SRS dose was prescribed to the 80% isodose line encompassing the entire nidus, with a median prescription dose of 20 ± 1.5 Gy, a median maximum dose of 27.7 ± 4.6 Gy. Median CI was 1.3 ± 0.3, GI 3.3 ± 0.4, and V12 30.7 ± 20.5 cm3.

AVM nidus obliteration outcomes

One patient was excluded from the outcome analysis because posttreatment follow-up was unavailable due to comorbidities. Among the remaining 18 patients, CR of the AVM nidus, confirmed by cerebral arteriography, was achieved in 5 patients (27.7%) after a median follow-up of 53 months, and 2 patients (11.1%) demonstrated complete obliteration on MRA at a median follow-up of 30.5 months. Cerebral arteriogram-confirmed resolution (n = 5) was considered the definitive endpoint, whereas MRA-confirmed complete obliteration (n = 2) was considered a secondary, provisional endpoint pending angiographic confirmation to exclude residual early draining veins not detectable on conventional MRA sequences.

Partial response was observed in 11 patients (61.1%) on follow-up MRA with a median follow-up of 35 months (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

Radiation-induced toxicity

Three patients developed S-RN after AVS-SRS treatment, occurring at intervals of 20 months, 2 days, and 20 months, respectively. One patient with a peri-Rolandic AVM (16.4 cm3; V12 = 28.5 cm3) developed acute symptoms after the first stage of AVS-SRS (National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [CTCAE] grade 3 radiation necrosis) and was treated with corticosteroids promptly. She subsequently developed radiographic radiation necrosis and left side weakness. She was then treated with a course of Bevacizumab (Avastin), which resulted in stabilization of neurological deficits and radiographic necrosis (the neurologic sequelae of left-sided weakness were permanent). The subsequent MRA follow-up showed partial response of the AVM nidus at 28 months. The other 2 patients developed headaches with imaging-confirmed necrosis and achieved CR with corticosteroids (CTCAE grade 2 radiation necrosis).

No associations were observed between S-RN status and AVM volume, CI, GI, or V12, although the analysis was limited by the small sample size (all P > .05; Table 2). There was no posttreatment hemorrhage, and all the patients survived through the follow-up period.

Discussion

Large-volume AVMs, typically defined as lesions with a nidus volume greater than 10 cm3, remain among the most challenging cerebrovascular anomalies to treat. Their management is complicated by deep anatomical location, proximity to critical OARs, and a complex, tortuous vascular architecture that is associated with an increased risk of hemorrhage. Furthermore, large AVMs carry a substantial risk of severe neurological sequelae when treatment is complicated by radiation-induced injury or hemorrhage. Surgical resection is often not feasible because of the extensive operative exposure required, the high risk of intraoperative bleeding, and the potential for injury to eloquent neural structures.1,3,4

In selected patients, targeted endovascular embolization followed by SRS provides an effective alternative for AVMs considered unsuitable for surgical resection. This combined approach offers reasonable rates of nidus obliteration while avoiding the morbidity associated with open surgery.14

A large nidus volume remains one of the strongest predictors of poor obliteration following single-fraction SRS. Previous studies have demonstrated that optimal obliteration rates are associated with marginal doses of at least 17 Gy, with ≥63% of the AVM volume receiving doses of ≥20 Gy.15,16 However, treatment of large AVMs with single-fraction SRS inevitably increases the volume of surrounding normal brain tissue receiving ≥12 Gy (V12 Gy), which is strongly associated with a higher risk of radiation-induced complications, including S-RN.6,8

To address these limitations, staged SRS techniques have been increasingly adopted in clinical practice. These approaches aim to deliver a therapeutically effective radiation dose to the AVM nidus while minimizing radiation exposure to adjacent normal brain tissue, thereby improving obliteration rates and reducing the risk of radiation necrosis and subsequent neurological morbidity.17,18

Two principal staged SRS strategies are currently employed: DS-SRS, also referred to as hypofractionated SRS, and VS-SRS. DS-SRS delivers the prescribed radiation dose over multiple fractions using lower doses per fraction, thereby exploiting radiobiological advantages that may decrease the risk of radiation-induced injury while maintaining an acceptable therapeutic effect.19

Although DS-SRS yields lower obliteration rates than VS-SRS, it is associated with a more favorable safety profile and lower rates of radiation-related toxicity, particularly for lesions located adjacent to critical OARs.16,19, 20, 21

Conversely, in VS-SRS, the AVM nidus is divided into distinct anatomical subtargets that are treated sequentially in separate sessions spaced weeks to months apart. This strategy permits delivery of an effective marginal dose to each subtarget while limiting the volume of surrounding normal brain exposed to high-dose radiation (V12 Gy) during any single treatment session, thereby reducing the risk of S-RN.

As summarized in Table 3, VS-SRS has been used for large cerebral AVMs with median nidus volumes ranging from 18 to 24 cm3, typically delivered in 2 to 4 treatment stages separated by intervals of 3 to 6 months. Median prescription doses per stage in earlier reports ranged from 16 to 18 Gy, whereas more recent studies have demonstrated a trend toward modest dose escalation. Reported complete obliteration rates vary, ranging from 12.9% to 61.4%, reflecting differences in lesion size, treatment planning strategies, follow-up duration, and radiographic criteria used to define obliteration.15 Partial response rates have generally been high, particularly in studies employing less stringent volumetric criteria for treatment response. Across published series, the incidence of S-RN has ranged from 10% to 15%, whereas posttreatment hemorrhage during the latency period has been reported in 12% to 20% of patients in several cohorts.15,19

The present study differs from prior series by employing a short interstage interval (2-3 weeks) for AVS-SRS, a higher median prescription dose (20 Gy), and treatment of smaller median AVM volumes. At a median follow-up of 44.5 months, complete angiographic-confirmed obliteration was achieved in 27.7% of patients, and MRA-confirmed nidus resolution was achieved in 11.1% of patients who were referred for a confirmatory cerebral arteriogram to confirm complete obliteration. A partial response was observed in 61.1% of patients. Notably, no patients experienced posttreatment hemorrhage or treatment-related mortality. Despite the higher per-stage prescription dose and accelerated treatment schedule, the rate of S-RN (16.6%) was comparable with that reported in previous VS-SRS series.

In the conventional VS-SRS paradigm, treatment stages are typically separated by 2 to 9 months and planned independently. This prolonged interval between stages permits radiation-induced vascular remodeling and morphological changes within the nidus, often necessitating reimaging and replanning. Such replanning may introduce dosimetric uncertainty at the interfaces between subtargets, resulting in either interstage dose overlap or under coverage. AVS-SRS treatment planning strategy differs from the conventional VS-SRS. In this concept, we adopted the CyberKnife-based dosimetric framework described by Ding et al, in which the AVM nidus is partitioned into subtargets that are treated sequentially over a short interval of (1-4) weeks using a single composite plan of individual subtargets plans optimized simultaneously. This approach achieved a composite planning target volume coverage of 96.3% ± 0.9% in dosimetric analyses. Specifically, we adapted the concept of sequential subtarget treatment over a short interstage interval to a LINAC workflow, incorporating intensity modulated radiation therapy/volumetric modulated arc therapy-based joint and simultaneous subtargets planning and optimization, LINAC-specific dose-gradient management, and composite dose assessment across treated subtargets.

Although Ding et al’s22 clinical outcomes using the described strategy have not yet been reported, the dosimetric results provided a strong rationale for clinical implementation using a modified LINAC-based approach.

This approach offers practical and logistical advantages, including completion of treatment using a single imaging data set (CTA, MRI, MRA, and digital subtraction angiography), thereby minimizing image-registration uncertainty. It also facilitates simultaneous planning and prospective optimization of all subtargets, thereby improving control of interstage dose overlap, undercoverage, and cumulative dose distribution. Using a single treatment plan imaging data set within a compressed timeframe to deliver all stages preserves the intended dose distribution and maintains consistent target coverage throughout the entire nidus. Furthermore, treatment of smaller subvolumes at each session reduces the volume of surrounding normal brain exposed to high radiation doses (V12), potentially lowering the risk of radiation necrosis. This principle is well established in the brain metastasis literature, where staged with short interstage interval or multifraction SRS for lesions’ diameter larger than 2 cm has been associated with significantly lower radionecrosis rates than single-fraction treatment (7.3% vs 23.1%).23

The interstage interval may also have important hemodynamic implications. The American Heart Association/American Stroke Association has emphasized that partial AVM obliteration does not confer protection against hemorrhage. Likewise, evidence from the embolization literature suggests that incomplete treatment alters intranidal hemodynamics, potentially increasing vascular instability and rupture risk, particularly during the first year after treatment. In conventional VS-SRS, prolonged interstage intervals may similarly allow partial radiation-induced vascular changes to modify pressure gradients within the untreated nidus while leaving the lesion incompletely treated, with reported latency hemorrhage rates of 3.02 per 100 patient-years.9 By compressing the interval between treatment stages to 2 to 3 weeks, our approach substantially shortens this period of potential hemodynamic vulnerability and may promote a near-concomitant endothelial radiobiological response across all staged nidus subtargets, more closely approximating the biological response profile of complete nidus irradiation while preserving the dosimetric advantages of volume staging. Consistent with this hypothesis, no interval or latency hemorrhages were observed during follow-up in the present cohort. Although our sample size is limited and definitive conclusions cannot be drawn, these findings suggest that AVS-SRS may offer a theoretical safety advantage by minimizing the duration of partial treatment while preserving favorable dosimetric characteristics.

Compared with previously published volume-staged SRS (VS-SRS) series, our findings suggest that AVS-SRS can achieve competitive obliteration outcomes without increasing hemorrhagic risk or mortality. Notably, no rebleeding events were observed in this cohort despite the recognized risk of latent-period hemorrhage reported in prior studies. This finding may reflect reduced perturbation of AVM hemodynamics associated with the shorter staging intervals employed in our AVS-SRS approach.17, 18, 19,24

It is noteworthy that the median follow-up duration for the partial response group (35 months) was substantially shorter than that of the complete obliteration group (53 months). This difference suggests that additional follow-up time may permit further nidus obliteration, potentially increasing the overall complete response rate with longer observation. Although the rate of S-RN remained within the range reported in the literature, continued vigilance is warranted. One patient with a peri-Rolandic AVM (nidus volume 16.4 cm3; V12 = 28.5 cm3) developed acute neurological symptoms 2 days after the first AVS-SRS stage, which was classified as CTCAE grade 3 central nervous system necrosis. The unusually rapid onset raises the possibility of acute perinidal edema, a vascular event, or seizure activity rather than classical radiation necrosis, which typically develops 6 to 18 months after SRS and manifests radiographically as perinidal T2-weighted hyperintensities.25 Recent evidence suggests that early post-SRS edema in patients with AVM may result from hemodynamic inflow-outflow imbalance, specifically due to disproportionate radiation-induced narrowing of draining veins relative to arterial feeders, rather than from true parenchymal necrosis.26

The patient was promptly treated with corticosteroids; however, she subsequently developed radiographic radiation necrosis accompanied by contralateral hemiparesis. This outcome is consistent with the elevated risk associated with a large V12 volume and an eloquent peri-Rolandic location.27 Treatment with Bevacizumab resulted in stabilization of both the neurological deficits and radiographic findings. Follow-up MRA at 28 months demonstrated a partial nidus response, consistent with the expected 2- to 4-year obliteration timeline after AVS-SRS.24

These observations underscore the importance of careful patient selection, individualized dose optimization, and close posttreatment surveillance to minimize treatment-related toxicity while maximizing efficacy.15,19,27,28

Overall, our results support the feasibility and safety of accelerated, short-interval, dose-escalated AVS-SRS as an alternative treatment strategy for appropriately selected patients with large-volume cerebral AVMs, or AVMs located in critical regions, or both. This approach may shorten the overall treatment course while maintaining acceptable efficacy and toxicity profiles. Nevertheless, larger multicenter studies with longer follow-up are required to validate these findings and to further define optimal staging intervals, dose prescriptions, and dose-volume constraints for future clinical implementation.

Conclusion

AVS-SRS is a novel staged radiosurgical technique developed for the treatment of large cerebral AVMs. This approach integrates modern multimodality imaging, including simulation CTA, multiparametric MRA, and catheter cerebral angiography, for target delineation and treatment planning. Treatments were delivered on a dedicated stereotactic LINAC platform equipped with a high-definition multileaf collimator, 6-degree-of-freedom robotic treatment couch, and stereotactic image guided radiation therapy protocols. Each AVS-SRS procedure was performed under image guidance with both a radiation oncologist and neurosurgeon present during treatment delivery. Unlike conventional VS-SRS approaches that typically employ interstage intervals of several months, AVS-SRS used a short interval of only 2 to 3 weeks between stages, accompanied by close clinical surveillance and serial MRA follow-up.

In this small retrospective cohort, AVS-SRS achieved encouraging obliteration outcomes with an acceptable toxicity profile. These results compare favorably with those reported for conventional single-session SRS and previously published VS-SRS techniques, while offering the potential advantages of reduced overall treatment duration, streamlined treatment planning, and minimized interstage anatomical and imaging variability. Although longer follow-up and larger multicenter studies are necessary to confirm these findings, the present results support the feasibility and safety of AVS-SRS as a promising treatment strategy for appropriately selected patients.
