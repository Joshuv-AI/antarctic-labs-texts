Randomized Crossover Trial of Low Sodium Dialysate on Tissue Sodium Accumulation in Hemodialysis
Authors: Lale A. Ertuglu, R. Gulsah Dilaver, Jorge Gamboa, Mert Demirci, Michael Pridmore, Rachelle L. Crescenzi, Cindy Mambungu, Andrew Guide, Robert Greevy, T. Alp Ikizler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatients on maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) have higher tissue sodium accumulation in skin and muscle. In this randomized, crossover clinical trial, we assessed the safety and efficacy of low sodium dialysate on skin and muscle sodium accumulation, and systemic markers of inflammation.MethodsPatients on MHD were assigned to initial treatment with either 4-weeks of low sodium dialysate (132 mEq/l) or high sodium dialysate (138 mEq/l). Tissue sodium was assessed with 23Na magnetic resonance imaging at the end of each treatment.ResultsOf 28 randomized participants, 23 completed both treatments. The least-squares mean treatment difference in tissue sodium from high-sodium to low-sodium dialysate were −0.8 for muscle sodium and −1.5 for skin sodium and did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.27 and 0.09, respectively). In models adjusted for baseline tissue sodium and ultrafiltration rate, low dialysate treatment was associated with decrease in skin sodium but not muscle sodium. Higher ultrafiltration rate was significantly associated with decrease in both skin and muscle sodium. No significant change was seen in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and prealbumin between treatments. There was no serious study-related adverse event (AE) during either treatment.ConclusionOur study suggests that both low-sodium dialysate and ultrafiltration rate may modify tissue sodium. Future larger studies are needed to evaluate the effects of low-sodium dialysate and high ultrafiltration rate, individually and combined on tissue sodium accumulation and clinical outcomes.

Research in the last 2 decades has established that sodium accumulates in the interstitium of tissue, particularly muscle and skin, without commensurate water retention.1,2 Sodium (23Na) magnetic resonance imaging (23Na-MRI), since its introduction by Kopp et al.3 over a decade ago, has allowed for noninvasive quantification of tissue sodium and identification of many conditions associated with increased tissue sodium accumulation including chronic kidney disease.4 Importantly, high sodium concentration in the tissue results in immune cell activation and systemic inflammatory response,5 and has been associated with cardiometabolic risk factors including insulin resistance,6 hypertension,7 and increased intermuscular adipose tissue accumulation.8

Patients with end-stage kidney disease have significantly higher tissue sodium accumulation compared with people without kidney disease.4 High tissue sodium may contribute to the high burden of cardiovascular risk factors, including systemic inflammation and left ventricular hypertrophy, observed in this population.—4,9,10 Higher skin sodium in patients on maintenance dialysis has also been associated with increased mortality.11

Total body sodium balance in patients on MHD is partly dependent on dialysate sodium concentration. Higher dialysate sodium prescription was found to correlate with increased skin sodium,11 and tissue sodium concentration decreases immediately after a single hemodialysis (HD) session, suggesting that tissue sodium content can be modulated by sodium removal via modulation of dialysate sodium in the long-term.

In this randomized, double-blinded, crossover pilot trial, we tested the hypothesis that a dialysate sodium prescription of 132 mEq/l would decrease tissue sodium accumulation in patients on MHD compared with a dialysate sodium prescription of 138 mEq/l. We also examined the differential effects of dialysate sodium concentration on nutritional and inflammatory markers.

Methods

Study Population

Twenty-eight patients on MHD were recruited from the Vanderbilt University Medical Center and Veteran Affairs Tennessee Valley Health Care System outpatient dialysis units between 2020 and 2023. Patients who were older than 21 years and were on MHD treatment for at least 6 months, dialyzed with a single pool Kt/V over 1.2 for at least 3 months and a well-functioning HD access were included. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy; presence of a metal object in the body that might interfere with MRI; severe, unstable, active, or chronic inflammatory disease (active infection, active connective tissue disorder, active cancer, or cancer history in the previous 1 year, HIV, liver disease, active chronic hepatitis B or C); type 1 or 2 diabetes who receive insulin therapy; hospitalization within 1 month before the study; treatment with steroids including inhaled steroid and high potency topical, with the exception of over the counter hydrocortisone cream and/or other immunosuppressive agents.

Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects before their participation and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of Vanderbilt University Medical Center and Veteran Affairs Tennessee Valley Health Care System. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04067752).

Experimental Protocol

Randomization and Study Procedures

This was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled cross-over study. Each subject was randomly assigned to receive 1 of the 2 study arms as initial treatment as follows: high dialysate sodium (HDNa; 138 mEq/l) versus low sodium dialysate (LDNa; 132 mEq/l) (Figure 1). Randomization was assigned by computer-generated sequence using permuted block randomization. Each study arm was 4 weeks, and there was no wash-out period in between arms. 23Na-MRI, body composition, and biochemical parameters were obtained at the beginning and at the end of each arm.Figure 1Study design. Study design. Na-MRI, sodium magnetic resonance imaging.

Intervention

Patients on the HDNa and LDNa arm were treated with dialysate solution with Na of 138 and 132 mEq/l, respectively, 3 times a week as part of their MHD. Each intervention arm lasted 4 weeks and included 12 HD sessions. During the study arms, patients were adequately dialyzed (eKt/V > 1.2) and electrolytes including bicarbonate, calcium, and phosphorus were kept within clinically recommended ranges. Patients continued to receive erythropoietin stimulating agents to keep hemoglobin within the range of 10 to 12 mg/dl per recommended clinical practice guidelines. Participants were instructed to keep their dietary nutrient intake constant during the intervention period. Dietary recalls were obtained weekly throughout the study.

Na-MRI

All 23Na-MRI studies were performed as described in previous publications.8,12 All studies were obtained on nondialysis weekdays, 1 day after last HD session. Briefly, peripheral tissue sodium content was quantified at the left leg mid-calf area with an MRI exam using a 3.0 T MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) and a single-tuned receive-only quadrature sodium coil (Rapid Biomedical GmbH, Rimpar, Germany). Study subjects were positioned supine, and the left lower leg was placed over a holder containing 4 solutions of saline at physiologic tissue sodium concentrations (10, 20, 30, 40 mmol/l NaCl in milli-Q water). The leg and standards were positioned in the center of the coil such that the leg remained supported at the knee and ankle, and the skin was closely in contact with the phantom holder. Tissue sodium content at the widest part of the calf was calculated based on a linear calibration of 23Na-MRI signal in the saline phantoms. Segmentations in the muscle (including all muscle groups) and skin (lower semi-perimeter) were performed manually as published previously.8,12

Dietary Recall

Dietary intake of sodium was collected and analyzed using Nutrition Data System for Research software version 2017 (Nutrition Coordinating Center, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN).13 24-hour dietary recalls were obtained on a weekday and on either Saturday or Sunday. These recalls were used to estimate a 7-day average for each week.

Laboratory Analysis

Blood samples were obtained at the Clinical Research Center and analyzed at Vanderbilt University Medical Center and Veteran Affairs central laboratories. Following blood draw, samples were transported on ice and kept in −80 °C after centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes. Biochemistry measurements were analyzed at Vanderbilt University Medical Center pathology laboratory. High-sensitivity particle-enhanced turbidimetric UniCel DxI Immunoassay system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) was used to measure hs-CRP levels.

Sample Size Calculation

A lack of previous data on the different effect of LDNa compared with HDNa, and the effect over more than 1 HD session on tissue sodium in patients on MHD precluded from a sample size calculation. An estimation was derived from a previous study which demonstrated a significant decrease in muscle sodium by 4.6 ± 3.6 mmol/l in nondiabetic patients on MHD after 1 HD session.14 To observe a comparable HD-induced reduction in muscle sodium, with 80% power at a 0.05 2-sided significance level, a sample size of at least 7 patients would be needed. With a comparable standard deviation and a sample size of 26 patients, a difference of a 2.1 mmol/l can be detected with 80% power at a 0.05 2-sided significance level.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome of the study was change in muscle and skin tissue sodium level after the intervention. Baseline data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Changes in tissue sodium across treatment arms were reported as means, SDs, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the means. The average differences between treatments were estimated using pairwise t-tests, and 2-sided P-values and 95% CIs of the difference were provided. Missing data was assumed to be missing completely at random.

Muscle sodium and skin sodium were analyzed as continuous variables and changes in these variables were compared using mixed effects models. These models included the treatment group (LDNa or HDNa), baseline tissue sodium levels, and a random intercept. The effect of ultrafiltration rate was assessed in a separate mixed effect model including ultrafiltration rate, baseline tissue sodium level, and a random intercept. A sensitivity analysis was carried out with muscle and skin sodium as main independent variable adjusted for treatment group, ultrafiltration rate, and baseline tissue sodium levels.

There was no evidence of carry-over effects, as provided in Supplementary Table S1. This was expected as a change in dialysate sodium is anticipated to result in immediate effect.

Analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0, R Foundation, Vienna Austria). Statistical significance threshold was set at a 2-sided α ≤ .05.

Results

A total of 35 participants were consented, 28 were randomized, and 23 completed the study (Figure 2). Of the 28 participants who were randomized, the median age (interquartile range) was 62.5 (57.8– 70.3), 46 % were female, and 82 % were Black. The median muscle and skin sodium concentrations (interquartile range) at baseline were 23.2 (20.7–27.4) and 18.6 (16.4–25.9) mmol/l, respectively. Exploratory Outcomes

We further conducted exploratory analysis comparing patients who exhibited any decrease in muscle sodium levels (muscle sodium responders) on the low Na+ treatment and those who did not (muscle sodium nonresponders), and patients who exhibited a decrease in skin sodium levels (skin sodium responders) on the low Na+ treatment and those who did not (skin sodium nonresponders). There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between responders and nonresponders in both muscle sodium and skin sodium groups (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively). Skin sodium nonresponders had higher dietary sodium intake compared with skin sodium responders, however this difference was not significant. Both groups were comparable in terms of age, sex, duration of HD, and baseline sodium levels.

Safety and Tolerability

A total of 11 AEs occurred during the study. There was 1 unrelated serious AE (colon perforation and colostomy placement) which resulted in withdrawal from study. There were no deaths. Two patients withdrew from the study because of unrelated nonserious AE—1 because of HD access infection and 1 because of hospitalization for pneumonia and shingles. There were 2 related AEs (hypertension and hypotension) in the low-dialysate sodium arm, and both patients were subsequently withdrawn from the study. One patient experienced abdominal pain and weakness during high-sodium dialysate arm which was mild and resolved without treatment. Other unrelated AEs included needle dislodgement during HD, fever, skin rash because of antibiotic allergy, elbow fracture, and COVID-19 infection. No clinically significant abnormalities were noted in blood chemistry results because of the treatments.

Discussion

In this randomized crossover pilot and feasibility clinical trial, we investigated the impact of low sodium dialysate on tissue sodium accumulation and systemic inflammatory and nutritional markers in patients undergoing MHD. Our findings showed that compared with high-sodium dialysate (138 mEq/l), the 4-week treatment with low-sodium dialysate (132 mEq/l) did not lead to significant changes in muscle sodium accumulation or inflammatory and nutritional markers. Low-sodium dialysate was associated with decrease in skin sodium after adjustment for ultrafiltration rate. Both treatments were generally well-tolerated, and no serious study-related AEs were observed.

Multiple studies have established that patients with advanced kidney disease, especially those with end-stage kidney disease, are prone to excessive accumulation of sodium in tissue which is proposed to be because of lack of renal sodium homeostasis.8,9,16 This is of particular concern because increased tissue sodium has been linked to increased cardiovascular risk10,17 as well as major cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in patients with end-stage kidney disease11 in epidemiological studies. These findings underscore the need for effective strategies to decrease tissue sodium levels.

Dialysate sodium concentration plays a critical role in determining the extent of sodium removal by diffusion, allowing for regulation of sodium balance during HD.13 Thus, modulating dialysate sodium has been suggested as a strategy to decrease total body sodium and potentially reduce sodium storage in tissue. Titze et al.16 previously reported a reduction in muscle and skin sodium immediately after a single session of HD with dialysate sodium of 137 mEq/l. Additionally, higher dialysate sodium levels have been associated with higher skin sodium in patients on MHD.11 In another study, patients on a dialysate prescription of 140 mEq/l were found to have significantly higher skin sodium compared with patients on dialysate prescription of 137 mEq/l based on different unit prescription protocols.18

The current study is designed to assess the effectiveness of decreasing sodium dialysate in modulating tissue sodium accumulation. A recent cross-over study by Kopp et al.19 including 10 male patients in 2024 has reported a significant increase in skin sodium from a baseline dialysate sodium of 138 mEq/l to 142 mEq/l and decrease from 142 mEq/l to 135 mEq/l. No change was observed in muscle sodium accumulation. Different from the current study, the modifications to dialysate sodium were stepwise over a period of 14 weeks with 1 mEq/l to 2 mEq/l changes per week with a plateau phase of 5 weeks and were not randomized. Despite encouraging data from previous observational studies and these recent findings, we did not find conclusive evidence that low sodium dialysate improves tissue sodium accumulation over 4 weeks. An important factor that may explain these findings is the effect of ultrafiltration rate. Ultrafiltration rate was not kept constant in the trial, and we observed a higher ultrafiltration rate during high sodium dialysate period compared with low sodium dialysate period. Notably, higher ultrafiltration rate was associated with statistically significant decrease in tissue sodium in both muscle and skin. Although we cannot conclusively determine the reasons behind the difference in ultrafiltration rates, it is possible that LDNa treatment requires a lower ultrafiltration rate. Since the staff was blinded to the assignment, it is unlikely that this was a treatment bias. A decrease in ultrafiltration rate, in return, decreases tissue sodium removal as expected. Thus, a direct effect of LDNa on tissue sodium removal may be obscured by the counter effect of the resulting decreased ultrafiltration rate. The results of the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S4) are also suggestive of this possibility. After adjustment for the effect of the change in ultrafiltration rate, low sodium dialysate treatment was found to significantly decrease skin sodium accumulation. Although the sample size of the current study is insufficient to draw conclusive results, the observed trends highlight a potential effect of both low sodium dialysate and ultrafiltration rate on tissue sodium accumulation and the need for further studies with larger cohorts to confirm these findings.

Our findings may be influenced by several other factors. Firstly, the intervention period of 4 weeks may have been insufficient to substantially impact large tissue compartments such as muscle and skin. Second, the amount of Na removal with low-Na dialysate could be too small for the large tissue Na collection. This is substantiated by the data showing that though a single HD session decreases tissue Na acutely, the levels revert to predialysis levels at the next dialysis session.20 In this study, we obtained 23Na-MRIs on nondialysis days to eliminate variability in measurements because of immediate effects of dialysis. It should be noted that in this study, HDNa was preset as 138 mEq/l, which is significantly lower than the HDNa of 142 mEq/l used by Kopp et al.19 The difference between dialysate sodium of 132 and 138 mEq/l may not lead to the same shift of tissue sodium as the difference between dialysate sodium of 135mEq/l and 142mEq/l. Furthermore, the study by Kopp et al. was based in Europe with a racially different cohort than the current study. It is noteworthy that more than 80% of the current cohort were Black adults. Previous studies found that Black patients on dialysis have greater muscle and skin Na+ accumulation compared with White patients,8 suggesting possible racial differences in pathophysiological mechanisms of tissue sodium regulation. The high variability in baseline skin and muscle sodium in our patients may also be influential, as patients with low baseline tissue sodium may not show a detectable decrease in tissue sodium. However, our exploratory analysis did not find a difference in baseline tissue sodium levels in patients who had decreased levels compared with patients who did not respond. It is also possible that there are multiple mechanisms driving exaggerated tissue sodium accumulation in chronic kidney disease in addition to diminished sodium excretion by the kidneys. Variability in body sodium content also depends on dietary sodium intake, residual urinary output, and extrarenal sodium regulatory mechanisms, including gut sodium sensing.21 The finding that dietary sodium intake was stable during the study suggests that our results are not influenced by these factors. Further, local mechanisms of tissue sodium accumulation and clearance including interstitial glycosaminoglycan composition,2 immune microenvironment,22,23 and vascular endothelial growth factor C signaling16 may represent different determinants of tissue sodium that are not modulated by total body sodium excretion.

Studies have shown that increased tissue sodium strongly associate with systemic inflammatory markers and blood pressure in both chronic kidney disease and nonchronic kidney disease populations.5,6,8,10,17 Previous studies have linked high tissue sodium levels with lower serum albumin9,11 and elevated inflammatory markers including interleukin-6 and CRP8,17 in patients on MHD. High extracellular sodium acts as a local inflammatory stimulus, leading to the production of cytokines including interleukin-1 and interleukin-6.4,23 Monocytes collected from people with high skin sodium levels display markers of sodium-induced activation.24 Furthermore, the chronic inflammatory state induced by chronic kidney disease may predispose to increased tissue sodium accumulation. In our study, we did not observe a change in hs-CRP with 4-week low-sodium dialysate treatment, consistent with the lack of change in tissue sodium.

The discussion surrounding the optimal sodium concentration in dialysate has been ongoing for decades. Low-sodium dialysate has benefits including enhanced sodium clearance, decreased interdialytic weight gain, and potentially improved cardiovascular function. However, these advantages come with increased risk of intradialytic hypotension and concerns about lower tolerability. In our study, 1 patient was withdrawn because of hypotension and weakness during the low-sodium dialysate treatment, which aligns with these known risks. Conversely, another patient was withdrawn because of intradialytic hypertension on low-sodium dialysate. Importantly, we did not observe any treatment-related serious AEs, and both treatments exhibited comparable safety profiles and were generally well-tolerated and feasible.

This study has several strengths. The use of 23Na-MRI to provide rigorous measurements of tissue sodium levels is unique and provides the best assessment of tissue Na content. The crossover design offered robust control for interindividual variability, enhancing the validity of our comparisons between treatments despite a modest sample size.

Our study also has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small, and the study duration was limited to 4 weeks, which may not be sufficient to observe significant changes in tissue sodium levels. The absence of a washout period between treatments might also have blunted outcomes, however there was no evidence of carry over effect and the effects of sodium removal by dialysis are not expected to persist beyond the consecutive days. As the majority of the current patient population were Black adults, whether the current results would be applicable to non-Black population is not clear. Furthermore, our findings suggests that ultrafiltration rate has a direct effect on tissue sodium removal, consistent with previous reports by others. The current trial was not designed to study the effect of changes in ultrafiltration rate independent of changes in dialysis sodium concentration in tissue sodium removal. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are warranted to further assess the effects of ultrafiltration rate and dialysate sodium in tissue sodium removal.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that low-sodium dialysate may modify tissue sodium removal, particularly in skin, when adjusted for baseline tissue sodium and ultrafiltration rate, and ultrafiltration rate plays a significant role in reducing both skin and muscle sodium. The 4-week treatment with low-sodium dialysate was not found to have a significant effect on systemic markers of inflammation and nutrition compared with high-Na dialysate. The treatments were generally well-tolerated, with no serious treatment-related AEs observed. Given the established links between tissue sodium accumulation, cardiovascular outcomes, and mortality, it remains crucial to explore effective strategies for excess tissue sodium accumulation in this patient population. Future studies should focus on longer intervention periods and larger sample sizes to assess potential benefits of manipulation of dialysate sodium content and ultrafiltration on tissue sodium accumulation and potentially other clinically relevant outcomes.
