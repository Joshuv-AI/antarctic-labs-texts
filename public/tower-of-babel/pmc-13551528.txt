Frameworks for Action: A Scoping Review of Organisational Patient Engagement Models in Healthcare
Authors: Titus A Chan, Queena Cheong, Brydne Edwards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePatient engagement (PE) is recognised as a key pillar of healthcare planning and delivery. However, there is a limited understanding of recent evidence‐based organisational frameworks supporting PE; this gap can impact the uptake, implementation, and spread of PE efforts across healthcare organisations. Our study aims to identify and describe frameworks for PE applicable at an organisational level in academic literature.MethodsOur scoping review applied Arksey and O'Malley's five‐stage methodology and included an optional sixth stage of community consultation. Four databases (MEDLINE, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, and Embase) were searched between 2014 and 2024. Included articles were double‐extracted and coded for synthesis using data tables.ResultsOut of 7870 total studies yielded by the search, 13 studies from North America, Europe, and Australia met inclusion criteria. Our study identified 11 organisational PE frameworks with varying structure and complexity. While no unifying PE model emerged, many frameworks conceptualised engagement on a continuum. Frameworks were predominantly hospital‐based, and many did not provide in‐depth theoretical underpinnings nor reported patient contributions towards development.ConclusionsBy synthesising the available evidence, this review provides insights for healthcare organisations seeking tools to systematically strengthen their PE initiatives. Conceptualising engagement on a continuum may offer a pragmatic approach to scaling PE institution‐wide. Future framework development should focus on tailoring evidence‐based frameworks to their organisational context, and integrate vital components such as health equity, theoretical foundations, and impact indicators. Emerging organisational frameworks should aim to be adaptable, inclusive, and reflective of diverse patient experiences.Keywords: health equity, healthcare co‐design, healthcare planning, organisational development, patient‐centred care, quality improvement

Background

Patient engagement (PE) in healthcare settings has been widely recognised as promoting a range of benefits to patients and organisations. These benefits include enhanced patient safety, improved health outcomes, more relevant services, improved patient satisfaction, and better care quality [1, 2, 3, 4]. As a result, many international healthcare organisations, including the Institute for Healthcare Improvement and the World Health Organisation, have emphasised PE as a pillar of health system transformation and improvement [5, 6]. In the health services field, the PE literature focuses predominantly on implementation at point‐of‐care, typically highlighting its benefit at the bedside to enhance patient decision‐making, improve clinical outcomes (e.g., reduced anxiety, treatment adherence), or support professional practice (e.g., interprofessional communication, best practice guidance) [1, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Understanding the central role of healthcare institutions in enabling PE, there is consistent recognition that meaningful PE must extend beyond the bedside to the organisational or system‐wide level [11].

Patient feedback, whether from consultation or co‐design, is a critical backbone to advancing impactful quality improvement (QI) initiatives across learning health systems, patient experience, and care outcomes at an organisational level [12]. These impacts demonstrate that PE can utilise staff from all levels of an institution to drive meaningful change, including frontline clinicians, administrative staff, and executive leadership [13]. This practice has been deemed essential for fostering transparency, accountability, and patient‐centred improvement [14]. Evidence on the numerous benefits of PE on organisational structures (e.g., staff role updates, improved governance), processes (e.g., discharge info packets for families), and priorities (e.g., strategic plan) also supports this growing shift of considering patient engagement beyond the bedside [15]. Thus, healthcare organisations are increasingly pursuing the integration of patient perspectives towards operations, QI, strategic planning, and governance.

Although recent evidence highlights the potential impact of incorporating patient lived experiences into the planning and delivery of healthcare [16], substantial variation exists in terms of the interpretations and definitions of PE, as well as its implementation across different care settings [3, 17]. A recent review by Sagen and colleagues [18] identified several gaps in the application of PE in health services. Most importantly, they argued that disingenuous and inappropriate PE activities could be ineffective or harmful by reinforcing power imbalances, discrimination, or stigma (e.g., professionals controlling engagement agenda, underestimating knowledge of patient partners). The absence of a widely accepted definition, theory, or framework for applying PE at the organisational level further complicates efforts to integrate and evaluate PE efforts across institutions in an effective and meaningful manner [19].

Despite the atheoretical nature of PE practices, PE frameworks have been proposed as useful tools to enable more evidence‐based organisational change [20]. Frameworks are structured models that can provide organisations with a more consistent and effective approach to meaningful PE. Frameworks may be particularly useful to facilitate and guide the work of healthcare leaders (e.g., health policymakers, administration, project managers, patient partners); their use may guide organisational infrastructure to enable meaningful PE, by emphasising an organisation's commitment to PE at a strategic leadership level, and fostering a common language across patients, families, healthcare providers, staff and leaders [4]. There is an established evidence base for PE frameworks in a health research context, with 14 models and frameworks identified in a 2022 scoping review [21]. However, no comprehensive review has similarly synthesised PE frameworks designed for health services specifically at the organisational level. Another recent scoping review identified frameworks that combined concepts of engagement and co‐creation as a secondary objective [22]. However, they focused only on the adult patient population and many of their identified frameworks pertained to health services research, not organisational or operational application.

The lack of synthesised knowledge of PE application at the organisation‐level specifically has contributed to a substantial research‐practice gap, leaving institutions without clear guidance on how to embed evidence‐based PE systematically across their programs, policies, and structures. While previous environmental scans have captured several PE frameworks in grey literature [23], researchers have called for more rigorous integration of evidence into PE practice [24].

The present study intended to examine and synthesise existing organisational PE frameworks as a first step in addressing this research‐practice gap. Specifically, the research objectives were to:

Methods

This study adopted Arksey & O'Malley's [25] five‐stage scoping review methodology, updated by Levac [26], drawing on a narrative approach to synthesising the findings [27]. A scoping review methodology was preferred over a systematic review due to the broad nature of the study aims and anticipated heterogeneity in article types and methodologies [28, 29]. In addition to the study aims, this review was undertaken to inform the development of an evidence‐informed PE framework at the authors' institution; accordingly, this study applied the additional consultation phase as a sixth stage. This step supported methodological rigour by directly engaging patient partners to inform the analytic process [26].

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) Checklist was used to structure our findings [30]. Institutional ethics approval was not required, as this study represented a review of published articles.

Identifying Relevant Studies

The supervising/third author (BE) and a faculty liaison and instruction librarian with expertise in health policy, public health, and scoping reviews developed the search strategy, which consisted of three key concepts: patient engagement; framework/model; healthcare services. Specifically, this scoping review focused on research that explored patient engagement at an organisational, or operational level, whereby frameworks could be applied beyond point‐of‐care. Our study adopted the PCC (population, concept, context) framework to structure the search strategy, consistent with the scoping review approach and other scoping reviews with similar objectives [22, 31].

Our study carried out an extensive search with the following databases: MEDLINE, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, and Embase. Final search terms were translated according to the database (see Table 1).

Study Selection

Our team imported and screened all references and abstracts using Covidence, a web‐based platform for organising and carrying out literature reviews. Eligibility criteria are embedded in Table 1. Inclusion criteria consisted of English‐language articles including the three key research concepts (patient engagement, framework/model, and health service delivery at the organisation level), regardless of geography, clinical setting, population or methodology (see Table 1). Database searches were limited to articles published between 2014 and 2024 to capture recent organisational frameworks following the implementation of Canada's national PE strategy [32], and it follows a systematic review from 2014 which synthesised frameworks prior to this period [4].

Diverse article types, including qualitative and quantitative studies, scoping reviews, literature reviews and systematic reviews exploring any aspect of at least one PE framework at the organisational level were eligible for inclusion. However, conference abstracts and book reviews were excluded. Similar to Chudyk et al.’s [21] scoping review of frameworks for health research, original and adapted frameworks were included if they addressed patient engagement at an organisational level of service delivery. Articles were included if they discussed frameworks at multiple environmental levels (e.g., point‐of‐care, organisational, public policy, etc.), but an organisational component was required for inclusion.

Screening

To promote accuracy and robustness of the title and abstract screening stage, interrater reliability was established within the research team by double‐screening an early sample (≥ 5% of total articles). A target rate of ≥ 95% agreement was established to indicate excellent reliability [33], exceeding the 90% benchmark as recommended in single‐author screening [34]. Agreement was separately established between a senior research team member (BE) and the other co‐authors (TC & QC).

Once the target agreement rate was achieved, the authors (TC, QC, & BE) single‐screened the remainder of the titles and abstracts, with an independent second screening when requested (e.g., due to uncertainty). During the full‐text screening stage, two members of the team screened each article. Regularly scheduled meetings were held to resolve any screening conflicts via consensus and critical discussion.

Data Extraction

The authors compiled and double‐extracted all included studies for synthesis. An initial extraction template was developed based on the study aims and then piloted among all authors. The final data extraction template was developed in Covidence to obtain information from each article such as: author(s), year of publication, study design, clinical setting(s), clinical population(s), study aims, and framework used. Articles which did not specify a singular clinical setting were labelled as Broad Healthcare Context, which we define as unspecified to any one care setting or level of care. A quality appraisal of included studies was not performed, consistent with methodological guidelines for scoping reviews [35].

To establish an in‐depth understanding of the frameworks, the authors iteratively created a coding and extraction template which was designed to capture similarities and differences among the frameworks in terms of their key characteristics. The team separately double‐coded and extracted all frameworks using this template, then compared results for consensus via discussion. A category on the level of health services‐related engagement mentioned was included, based on the Ecological Systems Theory and its use in healthcare framework development [36, 37]. Additional categories were also added and/or updated as authors became more familiar with the frameworks and after consultation with patient partners. Consultation

Consultation was implemented through one primary engagement and aligned with key practices identified by Pollock and colleagues [38]. During the synthesis work, we disseminated preliminary review results to a patient advisory council consisting of nine patient partners with prior experience in organisational PE initiatives. Our team received feedback on the interpretation of early findings and areas which patient partners would have liked to see explored in further depth. Categories in the extraction template were updated for greater clarity and specificity (e.g., ‘reporting requirements’ to ‘engagement‐related outcomes’) to support a subsequent round of extraction and synthesis. Overall, this consultation step provided good opportunities for knowledge transfer on the topic of organisational PE frameworks as relevant to patient partners, serving to enhance the depth of synthesis and enhance the utility of findings.

Results

The initial database search yielded 7867 articles, of which 1468 were duplicates. 6402 articles proceeded to the title and abstract review stage. TC and BE achieved an interrater reliability of 97.8% within the initial 5.1% of titles/abstracts (n = 324); QC and BE achieved an interrater reliability of 97.5% with 10.9% of titles/abstracts. The remaining articles (n = 5061) proceeded to single‐screening, after which 111 articles emerged as eligible for full‐text screening (see Figure 1). These 111 articles were also manually hand‐searched to identify additional relevant articles eligible for full‐text screening, to which three were identified and one proceeded to the data extraction stage. From the remaining sources, the authors excluded 96 articles during full‐text review using the pre‐determined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A final sample of 13 articles met the criteria for inclusion in this scoping review.

The majority of frameworks (n = 9) were also classified as having some degree of complexity via including two or more dimensions (see Table 4). Of the most complex frameworks coded as multidimensional, two were embedded within a larger model or initiative relating to broader health services reform [47, 48].

Eight frameworks cited at least one supporting conceptual or theoretical reference. Five of these were from the field of public/citizenship engagement, with four [39, 41, 43, 47, 48, 51] citing Carman and colleagues' existing multidimensional framework [52] and one [42] citing Abelson and colleagues' Principles for the Design and Evaluation of Public Participation Processes [54]. Three frameworks specifically named theories as foundational components [40, 46, 50]. Lastly, three frameworks cited grey literature sources or did not identify a single prior academic source as a conceptual or theoretical backing [44, 45, 49].

All frameworks conceptualised engagement at the mezzo level, most at the micro level (n = 10), and some at the macro level (n = 6; see Table 4). At the macro level, five frameworks included the context of government/public policy [39, 40, 41, 47, 49, 51]. Other terminology used at this level included Social Structures [40], Factors Influencing Engagement at the societal level [41, 47], and Factors That Influence Patient Commitment including culture and society [48]. Only three frameworks explicitly included some language relating to health equity, diversity, and inclusion (e.g., accuracy of representation, tokenism, patient community health disparities) as vital macro‐level related considerations [40, 42, 45], although two of these did not meet the threshold to be coded as frameworks explicitly incorporating engagement at a macro level [42, 45].

Just over half of included frameworks included a standalone component on engagement‐related outcomes or reporting [40, 42, 44, 46, 47, 49], with substantial variation in the language and terminology used in each instance (see Table 3). There was one framework which was designed uniquely with a focus on evaluating and reporting PE in the context of organisational mobile health applications or eHealth [44]. The remaining frameworks only provided examples of potential outcomes but did not specify outcomes or reporting as a core component.

Hospital settings were the most frequently cited context, appearing in seven frameworks [39, 42, 43, 45, 46, 49, 50, 51], while unspecified healthcare contexts more broadly are featured in four frameworks [40, 41, 47, 48]. Clinical populations varied across frameworks. Half of the frameworks (n = 6) did not specify a specific clinical population [40, 41, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51], two were developed specifically for use with cancer patients [42, 44], and one framework each for diabetic foot ulcer patients [39], intensive care unit patients [45], and paediatric patients [46].

Objective 3: Describe How Patient Engagement Frameworks Were Applied

The reported purposes of the frameworks as designed varied across studies, with some explicitly outlining their original conceptual goals while others did not provide a stated basis (see Table 5). Several frameworks were designed to support engagement practices within healthcare, such as guiding health organisations in patient engagement strategies through eHealth tools [44] and facilitating patient‐family collaboration by addressing psychological safety in intensive care [45]. Other frameworks focused on demonstrating engagement relationships, including Boivin et al. [40] who aimed to define and evaluate engagement dynamics across community and health systems, and Sheard et al. [50] who examined contextual and organisational factors influencing staff responses to patient feedback. Two frameworks were designed for implementation guidance, aimed at creating a shared understanding of patient and family engagement for practical application in healthcare settings [47] and embedding and enhancing consumer partnerships across multiple health system areas [49]. However, several studies did not explicitly report their framework's purpose [39, 41, 48, 51].

The application of frameworks varied across studies, depending on the research approach and objectives. Several review‐based studies used frameworks as analytical tools to guide study selection, categorisation, or synthesis of findings [39, 41, 46]. Other frameworks were used for data analysis, such as quantifying hospital planning PE activities [43] or providing a structured analysis matrix for use with qualitative interviews [42]. Five frameworks were also generated through theory‐building, examining the contextual enablers of PE [40, 46, 50] or how PE might be translated into action effectively [45, 47]. Lastly, case‐based applications of organisational PE frameworks were reported through implementation‐focused studies [48, 49].

Discussion

The goal of this review was to identify, describe, and synthesise organisational PE frameworks. Our findings are useful for healthcare planners, leaders and QI practitioners seeking to optimise organisational PE activities, as the results provide an overview of current PE framework characteristics and their application across a variety of clinical settings. Identified PE frameworks shared some commonalities which indicates their emerging utility across different healthcare contexts, but they lacked strong consensus on a universally adopted approach. Situated within current PE priorities, our findings suggest that existing frameworks are not yet fully developed towards equitable, system‐level, and impact‐aware engagement, highlighting several future practice directions for healthcare leaders, QI practitioners, researchers, and institutions leading organisational PE.

Conceptualising Engagement on a Continuum

A common theme among the PE frameworks was the integration of an engagement continuum that moves from patients having minimal, if any, impact on organisational decisions (e.g., keeping patients informed) to patients being actively involved in organisational decision‐making (e.g., empowered). Representing PE on a continuum is straightforward and more intuitive than more complex frameworks used in other related fields, like health research. For example, a recent scoping review demonstrated that patient engagement for health research is guided by 13 principles (e.g., transparency, respect, partnership, etc.), 26 foundational components (e.g., valuing patient perspectives, openness of team members to different perspectives, reciprocal relationships, etc.), 18 actions (e.g., opportunities for engagement, methods, action, etc.), 19 outcomes (e.g., sense of community, research culture, dissemination plans, etc.) and categorised by five domains (intrapersonal, interpersonal, environmental, process, health systems) across four contexts (desired goals of engagement, resources to support engagement, inputs, attitudes and expectations of engagement), which could be applied to four organisational levels (e.g., strategy, research, etc.) [21]. Although comprehensive, such a complex combination of elements may be impractical to guide PE in health services delivery and planning, as organisations would likely benefit from more pragmatic and straightforward actions and outcomes to support meaningful engagement. Pragmatism, rather than complexity, may be a useful approach for organisations as it assumes that knowledge is context‐dependent and not useful without action [59]. Visualising patient engagement as a continuum could help healthcare leaders understand how to engage patients at the most suitable level, and when appropriate, how to move PE to a higher level of engagement.

Theoretical Underpinnings and Evaluation of Engagement

The majority of frameworks mentioned a conceptual foundation, but lacked strong and direct explanations on theoretical backing, which is consistent with previous literature on patient engagement [19]. Although multiple articles cited Carman et al., [52] they did not fully describe the theoretical underpinning linking their conceptualisation to mechanisms of action. Carman and colleagues [52] organise patient engagement on a continuum, borrowing such conceptualisation from Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation [60]. Accordingly, the notion that patient engagement is on a continuum is likely conceptualised from the field of democratic public participation. Similarly, Fredriksson and Modigh [42] cite the Principles for the Design and Evaluation of Public Participation Processes without mention of its roots in Deliberative Democratic Theory. [54] Models and frameworks that are conceptually vague on theoretical roots may generalise poorly across different contexts and remain more descriptive; this can obscure why some frameworks may appear more ‘successful’ than others, as robust theories can assist healthcare planners and QI practitioners to discern the causal links between mechanisms and outcomes [61].

While numerous included frameworks recognised the importance of evaluating the impact of PE, a substantial proportion lacked an explicit directive on reporting engagement‐related outcomes. These results are consistent with a previous scoping review by McCarron and colleagues [7], which concluded that gaps remain in the PE evidence‐base around the evaluation of impact and effectiveness. The lack of robust evaluation methods being implemented has been a longstanding critique of PE in healthcare, specifically regarding insufficient metrics, low transparency, and inadequate accountability [62]. It stands to reason that a limited theoretical foundation contributes to the lack of clear evaluation or outcome criteria, given that a theoretical basis would explain what actions should be taken, why those actions should be taken, and what to expect if they are taken. Nonetheless, without a clear mandate on reporting PE‐related outcomes, organisations may struggle to demonstrate the value of PE (e.g., better clinical outcomes, high satisfaction, return on investment, etc.) or pursue QI initiatives based on patient feedback, thus limiting their ability to initiate or sustain meaningful improvements [12]. A standardised approach to incorporating theory‐informed, evidence‐based, and engagement‐specific outcomes into organisational frameworks appears to be growing but remains relatively elusive.

Engagement Contexts: Considering Equity and Patient Involvement

Most frameworks were either developed or applied for/to hospital settings, with the rest not specifying any one particular context. Including an aspect of contextual specificity can be helpful, as different sectors may have different requirements, expectations, and resources to support engagement, such as staffing models [63]. For example, home care and primary care comparatively suffer from chronic underfunding, presenting a vital barrier to both clinician and organisational capacity towards promoting patient‐centred care approaches and broader PE initiatives or models [64, 65]. Comparatively, hospitals may be better positioned to apply PE activities, due to close contact with their staff and patients, who provide and receive services on‐site. Hospitals also have an economic advantage, especially in countries such as Canada, where the sector is allocated greater funding than others [66, 67]. On the other hand, home care organisations provide services in patients' private homes and often work in silos [63]. Given these differences, organisations may choose to adopt a framework that is specific to their sector, which would leave home care and other sectors to adopt and adapt more generic frameworks, potentially diluting the framework's utility. Clavel and colleagues [68] recently conducted a review of patient engagement evaluation tools and noted the lack of evaluation tools representing the home and community sector, which may impede the application and measurement of patient engagement in this setting. Thus, the under‐representation of key healthcare sectors diminishes opportunities for organisations outside acute care to apply evidence‐based and sector‐specific patient engagement. Similar to the pragmatic approach described above, context is critical when applying knowledge, especially in healthcare [59].

In extending this contextual line of inquiry, key aspects of health equity, inclusion, and representation were critically underrepresented among existing frameworks. Marginalised patient groups face unique barriers to patient engagement, such as financial constraints (e.g., caregiving costs, missed work), lack of interpretation or language accommodations, or physically inaccessible venues, which research shows needs to be explicated to broaden diversity and thoughtfully support participation [69]. Organisations relying on frameworks absent of these considerations will need to seek additional resources to avoid reinforcing historical disparities and ensure meaningful inclusion of any marginalised patient communities.

Boivin and colleagues' Ecology of Engagement Model [40] offers a comprehensive example of how equity can be embedded as a visible pillar of a framework: this model explicitly spoke to how PE can either reduce or exacerbate health inequities depending on the diversity and power of engagement relationships. Patient partners have called for greater attention to pervasive challenges in the PE field around tokenism, unconscious bias, and lack of support or resources towards full inclusion by organisations [70]. Evolving understandings of what constitutes inclusive and meaningful PE have also shifted in literature, particularly in recent PE case examples which have incorporated approaches addressing power dynamics, inclusive messaging, and discourses of representativeness [71, 72]. These shifts have led to a growing focus on organisational planning and evaluation in healthcare which mobilises PE towards intersectional communities, attends to systemic inequalities, and incorporates anti‐racist principles [69, 73]. To this end, emerging organisational frameworks aiming to fill this gap could embed health equity as a core component and deliberately provide strategies that systematically foster PE diversity and intentional inclusion.

Patient involvement has been identified as a key priority in healthcare research [74]. This shift reflects a broader movement towards recognising the lived experiences of patient partners and validating these contributions. Despite consensus in literature that meaningful PE should position patients as equal partners in transforming healthcare systems [75], our findings indicate that the majority of identified organisational PE frameworks were developed without reported patient collaboration or acknowledgement. This absence of explicitly reported patient involvement or co‐design raises uncertainty about the extent to which these models could authentically reflect patient perspectives and lived experiences. Prior research has emphasised that engaging patients as co‐creators, rather than passive recipients of change, promotes meaningful and patient‐aligned change across multiple organisational levels [76, 77]. Notably, the Ecology of Engagement Model by Boivin et al. [40] was one of the few included models that explicitly labelled co‐production as a key framework component, described their process of patient collaboration in framework development, and recognised patient partner contributions in study acknowledgements. Our findings underscore a need for more frameworks that adopt a similarly visible approach to integrating co‐design and acknowledging patient contributions.

Limitations and Future Research

There are some limitations in this study, which could be addressed in future research. First, consistent with the scoping approach, the findings of this review represent a high‐level overview of the literature, as the quality of the studies and their frameworks could not be assessed [35]. Future studies might employ a systematic review approach to enable a more robust evaluation of study quality. Next, we did not assign values to framework characteristics (e.g., types of engagement, validity of theoretical backing) as this would have required making assumptions about which components affect engagement depth and effectiveness. Although this is an inherent limitation given the scoping methodology, no available tools could be identified to assess the quality of PE frameworks at an organisational level. Further work is therefore required in this area to develop rigorous evaluation criteria that confirm the degree to which emerging frameworks support best PE practices. To assist with this effort, it would be useful if subsequent research on PE focused on developing a stronger theoretical underpinning; this would enable institutions to make evidence‐based decisions about how to engage patients effectively given the engagement purpose and the expected mechanisms of engagement. Finally, we acknowledge that grey literature can serve as a valuable resource for health sector planners and policymakers by complementing peer‐reviewed research and increasing the diversity of the evidence [78]. Future research could expand upon our findings from empirical literature to compare how frameworks in grey literature might align or diverge.

Conclusion and Implications for Healthcare Planning

As PE becomes a strategic imperative in healthcare governance, evidence‐based PE frameworks can provide leaders with a structured means to take action towards advancing practical and systematic PE approaches. This scoping review of 11 organisational PE frameworks revealed considerable variation in how engagement is envisioned and operationalised. While no single, unifying model emerged, a trend of conceptualising engagement along a continuum suggests that a flexible, graduated approach may be both pragmatic and widely applicable. The absence of a one‐size‐fits‐all model reinforces the importance of tailoring PE frameworks to the objectives, patient population needs, and service contexts of each healthcare institution. Amidst growing recognition of the importance of PE frameworks towards QI and health systems transformation, our synthesis highlights the importance for healthcare planners to balance both a pragmatic and critical lens towards identified organisational frameworks. Healthcare leaders should also be cognizant of the alignment between existing frameworks and their organisational contexts to determine whether to adapt established frameworks or create new ones. Recommended directions for future framework development by health services organisations include supporting greater representation of engagement among non‐hospital sectors, fostering the integration of health equity principles, and strengthening theoretical grounding which can guide PE implementation or impact evaluation. Importantly, this review also underscores that while robust frameworks can be essential tools for guiding systematic PE efforts in organisational practice, their impacts ultimately depend on meaningfully centring the voices of patients, families, and carers.
