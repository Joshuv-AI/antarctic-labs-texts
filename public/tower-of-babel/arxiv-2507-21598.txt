A Tree-Shaped Tableau for Checking the Satisfiability of Signal Temporal Logic with Bounded Temporal Operators CCS: Software and its engineering Requirements analysis CCS: Mathematics of computing Solvers CCS: Theory of computation Modal and temporal logics CCS: Computer systems organization Embedded and cyber-physical systems
Beatrice Melani, Ezio Bartocci, Michele Chiari

Abstract
. Signal Temporal Logic (STL) is a widely recognized formal specification language to express rigorous temporal requirements on mixed analog signals produced by cyber-physical systems (CPS). A relevant problem in CPS design is how to efficiently and automatically check whether a set of STL requirements is logically consistent. This problem reduces to solving the STL satisfiability problem, which is decidable when we assume that our system operates in discrete time steps dictated by an embedded system’s clock. This paper introduces a novel tree-shaped, one-pass tableau method for satisfiability checking of discrete-time STL with bounded temporal operators. Originally designed to prove the consistency of a given set of STL requirements, this method has a wide range of applications beyond consistency checking. These include synthesizing example signals that satisfy the given requirements, as well as verifying or refuting the equivalence and implications of STL formulas. Our tableau exploits redundancy arising from large time intervals in STL formulas to speed up satisfiability checking, and can also be employed to check Mission-Time Linear Temporal Logic (MLTL) satisfiability. We compare our tableau with Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) and First-Order Logic encodings from the literature on a benchmark suite, partly collected from the literature, and partly provided by an industrial partner. Our experiments show that, in many cases, our tableau outperforms state-of-the-art encodings.



1.
Introduction

The formal specification of requirements is an essential engineering activity
(
Boufaied et al. 2021
)
throughout the development process of a cyber-physical system (CPS), from its design and testing/debugging
(
Boufaied et al. 2023
;
Bartocci et al. 2025
;
Bartocci et al. 2021
)
before deployment, to monitoring and runtime verification after deployment
(
Jaksic et al. 2015
)
.

Real-time temporal logics
(
Koymans 1990
)
such as Signal Temporal Logic
(
Maler and Nickovic 2004
)
(STL) provide a very well-established
(
Bartocci et al. 2018
)
and intuitive formal framework to express and reason about temporal requirements over discrete and continuous signals. These mixed-analog signals characterize both the external behavior of the physical environment that the CPS measures through its sensors and the internal behavior of the embedded computational device controlling the physical environment. STL has become widely used in various industrial settings, as evidenced by numerous scientific papers on its applications published by industrial research laboratories
(
Molin et al. 2023
;
Kapinski et al. 2016
;
Roehm et al. 2017
)
.

The process of requirement engineering is highly complex and heterogeneous: requirements are collected and documented at all stages of the CPS design process, initially in natural language, which is then manually translated into unambiguous formal specifications. This step requires significant skills in formal methods on the part of human designer.
More recently, transformer-based machine learning models
(
He et al. 2022
)
such as large language models (LLMs), have also been employed to assist the engineer in translating requirements from natural language to STL or other temporal logics, but this task also requires accurate human supervision
(
Mohammadinejad et al. 2024
)
, since LLMs are prone to errors and hallucinations. Furthermore, guessing requirements about the physical environment in which a CPS operates
can be very cumbersome when done manually. These formal specifications can be inferred from observed data using automated techniques such as specification mining methods
(
Bartocci et al. 2022
)
.

In this scenario,
an important open problem is how to validate efficiently a set of STL requirements and make sure that they are consistent
.
The definition of consistency of a requirement set that is adopted in the context of this paper is the same as the ISO/IEC/IEEE 2918 standard
(
ISO/IEC/IEEE 2018
)
in which a requirement set is considered consistent when it “does not have individual requirements which are contradictory”.

An example

Consider the following two requirements of a railroad system

STL Requirements for a Railroad system from

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[3,50]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[5,20]}}(a\geq 80))

For all time steps in

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,60]}}(a\geq 80\rightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60))

These two requirements are inconsistent, because there exists no signal that would satisfy these two STL formulas in conjunction. However, it is very hard for an engineer to understand at a glance that they are inconsistent.
Proving it manually requires several logical steps and a good knowledge of formal methods.
The first requirement is equivalent to another formula:

(1)

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[3,50]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[5,20]}}(a\geq 80))\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[8,55]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))

We can transform the implication in the second requirement by using
(\phi\rightarrow\psi)\leftrightarrow(\neg\phi\vee\psi)
and then we need to prove the consistency of this expression:

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[8,55]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,60]}}((a<80)\vee\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60))

If we consider STL interpreted over discrete-time signals we can rewrite the formula as follows:

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[8,29]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[30,55]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,60]}}((a<80)\vee\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60))

Using the rule
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i+k,j+k]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[l,m]}}\phi)\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[l+k,m+k]}}\phi)
s.t. (

(2)

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[8,29]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}({\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[20,35]}}(a\geq 80)})\wedge{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,60]}}((a<80)\vee\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60))}

We can split the third globally expression in
blue
in the conjunction as:

(3)

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}((a<80)\vee{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60)})\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[26,60]}}((a<80)\vee\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60))

The two formulas in
green

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}
in (

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60)

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}(a<80)

Using the rule
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\phi)\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\psi)\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\phi\wedge\psi)
s.t. (
i,j\in\mathbb{N},i<j
) and the distributivity of
\wedge

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[8,29]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[20,35]}}(a\geq 80)\wedge(a<80))}\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[26,60]}}((a<80)\vee\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[20,40]}}(a<60))

Using the rule
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\phi\wedge\psi)\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\phi)\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\psi)

{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[20,35]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}(a<80)}

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[8,29]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}(a<80)

By applying the following rule
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i+1,j]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[l,m]}}\phi)\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[l+i+1,m+i+1]}}(\phi)\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[i,j]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[l,m]}}\phi)

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[11,29]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,15]}}(a\geq 80))\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[8,23]}}(a\geq 80)\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[9,24]}}(a\geq 80)\wedge{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[10,25]}}(a\geq 80)\wedge\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[10,25]}}(a<80)}

The expression in red is always false because the value of

This contradiction makes the original requirement false for each possible signal of

.

Consistency checking

Consistency checking is a relevant issue in the context of requirement engineering. In fact, many software errors are introduced during the requirement phase of a project as a result of poor validation of the set of requirements. Fixing such errors, especially when they are detected late in the development cycle of the project, is difficult and costly. Furthermore, there is evidence that errors in functional and interface requirements are often the main source of critical safety software errors that could potentially cause serious accidents
(
Heitmeyer et al. 1996
)
.
When the set of requirements becomes large and complex, this task becomes impossible to perform manually. Verifying automatically whether a set of requirements is consistent amounts to checking the satisfiability of the requirement set, but it poses a challenge, especially when dealing with large-scale CPS.
To make the problem of STL satisfiability feasible, without losing the level of expressiveness needed to describe real cyber-physical systems, we focus our
work on bounded-time discrete-time STL. Although these limitations may seem restrictive, they are not significant on a practical level. Indeed, a finite time-horizon is an acceptable constraint, provided that the chosen horizon is long enough to assess the satisfiability of the requirements in a real-world scenario (i.e., the flight time of an aircraft, the length of the mission of a drone). As for the limitation to discrete-time STL, it is consistent with the discretization of continuous behaviors that occurs when using digital hardware.

A common approach to solving the satisfability problem for discrete-time STL is to encode the formula as a satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) problem.
For example, solving the satisfiability problem for the formula
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[2,3]}}(\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,1]}}(a\geq 80))
is equivalent to solving this SMT problem instance

((a_{2}\geq 80)\vee(a_{3}\geq 80))\wedge((a_{3}\geq 80)\vee(a_{4}\geq 80))
.
However, a clear issue of this approach is that it requires a number of variables that grows with the length of the considered time intervals (i.e., the formula horizon) and time step in the temporal operators.

Our approach

In this paper we consider a novel alternative approach. We solve the consistency problem for a set of discrete-time STL requirements with bounded-time temporal operators by constructing a tree-shaped, one-pass tableau. The idea behind our tableau is that in order to prove the consistency of a (discrete-time) requirement set within a finite time horizon, one does not need to check the consistency of requirements at every time instant. Indeed, requirements with non-intersecting time domains are trivially consistent with each other and for requirements with intersecting time domains the check can be performed only for those time instants in which a new constraint is imposed, while the time instants in between can be skipped. In practice, when in consecutive nodes of the tableau the propositional part of the temporal formula extracted by the decomposition of the temporal operators is the same, the evaluation of the consistency is performed only once.
Given the structure of the algorithm, which allows for temporal jumps, the method is particularly suitable to check the consistency of requirements that need to be upheld for a long time horizon (e.g., the flight time of an aircraft).

Our contribution

We summarize the contributions of this paper as follows:

•

We introduce a novel method, based on a tree-shaped, one-pass tableau, to decide the satisfiability problem for discrete-time STL with bounded temporal operators. This method can be applied in different applications, from checking the consistency of a set of CPS requirements to generating examples of signals that satisfy a formula or improving specification mining techniques
(
Bartocci et al. 2022
)
. We prove both the soundness and the completeness of our method.

•

We provide several heuristics and optimizations to considerably improve the efficiency of our approach in practice. These heuristics take advantage of several decomposition properties of bounded temporal operators such as Globally and Finally in the discrete-time setting.

•

We implement our approach in a prototype tool written in Python. We successfully applied our method to solve the consistency checking problem on several benchmarks, including a realistic set of requirements provided by one of our industry partners.

•

We evaluated our method by comparing it against an SMT-based approach, using SMT solvers for satisfiability checking. We also compare our method with another approach proposed in the literature to check the satisfiability of the requirements
in Mission-time LTL
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, a “bounded variant of Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) over naturals”. To compare with their approach, we restrict our language to accept only Boolean predicates.

Related Work

Signal Temporal Logic (STL)
(
Maler and Nickovic 2004
)
is a formal language that has become increasingly popular due to its expressiveness in describing properties of continuous signals.
STL extends Metric Interval Temporal Logic (MITL) restricted to temporal operators in bounded non-singleton time intervals (
MITL_{[a,b]}
)
(
Alur et al. 1996
)
, enriching it with predicates over real-valued signals.
Despite being widely used in the context of cyber-physical systems, STL still poses interesting challenges, in particular solving the satisfiability problem, which tries to determine whether there exists a set of signals that satisfy a given specification.
Deciding satisfiability for STL is equivalent to deciding the satisfiability of (
MITL_{[a,b]}
) and is EXPSPACE-complete
(
Alur and Henzinger 1993
)
.
Despite its complexity, solving the STL satisfiability problem is instrumental in many domains beyond consistency check, such as model predictive control
(
Raman et al. 2014
)
and automatic trace generation
(
Prabhakar et al. 2018
)
. Since STL is an extension of a fragment of MITL, a related relevant work to mention is the SMT encoding proposed in
(
Bersani et al. 2015
;
Bersani et al. 2016
)
to solve the satisfiability problem for different continuous-time temporal logics such as MITL and Quantified Temporal Logic (QTL)
(
Hirshfeld and Rabinovich 2005
)
. In contrast with these works, our satisfiability checking procedure supports discrete-time STL with Boolean predicates and inequalities over continuous values (which are not included in MITL) as basic propositions.

Dokhanchi et al. 2017
present a framework for debugging STL specifications
covering validity, vacuity, and redundancy checking.
They translate STL formulas into MITL by replacing real constraints with Boolean propositions through a brute-force approach.
Then, they translate MITL formulas into CLTL-over-clocks and use qtlSolver and Zot
(
Bersani et al. 2013
)
to check satisfiability.
They show that satisfiability of a restricted fragment of MITL is reducible to LTL satisfiability, which can be solved faster with existing tools
(
Cimatti et al. 2002
;
Rozier and Vardi 2010
)
.
While we do not consider vacuity and redundancy in this paper,
our tool for STL satisfiability checking could be used as a backend for the algorithms by
Dokhanchi et al. 2017
.
Our approach has the advantage that it does not need the costly preprocessing to translate STL formulas into MITL.

Raman et al. 2014
present an SMT-based encoding of discrete-time STL to address the control synthesis problem in model predictive control under an STL specification.
In Sec.
6
, we compare our tree-shaped tableau approach with their SMT-based encoding.

Li et al. 2022
address the satisfiability problem for requirements expressed in Mission-time LTL (MLTL)
(
Reinbacher et al. 2014
)
.
Although MLTL specifications can also be encoded in discrete-time STL, the two logics are not equivalent in expressivity because MLTL supports only Boolean signals.

Li et al. 2022
propose three methods for satisfiability checking:
the first converts the MLTL formula into LTL or LTL-on-finite-traces (LTLf), and exploits existing solvers for these logics;
the second translates the MLTL formula into an SMV
(
McMillan 1993
)
Boolean transition system to be checked with the NuXmv
(
Cavada et al. 2014
)
model checker;
the third encodes the MLTL formula into a first-order logic (FOL) formula and solves it using the Z3
(
de Moura and Bjørner 2008
)
Theorem Prover.
Their results show that the second approach performs well for formulas with small temporal intervals,
while the third scales better for larger intervals.
Our approach, in contrast, offers an alternative by using a tableau method that can skip time instants when they are not necessary to prove the inconsistencies, which can provide benefits in performance.
In the experimental section, we compare our approach with the FOL encoding proposed by
Li et al. 2022
using their benchmarks.

Other approaches
(
Bae and Lee 2019
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Hendrik Roehm and Althoff 2016
)
demonstrate how to perform STL bounded model checking of hybrid/dynamical systems modeling cyber-physical systems (CPS) as solving, using SMT-solvers, a satisfiability problem of “a first-order logic formula over reals”
(
Lee et al. 2021
)
.

Lee et al. 2021
also consider the problem of satisfiability of STL for the continuous-time setting.
In contrast, our approach considers only the discrete-time interpretation of STL.

Tableau methods have been used extensively to assess the satisfiability of Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
(
Pnueli 1977
)
specifications, most of them being graph-shaped and two-pass
(
Wolper 1985
;
Lichtenstein and Pneuli 2000
)

(i.e., first a graph representing all the paths of the transition system describing the formula is constructed and then such graph is traversed once more to find a model for the input formula).
A tree-shaped, one-pass tableau for LTL has been developed by
Reynolds 2016
,
and extended to LTL with past by
Gigante et al. 2017
.
This kind of tableau has the advantage of simultaneously building a branch of the tree and deciding whether it is an accepting branch.
Reynold’s tableau has been implemented by
Bertello et al. 2016
and later parallelized
(
McCabe-Dansted and Reynolds 2017
)
.
A one-pass, tree-shaped tableau for another real-time temporal logic,
Timed Propositional Temporal Logic (TPTL)
(
Alur and Henzinger 1994
)
, has been proposed by
Geatti et al. 2021
.
More recently,
Geatti et al. 2024
devised a SAT encoding of Reynold’s tableau to check the satisfiability of LTL and LTL interpreted on finite traces.

Our method takes inspiration from
Reynolds 2016
to design a single-pass, tree-shaped tableau for STL.
While Reynolds’
PRUNE
rule exploits node repetition to prune redundant tree branches,
our tableau exploits it to skip redundant portions of the tree arising from large time intervals.
Additionally, our tableau employs an SMT solver to check satisfiability of linear constraints in temporal formulas.
To the best of our knowledge, there are no other tableau methods to assess the satisfiability of discrete-time STL with bounded-time operators.

Signal Temporal Logic

STL
(
Maler and Nickovic 2004
)
is defined on a temporal domain
\mathbb{T}
, which is usually real numbers.
In this paper, however, we consider
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{N}
.
Let
S=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a set of signal variables:
a
signal
is a function
w:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We define the projection
w_{R}
of a signal
w
on a set of variables
R\subseteq S
in the usual way.
STL formulas follow the syntax:

\varphi:=\top\mid f(R)=k\mid f(R)>k\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}\mid{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{U}}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}

where

is any set of signal variables,

any linear function of signal values,

any rational constant,
and
I=[a,b]
with
a,b\in\mathbb{T}
.

The semantics of STL are given with respect to a signal
w
and a time instant
t\in\mathbb{T}
as follows:

\displaystyle(w,t)\models\top

(always true)

\displaystyle(w,t)\models f(R)=k

iff

\displaystyle f(w_{R}(t))=k

\displaystyle(w,t)\models f(R)>k

iff

\displaystyle f(w_{R}(t))>k

\displaystyle(w,t)\models\neg\varphi

iff

\displaystyle(w,t)\not\models\varphi

\displaystyle(w,t)\models\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}

iff

\displaystyle(w,t)\models\varphi_{1}\text{ or }(w,t)\models\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{U}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle\exists t^{\prime}\in[t+a,t+b]:(w,t^{\prime})\models\varphi_{2}\text{ and }\forall t^{\prime\prime}\in[t,t^{\prime}]:(w,t^{\prime\prime})\models\varphi_{1}

Note that it is possible to encode
atomic propositions
as signals by defining a variable
x_{p}

for each proposition

, and asserting
p
as
x_{p}=1
.

We employ derived propositional operators (
\land
,
\rightarrow
)
and derived temporal operators
eventually
(or
finally
)
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{I}}

and

(or

)
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{I}}

We define the

operator
\mathsf{R}
,
and two

variants of the until (
\mathsf{sU}
) and release (
\mathsf{sR}
) operators with the following semantics:

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{R}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle\forall t^{\prime}\in[t+a,t+b]:(w,t^{\prime})\models\varphi_{2}\text{ or }\exists t^{\prime\prime}\in[t,t^{\prime}]:(w,t^{\prime\prime})\models\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\exists t^{\prime}\in[t+a,t+b]:(w,t^{\prime})\models\varphi_{2}\text{ and }\forall t^{\prime\prime}\in[t+a,t^{\prime}-1]:(w,t^{\prime\prime})\models\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\forall t^{\prime}\in[t+a,t+b]:(w,t^{\prime})\models\varphi_{2}\text{ or }\exists t^{\prime\prime}\in[t+a,t^{\prime}-1]:(w,t^{\prime\prime})\models\varphi_{1}

A formula
\phi

,

,

\displaystyle{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{U}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\equiv\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,a]}}\varphi_{1}\land{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{(\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2})}

\displaystyle{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{R}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\equiv\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,a]}}\varphi_{1}\lor{(\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2})}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

,

4.1.
The Basic Tableau

We present a one-pass, tree-shaped tableau for STL satisfiability on a discrete time domain.
A tableau is a tree in which each node
u
is labeled with a set of formulas
\Gamma(u)
, and a time counter
t(u)
.

{forest}

Figure 1
.
Basic tableau for
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[1,2]}}x>0
.
Each node’s time counter is shown in bold face.

The root
u_{0}
is labeled with a formula
\phi
to be checked for satisfiability.
The tree is generated by iteratively applying to each node a set of rules that generate its children,
until either a branch is
accepted
, proving the existence of a model for
\phi
—i.e., a signal that satisfies
\phi
—or all branches are
rejected
, proving that
\varphi
is unsatisfiable.

Examples

We first illustrate informally the tableau built for formula

,

We assume w.l.o.g. that formula
\phi

Some of such rules may

\overline{\mathsf{sU}}

After repeated application of expansion rules, each branch ends with a node
that only contains terms and operators that either are marked,
or such that the lower bound of their interval is greater than the node’s current time.
We call such nodes
poised
.
We thus proceed by trying to build the next instant of the model signal, by applying the
STEP
rule.

STEP
:

Let
u
be a poised node.
If
\Gamma(u)
contains any (marked or unmarked) temporal operators,

u
has one child
u^{\prime}
such that
t(u^{\prime})=t(u)+1
, and

\displaystyle\Gamma(u^{\prime})=\

\displaystyle\{\mathop{\mathsf{A}_{I}}{\varphi}\in\Gamma(u)\mid\mathsf{A}\in\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}\}\}\cup\{{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u)\mid\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}\}

\displaystyle\cup\{\mathop{\mathsf{A}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi}\mid\mathop{\mathsf{\overline{\mathsf{A}}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi}\in\Gamma(u)\land\mathsf{A}\in\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}\}\land t(u)<b\}

\displaystyle\cup\{{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\mid{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{B}}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u)\land\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}\land t(u)<b\}

Let
u
be a poised node, and
\overline{u}
the branch from the root
u_{0}
to
u
.
If
\overline{u}
encodes a signal that satisfies
\phi

u
is

;
if
\overline{u}
cannot encode a prefix of any satisfying signal—i.e., there is no way to reach an accepted node by further developing the tree from

—then
u
is
rejected
.
The following
termination
rules are applied to all poised nodes right before the
STEP

and they can accept or reject them.

FALSE
:

If
\neg\top\in\Gamma(u)
, node
u
is rejected.

LOCALLY-UNSAT
:

If the set
\{f(R)=k,f(R)>k\in\Gamma(u)\}
is inconsistent,
u
is rejected.

UNTIL
:

If
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{sU}}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u)
or
\mathop{\mathsf{\overline{\mathsf{F}}}_{[a,b]}}\varphi\in\Gamma(u)
with

, then the node is rejected.

EMPTY
:

If
\Gamma(u)
contains no temporal operators and no rejecting rules apply,
then
u
is accepted.

The consistency of sets
\{f(R)=k,f(R)>k\in\Gamma(u)\}
is checked by a solver for linear real arithmetic.

4.2.
The
JUMP
Rule

{forest}

.
Tableau for formula
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,10]}}x>5\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,11]}}x<0

Consider, again, the example of Fig.
1
.
The labels of nodes
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are repeated identically in
u_{2}
’s descendants,
except for their time instant: these are
repeated
nodes.
The same can be said of nodes
u_{0}
,
u_{1}
, and
u_{2}
in Fig.
2
.
For formulas containing temporal operators with long intervals,
the basic tableau contains many repeated nodes, leading to unnecessary redundancy.
For instance, the basic tableau for
\phi^{\prime}=\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,20]}}{(x>0)}

consists of the repetition of nodes
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
for 20 times.
This redundancy is unnecessary: once we have created
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
,
we already know what the rest of the tableau will look like.
To avoid redundancy and speed up the construction of the tableau,
we introduce the
JUMP
rule, which we apply instead of the
STEP
rule
to “skip” nodes with repeated labels, making a temporal jump by more than one time unit.

4.2.1.
Example

Fig.
3
shows the
tableau for formula
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,10]}}x>5\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,11]}}x<0
.
First, we apply to the root the expansion rule for the
\mathsf{G}
operator,
which extracts
x>5
and marks the
\mathsf{G}
operator.
Then, the rule for
\mathsf{F}
creates two children:
one (left) in which
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,11]}}x<0
is satisfied, as
x<0
holds;
and one (right) in which its satisfaction is postponed, and the
\mathsf{F}
operator is left marked.
The left child is immediately rejected by the
LOCALLY-UNSAT
rule,
because the constraints
x<0
and
x>5
are contradictory.
The right child is a poised node, and we can apply the
STEP
rule to it.

If we proceeded as in Sec.

we would obtain a tree consisting of nine more repetitions of the first three levels of Fig.

Instead, we use a new rule, that “jumps” directly to time 10, when the
\mathsf{G}
operator reaches the end of its interval.
This rule, called the
JUMP
rule, picks the smallest time bound
k
of any operator that is greater than the current node’s time,
and creates a child node that has the same label as the node obtained by applying the
STEP
rule repeatedly until reaching
k
.

We apply again the expansion rules, and the right child created by the
\mathsf{F}
rule is a poised node to which the
STEP
rule applies.
Since the
\mathsf{G}
operator has exhausted its interval, the
STEP
rule creates a node that contains only
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,11]}}x<0
.
One application of the
\mathsf{F}
expansion rule creates an accepted node,
showing that
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,11]}}x<0
can be satisfied by imposing
x<0
at time 11.
We can obtain a signal that satisfies the root formula by visiting the tree from the root to the accepted node, and collecting real constraints in poised nodes.
The signal consists of solutions to these constraint sets.
To reconstruct signal instants skipped by the
JUMP
rule,
we just repeat the solution to constraints in the node to which we applied it
(
x>5
in the example).

Fig.
4
shows part of the tableau for formula
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}^{J_{0}}_{[0,10]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,1]}}x>0
.
Nodes are created with the same rules as in the previous examples, so we just highlight a few aspects.
Nodes connected to their parents by an edge labeled with
G
derive from the extraction of the
F
operator from the outer
G
.
Their (subscript) interval bounds are increased by the current time—for instance, the second application of the
G
expansion rule extracts
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}^{J_{1}}_{[1,2]}}x>0
.
Moreover, temporal operators are superscripted with the time interval of the operator from which they were extracted (or
[-1,-1]
if they have no parent).
Let us ignore this superscript for now (we will need it later on).

The
\mathsf{G}
operator produces a new instance of
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}^{J_{1}}_{[t,t+1]}}x>0
at every time instant.
These
\mathsf{F}
operators produce two children each.
Thus, the tree grows exponentially.

However,
\mathsf{F}
operators keep disappearing, because they must be satisfied within their intervals.
Thus, the number of occurrences of
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}^{J_{1}}_{[t,t+1]}}x>0
in nodes is bounded by their intervals’ width (2 in this case).
Moreover, nodes become highly redundant, even though not exactly identical:
for instance, nodes
u_{1}
and
u_{10}
only differ in the interval of the
\mathsf{F}
operator.
Thus, we cannot just jump from
u_{1}
to a node differing only in the time counter,
but the
JUMP
rule must update the intervals of
\mathsf{F}
operators extracted from
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}^{J_{0}}_{[0,10]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,1]}}x>0
accordingly.

4.2.2.
Formal definition

We now describe the tableau with the
JUMP
rule.
In the following, we omit unary operators
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
to simplify notation.
Rules for them can be easily obtained through the usual equivalences

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{I}}\varphi\equiv{\neg\top}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{I}}{\varphi}
and
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{I}}\varphi\equiv{\top}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{I}}{\varphi}
.

To use the new rule, the tableau decorates (in the superscript) nested temporal operators
with the interval of the operator in which they are nested,
which we call the
parent
formula.
This interval is used to keep track of whether the current time falls within
the interval in which the parent of a temporal operator is active.
For instance,
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}^{J_{1}}_{[t,t+1]}}x>0
is decorated with
J_{1}
,
that is the interval in which the parent formula
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}^{J_{0}}_{[0,10]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,1]}}x>0
is active.
Temporal operators in
\pcl(\Gamma(u_{0}))
—the root label—are decorated with the interval
[-1,-1]
,
meaning that they have no parent.

We use the expansion rules in categories P and J in Table
1
,
and re-define the temporal expansion of a formula
\varphi
with respect to interval
I
as follows (it remains the same for propositional operators):

\displaystyle\operatorname{tex}^{t}_{I}({\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}})={\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{I}_{[a+t,b+t]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\text{with }\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}

\Gamma(u)

{{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{sU}}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}}

\displaystyle\cup\{{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\mid{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{B}}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u)\land\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}\land t(u)\not\in J\land t(u)<b\}

(7)

\displaystyle\cup\{{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{[a+k,b+k]}}{\varphi_{2}}\mid{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{B}}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u)\land\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}\land t(u)\in J\land t(u)<b\}

Note that the conditions for applying the
STEP
and
JUMP
rules are mutually exclusive,
so one of them can always be applied to a poised node.

The
JUMP
rule can only be applied if
t(u)<b
for all marked operators,
so that consistency is checked with the basic tableau at all interval upper bounds.
Moreover, the jump length
k
is computed so that the child node’s time is the closest
bound of any operator that is greater than the current time.
These two constraints make sure that we always check consistency of all possible combinations of operators that can be active at any time.

Sets (
4
) and (
6
)
that compose the label of the new child
u^{\prime}
implement straightforward jumps,
like the ones in Fig.
3
.
Condition
t(u)\not\in J
selects only operators that are either not nested (
J=[-1,-1]
),
or that have been extracted from the arguments of other temporal operators
that are no longer active, because
u
’s time falls after their interval.
Such operators are placed in
\Gamma(u^{\prime})
without changing their time intervals,
only unmarking them if they are marked.

Sets (
5
) and (
7
) cover
cases like the one in Fig.
4
.
Here, to jump between
u_{1}
and
u_{10}
we cannot just put
\mathop{\mathsf{\overline{\mathsf{F}}}^{J_{1}}_{[1,2]}}p

into
\Gamma(u_{10})
, but we need to increment
both bounds
of its interval by the jump length
k=9
.
This kind of more complex jump, however, can only be performed once the tableau
has been explored enough by the
STEP
rule to have tried all possible combinations
of choices of whether to satisfy
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{R}
operators,
i.e., all possible applications of their expansion rules.
The second condition for applying the
JUMP
rule,
requiring that
t(u)
has reached or exceeded the end of the interval of all derived operators,
ensures this constraint.
In the example, the
JUMP
rule can be applied only at time 1,
when
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,1]}}p
has exhausted its interval.

Note that
\Gamma(u_{10})
, as obtained by applying the
JUMP
rule,
is not exactly the same as
u_{10}
’s label in the figure:
real-numbered constraints are dropped, and marked operators are unmarked.
Indeed, consistency has already been checked in the node
u

to which we applied the
JUMP
rule,
so it needs not be checked again.
Unmarked operators are marked again by expansion rules.

Termination rules are the same as in the basic tableau.

4.2.3.
Reconstructing signals

If the tableau contains an accepted node
u_{n}
, a satisfying signal
can be obtained from the branch
\mathbf{u}=u_{0}\dots u_{n}
that goes from the root
u_{0}
to
u_{n}
.
Let
\textbf{p}=p_{0}\dots p_{m}
be the sequence of indices
such that
u_{p_{0}}\dots u_{p_{m}}
is the sequence of poised nodes in
\mathbf{u}
.
For each
0\leq i\leq m
, we ask the linear-real-arithmetic solver
for a solution to the constraint set
\{f(R)=k,f(R)>k\in\Gamma(u_{p_{i}})\}
.
The resulting variable assignment will constitute time
t=p_{i}
of our satisfying signal.
If the
JUMP
rule was applied to some node
u_{p_{j}}
, for
0\leq j<m
, then we have
p_{j+1}>p_{j}
.
We associate to time instants
t
, with
p_{j}<t<p_{j+1}
,
of the signal the variable assignment obtained for

For instance, from Fig.

4.3.
Soundness

We prove that the tableau is sound, meaning that if a complete tableau has an accepted branch,
then the formula is satisfiable, and the set of requirements it represents is consistent.
To simplify the proofs, we only consider
\mathsf{sU}
and
\mathsf{sR}
operators,
from which
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
are derived.
We start by proving termination.

Definition 4.1.

The
Time Horizon

\horiz(\phi)
of a formula
\phi
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\horiz(\top)=\horiz(f(R)>k)=0

\displaystyle\horiz(\neg\varphi_{1})=\horiz(\varphi_{1})

\displaystyle\horiz(\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2})=\max(\horiz(\varphi_{1}),\horiz(\varphi_{2}))

\displaystyle\text{with }\circ\in\{\land,\lor\}

\displaystyle\horiz({\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}})=b+\max(\horiz(\varphi_{1}),\horiz(\varphi_{2}))

\displaystyle\text{with }\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}

Theorem 4.2 (Termination).

The complete tableau for a formula
\phi
is a finite tree.

Proof.

Expansion rules, and step and jump rules only add a finite number of children (at most two) to each node.
Thus, the tableau can be infinite only if it has at least an infinitely long branch.
First, note that expansion rules may only add a finite number of nodes to a branch,
because they only add children containing simpler or marked formulas.
Further, we prove that the step and jump rules can be applied only a finite number of times in each branch.
To do so, we prove that for any node
u
and for all temporal operators
\psi\in\Gamma(u)
,
we have
I_{u}(\psi)\leq\horiz(\phi)
.

New temporal operators can be introduced into nodes by the expansion rules.
For any formula
\psi\in\Gamma(u)
, for all temporal operators

\varphi\in\Gamma_{\psi}(u_{1})\cup\Gamma_{\psi}(u_{2})
,
we have
I_{u}(\varphi)\leq\horiz(\psi)
.
This fact is only non-trivial for temporal operators.
For instance, let
\psi={\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}^{J}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}
,
and let
{\varphi_{3}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{4}}\in\pcl(\varphi_{1})\cup\pcl(\varphi_{2})
,
with
\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}
.
In this case, we have
{\varphi_{3}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{I}_{[a+t,b+t]}}{\varphi_{4}}\in\pcl(\varphi)

for some
\varphi\in\Gamma_{\psi}(u_{1})\cup\Gamma_{\psi}(u_{2})
.
The expansion rule is triggered only if
t(u)\in I
, hence
t\leq I_{u}(\psi)
.
It is easy to prove by induction that, since
{\varphi_{3}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{4}}
is a subformula of
\psi
,
we have
I_{u}(\psi)+b\leq I_{u}(\psi)+\max(\horiz(\varphi_{1}),\horiz(\varphi_{2}))\leq\horiz(\psi)
,
hence
b+t\leq I_{u}(\psi)+b\leq\horiz(\psi)
.
The proof is analogous for the release operator.

This fact, together with
\horiz(\psi^{\prime})\leq\horiz(\psi)
for any subformula
\psi^{\prime}
of
\psi
,
concludes the proof.

The step rule leaves bounds of temporal operators unchanged.
The jump rule may increase the upper bound of an operator

{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}
with
\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}

and
J=[c,d]
by an amount
k
such that
k\leq d
, because
k\in K(u)
,
and if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u)
,
it must be derived by the expansion rules by an operator
\varphi\in\Gamma(u)

such that
I(\varphi)=[c,d]
.
Thus,
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}
is a subformula of
\varphi
,
and
b+d\leq\horiz(\varphi)
, hence
b+k\leq\horiz(\varphi)
.

For any node
u
, the node
u^{\prime}
added by a step or a jump rule is such that
t(u^{\prime})>t(u)
,
and for all
\varphi\in\Gamma(u^{\prime})
we have either
I_{l}(\varphi)>t(u)
for non-marked operators,
or
I_{u}(\varphi)>t(u)
.
Since
I_{u}(\varphi)<\horiz(\phi)
, at some point a node
u_{f}
is reached such that
either
\Gamma(u_{f})
contains no temporal operators, or
t(u_{f})\geq\horiz(\phi)
.
In both cases,
u_{f}
is a leaf.
∎

To prove the soundness of the tableau, we introduce the concept of
model
of a formula
\phi
, which formalizes the proof obligations that need to hold in each time instant of a satisfying signal.
In fact, in Lemma
4.4
we prove that we can build a satisfying signal from a model for
\phi
, and
vice versa
.
In Thm.

we exploit this fact by showing that if the tableau for

then it has a model, and therefore a satisfying signal.

Definition 4.3.

A

for a formula

is a sequence of
atoms

\mathbf{\Delta}=\Delta_{0}\Delta_{1}\dots\Delta_{n}
,
with

, that are the smallest sets of formulas that satisfy the following rules:

;

for each

(1)

if
\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}\in\Delta_{i}
then
\varphi_{1}\in\Delta_{i}
or
\varphi_{2}\in\Delta_{i}

(2)

if
\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}\in\Delta_{i}
then
\varphi_{1}\in\Delta_{i}
and
\varphi_{2}\in\Delta_{i}

and for each

(3)

if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j}
,
with
\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}
, and

{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j+1}
,

(4)

if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j}
and

\operatorname{tex}^{j}(\varphi_{2})\in\Delta_{j}
or
(
\operatorname{tex}^{j}(\varphi_{1})\in\Delta_{j}
and
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j+1}
),

(5)

if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j}
and
j=b
then

\operatorname{tex}^{j}(\varphi_{2})\in\Delta_{j}
,

(6)

if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j}
and
a\leq j<b
then either

\operatorname{tex}^{j}(\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2})\in\Delta_{j}
or
(
\operatorname{tex}^{j}(\varphi_{2})\in\Delta_{j}
and
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j+1}
),

(7)

if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Delta_{j}

\operatorname{tex}^{j}(\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2})\in\Delta_{j}
,

where
\operatorname{tex}^{j}
is defined as in Sec.
4.1.2
.

Lemma 0.

A formula
\phi
is satisfiable iff it has a model
\mathbf{\Delta}=\Delta_{0}\Delta_{1}\dots\Delta_{n}

such that for all
i=1,\dots,n
the set
\{f(R)=k,f(R)>k\in\Delta_{i}\}
is consistent.

Proof.

Given a model for
\phi
, we can build a signal
w
that satisfies
\phi
such that
w(i)
is an assignment that satisfies

\{f(R)=k,f(R)>k\in\Delta_{i}\}
for each
i=1,\dots,n
.

The fact that such a signal satisfies
\phi
can be proved by induction on the syntactic structure of
\phi

by applying the following laws:

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle(w,t+1)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\text{ for $\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}$}

if
t<a

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{2})\\
&\lor(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{1})\land(w,t+1)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\end{aligned}

if
a\leq t<b

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{2})

if
t=b

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{1})\land(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{2})\\
&\lor(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{2})\land(w,t+1)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}\end{aligned}

if
a\leq t<b

\displaystyle(w,t)\models{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

iff

\displaystyle(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{1})\land(w,t)\models\operatorname{tex}^{t}(\varphi_{2})

if
t=b

These laws can be proved similarly to the well-known expansion laws for the LTL until and release operators
(
Baier and Katoen 2008
)
,
but adapting them for STL’s timed operators.

If a signal satisfies
\phi
, one can build a model for it by applying the laws above bottom-up
w.r.t. the syntactic structure of the formula.
∎

Before proceeding with the soundness proof, we highlight the fact that nested temporal operators cause periodicity in sufficiently deep tableau branches.
We illustrate this phenomenon in Fig.
4
,
and we formalize it here to justify the correctness of the
JUMP

Lemma 0.

Let
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}^{J}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}
or
{\varphi_{2}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}^{J}_{I}}{\varphi_{1}}
in
\Gamma(u)

with
I=[a,b]
for some poised node
u
in the basic tableau such that
t(u)=a
.

If the conjunction of the formulas in
\Gamma(u)
is satisfiable, there exists a branch in which
for all formulas
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[c,d]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\pcl(\varphi_{1})
,
for all poised nodes
u^{\prime}
such that
a+d\leq t(u^{\prime})\leq b+d
, we have either

{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{I}_{[t(u^{\prime})+c-i,t(u^{\prime})+d-i]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u^{\prime})
or

{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{B}}}^{I}_{[t(u^{\prime})+c-i,t(u^{\prime})+d-i]}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u^{\prime})

for all
i=0,1,\dots,d
.

Proof.

The proof can be carried out by induction, observing that if we unfold a branch starting from
u

without ever using the
JUMP
rule,
and by always choosing the
\Gamma_{\phi}(u_{2})
part of expansion rules when possible
(i.e., when the current time is strictly lower than an operator’s upper bound),
new instances of nested temporal operators are extracted at each step,
with their time bounds increased by 1.
Since operators whose upper bound is lower than a node’s time disappear,
we reach a point (namely after time
a+d
) in which extracted operators become stationary.
I.e., we have
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{I}_{[t(u^{\prime})+c-i,t(u^{\prime})+d-i]}}{\varphi_{2}}

(or the marked counterpart) for
i=0,1,\dots,d
and, at each further step,

{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{I}_{[t(u^{\prime})+c-d,t(u^{\prime})]}}{\varphi_{2}}
disappears (with
i=d
),
while a new
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{I}_{[t(u^{\prime})+c,t(u^{\prime})+d]}}{\varphi_{2}}
appears (
i=0
).
∎

Given a set of formulas
\Gamma
from tableau node labels,
we define
\unmark(\Gamma)
as the set obtained by unmarking marked operators,
and removing interval superscripts from temporal operators in
\Gamma
.
For instance, if
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}^{J}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}
or
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\overline{\mathsf{sU}}}^{J}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}
are in
\Gamma
,
they are both replaced by
{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}
.
Instances of the release operator are replaced similarly, and all other operators remain unchanged.

Theorem 4.6 (Soundness).

If the tableau for formula
\phi
has an accepted branch,
\phi
is satisfiable.

Proof.

We show how to build a model for
\phi
from an accepted branch of the tableau,
as we did in Sec.

, but more formally.
Let
\mathbf{u}=u_{0}\dots u_{n}
be the accepted branch,
and let
\textbf{p}=p_{0}\dots p_{m}
be the sequence of indices
such that

is the sequence of poised nodes in
\mathbf{u}
.

For each

, we define

as the set
\bigcup_{j=p_{i-1}+1}^{p_{i}}\unmark(\Gamma(u_{j}))

\bigcup_{j=0}^{p_{0}}\unmark(\Gamma(u_{j}))
.
Note that all

1

We build a model
\mathbf{\Delta}=\Delta_{0}\dots\Delta_{t_{f}}

\Delta_{0}=\Delta(u_{p_{0}})
.
Note that
\phi\in\Delta_{0}
and

.
Then, let
\Delta_{t}=\Delta(u_{p_{i}})

If the

\displaystyle\delta_{j+1}=

\displaystyle\{{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}^{J}_{I}}{\varphi_{2}}\in\Gamma(u_{p_{i}})\mid\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}\land t(u_{p_{i}})\not\in J\}

4.4.
Completeness

The tableau method is complete: we prove that if a formula is satisfiable,
then the tableau built for the formula has at least one accepting branch.
We first prove the completeness of the basic tableau, built by only using the
STEP
rule,
and then show that if a formula has an accepting branch in it,
it has one also in a tableau using the
JUMP

Lemma
4.4
states that if there exists a signal that satisfies
\phi
,
then also a model for it exists.
We first show that, given a model for
\phi
,
we can descend the tableau built only by the

Lemma 0.

If a formula
\phi
is satisfiable, then its basic tableau has an accepting branch.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{\Delta}=\Delta_{0}\Delta_{1}\dots\Delta_{m}

We inductively build a node sequence

is a leaf

is not rejected, and
\unmark(\Gamma(u_{i}))\subseteq\Delta_{t(u_{i})}

The base case is trivially proved, because
\Gamma(u_{0})=\{\phi\}\subseteq\Delta_{0}

Let

\Gamma(u_{i})\subseteq\Delta_{t(u_{i})}
.
We choose

as follows.

•

If

is not a poised node, then an expansion rule from Table

has only one child, which we set to

Otherwise,
u_{i}
has two children

and

is replaced by
the contents of either

or
\Gamma_{\psi}(u_{2})
from Table

4.5.
Complexity

Consider formula
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,k_{1}]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[0,k_{2}]}}p\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,k_{1}+k_{2}]}}\neg p
.
An accepted branch in the tableau rooted in it must have at least
k_{1}+k_{2}
poised nodes,
because the
JUMP
rule can only be applied to nodes
u
with
t(u)\geq k_{1}+k_{2}
.
The tableau can be implemented as a depth-first search
that keeps in memory only the current branch,
using memory at most exponential in the binary representation of
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
.
Local consistency checks can be performed in polynomial time by a linear real arithmetic solver
(
Khachiyan 1980
)
, so they do not dominate complexity.

A lower bound for STL satisfiability follows from the satisfiability problem
of MTL on discrete time domains, which was proven EXPSPACE-hard
by a reduction from EXPSPACE-bounded Turing machines
(
Alur and Henzinger 1993
)
.
Thus, discrete-time STL satisfiability is EXPSPACE-complete.

5.
Heuristics and Optimizations

Our implementation includes heuristics and other optimizations that speed up tableau construction.
We describe them in this section, and empirically test their effectiveness in Sec.
6.3
.

5.1.
Early termination

Our implementation of the tableau stops generating the tree as soon as it finds an accepted branch,
instead of building the complete tableau.

5.2.
Syntactic Manipulations and other operator-specific rules

Specialized Rules

We already introduced special rules for the
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
operators in Sec.
4
.
We also use a special rule for implication: instead of using the standard equivalence
\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2}\equiv\neg\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
,
we add to category P of Table
1
a new expansion rule that generates two nodes in which
\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2}
is respectively replaced with
\neg\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
.
We observed empirically that asserting the antecedent together with the subsequent often improves performances
by causing the rejection of redundant subtrees, despite the resulting node being larger.

Nested temporal operators can make the tableau exponentially large.
To mitigate this problem, we add an expansion rule that replaces
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{I}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{I^{\prime}}}
formulas according to the equivalence

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,b]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi\equiv({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[a+c+1,a+d]}}\varphi}\lor({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a+c,a+c]}}\varphi\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a+d+1,a+d+1]}}\varphi}))\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a+2,b]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi

The right-hand-side formula “unrolls”
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,b]}}
by two time units:

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,a+1]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi
can be decomposed in

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[a+c,a+d]}}\varphi\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[a+1+c,a+1+d]}}\varphi
,
which is true if, either,
\varphi
holds anywhere in the overlapping part of the two
\mathsf{F}
intervals
(
blue formula
),
or if it holds at both times
a+c
and
a+d+1
(
purple formula
).

Removing redundant formulas

We use the following equivalences to merge operators with contiguous or overlapping intervals and reduce node size:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,b]}}\varphi\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,d]}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{if }a\leq c\leq b\leq d

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[a,b]}}\varphi\land\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi\leftrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{if }c\geq a\land d\leq b.

Shifting time intervals of nested operators

We generalize the first step (
1
) of the inconsistency proof for the example in Sec.
1
.
For any operator
\psi={\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}
, with
\mathsf{B}\in\{\mathsf{sU},\mathsf{sR}\}
,
such that
\Phi=\pcl(\varphi_{1})\cup\pcl(\varphi_{2})
contains only temporal operators,
we compute
c_{\min}
as the minimum lower bound of all operators in
\Phi
,
and replace
\psi
with
{\varphi^{\prime}_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{B}}_{[a+c_{\min},b+c_{\min}]}}{\varphi^{\prime}_{2}}
,
where
\varphi^{\prime}_{1}
and
\varphi^{\prime}_{2}
are obtained by shifting all time intervals of operators in
\Phi

back by
c_{\min}
.
We apply this transformation recursively, starting with innermost formulas and proceeding outwards.

5.3.
Early consistency check

Rather than performing the local consistency check (i.e., the
LOCALLY-UNSAT
rule)
only on poised nodes, we do it before each decomposition step, to avoid fully decomposing nodes that already contains inconsistent atomic expressions.

5.4.
Memoization

Nodes in the tableau can often be redundant, especially when generated by nested temporal operators.
To speed up tableau construction, we memorize rejected nodes,
and after each application of the
STEP
and
JUMP
rules we check if the resulting node
implies one of the previously rejected nodes.
If this is the case, the new node can be safely rejected without building the subtree rooted in it.
Since a proper implication check would be too expensive, we use a weaker one that is quicker,
and exploits the following relations:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,b]}}\varphi

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{if }a\leq c\leq d\leq b,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[a,b]}}\varphi

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[c,d]}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{if }c\leq a\leq b\leq d

\displaystyle{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sR}}_{[c,d]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\text{if }a=c\leq d\leq b,

\displaystyle{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[a,b]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\varphi_{1}}\mathbin{{\mathsf{sU}}_{[c,d]}}{\varphi_{2}}

\displaystyle\text{if }c=a\leq b\leq d

5.5.
Easy operators first

Checking nested temporal operators can be very slow, especially when the formula is not satisfiable and the tableau must be generated completely.
However, contradiction can often be assessed by checking just non-nested operators that are extracted by the expansion rules.
For instance, consider a tableau node labeled with
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,10]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[30,40]}}x>0,\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[30,50]}}x\leq 0
.
Since the
JUMP
rule can only be used after time 40,
we must develop the tableau by only applying expansion and the
STEP
rules until then.
The
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{I}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{I^{\prime}}}
formula causes the tableau size to grow exponentially,
making its construction infeasible.
However, the first application of the
\mathsf{G}
expansion rule creates a node labeled with

\mathop{\mathsf{\overline{\mathsf{G}}}_{[0,10]}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[30,40]}}x>0,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[30,50]}}x\leq 0,\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}_{[30,40]}}x>0}
:
we can infer unsatisfiability just from the last two formulas,
without needing to develop the entire tableau for the original formula.

{forest}

.
The
blue
node and its sibling are treated as AND nodes.
The
JUMP
rule can be applied to the purple node,
and the tableau quickly shows that its label is unsatisfiable,

p=x>0
.

Thus, we change the
STEP
and
JUMP
rules to return two children:
one containing only non-nested operators, and one as defined in Sec.

.
We treat the poised node as an AND-node in an AND-OR tree:
we check its first child—the one without nested operators—first,
and the second only if the first leads to an accepted branch
(cf. Fig.

).

This feature, combined with memoization, can prune entire subtrees,
proving very effective in speeding up the tableau for unsatisfiable formulas.

We implemented the tableau for STL in Python, using the Z3 SMT solver
(

)

The experimental evaluation comprises different benchmark sets taken from literature and industry.
We carried out all the experiments on a machine equipped with a 4.5GHz AMD CPU and 64 GB of RAM running Ubuntu 24.04.

(

Our tableau drastically outperforms the FOL encoding on randomly generated formulas.
A possible explanation for such differences is that the benchmarks in the NASA-Boeing suite on which the SMT solver performs better contain a large propositional part, on which the SMT solver performs very well, which overcomes the time required to solve temporal constraints.
The random benchmarks, on the other hand, contain several temporal operators with very large intervals, on which our tableau performs better.
The experiments suggest that our tableau could be used together with the FOL encoding by

Railroad, PCV, MTL, CPS, and Aerospace are the benchmarks that show the most significant variations when disabling optimizations,

For Railroad, this phenomenon can be explained by the particular structure of the two contradictory formulas analyzed in Sec.
1
.
The first one contains a
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}}
nested into a
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}}
—a worst-case scenario for the tableau—which prevents the
JUMP
rule from significantly shortening tree branches
(cf. Sec.
4.5
).
The second formula contains an implication nested into a
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}}
operator,
which causes branching at every time instant, thus exponentially increasing tableau size.
Since the two formulas are contradictory,
early termination does not apply (Sec.
5.1
)
and the tableau must be built entirely.

For the remaining benchmarks, the impact of optimizations can be explained by their large time horizon and number of requirements (hence, size).

Disabling the
JUMP
rule (2
nd
column)
mostly affects PCV, CPS, and Aerospace, and all MLTL benchmarks.
This is due to the high time horizon of these requirements (cf. Table
2
).
While MTL also has a long time horizon, it contains contradictions that are isolated by the easy-operators-first optimization (Sec.
5.5
),
and all branches are pruned after a few applications of the
STEP
rule.

The main benefit of using the specialized rules for unary operators
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}

is that the expansion rule for
\mathsf{G}
produces only one child node,
while both
\mathsf{sU}
and
\mathsf{sR}
expansion rules produce two children,
increasing tree branching.
If the specialized rules for unary operators
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
are disabled (3
rd
column), Railroad, MTL, and CPS are affected the most.
MTL and CPS are the benchmarks with the highest nesting depth, of mostly
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
operators,
which causes many temporal operators to extracted at every application of the expansion rules,
significantly increasing tree size.
Furthermore, both external and nested operators in CPS have large time horizons.
Railroad contains requirements that are worst-case scenarios for the tableau,
an issue exacerbated by the higher tree branching due to using
\mathsf{sU}
and
\mathsf{sR}
rules
instead of the specialized
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
rules.

Disabling optimizations based on syntactic manipulations (Sec.
5.2
)
affects mostly Railroad, CPS and Aerospace, mainly because they contain many instances of nested
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}}
operators.
Moreover, shifting time intervals is particularly beneficial for Railroad,
as we also used it in the manual proof of its inconsistency in Sec.
1
,
and Random MLTL benchmarks due to their large time intervals.

The early consistency check (Sec.
5.3
) greatly benefits CPS and Aerospace.
These benchmarks contain integer signals
that have been modeled with constraints of the form
x==0\lor x==1\lor x==2

(for a variable
x
that can only take one value among
0,1,2
).
They also contain many assume-guarantee requirements of the form

\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}_{[a,b]}}(x==k\rightarrow\varphi_{k})
, for
k\in\{0,1,2\}
,
whose conjunction results in many nodes containing clearly contradictory terms of the form
x==k,x==h
with
k\neq h
.
At the same time, these benchmark contain many
\lor
,
\rightarrow
, and
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}}
operators,
whose expansion rules create a large number of branches—precisely, a tree portion of size exponential in the number of such operators.
Early consistency checking prunes nodes before expansion rules can be applied,
avoiding the useless creation of large subtrees.

Memoization is very beneficial to Railroad, MTL, and CPS.
This phenomenon is mainly due to the high redundancy of tableau nodes generated by these benchmarks (cf. Fig.
4
).
In particular, the presence of nested
\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{G}}}\mathop{\mathsf{\mathsf{F}}}
operators causes large redundant subtrees,
that memoization recognizes and prunes effectively.

The optimization that checks easy operators first is very beneficial to MTL and CPS,
because these benchmarks contain contradictions that arise from non-nested operators,
and checking them first allows to skip the analysis of more complex parts of the requirements.
This optimization is, however, detrimental to almost all other benchmarks,
in a dramatic way for Random MLTL benchmarks (Fig.
8
).
Indeed, for formulas that are satisfiable, or for which finding a contradiction requires analyzing deeply nested operators,
checking a node containing only “easy” operators does not provide any benefit,
and causes additional overhead.
However, the speedup enjoyed by MTL and CPS justifies the use of this optimization in some cases.

7.
Conclusion

We propose a tree-shaped, one-pass tableau method to address the satisfiability of a set of discrete-time STL formulas with bounded temporal operators. Our approach integrates an SMT solver to check the satisfiability of linear constraints within the nodes of the tree. We prove that our method is sound and complete.

We evaluated our approach on several benchmarks and compared it against existing methods that encode the specification as SMT and FOL formulas. The experimental results demonstrate the applicability of our method to a large and complex set of requirements, and it outperforms existing approaches in most of the considered scenarios.

In future work, we plan to improve our method with new heuristics and extend its application to control synthesis problems and automatic example generation.
