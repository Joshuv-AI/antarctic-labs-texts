From efficacy to effectiveness: tonic motor activation in real-world management of refractory restless legs syndrome
Authors: Viviana Regina Konzen, Brian B Koo
Published: 2026

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) remains one of the most challenging chronic neurological sleep disorders to manage in clinical practice. Despite advances in treatment algorithms and growing recognition of the importance of iron replacement and alpha-2-delta ligands, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience inadequate symptom control, treatment intolerance, or loss of drug efficacy over time. Dopamine agonists can provide substantial symptomatic relief but are limited by the risk of augmentation, and thus are no longer standardly recommended [1]. Although being first line, alpha-2-delta ligands may be poorly tolerated or may have reduced effectiveness, particularly when RLS symptoms are severe [2]. Intravenous iron is guideline-recommended for appropriately selected patients, yet practical barriers related to access and insurance coverage may limit its use [3]. Low-dose opioids can be highly effective in refractory RLS; however, concerns regarding side effects, regulatory oversight, and prescribing practices continue to restrict their widespread adoption [4]. Consequently, there remains a significant unmet need for safe, effective, and durable treatment options for patients with moderate-to-severe RLS.

In this issue of SLEEP, Singh et al. report 90-day outcomes from the THRIVE study, providing the first large-scale prospective real-world evaluation of tonic motor activation (TOMAC) therapy for refractory RLS [5]. TOMAC is a novel therapy that delivers high-frequency stimulation to the peroneal nerves (common fibular nerve), resulting in TOMAC of the innervated anterior tibialis muscle and afferent feedback to the central nervous system in this way decreasing RLS symptoms. Previous randomized controlled trials established efficacy and safety under controlled experimental settings. In the published sham-controlled trial, peroneal nerve stimulation significantly improved RLS by approximately 7 points on the International RLS Study Group severity scale (IRLS), while also demonstrating a favorable safety profile [6]. This reduction in IRLS was similar to that seen in many drug trials [7]. Notably, therapeutic benefits continued to accrue during the subsequent open-label phase, suggesting that efficacy may increase with sustained use [8].

The THRIVE study extends these efficacy studies to a real worldclinical practice atmosphere and similarly shows effectiveness and tolerability of peroneal nerve stimulation for RLS. In this study’s real world cohort of 232 adults with moderate-to-severe refractory RLS, Singh et al. observed clinically meaningful improvements in RLS severity after 90 days of therapy with a decrease in the IRLS score of 8.4 points (95% CI 7.4, 9.4), closely mirroring reductions observed in prior controlled studies. Notably, 79 per cent of patients achieved a clinically meaningful improvement (≥ 3-point reduction), and 38 per cent experienced symptom relief significant enough to shift out of the “moderate-to-severe” classification entirely.

Perhaps the most important contribution of THRIVE is not that it demonstrates TOMAC efficacy, which had already been established in randomized controlled trials, but that it confirms effectiveness under the less controlled conditions of routine clinical practice. Real-world studies frequently reveal attenuation of treatment effects once therapies move beyond highly selected trial populations and intensive protocol-driven support. Instead, the magnitude of improvement observed in THRIVE closely parallels that reported in prior TOMAC trials, despite the inclusion of older patients with medical comorbidity and treatment by a diverse group of clinicians. At the same time, it is important to note that in a real world setting, other factors influencing RLS (e.g. sleep apnea and iron deficiency) were likely addressed and may have also led to RLS symptom improvement. This concern was largely addressed by the authors in sensitivity analyses. Together, these findings strengthen confidence that the benefits observed with peroneal nerve stimulation are both reproducible and clinically meaningful in everyday practice.

As peroneal nerve stimulation likely decreases RLS severity, it may allow a decrease in dose of adjunctive RLS medication. This has already been suggested in a single open-label clinical trial that demonstrated that 70 per cent (14 of 20) of participants were able to reduce their dosage of opioid medication while maintaining symptom control with the use of peroneal nerve stimulation.9 In the current THRIVE study, of the 232 participants, 19 per cent were able to decrease medication dosage, while 11 per cent needed a dose increase. Specifically, significantly more patients decreased rather than increased dosages for a DA. There was no significant difference in the rates of dosage increase versus decrease for alpha-2-delta ligands or opioids. Among patients taking DAs, 18 per cent safely achieved an average 52 per cent (0.33 pramipexole ME) reduction in their dose without experiencing a spike in RLS severity. These observations should not be interpreted as evidence that TOMAC independently facilitates DA withdrawal; the study was not designed to answer that question. Nonetheless, the findings suggest that TOMAC may be helpful in reducing RLS medication dosage particularly for DAs.

During the treatment of augmentation, a key component of the therapeutic algorithm is to reduce the dosage (and eventually discontinue) of DA. This period of time is difficult for patients as RLS invariably worsens. Any intervention that could ameliorate symptoms during this period and ease suffering would be welcome by both patients and RLS providers. Peroneal nerve stimulation may offer such an option, while having the added benefit of giving the patient a sense of control in that they themselves can use a non-pharmacologic device to decrease symptoms.

Encouragingly, peroneal nerve stimulation appears to have a favorable safety profile. Device-related adverse events were uncommon, predominantly mild or moderate, and included no serious or severe device-related events. Treatment discontinuation due to adverse effects occurred in only in a small minority of participants. Compared with the risks associated with long-term dopaminergic therapy, sedating alpha-2-delta-ligands, or opioid treatment, the safety characteristics reported in THRIVE appear favorable. Nevertheless, important questions remain. As an observational study without a concurrent control group, THRIVE cannot fully exclude placebo effects or other unmeasured influences on symptom improvement. In addition, longer-term data are needed to determine the durability of benefit beyond 90 days, identify predictors of response, and clarify whether TOMAC can meaningfully facilitate reduction or discontinuation of dopamine agonists in patients with augmentation.

The emerging evidence supporting the use of peroneal nerve stimulation comes at an opportune time. The AASM has recently conditionally recommended against the standard use of dopamine agonists to treat RLS.10 There is clearly a need to move beyond the reliance on dopamine agonists and perhaps even more on medications in general. There is need for a paradigm shift in RLS management toward a more diversified treatment model that includes neuromodulation, individualized risk–benefit assessment, and strategies aimed at minimizing long-term medication burden. As additional long-term data emerge, particularly regarding durability of response and facilitation of DA tapering, peroneal nerve stimulation may assume an increasingly prominent role within the therapeutic algorithm. The current study provides evidence that peroneal nerve stimulation is not merely efficacious under ideal trial conditions, but effective even amidst the complexities of real-world clinical practice. For patients with refractory RLS, that distinction matters.
