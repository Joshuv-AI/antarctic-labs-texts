Clinical presentation and outcomes of children hospitalized with sickle cell anemia at Gulu regional referral hospital, Uganda: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Keneth Okello, Morrish Obol Okello, Fred Sual Chemutai, Ezereth Nakasi, Ruth Catherine Ouma, Felix Bongomin, Vincent Kayina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSickle cell anemia (SCA) is a significant public health challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where 80% of affected individuals reside. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical presentation and outcomes of children with SCA hospitalized at GRRH. A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted, reviewing medical records of children aged 0–18 years hospitalized with SCA at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital (GRRH) between January 2019 and June 2024. Data on demographics, clinical presentations, interventions, and outcomes were collected and analyzed with descriptive statistics in Stata 18.0. Of 126 children enrolled, the median age was 7 years (IQR 3–10 years), with 55.6% male (n = 70). Painful crises (86.5%, n = 109) and fever (66.7%, n = 84) were the most common presentations, followed by jaundice (21.4%, n = 27) and respiratory distress (8.7%, n = 11). Most children (98.4%, n = 124) had hospital stays of less than one week, with 94.4% (n = 119) achieving complete recovery. Treatment included folic acid supplementation (99.2%, n = 125), antibiotics (96.8%, n = 122), and hydroxyurea (14.3%, n = 18). Mortality was 3.9% (n = 5), and 1.6% (n = 2) were transferred. Painful crises and fever were predominant clinical features, with high recovery rates and short hospital stays suggesting relatively favourable outcomes at GRRH. Mortality was 3.9%, which is lower than or comparable to reported inpatient mortality rates of 4–12% for paediatric sickle cell anaemia in other sub-Saharan African settings. However, limited hydroxyurea use highlights access barriers. Strengthening SCA healthcare policies, increasing hydroxyurea availability, and further research into severe cases are recommended to improve outcomes.Keywords: Paediatric haematology, Vaso-occlusive crisis, Hydroxyurea, UgandaSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Globally, Sickle cell anemia (SCA) affects millions, with sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) accounting for approximately 80% of cases1. Each year, approximately 300,000–400,000 babies are born worldwide with SCA (HbSS) or other severe sickle cell disease genotypes, with the majority in low and middle-income countries1,2. Children in SSA face heightened risks of complications such as acute chest syndrome, sepsis, and stroke3. SCA is a hereditary blood disorder caused by abnormal hemoglobin, leading to distorted red blood cells that trigger vaso-occlusive crises, infections, and organ damage4. In Uganda, the prevalence of SCA is estimated at 0.8%, with approximately 20,000 children born annually with the disease, significantly contributing to under-5 mortality5. The World Health Assembly Resolution 59.20 in 2006 highlighted SCA as a public health priority1.

Regional studies in sub-Saharan Africa have consistently reported varied presentation with painful crises as the dominant clinical presentation of SCA, affecting 70–90% of hospitalized children6,7. This presentation is often triggered by vaso-occlusive events that cause severe pain in bones, joints, or the abdomen4. Other accompanying symptoms always include fever, jaundice, and pallor8. Mortality mostly results from respiratory distress linked to acute chest syndrome, which is less common but critical9.

The burden of SCA in Uganda is exacerbated by systemic barriers, such as limited access to effective therapies like hydroxyurea and inadequate healthcare infrastructure in rural areas10. The Uganda Sickle Cell Surveillance Study highlighted the country’s high SCA birth prevalence, emphasizing the urgent need for localized research to inform evidence-based healthcare strategies11. Understanding the clinical profile and outcomes of children with SCA is critical for optimizing resource allocation, enhancing diagnostic precision, and improving patient care. Such data can guide healthcare providers in early diagnosis and timely interventions to mitigate complications and improve quality of life.

The limited use of hydroxyurea, reported in only 10–20% of cases in Uganda due to cost and availability barriers, indicates the need for improved access10. Prior studies have documented a high incidence of complications among hospitalized children with SCA. These include acute chest syndrome, alongside elevated mortality and readmission rates12–15.

Despite the high burden of sickle cell anaemia in Uganda and sub-Saharan Africa, contemporary hospital-based data on clinical presentation and short-term outcomes in children remain scarce, particularly from northern Uganda. Such data are essential to guide resource allocation, quality improvement initiatives, and policy advocacy. Therefore, this study aimed to describe the clinical presentation, management practices, and in-hospital outcomes of children admitted with sickle cell anaemia at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital between January 2019 and June 2024.

Methods

Study design

A facility-based retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted employing quantitative approach, reviewing medical records of children admitted between January 2019 and June 2024.

Study area

The study was conducted at GRRH, located in Gulu City, Northern Uganda, approximately 311 km north of Kampala, the capital city of Uganda. GRRH is the largest public health facility in the region, serving as a referral hospital for the Acholi sub-region, including Gulu City and nearby districts such as Omoro, Pader, Amuru, Kitgum, West Nile, and parts of South Sudan. As a teaching hospital for Gulu University’s Faculty of Medicine and other training institutions, GRRH provides specialized pediatric care.

Study population and eligibility

The study population included medical records of children aged 0–18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of SCA, based on laboratory or genetic testing, who were hospitalized at GRRH between January 2019 and June 2024. Children with other types of anemia, those outside the specified age range, or those with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Sampling procedure

GRRH was purposively selected due to its role as a regional referral hospital with a high patient load and diverse population. Consecutive sampling was used to select all eligible medical records of children meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period. This method involved reviewing every medical record that met the inclusion criteria in the order they were stored or retrieved from the hospital’s pediatric ward physical record system.

Study outcome

The Primary Outcomes of the study were clinical presentation, including the signs and symptoms observed at admission, and clinical outcomes at discharge, categorized as complete recovery, transferred/referred to another facility, or death, reported as proportions. Additionally, the length of hospital stay was recorded (categorized as less than 1 week, 1–2 weeks, 2–4 weeks, or more than 4 weeks) to describe the duration of hospitalization. Secondary outcomes included demographic characteristics, treatment modalities healthcare provider characteristics.

Definitions and outcome measures

Confirmed sickle cell anaemia:

Diagnosis previously established and documented in the medical record by haemoglobin electrophoresis, high-performance liquid chromatography, sickle solubility test plus typical clinical history, or genetic testing.

Vaso-occlusive (painful) crisis:

Severe bone, joint, chest, or abdominal pain requiring hospital admission and parenteral opioid analgesia, as recorded by the attending clinician.

Complete recovery:

Patient discharged alive with resolution of the acute presenting complaint and judged clinically stable for discharge by the treating clinician (no specific laboratory threshold required).

Transferred/referred:

Patient transferred to another facility (usually Mulago National Referral Hospital or Lacor Hospital) for specialised care not available at GRRH.

Length of hospital stay:

Number of days from the date of admission to the date of discharge or death (calculated inclusively).

Blood transfusion was defined as the administration of packed red blood cells or whole blood during the index admission and was required in 64 children.

Mortality

Death occurring during the index admission.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected over one month in March 2024 using structured questionnaires deployed via Kobo Toolbox (Kobo, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA) on Android devices. The questionnaire was pre-tested at a lower-level health facility (Awach Health Centre IV) in Gulu District to refine its validity and reliability. Minor adjustments were made to question wording and skip logic for clarity. A trained research assistant, a registered nurse, extracted data from medical records on demographics, clinical presentations, interventions, and outcomes.

Data analysis

Data were downloaded from Kobo Toolbox to Microsoft Excel for cleaning and coding, then exported to STATA MP Version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA) for further analysis. Results were reported as descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges due to non-normal distribution after Shapiro–Wilk. This study was designed to provide a descriptive overview of clinical presentation and in-hospital outcomes; therefore, only descriptive statistics were performed. Inferential statistics were not planned because the primary objective was to characterise the current pattern of disease rather than to test specific hypotheses or identify independent predictors.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

A total of 126 patient files were reviewed in the study, achieving a response rate of 100%. The median age of the participants was 7 years (IQR 3–10 years; range 0–17 years). Gender distribution revealed that 44.4% (n = 56) of the participants were female, while 55.6% were male (n = 70). Ethnicity was predominantly Acholi (80.2%, n = 101), with Langi (11.9%, n = 15) and others (7.9%, n = 10) making up the rest of the study population.

In terms of pre-existing conditions, 98.4% of the participants (n = 124) reported no pre-existing conditions, while 1.6% (n = 2) indicated the presence of such conditions (one with congenital heart disease and one with HIV on antiretroviral therapy). Clinical presentation of children hospitalized with SCA at Gulu regional referral hospital

The clinical presentation of children hospitalized with Sickle Cell Anemia (SCA) at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital included various symptoms. Vaso-occlusive crisis was observed in 86.5% (n = 109) of the cases, making it a common symptom among the children. Pallor was reported in 19.8% (n = 25) of the children, indicating anemia, which is a hallmark of SCA. Jaundice was noted in 21.4% (n = 27) of the participants, while fever was present in 66.7% (n = 84) of the children. Discussion

Our study aimed to determine the clinical presentation and outcomes of children hospitalized with Sickle Cell Anemia (SCA) at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital in Uganda. The high prevalence of painful crises (86.5%) among the children hospitalized with SCA indicates the severe nature of vaso-occlusive episodes, which are a hallmark of the disease4,9. This is understandable as most of our client’s reasons for the presentation were due to pain from microvasculature obstruction by sickled red blood cell under hypoxic situations and leading to prostaglandin release and pains. This finding aligns with previous studies that have reported high rates of painful crises in children with SCA6. For instance, a five–year review conducted in Nigeria also reported a high prevalence of vaso-occlusive crises among hospitalized children with SCA16. In Tanzania, a study showed that pain was the most common clinical presentation among patients hospitalized with SCA7. The high rate of painful crises indicates the need for effective pain management strategies and interventions to prevent these episodes, which can significantly impact the quality of life of affected children.

Fever was another common clinical presentation. This is consistent with previous studies that have identified fever as a frequent symptom in children with SCA in Tanzania7, Ethiopia7 and Sudan17. The presence of fever often necessitates thorough evaluation for potential infections, which children with SCA are particularly susceptible to due to their compromised immune function9. Fever is a symptom of infections, including Bacterial infections which are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in children with SCA18. The high rate of antibiotic treatment in our study reflects the need for prompt and effective infection management in this population.

The finding that about 2 in 10 of the children presented with jaundice highlights the impact of chronic hemolysis in SCA, leading to the accumulation of bilirubin8. This is in line with studies from other regions, such as Nigeria where review of patients charts showed that jaundice was the most common clinical presentation of children alongside foot and hand syndrome6. The presence of jaundice necessitates regular monitoring and appropriate interventions to manage hemolysis and prevent severe complications.

Respiratory distress and loss of consciousness, though few, are indicative of severe complications that require immediate medical attention19. The respiratory distress could be a symptom of acute chest syndrome20,21. However, no diagnosis of acute chest syndrome was made in our study. Respiratory distress, observed in 8.7% of cases, could also reflect decompensated severe anaemia rather than acute chest syndrome alone. These findings are similar to those reported in a study conducted in Tanzania, which also highlighted the occurrence of respiratory complications in 66.9% of children with SCA12. The management of such severe complications requires well-equipped healthcare facilities and trained personnel to provide timely and appropriate care22.

Regarding treatment modalities, the high rate of folic acid supplementation and antibiotic treatment reflects adherence to standard care guidelines for managing children with SCA. These treatment modalities align well with Uganda’s National Sickle Cell Disease Guidelines (2020)23, which recommend routine folic acid supplementation (1 mg daily) and empirical antibiotics for febrile episodes in SCA children. Antimalarial prophylaxis is also standard in malaria-endemic areas. However, hydroxyurea use remains low (14.3%) compared to guideline recommendations for primary prevention in high-risk children (e.g., those with recurrent crises or abnormal transcranial Doppler), likely reflecting access barriers. Blood transfusion practices followed guideline indications for severe anaemia (Hb < 6 g/dL) or acute complications.

Hydroxyurea has been shown to reduce the frequency of painful crises and improve overall outcomes in children with SCA24,25. However, utilization of hydroxyurea in most African countries is low due to barriers that range from individual, facility, and community levels that need to be resolved10. These include lack of availability, inadequate treatment guidance, and excessive costs26. The relatively low usage rate in our study aligns with these regional challenges and highlights the urgent need for policy interventions to improve access, such as subsidizing costs and incorporating hydroxyurea into national essential medicines lists, as recommended in recent WHO guidance27.

Our study also revealed that the length of hospital stay for most children was less than one week, which is a positive indicator of the efficiency of care provided at GRRH. A short hospital stay may suggest timely and effective management, allowing them to recover quickly and reducing the strain on hospital resources. This is consistent with a Tanzanian study, which showed that the median length of stay in hospital was 3 days7. However, the 1.6% of children who had prolonged hospitalization or required transfer to other facilities show the need for ongoing monitoring and support for more severe cases. This subset of patients faced more severe complications or co-existing conditions requiring extended care. Prolonged hospital stays indicate complex medical needs requiring specialized treatment, continuous monitoring, and more intensive support.

The observed in-hospital mortality of 3.9% is towards the lower end of published paediatric SCA inpatient mortality rates in sub-Saharan Africa, which range from 4 to 12% in most hospital-based studies7,12,15,28. This relatively low figure may reflect timely presentation and effective acute management at GRRH, although the small number of deaths and the retrospective design preclude firm conclusions about superiority of care. Potential contributing factors include delayed presentation to hospital, concurrent malaria or bacterial infections in a malaria-endemic region, and limited preventive use of hydroxyurea. These findings highlight the need for community education on early recognition of severe symptoms and improved access to preventive therapies like hydroxyurea to reduce mortality from acute complications. There is need for national programmes to subsidise hydroxyurea and improve its availability in regional hospitals, alongside strengthening diagnostic capacity for early identification and preventive management of severe complications in paediatric sickle cell anaemia.

Limitations

This retrospective cross-sectional study has several limitations. First, the reliance on medical records may have introduced bias due to incomplete or inconsistent documentation, potentially leading to underreporting of symptoms, complications, or treatments. Selection bias is also possible, as only children with confirmed SCA who were admitted and had sufficiently complete records were included. To minimize these biases, records with unconfirmed SCA diagnosis, missing admission/discharge dates were excluded.

Secondly, the single-centre design limits the generalizability of findings to other healthcare settings in Uganda or sub-Saharan Africa. Outcomes may therefore not reflect care in district hospitals or primary facilities with fewer resources. Third, the descriptive nature of the analysis precluded exploration of independent predictors of outcomes through multivariate regression. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up means readmission rates, post-discharge complications, and overall survival could not be assessed. Despite these limitations, the consecutive sampling of all eligible records over 5.5 years provides a comprehensive snapshot of paediatric SCA management at a major regional referral hospital.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that vaso-occlusive crises and fever were the predominant clinical presentations among children hospitalised with sickle cell anaemia at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital, with high rates of complete recovery and relatively short hospital stays. These patterns align with broader literature from Uganda and sub-Saharan Africa, where vaso-occlusive events and infections remain the leading causes of admission in paediatric SCA, often complicated by limited access to preventive therapies.

These findings highlight the need for improved diagnostic capacity and wider hydroxyurea availability in regional hospitals to reduce the frequency and severity of acute complications.

Continued research and capacity building in SCA management are essential, including prospective studies to evaluate long-term outcomes and implementation strategies for disease-modifying therapies in resource-limited settings.
