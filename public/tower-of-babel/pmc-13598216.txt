The ecology of urban segregation
Authors: Steward T. A. Pickett, Timon McPhearson
Published: 2026

Motivation and Components of an Ecology of Segregation

Segregation is a common pattern of social–economic exclusion and separation across cities, suburbs, and exurbs of the United States (1–3). Such patterns exist throughout the world (4–7), with important relationships to environmental quality and impacts on human wellbeing. There are many processes that can generate patterns of segregation, ranging from individual preferences (8) to intentional and institutionalized discrimination (9, 10). This paper introduces a special feature on the “ecology of segregation,” which is an emerging social–ecological integration. It brings together major strands from various social sciences, including scholarly literature on environmental justice, and from contemporary urban ecological science. The first strand is represented by deep literature studies in sociology, geography, political economy, and economics, among others, that address the patterns and processes of segregation (8, 11–13). The second strand emerged from the environmental justice movement that stimulated a scholarly tradition documenting the environmental hazards and exclusions from environmental amenities that are often associated with segregation (14–17). The third strand is from ecological science of urban processes. This is a newer tradition that has begun to clarify how patterns and processes of segregation intertwine with urban biodiversity, species interactions, and biogeochemistry (18–23) to generate human and urban ecosystems. The authors of this special feature have chosen to emphasize discriminatory processes behind segregation patterns because knowledge about such pathways may suggest remedies for the detriments associated with the resultant patterns of segregation. The time seems ripe for a closer and reciprocal integration among these traditions. Therefore, this special feature seeks to promote such cross-disciplinary integration by posing a fundamental question:

Do the social patterns, dynamics, and causes of racialized segregation, and the biogeophysical patterns and dynamics of cities mutually influence each other?

This question, expanded in Fig. 1, is different from the more familiar question that has long motivated social and environmental justice research: Does segregation relate to environmental deficits or hazards experienced by socially identified groups in different locations?

The ecology of segregation is a conceptual structure to encourage research that examines the mutual feedback that can exist between the social processes, the biophysical processes, and the urban spatial heterogeneities that each generates and responds to. This extends the traditional disciplinary focus of either social inequity-to-environment influence or the ecological focus of environment heterogeneity-to-social influence. Dynamism, history, and contingency are possible in the full feedback template.

Social scientific inquiry in the broad sense has engaged with the environment since at least the early 20th century “Chicago School” (11, 24), sometimes called a school of “urban ecology” (25). Although this introductory perspective paper cannot thoroughly review the social side of the conceptual integration for ecology of segregation, the discontents, extensions, refinement, and replacements of various tenets of that seminal school (11, 26, 27) have continued to deal with social–environment relationships. That social literature employs the term “ecology” in a variety of ways. Ecology can mean social and physical environmental context, an analytical level of aggregation (11, 12, 28), or a network of interactions (12). Therefore, it is important to clarify the ways in which “ecology” is used in introducing the special feature. The term can signal 1) a technical scientific definition; 2) a model of the interactions among actors and a specific environmental context; or 3) a metaphor for connection, complexity, or some other social value (29). Such metaphorical uses can embrace the nebulous yet evocative idea of “nature,” the economic, material, and psychological “ecosystem” of corporations (30), or supportive lived social–ecological worlds (31).

As practicing ecologists, we believe there is a need to clarify the character of contemporary ecological science to identify the ways ecology might be prepared for productive integration with social sciences, broadly conceived, to understand how this special feature views the ecology of segregation. We adopt a contemporary definition from Weathers et al. (32): Ecological science is the study of the processes influencing the distribution and abundance of organisms, the interactions among organisms and their environments, and the organismal transformation and flux of information, energy, and matter. “Organisms” in the definition can include humans, their aggregations, and institutions (33, 34). Human-produced transformations can include economic production, obtaining resources, discarding waste, and land change, for example. The contemporary definition of ecology is as applicable to wilderness as it is to, say, New York City. This definition has benefitted from the social–ecological integration evinced by the growth of urban ecological science over the last four decades (35–37). Urban ecology is less about “nature” in cities than about a mixture of interacting, dynamic social–ecological patterns and processes (38). We believe the ecology of segregation, as a synthetic pursuit, can build on this growth as well as on the massive foundation from the social sciences.

What Is Segregation?

Across the special feature, the term segregation refers to discriminatory spatial sorting based on various socially constructed status rankings. Segregation as a pattern can influence access to environmental and constructed amenities, exposure to environmental hazards, and limit the capacity for civic participation. Segregation is not only a spatial and material phenomenon, but it also manifests digitally and epistemologically. As will be expanded by Ellis-Soto and Chapman (39) in this special feature, this raises important questions concerning “epistemological segregation”: Which data count in environmental governance; Whose exposure, vulnerability, and ecological practices are measured; Whose absence from datasets remains invisible (40); How do digital mapping, smart-city programs, or environmental monitoring systems reinforce spatial and nonspatial exclusions? Segregation also has implications for access to power and environmental decision-making. Furthermore, segregation appears in spatial heterogeneities of residence, recreation, employment, and the routes and modes of travel. There are microsegregation practices that occur within shared ecological spaces. Public parks and other green spaces are often sites where certain activities are normalized and others policed; certain persons are welcomed and others surveilled; and informal gatherings, barbecues, sports, or cultural practices are differentially regulated. In the US context, the policing and even vigilante violence against African Americans in parks and public green spaces illustrates how racialized control extends into ecological spaces (41, 42). These dynamics demonstrate that segregation is not only about residential patterns but about behavioral governance, embodied access within ecological infrastructures, and the police state (43). Thus, the spatial templates of segregation filter the various interactions of individuals, groups, and institutions with natural and constructed environmental features at multiple scales (e.g., ref. 12). The remaining papers of the special feature expand upon these topics and present both US and international cases.

Segregation Is Multifaceted and Ongoing.

Segregation brings together well-known social and political processes. It reflects racism as a social–economic system involved in the creation, distribution, and impact of capital (44, 45); the differential governance of land allocation; environmental justice; social movements; policy (46); and concentrated poverty (47). This special feature links these social phenomena with the structures and processes that are the focus of modern ecological science. Although segregation has long histories, it is not merely a static historical artifact, but an ongoing system of oppression and exclusion (9, 10, 13). Contemporary segregation is intimately related to capitalisms that use the racial rankings of people or places to profit from the control of and reward (if any) for labor, the disposition of real property based on fear or duress (e.g., blockbusting), real estate speculation, urban “renewal,” or the degree of investment or disinvestment in buildings or infrastructure, among others (48). Such extractive uses of ethnic and racial rankings count as racial capitalism (44). Racial capitalism is a structuring system through which social groups and nature are hierarchized, separated, and exploited simultaneously (45, 49, 50). Such racial differentiation enables extraction and dispossession, with segregation often reinforcing racialized hierarchies. The often racialized and always socially ranked differences among groups remain crucial to what is called the mass capture and immobilization of labor (45, 51), which depends on a kind of segregation, e.g., plantations.

Racial capitalism has been critiqued as being undertheorized (52) or vague and analytically problematic and is therefore a changing discourse (53). Regardless of the outcome of the debates about formal theory of racial capitalism, we find it useful as a marker of the complex entanglement of race and capitalism. Therefore, we use it as a flexible conceptual “boundary object” (54–56) that can be useful in the interdisciplinary synthesis required by the ecology of segregation.

Extreme and enduring segregation, such as occurring in informal settlements in the Global South shaped by unequal land tenure regimes, making unequal enforcement of land use regulations, tenure insecurity, and environmental vulnerability inseparable (57–59). Land tenure determines access to nature, infrastructure, and environmental safety. In the Global North extreme spatial segregation takes place also through the prison system (43, 60), which constitutes a form of ecological segregation-removing people from civic space and concentrating them in environmentally hazardous, socially isolated sites.

More generally segregation determines: Who has access to secure and healthy housing, and where; Who benefits from urban greening and climate adaptation measures; Who has access to biodiversity, ecosystem services, and environmental amenities; protections from heat, flooding, pollution, and environmental risk, and who is served by related green and gray amenities? Thus, segregation appears to be a highly resilient feature of urban and regional systems (61–63). Yet, how such social–environmental resilience is normalized and continues to operate ecologically remains a research need. Consequently, both historical legacies and continuing dynamics of social sorting in relation to the environment require increased attention. And yet negative ecologies of segregation are not inevitable (64); they can be interrupted through antidisplacement measures, rent stabilization, community land trusts, social housing guarantees, and legal rights to remain in place amid ecological improvements.

Relevance to Policy and Sustainability.

Historically, grand policies and plans for city expansion, suburbanization, and transportation infrastructure have often facilitated or reinforced segregation (65, 66). The influence of new projects and investments in environmental equity requires understanding the origins and past reinforcement of inequity through segregation. The Justice40 Initiative in the United States provided evidence of the policy importance of good data on segregation and its environmental and social dynamics. City governments as well have recognized the role of equity and justice in their planning and operations. For example, in 2023 the City Mayors Society (67) listed 32 equity offices in US Cities. In April 2023, the White House established an Office of Environmental Justice in recognition of the significance of the processes and outcomes of discriminatory processes such as those entangled in segregation; however, US federal justice and equity programs are currently being rolled back. Of course, policy initiatives are mutable, and it remains to be seen how the current changes in US policy will shape commitments to mitigating the negative ecological effects of segregation in the long term.

The papers in the special feature demonstrate how both legacies and continuing trajectories of segregation can affect a wide range of urban decisions and actions, including planning and policy for climate adaptation and resilience, planning and maintenance of greening in cities and suburbs, planning and local investments for disaster risk reduction, the success of nature-based solutions for climate adaptation, the siting and management of parks and recreation areas, and many other tools of sustainability. As cities increasingly invest in diverse approaches to advance sustainability and resilience goals, we posit that the justness of such approaches not only requires asking whose goals they meet, but also asks whose knowledge and what power shapes policies and programs (68).

Segregation and Socially Constructed Hierarchies

There are many different socially constructed categories that may be ranked and subjected to segregation. In the United States and other settler colonial societies, race, and indigeneity are often major criteria of spatial exclusion and separation. Jones et al. (69), for example, speak of spatial racism. In many societies, skin color plays a similar role as race, but the two are not always congruent. Religion, immigrant origin, migration status, social class, economic status, linguistic group, educational attainment, and other criteria may also be used to impose or choose spatial segregation. The social rankings along these various axes of contrast are culturally, politically, legally, or habitually enforced (10, 70, 71). Such enforcement appears in the etymology of the term segregation itself, which derives from Latin “to set apart.” The core metaphor in the term is a flock (grex), from which some subset can be separated (se). It therefore implies something that is done, even if tacitly, to a subset of a flock. For example, Krysan and Crowder’s (12) Social Sorting Structural model of housing search and choice, indicates that seemingly unfettered and supposedly rational economic choices (8) are in fact constrained by racial differences across the multistage process of attempting housing mobility. Prior to a search, segregated lived experience, distinct social networks, and media biases may constrain knowledge about neighborhoods available. The active search stage adds anticipated discrimination, racial preferences, and perceptions of affordability to constrain neighborhoods searchers consider. The third stage, which reveals housing units to be considered, adds filters of time and resources available for searching. The final stage adds cost/benefit analysis, but importantly discrimination can be a roadblock even at this stage. This refined model of the process of housing segregation adds reality to the abstract economic models of preference (8), for example.

Segregation, Space, and Redlining.

Segregation is often indexed in residential patterns and the processes of their establishment. “Redlining” is any spatial classification that uses location or assumed aggregate characteristics of groups to distribute benefits or risks, regardless of the actual condition of individual properties, or credit worthiness of individual applicants. It is commonly applied in this general sense to actions of the banking and insurance industries (72). One of the most well-known historical cases is the process developed by the US Federal Home Loan Bank Board and the Federal Housing Administration during the Great Depression (73, 74). Federal agencies and local experts mapped their joint spatial judgment for mortgage-worthiness in 239 cities in the United States during the late 1930s. The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation (HOLC) formalized four categories of area-wide credit-worthiness, ranked from best (usually colored green on city maps of the era), down to red-shaded areas what were judged to be “hazardous” for mortgage lending. The Federal Housing Administration practiced an extreme form of redlining during the suburban boom after World War II, which restricted ownership in new suburbs to white buyers, and virtually shut out Black applicants from new home mortgages. A growing body of research exploits the available digitized maps of some 150 cities (75), and documents persistent, cross-generational implications of the segregation process (73, 74, 76).

Segregation can be found in many other urban phenomena besides residential lending; for example, modes and pathways of travel, sites of recreation and entertainment, places and times of worship, along with location of industries and employment. As an example of segregated recreational services, parks in wealthy neighborhoods of New York City were better maintained (i.e., trash picked up, adequate security provided) during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic when social distancing and access to parks was so critical for physical and mental health (77). The aggressiveness of policing is also spatially differentiated between high-status and excluded districts (69, 78). Although lending redlining is now illegal in the United States, cases of discriminatory awarding of subprime loans still exist (79). Ajibade et al. (80) in this special feature emphasize that the 1930s lending security maps created under the auspices of the HOLC do not address the more spatially extensive patterns of residential segregation that have emerged with suburban expansion in contemporary US urban regions. Similarly, Ellis-Soto and Chapman (39) in this special feature document contrasting scales of segregation in large metropolitan regions. The HOLC maps have historical significance and are important for documenting the lasting effects of past patterns of segregation and their legacies in today’s core cities. But they are not adequate for investigating larger regional patterns and continuing processes of segregation which is dynamic throughout urban regions. For example, in many US cities, processes of green and climate gentrification are recentering high-status populations in newly revitalized, greener, and more walkable urban cores. Meanwhile, in parts of Europe and Latin America (81) green and climate-oriented planning is also accompanied by a) dedensification and b) attraction of wealthier populations to greener, less polluted, climate-sheltered suburbs. This evolving geography underscores that segregation is not a static spatial arrangement but is dynamic through an evolving socioecological process reconfigured through green redevelopment, climate adaptation, and real estate speculation. Indeed, self-segregation among the very wealthy, who increasingly inhabit islands of green privilege—gated eco-enclaves, climate-resilient developments, securitized landscapes buffered from environmental risk and concentrated poverty (47)—occurs alongside migration of the Black middle class in US cities away from urban cores (82). Notably, mobile Black middle class households often end up in segregated places or less affluent neighborhoods than they originally targeted, experiencing racial steering and unfounded assumptions about what they can afford (12).

Why Segregation Exists

Appropriation of Land and Extraction of Labor.

There are many reasons that social hierarchies might be established. Familiar rationales in the West are the racial capitalism (50) of access to free or low-cost labor, such as via chattel slavery or exploitation of migrant workers (especially those who may be undocumented), the appropriation of indigenous lands (as in US “manifest destiny” or westward expansion), or the profitable exploitation of marginalized communities (such as real estate speculation in neighborhoods lacking access to secure deeds and legal advice). This is not an exhaustive list. In the United States, racially ranked hierarchies reflect the ideology of white supremacy, which arrays those identified as Black, Chinese, Mexican, Southeast Asian, Native Americans, or persons classed as mixed race to various degrees of disadvantage relative to whites (83, 84). This has echoes in historical plantation slavery and past and present Indigenous displacement in many countries in the Caribbean, South America, and elsewhere (85, 86).

Colonialism and Its Ideology.

Colonies, whether the familiar overseas outposts, or less widely recognized exploitative colonies within a country (16, 87) or within a city are a spatial manifestation of the ideology of white supremacy. This ideology has been used to justify the control of labor, land, and resources to the benefit of social categories originally associated with northern and western European colonial powers. The idea of whiteness is, like its dialectical pairings, socially created, and what peoples are considered to be white has differed with time and context (71, 88). The mechanisms of extractive or racial capitalism (89, 90) are expressions of white supremacy. For example, the hypersegregated neighborhoods of some US cities are sites of extractive racial capitalism (44), seen in disinvestment by absentee landlords, predatory subprime lending (79, 91), or neglected upkeep of municipal infrastructure (5, 92).

Two Registers of Racism.

Racism has two registers or kinds of expression. One is racism as a social-political-cultural system, and the other is racism as individual prejudice and bigotry. The section just above has described some of the systemic origins of racism as a social structure to facilitate European colonialism and imperialism. These were reinforced by philosophers, such as John Locke (93) whose thinking inspired important ideas in the US Declaration of Independence. Yet, his assertion of natural rights did not extend to persons of color, congruent with his investment in the British Royal Africa Company and the role it played in the slave trade to the Americas. The intent here is to give some sense of the connection of ideas, motivations, profits, and institutions that interacted to reinforce the supposedly biological ranking of distinct varieties of humans. These connections constitute a racialized system that underlies segregation even today (94).

Tools of Systemic Racism.

A powerful example of racism as a system appears after the US Civil War, which ended in 1865. Even with the legal rights of freedom and citizenship bestowed upon formerly enslaved Black persons by immediate post-Civil War amendments to the constitution, mechanisms of repression soon were reasserted. These included vigilante oppression, exclusion from public benefits, growing electoral disenfranchisement, and enactment of anti-Black laws by individual states. These structures generated a system of interlocking mechanisms that continued to keep Black persons deeply disadvantaged (95, 96). In the waning decades of the 19th century, this multifaceted system was popularly called Jim Crow. Segregation was a major aspect of the demise of the racial justice of Post-Civil War Reconstruction and the rise of Jim Crow (97, 98). The system of Jim Crow began to be unraveled by the judicial decisions and legal tools that emerged from the Civil Rights era of the mid-20th century, including the fair housing act and the voting rights act (98).

Voluntary vs. Enforced Segregation.

Some segregation is voluntary. Those afforded high ranks in social hierarchies likely have the resources and freedom to associate in enclaves of their choosing. Of course, elites are not compelled to live in any particular place, unlike persons ranked lower on social hierarchies. Unlike elites, those lower in a social hierarchy may be constrained in where they can live due to lack of access to decision-making processes, constrained economic resources, or legal strictures, and thus be compelled to occupy less-desirable or even hazardous locations (8, 99, 100) outside the enclaves of the wealthy elite. The lower-status areas may be severely policed to maintain their separateness from higher-status districts. The relocation of members of the Black middle class from core US cities has contributed to the increasing proportion of remaining Black residents in neighborhoods that are deeply disadvantaged by the intersection of class and race (101), while at the same time often meeting barriers of housing discrimination in higher status places they can actually afford (12). Where such high- and low-status enclaves tend to be located differs across nations. In the United States, high-status enclaves are often located in suburbs (102–104), while traditionally in European and Latin American cities, such desired enclaves tend to be more central. However, the European and Latin American (105) patterns are changing, driven by migration of the wealthy from the central cities, or even across a nation or from other countries (106). The proliferation of informal settlements in various parts of the world (107–109), or “social housing” for impoverished or struggling classes are examples of segregation. For example, Cook et al. (110) and Gulsrud et al. (111) in this special feature highlight the role of property rights in segregation. In some cases, disadvantaged groups that are forcibly segregated by exclusion may nurture community and positive institutions in segregated places as tools for sharing cultural resources or promoting their own well-being (31). Social and economic agency, creativity, and flexibility characterize some such segregated places (57, 112).

Segregation has been well studied from social, economic, and political perspectives. Details of the ecological differences exploited to achieve segregation, and the ecological hazards that may emerge from the social processes attending segregation appear in many of the papers in this special feature. Need to Examine Ecological Feedback.

An ecology of segregation is more than a one-way arrow of impact that runs from a social indicator to an environmental hazard or disamenity (Fig. 1) (64). As we discuss below, the papers in the special feature emphasize that systems of segregation have feedback through time between environmental patterns and processes, and social patterns and processes. Understanding and dynamically linking both realms is required for a comprehensive approach to the ecology of segregation. However, segregation from the perspective of the ecological sciences is relatively understudied. Indeed, the label “ecology of segregation” and its focus represent a recent exploratory synthesis that combines insights from scholarship on environmental justice, political ecology, and social movements, with the social–ecological science of the city (9, 62, 63). As an emerging synthesis that employs an ecological science lens, the ecology of segregation suggests research needs spelled out in the individual papers of the special feature.

Global Significance of Segregation.

Because segregation in its various dimensions is a common feature of urbanization worldwide (5), urban ecology must deal explicitly with the patterns and processes of segregation across a broad array of contexts. The upshot of this special feature is that diverse forms of segregation must be evaluated as factors in urban social–ecological–technological systems. Furthermore, even seemingly pure studies of bioecology in cities and other urban places must be aware of the role that segregation might play in the situations of interest. For example, questions such as these might be appropriate: “Is there contamination in place that reflects spatial legacies of segregation (e.g., ref. 125)? Are there rare but potentially impactful disturbances that have been or are likely to be differentially impactful in segregated locations (126)? Are there important legacies in segregated places that result from past management now discontinued (15, 125)? Are segregated enclaves of the disadvantaged as well investigated ecologically as enclaves of the elite (40)? In other words, contemporary urban ecology goes beyond the biological origins of ecology (35, 127) with a narrow focus on “nature.”

Transdisciplinarity.

Transdisciplinarity characterizes the broadening scope of urban ecology (128, 129). Dynamically uniting the social aspects of segregation with the broadening scope of urban ecology motivates this special feature. The urban scope keeps in focus the core concern of ecology with relationships that involve organisms, their metabolic fluxes, and the transformations and structures that organisms create or modify in their surroundings. But it also focuses on the ways and means by which those relationships reach beyond the traditional boundaries of ecology. Transdisciplinary science involving communities often employs open-ended systems approaches. Such approaches link ecology with other disciplines, because systems can embrace flows of influence, materials (including resources and wastes), energy (both as resource and waste heat), information (including culture and technologies), finances, and values (33, 130, 131). An emerging systems approach that will be illustrated in the special feature is the view that ecology of segregation itself is a system. Segregation as a social phenomenon is already recognized as systemic (61). However, this special feature will show how segregation can be an integrated social–ecological system, that is, an ecology of segregation (Fig. 1).

Systems approaches are not just abstractions. Systems approaches can facilitate comprehensive understanding of a phenomenon and promote corrective action (132). The emergence of sanitary cities as systems of infrastructure, ecology, and professionalized governance in the 19th century is an example (133, 134). The growing systems approach to segregation promotes the adoption of segregation as an ecological factor, but we believe it can help ensure that the ecological causes and consequences of segregation become better embedded in and employed across the transdisciplinary scope of urban ecology. In other words, the systems approach to ecology of segregation deals with both its spatial patterns and spatially differentiated processes. Systems of ecology of segregation are dynamic (8)

Plurality of Ecologies of Segregation.

The dimensions of segregation—its patterns, processes, and motivations—can operate differently and with different effects depending on context. Therefore, contrasts in culture, history, and governance (among others), may generate different ecologies, that is, specific social–ecological systems that characterize different places. Note the use of ecology in the plural here. Such pluralization and recognition of different situations or ecologies of different countries, peoples, social ranks, regions, cities, or neighborhoods is common within social sciences (135). For example, Nishime and Williams (31) assemble a collection of different “racial ecologies” ranging from the skills that enslaved Africans brought to management of introduced indigo and rice in the Carolinas, to the functioning of indigenous landscapes in the United States and New Zealand, to place-based social movements aimed at preventing or repairing industrial extraction and pollution, to the power of social gardening, to Afro-futurist visions (136). Thus, one question on the emerging agenda of ecology of segregation is to understand the ecological structures and processes that operate and interact with cultural and political contexts of these diverse spaces or systems. This question would require advancing the ecological component of research on ecology of segregation to meet the same high level of the social, political, and technological understanding of segregation and racial ecologies that social research has achieved.

Linkage with Ecology’s Large Ideas.

The ecology of segregation can address several “big ideas” in contemporary ecology as a systems science. These questions are related to fundamental ideas in ecological theory (32, 137). They address spatial heterogeneity, temporal dynamism, disturbance, succession, feedback, and adaptation (138). We have left these questions in abstract form here, and rely on the details exposed across the papers in the special feature to suggest how to ground them. Operationalizing such questions may take advantage of the pluralization of both ecology and segregation as nuanced research fields to address contextual complexity in the relationships between the two. The existing literature specializing on segregation deals with the same big ideas of spatial pattern formation and persistence, disruption, and adjustment to changing social and institutional conditions. These parallels suggest a comfortable fit for segregation as part of a social–ecological system science.

Given the often tacit nature of systemic racism and other drivers of segregation (140, 141), it may be the case that many people have some known or unknown stake in segregation, as perpetrators, beneficiaries, or victims. The stake may differ depending on the dimension of segregation in play, and the intersection of different social hierarchies a person experiences. The ecology of segregation seeks to make what may be a personally disturbing system of exclusions visible and thus available to social–ecological investigation in urban places. Thus, although it is difficult to examine racism, other criteria of social ranking, and the interaction between ecologies and kinds of segregation, we believe that such examination opens a neglected kind of ecology in urban systems as a research topic, and hence a source of novel empirical understanding, and a subject of potential intervention.

Emerging Ecology of Segregation Research: Overview of the Special Feature

Spanning diverse geographic contexts and methodological approaches, the papers in this special feature collectively demonstrate that socio-spatial segregation is deeply embedded in long-standing political, economic, and ecological processes that shape the structure and functioning of contemporary cities and larger urban regions. Together, these papers show that segregation is not simply a demographic or spatial condition, but a dynamic social–ecological system. Across diverse cases including in the United States, Europe, and Latin America, the special feature illustrates that ecological patterns—urban tree canopy, flood exposure, biodiversity, infrastructure resilience—cannot be disentangled from the social and political systems that produce and reproduce them.

Collectively, the special feature advances a comprehensive understanding of how segregated urban environments shape human and other-than-human life, ecological processes, and sustainability decision-making. The papers call for a reorientation of ecological and planning research toward transdisciplinary (142), justice-centered approaches that confront both the origins and contemporary expressions of social–ecological system inequality. They underscore the need for more inclusive planning, integrated infrastructure governance, equity-oriented climate adaptation, and collaborative knowledge co-production and co-creation with marginalized communities. By synthesizing evidence from multiple regions and disciplines, the special feature demonstrates that addressing the ecology of segregation requires confronting its structural drivers while advancing new models of equitable urban futures. Thus, it calls for community-centered ecological research, investments in equitable biodiversity monitoring, inclusive climate adaptation planning, and interdisciplinary approaches that consider both biophysical and socio-political dimensions of segregation. Addressing the ecology of segregation therefore requires confronting its structural drivers while building new models of urban governance and scientific practice capable of supporting equitable, biodiverse, and resilient cities. This special feature is a contribution to those needs, and contains seven additional papers.

Grove and Pickett (64) develop a conceptual framework integrating environmental justice with social–ecological systems theory to explain how racial inequities persist. They emphasize interactions and feedback among social power, and ecological processes that lead to temporal and spatial dynamics. Empirical examples from Baltimore illustrate how zoning decisions, tree planting, and disamenity siting shape environmental inequalities over time. The paper argues that segregation functions as a resilient socio-ecological system with lags and legacies that maintain inequity. This contribution provides theoretical grounding for understanding segregation as systemic and dynamic rather than static. It serves as a conceptual bridge that informs the empirical findings across the special feature.

Anguelovski et al. (50) examine how racial capitalism, settler colonialism, and exclusionary land-use practices have produced enduring social–ecological inequalities in cities. They identify five interconnected pathways—including undergreening, nature dispossession, and green gentrification—through which marginalized groups are systematically denied ecological benefits while being disproportionately exposed to hazards. The authors argue that contemporary green interventions often reproduce these inequities by enabling “green racial capitalism,” in which nature improvements accelerate displacement and consolidate privilege. Their analysis shows how degraded ecologies in marginalized areas are both products and drivers of segregation, creating reinforcing feedback between social exclusion and ecological harm. In the context of the special feature, the paper provides a conceptual anchor linking segregation’s historical roots to its ecological manifestations. This contribution connects directly with Grove and Pickett’s (64) systems framework and parallels Cook et al.’s (110) documentation of colonial and ecological dispossession.

Ajibade et al. (80) develop novel typologies of contemporary segregation—persistent exclusivity, persistent disadvantage, gentrification, and displacement—exemplified by environmental and health inequities in Portland, OR. Using extensive census, environmental, and health data, they show that historical redlining only partially predicts current inequities, which increasingly arise from ongoing city and regional restructuring. Persistently disadvantaged neighborhoods face severe ecological burdens, while exclusive areas retain environmental privilege. The paper shows that segregation is dynamic and shaped by contemporary market forces. Within the special feature, this work underscores the need to examine ongoing restructuring processes. Therefore, it corrects a major problem with much literature that takes segregation as equivalent to the 1930s redlining maps. It aligns with temporal arguments made by Grove and Pickett (64) and with green gentrification themes in Anguelovski et al. (50).

Cook et al. (110) apply the ecology of segregation framework to Valdivia, Chile, showing how colonial history, recent natural disasters, and neoliberal development jointly produced social–ecological inequities. They trace how indigenous Mapuche displacement, wetland reconfiguration by the 1960 earthquake, and rapid urbanization shaped unequal access to ecological amenities. The analysis shows how long-term (“press”) and episodic (“pulse”) disturbances interact to structure inequity. The paper highlights the importance of ecological systems in shaping segregation in Global South contexts. As a contribution to the special feature, this work demonstrates how historical and governance conditions beyond the Global North contribute to socio-ecological segregation. Its thematic links to Anguelovski et al. (50) underscore the global relevance of colonial legacies and capitalist practice.

Herreros-Cantis et al. (143) introduce an expanded framework for assessing pluvial flood risk by integrating direct and indirect exposures with vulnerability and critical infrastructure. Using flood hazard data in New York City, they show that extreme rainfall can disproportionately directly affect socially vulnerable communities and with high potential to disrupt essential services that indirectly also affect the more vulnerable. Their analysis quantifies mismatches between infrastructure, hazard exposure, and community capacity to cope. The findings highlight the potential for cascading infrastructural failures in neighborhoods shaped by historical disinvestment. The paper demonstrates how segregation amplifies climate-related risks. It complements Cook et al. (110) and Gulsrud et al. (111) on adaptation inequities and reinforces broader themes of climate vulnerability and environmental injustice of disproportionate exposure to hazards.

Gulsrud et al. (111) analyze how housing tenure structures the burdens of climate adaptation in Copenhagen. They show that stormwater management costs and risks fall disproportionately on residents in social housing despite the city’s reputation for equity. The authors argue that siloed techno-managerial approaches prioritize hydraulic performance over social equity, reproducing residential segregation. Their findings reveal how planning institutions unintentionally reinforce environmental injustice within a welfare-state context as opposed to the more usually examined free-market systems. As part of the special feature, the paper broadens geographic scope, showing that inequities persist even in high-capacity governance systems. It aligns with Herreros-Cantis et al. (143) in highlighting infrastructural inequity, resonates with Anguelovski et al. (50) on climate adaptation disparities, and with Cook et al. (110) in focusing on social housing.

Ellis-Soto and Chapman (39) quantify how historical redlining has produced enduring inequities—epistemological segregation—in biodiversity data availability across seven major taxonomic groups, using nearly 60 million observations from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) across 195 US cities. They find consistently lower sampling density in formerly redlined neighborhoods for all taxa, indicating that segregation shapes not only ecological conditions for people and nature but also the scientific visibility of urban biodiversity. Despite these disparities, species richness differences across the 1930s HOLC maps of grades representing mortgage worthiness were limited to birds and plants, suggesting that data inequality does not uniformly translate into biodiversity inequality. The paper underscores that biodiversity monitoring—now heavily dependent on volunteer science—is shaped by socioeconomic differentials, potentially biasing ecological inference and conservation decisions. This analysis reveals that broader units often have higher completeness and expected richness, driven by more diverse habitats. Within the special feature, the study expands the ecology of segregation to include epistemic inequities, showing that data blind spots themselves are socio-ecologically patterned. This contribution complements Anguelovski et al. (50) and Grove and Pickett (64) by demonstrating how structural racism shapes both ecological patterns and the knowledge systems used to study them.

Toward a Research Agenda for the Ecology of Segregation

Urban ecology and science of the urban environment have not yet widely incorporated segregation—in the broadest sense—as an important factor in their toolkits. Much of the scholarship on segregation emphasizes its social and historical roots, but neglects its reciprocal environmental–social interactions. There is much nuance in the field. For example segregation is both pattern and process, voluntary and enforced, multidimensional, and highly contextual within cities, regions, and globally. It has also elicited critique, for example in the use of racial capitalism (53). However, an important aspect of understanding segregation is to acknowledge the social origins and economic reinforcement of racial categories (50), rather than their presumed biological origin.
