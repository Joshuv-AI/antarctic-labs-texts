Gender-based analysis of visual and refractive characteristics in exotropia
Authors: Masoud Khorrami-Nejad, Mohammad Reza Akbari, Babak Masoomian, Arash Mirmohammadsadeghi, Haider Resan Hussein, Foroozan Narooie-Noori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo analyze gender-based differences in visual acuity, refractive error, angle of deviation, and amblyopia in patients with alternate exotropia (Alt XT) and intermittent exotropia (IXT).MethodA retrospective study of 4,417 surgical exotropia patients (2,970 Alt XT; 1,447 IXT) compared sex-based differences in CDVA, refraction, deviation angles, and amblyopia within each subtype. Alongside unadjusted tests, subtype-specific multivariable models were used: linear regression for right-eye logMAR CDVA (adjusting for age, near/distance deviation, spherical equivalent, and astigmatism), linear regression for distance deviation angle (adjusting for age, spherical refractive error, astigmatism, and amblyopia status), and binary logistic regression for amblyopia (yes/no) (adjusting for age, distance deviation, spherical refractive error, and astigmatism). An ordinal model for amblyopia severity was explored but interpreted cautiously due to sparse data.ResultsIn Alt XT, females had higher mean cylindrical error (0.86 ± 1.23 D vs. 0.76 ± 1.07 D; p = 0.024), while males had larger horizontal deviations at near (37.2 ± 16.6Δ vs. 35.0 ± 16.6Δ; p < 0.001) and distance (35.9 ± 16.0Δ vs. 34.3 ± 15.8Δ; p = 0.008) and a higher prevalence of severe amblyopia (12.2% vs. 7.8%; p < 0.001). In IXT, females were younger (16.7 ± 11.6 vs. 18.8 ± 11.9 years; p = 0.001), whereas males had larger deviations at near (28.1 ± 11.7Δ vs. 26.2 ± 10.0Δ; p = 0.001) and distance (28.4 ± 11.4Δ vs. 27.2 ± 9.8Δ; p = 0.025) and higher overall amblyopia prevalence (13.0% vs. 8.7%; p = 0.010), mainly mild (10.4% vs. 7.0%; p = 0.031). CDVA and spherical equivalent did not differ by sex in either subtype. In adjusted models, sex was not associated with right-eye logMAR CDVA in Alt XT (B = 0.005; p = 0.814) or IXT (B = 0.005; p = 0.506). Sex was associated with a small difference in distance deviation magnitude in Alt XT (B = 1.33 prism diopters; p = 0.018) but not in IXT (p = 0.114). In adjusted logistic regression, sex was not associated with amblyopia prevalence in Alt XT (OR = 1.12; p = 0.159) but was associated in IXT (OR = 1.51; p = 0.025).ConclusionGender-specific differences were observed in astigmatism, deviation magnitude, age at surgery, and amblyopia patterns across exotropia subtypes. After adjustment, sex remained associated with distance deviation in Alt XT and amblyopia prevalence in IXT, but not with CDVA in either subtype. Overall, sex appears to be a relevant clinical variable in exotropia and should be considered in assessment and future research.Keywords: Exotropia, Amblyopia, Strabismus, Refractive error, Visual acuity

Introduction

Strabismus represents a significant category of neuro-ophthalmological disorders that affect a notable portion of the global population, with reported prevalence rates varying from 2% to 5% in pediatric cohorts [1]. Among the diverse manifestations of strabismus, exotropia stands as a common form, particularly prevalent in certain geographical regions and ethnic groups [2]. Intermittent exotropia (IXT) is arguably the most common subtype, where the ocular deviation is not manifest at all times, with individuals often able to maintain binocular alignment for varying periods, particularly at near fixation or when attentive [3]. In contrast, constant exotropia, also referred to as alternate exotropia if fixation alternates between the eyes, involves a manifest outward deviation under all or most viewing conditions [4]. The distinction between these forms is crucial, as their management strategies, visual prognoses, and associated characteristics can differ substantially [5].

The clinical assessment of exotropia involves a comprehensive evaluation of several key parameters. The angle of deviation, quantified in prism diopters (PD), is a fundamental metric, typically measured at both distance and near fixation, and in various gaze positions to assess for any incomitance [6]. The magnitude of this deviation can influence treatment decisions, ranging from conservative measures like refractive error correction and occlusion therapy to surgical intervention [2]. Visual acuity in each eye is another critical aspect, as exotropia, especially if constant or unilateral, is a significant risk factor for the development of strabismic amblyopia [7].

Refractive errors are intimately linked with various forms of strabismus, and their role in exotropia is an area of ongoing study. While hyperopia is strongly associated with accommodative esotropia, the refractive profile in exotropia is more varied [8]. Some studies have suggested an association between myopia and intermittent exotropia, particularly in Asian populations, while others have found a broader spectrum of refractive states [9, 10]. Astigmatism is also a common refractive error, and significant or asymmetric astigmatism can independently contribute to meridional amblyopia [11]. Anisometropia, a difference in refractive power between the two eyes, is another potent amblyogenic factor and can coexist with exotropia, further complicating the visual status [12]. Therefore, meticulous cycloplegic refraction is indispensable in the evaluation of any patient with exotropia to identify and correct significant refractive errors that may influence the deviation or contribute to amblyopia.

In contemporary medical research, the consideration of sex and gender as biological variables has gained increasing prominence, as differences between males and females have been identified in the epidemiology, pathophysiology, and clinical presentation of numerous diseases, including various ophthalmological conditions [13, 14]. For example, gender-related variations have been noted in conditions such as dry eye syndrome, age-related macular degeneration, and glaucoma [15]. Within the domain of strabismus, epidemiological studies have sometimes reported gender disparities in the overall prevalence of exotropia, with some suggesting a slight female preponderance for certain types like intermittent exotropia, although these findings are not universally consistent across all populations and study designs [16, 17].

Despite these broad epidemiological observations, there remains a relative scarcity of comprehensive research specifically dedicated to elucidating detailed gender-based differences in the multifaceted clinical characteristics of exotropia, particularly when stratified by its major subtypes (e.g., alternate/constant versus intermittent). While individual studies might incidentally report on gender distributions or compare a limited set of parameters, a focused, large-scale analysis comparing a wide array of visual and refractive metrics—such as corrected distance visual acuity, individual components of refractive error (spherical power, cylindrical power, and spherical equivalent), the precise magnitude of horizontal and vertical deviations, and the prevalence and severity patterns of amblyopia—between male and female patients within distinct exotropia categories is less common in the literature.

Therefore, this study was designed to conduct a thorough gender-based comparative analysis of the key visual and refractive characteristics, as well as amblyopia patterns, in a large cohort of patients diagnosed with either alternate exotropia or intermittent exotropia. By systematically evaluating and comparing these ophthalmological parameters between male and female participants within each exotropia subtype, this research endeavors to identify and quantify any significant disparities. The overarching goal is to contribute to a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the clinical spectrum of exotropia, highlighting any gender-specific trends that could inform future research directions and potentially enhance clinical decision-making in the management of this common form of strabismus.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This investigation employed a retrospective review methodology, analyzing existing patient medical files. Records of suitable individuals were procured from the database system of Farabi Eye Hospital. This institution, a major tertiary ophthalmological referral center with academic ties to Tehran University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran, serves as a national focal point for managing complex strabismus disorders. The review included patient records from individuals who attended the strabismus service between January 2012 and January 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval No.: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1405.017). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Participant selection

This study intentionally included only patients scheduled for exotropia surgery at a tertiary referral center. Therefore, the study population may not represent patients managed conservatively, those with milder deviation, or individuals without access to surgical care.

The study included individuals scheduled for exotropia surgery, who were further sub-classified as having either alternate exotropia or intermittent exotropia according to clinical findings documented in their medical records. Inclusion necessitated a confirmed diagnosis of comitant exotropia of either subtype. A prerequisite for inclusion was the presence of comprehensive ophthalmological documentation, which had to contain specific details regarding ocular alignment, best-corrected distance visual acuity, and the findings from subjective refraction.

Several exclusion criteria were applied to refine the participant pool. Individuals with a history of prior intraocular surgeries (e.g., procedures for cataracts or retinal conditions) or any previous surgical interventions aimed at correcting strabismus were not included. The existence of significant ocular diseases that could potentially obscure the accurate evaluation of visual acuity or refractive state also served as a basis for exclusion; this included conditions like visually impactful corneal opacities, advanced-stage glaucoma, significant optic nerve disease, or retinal pathologies that independently limited vision. Patients diagnosed with restrictive forms of strabismus, such as Duane retraction syndrome, Brown syndrome, or thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy, were omitted. Similarly, those with paralytic strabismus, encompassing conditions like palsies of the third, fourth, or sixth cranial nerves, were not eligible for the study. If a patient presented with a dissociated vertical deviation (DVD) that materially interfered with the precise measurement of horizontal misalignment, or if DVD was the primary reason for their clinical consultation, they were excluded. Furthermore, individuals whose medical files lacked completeness regarding the key variables essential for the study, or those with known systemic neurological or muscular disorders that directly impact ocular motility (e.g., myasthenia gravis or chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia), were also excluded from the final study group.

Clinical assessments

Data were systematically retrieved from the medical charts of all qualifying patients by trained personnel, utilizing a pre-designed, standardized data abstraction form. The information compiled for each participant included several crucial ophthalmological parameters. Demographic information, specifically the age at the time of clinical assessment and the patient’s recorded sex, was documented.

Ocular alignment characteristics were meticulously recorded. The magnitude of exodeviation, quantified in prism diopters (PD), was determined using the alternate prism and cover test while the patient fixated on an accommodative visual target. These measurements were obtained for both distance (6 m) and near (1/3 meter) viewing conditions in the primary gaze position. Both the horizontal and any coexisting vertical components of the deviation were noted.

For the intermittent exotropia group, patients included were those who had undergone multiple follow-up visits and were referred for surgery due to a documented decline in exotropia control. Surgical criteria were based on serial deterioration in control, as assessed by both home and office control scales. Indicators of poor control included exotropia manifesting at least 50% of the time, an increase in the angle of deviation over successive follow-ups, or a progressive inability to regain alignment during manifest episodes. In the alternate exotropia group, surgery was indicated when deviation persisted despite adequate nonsurgical management and remained constant over two consecutive follow-up visits [4]. In these cases, all appropriate preoperative measures, including correction of significant refractive errors, amblyopia therapy, active vision therapy, and prism prescription when indicated, were completed prior to surgery.

Definition of alternate and constant exotropia

In this study, alternate exotropia was defined as a manifest exodeviation in which fixation alternated between the two eyes during alternate cover testing, without a persistent fixation preference. Constant exotropia was defined as a continuous manifest deviation present regardless of fixation. Alternation was confirmed through repeated alternate cover testing at both distance and near, by observing stable shifts of fixation between eyes without prolonged dominance. Fixation preference was assessed based on the consistency of refixation movements during cover–uncover cycles.

For the intermittent exotropia group, only patients without a clear ocular dominance or consistent fixation preference between the two eyes were included. Patients demonstrating marked or persistent dominance were excluded from this subgroup.

These diagnostic criteria were applied by experienced strabismus specialists and were used consistently throughout the study period (2012–2024). deviation not fulfilling constant exotropia criteria were categorized accordingly in our dataset [4, 18].

Visual acuity evaluation was a central element of the data collection process. Corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) was recorded for each eye independently. Visual acuity values were subsequently converted to the logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution (logMAR) scale to facilitate statistical analysis. CDVA was measured using age-appropriate charts; however, the specific chart type was not consistently documented across records. Amblyopia was defined as a best-corrected interocular difference of ≥ 0.2 logMAR (approximately ≥ 2 Snellen lines) and/or CDVA of ≤ 0.3 logMAR (20/40) in at least one eye, in the absence of identifiable organic ocular pathology. This operational definition was applied consistently across age groups based on recorded CDVA. If specified in the records, the type of amblyopia—whether strabismic, anisometropic, or a combination—was also registered, along with its documented severity (mild, moderate, or severe based on standard visual acuity thresholds). For analyses of CDVA and refractive error, the right eye was used.

Objective refraction was initially performed, and subjective refinement was conducted when possible. The refractive error documented for each eye included spherical power, cylindrical power, and the astigmatism axis. From the primary refractive components, the spherical equivalent (SE) was calculated as the sum of the spherical power and half of the cylindrical power. For classificatory purposes, myopia was defined as an SE of ≤ -0.50 diopters (D), hyperopia as an SE of ≥ + 0.50 D, and emmetropia as an SE falling within the range of -0.49 D to + 0.49 D. Astigmatism was considered present if the measured cylindrical power was ≥ 0.75 D. Anisometropia was defined as a difference in the SE between the two eyes of ≥ 1.00 D. Amblyopia severity was classified as mild (CDVA < 0.3 logMAR), moderate (0.3–0.7 logMAR), and severe (> 0.7 logMAR), according to commonly accepted clinical criteria.

Finally, details from other pertinent ocular examinations were reviewed. Information obtained from slit-lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundus evaluations was assessed to confirm the absence of other ocular conditions that could potentially explain any observed reduction in vision or influence the characteristics of the ocular alignment. The ocular motility assessment, which included evaluation of ductions and versions, was also reviewed to verify the comitant nature of the exotropia and to exclude the presence of significant incomitance, ocular restrictions, or paretic elements. Stereoacuity, suppression testing results, and standardized clinic control scores for intermittent exotropia were not consistently recorded across the full study period and therefore were not included in the primary analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics, stratified by sex and exotropia subtype (IXT and Alt XT). Sex-based comparisons within each exotropia subtype were initially conducted using independent-samples t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. To address the primary objective of a gender-based analysis while accounting for potential confounding, multivariable regression models were also fitted within each subtype: [1] linear regression models with distance horizontal deviation angle as the dependent variable and sex as the main predictor, adjusting for age, refractive error components (spherical refractive error and astigmatism), and amblyopia status; and [2] binary logistic regression models with amblyopia status (yes/no) as the dependent variable and sex as the main predictor, adjusting for age, distance horizontal deviation angle, spherical refractive error, and astigmatism. An ordinal logistic regression model was additionally explored for amblyopia severity (none/mild/moderate/severe), with the same covariates, and interpreted cautiously in the presence of sparse data. Unless otherwise specified, two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and effect estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

A total of 4,417 patients diagnosed with exotropia were included in this study. Of these, 2,970 patients (67.2%) had alternate exotropia, while 1,447 patients (32.8%) were diagnosed with intermittent exotropia. In the alternate exotropia group, 1,360 patients (45.8%) were female and 1,610 patients (54.2%) were male. In the intermittent exotropia group, 818 patients (56.5%) were female and 629 patients (43.5%) were male.

Alternate exotropia group

A gender-based comparison of visual acuity, refractive error, and angle of deviation in patients with alternate exotropia is summarized in Table 1. Among the significant findings, the mean cylinder refractive error was higher in females compared to males (0.86 ± 1.23 vs. 0.76 ± 1.07 D, p = 0.024). Regarding the angle of horizontal deviation in the primary position, males exhibited larger deviations both at near and far distances. At near, males had a mean horizontal deviation of 37.24 ± 16.57Δ compared to 35.02 ± 16.64Δ in females, with a mean difference of -2.22Δ (p < 0.001). Similarly, at far, males had a mean horizontal deviation of 35.88 ± 15.99Δ, which was higher than the 34.34 ± 15.77Δ observed in females, with a mean difference of -1.54Δ (p = 0.008). All other parameters, including age, corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), spherical equivalent (SE), and vertical deviation at both near and far, showed no significant differences between genders (P > 0.05).

In this gender-based analysis of the alternate exotropia subgroup, sex was not independently associated with logMAR CDVA after adjustment for age, refractive status (spherical equivalent and astigmatism), and horizontal deviation at near and distance (B = 0.005, 95% CI − 0.035 to 0.044; p = 0.814). Clinically, worse visual acuity (higher logMAR) was associated with older age and higher astigmatism, while deviation measures showed opposite directions for near versus distance. When deviation magnitude at distance was analyzed directly as the primary outcome (distance horizontal deviation angle, prism diopters), a multivariable linear regression adjusting for age, spherical refractive error (diopters), astigmatism (diopters), and amblyopia status demonstrated a small but statistically significant sex difference (adjusted mean difference = 1.33 prism diopters; 95% CI 0.23 to 2.44; p = 0.018).

Amblyopia status was also associated with distance deviation magnitude (adjusted difference = − 2.51 prism diopters; 95% CI − 3.67 to − 1.34; p < 0.001). In adjusted binary logistic regression, sex was not associated with amblyopia prevalence (odds ratio = 1.12, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.30; p = 0.159), whereas older age increased amblyopia odds and higher astigmatism increased amblyopia odds; greater distance deviation was associated with slightly lower amblyopia odds. Ordinal regression of amblyopia severity suggested a statistically significant sex term, but because of sparse cells and suboptimal model fit, severity findings were considered exploratory.

In patients with alternate exotropia, a significant difference was observed in the proportion of severe amblyopia between genders. In patients with intermittent exotropia, males had a higher proportion of mild amblyopia (10.4%) compared to females (7.0%) (P = 0.031). No significant differences were found in moderate amblyopia (P = 0.193), and severe amblyopia was only found in females. The total prevalence of amblyopia across all severities was higher in males (13.0%) than in females (8.7%) (p = 0.010).

In this gender-based analysis of the intermittent exotropia subgroup, sex was examined in adjusted models of visual acuity, distance deviation magnitude, and amblyopia. In the multivariable linear regression for logMAR CDVA (adjusting for age, near/distance horizontal deviation, spherical equivalent, and astigmatism), sex was not associated with logMAR CDVA (B = 0.005; 95% CI − 0.009 to 0.019; p = 0.506); more hyperopic spherical equivalent was associated with better acuity (B = − 0.020; p < 0.001) and higher astigmatism with worse acuity (B = 0.043; p < 0.001). In the adjusted linear regression for distance horizontal deviation angle (prism diopters) (covariates: sex, age, spherical refractive error, astigmatism, and amblyopia status), sex was not significant (adjusted mean difference = 0.845 prism diopters; 95% CI − 0.203 to 1.893; p = 0.114), while age was positively associated (B = 0.220; p < 0.001) and spherical refractive error was negatively associated (B = − 0.668; p < 0.001).

In adjusted binary logistic regression, sex was associated with amblyopia prevalence (OR = 1.512; 95% CI 1.054 to 2.168; p = 0.025), whereas the ordinal amblyopia severity model was treated as exploratory due to sparse cells (75% zero-frequency cells).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive gender-based analysis of visual and refractive characteristics in a large cohort of patients with exotropia, distinguishing between alternate and intermittent subtypes. Several key gender-related differences emerged. In patients with alternate exotropia, females exhibited significantly higher mean cylindrical refractive error, while males demonstrated significantly larger angles of horizontal deviation at both near and far fixation. No significant gender differences were observed in this group for age, CDVA, SE, or vertical deviation. Conversely, in the intermittent exotropia group, females were significantly younger at presentation, and males again showed significantly larger horizontal deviations at both near and far. The CDVA, SE, cylindrical error, and vertical deviation did not differ significantly between genders in this subtype. Regarding amblyopia, in alternate exotropia, severe amblyopia was significantly more common in males, though the total prevalence of amblyopia did not differ. In intermittent exotropia, males had a higher proportion of mild amblyopia and a higher total prevalence of amblyopia. It should be noted that although several parameters reached statistical significance, the absolute differences were small (e.g., approximately 1–2Δ in deviation and about 0.1 D in cylindrical error). Such small effect sizes may have limited clinical relevance and are unlikely to substantially influence routine clinical decision-making. These findings may partly reflect the large sample size and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

In multivariable regression analyses adjusting for potential confounders, sex was not independently associated with CDVA in either exotropia subtype. However, in the alternate exotropia group, sex remained independently associated with a small but statistically significant difference in distance deviation magnitude (adjusted mean difference: 1.33 prism diopters; p = 0.018). In the intermittent exotropia group, sex was independently associated with amblyopia prevalence (OR = 1.51; p = 0.025). These findings suggest that certain gender-based differences in exotropia persist after adjusting for clinical covariates, although the modest effect sizes warrant cautious clinical interpretation.

Interpretation in a surgery-based cohort: Because all participants were selected at the point of surgical scheduling, observed sex differences may reflect not only differences in clinical presentation (e.g., deviation magnitude, refractive profile, amblyopia status) but also differences in the pathway to surgery. These pathway factors may include referral patterns, frequency of follow-up, access to care, parental perceptions of cosmetic and functional impact, and clinician thresholds for recommending surgery (particularly for intermittent exotropia, where control and symptoms influence management). Consequently, the present results should be interpreted as sex-associated differences among surgical candidates, rather than evidence of intrinsic biological differences in exotropia pathophysiology.

The finding of higher cylindrical refractive error (astigmatism) in females with alternate exotropia is noteworthy. While some epidemiological studies on general populations have occasionally reported a higher prevalence or degree of astigmatism in females, this is not a universally consistent finding across all age groups or ethnic backgrounds [19]. The specific context of alternate exotropia, a more constant form of strabismus, might interact with astigmatic refractive errors to influence visual development or presentation. Higher or asymmetric astigmatism is a known risk factor for meridional amblyopia, and its increased presence in females within this group warrants clinical attention for meticulous refractive correction [20, 21].

Consistently across both alternate and intermittent exotropia subtypes, male patients in the present study exhibited significantly larger angles of horizontal deviation. This is a robust finding given the large sample size. Literature specifically addressing gender differences in the magnitude of exodeviation is not abundant, and previous studies have not always focused on or reported such distinctions [22]. Some earlier, smaller-scale studies or those with different population demographics may not have detected such differences, or methodological variations could contribute to discrepancies. A larger angle of deviation has direct implications for management, often influencing the decision for surgical intervention and the complexity of the surgical planning required to achieve satisfactory motor alignment and functional outcomes [23]. The reasons for males presenting with larger exodeviations could be multifactorial, potentially involving underlying anatomical differences in orbital structures or extraocular muscle mechanics, variations in vergence system development or function, or even differences in the natural progression of the condition between genders, though these remain speculative without further targeted research.

In the intermittent exotropia group, the finding that females were significantly younger at presentation is interesting. This could suggest that females with intermittent exotropia may become symptomatic or are brought to clinical attention earlier than males, or perhaps there are differences in the age of onset or progression of the condition. Alternatively, referral patterns or parental concern levels might differ by the child’s gender, although this is speculative. Epidemiological studies on the age of onset of intermittent exotropia have not consistently highlighted such gender differences [3, 24].

The patterns of amblyopia also revealed significant gender-based distinctions. In alternate exotropia, the higher proportion of severe amblyopia in males, despite no difference in overall amblyopia prevalence, is a critical observation. Alternate exotropia, being a more constant deviation, carries a substantial risk for amblyopia [25]. The predisposition for males to develop more severe forms in this group could imply differences in suppression patterns, treatment compliance if initiated earlier, or other coexisting risk factors not yet elucidated. Conversely, in intermittent exotropia, where the overall risk of amblyopia is generally considered lower than in constant deviations [4], males exhibited a higher total prevalence of amblyopia, driven by a higher proportion of mild amblyopia. This suggests that even in the intermittent form, males might be slightly more susceptible to developing some degree of amblyopia. The mechanisms underlying these gender-specific amblyopia patterns in different exotropia subtypes require further investigation.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that gender may be one of several factors to consider in the comprehensive evaluation of exotropia. However, given the small magnitude of the observed differences, it would be premature to recommend gender-specific adjustments to surgical timing, dosage calculations, or screening protocols based solely on these results. Instead, these observations underscore the need for individualized patient assessment, where gender-associated trends might inform, but not dictate, clinical judgment. The higher astigmatism in females with alternate exotropia, for instance, reinforces the universal importance of careful refraction in all patients. Similarly, the amblyopia patterns observed warrant further investigation but do not yet support gender-specific screening strategies. Scientifically, these results open avenues for research into the biological (e.g., genetic, hormonal) or developmental factors that might underpin these observed gender disparities in the clinical expression of exotropia.

This study benefits from a large sample size, allowing for robust statistical comparisons within two major exotropia subtypes. The detailed clinical data collected provides a comprehensive overview. However, limitations inherent in a retrospective, single-center study must be acknowledged. These include the potential for selection bias and reliance on data not initially collected for research purposes, which might lead to variability in documentation. Selection bias is likely because the cohort comprised surgical candidates from a tertiary referral center; consequently, deviation magnitude, amblyopia prevalence and severity, and refractive profiles may differ from population-based or clinic-based nonsurgical cohorts. The findings from a tertiary referral center may not be entirely generalizable to the broader population. In addition, sex-related differences in referral patterns, parental concern, and surgical thresholds could contribute to the observed associations. Lack of sensory and functional measures, including stereoacuity, suppression, and validated intermittent exotropia control scores, limits our ability to determine whether sex differences in amblyopia severity reflect differences in sensory adaptation, control deterioration patterns, or treatment history rather than sex itself. We could not directly measure referral source, socioeconomic factors, caregiver decision-making, duration of deviation, or clinician-specific surgical thresholds; thus, residual confounding related to selection into surgery may explain part of the observed sex differences. Furthermore, information on other potentially influential factors, such as specific etiologies (e.g., infantile vs. acquired), duration of strabismus before presentation, previous non-surgical treatments, or detailed sensory status (e.g., control scores for IXT), was not part of the provided results for this analysis, and these could interact with gender. Longitudinal data on treatment outcomes related to these baseline gender differences would also be valuable.

Prospective studies incorporating standardized control scales, stereoacuity, and suppression metrics are needed to test whether sex differences persist when sensory status and disease duration are accounted for.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this large single-center, subtype-stratified gender-based analysis demonstrates that male and female patients with exotropia differ in several clinically relevant baseline characteristics, including refractive astigmatism, deviation magnitude, age at surgery (in IXT), and amblyopia patterns. Importantly, the addition of multivariable models showed that not all unadjusted sex differences represented independent associations. After adjustment for key clinical covariates, sex was not independently associated with right-eye best-corrected distance visual acuity (logMAR CDVA) in either alternate exotropia or intermittent exotropia. However, sex remained independently associated with a small difference in distance deviation magnitude in alternate exotropia, and with amblyopia prevalence in intermittent exotropia. These findings support considering sex as a relevant clinical variable in the phenotypic characterization of exotropia, while also underscoring the need to interpret crude sex comparisons cautiously and within the context of confounding. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized measurement of additional potential confounders (e.g., anisometropia, strabismus duration, and control scores for intermittent exotropia) and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to confirm these associations and determine whether sex-related phenotypic differences translate into differential treatment responses or long-term visual outcomes.
