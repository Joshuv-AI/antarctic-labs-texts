Minimal residual disease combined with radiological tumor volume as a tool for identification of resected NSCLC patients at high risk of recurrence
Authors: Michela Verzè, Roberta Minari, Paola Bordi, Giovanni Bocchialini, Gianmarco Martines, Monica Pluchino, Simona D'Agnelli, Letizia Gnetti, Agnese Cosenza, Valeria Balestra, Luca Ampollini, Ludovica Leo, Gianluca Milanese, Camilla Roberti, Clarissa Gallani, Jim Hayes, Umberto Malapelle, Marcello Tiseo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCirculating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a valuable tool for assessing minimal residual disease (MRD) and predicting recurrence in resected non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. Combining ctDNA-detection with radiological tumor volume may improve risk stratification.MethodsPatients with stage I-III resectable NSCLC were prospectively enrolled in the RESIDUAL study. Plasma samples were collected before surgery (T0), at landmark (10 days after surgery), during surveillance (T2, 20 days, T3, 1 months after surgery and every 3 months for the first year and then at the end of the second year after surgery), and at relapse. Samples were analyzed using Guardant Reveal, a tissue-free methylation-based ctDNA assay. Receiver operating characteristic analysis associated T1 ctDNA status with tumor volume; volume thresholds were calculated via Youden's J, and Cox regression analysis was performed.ResultsForty-eight patients were enrolled (median age 72 years; 64.6% male). Most had stage I disease (54.2%) and adenocarcinoma histology (79.2%). Median follow-up was 41.8 months, and 19 patients (39.6%) relapsed. Overall ctDNA detection rate was 15.2% across all timepoints. Pre-surgical ctDNA detection was higher in squamous histology and stage II-III disease and was associated with worse disease-free survival (DFS; p = 0.022). Landmark MRD detection was also associated with worse DFS (p = 0.024). Serial surveillance sampling anticipated radiologic recurrence by a median of 2.6 months (range 2.0-7.5). Patients with tumor volume >26,378 mm3 had a significantly higher relapse risk (p < 0.001).ConclusionsMRD detection in NSCLC resected patients predicts relapse and poor outcome; integrating ctDNA with tumor volume enhances identification of high-risk patients.

Introduction

Lung cancer still represents a clinical challenge, being the highest cause of cancer-related death worldwide and having one of the poorest 5 years survivals rate among all cancer types [1]. Eighty-five percent of lung malignances present with Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC) histology subtype and, regrettably, is often diagnosed in the advanced stage requiring systemic treatment as the therapeutical option [2,3]. Only 25% of patients are diagnosed with an early-stage of the disease (I-III stage) and could undergo to surgery with curative intent. However, up to 50-60% of patients with early disease die due to cancer recurrence. Remarkably, 20% of stage I patients had a recurrence event within 5 years [4,5]. For a long time, the only strategy to reduce the risk of relapse was the adjuvant treatment based on four cycles of platinum-doublet chemotherapy, in stage II-IIIA NSCLC patients [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The goal of the post-operative treatments is to avoid minimal residual disease (MRD) that may persist after the curative treatment, therefore improving survival. Even for stage IB tumors which often display aggressive features (e.g. poorly differentiated histology, vascular invasion and pleura involvement) the use of adjuvant chemotherapy could be justified [5,10]. Conversely, adjuvant chemotherapy improves five-years survival rate by only 5%, suggesting that we treat many to save few [11]. Moreover, considering patients age and comorbidities, just approximately 30% of resected patients can tolerate the chemotherapy adjuvant treatment [12]. For these reasons, there is a strong need to find new therapeutic strategies in order to increase the survival rate in resected NSCLC patients. The results of two independent registered studies (ADAURA and ALINA studies) showed survival benefit of adjuvant treatment with Osimertinib or Alectinib in surgically resected patients who were positive for EGFR or ALK gene alterations, respectively [13,14]. Furthermore, immunotherapy has been introduced into the therapeutic landscape in various approaches (neo-adjuvant, peri-operative, and adjuvant), demonstrating a significant improvement in survival outcomes [8].

In this scenario, translational research focuses on identify prognostic and/or predictive biomarkers which may help clinicians to select those patients most likely to derive survival benefit from adjuvant therapies, thus reducing the risk of overtreatment and avoiding toxicities in this NSCLC setting.

Liquid biopsy has proven its strength in NSCLC advanced stage and holds great potential in the early stage of the disease setting, as well [15,16]. In NSCLC resected patients, the goal is to identify MRD through circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) detection in a blood sample from patients who have undergone surgery and are negative at diagnostics imaging. ctDNA is the most promising diagnostic tool in solid tumors, showing high sensitivity and specificity. In resected patients, ctDNA primarily originates from micro-metastases or isolated disseminated tumor cells located in regional lymph nodes or distant organs, which remain undetectable through radiographic imaging [[17], [18], [19]]. The study of MRD can be conducted via two approaches: tumor-informed or tumor-agnostic. The former is a personalized approach that monitor a tumor-specific molecular signature over time, obtained through tissue sequencing. On the other hand, the tumor-agnostic approach does not require prior tumor sequencing information, either by way of tracking a panel of defined genomic markers or through epigenomic marks [20]. Since pre-operative plasma ctDNA levels are limited and further decrease after surgery, detecting potential MRD is challenging, requiring advanced sensitive technologies [21,22]. In this context, the Guardant Reveal panel (Guardant Health) is globally recognized as a method to investigate MRD. It is a tumor-agnostic assay that leverages epigenomic markers associated with abnormal DNA methylation, enabling the detection of ctDNA without requiring concurrent tumor tissue analysis [23].

Therefore, we designed the RESIDUAL study with the aim of evaluating ctDNA as expression of MRD in resected NSCLC patients with pathological stage I–IIIA using the Guardant Reveal technology (Guardant Health, Palo Alto, CA, U.S.A.). The study assesses the impact of MRD on clinical outcomes and explores the integration of ctDNA detection with radiological tumor volume to enhance risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Patients and plasma samples collection

This study was conducted at the Department of Medical Oncology and Thoracic Surgery of the University Hospital of Parma, Italy. Patients were prospectively enrolled in the monocentric RESIDUAL study from December 2019 to July 2022. The original study protocol excluded patients with stage IA disease, those who had undergone sub-lobar resection, and those receiving adjuvant therapy. Due to high screening failure rate, a protocol amendment was performed and patients meeting these criteria were subsequently included in the study. Therefore, the final eligibility criteria included diagnosis of pathological I-III stage NSCLC and both pulmonary lobectomy and sub-lobar resection with hilar-mediastinal lymphadenectomy. Patients who undergo neo-adjuvant treatment were excluded. Patients’ informed consent was obtained before enrollment in the study. All studies approved by Ethical Committee were conducted in accordance with the precepts of the Helsinki declaration.

Plasma samples were collected before surgery (T0), at landmark (T1, 10-days after surgery) and at surveillance timepoints (T2, 20 days, T3, 1 months after surgery, every 3 months for the first year, and at 2 years after surgery). A plasma sample was further collected if relapse occurred (TRel), for up to a total of 10 timepoints per patient, when feasible.

Twenty mL of peripheral blood were harvested during a withdraw in EDTA tubes (BD vacutainer®) and plasma was separated within 2 h from collection and stored at −80°C until cell-free DNA (cfDNA) extraction.

Cell-free DNA extraction and MRD analysis

Plasma samples were transferred to Guardant Health facility to have MRD analyzed using the Guardant Reveal assay, a tumor-agnostic, epigenomic assay intended for detection and quantification of ctDNA. Briefly, a non-destructive method was used to physically partition cfDNA based on methylation status, subsequently tagged with molecular identifiers, amplified, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The assay covers over 20,000 differentially methylated regions (DMRs), including those in both lung and other cancers along with control regions. A proprietary bioinformatics software pipeline was trained to detect the presence or absence of ctDNA based on these DMRs and the resultant output is either “ctDNA detected” or “ctDNA not detected” based on a predefined statistical likelihood threshold that the patterns of methylation are tumor-derived.

Radiological tumor volume assessment

The tumor volume was retrospectively quantified in the imaging study closest to the surgery: the available imaging data were selected from the University Hospital of Parma's Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), anonymized, and imported to a dedicated workstation (Syngo.Via, Siemens Healthineers) for the following quantitative analyses. Volumetric imaging data were available for 44 of the 48 enrolled patients. Four patients were excluded from the volumetric analysis because pre-operative imaging was unavailable. Among the 44 evaluable patients, Computed tomography (CT) scans were available for 40/44 cases (91%), while in 4/44 cases (9%) the analyses were performed on CT scan of [18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose positron emission tomography (PET) studies. Although CT- and PET/CT-derived volumetric measurements are not strictly equivalent, PET-CT derived volumetric assessment was used in these cases because pre-operative CT images were unavailable. Therefore, the same type of anatomical volumetric measurement was used across all cases. A radiology resident (with 3 years of experience) performed the semi-automated volumetric measurements using a dedicated tool (MM_oncology). In brief, the reader identified the lesions in keeping with the radiological reports. After placing a seed point on the target lesion, the boundaries of the lesion were elaborated and highlighted for visual review. In cases of need, inconsistent segmentations were manually edited. When the reader deemed segmentations accurate, they were reviewed and approved by an experienced thoracic radiologist, finalizing the quantification of the volume of the segmented lesions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Statistical Software Version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and Jamovi Statistical Software Version 2.3 (Sydney, Australia). The nonparametric Fisher's exact test was used to examine the differences in categorical variables. Median Disease-Free Survival (DFS) and cancer specific Overall Survival (cOS) were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method and Cox's proportional hazards model. 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated with the Wald test. Differences in survival between groups were determined by Log rank test. All p-values were two sided and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. When not reached during the study period, median survival values are reported as not a number (NaN). Receiver operator characteristics (ROC) analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.1) programming language (pROC library) by associating the presence of ctDNA at T1 with the tumor volume calculated from CT scans. Cut-offs for optimal stratification of tumor volume were calculated based on Youden's J and the closest distance to the top left corner. A multivariable Cox's proportional hazards model was also performed to assess whether the prognostic value of the ctDNA-derived radiological volume classification was independent of pathological stage.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Forty-eight patients affected by early-stage (I-III) NSCLC and eligible for surgical resection were prospectively enrolled in the RESIDUAL study. Median age at diagnosis was 72 years (range 47 to 84) with the majority of the cohort being male (64.6%). Most patients were diagnosed with pathological stage I (54.2%), followed by stage II (29.2%) and stage III (16.6%), according to the 8th Edition of the AJCC/TNM classification. Considering the NSCLC histology subtype, 38 (79.2%) patients presented with adenocarcinoma (ADC), 9 (18.8%) patients with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), while 1 (2.0%) had mixed histology ADC and SCC. Five (10.4%) patients had a systemic adjuvant treatment, while 3 additional patients (6.3%) had a local radiotherapy (RT) treatment. A detailed description of these patients is provided in Supplementary Table 1. All of the patients had all 48 T0 and T1 collections, with the number of collections at timepoints T2-T8 decreasing over time, with 14 samples were collected at the time of radiographic relapse. Plasmatic clearance after surgery

All 48 of the patients had plasma collected before and after surgery, allowing for the investigation of ctDNA clearance, here defined as going from a ctDNA detected to ctDNA not detected. Ten of the 13 (76.9%) of patients had detectable ctDNA before surgery showed plasma clearance at first timepoint (T1) (Fig. 4). Since 4 patients were lost to FU, only 6 patients collected plasma samples during surveillance timepoints. We observed that all the patients who turned their ctDNA status into positive during the MRD monitoring experienced relapse; conversely just 1 patient out of 3 that had a persistent negative ctDNA status during MRD monitoring experienced relapse. Three patients out of 13 were not able to clear plasma at T1. Only 2 patients collected the surveillance timepoints and, among them, 1 experienced relapse. According to ctDNA status, median DFS was 6.1 months (3.3 vs NaN months; HR 6.70; 95%CI 1.35-33.29, p = 0.02) in patients with a persistent positive ctDNA status, 29.6 months (11.0 vs NaN months; HR 2.49; 95%CI 0.9-6.89, p = 0.08) in patients with cleared plasma and 52.1 months (40.3 vs NaN months; HR not assessable) in patients with never detected ctDNA. Given the limited number of patients in some subgroups, these analyses should be considered exploratory. No statistically significant association was found between ctDNA detection and cOS (Fig. 3B).

Landmark analysis

Landmark timepoint was set at the first (T1) plasma sampling, ten days after surgery, and the ctDNA positive rate was 6.3% (3/48; Supplementary Table 1). A positive MRD status at landmark timepoint was associated with a significant worst DFS (ctDNA detected yes vs. no: 6.1 vs. 52.1 months; HR 4.91; 95%CI 1.06-22.75, p = 0.024), while a trend of shorter cOS was found (ctDNA detected yes vs. no: 41.1 vs NaN months; HR 1.83; 95%CI 0.23-14.52, p = 0.56. Fig. 3C). Given that only three patients were ctDNA-positive at the landmark timepoint, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory despite its statistical significance.

Surveillance analysis

The biological FU consisted of eight plasma samplings (T1, ten days, T2, 20 days, T3, 1 months after surgery and every 3 months for the first year and then at the end of the second year after surgery).

First, it was investigated whether having a positive ctDNA status at least once during the surveillance period affected the clinical outcome of the patients. It was observed that patients who have at least once a positive ctDNA status during surveillance timepoints reached significant shorter DFS (ctDNA detected yes vs. no: 11.7 vs. 52.1 months; HR 4.39; 95%CI 1.59-12.13, p = 0.0019) than patients who remain negative during all the FU-period. Cancer specific OS was also significantly affected (ctDNA detected yes vs. no: 41.2 vs NaN months; HR 4.63; 95%CI 1.27-16.91, p = 0.011. Fig. 3D).

Moreover, plasma monitoring during the surveillance period allowed for a comparison between radiological relapse and plasma relapse timing, intended as the first positive ctDNA samples during surveillance. In our cohort 19 (39.6%) patients experienced disease recurrence (in Supplementary Table 3, the sites of disease recurrence for each patient are listed). Overall, the patient-level sensitivity was 36.8% (7/19) with a high level of specificity of 97% (131/135). Patient-sensitivity increased to 50% (6/12) when restricting to patients who had multiple post-surgical samples with one sample taken within 1 year of relapse. These findings indicate that a proportion of patients who eventually relapsed remained ctDNA-negative during follow-up. In this subset of 6 patients, MRD was detected prior to radiological relapse with a median lead time of 2.6 months (2.0-7.5), while in the overall analysis, not restricted to samples collected within one year of recurrence, the median lead time was 5.3 months.

Combined analysis of ctDNA and radiological tumor volume improves prediction of clinical outcomes

Data showed so far demonstrated that the presence or absence of MRD in plasma samples from surgically resected patients correlates with the risk of recurrence. Nevertheless, a subset of patients remains for whom ctDNA analysis does not accurately predict clinical outcome. To enhance the prognostic power of this tool, we considered integrating liquid biopsy data (ctDNA status at T1) with radiological measurements of tumor volume. We used Youden's J index and the minimal distance to the top left corner to integrate these parameters and determine the optimal tumor volume cut-off in order to stratify patients into two groups: low risk for those with tumor volume below the cut-off, and high risk for those with tumor volume above the cut-off (Supplementary Table 4). Patients in the high-risk group had a significantly higher risk of relapse (volume above the threshold yes vs. no: 5.95 vs. NaN; HR 8.48; 95%CI 3.06-23.49, p < 0.0001) and significantly worst cOS (volume above the threshold yes vs. no: 36.5 vs. NaN; HR 7.73; 95%CI 2.05-29.12, p = 0.00038. Fig. 3E), compared to patients in the low-risk group. In the multivariable Cox's proportional hazards model adjusting for pathological stage, the high-risk group remained significantly associated with shorter DFS (HR 7.84, 95% CI 2.00–30.68, p = 0.003) and cOS (HR 6.80, 95% CI 1.19–38.75, p = 0.031).

Discussion

In this study we explore the use of a tumor-agnostic assay for the detection of MRD in early-stage NSCLC patients treated with surgical curative intent. The analysis of ctDNA content was executed through the tissue-agnostic, epigenomic-based Guardant Reveal MRD assay, firstly validated in colorectal cancer [24]. The use of tumor-agnostic approaches can broaden access to MRD testing, particularly for patients lacking adequate tumor tissue for tissue-informed assays or those needing rapid results after definitive treatment. This represents a well-recognized limitation in real-world experience, in which a proportion of tumor samples fails quality control steps. For instance, Wang et al. [25] reported an exclusion rate of 8.1%, while more recent studies indicate a success rate of approximately 80% for personalized WGS panels, primarily constrained by tissue availability and sample quality [26]. However, tissue exclusion rates vary widely across studies, highlighting a broad range of performance and the potential value of a tissue-free-based approach. In this context, the Guardant Reveal panel represents a significant advancement, as it integrates epigenetic markers with aberrant methylation profiles to enhance the sensitivity and accuracy of MRD detection. Furthermore, this method avoids the biological confounding background of the clonal hematopoiesis-derived variants, thereby removing the necessity for parallel sequencing of leukocyte genetic material and enhancing the overall reliability of the assay [27].

In our experience, positive detection rate in pre-surgical plasma samples is 27.1%. These data are consistent with the findings reported by Rosenlund et al. [28], in a study with a similar design and patient cohort. Furthermore, in a previously published systematic review by our group, we collected literature data on ctDNA detection prior to surgery. Our analysis revealed that the performance was variable, with an average ctDNA detection rate of approximately 50% [29]. Through a correlation analysis of positivity rate and disease stage, we were able to confirm the hypothesis that ctDNA detection occurred more frequently in patients presented with stage II-III of the disease and squamous histology, while no significant correlations were found with other clinical-pathological variables. These findings are consistent with those reported by other groups, who demonstrated that the ctDNA positivity rate increases approximately five-fold in stage II-III disease compared to earlier stages IA-IB and squamous histology [[30], [31], [32]]. In addition, pre-operative detection of ctDNA has been reported in the literature to be associated with poorer clinical outcomes [33,34]. In our cohort, pre-operative ctDNA positivity was significantly associated with short DFS, strengthening the evidence that early molecular detection reflects a more aggressive disease biology. Even more informative was the post-surgical analysis of ctDNA, specifically the assessment of MRD. We first evaluated ctDNA clearance in patients who had detectable ctDNA prior to surgery. Although patients who cleared ctDNA after surgery showed longer DFS than those with persistently positive ctDNA, this comparison was based on a very limited number of patients and requires validation in larger cohorts. In the broader comparison including patients who never had detectable ctDNA, this group had the most favorable outcome (52.1 months). Taken together, these findings support the potential role of ctDNA analysis as a biomarker for MRD after surgery. In our study, it was not possible to conduct any analyses concerning adjuvant therapy, as the limited number of patients who received such treatment precluded a statistically robust evaluation. Nonetheless, several studies in the literature have already investigated the use of ctDNA to stratify patients according to the potential risk of recurrence. For instance, patients with a persistently positive ctDNA status may be considered at highest risk of relapse and therefore be candidates for adjuvant therapy after surgery. In contrast, patients who cleared or those with never detected ctDNA may be considered at lower risk and could undergo to a de-escalated adjuvant treatment or surveillance FU [[35], [36], [37], [38]].

The design of the RESIDUAL study included three timepoints within one month after surgery (T1, T2, and T3), with the first collected 10 days after surgery, designated as the landmark timepoint. By analyzing data, we observed that ctDNA assessment at this early timepoint was already suitable of robustly predicting patients’ clinical outcomes. Furthermore, this timing typically coincides with patient discharge from the hospital, making it a practical and feasible moment for sample collection. Previous studies have demonstrated that MRD detection at the landmark timepoint is highly associated with a poorer clinical outcome [33,39,40]. In our cohort we confirmed this evidence, as proved by a median time to relapse of 6.1 months, compared to 52.1 months in patients who were MRD-negative at the landmark timepoint (p = 0.024). Given the predictive value of landmark ctDNA for relapse risk, it should be considered for inclusion in routine clinical practice, potentially by integrating it into the TNM staging system. This approach was demonstrated by Chen et al. [31], who showed that incorporating MRD data into staging (TNMB) significantly improved prognostic prediction in resected NSCLC patients (p < 0.001).

The landmark timepoint does not universally predict recurrence risk across all patients, due to the heterogeneous clinical-pathological and biological characteristics of tumors. It is well established that the first two years following surgery represent the period of highest risk for disease relapse; therefore, patients require more intensive FU during this interval. Accordingly, the plasma-based surveillance protocol in our study includes MRD monitoring starting from 20 days after surgery, followed by assessment every three months during the first year and every six months during the second year. Indeed, we observed that detecting ctDNA at least once during the surveillance period is associated with a shorter DFS compared to patients with persistently negative ctDNA. These findings suggest that persistently negative ctDNA is associated with a more favorable prognosis. However, ctDNA negativity does not exclude the presence of residual disease or the possibility of future relapse. Therefore, longitudinal surveillance remains important and may provide additional prognostic information beyond a single assessment at the landmark/post-surgical timepoint.

Among the 48 patients included in the study, 19 experienced disease recurrence occurred within the surveillance period. The overall median lead time between MRD detection and imaging confirmed recurrence was 5.3 months, while the time-bounded analysis gave a median lead time of 2.6 months. In support of our findings, other studies have demonstrated that MRD detection can precede radiological gold standard, with a median lead time of approximately 6 months [25,41,42]. This suggests that MRD monitoring may offer an earlier indication of relapse compared to conventional imaging, highlighting its potential as a valuable tool for early intervention.

However, MRD assessment in NSCLC remains biologically challenging. Indeed, in a subset of patients, liquid biopsy is non-informative, compromising the early detection of relapse. This may be explained by biological and technical factors, including ctDNA shedding, limited tumor burden, specific metastatic site, and the intrinsic sensitivity of the assay. Accordingly, ctDNA negativity should not be interpreted as the absence of residual disease, as a proportion of patients who eventually relapsed remained ctDNA-negative during surveillance. One of the main goals of research in this disease setting is to enhance the utility of ctDNA, including its integration with other biological and tumor-related parameters. Based on evidence from the existing literature, we evaluated whether integrating ctDNA data with radiological tumor volume could generate a tool capable of improving patient stratification according to risk of relapse. By integrating data from ctDNA status at landmark timepoint and the radiological data we extrapolated an optimal tumor volume threshold and classified patients as low or high-risk. Patients in the high-risk group had a significantly higher risk of relapse and shorter cOS, compared to patients in the low-risk group. This is in line with the previous published work from Tran et al. [43] which firstly demonstrated the usefulness of this combined tool. Moreover, our ctDNA-derived radiological volume stratification remained significantly associated with DFS and cOS after adjustment for pathological stage.

This study has some limitations. First, the size of the cohort, and particularly the limited number of patients in some subgroup analyses resulted in wide confidence intervals and imprecise hazard ratio estimates. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger prospective cohorts. Moreover, the size of the cohort did not allow for analysis of the potential role of MRD in guiding adjuvant therapy management. Second, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, biological follow-up was not feasible in some cases, and several patients were unable to provide all planned samples. Lastly, our findings need validation in prospective multicenter studies.

In conclusion, our study supports existing evidence that MRD detection in resected NSCLC is associated with poorer outcomes and can anticipate radiological relapse by several months. Furthermore, the integration of ctDNA with radiological tumor volume enables the development of a combined tool that may improve the identification of patients at high risk of recurrence.

Conclusions

Tumor-agnostic MRD assessment using the Guardant Reveal assay represents a feasible approach for the detection of residual disease in resected NSCLC patients. Post-surgical ctDNA positivity was associated with an increased risk of recurrence and shorter survival outcomes, while longitudinal monitoring enabled the identification of molecular relapse months before radiological progression. Moreover, the integration of ctDNA status with radiological tumor volume improved risk stratification and identified a subgroup of patients who may be at higher risk of recurrence. Although these findings require validation in larger prospective cohorts, our results support the potential clinical utility of combining molecular and radiological biomarkers to refine postoperative risk assessment and guide personalized surveillance strategies in early-stage NSCLC.
