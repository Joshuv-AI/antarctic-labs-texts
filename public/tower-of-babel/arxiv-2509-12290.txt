Secure Human Oversight of AI: Exploring the Attack Surface of Human Oversight
Jonas C. Ditz, Veronika Lazar, Elmar Lichtmeß, Carola Plesch, Matthias HeckFederal Office for Information SecurityBonnGermany veronika.lazar@bsi.bund.de

Abstract
Human oversight of AI is promoted as a safeguard against risks such as inaccurate outputs, system malfunctions, or violations of fundamental rights, and is mandated in regulation like the European AI Act. Yet debates on human oversight have largely focused on its effectiveness, while overlooking a critical dimension: the security of human oversight. We argue that human oversight creates a new attack surface within the safety, security, and accountability architecture of AI operations. Drawing on cybersecurity perspectives, we analyze attack vectors that threaten the requirements of effective human oversight, thereby undermining the safety of AI operations. Such attacks may target the AI system, its communication with oversight personnel, or the personnel themselves. We then outline hardening strategies to mitigate these risks. Our contributions are: (1) introducing a security perspective on human oversight, and (2) providing an overview of attack vectors and hardening strategies to enable secure human oversight of AI.

Likewise, being threatened by another person can also undermine self-control, as the actions performed by the oversight personnel are then steered by another agent. Fitting intentions means that personnel are motivated to carry out their responsibilities with the right intentions. In an extreme case, a malicious actor might be hired as oversight personnel and deliberately undermine AI safety. In a less extreme case, poor working conditions may erode motivation and attention, weakening oversight effectiveness

Attack Vectors and Hardening Strategies in Cybersecurity

The Human Oversight Attack Surface

Human oversight can serve as part of the safety, security, and accountability architecture of AI systems, as oversight personnel are expected to detect and mitigate risks. In addition, oversight personnel may be required to actively confirm that an AI system is in a safe state. Human oversight may become even more important in AI operations if it evolves into a central requirement of AI governance and regulation in high-risk contexts
[
2
,
6
,
3
,
1
]
.

With a more wide-spread implementation, human oversight will also become an increasingly attractive target for malicious actors seeking to corrupt or disrupt AI-based operations. Such actors could be cybercriminals targeting high-risk AI systems for ransom or foreign governments attacking a country’s critical infrastructure in hybrid or open conflict. Furthermore, sufficiently powerful AI agents that are successfully attacked could themselves become malicious actors by attempting to evade oversight

3.1
Poisoning Attacks

Poisoning attacks target the core functionality of AI models, namely the association of patterns in data with outcomes (e.g., real-valued numbers in regression, class probabilities in classification, or new text/image data in generative AI)
[
71
]
. These attacks exploit the fact that modern AI models are often trained on large volumes of (semi-)automatically collected data, for example through web crawlers. Malicious actors can insert triggers, or predefined patterns, into such datasets. When models are trained on compromised data, they may consistently exhibit malicious behaviors whenever a trigger is presented
[
72
]
.

Poisoning attacks can compromise both the epistemic access and the self-control of oversight personnel. They undermine epistemic access because poisoned samples containing hidden triggers cause AI responses that deviate from normal outputs. A carefully crafted trigger, such as an imperceptible pixel pattern in images
[
73
]
, can prevent oversight personnel from recognizing the behavioral shift. Even when oversight personnel detect that something is amiss, hidden triggers may still make it difficult to understand the malicious behavior.

Poisoning attacks can also erode self-control. If triggered outputs cause the oversight interface to generate excessive micro-notifications or confirmation requests, malicious actors can exploit this to induce fatigue by overwhelming oversight personnel with trivial but time- and energy-consuming tasks. This not only reduces oversight efficiency by consuming resources but also increases the likelihood of errors once fatigue reaches a critical level, thereby undermining the security of human–AI interaction.

3.2
Adversarial Attacks

Adversarial attacks aim to induce misclassifications in the outputs of an AI model
[
71
]
. By feeding the system with adversarial examples, attackers seek either to fool the model or to reduce its accuracy and performance. These adversarial examples typically resemble normal inputs but include subtle perturbations that may not be perceptible to oversight personnel, leading to misclassifications aligned with the attacker’s goals
[
72
]
.

Adversarial attacks primarily compromise the epistemic access of oversight personnel. A crucial part of understanding AI behavior is knowing how input patterns are mapped to outputs (e.g., which structures or features in an input image correspond to specific output classes). Without this knowledge, reliably determining whether the system behaves acceptably becomes exceedingly difficult. Adversarial perturbations obfuscate the relationship between inputs and outputs, reducing epistemic access to the model’s true behavior. As a result, oversight personnel may fail to detect unwanted model behavior or even system failures.

3.3
Explainability Attacks

Attacks on explainability specifically target explainability and interpretability approaches and tools. These attacks manipulate or deceive the evidence for a model’s reasoning. A key characteristic of such attacks is their ability to alter the explanation while keeping the model’s output unchanged. This means that the AI system may still produce the same output, but the explanation for that output is inaccurate or misleading
[
74
]
.

Explainability attacks primarily undermine the epistemic access of oversight personnel. Misleading or inaccurate explanations prevent personnel from understanding the AI system’s true behavior. Attackers can exploit these attacks to fabricate behavior that serves their goals. Even with extensive querying, oversight personnel may be unable to detect such manipulations
[
75
]
. Moreover, a mismatch between outputs and explanations adversely affects personnel who rely on explanations to identify problematic AI behavior
[
25
,
76
]
.

These attacks can also induce a state of deceptive epistemic access, where the system appears transparent but provides inaccurate explanations. This scenario can be more dangerous than a “black box” system because it fosters unjustified trust and makes detecting incorrect outputs more difficult. Consequently, a key focus of explainability for human oversight must be ensuring the fidelity and robustness of explanations
[
77
,
78
]
.

3.4
(Distributed) Denial of Service Attacks

Denial of service (DoS) attacks refer to methods that render a system inaccessible
[
79
]
. The core principle of DoS attacks is to overwhelm a target system with more requests than it can handle, causing the system to become nonfunctional. Various techniques exist to perform a DoS attack, such as exploiting the TCP/IP protocol (SYN flooding), using the ping functionality (ping flooding), or targeting mail servers (mail flooding). A variation of DoS attacks is the distributed denial of service (DDoS) attack, which distributes requests across multiple systems rather than originating from a single source
[
80
]
.

(Distributed) DoS attacks can undermine the causal power of oversight personnel. Modern computing systems are increasingly implemented as network-based, distributed systems—a standard likely to apply in human oversight settings, where the AI system runs on a cloud-based or on-premise server while oversight personnel interact with it via network protocols. (D)DoS attacks can make either the AI system or the human oversight interface inaccessible. This threat is especially severe for cloud-based AI systems: because the AI system and oversight personnel are physically separated, malicious actors can effectively disable oversight through (D)DoS attacks while the AI system continues to operate in an unsafe state or produces inaccurate outputs.

3.5
Man-in-the-Middle Attacks

An attack vector that can be used in computer networks is the Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attack
[
44
]
. When successful, this strategy allows malicious actors to intercept and control data traffic between two or more systems, granting them access to the exchanged information and enabling them to manipulate it. Additionally, attackers can conceal their presence, tricking the involved systems into believing they are communicating securely with each other
[
44
]
. Various methods exist to carry out a MitM attack: ARP spoofing can be used when the attacker and target are on the same local area network (LAN), snarfing can be applied in WLAN-based attacks, and domain name system (DNS) cache poisoning is another possible approach.

MitM attacks can compromise the epistemic access and causal power of oversight personnel, depending on which direction the manipulated information travels. If the attacker intercepts and alters information from the AI system to the oversight personnel, epistemic access is undermined: the true behavior, functionality, or outputs of the AI system may remain hidden.

If the manipulated information flows from the human to the AI system, causal power is compromised. For example, if an attacker modifies commands issued by oversight personnel, they lose the ability to interact effectively with the AI system and cannot intervene in AI processes. This situation is particularly dangerous because the attacker can send manipulated responses back to the personnel, creating an illusion of control and misleading them into believing they are still effectively supervising the AI system.

3.6
Vulnerability Exploitation

Vulnerability exploitation refers to utilizing existing flaws in hardware and software for attacks, such as remote code execution or exfiltration of sensitive data. Since modern digital systems are complex combinations of hardware and software components and their respective supply chains, flaws can accumulate quickly and pose a significant threat. One of the most dangerous vulnerability exploitation is the zero-day exploit
[
81
]
. This term refers to the exploitation of a specific vulnerability within a hardware or software component that is unknown even to the manufacturer of the component. This gives the attacker the time advantage of day zero, before the manufacturer can detect, identify and fix the vulnerability.

Attacks based on vulnerability exploitation can target epistemic access or causal power. Attackers can use the vulnerabilities to get unauthorized access to the system and in this way manipulate the system output which the human oversight person uses to detect deviations in system behavior. If the attacker can obfuscate their actions from the human oversight personnel, the personnel are prevented from understanding the real functioning of the AI system. Similar effects can be achieved by manipulating other information that human oversight personnel uses to assess the AI system.

Causal power is affected by vulnerability exploitation if attackers prevent human oversight personnel from appropriately communicating with the AI system. This can be achieved by dropping commands send by the human oversight personnel or disabling crucial components of the human oversight software. Depending on the exploitation, attackers might be able to disguise the attack. For example, and similar to the example for MitM attacks, they could drop commands by human oversight personnel but fake a response by the AI system that suggests the command was successfully executed.

3.7
Malware

Malware is software that executes harmful functionality on an IT system, often without the user’s knowledge. Its potential targets and effects are highly diverse. Ransomware encrypts the file system and typically demands a ransom for decryption. Spyware extracts information from the infected system. These are just two of the most common types, but malware encompasses a wide range of threats designed for various purposes
[
82
,
45
]
.

Due to the diversity of malware functionality, it can compromise epistemic access and causal power of oversight personnel. Regarding epistemic access, malware can alter the core functionality of an AI system. For example, it could insert system prompts into deployed large language models (LLMs), significantly changing their behavior
[
83
]
. Malware can also intercept or manipulate data traffic from the AI system to oversight personnel, or modify, encrypt, or obfuscate system logs. These actions prevent personnel from understanding the system’s true functionality and therefore undermine epistemic access.

Causal power can be compromised if malware intercepts or manipulates data traveling from oversight personnel to the AI system. For instance, if malware prevents personnel from interacting with the AI system or locks them out of controlling its core functions, they lose the ability to intervene in system processes or outputs. Similarly, if malware secretly alters commands issued by oversight personnel, it further undermines their causal power.

3.8
Social Engineering

Social engineering refers to attacks that exploit human characteristics
[
84
]
. These attacks leverage traits such as fear, respect for authority, or the willingness to help, tricking victims into revealing personal information, disabling security measures, or installing malware. The widespread use of digital communication channels has provided malicious actors with effective and scalable tools for conducting social engineering attacks
[
85
]
.

Social engineering can compromise epistemic access and self-control of oversight personnel. If an attacker impersonates individuals with technical expertise or authority (e.g., trusted colleagues, management, external experts) they can distort personnel’s understanding of relevant information needed to oversee AI systems. For example, an attacker might pretend to be a technician from the AI provider, convincing human oversight personnel that certain graphical user interface notifications indicate a bug. This could lead personnel to ignore important alerts or install malware disguised as a hotfix. Similarly, attackers impersonating management may convince oversight personnel that specific system notifications are non-critical, framing them as part of corporate processes. In both cases, social engineering feeds false information about the AI system’s functionality to oversight personnel, thereby undermining epistemic access.

Social engineering can also erode self-control. By impersonating senior colleagues or management, attackers can exploit perceived authority to pressure oversight personnel into taking specific actions. If the attack is credible, personnel might follow instructions against their better judgment, believing the directives come from a legitimate authority.

3.9
Coercion

Coercion is a broad concept that extends beyond IT security and involves using force, often through threats or violations, to influence a party. In the context of human oversight, coercion refers to pressure or force that alters the intended actions of oversight personnel.

Coercion undermines the self-control of oversight personnel. By influencing oversight personnel to act according to the instructions of a malicious actor rather than their own judgment, coercion renders human oversight ineffective. Even when oversight personnel detect a need for intervention, they may be unable to act according to their assessment. In extreme cases, a malicious actor could threaten the oversight personnel to gain control over their actions, effectively nullifying the intended benefits of human oversight.

3.10
Bribery

Bribery refers to the act of persuading a person, organization, or authority to act in accordance with the goals of a malicious actor, typically by offering monetary incentives, status, or similar benefits.

Bribery undermines the fitting intentions of human oversight personnel. Usually, the intentions of human oversight personnel should align with fulfilling their oversight duties, such as detecting inaccurate outputs or system failures
[
1
]
. A successful bribe can misalign these intentions, causing personnel to act in accordance with the malicious actor’s goals instead. Such goals might include damaging critical infrastructure or compromising the functioning of AI in high-risk applications, such as in hospitals.

3.11
Insider Threats

Insider threats encompass attack scenarios in which malicious actors infiltrate the organization they intend to compromise
[
86
]
. Such attacks share the common characteristic that the attacker either has direct access themselves or exploits an employee’s access rights, whether voluntarily or accidentally. Two pathways to insider threats are discussed under the sections on coercion (see
3.9
) and bribery (see
3.10
). Another common pathway is the exploitation of disgruntled employees.

Insider threats can undermine epistemic access, causal power, and fitting intentions of oversight personnel. They can erode epistemic access by altering or falsifying monitoring data from the AI system. Since human oversight personnel rely on this data to accurately assess AI behavior and intervene when necessary, this prevents them from understanding the system. Insider threats can also obfuscate AI system documentation by deleting or modifying key information, further impeding epistemic access.

Insider threats can also compromise the causal power of oversight personnel. For example, possible sequences of actions that can be taken as a response to detecting problematic system behavior can be ambiguous and the effectiveness of different sequences of actions can differ vastly. Attackers may be able to change organizational oversight policies on how to deal with inaccurate outputs or system malfunctions. In the worst case, such adapted policies can prevent oversight personnel from acting appropriately after detecting problematic system behavior which negatively affects causal power.

Another example of how insider threats may undermine causal power is by achieving and thereby exploit under-staffing. Redirecting resource allocation away from human oversight or preventing human oversight personnel from doing their work (e.g., by force) can reduce the workforce available to human oversight. While this requires substantial access and intervention capabilities, it can prevent oversight personnel from exercising causal power effectively.

Insider threats can additionally undermine the fitting intentions of oversight personnel. Malicious actors with insider access can manipulate the organizational environment to align human oversight personnel’s behavior with their goals. One method is positive punishment, such as establishing a “culture of fear” that discourages human oversight personnel in engaging in behavior that is aligned with their oversight duties. Another method is positive reinforcement, where incentive structures reward behaviors deemed advantageous by the malicious actor.

4.1
Network Management

As discussed in section
3.4
, (D)DoS attacks overwhelm a network by generating excessive traffic. Defending against these attacks requires a multi-step approach
[
87
,
88
]
. The first step is to deploy monitoring systems that analyze network traffic and trigger alerts if a potential (D)DoS attack is detected. The second step involves methods to distinguish between benevolent traffic (typically generated by humans) and malicious traffic (often produced by bots). Finally, filtering techniques can mitigate (D)DoS attacks by restricting or limiting what users – humans or bots – can do within the network.

Hardening strategy
: Establish robust network management procedures within the human oversight architecture.

4.2
Intrusion Detection System

Intrusion detection involves the active monitoring of computers and networks to identify attacks and misuse. An intrusion detection system (IDS) typically combines multiple components and methods to perform this monitoring. The core technical components of an IDS include network or host sensors (depending on the IDS type), a database, a management module, and an analysis module. Host and network sensors monitor the system and collect the necessary information needed for the detection of attacks. The database needs to ensure that the data collected by sensors is stored with a sufficient degree of detail and for a sufficient amount of time. The management module provides all functionalities needed for configuring and calibrating the IDS. The analysis module provides all functionalities necessary for analyzing stored data and performing incident detection. A well-designed and maintained IDS can significantly enhance the cybersecurity of IT systems
[
89
,
59
]
. Intrusion detection systems may counter multiple attack vectors, including (D)DoS attacks, MitM attacks, malware, and insider threats.

Hardening strategy:
Organizations should implement intrusion detection systems to protect their networks broadly and specifically to safeguard human oversight operations and the tools used by oversight personnel from malicious actors.

4.3
Encryption

Encrypted communication is the standard for data traffic on the internet. However, non-encrypted or weakly encrypted data traffic is still commonly used, particularly for communications that do not involve direct interaction between a user and a website. Such vulnerable traffic enables malicious actors to carry out Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attacks relatively easily, often without requiring technical expertise or expensive software and hardware. To ensure adequate security, modern cryptographic methods with strong security guarantees must be employed to encrypt data traffic
[
90
,
91
]
.

Hardening strategy
: All communication between human oversight personnel and the AI system must be encrypted. When personnel and the AI system are not co-located, using a virtual private network to protect data traffic is recommended.

4.4
Transparency

One major challenge in securing AI systems is their complexity. Enhancing transparency of both the system and its communication with human oversight personnel can improve cybersecurity by facilitating the detection of attacks. While transparency itself is not typically considered a cybersecurity measure, it provides access to key security-relevant information, such as implemented components, training data, system functionality, development processes, and dependencies. When combined with other cybersecurity tools, such as vulnerability management, the information made available through transparency increases the likelihood of detecting attacks. Additionally, transparency supports auditing and compliance attestations by making security-relevant information easily accessible and enables model tracking. Overall, ensuring transparency can also help human oversight personnel’s response time, as decision-relevant information becomes more readily available. An appropriate level of transparency can help to counter all of the attack vectors mentioned in this work.

Hardening strategy
: Promote transparency to enhance attack detection by making security-relevant information accessible and making potential manipulations of the system easier to identify.

4.5
Governance Principles and Audit Frameworks

Governance principles and audit frameworks are essential for effectively managing security risks in highly automated systems. Governance principles provide the fundamental guidelines, rules, and processes that define how an organization makes decisions and ensures accountability. They establish the framework for achieving strategic goals while minimizing ethical, legal, and operational risks. In the context of AI, these principles specify who is responsible for risk assessment, how transparency is ensured, and which standards apply to data security.

A prominent example of implementing these principles is the AAA Framework, which stands for Prospective Assessments, Audit Trail, and Enforceable Compliance. These three pillars offer a comprehensive approach to strengthening system integrity and human oversight. Prospective Assessments, conducted before system implementation, identify and mitigate potential risks early, incorporating the perspectives of affected individuals and groups
[
66
]
. A robust Audit Trail, supported by high-quality data and system transparency, is essential for analyzing errors and assigning responsibility. Enforceable Compliance ensures that legal requirements are actionable, with independent audits verifying compliance and enhancing confidence in system integrity. Another well-established framework is the information security management system (ISMS) specified by ISO/IEC 27001
[
92
]
. An ISMS provides the technical and organizational foundation to ensure integrity and reliability of information such as data, commands, and system statuses. Governance principles and audit frameworks are a countermeasure against vulnerability exploitations and insider threats.

Hardening strategy
: Organizations need to establish governance principles and audit frameworks after established standards like the AAA framework and ISO/IEC 27001. These principles and frameworks have to include all components of human oversight that are implemented within an organization.

4.6
Red Teaming

The term red team refers to an independent group that investigates an organization’s IT systems to identify vulnerabilities and security flaws
[
93
]
. The red team simulates the behavior of malicious actors, employing all methods that real attackers might use. Its goal is to uncover as many exploitable security issues within the tested system as possible. Red teaming can help counter several different attack vectors, including poisoning attacks, adversarial attacks, explainability attacks, and vulnerability exploitation.

Hardening strategy
: Organizations should conduct regular red teaming exercises to detect and address security flaws within their human oversight processes.

4.7
Training of Human Oversight Personnel

Training aims to strengthen the resilience of human oversight personnel against attacks
[
94
]
. Training initiatives can help personnel to recognize, resist, and properly respond to manipulation attempts. Training may include social engineering awareness, teaching personnel to identify common manipulation tactics such as phishing or impersonation of authority figures. Practical exercises, such as simulated phishing campaigns or role-play scenarios, reinforce recognition and proper reporting behaviors.

Training can also address resilience against coercion, preparing personnel to respond to threats or undue pressure. This includes protocols for escalating coercion attempts, access to secure whistleblowing channels, and preparation to maintain effective human oversight behavior under stress. Anti-bribery training can teach personnel to recognize undue influence. Clear organizational policies defining conflicts of interest and mandatory disclosure mechanisms further reduce the risk of misaligned intentions. Training of human oversight personal counters social engineering, coercion, and bribery.

Hardening strategy
: Organizations should implement recurring, context-specific training programs for human oversight personnel. By institutionalizing such training and embedding it in the organization’s security culture, human oversight personnel can develop the awareness, self-efficacy, and procedural support needed to counteract manipulation attempts, thereby contributing to secure human oversight.
