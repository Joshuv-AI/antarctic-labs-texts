The impact of unmanned aerial vehicle applications on agricultural carbon emission
Authors: Xiaolin Li, Chunyan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgricultural carbon emissions constitute a major contributor to global warming, yet the specific impact of drone technology on such emissions remains unclear. This study attempts to fill this research gap. Taking agricultural drones as the key carrier, this paper constructs a Spatial Autoregressive Model by means of spatial econometric methods based on the panel data of 31 Chinese provinces spanning from 2014 to 2023. It empirically examines the impact of agricultural drones on agricultural carbon emission intensity and its spatial spillover effects, while also analyzing the synergistic effect between agricultural drones and traditional agricultural mechanization. The results show that the application of agricultural drones significantly reduces the agricultural carbon emission intensity of both local and adjacent areas. However, there exists a “synergistic paradox” between agricultural drones and traditional agricultural mechanization—their combined application will instead increase agricultural carbon emission intensity. On this basis, this paper puts forward policy recommendations including advancing the localization of drone technology, implementing “policy linkage” to guide the electrification of agricultural machinery, and strengthening the orientation of green R&D. These measures are designed to fully unlock the agricultural emission reduction benefits of drone technology and promote the coordinated development of low-altitude technology and green agriculture.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

As the world’s largest carbon dioxide emitter, China accounts for approximately 30% of global carbon emissions, with agricultural carbon emissions representing 17% of the national total1, highlighting the urgency of addressing this sector to achieve carbon neutrality goals. Under the policy framework of integrating the “dual carbon” strategy with agricultural modernization, reducing agricultural carbon emissions has become critical for sustainable development. Although traditional measures like chemical fertilizer reduction and water-saving irrigation have lowered carbon emission intensity per unit output between 2014 and 2023, the expansion of production scale under food security strategies has intensified emission reduction pressures2. Traditional approaches face limitations due to diminishing marginal effects and uneven input application, hindering further progress.

The rapid development of low-altitude technology, particularly drone applications, offers a transformative solution. Drones equipped with radar and visual recognition enable precise operations such as variable fertilization and pesticide application, reducing input-related emissions and optimizing energy consumption through path optimization3. This raises key questions: How does drone penetration affect agricultural carbon emission intensity4? Does it exhibit spatial spillover effects5? What is the compound impact of synergizing drones with traditional agricultural mechanization6? Addressing these questions can enrich agricultural technology emission reduction theory, support low-altitude technology integration, and provide empirical basis for regional policies, thereby advancing the dual goals of emission reduction and food security7.

Following the introduction, a systematic literature review is conducted to establish the theoretical foundation, examining studies on agricultural carbon emissions and the application of drone technology. Subsequently, the research model and hypotheses are developed to articulate the theoretical framework and mechanistic pathways. The empirical section then elaborates on the methodology, data sources, and spatial econometric model specification. Following the analysis of results and robustness checks, the study concludes by discussing the empirical findings, deriving policy implications, and outlining directions for future research.

Literature review

Agricultural carbon emissions

Agricultural carbon emissions have been extensively studied as a key component of global climate change research. International studies, such as West et al8., compared carbon sinks and emissions across tillage methods, confirming agriculture’s significant impact on greenhouse gas emissions. Pathak et al9. quantified emission coefficients for production systems, providing a basis for calculations. Domestically, Ding Baogen10noted that Chinese scholars have developed a research framework focusing on calculation methods, driving factors, and spatial characteristics, aligned with national conditions. Calculation methods often employ emission coefficient approaches; for instance, Tian Yun et al11. applied this to inputs like fertilizers and pesticides, refining estimates for rice fields and livestock. Driving factors have been analyzed using models like STIRPAT and LMDI by researchers including Huang Rui12, Zhang Leqin13, and Li Guozhi et al14., identifying influences such as economic development and technological progress. Liu Qitao15emphasized intensive production and technology depth as core drivers of regional differences. Spatial characteristics studies, using econometric methods, reveal significant spatial correlation; Cao Jianfei et al16. found heterogeneity in digital rural construction effects. However, these studies overlook spatial spillover mechanisms specific to low-altitude technology.

Agricultural application of drone technology

Drone technology has gained attention for its agricultural potential. Foreign research, including Kim17, Del Pozo S18, and Bendig J19, demonstrated that drones with multispectral sensors improve fertilizer efficiency. Tang Q et al20. showed that UAV plant protection enhances pesticide utilization from 30% to 65% via rotor-induced fog uniformity. Domestically, studies focus on adaptability and emission reduction: Li Juan et al21. used prescription maps for variable fertilization, reducing nitrogen use by 27.34% in rice fields; Wang Lei et al22. found that drones replacing traditional machinery cut field entries by 60%, reduced soil compaction, and increased carbon storage; Zhao Junming23reported that UAV-based monitoring systems cut pesticide use by 50–60%, indirectly lowering emissions. Despite these advances, gaps remain in quantifying emission reduction effects under low-altitude technology frameworks. For example, Guan Zichen et al24. highlighted efficiency gains but overlooked technology diffusion, and policy regulation aspects are underexplored. This study aims to address these gaps by analyzing the impact mechanism of drone technology on carbon emission intensity.

Research model and hypothesis development

The paradox of effect superposition and synergy

From a theoretical perspective, the impact of drone technology on agricultural carbon emission intensity can be achieved through two paths: direct effect and spatial spillover effect25. As the core carrier of low-altitude technology empowering agriculture, drones directly optimize the allocation of production factors through precise operations. The radar, visual recognition and remote sensing monitoring equipment equipped can dynamically obtain data on soil fertility and crop growth, realize variable fertilization, precise pesticide application and optimization of agricultural machinery operation paths, reduce the waste of chemical fertilizers and pesticides and fossil energy consumption, and reduce carbon emissions from the source. At the same time, affected by the First Law of Geography26 and the theory of new technology diffusion, the environmental effects of drone technology will also break through regional boundaries, generate spatial spillover through technology demonstration, talent flow and service networks, and the regional diffusion of low-altitude technology-related policies will further strengthen this effect, providing institutional support for technology promotion through the sharing of pilot experience and resource coordination. Based on this, this paper puts forward the following research hypotheses:

H1 The use of agricultural drones has a significant inhibitory effect on agricultural carbon emission intensity.

H2 The use of agricultural drones has a significant spatial spillover effect on inhibiting agricultural carbon emission intensity.

In the dimension of technological synergy, traditional agricultural mechanization is powered by fuel, which improves operation efficiency but also increases carbon emissions; while drones are powered by electricity and have low-carbon attributes. The combined application of drones and traditional agricultural mechanization forms a “low-altitude - ground” three-dimensional operation system, which can shorten the operation cycle and expand the coverage through efficiency improvement. However, the synergistic use of the two may trigger an “energy consumption superposition” effect: on the one hand, the overlapping operation scope of drones and traditional agricultural machinery may lead to repeated investment, increasing energy consumption per unit of cultivated land; on the other hand, after drones improve operation efficiency, they may stimulate farmers to increase the use frequency of traditional agricultural machinery, indirectly increasing carbon emission intensity (Wang & Zhang, 2025)27, resulting in a “synergistic paradox”. Based on this, this paper puts forward the hypothesis:

H3 The synergistic effect of agricultural drones and traditional agricultural mechanization will increase carbon emission intensity.

Research methods and model setting

Data source and variable selection

This study selects panel data from 31 provincial administrative regions in China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan) from 2014 to 2023 as the research sample. The data are mainly from the China Statistical Yearbook (2014–2023), China Rural Statistical Yearbook (2014–2023), China Environmental Statistical Yearbook (2014–2023), China Science and Technology Statistical Yearbook (2014–2023) and statistical yearbooks of various provinces from 2014 to 2023. The time interval covers both the starting point of large-scale application of plant protection drones in 2014 and the latest data node in 2023. The design of core variables refers to Tian Yun’s11 research on agricultural carbon emission calculation, using the multi-source emission coefficient method to measure agricultural carbon emission intensity; drawing on Lan Yubin’s28 analysis of drone technology application, the ratio of the number of plant protection drones to cultivated land area is used to measure drone penetration, the ratio of total agricultural machinery power to cultivated land area is used to measure agricultural mechanization rate, and the interaction term between the two is added to capture the synergistic effect.

To ensure data quality, the following steps were taken: (1) Linear interpolation was applied to fill missing values for a few province-year observations (specifically, Inner Mongolia (2015) and Tibet (2018) for drone counts; less than 2% of total observations were affected). (2) All monetary variables (agricultural output value, R&D expenditure, GDP) were deflated to constant 2014 prices using provincial GDP deflators obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook. (3) All continuous variables were winsorized at the 1 st and 99th percentiles to mitigate the influence of outliers.

Among them, the dependent variable: agricultural carbon emission intensity (CI) is calculated by the multi-source emission coefficient method. Referring to Tian Yun’s 11 method, it covers five major emission sources: chemical fertilizer application, pesticide use, agricultural film use, diesel consumption of agricultural machinery, and methane emission from rice fields. The total carbon emissions are calculated and divided by the total agricultural output value to obtain the intensity indicator. Independent Variables: drone penetration rate (DR) is the ratio of the number of plant protection drones to the cultivated land area; aerial operation ratio (AERO) is the ratio of the Unmanned Aerial Vehicle operation area to the cultivated land area; drone-mechanization interaction (DR×MECH) is obtained by multiplying the drone penetration rate by the agricultural mechanization rate, where the agricultural mechanization rate is the ratio of the total agricultural machinery power to the cultivated land area (Xu Qinghua & Zhang Guangsheng, 2022)25. Control variables: agricultural R&D investment (RD) is the ratio of agricultural scientific research expenditure to agricultural GDP; policy pilot (POLICY) is obtained by the dummy variable assignment method, taking 1 if it belongs to a national low-altitude technology pilot area, otherwise 0. Model selection

Following the LM test results in Table 4, both Robust LM-lag and Robust LM-error are significant, which according to Anselin & Griffith (1988)29 suggests that the Spatial Durbin Model (SDM) might be a more general starting point. We therefore estimated an SDM and conducted Wald tests for the hypotheses that it simplifies to either SAR or SEM. The Wald test for SAR simplification (θ = 0) yielded a p-value of 0.218, while the test for SEM simplification (θ + ρβ = 0) gave p = 0.086. Based on these results and the principle of parsimony, we chose the SAR model as the final specification. Although the SAR model does not include spatial lags of the independent variables, its spatial autoregressive parameter ρ generates indirect effects that are transmitted through neighboring outcomes. Following LeSage and Pace (2009), we compute short‑term and long‑term direct, indirect, and total effects from the SAR estimates. This interpretation is standard and does not require the SDM framework.

Hypothesis testing

The spatial econometric regression results provide a systematic examination of the research hypotheses. First, the significantly negative short-term main effect (−0.0444) and long-term direct effect (−0.0483) of the drone penetration rate (DR) demonstrate that the increase in UAV equipment reserves and the deepening of technological application effectively reduce local agricultural carbon emission intensity, thus supporting H1. Concurrently, the emission reduction effect of the aerial operation ratio (AERO) is more pronounced (short-term: −0.1346; long-term: −0.1508), indicating that practical field operations exert a stronger inhibitory impact on carbon intensity than mere equipment quantity, a finding consistent with Zhao Junming’s23 micro-level evidence on the improved efficiency of UAV precision pesticide application.

Second, the significantly negative long-term indirect effects of both DR (−0.0498**) and AERO (−0.1595) confirm notable spatial spillover effects of drone technology. This suggests that the technological dividends can diffuse to neighboring regions through geographical proximity, thereby validating H2. This result aligns with the mechanism of spatial technology diffusion discussed by Liu Xianjiang et al30., further corroborating the inter-regional linkage characteristics of agricultural carbon emissions.

However, a “synergistic paradox” emerges from the interaction between drones and traditional agricultural mechanization. The significantly positive short-term main effect (0.0029) and long-term direct effect (0.0033) of the interaction term (DR × MECH) indicate that their combined application inadvertently increases carbon emission intensity, supporting H3. This phenomenon resonates with the energy consumption superposition effect noted by Wang & Zhang27, suggesting that without systematic integration, the efficiency gains from drones might stimulate increased usage frequency of traditional machinery, partially offsetting their emission reduction benefits.

Test of replacing the regression matrix

Using an economic-geographical nested matrix (W2), AERO’s direct effect (−0.1147) and DR×MECH’s effect (0.0027) remain significant, with coefficient signs consistent across models. Control variables (e.g., POLICY, −0.0176***) show stable impacts, indicating minimal bias from spatial structure choices.

When using the economic-geographical matrix W2, the direct effect of DR becomes statistically insignificant, although it retains a negative sign. This suggests that the spatial spillover of drone penetration is sensitive to economic distance, possibly due to the ‘economic gradient barrier’ discussed earlier. In contrast, the effects of AERO and the interaction term remain significant and maintain their signs, indicating that the main conclusions regarding operational intensity and the synergistic paradox are robust to alternative spatial weight specifications. However, when the model incorporates differences in economic development levels, such spillover effects are significantly weakened. The root cause lies in the “economic gradient barrier” introduced by the economic-geographical matrix: On one hand, underdeveloped regions lack supporting conditions such as power infrastructure (e.g., charging stations) and drone operator training systems required for drone operations, resulting in high technology adaptation costs. On the other hand, regions with lower economic levels prioritize ensuring food production and exhibit lower willingness to adopt drone technology compared to higher-income regions, making it difficult for the equipment stock dividend of core provinces to transfer across economic gradients.

While additional robustness checks (e.g., excluding influential provinces, alternative lag structures, placebo tests) are valuable, the two presented tests—replacing the dependent variable and replacing the spatial weight matrix—already demonstrate that the main coefficients (DR, AERO, DR×MECH) are not artifacts of a single measurement or spatial structure. The sensitivity of DR’s significance under the economic‑geographical matrix (Table 7) is explicitly discussed as evidence of an ‘economic gradient barrier’, which is a substantive finding rather than a model‑dependent artifact. Future research with longer panels should extend these tests.

Endogeneity test

To alleviate the potential endogeneity issues in the model (such as omitted variables, reverse causality), this paper follows the model design of Liu & Chen31and constructs an interaction term between terrain undulation and low-altitude economic policy pilot projects (terrain_policy) as a tool variable for the penetration rate of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles. The construction basis is as follows: First, the interaction between terrain undulation and policy pilot projects can more accurately capture the dual constraints of institutional and geographical on the promotion of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles. In policy-supported areas, the constraining effect of terrain complexity on technological diffusion is more prominent; Second, Yin et al32. believe that policy pilot projects, as exogenous institutional shocks, enhance the exclusivity of the tool variable; Third, this interaction term avoids the endogeneity problem that may exist when using terrain undulation alone. The 2SLS estimation shows that after controlling for endogeneity, the penetration rate of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles still has a significant inhibitory effect on agricultural carbon emission intensity.

It is noteworthy that the 2SLS estimates yield a positive and significant coefficient for AERO (2.1222) and a negative (though insignificant) coefficient for DR×MECH (−0.0342). These sign reversals relative to the baseline SAR results can be attributed to two factors. First, the IV approach isolates the exogenous variation in drone penetration (DR) using terrain_policy, but AERO and the interaction term remain potentially endogenous, which may bias their coefficients. Second, the strong collinearity between DR and AERO (correlation = 0.73) and between DR and DR×MECH is exacerbated in the 2SLS setting, altering the partial correlations. Importantly, the primary coefficient of interest—DR—remains negative and significant, confirming the core hypothesis that drone adoption reduces carbon intensity after addressing endogeneity.

The exclusion restriction is justified as follows: Terrain ruggedness itself is a purely geographical feature that does not directly influence agricultural carbon emissions, nor is it correlated with other unobserved emission determinants after controlling for province and year fixed effects. Its interaction with the policy pilot—a centrally assigned, exogenous institutional shock—affects carbon emissions only through UAV adoption because: (i) rugged terrain increases the relative advantage of drones over ground machinery (relevance), and (ii) policy pilot status exogenously varies institutional support for UAVs across provinces. There is no theoretical or empirical basis suggesting that this interaction term directly alters fertilizer use, irrigation practices, or energy consumption other than via drone diffusion. A falsification test (available upon request) shows no significant effect of the instrument on pre‑2014 carbon emissions, further supporting the exclusion restriction.

Conclusion

Summary of key findings

The results confirm that drone penetration significantly reduces agricultural carbon intensity, with effects spilling over to neighboring regions. However, a synergistic paradox emerges when drones are combined with traditional mechanization.

Policy implications

To harness drone technology’s emission reduction potential, policymakers should: (1) Promote regional cooperation to overcome economic gradients, leveraging spatial spillovers; (2) Shift focus from drone equipment subsidies to supporting actual operations (e.g., training and infrastructure); and (3) Design integrated policies that avoid the synergistic paradox, such as by incentivizing electric machinery alongside drones. These measures can align with national goals like the “dual carbon” strategy and low-altitude economy pilots25.

Limitations and future prospects

Provincial-level drone data from official statistical sources lacks sufficient accuracy to capture intra-provincial differences in applications between plains and mountainous areas, while agricultural carbon emission calculations relying on emission coefficients may deviate from actual scenarios; additionally, the study does not deeply explore the heterogeneous impacts of drones on specific carbon sources such as fertilizers, agricultural machinery, and rice field methane, nor fully account for the regulatory role of micro-characteristics like farmers’ operational scale and education level in technology adoption, and its conclusions—contextualized within China’s agricultural landscape—require further verification for applicability to other regions’ agricultural systems and policy environments.

Building upon our findings, targeted policy interventions are recommended. Regionally, it is crucial to break the “economic gradient barrier” by fostering synergies between geographically proximate and economically comparable areas. Technologically, the focus should shift from mere equipment accumulation to ensuring the effective implementation of actual field operations. Systemically, a transition towards a “low-carbon integrated” system that harmonizes low-altitude and ground-based agricultural practices is needed to systematically unlock the agricultural emission reduction potential of UAVs.

Future research on the agricultural application of low-altitude technology can be advanced by integrating satellite remote sensing to monitor UAV operation trajectories and conducting global farmer surveys for more accurate data, introducing “carbon emission source segmentation” and incorporating farmers’ characteristics to deepen mechanism analysis, and expanding to an international perspective to compare drone application effects across diverse agricultural modes (e.g., large-scale agriculture in North America, small-scale farming in Europe, intensive agriculture in Asia), learn from international practices such as the U.S. integration of UAVs with smart agriculture systems and Japan’s policy support for low-altitude technology, and explore the integration of drone-related emission reduction benefits into global carbon trading and accounting frameworks to support the global agricultural sector’s sustainable development and emission reduction goals.

Data Availability Statement

All data supporting the findings of this study are derived from publicly accessible sources: the China Statistical Yearbook, China Rural Statistical Yearbook, China Environmental Statistical Yearbook, China Science and Technology Statistical Yearbook, and provincial statistical yearbooks (2014–2023). These raw datasets can be freely accessed through the official website of the National Bureau of Statistics of China (https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/tjgb/ndtjgb/).The processed panel data, spatial weight matrices, and all replication code (Stata) are publicly available in the Zenodo repository under a CC BY 4.0 license: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19245492. The repository includes a detailed README file describing the data structure, variable definitions, and step-by-step instructions for reproducing all tables and figures in the manuscript.
