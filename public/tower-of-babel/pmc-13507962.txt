Investigation of the inhibition performance of a newly synthesized Schiff base and a mono-cationic surfactant for the protection of carbon steel tubulars during oil and gas well acidization
Authors: M. A. Hegazy, Mohamed M. Asab, S. M. Rashwan, Medhat M. Kamel, Emad M. Gad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the corrosion inhibition effectiveness of a newly synthesized Schiff base and its corresponding cationic surfactant on carbon steel exposed to an acidic environment consisting of 1.0 M HCl. The evaluation was conducted using gravimetric mass-loss tests, potentiodynamic polarization techniques, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Experimental results showed that both tested inhibitors were highly effective in reducing the corrosion rate; protective efficiency increased with inhibitor concentration and decreased as the temperature rose. The cationic surfactant demonstrated superior performance, achieving a maximum protective efficiency of 92.28% at a concentration of 5 × 10−3 mol L−1 and a temperature of 25 °C, whereas the Schiff base recorded a value of 71.65%. Electrochemical analyses demonstrated that both inhibitors suppress anodic and cathodic reactions through adsorption onto the carbon steel substrate, following the Langmuir adsorption model. Thermodynamic and quantum chemical analyses confirmed that the inhibition mechanism is primarily based on physisorption, with a chemisorption component. SEM and EDX analyses confirmed the formation of a protective adsorbed layer on the metal surface. Overall, the synthesized cationic surfactant exhibited promising corrosion inhibition performance under the investigated laboratory conditions, indicating its potential relevance to acidic environments encountered in petroleum-related operations. Further evaluation under more representative acidizing conditions is required to establish its practical field applicability.

Introduction

Carbon steel remains a widely used material in industry, particularly in construction, mechanical engineering, and pipelines. Its widespread use is due to its favorable mechanical properties, including high tensile strength, ease of fabrication, and cost-effectiveness. Despite its advantages, carbon steel is susceptible to corrosion, especially in aggressive environments characterized by acidic or saline conditions. Corrosion can significantly reduce the service life of carbon steel components, potentially causing failures requiring costly repairs and operational downtime. Therefore, corrosion mitigation strategies, such as the use of inhibitors are essential to maintaining the functionality and durability of carbon steel across various industrial sectors.1–3

Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is a key reagent in the petroleum industry, widely used for well stimulation, pipeline maintenance, and scale removal. Its ability to dissolve mineral deposits and facilitate hydrocarbon flow within reservoirs makes it indispensable for oilfield operations. However, the use of hydrochloric acid in these processes often leads to increased corrosion of the metal components involved, particularly carbon steel. Upon contact with hydrochloric acid, carbon steel undergoes an electrochemical reaction that causes dissolution of the metal surface, the evolution of hydrogen gas, and the formation of corrosion by-products such as iron chloride. Consequently, these reactions can cause significant material deterioration, potentially leading to pipeline failures, equipment malfunctions, and safety risks.4–6

In this study, a pair of structurally related inhibitors, a Schiff base and its corresponding quaternized surfactant was selected to isolate the effect of quaternization on corrosion inhibition performance while minimizing structural variations. The compounds were characterized using FTIR spectroscopy, 1H NMR, and mass spectrometry, and comparatively evaluated as corrosion inhibitors.

These compounds adsorb onto the metal surface, where their structure promotes the formation of a protective barrier that prevents further reactions between the aggressive environment and the metal material. The presence of characteristic functional groups, such as imine or hydroxyl groups, within the Schiff bases enhances their ability to interact with the metal surface, providing both physical and electrochemical barriers against corrosion.7

Research has demonstrated that Schiff base-based inhibitors offer excellent corrosion resistance. Schiff bases represent a promising class of corrosion inhibitors, as they are capable of providing effective protection in acidic environments. These inhibitors have been found to possess exceptional anti-corrosion properties, particularly in hydrochloric acid solutions.

Furthermore, they can be tailored to specific industrial applications to enhance the functionality and durability of carbon steel components.8,9 Surfactant-based inhibitors constitute another class of substances widely studied for reducing corrosion in the oil industry, particularly in the presence of hydrochloric acid. As amphiphilic molecules, surfactants consist of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic moieties. When added to an acidic solution, they adhere to the steel surface, forming a protective layer that limits the alloy's exposure to corrosive agents.

A surfactant's ability to act as an anodic, cathodic, or mixed-type inhibitor (protecting both sides of the electrochemical reaction) depends on its specific nature. By hindering the electrochemical processes responsible for metal degradation, surfactant inhibitors, whether cationic, anionic, or non-ionic prove particularly effective in preventing localized corrosion, including pitting. Furthermore, surfactant-based inhibitors are often cost-effective and environmentally friendly, making them an advantageous choice for large-scale industrial applications where corrosion poses a significant challenge.10,11

Despite the extensive investigation of Schiff bases and surfactants as corrosion inhibitors, a clear understanding of how the systematic conversion of a Schiff base into its corresponding cationic surfactant affects corrosion inhibition remains limited. In this context, the novelty of the present work lies in the rational structural transformation of a newly synthesized Schiff base into its corresponding mono-cationic surfactant through quaternization with a long-chain alkyl halide, followed by a direct comparative evaluation of the two structurally related compounds under identical conditions. This molecular design provides a suitable platform for establishing a structure–performance relationship between the parent Schiff base and its quaternized derivative. The effect of introducing the quaternary ammonium center and C12 hydrophobic chain on adsorption behavior, surface coverage, interfacial properties, electronic characteristics, and corrosion inhibition efficiency was systematically investigated using gravimetric, potentiodynamic polarization, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, SEM/EDX, adsorption thermodynamics, surface activity measurements, and DFT calculations. The substantially higher inhibition efficiency of the mono-cationic surfactant compared with the parent Schiff base demonstrates the beneficial role of the amphiphilic cationic architecture in enhancing interaction with the carbon steel/electrolyte interface. Thus, the study provides a combined experimental and theoretical structure–property framework for understanding how quaternization of a Schiff-base framework can be exploited to improve corrosion protection of API 5L X70 carbon steel in hydrochloric acid.

Materials and practical methodologies

Materials

Formulation of inhibitors

In organic chemistry, the condensation of aldehydes with amines is a well-known method for synthesizing Schiff bases. In this study, furfural and N,N-dimethylpropane-1,3-diamine were reacted to form a Schiff base; the reaction proceeded via the interaction of the nucleophilic center of the amine moiety with the carbonyl carbon of the furfural, followed by dehydration to form the imine bond (–CN–). Furfural-derived Schiff bases are of particular interest due to their conjugated heteroaromatic structure, which confers enhanced stability. To introduce a hydrophobic tail, the resulting Schiff base was subsequently alkylated with 1-bromododecane, a long-chain alkyl halide; the secondary amine nitrogen of the Schiff base attacks the electrophilic carbon of the bromododecane, resulting in the displacement of the bromide ion during the nucleophilic substitution reaction.12

The result is a new amphiphilic compound that acts as a surfactant, owing to the presence of a non-polar dodecyl chain and a polar imine-type head group. Since such surfactants are capable of self-assembling into micelles and reducing surface tension, they are used in various industrial applications, as illustrated in Scheme 1. Solutions

Stock solutions of the inhibitor (0.005 mol L−1) were made in a 100 ml volumetric flask through dissolution of calculated dosage of inhibitor for 10 ml of ethyl alcohol and subsequently finishing with 90 ml of absolute ethanol. The investigated inhibitors were obtained through dilution of the stock solution and used in the selected dose range: 1 × 10−4, 5 × 10−4, 1 × 10−3 and 5 × 10−3 mol L−1. 37% high-purity hydrochloric acid solution was diluted with ultra-pure demineralized water to create a fresh corrosive media (1.0 mol per L HCl).

Carbon steel specimens

Carbon steel strip coupons with estimated measurements of 2 × 2 × 0.2 cm were utilized for the experimental work. API 5L X70 carbon steel specimen, predominantly iron and containing traces of micro-alloy elements, primarily C, Mn, Nb, V, and Ti, was used to cut out carbon steel coupons. Using a variety of sandpaper grades (220–2000), the carbon steel coupons were first refined up to the point where a mirror-like appearance was visible. The samples were subsequently rinsed with ultra-pure demineralized water then dried at ambient thermal conditions.

Weight loss (WL) method

A faster and more effective method involves measuring mass loss in a corrosion laboratory to determine the effectiveness of inhibitors in providing corrosion resistance. Carbon steel samples were pre-treated as described in the section regarding carbon steel specimens. These samples were weighed before and after immersion in the test medium (100 ml of 1.0 M HCl, both in the absence and presence of various inhibitor concentrations) to determine mass loss.

The evaluation was conducted over a 24 hours period at various temperatures (25, 45, 65, and 85 °C). Surface coverage (θ), corrosion rate (k), and inhibition efficiency (η) were determined using the following mathematical expressions.13,14123η = θ × 100where A denotes the specimen's total surface area (cm2), t represents the duration of immersion in (h), and W represents the decrease in the coupon weight (mg). W and Wo correspond to the rates of corrosion measured for coupons in inhibited and uninhibited media, in that order.

Electrochemical experiments

Electrochemical tests were performed using a three-electrode setup consisting of a saturated calomel electrode (reference), a platinum wire counter electrode, and a carbon steel (API 5L X70) working electrode by a Gamry Reference 3000 potentiostat/galvanostat/ZRA. A carbon steel sample measuring 1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm, welded to a copper rod and embedded in a glass tube, served as the working electrode. Tests were conducted at room temperature (25 °C) in a 1.0 M HCl solution, both in the absence and presence of the inhibitor at various dosages. Prior to the electrochemical experiments, open-circuit potential measurements were taken to reach a steady-state condition; the working electrode was immersed in the test solution for thirty minutes. Electrochemical techniques, such as potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, were employed to analyze corrosion behavior.

For the potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) measurements, the polarization curves were recorded at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s−1 over a potential range from −800 to −200 mV vs. SCE. The corrosion potential (Ecorr), corrosion current density (icorr), and anodic and cathodic Tafel slopes (βa and βc) were obtained from the polarization curves by Tafel extrapolation.

The multi-frequency alternating current (AC) electrochemical method, used for electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests at open-circuit potential, employed a signal with an amplitude of 10 mV and a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.2 Hz. The EIS results were used to determine the solution resistance (high-frequency region), polarization resistance (low-frequency region), and double-layer capacitance. The impedance spectra were fitted using the equivalent electrical circuit presented in the revised manuscript, and the goodness of fit was evaluated using the chi-square (χ2) statistic obtained from ZSimpWin. The corresponding χ2 values are reported together with the EIS parameters.

The electrochemical measurements were performed as single measurements under each experimental condition.

The corrosion rate is typically calculated from the polarization resistance,15 while the obtained EIS results were represented using Nyquist and Bode plots.16

Surface morphology assessments (SEM and EDX)

The surface elemental composition and structure of polished carbon steel samples subjected to corrosion, both in the absence and presence of inhibitors, were analyzed using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with scanning electron microscopy (JEOL-JSM-6510LV model, Japan) to characterize the surface composition and evaluate the effect of the inhibitors on surface morphology. The samples were immersed in a 1.0 M HCl solution for 24 hours, testing conditions both without and with inhibitors (at the optimal dosage). Prior to analysis, the samples were allowed to dry under ambient conditions.

Computational approaches – theoretical quantum modeling

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the electronic characteristics of the synthesized inhibitors and to establish the relationship between their molecular properties and corrosion inhibition efficiency.17,18 Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the hybrid B3LYP functional combined with the 6-311G basis set. The software is Gauss view 6 with Gaussian 09 package. A restricted RB3LYP treatment was employed because the investigated molecular systems were considered closed-shell singlet systems with equal numbers of α and β electrons (49 α and 49 β electrons). All calculations were performed in the gas phase.

The self-consistent field (SCF) calculations were tightly converged to 1.0 × 10−8 using the root-mean-square (RMS) density-matrix convergence criterion. Geometry optimization was performed until a stationary point satisfying the four standard convergence criteria was obtained: maximum force = 3.9 × 10−5 a.u., RMS force = 6.0 × 10−6 a.u., maximum displacement = 1.72 × 10−3 a.u., and RMS displacement = 3.38 × 10−4 a.u. No empirical dispersion correction or orbital cutoff was employed. Gaussian-type orbitals (GTOs) with continuous standard integration grids were used. Thermal broadening (smearing) was not applied because the calculations correspond to the electronic ground state at 0 K.

The optimized molecular structures were subsequently used to calculate the energies of the highest occupied molecular orbital (EHUMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (ELUMO), as well as the LUMO–HOMO energy gap (ΔE), ionization potential (I), electron affinity (A), global electronegativity (χ), chemical hardness (η), chemical softness (σ), electrophilicity index (ω), and fractional electron transfer (ΔN).Furthermore, optimized molecular structures and molecular electrostatic potential (ESP) maps were analyzed to identify the electron-rich and electron-poor regions responsible for intermolecular interactions with the carbon steel surface. This set of theoretical descriptors provides molecular-level insight into the inhibition mechanism and complements the experimental electrochemical results.

Results and discussion

Structure confirmation

FT-IR spectroscopy

The FTIR spectrum of the Schiff base compound (1), (E)-3-((furan-2-ylmethylene)amino)-N,N-dimethylpropan-1-amine, is shown in Fig. 1. The spectrum exhibits characteristic absorption bands consistent with the proposed Schiff base structure. The absorptions at 2940.62 and 2773.56 cm−1 are mainly associated with aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations. The characteristic band at 1588.64 cm−1 is assigned to the stretching vibration of the azomethine CN group, providing important spectroscopic evidence for the formation of the Schiff base linkage. The bands at 1457.04 and 1369.63 cm−1 are attributed mainly to CH2 and CH3 deformation vibrations. The absorptions at 1156.18 and 1015.13 cm−1 are associated with C–O–C/C–N and heteroaromatic ring vibrations, consistent with the furan-containing structure. The bands at 882.42 and 740.51 cm−1 are attributed mainly to out-of-plane/ring deformation vibrations, while the low-frequency band at 596.04 cm−1 may be associated with skeletal deformation of the heteroaromatic framework. A broad band centered at 3389.55 cm−1 is tentatively attributed to hydrogen-bonded O–H stretching arising from adsorbed moisture. Overall, the FTIR spectrum provides evidence for the presence of the expected azomethine and heteroaromatic functionalities of the Schiff base compound (1).

FTIR spectrum of the studied Schiff base.

The FTIR spectrum of the surfactant compound (2), (E)-N-(3-((furan-2-ylmethylene)amino)propyl)-N,N-dimethyldodecan-1-aminium bromide, is shown in Fig. 2. Several characteristic absorption bands are observed that support the proposed structure. The bands at 2925.55 and 2856.51 cm−1 are assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of aliphatic CH2 groups, respectively, and are particularly consistent with the presence of the long dodecyl chain. The absorption at 1469.50 cm−1 is mainly attributed to CH2/CH3 deformation vibrations, while the band at 736.43 cm−1 is consistent with CH2 rocking vibrations associated with a long aliphatic chain. Importantly, the absorption at 1641.16 cm−1 is assigned to the azomethine CN stretching vibration, demonstrating retention of the Schiff base functionality in the surfactant compound (2). The band at 1022.08 cm−1 can be associated with C–N/C–O and heteroaromatic vibrations. The broad absorption band at 3423.93 cm−1 is tentatively attributed to hydrogen-bonded O–H stretching associated with adsorbed moisture and is not considered a diagnostic structural feature.

FTIR spectrum of the studied surfactant.

Comparative analysis of the two spectra provides additional evidence for the structural relationship between the parent Schiff base and its corresponding mono-cationic surfactant. The characteristic azomethine vibration is retained in the surfactant spectrum, indicating preservation of the Schiff base framework, whereas the pronounced absorptions at 2925.55 and 2856.51 cm−1, together with the band at 736.43 cm−1, are consistent with the presence of a long aliphatic chain. Thus, the FTIR results support the proposed structural modification while demonstrating retention of the principal Schiff base/furan framework. Nevertheless, FTIR alone is not considered definitive evidence of quaternization; therefore, the structural assignment is supported collectively by the FTIR, 1H NMR, and mass spectrometric data.

Mass spectroscopy

The mass spectrum (Fig. 3) of the assessed Schiff base, (E)-3-((furan-2-ylmethylene)amino)-N,N-dimethylpropan-1-amine exhibits a molecular ion at m/z 192.9, which is consistent with the molecular mass of the proposed Schiff-base structure. In addition to the molecular ion, several characteristic fragment ions are observed, including prominent signals at m/z 55.0, 67.0, 81.0, 96.0, 110.0, 123.0, 136.9, 149.9, and 165.9. The intense fragment at m/z 55.0 represents the base peak and can be attributed to a particularly stable fragment generated during electron-impact fragmentation. The higher-mass fragment ions provide additional information on fragmentation of the furan/azomethine-containing molecular framework. The observation of the molecular ion at m/z 192.9 together with the characteristic fragmentation pattern provides strong mass-spectrometric support for the proposed molecular structure of the Schiff base compound (1).

Mass spectrum of the studied Schiff base.

1H NMR spectroscopy

The 1HNMR spectrum (Fig. 4) of the surfactant compound (2) provides further evidence supporting its proposed molecular structure. The spectrum displays the characteristic proton resonances expected for the 4-methoxybenzylidene moiety, the quaternary ammonium-containing aliphatic segment, and the long dodecyl chain. The aromatic protons of the para-substituted benzene ring are expected to appear as two sets of signals in the downfield aromatic region, corresponding to the chemically non-equivalent pairs of aromatic protons. The azomethine proton (–CHN–) is expected in the downfield region, providing an important diagnostic signal for the Schiff base functionality.

1HNMR spectrum of the studied surfactant.

The signal appearing at approximately δ 3.77 ppm is assigned to the methoxy group (–OCH3) attached to the aromatic ring, while the resonances in the δ 3.0–3.6 ppm region are attributed mainly to methylene and N-methyl protons located in the vicinity of the quaternary ammonium center and the oxygen-containing spacer. The intense aliphatic resonances in the lower-field aliphatic region are consistent with the methylene protons of the long dodecyl chain, whereas the signal at approximately δ 0.9–1.0 ppm is assigned to the terminal methyl group (–CH3) of the dodecyl chain.

The presence of the characteristic methoxy, azomethine, quaternary-ammonium-associated, and long-chain aliphatic proton environments is consistent with the proposed structure of the surfactant compound (2).

In combination with the FTIR and mass spectrometric results, the 1H NMR data provide complementary evidence for the successful formation of the synthesized mono-cationic surfactant.

We used the following relation to estimate the values of ΔG0ads based on the obtained Kads results:296where R and T represent gas constant and the Kelvin absolute temperature, respectively. H2O water content is 55.5 in mol L−1. Table 4 reports the determined values of Kads and ΔG0ads.

It was previously stated that the magnitude of ΔG0ads can indicate the type of adsorption; standard thermodynamic criteria establish that values equal to or greater than −20 kJ mol−1 (i.e., less negative) correspond to physisorption, driven primarily by electrostatic attraction between the charged metal substrate and the ionized inhibitor species. Conversely, values lower than −40 kJ mol−1 (i.e., more negative) indicate a chemisorption mechanism, involving the formation of a coordination bond between the metal atoms and the inhibitor molecules.30,31

The negative values of ΔG0ads obtained from the Langmuir adsorption analysis (−33.29 to −38.99 kJ mol−1 for the Schiff base and −35.26 to −40.69 kJ mol−1 for the surfactant) primarily demonstrate that adsorption is spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable. We now explicitly state that these values alone cannot be regarded as definitive proof of simultaneous physisorption and chemisorption.

Although the magnitude of ΔG0ads has frequently been used as a qualitative indication of the nature of inhibitor adsorption, the boundary values commonly reported for distinguishing physisorption from chemisorption should not be regarded as absolute criteria. Therefore, the present ΔG0ads values are interpreted primarily as evidence of favorable and spontaneous adsorption rather than as definitive proof of a mixed adsorption mechanism. Moreover, the ΔG0ads values become more negative as the temperature rises, indicating that at higher temperatures the adsorption strength decreases.

Furthermore, the relatively low negative values of ΔH0ads (−4.80 kJ mol−1 for the Schiff base and −8.23 kJ mol−1 for the surfactant) together with the increase in the apparent activation energy upon addition of the inhibitors provide stronger support for a predominantly physical adsorption mechanism, involving electrostatic interactions between the protonated/cationic inhibitor species and the chloride-modified steel surface.

Accordingly, the adsorption of the investigated inhibitors onto carbon steel is considered to occur predominantly through physical interactions, particularly electrostatic attraction between the cationic inhibitor species and the negatively charged/chloride-modified steel surface. Possible contributions from donor–acceptor interactions involving heteroatoms and π-electron systems cannot be completely excluded; however, the available experimental data do not allow these interactions to be described as definitive chemisorption.

The application of the Gibbs–Helmholtz eqn (8) combined with Gibbs free energy and enthalpy values, allows for the calculation of the standard entropy of adsorption (ΔS0ads) under various thermal conditions.32 The positive value of ΔS0ads indicates an increase in the disruption at inhibitor-metal interface, likely due to the displacement of H2O molecules from the metal surface during the adsorption process.34

Electro-chemical investigations

Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) experimental results

The kinetic parameters of the anodic and cathodic processes were examined by applying the Tafel polarization method.

The higher efficiency of the surfactant suggests stronger adsorption and development of a denser barrier film over the steel substrate. The lack of significant changes in the Tafel slope values indicates that the inhibitors act primarily through a surface blocking mechanism involving adsorption, rather than altering the fundamental corrosion mechanism. This implies that the inhibitors under investigation inhibited both hydrogen evolution at the cathode site and metal dissolution at the anodic site.38,39

The corrosion potential (Ecorr) values exhibit only slight shifts upon introducing both inhibitors relative to the blank medium. The variation in Ecorr values is under 85 mV, implying that both Schiff base and surfactant act as mixed-type corrosion inhibitors, impacting both reactions, anodic dissolution of the metal and cathodic H2 evolution without preferentially suppressing one mechanism over the other.25,40

Inhibition mechanism

Numerous factors, such as the chemical reactivity of aromatic ring substituents, functional groups, surface charges, electron density, and the nature of the corrosive environment, can influence metal-inhibitor interactions and, consequently, inhibition efficiency.55 The inhibition behavior of the investigated compounds is governed by their ability to adsorb onto the carbon steel surface and block the active sites involved in both anodic metal dissolution and cathodic hydrogen evolution. The experimental results indicate that the adsorption process is predominantly physical in nature. In the acidic medium, protonation of the nitrogen-containing functionalities generates cationic inhibitor species, while chloride ions can adsorb preferentially on the steel surface. Electrostatic attraction between the cationic inhibitor species and the chloride-modified steel surface therefore provides a plausible contribution to the adsorption process.

The substantially higher inhibition efficiency of the surfactant compared with the Schiff base can be associated with its cationic character and amphiphilic structure, particularly the presence of the long hydrophobic alkyl chain, which can promote extensive surface coverage and formation of a more compact barrier layer. This interpretation is consistent with the experimental inhibition results, where the surfactant exhibits stronger corrosion protection than the Schiff base.

The inhibitor molecules also contain nitrogen-containing and π-electron functionalities that may participate in donor–acceptor interactions with the metal surface. The DFT calculations support the presence of favorable electronic characteristics for surface interaction; for example, the surfactant exhibits a higher HOMO energy, lower energy gap, greater softness, and higher calculated electron-transfer tendency than the Schiff base. However, these computational parameters should be considered as evidence for potential surface interaction rather than direct experimental proof of chemisorption.

Therefore, the overall adsorption mechanism proposed in this work is predominantly physisorption, accompanied by possible weak donor–acceptor contributions, rather than a definitively established mixed physisorption–chemisorption mechanism.

Conclusion

• In the present study, a Schiff base compound and its corresponding cationic surfactant were synthesized and evaluated as corrosion inhibitors for API 5L X70 carbon steel in 1.0 M HCl using complementary gravimetric, electrochemical, surface, and computational approaches. The inhibition efficiency of both compounds increased progressively with increasing inhibitor concentration, with the cationic surfactant consistently exhibiting superior protection compared with the Schiff base. At 5 × 10−3 mol L−1 and 25 °C, the weight loss measurements showed inhibition efficiencies of 71.65% for the Schiff base and 92.28% for the surfactant, confirming the pronounced protective effect of the surfactant.

• The principal scientific contribution of this work lies in the comparative structure–performance assessment of the Schiff base functionality and its cationic amphiphilic derivative, demonstrating the beneficial effect of the cationic surfactant architecture on corrosion protection. The superior performance of the surfactant is supported by its higher surface coverage and interfacial protection, as reflected by the electrochemical and gravimetric results.

• In particular, EIS measurements showed a substantial increase in Rct, from 59.59 Ω cm2 for the uninhibited system to 613.90 Ω cm2 at the highest surfactant concentration, accompanied by a pronounced decrease in Cdl, indicating enhanced interfacial resistance and adsorption of inhibitor species at the steel/electrolyte interface.

• The adsorption behavior was best described by the Langmuir isotherm, while the negative values of ΔG0ads confirmed that adsorption is spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable. In view of the relatively low negative values of ΔH0ads and the kinetic results, the adsorption process is interpreted as predominantly physical, with possible contributions from donor–acceptor interactions. The latter are considered supportive interactions rather than definitive evidence of chemisorption.

• Surface characterization further supported inhibitor adsorption. SEM examination revealed substantially reduced surface deterioration in the inhibited specimens compared with carbon steel exposed to uninhibited HCl. EDX analysis provided additional compositional evidence through the appearance of nitrogen on the inhibitor-treated surfaces, with N contents of 2.61 wt% for the Schiff base and 2.31 wt% for the surfactant, compared with its absence in the uninhibited specimen. These findings, together with the electrochemical response, support the formation of an adsorbed protective surface layer.

• The DFT calculations provided molecular-level support for the experimentally observed inhibition trend. The surfactant exhibited a higher EHOMO (−4.668 eV), lower LUMO–HOMO energy gap (3.451 eV), lower chemical hardness (1.726 eV), higher softness (0.579 eV−1), and higher fractional electron-transfer value (1.176 e) than the Schiff base, indicating a more favorable electronic profile for interaction with the metal surface.

• The inhibition efficiencies of the synthesized compounds were comparable to those of structurally related inhibitors reported in the literature under similar experimental conditions.

• The computational results provided valuable molecular-level insights into the electronic properties and adsorption propensity of the investigated inhibitors. The higher HOMO energy, lower LUMO–HOMO energy gap, lower chemical hardness, higher softness, and higher fractional electron-transfer value calculated for the surfactant support its greater electronic propensity to interact with the steel surface, thereby rationalizing its superior experimentally observed inhibition performance.

• Overall, the combined experimental and theoretical findings demonstrate that the cationic amphiphilic modification of the Schiff base structure significantly enhances its corrosion-inhibition performance, making the investigated surfactant a promising candidate for further development as a corrosion inhibitor in acidic environments relevant to petroleum-related operations. However, the present results represent a controlled laboratory-scale assessment in 1.0 M HCl; therefore, further studies under more representative acidizing conditions, including higher acid concentrations, dynamic flow, elevated pressure and temperature, and compatibility with acidizing additives, are required before practical field application can be established.
