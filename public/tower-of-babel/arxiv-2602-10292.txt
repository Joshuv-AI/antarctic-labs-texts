On supersaturation in the Erdős–Sós problem
Andrey Kupavskii, Yakov Shubin

Abstract
The following classical question in extremal set theory is due to Erdős and Sós: what is the size of the largest family ℱ ⊂ ( [ n ] k ) \mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k} with no two sets F 1 , F 2 ∈ ℱ F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F} such that | F 1 ∩ F 2 | = t |F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=t ? In this paper, we address a supersaturation question for this extremal function. For a family ℱ ⊂ ( [ n ] k ) \mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k} of a fixed size ℓ \ell , what is the smallest number of pairs F 1 , F 2 ∈ ℱ F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F} with | F 1 ∩ F 2 | = t |F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=t it may induce? For fixed k k and n → ∞ n\to\infty , we find the exact threshold when the minimum number of pairs matches the expected number of pairs in a random ℓ \ell -element family up to a constant factor. We also find an exact answer for ℓ \ell slightly above the extremal function.

Introduction

For a positive integer
n
, denote
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Given a set
X
and an integer
k\geqslant 0
, denote by
2^{X}
and
\binom{X}{k}
the collection of all subsets and all
k
-element subsets (
k
-sets) of
X
, respectively. A

is simply a collection of sets.

One of the classical topics in extremal set theory is the study of systems with restricted intersections. It was initiated with the paper of Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
13
]
. For all
n,k
they determined the size of the largest family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
that is
intersecting
, i.e., for any two sets
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
we have
A\cap B=\emptyset
. We may also say that such
\mathcal{F}

avoids intersections of size
0
. Erdős, Ko and Rado also determined for
n>n_{0}(k,t)
the largest
t
-intersecting families in
{[n]\choose k}
, i.e., families
\mathcal{F}
with the condition
|A\cap B|\geq t
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
. They also posed several questions in that regard. Answering one of those, Katona
[
22
]
determined the size of the largest
t
-intersecting subfamily of
2^{[n]},
for all
n,t.
The uniform case of the problem, however, turned out to be much harder. Frankl
[
14
]
and Wilson
[
38
]
determined the size of the largest
t
-intersecting subfamily of
{[n]\choose k}
for

, which is exactly the range when the answer to the question is
{n-t\choose k-t}.
After a partial result of Frankl and Füredi
[
17
]
, Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
2
]
confirmed the conjecture of Frankl
[
14
]
and found the exact solution to the

-intersecting problem for all

Erdős and Sós
[
12
]
suggested a variant with a weaker condition: what is the largest family
\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
that
avoids intersection
t
, i.e. such that
|A\cap B|\neq t
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
? More generally, Deza, Erdős and Frankl
[
7
]
studied
(n,k,L)
-systems, i.e., families
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
such that for any two sets

we have
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|\in L.
They introduced the Delta-system method, which was later developed largely in connection with the study of
(n,k,L)
-systems. We refer to a recent survey of the first author
[
27
]
on the Delta-system method.
We should also mention the results of Ray-Chaudhuri and Wilson
[
5
]
, as well as the result of Frankl and Wilson
[
19
]
, which give bounds for the sizes of
(n,k,L)
-systems via the linear-algebraic method.

Returning to the Erdős–Sós problem, the answer to this question is not known in general. One of the achievements of the Delta-system method was an almost complete resolution of this question for
n>n_{0}(k)
by Frankl and Furedi
[
16
]
. Their methods play a very important role in this paper. It would be convenient for us to formulate their result in graph terms. Let
G(n,k,t)
be the Generalized Johnson graph with
V(G(n,k,t))=\binom{[n]}{k}
and

E(G(n,k,t))=\left\{\{F_{1},F_{2}\}:|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=t,F_{1},F_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\right\}.

In these terms, the Erdős–Sós problem asks to determine the independence number
\alpha(G(n,k,t))
of
G(n,k,t).

Theorem 1

(Frankl and Füredi,
[
16
]
)
.

(i) Fix positive integers
k,t
with
k>2t+1
. There exists
n_{0}
such that for
n>n_{0}

\alpha(G(n,k,t))=\binom{n-t-1}{k-t-1}\sim\cfrac{n^{k-t-1}}{(k-t-1)!}.

(ii) Fix positive integers
k,t
with
k\leqslant 2t+1
. Then

\alpha(G(n,k,t))=\Theta(n^{t}).

Theorem
1
has two regimes. The extremal example in the first case is the same as in the Frankl
[
14
]
and Wilson
[
38
]
result: the family of all sets containing some fixed
t+1
elements. That is, the extremal family is
(t+1)
-intersecting. In the second regime, the example is supposedly design-like (or Steiner system-like). E.g., the largest family of
k
-sets with pairwise intersections at most
t-1
has size
\Omega(n^{t})
and avoids intersection
t
. The actual supposedly extremal example is a bit more complicated, but is still based on a Steiner system. Frankl and Füredi in
[
16
]
verified that this example is indeed extremal in some cases via linear algebra.

More recently, new methods were introduced to the study of the Erdős–Sós problem, including the junta method, hypercontractivity and spread approximations. See the recent papers by Keller and Lifshitz
[
25
]
, Ellis, Keller and Lifshitz
[
10
]
, Kupavskii and Zakharov
[

]
, Kupavskii and Noskov
[

]
. All of them address the first regime, resolving the problem under different dependencies between the parameters

.

A popular theme in extremal combinatorics are supersaturation questions, which could be vaguely formulated as follows: once the object under study is past the extremal threshold, how many copies of the forbidden structure would it contain? One notable example is the asymptotically sharp supersaturation result of Razborov
[
32
]
, then upgraded to an exact result by Liu, Pikhurko, and Staden
[
30
]
, which gives the minimum number of triangles in an
n
-vertex graph with a fixed number of edges.

This paper is devoted to supersaturation for the Erdős–Sós problem, which may be conveniently formulated in terms of the Generalized Johnson graph:

for a fixed number
\ell
, what is the minimum number
\rho(\ell)
of edges in an induced subgraph of
G(n,k,t)
on
\ell
vertices?

Note that, by definition,
\rho(\ell)=0
for
\ell\leq\alpha(G(n,k,t)).
We may formulate the question directly in terms of set families. For a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
, let
\rho(\mathcal{F})
be the number of pairs
F_{1},F_{2}
such that
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=t.
Then

\rho(\ell)=\min_{|\mathcal{F}|=\ell,\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}}\rho(\mathcal{F}).

One case of this problem that is particularly well-studied is supersaturation in Kneser graphs, i.e., graphs
G(n,k,0),
as well as its analogue for
2^{[n]}.
The Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem states that, for
n\geq 2k
,
\alpha(G(n,k,0))={n-1\choose k-1}.
Several groups of researchers showed that lexicographic families have the smallest or asymptotically smallest number of disjoint pairs in different regimes of the parameters (the three parameters being
n,k,s
, where
s\sim|\mathcal{F}|/{n-1\choose k-1}
): see Poljak and Tuza
[
36
]
, Das, Gan and Sudakov
[
6
]
, Frankl, Kohayakawa and Rödl
[
18
]
, Balogh, Das Liu, Sharifzadeh, and Tran
[
3
]
. The question itself was posed by Ahlswede
[
1
]
. The non-uniform variant of this question was independently resolved by Frankl
[
15
]
and by Ahlswede
[
1
]
. Bollobás and Leader
[
4
]
reproved the result of
[
15
,
1
]
and also proved an exact result concerning
t
-disjoint pairs (pairs with intersection
<t
), albeit for sizes of the form
\sum_{i=0}^{k}{n\choose i}
only. An example in the paper by Frankl
[
15
]
shows that the answer to the question for arbitrary sizes can be more complicated than what one may expect. Supersaturation for
t
-disjoint pairs in
{[n]\choose k}
was treated in
[
6
]
and then in
[
18
]
.

Let us briefly comment on the differences between the problem of determining
\rho(\ell)
and its ‘monotone’ counterpart of determining the minimum number of
t
-disjoint pairs for a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
of size
\ell.

Frankl, Kohayakawa and Rödl
[
18
]
found an elegant and rather simple way of getting approximate results on supersaturation for disjoint pairs or
t
-disjoint pairs in
{[n]\choose k}
(i.e., edges in induced subgraphs of the graph
G(n,k,<t)
) for moderately large
n
. Let us treat the case of disjoint pairs and a family
\mathcal{F}
of size
s{n-1\choose k-1}
. Take a random perfect matching
\mathcal{M}
in
{[n]\choose k}
. Its expected intersection with
|\mathcal{F}|
has size
s
, and thus gives rise to at least
s^{2}/2
disjoint pairs on average (here, we are using Jensen’s inequality for the function
{x\choose 2}
). At the same time, the probability that an edge ‘survives’ when intersected with a random matching is the probability that both endpoints survive, which is roughly
|\mathcal{M}|^{2}/{n\choose k}^{2}={n-1\choose k-1}^{-2}.
By linearity of expectation, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}
induces at least
\frac{s^{2}}{2}{n-1\choose k-1}^{2}
edges. For integer
s
and
n\gg k^{2},
this is the correct asymptotics. A similar argument, but with a Steiner system replacing a perfect matching works for
t
-disjoint pairs. There are also other tools available for Kneser graphs and graphs
G(n,k,<t)
, such as Hoffman/expander mixing-type bounds. They also give asymptotically tight bounds on the number of edges in vertex sets beyond the independence number.

The situation for pairs with intersection exactly
t
, i.e., for graphs
G(n,k,t)
is much more complicated, in particular because neither Steiner system-type nor spectral-type argument is available. The behavior of the function
\rho(\ell)
is also very different. For
t
-disjoint pairs and families of size
\gg{n-t\choose k-t}

most
pairs were
t
-disjoint, but this is far from being the case for the pairs with intersection exactly
t
(and families of size
\gg{n-t-1\choose k-t-1}
). Lastly, there is of course the difficulty that we do not even know the independence number of
G(n,k,t)
in general. Indeed, some previous works (e.g.,
[
33
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
35
]
) treated the problem of determining
\rho(\ell),
but could not determine the correct order of magnitude in general. For the most part, these works studied the case
k=3,t=1
. Even in this specific setting, the exact multiplicative constant for
\ell=\Theta(n^{2})
remained unknown. For any constant
k,t
and
n\to\infty
the correct order of magnitude was determined for the case
\ell\gg n^{k-2}

[
34
]
. In the paper
[
9
]
, the authors studied a related problem of the minimum number of induced cliques in subgraphs of
G(n,k,t)
.

In this paper, we managed to determine the order of magnitude of the function
\rho(\ell)
for almost any regime, provided
k,t
are fixed and
n\to\infty.
In some cases, we even determined the exact value of
\rho(\ell).

1.1
Results

The main results of this paper are the lower bounds on
\rho(\ell)
. We, however, start with the upper bounds in order to put the lower bounds into context.

The following easy upper bound follows from averaging: it gives the average number of edges induced on a random subset of vertices of
G(n,k,t)
.

Claim 2
.

For each
\ell>\alpha(G(n,k,t))
we have

\rho(\ell)\leqslant(1+o(1))\cdot\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}}\cdot\frac{t!}{2}\cdot\binom{k}{t}^{2}.

Proof.

For convenience, denote
G=G(n,k,t)
and
V=V(G)
,
E=E(G)
. Note that
|V|=\binom{n}{k}
and

|E|=\frac{|V|\binom{k}{t}\binom{n-k}{k-t}}{2}=(1+o(1))\frac{|V|^{2}\binom{k}{t}\prod_{i=0}^{k-t+1}(k-i)}{2n^{t}}=(1+o(1))\frac{|V|^{2}\binom{k}{t}^{2}}{2n^{t}t!}.

Let us choose a uniformly random induced subgraph
H
of
G(n,k,t)
on
\ell
vertices. The probability of any given edge to fall into
H
is
{|V|-2\choose\ell-2}/{|V|\choose\ell}
. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of edges in
H
is thus equal to

|E|\frac{\binom{|V|-2}{l-2}}{\binom{|V|}{\ell}}=|E|\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{|V|(|V|-1)}=(1+o(1))\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}}\cdot\frac{t!}{2}\cdot\binom{k}{t}^{2}.

Take any subgraph which induces at most that many edges. The claim follows.
∎

In the case
n^{k-1}=o(\ell)
Claim 2 gives us an asymptotically tight bound, see
[
33
]
. However, in most other regimes we can improve upon this bound. For instance, the following bound was shown in
[
33
]
via an explicit construction.

Claim 3
.

Fix positive integers
k,t
such that
k\geqslant 2t+1
. Let
n^{k-t-1}=o(\ell)
and
\ell=o(n^{k-t})
. Then

\rho(\ell)\leqslant(1+o(1))\cdot\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}}\cdot\frac{t!}{2}\cdot\binom{k-t-1}{t}^{2}.

We now turn to the upper bounds. We know the order of magnitude of
\alpha=\alpha(G(n,k,t))
, and thus we can apply Turan’s Theorem
[
37
]
. It gives us the bound

\rho(\ell)\geqslant\frac{\alpha}{2}\left[\frac{\ell}{\alpha}\right](\left[\frac{\ell}{\alpha}\right]-1).
It is easy to see that in the case
k\leqslant 2t+1
the lower bound on
\rho(\ell)
from Turan’s Theorem is of the form
\Theta(\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}})
, which matches the upper bounds from Claims
2
and
3
up to a constant factor. In the case
k>2t+1
, however, the exponent of
n
does not match.

In Section
3
, we prove the following result.

Theorem 4
.

Fix positive integers
k
,
t
such that
k\geqslant 2t+1
and a function
\ell(n)
satisfying
n^{k-t-1}=o(\ell)
. Then

\rho(\ell)\geqslant(1+o(1))\cdot\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}}\cdot\frac{t!}{2}.

Theorem
4
gives us the correct order of magnitude. Moreover, in the case
k=2t+1
it asymptotically matches the bound in Claim
3
.

Theorem
4
, however, does not cover the case
\ell=\Theta(n^{k-t-1}).
For the next two results, it is convenient for us to parametrize
\ell(n)=\binom{n-t-1}{k-t-1}+r(n),
where
r=r(n)>0
and
r=O(n^{k-t-1})
.

Theorem 5
.

Fix positive integers
k,t
with
k\geqslant 2t+3
and a function
\ell(n)=\binom{n-t-1}{k-t-1}+r(n)
such that
r=r(n)
is a positive integer-valued function.

(i)

If
r=o(n^{k-t-1})
, then

\rho(\ell)=\Theta(rn^{k-2t-1}).

(ii)

If
r=\Theta(n^{k-t-1})
, then

\rho(\ell)=\Theta(n^{2k-3t-2}).

Speaking of the supersaturation phenomenon, we see that in Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
(i), we have
\rho(\ell)=\Theta(\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}})
, but in the case
r=o(\alpha(G(n,k,t)))
of Theorem
5
(ii) we observe that
\rho(\ell)=o(\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}})
. That is, the minimum number of edges in a subgraph of
G(n,k,t)
of size
\ell
and the average number of edges in a random subgraph of the same size are the same up to a constant factor iff
\ell>(1+\epsilon)\alpha(G(n,k,t)).

The final theorem of this section determines the exact minimum number of edges for small
r(n)
.

Theorem 6
.

Fix positive integers
k,t
with
k\geqslant 2t+3
and a function
\ell(n)=\binom{n-t-1}{k-t-1}+r(n)
such that
r=r(n)
is a positive integer-valued function. If
r=o(n^{k-2t-1})
, then there exists
n_{0}
such that for
n>n_{0}

\rho(\ell)=r\binom{k}{t}\binom{n-k-t-1}{k-2t-1}.

We organize the rest of the paper as follows. In the next section, we introduce the notation, several important tools for the proofs and sketchs of the proofs. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
4
. In Subsections
4.1
and
4.2
, we prove the lower bound in Theorem
5
(i) and give two different extremal configurations for the upper bound. In Section
4.3
, we adapt the proof of Theorem
5
(i) to derive the lower bound in Theorem
5
(ii). In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
6
and introduce one more construction that demonstrates the optimality of the bound on
r(n)
in Theorem
6
.

2
Preliminaries

We start with several classical definitions.

A family
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{s}\}
is a
sunflower
with
s
petals and kernel
D
if
F_{i}\cap F_{j}=D
holds for any distinct
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
from
\mathcal{F}
. Sunflowers are also called
\Delta
-systems
; therefore, it can be said that we will employ the
\Delta
-system method in our proofs.

A family
\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
is
k
-partite
if there exists a partition
[n]=X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup X_{k}
such that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
i\in[k]
we have
\left|F\cap X_{i}\right|=1
. Further, for each set
B\in\mathcal{F}
define the trace
\left.\mathcal{F}\right|_{B}:=\{A\cap B:A\in

\mathcal{F},A\neq B\}
. We exclude
B
itself from the trace for convenience. If
\mathcal{F}
is
k
-partite with parts
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
, then each set in
\left.\mathcal{F}\right|_{B}
intersects each part
X_{i}
in at most 1 element. The following notion encodes, which parts are intersected. Define the projection
\pi:2^{[n]}\rightarrow[k]
as follows:
\pi(Y)=\left\{i\in[k]:Y\cap X_{i}\neq\emptyset\right\}
. We can naturally extend this definition to
\pi\left(\left.\mathcal{F}\right|_{B}\right)\subset 2^{[k]}
. Define
intersection structure

\operatorname{Int}(\mathcal{F})
as follows:

\operatorname{Int}(\mathcal{F})=\{\pi(E\cap F):E,F\in\mathcal{F},E\neq F\}.

Also, define

F_{J}:=F\cap\left(\bigcup_{i\in J}X_{i}\right),

where
J\subset[k].

In what follows, we state Füredi’s Structural Theorem
[
20
]
, a key ingredient of the
\Delta
-system method.

Theorem 7
.

(Füredi). Fix some positive integers
s,k
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of
k
-element sets. Then there exists
c(k,s)>0
and a
k

\mathcal{F}^{*}\subset\mathcal{F}
such that

(1)
\left|\mathcal{F}^{*}\right|\geqslant c(k,s)|\mathcal{F}|
;

(2)
\pi\left(\left.\mathcal{F}^{*}\right|_{A}\right)=\pi\left(\left.\mathcal{F}^{*}\right|_{B}\right)
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
;

(3) for each
A,B\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
their intersection
A\cap B
is a kernel of a sunflower with
s

\mathcal{F}^{*}
.

We will also use an alternative, more quantitative version of this theorem due to Jiang and Longbrake
[

.

For a family
\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(X)=\{F\setminus X:F\in\mathcal{F},X\subset F\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[X]=\{F:F\in\mathcal{F},X\subset F\}.

Given a positive real number

\forall v\in[n],d_{\mathcal{H}}(v)\leqslant(1/s)|\mathcal{H}|,

.

Theorem 8
.

Fix some integers
s,k\geqslant 2
such that
s\geqslant 2k
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of
k
-element sets. Then there exists
c(k,s)>0
and a
k
-partite family
\mathcal{F}^{*}\subset\mathcal{F}

(1)
\left|\mathcal{F}^{*}\right|\geqslant c(k,s)|\mathcal{F}|

(2) For each

A key property of

.

Another ingredient, we need is the famous Kruskal-Katona Theorem
[
26
]
,
[
23
]
. For an integer

.

(
[

]

Fix some integers

Theorem 10
.

(
[
16
]

Fix some integers

Suppose

2.1
Sketch of the proofs

We begin the proof of Theorem
4
by an iterative application of Theorem
7
in the spirit of the approach of Frankl and Füredi
[
16
]
. This allows us to decompose almost all of our family into several disjoint highly structured subfamilies as in Theorem
7
. These subfamilies will be sufficiently large to guarantee that they are not
t
-avoiding. The conditions of Theorem
7
will imply that all sets within these subfamilies have intersections of size
t
. Each intersection is, in fact, a center of a sunflower. Using this fact, we are able to extract a small number of sets that correspond to an almost-dominating set within our graph

G(n,k,t)
. We then remove this set and repeat the entire procedure, including the decomposition step. This resembles one of the proofs of Turán’s Theorem, in which we iteratively remove small dominating sets in the graph to obtain a lower bound on the number of edges.

The proofs of Theorems
5
and
6
have a common proof strategy. First, we prove a harder Theorem
5
(i), and then modify/simplify its proof to obtain Theorem
5
(ii) and Theorem
6
. The approach combines decomposition, bootstrapping and peeling arguments in the spirit of Frankl and Füredi
[
16
]
. We were also inspired by the paper of Jiang and Longbrake
[
21
]
who used the Delta-system method for a supersaturation question.
Let us outline the proof of Theorem
5
(i) step by step.

1)

Using Theorem
8
iteratively, decompose almost all the original family into disjoint regularized subfamilies. Applying Claim
11
and Lemma
9
, we will show that the intersection structure of these subfamilies must necessarily satisfy property (a) or property (b) of Lemma
9
.

2)

Assume that one of these subfamilies is not
t
-avoiding, i.e., if the corresponding intersection structure satisfies property (a). We then find enough intersections of size
t
inside that family. In what follows, we assume that all intersection structures of our subfamilies satisfy property (b).

3)

As in
[
16
]
and
[
21
]
, by counting
t
-shadows, we can find a small number of
(t+1)
-stars, which cover a bulk of our family. Recall that family
\mathcal{F}
is an
s
-star if there exists a set
S
such that
|S|=s
and
\forall F\in\mathcal{F}:S\subset F
.

4)

Next, we apply a certain peeling/cleaning procedure to the obtained
(t+1)
-stars. This will allow us to state that, in the resulting family, almost each common
t
-shadow of the
(t+1)
-star families yields a sufficient number of
t
-intersections. Via the Kruskal–Katona theorem, this either leads to sufficiently many
t
-intersections, or to the conclusion that one of the
(t+1)
-stars forms a bulk of our family.

5)

We arrive at a situation when one
(t+1)
-star
\mathcal{A}
contains most of the sets, and we have a remainder family
\mathcal{B}
. We find sufficiently many edges either between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, or inside the family
\mathcal{B}
itself. Interestingly, both scenarios in some regimes have a corresponding example with the correct order of the number of edges. We give those in Section

4.2
Two extremal constructions

We want to highlight that there are at least two different types of constructions with
\Theta(rn^{k-t-1})
edges. This is one of the reason for the complications in the previous section. Put
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{F:F\in\binom{[n]}{k},[t+1]\subset F\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{F:F\in\binom{[n]}{k},[t+2,2t+2]\subset F,[t+1]\cap F=\emptyset\}
. We say that a family of sets is a
s
-star
, if all sets in this family contain common
s
elements (the set of these elements is called the center). Thus,
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are
(t+1)
-stars with disjoint centers. The most natural construction with few edges consists of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and an arbitrary subfamily of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
with
r
sets. In this case, the corresponding subgraph contains exactly
r\cdot\binom{k}{t}\binom{n-k-t-1}{k-2t-1}
edges. This example corresponds to the case in our proof, where we found the required number of edges between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Clearly, we can extend this example to
d=\Theta(n^{k-t-1})
. Simply take a constant number of entire
(t+1)
-stars with disjoint centers and a part of another one such
(t+1)
-star.

Now let us describe another type of construction. The family
\mathcal{A}_{1}
will form a bulk of this construction as well. Initially, find the smallest positive integer
x=x(n)
such that

\displaystyle\binom{x}{k}\geqslant r+\binom{x}{t}\binom{n}{k-2t-1}.

(6)

Consider the families

\mathcal{A}_{3}=\binom{[t+2,t+1+x]}{k},

\mathcal{A}_{4}=\{F:F\in\mathcal{G}_{1},|F\cap[t+2,t+1+x]|<t\}.

Clearly,
|\mathcal{A}_{3}|=\binom{x}{k}
and
|\mathcal{A}_{4}|\geqslant\binom{n-t-1}{k-t-1}-\binom{x}{t}\binom{n}{k-2t-1}
. It is easy to see that
\rho(\mathcal{A}_{3})=\frac{1}{2}\binom{x}{k}\binom{k}{t}\binom{x-k}{k-t}
. Construct a family that contains
\mathcal{A}_{4}
, as well as arbitrary
\ell-|\mathcal{A}_{4}|
sets from
\mathcal{A}_{3}
. Thus, all induced edges are inside
\mathcal{A}_{3}
. Therefore, the number of edges in our subgraph does not exceed
\frac{1}{2}\binom{x}{k}\binom{k}{t}\binom{x-k}{k-t}
. Suppose that
r\sim n^{\frac{k(k-2t-1)}{k-t}}
. We have
r=o(n^{k-t-1})
, since
k(k-2t-1)<(k-t)(k-t-1)
. In this case functions, the
\binom{x}{k},r
and
\binom{x}{t}\binom{n}{k-2t-1}
all have the same order of growth, see (6). We obtain the required magnitude of the number of edges, since
\binom{x}{k}\binom{x-k}{k-t}=\Theta(rn^{k-2t-1})
. This example corresponds to the case of our proof, when we found many edges inside
\mathcal{B}
.

4.3
Case

We now adapt the proof from Section
4.1
to the case of
r=\Theta(n^{k-t-1})
by modifying the relevant parameters.

Take a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that
\ell\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\binom{n-t-1}{k-t-1}.
The first step of the proof is applying Theorem
8
. We stop the procedure that finds the families
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, when the number of remaining sets is less than
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\binom{n}{k-t-1}
. In this case, we get

|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\geqslant\frac{c(k)\varepsilon}{2}\binom{n}{k-t-1}
for each

. Therefore, we can upper bound

by some constant. If some
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is of type 1, then by Claim
12
we have
\Omega(\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{t}})
edges inside
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Thus, we again have families of type 2 only.

The next essential difference is the choice of
h
for Claim
14

\sum_{i=1}^{h}|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\geqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\binom{n}{k-t-1}.

In this way we can upper bound
h
by a sufficiently large constant.

For Claim
15
we can put
\tau(n)=\ln n
, which is enough to fulfill the conditions. We get a constant number of families

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{G}_{2}^{\prime}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\mathcal{G}_{h}^{\prime},

satisfying the conditions (2)-(4) of Claim
15
and such that
\sum_{i=1}^{h}|\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}|\geqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\binom{n}{k-t-1}-o(n^{k-t-1})
. As in previous proof, we have

\displaystyle\rho(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{G}_{j}^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{\tau-k}{\tau}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset[n]\setminus W,\\
|X|=t\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}(X)||\mathcal{G}_{j}^{\prime}(X)|.

(7)

From this point we can deviate from the previous proof and find a sufficient number of edges by double counting.

If we combine the bound (7) for all
i\neq j
and
i,j\in[h]
, then we obtain

\rho(\mathcal{F})\geqslant\frac{\tau-k}{2\tau}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset[n]\setminus W,\\
|X|=t\end{subarray}}\left(\left(\sum_{i\in[h]}|\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}(X)|\right)^{2}-\sum_{i\in[h]}|\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}(X)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau-k}{2\tau}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset[n]\setminus W,\\
|X|=t\end{subarray}}\left(|\mathcal{G}(X)|^{2}-\sum_{i\in[h]}|\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}(X)|^{2}\right).

\mathcal{G}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset[n]\setminus W,\\
|X|=t\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{G}(X)|=\binom{k-t-1}{t}|\mathcal{G}|

holds.

Therefore, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset[n]\setminus W,\\

Note that for all

Now we combine these bounds and get

\geqslant\cfrac{\left(\binom{k-t-1}{t}|\mathcal{G}|\right)^{2}}{\binom{n}{t}}-\binom{n}{k-2t-1}\binom{k-t-1}{t}|\mathcal{G}|

One more construction
