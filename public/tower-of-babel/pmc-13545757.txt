Antimicrobial Surfaces for Reusable Food Packaging: A Comprehensive Review of Functionality and Suitability
Authors: Ronan Farrell, Romina Pezzoli, Declan M Devine, Yvonne J Cortese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWith the recent introduction of the European Union's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) in February 2025, reusable plastic packaging schemes have gained increasing attention as a more circular and sustainable alternative to single‐use plastics (SUPs). However, a major barrier to the widescale adoption of these schemes lies in ensuring effective sanitization and disinfection after each reuse cycle. The risk of cross‐contamination between use cycles presents the hazard of foodborne illness, which could undermine consumer trust and result in nonengagement with such systems. Antimicrobial surfaces represent a promising solution for enhancing the microbial safety and improving the cleanability of reusable packaging. These surfaces are designed to inhibit the growth and survival of microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi that contribute to food spoilage and foodborne illness. Their integration into packaging systems could potentially improve consumer confidence and accelerate adoption by facilitating easier sanitization and reducing the risk of microbial cross‐contamination. A range of strategies exists for fabricating antimicrobial surfaces, broadly categorized into chemical and physical approaches, each with distinct advantages and limitations in the context of food packaging. This review critically examines the functionality of these surface types and evaluates their potential for reusable food packaging applications.Keywords: antifouling, antimicrobial surfaces, bactericidal, reusable food packaging, surface topography

Introduction

Plastic materials have become synonymous with the packaging industry, offering a well‐rounded balance of material properties, durability, and relatively low cost while remaining lightweight compared to traditional packaging materials such as glass, metal, and paper (Farrell et al. 2024; Yuen et al. 2025). Accounting for 40% of virgin polymer consumption in Europe, plastic packaging plays a key role in protecting and preserving the quality and safety of produce as it moves through global supply chains (Coelho et al. 2020; Farrell et al. 2025). Unfortunately, the majority of plastic packaging is used in single‐use applications, resulting in vast quantities of single‐use plastic (SUP) being produced annually (Marangoni et al. 2022). In 2022, 18.5 million tonnes of post‐consumer packaging waste were collected in Europe, of which approximately 38% was recycled, 45% sent for energy recovery, and 17% discarded in landfills (Plastics Europe 2024). If this trend in plastic waste generation continues, the EU is projected to see an additional 19% increase in packaging waste by 2030, with plastics being a major contributor (Accorsi et al. 2025).

To address the escalating plastic waste crisis, the European Union has introduced a series of regulations over the last decade aimed at reducing the use of SUPs. These include the European Commission's Circular Economy Action Plan (2015) and Strategy for Plastics (2018) (European Commission 2015, 2018). More recently, in February 2025, the new Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) came into effect, replacing the existing EU Packaging Directive (94/62/EC) (Farrell et al. 2024; European Commission 2025). This regulation places numerous obligations on producers, manufacturers, and importers placing packaged products on the EU market. It establishes progressive waste reduction targets of 5% by 2030, 10% by 2035, and 15% by 2040, while also restricting the use of certain SUPs for on‐the‐spot consumption applications in the HORECA (hotel, restaurant, and catering) sector (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024; European Commission 2022; Saldaña‐Pierard et al. 2025).

In the United States, legislation aimed at tackling plastic packaging waste is primarily implemented at the state level. Currently, seven states—California, Colorado, Oregon, Maine, Maryland, Minnesota, and Washington—have enacted extended producer responsibility (EPR) laws, under which producers are responsible for managing the end‐of‐life impacts of their products. These laws also establish specific targets (Thapliyal et al. 2024). For example, California's Senate Bill 54 (SB 54) mandates that beverage bottles contain at least 25% recycled plastic by 2025, with this requirement increasing to 50% by 2030 (Thapliyal et al. 2024; California State Senate 2022).

In Asia, China has also taken significant action to reduce plastic waste by implementing bans on SUP bags in certain regions and progressing toward a nationwide ban on nondegradable plastic bags, straws, and cutlery (Thapliyal et al. 2024). Consequently, businesses of all sizes across the globe are redesigning their business models to transition from SUPs to circular, reusable packaging systems. However, significant challenges and barriers remain to the widespread adoption of reusable packaging (Woolley et al. 2024; Licciardello 2024).

In the transition to reusable packaging systems, several challenges and barriers to widespread adoption have been identified. According to Woolley et al. (2024), one critical technical barrier is the ability to effectively clean and assess the cleanliness of reusable food packaging. They emphasize that the food industry has both a legal and moral obligation to ensure that food reaches the consumer in a safe and contaminant‐free condition, while consumers themselves regard hygiene as the greatest concern regarding the potential implementation of reusable systems (Woolley et al. 2024). The risk of cross‐contamination between use cycles presents the hazard of foodborne illness, which could undermine consumer trust and result in nonengagement with such systems (Woolley et al. 2024; Nahar et al. 2022). A scientific report by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) (2023) revealed that in 2022, a total of 5763 foodborne outbreaks occurred in the EU, an increase of 44% compared with 2021. These outbreaks resulted in 48,605 cases of illness, 2783 hospitalizations, and 64 deaths. The number of deaths observed in 2022 was the highest reported to EFSA in the past decade. Listeria monocytogenes accounted for approximately 44% of these fatalities, while Salmonella was the most commonly identified pathogen, responsible for 18% of foodborne outbreaks and associated with 51% of hospitalizations. According to Licciardello (2024), plastic surfaces can be particularly susceptible to microbial colonization and the formation of biofilms. Biofilms that develop on moist food‐contact surfaces are often formed by pathogenic or spoilage microorganisms. Microorganisms embedded within biofilms are typically shielded from chemical disinfectants, as these agents struggle to penetrate the biofilm's extracellular polymeric matrix. Consequently, bacteria within biofilms exhibit significantly greater resistance to conventional sanitization methods compared to free‐floating cells (Licciardello 2024). Given the severity of food‐related pathogens, it is essential to implement measures that preserve the microbial safety of reusable packaging throughout its entire lifecycle.

Antimicrobial surfaces represent one potential solution for preserving microbial safety and improving the cleanability of reusable food packaging (H. Wang et al. 2025). These surfaces are designed to inhibit the growth and survival of microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi that contribute to food spoilage (Fadiji et al. 2023). They are already applied in health care, medical devices, food packaging, and high‐touch public spaces to reduce contamination and infection risks (Bento De Carvalho et al. 2024). Several approaches to fabricating antimicrobial surfaces currently exist. One strategy involves modifying a substrate material with an antimicrobial agent that disrupts microbial processes essential for cell growth and division (Fadiji et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2013). Another approach focuses on engineering superhydrophobic or superhydrophilic surfaces, which passively hinder microbial adhesion (Zhang et al. 2013). Although these surfaces show promise in preventing microbial attachment and subsequent biofilm formation, concerns have been raised regarding their fabrication methods, which may limit their suitability for food packaging applications. Key issues include the potential migration of toxic substances, reduced antimicrobial efficacy over prolonged use, incompatibility with recycling separation processes, and contributions to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) (Pedroni et al. 2023; Y. Wang et al. 2024).

Another approach to producing an antimicrobial surface is through the physical alteration of surface topography. Surface topography refers to the micro‐ and nanoscale three‐dimensional features and roughness that define the texture of a material's surface (Munir et al. 2020). It plays a key role in determining how a surface interacts with its environment, influencing properties such as friction, wettability, surface energy, and microbial adhesion (L. Cui et al. 2021). In recent years, numerous studies have investigated how micro‐ and nanoscale surface architectures can be engineered to create unfavorable conditions for microbial attachment and biofilm formation (S. W. Lee et al. 2021). Advances in nanofabrication technologies have also enabled researchers to replicate bio‐inspired designs, such as the superhydrophobic properties of the lotus leaf and the bactericidal activity of cicada wings (Hsieh and Chien 2023). A notable advantage of antimicrobial surfaces produced through topographical modification is that they rely exclusively on physical changes to surface roughness and morphology. This avoids concerns related to the migration of harmful substances, incompatibility with recycling waste streams, and contributions to AMR. The purpose of this article is to review the different strategies for fabricating antimicrobial surfaces, evaluate their suitability for reusable packaging applications, and highlight current knowledge gaps while identifying avenues for further research.

The Morphology of Microorganisms in Food

Microorganisms play a fundamental role in food science. They can be beneficial and convert food sources into value‐added food products such as fermented foods or be a root cause of food spoilage and foodborne illness. The primary microbial groups of concern in the food industry include various species of bacteria, fungi, and viruses (Ray 2004). In reusable food packaging systems, particular concern is associated with microorganisms capable of surviving on food‐contact surfaces between reuse cycles, as these organisms may contribute to cross‐contamination, food spoilage, and foodborne illness if cleaning and sanitization procedures are insufficient (Woolley et al. 2024; Farrell et al. 2026). Although other microorganisms, such as algae, protozoa, and nematodes (worms), can pose health risks, they are beyond the scope of this review.

Microorganisms in food systems can generally be classified into three categories: spoilage, pathogenic, and beneficial (National Research Council [US] 1985). Spoilage microorganisms grow in food, causing undesirable changes in flavor, texture, color, and overall quality (National Research Council [US] 1985). Pathogenic microorganisms, on the other hand, compromise food safety either by serving as transport for infectious agents that colonize the gastrointestinal tract, such as Salmonella enterica and Shigella dysenteriae, or by producing toxins in food before consumption, as seen with Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus (National Research Council [US] 1985). In contrast, beneficial microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria and certain yeasts, are important in food fermentation, while other yeast species such as Candida spp. can also contribute to food spoilage or opportunistic infections (National Research Council [US] 1985; de Melo Pereira et al. 2022). Given the many differences in morphology and structural characteristics among food‐associated microorganisms, their interactions with surfaces may vary significantly. Before discussing the design and functionality of different antimicrobial surfaces for food packaging applications, it is first beneficial to understand how different microbes interact with surfaces.

Bacteria

Bacteria are prokaryotic microorganisms, characterized by their single‐cell structure that lacks a nucleus and other membrane‐bound organelles (Doron and Gorbach 2008). They typically range in size from 1 to 3 µm and exhibit different morphologies, including spherical (cocci), rod‐shaped (bacilli), and spiral‐shaped (spirilla) (Mahanta et al. 2021; Cabeen and Jacobs‐Wagner 2005; Gnarpe 2012). Based on differences in cell wall composition, bacteria are broadly classified as either Gram‐positive or Gram‐negative. Gram‐positive bacteria, such as S. aureus, possess a cytoplasmic membrane surrounded by a thick peptidoglycan layer, as illustrated in Figure 1a (Mahanta et al. 2021; Bitrus et al. 2018).

In contrast, Gram‐negative bacteria, such as Escherichia coli, have a relatively thinner cell wall comprising a cytoplasm surrounded by a thin peptidoglycan layer and an additional lipid membrane (Mahanta et al. 2021; Jang et al. 2017). This distinction is particularly relevant to antimicrobial surface design, as differences in cell wall structure can influence adhesion behavior and susceptibility to both chemically functionalized and topographically modified antimicrobial surfaces (Zheng et al. 2021; Linklater et al. 2017)

Bacteria can be further classified based on their ability to survive and grow in the presence or absence of oxygen (Doron and Gorbach 2008). Aerobic bacteria (aerobes), such as Micrococcus luteus, require oxygen for growth, while anaerobic bacteria (anaerobes) such as Clostridium botulinum cannot grow in oxygen‐rich environments (Doron and Gorbach 2008; Y. Liu, Li, et al. 2024; C.‐H. Huang et al. 2019). Facultative anaerobic bacteria can grow in the presence and absence of oxygen (Doron and Gorbach 2008). For example, E. coli and S. enterica grow optimally with oxygen but can grow without it (Mueller and Tainter 2023; Andino and Hanning 2015). Finally, microaerophilic bacteria such as Campylobacter jejuni grow best under low oxygen conditions (5%–6%) and are inhibited by an oxygen concentration of greater than 10% (Y. Liu, Li, et al. 2024).

Many bacterial species colonize surfaces through a dynamic, multistage process known as “biofilm formation,” although the ability to form biofilms varies between species and environmental conditions (Tolker‐Nielsen 2015). A biofilm is defined as a community of bacteria embedded in a self‐produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, which is composed of polysaccharides, proteins, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), and other components (C. Liu et al. 2020; Coenye 2013; Toyofuku et al. 2016). These structures protect bacteria from a variety of stresses, including host immune system responses, antimicrobials, oxidative damage, and other environmental conditions (Rather et al. 2021). The “biofilm formation” process consists of five distinct stages, as illustrated in Figure 1b. In the first stage, “initial attachment,” planktonic (free‐floating) bacterial cells approach the surface via electrostatic and hydrodynamic interactions (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). Most bacterial cells carry a net negative charge due to ionized carboxyl and phosphate groups in their structure. Electrostatic attraction is strongest when the surface has an opposite charge; however, even when both the bacterial cell and the surface are negatively charged, adhesion can still occur (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). This is because some bacterial species can modify the chemical profile and surface charge density of their microbial envelope to facilitate adherence (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). Extracellular structures such as flagella and pili also further enhance motility and aid in navigating initial contact.

The second stage, “irreversible attachment,” occurs when bacterial cells firmly anchor to the surface through the production of proteins that bind to specific molecules, as well as through the action of their flagella and pili. At this stage, cells also begin secreting EPS. This phase can last for several hours, and once complete, detachment is unlikely and generally irreversible (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023).

Following this, the “early maturation” stage begins, during which adherent cells proliferate via cell division while additional planktonic cells are recruited. Attachment to the surface triggers responses that lead to changes in gene expression and upregulation of factors that maintain adherence (Goel et al. 2021). EPS production intensifies, providing structural stability and protection against environmental stresses (Toyofuku et al. 2016). Within the EPS matrix, signaling molecules responsible for quorum sensing coordinate community behavior and facilitate genetic exchange among cells (Toyofuku et al. 2016; Percival et al. 2015).

During the “late maturation” stage, the biofilm develops into a complex three‐dimensional structure consisting of microcolonies interconnected by water channels that enable nutrient distribution and waste removal (Percival et al. 2015). Genes responsible for motility are downregulated, while those beneficial for biofilm preservation are upregulated. Resistance to antimicrobials and other environmental stresses is also further increased (Rather et al. 2021).

Finally, in the “dispersion” stage, cells detach and are released as planktonic cells from the biofilm (Toyofuku et al. 2016). This process is mediated by enzymatic degradation of the EPS, triggered by factors such as overpopulation, intense competition, nutrient depletion, environmental fluctuations (e.g., temperature, oxygen deficiency, metabolite accumulation), as well as the upregulation of genes involved in motility and EPS degradation and the downregulation of genes responsible for polysaccharide and fimbriae synthesis (Toyofuku et al. 2016; Rather et al. 2021). Dispersion enables recolonization of new surfaces and reinitiation of the biofilm cycle.

The formation of biofilms on reusable food packaging surfaces may be of particular concern because cells embedded in the EPS matrix may exhibit increased resistance to conventional cleaning and sanitization procedures. Consequently, preventing initial bacterial attachment and biofilm development represents a key objective in the design of antimicrobial surfaces for reusable packaging applications.

Fungi

In the early days of microbiology, fungi were initially classified as plants due to shared characteristics, including the presence of a cell wall and a lack of motility (neither can move under its own power). However, today, fungi and plants are considered two different groups because plants obtain nutrients via photosynthesis using chlorophyll, whereas fungi are heterotrophic organisms that acquire nutrients by absorbing organic material from other organisms’ tissue (parasite) and waste (saprophyte) (Walker and McGinnis 2014). Fungi are eukaryotic organisms, meaning their cells contain a clearly defined nucleus. A distinguishing feature of their structure is their rigid cell wall, which is composed of chitin, a polysaccharide also found in the exoskeletons of arthropods. Compared to bacteria, fungal cells are larger, typically ranging from 20 to 100 µm in size (Ray 2004). They can exist as either unicellular yeasts or multicellular molds. Yeast cells are oval, spherical, or elongated in shape and have a cell wall composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids (Ray 2004). They reproduce asexually through a process known as “budding,” in which daughter cells, referred to as blastoconidia, are formed and subsequently separated from the parent cell via mitosis, as shown in Figure 1c (McGinnis and Tyring 1996). In contrast, molds are filamentous and branched cell structures with a cell wall composed of cellulose and chitin. These cells form intricate network structures known as mycelium, which consist of a large number of 2‐ to 10‐µm filaments known as hyphae (Corbu et al. 2023). Reproduction occurs when hyphae extend into the surrounding environment and generate exospores. These exospores can be conidiospores (free) or sporangiospores (enclosed in a protective sack) (Ray 2004).

Fungal biofilm formation is a sequential process involving the adherence of fungal cells to a substrate, proliferation and accretion of cells over the surface, and subsequent hyphal formation. An extracellular matrix (ECM) then accumulates as the biofilm matures, contributing to cell cohesion, acting as a storehouse for cell nutrients, and providing a protective layer for the enclosed cells (Fanning and Mitchell 2012). Fungal contamination is particularly relevant to food packaging applications due to its role in food spoilage, off‐flavor development, and shelf‐life reduction. Consequently, antimicrobial surfaces intended for reusable packaging should ideally demonstrate activity against both bacterial and fungal contaminants.

Viruses

Viruses are classified as obligate intracellular parasites, meaning they are entirely dependent on the environmental conditions of their host cell throughout all stages of infection, replication, and spread (Louten 2016). They are the smallest microorganisms discussed thus far, ranging in size from 20 to 300 nm (Z. Sun and Ostrikov 2020). They can be polyhedral or helical in shape or have more complicated architecture (Louten 2016). Structurally, a virus typically consists of a nucleic acid, such as DNA or ribonucleic acid (RNA), enclosed within a protein shell known as a capsid, as shown in Figure 1d. The capsid serves to protect the nucleic acid from harsh environmental conditions. Many viruses also feature an additional host‐derived lipid bilayer called an envelope, which surrounds the capsid and protects against the host cell's immune response system. Additionally, the envelope contains glycoproteins that mediate the interactions between the virus and the host cell and facilitate fusion (Louten 2016).

A critical factor influencing viral transmission is the ability of a virus to remain viable in a given environment. Depending on the type of virus, it can remain viable on a surface for varying amounts of time, ranging from hours to days (Mahanta et al. 2021). For instance, it has been reported that the coronavirus SARS‐CoV‐2 virus can remain viable on glass surfaces for 4 days and for up to 7 days on smooth stainless‐steel and plastic surfaces (Z. Sun and Ostrikov 2020). Viruses can also persist within food, posing a risk for the transmission of foodborne diseases such as hepatitis A (HAV) and Norwalk‐like viruses (Ray 2004). These viruses are classified as enteric, meaning they are transmitted via the fecal–oral route and have been responsible for foodborne illness outbreaks in the past (Ray 2004; McClure et al. 2022). Regarding the ability of viruses to form biofilms, this concept is still under extensive research. Viruses such as human T‐cell leukemia (HTLV‐1) have been shown to encase themselves in viral assemblies of similar structure and composition to bacterial biofilms (Thoulouze and Alcover 2011). However, the main difference between a bacterial biofilm and viral assembly is that for bacteria, the ECM is constructed by the bacterial cells themselves. In contrast, for viruses, assemblies are formed by the host cell infected by the virus (Mahanta et al. 2021). Although viral contamination is less frequently considered in antimicrobial packaging studies than bacterial contamination, the ability of food‐contact surfaces to act as reservoirs for enteric viruses highlights the importance of surface hygiene in reusable packaging systems. The persistence of viral particles on polymer surfaces further reinforces the need for effective cleaning and sanitization strategies.

Collectively, the microorganisms discussed in this section present different challenges for reusable food packaging systems due to differences in morphology, cell envelope structure, adhesion mechanisms, and biofilm‐forming behavior. These characteristics influence how microorganisms interact with packaging surfaces and determine the effectiveness of different antimicrobial strategies. Therefore, understanding these microbial characteristics is essential when designing antimicrobial surfaces intended to minimize contamination, facilitate cleaning, and improve the microbial safety of reusable packaging.

Surface Modification Strategies for Antimicrobial Reusable Food Packaging

The ability of a microorganism to adhere to a surface and proliferate is largely governed by the physical and chemical interactions between the microbe's outer membrane and the surface. The strength of these interactions is governed by various factors such as surface charge and wettability. Antimicrobial surfaces, which are designed to inhibit the attachment, growth, and survival of microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi, are typically fabricated through chemical or physical modifications (Mahanta et al. 2021; Tiller 2010). Chemical modification strategies often involve the application of low‐surface‐energy coatings to enhance hydrophobicity or the incorporation of antimicrobial agents such as antibiotics or nanoparticles. In contrast, physical approaches manipulate surface topography to disrupt microbial adhesion or actively eliminate cells upon contact (Mahanta et al. 2021; Tiller 2010).

The classification of antimicrobial surfaces varies across the literature. For instance, Tiller (2010) broadly categorizes them into two primary types based on their mechanism of action: microbe‐repelling surfaces, which prevent adhesion, and microbe‐killing surfaces, which eliminate cells upon contact. Similarly, Nagaraja et al. (2020) and Sjollema et al. (2018) propose more detailed classifications that include antibacterial agent‐releasing surfaces, contact‐killing surfaces, and antiadhesion or bacteria‐repelling surfaces. Alternatively, Mahanta et al. (2021) categorize antimicrobial surfaces based on the nature of the surface modification, emphasizing whether the modification imparts microbe‐repelling or microbe‐killing properties, as shown in Figure 2.

The classification of antimicrobial surfaces based on their mechanism of action and surface modification strategy.

While all surface types discussed in this review exhibit antimicrobial functionality, they differ significantly in their primary mechanism of action, fabrication approach, durability, and suitability for reusable food packaging applications. Functionalized surfaces rely on the incorporation of antimicrobial agents, superwettable surfaces inhibit microbial attachment through manipulation of surface wettability, smart surfaces respond dynamically to environmental stimuli, and patterned surfaces utilize micro‐ and nanoscale topographies to influence microbial adhesion or induce cell damage. Therefore, the categorization adopted in this review is based on the underlying surface modification strategy and its associated practical advantages and limitations rather than antimicrobial functionality alone.

Functionalized Surfaces

Functionalized antimicrobial surfaces are manufactured by incorporating or coating materials with agents that actively kill or inhibit microbial growth. These surfaces operate through various mechanisms, dependent upon the chemistry of the incorporated material. For instance, surfaces fused with nanoparticles, such as silver (Ag), release Ag ions that can bind to and disrupt bacterial cell membranes, interfere with essential enzyme functions, and induce DNA damage, resulting in bacterial cell death, as shown in Figure 4a (More et al. 2023). Similarly, surfaces coated with photocatalytic agents like zinc oxide (ZnO) and titanium dioxide (TiO2) produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon exposure to light. These ROS inflict oxidative damage on bacterial cell components, leading to cell death (O'Neill et al. 2023). Furthermore, antimicrobial peptides such as nisin function by forming pores in the bacterial membrane and disrupting cell wall biosynthesis through interactions with lipid II, thereby compromising the structural integrity of the bacterial cell (Prince et al. 2016).

(b) Superhydrophobic antimicrobial surface illustrating reduced bacterial adhesion through limited liquid–surface contact and self‐cleaning behavior. (c) Superhydrophilic antimicrobial surface showing the formation of a stable hydration layer that reduces protein adsorption and bacterial attachment while facilitating cleaning. (d) Stimuli‐responsive (“smart”) antimicrobial surface illustrating a kill‐and‐release mechanism, in which bacteria are inactivated during one surface state and subsequently released following an externally triggered change in surface properties.

In recent years, significant research has been devoted to evaluating various antimicrobial agents incorporated into food packaging, targeting a range of microbial species. These agents include natural substances like essential oils derived from plants (e.g., basil, thyme, oregano, cinnamon, clove), animal‐derived proteins and enzymes (e.g., lysozyme, lactoferrin), antimicrobial peptides (e.g., nisin, natamycin), organic acids, metal and metal oxide nanoparticles (e.g., Ag, ZnO, TiO2), and intrinsically antimicrobial polymers (e.g., chitosan, poly‐ε‐lysine) (Vasile and Baican 2021; Duda‐Chodak et al. 2023).

For instance, González‐Ceballos et al. (2022) developed a fully organic antimicrobial polymer by synthesizing films from acrylic monomers methyl methacrylate (MMA), N‐vinyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (VP), and 4‐aminostyrene (SHN2) and covalently linking them to vanillin, a naturally occurring antimicrobial essential oil. Bacterial assays demonstrated that the films achieved inhibition rates of up to 99% for E. coli, S. aureus, and L. monocytogenes. The antibacterial mechanism was attributed to cell membrane rupture, as confirmed by propidium iodide staining and microscopy, which showed increased fluorescence in damaged cells. In a proof‐of‐concept experiment, pork loins packaged with these films under a modified atmosphere (75% N2 and 25% CO2) and stored at 4°C retained their quality for 15 days, compared with 10 days for pork loins packaged with nonantimicrobial films, representing a 50% increase in shelf life. The films also retained their antimicrobial efficacy after 10 reuse and washing cycles, with only a slight reduction in bacterial inhibition (<0.1%) observed (González‐Ceballos et al. 2022).

Massad‐Ivanir et al. (2023) investigated the antimicrobial behavior of LDPE films loaded with carvacrol and halloysite nanotubes (HNTs), produced via melt compounding and cast film extrusion. These films demonstrated significant antifungal activity, inhibiting Penicillium commune growth by 53%. When tested on bread and fresh cherries under realistic storage conditions (bread: stored at 20°C for 14 and 19 days; cherries: stored at 2°C for 14 days followed by 4 days at 20°C), the films extended the shelf life of bread by 73% compared to commercially available films. Similarly, cherries packaged in the antimicrobial films showed a reduced decay rate and an overall 40% extension in shelf life compared to conventional packaging. The authors concluded that essential oils like carvacrol are effective in prolonging the shelf life of food products (Massad‐Ivanir et al. 2023).

Maresca and Mauriello (2022) developed antimicrobial films by coating polyethylene (PE), PP, and polylactic acid (PLA) films with a solution of nisin and cellulose nanofibers (CNFs). These films demonstrated antimicrobial activity against L. monocytogenes and Brochothrix thermosphacta after 7 days of storage at 4°C. In a practical application, hamburgers contaminated with L. monocytogenes and stored in the nisin–CNF films exhibited a significant reduction in bacterial count during the initial 2 days of storage. Although bacterial populations increased slightly thereafter, the overall microbial load remained substantially lower than in control samples. The study highlighted CNF's potential in enhancing the safety of food packaging by enabling the controlled release of nisin to combat spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms (Maresca and Mauriello 2022). The functionalized antimicrobial surfaces reviewed in this section demonstrate considerable potential in inhibiting the adhesion and growth of various microbial species, thereby contributing to the extension of the shelf life of different food products. However, upon closer examination of their application in reusable food packaging, challenges arise regarding the mechanisms by which these surfaces function and are manufactured, specifically through the incorporation of additives or the application of coatings. These approaches present three significant barriers that potentially hinder the widespread use of such antimicrobial surfaces in reusable packaging systems.

The first barrier to the reuse of these surfaces is consumer health and safety concerns that arise from the potential migration of additives, specifically metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, from the food packaging into the food itself. While nanoparticles such as Ag, TiO2, and ZnO exhibit notable antibacterial properties and can enhance the barrier properties of packaging, thus extending the shelf life of food products, they also pose risks due to their potential to migrate into food (Ashfaq et al. 2022). This has triggered widespread governmental and public scrutiny over the health implications of incorporating nanoparticles in food packaging (Gupta et al. 2024). Consequently, numerous scientific investigations have been conducted in recent years to explore the interactions between nanoparticles and biological systems, aiming to evaluate the associated risks (Gupta et al. 2024).

Several studies have suggested that nanoparticles may cause adverse health effects, including oxidative stress, inflammation, genotoxicity, and cellular dysfunction. For example, Sousa et al. (2023) demonstrated that daily voluntary oral administration of 1‐ and 10‐mg/kg doses of polyvinylpyrrolidone‐coated Ag nanoparticles to mice over a 14‐day period resulted in a localized inflammatory response in the intestine and significant vascular and cellular changes in intestinal tissues. Similarly, X. Zhu et al. (2023) observed that oral administration of 1000‐mg/kg doses of different ZnO nanoparticles to Wistar albino rats over 28 days induced mild oxidative stress. C. Yang, Tan, et al. (2022) further reported that administering TiO2 nanoparticles at 100 mg/kg to adult mice over a 13‐week period led to alterations in intestinal microbes. These findings highlight the potential health risks posed by the ingestion of nanoparticles, prompting governments and regulatory bodies to implement policies and regulations governing the use of nanoparticles in food packaging, along with specific migration limits. In the European Union, these regulations include Regulation (EC) No. 1935/2004 and Regulation (EU) No. 10/2011, concerning materials and articles intended to come into contact with food, and No. 450/2009, which addresses active and intelligent materials intended for food contact (Störmer et al. 2017; EU Commission 2011; European Commission 2009; European Parliament 2004). In contrast, while the United States lacks specific regulations on the use of nanoparticles in food‐contact applications, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) does regulate the use of nanomaterials permitted for such purposes (Störmer et al. 2017; Paidari et al. 2021).

According to Fu and Dudley (2021), functionalized antimicrobial surfaces should be designed and optimized to release antimicrobial agents at the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the targeted microorganism. However, this value must also be lower than the legally permissible limit to comply with safety regulations. The extent of nanoparticle migration from packaging into food is influenced by factors such as temperature, pH, and mechanical stress during storage and transportation (Gupta et al. 2024). Reusable packaging materials, which are often subjected to industrial sanitization processes involving elevated temperatures and caustic detergents, may be particularly vulnerable to promoting nanoparticle migration. Moreover, the accelerated release of nanoparticles from the packaging material could reduce the antimicrobial efficacy of the packaging over repeated use cycles, potentially compromising its ability to preserve the quality of its contents. Therefore, it is crucial to thoroughly investigate the migration behavior, toxicity, permissible limits, and interactions of nanoparticles with packaging materials before fabricating any nanocomposite intended for direct food contact (Ashfaq et al. 2022).

One potential strategy for addressing the safety and regulatory concerns associated with functionalized antimicrobial surfaces is the use of natural antimicrobial agents (NAMAs), including several compounds classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the FDA, some of which have been investigated for edible food packaging applications (Bangar et al. 2021). Materials such as chitosan, nisin, natamycin, citric acid, essential oils, and other plant‐derived antimicrobial compounds have demonstrated antimicrobial activity against a broad range of foodborne pathogens and spoilage microorganisms while generally exhibiting low toxicity (Bangar et al. 2021; Angane et al. 2022; Saeed et al. 2019). Furthermore, many of these compounds have been investigated for food‐contact applications and may be used in food packaging systems subject to compliance with applicable food‐contact regulations, including Regulation (EC) No. 1935/2004 and Regulation (EU) No. 10/2011 in the European Union and relevant FDA food‐contact regulations (21 CFR Parts 174–178) in the United States (EU Commission 2011; European Parliament 2004; Angane et al. 2022; US Food and Drug Administration 2026). This regulatory familiarity may facilitate commercial implementation and improve consumer acceptance. However, despite their generally favorable safety profiles, some NAMAs may elicit allergic reactions, while prolonged exposure or consumption at elevated doses has been associated with adverse health effects (Angane et al. 2022). Future research should therefore focus on achieving an optimal balance between antimicrobial efficacy and safety, as well as developing durable NAMA‐based antimicrobial surfaces and evaluating their long‐term performance under realistic reusable packaging conditions.

The second barrier to the reuse of functionalized antimicrobial surfaces relates to recyclability and end‐of‐life considerations. While the incorporation of nanoparticles and the application of surface coatings can effectively enhance antimicrobial properties and meet the stringent performance requirements of food packaging materials, these modifications can also complicate and restrict the recyclability of the base material. Effective recycling of materials like paper and plastics necessitates the removal of additives and coatings from the packaging, a process that poses significant technical challenges (Farrell et al. 2024; Jung et al. 2023). For polymers, in particular, failure to remove these substances can result in reduced material performance due to the promotion of chemical degradation reactions. In addition, these additives can alter the physical properties of polymers, such as their density, potentially disrupting automated sorting processes that rely on density‐based separation. Under the new PPWR, composite packaging materials are defined as those made up of two or more separate materials that cannot be separated manually, where the additive material constitutes more than 5% of the total mass of the packaging item (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024). In line with this, organizations such as RecyClass recommend that any additional substance incorporated into plastic packaging should not exceed 5% of its weight in order to maintain full compatibility with established recycling streams (RecyClass 2025). The removal of additives and coatings from plastic packaging materials requires advanced technological processes, which are not widely available in current recycling facilities (Farrell et al. 2024; Ügdüler et al. 2020). As reported by Ügdüler et al. (2020), only a limited number of pilot‐scale additive extraction processes are commercially available, focused on plastics. Furthermore, Rasilainen et al. (2024) state that the recycling of plastic nanocomposite waste remains confined to laboratory‐scale research, which predominantly utilizes high‐quality plastic waste, such as plastic pellets, that do not accurately reflect real‐world waste streams. Given the lack of necessary infrastructure, industrial recycling efforts are often focused on mono‐materials, which are easier to separate and process (Farrell et al. 2024). For functionalized antimicrobial surfaces in packaging to achieve broader adoption, additional research is required to advance industrial recycling processes capable of handling composite materials. This will involve not only developing new technologies but also scaling existing methods to accommodate the complexities of recycling materials modified with antimicrobial additives.

The final barrier to the implementation of these surfaces in food packaging applications is the contribution of these surfaces toward AMR. AMR is a natural phenomenon in microorganisms that develops due to their intrinsic evolutionary nature (Chakraborty et al. 2022; Balderrama‐González et al. 2021). This resistance reduces the efficacy of antimicrobial treatments and enables pathogenic microorganisms to evade conventional control methods. According to Kamat and Kumari (2023), antimicrobial nanoparticles have been recognized as a new generation of treatments capable of killing bacterial pathogens through multiple mechanisms; however, resistance to nanoparticles acquired by some bacterial pathogens has raised concerns. For instance, Hochvaldová et al. (2024) reported the development of resistance in E. coli and S. aureus to Ag nanoparticles when exposed to sub‐inhibitory concentrations, resulting in increased MIC values for both species over repeated cultivation steps. Both strains developed resistance through nanoparticle aggregation; however, using different mechanisms. Escherichia coli relied on the production of adhesive proteins (flagellin) from its flagella, whereas S. aureus utilized EPS components. In a similar study, Panáček et al. (2018) also reported resistance development in E. coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa to Ag nanoparticles, again mediated by nanoparticle aggregation through flagellin production. According to a systematic analysis of the global burden of AMR in 2019, 1.27 million deaths were directly attributed to AMR‐related infections, while 4.95 million deaths were indirectly linked (Murray et al. 2022). If the current trend of antimicrobial use continues, annual AMR‐related deaths are projected to reach 10 million by 2050 (O'Neill 2016). While AMR is a broader public health concern, the overuse of antimicrobial agents in the food and packaging industries could potentially exacerbate the issue and compromise their effectiveness in critical medical applications. To avoid this, research is needed into alternative approaches for fabricating antimicrobial surfaces for food packaging, focusing on materials with low resistance potential and nonchemical strategies.

Overall, functionalized antimicrobial surfaces offer some of the strongest antimicrobial performance reported in food packaging studies, particularly when metal nanoparticles, essential oils, organic acids, or antimicrobial peptides are incorporated into the packaging matrix. However, their suitability for reusable food packaging remains limited by several interconnected challenges. Reusable‐based systems may lose efficacy over repeated use cycles, while nanoparticle‐containing materials raise concerns regarding migration, toxicity, recyclability, and potential contribution to AMR. Therefore, although functionalized surfaces are promising for short‐term or single‐use food packaging applications, their translation onto reusable systems requires stronger evidence of long‐term antimicrobial stability, controlled release behavior, regulatory compliance, and compatibility with end‐of‐life recycling. Beyond shelf‐life extension, these surfaces may find application in reusable takeaway containers, transport crates, food service packaging, and closed‐loop packaging systems where repeated handling and cleaning increase the risk of microbial contamination. In such applications, antimicrobial functionality could reduce biofilm formation between reuse cycles, improve cleanability, and enhance consumer confidence in reusable packaging schemes. However, the suitability of these surfaces will ultimately depend on their ability to retain antimicrobial performance throughout the service life of the packaging without compromising recyclability, food‐contact safety, or regulatory compliance.

Superwettable Surfaces

The goal of superwettable surfaces is to modify surface wettability in order to prevent the initial attachment of microbes, thus preventing their progression to irreversible adhesion and ECM formation stages of biofilm formation. Surface wettability can be modified through chemical alterations, such as the application of low‐surface‐energy coatings, or through physical modifications, such as the creation of hierarchical micro/nanostructures to adjust surface roughness (Baba et al. 2015). This section focuses on wettability changes induced by chemical modifications, while physical modifications such as engineered surface topography will be discussed later.

In the context of water, wettability refers to whether water spreads across a solid surface (hydrophilic) or remains in droplet form (hydrophobic). This wetting behavior is determined by the adhesive forces between the liquid and the solid surface. As first described by Young in 1805, surface energy is governed by the balance between adhesive and cohesive forces, which dictate whether wetting occurs (Kwok and Neumann 1999; Pham et al. 2020). When a liquid interacts with a solid surface, three interfacial boundaries come into play: solid–vapor (γ SV), solid–liquid (γ SL), and liquid–vapor (γ LV) (T. Wu and Wu 2021). Superhydrophobic Surfaces

Superhydrophobic surfaces are characterized by a WCA ≥150° and a contact angle hysteresis (CAH) ≤10° (Zaman Khan et al. 2022). On these surfaces, water droplets maintain their shape and roll off even at low tilt angles due to low adhesion forces. Unlike functionalized antimicrobial surfaces that actively kill microbes on contact, superhydrophobic surfaces exhibit antimicrobial properties by preventing the adhesion and growth of microorganisms, as shown in Figure 4b.

These surfaces display characteristics such as self‐cleaning, anticorrosion, anti‐biofouling, and drag reduction, and they can be fabricated using a variety of chemical techniques (Zaman Khan et al. 2022). Superhydrophobic surfaces have been shown to be effective against a wide range of microorganisms.

Sahin et al. (2022) developed multifunctional superhydrophobic surfaces that exhibited repellence toward both water and common liquid foods, along with bactericidal effects against E. coli and S. aureus. The surfaces were created by using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) imprints from paper, coated with carnauba wax, and then further modified with Ag nanoparticles via physical vapor deposition. The inclusion of carnauba wax significantly enhanced hydrophobicity, with WCA values ranging from 150° to 170°, while Ag nanoparticles contributed to the antimicrobial properties. The surfaces demonstrated reduced bacterial adhesion, particularly for E. coli, as the hydrophobicity increased. However, no clear correlation between hydrophobicity and S. aureus adhesion was observed, which the authors attributed to the morphology and colony‐forming behavior of S. aureus. The presence of Ag nanoparticles drastically reduced bacterial adhesion for both E. coli and S. aureus, with antimicrobial efficacy reaching approximately 99% and 93%, respectively (Sahin et al. 2022).

In another study, X. Jiang et al. (2022) investigated cellulose‐based degradable paper with superhydrophobic and antibacterial properties for food packaging. The surfaces were prepared by immersing cellulose paper in a polylactic acid (PLA) and cinnamaldehyde (CIN) coating, followed by spraying with stearic acid and silicon dioxide (SiO2) nanoparticles. This combination increased the WCA of the surfaces from 85° to 156° and was attributed to the low surface energy of stearic acid and the enhanced surface roughness caused by the SiO2 nanoparticles. In addition, the surfaces exhibited self‐cleaning capabilities, successfully removing carbon black powder when tilted at 20°. In terms of antimicrobial efficacy, the PLA/CIN coating, combined with the SiO2 nanoparticles, achieved 100% antibacterial efficiency against E. coli and S. aureus. The hydrophobicity of the surfaces was noted to reduce bacterial adhesion and prevent biofilm formation. X. Jiang et al. (2022) concluded that these surfaces hold promise for use in degradable food packaging applications.

Similarly, Yu et al. (2023) developed multifunctional paper‐based food packaging with superhydrophobic properties. The surfaces were produced by immersing paper in a dopamine (DA) solution, followed by a coating of PDMS, stearic acid, and HNTs. Contact angle analysis showed that the WCA values of the surfaces increased substantially, with the highest value of 158° observed when DA, PDMS, stearic acid, and HNTs were combined on the same surface. The authors attributed the superhydrophobic behavior to the low surface energy of PDMS and stearic acid, as well as the micro/nano roughness introduced by DA and HNTs. The surfaces exhibited strong liquid repellence against various food‐based liquids, such as tea, coffee, milk, and honey. Antifouling and self‐cleaning tests further demonstrated the surface's ability to repel water and remove contaminants. Bacterial antiadherence tests revealed significant reductions in E. coli and S. aureus adhesion compared to uncoated control samples, with the superhydrophobic surfaces suppressing bacterial adhesion by over 87%. The authors concluded that the superhydrophobic packaging materials developed offer substantial potential for practical food packaging applications due to their antiadhesion and self‐cleaning properties (Yu et al. 2023). The studies reviewed demonstrate that chemical modification methods can effectively produce superhydrophobic surfaces with anti‐biofouling, self‐cleaning, and antimicrobial properties for food packaging applications. However, similar to functionalized antimicrobial surfaces, there are potential barriers to implementing these technologies in reusable food packaging. As previously discussed, these barriers have health and safety concerns related to the incorporation of additives and nanoparticles, their potential impact on the recyclability of packaging materials, and their contribution to AMR.

An important observation across the studies reviewed is that the addition of additives and nanoparticles not only changed surface chemistry but also altered surface roughness. For all studies, this change in roughness proved beneficial, enhancing hydrophobicity and reducing bacterial adhesion by creating air pockets that limit the contact area between the surface and bacteria. Additionally, some surfaces were modified with bactericidal additives such as Ag, ZnO, and TiO2, which are likely to have contributed to the observed antimicrobial performance. These findings suggest that achieving optimal superhydrophobic and antimicrobial surfaces may require a combination of both chemical and physical modification techniques, rather than relying on a single approach.

Finally, from a reusable packaging perspective, superhydrophobic surfaces are attractive because they can reduce microbial attachment without necessarily relying on continuous antimicrobial release. Their self‐cleaning and antiadhesive behavior could potentially reduce cleaning requirements, water consumption, and microbial carryover between reuse cycles, thereby supporting the wider adoption of circular packaging systems. However, these systems still depend on coatings, nanoparticles, or low‐surface‐energy treatments, which may be vulnerable to mechanical abrasion, detergent exposure, and repeated sanitization. As a result, their practical value for reusable food packaging depends less on initial antimicrobial efficacy alone and more on whether the wetting state can be maintained after repeated handling, cleaning, and food‐contact cycles. Further research is needed to assess the durability and longevity of superhydrophobic antimicrobial surfaces, particularly those fabricated via chemical methods, under the conditions associated with repeated use. This will be essential in determining the practical viability of these surfaces in real‐world applications.

Superhydrophilic Surfaces

Superhydrophilic surfaces, in contrast to superhydrophobic ones, exhibit a strong affinity for water, with WCA values of ≤10° (Otitoju et al. 2017). Water droplets on these surfaces readily spread to form a thin, compact water layer that acts as a barrier, inhibiting microbial adhesion as shown in Figure 4c.

Like superhydrophobic surfaces, superhydrophilic surfaces can display antifouling and self‐cleaning properties and can be produced using a variety of fabrication methods.

Chien et al. (2020) developed an efficient one‐step method to create antibacterial, superhydrophilic surfaces on PET by dip‐coating the substrate in a mixture of polydopamine (PDA) and sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA) in the presence of sodium periodate (NaIO4). The application of PDA reduced the WCA of PET from 70° to 19.5°, owing to the hydrophilic nature of PDA. When both PDA and SBMA were applied, the WCA dropped further to 7.5°, indicating superhydrophilicity. This further reduction was attributed to the sulfobetaine groups in SBMA, which promote high hydration via electrostatic interactions with water. Antimicrobial testing showed a correlation between increasing the PDA/SBMA ratio and reduced bacterial adhesion, with the lowest adhesion observed at the highest ratio (1:25). The authors highlighted that the trend in bacterial adhesion was consistent with changes in WCA, demonstrating the role of hydrophilicity in enhancing fouling resistance (Chien et al. 2020).

In another study, Madeira et al. (2022) investigated the wettability and antibacterial properties of fluorine‐doped tin oxide (FTO) glass slides deposited with sodium titanate nanotubes. The addition of these nanotubes reduced the WCA of the surfaces from 37.9° to 9.5°, and photoirradiation further decreased it to 0°, rendering the surface fully wettable. This phenomenon was explained by photoinduced superhydrophilicity, driven by surface hydroxylation and decomposition of organic pollutants. The authors noted that when TiO2 in the surface material is exposed to radiation, oxygen‐containing photogenerated holes react with TiO2, weakening titanium–oxygen bonds and forming hydroxyl (OH) groups. These OH groups increase surface energy and hydrophilicity, while photogenerated radicals decompose organic matter. Bacterial adhesion tests revealed a significant reduction in the attachment of E. coli and S. aureus, particularly for E. coli, which did not adhere at all. The authors suggested that the superhydrophilic surface forms a thin water layer that reduces bacteria–substrate interaction and prevents biofilm formation (Madeira et al. 2022).

Y. Zhu et al. (2024) developed a transparent hydrophilic (PAT) coating on glass substrates, exhibiting self‐cleaning, antifogging, and antibacterial properties. The coating, made from polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), alkyl‐polyglycoside (APG), and tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), resulted in a WCA below 10°, attributed to the abundance of OH groups in PVA and APG that promote the spreading of water. The coating demonstrated strong mechanical and chemical stability during various tests, including tape peeling, wiping with ethanol, and abrasion with steel wool, retaining its superhydrophilic and antifogging properties. Long‐term testing over 60 days showed that the WCA remained below 10°, with antifogging performance being preserved. Self‐cleaning tests revealed that the PAT coating successfully repelled silicone oil, while uncoated surfaces retained residues. The coating's antibacterial properties were confirmed using a plate counting method, showing significantly reduced E. coli and S. aureus colonies compared to uncoated glass. This was attributed to the broad‐spectrum antibacterial activity of APG and the superhydrophilic nature of the coating, which inhibits bacterial adhesion. The authors concluded that the PAT coating has potential applications in food packaging and medical endoscopy, given its durable self‐cleaning and antimicrobial performance (Y. Zhu et al. 2024). The superhydrophilic surfaces discussed in this section show considerable promise in preventing bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation. However, a notable gap in the literature is the limited focus on developing these surfaces specifically for food packaging applications. Among the studies reviewed, only one mentioned the potential for applying superhydrophilic surfaces in food packaging, highlighting an absence of research in this area. One possible explanation for this lack of focus is that superhydrophilic surfaces promote liquid spreading and wetting, which could lead to the accumulation of food residues on food packaging surfaces. This could be problematic in scenarios where food residue might interact with the packaging material, potentially leading to contamination, spoilage, and high levels of staining. For reusable food packaging, in particular, the buildup of residues could complicate cleaning and make it more difficult to maintain hygienic conditions over repeated uses. Even during cleaning, the spreading and retention of chemical residue from detergents and cleaning agents could pose concerns regarding potential toxicity. For example, Ogulur et al. (2023) previously reported that professional dishwasher rinse aids, particularly those containing alcohol ethoxylates, can leave residues on dishware that damage gut epithelial cells and impair barrier function.

Another concern with the use of these surfaces on reusable packaging is in relation to drying efficiency on industrial‐scale washing lines. In these automated processes, air is often forcefully blown post‐rinsing to remove liquid droplets from the packaging surface. However, with increased liquid spreading and adhesion, this process may be less effective, making superhydrophilic surfaces unsuitable for high‐speed industrial sanitization. Given these concerns, further research is necessary to evaluate the effectiveness and suitability of superhydrophilic antimicrobial surfaces in the context of reusable food packaging. Future studies should focus on investigating the potential safety risks of food and chemical residue accumulation and determining whether these surfaces can be easily cleaned while maintaining their antimicrobial properties in real‐world applications.

Smart Surfaces

As discussed in the previous sections, antimicrobial surfaces can be fabricated using various techniques, such as impregnating materials with antimicrobial agents or modifying surface chemistry to induce changes in wettability that inhibit microbial adhesion. However, these strategies often rely on a single functional mechanism, which can present limitations (Y. Wang et al. 2020). For instance, antifouling surfaces can be susceptible to alterations in surface chemistry due to environmental exposure, potentially compromising their antiadhesion properties over time and leading to biofilm formation (Mahanta et al. 2021). Similarly, while biocidal functionalized surfaces effectively kill microbes on contact, the accumulation of microbial debris on these surfaces can diminish their functionality and provide nutrients for subsequent bacterial colonization (Mahanta et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2020). To address these challenges, significant efforts have been directed toward developing “smart” or “kill‐and‐release” antimicrobial surfaces, which combine the mechanisms of bactericidal and antifouling surfaces (T. Wei et al. 2016; Ma et al. 2019). Like traditional functionalized antimicrobial surfaces, these advanced surfaces kill microbes on contact, typically through the incorporation of antimicrobial agents. However, they include an additional functionality: the ability to release microbial debris post‐cell death, as illustrated in Figure 4d. This is typically achieved using stimuli‐responsive polymers, which respond to environmental changes such as variations in temperature, pH, light, or electric and magnetic fields (Mahanta et al. 2021; M. Wei et al. 2017). These polymers enable controlled manipulation of the surface properties, thereby enhancing the long‐term effectiveness of antimicrobial surfaces.

For example, T. Wei et al. (2016) developed a pH‐responsive antibacterial silicone surface with dynamic, on‐demand capabilities for killing and releasing bacteria. This surface was constructed using silicone nanowire arrays (SiN) modified with polymethacrylic acid (PMAA), which acted as a reservoir for controlled lysozyme release and a self‐cleaning platform for removing dead bacteria. The SiN–PMAA surface demonstrated effective lysozyme binding at acidic conditions (pH 4) and released the enzyme at neutral conditions (pH 7), where lysozyme retained its antimicrobial activity against E. coli. In alkaline conditions (pH 10), the surface efficiently detached dead bacterial debris. The pH‐dependent behavior of the system was attributed to the interplay between the pH‐sensitive PMAA polymer and the nanostructured SiN surface. At low pH, hydrogen bonding between PMAA's COOH groups and lysozyme's CONH groups enabled lysozyme adsorption. As pH increased, ionization of COOH to COO− disrupted these interactions, reducing lysozyme binding. At higher pH values, the ionized PMAA chains became hydrophilic, forming hydration layers and repelling bacteria via electrostatic repulsion, polymer chain extension, and reduced hydrophobic interactions. The authors suggest this approach offers a versatile platform for engineering multifunctional surfaces with applications in biomedical and biotechnology fields, emphasizing its potential for tailored antibacterial and self‐cleaning functionalities (T. Wei et al. 2016).

Y. Wang et al. (2020) developed a smart antibacterial surface with dual functionalities: NIR‐activated (near‐infrared radiation) photothermal bacteria killing and thermally triggered bacteria release. The surface was fabricated on gold substrates by depositing a tannic acid (TA) and iron ions (Fe3 +) hybrid film, followed by immobilizing NH2‐terminated poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm) via Michael addition or Schiff base reactions. Under NIR irradiation, live/dead staining showed that 99% of bacterial cells on the TA–Fe3 +–PNIPAAm surface were severely damaged, whereas surfaces without NIR irradiation and the gold control surface subjected to NIR irradiation demonstrated no bactericidal activity, confirming the occurrence of photothermal effects. Thermally triggered bacteria release was evaluated after incubating E. coli on the surface, irradiating with NIR, and rinsing with cold water. Surfaces without PNIPAAm showed minimal release, while TA–Fe3 +–PNIPAAm surfaces repelled 90% of attached bacteria due to a temperature‐induced transition in wettability from bacteria‐attractive to bacteria‐repellent. The surfaces maintained high killing and release efficiency across multiple “attach–kill–release” cycles, and their functionality remained stable after 10 days of storage in air and phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), with only a slight reduction in bacterial release recorded in the latter (Y. Wang et al. 2020).

Liang et al. (2020) developed a multifunctional polymeric coating combining bactericidal and bacterial release properties. The coating was synthesized via rapid deposition of PDA, followed by in situ generation of Ag nanoparticles and grafting of a novel salt‐responsive zwitterionic monomer, 3‐(dimethyl(4‐vinylbenzyl)amonio)butanesulfonate (DVBABS). Its antibacterial performance on titanium surfaces was tested using E. coli and S. aureus. While analysis of bare titanium surfaces revealed the presence of numerous bacterial colonies, the coated surfaces exhibited a bactericidal efficiency of greater than 99% for both bacterial strains. Bacterial release was assessed by fluorescence microscopy after NaCl solution treatment, revealing that approximately 96% of E. coli and 95% of S. aureus were released from the coating, with almost no attached bacteria remaining. This effect was attributed to the salt‐responsive behavior of DVBABS brushes, which altered the coating's wettability in saline conditions. The coating maintained its effectiveness over four “attach–kill–release” cycles. The versatility of the coating was further demonstrated on different substrates, including cotton textiles, PP, and PDMS. Uncoated substrates showed no antibacterial activity, whereas coated ones created large inhibition zones for both bacterial strains. The authors propose that the desirable attributes of this coating may result in its application in the modification of medical devices and biomedical implants (Liang et al. 2020). Smart “kill‐and‐release” antimicrobial surfaces exhibit good potential for enhancing the microbial safety of reusable packaging and extending the shelf life of packaged goods. The dual functionality of these surfaces is particularly appealing for food packaging applications, as they not only eliminate bacteria upon contact but also facilitate the removal of microbial debris following cell death, thereby maintaining surface efficacy. The incorporation of stimuli‐responsive polymers could further enhance their utility, offering the possibility of designing materials specifically tailored for reusable packaging. For instance, such materials could be designed to be bactericidal under ambient conditions and to release microbes under the elevated temperatures or pH of an industrial cleaning line. Unfortunately, a critical concern with these chemically modified antimicrobial surfaces is their durability under repeated use. Among the studies reviewed, none specifically addressed the application of smart antimicrobial surfaces in food packaging. Consequently, the effects of repeated exposure to food substances or harsh environmental conditions on the surfaces’ chemistry and functionality remain unknown. To address these gaps, further research is necessary to evaluate the suitability of smart antimicrobial surfaces for food packaging and to investigate the impact of prolonged use and cleaning processes on their long‐term performance.

Patterned Surfaces

Among the various strategies used to fabricate antimicrobial surfaces, the application of micro‐ and nanoscale patterns has garnered significant attention (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). Unlike chemically modified surfaces, patterned antimicrobial surfaces rely exclusively on their surface architecture, which is designed to physically inhibit microbial adhesion, proliferation, and biofilm formation. Recent advances in nanofabrication and material science have facilitated the precise replication and customization of these natural structures, broadening their application across sectors such as health care, food safety, and environmental hygiene. This approach avoids some of the challenges associated with chemically modified antimicrobial surfaces, such as potential toxicity, recyclability, and the evolution of AMR (X. Yang, Zhang, et al. 2022; Adlhart et al. 2018).

A review of the literature reveals substantial interest in the development of bio‐inspired antimicrobial surfaces. Over millions of years of evolution, nature has devised remarkable strategies to prevent bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation in biological systems (X. Yang, Zhang, et al. 2022). One notable example is the nanopatterned structure of cicada wings (Psaltoda claripennis), which exhibit bactericidal properties (X. Yang, Zhang, et al. 2022). These wings are covered with an array of hexagonally arranged nanopillars, characterized as nanoscale protrusions approximately 200 nm in height, 100 nm in diameter at the base, 60 nm in diameter at the cap, and spaced 170 nm apart center‐to‐center (Ivanova et al. 2012). These nanopillars mechanically disrupt bacterial cells upon contact, effectively killing them through a mechanism termed mechano‐bactericidal action (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023; Soni and Brightwell 2022).

Another well‐known case is the lotus leaf (Nelumbo nucifera), which demonstrates exceptional superhydrophobic and self‐cleaning properties (X. Yang, Zhang, et al. 2022). The surface of the lotus leaf features microscopic papillae overlaid with nanoscale wax tubes, creating a hierarchical micro/nanoscale structure (Soni and Brightwell 2022). This architecture causes the famous “lotus effect,” where air cushions form at the liquid–solid interface, significantly reducing the contact area between water droplets and the surface (R. Jiang et al. 2020). As a result, water beads up and rolls off the leaf, carrying dirt particles and microbial debris with it. This self‐cleaning mechanism prevents bacterial adhesion by limiting direct contact between the surface and potential contaminants (Koch et al. 2009).

Based on their mechanism of action, antimicrobial surfaces engineered through surface topography manipulation can be classified into two main categories: antifouling or self‐cleaning surfaces and bactericidal surfaces. Antifouling surfaces (i.e., lotus leaf) deter microbial adhesion, often through superhydrophobic properties, while bactericidal surfaces (i.e., cicada wings) employ physical mechanisms, such as nanopillar‐induced cell rupture, to eliminate microbes on contact (X. Yang, Zhang, et al. 2022).

Antifouling and Self‐Cleaning Surfaces

Similar to the superhydrophobic surfaces fabricated through chemical methods discussed earlier, antifouling and self‐cleaning surfaces are designed to inhibit the initial attachment of microbes and facilitate their removal, thereby preventing the formation of biofilms. However, these surfaces rely on the geometry of their surface features rather than the chemical application of low‐surface‐energy coatings. As highlighted previously, wettability is influenced by several factors, including the cohesive forces within a liquid droplet, the adhesive forces between the droplet and the solid surface, and the surface's roughness and morphology. While surface roughness and morphology are often discussed collectively under unique parameters, they represent distinct aspects. Surface morphology pertains to the shape and arrangement of surface structures, such as channels, pillars, domes, and mushroom/flower‐like features (Darmanin and Guittard 2014). In contrast, surface roughness refers to the small‐scale irregularities or deviations in surface texture relative to an ideal flat plane, quantifying variations in height or texture at microscopic and nanoscopic scales. According to Darmanin and Guittard (2014), distinguishing between surface morphology and roughness is critical because the shape of certain surface features can induce an effect known as “pinning.” This phenomenon occurs at the liquid–solid interface, where a droplet becomes immobilized by the corners or sharp edges of surface features, restricting its ability to spread. Pinning also results in air being trapped beneath the surface features, contributing to various liquid‐repellent properties. Consequently, it is possible for surfaces with different morphologies but similar surface roughness to exhibit different liquid‐repellent behaviors (Darmanin and Guittard 2014).

The Relationship Between Surface Roughness and Wettability

When a droplet interacts with a patterned or roughened surface, it typically exists in one of two wetting states (Celia et al. 2013). The first is the Wenzel state, in which the droplet penetrates the surface features, resulting in increased contact between the liquid and the surface (Celia et al. 2013). The second is the Cassie–Baxter state, characterized by the formation of air pockets at the liquid–solid interface (Celia et al. 2013). In this state, the droplet is suspended atop the surface features, minimizing direct contact with the substrate. These states are schematically illustrated in Figure 5a,b.

The Wenzel Wetting Regime

The Wenzel wetting state was first described by Wenzel (1936), who proposed a relationship between surface roughness and wettability. His theory proposed that a roughened surface increases the liquid–solid interface area compared to the projected area of a smooth surface. For a smooth surface, r is equal to 1, while for roughened surfaces, r is greater than 1 (Ubuo et al. 2021). The Wenzel model demonstrates that increasing the surface roughness of a solid will enhance the wettability behavior that is intrinsic to the surface chemistry of the material. For instance, a surface that is intrinsically hydrophilic (θ < 90°) will become more hydrophilic with an increase in surface roughness, while a surface that is naturally hydrophobic (θ > 90°) will become more hydrophobic with increasing roughness (Ubuo et al. 2021).

The validity of the Wenzel wetting model is widely acknowledged, yet the mechanisms underlying amplified wettability remain a topic of debate (Ubuo et al. 2021; Gao and McCarthy 2007; McHale 2007). Some researchers attribute amplified wettability in the Wenzel state to the increased surface area provided by roughness, which alters the surface energy of the solid. Others contend that the surface chemistry and roughness of the contact area beneath the droplet have no effect; instead, wettability depends on the surface roughness at the three‐phase contact line, defined as the perimeter of the droplet where the liquid, solid, and vapor phases meet.

In their investigation, Ubuo et al. (2021) studied how chemical treatment and surface roughening affected the wettability of glass. They used collision theory to explain why roughened surfaces are more reactive than smooth surfaces. They explain that roughening increases surface area, which provides more bonding sites and raises the chance of interaction. For hydrophilic materials in contact with polar liquids like water (H2O), roughness creates more polar bonding sites that cause water to spread. In contrast, roughening of hydrophobic materials exposes more nonpolar groups that repel water and cause droplets to bead. In their experiments, Ubuo et al. (2021) showed that roughening hydrophilic glass reduced the WCA from 22° to 7°, attributed to more OH bonding sites. For glass treated with hydrophobic dichlorodimethylsilane (DCDMS), the WCA increased from 108° on smooth surfaces to 121° on roughened ones, linked to the presence of more CH3 groups.

In contrast, Gao and McCarthy (2007) propose that wettability is controlled by the surface chemistry and roughness only at the three‐phase contact line (where the solid, liquid, and gas phases meet), and not under the droplet itself. To test this, they examined surfaces with smooth and rough regions. They found that WCA changed as the contact line crossed between these regions. A rough inner spot led to a lower WCA as the line moved outward, while a smooth inner spot surrounded by an area of higher roughness led to a higher WCA. They concluded that wettability depends solely on the three‐phase contact line, making Wenzel's equation useful only within limits (Gao and McCarthy 2007).

In response to the study by Gao and McCarthy (2007), McHale (2007) defended the Wenzel and Cassie–Baxter models. They argued they remain valid if changes in roughness of the surface are accounted for locally. They also pointed out challenges in superhydrophobic surfaces, where droplets may have multiple three‐phase contact lines or misaligned droplet perimeters on textured surfaces such as micropillars (McHale 2007).

These studies highlight that while the Wenzel wetting model retains its validity in appropriate contexts, further research is needed to investigate the underlying mechanisms governing wettability. Whether these mechanisms are purely physical or involve a combination of physical and chemical factors remains a question in the field.

The Cassie–Baxter Wetting State

As discussed in the previous section, the Wenzel wetting model can predict the apparent contact angle on roughened surfaces. However, its validity may be compromised in specific contexts, such as when surface defects or irregularities are present. Another limitation of the Wenzel model arises when the liquid fails to infiltrate the grooves and valleys of the surface, instead remaining suspended on the protrusions of the surface features (C. Wu et al. 2022). To address these observations, Cassie and Baxter (1944) proposed a theoretical model in 1944 to explain the high contact angle values observed on porous materials. Their model accounts for the trapping of air between surface features when a liquid droplet contacts a roughened surface, leading to a composite state where the droplet interacts with both the solid surface and air (Deng et al. 2020). From Equation (3), it is evident that the apparent contact angle is largely influenced by f 1 and f 2. As f 1 approaches 1 and f 2 approaches 0, the contact area beneath the droplet becomes predominantly solid and θ* approaches θ. Conversely, as f 1 approaches 0 and f 2 approaches 1, the contact area beneath the droplet is mostly air, causing θ* to approach 180° (C. Wu et al. 2022). Therefore, by manipulating f 1 and f 2, it is possible to achieve a desired apparent contact angle on a patterned surface. This principle was demonstrated in a study by C. Wu et al. (2022), who investigated the surface wettability of hydrophobic micropillar arrays fabricated using micro stereolithography (µSLA) and coated with a low‐surface‐energy fluorosilane solution. The study measured the WCA of micropillar arrays with varying diameters, pitches, and solid–liquid contact areas (f). The highest WCA value of 143.6° was achieved for pillar arrays with a diameter of 30 µm, a spacing of 90 µm, and an f value of 8.7%. For pillar arrays with diameters of 50 and 100 µm, optimum pitch values were determined to be 150 and 250 µm, respectively. Beyond these spacing values, water droplets transitioned from the Cassie–Baxter wetting state to the Wenzel regime, resulting in reduced WCA values. The authors concluded that for the optimal Cassie–Baxter wetting effect, the ratio of pillar spacing to pillar diameter should be maintained between 2.5 and 4, with f values between 8.7% and 12.6% (C. Wu et al. 2022).

CAH and Sliding Angle

When discussing wettability, contact angle hysteresis (CAH) is an important parameter used to characterize the interaction between a liquid and a surface. Considering an ideal smooth and homogeneous surface, if liquid is continuously added to a droplet, the droplet's volume and perimeter will expand while the contact angle at the three‐phase contact line remains constant. Conversely, when liquid is continuously withdrawn from a droplet, the droplet's volume and perimeter decrease, but the contact angle remains unchanged (Deng et al. 2020).

However, real‐world surfaces are rarely ideal; they possess a degree of roughness and nonuniformity. On such surfaces, when liquid is added to a droplet, the droplet's volume increases, but the perimeter does not immediately expand. This results in an increase in the contact angle. As the droplet volume and contact angle continue to increase, a critical point is eventually reached where the droplet's perimeter (or the three‐phase contact line) abruptly advances. This sudden expansion causes the contact angle to decrease. The contact angle just before this event occurs is known as the advancing contact angle (θ A), which represents the maximum stable contact angle achievable as a liquid advances on a surface, as illustrated in Figure 5c (Deng et al. 2020).

Conversely, when liquid is gradually withdrawn from a droplet, the droplet's volume and contact angle decrease while the perimeter remains stable. As the droplet volume and contact angle continue to decline, a point is reached where the droplet's perimeter and three‐phase contact line suddenly contract. The contact angle immediately before this contraction is referred to as the receding contact angle (θ R), which is the minimum stable contact angle attainable as a liquid recedes across the surface, as illustrated in Figure 5c (Deng et al. 2020). The difference between the advancing contact angle (θ A) and the receding contact angle (θ R) defines the CAH of a surface.

The CAH of a droplet on a solid surface provides insight into the adhesion between the liquid and the surface. A high CAH indicates resistance to droplet movement, often caused by surface roughness or irregularities. Conversely, a low CAH suggests that the liquid can move more freely across the surface, indicative of a smooth and homogeneous surface. This phenomenon can be visualized using a tilt test, where the sliding angle (SA) of a droplet is measured. Droplets with low CAH tend to roll off surfaces at low inclinations, resulting in a low SA due to minimal resistance to movement, as illustrated in Figure 5d. In contrast, droplets with a high CAH require higher angles of inclination to overcome the resistance imposed by surface roughness and irregularities, leading to a higher SA. Thus, SA serves as a useful indicator of the degree of adhesion between a liquid and a surface, with higher SA values corresponding to greater adhesion between the liquid and surface.

Wettability Favorable for the Removal of Microbes on Reusable Food Packaging

As discussed in the previous sections, surface roughness and texture can significantly influence the wetting behavior of liquids on solid surfaces. For applications such as antimicrobial reusable food packaging, this raises the critical question of which degree of surface roughness or type of wetting behavior is most effective in preventing microbial adhesion. In the Wenzel wetting regime, liquid droplets penetrate into the grooves and valleys of a rough surface. Depending on the intrinsic chemistry of the material, this can result in hydrophobicity or hydrophilicity. However, due to the droplet's immersion within surface features, resistance to movement is increased, typically resulting in high CAH values, meaning greater adhesion (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023; Jeevahan et al. 2018). Surfaces with high CAH are less effective at preventing microbial adhesion, as they impede droplet movement and require high inclination to facilitate droplet roll‐off (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). In contrast, the Cassie–Baxter wetting state occurs when droplets rest on the peaks of surface features, trapping air within the valleys beneath. This state reduces the contact area available for microbial adhesion and minimizes resistance to droplet motion, leading to low CAH values (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). Consequently, droplets can easily roll off at low inclinations, carrying away microbial debris in a process referred to as self‐cleaning (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023). Having said that, a liquid droplet with a high WCA in the Cassie–Baxter state may not always roll off a surface. Jeevahan et al. (2018) describe a phenomenon observed on rose petals, where high‐contact‐angle droplets remain adhered even when the petal is held upside down. They explain that rose petals feature a surface of nanofolds that increases surface roughness and restricts droplet movement. This is referred to as the “petal effect,” where high‐contact‐angle droplets do not roll off a surface. This is the opposite of the “lotus effect,” where droplets roll off at low inclinations (Jeevahan et al. 2018). Considering these factors, surface roughness or patterns that promote a Cassie–Baxter wetting effect and facilitate droplet movement at low inclination angles are most favorable for the fabrication of antimicrobial surfaces for reusable food packaging. Such surfaces should exhibit a WCA greater than 150° and a CAH of less than 10° to effectively prevent microbial attachment and facilitate their removal, as illustrated in Figure 6 (M.‐S. Lee et al. 2023; Jeevahan et al. 2018).

Francone et al. (2021) investigated the effectiveness of hierarchical micro/nano surface topographies fabricated using nanoimprint lithography (NIL) in reducing bacterial adhesion. The hierarchical surfaces consisted of micro‐protrusions with nanoscale spikes positioned either on top or at the base of these features. Contact angle analysis revealed that these structures exhibited increased hydrophobicity, with WCA values ranging from 133° to 147°, compared to 98° to 117° for flat and single‐layer micro‐protrusion surfaces. Additionally, CAH values remained below 15°, indicating low water droplet adhesion.

The antimicrobial performance of these surfaces was assessed using the touch transfer assay method to quantify E. coli and S. aureus adhesion. Single‐layer surfaces with either micro‐protrusions or nanospikes were first evaluated, where the presence of microstructures led to a significant reduction of 46% in S. aureus attachment but had no notable effect on E. coli, indicating limited effectiveness of single‐layer topographies against both strains. In contrast, hierarchical structures with nanospikes at the base of micro‐protrusions demonstrated significant reductions in bacterial attachment of 82% for E. coli and 84% for S. aureus. Conversely, hierarchical patterns with nanospikes positioned on the micro‐protrusions only reduced S. aureus attachment by 44%, suggesting that surface topography patterns can influence different bacterial strains to different extents.

The authors argue that while wettability affects bacterial attachment, it is not a sole predictor of bacterial adhesion behavior. Instead, bacterial interactions with surfaces depend on a combination of wettability, roughness, and topography, mediated by surface‐sensing mechanisms of bacteria such as flagella and membrane‐associated sensor proteins. Their findings suggest that E. coli, a rod‐shaped bacterium, appears to find it more difficult to adhere to densely packed nanospike structures positioned at the bottom of the micro‐protrusions rather than the top, whereas S. aureus, which is spherical, shows no clear preference for nanospike positioning. The authors conclude that bacterial attachment is a complex process that cannot be explained solely by surface energy interactions but requires a broader understanding of how bacterial appendages interact with various types of surface architecture. Addressing this complexity requires validation of a wide range of surface modifications across different pathogens to establish effective antibacterial strategies (Francone et al. 2021).

Chien et al. (2019) investigated the relationship between surface roughness, wettability, and biofilm formation using biomimetic shark skin‐patterned surfaces. In their study, polymeric replicas of shark skin denticles were fabricated by casting polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) onto PDMS molds imprinted from shark skin samples collected from the tail–abdomen and pectoral fin regions. Wettability analysis revealed that increased surface roughness correlated with higher hydrophobicity, with water WCA measurements of 115.8° and 105.6° for patterns fabricated from the tail–abdomen and pectoral fin, respectively, compared to 73.8° for smooth PMMA surfaces. These results aligned with the surface roughness (Ra) values recorded, where shark skin patterns exhibited Ra values of 6.08 µm (tail–abdomen) and 3.57 µm (pectoral fin), significantly higher than the 0.17 µm Ra value observed for smooth PMMA. The enhanced hydrophobicity of these biomimetic surfaces was attributed to gas entrapment within the microstructure valleys, reducing bacterial adhesion.

To assess antimicrobial performance, E. coli and S. aureus were inoculated onto the patterned and smooth surfaces, and biofilm formation was quantified using crystal violet staining after 1, 7, and 14 days. No difference in early‐stage S. aureus attachment was observed between patterned and smooth surfaces. However, by Day 7, biofilm formation was evident on smooth surfaces but absent on patterned samples. By Day 14, mature biofilms were well‐established on smooth surfaces, whereas no biofilm formation was detected on the patterned surfaces. A similar trend was observed with E. coli, although at the 1‐day interval, bacterial attachment was initially higher on the patterned surfaces than on smooth controls. However, biofilm accumulation did not increase over time, suggesting that while increased roughness may promote early‐stage bacterial attachment due to the larger surface area and density of attachment sites, it physically restricts the expansion of bacterial microcolonies, thereby preventing biofilm maturation. The lower initial attachment of S. aureus was attributed to the high hydrophobicity of the shark skin patterns, where trapped air between surface features likely hindered bacterial adhesion. The authors hypothesized that limited wetting of the surface by the bacterial medium in the initial contact phase reduced bacterial settlement. The authors conclude that their study provides a valuable insight into the interaction between bacterial and micro‐structured topography that may support the development of effective solutions for biofilm control (Chien et al. 2019).

Schwibbert et al. (2019) investigated the impact of laser‐induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS), commonly referred to as “ripples,” on high‐density polyethylene (HDPE) substrates fabricated via femtosecond laser interference patterning. Surface characterization using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed an increase in surface roughness following laser treatment, which was accompanied by an increase in WCA from 64.5° on the pristine HDPE surface to 120.5° on the modified surface. The increase in hydrophobicity was attributed to both the increased surface roughness and potential chemical modification of the surface induced by laser processing. To assess bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, E. coli and S. aureus were incubated on the surfaces for up to 21 h. After 21 h, E. coli exhibited an extensive number of multilayer bacterial colonies on the unmodified surface, whereas bacterial adhesion was significantly reduced on the laser‐processed surface, with only a small number of dispersed cells observed. In contrast, S. aureus colonized both pristine and roughened surfaces uniformly, forming dense biofilms. The authors propose that these differences in colonization arise from the interaction between bacterial morphology and the nanoscale surface topography. Specifically, the valley structures on the modified surface, ranging from 0.6 to 2 µm in size, provide favorable attachment sites for spherical S. aureus cells of 0.5–1 µm in diameter, whereas the rod‐shaped E. coli cells of less than 1 µm in length experience difficulty in attaching and are forced to lie on the top of surface features. Despite the increased total surface area of the laser‐modified material, the study highlights that not all of this area is accessible for bacterial adhesion. The authors conclude that femtosecond laser interference patterning is a promising technique for influencing bacterial adhesion and colonization. However, they emphasize that due to the complex interplay between bacterial cells and surface properties, it remains challenging to design a universal antifouling surface that is effective against all bacterial species in all environments. Instead, they recommend that antifouling strategies be tailored to specific environmental conditions, prioritizing easy‐to‐clean surfaces and optimized cleaning protocols (Schwibbert et al. 2019).

The antifouling and self‐cleaning surfaces discussed exhibit significant potential for preventing the adhesion and proliferation of bacterial species. In the context of reusable food packaging, this could contribute to the preservation of microbial safety and the extension of the shelf life of food products. A notable observation from these studies is that while surface wettability serves as an indicator of microbial adhesion behavior, it is not the sole determining factor (Francone et al. 2021). Specifically, bacterial cell shape and dimensions have been shown to influence microbial interactions with surfaces. For example, research by Francone et al. (2021) and Schwibbert et al. (2019) demonstrated that variations in cell morphology resulted in differential adhesion behaviors between E. coli and S. aureus. Similarly, the study by Chien et al. (2019) reported that although initial adhesion of E. coli on patterned surfaces was higher than on control surfaces, the surface architecture was still sufficiently small to prevent the spread of microcolonies, thereby inhibiting biofilm formation.

Given the variability in bacterial interactions with different surfaces, the application of this technology to food packaging necessitates validation against microbial species relevant to the food industry. Pathogenic bacteria responsible for foodborne illnesses, such as B. cereus and L. monocytogenes, may exhibit their own distinct morphological characteristics that may affect their adhesion and proliferation on these surfaces (Griffiths and Schraft 2017; Bucur et al. 2018). Therefore, it is imperative to assess the functionality of fabricated surfaces against a variety of microbial strains of interest to ensure their efficacy in enhancing food safety.

A desirable aspect of manufacturing microbe‐repellent and self‐cleaning surfaces through surface topography manipulation for the food packaging industry is its reliance solely on the physical modification of surface roughness and morphology. Unlike approaches that utilize chemical modifications such as the development of functionalized, superwettable, or smart antimicrobial surfaces, this method can be applied to commercially available food‐contact‐approved materials without concerns regarding the migration of potentially harmful substances into food products. Additionally, this approach does not interfere with the recycling compatibility of the packaging, ensuring its effective integration into established municipal waste streams. Furthermore, as it does not involve the inclusion of antimicrobial agents, it mitigates the risk of contributing to AMR.

Despite these advantages, the long‐term durability and functionality of these surfaces remain a concern. As mentioned previously, reusable food packaging is often subjected to industrial sanitization processes involving elevated temperatures and mechanical forces. This raises questions about the resilience of micro‐ and nanoscale surface features under such environmental conditions. Any deformation of these surface features could significantly impact their effectiveness, as bacterial adhesion is closely related to surface architecture. For instance, substantial changes in surface roughness due to deformation could either promote or inhibit microbial adhesion, depending on the effect on wettability and on whether the change in roughness is large relative to the dimensions of the bacterial cells. Therefore, if these surfaces are to be utilized in reusable food packaging applications, it is essential to investigate their long‐term durability under repeated use and industrial sanitization conditions. Such investigations should assess changes in surface roughness and morphology and evaluate their subsequent impact on microbial adhesion and biofilm formation.

Bactericidal Surfaces

Bactericidal surfaces, sometimes referred to as contact‐killing surfaces, are inspired by natural surfaces such as the wings of species of cicadas (P. claripennis) and dragonflies (Diplacodes bipunctata), which can physically disrupt microbial cells upon contact (Jenkins et al. 2020). This bactericidal effect is primarily attributed to the specific geometry of high‐aspect‐ratio nanostructures, such as nanopillars and nanospikes, which exert mechanical forces on microbial cells, leading to cell lysis and subsequent death, as illustrated in Figure 8. Recent advances in nanofabrication techniques, such as NIL and reactive ion etching (RIE), have facilitated the precise replication of these structures. Consequently, these techniques enable the fabrication of bactericidal surfaces on a wide range of materials, expanding their applicability across various fields.

The mechanism of action of a contact‐killing antimicrobial surface where bacteria are pierced on contact and further stretched and ruptured as they sink into the surface structure, resulting in cell death.

Q. Cui et al. (2020) systematically investigated the bactericidal activity of nanopillar structures on polycarbonate (PC), varying geometrical parameters such as height, interpillar spacing, and cap diameter, as shown in Figure 9a,c,e. These nanopillar structures were fabricated using anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) template‐assisted hot embossing and wet etching techniques. Wettability analysis demonstrated that the fabrication of nanopillars resulted in all PC surfaces becoming hydrophobic, with the WCA of surfaces increasing from 86° for the control sample to a range of 116°–123° for the patterned surfaces.

(a, c, e) Nanopillar arrays with heights of 100, 200, and 300 nm, respectively, and an interpillar spacing of 170 nm. (b) Viable E. coli cells on a surface with 100‐nm‐high nanopillars and 170‐nm interpillar spacing. (d, f) Nonviable E. coli cells on nanopillar arrays with heights of 200 and 300 nm, respectively, and an interpillar spacing of 170 nm. The images show E. coli cells pierced, stretched, and deformed, rendering them nonviable (Q. Cui et al. 2020).

To evaluate the influence of nanopillar geometry on bactericidal efficiency, a series of control and nanostructured surfaces were investigated using E. coli. The results revealed that bacterial adhesion was prominent on the flat control surface, where nearly all adhered cells remained viable, resulting in a bactericidal efficiency of less than 3%. This finding confirmed that unmodified PC surfaces were ineffective against E. coli. In contrast, PC surfaces featuring nanopillars with a height exceeding 200 nm, an interpillar spacing of 170 nm, and an average cap diameter of 45 nm exhibited enhanced bactericidal behavior, achieving an efficiency of 98%.

Through their systematic analysis, the authors identified optimum geometric parameter values to maximize bactericidal efficiency. First, a minimum nanopillar height of 200 nm was identified as necessary to induce bacterial cell rupture. When interpillar spacing was held constant at 170 nm, surfaces with nanopillar heights of 150 nm or below displayed bactericidal activity comparable to that of the control surfaces. Only when nanopillar heights reached 200 nm or higher did bactericidal efficiency exceed 93%. The authors attributed this finding to the ability of bacterial cells to sufficiently sink into the nanopillar structures at a height of 200 nm or greater, leading to bacterial cell deformation and rupture, as depicted in Figure 9d,f. Conversely, shorter nanopillars limited cell penetration, preventing rupture and cell death, as shown in Figure 9b. However, excessively tall nanopillars were cautioned against, as they were prone to collapse and clustering, which could potentially compromise functionality. In addition to nanopillar height, the study highlighted the sensitivity of bactericidal activity to interpillar spacing. A decline in bactericidal efficiency was observed when interpillar spacing deviated from the optimum value of 170 nm, decreasing to 58% at 100 nm and 78% at 300 nm. This suggests the existence of an optimal interpillar spacing for maximizing bactericidal performance.

Furthermore, cap diameter also played a crucial role, with cap diameters below 60 nm exhibiting the highest bactericidal efficiency. The authors noted that smaller cap diameters corresponded to sharper nanopillars, enhancing their ability to rupture bacterial cells. However, a trade‐off was identified, as smaller cap diameters also resulted in thinner nanopillars, which were more susceptible to mechanical deformation and potential loss of bactericidal activity. Q. Cui et al. (2020) emphasized that in designing bactericidal nanostructures, mechanical properties must be considered alongside antimicrobial performance. Their findings provide valuable insights for optimizing nanopillar geometry to enhance the bactericidal efficiency of nanostructured surfaces (Q. Cui et al. 2020).

Linklater et al. (2017) investigated the influence of nanoscale topography on the bactericidal efficiency of black silicon surfaces. Using RIE, high‐aspect‐ratio nanopillars with three different heights (280, 430, and 610 nm) were fabricated on silicon wafers. The bactericidal performance of these surfaces was evaluated using S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, with bacterial adhesion quantified through confocal microscopy combined with live/dead staining. The study found that, after 24 h of incubation, S. aureus exhibited the lowest attachment rate on surfaces with nanopillars with a height of 430 nm, a spacing of 99.5 nm, and a density of 26.6 pillars/µm2, with bacterial adhesion measured at approximately 700 cells/mm2. In contrast, the highest adhesion rate of approximately 2000 cells/mm2 occurred on surfaces with nanopillars with a height of 600 nm, a spacing of 139.7 nm, and a density of 23.7 pillars/µm2. Pseudomonas aeruginosa displayed different attachment behavior, with the lowest adhesion rate of approximately 2000 cells/mm2 observed on surfaces with nanopillars with a height of 280 nm, a spacing of 62.1 nm, and a density of 86.7 pillars/µm2. The highest adhesion of approximately 23,000 cells/mm2 was found on surfaces with 600‐nm‐high nanopillars. The authors attributed these differences in bacterial adhesion to variations in cell morphology. They explain that P. aeruginosa is a flagella‐producing bacteria and that previous studies have demonstrated how these types of bacteria can use this appendage to sense and interact with surface structures. When interpillar spacing is low, preventing bacterial cells from fully sinking into and being punctured by the nanostructures, P. aeruginosa can use its flagella to weave bridges between pillars, creating additional attachment sites and promoting adhesion.

Cell viability analysis revealed that surfaces with 280‐nm nanopillars exhibited the highest bactericidal efficiency, with killing rates of approximately 85% for S. aureus and 89% for P. aeruginosa. This corresponded to a sixfold increase in bactericidal activity against S. aureus and a fourfold increase against P. aeruginosa relative to flat control surfaces. The authors suggested that the slightly higher bactericidal efficiency against P. aeruginosa was due to differences in cell wall structure. As a Gram‐negative bacterium, P. aeruginosa has a thin but rigid cell wall, making it more susceptible to mechanical rupture. In contrast, S. aureus, a Gram‐positive bacterium, possesses a thicker and more flexible peptidoglycan layer that allows for greater deformation without membrane rupture, providing increased resistance to nanopillar‐induced damage. The study concluded that the fabrication method utilized represents a simple and effective approach for producing bactericidal surface topographies. The authors recommended further research to replicate these structures on clinically relevant materials for potential biomedical applications (Linklater et al. 2017).

Finally, Ivanova et al. (2020) conducted a study to investigate the bactericidal activity of silicon nanopillar arrays, providing new insights into the role of nanopillar flexibility in bacterial cell death. The authors systematically examined the influence of nanopillar height (220, 360, and 420 nm) on bactericidal efficiency while maintaining a constant nanopillar diameter of 35 nm and an interpillar spacing of 90 nm. The nanopillar arrays were fabricated on silicon wafers using UV immersion lithography followed by plasma etching. Confocal microscopy with live/dead staining was used to assess the viability of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa on the surfaces.

The results demonstrated that nanopillars with a height of 360 nm exhibited the highest bactericidal efficiency, achieving 95% and 83% bacterial cell death for P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, respectively. In comparison, bactericidal efficiency for P. aeruginosa was 53% and 89% on nanopillars of 220 and 420 nm, while for S. aureus, it was 58% and 77%, respectively. SEM imagery revealed significant changes in the morphology of bacterial cells on the nanopatterned surfaces relative to flat controls. Bacteria adhered to the nanopatterned substrates appeared deflated and elongated, with nanopillar tips visibly protruding through the upper membrane of collapsed cells. Notably, this effect was more pronounced for P. aeruginosa than for S. aureus, aligning with previous studies that indicate a greater susceptibility of Gram‐negative bacteria to mechanical rupture due to differences in cell wall structure.

In addition to assessing bactericidal efficiency, the authors investigated the interaction between bacterial cells and nanopillars. On nanopillar arrays of 360 and 420 nm in height, pillars located at the perimeter of bacterial cells were observed to bend, while those beneath the central mass remained upright. At a height of 420 nm, many nanopillars exhibited clustering due to tip‐to‐tip contact, suggesting that excessive nanopillar height may influence structural integrity. Theoretical calculations were performed to estimate pillar deflection and mechanical energy across the different nanopillar arrays. The results indicated that the stored mechanical energy of the 360‐ and 420‐nm nanopillars was approximately one order of magnitude greater than that of the 220‐nm nanopillars. However, only a marginal increase in elastic forces was observed between the 420‐ and 360‐nm arrays compared to the 220‐nm structures.

The authors attributed the enhanced bactericidal activity of the 360‐ and 420‐nm nanopillars compared to the 220‐nm‐high nanopillars to their ability to deflect in response to externally applied forces, which is governed by material and surface properties such as aspect ratio and stiffness. The increased bactericidal activity was linked to stress‐induced nanopillar deflection, which amplifies membrane stretching upon bacterial adhesion. These findings suggest the existence of a previously unrecognized mechano‐bactericidal mechanism driven by nanopillar elasticity. The authors propose that the understanding of this mechanism could facilitate the design of surfaces with tunable bactericidal properties for biomedical and industrial applications (Ivanova et al. 2020). The bactericidal surfaces discussed demonstrate significant potential for neutralizing bacterial cells and preventing biofilm formation. In the context of reusable food packaging, this technology could be particularly advantageous, as it may help mitigate microbial adhesion and biofilm formation, reduce microbial load, and assist in sanitization, thereby extending the shelf life of packaged products and enhancing the longevity of the packaging material.

The discussed studies highlight two critical factors that influence the efficiency of bactericidal surfaces: the dimensions of surface features and the morphology of bacterial cells. Q. Cui et al. (2020) identified specific ranges for feature dimensions, such as height and spacing, that optimize bactericidal efficiency. Their findings suggest that nanopillar structures must possess sufficient height and appropriate spacing to allow bacterial cells to penetrate the structure, undergo stretching, and eventually rupture. A similar phenomenon was observed by Linklater et al. (2017), who reported that when the interpillar spacing was too narrow, P. aeruginosa cells were unable to settle within the nanopillar array. Instead, the bacteria utilized their flagella to form bridge‐like structures, which served as additional attachment sites, reducing bactericidal effectiveness. Bacterial morphology is another key consideration in the design of nanopillar structures. Studies by Linklater et al. (2017) and Ivanova et al. (2020) indicate that differences in cell wall structure between Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria influence bactericidal efficiency. For instance, nanopillar arrays were highly effective at inducing cell rupture in P. aeruginosa, a Gram‐negative species characterized by a thin and rigid cell wall. In contrast, while bactericidal activity was still observed against Gram‐positive S. aureus, it was somewhat reduced, likely due to the greater flexibility and thickness of its cell wall, which made it more resistant to stretching and rupture. These findings suggest that nanopillar array designs must be tailored and validated against specific target microbial species relevant to the intended application.

As with antifouling and self‐cleaning surfaces previously discussed, a key advantage of contact‐killing surfaces generated through surface topography manipulation is their reliance on purely physical modifications. Unlike antimicrobial coatings or chemically modified materials, this approach does not involve the incorporation of antimicrobial agents, thereby avoiding concerns related to the migration of potentially harmful substances, recycling compatibility, and the emergence of AMR. However, this strategy also raises concerns regarding the long‐term durability and functionality of these surfaces under repeated use conditions. Currently, no research has investigated the impact of repeated industrial sanitation processes on the structural integrity of micro‐ and nanoscale surface features. Given that bactericidal activity is highly dependent on precise feature dimensions, structural deformation due to repeated washing could diminish antimicrobial performance. Additionally, in the case of nanopillar arrays, detachment of surface features could contribute to microplastic release, posing a potential safety risk for food packaging applications.

To facilitate the implementation of these surfaces in reusable food packaging, future research should focus on evaluating their long‐term durability under repeated industrial washing conditions. Such investigations should assess changes in surface roughness and micro/nano‐architecture and their effects on microbial adhesion and biofilm formation, and the potential for increased microplastic release.

Summary of Antimicrobial Surfaces for Reusable Food Packaging

As discussed throughout this review, a range of strategies have been developed for fabricating antimicrobial surfaces, each with distinct advantages and limitations in the context of reusable food packaging. Chemical modification approaches, including those incorporating antimicrobial agents and low‐surface‐energy coatings, demonstrate strong antimicrobial efficacy but raise concerns regarding consumer safety, additive leaching, recycling compatibility, and AMR. In contrast, surface topography modification may avoid many of these concerns and offer a chemical‐free alternative by physically inhibiting microbial attachment or inducing bacterial cell rupture through engineered micro‐ and nanoscale features. However, challenges remain regarding the durability of these surface features under repeated use and washing, as well as their potential contribution to microplastic generation. Future research should therefore move beyond short‐term antimicrobial screening and focus on realistic reuse scenarios, including repeated washing, abrasion, food residue accumulation, ageing, and post‐consumer recycling compatibility. From a practical perspective, the selection of an antimicrobial surface should extend beyond antimicrobial efficacy alone. The most appropriate strategy depends on the intended food application, target microorganisms, packaging material, expected cleaning regime, regulatory requirements, and desired service life of the packaging. Different food products present distinct contamination risks. For example, fresh meat, poultry, and seafood can commonly be associated with bacterial pathogens such as E. coli, L. monocytogenes, Salmonella spp., and Campylobacter spp., whereas bakery products may be susceptible to fungal spoilage, and fresh produce is often affected by both bacterial and fungal deterioration under high‐moisture conditions. Consequently, antimicrobial surfaces should be selected according to the predominant microbial hazards, anticipated microbial load, and intended operating environment rather than adopting a universal approach. Conclusion

Antimicrobial surfaces represent a promising strategy for enhancing the microbial safety and shelf life of food goods supplied in reusable packaging. Their incorporation could improve consumer engagement and accelerate adoption by facilitating effective decontamination and sanitization, thereby reducing the likelihood of cross‐contamination with foodborne pathogens. By inhibiting the growth and survival of microorganisms responsible for food spoilage and illness, antimicrobial surfaces can provide an added layer of assurance that food is delivered in a safe and hygienic medium.

As discussed throughout this review, multiple strategies exist for fabricating antimicrobial surfaces on materials. Functionalized, superwettable, and smart or “kill‐and‐release” surfaces, typically produced through chemical coatings or incorporation of antimicrobial agents, have demonstrated strong efficacy against a wide range of microbial species. However, their application in food packaging raises critical concerns, including the potential migration of toxic components into food, interference with recycling and circularity, contributions to AMR, and the leaching of additives that may diminish long‐term performance.

In contrast, antimicrobial surfaces fabricated through physical modification of roughness and texture, often inspired by natural systems, avoid these drawbacks by relying solely on micro‐ and nanoscale structural features. Depending on their geometry, such surfaces can either prevent microbial adhesion through antifouling, superhydrophobic properties or kill cells on contact through mechano‐bactericidal action. While these approaches show considerable promise for reusable food packaging, challenges that must be overcome remain. In particular, the durability of surface features under repeated washing and reuse conditions, their long‐term antimicrobial efficacy, and their potential contribution to microplastic generation must be thoroughly investigated before large‐scale adoption can be realized.

Looking ahead, the successful integration of antimicrobial surfaces into reusable food packaging systems will depend on balancing functionality, safety, and sustainability. While chemical‐based approaches have demonstrated effectiveness, their potential risks highlight the importance of developing alternative approaches, such as antimicrobial surface topography patterns, that align more closely with circular economy principles and consumer health and safety priorities for packaging. Therefore, surface topography‐based solutions offer a compelling pathway forward, but their practical implementation requires rigorous assessment under real‐world conditions. Addressing durability and long‐term performance will be essential for narrowing the gap between laboratory innovation and widespread industrial adoption.

Finally, from an industrial implementation perspective, antimicrobial surfaces are unlikely to be adopted as a standalone preservation technology and may instead form part of a combined strategy for extending product shelf life. Combining antimicrobial surfaces with established preservation technologies, such as modified atmosphere packaging, vacuum packaging, active packaging systems, oxygen scavengers, and freshness indicators, may provide complementary benefits by simultaneously reducing microbial contamination and suppressing microbial growth. Such integration may also reduce the level of antimicrobial activity required from the surface itself, potentially mitigating concerns regarding long‐term efficacy while facilitating the development of safer, more sustainable, and commercially viable reusable food packaging systems.
