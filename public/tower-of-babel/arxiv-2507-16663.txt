Self-Contradiction as Self-Improvement: Mitigating the Generation-Understanding Gap in MLLMs
Yujin Han 1,8 , Hao Chen 2 , Andi Han 3,4 , Zhiheng Wang 5,6 , Xinyu Lin 7 Yingya Zhang 8 , Shiwei Zhang 8 † , Difan Zou 1

Abstract
Despite efforts to unify multimodal generation and understanding tasks in a single model, we show these MLLMs exhibit self-contradiction where generation produces images deemed misaligned with input prompts based on model’s own understanding. We define a Nonunified score that quantifies such self-contradiction. Our empirical results reveal that the self-contradiction mainly arises from weak generation that fails to align with prompts, rather than misunderstanding. This capability asymmetry indicates the potential of leveraging self-contradiction for self-improvement, where the stronger model understanding guides the weaker generation to mitigate the generation–understanding gap. Applying standard post-training methods (e.g., SFT, DPO) with such internal supervision successfully improves both generation and unification. We discover a co-improvement effect on both generation and understanding when only fine-tuning the generation branch, a phenomenon known in pre-training but underexplored in post-training. Our analysis shows improvements stem from better detection of false positives that are previously incorrectly identified as prompt-aligned. Theoretically, we show the aligned training dynamics between generation and understanding allow reduced prompt-misaligned generations to also improve mismatch detection in the understanding branch. Additionally, the framework reveals a potential risk of co-degradation under poor supervision—an overlooked phenomenon that is empirically validated in our experiments. Notably, we find intrinsic metrics like Nonunified score cannot distinguish co-degradation from co-improvement, which highlights the necessity of data quality check. Finally, we propose a curriculum-based strategy based on our findings that gradually introduces harder samples as the model improves, leading to better unification and improved MLLM generation and understanding.

Alongside improvements in both generation and unification, we also observe a

This helps explain the improvement in understanding, as shared training dynamics allow generation-side optimization to reduce the probability of producing prompt-misaligned outputs also enhance the understanding branch’s ability to recognize such misalignments.

Interestingly, our learning dynamics analysis also indicates the potential for

These findings highlight the necessity of pre-validating data quality, e.g., using external models, prior to initiating self-improvement.

Finally, we explore whether the synchronized mechanism between generation and understanding can further enhance self-improvement in MLLMs. The observed co-improvement effect motivates us to introduce curriculum learning

This online strategy leverages the evolving capabilities of both branches to progressively expand the training data based on sample difficulty and further improve both the performance and unification of MLLMs.

Nonunified Generation and Understanding of MLLMs

Takeaway on self-contradiction

MLLMs such as Janus-Pro-7B exhibit a self-contradiction phenomenon, reflecting a disparity between generation and understanding capabilities. Further analysis reveals that the self-contradiction is primarily attributed to weak generation strength, while the understanding branch correctly identifies prompt-misaligned images.

We first propose the
Nonunified score
to quantify self-contradiction, i.e., the capability asymmetry between the generation and understanding. Specifically, consider an MLLM
\pi_{\theta}
and a prompt
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the prompt space. The generation branch produces an image
\mathbf{x}=\pi^{\text{gen}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})
, and the pair
(\mathbf{x},q(\mathbf{y}))
, where
q(\mathbf{y}):=\text{``Does this image describe }\mathbf{y}\text{?''}
, is then passed into the understanding branch
\pi^{\text{und}}_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot)
. If the understanding branch deems
\mathbf{x}
aligned with prompt
\mathbf{y}
score 1; otherwise, score 0. We define the
Nonunified score
as the proportion of score 0, i.e.,

\text{Nonunified score}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{I}\left[\pi^{\text{und}}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x},\ q(\mathbf{y})\right)\neq 1\right].

(1)

We evaluate the Nonunified score of Janus-Pro-7B on T2I-CompBench
[
30
]
in
Figure

2
and find that the Nonunified score is consistently greater than zero across all tasks. Notably, on Texture dataset, the score reaches over 43%, indicating for 43% of prompts, the generated images are judged by its own understanding branch as failing to align the given prompts. The results suggest Janus-Pro-7B exhibits self-contradiction, where generation and understanding fail to demonstrate unified behavior.

To further investigate the causes of generation–understanding gap, we assign each generated image a ground-truth label
s_{\mathrm{Qwen}}\in\{0,1\}
based on scores from Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct. Among the cases where the Janus-Pro-7B understanding branch assigns a score of 0 (i.e., rejects the generation), we identify two types of misalignment:

•

Weak Generation:
Understanding branch correctly rejects a misaligned image produced by the generation branch. This corresponds to both Janus-Pro-7B and Qwen assigning a score of 0:

\displaystyle\text{Weak Generation}:=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\pi^{\text{und}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\ q(\mathbf{y}))=s_{\mathrm{Qwen}}\,\big|\,\pi^{\text{und}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\ q(\mathbf{y}))=0\big).

•

Weak Understanding:
Generation branch produces a correct image, but understanding branch mistakenly rejects it. This occurs when Janus-Pro-7B assigns a score of 0 while Qwen assigns 1:

\displaystyle\text{Weak Understanding}:=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\pi^{\text{und}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\ q(\mathbf{y}))\neq s_{\mathrm{Qwen}}\,\big|\,\pi^{\text{und}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\ q(\mathbf{y}))=0\big).

Figure

2
shows that the majority of misalignments (over 85%) fall into the first type, where Janus-Pro’s understanding branch correctly rejects prompt-misaligned generations. These results suggest that mitigating unification issues in MLLMs through self-improvement can be achieved by leveraging the stronger understanding branch to guide the weaker generation branch.

3.1
Leveraging Self-Contradiction for MLLMs’ Self-Improvement

Takeaway on self-improvement

In self-improvement of MLLMs, we find:

•

Leveraging self-contradiction by using the understanding branch as an internal reward model can effectively improve generation and promote unification.

•

Generation-targeted self-improvement also enhances model’s understanding, indicating co-improvement between generation and understanding exists in post-training as well.

We consider classical post-training strategies, such as DPO and SFT. Specifically, using DPO as an example, we construct preference pairs as follows: for a given image generation prompt
\mathbf{y}
, the MLLM
\pi_{\theta}
first generates
N
candidate images
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}=\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})
. Next, each candidate images in
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is paired with the question “Does this image describe
\mathbf{y}
?” and passed back into
\pi_{\theta}
for an understanding task. The model then selects images that align with the prompt (chosen image) and those that do not (rejected image), forming preference data
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{\text{chosen}},\mathbf{x}_{\text{rejected}})
for DPO on the generation branch. For SFT, only the chosen samples are used for supervised fine-tuning.

Better Generation and Unification
.
We consider Janus-Pro-7B on 3D Spatial subtask and report two metrics: generation quality, evaluated by UniDet score
[
31
]
and unification between generation and understanding, measured by Nonunified score.
Figure

3a
shows generation quality improves with a 5% increase in UniDet score, while Nonunified score decreases by 10%, demonstrating the effectiveness of leveraging self-contradiction to enhance both MLLM generation and unification without any external signals. Co-Improvement Effect.
To quantify changes in understanding, we evaluate Janus-Pro-7B model at different post-training epochs (see more details in
Section

A.1
) and evaluate post-trained models’ improvement over the pre-trained model via a
WinRate
metric. Specifically, the win rate (excluding ties) is defined as the proportion of samples where the understanding prediction changes after self-improvement and agrees with the score of stronger judge—Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct.
Following
Section

2
, we let
\pi_{\mathrm{pre}}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{post}}
denote the pre-trained and post-trained MLLMs, respectively. We define the initial generations as
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}}=\pi^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\mathrm{pre}}(\mathbf{y})
for the prompt
\mathbf{y}
. The corresponding WinRate is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{WinRate}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sum\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{I}\left[\pi^{\mathrm{und}}_{\mathrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}},q(\mathbf{y}))\neq\pi^{\mathrm{und}}_{\mathrm{post}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}},q(\mathbf{y}))\ \land\ \pi^{\mathrm{und}}_{\mathrm{post}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}},q(\mathbf{y}))=s_{\mathrm{Qwen}}\right]}{\sum\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{I}\left[\pi^{\mathrm{und}}_{\mathrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}},q(\mathbf{y}))\neq\pi^{\mathrm{und}}_{\mathrm{post}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}},q(\mathbf{y}))\right]}

(2)

where
s_{\mathrm{Qwen}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{pre}},q(\mathbf{y}))\in\{0,1\}
is oracle label provided by Qwen.

Intuitively, if the pre-trained model and the post-trained model are comparable in understanding ability, then both should be equally likely to agree with the oracle, resulting in a WinRate of 0.5. A WinRate above 0.5 indicates that the post-trained model achieves higher understanding accuracy than the pre-trained model, whereas a value below 0.5 suggests a degradation in understanding.

Figure

3b
quantifies generation and understanding performance before and after self-improvement using UniDet score and WinRate. A clear co-improvement effect emerges: all post-trained variants lie in the area where both generation and understanding surpass the pre-trained model.

3.2
Generation–Understanding Interplay Mechanism in Self-imropvement

Takeaway on interplay mechanism

The interplay mechanism between generation and understanding can be:

•

Co-improvement
: Understanding improvement primarily stems from the model’s enhanced ability to detect false-positive generations that were previously mistaken as prompt-aligned. This is facilitated by aligned training dynamics between generation and understanding, where reducing prompt-misaligned generations helps the understanding branch better identify such misalignments.

•

Co-degradation
: This phenomenon is particularly likely to occur under poor post-training data quality or when using smaller-scale models. Notably, internal metrics such as the Nonunified score are unable to distinguish co-improvement from co-degradation.

We observe an interplay mechanism, i.e., co-improvement, between the reward model (understanding) and the fine-tuned model (generation) during self-improvement. The reward model evolves alongside the fine-tuned model is unique to MLLMs’ post-training, in contrast to traditional post-training settings where the reward model and the fine-tuned model are structurally decoupled
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. Motivated by this observation, we pose the following questions:
(1)
Cause of co-improvement
: why does generation-targeted self-improvement result in co-improvements in both branches? (2)
Co-degradation
: Does the synergy between generation and understanding manifest only as co-improvement, or can both branches also degrade together?

3.2.1
Learning Dynamics Theory of Generation and Understanding

To address these questions, we need to understand the interplay mechanism between generation and understanding. We adopt the learning-dynamics framework
[
27
]
and extend it to the multimodal setting. The self-improvement process in
Section

3.1
can be formally described as: at epoch
t
,

•

Prompt Sampling
: Sampling
m
prompts
\mathbf{y}_{0}
from a uniform distribution:
\mathbf{y}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{y}_{m}\sim\mathcal{U}
.

•

Image Generation
: For each prompts
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, generate
N
corresponding images sampled from the conditional distribution:

\mathbf{x}_{i,1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{i,N}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{y}_{i})

where
\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{y}_{i})
denotes the MLLM’s generation distribution given prompt
\mathbf{y}_{i}
.

•

Image Understanding
:
Generated images are self-evaluated by based on
\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{i,n})
, where
\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_{i,n})
denotes the MLLM’s understanding distribution given an image
\mathbf{x}_{i,n}
. Given a generation distribution
\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
, we define the understanding distribution from Bayes rule under uniform prior of
\mathbf{y}
. That is, for any
\mathbf{y}
,

\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}^{\prime})}

•

Update Generation
: Forming a self-improvement dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
with selected data
(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
, then update parameters via post-training strategy to
\theta_{t+1}
using
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

The analysis aims to explain the observed co-improvement during post-training by examining the training dynamics between
\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
and
\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
. We first assume the prompt and the image are represented by tokens selected from the vocabulary
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}
, respectively. Formally, the prompt is
\mathbf{y}=\{y_{i}\}\in\mathcal{Y}^{L}
and the image is
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{i}\}\in\mathcal{X}^{L}
. Here we assume single token
L=1
and following
[
27
]
, the analysis can be easily extended to the case where
L>1
. We update the parameters
\theta
with gradient descent on a single fine-tuning pair
(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
under a loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
:

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})

We now define the one-step dynamics of both generation and understanding distribution at epoch
t
as

\displaystyle\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}):=\log\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})-\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})

(Generation)

\displaystyle\Delta U_{t}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\log\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})-\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})

(Understanding)

We now relate the dynamics of understanding and generation in the subsequent proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let the logit network be
\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{y})=h_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})
and the generation distribution
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})=\sigma_{S}({\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{y})})
and let
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\sigma_{S}({\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{y})})
, where
\sigma_{S}(\cdot)
denotes the Softmax function.

•

At epoch
t
, the one-step dynamics of the
generation branch
is

\displaystyle\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=-\eta\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\eta^{2}\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}_{u})\|^{2}_{\text{op}}\bigr),

where
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{\mathbf{A}}={\rm trace}(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v})
,
\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}))\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}=\mathbf{x}-\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{y})
,
\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})=\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}_{u})^{\top}
is the empirical neural tangent kernel (eNTK) of the logit network, and
\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}
.

•

At epoch
t
, the one-step learning dynamics of the
understanding branch
decompose as

\displaystyle\Delta U_{t}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\eta\sum_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{y}}w_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{x})\big(\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}),\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{u})}-\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\eta^{2}|\mathcal{Y}|\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}_{u})\|^{2}_{\text{op}}\bigr)

where the weights
w_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})}{\sum_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}^{\prime})}=\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\geq 0
and
|\mathcal{Y}|
is prompt set size.

Proposition

1
shows when the prompt
\mathbf{y}
is similar to the supervised prompt
\mathbf{y}_{u}
, and other prompts
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
are less similar,
\|\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})\|_{F}\geq\|\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{y}_{u})\|_{F}
. In this case, both the generation and understanding branches are mainly influenced by the alignment
\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\ \mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})}
, leading to consistent update signs. When
\mathbf{y}
is dissimilar to
\mathbf{y}_{u}
, but there exists another prompt
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
that is more similar to
\mathbf{y}_{u}
, the generation update may be dominated by the positive inner product and the understanding update is influenced by a positively aligned term
\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}),\ \mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{u})}
, thus potentially preserving sign consistency between the two branches. This suggests that although generation and understanding are task-decoupled, they tend to follow similar learning dynamics, offering a perspective on why optimizing generation can also improve understanding.

Case Study.

To further illustrate
Proposition

1
, we consider a simplified linear network where
h_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the fixed token embedding of
\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathbf{W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times d}
. Then we have
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{y})=\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{y}})
. We consider SFT loss as
\mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})=-\mathbf{x}_{u}^{\top}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
and obtain

\displaystyle\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\eta\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})

\displaystyle\Delta U_{t}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=\eta\sum_{y^{\prime}\neq y}w_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{x})\Big(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})

where the residual alignment term
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{x}
.

In general, we consider prompt
\mathbf{y}
similar to fine-tuned prompt
\mathbf{y}_{u}
, while the remaining prompt is less similar. This leads to
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}>\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}
and tend to have
|r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})|\leq|r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})|
. Therefore, the sign of the learning dynamics for the generation and understanding branches tends to depend on the term
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
, leading to
\operatorname{sign}\left(\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\right)=\operatorname{sign}\left(\Delta U_{t}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\right)
. Specially, the sign can be determined by the residual alignment term:

•

Positive residual alignment, i.e.,
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\geq 0
leads to increase in likelihood of generating
\mathbf{x}
given
\mathbf{y}
and increase in likelihood of predicting
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{x}
.

•

Negative residual alignment, i.e.,
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\leq 0
leads to decrease in likelihood of generating
\mathbf{x}
given
\mathbf{y}
and decrease in likelihood of predicting
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{x}
.

Negative residual alignment may arise when the fine-tuning pair
(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
is mismatched, , potentially causing simultaneous degradation in both generation and understanding. We provide a detailed discussion in
Section

3.2.3
. When
\mathbf{y}
is dissimilar to the fine-tuned prompt
\mathbf{y}_{u}
, while the remaining prompt is more similar, the same conclusion holds: the sign of
\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
tends to be consistent with that of
\Delta U_{t}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
.

3.2.3
Generation–Understanding Interplay: Co-Degradation

While the previous results demonstrate co-improvement between generation and understanding,
Proposition

1
also implies the opposite direction: under certain conditions, both branches can degrade simultaneously. We consider the linear case discussed in
Section

3.2.1
, involving well-paired image–prompt data
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. Suppose we have a worse-aligned fine-tuning pair
(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
such that similar prompts
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{u}>0
but the corresponding images
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}_{u}
are entirely different. In this case, the residual alignment
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}r(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
tend to be negative, leading to an undesired decrease in both
\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
and
\Delta U_{t}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
. For example, consider the target sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
where
\mathbf{x}
is a cat image and
\mathbf{y}
is the prompt “generate a cat”. If the fine-tuning pair
(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})
consists of a prompt like “generate a white cat” but the images are completely misaligned e.g., a corrupted image.
Proposition

1
indicates low-quality data may lead to simultaneous degradation in both branches.

To empirically validate the above theoretical insights, we compare two settings: one using correct SFT labels (i.e., the better image from DPO), and the other using corrupted labels—assigning the same corrupted image with a fixed image token ID as the SFT label for each prompt. More setting details are provided in
Section

.

•

Additionally,

This observation reveals a core limitation: without external supervision, internal metrics like unification cannot distinguish co-improvement from co-degradation. This phenomenon parallels findings in LLMs, where reliance on internal signals, such as self-critique, can result in model collapse

A straightforward way to avoid co-degradation is to pre-validate data quality using external models (e.g., Qwen). For instance, Qwen can reliably identify corrupted samples, thereby preventing the use of low-quality data during self-improvement. However, this approach still depends on stronger external models. An interesting direction is to explore whether any internal signals within MLLMs can be used to detect such risks, an open question we leave for future work.

4
Curriculum-based Self-Improvement: Leveraging Co-Improvement for Better MLLMs

We next discuss how to further leverage the synergy between generation and understanding to promote self-improvement in MLLMs. While co-degradation is possible, it predominantly occurs in smaller models such as Janus-Pro-1B when trained with severely corrupted fine-tuning data. In contrast, larger models like Janus-Pro-7B (
Figure

5b
) are more robust to noisy supervision and less likely to produce low-quality data for self-improvemnet. Therefore, we focus on leveraging the more prevalent co-improvement phenomenon to further enhance MLLMs.

4.1
Method

The co-improvement observed in
Section

3.1
suggests that as MLLMs’ generation and understanding capabilities improve, they may become able to handle increasingly difficult samples. This motivates us to further enhance the offline fine-tuning strategy by introducing a
C
urriculum
L
earning-based
O
nline (CLO) post-training approach. Specifically, taking the construction of DPO data as an example,
training prompts can be categorized into three types based on whether they can be utilized by the generation or understanding branches during post-training:

Figure 6:
Outer ring shows only 49.7% of prompts (Ideal Set) are usable in pre-trained model. After SFT, improved generation and understanding allow 9.7% (inner ring) of previously FC/FR sets to be incorporated.

•

Ideal Set
: the candidate generations contain both samples that satisfy and not satisfy the prompt by the understanding branch—these are ideal for constructing preference pairs.

•

Fully Rejected (FR) Set
: all candidate generations are considered to fail the prompt—this may indicate that the prompt is too complex for the generation branch.

•

Fully Chosen (FC) Set
: all candidate generations are considered to satisfy the prompt—this could mean the prompt is simple for the generation branch, or that incorrect generations are too hard for the understanding branch to distinguish.

For the 3D Spatial subtask,
Figure

6
shows that with the pre-trained Janus-Pro model,
the standard offline post-training strategy can only utilize 49.7% of the available data (the ideal set), while a substantial portion remains unusable due to generation or understanding failures, namely, the FC and FR sets. After SFT, we observe that 9.7% of these previously unusable FC and FR samples become usable again, driven by the co-improvement in generation and understanding. This observation further validates the rationale for introducing curriculum learning into the post-training process, enabling dynamic expansion of the training set as model capabilities improve.

Figure

7
accordingly proposes a CLO-based fine-tuning strategy, in which both the training data and MLLM are updated during the post-training process. The curriculum is constructed in a prompt complexity-wise manner: training samples are introduced progressively from simple to difficult, starting with Ideal prompts and gradually incorporating FR and FC prompts. This training order naturally aligns with the evolving capabilities of generation and understanding, only when the model’s capacity improves can it extract useful training signals from FR and FC prompts. Moreover, it reflects the well-established principle of curriculum learning: learning from easy to hard examples, a strategy that has been proven effective in both human and machine learning
[
29
,
37
,
38
]
.

5
Related Work

Prior work typically focuses on enhancing specific capabilities of MLLMs, such as generation
[
16
,
39
]
, rather than emphasizing unification between generation and understanding. In these work, understanding branch primarily serves to provide additional details for generation prompt expansion. By combining enriched prompts with external reward models (e.g., BLIP
[
40
]
, Human Preference Model
[
41
,
42
]
), these studies improve generation quality of MLLMs. In contrast, we center on mitigating internal inconsistency within MLLMs, rather than merely improving single-task performance and aim to uncover why generation and understanding can co-improve during post-training, and leverages this property to better enhance alignment without any external signal.

Additionally, some studies
[
15
,
17
,
43
]
have explored self-improvement for MLLMs by applying methods such as SFT, DPO and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
44
]
. However, these methods do not explicitly explore the synergy between generation and understanding. Instead, they often treat them as separate tasks to be co-optimized, typically relying on external reward models or additional data construction. In contrast, our work focuses on self-improvement in MLLMs without relying on any external signals, and aims to explain the interaction mechanism that arises from the shared architecture between the generation (post-trained model) and understanding (reward model) branches, which has been overlooked in prior work. More importantly, our approach is not in conflict with existing methods. Instead, it can be viewed as a complementary and cost-free enhancement: once self-improvement is achieved, techniques such as Chain-of-Thought prompting
[
16
,
45
,
46
]
or QA-based supervision
[
17
]
can be further applied to boost MLLM performance.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This work serves as an exploratory prelude, offering a systematic investigation into the self-improvement capabilities of MLLMs. We show that the inherent asymmetry between generation and understanding offers a unique opportunity to improve both performance and unification of MLLMs. For future work, we plan to extend our analysis to a broader range of models and benchmarks. Specifically, we aim to: (1) compare self-contradiction and self-improvement behaviors across more unified architectures, such as Show-O
[
1
]
; and (2) investigate the effectiveness of internal versus external reward signals in driving co-improvement of generation and understanding. Furthermore, the current learning dynamics framework introduced in
Section

3.2.1
primarily focuses on single-token SFT scenarios. We plan to generalize it to multi-token settings and explore its applicability to a wider set of post-training strategies, including DPO.

B.1
Proof Details

Lemma 1
.

At epoch
t
, the one-step learning dynamics of the generation branch decompose as

\displaystyle\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=-\eta\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\eta^{2}\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}_{u})\|^{2}_{\text{op}}\bigr)

where
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{\mathbf{A}}={\rm trace}(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v})
,
\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}))\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}=\mathbf{x}-\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{y})
,
\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})=\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}_{u})^{\top}
is the empirical
neural tangent kernel (eNTK) of the logit network, and
\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}
.

Proof.

We first analyze the learning dynamic of understanding branch
\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
and we have

\displaystyle\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\log\pi_{\theta^{t+1}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})-\log\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}),\theta^{t+1}-\theta^{t}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\|\theta^{t+1}-\theta^{t}\|^{2}\bigr).

The gradient descent update with the learning rate
\eta
gives

\displaystyle\theta^{t+1}-\theta^{t}=-\eta\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}),\theta^{t+1}-\theta^{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\eta\langle(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z}^{t})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y})),\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\eta\langle\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}))^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z}^{t}),(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}_{u}))^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\eta\bigl\langle\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}))^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z}^{t})^{\top},\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}_{u}))^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=-\eta\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})}

where we let
\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})\coloneqq\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta^{t}}(\mathbf{y}_{u})^{\top}
. We explicitly write out the dependence on
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u}
and let
\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\coloneqq\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}))\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}=\mathbf{x}-\pi_{\theta^{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{y})
and
\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t};\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\bigr|_{\mathbf{z}^{t}}^{\top}
. We also denote
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{\mathbf{A}}={\rm trace}(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v})
.

With bounded gradient
\|\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{y}_{u})\|^{2}_{\text{op}}
, the second order term can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\bigl(\|\theta^{t+1}-\theta^{t}\|^{2}\bigr)=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\eta^{2}\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}_{u})\|^{2}_{\text{op}}\bigr)

Therefore, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\Delta G_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=-\eta\langle\mathcal{A}^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\mathcal{G}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{u})\rangle_{\mathcal{K}^{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}_{u})}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\eta^{2}\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{z}^{t}(\mathbf{y}_{u})\|^{2}_{\text{op}}\bigr)

The proof is complete.
∎
