Spatiotemporal Progression Patterns of Striatal Dopamine Depletion and Cerebral Hypoperfusion in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Yeeun Sun, Youngseok Choi, Chan Wook Park, Han Kyu Na, Hye Sun Lee, Yun Joong Kim, Young H. Sohn, Phil Hyu Lee, Jeyeon Lee, Seok Jong Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe identification of Parkinson's disease (PD) subtypes is crucial for predicting the disease course and designing personalized therapeutic strategies.ObjectivesThe aim of the study was to characterize the heterogeneity of the spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion in PD.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 708 patients with newly diagnosed PD and 50 healthy controls who underwent dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET scans. We applied subtype and stage inference (SuStaIn) to delineate three PD subtypes after quantifying the dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET data. We compared the baseline clinical features as well as the longitudinal motor and cognitive outcomes between subtypes.ResultsSubtype 1 (n = 406) initially presented with asymmetric dopamine loss in the posterior putamen, followed by dopaminergic deficits in other striatal subregions and, later, cerebral hypoperfusion. Subtype 2 (n = 205) exhibited diffuse and symmetric striatal dopamine loss in the early stages, followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in the mid‐stages. Subtype 3 (n = 89) initially showed the parieto‐occipital hypoperfusion, followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in other cortical areas and striatal dopamine depletion. Subtype 2 had an older age of onset, and subtype 3 had a younger age of onset and a lower prevalence of rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder. Time‐dependent Cox regression models showed that the risk of dementia conversion within a follow‐up period of 4 years was higher in subtypes 2 and 3 (hazard ratios 1.772 and 2.802, respectively) than subtype 1, after the confounding effects were adjusted. The risk of developing levodopa‐induced dyskinesia and freezing of gait was comparable between subtypes.ConclusionsThe spatiotemporal trajectories of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion on dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images could serve as a potential progression marker for cognitive decline in early‐stage PD. © 2026 The Author(s). Parkinson's disease (PD) is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder with individual variability in the clinical manifestations and rate of progression. 1 There have been several attempts to define distinct subtypes of PD to predict the disease course, understand its pathophysiology, and design personalized therapeutic strategies. 2 To date, the definition of PD subtypes has predominantly been based on demographic variables (ie, age), clinical features (ie, motor and nonmotor symptoms), 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 neuroimaging data, 9 , 10 , 11 and occasionally biological biomarkers and genetic information. 8 , 12 In our previous studies, PD subtypes were delineated based on the patterns of striatal dopamine depletion, 13 , 14 , 15 regional cerebral perfusion alterations, 16 and cognitive profiles, 17 , 18 as these variables could provide information on long‐term prognosis, including the development of motor complications and dementia in the early stages of the disease. However, most previous attempts to delineate PD subtypes have relied on predefined clinical data derived from cross‐sectional studies, which have inherent limitations because they cannot reflect the temporal evolution of input features that may dynamically affect disease progression across disease stages.

Therefore, developing a comprehensive picture that encompasses both phenotypic and temporal heterogeneity in individuals with PD would enable accurate and fine‐grained subtype classification. Recently, a probabilistic machine learning method known as subtype and stage inference (SuStaIn) was introduced to uncover disease phenotypes with distinct temporal progression patterns from cross‐sectional patient studies, 19 and this novel method has been widely applied to neurodegenerative disorders. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 In PD populations, several studies have identified distinct subtypes with common patterns of disease progression using the SuStaIn algorithm, 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 and most of them were based on the trajectories of subcortical and cortical atrophy. However, neuronal loss indexed by gray matter atrophy is not congruent with α‐synuclein accumulation and is a relatively late event in the pathogenesis of PD. 30 Instead, alterations in regional patterns of cerebral perfusion could represent a more sensitive neuroimaging biomarker to uncover the clinical heterogeneity in early‐stage PD. 16 , 31 Moreover, nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurodegeneration is one of the earliest events in PD progression. Therefore, in this study, we applied the SuStaIn algorithm to define PD subtypes based on the spatiotemporal progression patterns of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion using dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images collected at the time of PD diagnosis. We then investigated whether the SuStaIn‐derived PD subtypes differed in terms of baseline clinical characteristics, as well as longitudinal motor and cognitive prognosis.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively reviewed detailed medical records of 708 consecutive patients with newly diagnosed PD who visited the Movement Disorders outpatient clinic at Severance Hospital (n = 385, between January 2015 and November 2018) and Yongin Severance Hospital (n = 323, between June 2020 and September 2024). PD was diagnosed according to the Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria. 32 All patients underwent dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET scans at initial diagnosis and showed appropriate reductions in striatal dopamine transporter (DAT) availability on late‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET scans. Parkinsonian motor deficit severity was assessed using the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS‐III), and motor subtypes (ie, tremor‐dominant, postural instability/gait difficulty [PIGD], or indeterminate) were determined based on UPDRS scores. 33 Olfactory function was measured using the cross‐cultural smell identification test (CCSIT), and depression was evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). The presence of rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) was determined using the 13‐item RBD Screening Questionnaire with a cutoff score of 5/6. 34 The Korean version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination (K‐MMSE) was used to assess the general cognition in each patient. 35 At the initial assessment, 476 (67.2%) patients with PD underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery in the Korean language, that is, the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery, 36 which covers five cognitive domains: attention/working memory, language, visuospatial, memory, and frontal/executive function (Supporting Information Methods). Additionally, 50 healthy individuals with no history of neurological disease (mean age, 69.11 ± 8.01 years; female, 54.0%) voluntarily underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET scans and were included in the control group. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Yonsei University Severance Hospital (no.: 4–2022‐1421). The need for informed consent from patients with PD was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Quantitative Analyses of Dual‐Phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET Images

Acquisition of Dual‐Phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET Images

18F‐FP‐CIT PET was performed using a Discovery 600 PET/CT scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). Image acquisition was performed according to previously described methodology (Supporting Information Methods). 37

Early‐Phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET Images

Early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK) 38 and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). 39 Each positron emission tomography (PET) image was rigidly registered to the subject's corresponding T1‐weighted MRI using six degrees of freedom. The coregistered images were then spatially normalized to the Mayo Clinic Adult Lifespan Template (MCALT; https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mcalt) 40 using diffeomorphic registration implemented in ANTs. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) images were computed by normalizing voxel intensities to the median uptake in the cerebellar gray matter, which served as the reference region. 41 , 42 Regional SUVRs were subsequently extracted based on a locally defined anatomical parcellation developed in house. The selected cortical regions included the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes, as well as the insula, precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, and limbic areas.

To characterize disease‐related perfusion patterns, a PD‐related pattern (PDRP) was identified from early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images by applying scaled subprofile modeling with principal component analysis (SSM/PCA). To minimize potential circularity, the pattern was derived from an external cohort that was entirely independent of the present study cohort. Thirty patients with PD were randomly selected from this independent cohort and combined with 30 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls randomly selected from the present study cohort for pattern derivation. In this process, the first 10 principal components were retained, explaining 57.42% of the total variance in the dataset. This disease‐related spatial pattern was derived using the ScAnVP toolbox (https://feinsteinneuroscience.org/). The resulting pattern was then applied to the present study cohort by projecting the SUVR image from each participant onto it, yielding individual PDRP expression scores that reflected the degree of disease‐related perfusion abnormalities. 43

Late‐Phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET Images

Late‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK) 38 and in‐house software implemented in MATLAB R2021a. DAT availability in each striatal subregion (anterior caudate, posterior caudate, anterior putamen, posterior putamen, ventral putamen, and ventral striatum) was estimated using previously established methodologies (Supporting Information Methods). 44 Additionally, asymmetry index was calculated based on DAT availability in the posterior putamen using the following formula: (less‐affected side – more‐affected side)/(less‐affected side + more affected side).

Subtyping and Progression Patterns Modeling Using Dual‐Phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET Images

After the dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images were quantified, we applied SuStaIn to characterize the heterogeneity of spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns of striatal dopamine depletion and regional cerebral hypoperfusion in PD. 19 The model was implemented using the ZscoreSustain module from the pySuStaIn framework (https://github.com/ucl-pond/pySuStaIn), which infers subtype‐specific sequences of biomarker abnormality events by assuming linear accumulation of z scores between discrete thresholds.

A total of 20 region‐specific imaging biomarkers were derived and utilized as input features for SuStaIn modeling: eight from early‐phase PET images (reflecting cerebral hypoperfusion) and 12 from late‐phase PET images (reflecting dopamine depletion). The cortical regions included the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes, as well as the insula, precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, and limbic areas. The striatal regions included the more‐ and less‐affected anterior caudate, posterior caudate, anterior putamen, posterior putamen, ventral putamen, and ventral striatum.

Data from each participant were arranged into a 758 × 20 input matrix (subjects × biomarkers). z scores were calculated for each biomarker using data from 50 healthy controls, after the effects of age and sex were regressed. To ensure directional consistency across biomarkers, all values were sign‐inverted so that lower values (ie, greater dopamine depletion or hypoperfusion) resulted in higher z scores. 23 , 24 , 25 , 29 , 45 Three z score events (z = 1, 3, and 5) were defined per biomarker, with a maximum threshold (Zmax) set to 7. These settings enabled the model to capture multistage abnormality progression across regions and simultaneously infer both subtype membership and individual disease stages. The optimal number of subtypes was determined by evaluating model performance across a range of possible subtype solutions using 10‐fold cross‐validation. Specifically, we examined the cross‐validation information criterion (CVIC), averaged out‐of‐sample log likelihoods, and convergence patterns of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations. The model with the lowest CVIC and a stable log‐likelihood plateau was selected as the most appropriate. 19

Assessment of Disease Progression in Patients with PD

After the diagnosis of PD, the patients visited the outpatient clinic at 3‐month intervals. Two movement disorder specialists from Severance Hospital (P.H.L. and Y.H.S.) assessed the conversion to dementia or development of levodopa‐induced dyskinesia (LID) and freezing of gait (FOG) during the follow‐up period (>3 years; Supporting Information Methods). 16 Time‐to‐event was defined as the time from the diagnosis of PD to the occurrence of dementia, LID, or FOG, or to the last clinic visit for patients who did not exhibit any of these outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic characteristics, level of cognitive performance, striatal DAT availability, and regional SUVR values on the early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images were compared between PD subtypes using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Pearson's χ2 tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparison corrections after one‐way ANOVA. Pearson's correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the relationship between several clinical parameters (ie, UPDRS‐III scores, DAT availability in the posterior putamen, PDRP expression, and K‐MMSE scores) and SuStaIn stages in each PD subtype. To test whether these correlations differed across subtypes, we compared the r values using Fisher's r‐to‐z transformation. Correlation coefficients were transformed into z‐values, and group‐wise differences were assessed using a standard normal distribution.

The log‐rank test was used to compare the risk of dementia conversion or development of LID and FOG between PD subtypes. Before conducting Cox regression analyses to assess the effect of PD subtypes on dementia conversion, we tested the proportional‐hazards assumption by including interaction terms between subtypes and follow‐up time with a statistical significance of P < 0.1. The time‐dependent covariate analysis results were statistically significant between a (subtype 3 – subtype 1) × time interaction term (P = 0.088), suggesting that the proportional‐hazards assumption was not valid. Thus, we used a time‐dependent Cox regression model to assess the effects of PD subtypes on dementia conversion based on a cutoff time of 4 years (ie, follow‐up period within 4 years vs. >4 years) because the Kaplan–Meier curves for subtypes 1 and 3 intersected at this time point. The effects of potential confounding factors such as age, sex, years of education, and DAT availability in the anterior putamen were also assessed using a time‐dependent Cox regression model. Additionally, Cox regression analyses were performed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the development of either LID or FOG based on PD subtype, while adjusting for age, sex, DAT availability in the posterior putamen, and l‐dopa‐equivalent dose 46 (ie, the dose of PD medications at the time of LID or FOG onset or at the last visit to the outpatient clinic in patients without LID or FOG). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the R software package (version 4.0, http://www.r-project.org/). Results with a 2‐tailed P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Subtyping and Stage Inference of Individuals with PD

To determine the optimal number of subtypes, we evaluated the model performance across a range of candidate subtype solutions using 10‐fold cross‐validation. The CVIC showed a substantial decrease up to the three‐subtype model and reached its minimum at three subtypes, suggesting that this is the configuration that best fits the data (Fig. 1A). Correspondingly, the log likelihood increased to three subtypes and reached a plateau thereafter (Fig. 1B), further supporting the selection of three subtypes as the optimal number. To assess convergence and model stability, we examined the MCMC trace plots for each subtype solution (Fig. 1C). The MCMC trace stabilized over iterations for the three‐subtype model, with consistent trajectories across chains, indicating reliable parameter estimation. In addition, there was no further improvement in likelihood beyond the three‐subtype model. Based on these findings, we selected a three‐subtype model for all subsequent analyses.

SuStaIn analysis optimally delineated three subtypes of PD with distinct spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion (Fig. 1D–G). Out of the 708 patients with newly diagnosed PD, 406 (57.3%) were assigned to subtype 1, which initially presented with selective and asymmetric dopamine loss in the posterior putamen, followed by dopaminergic deficits in the associative and limbic striatum and, later, cerebral hypoperfusion progressing from the occipital region to the temporal, frontal, and parietal regions. Two hundred and five patients (29.0%) were assigned to subtype 2, which exhibited diffuse and symmetric striatal dopamine loss in the early stages, followed by cerebral hypoperfusion progressing from the occipital region to the temporal, frontal, and parietal regions in the mid‐stages. Finally, 89 patients (12.6%) were assigned to subtype 3, which was characterized by cerebral hypoperfusion in the parieto‐occipital regions in the early stages, followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in other cortical areas and striatal dopamine depletion. The remaining 8 patients (1.1%) with PD were designated as stage 0, as their imaging findings (ie, mild decreases in DAT availability in the posterior putamen and relatively preserved cortical uptake on early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images) were not sufficiently abnormal to allocate them to any of the three subtypes.

To evaluate the robustness of the inferred subtype structure and stage estimates, we additionally performed a cross‐cohort validation analysis using the two independent cohorts included in this study. A SuStaIn model trained on the Severance Hospital cohort was applied to the Yongin Severance Hospital cohort to assign the subtype and stage to each individual, and the results were compared to those obtained from a SuStaIn model trained on the Yongin Severance Hospital cohort for the same individuals. The agreement between subtype assignments was substantial (Cohen's κ = 0.826). In addition, stage estimates showed an extremely strong linear relationship between models (γ = 0.994, P < 0.001), indicating a high level of consistency in inferred disease stages. The majority of subjects were consistently assigned to the same subtype, and stage estimates closely followed a one‐to‐one correspondence across models (Fig. S1).

Demographic Characteristics of the Study Participants

The baseline demographic characteristics of the patients included in each PD subtype are presented in Table 1. Subtype 1 included a higher proportion of female patients (63.5%), and these patients had a lower level of educational attainment. Subtype 2 was characterized by an older age of onset (72.12 ± 7.98), relatively lower CCSIT scores (5.75 ± 2.57) and lower cognitive composite scores of attention/working memory (−0.43 ± 0.80), and visuospatial function domains (−0.90 ± 1.84). Subtype 3 was characterized by a younger age of onset (63.89 ± 12.32), a lower proportion of female patients (30.3%), a lower prevalence of RBD (31.5%), hypertension (34.8%) and dyslipidemia (20.2%), and relatively higher K‐MMSE scores (26.89 ± 3.52). There were no significant differences in baseline UPDRS‐III scores, motor subtypes, and BDI scores.

Dual‐Phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET Imaging Analyses of PD Subtypes

Striatal DAT Availability

DAT availability in the posterior putamen was compared between subtypes. Subtype 1 exhibited relatively preserved DAT availability in the ventral putamen (2.54 ± 0.85) and ventral striatum (3.14 ± 1.05) compared to other subtypes, whereas subtype 2 exhibited more severely decreased DAT availability in the associative striatum (anterior caudate, 2.70 ± 0.73; posterior caudate, 1.71 ± 0.48; anterior putamen, 2.99 ± 0.96) and symmetric striatal dopamine loss (asymmetry index, 0.075 ± 0.069; Table 1 and Fig. S2).

Regional Cerebral Perfusion

Subtype 1 exhibited relatively preserved cerebral perfusion in the entire cortices and a lower PDRP expression (1.074 ± 3.479) on early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT images compared to subtype 2 (PDRP expression, 1.849 ± 4.107, P < 0.001) and subtype 3 (PDRP expression, 1.274 ± 3.919, P < 0.001). Subtype 3 exhibited more extensive cerebral hypoperfusion in the frontal (SUVR, 0.911 ± 0.040), parietal (SUVR, 0.886 ± 0.048), and temporal (SUVR, 0.848 ± 0.045) regions compared to other subtypes (Table 1 and Fig. S2).

Correlation Analyses Between Clinical Parameters and Stages in Each Subtype

Correlation analyses demonstrated that stages of each subtype correlated well with several parameters for disease severity, including UPDRS‐III scores (subtype 1, γ = 0.348, P < 0.001; subtype 2, γ = 0.534, P < 0.001; subtype 3, γ = 0.352, P < 0.001), DAT availability in the posterior putamen (subtype 1, γ = −0.564, P < 0.001; subtype 2, γ = −0.793, P < 0.001; subtype 3, γ = −0.450, P = 0.010), PDRP expression (subtype 1, γ = 0.343, P < 0.001; subtype 2, γ = 0.624, P < 0.001; subtype 3, γ =0.495, P < 0.001), and K‐MMSE scores (subtype 1, γ = −0.419, P < 0.001; subtype 2, γ = −0.380, P < 0.001; subtype 3, γ = −0.430, P < 0.001; Table 2 and Fig. S3). To test whether the strength of these associations differed between subtypes, group‐wise comparisons of the correlation coefficients were conducted using Fisher's r‐to‐z transformation. Significant differences were observed between subtypes 1 and 2 for UPDRS‐III (P = 0.007), DAT availability (P < 0.001), and PDRP expression (P < 0.001), and between subtypes 2 and 3 for DAT availability (P < 0.001). Other comparisons did not reach statistical significance.

Longitudinal Cognitive and Motor Outcomes Based on the PD Subtypes

Conversion to Dementia

During the follow‐up period (6.33 ± 1.88 years), 45 out of 161 patients with subtype 1 PD (28.0%), 39 out of 99 patients with subtype 2 PD (39.4%), and 13 out of 54 patients with subtype 3 PD (24.1%) developed dementia. The log‐rank test demonstrated that patients with subtype 2 PD had a higher risk of developing dementia than those with subtype 1 (P Log‐rank = 0.023) or subtype 3 (P Log‐rank = 0.037), whereas the risk of dementia conversion was comparable between subtypes 1 and 3 (P Log‐rank = 0.504; Fig. 2). However, the Kaplan–Meier curves showed that the decline in dementia‐free survival was relatively steep initially for subtype 3 but became less steep 4 years after the diagnosis of PD. Moreover, the Kaplan–Meier curves for subtypes 1 and 3 intersected each other at this time point.

Development of Levodopa‐Induced Dyskinesia

During the follow‐up period, LID developed in 21 out of 155 patients (13.5%) with subtype 1 PD, in 9 out of 92 patients (9.8%) with subtype 2 PD, and in 11 out of 52 patients (21.2%) with subtype 3 PD. The log‐rank test demonstrated that each PD subtype had a comparable risk of developing LID (subtype 1 vs. subtype 2, P Log‐rank = 0.373; subtype 1 vs. subtype 3, P Log‐rank = 0.216), although subtype 3 tended to have a higher risk of developing LID than subtype 2 (P Log‐rank = 0.068; Fig. S4). Cox regression analyses also demonstrated that all PD subtypes had a comparable risk of developing LID (P = 0.342) after potential confounding factors were adjusted (Table S1).

Development of Freezing of Gait

During the follow‐up period, FOG developed in 26 out of 157 patients (16.6%) with subtype 1 PD, in 16 out of 95 patients (16.8%) with subtype 2 PD, and in 7 out of 51 patients (13.7%) with subtype 3 PD. The log‐rank test demonstrated that each PD subtype had a comparable risk of developing FOG (subtype 1 vs. subtype 2, P Log‐rank = 0.986; subtype 1 vs. subtype 3, P Log‐rank = 0.573; and subtype 2 vs. subtype 3, P Log‐rank = 0.430; Fig. S4). Cox regression analyses also demonstrated that all PD subtypes had a comparable risk of developing FOG (P = 0.893) after potential confounding factors were adjusted (Table S1).

Discussion

The present study aimed to delineate subtypes with distinct spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion based on a population of 708 patients with early‐stage PD. The major findings were as follows: (1) The SuStaIn analysis defined three subtypes of PD. These included subtype 1 (n = 406), which initially exhibits selective and asymmetric dopamine loss in the posterior putamen followed by dopaminergic deficits in other striatal subregions and cerebral hypoperfusion in later stages; subtype 2 (n = 205), which initially exhibits diffuse and symmetric striatal dopamine loss followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in mid‐stages; and subtype 3 (n = 89), which initially exhibits parieto‐occipital hypoperfusion followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in other cortical areas and striatal dopamine loss. (2) Subtype 1 was characterized by relatively preserved cerebral perfusion on early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT images. Subtype 2 patients were older at the time of onset and exhibited diffuse and symmetric striatal dopamine loss. Subtype 3 was characterized by a younger age of onset, a lower prevalence of RBD, and more extensive cerebral hypoperfusion. (3) Patients with subtypes 2 and 3 had a higher risk of developing dementia within 4 years of the initial diagnosis compared to those with subtype 1. The risks of developing LID and FOG were comparable among all subtypes. These findings suggest that the spatiotemporal trajectories of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion on dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT images could serve as a potential progression marker for cognitive (but not motor) symptoms in early‐stage PD.

Lewy bodies (LB), abnormal aggregates of α‐synuclein, are a pathological hallmark of degenerating neurons in the brains of patients with PD. 47 Ample evidence suggests that α‐synuclein aggregates exhibit prion‐like cell‐to‐cell transmission, 48 , 49 and LB pathology can propagate along the predictable anatomical pattern. 50 , 51 In this regard, in vivo imaging of LB pathology could be useful as a biomarker for tracking PD progression. Furthermore, α‐synuclein imaging may potentially elucidate the neural correlates of the clinical heterogeneity that characterizes PD based on the extent or spatial distribution of LB. 52 However, there are currently no validated PET radiotracers for α‐synuclein. Instead, indicative imaging biomarkers such as functional imaging using established radiotracers (eg, 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose [18F‐FDG] PET 53 , 54 and cerebral perfusion scan31) or predictive models of α‐synuclein spread based on brain MRI data 55 , 56 have been used to explore disease progression. The present study used dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images, which can provide information on both striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral perfusion in a single PET session at the time of PD diagnosis. 31 , 57 Nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurodegeneration and changes in regional cerebral perfusion are the earliest events in PD 58 and could therefore become sensitive neuroimaging markers for characterizing the spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns of neurodegeneration in patients with early‐stage PD.

It is well established that dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra are not uniformly affected by PD, 59 and that selective involvement in the posterior putamen as well as asymmetry of the interhemispheric dopaminergic denervation are the core pathological features of the nigrostriatal pathway in patients with PD. 60 , 61 These spatial patterns of striatal dopamine depletion appear to be consistent over the course of the illness and specific to each patient with PD. 62 , 63 In other words, there is individual variability in the anteroposterior gradient and side‐to‐side asymmetry of striatal dopamine deficits in PD, providing additional clinical information about age at onset, 15 , 64 dopaminergic responsiveness, 60 , 65 nonmotor symptom burden, 66 and long‐term motor and cognitive prognosis. 13 , 60 In terms of topographic profiles for cerebral metabolic or perfusion alterations in PD, some evidence indicates that decreased cortical metabolism or blood flow in the occipital region extends to the parietotemporal regions, 67 , 68 whereas other studies suggest that cerebral hypometabolism or hypoperfusion extends from the lateral temporal and frontal to the parieto‐occipital cortices. 16 , 54 Generally, posterior cortical hypoperfusion or hypometabolism is considered a good predictor of early dementia conversion in patients with PD. 67 , 69 In the present study, we applied the SuStaIn algorithm to delineate three distinct subtypes of PD based on the sequence and pattern of cerebral hypoperfusion and striatal dopamine depletion on dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images. Approximately 60% of the patients were classified into subtype 1, with the typical patterns of striatal dopamine loss followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in later stages; approximately 30% were classified into subtype 2, with relatively diffuse striatal dopamine loss followed by cerebral hypoperfusion in mid‐stages; and another 10% were classified into subtype 3, with early parieto‐occipital hypoperfusion along with a selective dopamine loss in the posterior putamen. The stages of each subtype inferred using SuStaIn analysis showed significant correlations with several parameters of disease severity, supporting the validity of the analysis.

Subtype 2 was characterized by an older age of onset and worse cognitive performance at baseline, as well as more diffuse and symmetric nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration compared to other subtypes. These findings are in line with those of our previous studies, suggesting that age at onset may be one of the major determinants of heterogeneous imaging findings and clinical features in PD. 15 , 16 , 60 Furthermore, subtype 2 exhibited relatively earlier alterations in cerebral perfusion and had a higher risk of dementia conversion within 4 years than subtype 1. Ample evidence indicates that reduced cerebral perfusion is an early feature that precedes cognitive decline in patients with PD. 58 , 70 , 71 In particular, alterations in posterior cortical activity may reflect cholinergic denervation secondary to forebrain neuronal losses, 72 which is an important contributor to cognitive dysfunction in PD. 73 Additionally, the more severe dopamine deficits in the caudate and anterior putamen observed in subtype 2 may play a role in the early progression to dementia. 13 , 74 Taken together, our findings suggest that subtype 2 may represent a relatively diffuse malignant subtype compared to subtype 1, which included the majority of patients.

Compared to other subtypes, subtype 3 was characterized by a younger age of onset. In addition, this subtype had a lower prevalence of RBD and exhibited more extensive cerebral hypoperfusion prior to nigrostriatal degeneration. 51 Several mechanisms have been proposed to underlie cerebral perfusion changes in patients with PD. Dopamine loss in the nigrostriatal pathway, the earliest event for motor manifestations, may lead to remapping of the corticostriatal network 75 and subsequently result in the disruption of regional cerebral perfusion in PD. 31 , 41 Alternatively, dysregulation in other neurotransmitter systems (eg, the cholinergic system 73 ), the presence of cortical LB pathology 76 or other copathologies, 77 and genetic factors 78 may contribute to alterations in cerebral perfusion, which may be more relevant in subtype 3. This subtype also showed a slightly different spreading pattern of cerebral hypoperfusion than the other subtypes, with the parietal region affected relatively earlier. In terms of cognitive prognosis, subtype 3 tended to have a lower risk of dementia conversion than subtype 2 when the data were not adjusted for age as a confounding effect; however, when potential confounding factors (including age) were included in the statistical model as covariates, the risk of developing dementia was similar for subtypes 2 and 3. Moreover, the risk of dementia conversion within 4 years was even higher for subtype 3 patients compared to those with subtype 1. Our findings highlight the critical role of age in the future cognitive decline of patients with PD 79 and suggest that age should be included as a covariate in the analysis of the risk of dementia. Furthermore, when cognitive decline is present in relatively younger patients with PD, its neuroimaging correlates may mirror those observed in older patients. 80 Our findings also suggest that subtype 3 may be a subtype that is not clearly classified according to the brain‐first and body‐first model of PD, although this interpretation remains speculative in the absence of α‐synuclein biomarkers or pathological confirmation. 81

In terms of motor prognosis, we found that the risk of developing LID and FOG did not differ between the PD subtypes. In fact, the underlying pathophysiology of LID and FOG is somewhat complex, with nigrostriatal dopamine depletion at the presynaptic level as the primary predisposing factor for LID, and plastic alterations of the basal ganglia at the postsynaptic level as a later event being also implicated. 82 Regarding FOG, neurodegeneration across multiple levels of the locomotor system, including the cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and cerebellar regions, is known to be involved. 83 Therefore, the pattern of early changes in striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral perfusion may not be a sensitive indicator of the risk of developing LID and FOG in patients with newly diagnosed PD. Actually, there was no difference in DAT availability in the posterior putamen between the subtypes, which may play a crucial role in determining the timing of LID or FOG onset. 84 , 85

Beyond these subtype‐specific findings, an important consideration is how such probabilistic classifications can be translated into clinical practice. SuStaIn‐derived subtypes should be interpreted as probabilistic representations of disease trajectories rather than discrete categories. Accordingly, their clinical utility lies in risk stratification rather than deterministic classification. Furthermore, the trained SuStaIn model can be applied to unseen individual data to infer subtype assignment and disease stage, enabling patient‐specific positioning along subtype‐specific trajectories. This framework can provide information on a relative risk of future cognitive decline and the expected disease course in a more personalized manner.

Our study has some limitations. First, the SuStaIn model assumes independence between biomarkers; however, in reality, many biomarkers co‐vary because of shared biological pathways. Striatal dopamine depletion and altered cerebral perfusion may be closely linked. 54 Furthermore, the same z score event threshold was applied across multimodal data, which may not fully account for modality‐specific variability. Second, although DAT scans are a widely used imaging modality in clinical practice, DAT tracers may not be a reliable surrogate marker for dopaminergic nerve terminal density in early‐stage PD because of the possibility of DAT downregulation. Additionally, although growing evidence suggests that early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images resemble 18F‐FDG PET or perfusion imaging, 31 , 86 further validation is required to demonstrate the sensitivity of early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET images as a surrogate marker for cerebral perfusion. In this study, of the 708 patients with PD, 12 (1.7%) patients additionally underwent 18F‐FDG PET scans to exclude the possibility of atypical parkinsonism within 6 months of 18F‐FP‐CIT PET scans. Forty‐eight healthy controls also voluntarily underwent 18F‐FDG PET scans for research purpose. We found a strong correlation between PDRP expression measured from the early‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT PET and 18F‐FDG PET images (γ = 0.628, P < 0.001; Fig. S5), which is consistent with the previous study results. 31 Third, the data on dementia or FOG onset may have been inaccurate for some patients, although we attempted to capture the occurrence of these events at every visit. Fourth, this probabilistic machine learning method can be sensitive to all sorts of influences. Although we made an effort to minimize the variations in the image acquisition process throughout the study period, subtle differences in image acquisition or clinical evaluations between institutions may have affected the results of this study.

In conclusion, this study delineated three PD subtypes with distinct spatiotemporal trajectories of neurodegenerative processes using dual‐phase 18F‐FP‐CIT imaging. Our findings suggest that the evolutionary patterns of striatal dopamine depletion and cerebral hypoperfusion may serve as potential markers of disease progression and cognitive prognosis in patients with PD.
