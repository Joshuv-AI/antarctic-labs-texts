SPIRAL: A versatile online single time-point circadian analysis platform for rice
Authors: Yabo Shi, Li Yao, Zhenxian Han, Yingke Ma, Yufeng Xu, Xingwei Wang, Zhaoxiong Jiang, Shuyu Wang, Mian Zhou, Dong Zou, Zhang Zhang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The circadian clock synchronises plant physiology with environmental oscillations to promote plant fitness. The commonly-used methods for rhythm monitoring in dicots include rhythmic leaf movement tracking and luciferase-based imaging. For monocots, however, the leaf erectness makes these methods ineffective. Leveraging over 11,000 transcriptome samples, the circadian time-course profiling, the simulation-based algorithm optimisation, and the experimental validation, we developed SPIRAL, an online single time-point circadian analysis platform for rice and unexpectedly revealed a ∼28-hour endogenous rhythm in V4-stage Nipponbare leaves, making period-matched or long-day photoperiods comparatively more permissive growth conditions for the assayed experimental system. We demonstrated the versatility of SPIRAL by quantifying global rhythm sensitivity to abiotic stresses, pinpointing when nitrogen deficiency starts to perturb rhythms, a temporal resolution surpassing that of the state-of-the-art methods, and identifying candidate components connecting the clock to stresses through factorial analyses. Online deployment of SPIRAL enables platform-independent analysis of public and user-supplied rice transcriptomes to accelerate discoveries in crop adaptation and chronoculture.

INTRODUCTION

The Earth’s rotation on its own axis results in the daily oscillation of various environmental factors. Rather than passively responding to these daily fluctuations, most organisms possess an intrinsic circadian clock to quantify time and proactively anticipate the diurnal changes. Circadian rhythms generated by the circadian clock can be sustained even when the rhythmic environmental signals are removed. This capacity to align their growth and development with the recurring environmental changes through anticipation enables organisms to efficiently utilise resources and energy, evade or mitigate the impact of unfavourable conditions, and markedly enhance their adaptation to the dwelling environment (1–3).

Being photosynthetic organisms, plants are strongly influenced by the daily oscillation of light and temperature. The plant circadian clock has a profound impact on plant physiologies, regulating the rhythmic expression of one-third of the expressed Arabidopsis genes (4) as well as a similar proportion of genes in crops like rice, maize, soybean and tomato (5–9). Seminal studies over the past few years discovered the causal relationship between the plant circadian clock and the latitudinal adaptation of tomato (10) and soybean (11), suggesting the necessity to explore the interplay between the crop circadian clock and environmental adaptation for a better application in agriculture (12).

To initiate such explorations, the monitoring of plant circadian rhythms is unarguably a pivotal step. For dicotyledonous plants like Arabidopsis, the tracking of rhythmic leaf movement (13) and the recording of the rhythmic luciferase activity driven by the promoters of oscillatory genes (14) are the two most widely-used methods, owing to their non-invasive and relatively high-throughput features. However, for monocotyledonous plants like rice, wheat and maize, these two methods become ineffective (15). The erectness of rice leaves makes rhythmic leaf movement practically undetectable. It also drastically shrinks the leaf area available for robust and reliable imaging of the luciferase activity.

Aside from these two non-invasive methods, the invasive circadian time-course expression analysis of rhythmic genes remains an alternative. However, this approach is labour-intensive and suffers from a high cost associated with the expression analysis of a large number of samples from the time-course experiments. Consequently, our knowledge of the interplay between rice circadian clock and the environment still remains limited. On the other hand, however, rapid development in transcriptomics technologies over the past two decades has led to the accumulation and public release of over 11,000 rice transcriptomes. Yet, the majority of these publicly available rice transcriptome datasets are not designed or suitable for conventional circadian analysis.

The molecular timetable method originally developed in the mammalian system realises the analysis of the global rhythm using the genome-wide expression profile from a single time-point dataset (16). Its design principle relies on the identification of a set of time-indicating genes whose expression patterns display robust circadian rhythms under the control condition with peak expression time (phase) spanning across a day (16). Sorting the normalized expression levels of these time-indicating genes in a specific sample based on their phases under the control condition in ascending order enables the assessment of the global rhythm of this sample (16). Comparing the global rhythms from samples with different treatments or from different genotypes can reveal whether these treatments or genotypes may affect the global rhythm. Therefore, the molecular timetable method could turn the abovementioned over 11,000 archived rice transcriptome datasets into an invaluable treasure for rice circadian clock studies.

In this study, we systematically surveyed over 11,000 publicly available rice transcriptome samples through re-mapping to the rice genome and uniform re-normalisation to identify the most representative rice cultivar and culture condition to maximise the generalisability of the subsequently derived rice molecular timetable. Through circadian time-course RNA-seq analysis, simulation-based algorithm optimisation, data-based validation and online deployment, we developed SPIRAL, an online single time-point circadian analysis platform for rice. We demonstrated its versatility through comprehensive analysis of abiotic stress related transcriptomes and validation using wet-lab experiments. We surprisingly found that the endogenous circadian rhythm in V4-stage leaves of Nipponbare, the most widely-used rice cultivar, cycles with a period of ∼28 hours a day. The SPIRAL-enabled analysis of global rhythms of publicly available rice transcriptome samples as well as user-supplied datasets in a platform-independent manner may expedite the studies of the rice circadian clock.

RESULTS

Survey of publicly available rice transcriptomes and algorithm optimisation for determination of rhythmicity

The key to the molecular timetable method is the identification of the time-indicating genes whose expression patterns show robust circadian rhythms (17). However, the rhythmic genes derived from different tissues under different conditions can be different. Although we have previously shown that the molecular timetable based on the time-indicating genes derived from soybean leaves could still be used for the analysis of soybean root transcriptomes, their performance might not be the best (9). In light of the heterogeneity in the experimental conditions of publicly available rice transcriptome datasets, identification of the most representative experimental condition could maximise the scope of application of the time-indicating genes.

We retrieved a total of 11,116 rice (Oryza sativa L.) transcriptome samples from NCBI using the E-utilities tools (18) and systematically summarised the meta-information of the RNA-seq and microarray datasets based on the key features related to the specific experimental conditions including the cultivars, tissues and developmental stages. Among the samples with clear description on the specific experimental conditions, we found that O. sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare is undoubtedly the most representative cultivar, accounting for 33.01% of all the samples, almost ten times as frequent as that of the second most frequently-used cultivar (fig. S1A). The tissues used for transcriptome sequencing mainly include leaves, roots, shoots, and whole plants. Among them, leaf accounts for as much as 41.21% of all samples, making leaf the most representative tissue (fig. S1B). Although leaf is the most widely used tissue for transcriptomics analysis of rice, the specific developmental condition used varies among different samples. Finally, we found that the V4 stage (14th day after germination of the seed) is the most frequently-used stage when the Nipponbare leaves are used as the experimental material (fig. S1C). In most cases, multiple samples are grouped to one project reported by one research group. Therefore, besides the sample-level survey, we also performed project-level summarisation. Consistent with the conclusion based on the sample-level survey, the project-level summarisation also identified Nipponbare, leaf and V4 as the most representative cultivar, tissue and developmental stage respectively (fig. S1, D to F).

Using these representative experimental conditions, we performed circadian time-course RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 1A). The rice seedlings of the Nipponbare cultivar were grown under the diurnal condition (16 hours light/8 hours dark) till the V4 stage. At the end of the 14th day, the photoperiod was switched to the constant light condition. After the one-day transition under the constant light condition to mitigate the impact from the diurnal photoperiod, the mature leaves were harvested every 4 hours for 2 days starting at CT24 (Time under the constant light condition) to investigate the status of the endogenous circadian rhythm. Subsequent RNA-seq analysis generated 232.6 G clean data with stable and high sequencing quality (fig. S2A), expected GC content distribution (fig. S2B), minimal library bias (fig. S2C), high read mapping efficiency (fig. S2D) and good reproducibility among the three biological replicates demonstrated by the correlation heatmap (Fig. 1B). Global analysis of this RNA-seq dataset through principal component analysis (PCA) also revealed good reproducibility manifested by the closely clustered biological replicates (refer to the circles by the solid color lines in Fig. 1C). Surprisingly, samples taken 28 hours apart were clustered closer than those taken 24 hours apart (refer to the circles by the solid black lines in Fig. 1C). This implies that the period of the endogenous circadian rhythm of Nipponbare leaves might be 28 hours instead of 24 hours.

Identification of rhythmic genes and derivation of period, phase, amplitude and average expression level from their expression profiles are essential to the selection of the time-indicating genes. Diverse analysis algorithms have been used in the circadian research field. An online analysis tool called BioDare (19) and its successor BioDare2 (20) have assembled several widely-used algorithms such as fast Fourier transform–nonlinear least squares (FFT-NLLS), mFourfit, maximum entropy spectral analysis (MESA), Enright periodogram (EPR), Lomb–Scargle periodogram (LS) and spectrum resampling (SR). A previous comparison study found that a Cosinor-based nonlinear regression method has a better overall performance compared to the abovementioned algorithms (9). In this method, a cosine wave combined with a linear trend was fit to the expression profile through nonlinear regression. Therefore, detrending was performed simultaneously with the fitting of the cosine wave. However, depending on the specific expression profile, detrending before the nonlinear fitting of the cosine wave could occasionally have a poor performance (fig. S3, A and B). This could be due to the fact that the expression profile of certain genes did not cover two full cycles of oscillation. In these cases, truncation of the last one or two sampling time points could even improve the performance of the nonlinear fitting (Fig. 1D).

Considering the prevalence of these situations in the RNA-seq dataset (fig. S4), it is important to test whether simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting or detrending before nonlinear fitting has a better overall performance. First, we generated a noise-free simulated expression profile dataset (“Sim68” in Fig. 1E) using the cosinusoidal parameters of period, phase, amplitude, and intercept and slope of the linear trend derived from the RNA-seq dataset (“Raw_data68” in Fig. 1E). The benefit of generating the simulated dataset based on the actual wet-lab data lies in its ability to capture the statistical distribution of the cosinusoidal parameters, providing a better representation of the empirical findings. The periods used for the generation of the simulated dataset serve as the ground truth to test the performance of different variants of the algorithm (“CK” in Fig. 1E). To test the effect of truncation of the last two sampling time points, the simulated dataset was divided into three subsets. “Sim68”, “Sim64” and “Sim60” contained the samples with sampling time up to 68 hours, 64 hours or 60 hours respectively. Different levels of Gaussian noise (10%, 15%, and 20%) were added during the simulation which generated “Sim68_noise”, “Sim64_noise” and “Sim60_noise” to simulate the biological and technical noises.

To test the performance of detrending before nonlinear fitting, the linear detrending procedure was applied to “Sim68_noise”, “Sim64_noise”, and “Sim60_noise” to generate “Sim68_noise_detrend”, “Sim64_noise_detrend”, and “Sim60_noise_detrend” respectively. Nonlinear least squares regression (NLLS) was then performed on these datasets for the estimation of the periods (“data68/64/60_NLLS”, and “data68/64/60_detrend_NLLS”, respectively in Fig. 1E) for comparison with the ground truth (“CK” in Fig. 1E). For the performance assessment of simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting, NLLS using the cosine wave combined with a linear trend was applied to “Sim68_noise”, “Sim64_noise” and “Sim60_noise” which derived the periods (“data68/64/60_NLLS_detrend” in Fig. 1E) for comparison with the ground truth (“CK” in Fig. 1E). The mean absolute value of the error comparing the periods derived from the simulated datasets and “CK” was used as the criterion to determine the performance of different algorithms. A lower mean absolute error represents a better performance of the algorithm.

The periods used for the generation of the simulated expression profile dataset Sim68 span from 15 hours to 45 hours (refer to the inset showing the distribution of the period in Fig. 1F). Different combinations of truncation of time points, pre- or simultaneous detrending and noise levels resulted in different estimation performance. The estimation performance of detrending before nonlinear fitting was poor when all the time points were used (“data68_detrend_NLLS-CK” in Fig. 1F) but got better as the number of truncated time points increased (“data64_detrend_NLLS-CK”, and “data60_detrend_NLLS-CK” in Fig. 1F). This indicated that the expression profile of certain genes, which did not cover two full cycles of oscillation, could lead to deteriorated performance of the general detrending procedure when NLLS was used to estimate the cosinusoidal parameters of the datasets. Truncation of one or two time points reduced the overall degree of freedom, thus lowering the estimation performance when NLLS was fitted on datasets with fewer sampling time points (“data68_NLLS-CK”, “data64_NLLS-CK”, and “data60_NLLS-CK” in Fig. 1F). Notably, across all the noise levels, simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting gave rise to the lowest mean absolute error among the corresponding truncation datasets (“data68 datasets”, “data64 datasets”, and “data60 datasets” respectively in Fig. 1F), indicating its superior estimation performance. More importantly, the simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting on “full-length” datasets performed the best among all the simulation groups (“data68_NLLS-detrend-CK” in Fig. 1F). When we focused on a more physiologically relevant range of periods (20–36 hours), simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting using all the time points also outperformed the other algorithms (Fig. 1G).

Taken together, through data-driven simulation studies, we demonstrated that simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting using all the time points is the most preferred algorithm and will be used for the subsequent analyses in this study.

Nipponbare has an endogenous circadian rhythm of 28 hours a day

Using the simultaneous detrending and nonlinear fitting method, we examined the rhythmicity of the expressed genes (21,751 genes in total) in the circadian RNA-seq dataset and estimated their circadian parameters, including period, phase and amplitude (table S1). Following the common practice in the plant circadian clock research field (21), we also calculated the circadian oscillation correlation for each gene to determine the levels of rhythmicity, a metric defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient between the observed expression values and the corresponding model-fitted values obtained from the Cosinor-based nonlinear regression (table S1). According to the density plot of the circadian oscillation correlations, the number of the expressed genes increased with the increase of the circadian oscillation correlation, reaching a maximum when the circadian oscillation correlation was around 0.9 (Fig. 2A). More than two-thirds of the expressed genes had a circadian oscillation correlation over 0.6, implying that a substantial proportion of rice genes might be regulated by the circadian clock (Fig. 2B). To achieve a global view of the key circadian parameters, we performed a comprehensive survey of the distribution of period, phase and amplitude under different cut-offs of the circadian oscillation correlation (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, regardless of the cut-offs of circadian oscillation correlation used, the modes of period significantly deviated from 24 hours and all pointed to 28 hours. This is consistent with the implication from the PCA plot that samples differing by 28 hours instead of 24 hours were closer to each other (Fig. 1C). Such deviation from 24 hours has also been documented in Arabidopsis. For instance, a comparable study of 150 accessions of Arabidopsis sampled across the Northern Hemisphere revealed a wide range of circadian periods from 22.0 to 28.5 hours, loosely correlated with the latitude (22, 23). Likewise, to adapt to the long-day photoperiod during the summer in high latitude places, the endogenous circadian period of cultivated tomato varieties becomes 2 hours longer than that of their ancestral accessions (10). Similar to the period, the mode of phase centred around 15 hours regardless of the circadian oscillation correlation cut-offs (Fig. 2C). For amplitude, its mode increased from 0.67 a.u. to 2.5 a.u. when a cut-off of 0.9 was applied, suggesting that genes with a more significant oscillatory expression pattern tended to have a larger amplitude (Fig. 2C).

Revelation of the 28-hour endogenous rhythm of Nipponbare challenges the common intuition that as the most widely used “wild-type” rice cultivar, Nipponbare might have a close to 24-hour endogenous rhythm. Therefore, validation of this conclusion by other approaches is essential.

We first quantified the expression dynamics of key oscillator components, including OsCCA1, OsTOC1, OsPRR37 and OsGI through RT-qPCR, and calculated their periods using nonlinear regression fitting (fig. S5). These core clock genes exhibit distinct, sustained rhythms. Crucially, the periods estimated for their expression oscillations are consistently and significantly longer than 24 hours.

Another routine method for the assessment of the endogenous circadian rhythm of dicotyledonous plants is the tracking of the rhythmic leaf movement (24). However, for monocotyledonous plants like rice, the erectness of their leaves makes reliable recording of the rhythmic leaf movement impractical. Besides leaf movement, the primary metabolism is also regulated by the output pathways of the circadian clock, which could be utilised as a proxy to study the circadian rhythm. Previous studies have shown that the primary product of leaf starch degradation products like maltose exhibited a robust circadian rhythm in Arabidopsis under the constant light condition (25–27). We speculated that the content of maltose may also oscillate in rice. Thus, we quantified the content of maltose in rice leaves under the constant light condition for two consecutive days (Fig. 2D). NLLS analysis of the pattern of maltose content and the F test comparing a cosine-wave model and a straight-line model demonstrated a statistically significant oscillatory pattern of maltose content (P = 4.46 × 10−2). Importantly, the estimated period of the maltose content rhythm was 29.3 hours (Fig. 2E), consolidating the conclusion deduced from the circadian time-course RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 2C).

Studies of cyanobacteria, Arabidopsis and tomato circadian clock suggest that alignment of the internal circadian rhythm with the external diurnal cycles of environmental factors confers a competitive advantage concerning the growth of plants, a phenomenon known as circadian resonance (10, 28, 29). Therefore, if Nipponbare does have a 28-hour endogenous circadian rhythm, it should grow better under the condition with 28 hours a day. To test this, we performed a T-cycle experiment (Fig. 2F). The rice seedlings were grown in 12 hours light/12 hours dark (T24, 24 hours a day), 14 hours light/14 hours dark (T28, 28 hours a day) or 16 hours light/16 hours dark (T32, 32 hours a day) conditions. Importantly, the light intensity under these three T-cycles was individually adjusted so that the total number of photons were kept the same across different T-cycles. Therefore, the growth difference under different T-cycles could only be determined by the difference in the period of environmental cycles rather than the total amount of photons received. After 14 days, the fresh weight and the dry weight were measured (Fig. 2G). Growth under the T28 cycle was significantly better than that under the T24 or T32 cycle for both fresh weight and dry weight (Fig. 2G). Therefore, the 28-hour environmental cycle represents the most suitable rhythm in sync with the endogenous rhythm of Nipponbare.

Despite the abovementioned synthetic T-cycles, the Earth cycles with a fixed period around 24 hours. When the external environmental cycles with a fixed period, organisms having an endogenous rhythm with longer period tend to exhibit a better growth under a longer daylength condition (10, 30–33). Therefore, we utilised this phenomenon to further verify whether Nipponbare has an internal rhythm of 28 hours a day. The Nipponbare seedlings were grown under 8 hours light/16 hours dark (8:16), 12 hours light/12 hours dark (12:12) or 16 hours light/8 hours dark (16:8) conditions (Fig. 2H). Again, we adjusted the light intensity of these three conditions to ensure the same total number of photons for all scenarios. After 14 days’ growth, both the fresh weight and the dry weight were found to be the highest under the 16:8 condition (Fig. 2I).

Taken together, evidences from circadian time-course RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 2C), qPCR analysis (fig. S5), oscillatory pattern of maltose content (Fig. 2D), circadian resonance experiments (Fig. 2, F and G) and daylength experiments (Fig. 2, H and I) all reached the same conclusion that the period of the endogenous circadian rhythm of Nipponbare is 28 hours a day.

Establishment of rice molecular timetable

The legacy algorithm of the molecular timetable method developed initially in mice assumed a 24-hour endogenous circadian rhythm (16). Since Nipponbare has a 28-hour endogenous rhythm, it remains to be determined whether a 28-hour-based molecular timetable might have a better performance.

To assess the prediction performance of the 28-hour-based molecular timetable, we designed two pipelines, Cosfilter28 and Cosfilter24 to identify the time-indicating genes for the 28-hour-based and 24-hour-based molecular timetables respectively (Fig. 3A). For these two pipelines, the expression profile of each gene derived from the triplicate samples of the circadian time-course RNA-seq dataset was fitted against 1,680 and 1,440 cosine curves whose phases differ by one minute for 28 hours and 24 hours respectively (i.e., 1 curve/min × 60 min/hour × 28 hours = 1,680 curves for 28 hours). The correlation coefficients (R) from these linear regressions, the coefficient of variation (CV) and phase of the best-fitting curve were assigned to each gene. The fitted genes were then sorted based on the ascending order of the estimated phases and abbreviated as Genes_28 and Genes_24 for Cosfilter28 and Cosfilter24 respectively.

Besides the modulation of their phases, different rounding methods could also result in different assignment of CT (circadian time) groups. For example, a gene with a phase of 0.6 h would be assigned to CT0 or CT1 group when floor (nearest integer down), round (half round up) or ceiling (nearest integer up) method is applied respectively. By taking this factor into consideration, the phases of fitted genes from Cosfilter28 and Cosfilter24 were processed by three rounding strategies respectively (i.e. Genes_28_Round, Genes_28_Ceiling, Genes_28_Floor, Genes_24_Round, Genes_24_Ceiling, and Genes_24_Floor) (Fig. 3A).

The correlation coefficient (R) and the coefficient of variation (CV) of each gene were used to identify the time-indicating genes. For Cosfilter28, a total of 1776 genes with R ≥ 0.9 and CV ≥ 0.3 were chosen as the core time-indicating genes (Fig. 3A). For Cosfilter24, the same criteria of R ≥ 0.9 and CV ≥ 0.3 would result in a significantly smaller number of the time-indicating genes, since most rhythmic genes of Nipponbare had a period around 28 hours. This reduction in the number of the time-indicating genes will lead to a lower degree of freedom for the downstream statistical comparisons. In such scenarios, one could not tell whether the inferior prediction performance of the time-indicating genes identified by Cosfilter24 is due to the lower statistical power caused by the reduced degree of freedom or their authentic mismatch for the analysis of an organism with a 28-hour endogenous rhythm. To avoid this ambiguity, we loosened the filtering criteria for Cosfilter24 to R ≥ 0.8 and CV ≥ 0.3 to obtain an even larger number of the core time-indicating genes (1,800 genes in total) than that of Cosfilter28. To ensure analytical robustness for all CT groups, we required a minimum threshold of 7 genes per CT group. CT groups containing fewer than 7 genes were supplemented using genes based on less stringent selection criteria (R ≥ 0.7 and CV ≥ 0.2), thereby maintaining balanced representation across all CT groups (fig. S6A). In summary, 6 different sets of molecular timetables were selected for the downstream comparison (TiGs_28_Round, TiGs_28_Ceiling, TiGs_28_Floor, TiGs_24_Round, TiGs_24_Ceiling, and TiGs_24_Floor) (Fig. 3A).

Since notable overlap between TiGs_28 group and TiGs_24 group was observed (fig. S6B), we wondered whether a 24-hour molecular timetable using TiGs_28 group might have a better performance. To test this, we re-normalized the phases of Genes_28 to a 24-hour period and selected the time-indicating genes using the same filtering criteria of TiGs_28 group, generating TiGs_28to24_Round, TiGs_28to24_Ceiling, TiGs_28to24_Floor. Finally, we ended up with 9 different sets of molecular timetables for comparison (Fig. 3A). Expression heatmaps of the time-indicating genes provided an overview of the oscillatory behaviour of these genes (Fig. 3B). While the 9 different sets of molecular timetables displayed a clear oscillatory expression pattern, a 28-hour rhythm was evident even for TiGs_28to24 groups and TiGs_24 groups. These findings collectively confirm that most rhythmic genes of Nipponbare had a period around 28 hours.

To compare the prediction performance of these 9 sets of molecular timetables, we applied them to the circadian time-course RNA-seq dataset and derived Phase28 of each sample for TiGs_28 and Phase24 of each sample for TiGs_28to24 and TiGs_24 (Fig. 3, C to E). Phase28 and Phase24 are period-corrected estimations of the body time of each sample. Theoretically, the estimated body time should correlate well with the actual sampling time of each sample (16). Therefore, we performed linear regression analyses using the 9 sets of molecular timetables (Fig. 3, C to E). In theory, a perfect match to the actual sampling time would result in a fitted straight line with slope equal to one. So, we also plotted the diagonal line (y = x) as a reference for perfect prediction. To compare the performance of the 9 sets of molecular timetables, we firstly calculated the −log10-transformed absolute value of the difference between the fitted slope and one (Fig. 3F). A closer-to-one slope would lead to a larger −log10-transformed absolute value of the difference. Based on this criterion, we found that TiGs_28_Round, TiGs_28_Ceiling, and TiGs_28_Floor exhibited better performances than the other 6 sets of molecular timetables with TiGs_28_Floor demonstrating the optimal predictive ability (colored in red in Fig. 3F). Furthermore, all 9 sets of molecular timetables achieved a high goodness of fit, with R2 > 0.99 (Fig. 3G). Therefore, TiGs_28_Floor was used as the standard molecular timetable for the rest of the study.

Test of application range of rice molecular timetable

Through comprehensive survey and summarisation of publicly available rice transcriptome datasets, we selected the most representative experimental condition for the derivation of rice molecular timetable. However, aside from the most representative condition, there are still many rice transcriptome datasets whose experimental conditions deviate from that used for the derivation of the rice molecular timetable. To test the application range of the rice molecular timetable, we assessed its performance using datasets from different organs (Fig. 4, A to D). Since the rice molecular timetable was derived from leaves, it performed pretty well when applied to the estimation of the Phase28 (Phase28: the period-corrected estimation of the body time for a sample, derived specifically from the application of the TiGs_28_Floor molecular timetable; Phase24: the equivalent estimation when using timetables based on a 24-hour period) of the transcriptome datasets of the shoots, predicting a linear relationship between Phase28 and relative sampling time with an R2 of 0.9574 (Fig. 4C). For the root samples, the R2 associated with the linear regression between Phase28 and relative sampling time also reached 0.9333, indicating the good performance of rice molecular timetable on the roots (Fig. 4D). Notably, the internal phases of ZT0 and ZT24 under the diurnal condition were estimated to be approximately equivalent, further demonstrating the good performance of the rice molecular timetable under diurnal condition (Fig. 4, A to D). For different rice cultivars, we tested the performance of rice molecular timetable on the indica rice cultivars since the molecular timetable was originally derived from the japonica cultivar (Fig. 4, E to H). Again, the rice molecular timetable achieved good performance with R2 over 0.9 for both of the two datasets we tested. Taken together, these proof-of-principle analyses demonstrated that the application range of the rice molecular timetable goes beyond the original experimental condition under which it was derived.

Application of rice molecular timetable reveals the impact of abiotic stresses on the global rhythm of rice

With the establishment of the rice molecular timetable, next we sought to survey the impact of various abiotic stresses on the global rhythm of rice through application of the rice molecular timetable on publicly available rice transcriptomes.

We found that the global rhythm of rice seedlings treated with mild drought of 50% relative soil water content (RSWC, 1.5 g/g SWC) for 10 days displayed a reversed phase compared to that of the control seedlings (Fig. 5, A and B). A more severe drought treatment of ∼41% RSWC (1.25 g/g SWC) abolished the global rhythm (Fig. 5, A and B). This high sensitivity to drought stress is in sharp contrast to that of soybeans. For soybean seedlings, drought stress of 30% RSWC only caused minimal alterations to the global rhythm of soybean (9). Therefore, compared to that of soybean, the global rhythm of rice is more vulnerable to drought stress, likely due to its irrigated habitats.

While drought stress usually takes a relatively long period to manifest its effect on rice, a 30-min or 60-min heat shock treatment could cause drastic perturbations on the global rhythm (Fig. 5C). Since the relative expression profiles of the time-indicating genes no longer displayed a robust oscillatory pattern under the heat shock stress (Fig. 5C), it was not possible to derive the phase of these expression profiles directly. To solve this problem and enable quantitative comparison between 30-min and 60-min heat shock stresses, we employed a time series analytical method known as moving correlation (17). This method transforms the expression profiles of the time-indicating genes under the three experimental conditions to the moving correlation coefficients of the two treatment conditions compared to the control condition (Fig. 5D). Through NLLS analysis, the phase shifts between each treatment condition and the control condition could be deduced from the moving correlation coefficients (Fig. 5E). Both 30-min and 60-min heat shock stresses induced a 10-hour phase shift compared to the control condition. This suggests that a 30-min heat shock is equivalent to a 60-min treatment regarding the effect on the global rhythm. Although the heat shock stress could significantly perturb global rhythm within a short period of time, the recovery from heat shock was also swift (Fig. 5, F to J). The estimated Phase28 could be restored within 2 hours after recovery from the heat shock stress (Fig. 5K). Similar to heat shock, cold stress also perturbed the global rhythm of rice (fig. S7A). Mild cold stress (13°C) already induced significant phase shift compared to the control condition and the severe cold stress (11°C) caused an even larger phase shift (fig. S7, B and C).

Taken together, these discoveries suggest that the global rhythm of rice is highly responsive to various abiotic stresses, echoing the pivotal role of the circadian clock in coordinating the perception and integration of diverse environmental signals in Arabidopsis.

Molecular timetable analysis unveils tissue-specific responses to salt stress

The rice molecular timetable can also be applied to examine the tissue-specific responses of global rhythm to stress conditions. Salt stress tremendously affects rice growth since rice is one of the glycophytes that cannot tolerate salt stress (34). Zheng et al. previously obtained the leaf and root transcriptomes under the salt stress (35), providing us an opportunity to assess tissue-specific responses of global rhythm to salt stress. Through molecular timetable analysis, we found that salt stress caused opposing changes of phase in roots and leaves (Fig. 6, A to D). Since the phase difference cannot exceed 14 hours, Phase28 values for such conditions were adjusted by subtracting 28 hours. This adjustment is not an error but a necessary step to prevent visual inflation of the actual phase difference. The phase was advanced in leaves but delayed in roots, suggesting that different tissues of rice respond to salt stress differently. It is generally believed that the salt concentration around roots increases more rapidly than that around leaves under the salt stress conditions, making roots more susceptible than leaves. However, the phase shift in leaves was more dramatic than that in roots, indicating that the global rhythm of leaves is more responsive to salt stress than that of roots (Fig. 6, C and D). We have also performed the post hoc tests to evaluate the treatment effect within each organ. This analysis confirms that salt stress induces a statistically significant shift in Phase28 both in leaves (P < 0.0001 for leaf control vs. leaf salt) and in roots (P < 0.0001 for root control vs. root salt) (Fig. 6C). In leaves of salt-sensitive rice cultivars, salt is transferred to leaf sheaths and leaf blades, where it is mainly accumulated and stored (36, 37). Leaf sheaths can remove salt from xylem vessels and sequester salt into the fundamental parenchyma cells. To assess the effect of salt stress on the global rhythm of leaf sheaths, we applied rice molecular timetable to the transcriptomic datasets of the central fundamental parenchyma cells and peripheral tissues of leaf sheaths (Fig. 6, E to H). Similarly, the opposing changes of phase caused by the salt stress were also evident in the central fundamental parenchyma cells and peripheral tissues of leaf sheaths (Fig. 6, G and H). However, salt stress caused a more dramatic phase shift in the peripheral tissues than the central fundamental parenchyma cells (Fig. 6, G and H). This implies that the vacuole sequestration of salt in the central fundamental parenchyma cells might alleviate the levels of salt stress, preventing the damage of leaf sheaths.

Molecular timetable analysis enables quantitative comparison between different stresses

Different abiotic stresses can cause different damage to the yield of rice. Salt stress with a moderate salt treatment could reduce rice yield by up to 50% (38). Heat stress could reduce the yield of rice per plant by about 86% and the panicle number per plant by about 35% (39). Drought stress during the reproductive stage of rice accounts for about 53%–94% of total economic loss and causes a loss of up to 60% if it happens during the filling stage (40, 41). While salt, heat and drought stresses are all detrimental to the growth of rice, the relative sensitivity of the global rhythm of rice to different stresses remains unclear.

To quantitatively compare the effects of different stresses on the global rhythm of rice, we applied the molecular timetable analysis (Fig. 7A) and the moving correlation analysis (Fig. 7B) to a composite dataset encompassing comparable time-series transcriptomes of rice under the salt, heat or drought stress performed by the same research group (42). To quantitate the impact of these stresses on the global rhythm of rice, we performed nonlinear regression to analyse the relationship between the relative sampling time and the estimated phase shift derived from the moving correlation coefficients in Fig. 7B. If the stress has an insignificant impact on the global rhythm, a linear relationship between the relative sampling time and the estimated phase shift is expected. However, the F tests comparing the goodness of fit between the quadratic curves and the straight lines demonstrated that the relationship between relative sampling time and the estimated phase shift could be better described by the quadratic curves for all three stresses, suggesting a significant influence of these stresses on the global rhythm of rice (Fig. 7C). Comparison of the absolute values of the curvatures (the coefficients for the quadratic term highlighted in red in Fig. 7C) of these three stresses through one-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Šídák’s multiple comparisons test suggested that the global rhythm of rice is more sensitive to drought stress followed by salt and heat stresses (Fig. 7D). This discovery agrees with the irrigated habitats of rice.

Similarly, we also applied the molecular timetable analysis (fig. S8, A to C) and the moving correlation analysis (fig. S8, D to F) to compare the impact of deficiency in the three most common macronutrients, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) on the global rhythm of rice using a composite dataset (43). Even for the milder nutrient deficiency condition of the three macronutrients (1/4 of the normal condition), this analysis revealed the marked impact on the global rhythm (fig. S8G). P and K deficiency caused weaker perturbation to the global rhythm than N deficiency under the most severe nutrient deficiency condition (1/64 of the normal condition) (fig. S8, G and H). Therefore, this quantitative comparison demonstrates that the global rhythm of rice is much more sensitive to N deficiency under severe nutrient deficiency condition.

Collectively, these examples showcased the ability of the molecular timetable analysis for quantitative comparison of the effect of different stresses on the global rhythm of rice, even in the absence of the paired control samples (Fig. 7).

Molecular timetable analysis realises detection of the global rhythm changes with unsurpassable temporal resolution

Since the key readout of the circadian clock is the generation of oscillatory patterns, conventional approaches for circadian studies require the recording of the oscillatory behaviours for at least 2 days. This restricted the temporal resolution of such studies, making these approaches not conducive to detection of rhythmic changes within 1 or 2 days after the treatment.

Take N deficiency for example, although N deficiency could significantly perturb the global rhythm of rice after 5 days of treatment (fig. S8), whether a one-day N deficiency is sufficient to disturb the circadian rhythm of rice has never been reported. To fill this gap and pinpoint when N deficiency starts to manifest its impact on the global rhythm of rice, we obtained rice samples with or without N deficiency for 0 hours, 0.5 hours, 2 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, 48 hours, 72 hours and 96 hours with three independent biological replicates (Fig. 8A) and subjected them to RNA-seq analysis. PCA plot of top 500 most variable genes not only demonstrated sound reproducibility of biological replicates but also revealed the distinct temporal patterns of the control and treated samples (Fig. 8B). Under the control condition, the path formed in chronological order of the sampling time appeared as a closed loop moving largely along PC1 (the blue path in Fig. 8B). Under the N deficient condition, the path formed by all the treated samples moved along PC2 and no longer maintained a closed loop, suggesting the dramatic impact of N deficiency on the global rhythm of rice (the red path in Fig. 8B). To further dissect this dataset, we performed the molecular timetable analysis (Fig. 8C) and derived Phase28 for each sample (Fig. 8D). Statistical tests suggested that N deficiency started to trigger significant phase advancement after 24 hours’ treatment (Fig. 8D). Such a short duration is beyond the temporal resolution of routinely-used approaches for circadian studies like the recording of the rhythmic leaf movement or luciferase imaging of the circadian marker gene expression. Therefore, the molecular timetable method enables high temporal resolution for monitoring dynamic circadian changes, supporting sensitive detection of early circadian variations.

Molecular timetable analysis pinpoints candidate hubs connecting abiotic stresses and rice circadian clock

Besides the ability to reveal the impact of abiotic stresses on the global rhythm of rice, the rice molecular timetable could also be used to screen for the key components mediating the cross-talk between different abiotic stresses and the circadian clock, when the datasets with 2 × 2 factorial design (genotype × treatment) are analysed. We identified such datasets encompassing a total of 12 different genotypes covering the treatment conditions like drought, heat, salt, and cold stresses (Fig. 9 and figs. S9 to S11). Through molecular timetable analyses, moving correlation analyses, and nonlinear regression analyses, we obtained the Phase28 for each genotype under different treatment conditions (Fig. 9, A to E, and figs. S9 to S11). Subsequent two-way ANOVA tests examined the statistical significance of the interaction between the genotype and the treatment. Seven genotypes displayed statistically significant differences in response to different abiotic stresses compared to that of the wild-type samples (Fig. 9F). These seven genotypes involve three NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC) family transcription factors (44–46), three dehydration-responsive element (DRE) binding protein 1/C-repeat binding factor (DREB1/CBF) transcription factors (47, 48) and one rice A2 type heat stress transcription factor OsHsfA2e (49). The Phase28 of onac127, osnac006, ntl3 and oshsfa2e showed even stronger responses to the drought or heat stress than that of the wild-type seedlings (Fig. 9, A to D, and fig. S10). This implies that these NACs and HsfA2e might be negative regulators of stress-induced phase changes, making their mutants hypersensitive to the corresponding stresses. It should be noted that the Phase28 of these nac and oshsfa2e mutants were different from that of the wild-type seedlings even under the control conditions (fig. S10). Therefore, they may be directly involved in the regulation of rice circadian rhythm. In contrast, the Phase28 responses of the mutants of DREBs were more specific to the stress condition (Fig. 9E and fig. S9), making them potential hubs connecting the cold stress and the circadian rhythm rather than direct regulators of the circadian clock. Identification of these candidate hubs facilitates more rational design of specific experiments to further dissect the interface between the abiotic stresses and the rice circadian clock in future studies.

SPIRAL enables platform-independent online molecular timetable analysis for publicly available and user-supplied unpublished rice transcriptomes

The analyses described above provided proof-of-principle demonstrations of the versatility of the rice molecular timetable. However, the coding skills needed for the deployment of the molecular timetable analysis may hinder its popularisation. To facilitate the use of the rice molecular timetable, we developed SPIRAL, a platform-independent online single time-point circadian analysis platform (Fig. 10 and fig. S12). SPIRAL contains over 11,000 uniformly re-normalized publicly available rice transcriptome datasets, realising molecular timetable analysis as well as derivation of moving correlation coefficients based on the control condition chosen by the users. Considering the heterogeneity in the quality of different datasets, SPIRAL allows the users to determine whether the molecular timetable analysis is applied to the average of the replicates or to the individual replicates. This mode enables a quick visual inspection of the reproducibility of the dataset per se. Besides publicly available rice transcriptome datasets, the users can also upload their unpublished datasets for analysis in SPIRAL. The resulting figures are fully interactive and the vectorised figures from these analyses can be downloaded for further use in publication. SPIRAL also provides demo datasets for easy acquaintance of this platform for the first-time users.

DISCUSSION

The pervasive control of the plant circadian clock on the transcriptome (4–9) and the demonstration of the tight connections between the plant circadian clock and important agronomic traits (50) gave rise to the proposition the concept of “chronoculture”, which aims to optimise the agronomic practices according to the most favourable diurnal periods or strategically modify circadian traits in crops (12). The key foundation for the realisation of these propositions lies in the deeper understanding of the circadian clock of crop plants, especially about how the crop circadian clock interacts with the environment. Among the four major crops (rice, maize, wheat and soybean), monocotyledonous plants constitute the majority. However, tracking of rhythmic leaf movement and recording of the rhythmic luciferase activity driven by the promoters of oscillatory genes, two most widely-used rhythm monitoring methods in the studies on dicotyledonous plants like Arabidopsis, are less effective for monocotyledonous plants due to the erectness of their leaves. This technical bottleneck has hindered the in-depth studies of the interplay between the circadian clock of monocotyledonous plants and the environment. Through systematic collection, manual curation, summarisation and re-normalisation of over 11,000 publicly available rice transcriptome samples, experimental derivation and algorithm optimisation of the rice molecular timetable, proof-of-principle demonstration of its versatility, and online deployment, we established SPIRAL as a technical alternative for efficient assessment of the effect of diverse environmental and genetic factors on the global rhythm of rice.

During the development of the rice molecular timetable, we made the unexpected discovery that the endogenous circadian rhythm of V4-stage leaves of the japonica cultivar Nipponbare cycles with a period of approximately 28 hours. This conclusion is robustly supported by convergent lines of evidence, including circadian time-course transcriptomics (Fig. 2C), oscillations in a key metabolic output (maltose; Fig. 2D), and phenotypic assays of circadian resonance and photoperiodic growth (Fig. 2, F to I). The emergence of this non-24-hour period in a widely used “wild-type” cultivar is intriguing and may be a consequence of the extensive domestication and selective breeding of rice, which has driven considerable genetic diversification—particularly in flowering time—across a vast latitudinal range (from 18°N to 53°N) (51, 52). Given the well-established genetic link between circadian clock parameters and the control of flowering time (11), it is plausible that the observed variation in flowering time among rice cultivars is associated with underlying differences in their endogenous circadian periods. A comprehensive survey of circadian periods across diverse rice accessions, analogous to studies in Arabidopsis, will be necessary to test this hypothesis and to determine whether the 28-hour period is common or specific to Nipponbare.

The principle of circadian resonance suggests that a molecular timetable derived from TiGs matching the endogenous period of a given cultivar should yield optimal performance for predicting absolute circadian phase. Consequently, while the 28-hour-based TiGs were established from the most representative experimental condition among publicly available datasets, they may not constitute the optimal reference for all cultivars under all conditions. In fact, as our validation shows, a 24-hour-based molecular timetable consistently outperforms the 28-hour version for predicting absolute internal phase under standard diurnal conditions, where the clock is entrained to a precise 24-hour cycle (fig. S13).

The primary and powerful utility of SPIRAL and the molecular timetable method, however, lies not in assigning an absolute endogenous period to every genotype, but in its exceptional sensitivity for detecting relative changes in the global circadian phase induced by genetic or environmental perturbations. Crucially, this utility is effectively maintained even when the reference period does not perfectly match the genotype’s endogenous rhythm. This is demonstrated by our re-analysis of the salt stress response: both 24-hour and 28-hour timetables converged on the same biological conclusion—that salt stress induces opposite, tissue-specific phase shifts, advancing the phase in leaves while delaying it in roots (fig. S14, Fig. 6, A to D). Both frameworks reliably captured the direction and magnitude of this stress-induced reprogramming.

Therefore, while an endogenous 28-hour period may not be universal across cultivars, the Nipponbare-derived 28-hour molecular timetable serves as a robust and sensitive sensor for detecting perturbations to the circadian system. The core, practical value of the SPIRAL platform, as demonstrated throughout this study, is this capability to reveal stress-induced alterations in circadian phase with high sensitivity and temporal resolution across diverse cultivars and experimental datasets, thereby accelerating the exploration of crop-environment interactions in the era of chronoculture.

Several computational methods, including CYCLOPS (53), ZeitZeiger (54), and ChronoGauge (55), can infer circadian time from transcriptomic data. We selected the molecular timetable approach because it requires only one or a few samples, separates reference construction from application for broad reuse, and achieves high robustness by integrating signals from multiple time-indicating genes, making it particularly suitable for large-scale mining of public rice transcriptomes. Although the molecular timetable provides a powerful strategy for large-scale, single time-point circadian analysis of transcriptomic data, it has limitations, including the requirement for a species-specific reference and limited cross-species transferability. Furthermore, because its predictions represent an organ-averaged phase, interpretations regarding stress-induced phase shifts should be made with caution, and future validation at cellular resolution will be necessary to dissect finer-scale tissue heterogeneity.

The current version of the SPIRAL platform has incorporated more than 11,000 publicly available rice transcriptome datasets. Future updates will include more publicly available transcriptome datasets from crops like soybean and maize, providing a user-friendly and platform-independent tool for the molecular timetable analysis of the crop global rhythm.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

O. sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare was used in this study. For the circadian time-course RNA-seq analysis for the construction of the rice molecular timetable, Nipponbare seedlings were grown under the diurnal condition (16-hours light at 28°C and 8-hours dark at 25°C) for 14 days with ∼150 μmol photons m−2 s−1 light intensity and ∼60% relative humidity in a growth chamber. At the end of the 14th day, the seedlings were subjected to the constant light condition with the constant temperature of 28°C at CT0 (Time under constant light condition 0 hours). After a one-day transition, the last fully expanded leaves were collected every 4 hours for 2 days starting at CT24. For each time point, 3 biological replicates were collected.

RNA-seq and data processing

First, total RNA was extracted from the V4-stage Nipponbare leaves using the RNeasy Plant Mini kits (Qiagen, 74904) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then used for library construction and RNA sequencing (Novogene). Next, the quality of the raw data was evaluated by FastQC (v0.11.9) and fastp (v0.21.0) with default parameters to filter out adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. The clean reads were mapped to the Nipponbare reference genome (Os-Nipponbare-Reference-IRGSP-1.0) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Basic quality control metrics were systematically evaluated, including per-base sequence quality, per-sequence GC content, sequence duplication levels, and mapping efficiency. All samples passed QC thresholds with high sequencing quality, normal GC distribution, low library bias, and high unique mapping rates, confirming high data reliability (fig. S2). The expression of each gene was quantified using StringTie (v2.1.5), generating the FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million mapped fragments) matrices and raw count matrices. For further quality control and statistical analysis, the raw count matrices were processed using the limma (v3.62.2) and edgeR (v4.4.2) packages from R (v4.4.3). Gene expression filtering was initially performed using the filterByExpr algorithm to exclude low-abundance transcripts lacking statistical power for subsequent analyses. The correlation heatmap and PCA (principal component analysis) were performed using the pheatmap (v1.0.12) and the plotPCA function in DESeq2 (v1.46.0), after regularised log transformation with DESeq2.

Identification of rhythmic genes

The expression of genes with nonzero FPKM in 36 samples were defined as the expressed genes for the subsequent analyses. The FPKM values of each expressed gene were processed by simultaneous detrending and nonlinear least squares regression fitting using the following formulaExpression level=Amplitude×cos(2πPeriod×time−2π28×Phase28)+a(1+b×time)

To identify the rhythmic genes with a physiologically relevant range of the circadian rhythm parameters, it is necessary to set the range of parameters. The amplitude was constrained to be more than zero. The period was constrained to be greater than 15 hours but less than 45 hours. The phase was not constrained but the Phase28 was re-scaled to a 24-hours period (Phase24) to facilitate comparability among groups. The parameters of a and b were not constrained and used to estimate the linear trend during the NLLS fitting. Parameter a represents the baseline expression level at time zero. Parameter b quantifies the relative rate of linear drift per unit time (relative to the baseline a). Using this relative rate allows for meaningful comparison of linear trends across genes with different absolute expression scales. Genes with non-convergent fits were considered arrhythmic. For the rhythmic genes, the best-fitted value of the circadian rhythm parameters (Amplitude, Period, and Phase), and their corresponding standard error of the mean (SEM) and degree of freedom were used for statistical tests.

Simulation-based algorithm optimisation

The periods, phases, amplitudes, intercepts and linear slopes derived from the time-course RNA-seq experiment (Raw_data68) by NLLS served as the ground truth and constituted the simulated dataset of Sim68. Next, the simulated dataset was divided into three subsets, Sim68 (sampling duration up to 68 hours), Sim64 (sampling duration up to 64 hours) and Sim60 (sampling duration up to 60 hours). Within each subset dataset, the expression level of each gene at each time point was added with the Gaussian noise at varying levels (10%, 15%, and 20%), which generated the simulated datasets of Sim68_noise, Sim64_noise, and Sim60_noise. In addition, a linear detrending procedure was performed on each gene within the datasets to generate the detrended simulated datasets (Sim68_noise_detrend, Sim64_noise_detrend, and Sim60_noise_detrend). Next, NLLS were performed on datasets of Sim68/64/60_noise and Sim68/64/60_noise_detrend to obtain the results of data68/64/60_NLLS and data68/64/60_detrend_NLLS. Meanwhile, simultaneous detrending and NLLS was performed on Sim68/64/60_noise to obtain the results of data68/64/60_NLLS_detrend. Finally, the mean absolute error of period across the corresponding simulated datasets after parameter estimation via NLLS fitting was calculated, respectively.

Circadian resonance experiments and daylength experiments

For the circadian resonance experiments, the germinated Nipponbare seeds were grown in different T-cycles for 14 days: 12 hours light/12 hours dark (T24), 14 hours light/14 hours dark (T28) or 16 hours light/16 hours dark (T32). The condition in the growth chamber was maintained at 28°C and ∼60% relative humidity. The light intensity for each T-cycle experiment was individually adjusted to keep the same amount of the total number of photons according to the total cultivated time of 336 hours. At the end of the experiments, the fresh weight and the dry weight of the last fully expanded leaves were measured, respectively.

For the daylength experiments, the germinated Nipponbare were grown in different photoperiods for 14 days: 8 hours light/16 hours dark (8:16), 12 hours light/12 hours dark (12:12) or 16 hours light/8 hours dark (16:8). The condition in the growth chamber was maintained at 28°C and ∼60% relative humidity. The light intensity for each photoperiod experiment was individually adjusted to keep the same total number of photons according to the total cultivated time of 336 hours. At the end of the experiments, the fresh weight and the dry weight of the last fully expanded leaves were measured, respectively.

Quantification of the maltose content

The plant materials, growth conditions, and treatments were the same as those of the circadian time-course RNA-seq experiments. The Nipponbare seedlings were grown under the diurnal condition until the V4 stage (14 days) and transferred to the constant light conditions for 1 day. Under the constant light condition, ∼0.1 g of the last three fully expanded leaves was collected every 6 hours for ∼2 days, yielding 10 sampling time points, and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. For each time point, 3 biological replicates were collected. Following homogenisation in the extraction buffer, the maltose content levels were determined by the TECAN Spark microplate reader using the Maltose Assay kit (Grace Biotechnology, G0566W) with the following formulaMaltose(mg/g)=V×[Atest−(Acontrol−Ablank)]×D(Astandard−Ablank)×W

In this formula, V represents the volume of extraction buffer (mL), W represents the weight of the sample (g), D represents the dilution factor.

Identification of the time-indicating genes

To optimise the computational analysis pipeline compatible for a longer period of 28 hours, Cosfilter28 and Cosfilter24 were designed. Specifically, the FPKM values of each gene (38,975 genes) were fitted against 1680 or 1440 test cosine curves (one curve per minute, a total of 1680 min (28 hours) and 1440 min (24 hours), respectively) by linear regression analysis using the pipelines of Cosfilter28 and Cosfilter24 to calculate the correlation coefficient (R), coefficient of variation (CV), and phase of the best-fitted curve.

Cosfilter formulacorrelation=cor[genes,Amplitude×cos(2πPeriodtime−2πJPhase.n)]

For Cosfilter28, Period = 28; Phase.n = 1680; J ∈ [1:1680]

For Cosfilter24, Period = 24; Phase.n = 1440; J ∈ [1:1440]

The genes were then arranged by phases. The Phase28 was re-normalized to Phase24 and three different rounding methods (Round, Ceiling, and Floor) were tested. For Cosfilter28, the filtering criteria of R ≥ 0.9 and CV ≥ 0.3 identified 1776 core time-indicating genes, with additional genes incorporated through expanded selection criteria of R ≥ 0.7 and CV ≥ 0.2 for CT groups containing fewer than 7 genes. For Cosfilter24, the filtering criteria of R ≥ 0.8 and CV ≥ 0.3 identified 1800 core time-indicating genes, with additional genes incorporated through expanded selection criteria of R ≥ 0.7 and CV ≥ 0.2 for CT groups containing fewer than 7 genes.

Application of the molecular timetable method

The expression profiles of time-indicating genes were extracted from the re-normalized transcriptome datasets. Each time-indicating gene was standardised across different samples within a single experiment. A second standardisation was applied to each sample within the experiment, so that each sample had a uniform average expression level and SD. Three summary statistics were calculated for each of the 28 CT groups: the number of genes, the mean, and the standard deviation of the standardised expression levels of the time-indicating genes. These statistics were then used to estimate the circadian rhythm parameters for each sample and weighted linear regression (with 1/SD2 weights) was performed in GraphPad Prism 9 using its nonlinear regression function to fit a straight line.

Nitrogen deficiency treatment

For the circadian time-course RNA-seq analysis under the nitrogen deficient condition, Nipponbare seeds were surface sterilised using 70% ethanol and 1% sodium hypochlorite, followed by 3-day dark imbibition in distilled water at 28°C. The germinated seeds were transferred to the solution of Yoshida rice nutrient salt mixture with pH adjusted to 5.7 (Coolaber, NSP1040). After 14 days, the rice seedlings were divided into the CK group and −N group. The rice seedlings of CK group were cultured in Yoshida rice nutrient salt mixture solution, while the seedlings of −N group were cultured in the nitrogen-deficient Yoshida rice nutrient salts mixture solution (Coolaber, NSP1040-N). During treatment, the nutrient solution was replaced with the fresh solution daily. The last fully expanded leaves were collected at 0 hours, 0.5 hours, 2 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, 48 hours, 72 hours, and 96 hours with 3 independent biological replicates.

Establishment of the SPIRAL platform

The platform-independent online single time-point circadian analysis platform (SPIRAL) was built using the Shiny package in R (R Core Team, 2024). The cross-platform compatibility testing was conducted across major operating systems (Windows 10, macOS Monterey, iPadOS 18, iOS 18, and HarmonyOS 5) using the following browsers: Google Chrome, Safari, and Huawei Browser respectively. The interactive plot was built using the highcharter package (v0.9.4). The catalogue IDs of the rice transcriptome datasets were collected from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), Sequence Read Archive (SRA) using the keywords (Organism: O. sativa, Data type: Transcriptome or Gene expression) with the help of the NCBI E-utilities tools (18). Most of the raw datasets of the RNA-seq experiments from SRA were sourced from the PPRD (https://plantrnadb.com/ricerna) (56), while the remaining part of raw datasets of the RNA-seq experiments were processed according to the procedures described in the “RNA-seq and data processing” section. For microarray datasets, the single-color microarray and 2-color microarray datasets were processed based on their manufacturing protocols. SPIRAL offered more than 11,000 uniformly re-normalized rice transcriptome samples. In the “Upload” table, a demo dataset was provided and the users could upload their unpublished rice transcriptome datasets (up to 30 M) for the circadian analysis in SPIRAL.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.4.3) and GraphPad Prism 9.
