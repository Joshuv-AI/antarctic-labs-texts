Interfacial Effects Determine Nonequilibrium Phase Behaviors in Chemically Driven Fluids
Yongick Cho, William M. Jacobs

Abstract
Coupling between chemical fuel consumption and phase separation can lead to condensation at a nonequilibrium steady state, resulting in phase behaviors that are not described by equilibrium thermodynamics. Theoretical models of such “chemically driven fluids” typically invoke near-equilibrium approximations at small length scales. However, because dissipation occurs due to both molecular-scale chemical reactions and mesoscale diffusive transport, it has remained unclear which properties of phase-separated reaction–diffusion systems can be assumed to be at an effective equilibrium. Here we use microscopic simulations to show that mesoscopic fluxes are dependent on nonequilibrium fluctuations at phase-separated interfaces. We further develop a first-principles theory to predict nonequilibrium coexistence curves, localization of mesoscopic fluxes near phase-separated interfaces, and droplet size-scaling relations in good agreement with simulations. Our findings highlight the central role of interfacial properties in governing nonequilibrium condensation and have broad implications for droplet nucleation, coarsening, and size control in chemically driven fluids.

In this work, we show that steady-state mesoscopic fluxes are directly related to nonequilibrium fluctuations in phase-separated chemically driven fluids, and we propose a first-principles theory to explain how these microscopic and mesoscopic effects are coupled at condensate interfaces.
Our simulations and theoretical analysis demonstrate that mesoscopic fluxes originate from, and are thus concentrated near, condensate interfaces despite the presence of molecular-level dissipation throughout the system.
We further show that both the conditions for phase coexistence and the size scaling of condensates are strongly influenced by nonequilibrium interfacial effects that can be understood via a microscopic theory.
Our theory predicts a broad range of conditions in which nonequilibrium interfacial effects make significant contributions to the stability and dynamics of nonequilibrium condensates, highlighting the importance of condensate interfaces in chemically driven phase-separated mixtures.

Microscopic Model of a Chemically Driven Nonequilibrium Steady State

As a minimal model of a chemically driven fluid, we consider a ternary system in which macromolecules (referred to as molecules from this point on) undergo chemical reactions between conformational states while diffusing in an implicit solvent.
Each molecule is in either a bonding (B) state or an inert (I) state, whose internal energy differs by
\Delta f
(Fig.
1
A).
Importantly, molecules in the B state experience a short-ranged attractive interaction with other B-state molecules only.
The size of a molecule sets the unit length scale, and unoccupied space represents the solvent.

We model the fluid using a two-dimensional square lattice, in which the dimensionless nearest-neighbor interaction energy among B-state molecules is
\beta\epsilon=-2.95
and
\beta
is the inverse temperature of the solvent.
This choice ensures that the fluid can phase-separate into dilute and condensed phases at equilibrium
[
44
]
.
B-state molecules engage in intermolecular interactions according to the potential energy
u=\epsilon n_{\text{B}}
, where
n_{\text{B}}
is the number of nearest-neighbor B-state molecules.
On the other hand, I-state molecules always have
u=0
, as they do not participate in attractive intermolecular interactions.
Molecules diffuse with mobility
\Lambda
, so that the mean squared displacement over time is proportional to
\Lambda
in the dilute limit.
In simulations, diffusion is affected by both the attractive interactions among B-state molecules and the hard-core repulsion that prohibits the occupancy of more than one molecule per lattice site.
The rate of diffusion to an adjacent lattice site is thus
\Lambda\exp(\beta u)\delta_{\text{unocc}}/4
, where
\delta_{\text{unocc}}
is 1 if the destination lattice site is unoccupied and 0 otherwise, and the factor of 4 accounts for the coordination number of the square lattice (see
Materials and Methods
).

Chemical reactions between B and I states proceed through two distinct reaction pathways.
For simplicity, we consider a single driven pathway that is coupled to a chemical drive,
\Delta\mu
, which originates from the chemical potential difference between chemical fuel and waste.
Positive values of
\Delta\mu
accelerate reactions in the
\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}
direction (Fig.
1
A).
By contrast, the passive pathway is independent of
\Delta\mu
.
We note that this scheme could also be modified to represent two driven pathways in opposing directions with different chemical drives.
Utilizing a reaction scheme from prior work
[
2
,
3
]
, we set the
\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}
reaction rate constants as

\displaystyle k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=1

(1)

\displaystyle k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}

\displaystyle=\eta\min[1,\exp(-\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}-\beta\Delta f-\beta\Delta\mu)],

where the superscripts
p
and
d
indicate the passive and driven pathways, respectively, and
\eta
is a constant that controls the ratio of reactive fluxes along the two pathways.
The passive rate is a constant that sets the fundamental time unit for the simulation, whereas the
n_{\text{B}}
-dependence of the driven reaction rate accounts for concentration-dependent rate constants.
Specifically, the driven reaction rate constant in Eq. (
1
) is enhanced at high B-state concentrations (Fig.
1
B).
This particular choice can describe, for example, a scenario in which the enzyme that catalyzes the driven pathway partitions into the B-state-rich condensate
[
4
]
or, alternatively, a scenario in which B-state molecules directly catalyze reactions involving other nearby molecules subject to a maximum turnover rate.
Alternative schemes, which confirm the generality of our results, are presented in the
Supplementary Information (SI)
.
We fix
\eta=1
throughout this paper because the mesoscopic effects of the driven reactions are maximized when the reactive fluxes along the two pathways are comparable
[
3
]
(Fig. S1).

Assuming that the solvent is at equilibrium, stochastic thermodynamics
[
45
]
constrains the rate constants along the driven and passive pathways in terms of the total free-energy change:

\displaystyle k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\text{p}}/k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\exp(\beta\Delta f+\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}})

(2)

\displaystyle k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\text{d}}/k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}

\displaystyle=\exp(\beta\Delta f+\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}+\beta\Delta\mu).

The driven reaction in the
\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}
direction is thus accelerated over the
\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}
direction by a factor
\exp(\beta\Delta\mu)
, regardless of the local B-state concentration (Fig.
1
B).
Because the overall chemical reaction rate is the sum of the rates along the two pathways, applying nonzero
\Delta\mu
means that the system is no longer governed by equilibrium statistical mechanics.
The probability of observing a specific sequence of reactions and transitions of molecules between lattice sites is therefore different from that of the time-reversed sequence of these events, leading to steady-state entropy production
[
45
]
.
Importantly, entropy production is a direct measure of molecular-scale dissipation and is always positive for nonzero
\Delta\mu
.

Kinetic Monte Carlo simulations of a condensed-phase droplet with a curved interface of radius
R
(Fig.
1
D) and a flat interface in the macroscopic limit
R\rightarrow\infty
(Fig.
1
C) allow us to calculate the spatial dependence of the steady-state entropy production rate density (see
Materials and Methods
).
The rate of entropy production is lower in the condensed phase due to the slower rate of
\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}
reactions when the attractive interactions among B-state molecules must be disrupted (Fig.
1
E,F).
In the dilute phase, entropy production is primarily due to “futile cycles”, which execute closed loops around the driven and passive pathways.
However, we observe that the entropy production rate density does not necessarily decrease monotonically upon approaching the condensed phase, but may even increase again near the phase-separated interface (Fig. S2).
This observation hints that nonequilibrium effects at the interface may differ from the bulk behavior of the coexisting phases.

Although entropy production occurs throughout the entire system, we find that mesoscopic nonequilibrium effects, represented by the net diffusive and reactive fluxes, exist only near the interface.
Example simulations for both curved and flat interfaces are shown in Fig.
2
, where the time-averaged location of the interface is specified by the Gibbs dividing surface at which the B-state concentration is
\rho_{\text{B}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
(Fig.
2
A).
The net diffusive flux densities,
j_{\text{B/I}}
(Fig.
2
B), and the net reactive flux density in the
{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
direction,
s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
(Fig.
2
C), are nonzero only near the Gibbs dividing surface (see
Materials and Methods
).
These mesoscopic fluxes decay rapidly to zero in the bulk phases in simulations with both flat and curved interfaces, primarily because the contributions from futile cycles average out.

To understand the origin of these mesoscopic effects, we divide the observed net fluxes (thick solid lines in Fig.
2
B,C) into contributions from molecules in the bulk dilute phase (thin solid lines) and from molecules that are within one lattice spacing of the instantaneous interface between the two phases (dashed lines; see
Materials and Methods
).
The contribution from molecules in the condensed phase is negligible and is thus not shown for clarity.
For both the diffusive and reactive fluxes, the interfacial contribution is centered at the Gibbs dividing surface by construction.
We note that the time-averaged interfacial contribution to each of these fluxes, as well as the time-averaged concentration profiles of the B and I-state molecules in Fig.
2
A, is spatially smeared out due to steady-state fluctuations of the interface, even though the instantaneous interface is locally sharp (Fig.
1
C,D).
By contrast, the contribution from molecules in the dilute phase necessarily occurs to the right of the interface, so that the total mesoscopic fluxes are asymmetric with respect to the Gibbs dividing surface.
The dilute-phase contribution to the diffusive flux arises due to a steady-state concentration gradient, in which the B-state concentration monotonically increases towards the far-field value,
\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}
, as a function of the distance from the interface.
This gradient can be observed directly by plotting the contribution to
\delta\rho_{\text{B}}\equiv\rho_{\text{B}}-\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}
from molecules in the bulk dilute phase, so that interfacial fluctuations do not obscure this effect (open circles in Fig.
2
A; see also Fig. S3).
In sum, the total net diffusive flux is peaked on the dilute-phase side of the interface (Fig.
2
B), in alignment with the inflection point of the total net reactive flux (Fig.
2
C).

This analysis suggests a simple mechanistic picture of the mesoscopic fluxes, in which the molecules execute a reaction-diffusion cycle at steady state.
When
\Delta\mu>0
, excess B-state molecules in the dilute phase, resulting from dilute-phase
{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
reactions, drive a flux of B-state molecules towards the condensed phase until they are adsorbed at the interface.
Adsorbed B-state molecules tend to undergo
{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
chemical reactions (
s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}>0
) and subsequently desorb from the interface due to the lack of attractive interactions between I-state molecules and the condensed phase.
I-state molecules are eventually converted back to the B-state in the dilute phase (
s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}<0
).
More generally, the direction of these fluxes may be reversed if either the sign of
\Delta\mu
or the
n_{\text{B}}
-dependence of the reaction rate constants is reversed (Fig. S4).

Interfacial Fluctuations Deviate from Thermal Equilibrium

Examination of the microscopic configurations reveals further deviations from thermal equilibrium at the microscopic scale.
We first compute the spectrum of interfacial height fluctuations, commonly referred to as capillary waves
[
46
]
, which describes spatial correlations in the position of the instantaneous interface relative to the Gibbs dividing surface.
At equilibrium, capillary fluctuations of a macroscopically flat interface (i.e.,
R\rightarrow\infty
; cf. Fig.
1
C) only depend on the reduced B-state interaction energy,
\beta\epsilon
, and the transverse dimension of the simulation,
L
, since the I-state molecules are energetically indistinguishable from the implicit solvent in our simulations.
Moreover, due to the equipartition theorem, the average power spectrum of capillary fluctuations,
\langle|\Delta h(q)|^{2}\rangle
, is proportional to
1/\beta\sigma q^{2}
in equilibrium, where
\sigma
is the interfacial tension and
q
is a wavevector parallel to the interface
[
46
]
.
For the example conditions analyzed in Fig.
2
, we find that capillary fluctuations in the presence of steady-state fluxes deviate only slightly from the equipartition
q^{-2}
scaling at small
q
but differ significantly from the equilibrium spectrum in their overall amplitude (pink marks in Fig.
3
A).
This upshift in the spectrum can be interpreted as a reduction in the interfacial tension,
\sigma
, relative to the equilibrium value.
This effect also leads to a broadening of the time-averaged interfacial width (Fig.
2
A), which can be quantified by the mean-squared interfacial fluctuation,
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
.
Reversing the direction of the chemical drive under otherwise equivalent conditions results in an increased interfacial tension (teal marks in Fig.
3
A) and a narrowing of the time-averaged interfacial width, along with reversed mesoscopic fluxes (Fig. S4).

Next, we look more closely at the identities of the molecules within one lattice spacing of the instantaneous interface.
At equilibrium, the ratio of B-state to I-state molecules follows the Boltzmann distribution,
p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})/p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})=\exp(-\beta\Delta f-\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}})
,
where
n_{\text{B}}\in\{1,2,3\}
at the interface.
However, when steady-state fluxes are present, we find that the conditional distributions
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
are incompatible with Boltzmann statistics.
These deviations are most easily seen by inverting the Boltzmann distribution to define
\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}(n_{\text{B}})\equiv-\ln[p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})/p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})]-\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}

[
3
]
.
For the example conditions analyzed in Fig.
2
,
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}
increases with
n_{\text{B}}
(pink marks in Fig.
3
B), indicating that the nonequilibrium population of molecules at the interface is biased more towards the B state when
n_{\text{B}}=1
and more towards the I state when
n_{\text{B}}=3
.
An analogous calculation of the marginal distribution of lattice sites with
n_{\text{B}}
B-state neighbors on the interface,
p(n_{\text{B}})
, further reveals a decrease in the populations of
n_{\text{B}}=1
and
n_{\text{B}}=3
lattice sites relative to equilibrium (pink marks in Fig. S5), which is consistent with the observed increase in capillary wave amplitudes under these conditions.
These trends are reversed when the chemical drive is applied in the opposite direction (teal marks in Figs.
3
B and S4).

Taken together, these simulation results indicate that both spatial and compositional fluctuations of phase-separated interfaces can deviate from thermal equilibrium under conditions where nonequilibrium reactions drive steady-state mesoscopic fluxes.
Consequently, we anticipate that equilibrium approximations of phase-separated interfaces are unlikely to quantitatively describe the phase behavior of this model fluid under generic nonequilibrium conditions.

A microscopic theory predicts nonequilibrium phase coexistence

Motivated by these observations, we propose a first-principles theory that combines a microscopic treatment of the interface with a mesoscopic description of the coexisting bulk phases (see
Materials and Methods
).
This approach allows us to predict the conditions for nonequilibrium phase coexistence directly from the microscopic details of the molecular interactions and reaction rate constants, without relying on local equilibrium assumptions or equilibrium free-energy densities.
We model the dilute phase using a standard reaction–diffusion equation for an ideal mixture of B and I-state molecules at steady state,

0=\xi^{2}\nabla^{2}\delta\rho_{\text{B/I}}-\delta\rho_{\text{B/I}},

(3)

where
\delta\rho_{\text{B}}
and
\delta\rho_{\text{I}}
represent the differences in the dilute-phase concentrations of the B and I-state molecules relative to the far-field values,
\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}
and
\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{v}}
, respectively.
The reaction–diffusion length
\xi
and the ratio
\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}/\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{v}}
can be predicted from the mobility,
\Lambda
, and the total reaction rates,
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}=k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{p}}+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}
and
k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}=k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\text{p}}+k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\text{d}}
, in the dilute limit.
Eq. (
3
) accurately describes the concentration profile in the dilute phase (Figs.
2
A and S5) but requires two boundary conditions at the phase-separated interface.
To this end, we describe the microscopically sharp interface in terms of a microscopic probability distribution
p(i,n_{\text{B}})
representing the probability that a tagged lattice site is occupied by a molecule or implicit solvent of species
i
while being surrounded by
n_{\text{B}}
B-state neighbors.
This distribution must be stationary at steady state,
\dot{p}=0
.
For simplicity, we do not explicitly model the condensed phase, and we assume that all fluxes in the condensed phase are negligible as observed in our simulations; however, our approach could be generalized to include such effects as well under appropriate conditions (see SI).

We then connect the microscopic description of the interface to the mesoscopic description of the dilute phase by employing a perturbative Fixed Local Environment approXimation (FLEX)
[
2
,
3
]
, in which the configuration around a tagged lattice site at the interface is assumed to change slowly compared to single-site fluctuations.
This approximation implies that the stationary condition for
p(i,n_{\text{B}})
can be solved separately for each value of
n_{\text{B}}
and that the marginal distribution,
p(n_{\text{B}})
, is unperturbed by the chemical drive.
We solve for the dilute-phase concentrations adjacent to the interface,
\rho_{\text{B/I}}(R+)
, by matching the microscopic and mesoscopic diffusive fluxes,

\left\langle j_{\text{B}}^{\text{FLEX}}[p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}),p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})]\right\rangle=-(\Lambda/4)\nabla\rho_{\text{B}}\big|_{R+},

(4)

where
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
is the conditional distribution given a local environment with
n_{\text{B}}
B-state neighbors and
\langle\cdot\rangle
indicates an average over
n_{\text{B}}
weighted by
p(n_{\text{B}})
.
Finally, we establish phase coexistence using a kinetic definition of the supersaturation that exploits the particle–hole symmetry of the lattice gas
[
2
]
,

\ln\left[\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle/\left(1-\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle\right)\right]-\beta\sigma R^{-1}=0,

(5)

where the second term is the Gibbs–Thomson correction for a curved interface with nonequilibrium interfacial tension
\sigma

[
8
]
.
Eq. (
5
) is equivalent to the equal pressure condition for phase coexistence at equilibrium and, under nonequilibrium conditions, ensures that
\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
at the Gibbs dividing surface in the case of a flat interface (i.e.,
R\rightarrow\infty
).
Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) constitute the interfacial boundary conditions needed to determine the dilute-phase concentration profile, and thus to predict the total far-field concentration
\rho^{\text{v}}
, given the reaction rates specified by Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) and the mobility
\Lambda
.
Importantly, nonzero steady-state diffusive fluxes imply that reactive fluxes take place on the interface due to mass conservation and the stationary condition for
p(i,n_{\text{B}})
.

Comparisons between theoretical predictions and simulation results over a wide range of
\Delta\mu
and
\Lambda
values demonstrate the unique advantages of this approach.
We first examine the case of a macroscopically flat interface, which we simulate using direct-coexistence simulations in a slab geometry
[
3
]
, in Fig.
4
.
In Fig.

These curves describe how the internal energy difference between the B and I states,
\Delta f
, must be tuned in order to maintain a constant far-field concentration of
\rho^{\text{v}}=0.05
in the dilute phase, which guarantees that the dilute phase is always approximately ideal.
We also plot the predictions of an alternative theoretical approach in which the interfacial boundary conditions for Eq. (
3

10

(see SI).
By contrast with Eqs. (
4

However, the two theories yield similar results in the limit

Materials and Methods
).
Analogous predictions in which we hold
\Delta f
constant and predict

at phase coexistence are presented in Fig. S6.

We also obtain good agreement between the predicted and measured reaction–diffusion length in the dilute phase,
\xi
(Fig.

Further comparisons between theory and simulation results are shown for other concentration-dependent reaction schemes in Fig. S7.

Nonequilibrium Interfacial Fluctuations are Coupled to Steady-state Fluxes

Crucially, our theory also predicts nonequilibrium variations in the fluctuations at the interface.
Within the FLEX approximation, we find that the steady-state conditional distribution
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
does not follow the Boltzmann distribution when
j_{\text{B}}(R+)/\Lambda\neq 0
.
This prediction, which is consistent with the simulation results shown in Fig.
3
B, can be understood by noting that both
p(i,n_{\text{B}})
and the dilute-phase concentration profiles are uniquely determined by our theory, so that further imposing the Boltzmann distribution at the interface leads to an overdetermined system of equations.
The only way that
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
can satisfy the Boltzmann distribution for all values of
n_{\text{B}}
within the FLEX approximation is if the entire fluid is described by a single effective equilibrium model, which occurs when
j_{\text{B}}(R+)/\Lambda=0
and the dilute-phase concentration gradient vanishes (see SI for further discussion).

This prediction of our theory also suggests why the interfacial capillary fluctuations, which are related to the marginal distribution
p(n_{\text{B}})
, can differ from equilibrium when
j_{\text{B}}(R+)/\Lambda\neq 0
, as shown in Fig.
3
A.
Although the FLEX approximation does not resolve the full distribution
p(i,n_{\text{B}})
, we can predict this effect qualitatively by computing an effective “single-bond” nearest-neighbor interaction energy for an adsorbed B-state molecule (
n_{\text{B}}=1
) and then determining
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
from an equilibrium model of capillary waves
[
3
]
(see
Materials and Methods
).
Because interfacial height fluctuations decrease monotonically with the effective single-bond interaction strength
[
7
]
, weakening the effective interactions leads to larger
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
and vice versa.
Comparisons between this prediction and simulation results reveal similar qualitative dependencies on
\Delta\mu
and
\Lambda
(Fig.
4
D), whereby
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
increases monotonically with respect to
\Delta\mu
and goes through a maximum value with respect to
\Lambda
when
\Delta\mu>0
.
Specifically,
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
increases with
\Lambda
in the reaction-dominant (small
\Lambda
) regime, but necessarily approaches the equilibrium value,
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle_{\text{eq}}
, in the diffusion-dominant (large
\Lambda
) regime where
j_{\text{B}}(R+)/\Lambda\rightarrow 0
(see
Materials and Methods
).
Nonetheless, the FLEX approximation tends to predict lower values of
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
than what is observed in simulations.
One cause of this underprediction is likely a breakdown in the timescale separation assumption that underlies FLEX, particularly when
\Lambda
is large.
Coupling between fluctuations at a tagged lattice site and fluctuations in its local environment would therefore have to be taken into account to quantitatively match the peak values of
\Delta h
that are seen in simulations.
Moreover, the capillary fluctuation spectra show greater deviations from the equipartition
q^{-2}
scaling at small
q
when
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle\gg\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle_{\text{eq}}
(Fig. S8), which cannot be explained by an effective equilibrium model of capillary waves and thus also contributes to the quantitative underprediction of
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
in Fig.
4
D.

Overall, the key insight from our theoretical approach is that mesoscopic fluxes and nonequilibrium interfacial properties both arise from non-Boltzmann statistics at phase-separated interfaces.
Whereas altered capillary waves are a direct manifestation of nonequilibrium interfacial fluctuations, mesoscopic fluxes in the dilute phase indirectly indicate the presence of microscopic fluxes and non-Boltzmann statistics at the interface.
Consequently, our theory predicts that nonequilibrium interfacial properties should be expected in this model whenever steady-state concentration gradients appear in the coexisting bulk phases.
Moreover, driving the fluid away from equilibrium in a manner that does not generate non-Boltzmann statistics, for example by choosing an
n_{\text{B}}
-independent form for
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}
in Eq. (
1
), generates neither nonequilibrium interfacial properties
[
2
]
nor mesoscopic fluxes (Fig. S9).

Nonetheless, these results collectively demonstrate that our theoretical approach provides a comprehensive description of droplet phase coexistence under a wide range of nonequilibrium conditions.

Discussion

In this paper, we show that phase coexistence and mesoscopic fluxes in chemically driven fluids are governed by nonequilibrium interfacial effects.
Molecular-level simulations reveal that non-Boltzmann interfacial statistics and nonequilibrium capillary fluctuations emerge together with mesoscopic fluxes, which are confined near the interface.
Inspired by these observations, we propose a nonequilibrium theory of phase coexistence that connects a microscopic description of the interface to a mesoscopic model of the coexisting bulk phases.
This theory predicts accurate phase diagrams for both macroscopic and finite-size droplets and explains the interdependence of non-Boltzmann statistics and mesoscopic fluxes.
Our approach contrasts with alternative theories that assume equilibrium interfacial properties and are thus only valid for reactions that are weakly driven or extremely slow by comparison to molecular diffusion.
Taken together, our results highlight the importance of nonequilibrium behaviors at all length scales in fluids driven by molecular-scale reactions.

This study clarifies the extent to which various properties of chemically driven fluids can be considered to be at effective equilibria.
Far from the phase-separated interface, the bulk-phase entropy production is dominated by futile cycles, which average out when we coarse-grain to obtain mesoscopic properties
[
5
]
.
Each bulk phase may therefore be reasonably described at a mesoscopic level in terms of an effective equilibrium.
This observation also explains why the total entropy production rate provides little insight into the phase behavior and is insufficient to predict the existence of mesoscopic fluxes in general.
By contrast, effective equilibrium models do not provide accurate descriptions of the near-interfacial region, since the inhomogeneous local environments near a phase-separated interface make it impossible to define a consistent Boltzmann distribution.
Although we present results for a particular chemical reaction scheme in the main text, these central conclusions can be straightforwardly extended to alternative concentration-dependent reaction schemes with one or more driven pathways.

The theory developed in this work succeeds by combining models of inhomogeneous fluids at different length scales.
In general, mesoscopic reaction–diffusion models of chemically driven fluids
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
50
]
require additional assumptions regarding the validity of equilibrium free energy functionals and the nature of phase-separated interfaces in order to provide a complete description of phase coexistence.
Although near-equilibrium assumptions are appropriate for small driving forces (
|\beta\Delta\mu|\ll 1
) or shallow concentration gradients (
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
), our simulations demonstrate that the predictions of near-equilibrium theories can fail qualitatively under more general conditions.
Our theoretical approach solves this problem by treating the microscopically sharp interface as a boundary condition via the FLEX framework, which accounts for the molecular interactions and the details of the reaction scheme without assuming an effective equilibrium at any length scale.
Nonetheless, because the approximations invoked by FLEX are most effective when the capillary fluctuations are small, further work will be needed to improve the predictions of our theory near critical points.
Future studies will also be needed to pinpoint the precise relationship between the nonequilibrium interfacial tension and the capillary fluctuation spectrum under far-from-equilibrium conditions.

Finally, we emphasize that our simulation results and theoretical predictions are crucial for understanding the nucleation and coarsening behavior of nonequilibrium biomolecular condensates, as well as their ability to function as localized centers for chemical reactions
[
51
]
.
Nonequilibrium interfacial properties in particular strongly affect the kinetics of nucleation by controlling the height of the nucleation barrier that must be surmounted in order for a small droplet to grow
[
2
,
52
]
.
Accurately describing phase-separated interfaces is also likely to be important in the context of droplet coarsening
[
19
,
9
]
and droplet fusion, as these processes are driven by the minimization of the interfacial free energy in equilibrium fluids
[
16
,
17
]
.
In addition, we anticipate that nonequilibrium interfacial fluctuations will affect the adsorption of surfactant-like molecules
[
10
,
53
]
, the transport of metabolites
[
54
,
55
]
, and the catalytic properties of condensate interfaces
[
56
]
.
These wide-ranging implications underscore the need for quantitative experimental measurements of nonequilibrium interfacial properties, such as the measurements of droplet size scaling that we have prototyped via simulation in this work, which could be used to test our predictions for chemically driven fluids, especially in the important case of biomolecular condensates
in vivo
.

Materials and Methods

Simulation Model—

We simulate a minimal model of a chemically driven fluid using a two-dimensional square lattice with periodic boundaries, in which the total number of molecules is conserved.
Chemical reactions and molecular diffusion are simulated via the kinetic Monte Carlo algorithm
[
57
]
following the kinetic scheme described in the main text, with
\Delta\mu
held constant over the course of each simulation.
For flat-interface simulations, we initialize the system with a
50\times 50
block of B-state molecules at the center of a
50\times 200
lattice.
For finite-droplet simulations, we initialize the system of size
L\times L
by filling all lattice site within a distance
R
from the center with B-state molecules, where
R\in[8.5,25]
and
L\in[101,171]
depending on the simulation parameters.
In both cases, additional molecules are randomly distributed in the dilute phase to initialize the simulation close to the steady-state dilute-phase concentration
\rho^{\text{v}}
.
The system is then relaxed to steady state over
5\times 10^{5}
sweeps of events, after which measurements of steady-state quantities are accumulated over another
10^{6}
sweeps.
Samples are collected every
10^{2}
sweeps.
The reaction–diffusion length
\xi
is measured directly in simulations of the dilute phase only (Fig. S3).

To define the instantaneous interface in a microscopic configuration obtained via simulation, we first identify the condensed phase as the largest contiguous cluster of B-state molecule-occupied lattice sites and the dilute phase as the largest contiguous cluster of either implicit solvent-occupied or I-state molecule-occupied lattice sites.
We then define the instantaneous interface as the set of all lattice sites in each phase that neighbor lattice sites in the other phase.
Consequently, lattice sites at the interface can have
n_{\text{B}}\in\{1,2,3\}
.
We obtain the time-averaged mesoscopic concentration profiles
\rho_{\text{B/I}}
from simulations by averaging the position of the instantaneous interface with respect to either
z
or
r
at steady state, where we first compute the center of mass of the condensed-phase cluster in each instantaneous configuration to define the origin,
r=0
.
The Gibbs dividing surface is then defined from the concentration profile where
\rho_{\text{B}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
In flat-interface simulations, interfacial height fluctuations,
\Delta h
, are defined as the distance from the instantaneous interface to the Gibbs dividing surface.
We compute
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle
by averaging over the entire interface in each of the microscopic configurations sampled at steady state.
The power spectrum of height fluctuations,
\langle|\Delta h(q)|^{2}\rangle
, is computed by first calculating
\Delta h(q)
for each microscopic configuration via a discrete Fourier transform and then taking the average over the spectra sampled at steady state.

The total entropy production rate density,
\dot{\Sigma}
, is calculated from a trajectory of duration
T
, comprising a total of
N
reactive or diffusive events that occur at a particular lattice site
i
,

\textstyle\dot{\Sigma}(i)\equiv T^{-1}k_{\text{B}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln(k_{n}/\tilde{k}_{n}),

(6)

where
k_{\text{B}}
is Boltzmann constant and
k_{n}
and
\tilde{k}_{n}
are the actual and time-reversed rates, respectively, associated with event
n
at lattice site
i

[
45
]
.
The mesoscopic diffusive flux density is measured by averaging the net number of diffusive events moving right,
N^{\rightarrow}_{\text{B/I}}
, versus left,
N^{\leftarrow}_{\text{B/I}}
, at lattice site
i
within the simulation duration
T
,

j_{\text{B/I}}(i)=\left[N^{\rightarrow}_{\text{B/I}}(i)-N^{\leftarrow}_{\text{B/I}}(i)\right]/T.

(7)

The mesoscopic reactive flux density in the
{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
direction,
{s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}}
, at lattice site
i
is similarly defined as

s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(i)-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(i)=\left[N_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(i)-N_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(i)\right]/T,

(8)

where
N_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
and
N_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
are the numbers of
{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
and
{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
chemical reactions, respectively, occurring at lattice site
i
within the simulation duration
T
.
The mesoscopic diffusive and reactive fluxes in flat-interface simulations are averaged perpendicular to the
z
axis after aligning the microscopic configurations with respect to the center of mass of the condensed phase.
In the curved-interface simulations, the concentration profiles and flux densities are measured along the principal axes passing through the center of mass of the droplet in accordance with the four-fold symmetry of the square lattice.
When decomposing the mesoscopic fluxes into contributions from the bulk phases and interface in Fig.
2
B,C, we attribute the contribution to the total fluxes in Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) to the interface if the lattice site
i
is part of the instantaneous interface, as defined above, and otherwise to one of the bulk phases.
The dilute-phase contribution to the B-state concentration profile in Fig.

All simulation data presented in the main text are averaged over 96 independent simulations.
Uncertainties associated with simulation measurements are comparable to or smaller than the size of symbols in all figures unless indicated explicitly.

Microscopic–Mesoscopic Theory of Nonequilibrium Phase Separation—

Here we summarize our theoretical approach, which combines a microscopic treatment of the interface with a mesoscopic description of the dilute phase.
For a detailed derivation and discussion, we refer the reader to the SI.
The alternative sharp-interface theory that assumes equilibrium boundary conditions at the interface
[
9
,
10
]
, whose predictions are shown by dotted lines in Fig.
4
A, is also described in the SI.

The dilute phase is governed by Eq. (
3
) for
r>R
, which predicts that
|\delta\rho_{\text{B/I}}|
is greatest where the dilute phase contacts the interface at
r=R+
and decays to zero according to the length scale
\xi
.
Assuming that the dilute phase is ideal (so that
n_{\text{B}}=0
), we predict

\xi=\sqrt{D/[k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)]},

(9)

where
D=\Lambda/4
is the self-diffusion coefficient on the two-dimensional square lattice in the ideal limit and
k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
and
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
represent the summed rate constants over the passive and driven pathways.
Solving Eq. (
3
) yields the diffusive flux densities,

\displaystyle j_{\text{B/I}}(r)

\displaystyle=-D\nabla\rho_{\text{B/I}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{D[\rho_{\text{B/I}}(R+)-\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{v}}]}{\xi}\dfrac{K_{1}(\xi^{-1}r)}{K_{0}(\xi^{-1}R)},

(10)

where
K_{n}
is the
n
th order modified Bessel function of the second kind.
Eq. (
10
) applies to the flat interface in the macroscopic limit,
R\rightarrow\infty
, but with both
K_{n}
’s replaced with exponential functions.

We next describe the microscopic interface by employing a Fixed Local Environment approXimation (FLEX)
[
2
,
3
]
, which assumes that the marginal distribution
p(n_{\text{B}})
is unperturbed by nonequilibrium driving.
This approach allows us to compute the steady-state probabilities
p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})
and
p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})
of finding B and I-state molecules, respectively, at a tagged lattice site on the interface with
n_{\text{B}}
B-state nearest neighbors by accounting for chemical reactions and diffusive exchange with the dilute phase.
At steady state,

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})=0=[

\displaystyle p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})]-j_{\text{B}}^{\text{FLEX}}(n_{\text{B}}),

(11)

where the term in brackets is the net reactive flux density,
{s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})}
, and the net diffusive flux density is

\displaystyle j_{\text{B}}^{\text{FLEX}}(n_{\text{B}})=D\{

\displaystyle p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})e^{n_{\text{B}}\beta\epsilon}[1-\rho_{\text{B}}(R+)-\rho_{\text{I}}(R+)]

(12)

\displaystyle-\rho_{\text{B}}(R+)[1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})]\}.

Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), along with analogous equations for
\dot{p}_{\text{I}}
and
j_{\text{I}}^{\text{FLEX}}
, uniquely determine the conditional distribution
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
for each value of
n_{\text{B}}
on the interface.
The interfacial boundary conditions Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), along with conservation of mass at steady state,
j_{\text{B}}+j_{\text{I}}=0
, and the far-field condition for an ideal dilute phase,
\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}/\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{v}}=k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)/k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)
, then uniquely determine the four unknowns
\rho_{\text{B/I}}(R+)
and
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{v}}
at nonequilibrium phase coexistence.

Within the FLEX approximation, we predict qualitative variations in the interfacial height fluctuations by computing an effective nearest-neighbor interaction energy,
\tilde{\epsilon}
, for an adsorbed B-state molecule,

\beta\tilde{\epsilon}=\ln\left[p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=1)/(1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=1))\right],

(13)

and then applying an equilibrium theory of capillary fluctuations
[
7
]
in which
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle\propto\sinh^{2}(\beta\tilde{\epsilon}/4)
at phase coexistence.
Empirical values of
\sigma
are extracted from simulations of finite-size droplets carried out with a constant simulation volume but varying overall concentration, resulting in a range of steady-state droplet radii (Fig.
5
A,B).
The far-field total concentration
\rho^{\text{v}}
for each simulation is determined by averaging the concentration far from the droplet interface, i.e.,
r>R+2\xi
.
Finally, we determine
\sigma
by first predicting the conditions for phase coexistence in the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
and then fitting the theory to the simulation data
\ln(\rho^{\text{v}}/\rho^{\text{v,flat}})
versus
R^{-1}
using
\sigma
as the sole fitting parameter (Fig.
5

The dilute-phase concentration gradient is predicted by this theory to vanish in both the reaction-dominant limit (
\Lambda\rightarrow 0
) and in the diffusion-dominant limit (
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
) for finite
\beta\Delta\mu
.
In the diffusion-dominant limit,
J_{\text{B}}/\xi
is nonzero (Fig.
4

j_{\text{B}}(r=R+)/D\rightarrow 0
since
j_{\text{B}}(r=R+)
must be finite to satisfy the FLEX boundary condition given by Eq. (
4
).
Consequently, this limit corresponds to an effective equilibrium and the interfacial statistics approach the Boltzmann distribution, implying
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle\rightarrow\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle_{\text{eq}}
(Fig.
4

\sigma\rightarrow\sigma_{\text{eq}}
.
In the reaction-dominant limit, both
j_{\text{B}}(r=R+)\rightarrow 0
and
J_{\text{B}}/\xi\rightarrow 0

Supplementary Information for “Interfacial Effects Determine Nonequilibrium Phase Behaviors in Chemically Driven Fluids”

In this supporting material, we provide a complete description of the microscopic–mesoscopic theory of phase coexistence in chemically driven fluids that is introduced in the main text, and we compare this theory with an alternative approach in which the phase-separated interface is assumed to be at thermal equilibrium.

Mesoscopic Description of the Dilute Phase

We consider a two-dimensional system at a steady state in which a condensed-phase droplet with radius
R
is centered at the origin,
r=0
.
The coordinate
r
thus indicates the distance from the center of mass of the droplet.
At a mesoscopic level, the dilute phase in the region
r>R
can be described by coupled reaction–diffusion equations at steady state,

\displaystyle 0=\dot{\rho_{\text{B}}}=D_{\text{B}}\nabla^{2}\rho_{\text{B}}-s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}})+s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}})

(1a)

\displaystyle 0=\dot{\rho_{\text{I}}}=D_{\text{I}}\nabla^{2}\rho_{\text{I}}+s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}})-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}}),

(1b)

where
\rho_{\text{B/I}}
and
D_{\text{B/I}}
are the concentration profiles and self-diffusion coefficients for the B-state and I-state molecules, respectively, and
s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}
and
s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
are the reactive flux densities for the chemical reactions in the indicated directions.
Here we have assumed that the dilute phase is ideal, since the molecules are sparsely distributed, and that the self-diffusion coefficients are independent of concentration.
For small changes in the concentration profiles,
\delta\rho_{\text{B/I}}\equiv\rho_{\text{B/I}}-\rho^{\text{v}}_{\text{B/I}}

\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{v}}

\displaystyle 0=\delta\dot{\rho}_{\text{B}}=D_{\text{B}}\nabla^{2}\delta\rho_{\text{B}}-k^{\circ}_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}\delta\rho_{\text{B}}+k^{\circ}_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}\delta\rho_{\text{I}}

(2a)

\displaystyle 0=\delta\dot{\rho}_{\text{I}}=D_{\text{I}}\nabla^{2}\delta\rho_{\text{I}}+k^{\circ}_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}\delta\rho_{\text{B}}-k^{\circ}_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}\delta\rho_{\text{I}},

(2b)

k^{\circ}_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}

Summing the two equations in Eq. (

Because the system is azimuthal-symmetric and the far-field concentrations should not diverge, Eq. (

0=D_{\text{B}}\delta\rho_{\text{B}}+D_{\text{I}}\delta\rho_{\text{I}}.

(4)

In Eq. (

\rho_{\text{B/I}}(r)=\left[\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{+}-\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{v}}\right]\dfrac{K_{0}(\xi^{-1}r)}{K_{0}(\xi^{-1}R)}+\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{v}},

(7)

where
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{+}\equiv\rho_{\text{B/I}}(R+)

is the nth order modified Bessel function of the second kind.
When the concentration profiles in Eq. (

j_{\text{B/I}}(r)=-D_{\text{B/I}}\nabla\rho_{\text{B/I}}=\dfrac{D_{\text{B/I}}[\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{+}-\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{v}}]}{\xi}\dfrac{K_{1}(\xi^{-1}r)}{K_{0}(\xi^{-1}R)}.

(8)

7

In the limit of a macroscopically flat interface, i.e.,

The assumption of ideal behavior in the dilute phase allows for further simplifications with regard to the self-diffusion coefficients and linearized reaction rates.

Mesoscopic Description of the Condensed Phase

An analogous reaction–diffusion description can also be applied to the condensed phase.
In our microscopic model, molecules can only be displaced when they are adjacent to an unoccupied lattice site (i.e., a “hole”) so that the mobility is effectively scaled by the concentration of holes,
\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}}
, relative to the dilute phase.
Assuming that the holes are sparsely distributed in the condensed phase, so that molecules are adjacent to at most one hole at a time, the condensed-phase self-diffusion coefficients for the B and I-state molecules are approximately

\displaystyle D_{\text{B}}^{\text{l}}=(\Lambda/4)\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}}\zeta^{-1}

(17a)

\displaystyle D_{\text{I}}^{\text{l}}=(\Lambda/4)\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}},

(17b)

where the factor
\zeta^{-1}\equiv e^{3\beta\epsilon}
accounts for the fact that B-state molecules that are adjacent to a hole in the condensed phase have
n_{\text{B}}\approx 3
B-state nearest neighbors in this approximation.
Linearizing the reaction–diffusion equations around the far-field condensed phase densities
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\text{l}}
again leads to Eq. (
5
), but with
\xi
replaced by
\xi^{\text{l}}
in the condensed phase.
Boundary conditions for Eq. (
5
) are similarly required where the condensed phase contacts the interface,
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{-}\equiv\rho_{\text{B/I}}(R-)
.

Nonetheless, in the case of the strongly phase-separated conditions that we study in this work, we observe negligible mesoscopic fluxes in the condensed phase.
By comparing
\xi^{\text{l}}
with
\xi
,

\left(\dfrac{\xi^{\text{l}}}{\xi}\right)^{2}=\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}}\dfrac{k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)}{\zeta k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}=4)+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=4)},

(18)

where we have assumed
n_{\text{B}}\approx 4
in the condensed phase, we find that
(\xi^{\text{l}})^{2}/\xi^{2}\ll 1
, which implies that the range of mesoscopic fluxes in the condensed phase is much smaller than in the dilute phase.
This prediction is supported by the lack of any noticeable contribution from the condensed phase to the mesoscopic flux densities in Fig. 2B,C in the main text.
Evaluating Eq. (
18
) at the conditions corresponding to that figure,
(\beta\epsilon,\beta\Delta\mu,\Lambda,\beta\Delta f)=(-2.95,2,10^{2},1.7337)
, in which the observed hole concentration is
\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}}=2.5\times 10^{-3}\approx\exp(2\beta\epsilon)
, leads to
(\xi^{\text{l}})^{2}/\xi^{2}=4.2\times 10^{-3}
.
We therefore ignore contributions from the condensed phase in our theory and focus only on the contributions from the dilute phase and the interface in the main text.

Modeling Nonequilibrium Phase Coexistence with a Sharp Interface

We now develop a sharp-interface theory of nonequilibrium phase coexistence.
We first consider the case of a macroscopically flat interface (i.e.,
R\rightarrow\infty
) and return to the case of a curved droplet interface later.
In the case of a macroscopically flat interface, the mesoscopic concentration profiles for the two coexisting phases meet at the sharp interface at
z=0
, as shown schematically in the following diagram:

By introducing “(non)equilibrium constants” for the far-field boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}}{\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{v}}}=\frac{k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\circ,\text{v}}}{k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\circ,\text{v}}}\equiv K^{\text{v}}

(19a)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{l}}}{\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{l}}}=\frac{k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\circ,\text{l}}}{k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\circ,\text{l}}}\equiv K^{\text{l}},

(19b)

we can write the steady-state concentration fields in the dilute and liquid phases as

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{B}}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{K^{\text{l}}}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}+\zeta\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})+\left[\frac{\zeta}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}-\frac{\zeta K^{\text{l}}}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}\right]e^{z/\xi^{\text{l}}}&z<0\\
\displaystyle\frac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}+\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})+\left[\frac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\frac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}\right]e^{-z/\xi}\quad&z>0\end{cases}

(20a)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{I}}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}+\zeta\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})-\dfrac{1}{\zeta}\left[\frac{\zeta}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}-\frac{\zeta K^{\text{l}}}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}\right]e^{z/\xi^{\text{l}}}&z<0\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}+\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})-\left[\frac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\frac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}\right]e^{-z/\xi}&z>0.\end{cases}

(20b)

From these expressions, we can determine the steady-state concentration profiles, and thus the far-field concentrations, from the boundary conditions at the interface,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}=\frac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}+\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})

\displaystyle\qquad\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{v}}=\frac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}+\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})

(21a)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{l}}=\frac{K^{\text{l}}}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}+\zeta\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})

\displaystyle\qquad\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{l}}=\frac{1}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}+\zeta\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}).

(21b)

We therefore need four boundary conditions at the sharp interface to determine
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
at phase coexistence.

Nonequilibrium Interfacial Boundary Conditions Using the Fixed Local Environment ApproXimation (FLEX)

To describe the nonequilibrium steady-state properties of the interface at a microscopic level, we utilize the Fixed Local Environment approXimation (FLEX) introduced in Refs.
[
2
,
3
]
.
Within the FLEX framework, we focus on a tagged lattice site that is assumed to be at steady state with respect to a frozen local environment.
Specifically, we assume that the number of nearest-neighbor B-state molecules,
n_{\text{B}}
, remains constant for each tagged lattice site at the interface.
This approximation is valid when the chemical reactions and diffusion at the tagged site occur much faster than the collective rearrangement of the interface.
In light of this, we expect the FLEX predictions to be most accurate in the reaction-dominant limit,
\Lambda\rightarrow 0
, where the rearrangement of the interface via diffusion is suppressed.
Importantly, this approach allows reactions to take place on the interface itself, which is a qualitative distinction from the equilibrium interface approach that we discuss later.
Moreover, this approach makes it possible to resolve fluctuations with respect to the conditional distribution
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
on the interface; however, the FLEX approximation implies that the marginal distribution
p(n_{\text{B}})
is unperturbed relative to equilibrium.

Within the FLEX approximation, we treat each local environment separately, resulting in two steady-state equations for each value of
n_{\text{B}}\in\{1,2,3\}
on the interface,

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=[s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})]-j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})+j_{\text{B}}^{-}(n_{\text{B}})=0

(22a)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=[s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})-s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})]-j_{\text{I}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})+j_{\text{I}}^{-}(n_{\text{B}})=0.

(22b)

Transport of molecules to the interface occurs via net diffusive fluxes, which couple the interfacial region to the two bulk phases.
Based on the microscopic dynamical scheme presented in the main text, the net fluxes in each direction involving a lattice site with
n_{\text{B}}
neighbors are

\displaystyle j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=D\left[e^{\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}}p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})(1-\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})-\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}(1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}))\right]

(23a)

\displaystyle j_{\text{I}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=D\left[p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})(1-\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})-\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}(1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}))\right]

(23b)

\displaystyle j_{\text{B}}^{-}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=-D\left[e^{\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}}p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})(1-\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}-\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})-e^{3\beta\epsilon}\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}(1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}))\right]

(23c)

\displaystyle j_{\text{I}}^{-}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=-D\left[p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})(1-\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}-\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})-\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}(1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}))\right],

(23d)

where
D=\Lambda/4
and
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
indicates the conditional probability that the lattice site is in state
i
given that it has
n_{\text{B}}
neighbors.
The effect of excluded volume interactions on the diffusive flux densities is included via a mean-field approximation.
Because the reactive flux densities

\displaystyle s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})

(24a)

\displaystyle s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})

(24b)

only involve
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
and the rate constants prescribed by the reaction scheme, the FLEX approximation provides a unique solution for the conditional probabilities
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
at the interface given the mesoscopic boundary conditions
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
.
Moreover, because we ignore fluxes in the condensed phase for the conditions studied in our simulations, Eq. (
22

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})+j_{\text{I}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})

(25)

\displaystyle j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})

\displaystyle=s_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})-s_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}).

(26)

Note that in the main text, we refer to
j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})
as
j_{\text{B}}^{\text{FLEX}}(n_{\text{B}})
.

The interfacial boundary conditions (BCs) for determining
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
are as follows:

BC (1).

\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}=\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{eq,l}}

The condensed-phase B-state concentration is assumed to be unperturbed relative to equilibrium for the parameter regime that we study in simulations, i.e., the strongly phase-separated regime.

BC (2).

\langle j_{\text{B}}^{-}(n_{\text{B}})\rangle\equiv\sum_{n_{\text{B}}\in\{1,2,3\}}p(n_{\text{B}})j_{\text{B}}^{-}(n_{\text{B}})=-(\Lambda/4)\nabla\rho_{\text{B}}\big|_{z=0-}=0
:

In the strongly phase-separated regime, we assume that fluxes in the condensed phase are negligible.
This condition fixes
\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{l}}=\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{eq,l}}
in conjunction with BC (1), which in turn fixes
\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{l}}=\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}=\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{eq,l}}/K^{\text{l}}
.
In this expression and henceforth, the notation
\langle\cdot\rangle
indicates an average over
n_{\text{B}}
on the interface weighted by
p(n_{\text{B}})
.

BC (3).

\langle j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})\rangle\equiv\sum_{n_{\text{B}}\in\{1,2,3\}}p(n_{\text{B}})j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})=-(\Lambda/4)\nabla\rho_{\text{B}}\big|_{z=0+}
:

To ensure mass conservation everywhere, we must match the diffusive flux between the interface and the dilute phase.
In keeping with the spirit of FLEX, we use the marginal distribution
p(n_{\text{B}})
obtained from an equilibrium simulation for this calculation.
According to Eq. (
20
), the mesoscopic flux on the right-hand side is

j_{\text{B}}(z=0+)=\frac{D}{\xi}\left[\frac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\frac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}\right]

(27)

for a macroscopically flat interface.

BC (4).

\ln[\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle/(1-\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle)]=0
:

This equation states that the nonequilibrium driving force for growth of the condensed phase, which is well approximated by the left-hand side of this equation
[
2
]
, must be zero in order for the phases to be at coexistence at a nonequilibrium steady state.
The application of this expression to nonequilibrium conditions is motivated by the particle–hole symmetry of the lattice gas
[
2
]
.
As shown in Ref.
[
3
]
, this condition can be interpreted as mechanical balance, which is equivalent to the condition of equal osmotic pressure at equilibrium.
This BC also implies
\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for a macroscopically flat interface, which is the definition of the Gibbs dividing surface at coexistence.

The predictions of this theory are compared with simulation results in Figs. 4 and 5 in the main text and in Supplementary Figs.
6
and
7
.
In principle, the FLEX framework can be extended to describe nonequilibrium phase coexistence in which mesoscopic fluxes in the condensed phase are not negligible.
In this case, BC (1) would be removed, BC (2) would be straightforwardly modified to allow for nonzero fluxes in the condensed phase, and BCs (3) and (4) would remain unchanged.
A new fourth BC would then be needed to fully determine the conditions for nonequilibrium phase coexistence.
We leave this question to future work, as additional simulation results would be needed to test any proposals.

Nonequilibrium Interfacial Fluctuations are Required for Mesoscopic Fluxes

To study fluctuations on the interface, we define an effective internal energy difference between the B and I states for each local environment according to the relation

p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})=p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})e^{-\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}(n_{\text{B}})-\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}}.

(28)

If the fluctuations are Boltzmann-distributed, then
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}(n_{\text{B}})
must be the same for all values of
n_{\text{B}}
.

Using the definition in Eq. (
28
), we can rearrange Eq. (
23
) and use the steady-state condition, Eq. (
25
), to write

\left(e^{-\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}(n_{\text{B}})+\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}}-1\right)\left(\frac{p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})}{\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}}\right)(1-\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})=\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}}+\frac{1}{\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}}\right)\left(\frac{j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})}{D}\right)\,\forall n_{\text{B}},

(29)

where
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}
is defined equivalently to Eq. (
28
) for
\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}
and
\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}
with
n_{\text{B}}=0
.
Now let us assume that the fluctuations are Boltzmann-distributed.
If there are
m
possible values of
n_{\text{B}}
on the interface, then we obtain
m-1
additional constraints.
Therefore the system of equations for
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
and
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
is now overdetermined, and as a result there is no solution for arbitrary parameter choices.
Hence, the fluctuations cannot be Boltzmann-distributed in general.
To find the conditions under which we do have Boltzmann-distributed fluctuations, we need Eq. (
29
) to be trivially satisfied for all
n_{\text{B}}
, which can only occur if
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}(n_{\text{B}})=\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}
for all
n_{\text{B}}
.
This condition then implies that
j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})/D=0
for all
n_{\text{B}}
, and consequently that the mesoscopic quantity
j_{\text{B}}(z=0+)/D=0
.
Moreover, meeting these conditions requires that the right-hand side of Eq. (
26
) is zero, which means

p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})=p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})\,\forall n_{\text{B}}.

(30)

Using Eq. (
28
), this relation becomes

\frac{k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})}{k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})}=e^{\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}+\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}}\,\forall n_{\text{B}}.

(31)

Eq. (
31
) is the condition for local detailed balance with
n_{\text{B}}
-independent backward rate constants
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})=\text{const.}
, which we refer to as a homogeneous reaction scheme
[
2
]
.
Because this equation must also hold for
r>R
, where we have assumed that
n_{\text{B}}=0
in the dilute phase, we can write
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}
explicitly as

\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}=\beta\Delta f+\ln\left[\frac{k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{p}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)e^{\beta\Delta\mu}}{k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{p}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)}\right].

(32)

In summary, achieving Boltzmann statistics for the conditional probabilities on the interface requires that
j_{\text{B}}(z=0+)/D=0
, which in turn requires that
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
is independent of
n_{\text{B}}
.
If we instead assume that mesoscopic fluxes are present in the dilute phase with a finite self-diffusion coefficient
D
, which requires
j_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})\neq 0
for some
n_{\text{B}}
in Eq. (
29
), then we must have
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}(n_{\text{B}})\neq\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}
, which means that the statistics cannot be Boltzmann throughout the system and
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
must have a non-constant
n_{\text{B}}
-dependence.

In the case of a homogeneous reaction scheme, which may correspond, for example, to a scenario in which the enzymes that are required for the driven reaction pathway are homogeneously distributed in the condensed and dilute phases
[
4
,
2
]
, the reactions must still satisfy the local detailed balance constraint given by Eq. (2) in the main text.
The driven reaction rate constants in this scenario are thus

\displaystyle k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}^{\text{d}}(n_{\text{B}})=\eta

(33a)

\displaystyle k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}^{\text{d}}(n_{\text{B}})=\eta\exp(\beta\Delta f+\beta\Delta\mu+\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}}),

(33b)

while the passive reaction rate constants remain unchanged, so that Eq. (
32
) reduces to

\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}=\beta\Delta f+\ln\left[\dfrac{1+\eta e^{\beta\Delta\mu}}{1+\eta}\right].

(34)

Simulations confirm the prediction that the reaction scheme given by Eq. (
33
) does not result in mesoscopic fluxes, even when the system is driven far from equilibrium (Fig.
9
).
This concentration-independent reaction scheme dissipates energy at a higher rate in the dilute phase (Fig.
9
A), but nonetheless leads to zero net mesoscopic diffusive flux (Fig.
9
B).
This result demonstrates that spatially inhomogeneous entropy production due to microscopic transitions occurring in coexisting phases does not necessarily guarantee observable nonequilibrium effects at mesoscopic scales
[
5
]
.
Rather, the emergence of mesoscopic nonequilibrium effects depends on the microscopic details of the system and, according to our theory, is mediated by fluctuations at the phase-separated interface.

Limiting Behaviors in the Diffusion-dominant and Reaction-dominant Regimes

Elements of the effective equilibrium analysis introduced in the previous section are also useful for investigating the limiting behaviors in the diffusion-dominant (
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
) and reaction-dominant (
\Lambda\rightarrow 0
) regimes.
In the diffusion-dominant limit, the self-diffusion coefficient
D=\Lambda/4
diverges.
However, the diffusive flux
j_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})
must remain finite for all values of
n_{\text{B}}
to satisfy Eq. (
26
) within the FLEX framework for finite
\beta\Delta\mu
and physical values of
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
.
Therefore,
j_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})/D
approaches zero in this limit, and the interfacial statistics follow the Boltzmann distribution as indicated by Eq. (
29
), with the effective internal energy difference
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}^{+}
in Eq. (
32
) governing the distributions in both the dilute phase and on the interface simultaneously.
For this reason, the capillary fluctuations approach the equilibrium distribution in this limit, as shown in Fig. 4D in the main text.
Finiteness of
j_{\text{B}}
also implies that the concentration gradient
\nabla\rho_{\text{B}}|_{R+}
associated with the flux in BC (3) must vanish, leading to a uniform concentration profile in the dilute phase, such that the effective equilibrium description on the interface extends to the far field.

This effective equilibrium behavior in the diffusion-dominant limit can also be understood intuitively by considering the implied separation of timescales.
In the kinetic scheme introduced in Eq. (1) in the main text, the fundamental time unit is defined by the passive
{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}
reaction.
However, if time were instead measured with respect to the timescale for diffusive events, then reactions would occur at a vanishingly slow rate in the rescaled time units, which is consistent with the notion of an effective thermal equilibrium.
Viewed this way, it is clear that the behavior in the diffusion-dominant regime is consistent with the assumptions of linear irreversible thermodynamics
[
6
]
, which is valid for small perturbations from equilibrium (or for small perturbations from an effective equilibrium in which the system is globally described by Boltzmann statistics with a common
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}
).
This observation explains why our nonequilibrium sharp-interface theory and the alternative theory shown by the dotted lines in Fig. 4A in the main text (see below for details) match in the diffusion-dominant limit, since the latter assumes that all thermodynamic potentials and interfacial material properties are equivalent to the equilibrium system.

By contrast, in the reaction-dominant regime, the statistics of interfacial fluctuations do not necessarily follow the Boltzmann distribution, and the system cannot be described by a global effective equilibrium with a common value of
\Delta f_{\text{eff}}
when the reaction scheme involves concentration-dependent rate constants.
In this case,
j_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})
approaches zero due to the vanishing self-diffusion coefficient.
This eliminates the net reactive flux in Eq. (
26
) so that the interfacial distributions
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
must be given by Eq. (
30
).
In Eq. (
30
), any dependence of
k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})
on the local concentrations leads to deviations from Boltzmann statistics, since the ratio
p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}})/p_{\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}})
is no longer proportional to
\exp(-\beta\Delta f_{\text{eff}}-\beta\epsilon n_{\text{B}})
unless the reaction scheme is homogeneous.
For these reasons, the capillary fluctuations deviate from equilibrium in the reaction-dominant limit, as shown in Fig. 4D in the main text.
Nonzero finite values of
j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})/D
indicate that the mesoscopic quantity
j_{\text{B}}^{+}/D
also remains finite in this limit.
However, the mesoscopic fluxes vanish, as shown by
J_{\text{B}}/\xi
in Fig. 4C in the main text, as does the dilute-phase concentration gradient for
r>R
.
We note that this limit is closely related to the non-conservative simulation scheme studied in Refs.
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
.

Nonequilibrium Effects are Maximized when Fluxes Through the Passive and Driven Reaction Pathways are Comparable

Nonequilibrium effects due to the chemical drive vanish in both the passive-reaction dominant
(\eta\rightarrow 0)
and the driven-reaction dominant
(\eta\rightarrow\infty)
limits.
In the former limit, the steady state reaches thermal equilibrium with the internal energy difference
\Delta f
.
In the latter limit, the steady state distribution is equivalent to an equilibrium distribution with
\Delta f\rightarrow\Delta f-\Delta\mu
, since both the forward and backward reactions along the dominant pathway are modified by
\Delta\mu
in accordance with the local detailed balance condition.
These limiting behaviors are predicted for the homogeneous reaction case by Eq. (
34
) and are consistent with the phase diagrams determined for the inhomogeneous reaction scheme with concentration-dependent rate constants that is studied in the main text (Fig.
1
A).

By contrast, nonequilibrium fluctuations are maximized when the reactive fluxes along the two pathways are comparable.
In Fig.
1
B,C, we show that both the nonequilibrium diffusive flux densities and the deviation of the interfacial tension from the equilibrium value are most pronounced near
\eta\approx 1
for the inhomogeneous reaction scheme studied in the main text.

Predicting Nonequilibrium Interfacial Height Fluctuations

Although the FLEX framework assumes that the marginal distribution of local environments,
p(n_{\text{B}})
, is unperturbed by the nonequilibrium chemical drive, we can nonetheless predict qualitative changes in the capillary fluctuations by following the effective-equilibrium approach introduced in Refs.
[
2
,
3
]
.
Specifically, we apply equilibrium capillary fluctuation theory by considering an effective “single-bond” interaction strength between a microscopically flat interface and a single adsorbed B-state molecule.
From the conditional distribution
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
at phase coexistence, we compute the effective interaction

\beta\tilde{\epsilon}\equiv\ln\left[\dfrac{p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=1)}{1-p_{\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=1)}\right],

(35)

from which we can predict the associated interface height fluctuations based on an equilibrium solid-on-solid model
[
7
]
,

\dfrac{\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle}{\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle}_{\text{eq}}=\dfrac{\sinh^{2}(\beta\epsilon/4)}{\sinh^{2}(\beta\tilde{\epsilon}/4)}.

(36)

We emphasize that this approach to predicting nonequilibrium height fluctuations is highly approximate, since we only consider a single local configuration (
n_{\text{B}}=1
) and because the FLEX approximation ignores coupling between chemical reactions and collective rearrangements of the interface.
Although our approach captures qualitative trends, the predictions are generally not quantitatively accurate, as shown in Fig. 4D in the main text.
Inaccuracies are particularly pronounced when the mobility
\Lambda
is large, since the approximations underlying FLEX are less applicable in this regime.
Moreover, our approach may predict
\beta\tilde{\epsilon}=\beta\epsilon
, and thus
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle=\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle_{\text{eq}}
, even when there are non-Boltzmann fluctuations at the interface, which is not consistent with the simulation results shown in Fig. 4D in the main text.
This inaccuracy occurs because the effective bond strength predicted by the FLEX approximation with
n_{\text{B}}=1
, Eq. (
35
), may be extremely similar to the actual bond strength even when the conditional distribution at the interface does not follow Boltzmann statistics overall.
Finally, because FLEX does not predict changes in the marginal distribution
p(n_{\text{B}})
, this approach does not predict changes in the
q
-dependent scaling of the fluctuation spectrum that are observed in simulations when
\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle\gg\langle\Delta h^{2}\rangle_{\text{eq}}
(Fig.
8
).
Nonetheless, the qualitative predictions of this approach are consistent with the formalism developed above in the sense that this approach always predicts
\Delta h=\Delta h_{\text{eq}}
when the interfacial statistics follow the Boltzmann distribution.
Moreover, it predicts the correct limiting behaviors in the reaction-dominant and diffusion-dominant regimes.

Application to Finite-size Droplets with Curved Interfaces

To apply this theory to a droplet with finite radius
R
, we modify the last two interfacial boundary conditions as follows:

BC (3).

\langle j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})\rangle\equiv\sum_{n_{\text{B}}\in\{1,2,3\}}p(n_{\text{B}})j_{\text{B}}^{+}(n_{\text{B}})=-(\Lambda/4)\nabla\rho_{\text{B}}\big|_{R+}
:

According to Eq. (
8
), the mesoscopic flux on the right-hand side is now

j_{\text{B}}(r=R+)=\frac{D}{\xi}\left[\frac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\frac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}\right]\frac{K_{1}(\xi^{-1}R)}{K_{0}(\xi^{-1}R)}.

(37)

BC (4).

\ln[\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle/(1-\langle p_{\text{B}}\rangle)]-\beta\sigma/R=0
:

This equation relates the nonequilibrium driving force for the growth of the condensed phase to a phenomenological Gibbs–Thomson correction with nonequilibrium interfacial tension
\sigma
.
At equilibrium, the first term represents the osmotic pressure difference between the two phases, so that this BC is equivalent to the Young–Laplace equation for a two-dimensional fluid,
\beta\Delta P=\beta\sigma_{\text{eq}}/R
, with the equilibrium interfacial tension
\sigma_{\text{eq}}

[
8
]
.
Here we extend this concept to nonequilibrium conditions, whereby the first term represents the nonequilibrium potential difference across the interface
[
3
]
and the second term assumes that a similar Gibbs–Thomson-like correction still plays a role, albeit with a nonequilibrium interfacial tension, leading to mechanical balance across the curved interface at phase coexistence.
This application of a Gibbs–Thomson-like correction is consistent with prior simulations of chemically driven droplet nucleation
[
2
]
, which can be described quantitatively by assuming a
\beta\sigma/R
term in the expression for the nonequilibrium nucleation barrier.

At equilibrium, BC (3) does not affect the size scaling of droplets due to the absence of diffusive fluxes.
Yet under nonequilibrium conditions, the droplet curvature appears in both BC (3) via Eq. (
37
) and explicitly in BC (4).
Whether the droplet follows an equilibrium-like size scaling relation therefore depends on the relative size of the droplet compared to the reaction–diffusion length in the dilute phase.

We first consider the scenario in which the reaction–diffusion length is small compared to the droplet radius,
\xi\ll R
.
In this case, the Bessel function term in Eq. (
37
) remains close to unity (Fig.
13
), such that BC (3) becomes insensitive to
R
.
Qualitatively, this means that the interface appears to be locally flat on length scales comparable to
\xi
.
The droplet-size dependence of the coexistence conditions is then entirely contained within BC (4) so that we may expect an equilibrium-like scaling of the form
\ln(\rho^{\text{v}}/\rho^{\text{v,flat}})\sim R^{-1}
, which matches the simulation observations in Fig. 5A in the main text.

In the opposite scenario where the reaction–diffusion length is comparable to or greater than the droplet radius,
\xi\gtrsim R
, the scaling relation can be more complicated due to the
R
-dependence of the nonequilibrium diffusive flux.
In this case, the Bessel function term in Eq. (
37
) is sensitive to
R
(Fig.
13
), so that the size scaling is governed by both boundary conditions.
Consequently,
\ln(\rho^{\text{v}}/\rho^{\text{v,flat}})
can become a nonlinear function of
R^{-1}
, as shown in Fig. 5B in the main text.
Nonetheless, our theory predicts that nonequilibrium droplets in both the reaction-dominant
(\Lambda\rightarrow 0)
and the diffusion-dominant
(\Lambda\rightarrow\infty)
limits revert to the equilibrium-like scaling form
\ln(\rho^{\text{v}}/\rho^{\text{v,flat}})\sim R^{-1}
.
In the reaction-dominant regime, the diffusive flux vanishes so that the droplet-size dependence only enters BC (4), as in equilibrium; however, the interfacial tension may differ from equilibrium since the interfacial fluctuations may not follow Boltzmann statistics in this limit.
On the other hand, in the diffusion-dominant limit, both the scaling form and the interfacial tension are the same as in equilibrium, since the system approaches a global effective equilibrium due to the vanishing concentration gradient in the dilute phase.

We emphasize that the effect of nonequilibrium fluxes in BC (3) is separate from the change in the interfacial tension and that both effects are required to explain the observed size scaling under nonequilibrium conditions.
For demonstration purposes, we show the predictions of an alternative theory in which the interfacial tension in BC (4) is fixed to the equilibrium value,
\sigma_{\text{eq}}
, in Fig.
10
.
This alternative theory accurately predicts nonlinear size scaling for intermediate values of
\Lambda
but does not agree quantitatively with the simulation data due to the neglect of the nonequilibrium interfacial tension.

Alternative Sharp-interface Theory with Equilibrium Interfacial Boundary Conditions

Existing sharp-interface theories, including Ref.
[
9
]
and Ref.
[
10
]
, assume that equilibrium phase-coexistence conditions apply at the sharp interface in the presence of nonequilibrium mesoscopic fluxes.
Here we follow the approach taken in Ref.
[
10
]
, although other theories that assume equilibrium phase coexistence at the interface, such as Ref.
[
9
]
, lead to similar conclusions.

In this approach, it is first necessary to propose an equilibrium model for the free energy of the system.
To this end, we utilize a ternary regular solution model
[
8
]
to compute the chemical potentials of the two solute species,
\mu_{\text{B}}
and
\mu_{\text{I}}
, and the osmotic pressure,
P
, as a function of
\rho_{\text{B}}
and
\rho_{\text{I}}
:

\displaystyle\beta\mu_{\text{B}}(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}})

\displaystyle=\ln\rho_{\text{B}}-\ln(1-\rho_{\text{B}}-\rho_{\text{I}})+4\epsilon\rho_{\text{B}}

(38a)

\displaystyle\beta\mu_{\text{I}}(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}})

\displaystyle=\ln\rho_{\text{I}}-\ln(1-\rho_{\text{B}}-\rho_{\text{I}})

(38b)

\displaystyle\beta P(\rho_{\text{B}},\rho_{\text{I}})

\displaystyle=-\ln(1-\rho_{\text{B}}-\rho_{\text{I}})+2\epsilon\rho_{\text{B}}^{2}.

(38c)

These formulas are accurate for lattice models when applied far from a critical point and are thus appropriate for modeling the strongly phase-separated regime that we consider in our simulations.

By contrast to our theoretical approach, an equilibrium model of the sharp interface implies that no reactions take place on the interface itself.
Maintaining mass conservation therefore requires that we consider mesoscopic fluxes in both phases.
Specifically, conservation of molecules at a macroscopically flat interface requires

j_{\text{B/I}}(z=0-)=j_{\text{B/I}}(z=0+),

(39)

which, by applying Eq. (
20
), leads to

-\dfrac{\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}}}{\xi^{\text{l}}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{-}-\dfrac{K^{\text{l}}}{\zeta+K^{\text{l}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}\right]=\dfrac{1}{\xi}\left[\dfrac{1}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}-\dfrac{K^{\text{v}}}{1+K^{\text{v}}}\rho_{\text{I}}^{+}\right].

(40)

In the strongly phase-separated limit, the reaction–diffusion lengths in the dilute and condensed phases are

\xi=\sqrt{\Lambda/4[k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)+k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=0)]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\xi^{\text{l}}=\sqrt{\rho_{\text{S}}^{\text{l}}\zeta^{-1}\Lambda/4[k_{\text{B}\rightarrow\text{I}}(n_{\text{B}}=4)+\zeta^{-1}k_{\text{I}\rightarrow\text{B}}(n_{\text{B}}=4)]},

(41)

respectively.
Importantly, the expressions for
\xi
and
\xi^{\text{l}}
imply that the mobility
\Lambda
drops out of Eq. (
40
).
For this reason, the phase-coexistence predictions of this theory are independent of
\Lambda
in Fig. 4A in the main text and in Supplementary Fig.
7
B.

Following Ref.
[
10
]
, we then define four interfacial boundary conditions to uniquely determine
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
:

BC (1).

\mu_{\text{B}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+},\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})=\mu_{\text{B}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-},\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})
: Equal chemical potentials for the B-state molecule in the coexisting phases,

BC (2).

\mu_{\text{I}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+},\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})=\mu_{\text{I}}(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-},\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})
: Equal chemical potentials for the I-state molecule in the coexisting phases,

BC (3).

P(\rho_{\text{B}}^{+},\rho_{\text{I}}^{+})=P(\rho_{\text{B}}^{-},\rho_{\text{I}}^{-})
: Equal osmotic pressures in the coexisting phases, and

BC (4).

Eq. (
40
): Mass conservation at the interface.

The predictions of this theory for the case of a macroscopically flat interface are shown with dashed lines in Fig. 4A in the main text and in Supplementary Fig.
7
B.
As noted above, the nonequilibrium coexistence curve predicted by this theory is always independent of
\Lambda
since
j_{\text{B/I}}\propto\Lambda
on both sides of the interface
(z=0\pm)
, leading to exact cancellation of the
\Lambda
dependence on the left and right-hand sides of Eq. (
39
).
Furthermore, if we compute phase coexistence as a function of
\Delta f
while holding the total far-field concentration
\rho^{\text{v}}\equiv\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{v}}+\rho_{\text{I}}^{\text{v}}
constant, then we find that the values of
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
predicted by this theory are also independent of
\Delta\mu
.
This is because the ideal-gas assumption for the dilute phase, which is used in the derivation of Eq. (
40
), leads to
\rho_{\text{B}}^{+}+\rho_{\text{I}}^{-}=\text{const.}
under this constraint.
Then, because the equilibrium conditions in BCs (1)–(3) define a one-dimensional binodal in the
\rho_{\text{B}}
–
\rho_{\text{I}}
plane, this constraint on
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{+}
always picks out the same coexistence point and tie line.
Consequently, we can solve for coexistence under the constraint
\rho^{\text{v}}=\text{const.}
simply by evaluating Eq. (
21
) with
\rho_{\text{B/I}}^{\pm}
set equal to their values when
\Delta\mu=0
and
\Delta f=\Delta f_{\text{eq}}
.
As shown in Fig. 4A in the main text, these predictions only agree with the simulation results in the limit
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
, since the system approaches a global effective equilibrium in this regime.

Finally, we note that this theory predicts that the microscopic and mesoscopic properties of the phase-separated interface remain unperturbed relative to equilibrium by construction.
Consequently, this theory predicts that the conditional distribution
p_{\text{B/I}}(n_{\text{B}})
always follows Boltzmann statistics, which is implicitly assumed in the definitions of
\mu_{\text{B}}
,
\mu_{\text{I}}
, and
P
in Eq. (
38
), and that the capillary fluctuations are unchanged relative to equilibrium.
This fundamental feature of the theory is qualitatively at odds with the simulation results shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. 4D in the main text.
Moreover, this theory does not predict a nonequilibrium effective interfacial tension due to the chemical drive.
Therefore, this theory is also unable to predict the changes in the
R
-dependent far-field concentrations that are presented in Fig. 5 in the main text.
