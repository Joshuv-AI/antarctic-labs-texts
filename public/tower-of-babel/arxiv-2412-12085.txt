Qubit-channel metrology with highly noisy initial states and additional undesirable noisy evolution
David Collins, Taylor Larrechea

Abstract
We consider protocols for estimating the parameter in a single-parameter unital qubit channel, assuming that the available initial states are highly mixed with very low purity. We compare two protocols, each invoking the channel once, via their quantum Fisher informations. One uses n n qubits prepared in a particular correlated input state and subsequently queries the channel on one qubit. The other uses a single qubit. We extend the results of Collins [ 1 ] by allowing for additional noisy evolution on the spectator qubits in the n n -qubit protocol. We provide simple algebraic expressions that will determine when the n n -qubit protocol is superior. We provide a technique that can alleviate certain types of noise. We show that for certain types of noisy evolution the n n -qubit protocol will be inferior but for others it will be superior.

Quantum metrology assesses the accuracy of protocols in terms of the statistical fluctuations in parameter estimates and compares competing protocols, aiming for the most accurate.

A well-established measure of accuracy is the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, which bounds the variance in the estimate
[
10
]
. Let
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots
, be the measurement outcomes of the protocol and let
p(m_{1},m_{2}\ldots|\lambda)
be the associated probability. An estimate for the parameter is generated via
\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots)
, where
\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}(\ldots)
is an estimator function. The requirements are that the estimator is unbiased, i.e.
\langle\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}\rangle=\lambda
where
\langle\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}\rangle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots}\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots)p(m_{1},m_{2}\ldots|\lambda)
and that the variance
\variance{(\lambda_{\mathrm{est}})}:=\left<\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}-\langle\lambda_{\mathrm{est}}\rangle\right)^{2}\right>
is minimized. The variance depends on the choice of estimator but, regardless of this choice, is bounded by classical Cramér-Rao bound (CRB)
[
42
,
10
]
,
\variance{(\lambda_{\mathrm{est}})}\geqslant 1/F(\lambda)
where the Fisher information is

F(\lambda)=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots}\left[\frac{\partial\ln{p(m_{1},m_{2}\ldots|\lambda)}}{\partial\lambda}\right]^{2}p(m_{1},m_{2}\ldots|\lambda).

The maximum likelihood estimator, determined by solving
\frac{\partial p}{\partial\lambda}=0
for
\lambda
, saturates the bound
[
42
]
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

For measurements performed on quantum systems, the system state immediately prior to measurement,

and the measurement choice dictate the measurement outcome statistics. Regardless of the measurement choice, the Fisher information satisfies the quantum Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB)

(1)

\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\;\left[\hat{L}(\lambda)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}(\lambda)+\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}(\lambda)\hat{L}(\lambda)\right].

(2)

II.1
Noisy initial states

We assume that the qubit states at the outset of the protocol (initial states) are noisy with no possibility of noise reduction. A prototypical example is room-temperature, solution-state NMR, which exposes an ensemble of identical molecules to physical interactions which depend on potentially interesting parameters. Nuclear spins in a single molecule provide the qubits used in the protocol; the entire ensemble duplicates the qubits without interaction between the molecules. At room temperature, the state of any spin is highly mixed and there is no practical way to purify it
[
32
,
57
,
58
]
. Any metrology protocol on such systems needs to be restricted to noisy initial states and it could be that the relative strengths and weaknesses of competing protocols may differ from the case where pure states are available.

III
Qubit channel formalism

We briefly digress to discuss a description, essential throughout this work, of qubit channels in terms of mappings of Bloch-sphere vectors.

The state of any qubit is described by a density operator

\hat{\rho}=(\hat{I}+\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}})/2

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}
is a vector in three dimensions (the Bloch-sphere vector) with
0\leqslant\left|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}\right|\leqslant 1.

III.1
Qubit channel evolution

The evolution of a quantum system is described by a completely-positive, trace-preserving map
[
65
]
and the associated channel,
\hat{\Gamma},

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\hat{I}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{$i$}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\xmapsto{\hat{\Gamma}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\hat{I}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}\right)

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{$i$}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{f}}
are vectors related by
[
57
,
66
]

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{f}}=M\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{$i$}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}.

(4)

M
is a linear operator represented by a
3\times 3
real matrix, called the
channel Bloch-sphere matrix
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}
is a vector. We shall consider unital channels, for which
\hat{I}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hat{\Gamma}}}{{\mapsto}}\hat{I}
; here
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}=0.
Thus the channel Bloch-sphere matrix completely characterizes the associated unital channel.

In the three-dimensional Cartesian orthonormal basis
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\right\}
the channel Bloch-sphere matrix has form

M=\begin{pmatrix}M_{xx}&M_{xy}&M_{xz}\\
M_{yx}&M_{yy}&M_{yz}\\
M_{zx}&M_{zy}&M_{zz}\end{pmatrix}

(5)

with components, for unital cases, determined by

\displaystyle M_{xx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Trace{\left[\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{\sigma}_{x})\right]},

(6a)

\displaystyle M_{xy}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Trace{\left[\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{\sigma}_{y})\right]},\textrm{etc,\ldots}

(6b)

For example, the unitary phase-shift channel
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hat{\Gamma}}}{{\mapsto}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{U}
where
\hat{U}:=e^{-i\lambda\hat{\sigma}_{z}/2}
is described by

M=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\lambda}&-\sin{\lambda}&0\\
\sin{\lambda}&\cos{\lambda}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Further examples are provided in section
X
.

In the metrology protocols that follow, the channel Bloch-sphere matrix for
\hat{\Gamma}(\lambda)
will be denoted
M_{1}
and those for the spectator channels
\hat{\Gamma}_{j}
by
M_{j}
, the latter each assumed to be independent of
\lambda
.

III.2
Channel Bloch-sphere matrix products

We will see that metrology protocols can be assessed in terms of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
and
M_{2}^{\top}M_{2},\ldots,M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}
where the dot indicates differentiation with respect to
\lambda.
We briefly describe their important features. These are positive semi-definite since
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}^{\top}M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}
, for any vector
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}
, is the inner product of
M_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}
with itself and this is necessarily positive semi-definite. The singular value decomposition for
\dot{M}_{1}
and each of
M_{j}
implies that
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
and
M_{2}^{\top}M_{2},\ldots,M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}
can each be diagonalized; the eigenvalues will be positive semi-definite. Thus

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}+\beta\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\delta\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\quad\textrm{and}

(8a)

\displaystyle M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}^{\top}+\tau_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}^{\top}+\upsilon_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}^{\top}

(8b)

where products such as
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}^{\top}
signify operators on vectors, defined via
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}\right):=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}\right)
for any vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}},
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}.
The eigenvalues are arranged to satisfy

\alpha\geqslant\beta\geqslant\delta\geqslant 0

and
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\right\}
form an orthonormal set of vectors, ordered in terms of magnitude of associated eigenvalues. In general, all of these eigenvectors and eigenvalues associated with the channel
\hat{\Gamma}(\lambda)
could depend on
\lambda.
Similarly,

1\geqslant\sigma_{j}\geqslant\tau_{j}\geqslant\upsilon_{j}\geqslant 0

and
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}\right\}
form an orthonormal set of vectors, also ordered, but these eigenvectors and eigenvalues are all independent of
\lambda
.

Note that this description differs from that of
[
1
]
, which used singular values of
\dot{M}_{1},M_{2},\ldots M_{n}.
The two descriptions can be reconciled by noting that the eigenvalues of the product matrices are the squares of the singular values of the original matrices.

\displaystyle=

V
Single-qubit, single-channel protocols

We establish a baseline for estimation via the SQSC protocol. The channel input state can be assumed to be the same as the initial state. Thus the estimation protocol is determined by chosing
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}
and the final post-channel measurement.

Then
\hat{\rho}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{i}}=\hat{I}
and
\hat{\rho}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{i}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}},
giving

\hat{\rho}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{f}}=\left(M_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}

(14)

Equation (
13
) gives that the lowest-order, non-zero term in the QFI is

H=r^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}.

(15)

The diagonal form for
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
given by Eq. (
8a
) gives
[
1
]

H\leqslant r^{2}\alpha

(16)

with the upper bound attained for the choice
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}.

Thus the
optimal SQSC protocol
uses
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
and gives lowest optimal order SQSC protocol QFI

H_{\textrm{s opt}}=r^{2}\alpha.

(17)

Other protocols will be compared to this via their QFIs.

V.1
Global versus local protocols

The optimal SQSC protocol uses
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
, the eigenvector associated with the maximum eigenvalue of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
. If
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
is independent of
\lambda
then the protocol, up to the measurement stage, is constructed globally (independently of the parameter). Examples of such global estimation protocols are unitary, flip and depolarizing channel parameter estimation (see section
X
).

If
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
depends on
\lambda
, the optimal protocol requires knowledge of the parameter it aims to determine. Instead a local optimal protocol is possible. This assumes that
\lambda
is in the vicinity of a known value
\lambda_{0}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
be the known eigenvector associated with the maximum eigenvalue of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
when
\lambda=\lambda_{0}.
Then (see Appendix
A
),

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}+{\cal O}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}.

(18)

Thus, to
{\cal O}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}
, the optimal QFI is returned using a protocol with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
, corresponding to known parameter value
\lambda_{0}
. This is a parameter-independent local estimation protocol in the vicinity of
\lambda_{0}
.

VI.1
General QFI bounds

Finding
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}
that yield the optimal QFI in various situations is not always clear. However, we establish general bounds for the QFI with noisy spectators (see Appendix
D
). If the eigenvalues of
M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}
are
1\geqslant\sigma_{j}\geqslant\tau_{j}\geqslant\upsilon_{j}\geqslant 0
then,
to lowest order in the purity,

H_{\textrm{corr}}\leqslant\begin{cases}nH_{\textrm{s opt}}\,\Sigma&\text{if $n\geqslant 1/\Sigma$,}\\
H_{\textrm{s opt}}\,&\text{if $n\leqslant 1/\Sigma$}\\
\end{cases}

(24)

where
\Sigma=\sigma_{2}\ldots\sigma_{n}
describes the least destructive effects of spectator noise. Thus for sufficiently destructive spectator noise and low enough numbers of spectators, i.e.
n\Sigma\leqslant 1
, the SQSC protocol provides a better QFI than the CS protocol. This provides an absolute threshold of spectator noise, beyond which the CS protocol is disadvantageous regardless of the choices of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}
.

Similarly (see Appendix
D
)
to lowest order in the purity,

H_{\textrm{corr}}\geqslant\begin{cases}n\dfrac{\delta}{\alpha}\,H_{\textrm{s opt}}\,\Upsilon&\text{if $n\geqslant 1/\Upsilon$,}\\
\dfrac{\delta}{\alpha}\,H_{\textrm{s opt}}&\text{if $n\leqslant 1/\Upsilon$}\\
\end{cases}

(25)

where
\Upsilon=\upsilon_{2}\ldots\upsilon_{n}
describes the most destructive effects of spectator noise. However, we shall show that the lower bound can be increased by manipulation of spectator channels and particular choices of Bloch-sphere initial- and control directions. We illustrate this for two extreme cases.

VI.2
Clean-spectator case

The
clean-spectator case
is that where there is no further evolution of the spectator qubits after the preparatory unitary and before measurement. This is the case considered previously
[
1
]
. Then
M_{j}=I
for
j=2,\ldots,n
. Here
\sigma_{1}=\ldots=\sigma_{n}=1
and
\Sigma=1
. In Eq. (
24
)
n\geqslant 1/\Sigma
and the lowest order QFI satisfies
H_{\textrm{corr}}\leqslant nH_{\textrm{s opt}}.

For the lower bound,
\upsilon_{1}=\ldots=\upsilon_{n}=1
. Thus
\Upsilon=1
and
n\Upsilon\geqslant 1
. This would appear to yield a lower bound
H_{\textrm{corr}}\geqslant n\delta/\alpha\,H_{\textrm{s opt}}.
However, the special choices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}},
where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
are the “maximal” (associated with the two largest eigenvalues) eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
, and Eq. (
22
) give

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{corr}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\left[\beta+(n-1)\alpha\right]

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+n-1\right)

and straightforward algebra shows that this is never smaller than
nH_{\textrm{s opt}}\delta/\alpha
provided that
n\geqslant 1
and
\beta\geqslant\delta.
Since these are assumed, this establishes bounds on the clean spectator CS QFI,
H_{\textrm{clean}}
, as

\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+n-1\right)H_{\textrm{s opt}}\leqslant H_{\textrm{clean}}\leqslant nH_{\textrm{s opt}},

(27)

which is tighter than Eq. (
25
).
This is equivalent to the expression of
[
1
]
. Note that the lower bound is attained by the protocol with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
. The upper bound would require a protocol with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}
perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}
but there is no guarantee that it can be attained. If
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
are independent of
\lambda
then the protocol will be global. If either depends on
\lambda
then, as in the SQSC case, this could give a local protocol about a known parameter value
\lambda_{0}
and this would be correct to
{\cal O}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}.

VI.3
Depolarizing-spectator case

Suppose that each spectator is subjected to a depolarizing channel after the preparatory unitary and before measurement. This maps
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\xmapsto{\hat{\Gamma}_{j}}\epsilon_{j}\Trace{[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}]}\hat{I}/2+(1-\epsilon_{j})\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}
where
0\leqslant\epsilon_{j}\leqslant 1
is the depolarizing channel parameter
[
65
]
(a lower parameter implies weaker noise). Then
M_{j}=(1-\epsilon_{j})\hat{I}
and
M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}=\sigma_{j}I
where
\sigma_{j}=(1-\epsilon_{j})^{2}.
Equation (
22
) gives

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{corr}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\left(\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\otimes I\otimes\cdots\otimes I\right)\boldsymbol{\mathrm{V}}\,\prod_{j=2}^{n}(1-\epsilon_{j})^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle+r^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}

When
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}
are perpendicular, this reduces the clean spectator correlated-state QFI by a multiplicative factor. This reduction extends throughout any subsequent analysis. For example, the clean spectator QFI bounds Eq. (
27
) are reduced by a factor of
\prod_{j=2}^{n}(1-\epsilon_{j})^{2}
.

VII
Spectator noise alleviation

One can alleviate the effects of the spectator noise on the estimation accuracy and improve the QFI beyond the lower bound suggested by Eq. (
25
). Inserting appropriately chosen unitaries before and after each spectator channel evolution, as illustrated in Fig.
4
, can modify these so as to reduce their destructive effects.

The strategy that raised the lower bound for the clean-spectator case, resulting in Eq. (
27
) is suggestive. That used the two (“maximal”) eigenvectors associated with the largest eigenvalues of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
. In Eq.(
22
) the contributions to the QFI from the spectator channels will be determined by the same two maximal eigenvectors and each of
M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}
. It would be advantageous for these eigenvectors to extract the largest eigenvalues of each of
M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}
. The strategy is then to manipulate the spectator channels so as to align their maximal eigenvectors with those of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
.

Figure 4:
Symmetric pairwise correlated protocol with twisted spectator channels. Each spectator noisy channel is preceded by a channel-dependent unitary
\hat{U}_{j}
and is followed by
\hat{U}_{j}^{\dagger}.

For example, suppose that
\hat{\Gamma}_{j}
is a phase-flip channel, i.e.
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\xmapsto{\hat{\Gamma}_{j}}(1-\lambda_{j})\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}+\lambda_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{z}
where
0\leqslant\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
. If
\hat{U}_{j}=e^{i\hat{\sigma}_{y}\pi/4}
, then, under the combination
\hat{U}_{j}\rightarrow\hat{\Gamma}_{j}\rightarrow\hat{U}_{j}^{\dagger}
,

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\mapsto(1-\lambda_{j})\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}+\lambda_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{x}.

(29)

Thus the particular unitary has transformed the phase-flip channel into a bit-flip channel with the same parameter
\lambda_{j}
. We call this process
twisting the channel.

This has important effects on the associated Bloch-sphere matrices. In the example the Bloch-sphere matrix for the phase-flip is

M_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}1-2\lambda_{j}&0&0\\
0&1-2\lambda_{j}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

(30)

while that for the bit flip is

M_{j}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1-2\lambda_{j}&0\\
0&0&1-2\lambda_{j}\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

These are related by the Bloch-sphere matrix for
\hat{U}_{j}
,

R_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(32)

and
M_{j}^{\prime}=R_{j}^{\top}M_{j}R_{j}.
This particular example of twisting would be advantageous if the maximal eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
were
\boldsymbol{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{\mathrm{y}}}
. Without twisting these eigenvectors would always extract factors of
1-2\lambda_{j}
in Eq. (
22
) but with the indicated twisting they would sometime extract factors of
1-2\lambda_{j}
and other times factors of
1
. The twisted version would give a larger QFI.

Denote the combination
\hat{U}_{j}\rightarrow\hat{\Gamma}_{j}\rightarrow\hat{U}_{j}^{\dagger}
by
\hat{\Gamma}_{j}^{\prime}.
In general,
\hat{U}_{j}
will be represented by an orthogonal rotation Bloch-sphere matrix
R_{j}
and the Bloch-sphere matrices for
\hat{\Gamma}_{j}
and
\hat{\Gamma}_{j}^{\prime}
will be related by
M_{j}^{\prime}=R_{j}^{\top}M_{j}R_{j}.
We can always choose
\hat{U}_{j}
so as to rotate the eigenvectors of
M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}
to coincide with the eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
in the same decreasing order. The relevant single qubit unitary corresponds to the Bloch-sphere matrix
R_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}.
Then
M_{j}^{\prime\top}M_{j}^{\prime}=R_{j}^{\top}M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}R_{j},
gives

M_{j}^{\prime\top}M_{j}^{\prime}=\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}+\tau_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\upsilon_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}.

(33)

The expression for the QFI of Eq. (
22
) could then be modified by replacing the Bloch-sphere matrices by their primed versions,
provided that each rotation
R_{j}
is independent of
\lambda.
The rotations are constructed from the eigenvectors of
M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}
, which are, by assumption, independent of
\lambda
, and also the eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
. In some cases (e.g. phase shift, Pauli channel, depolarizing channel) the latter are independent of
\lambda
but there are possibilities where they are not. If the eigenvectors do depend on
\lambda
, the expression of Eq. (
22
) would need to include derivatives of the spectator channel Bloch-sphere matrices; this will yield a local estimation protocol.

VII.1
Parameter-independent twisting

Suppose that the eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
are independent of
\lambda.
Then the spectator channels could be twisted by parameter-independent unitaries and thus take the form (dropping the prime superscripts for convenience)

M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}=\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}+\tau_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\upsilon_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}.

(34)

Choosing
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
, Eq. (
22
) gives

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{corr}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{\tau_{2}}{\sigma_{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\tau_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)\,\Sigma

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{s opt}}\,\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{\tau_{2}}{\sigma_{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\tau_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)\,\Sigma.

Straightforward algebra shows this exceeds
nH_{\textrm{s opt}}\Upsilon
of Eq. (
25
). Thus with twisted spectator channels,

H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\Sigma\leqslant H_{\textrm{corr}}\leqslant nH_{\textrm{s opt}}\Sigma.

(36)

Thus when twisting can be done independently of
\lambda,
a potentially fruitful CS protocol uses twisting and the Bloch-sphere direction choices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
.

VII.2
Parameter-dependent twisting

Suppose that only
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
depends on
\lambda.
Then let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
be the eigenvector associated with the maximum eigenvalue of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
when the parameter assumes a known value,
\lambda_{0}.
Then, using the language of Appendix
A
the twisting rotation is

R_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}_{0}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}^{\top}_{0}\Delta\lambda\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}+{\cal O}(\Delta\lambda)^{2}

(37)

where
\Delta\lambda=\lambda-\lambda_{0}.
This gives

\displaystyle M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}_{0}+\tau_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}_{0}+\upsilon_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}_{0}

(38)

\displaystyle+\sigma_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}^{\top}_{0}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}_{0}\right)\Delta\lambda+{\cal O}(\Delta\lambda)^{2}.

As in Appendix
A
, the orthogonality of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
and each of
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}_{0}
gives that using a protocol where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
yields Eq. (
36
) correct to
{\cal O}(\Delta\lambda)^{2}
. The same argument extends to cases where all the eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
are parameter-dependent.

To summarize, if the eigenvectors of
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
are parameter-dependent, then the twisting could be invoked using eigenvectors at some known parameter value. The resulting protocol will then return the same bounds as for the parameter-independent case but will only be accurate to second order in the deviation from this known parameter value. This will be a local estimation protocol.

VIII
Saturating measurements for symmetric pairwise correlated protocol

We ask whether there is a measurement such that the resulting classical Fisher information,
F
, attains the lower bound QFI of Eq. (
36
). It does not appear that there is one measurement scheme that works in all circumstances. Consider the symmetric pairwise correlated protocol with spectators that are twisted and where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}
is perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}
. We describe two possibilities, one which saturates the bound in certain situations and the other which does not but is close for large
n
. Both possibilities only apply when the the
channel Bloch-sphere matrix for each spectator can be diagonalized
. Some, but not all, examples of this are spectator channels that are unitary, flip-type or depolarizing.

VIII.1
Generic measurement on all qubits

A generic scheme invokes the inverse of the preparatory unitary after all channel actions and follows this by single qubit projective measurement along the initial Bloch-sphere direction each qubit, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. A projective measurement with operators
\hat{\Pi}_{\pm}=(\hat{I}\pm\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}})/2
is ultimately carried out on each qubit.

Figure 5:
Generic measurement scheme. The projection operators associated with the measurement are
\hat{\Pi}_{\pm}=\left(\hat{I}\pm\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}\right)/2.

The Fisher information is computed from the probabilities of the measurement outcomes. Assuming the choices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
, we show (see Appendix
E
) that
to lowest order in the purity
,

F=r^{2}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}\right)^{2}+\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right]\Sigma.

(39)

This will not always be able to produce the lower bound of Eq. (
36
). For example, it cannot do this if the parameter-dependent channel is a unitary phase shift (see section
X.1
).

However, suppose that
\dot{M}_{1}
admits a real diagonal decomposition, i.e.
\dot{M}_{1}=m_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}^{\top}+m_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}^{\top}+m_{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}^{\top}
, where
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}\right\}
form an real orthonormal basis and
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
are real. Then the diagonal basis for this is the same as that for
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
and the eigenvalues of the latter are the squares of the former. Then the choices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
give

F=r^{2}\left(\beta+\alpha\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\Sigma,

(40)

and this is exactly the lower bound of Eq. (
36
).

Thus,
if
\dot{M}_{1}
admits a real diagonal decomposition,
then choosing
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
gives a Fisher information of

F=H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\Sigma.

(41)

Note that for clean spectators,
\tau_{j}=\sigma_{j}
for each channel and Eq. (
41
) gives

F=H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+n-1\right),

(42)

which is the result of
[
1
]
, with
\alpha=s_{1}^{2}
and
\beta=s_{2}^{2},
giving an almost
n
-fold enhancement of the Fisher information.

VIII.2
Tailored measurement scheme

If
\dot{M}_{1}
does not admit a real diagonal decomposition, consider the modified measurement scheme of Fig.

Here the pre-measurement unitary is restricted to the spectator qubits and we allow a general measurement direction on the parameter-dependent qubit. This is described by a unit vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{\mathrm{m}}}
, the Bloch-sphere measurement direction, and the associated projection operators,

Then
to lowest order in the purity
(see Appendix
F

The protocol choices require the singular value decomposition,
\dot{M}_{1}=ASB
where

and

are orthogonal matrices and
S
is diagonal with non-negative entries (the singular values of
\dot{M}_{1}
). Let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
eigenvectors of

and the eigenvectors of

,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}=B^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}=B^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
. Then choosing
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=B^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=B^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}=A\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}

\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{2}=\alpha

tailored to the channel
.

Thus there are choices of Bloch-sphere control- and measurement directions so that the tailored measurement scheme yields,
to lowest order in the purity

F=r^{2}H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\,\Sigma.

(44)

Note that for clean spectators,
\tau_{j}=\sigma_{j}
for each channel and Eq. (

F=r^{2}H_{\textrm{s opt}}(n-1).

(45)

This corrects the result of
[
1
]
, which overlooked cases where
\dot{M}_{1}
might not admit a real diagonalization. This fails to saturate the lower bound for the QFI, but there is negligible difference if
n
is large enough.

Again if any of
A,B
or
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
depend on
\lambda,
then local protocols using variants of these for a known parameter value
\lambda_{0}

(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}.

IX
Summary of the Estimation Protocol

The correlated state estimation protocol offered here can be summarized as follows:

1.

Describe the parameter-dependent channel by the channel Bloch-sphere matrix
M_{1}
. Describe the spectator channels by channel Bloch-sphere matrices
M_{j}
for
j=2,\ldots n.

2.

Determine the diagonal decompositions

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}+\beta\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\delta\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\quad\textrm{and}

(46)

\displaystyle M_{j}^{\top}M_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}^{\top}+\tau_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}^{\top}+\upsilon_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}^{\top}

(47)

where
\alpha\geqslant\beta\geqslant\delta\geqslant 0
and
1\geqslant\sigma_{j}\geqslant\tau_{j}\geqslant\upsilon_{j}\geqslant 0.

3.

Let
\Sigma:=\sigma_{2}\ldots\sigma_{n}
. If
n\Sigma\leqslant 1
then the SQSC protocol is optimal regardless of any other considerations. If
n\Sigma>1
then the CS protocol might yield an advantage.

4.

Implement the symmetric pairwise correlated protocol of Fig.
4
, where the
\hat{U}_{j}
is the unitary corresponding to Bloch-sphere rotation
R_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}.
Choose
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{\textrm{0}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}.
The CS protocol QFI is bounded from below via

H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\Sigma\leqslant H_{\textrm{corr}}.

(48)

5.

Determine whether
\dot{M}_{1}
admits a real diagonal decomposition. If it does, use the generic measurement scheme of Fig.
5
yielding a Fisher information that saturates the lower bound, i.e.

F=H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\Sigma.

(49)

If
\dot{M}_{1}
does not admit a real diagonal decomposition, determine the singular value decomposition
\dot{M}_{1}=ASB
, where
A
and
B
are orthogonal matrices and
S
is a singular value matrix. Then use the tailored measurement scheme of Fig.
6
with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}=A\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}
where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}
is the vector associated with the maximum singular value in
S.
This will yield a Fisher information less than the lower bound, i.e.

F=H_{\textrm{s opt}}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\tau_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)\,\Sigma.

(50)

If
\dot{M}_{1}^{\top}\dot{M}_{1}
has parameter dependent eigenvectors,
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}\right\},
then the choices of Bloch-sphere initial-state and control vectors and the twisting unitaries will be parameter independent. A general-parameter dependent protocol is impossible. However if
\lambda
is known to be close to
\lambda_{0}
, then a local scheme with protocol choices arising from
\lambda_{0}
will allow for an estimation protocol correct to order
(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}.

Note that the approximations used in the series approach are only useful
[
1
]
provided that
nr^{2}\ll 1
and this limits the number of useful spectators in the correlated-state protocol to
n\ll 1/r^{2}.

XI
Conclusion

We have compared two protocols for metrology of single-parameter, unital, qubit-channel evolutions. These are applicable when the available initial states have very low purity and the channel is queried once. They extend previous results
[
1
]
by including additional noise on spectator qubits after input state preparation.

We have provided a procedure for deciding when the additional noise is so severe that the specific correlated-state protocol under consideration reduces the QFI to below that of a single-qubit protocol. We have also identified spectator noise levels that are low enough that the correlated-state protocol can enhance the estimation accuracy; these depend on the nature of the noise in the spectator channels. If, for example, they undergo a flip-type channel, then the correlated-state protocol can always be chosen to enhance the estimation accuracy. if they are depolarizing channels, then possible enhancement depends on the severity of the depolarization. If this can be characterized via Bloch-sphere operations, then we have provided simple algebraic expressions for deciding when enhancement may or may not occur.

We have shown that the effects of spectator noise on estimation accuracy can be alleviated by twisting the spectator channels, in a manner sympathetic to the parameter-bearing channel. The twisting process adds two unitary operations to each spectator channel; these are decided by a combination of the parameter-bearing channel and the spectator channel. Twisting can turn a potentially disadvantageous spectator noise situation (for example, flip channels) into one where the correlated-state protocol will be advantageous regardless of the severity of the noise in the spectator channels.

It remains to determine the effects of non-unital spectator noise, such as amplitude damping, on the ability of the correlated-state protocol to enhance estimation accuracy. Mathematically, this has a slightly different description to unital noise and would require some modifications to the analysis offered here although the general framework would still apply.

Our analysis considers one particular correlated-state protocol and this could be generalized by generalizing
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{prep}}.
Whether there are fruitful alternatives or what the optimal preparatory unitary might be are open questions.
