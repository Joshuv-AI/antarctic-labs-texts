Effective resource allocation to combat invasions of the spotted lanternfly ( Lycorma delicatula ) and similar pests
Daniel Strömbom, Julianna Hoitt, Jinrong Hu, Swati Pandey, Elizabeth Batchelar

Abstract
The spotted lanternfly is rapidly establishing itself as a major insect pest with global implications. Despite significant management efforts, its spread continues in invaded regions, and refined management strategies are required. A recent study introduced a model that generalized the results of empirical control efficacy studies by incorporating population dynamics and incomplete delivery. In particular, a generalized population growth formula was derived, providing the minimum proportion of a population that must be treated with a given control to induce population decline. However, this model could not address the more relevant question of how best to deploy a given control to minimize population growth. Here, we extend this model and formula to address this question in a variety of settings. When the effect of control is proportional to effort, we show that exhaustive sequential deployment of stage‑specific controls, ordered by efficacy, is the optimal strategy. When control effects exhibit diminishing returns with effort, we derive a formula for when to switch controls, providing an effective strategy in situations where management resources may vary or be cut at any time. We also use numerical global optimization methods to obtain strategies when resources are fixed a priori. Both strategies outperform random deployment, which we show can result in disastrous outcomes. Our results demonstrate that adopting an effective strategy for deploying stage‑specific controls is essential for managing the spotted lanternfly. However, we found no papers or reports addressing this topic in the lanternfly literature, nor information on whether such strategies are used in practice. Given the limited resources available to combat the lanternfly, it is critical that they are used effectively. The approach introduced here, along with other optimization methods commonly employed in related fields, can contribute to this goal.

Should the available resources be allocated equally across all life stages, should we concentrate the efforts on one life stage, or use some other resource allocation scheme? To address questions of this type, a more sophisticated approach is required. In particular, one that distinguishes the proportion treated in the different life-stages, rather than having a common proportion treated for all, given that the available controls have different stage-specific efficacies. In already infested regions, such models could serve as tools for the effective allocation of resources to manage populations. For future invasions, if detected early enough, this information could potentially contribute to eradication.

Result 2

3

Discussion

The overall conclusion of this work is that adopting a suitable strategy for the deployment of stage-specific controls is essential for effective management. In particular, random deployment of controls may be inefficient and can lead to disastrous outcomes. Care should therefore be taken when deciding whether to opt for a ”greedy” strategy, such as formula (
8
), or a strategy based on numerical global optimization results. For a fixed guaranteed amount of resources, the numerical optimization strategy is always preferable. However, if resources are not fixed and may be subject to change, a strategy based on formula (
8
) may be preferable. We find that considerations of this type are largely absent from the quantitative pest control literature that focuses on using optimization approaches (e.g. those in
[
30
,
31
]
), despite the fact that resources often are subject to change
[
32
]
. This suggests that many methods that are marketed as optimal may produce suboptimal results in practice, unless the problem has an optimal substructure. This distinction is also useful to communicate to funders of, and agencies responsible for executing, management efforts, namely, that the same amount of resources will yield better results if provided through a stable, long-term program than if they are provided on a more unreliable, short-term basis and subject to potential change. Delays and lags in management efforts resulting from budgetary or urgency-of-action considerations are known to adversely impact the prevention and eradication of invasive species
[
32
]
. For example, the management strategy of Berks County, PA, was set back by a loss of state funding in 2021, despite a continued threat from the spotted lanternfly to the agricultural industry and businesses in the county
[
33
]
. In 2017, in Pennsylvania alone, the spotted lanternfly was projected to cause
\displaystyle 42.6
million in annual damages. Maintaining management practices to slow the spread could help minimize the risk of economic damage to both local and federal governments
[
34
]
.

The re-written version (
2
) of the original generalized growth formula (
1
) from
[
22
]
makes it easier to recognize that an underlying assumption of the original model is that the effect of an applied control is proportional to the effort expended. While this might be a reasonable assumption in some situations, such as when managing lanternfly populations in a small, limited geographical area, it is likely that diminishing returns will be substantial in many real-world scenarios
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Identifying this assumption and developing approaches that incorporate diminishing returns is therefore critical, not only for the application of the methods introduced here, but also for extending the work in
[
22
]
and other influential models of invasive species control across taxa, e.g.
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
30
,
31
]
.

Here, we have only considered the switching time formula (
8
) in the range that is appropriate for our specific application, i.e. with
\displaystyle k_{i}\in(0,1)
and
\displaystyle r_{i}=1-k_{i}\in(0,1)
. However, it is worth noting that formula (
8
) has a region of validity for
\displaystyle r_{i}>1
as well, which may be useful for other applications involving diminishing returns, for example, if the
\displaystyle r_{i}
represent rates that can exceed 1, which is common. In such cases, formula (
8
) can be used to determine switching times in the same way we have used it here, as long as
\displaystyle r_{i}>r_{i+1}>\sqrt{r_{i}}
for all
\displaystyle i
.

For practical purposes, the function
\displaystyle p_{i}=p_{i}(\epsilon_{i})
will, in general, be unknown, and biological data and arguments should be used to estimate it based on the specific biological situation. It may also be the case that, in practice, each stage has a different such function. To handle situations like these, numerical methods will often be required. We used the Mathematica function
Minimize

[
38
]
to obtain the global optimization results here and in the Methods and Calculations section we explain how the command we used can be modified to perform the corresponding optimization in a number of different situations, including different stage-specific
\displaystyle p_{i}=p_{i}(\epsilon_{i})
functions.

Given that the resources available to combat the lanternfly are limited, we should strive to ensure that they are used effectively. The work introduced here, as well as other optimization approaches that are used regularly in other areas
[
28
,
29
,
27
]
, can contribute to this goal. The fact that we were unable to find any information on the allocation strategies used for lanternfly management, nor any studies focusing on evaluating such strategies, is concerning. In part, this raises the possibility that some kind of heuristic such as ”we use what we have for whatever action seems most urgent or convenient at the time” may, in fact, be used in practice. This would amount to an instance of random deployment, as we have defined it here, and as we have shown, is likely to be suboptimal with respect to the effective use of resources and may lead to disastrous management outcomes.

Here we present the main calculations, derivations, and methods used to obtain the results.

Result 1

The generalized population growth formula presented as Equation 4 in
[
22
]
is

\lambda^{x}(p)=[ps_{PO}^{x}+(1-p)s_{PO}][ps_{NP}^{x}+(1-p)s_{NP}][ps_{AN}^{x}+(1-p)s_{AN}][pF^{x}+(1-p)F].

(9)

First, we replace the common
\displaystyle p
in this equation with individual proportions for each stage,
\displaystyle p_{PO}
,
\displaystyle p_{NP}
,
\displaystyle p_{AN}
, and
\displaystyle p_{F}

\lambda^{x}(\bar{p})=[p_{PO}s_{PO}^{x}+(1-p_{PO})s_{PO}][p_{NP}s_{NP}^{x}+(1-p_{NP})s_{NP}][p_{AN}s_{AN}^{x}+(1-p_{AN})s_{AN}][p_{F}F^{x}+(1-p_{F})F].

(10)

Next, we express the parameter values under control as proportions of the natural parameter values, i.e.
\displaystyle s_{PO}^{x}=k_{PO}s_{PO}
,
\displaystyle s_{NP}^{x}=k_{NP}s_{NP}
,
\displaystyle s_{AN}^{x}=k_{AN}s_{AN}
, and
\displaystyle F^{x}=k_{F}F
, which gives

\lambda^{x}(\bar{p})=\lambda_{0}(1-p_{PO}(1-k_{PO}))(1-p_{NP}(1-k_{NP}))(1-p_{AN}(1-k_{AN}))(1-p_{F}(1-k_{F})),

(11)

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=s_{PO}s_{NP}s_{AN}F
is the natural annual growth rate (Equation 2 in

]

Finally, if we replace the subscript notation with indices
\displaystyle i=1,2,3,4
, the expression becomes

\lambda^{x}(\bar{p})=\lambda_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{4}(1-p_{i}(1-k_{i})),

(12)

where
\displaystyle k_{i}=s^{x}_{i}/s_{i}
. We note that the variables satisfy
\displaystyle p_{i}\in[0,1]
, since the
\displaystyle p_{i}
represent proportions treated, and
\displaystyle k_{i}\in[0,1)

Result 2

Result 2 states that if
\displaystyle k_{i}<k_{j}
and
\displaystyle p_{i}>p_{j}\frac{1-k_{j}}{1-k_{i}}
, then

\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{i}}<\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{j}}.

To show this, define
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=1-p_{i}(1-k_{i})
so that (
3
) can be written as

\lambda^{x}(\bar{p})=\lambda_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_{i},

and note that the derivative of
\displaystyle\lambda^{x}(\bar{p})
with respect to
\displaystyle p_{i}
is given by

\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{i}}=-\lambda_{0}(1-k_{i})\prod_{k\neq i}\alpha_{k}.

Then we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{i}}}{\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{j}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{0}(1-k_{i})\prod_{k\neq i}\alpha_{k}}{\lambda_{0}(1-k_{j})\prod_{k\neq j}\alpha_{k}}=\frac{(1-k_{i})\alpha_{j}}{(1-k_{j})\alpha_{i}}\ =\left(\frac{1-k_{i}}{1-k_{j}}\right)\left(\frac{1-p_{j}(1-k_{j})}{1-p_{i}(1-k_{i})}\right)>1\cdot\left(\frac{1-p_{j}(1-k_{j})}{1-p_{i}(1-k_{i})}\right)>1,

where the first inequality holds because
\displaystyle k_{i}<k_{j}
and the second holds when
\displaystyle p_{i}>p_{j}\frac{1-k_{j}}{1-k_{i}}
. Therefore,

\frac{\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{i}}}{\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{j}}}>1,

which implies that

\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{i}}<\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial p_{j}},

where the switch in inequality direction results from the fact that the partial derivatives are strictly negative.
We also note that, when formulated in more general terms, this is a well-known result typically established via linear programming

]

Result 3

To see that for the diminishing returns case,
\displaystyle p^{d}(\epsilon)=1-e^{-r_{i}\epsilon}

\displaystyle p^{p}(\epsilon)=\epsilon(1-k_{i})
, at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
, we must have
\displaystyle r_{i}=1-k_{i}
, note that

\frac{dp^{d}}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=r_{i}e^{-r_{i}\cdot 0}=r_{i}

and

\frac{dp^{p}}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=1-k_{i},

so for them to match, we require
\displaystyle r_{i}=1-k_{i}

Result 4

To derive formula (
8
), define
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\epsilon_{i})=1-r_{i}(1-e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}})
so that (
7
) can be written as

\lambda^{x}(\hat{\epsilon})=\lambda_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\sigma_{i}(\epsilon_{i})

and note that the derivative of
\displaystyle\lambda^{x}(\hat{\epsilon})
with respect to
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}
is given by

\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial\epsilon_{i}}=\lambda_{0}r_{i}^{2}e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\prod_{j\neq i}\sigma_{j}(\epsilon_{j}).

Assume that the controls are ordered sequentially by effectiveness, i.e.
\displaystyle k_{i}<k_{i+1}
, or equivalently
\displaystyle r_{i}>r_{i+1}
, or equivalently
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(0)>\sigma_{i+1}(0)
. To find the
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}
at which further increasing
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}
will yield a lower reduction in population growth rate than starting to put effort
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i+1}
into the initially less effective control, we solve

\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial\epsilon_{i}}=\frac{\partial\lambda^{x}}{\partial\epsilon_{i+1}}\bigg|_{\epsilon_{i+1}=0}

r_{i}^{2}e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\prod_{j\neq i}\sigma_{j}(\epsilon_{j})=r_{i+1}^{2}\prod_{j\neq i+1}\sigma_{j}(\epsilon_{j})

r_{i}^{2}e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\sigma_{i+1}(0)=r_{i+1}^{2}\sigma_{i}

r_{i}^{2}e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\left(1-r_{i+1}(1-e^{-r_{i+1}0})\right)=r_{i+1}^{2}\left(1-r_{i}(1-e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}})\right)

r_{i}^{2}e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}=r_{i+1}^{2}\left(1-r_{i}(1-e^{-r_{i}\epsilon_{i}})\right)

\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{r_{i+1}^{2}}=e^{r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\left(1-r_{i}\right)+r_{i}

e^{r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\left(1-r_{i}\right)=\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{r_{i+1}^{2}}-r_{i}

e^{r_{i}\epsilon_{i}}=\frac{r_{i}^{2}-r_{i}r_{i+1}^{2}}{r_{i+1}^{2}(1-r_{i})}=\frac{r_{i}(r_{i}-r_{i+1}^{2})}{r_{i+1}^{2}(1-r_{i})}

\epsilon_{i}=\frac{1}{r_{i}}\ln\frac{r_{i}(r_{i}-r_{i+1}^{2})}{r_{i+1}^{2}(1-r_{i})}.

This formula is valid for all
\displaystyle r_{i},r_{i+1}\in(0,1)
, which is the relevant range for our application, as long as the controls are ordered by initial efficacy so that
\displaystyle r_{i}>r_{i+1}
. This follows from the fact that the logarithm part of the formula is well-defined for all

\displaystyle r_{i+1}<\sqrt{r_{i}}

\displaystyle r_{i}<\sqrt{r_{i}}

\displaystyle r_{i}
is larger than
\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle r_{i}>r_{i+1}
, it automatically holds that
\displaystyle r_{i+1}<\sqrt{r_{i}}
for all
\displaystyle r_{i},r_{i+1}\in(0,1)
.

\displaystyle r_{i}>1

\displaystyle r_{i+1}>\sqrt{r_{i}}

\displaystyle r_{i}>1

\displaystyle r_{i}>\sqrt{r_{i}}

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle r_{i}
