The Dilemma of Renal Dysfunction and Coronary Artery Revascularization
Authors: Wenhui Li, Łukasz Kuźma, Gregory Y.H. Lip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) is not a single-risk clinical state, and renal dysfunction is one of the clearest markers of this heterogeneity.1 Patients with impaired kidney function are typically older, more likely to have diabetes and vascular disease, and more often present with complex and frequently calcified coronary artery disease, elevated bleeding risk, and competing risks related to comorbidity and kidney disease progression.2,3 Consequently, clinicians may be reluctant to pursue invasive assessment or revascularization, even though observational data suggest that selected high-risk patients may derive survival benefit from invasive management.4,5

This pattern reflects the established risk-treatment paradox: The patients perceived to be at greatest procedural risk may also have the highest absolute ischemic risk and the most to gain from effective revascularization.6,7 Renal dysfunction is a particularly clear example because it is simultaneously a marker of procedural vulnerability and of advanced systemic vascular disease.8,9 A more useful framing is therefore to treat chronic kidney disease (CKD) less as a stand-alone reason to avoid percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and more as a marker identifying patients in whom procedural planning and net-benefit assessment should become more individualized.4,10,11

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Kim et al12 address this question using an individual patient-level pooled analysis from the KAMIR-NIH and KAMIR-V registries. The investigators studied 15,255 patients with NSTEMI, of whom 12,982 underwent PCI and 2,273 were managed without PCI. Patients were stratified by baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) into 4 clinically intuitive groups: ≥90, 60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2. The primary outcome was 3-year major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), defined as all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, or stroke.

The main findings are clinically important. Across both PCI and non-PCI groups, worsening renal function was associated with progressively higher MACCE and mortality. However, when PCI was compared with non-PCI within eGFR strata, PCI was associated with lower adjusted risks of MACCE and mortality in the eGFR 60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 groups, but not in patients with eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2. Adjusted hazard ratios for MACCE with PCI versus non-PCI were 0.58, 0.66, and 0.60 across the 3 lower eGFR strata, respectively. Recurrent myocardial infarction did not differ consistently after adjustment, suggesting that the observed association was driven largely by mortality rather than by a uniform reduction in all ischemic events.

This matters because renal dysfunction is often treated, implicitly if not explicitly, as a reason to step back from invasive care. Kim et al12 do not prove that PCI should be performed in all patients with CKD, but their findings argue against using eGFR as a stand-alone reason to defer revascularization. The study is especially useful because it separates renal risk from the broader treatment context by reporting reasons for non-PCI, including possible coronary artery bypass graft indications, myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries, refusal, and absence of coronary angiography.

This distinction is important: A patient not undergoing PCI because of nonobstructive disease is very different from a patient not undergoing PCI because advanced CKD made the procedure appear too risky. By making these categories visible, the study helps move the discussion from whether CKD patients should undergo PCI as a group to which CKD patients may still have a favorable balance of ischemic benefit and procedural risk.

Prior evidence has consistently shown that patients with NSTEMI and CKD are less likely to receive invasive management and guideline-directed medical therapy, and experience worse long-term ischemic and bleeding outcomes.2,4,13 Meta-analytic and observational data also suggest that an invasive or PCI-based strategy may improve outcomes in CKD, although the evidence is largely observational and attenuates in advanced CKD.10,14,15 Contemporary acute coronary syndrome and revascularization guidelines support an invasive strategy in high-risk patients while emphasizing individualized assessment of comorbidity, bleeding risk, coronary anatomy, and patient preference.16

Against this background, Kim et al12 provide useful data from a nationwide cohort. The study does not overturn guideline principles; rather, it refines how they may be interpreted in NSTEMI patients with impaired renal function. Its contribution is therefore not simply that PCI was associated with better outcomes in lower eGFR strata, but that the association was examined within a clinically familiar renal-function framework that clinicians can recognize at the bedside.

Physiologically, the findings are plausible. CKD is associated with more extensive atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, vascular calcification, inflammation, anemia, and thrombo-inflammatory abnormalities, all of which may amplify the consequences of untreated coronary obstruction.17,18 Conversely, advanced CKD also magnifies bleeding risk, procedural complications, and vulnerability to contrast-associated acute kidney injury.3,8,14 The clinical decision is therefore not a binary contest between kidney protection and coronary protection, but a net-benefit judgment in which ischemic risk, renal reserve, anatomy, frailty, and patient goals are considered together (Figure 1). The credibility of the present analysis is strengthened by several sensitivity analyses, including a 48-hour landmark analysis to address immortal time bias, analyses excluding patients with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries and possible coronary artery bypass graft indications, and models accounting for center and registry cohort. These steps provide reassurance that the observed pattern is not solely an artifact of one analytic choice. Importantly, however, they do not eliminate the central limitation of the study.

The major deficiency is strong confounding by indication. Treatment was not randomized, and the authors found insufficient propensity score overlap for matching, inverse probability weighting, or overlap weighting. This is more than a statistical inconvenience. It indicates that patients selected for PCI and those managed without PCI may represent fundamentally different clinical populations. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including frailty, proteinuria, dialysis status, lesion complexity, medication adherence, and patient preference, cannot be excluded. Multivariable adjustment can improve comparability, but it cannot fully recover the counterfactual question of what would have happened had the same patient received the alternative strategy. The study was also in an Asian cohort, and ethnic differences in thrombotic and bleeding outcomes between Asians and non-Asians have been described.19,20

The safety side of the equation also remains incompletely measured. Data on contrast-associated acute kidney injury and periprocedural complications were incomplete, and bleeding could not be fairly compared between PCI and non-PCI because antiplatelet strategies differed. Moreover, coronary artery bypass grafting was uncommon and excluded from the main comparison, despite its relevance in advanced CKD with multivessel disease.14,21 These gaps matter because the clinical decision in advanced CKD depends on net benefit, not ischemic risk alone.1,10

In conclusion, Kim et al12 support a careful but active posture: Renal dysfunction should prompt more thoughtful invasive decision-making, not reflexive avoidance of invasive care. It should trigger deliberate assessment of coronary anatomy, anticipated contrast exposure, renal reserve, bleeding risk, access-site and antithrombotic strategy, frailty, and patient priorities. Future studies should move beyond eGFR alone toward individualized net-benefit assessment, ideally incorporating serial renal function, procedural characteristics, quality of life, and patient preference alongside ischemic and safety outcomes. Until such data are available, the most defensible clinical message is measured rather than maximalist: CKD should make clinicians plan PCI more carefully, not abandon the possibility of revascularization prematurely.
