Edge subdivisions and the L 2 L^{2} -homology of right-angled Coxeter groups
Grigori Avramidi, Boris Okun, Kevin Schreve

Abstract
If L L is a flag triangulation of S n − 1 S^{n-1} , then the Davis complex Σ L \Sigma_{L} for the associated right-angled Coxeter group W L W_{L} is a contractible n n -manifold. A special case of a conjecture of Singer predicts that the L 2 L^{2} -homology of such Σ L \Sigma_{L} vanishes outside the middle dimension. We give conditions which guarantee this vanishing is preserved under edge subdivision of L L . In particular, we verify Singer’s conjecture when L L is the barycentric subdivision of the boundary of an n n -simplex, and for general barycentric subdivisions of triangulations of S 2 ​ n − 1 S^{2n-1} . Using this, we construct explicit counterexamples to a torsion growth analogue of Singer’s conjecture.

Introduction

Suppose
Y
is a CW-complex and
G
a group which acts properly, cellularly, and cocompactly on
Y
.
One can define the reduced
L^{2}
-homology groups
L^{2}H_{*}(Y)
, which via a combinatorial Hodge decomposition can be identified with the Hilbert spaces of square summable harmonic chains of
Y
.
Using the group action, one can define associated dimensions for these homology groups, the
L^{2}
-Betti numbers
b_{i}^{(2)}(Y;G)
.

This paper is motivated by the following conjecture of Singer:

Singer conjecture
.

Let
M^{n}
be a closed aspherical manifold and
\widetilde{M}^{n}
its universal cover.
Then

b_{\neq n/2}^{(2)}(\widetilde{M}^{n};\pi_{1}(M^{n}))=0.

In
[
7
]
, Davis and the second author developed a program for proving the Singer conjecture for certain manifolds constructed using right-angled Coxeter groups.
In particular, if
L
is any flag triangulation of
S^{n-1}
, then a construction of Davis outputs a closed, locally CAT(0)
n
-manifold
P_{L}
, whose fundamental group is the commutator subgroup of the associated right-angled Coxeter group
W_{L}
.
The universal cover of
P_{L}
is the Davis complex
\Sigma_{L}
.
For these manifolds
P_{L}
, they proved the Singer conjecture in dimension
4
.

For triangulations of
S^{\geq 4}
, very little was known, though Davis and the second author showed the Singer conjecture for barycentric subdivisions of triangulations of
S^{5}
and
S^{7}

[
8
]
.
In this paper, we extend this result to all even dimensions.
This was claimed by the second author in
[
25
]
, but there is a gap in the argument.
The crucial computation is a resolution of the Singer conjecture for the manifold
P_{b\partial\Delta^{n}}
where
b\partial\Delta^{n}
is the barycentric subdivision of the boundary of an
n
-simplex.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Singer conjecture holds for
P_{L}
if

(1)

L=b\partial\Delta^{n}
for all
n
.

(2)

L
is the barycentric subdivision of a triangulation of
S^{2n-1}
.

Though the first item of Theorem
1.1
may seem a bit artificial, it is used to prove the second item.
In fact, the manifolds
P_{b\partial\Delta^{n}}
come up in various ways in geometric topology/geometric group theory.
For example, Tomei
[
27
]
showed that the space
T^{n}
of the real, symmetric, tridiagonal
(n+1)\times(n+1)
-matrices with fixed simple spectrum is a closed, aspherical manifold, and Davis
[
9
]
showed that
T^{n}
is commensurable with
P_{b\partial\Delta^{n}}
.
These also come up in various hyperbolization procedures.
Gromov introduced the “Möbius band procedure” and the “product with interval procedure”, which inputted a cell complex (cubical in the first case) and outputted a nonpositively curved cube complex.
In
[
5
]
, Charney and Davis showed that the Möbius band procedure applied to
\partial[0,1]^{n}
and the product with interval procedure applied to
\partial\Delta^{n}
are again commensurable to
P_{b\partial\Delta^{n}}
.

We now explain why computing the
L^{2}
-homology of
\Sigma_{bL}
for barycentric subdivisions is easier than for general flag triangulations.
The program developed in
[
7
]
relied on Mayer–Vietoris arguments, where a flag triangulation
L
is cut into pieces along links of vertices:
L=L-v\cup_{\Lk v}\St v
.
This leads to a decomposition of
\Sigma_{L}
into pieces whose components are copies of
\Sigma_{L-v}
and
\Sigma_{\St v}
intersecting along copies of
\Sigma_{\Lk v}
.
For barycentric subdivisions, the link structure of vertices is well known: they decompose as joins of barycentric subdivisions of lower dimensional complexes.
Therefore, via Künneth formulas in
L^{2}
-homology they often can be shown to have trivial
L^{2}
-homology, hence by Mayer–Vietoris we can remove them from
L
without changing
b_{\ast}^{(2)}(\Sigma_{L})
.
For barycentric subdivisions of
S^{2n-1}
, for
n\leq 4
, there are enough vertices with
\Sigma_{\Lk v}

L^{2}
-acyclic such that the resulting complex has dimension
n-1
.
Therefore, the Davis manifolds have the same
L^{2}
-Betti numbers as an
n
-dimensional subcomplex; so in particular the
L^{2}
-homology of
\Sigma_{L}
vanishes above the middle dimension and Poincaré duality implies it vanishes below the middle dimension.

However, this strategy breaks in odd dimensions.
For example, if
L=b\partial\Delta^{3}
, then we can remove from
L
all barycenters of edges, as their links are
4
-cycles, and
\Sigma_{\Lk v}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
which is
L^{2}
-acyclic.
We are left with the
1
-skeleton of a
3
-cube, there are no more vertices with
L^{2}
-acyclic links, and it is not obvious that the
L^{2}
-homology of the corresponding Davis complex vanishes.
In this specific case, the group acts on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by reflections in the faces of an ideal right-angled octahedron, which implies
L^{2}
-acyclicity by analytic methods, see e.g.

[
20
]
.
Or, one can use Lück’s mapping torus theorem
[
22
]
as the RACG virtually fibers via the “combinatorial game” of Jankiewicz–Norin–Wise
[
15
]
, but (as far as we know) these proofs cannot be adapted to higher dimensions.

To handle this, the new tool comes from the skew field approach to
L^{2}
-Betti numbers, first introduced by Linnell for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients
[
19
]
and explored by Jaikin-Zapirain, Henneke–Kielak, and others for general
{\mathbb{F}}
-coefficients
[
14
]
,
[
11
]
.
For a large class of groups
{\mathbb{Q}}G
embeds into a skew field
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{Q}}G}
, and if
Y
is a
G
-complex, one can compute the usual
L^{2}
-Betti numbers of
Y
by taking the associated Betti numbers
b^{G}_{\ast}(Y;\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{Q}}G})
of the equivariant cohomology of
Y
with
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{Q}}G}
coefficients.
Moreover, Jaikin-Zapirain
[
14
]
showed that if
G
is residually (locally indicable amenable), this embedding is
universal in Cohn’s sense
.
By definition, this says that for any skew field
D
and homomorphism
\phi:{\mathbb{Q}}G\to D
, we have the inequality
b^{G}_{*}(Y;\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{Q}}G})\leq b^{G}_{*}(Y;D)
.

Universality allows us to remove open stars of edges of
L
with
L^{2}
-acyclic links rather than vertices and still have some control over
b_{\ast}^{(2)}
.
Removing edges is somewhat unnatural, as the remaining complex
R=L\smallsetminus\mathring{\St}_{L}e
is not a full subcomplex of
L
, hence the homomorphism
W_{R}\rightarrow W_{L}
induced by inclusion is not injective and the lifts of
P_{R}
to
\Sigma_{L}
are not copies of
\Sigma_{R}
.
However, we still get a decomposition of
\Sigma_{L}
, so can use Mayer–Vietoris, and the resulting lift of
P_{R}
in
\Sigma_{L}
is precisely the covering space associated to the homomorphism
W_{R}\rightarrow W_{L}
.
Therefore, we get an intermediate cover of
P_{R}
which has the same
L^{2}
-Betti numbers as
\Sigma_{L}
.

Since the commutator subgroups
C_{L},C_{R}
of
W_{L}
and
W_{R}
respectively are special in Haglund and Wise’s sense, they are residually torsion-free nilpotent, and hence the
L^{2}
-Betti numbers of both the universal cover and the intermediate cover can be computed using the skew fields
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{Q}}C_{R}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{Q}}C_{L}}
respectively.
So, universality implies that the
L^{2}
-Betti numbers of the universal cover
\Sigma_{R}
are bounded above by the
L^{2}
-Betti numbers of the intermediate cover; hence if
\Sigma_{L}
has vanishing
L^{2}
-homology so does
\Sigma_{R}
.

For example, if
L
is the
1
-skeleton of a
3
-cube, then
L
is a subcomplex of the suspension of a hexagon, and is obtained from this suspension by removing
6
edges.
The link of each of these edges is two points, hence
\Sigma_{\Lk e}\cong{\mathbb{R}}
which is
L^{2}
-acyclic.
Since the Davis complex of the suspension of the hexagon is
L^{2}
-acyclic and removing one edge does not change the links of the others, the
L^{2}
-acyclicity of
\Sigma_{L}
follows.

One can also phrase these edge removals in terms of edge subdivisions, and it is usually more convenient to think in these terms.
As the Davis complex associated to the link of a midpoint of a subdivided edge is
L^{2}
-acyclic, Mayer–Vietoris implies that the Davis complexes of the subdivided complex and
L\smallsetminus\oSt e
have the same
L^{2}
-Betti numbers.
We use the fact that
b\partial\Delta^{n}
is an iterated edge subdivision of the boundary of the
n
-octahedron to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
(
1
), and using part (
1
), part (
2
) follows from similar Mayer–Vietoris arguments as in
[
8
]
.
More generally, Volodin in
[
28
]
showed that if
L
is a flag nested set complex associated to a building set for
\partial\Delta^{n}
(see e.g.

[
26
]
), then
L
is an iterated edge subdivision of the boundary of an
n
-octahedron.
Our argument proves the Singer conjecture for such
P_{L}
.

1.1
Torsion growth

In Theorem
4.16
and Corollary
4.18
, we prove a more general version of Theorem
1.1
.
Firstly, we prove vanishing of homology with
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
-coefficients for general
{\mathbb{F}}
rather than just the usual
L^{2}
-Betti numbers.
We also consider relative barycentric subdivisions, where we only subdivide simplices outside of some flag subcomplex.
One motivation for considering these two generalizations comes from a variation of the Singer conjecture for torsion homology growth for closed aspherical manifolds.

Torsion concentration conjecture

(
[
21
]
*1.12(2))
.

Let
M^{n}
be a closed aspherical
n
-manifold with residually finite fundamental group.
Let
G_{k}\mathrel{\lhd}G=\pi_{1}(M^{n})
be any normal chain of finite index subgroups with
\bigcap_{k}G_{k}=1
.
If
i\neq(n-1)/2
, then

\limsup_{k}\frac{\logtor H_{i}(G_{k};{\mathbb{Z}})}{[G:G_{k}]}=0.

Lück’s Approximation Theorem identifies
b_{\ast}^{(2)}(\widetilde{M}_{n};G)
with the rational homology growth of the
G_{k}
.
Even when
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}G}
is known to exist, there is no complete statement relating
b_{\ast}^{G}(\widetilde{M}^{n};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}G})
with the
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-homology growth, however there are approximation results for certain chains.
Now, in
[
1
]
, we showed that there existed flag triangulations of
S^{6}
such that the corresponding manifolds
P_{S^{6}}
had linear
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-homology growth outside of the middle dimension (for some chains).
If these manifolds additionally satisfied the Singer conjecture, then universal coefficients would imply these manifolds were counterexamples to the Torsion concentration conjecture.
However, we could not prove the Singer conjecture for these examples, hence could only say the two conjectures were incompatible.

Our construction in
[
1
]
started from a flag triangulation
T
of
S^{6}
which was a barycentric subdivision relative to an embedded complex
OL
with
b_{4}^{(2)}(W_{OL};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})\neq 0
.
We showed that the
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
-version of the Singer conjecture failed either for the right-angled Coxeter group associated to
T
or to the link of an odd-dimensional simplex in
T
.
In either case, this produced a
7
-dimensional counterexample, though in a non-explicit way.

Using the computation in Theorem
4.16
, we can improve these counterexamples in two ways.
We can show now that
T
itself is a counterexample to the
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
-version of the Singer conjecture.
Moreover, we also produce flag triangulations of
S^{13}
, which satisfy the Singer conjecture but violate the
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
-version.
We can promote these examples to higher even dimensions.
These appear to be the first examples of closed aspherical manifolds of this sort.
Although we still do not know of any such manifolds in odd dimensions, we obtain explicit counterexamples to the Torsion concentration conjecture in even dimensions
\geq 14
and all dimensions
\geq 21
.
We note that in
[
16
]
*Conjecture 6.11, a modified version of the conjecture has been proposed, which is still open.
Instead of concentration, it predicts that the alternating sum of torsion growths of an odd-dimensional closed aspherical manifold limits to the
L^{2}
-torsion of the universal cover.

Let

Suppose that

2.1
Linnell skew fields

Recall that a homomorphism to a skew field
D
is
epic
if the division closure of its image is
D
.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that an epic skew field
\phi:{\mathbb{F}}G\rightarrow D
is
Linnell
if for any subgroup
K<G
, the map

D_{|K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{{\mathbb{F}}K}}{\mathbb{F}}G\rightarrow D;\qquad d\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits}f\rightarrow d\phi(f)

is injective, where
D_{|K}
denotes the division closure of
\phi({\mathbb{F}}K)
in
D
.

Note that if
D
is a Linnell skew field, then
\phi:{\mathbb{F}}G\rightarrow D
is injective, so at the very least
G
must be torsion-free.
A fundamental theorem of Hughes
[
12
]
implies that for locally indicable groups Linnell skew fields are unique up to
{\mathbb{F}}G
-isomorphism.
We will say
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
exists
to mean that
G
is locally indicable and there is a Linnell skew field
\phi:{\mathbb{F}}G\to\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
.
This condition passes to subgroups: if
K<G
and
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
exists, then
{\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}}_{|K}
is Linnell over
{\mathbb{F}}K
, hence
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}K}={\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}}_{|K}
exists.

Suppose
H<G
is a finite index subgroup and
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
exists.
Then the Linnell condition implies that
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}\cong\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}H}^{[G:H]}
as vector spaces over
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}H}
, and therefore the
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
-Betti numbers satisfy the same multiplicativity as the usual
L^{2}
-Betti numbers:

b^{H}_{*}(Y;\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}H})=[G:H]b^{G}_{*}(Y;\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}).

This multiplicativity allows us to extend skew field Betti numbers to a virtual setting.
We will say
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}G}
virtually exists

3.2

We now describe the effect of vertex and edge removal on
{\mathbb{F}}
-

-Betti numbers of the Davis complex
\Sigma_{L}
with respect to the
W_{L}
-action.
By abusing terminology, we will sometimes say a flag complex

is

-acyclic if
b_{*}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
.

Lemma 3.2

(cf.

[
1
]
*Lemma 11)
.

If
L
is a flag complex and
v\in L
is vertex with
b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk v};{\mathbb{F}})=0
, then

b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L-v};{\mathbb{F}})\leq b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}}),

and, if in addition,
b_{i-1}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk v};{\mathbb{F}})=0
, then

b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L-v};{\mathbb{F}})=b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}}).

Proof.

If
v
is a vertex of a flag complex
L
, then the star
\St v
, the link
\Lk v
, and
L-v=L\smallsetminus\oSt v
are all full subcomplexes of
L
.
The decomposition

L=\St_{v}\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{\Lk v}(L-v)

induces a decomposition

\Sigma_{L}=W_{L}\Sigma_{\St v}\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{W_{L}\Sigma_{\Lk v}}W_{L}\Sigma_{L-v},

Since all subcomplexes are full, we have a decomposition:

\Sigma_{L}=W_{L}\times_{W_{\St v}}\Sigma_{\St v}\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{W_{L}\times_{W_{\Lk v}}\Sigma_{\Lk v}}W_{L}\times_{W_{L-v}}\Sigma_{L-v},

hence a Mayer–Vietoris sequence with
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}}
-coefficients:

\dots\to H_{i+1}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}\times_{W_{\Lk v}}\Sigma_{\Lk v})\to H_{i}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}\times_{W_{\St v}}\Sigma_{\St v})\oplus H_{i}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}\times_{W_{L-v}}\Sigma_{L-v})\to H_{i}^{C_{L}}(\Sigma_{L})\to\dots

Since the star is the cone on the link, its Davis complex and the associated RACG split as products

\Sigma_{\St v}\cong\Sigma_{\Lk v}\times[0,1],\qquad W_{\St v}\cong W_{\Lk v}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2.

Hence
b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{\St v};{\mathbb{F}})=\frac{1}{2}b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk v};{\mathbb{F}})
, and the assumptions and Lemma
2.4
imply vanishing of the corresponding terms in the sequence, which implies the inequalities.
∎

The corresponding statement for the removal of edges is our main technical result in this paper.
Here, we treat edges as single simplices of

, not subcomplexes.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose we have a flag complex
L
and an edge
e
whose link
\Lk e
has
b_{i-1}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk e};{\mathbb{F}})=0
for some

.
Then

b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L-e};{\mathbb{F}})\leq b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}}).

In particular, if
b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
then

b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{L-e};{\mathbb{F}})=0.

Proof.

Let
R=L-e
.
Then we have
R=L\smallsetminus\oSt e
, and a decomposition

L=\St e\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{S\Lk e}R

where the star
\St e
is a full subcomplex, but the suspension of the link and complement are flag
non-full
subcomplexes of
L
.
This gives a decomposition
K_{L}=K_{\St e}\cup_{K_{S\Lk e}}K_{R}
, and hence a decomposition of
\Sigma_{L}
:

\Sigma_{L}=W_{L}\Sigma_{\St e}\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{W_{L}K_{S\Lk e}}W_{L}K_{R}.

Consider the long exact sequence of the pair
(\Sigma_{L},W_{L}K_{R})=W_{L}(K_{L},K_{R})
.

\dots\to H_{i+1}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}(K_{L},K_{R});\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})\to H_{i}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}K_{R};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})\to H_{i}^{C_{L}}(\Sigma_{L};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})\to\dots

The pair
W_{L}(K_{L},K_{R})
excises to the pair
W_{L}(K_{\St e},K_{S\Lk e})
, and the key observation is that since the star
\St e
is full, this pair is just
W_{L}\times_{W_{\St e}}(\Sigma_{\St e},W_{\St e}K_{S\Lk e})
.
Next, the splitting
\St e=\Lk e*e
gives splittings

K_{\St e}=K_{\Lk e}\times K_{e},\quad K_{S\Lk e}=K_{\Lk e}\times K_{\partial e},\quad W_{\St e}=W_{\Lk e}\times W_{e}.

Therefore,
(\Sigma_{\St e},W_{\St e}K_{S\Lk e})\cong\Sigma_{\Lk e}\times W_{e}(K_{e},K_{\partial e})
.
Now, since
W_{e}(K_{e},K_{\partial e})\cong([0,1]^{2},\partial[0,1]^{2})
, we have that the pair
W_{L}(K_{L},K_{R})
excises to

W_{L}\times_{W_{\St e}}\left(\Sigma_{\Lk e}\times([0,1]^{2},\partial[0,1]^{2})\right).

Note that
C_{L}
acts trivially on
[0,1]^{2}
, hence, by the Künneth formula
b_{i+1}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}(K_{L},K_{R});\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})=b_{i-1}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}\times_{W_{\St_{e}}}\Sigma_{\Lk e};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})
, which vanishes by Lemma

and hypothesis, and therefore, from the long exact sequence,
b_{i}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}K_{R};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})\leq b_{i}^{C_{L}}(\Sigma_{L};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})
.

Furthermore, since
W_{L}K_{R}=\Sigma_{R}/\ker\phi_{RL}
and

, Lemma

implies
b_{i}^{C_{R}}(\Sigma_{R};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{R}})\leq b_{i}^{C_{L}}(W_{L}K_{R};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})
.
Thus
b_{i}^{C_{R}}(\Sigma_{R};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{R}})\leq b_{i}^{C_{L}}(\Sigma_{L};\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbb{F}}C_{L}})
, and the theorem follows by normalizing by the index, since the commutator subgroups have the same index

.
∎

One annoyance is that in general

This generalizes easily to when

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that

is a full subcomplex of

.
Let

be an iterated edge subdivision of

the induced iterated edge subdivision of

.
Then

depends only on

.

It suffices to show that any simplex

is subdivided identically in

.
Since

is a sub-simplex

Any choice of edge subdivisions of

obviously produces the same final subdivision

of

is subdivided into the join

∎

4.2
Relative barycentric subdivisions

Given a simplicial pair
(L,K)
, we can form the

relative to

, by barycentrically subdividing simplices of
L
not in

.
More precisely, we construct
b(L,K)
by induction on dimension, the 0-th stage being the set of vertices of
L
.
To construct the
i

(i-1)
-st stage, for each simplex
\sigma\in L^{(i)}
, if
\sigma\in K
we leave it unchanged, otherwise we introduce a new vertex
v_{\sigma}
, and cone
b(\partial\sigma,\partial\sigma\cap K)
to
v_{\sigma}
.
Note that
b(L,\emptyset)=b(L,pt)
is the usual barycentric subdivision of
L
, which we shorten to
b(L)
.

Combinatorially, vertices of
b(L,K)
are simplices of
L\smallsetminus K
together with vertices of
K
, and simplices of
b(L,K)
are chains of simplices of
L
with at most one simplex in
K
.
Note that
K
is a full subcomplex of
b(L,K)
, and if
K
is flag, then
b(L,K)
is also flag.

The following lemma is well-known in the case of barycentric subdivisions.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
b(L,K)
be the relative barycentric subdivision of a simplicial pair
(L,K)
.
If
v
is a vertex that is the barycenter of a simplex
\sigma\in L-K
then

\Lk_{b(L,K)}v=b(\partial\sigma,\partial\sigma\cap K)\ast b\Lk_{L}\sigma.

If
v
is a vertex of
K
, then

\Lk_{b(L,K)}v=b(\Lk_{L}v,\Lk_{K}v).

Proof.

If
v
is the barycenter of
\sigma
, simplices of
\Lk_{b(L,K)}v
correspond to chains of simplices of
L
with at most one simplex in
K
such that the addition of
\sigma
is still a chain.
Every such chain is a union of two chains, one contained in
\sigma
and the other containing
\sigma
.
Since
\sigma
is not in
K
, any simplex in the chain that is in
K
must be contained in
\sigma
.
Therefore, the chains contained in
\sigma
correspond to
b(\partial\sigma,\partial\sigma\cap K)
, the chains containing
\sigma
correspond to
\Lk_{L}\sigma
, and the join structure is clear.

For
v\in K
,
\Lk_{b(L,K)}v
consists of chains of simplices of
L
properly containing
v
with at most one simplex in
K
, which is precisely a simplex in
b(\Lk_{L}v,\Lk_{K}v)
.
∎

The next lemma follows immediately from the definition of

.

Lemma 4.9
.

If
L=L_{1}\cup_{L_{0}}L_{2}
,

, and
K_{i}=L_{i}\cap K
, then

b(L,K)=b(L_{1},K_{1})\cup_{b(L_{0},K_{0})}b(L_{2},K_{2}).

Lemma 4.10

Lemma 4.11
.

If
K
is a flag subcomplex of
L

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the number of simplices of

The inductive claim is that we have a sequence of edge subdivisions which turns

(i)

For any simplex

(ii)

If
\tau

\rho

\sigma

In the base case

Now, assume

.
Now perform the same sequence in

.
This clearly does not produce any vertices in the interior of

Assume that the barycenter of

Since the edge goes through the interior,

By condition (i) the barycenter of

If

Remark 4.15
.

More general versions of Lemmas
4.11
and
4.12
and Corollary
4.14
follow from earlier work of Feichtner–Müller
[
10
]
, Postnikov–Reiner–Williams
[
26
]
, and Volodin
[
28
]
.
Given a simplicial complex
L
, a
building set
is a collection of simplices
\mathcal{B}
containing
L^{(0)}
such that if
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{B}
with
\sigma\cup\tau\in L
then
\sigma\cup\tau\in\mathcal{B}
if
\sigma\cap\tau\neq\emptyset
.
Any building set determines a corresponding nested set complex, which ends up being a subdivision of
L
where new vertices correspond to elements in
\mathcal{B}\smallsetminus L^{(0)}
.
If
K\subset L
is a subcomplex, it is easy to see that the vertices of
L
together with all simplices not contained in
K
forms a building set, and the corresponding nested set complex is isomorphic to
b(L,K)
.

Feichtner and Müller
[
10
]
*Theorem 4.2 show that if
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{B}
are two building sets, then the nested complex for
\mathcal{B}
is an iterated stellar subdivision of the nested complex for
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
.
Volodin
[
28
]
*Lemma 6 shows that if the nested set complex for
B
is flag then this iterated stellar subdivision can be chosen to be an iterated edge subdivision.
(Volodin only considers
L=\partial\Delta^{n}
and assumes both nested set complexes are flag, but his argument goes through in this more general setup.) Finally, Postnikov, Reiner and Williams
[
26
]
*Section 7 show that any building set for
L=\partial\Delta^{n}
with flag nested set complex contains a building set whose nested set complex is isomorphic to
O^{n}
.

The dual polytopes of nested set complexes associated to building sets for
\partial\Delta^{n}
are called
nestohedra
, they include permutohedra and associahedra, see
[
26
]
*Section 10 for these and some other examples.

Using Theorem

Theorem 4.17
.

Suppose
L
is a complex of dimension
\leq 2n-1
, and
K
is a flag subcomplex.
Then

b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{b(L,K)};{\mathbb{F}})=b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{K};{\mathbb{F}})\text{ for }i>n,

and

b_{n}^{(2)}(W_{b(L,K)};{\mathbb{F}})\geq b_{n}^{(2)}(W_{K};{\mathbb{F}}).

Proof.

The barycenter of an odd-dimensional simplex
\sigma
in
L\smallsetminus K
has link equal to
b(\partial\sigma,\partial\sigma\cap K)\ast b\Lk_{L}\sigma
by Lemma
4.8
.
Since
K
is flag,
\partial\sigma\cap K
is flag, so Theorem
4.16
guarantees that the first factor is
{\mathbb{F}}
-
L^{2}
-acyclic, hence the link is
{\mathbb{F}}
-
L^{2}
-acyclic by the Künneth formula, so we can remove these vertices from
b(L,K)
without changing
b_{*}^{(2)}(\cdot;{\mathbb{F}})
.
Furthermore, if we remove the barycenters of odd-dimensional simplices in order of decreasing dimension, then the first factor in the link is unchanged at each stage, so by Lemma
3.2
we can remove all the odd-dimensional barycenters.

At this point, we have a subcomplex of
b(L,K)
spanned by the vertices of
K
and the even-dimensional barycenters of
L\smallsetminus K
.
We now remove the even-dimensional barycenters in order of increasing dimension to obtain
K
.

For a
0
-dimensional barycenter
v
(a vertex of
L\smallsetminus K
), the new link is a subcomplex of
b\Lk_{L}v
spanned by the even-dimensional barycenters of dimension
\geq 2
.
Therefore, this subcomplex is at most
(n-2)
-dimensional, hence the link has trivial
b_{i}^{(2)}(\cdot;{\mathbb{F}})
for
i>n-1
, hence removing these vertices does not affect
b_{>n}^{(2)}(\cdot;{\mathbb{F}})
and decreases
b_{n}^{(2)}(\cdot;{\mathbb{F}})
.
Now, if
v_{\tau}
is an even-dimensional barycenter of a simplex
\tau
with
\dim\tau\geq 2
, in our procedure we have removed all barycenters of faces of
\tau
except those in
K
.
Therefore, the link of
v
decomposes as a join of
\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cap K
with a subcomplex of
b\Lk_{L}\tau
spanned by even-dimensional barycenters.
This subcomplex is at most
(n-2-\dim\tau/2)
-dimensional, and, since
W_{\tau^{\prime}}
is finite, removing these vertices again does not affect
b_{\geq n}^{(2)}(\cdot;{\mathbb{F}})
.
∎

Combining Theorem
4.17
with Poincaré duality proves a more general version of the second part of our main theorem from the introduction:

Corollary 4.18
.

Suppose
L
is a barycentric subdivision of a triangulation of
S^{n-1}
.

(1)

If
n
is even, then
b_{\ast}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
for
\ast\neq\frac{n}{2}
.

(2)

If
n
is odd then
b_{\ast}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
for
\ast\neq\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2}
.

Remark 4.19
.

Note that in Corollary
4.18
we make no assumptions on the triangulation of
S^{n}
, in particular it does not have to be piecewise-linear.
In fact, if we start with a triangulation of a generalized homology sphere
GHS^{n}
(by definition a homology
n
-manifold with the same homology as
S^{n}
), then the Davis complex is a homology
n
-manifold and Poincaré duality combined with Theorem
4.17
implies the same vanishing result.

5
Torsion growth

In this section we give explicit counterexamples to the Torsion concentration conjecture from the introduction.

5.1
Universal coefficients and torsion growth

Given a group
G
, and a residual chain
G_{k}
of finite index normal subgroups, let

t_{i}^{(2)}(G):=\limsup\frac{\logtor H_{i}(G_{k};{\mathbb{Z}})}{[G:G_{k}]}.

This may depend on the chain, but we omit it from the notation.

We will also let
t_{i}(G)_{p}:=\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}\Tor(H_{i}(G;{\mathbb{Z}});{\mathbb{F}_{p}})
denote the number of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{k}}
-summands in
H_{i}(G;{\mathbb{Z}})
, and set

t_{i}^{(2)}(G)_{p}:=\limsup\frac{t_{i}(G_{k})_{p}}{[G:G_{k}]}.

Note that

t_{i}^{(2)}(G)\geq\max_{p}t_{i}^{(2)}(G)_{p}\log p.

Recall that
\mathcal{P}(G)
denotes the poset of finite index normal subgroups
H<G
with
G/H
a
p
-group.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
G
is residually torsion-free nilpotent, and
b_{n+1}^{(2)}(G;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})>b_{n+1}^{(2)}(G,{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Then, for any normal residual chain
G_{k}\mathrel{\lhd}G
of finite index subgroups,

t_{n}^{(2)}(G)+t_{n+1}^{(2)}(G)>0.

If additionally we have
b_{n+2}^{(2)}(G;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=0
, then

t_{n}^{(2)}(G)>0

for any cofinal chain of subgroups in
\mathcal{P}(G)
.
1
1

1

This is not just vacuously true: cofinal chains in
\mathcal{P}(G)
do exist.
For instance, one can take
G_{k}
to be the intersection of all subgroups of index
p^{i}
for
i\leq k
.

Proof.

For any normal residual chain of finite index subgroups, Lück’s approximation theorem implies
b_{n+1}^{(2)}(G;{\mathbb{Q}})=\lim_{k}\frac{b_{n+1}(G_{k};{\mathbb{Q}})}{[G:G_{k}]}
, and Theorem
2.5
implies
b_{n+1}^{(2)}(G;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\leq\frac{b_{n+1}(G_{k};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})}{[G:G_{k}]}
.
The universal coefficient theorem gives us that

t_{n}(G_{k})_{p}+t_{n+1}(G_{k})_{p}=b_{n+1}(G_{k};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})-b_{n+1}(G_{k};{\mathbb{Q}}).

Hence, taking
\limsup
and using the assumption gives
t_{n}^{(2)}(G)_{p}+t_{n+1}^{(2)}(G)_{p}>0
, and therefore
t_{n}^{(2)}(G)+t_{n+1}^{(2)}(G)>0
.

Now, let
\{G_{k}\}
be a cofinal chain in
\mathcal{P}(G)
.
Note that
\{G_{k}\}
is residual, since
G
is residually
p
-finite.
Assuming that
b_{n+2}^{(2)}(G;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=0
, Theorem
2.7
gives
\lim\frac{b_{n+2}(G_{k};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})}{[G:G_{k}]}=0
.
Then by the universal coefficient theorem in degree
n+2
,
t_{n+1}^{(2)}(G)_{p}=0
, and therefore
t_{n}^{(2)}(G)_{p}>0
, hence we get
t_{n}^{(2)}(G)>0
.
∎

Combining this lemma with Theorem
4.17
, and noting that
b_{i}^{(2)}(W_{K};{\mathbb{F}})=0
for
i>\dim K+1
gives the following corollary:

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose
L
is a complex of dimension
\leq 2n-1
, and
K
is a flag subcomplex of dimension
n
with
b_{n+1}^{(2)}(W_{K};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})>b_{n+1}^{(2)}(W_{K};{\mathbb{Q}})=0
.
Then

\displaystyle b_{>n}^{(2)}(W_{b(L,K)};{\mathbb{Q}})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b_{>n+1}^{(2)}(W_{b(L,K)};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b_{n+1}^{(2)}(W_{b(L,K)};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})

\displaystyle>0,

and, for any cofinal chain in
\mathcal{P}(W_{b(L,K)})
,

\displaystyle t_{n}^{(2)}(W_{b(L,K)})

\displaystyle>0.

5.2
Counterexamples to the Torsion concentration conjecture

Another corollary is an improved version of the main examples in
[
1
]
.
Let
p
be an odd prime, and let
OL
be the flag
3
-dimensional complex from
[
1
]
that has
b_{4}^{(2)}(W_{OL};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\neq 0
and
b_{*}^{(2)}(W_{OL};{\mathbb{Q}})=0
, and which embeds as a full subcomplex into a triangulation of
S^{6}
.
Define a flag triangulation
T
of
S^{6}
by taking
T
to be the relative barycentric subdivision
b(S^{6},OL)
.

Since
OL
is a full subcomplex of
T
, the join
OL\ast OL
is a full
7
-dimensional subcomplex of
T\ast T\cong S^{13}
, and the triple join
OL*OL*OL
is a full
11
-dimensional subcomplex of
T*T*T\cong S^{20}
,

Let
T^{\prime}=b(T*T,OL\ast OL)
, and
T^{\prime\prime}=b(T*T*T,OL*OL*OL)
.
So
T
,
T^{\prime}
, and
T^{\prime\prime}
are flag triangulations of
S^{6}
,
S^{13}
, and
S^{20}
, respectively.

Theorem 5.3
.

•

P_{T}
violates the
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
-version of the Singer conjecture.

•

P_{T^{\prime}}
satisfies the Singer conjecture, and violates the
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
-version and the Torsion concentration conjecture.

•

P_{T^{\prime\prime}}
violates both the
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
-version of the Singer conjecture and the Torsion concentration conjecture.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
4.17
with
n=4
to
T
gives
b_{4}^{(2)}(W_{T};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})\geq b_{4}^{(2)}(W_{OL};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})\neq 0
, proving the first claim.

By the Künneth formula,
b_{8}^{(2)}(W_{OL*OL};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\neq 0
and
b_{8}^{(2)}(W_{OL*OL};{\mathbb{Q}})=0
.
So, applying Corollary
5.2
with
n=7
to
T^{\prime}
gives
b_{8}^{(2)}(W_{T^{\prime}};{\mathbb{F}_{p}})\neq 0
,
b_{>7}^{(2)}(W_{T^{\prime}};{\mathbb{Q}})=0
, and
t^{(2)}_{7}(W_{T^{\prime}})\neq 0
.
Also, by Poincaré duality,
b_{<7}^{(2)}(W_{T^{\prime}};{\mathbb{Q}})=0
.
This proves the second claim.

Similarly,
b_{12}^{(2)}(W_{OL*OL*OL};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\neq 0
and
b_{12}^{(2)}(W_{OL*OL*OL};{\mathbb{Q}})=0
.
Applying Corollary
5.2
with

.
∎

Taking joins with

At this point, we cannot prove the Singer conjecture for any odd-dimensional non-

-

-acyclic example.

6
Minimally branching complexes

We end with a non-manifold application of Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 6.1
.

If
L
is a connected flag graph with vertices of degree
\leq 3
, then
b_{2}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
unless
L=K_{3,3}
.

Proof.

If
L
has a vertex of degree
\leq 2
, we can iteratively remove the vertices of
L
, at each stage removing a vertex of degree
\leq 2
.
These removals does not affect
b_{2}^{(2)}(;{\mathbb{F}})
, hence
b_{2}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
.
Therefore, we can assume that
L
is trivalent.
Furthermore, note that if
L
has a proper full subcomplex
A
so that
L-A
is disconnected and
b_{1}^{(2)}(W_{A};{\mathbb{F}})=0
, then we get a decomposition
L=L_{1}\cup_{A}L_{2}
where each
L_{i}
has a vertex of degree
\leq 2
.
Therefore,
b_{2}^{(2)}(W_{L_{i}};{\mathbb{F}})=0
so
b_{2}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
by Mayer–Vietoris.
In particular, we are done if
L
has a separating edge or separating pair of non-adjacent vertices, so assume that this does not occur and that
L\neq K_{3,3}
.

We now show that
L
has two non-adjacent vertices
u
and
v
with non-separating stars.
There are two cases: either
L
contains a copy of a suspension of three points as a full subcomplex, or it does not.

Suppose there is a suspension of three points inside of
L
with
x
and
y
suspension points and base
\{a,b,c\}
.
Let
\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\}
denote the endpoints of edges starting at
\{a,b,c\}
which are not in the suspension.
Note that
\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\}
are all different.
Indeed, since
L\neq K_{3,3}
, they cannot be all the same, and if
a^{\prime}=b^{\prime}
, then
a^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}
would separate the suspension from the rest of the complex.

Next we claim that the stars of
\{a,b,c\}
are all non-separating.
Consider a vertex
z\in L-\St a-c
.
Since the pair
a^{\prime},c
does not separate, there is a geodesic in
L-a^{\prime}-c
from
z
to
b
.
Such geodesic cannot go through
x
,
y
and
a
, so
z
is connected to
b
in
L-\St a-c
.
Thus
L-\St a-c
is connected, and since
c
is adjacent to
c^{\prime}
,
L-\St a
is connected as well.
Therefore we can take
a
and
b
as
u
and
v
.

For the second case, we claim that we can take
u
and
v
to be points of maximum distance.
The maximality of the distance implies that for any vertex in
\Lk v
at least one of the edges from it points in the direction of
u
.
Suppose, to the contrary, that
\St v
separates, and let
C
be a component of
L-\St v
not containing
u
.
Then the first edge of a geodesic in
L
from any vertex of
C
to
u
must have the endpoint in
\Lk v
.
Therefore, vertices in
\Lk v
are adjacent to at most one vertex in
C
, and vertices in
C
are all adjacent to a vertex in
\Lk v
.
So,
C
has at most three vertices.

C
cannot be a single vertex, since
L
does not contain suspensions of three points.
If there are two vertices in
C
, then they are adjacent and each is adjacent to two vertices in
\Lk v
, so one vertex in the link is adjacent to both, contradiction.
If there are three, then each is adjacent to a unique vertex in the link, since
L
is trivalent they all must be adjacent, so we get a triangle, contradicting flagness.
Thus stars of
u
and
v
do not separate, and we are done with the second case.

Now,
L
is a subcomplex of the suspension
S(L-u-v)
which has
b_{\ast}^{(2)}(W_{S(L-u-v)};{\mathbb{F}})=0
(we think of
u
and
v
as the two suspension points).
By repeated application of Theorem
3.3
, we will be done if we can iteratively remove the extra edges of
S(L-u-v)
until we get to
L
, so that at each step,
b_{1}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk e};{\mathbb{F}})=0
.

We first remove edges containing
u
which join
u
to vertices
\notin\Lk_{L}u
, starting with an edge whose other endpoint is
w\in\Lk_{L}v\smallsetminus\Lk_{L}u
.
(Note that since
u
and
v
are note adjacent and
L\neq K_{3,3}
the links of
u
and
v
are different.) The link of this edge is the same as the link of
w
in
L-u-v
, so it is
2
points and hence
b_{\ast}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk e};{\mathbb{F}})=0
.
We continue removing all edges emanating from
u
and ending outside of
\Lk_{L}u
.
Since
L-\St u
is connected, any vertex in
L-\St u
is connected by a path to
v
, and hence connected to a vertex in
\Lk v
.
Therefore, we can always remove these edges so that links are
\leq 2
points.
Removing these edges containing
u
does not change any of the links of edges containing
v
, so we do the same procedure starting at a vertex in
\Lk_{L}u\smallsetminus\Lk_{L}v
, until we are left with
L
.
∎

The higher dimensional version of Theorem
6.1
is easier to prove, due to the following lemma.
Let
3^{*n}
denote the
n
-fold join of
3
points with itself.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
L
is an
n
-dimensional connected flag complex with links of vertices
\cong 3^{*n}
.
If
n\geq 2
, then
L=3^{*n+1}
.

Proof.

Let
v\in L
, and let
x\in\Lk v
.
Then, since
\Lk(x)\cong 3^{*n}
,
x
is adjacent to two vertices
u
and
w
that are not in
\St v
, and
\{v,u,w\}
is a
3
point factor of
\Lk(x)
.
Hence, if
y\in\Lk(v)
is adjacent to
x
, both
u
and
w
are adjacent to
y
.
Since
\Lk v\cong 3^{*n}
is connected for
n\geq 2
, it follows that
u
and
w
are the same for all vertices in
\Lk v
.
So we get a copy of
3^{*n+1}
in
L
, and since
L
is connected we are done.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose that
L
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional flag complex such that every
(n-2)
-simplex is contained in at most three
(n-1)
-simplices and links of
k
-simplices are connected for
-1\leq k<n-2
.
Then
b_{n}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})=0
unless
L=3^{*n}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
\dim L
.
If
L
is
0
-dimensional it is obvious and if
L
is
1
-dimensional, then it is Theorem
6.1
.
Now assume that the statement holds for all such
(n-1)
-dimensional flag complexes, and let
L\neq 3^{*n+1}
be an
n
-dimensional flag complex with
n\geq 1
which satisfies the hypotheses.
Note that
L
is connected and if
v
is a vertex of
L
then
\Lk v
satisfies the conditions of the theorem, since the link of
\sigma
in
\Lk v
is the link of
v\sigma
in
L
.

We can assume that the link of each vertex in
L
is
3^{*n}
.
Otherwise by induction it would have
b_{n}^{(2)}(W_{\Lk v};{\mathbb{F}})=0
, and hence its removal does not affect
b_{n+1}^{(2)}(W_{L};{\mathbb{F}})
by Lemma
3.2
.
So, we are done by Lemma
6.2
.
∎
