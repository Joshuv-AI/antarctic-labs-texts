Association of internalised weight stigma and physical activity of patients with diabesity in a primary care clinic in Hong Kong: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sau Kuen Wong, Sau Nga Fu, Wan Luk, Man Chi Dao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesInternalised weight stigma (IWS) has been associated with lower physical activity (PA) levels in Western populations with diabetes, but evidence from Asian primary care settings remains limited. This study examined factors associated with PA levels, including IWS, among obese type 2 diabetic patients in Hong Kong primary care.DesignCross-sectional questionnaire study.SettingA single general outpatient clinic in Hong Kong.Participants310 Chinese adults aged 18–69 years with type 2 diabetes and Body Mass Index ≥25 kg/m² who attended routine follow-up appointments were randomly sampled.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was to identify factors, particularly internalised weight stigma, associated with adequate physical activity levels. PA levels were assessed using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Long Form, with ‘adequate PA’ defined as achieving moderate or high PA levels. IWS was assessed with Chinese Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire. Secondary outcomes were the proportion of participants achieving adequate PA levels, the proportion with high IWS, and the sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with high IWS. Multivariate logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between these predictors and PA levels.Results18.1% of participants had inadequate PA levels and 9.4% had high IWS. In multivariate logistic regression, high IWS (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.273; 95% CI 0.114 to 0.655; p=0.004), female sex (aOR 0.480; 95% CI 0.249 to 0.926; p=0.029), polypharmacy (aOR 0.339; 95% CI 0.170 to 0.675; p=0.002) and presence of chronic pain (aOR 0.249; 95% CI 0.112 to 0.554; p=0.001) were negatively associated with adequate PA levels independently (all p<0.05).ConclusionsInternalised weight stigma is independently associated with lower odds of achieving adequate PA levels among obese diabetic patients in primary care. Screening for IWS and addressing psychosocial barriers may represent a useful target for future intervention studies.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major public health concern in Hong Kong. In 2024, it was the 11th leading cause of death, accounting for 442 deaths.1 The local adult prevalence of DM or raised blood glucose is 8.5%,1 and 29.9% of these patients are obese.2 Based on WHO Asian-specific Body Mass Index (BMI) cut-offs, obesity is classified as class I (25.0–29.9 kg/m²) or class II (≥30.0 kg/m²).3 The coexistence of type 2 diabetes and obesity, termed diabesity, reflects a strong pathophysiological link where excess adiposity induces multi-organ insulin resistance and impairs pancreatic beta-cell function.4 5 Regular physical activity (PA) is essential for managing diabesity, as it improves glycaemic control, supports weight management, and reduces cardiovascular risk.5 6

Despite these benefits, many obese diabetic patients still fail to achieve adequate PA levels. In Hong Kong, 14.8% of the adult population report insufficient exercise.7 Identifying barriers to PA in this high-risk group is essential for improving health outcomes. While clinical factors associated with PA levels in type 2 diabetes patients are well-documented,8–11 psychosocial barriers are increasingly recognised as important contributors.12 Internalised weight stigma (IWS), also known as weight self-stigma or weight bias internalisation, refers to internalisation of negative societal stereotypes about weight.13 It is linked to adverse outcomes, including hypertension, hyperglycaemia and chronic pain.14 The WHO emphasises addressing IWS due to its profound psychological consequences, such as reduced self-esteem, depression, anxiety and disordered eating.13 These effects may be associated with reduced exercise self-efficacy, increase avoidance behaviours due to fear of negative judgement and may contribute to lower overall motivation for PA.13 Although a systematic review found inconsistent associations between IWS and PA overall,15 recent evidence from Western populations with type 2 diabetes suggests higher IWS is associated with lower PA levels.16 Data from primary care settings where most obese diabetic patients receive care in Asia remain scarce. It is currently unknown whether this negative association holds true in Hong Kong’s distinct sociocultural and clinical context. Addressing this research gap is vital for developing culturally appropriate lifestyle interventions.

This study investigated factors associated with PA levels among obese type 2 diabetic patients managed in Hong Kong primary care, with particular emphasis on IWS. The primary outcome was to identify factors, particularly IWS, associated with adequate PA levels. Secondary outcomes were to determine the proportion of participants achieving adequate PA levels, the proportion with high IWS and the sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with high IWS. We hypothesised that higher IWS would be negatively associated with adequate PA levels.

Methodology

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional questionnaire study recruited patients attending a general outpatient clinic (GOPC, renamed to family medicine clinic since 2025) in Kwai Tsing District in Hong Kong between November 2023 and May 2024 by simple random sampling.

The inclusion criteria were (a) Chinese adults aged 18–69 years, as specified by the age limit for using International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Long Form Chinese version (IPAQ-LF-C); (b) diagnosed with type 2 DM, with T90 coded in International Classification of Primary Care–2nd edition (ICPC-2); (c) BMI 25kg/m2 or above; and (d) able to understand Cantonese or written Chinese.

Exclusion criteria were (a) being wheelchair-bound or bed-bound which limits the ability for PA; (b) limited exercise tolerance due to pre-existing cardiovascular diseases, such as decompensated heart failure, severe aortic stenosis, uncontrolled arrhythmia and/or acute coronary syndrome; (c) pregnancy; and (d) suffering unstable major psychiatric illnesses including schizophrenia, psychotic disorders and bipolar disorder.

Recruitment logistics

A list of eligible patients with scheduled appointments was generated from the Clinical Management System (CMS) 2 weeks before their visit. Every fifth patient was selected using a computer-generated random number in Microsoft Excel. A reminder note was entered, and attending doctors invited eligible patients during routine follow-up visits. A total of 339 participants were approached. 18 of them refused, yielding a response rate of 94.7%. Three were excluded per exclusion criteria, and eight incomplete questionnaires were excluded according to IPAQ-LF-C analysis guidelines, leaving 310 participants for final analysis (figure 1).

Measurements—instruments and tools

Internalised weight stigma

The Chinese version of the Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire (C-WSSQ) was used to assess IWS. It includes 12 items across two subscales: self-devaluation (Q1–6) and fear of enacted stigma (Q7–12), rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree). A total score ≥41 indicated high level of IWS,17 18 as this threshold is associated with greater psychiatric distress, including severe depression and anxiety.18 The same cut-off was subsequently adopted in a Taiwan study,17 a population sharing similar cultural characteristics with Hong Kong, with validation of the C-WSSQ.19 C-WSSQ had good reliability, with reproducibility and Cronbach’s α being 0.892 and 0.880 respectively.19

Physical activity (PA) level

PA was assessed using IPAQ-LF-C, which covers leisure, domestic or gardening, work and transport domains for individuals aged 15–69 years. Data were processed according to IPAQ guidelines: implausible reports (>16 hours/day) and cases with missing, ‘don’t know,’ or ‘refused’ responses were excluded; episodes <10 min were recoded to zero. The metabolic equivalent of task (MET) values were assigned as walking=3.3, moderate=4.0, vigorous=8.0, and total MET-minutes/week were calculated as MET × minutes × days. Participants were classified into low, moderate or high PA levels, with moderate and high combined as ‘adequate PA’.20 This dichotomous classification was specifically chosen to align with established public health guidelines, which focus on whether individuals meet the minimum recommended threshold for health benefits, thereby yielding highly actionable targets for primary care providers.

IPAQ-LF-C demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in assessing overall levels of PA among Hong Kong adults. The IPAQ had satisfactory test-retest reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.74 to 0.97.21

Both questionnaires were self-administered. For participants with reading or comprehension difficulties, trained nurses provided assistance. To ensure standardisation and minimise bias, nurses attended a briefing session and were instructed to read questions verbatim in a neutral tone and avoid leading examples or subjective interpretations. (see online supplemental appendix I).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Sociodemographic data (age, gender, education, employment, smoking history) were collected via self-administered questionnaires. Clinical parameters (insulin use, latest glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c)) were retrieved from medical records with written consent. Body weight and height were measured by trained nurses using standardised procedures during clinic visits and recorded in the CMS. BMI was calculated as weight/height², and participants were classified as obese class I (25.0–29.9 kg/m²) or class II (≥30.0 kg/m²) according to WHO Asian-specific cut-offs.3

Potential confounding factors affecting physical activity level

The following factors were included to address their potential confounding effects on PA level:

Multimorbidity8: This was defined as participants having two or more chronic diseases on active follow-up or treatment in the past 2 years. A total of 43 common chronic diseases across 15 categories were identified according to a previous literature on multi-morbidity in Hong Kong, including hypertension, lipid disorder, diabetes, chronic kidney diseases, etc.22

Sample size calculation

An unpublished internal study was conducted with 33 participants recruited in November 2023 to determine the sample size for this research project. 9.1% of participants were found to have high IWS. The proportion of participants with adequate PA level among those without high IWS was 93.3% (P0), while among those with high IWS was 66.7% (P1). According to the formula below:

The OR was calculated to be 0.143, meaning that participants without high IWS were seven times (1/OR=1/0.143) more likely to have an adequate PA level than those with high IWS. Assuming a bilateral alpha (level of significance) of 5% and a power of 90%, the sample size required was calculated to be 225 using the power analysis & sample size software PASS2023 (NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA). Considering the response rate was 80.5% (33 out of 41 participants responded), together with prudent estimation of incomplete questionnaires, the number of participants required was around 300.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 25. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean±SD or median (IQR), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed by visual inspection and statistical tests. Group comparisons used Pearson’s χ² test for categorical variables, Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U test for non-normal variables. Variables that showed a p value <0.05 in the univariate analysis were incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Additionally, to rigorously adjust for potential confounding factors, a priori sociodemographic variables, including age and employment status, were incorporated into the model regardless of their univariate significance. Prior to finalising the model, collinearity diagnostics were performed to assess potential multicollinearity among the variables.

A variance inflation factor (VIF) of less than 5 was established as the threshold to indicate the absence of significant multicollinearity. The goodness-of-fit of the multivariate logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. As for the analysis of participants with or without high IWS, the descriptive statistics and univariate analysis were applied to compare their sociodemographic and clinical factors, adopting the similar methodology as described previously. The findings were considered as statistically significant if the p value <0.05.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Factors associated with physical activity level

From our study, 18.1% of the participants did not achieve an adequate PA levels. In the univariate analysis (tables 1 and 2), gender, BMI (degree of obesity), presence of polypharmacy, presence of chronic pain and high IWS were found to be significantly associated with adequacy of PA levels (p<0.05). To determine the adjusted effect of the individual factors on adequate PA level, they were included in the multivariate logistic regression as shown in table 3. Collinearity diagnostics confirmed the absence of significant multicollinearity (highest VIF 1.299). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated a good model fit (p=0.359). High IWS was found to be negatively associated with adequate PA level (adjusted OR, aOR 0.273; 95% CI 0.114 to 0.655; p=0.004). Additionally, being female (aOR 0.480; 95% CI 0.249 to 0.926; p=0.029), those with polypharmacy (aOR 0.339; 95% CI 0.170 to 0.675; p=0.002) and presence of chronic pain (aOR 0.249; 95% CI 0.112 to 0.554; p=0.001) were also found to be negatively associated with adequate PA level. BMI, age and employment status were not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the association between IWS and PA levels in obese diabetic patients in primary care setting of Hong Kong. Our findings support the hypothesis that high IWS is negatively associated with adequate PA levels among this population.

In our study, 18.1% of participants had inadequate PA levels, compared to 14.8% reported in the Hong Kong Health Behaviour Survey 2023.23 This slight difference may be attributable to the use of different assessment tools (IPAQ-LF-C vs Global Physical Activity Questionnaire) and the higher comorbidity burden in our studied population relative to the general adult population.

Associated factors with adequate physical activity level

Internalised weight stigma

This study found that obese diabetic patients with high IWS had 73% lower odds to achieve an adequate PA level compared to those without high IWS, after adjusting the effects of other confounding factors. Several possible mechanisms could be hypothesised: (i) patients with high IWS may have fear of judgement by others and social embarrassment,24 which may contribute to lower participation in outdoor exercises or visiting public places like gyms; (ii) they may have low self-esteem due to IWS, which is linked to a lack of confidence to maintain regular exercise13; and (iii) they may adopt maladaptive coping strategies to deal with the weight problem including social isolation and avoidance behaviour.13 18 These mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between high IWS and lower PA levels. However, as our study did not directly measure these specific psychological constructs, the proposed pathways remain speculative and warrant confirmation in future longitudinal studies.

Gender

Consistent with the local population data,23 our study found that a higher proportion of male participants (145 out of 167, 86.8%) achieved an adequate PA level compared to females (109 out of 143, 76.2%), and females had 52% lower odds to reach an adequate PA level than male. This gender difference may be partly attributable to occupational patterns in Hong Kong, where men are more likely to engage in physically demanding jobs such as construction and transportation.25 Targeted strategies to promote leisure-time and domestic PA among female patients may therefore be warranted. Similar gender disparities in PA have been reported among adults with type 2 diabetes both locally and internationally.23 26

Polypharmacy

In our study, 50% of patients had polypharmacy, higher than the 37.3% reported in a local study of DM patients conducted in 2014–2015.27 The mean number of medications per patient in the current study was 4.8, compared with 4.2 in that study. This difference may be attributed to the advancements in medication development, and the growing complexity of managing chronic diseases in GOPCs over the past decade, leading to more prescribed medications.28 Polypharmacy was associated with 66.1% lower odds of achieving adequate PA. While the exact mechanism requires further investigation, this may stem from an increased likelihood of medication side effects,9 such as statin-induced myalgia, hypoglycaemia from oral anti-diabetic drugs, or hypotension from antihypertensive medications.

Presence of chronic pain

This study found that obese diabetic patients who experienced chronic pain had 75.1% lower odds to achieve an adequate PA level compared to those without, which is consistent with previous studies.10 11 According to the fear-avoidance model, individuals with chronic pain may exhibit avoidance behaviours as a protective strategy against pain exacerbation. This pattern has been associated with lower PA levels.29

Multimorbidity in obese diabetic patients

Multimorbidity was prevalent among obese diabetic patients, with 97.4% of them having at least two coexisting chronic conditions. This indicates that alongside diabetes, they had at least one additional chronic condition. The mean number of comorbidities per participants was 3.3. It was consistent with the reported rate of multimorbidity at 99.2% among the DM patients in a local study.27

Associated factors with high internalised weight stigma

Our study found that, among obese diabetic patients, median BMI was significantly higher in those with high IWS compared to those without (31.5 vs 28.0 kg/m², p<0.001). This finding is consistent with prior US research demonstrating greater IWS among individuals with higher BMI.30 31

Comparison with international literature

Our findings are broadly consistent with those from the second Diabetes MILES–Australia (MILES-2) study, which also reported negative associations between IWS and PA among adults with type 2 diabetes.16 In the Australian study of 726 participants who self-identified as overweight and expressed weight management concerns, higher WSSQ scores were associated with fewer days of PA (β= −0.21 in non-insulin-treated participants). Similarly, we found that high IWS (C-WSSQ ≥41) was independently associated with lower odds of achieving adequate PA level (aOR 0.273, 95% CI 0.114 to 0.655).

However, important differences in the severity and prevalence of IWS were observed. Participants in our Hong Kong cohort had a substantially lower mean total C-WSSQ score (32.2±6.65) and prevalence of high IWS (9.4%) compared with the Australian sample (mean WSSQ score 36.2±9.7). The BMI of our subjects were also lower in our study (median 28.3 kg/m²) than in MILES-2 (mean 34.0 kg/m²). These differences likely reflect variations in recruitment strategies (objective BMI criterion and primary care setting vs self-perceived overweight plus weight concern) as well as potential sociocultural factors influencing the expression and internalisation of weight stigma in Chinese vs Western populations.

These studies provide convincing evidence that IWS represents a clinically relevant barrier to PA in obese diabetic adults, while highlighting the need for culturally tailored approaches in Asian primary care setting.

Implication of study

The findings of this study have significant implications for the management of obese diabetic patients in our locality. Despite the clinical importance of exercise, nearly one-fifth of our participants failed to achieve adequate PA levels. Our results suggest that IWS is a significant, yet frequently overlooked, barrier to PA. Currently, local diabetes management guidelines do not address the impact of weight-related stigma.

To improve health outcomes, we recommend incorporating IWS screening into existing annual diabetes complication screening programme within Hong Kong GOPCs. Specifically, the C-WSSQ could be targeted toward high-risk subgroups, such as those with Class II obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²), as identified in this study.

For patients identified with high IWS, primary care providers should consider multidisciplinary interventions. Although our study did not measure healthcare provider stigma or evaluate system-level care models, international evidence suggests that integrated chronic care frameworks and psychological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioural therapy or acceptance-based therapies, can effectively reduce IWS and promote health-seeking behaviours.32 33 In the local context, clinicians should shift from traditional weight-centric advice toward stigma-informed communication to avoid exacerbating IWS, which may contribute to lower PA.13 18

Strengths of study

This is the first study in Hong Kong to investigate the relationship between IWS and PA levels among obese diabetic patients, offering valuable insights into how weight-related perceptions may influence exercise behaviours within a local primary care population. A key strength of this study is the inclusion of wide range of potential confounding factors. The selection of sociodemographic (age, sex, educational level, employment, smoking status) and health-related variables (BMI, chronic pain, multimorbidity, polypharmacy) was informed by established literature identifying these as key determinants of PA.8–12 As reflected in a recent systematic review by Yu et al, PA is influenced by interaction of a complex of physical and psychosocial factors.12 By adjusting for these factors, we were able to study the independent association between IWS and PA levels. Furthermore, validated questionnaires adapted for Chinese populations ensured cultural relevance, while objective clinical parameters including BMI, latest HbA1c, medication records and chronic disease status were retrieved from the CMS. The integration of culturally validated self-report and objective clinical data strengthens the reliability and internal validity of the study.

Limitations of study

First, this study relied on self-reported PA data, which may be affected by recall bias in estimating activity time and intensity. More objective measures, such as accelerometry and smart watches, are recommended for future research. IWS scores may also be influenced by social desirability bias, as participants could under-report stigma concerns. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the relationship between IWS and PA may be bidirectional. High IWS can act as a barrier to exercise, while lower PA may worsen weight management and health outcomes, thereby increasing susceptibility to internalising weight stigma. Thirdly, high IWS was defined using a C-WSSQ cut-off of ≥41, adopted from an international study18 and a Taiwanese study.17 Although Hong Kong and Taiwan share similar Confucian cultural backgrounds, differences in sociocultural context and healthcare environment may affect the applicability of this cut-off. Local validation in the Hong Kong population is therefore warranted. Furthermore, the low prevalence of high IWS in our cohort resulted in a relatively small exposed group (n=29). This small subgroup size limits statistical power and results in wider CIs, which could potentially affect the precision of our regression estimates. These findings should therefore be replicated in larger cohorts. Fourth, residual confounding may remain from variables not captured in this study, such as behavioural and psychosocial factors (eg, depression, exercise self-efficacy, health literacy, social support and motivation) and environmental factors (eg, access to exercise facilities), which could influence both IWS and exercise habits. Fifth, using data from a single centre is prone to selection bias, as people attending for follow-up may be more health-conscious and keen to exercise. While recruitment from a single public general outpatient clinic may limit generalisability, it is important to note that the public sector is responsible for more than 90% of diabetic patient care in Hong Kong.34 This centre serves a large district with a substantial case load of DM patients at around 4500 per year. Given Hong Kong’s high population density and high mobility, regional differences in socioeconomic status and lifestyle behaviours are minimal, suggesting that our sample remains reasonably representative of the local population of obese diabetic patients. However, this single-centre recruitment makes extrapolation of these findings to other Asian or international populations uncertain. Finally, the complete-case analysis may have introduced bias if the missing cases were not random (eg, due to stigmatisation of low PA level).

Future research

To further study the relationship between IWS and PA, longitudinal studies and qualitative studies will be essential. Future research could also investigate the effectiveness of interventions to reduce IWS on increasing PA levels and subsequent improvements in the management of diabetes and obesity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant negative association between IWS and PA levels among obese diabetic patients in Hong Kong. Being female, those with chronic pain, on multiple chronic medications and with high IWS were negatively associated with adequate PA level. The high prevalence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy in this population underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary and holistic approach to patient-centred care. Healthcare providers are advised to be aware of both the physical and psychological barriers, particularly the often-overlooked burden of IWS. Screening for IWS and addressing these psychosocial barriers may represent a useful target for future intervention studies particularly in this vulnerable population.
