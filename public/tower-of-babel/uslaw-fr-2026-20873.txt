AGENCY: National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS), National Oceanic and 
Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), Commerce.

ACTION: Final rule.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

SUMMARY: This rulemaking implements gear marking requirements and 
entanglement risk reduction measures for portions of the Pacific coast 
groundfish fishery. This rulemaking consists of mandatory requirements 
and voluntary measures. These new requirements and voluntary measures 
are established pursuant to the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation 
and Management Act (Magnuson-Stevens Act). The intent of the gear 
marking requirements is to increase the likelihood of attributing 
entanglements to a specific fishery and gear type. The intent of the 
risk reduction measures is to reduce bycatch by decreasing the 
likelihood of marine animal entanglements with fishing line.

DATES: 
    Effective date: This final rule is effective January 11, 2027, with 
the exception of Sec. Sec.  660.219(a)(1) and 660.319(a)(1), which are 
effective October 13, 2026.
    Compliance date: The compliance date for Sec. Sec.  660.219(b)(2) 
and 660.319(b)(2), is January 11, 2030.

SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION:  The Pacific Coast groundfish fishery in the 
U.S. exclusive economic zone (EEZ) seaward of Washington, Oregon, and 
California (the West Coast) is managed under the Pacific Coast 
Groundfish Fishery Management Plan (Groundfish FMP). This final rule 
implements gear marking requirements and entanglement risk reduction 
measures for portions of the Pacific Coast groundfish fishery. These 
measures are consistent with the authority provided through the 
Magnuson-Stevens Act section 303(a)(11) (16 U.S.C. 1853(a)(11)) for 
conservation and management measures that, to the extent practicable, 
minimize bycatch; and with the authority provided at Magnuson-Stevens 
Act section 303(b)(4) (16 U.S.C. 1853(b)(4)) for measures that allow 
for prohibiting, limiting, conditioning, or requiring use of specified 
types of fishing gear. The framework for gear definitions and 
restrictions is described in the Groundfish FMP in section 6.6, which 
notes that gear definitions and restrictions may be revised through a 
rulemaking process. Additionally, section 6.10.3 of the Groundfish FMP 
authorizes gear identification requirements, and provides that these 
requirements may be modified as necessary in Federal regulations.
    This rulemaking consists of new mandatory requirements and 
voluntary measures and is promulgated pursuant to the Magnuson-Stevens 
Act section 303(c) and 304(b)(1)(A) (16 U.S.C. 1853(c) and 16 U.S.C. 
1853(b)(1)(A)). The intent of the gear marking requirements for buoys 
and portions of lines is to increase the likelihood of attributing 
entanglements to a specific fishery. The intent of the risk reduction 
measures is to reduce bycatch by decreasing the likelihood of marine 
animal entanglements with fishing line. This rulemaking is, in part, in 
response to increased rates of entanglement of humpback whales with 
Pacific coast fishing gear since 2014. This rule also clarifies 
requirements regarding the position of escape panels on pot gear to 
prevent the escape panel from being on the bottom of the pot and to 
promote consistency across the escape panel requirements for Federal 
groundfish pot fisheries off Alaska and the three West Coast states. 
This rulemaking also clarifies bottom longline regulations throughout 
50 CFR part 660 for consistency with Pacific Fishery Management Council 
(Council) recommendations implemented in this rulemaking.
    Pursuant to Magnuson-Stevens Act section 303(c)(2) (16 U.S.C. 
1853(c)(2)), the Council deemed the regulations in this rulemaking 
necessary and appropriate to implement these actions in a December 20, 
2024, letter from Council Executive Director, Merrick Burden, to 
Regional Administrator Jennifer Quan. The proposed rule was published 
on February 2, 2026 (91 FR 5408). The public comment period was open 
through March 9, 2026. This rulemaking implements the Groundfish FMP, 
consistent with the authority provided in sections 6.6 and 6.10.3 of 
the Groundfish FMP, for gear restrictions being described in this final 
rule.

Background

    This final rule is based on the Council's final recommendations for 
gear marking and entanglement risk reduction in the groundfish fishery 
made at its June 2024 meeting in San Diego, CA. In developing the 
recommended measures, the Council considered the results of a 2022 
workshop with the groundfish fishing industry, ``Improving Gear Marking 
in the U.S. West Coast Sablefish Pot Fishery,'' as well as reports from 
its advisory bodies and public testimony. In spring 2026, NMFS 
considered public comments on the proposed rule and advice from the 
Council's enforcement consultants in developing this final rule.

The Needs and Scope of This Rulemaking

    Marine animals, like whales and turtles, can get caught in fishing 
gear, which can prevent them from eating or cause serious injury or 
death. The animal most often entangled in gear associated with the 
Pacific Coast groundfish fishery is the humpback whale.
    This rulemaking removes the regulatory requirement to use surface 
gear at both ends of groundfish pot and bottom longline gear sets, 
allowing vessels to reduce the number of vertical lines in the water. 
This voluntary measure is expected to reduce the

[[Page 64792]]

likelihood of entanglements. This rulemaking is also expected to help 
NMFS track the source of entanglements by requiring gear-specific 
marking on two types of commercial ``fixed gear'' used for catching 
groundfish off the coasts of Washington, Oregon, and California: (1) 
pot gear; and (2) bottom longline gear.
    Groundfish pot gear (both traditional and slinky pot) uses a series 
of pots connected by a rope (groundline) on the seafloor. A vertical 
line and a surface buoy mark the gear's location. Pot gear may sit 
unattended for 1.5 to up to 7 days, generally a longer period than for 
bottom longline gear (2 to 48 hours). Because pot gear is heavier than 
bottom longline gear, it may pose a higher risk of serious injury if an 
animal becomes entangled.
    Bottom longline gear is a horizontal rope (or groundline) with many 
hooks that lies along the bottom of the sea floor, also marked by 
vertical lines and surface buoys. It soaks for a shorter time, usually 
2 to 48 hours, but may sit unattended for up to 7 days. Groundline for 
bottom longline gear poses entanglement risk when marine mammals 
attempt to eat hooked fish.
    Entanglement can happen with these fixed gears on the surface with 
surface lines or surface buoys/markers, in the water column with 
vertical lines, or at/near the seafloor with the groundline or pots/
hooks. Entanglements can also happen with any component as the gear is 
being hauled to the surface and retrieved. These pot and bottom 
longline groundfish gears, when fished in Federal waters, are the only 
gear types affected by this rulemaking. These two gear types, when used 
to harvest groundfish, are hereafter collectively referred to as the 
``subject gears'' or the ``subject gear types.'' This rulemaking does 
not change the requirement that the subject gear types be attended at 
sea at least every 7 days.

Gear Marking

    This rulemaking implements new requirements in the Pacific Coast 
groundfish fishery for marking all buoys and the top 20 fathoms (fm) 
(120 feet (ft) or 37 meters (m)) of the vertical line used with the 
subject gears. For the first 3 years after the initial effective date 
of these requirements, temporary marking methods may be used to satisfy 
the line marking requirements, and thereafter the top 20 fm (37 m) of 
vertical line must be line manufactured in the designated color 
schemes. NMFS is implementing two color-coded schemes for the new gear 
marking requirements for the subject gears, as recommended by the 
Council: one for groundfish pot gear and one for groundfish bottom 
longline gear.

New Requirements for Marking Buoys

    NMFS is implementing a new requirement for either gear-specific 
buoy tags to be attached to each surface buoy, or gear-specific marks 
to be made directly on each buoy. Tag and buoy markings are ``GP'' for 
pot and ``GL'' for bottom longline. Vessel identification information 
must be on at least one buoy, per current regulations, but is also 
required by this rulemaking to be marked on all other buoys used for 
the subject gear types, either on the tag or directly on the buoy. 
Markings are required on every buoy because it is possible that not all 
buoys would remain connected if the surface gear were to become 
entangled.
    Each tag must have a physical indentation and a contrasting color. 
Buoy tags on every surface buoy must be in a gear-specific color (table 
1), with a gear-specific letter combination legibly marked on it. Tags 
must be attached to fishing gear when deployed and must be on board the 
vessel on a trip where groundfish pot or longline gear will be or has 
been used to take and retain groundfish.
    Gear-specific marks directly on buoys must be in capital letters at 
least 3 inches (7.7 cm) in height and must be present at all times on a 
trip in which the vessel is taking and retaining groundfish with the 
subject gears, if gear marking tags are not on board.
    In the proposed rule, NMFS proposed that the new buoy marking 
requirements would require tags attached to each and every surface buoy 
with a gear-specific color and engraved mark: capital letters ``P'' for 
pots and ``L'' for bottom longline. Buoy tags were proposed because 
they can be swapped out, which would allow a surface gear set up, 
including the large buoys used with groundfish gear, to be used to 
float different gear types. In this final rule, NMFS is requiring that 
pot and bottom longline gear buoys are marked either by using buoy 
tags, as proposed, or through gear-specific marks directly on the buoys 
in response to public comments.
    As described below in the Comments and Responses section, one 
fisherman submitted comments on the proposed rule asking that vessel 
operators be allowed the option for permanent fishery and gear-specific 
marks on the buoys that are used to mark surface gear, in lieu of buoy 
tags, because this option would be more efficient for certain operators 
and require less maintenance than the proposed gear-specific buoy tags. 
The State of California requires lobster pots to have buoys marked with 
a ``P.'' NMFS considered the request in development of this final rule, 
and this final rule implements the option to allow for buoy marking 
using tags or directly on buoys. This final rule also includes a 
requirement that the buoy marking include a fishery-specific mark as 
well as a gear-specific mark. These fishery and gear-specific markings 
must be ``GP'' for pot and ``GL'' for longline. See Changes from the 
Proposed Rule for additional details.
    NMFS notes that current regulations require that all fixed 
groundfish gears, including the subject gears, have at least one buoy 
with the vessel identification legibly marked on it, per current 
regulations at 50 CFR 660.219 and 660.319. This requirement is not 
revised with this rulemaking.

New Requirements for Marking Lines

    This rulemaking requires two-color, gear-specific color schemes for 
marking the top 20 fm (37 m) of each vertical line deployed in the 
commercial, non-tribal, limited entry and directed open access 
groundfish fishery for vessels using pot or bottom longline gears. 
Lines must be marked in the specified colors by the manufacturer, with 
strands in each color, except that temporary methods of marking (with 
the same color scheme) is allowed for 3 years after the effective date 
of the final rule.

[[Page 64793]]

    With this rulemaking, NMFS requires that at least the top 20 fm (37 
m) of vertical line be continuously marked. Manufactured line must 
consist primarily of only the two gear-specific colors A and B 
specified in table 1. Both colors must be used. NMFS requires that at 
least one-third of the strands in the rope be one color and all the 
other strands be the other color required in the gear-specific two-
color scheme. Presence of a third color, other than white, is 
prohibited in the top 20 fm (37 m) to reduce the likelihood of mistaken 
gear identification for gear that is observed entangled on marine 
animals. NMFS acknowledges that some types of manufactured line may 
have an inner core color that is necessary and shows through on the 
outer surface of the line (i.e., a tracer). This core is usually white. 
Manufactured line can be ordered by the coil; a standard coil has a 
line length of 1,200 ft (365.8 m) and costs approximately $200-$500, 
with custom colors available at no additional cost. Some manufacturers 
offer different size coils. Manufactured line may be used to satisfy 
the marking requirements for groundfish pot and bottom longline gears 
immediately upon the effective date of a final rule.
    NMFS expects that the most efficient option for complying with new 
line marking requirements will be manufactured line in the specified 
color schemes. Manufactured line is expected to have the greatest 
longevity. However, some operators may have difficulties in initially 
procuring manufactured line. Thus, to afford fishery participants 
sufficient flexibility, and to achieve timely compliance with the line 
marking requirements in this final rule, this rulemaking includes the 
allowance to use temporary marking of existing lines for 3 years after 
this rule's effective date. Any temporary marking scheme requires 
continuous, alternating bands of the color schemes for each gear type 
on at least the top 20 fm (37 m) of every vertical line. Alternating 
marks must consist of colored rope sections that result in a 
continuous, alternating two-color scheme for each groundfish fixed gear 
type, as detailed in table 1 above. Each colored band for temporary 
marks is required to be greater than 18 inches and up to 28 inches (46-
71 centimeters (cm)) of linear rope length. Presence of any color rope, 
besides white, other than the color scheme shown in table 1 in the top 
20 fm (37 m) of the vertical line is prohibited for vessels fishing for 
groundfish with the subject gear types. In order to give fishery 
operators flexibility in complying with this requirement, NMFS is not 
specifying a method or material for applying temporary marks in the 
first 3 years after this final rule is effective. In the development of 
this action, the Council discussed multiple ways to temporarily achieve 
the required marking scheme for vertical lines, such as paint (sprayed 
or dipped) or colored tape. NMFS expects temporary marks will have a 
lower immediate materials cost than manufactured line but may require 
more time for maintenance. NMFS is implementing this flexibility in 
this rulemaking, as recommended by the Council.

Summary of Gear Marking Requirements

    The gear marking requirements implemented through this final rule 
apply to vessels fishing in the limited entry or directed open access 
groundfish fisheries with bottom longline or pot gear. This final rule 
requires vessels using the subject fishing gears to comply with the 
following gear-specific marking schemes (summarized in table 2).
    All buoys deployed on limited entry or directed open access 
groundfish pot surface gear must have either: (1) gear-specific orange 
tags attached with capital letters ``GP'' engraved on both sides and 
the letters must be in a contrasting color; or (2) capital letters 
``GP'' permanently marked directly on the buoy in a contrasting color 
and at least 3 inches (7.7 cm) in height. The vessel identification 
number must be included on all buoy tags or directly on all buoys. All 
markings must be maintained to be legible on an ongoing basis.
    All buoys deployed on limited entry or directed open access 
groundfish bottom longline surface gear must have either: (1) gear-
specific brown tags attached with capital letters ``GL'' engraved on 
both sides and the letters must be in a contrasting color; or (2) 
capital letters ``GL'' permanently marked directly on the buoy in a 
contrasting color and at least 3 inches (7.7 cm) in height. The vessel 
identification number must be included on all buoy tags or directly on 
all buoys. All markings must be maintained to be legible on an ongoing 
basis.
    At least the top 20 fm (37 m) of vertical line used to deploy 
bottom longline or pot gear in the limited entry or directed open 
access fishery must be continuously marked in the appropriate gear-
specific color scheme. Bottom longline vertical lines are required to 
be continuously marked in brown and blue. Pot gear vertical lines are 
required to be continuously marked in orange and blue. Both colors must 
be used; manufactured lines must be at least one-third of strands in 
one color and all remaining strands in the other color, with any other 
color besides white prohibited in the top 20 fm (37 m). Temporarily 
marked lines are required to be gear-specific, two-color alternating, 
in continuous bands between 18 and 28 inches (46 and 71 cm) each for a 
minimum of 20 fm (37 m) at the top of every vertical line.
    NMFS is requiring that manufactured line, and only manufactured 
line, be used to mark vertical lines for the subject gears, beginning 
January 11, 2030. After that date, temporary markings will no longer 
satisfy line marking requirements.

Entanglement Risk Reduction

    This section describes voluntary and required management measures 
that are being implemented with the goal of reducing the risk of 
entanglement of protected species and bycatch in the subject gear 
types. These measures affect the same groups of fishermen subject to 
gear marking requirements described above that use bottom longline or 
pot gear in directed groundfish fisheries off the West Coast.
    Fishing lines on or near the surface generally pose a higher risk 
of entanglement compared to fishing gear at deeper depths or on the 
bottom of the sea floor. Therefore, the measures implemented through 
this rulemaking are designed to reduce the amount of vertical lines 
required in the fishery and reduce the amount of surface line allowed 
for each gear set. NMFS expects these measures will reduce the risk of 
entanglement with surface and vertical lines in Pacific Coast 
groundfish pot and bottom longline gears.

Single Line Allowance for Gear Identification at the Surface

    As noted above, vertical lines and surface gear both pose a risk of 
entanglement to protected species. Therefore, allowing vessels to 
reduce the amount of surface gear and the number of lines in the water 
may reduce risk of entanglement. This rulemaking removes the 
requirement that pot and bottom longline gear be marked on the surface 
at both terminal ends (i.e., creates a single surface gear and vertical 
line allowance). This voluntary measure allows vessel operators to use 
a vertical line and surface gear at one or both ends of their gear, at 
their own discretion. Gear would still be required to be marked at the 
surface on at least one terminal end in a manner consistent with the 
current gear identification and marking requirements. This single 
surface gear and vertical line allowance and the manner in which the 
subject gears must be marked at the surface are articulated in revised 
regulations at 50 CFR 660.219 and 660.319.

Limiting Surface Line

    Surface gear consists of the buoys and flags associated with a gear 
set and the surface line between them. Surface gear marks the location 
of the terminal end(s) of the groundline anchoring the strings of pots 
and bottom longline gear set on the ocean floor and usually serves as 
the primary mechanism for identifying and retrieving gear. Surface 
lines need to have enough slack to adjust to changes in ocean 
conditions that may occur while gear remains in the water so that the 
buoys are not submerged completely.
    This final rule restricts the maximum length of surface line, 
measured from the last trailing buoy (or the highflyer) to the first 
buoy (main buoy) closest to the vertical line. Specifically, surface 
line used for the subject gears may not exceed 60 ft (10 fm (18 m)) in 
length, measured from the main buoy to the last trailing buoy. By 
prohibiting excess surface line, this rulemaking intends to reduce the 
risk of entanglement and the bycatch of protected species in the 
groundfish fishery with the subject gears. To enact this restriction, 
new definitions of surface line, vertical line, and main buoy are added 
to the definition of ``Fishing gear'' for the subject gear types at 50 
CFR 660.11.

Additional Regulation Changes

    Beyond the gear marking and risk reduction measures described 
above, this rulemaking also includes two additional changes (see the 
Proposed Rule for additional context and rationale for these changes). 
Current regulations require that pot and trap gears are fitted with an 
escape panel that biodegrades to create an opening of specified 
dimensions so that the pot does not perpetually entrap animals if the 
gear is lost (660.230(b) and 660.330(b)). Current regulations provide 
no specificity about the appropriate placement of the escape panel. 
This rulemaking prohibits escape panels from being positioned on the 
bottom of a pot, similar to regulations for fishing off the coast of 
Alaska and includes an exception from this requirement for collapsible 
pots in order to accommodate differences in gear

[[Page 64795]]

configurations. To facilitate this collapsible pot exception, a 
definition of ``collapsible pot'' is added to the definition of 
``Fishing gear'' at 50 CFR 660.11.
    Additionally, this rulemaking changes the maximum twine size for 
the cotton biodegradable twine used for closing escape panels for pots 
used in the Pacific Coast groundfish fishery. Specifically, this 
rulemaking revises the maximum untreated cotton twine size for closing 
escape panels from ``not to exceed number 21'' to ``not to exceed 
number 30.'' This modification aligns the maximum twine size for 
groundfish pot gear used in the Pacific Coast groundfish fishery with 
the twine size required for vessels fishing off the coast of Alaska 
(i.e., the North Pacific).
    NMFS is making technical changes to 50 CFR part 660 to revise 
references in the relevant regulations to longline and bottom longline 
gears to clarify key gear terminology, so that it is clear what type of 
longline gear is affected by this rulemaking. This rulemaking replaces 
``longline'' with ``bottom longline'' in the definition of ``fixed 
gear'' at 50 CFR 660.11, and throughout limited entry and open access 
commercial groundfish regulations, where applicable.
    This rulemaking also amends the current prohibition at 50 CFR 
660.12(a)(2) about falsifying or failing to affix or maintain gear 
markings required under the regulations by adding the word 
``identifications'' to revise the text to align with the current 
section titles for the regulations referenced in the prohibition (50 
CFR 660.219 and 660.319), which are, respectively ``fixed gear 
identification and marking'' and ``open access fishery gear 
identification and marking.''

Comments and Responses

    The proposed rule was published on February 6, 2026 (91 FR 5408), 
and the public comment period ended on March 9, 2026. NMFS received 
comment letters from 28 individuals or organizations. The following 
themes were raised across individual submissions. NMFS received comment 
letters from five commenters expressing general opposition to the 
proposed measures in this rulemaking, of which three letters 
specifically described concerns with gear loss related to the measure 
that would remove the requirement that groundfish bottom longline or 
pot gear sets be marked with vertical lines and surface gear at both 
terminal ends (referred to henceforth as ``the single-line 
allowance''). Of the five comment letters generally in opposition to 
the rule, three of those letters expressed general support for the gear 
marking and surface line length restrictions in this rule and their 
objective to indirectly conserve marine mammals. However, commenters 
also expressed concern with the single-line allowance, asserting that 
the allowance would increase the risk of gear loss. NMFS received 13 
comment letters that were generally in support of this rulemaking. 
Seven of these letters supported the bycatch reduction measures in this 
rulemaking but also expressed support for additional measures to 
conserve marine animals, above and beyond those proposed in this 
rulemaking. Of the 28 comment letters, 1 letter included comments 
regarding the PRA collection, which is responded to below and in the 
PRA supporting statement. Overall, most comments generally expressed 
support for this rulemaking.
    NMFS appreciates the thoughtful comments on the proposed rule and 
has considered them thoroughly. NMFS collated the comments in the 28 
letters, and is responding to 20 subject areas included across the 
unique comment letters below.
    Comment 1: Two commenters opposed this rulemaking, describing it as 
unnecessary, and asserting that entanglements of marine animals in 
fishing gear are rare and are not a consequential conservation issue.
    Response: Entanglements involving commercial fishing gear are 
estimated based on best scientific information available, and the gears 
that are the subject of this rulemaking have had multiple documented 
entanglements with marine animals. NMFS acknowledges that these 
observed and otherwise documented entanglements in groundfish fixed 
gear are relatively rare; however, documented entanglements of marine 
animals in fishing gear of unknown origin is not infrequent and should 
also be considered. The new requirements in this rule balance the need 
for reasonable steps forward toward reducing the risk of entanglements 
with the subject gears and better attributing entanglements in general 
to the gear-type, with a small additional cost and operational burden 
on fishermen.
    Comment 2: Two commenters express concern with over-regulation of 
fisheries and assert that the measures in this rulemaking are 
unnecessarily burdensome to the regulated fishermen. One of these 
commenters also states that this rule will increase gear expenses and 
wear and tear on gear, and that fishermen should be financially 
compensated for the burden of complying with the measures implemented 
through this rulemaking.
    Response: Gear marking measures implemented by this rulemaking are 
expected to increase the likelihood that NMFS can rule out the 
groundfish fixed gear fishery as the source of documented 
entanglements, thus reducing the need for further regulation in the 
future. However, NMFS acknowledges that the approximately 335 vessels 
that currently fish groundfish with bottom longline or pot gears will 
be required to incur costs over the next 3 years to comply with the 
gear marking measures in this rulemaking. NMFS is obligated under the 
PRA to annualize burden hours and costs over the initial 3-year period 
of an approved information collection (gear marking requirements are 
considered an information collection). Since it is unlikely that any 
vessel would need to purchase gear compliant with new gear marking 
regulations more than once in this 3-year period, annualized materials 
costs are one-third of the estimated total materials cost. The average 
cost burden for compliance with new gear marking regulations in this 
rulemaking is estimated to be $809 per respondent (e.g., vessel 
operator) to temporarily mark and replace their gear once during the 
three-year period preceding the compliance date for permanently marking 
fixed gear using manufactured line. This total accounts for the costs 
of gear-specific marked line, gear-specific marked buoy tags, and paint 
or markers. NMFS understands from fishery representatives that lines 
used in the fishery need to be replaced once every 5-10 years, with the 
actual lifetime of the gear depending on the intensity of use, method 
of storage, and other variables. To reduce the main source of costs 
associated with this rulemaking (i.e., gear-specific manufactured 
marked line in a specified color scheme), line marking is only required 
for the top 20 fm (37 m) of the vertical line (allowing more sets of 
gear to be marked per spool of line purchased) and a 3-year transition 
period is included to allow more cost-effective temporary marking 
schemes to be used initially in lieu of manufactured line. The goal of 
this transition period is to allow current vertical lines used in the 
fishery to be phased out and replaced towards the end of their lifespan 
with gear-specific manufactured marked lines, avoiding unnecessary 
waste and defraying costs. This rulemaking is not expected to increase 
wear and tear on fishing gear beyond what usual use would do. In 
conclusion, NMFS finds that the cost burden on the regulated community 
imposed by this rule does not outweigh the conservation benefits from 
this rule.

[[Page 64796]]

Therefore, NMFS is implementing the proposed gear marking requirements 
in this rulemaking.
    Comment 3: One commenter expressed concern that oil spills and

    Response: NMFS does not discount the potential adverse impacts of 
other environmental conditions, such as oil spills and marine debris, 
which pose threats to marine animals. However, this action is taken 
under the authority of the Magnuson-Stevens Act and, as such, its 
regulatory scope is limited to the effects of federally managed 
fisheries on marine animals. The primary risk these fisheries pose to

    Comment 4: Two commenters state that the amount of fishing gear 
used in the West Coast EEZ could or will increase in the future, which 
could subsequently increase the risk of entanglement in the area. One 
of these commenters expressed concern that this rulemaking does nothing 
to mitigate this risk.
    Response: NMFS acknowledges that there are no current restrictions 
on the total amount of gear sets or number of vertical lines that may 
be deployed annually in the Pacific Coast groundfish fixed gear 
fisheries, nor were any proposed at the proposed rule stage. However, a 
variety of facets of the groundfish fishery management regime coincide 
to keep the amount of fishing gear deployed in the EEZ annually 
relatively stable through time. For example, the groundfish sector with 
the majority of the fixed gear fishing effort, both in harvest volume 
and quantity of gear deployed (i.e., the limited entry fixed gear 
(LEFG) sector), is Federally permitted through a limited entry program. 
These federal permits are transferable, but limit vessel size (and 
therefore limit harvest capacity), and are of a limited quantity (no 
new permits have been issued for over 20 years). This closed class 
limits the amount of gear that can be fished at any one time or in any 
season. Participants in the directed open access fishery may use any 
legal gear type, including pots and bottom longline gears, but are 
incentivized to use non-bottom contact gears (i.e., gear that is not 
left unattended) because those gears are allowed to be fished in areas 
that are closed to bottom longline and pot gears. Most vessels fishing 
with bottom longline and pot gear in the open access fishery are 
targeting sablefish in deeper waters outside gear-specific area 
restrictions. The amount of overall effort to harvest sablefish in the 
open access fishery is also limited by the sector's annual allocation 
and through inseason trip limit adjustments to keep harvest within that 
allocation. Meaning, if more vessels fish sablefish, trip limits for 
these vessels decrease to keep catch of sablefish within the annual 
sector allocation, which reduces the incentives for new entrants to 
join the fishery. In this manner, the number of vessels targeting 
sablefish in the open access fishery (regardless of gear type) is self-
mitigating. Thus, all other things being equal, the single-line 
allowance established in this rulemaking is expected to decrease the 
total number of vertical lines that would otherwise be fished annually 
in the EEZ than without it. In addition, the single-line allowance 
creates the opportunity within the fishery for fishermen to test on-
demand buoy lines and systems (i.e., ``pop-up gear''), if so desired, 
without an exempted fishing permit, and using their otherwise 
conventional and legal pot and bottom longline gear configurations.
    Comment 5: Five commenters expressed concern for gear loss if

single-line allowance during early scoping of this rulemaking. During 
scoping, concerns were expressed on multiple fronts. For example, 
industry representatives were concerned that relatively lighter-weight 
gear types (such as slinky pots and longline gears) would be much more 
likely to be lost if only a single-line were used, either voluntarily

a non-mandatory single-line would be ineffectual to reduce the risk of 
entanglement. All comments were considered by the Council, and the 
Council ultimately recommended a voluntary single-line allowance for 
the subject gear types. NMFS has determined that the Council-
recommended single-line allowance is appropriate because it will 
provide the flexibility to allow vessels to mark only one end of their 
pot or bottom longline gear if fishermen consider ocean conditions 
exist that are likely to enable full retrieval of the gear using a 
single vertical line. Some vessels may choose to use heavier single 
lines to increase the likelihood of preventing their gear from parting 
during retrieval. Other fishermen may choose to continue to use current 
gear configurations, with lines and surface gear at both ends, despite 
the rule change. Regardless, NMFS is confident that fishermen are both 
strongly disincentivized from losing their gear and from deploying 
extraneous gear, and will make their future gear use decisions 
accordingly. NMFS has heard testimony from multiple fishermen that they 
are likely to utilize the single-line allowance, which will reduce the 
number of vertical lines in the water. For these reasons, NMFS 
concludes that the voluntary single-line allowance appropriately 
balances the need to reduce the risk of entanglement in fishing gear 
against the need to prevent gear loss and subsequent ghost fishing.
    Comment 6: Two commenters expressed concern with the limitation on

try to reduce the risk of entanglements. In developing this measure, 
the Council heard from fishing industry representatives that, in their 
experience, 10 fm (18.3 m) or less of surface line would be an adequate 
length to prevent the gear from being submerged in most ocean 
conditions. NMFS acknowledges that allowing any fishing with fixed 
gears poses a greater than zero risk of entanglement,

[[Page 64797]]

regardless of the length of surface line. The goal of this rulemaking 
is to reduce the risk of entanglement. NMFS has determined that the 
surface line limitation of 10 fm (18.3 m) is the appropriate length for 
the surface line limitation to balance the need to reduce the risk of 
entanglement, and thus minimize the bycatch of marine animals with the 
subject gears, against the threat of gear submergence and loss.

mandatory single-line) at the surface was considered, but not 
recommended, by the Council. See also Response to Comment 5 and 
Response to Comment 7, which are incorporated here. Groundfish are 
harvested at a variety of depths, but sablefish are abundant and 
commonly harvested with the subject gear types at depths of 600 fm

conditions persist. Gear that is not successfully retrieved can 
continue to harvest fish (i.e., ghost fishing) and pose a continued 
risk of entanglement. Gear requirements that are reasonably expected to 
increase gear loss are not consistent with the Magnuson-Stevens Act 
mandates regarding minimizing bycatch to the extent practicable, and, 
as such, the Council did not recommend that NMFS adopt a mandatory 
single-line requirement. For these reasons, and the reasons stated in 
Response to Comment 5 and Response to Comment 7, NMFS has determined 
that the voluntary single-line allowance fishery management measure to 
minimize bycatch is consistent with the Magnuson-Stevens Act and other 
applicable laws, including the ESA and MMPA.

    Comment 15: One commenter asked NMFS to provide guidance with 
regards to line marking requirements, including temporary marking. They 
asked how to account for gaps, knots, wear, or the use of non-solid 
``tracer'' lines. Also, they requested further guidance on acceptable

    Response: NMFS agrees that steps should be taken to reduce 
compliance uncertainty and enforcement inconsistency. NMFS offers the 
following clarifications to facilitate consistent enforcement of gear 
marking requirements promulgated in this rulemaking, in addition to 
forthcoming informational guides.
    The gear marking requirements implemented through this final rule 
apply to vessels fishing groundfish in the commercial, non-tribal, 
limited entry or directed open access fisheries using bottom longline 
or pot gear.
    With regards to buoy marking, before leaving port on a trip where 
gear marking requirements apply, the vessel is required to have either 
enough buoy tags of the required color and marking to mark all 
applicable gear(s) on board the vessel, or all buoys must have 
compliant gear-specific markings directly on the buoys. Every buoy used 
to mark bottom longline or pot gear must have legible vessel 
identification, directly marked on the surface of at least one buoy on 
every set, and either directly on all other buoys or on marked tags 
ready to be attached for all other buoys. Buoys on board that are 
clearly marked for fishing in non-groundfish fisheries (e.g., pot gears 
marked for fishing spiny lobster) are not subject to groundfish gear 
marking requirements implemented in this rulemaking.
    With regards to line marking, before leaving port on a trip where 
gear marking requirements apply, the vessel is required to have a 
sufficient length of marked line to mark at least the top 20 fm (37 m) 
of all vertical lines to be deployed on each set. Line is considered 
``marked'' when it is manufactured in the applicable two-color scheme 
found in table 1, with at least one-third of strands being in one color 
and the remainder being in the other color. Presence of a third color, 
besides white, is prohibited in the top 20 fm (37 m) to reduce the 
likelihood of mistaken gear identification for gear that is observed 
entangled on marine animals. NMFS acknowledges that some types of 
manufactured line may have an un-customizable inner core color that 
shows through on the outer surface of the line (i.e., a tracer). This 
core is usually white. Tracers that are not solid white will be found 
in non-compliance with line marking requirements. Until January 11, 
2030, line is considered ``marked'' when original line is temporarily 
marked continuously in the applicable two-color scheme found in table 
1, with alternating bands of color, for at least the top 20 fm (37 m) 
of every vertical line deployed with the subject gears. In the context 
of temporary line marking, ``continuous'' means marking is 
uninterrupted two-color (table 1) alternating bands of markings in the 
top 20 fm (37 m) of line, with the exception that the original line may 
be partially visible for less than 6 inches and not at all visible in 
the full circumference of the temporarily marked portion of the line. 
Markings that are partially worn (i.e., discolored or missing such that 
original line color shows through) on one or more sides of the line for 
a distance greater than 6 inches (15.2 cm) are not ``continuous'' and 
do not satisfy line marking requirements. Additionally, any amount of 
original line showing in the full circumference of the line is not 
``continuous'' and

[[Page 64799]]

therefore does not satisfy line marking requirements. NMFS does not 
prescribe temporary marking methods, only the outcome: 20 fm (37 m) of 
continuous marking in the required length intervals and color scheme.

expense for most vessel operators and fishing businesses. It is likely 
that they would commonly budget to replace line on a small proportion 
of their gear each year and on an as needed basis. As discussed at the 
Gear Marking Workshop in 2022, the larger proportion of gear that is 
required to be marked, the greater the costs to the industry to comply. 
Industry representatives voiced concerns that the initial costs to 
replace all gear at once could be very expensive and asked for

    As NMFS stated in the 2022 Gear Marking Workshop, based on current 
entanglement information, line marking on the upper portion of the gear 
would be most helpful at identifying the source of entanglements. This 
is because most entanglements involve buoys (i.e., upper or surface 
portion of fishing gear) or portions of line identified to be from the 
upper portion of fishing gear. The Council considered various marking 
distances, including marking surface lines and marking more and less

Council did not recommend gear-specific surface line markings for the 
same reasons they did not recommend gear-specific markings on buoys: 
surface lines are commonly used to float different gear types or in 
different fisheries. The Council recommended the intermediate 
alternative for the distance of vertical line marking, the top 20 fm

documented in entanglements, and usually the amount of line in an 
entanglement is only 6 to 20 ft (2 to 6 m). The Council also 
recommended this distance because the average-sized groundfish pot 
fishing operation could mark all their groundfish pot gear (2 to 5 
sets) by purchasing a single coil of line for approximately $500, while 
larger operations may only need to buy two coils of line for 
approximately $1,000. For these reasons, and for reasons described 
above in Response to Comment 13, NMFS has determined that the measures 
implemented in this rulemaking minimize bycatch to the extent 
practicable and are reasonably expected to improve accountability in 
the groundfish pot and bottom longline fisheries, if entanglements 
occur in the future, while balancing costs to industry.
    Comment 17: Two commenters state that the measures in this

Magnuson-Stevens Act, is consistent with NMFS' obligations under the 
Magnuson-Stevens Act, ESA, MMPA, and other laws. As discussed above, 
minimizing bycatch to the extent practicable is a necessary part of 
NMFS' fisheries management. Given the removal of the regulatory 
requirement to mark gear sets on the surface at both terminal ends 
(i.e., voluntary single-line allowance), NMFS expects this rulemaking 
to reduce the number of vertical lines used to identify pot gear in

minimizing bycatch from entanglements with active fishing gear against 
the flexibility fishermen need to keep bycatch from ghost fishing gear 
low. The surface line length limitation balances minimizing excess 
surface line in the water against the flexibility fishermen need to set 
gear that can withstand changing ocean conditions and tides, without 
submergence. Thus, these entanglement risk reduction measures minimize 
bycatch to the extent practicable, consistent with legal requirements, 
including the National Standards of the Magnuson-Stevens Act.

trawl gear, including the subject gears, under gear switching 
provisions in the Shorebased Individual Fishing Quota (IFQ) Program 
(historically quota that was harvested with bottom trawl gear). This 
IFQ fishing has been observed at much higher rates (approximately 100 
percent) than in years prior. From 2011 through 2025, no entanglements 
were observed in pot or bottom longline gear in this sector. However, 
during that same period, 4 of the other 6 documented entanglements in 
groundfish fixed gear were observed (2014, 2016, 2023, and 2025) in 
non-IFQ sectors of the groundfish fishery. There have also been initial 
reports of entanglements in groundfish fixed gear during the 2026 
fishing season. For example, on July 7, 2026, a deceased humpback whale 
was observed entangled in groundfish pot gear. Additionally, on July 
23, 2026, there was a report of a whale entanglement associated with 
multiple gear types off the coast of Oregon, including sablefish pot 
gear. An investigation into the circumstances of these entanglements is 
ongoing by NMFS scientists and entanglement experts. Further 
information on the entanglements, and potential implications for future 
action(s) to address the mortality and serious injury of humpback 
whales in the sablefish pot fishery, will be discussed by the West 
Coast Take Reduction Team (TRT). The TRT web page is available at 
www.fisheries.noaa.gov by searching for ``west coast take reduction 
team.''
    To date, no entanglement cases have been clearly identified as 
involving a mobile, free-swimming entanglement involving sablefish pot 
fishing gear. Since the 2012 biological opinion, NMFS has completed 2 
additional consultations and biological opinions addressing humpback 
whale bycatch in the sablefish pot fishery, including consideration of 
reported entanglements that have occurred since 2012. Additionally, 
NMFS has modified the bycatch estimation approach since 2012 to better 
deal with relatively rare events like humpback whale entanglements in 
the groundfish fishery and limited observer coverage. NMFS has adopted 
and utilized methodologies for extrapolating observed entanglements to 
inform fleet-wide estimates in acknowledgement of limited observer 
coverage since 2020. Given the weight of updated information, the 
statement from the 2012 biological opinion is not representative of 
NMFS' current perspective. NMFS finds that it is unlikely that a whale 
that becomes entangled in groundfish pot gear will move the gear away 
from its original location due to the gear's weight and its propensity 
to anchor whales in place when entanglements have been documented in 
the past, and that there is an extremely low amount of gear loss 
documented in the fishery. If mobile entanglements occurred, rates of 
gear loss would be higher. Line marking requirements in this rulemaking 
will allow NMFS to ground truth this hypothesis.

Changes From the Proposed Rule

Additional Buoy Marking Method

    Based on public comments, NMFS is adding an additional method for 
complying with the requirement for gear-specific marks on buoys. 
Instead of buoy tags being the only method of compliance for the gear-
specific buoy marking required, as proposed in the proposed rule, the 
final rule adds the option for gear-specific marks directly on buoys. 
This will allow vessel operators that use dedicated, gear-specific 
surface gear to float either pot or bottom longline gear, exclusively, 
to permanently and directly mark all buoys. As described in the 
proposed rule, this method of marking was considered by the Council, 
but was not recommended because some fishermen testified that they 
often used surface lines and buoys to float multiple gear types within 
a fishing vessel's portfolio of target species. For those fishermen, 
marking directly on buoys was disfavored because it would force the 
purchase and storage of extra surface gear. However, the Council did 
not consider allowing multiple methods of buoy marking during 
development of its recommendation. NMFS finds that expanding the 
allowable methods for compliance with the gear-specific buoy marking 
requirements in this rule is appropriate and consistent with the 
Council's recommendation. Adding this method of marking buoys to the 
final rule provides operational efficiencies to the fleet, while still 
achieving the intended goal of gear-specific markings on all pot and 
bottom longline buoys. As such, this change to the final rule is 
consistent with NMFS' original purpose for this action and considered a 
logical outgrowth of the proposed rule. The change neither raises new 
issues not addressed in, nor represents a substantial departure from 
the proposed rule. For these reasons, NMFS is revising regulatory text 
at 50 CFR 660.219(b)(1) and 660.319(b)(1) to add an additional method 
of complying with this rule's buoy marking requirements by marking 
directly on the buoys, using capital letters ``GL'' for bottom longline 
buoys and ``GP'' for pot buoys.
    Additionally, NMFS is making technical revisions to these 
regulations providing that all buoy markings, whether directly on the 
buoy or via buoy tag, use the capital letters ``GL'' for bottom 
longline buoys/tags and ``GP'' for pot buoys/tags, rather than the 
``P'' and ``L'' markings provided in the proposed rule. These technical 
edits are intended to ensure the fishery-specific and gear-specific 
buoy marks required for the groundfish fishery under this

[[Page 64801]]

rule are not too similar to buoy markings currently required in other 
non-groundfish fisheries, including those managed by the State of 
California. NMFS' revisions to Sec.  660.12(a)(9), as contained in the 
proposed rule, included gear-specific buoy marking tags as a required 
component of fishing gear (whether attached to the gear or onboard the 
vessel) that must be presented to an authorized officer for inspection 
upon request. In this final rule, gear-specific buoy marking tags are 
required only if buoys are not directly marked with permanent fishery 
and gear-specific markings. Therefore, this final rule adds ``when 
required'' to the buoy tag inspection requirement at Sec.  
660.12(a)(9).

Clarifying Edits to Temporary Line Marking Requirements

    Based on public comments, NMFS is adding clarifying text to 
regulations describing continuous temporary markings for vertical lines 
in the gear-specific color schemes (table 1). NMFS acknowledges that 
``continuous'' may be open for interpretation, and clarifies specific 
allowances for original line color showing in the portion of line 
required to be marked. Line marked in alternating bands of color 
according to the temporary line marking requirements at Sec.  
660.219(b)(2)(ii) and Sec.  660.319(b)(2)(ii) may have up to 6 inches 
of original line visible, and may not have any original line showing 
through in the full circumference in the portion of the line required 
to be marked. See also Response to Comment 15.

Clarifying Edits to Biodegradable Escape Panel Placement Requirements

    The proposed rule added specificity in the regulations regarding 
the required location of the opening of a pot's biodegradable escape 
panel. The goal of this regulatory change being to ensure that the 
opening is not on the bottom of the pot or too close to the bottom of 
the pot to be obstructed by silting or other benthic obstructions. The 
proposed language was identical to the rules for biodegradable escape 
panels used in pot fishing off the coast of Alaska. Based on public 
comments, and after further consultation with regional enforcement 
agents, NMFS is rewording the requirement at Sec.  660.230(b)(4) and 
Sec.  660.330 (b)(2)(iii) to simplify the escape panel language and 
make it slightly less prescriptive by removing the phrase ``parallel 
to,'' and to make the intent clearer by stating the escape panel 
opening must be positioned near but not on the bottom of the pot. The 
language revisions are consistent with NMFS' original purpose for this 
regulatory change and neither raise new issues not addressed in, nor 
represent a substantial departure from the proposed rule action. Thus, 
these revisions are considered a logical outgrowth of the proposed 
rule.
    Regulations at Sec.  660.219 are revised from the proposed rule to 
expand the existing gear marking and identification requirements that 
would have been applicable to only bottom longline and pot gears to 
apply to all fixed gears authorized for use in the limited entry non-
trawl fishery. This change revises the regulations, including language 
that was not proposed to change in the proposed rule, to bring these 
provisions into compliance with substantive regulatory amendments that 
were made in a separate but related rulemaking: the Limited Entry Fixed 
Gear (LEFG) Follow-On action, for which the final rule published on 
July 22, 2026 (91 FR 46000). The LEFG Follow-On action authorizes more 
types of fixed gear to be used in the limited entry non-trawl fishery 
(formerly limited entry fixed gear fishery). This means there are more 
types of fixed gear that need to have appropriate identification, per 
existing requirements in Sec.  660.219. These changes are isolated in 
paragraphs (a)(1) and (a)(2) of Sec.  660.219. The regulations at Sec.  
660.219 pertaining to the final gear marking regulation changes 
implemented by this rulemaking for bottom longline gear and pot gear at 
(a)(1) and (b) are not substantively changed from the proposed rule, 
other than the additional buoy marking method described above for pot 
and bottom longline types of fixed gear.

Surface Line Length Restrictions for the Limited Entry and Open Access 
Fisheries

    The proposed rule included restrictions on the length of surface 
lines that may be used for bottom longline and pot gear in the limited 
entry fishery at Sec.  660.230(b)(7). Since the proposed rule, the 
paragraph numbering in Sec.  660.230(b) has been revised through a 
separate rulemaking. Accordingly, the surface line length restriction 
for the limited entry fishery is now published, as proposed, at Sec.  
660.230(b)(6) rather than Sec.  660.230(b)(7). This technical 
correction is made simply to make the regulatory changes implemented 
through this final rule consistent with the new paragraph numbering in 
Sec.  660.230(b).
    Additionally, the proposed rule for this action mistakenly omitted 
the surface length restriction for the subject gears that is being 
implemented in this rulemaking in the open access fishery gear 
restrictions, which are at Sec.  660.330(b). As described in the 
preamble to the proposed rule, the intent of the surface line length 
restriction implemented in this rulemaking was that it would be 
applicable to all commercial fishing for groundfish on the West Coast 
using the subject gears, including vessels fishing for groundfish in 
the open access fishery. Accordingly, the proposed rule's omission of 
the surface line length restriction in the regulatory text at Sec.  
660.330(b) was a mistake and this final rule corrects that mistake by 
including the new surface line length restriction for vessels fishing 
with the subject gears in the open access fishery at Sec.  
660.330(b)(2)(v). This addition to the regulatory changes implemented 
through this final rule is consistent with NMFS' original purpose for 
this action and neither raises new issues not addressed in, nor 
represents a substantial departure from the purpose and intent for this 
action as described in the proposed rule. Therefore, this change is 
considered a logical outgrowth of the proposed rule.

    The NMFS Assistant Administrator finds that the requirement for a 
30-day delay in the date of effectiveness for select regulations in 
this rulemaking does not apply pursuant to 5 U.S.C. 553(d)(1) and (3) 
and makes the regulations implementing the voluntary single-line 
allowance effective upon publication of this final rule in the Federal 
Register.
    Pursuant to 5 U.S.C. 553(d)(1), this rule relieves a restriction on 
fishing because it creates additional flexibility in the amount and 
type of fishing gear required to be deployed for the subject gears by 
allowing fixed gear groundfish vessels to voluntarily mark only one 
terminal end of a pot or bottom longline gear set when a fisherman 
considers ocean conditions make doing so appropriate. This means that 
fishermen would be allowed to voluntarily decide to use surface gear 
(i.e., a pole, flag, light, radar reflector, and a buoy) and a vertical 
line at only one terminal end of a pot or bottom longline gear set. 
Fishery participants are currently restricted from deploying the 
subject gear sets without the sets being marked at the surface on both 
terminal ends (per current regulations at 50 CFR 660.219(a)(1) and 
660.319(a)(1)); thus, the voluntary single-line allowance provided for 
under this final rule will relieve that restriction and, pursuant to 5 
U.S.C. 553(d)(1), the 30-day delay in effective date requirement does 
not apply to the regulations implementing the single-line allowance.
    Additionally, pursuant to 5 U.S.C. 553(d)(3), the 30-day delay in 
effective date requirement does not apply to the single-line allowance 
in this final rule because a 30-day delay would be contrary to the 
public interest as it would delay entanglement risk reduction measures 
intended to provide conservation benefits to marine animals, 
particularly humpback whales, by reducing the number of vertical lines 
required to be deployed in the groundfish fishery during the 2026 
fishing year. This provision taking effect without a 30-day delay will 
benefit the public because it will provide additional flexibilities for 
fishermen during the 2026 summer harvest of sablefish, which would 
likely reduce the number of vertical lines deployed, and thus reduce 
the likelihood of humpback whale and other entanglements, particularly 
over the summer months when whale presence is highest and overlaps with 
fishing using the subject gears. As described above in the response to 
comments, during summer 2026, there have been multiple reported 
entanglements where initial reports indicate the presence of groundfish 
gear, including pot gear. Therefore, NMFS feels that it is in the 
public's interest to promptly implement the single-line allowance to 
provide fishermen with the flexibility to use only one set of surface 
gear and vertical lines with the subject gears, and thus to reduce the 
risk of entanglement, accordingly.
    Additionally, NMFS finds it is in the public interest to make the 
single-line allowance effective upon publication of this rule in the 
Federal Register because the gear flexibility created by the single-
line allowance is expected to increase efficiency within the fishery by 
reducing the amount of fishing gear required when using the subject 
gear types. The sooner fishery participants are allowed to use this 
flexibility during the 2026 season, the sooner the economic benefits of 
these efficiencies can be realized for fishery participants and fishing 
communities.
    Finally, a 30-day delay in effective date is not necessary to 
provide sufficient notice to the fishing community to adjust to the 
single-line allowance. One of the general policy purposes for allowing 
for a delay in a rule's effectiveness is to give the regulated 
community time to adjust their practices to come into compliance with 
the requirements of a new rule. The voluntary single-line allowance 
promulgated in this final rule creates no new compliance burdens for 
the fishing community affected by this rule. Instead, the rule creates 
increased operational flexibility on the part of fishery participants. 
Therefore, there are no policy reasons to delay the effectiveness of 
the single-line allowance.
    For the foregoing reasons, the 30-day delay in the effectiveness 
pursuant to 5 U.S.C. 553(d)(1) and (3) does not apply to the single-
line allowance established at 50 CFR 660.219(a)(1) and 660.319(a)(1) 
and these provisions will be effective immediately upon the publication 
of this rule in the Federal Register.

    A final regulatory flexibility analysis (FRFA) was prepared, as

ADDRESSES).
    For purposes of the RFA (5 U.S.C. 601 et seq.) only, NMFS has 
established a small business size standard for businesses, including 
their affiliates, whose primary industry is commercial fishing (see 50 
CFR 200.2). A business primarily engaged in commercial fishing is 
classified as a small business if it is independently owned and 
operated, is not dominant in its field of operation (including its 
affiliates), and has combined annual receipts not in excess of $11 
million for all its affiliated operations worldwide. This standard 
applies to all businesses classified under North American Industry 
Classification System (NAICS) code 11411 for commercial fishing, 
including all businesses classified as commercial finfish fishing 
(NAICS 114111), commercial shellfish fishing (NAICS 114112), and other 
commercial marine fishing (NAICS 114119) businesses (50 CFR 200.2; 13 
CFR 121.201).
    All commercial groundfish participants that use bottom longline or 
pot gears in the limited entry or directed open access fisheries in the 
EEZ off the West Coast, which are managed under the Groundfish FMP, may 
be affected by this rulemaking. The gear marking and entanglement risk 
reduction measures require the specified gear marking and surface line 
limitation described in this final rule for non-Tribal commercial 
vessels deploying either gear type. Examples of gears that are not 
affected by this rulemaking include select non-trawl gear types (i.e., 
troll gear and commercial vertical hook-and-line gear not anchored to 
the bottom, such as vertical jig gear or rod-and-reel gear with weights 
suspended off the bottom).
    This final rule is needed to increase NMFS' ability to attribute 
protected species entanglements to specific fisheries and ultimately 
aid in understanding and reducing the risk of protected species 
entanglement in groundfish pot and longline gear. The gear marking and 
entanglement risk reductions in this rule are expected to improve NMFS' 
ability to attribute the source of protected species entanglements. 
This rulemaking is also needed to improve the effectiveness of the 
currently required biodegradable

[[Page 64803]]

escape mechanisms in pot gear. Regulations describing in greater detail 
the location of escape panels in pot gear is expected to ensure 
efficacy.
    No public comments were received regarding the initial regulatory 
flexibility analysis and no significant issues were raised related to 
the analysis. Changes from the proposed rule are not expected to change 
the analyses and findings in the initial regulatory flexibility 
analysis. No comments were filed by the Chief Counsel for Advocacy of 
the Small Business Administration in response to the proposed rule.
    Given that this action would apply to the entirety of all entities 
fishing groundfish with bottom longline and pot gear and that the 
majority are considered small entities, this rule is expected to have 
an impact on a substantial number of small entities. All open access 
groundfish fishing vessels are assumed to be small entities, with ex-
vessel revenues for all landings (groundfish and non-groundfish) 
averaging $85,601 in 2023. From 2019-2023, between 72 and 152 vessels 
harvested groundfish in federal waters in the directed open access 
fishery using one or both of the subject gears. In 2023, 197 of the 223 
federal permit owners or holders (required to fish in the primary or 
limited entry fixed gear sectors) reported as small entities. For gear 
switching vessels, using non-trawl gear to harvest groundfish in the 
Shorebased IFQ Program, of the 10 that participated in 2023, all 
reported as small entities.
    Note that there is not a strict one-to-one correlation between 
vessels, permits, operators, or entities. Some persons or firms likely 
have ownership interests in more than one vessel or permit. Multiple 
permits may be registered for use with a single vessel. Given these 
factors, the actual number of entities regulated by this action could 
be lower than the preceding estimates.
    All vessels subject to this action would be subject to the new 
requirements to have lines marked in gear-specific colors (temporary 
markings allowed for interim period followed by requirements for 
manufactured lines) and buoys marked with gear specific tags or 
markings and vessel identification. These requirements are subject to 
the Paperwork Reduction Act (PRA). It is expected that commercial 
fishing vessel operators, or their employee(s) or other agent(s), would 
meet the requirement for line marking by marking the lines with 
temporary methods (e.g., paint or tape) in the transition period or 
ordering manufactured lines in the gear-specific color scheme. Vessels 
are only expected to acquire manufactured line in the required color 
scheme once during the three-year interim period, as fishing lines 
often last 6 years or more. The three-year interim period is intended 
to allow current vertical lines used in the fishery to be phased out 
and replaced towards the end of their lifespan to avoid unnecessary 
waste and costs. Additionally, after initial acquisition, replacement 
of manufactured line in the required color scheme is not expected to 
increase incremental costs, as compared to what routine gear 
replacement costs would have been without this new gear marking 
requirement.
    For buoy marking, buoys may be permanently marked directly on the 
buoy, or cattle ear tags could be ordered and the required 
identification information etched or stamped on each tag, then affixed 
to each buoy prior to setting the gear. NMFS estimates that during the 
3-year period of this collection, each respondent will only need to 
purchase tags and line once and then perform ongoing maintenance of 
those markings.
    To annualize gear marking costs, the number of respondents is 
divided by the 3-year period of this collection, resulting in an 
average of 112 respondents per year. NMFS estimates it takes 
approximately 5 hours to replace lines and mark buoys; therefore, the 
total burden would be approximately 560 hours per year. The estimated 
average cost burden for compliance with the new gear marking 
regulations in this rule is estimated to be $809 per respondent (e.g., 
vessel operator). This total accounts for the costs of gear-specific 
marked line, gear-specific marked buoy tags, and paint or markers. This 
cost per vessel does not always equate to the cost per entity.
    NMFS does not anticipate any additional compliance burden to the 
vessels subject to this action from complying with the biodegradable 
escape panel placement specifications or twine size changes in this 
rule. This is because industry representatives attest that no 
retrofitting of existing pots will be required to comply with revised 
regulations.
    Economic impacts from this rulemaking come primarily from the gear 
marking requirements. The gear marking alternative adopted in the final 
rule requires marking of only approximately 1 percent of vertical 
lines; specifically, the portion of the line that is most commonly 
documented in entanglements. The rulemaking also requires marking every 
buoy because at least one buoy is observed in the majority of 
entanglements. For these reasons, the alternative adopted in this final 
rule provides the best result in terms of assessing minimal costs to 
industry and providing benefits in terms of being able to positively or 
negatively attribute entanglements.
    There are no expected economic impacts from any other measures in 
this final rule, including the voluntary single-line allowance to 
reduce risk of entanglement. The single-line allowance is voluntary in 
nature to minimize the risk of gear loss, while removing a regulatory 
barrier to reducing the number of vertical lines in the water. This 
entanglement risk reduction measure therefore implements NMFS' 
statutory obligation under the Magnuson-Stevens Act to minimize bycatch 
to the extent practicable. None of the other alternatives considered 
during this rulemaking would meet the stated objectives and minimize 
any significant economic impact of the rule as compared to the final 
action.
    Section 212 of the Small Business Regulatory Enforcement Fairness

Sec.  660.219  Fixed gear identification and marking.

    (a) Gear identification. (1) Fixed gears authorized for use in the 
limited entry fishery (i.e., bottom longline, trap or pot, and 
stationary hook-and-line gear, including commercial vertical hook-and-
line gear) must be marked at the surface at each terminal end, with a 
pole, flag, light, radar reflector, and a buoy, except that bottom 
longline and pot gears (as defined at Sec.  660.11) may be marked, as 
described in this paragraph (a)(1), at just one terminal end (i.e., 
gear may be marked at either both terminal ends or one terminal end).
    (2) Limited entry commercial vertical hook-and-line gear that is 
closely tended as defined at Sec.  660.311, Subpart F, may be marked 
only with a single buoy of sufficient size to float the gear.
    (3) At least one buoy used to mark fixed gear under paragraph 
(a)(1) or (a)(2) of this section must be marked with a number clearly 
identifying the owner or operator of the vessel. In addition, all buoys 
used to mark bottom longline or pot gear in the limited entry fishery 
must be marked with a legible number clearly identifying the owner or 
operator of the vessel. For these two gear types, the number must be 
marked directly on at least one buoy, and all other buoys must have the 
number directly on the buoy or included on gear marking tags attached 
to the buoy, as specified in paragraph (b)(1) of this section. For all 
these marking requirements, the number may be either:
    (i) If required by applicable state law, the vessel's number, the 
commercial fishing license number, or buoy brand number; or
    (ii) The vessel documentation number issued by the USCG, or, for an 
undocumented vessel, the vessel registration number issued by the 
state.
    (b) Gear marking. Bottom longline and trap or pot gear, as defined 
at

[[Page 64805]]

Sec.  660.11, used in the limited entry fishery must have gear-specific 
markings on any vertical lines (as defined at Sec.  660.11) and all 
buoy(s). These gear-specific markings are in addition to identification 
requirements set out in paragraph (a) of this section and applicable 
gear restrictions in Sec.  660.230.
    (1) All buoys attached to the surface line of the subject gears in 
this paragraph (b)(1) must have a gear-specific marking. Marking must 
be either a permanent mark directly on the buoy or on a durable tag for 
each buoy; both are not required. Marks directly on the buoy must 
appear at least twice on each buoy on opposing sides. Tags must be 
stamped or engraved, where there is a physical indentation along with a 
contrasting color. Prior to gear deployment, if the tags are not 
securely attached to each buoy, tags for each buoy must be shown, upon 
request, to authorized agents. On a trip where a vessel takes and

with fishing gear are required to be marked either directly on the buoy 
or with a tag attached to the buoy with a single, exclusive gear-
specific mark, as described in this paragraph (b)(1).
    (i) Permanent buoy markings for bottom longline gear must be the 
capital letters ``GL,'' be legible, be at least 3 inches (7.7 cm) in 
height, and a gear-specific mark for pot gear may not be present. Tags 
used to mark buoys attached to bottom longline gear must be brown and 
large enough to accommodate a letter ``GL'' engraved that is at least 
1.75 inches (4.4 cm) in height.
    (ii) Permanent buoy markings for pot gear must use the capital 
letters ``GP,'' be legible, be at least 3 inches (7.7 cm) in height, 
and a gear-specific mark for bottom longline gear may not be present. 
Tags used to mark buoys attached to pot/trap gear must be orange and 
large enough to accommodate a letter ``GP'' engraved that is at least 
1.75 inches (4.4 cm) in height.
    (2) All vertical lines (as defined at Sec.  660.11) of the subject 
gears in this paragraph (b)(2) must have gear-specific marks. Marks 
must be continuous in at least the top 20 fm (37 m) of the vertical 
line, measured from the main buoy down towards the groundline.
    (i) Line manufactured in a gear-specific color scheme must be used 
for the marked portion of the line. Lines must be both brown and blue 
for use with bottom longline gear. Lines must be both orange and blue 
for use with pot gear. At least one-third of strands in the marked line 
must be one of the gear-specific colors specified here, and the 
remainder of the strands the other color. Presence of any third color, 
besides white, is prohibited in the portion of the line required to be 
marked.
    (ii) Until January 11, 2030, the colors specified in paragraph 
(b)(3)(i) of this section may be applied by temporary methods such that 
the colors are clearly visible and in continuous, alternating bands of 
18-28 inches (46-71 cm) each, over the entire portion of the line 
required to be marked in this paragraph (b)(2)(ii). Marking is 
continuous if no more than 6 inches of the original, unmarked line 
color shows through. If any amount less than 6 inches of original line 
is visible it may not be in the full circumference of the line.
