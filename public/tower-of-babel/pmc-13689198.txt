Synovial oxidative stress biomarkers are not associated with radiological severity in knee osteoarthritis: a prospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Hakan Yolaçan, Hüseyin Erdal, Serkan Güler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis prospectively enrolled cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the relationship between synovial fluid oxidative stress parameters and radiographic severity in patients with knee osteoarthritis and to determine whether these biomarkers reflect radiographic stage.MethodsA total of 98 patients aged 50 years and older with primary knee osteoarthritis were prospectively enrolled in this cross-sectional study. Radiological severity was classified using the Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grading system as early-stage (KL grades 1–2) and late-stage (KL grades 3–4). Clinical severity was evaluated using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Synovial fluid samples were analyzed for total thiol (TT), native thiol (NT), disulfide (DS), total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), oxidative stress index (OSI), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) levels. Logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, correlation analysis, and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) were performed.ResultsWOMAC pain, stiffness, function, total scores, and VAS scores were significantly higher in patients with late-stage osteoarthritis (p < 0.001). Among oxidative stress parameters, only total oxidant status levels were significantly higher in late-stage osteoarthritis (p = 0.007), whereas TT, NT, DS, TAS, OSI, and GPx levels did not differ significantly between early-stage and late-stage osteoarthritis groups. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, WOMAC total score and VAS score were independently associated with late-stage osteoarthritis, whereas oxidative stress biomarkers were not. ROC analysis showed excellent discriminative performance for WOMAC total and VAS scores, whereas oxidative stress parameters showed poor to moderate discriminative performance. Correlation and ANCOVA analyses demonstrated no significant association between most oxidative stress parameters and radiological osteoarthritis stage.ConclusionSynovial oxidative stress biomarkers are not reliable indicators of radiological severity in knee osteoarthritis, although total oxidant status may increase in advanced disease. Clinical severity scores showed the strongest cross-sectional association with radiographic stage. These findings suggest that oxidative stress may contribute to the pathophysiology of knee osteoarthritis but does not adequately reflect radiographic disease severity.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common degenerative joint disease worldwide and a leading cause of pain, functional limitation, and reduced quality of life, particularly in the elderly population. The knee joint is the most frequently affected joint, and knee osteoarthritis is one of the major causes of chronic disability due to increased life expectancy and the rising prevalence of obesity [1, 2]. Although OA was traditionally considered a disease limited to articular cartilage, it is now recognized as a whole-joint disease involving the articular cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, menisci, ligaments, infrapatellar fat pad, joint capsule, and periarticular muscles. The pathogenesis of osteoarthritis is multifactorial and includes mechanical stress, inflammation, metabolic factors, and oxidative stress [3–6].

Oxidative stress has been increasingly recognized as an important contributor to the development and pathophysiology of osteoarthritis. Oxidative stress occurs when reactive oxygen species (ROS) production exceeds antioxidant defense capacity, resulting in cellular damage. Excessive ROS production in articular cartilage leads to chondrocyte apoptosis, extracellular matrix degradation, and activation of inflammatory pathways. Reactive oxygen species also stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which increase matrix metalloproteinase activity and accelerate cartilage degradation [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that oxidative stress levels are elevated and antioxidant capacity is reduced in patients with knee osteoarthritis [9].

In recent years, there has been growing interest in identifying biochemical biomarkers that can be used for early diagnosis, assessment of disease severity, and evaluation of disease status in osteoarthritis. Biomarkers obtained from synovial fluid are particularly important because synovial fluid directly reflects intra-articular pathological processes and provides more specific information about joint metabolism compared with serum biomarkers [10, 11]. However, despite numerous studies investigating biochemical markers in osteoarthritis, reliable biomarkers that can clearly reflect disease severity and radiographic stage are still lacking.

Among oxidative stress–related biomarkers, antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx) play an important role in cellular redox regulation by reducing hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides and protecting cells from oxidative damage [12]. In addition to individual antioxidant enzymes, total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) are widely used to evaluate overall oxidative balance, and the oxidative stress index (OSI), calculated as the ratio of TOS to TAS, provides an overall measure of oxidative stress status [13]. These parameters are practical, rapid, and cost-effective indicators of oxidative stress compared with measuring individual oxidant and antioxidant molecules separately.

Although oxidative stress has been implicated in osteoarthritis pathophysiology, it is still unclear whether synovial oxidative stress biomarkers reflect radiological disease severity. Most previous studies have evaluated serum oxidative stress markers, and studies investigating synovial oxidative stress parameters in relation to radiological severity are limited. In addition, prospectively enrolled studies evaluating synovial oxidative stress parameters together with clinical severity and radiographic staging are scarce. Therefore, the aim of this prospective study was to investigate the relationship between synovial fluid oxidative stress parameters and radiological severity in patients with knee osteoarthritis. We hypothesized that oxidative stress parameters would be higher in advanced radiological stages of osteoarthritis and could be associated with disease severity.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a prospectively enrolled cross-sectional observational study conducted between June 2025 and February 2026 at a tertiary care referral center. Consecutive eligible patients were prospectively recruited during the study period, and all clinical, radiographic, and biochemical assessments were performed at a single time point. Patients aged 50 years and older who were diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria were included in the study [14]. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This study was observational in nature and did not involve any interventional procedures; therefore, clinical trial registration was not required. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies.

Patients with primary knee osteoarthritis were eligible for inclusion in this study. Patients with conditions that could influence oxidative stress or antioxidant status were excluded. The exclusion criteria included chronic inflammatory diseases, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, renal disease, neurodegenerative disorders, history of malignancy, metabolic bone disease, immune system disorders, previous intra-articular injection, history of septic arthritis, arthroscopic surgery or fracture in the same knee, steroid use, regular use of anti-inflammatory drugs, smoking, alcohol consumption, substance abuse, morbid obesity, unstable clinical conditions, and use of antioxidant supplements such as vitamins C and E or selenium. In patients with bilateral knee osteoarthritis, the knee with more severe symptoms was selected for analysis. Patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria or met any of the exclusion criteria were not included in the study.

Radiographic evaluation and osteoarthritis staging

Standardized weight-bearing anteroposterior radiographs were obtained in full extension together with lateral radiographs. Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grading was based on the compartment demonstrating the greatest radiographic severity [15]. According to this classification, Grade 0 indicates a normal joint with no signs of osteoarthritis; Grade 1 indicates minimal joint space narrowing and possible osteophyte formation; Grade 2 indicates definite joint space narrowing with mild osteophytes; Grade 3 indicates significant joint space narrowing, minimal subchondral sclerosis, and moderate osteophytes; and Grade 4 indicates severe joint space narrowing, marked subchondral sclerosis, and bone deformity with large osteophytes. Grades 1 and 2 were defined as early-stage osteoarthritis, while Grades 3 and 4 were defined as late-stage osteoarthritis. All radiographs were evaluated by an experienced orthopedic surgeon who was blinded to the clinical and biochemical data.

Clinical evaluation

At the time of admission, clinical symptoms were evaluated using the previously validated Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). The WOMAC is a disease-specific questionnaire developed to assess pain, stiffness, and physical function in patients with hip and knee osteoarthritis and has been extensively validated for clinical and research use [16]. In the present study, the Likert version of the WOMAC was used. Each item was scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (none) to 4 (extreme), yielding maximum scores of 20 for pain, 8 for stiffness, and 68 for physical function, with a total possible score of 96. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. Pain intensity was assessed using an 11-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain) [17]. All clinical assessments were performed before synovial fluid sampling.

Synovial fluid sampling and biochemical analysis

Synovial fluid samples were collected from all patients at the time of admission under sterile conditions. Immediately after collection, the samples were centrifuged at 7,500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatants were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analysis. Each sample underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle before analysis. Total thiol (TT), native thiol (NT), disulfide (DS), total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), oxidative stress index (OSI), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) levels were analyzed using commercially available assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Biochemical analyses were performed using a Mindray BS-400 automated chemistry analyzer (Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Shenzhen, China) following sample preparation with a Hettich Mikro 200 centrifuge (Andreas Hettich GmbH & Co. KG, Tuttlingen, Germany). Each sample was analyzed once according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All biochemical analyses were performed in the same laboratory by the same experienced technician, who was blinded to the clinical and radiographic data. Additionally, demographic data including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and affected side were recorded for all patients.

Thiol/disulfide homeostasis

Thiol/disulfide homeostasis parameters were measured using a novel automated spectrophotometric method developed by Erel and Neşelioğlu and commercially available (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Turkey) [18]. In this method, dynamic and reducible disulfide bonds in the samples were reduced to free functional thiol groups using sodium borohydride. Excess sodium borohydride was removed with formaldehyde to prevent interference with the measurement. Native thiol and total thiol levels were measured after reaction with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB). Disulfide levels were calculated as half of the difference between total thiol and native thiol levels.

Total antioxidant status (TAS)

TAS levels were measured using commercially available kits (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Turkey) based on the automated method developed by Erel [13]. This method is based on the bleaching of the characteristic color of the stable ABTS radical cation by antioxidants present in the sample. Results were expressed as mmol Trolox equivalent per liter.

Total oxidant status (TOS)

TOS levels were measured using commercially available kits (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Turkey) based on the automated colorimetric method developed by Erel [19]. In this method, oxidants in the sample oxidize the ferrous ion–o-dianisidine complex to ferric ions. The ferric ions form a colored complex with xylenol orange in an acidic medium, and the color intensity measured spectrophotometrically is proportional to the total oxidant level. Results were expressed as µmol hydrogen peroxide equivalent per liter.

Oxidative stress index (OSI)

The oxidative stress index was calculated as the ratio of TOS to TAS [13]. TAS values were converted to µmol/L before calculation, and OSI was calculated using the following formula: OSI = TOS (µmol H₂O₂ equivalent/L) / TAS (µmol Trolox equivalent/L).

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx)

Glutathione peroxidase activity was measured using the method described by Paglia and Valentine [20]. GPx catalyzes the oxidation of glutathione by cumene hydroperoxide. In the presence of glutathione reductase, oxidized glutathione is converted back to its reduced form with simultaneous oxidation of NADPH to NADP+. The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm was measured spectrophotometrically.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and none of the continuous variables showed a normal distribution. Therefore, continuous variables were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were presented as number and percentage. Effect sizes were calculated in addition to statistical significance. To reduce the probability of type I error due to multiple comparisons in biochemical parameters, false discovery rate correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method [21].

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) models were constructed for all continuous outcome variables. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent variables associated with osteoarthritis stage, and results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p values. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) values, and since all VIF values were below 2, no multicollinearity was detected.

Power analysis was performed for sample size estimation. Based on previous clinical studies evaluating biochemical and clinical parameters in osteoarthritis patients, a medium effect size (d = 0.5) was assumed. With an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 68 participants. Since the present study included 98 participants, the study had sufficient statistical power. All analyses were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 98 patients with knee osteoarthritis were included in the study. The median age of the study population was 69 years, and the median body mass index (BMI) was 31.25 kg/m². The majority of the participants were female (81.6%). Early-stage osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence grades 1–2) was present in 41 patients (41.8%), whereas late-stage osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence grades 3–4) was present in 57 patients (58.2%). Among the 98 patients, 20 were classified as Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade 1, 21 as grade 2, 31 as grade 3, and 26 as grade 4. The median WOMAC total score was 56, and the median VAS score was 7. When demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables were compared between early-stage and late-stage osteoarthritis groups, no significant differences were observed in age, BMI, sex distribution, or affected side between the groups (p > 0.05). However, WOMAC pain, stiffness, function, and total scores were significantly higher in patients with late-stage osteoarthritis compared with early-stage osteoarthritis (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). However, no significant differences were observed between early-stage and late-stage osteoarthritis groups in total thiol (TT), native thiol (NT), disulfide (DS), total antioxidant status (TAS), oxidative stress index (OSI), or glutathione peroxidase (GPx) levels. Discussion

The present prospectively enrolled cross-sectional study investigated the relationship between synovial fluid oxidative stress parameters and radiographic stage in patients with knee osteoarthritis. The main finding of this study was that clinical scores, particularly WOMAC and VAS scores, were strongly associated with osteoarthritis stage and were independently associated with late-stage disease, whereas most synovial oxidative stress parameters were not significantly associated with disease stage. However, total oxidant status levels were significantly higher in patients with late-stage osteoarthritis, suggesting that oxidative stress may be more pronounced in patients with advanced radiographic knee osteoarthritis. Because the present study employed a cross-sectional design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal relationships between oxidative stress and radiographic stage.

Osteoarthritis is now widely accepted as a complex whole-joint disease involving cartilage degeneration, synovial inflammation, subchondral bone remodeling, and metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms. In recent years, oxidative stress has been increasingly recognized as an important contributor to osteoarthritis pathophysiology. Reactive oxygen species have been shown to induce chondrocyte apoptosis, extracellular matrix degradation, and activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, which together accelerate cartilage destruction and joint degeneration [22–24]. Therefore, oxidative stress biomarkers have attracted attention as potential indicators of disease activity and disease severity in osteoarthritis.

In the present study, total oxidant status levels were significantly higher in patients with late-stage osteoarthritis compared with early-stage disease. This finding is consistent with recent studies reporting increased oxidative stress markers in advanced osteoarthritis and suggesting that oxidative stress is associated with disease severity and joint damage [25, 26]. Nevertheless, this association should be interpreted cautiously because statistical significance was not retained after multivariable logistic regression or ANCOVA adjustment. Therefore, the observed difference in TOS should be considered exploratory rather than conclusive. Increased oxidant levels in late-stage osteoarthritis may be related to chronic synovial inflammation, increased metabolic activity within the joint, and more extensive structural joint damage. However, because this study employed a cross-sectional design, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal changes in oxidative stress. Rather, they suggest that oxidative stress may be more prominent in patients with advanced radiographic osteoarthritis.

Interestingly, total antioxidant status, oxidative stress index, thiol/disulfide homeostasis parameters, and glutathione peroxidase levels did not differ significantly between early-stage and late-stage osteoarthritis groups in this study. Similar findings have been reported in recent studies evaluating antioxidant enzyme activity and thiol/disulfide balance in osteoarthritis patients, where antioxidant parameters were not consistently associated with radiological severity [27, 28]. One possible explanation is that antioxidant defense mechanisms may remain relatively stable despite increased oxidant production, especially in chronic degenerative diseases. Another explanation is that oxidative stress may occur locally in cartilage tissue and may not be fully reflected in synovial fluid antioxidant parameters. Additionally, antioxidant enzyme activity may be influenced by systemic factors such as age, metabolic status, diet, and comorbidities, which may obscure the relationship between antioxidant parameters and disease severity. To further investigate whether advanced structural degeneration influences synovial oxidative stress, an additional subgroup analysis comparing KL grade 3 and KL grade 4 patients was performed. Despite the expected reduction in residual cartilage in KL grade 4 knees, none of the evaluated synovial oxidative stress biomarkers differed significantly between the two groups. These findings further support our primary observation that synovial oxidative stress biomarkers have limited discriminatory ability for radiographic severity, even within advanced stages of knee osteoarthritis.

Another important finding of this study was that WOMAC total score and VAS score were independently associated with late-stage osteoarthritis in the cross-sectional logistic regression model and demonstrated excellent discriminative performance in ROC analysis. These findings indicate that clinical severity scores remain the most reliable indicators of disease severity in knee osteoarthritis. Recent clinical studies have also reported strong associations between WOMAC scores, pain severity, functional limitation, and radiological osteoarthritis severity [29, 30]. Although biochemical markers may provide valuable information about disease mechanisms, clinical scoring systems still appear to be more useful for evaluating disease severity and functional impairment in clinical practice. These findings represent cross-sectional statistical associations and should not be interpreted as providing longitudinal or prognostic information.

The strong association between clinical scores and osteoarthritis severity observed in this study was expected and is consistent with previous literature. However, the main aim of this study was not to demonstrate this relationship but to investigate whether synovial oxidative stress biomarkers could reflect radiological disease severity. The findings of this study suggest that synovial oxidative stress parameters are not reliable indicators of radiological severity despite their potential role in osteoarthritis pathophysiology.

Correlation and adjusted analyses in this study also demonstrated that biochemical parameters were not significantly associated with osteoarthritis stage after controlling for age and body mass index. These findings suggest that demographic factors and clinical severity may have a stronger relationship with osteoarthritis stage than synovial oxidative stress parameters. Therefore, oxidative stress biomarkers alone may not be sufficient to determine disease severity but may still provide important information about disease pathophysiology and joint metabolism.

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether synovial oxidative stress biomarkers reflect radiological severity in knee osteoarthritis. The findings of this study indicate that most oxidative stress parameters were not associated with radiological disease stage, suggesting that these biomarkers may be more closely related to disease pathophysiology than to radiographic severity. These results suggest that oxidative stress biomarkers may have limited value for disease staging but may still provide important information about intra-articular metabolic activity.

To our knowledge, this is one of the few prospectively conducted studies evaluating multiple synovial oxidative stress biomarkers together in relation to radiological severity in knee osteoarthritis. This study has several strengths. The prospective enrollment of participants, evaluation of synovial fluid samples that directly reflect intra-articular processes, assessment of multiple oxidative stress parameters, and use of statistical analyses including logistic regression, ROC analysis, ANCOVA, and multiple-comparison correction strengthen the study. However, there are also several limitations. The sample size was relatively limited, the study was conducted at a single center, and only synovial fluid oxidative stress parameters were evaluated. Additionally, biochemical measurements were performed as single determinations; therefore, within-assay analytical variability could not be directly assessed. The restrictive exclusion criteria were applied to minimize the potential effects of comorbidities, medications, and lifestyle-related factors on oxidative stress measurements. However, these criteria may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of patients with knee osteoarthritis, among whom metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities, obesity, medication use, smoking, and other factors influencing oxidative status are common. Radiographic grading was performed by a single experienced orthopedic surgeon, and intra- and interobserver reliability were not formally assessed; therefore, observer-related variability cannot be excluded. In addition, the cross-sectional design did not allow the evaluation of temporal changes or causal relationships. Future multicenter longitudinal studies with larger and more representative patient populations are required to evaluate temporal associations between oxidative stress biomarkers and structural or clinical outcomes in knee osteoarthritis.

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that oxidative stress contributes to the pathophysiology of knee osteoarthritis; however, synovial oxidative stress parameters alone do not adequately discriminate radiographic disease severity. The relationship between oxidative stress and the pathophysiology of osteoarthritis appears to be complex and may involve multiple local and systemic factors. Therefore, oxidative stress biomarkers should be interpreted together with clinical and radiological findings rather than used alone for disease staging. Further longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether temporal changes in oxidative stress biomarkers are associated with structural and clinical outcomes in knee osteoarthritis.

Conclusıon

In conclusion, this prospectively enrolled cross-sectional study demonstrated that synovial oxidative stress biomarkers are not reliable indicators of radiographic severity in knee osteoarthritis. Although total oxidant status was higher in patients with late-stage disease in the unadjusted analysis, this association was not maintained after adjusted analyses. Clinical severity scores showed the strongest cross-sectional association with radiographic stage. These findings suggest that oxidative stress contributes to the pathophysiology of knee osteoarthritis but does not adequately discriminate radiographic disease severity. Further longitudinal studies are required to determine whether temporal changes in oxidative stress biomarkers are associated with structural or clinical outcomes.
