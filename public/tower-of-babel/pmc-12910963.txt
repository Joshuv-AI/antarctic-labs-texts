Recent advances in methods for establishing animal models of premature ovarian failure
Authors: Xinru Wang, Xiaoru Gao, Shuping Li, Rong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Premature ovarian failure is a condition characterized by the progressive decline in ovarian function resulting from various etiologies, significantly affecting the reproductive health, psychological well-being, and overall quality of life of women of reproductive age. Understanding its pathophysiology, investigating effective prevention and therapeutic interventions, and developing standardized animal models are of critical importance. This review systematically examines and compares the primary methodologies employed by researchers worldwide to establish animal models of premature ovarian failure, including pharmacological agents, iatrogenic factors, environmental and chemical exposures, metabolic dysregulation, and immune-mediated processes. The discussion highlights their clinical relevance, aiming to provide a theoretical framework and reference for model selection in foundational studies of Premature ovarian failure.

Introduction

In 2016, the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) updated its terminology, replacing “premature ovarian failure (POF)” with “premature ovarian insufficiency (POI).” The diagnosis is characterized by persistent menstrual irregularities—amenorrhea or oligomenorrhea—for at least four months, accompanied by two serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) tests (conducted at least four weeks apart) both measuring ≥ 25 IU/L, along with diminished serum estradiol (E2) and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels [1].The etiology of POI involves a multifactorial pathogenesis, including genetic predispositions, autoimmune processes, iatrogenic damage, environmental toxic exposures, and metabolic abnormalities [2].

The ovaries, as essential reproductive and endocrine organs, are responsible for maintaining fertility and hormonal balance; impairment can lead to infertility and decreased sexual function. Due to diminished estrogen production, patients with premature ovarian failure frequently experience menopausal symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats, and sleep disturbances. Furthermore, this condition elevates their risk for cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, cognitive decline, and type 2 diabetes [3]. Compared to women with other chronic illnesses, those with POF often endure greater psychological distress, characterized by heightened anxiety and depression [4].

Currently, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying POF and effective regenerative strategies for restoring ovarian reserve remain inadequately characterized, emphasizing the necessity for comprehensive basic research. Due to the challenges in procuring human ovarian tissue, murine models present significant advantages, including ease of acquisition, straightforward husbandry, and rapid reproductive cycles. Moreover, murine ovarian ontogeny and functionality closely mirror human physiology, and they display comparable genetic pathway regulation associated with POI, establishing them as optimal models for POI research [2]. Utilizing animal models to simulate the pathological progression of human POI is crucial for elucidating disease mechanisms, identifying potential therapeutic targets, and evaluating the efficacy of various interventions. This review examines diverse modeling methodologies—encompassing iatrogenic, genetic, environmental/chemical, metabolic, and immunological factors—highlighting their clinical relevance and translational potential to inform model selection in premature ovarian insufficiency studies.

Medical factors

Radiotherapy

Radiation therapy, as a pivotal modality in oncologic management, has markedly enhanced survival outcomes among cancer patients. Data show that survival rates for pediatric, adolescent, and young adult populations can attain 70% to 80% [5]. Nonetheless, despite these promising prognoses, clinical concerns remain regarding the potential gonadotoxic effects of radiation exposure on ovarian reserve and function. The high radiosensitivity of oocytes and DNA damage induced by oxidative stress constitute the primary mechanisms driving radiation-induced gonadotoxicity. The severity of ovarian injury correlates with variables such as patient age, radiotherapy dose, and irradiation fields. Long-term studies reveal that total body irradiation results in ovarian failure in approximately 90% of cases, while abdominal irradiation can lead to ovarian failure in up to 97% [6]. These findings highlight the necessity of integrating reproductive health preservation and fertility-sparing interventions into the multidisciplinary care plan for young female cancer patients.

The detrimental effects of ionizing radiation on ovarian function can be classified into direct and indirect mechanisms: direct damage to biomolecules such as DNA, RNA, proteins, and lipids, or indirect impairment mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generating oxidative stress. Radiation promotes primordial follicle attrition and follicular atresia by activating AMPK/AKT and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, leading to increased oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. It also causes oocyte apoptosis and mitochondria-triggered necrosis, resulting in normal oocyte loss. Furthermore, radiation may contribute to ovarian vascular injury, tissue degeneration, inflammatory processes, and fibrotic remodeling [7–10]. Mahgoub et al. [8]established a POF model in rats following a single systemic γ irradiation of 3.2 Gy (dose rate: 0.48 Gy/min). Compared with the control group, the irradiated group exhibited significantly decreased E2 levels (78%) and significantly increased AMH levels (19%). Histological sections revealed a reduction in primordial follicle count and an increased proportion of atretic follicles. Marked proliferation of ovarian connective tissue was observed, accompanied by significant vascular dilation in the ovaries and infiltration by lymphocytes and macrophages.

The development of a POF model using radiotherapy offers a precise simulation of the gonadotoxic effects of clinical radiation on ovarian tissue. This approach is straightforward, exhibits a high procedural success rate, minimizes procedural duration, and is reproducible, providing a robust animal model for studying the pathophysiology and mechanisms of radiotherapy-induced ovarian insufficiency. Nevertheless, experimental caution must be exercised to prevent collateral damage to adjacent organs such as the bone marrow and liver, which could confound the experimental outcomes.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapeutic agents can be systematically categorized into five principal classes: platinum-based compounds, alkylating agents, antimetabolites, taxanes, and antineoplastic antibiotics. Based on their mechanism of action relative to the cell cycle, they are further subdivided into cell cycle-specific and non-cell cycle-specific agents. Cell cycle-specific chemotherapeutics, as the designation indicates, target and exert cytotoxic effects during particular phases of cellular replication. Conversely, non-cell cycle-specific agents, such as cyclophosphamide and cisplatin, possess the capability to induce cytotoxicity throughout all phases of the cell cycle, including the G0 quiescent phase [11]. Compared to targeted radiotherapy, systemic chemotherapy agents are predominantly delivered via intravenous infusion and demonstrate less specificity for malignant tissues and cellular targets. As a result, although they effectively induce cytotoxicity in neoplastic cells, they can also harm ovarian tissue, thereby elevating the risk of ovarian failure and infertility.

Cisplatin

The pathophysiology of cisplatin (CDDP)-induced ovarian cytotoxicity involves ROS generation and disruption of antioxidant defenses. Excessive ROS accumulation causes DNA strand breaks in granulosa cells and oocytes, lipid peroxidation, and activation of apoptotic pathways such as mitochondrial apoptosis, leading to increased oocyte apoptosis and follicular atresia. Evidence indicates that post-CDDP administration, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, granulosa cell apoptosis, and ferroptosis are observed. Data show a 55% elevation in malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in ovarian tissue, coupled with significant reductions in antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activity (by 48% and 47%), along with downregulation of Nrf2, hnRNPA2B1, and SLC7A11 expression [12–14]. Additionally, the PTEN/AKT/mTOR and AMPK signaling pathways, as well as TLR4-mediated inflammatory responses, are critically involved in the process [15, 16].

Currently, researchers principally employ either low-dose continuous infusion or high-dose intermittent administration to establish POI models. Specifically, this involves either daily intraperitoneal injections of 1.5 mg/kg CDDP for six consecutive days or biweekly intraperitoneal injections of 2.5 mg/kg CDDP over a four-week period [16, 17]. The experimental model exhibits severe ovarian histopathological alterations, characterized by a reduction in healthy follicle counts and an increase in atretic follicles; significantly decreased serum levels of E2 and AMH, along with markedly elevated FSH levels; reduced activities of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and glutathione (GSH), coupled with increased MDA levels; upregulated expression of Keap1, TGFβ−1, caspase-3, and BAX; and downregulated expression of Nrf2 and Bcl-2.

Cyclophosphamide

Cyclophosphamide (CTX) can induce hyperactivation of primordial follicles, leading to their accelerated atresia, and cause DNA damage within ovarian cells while impairing the ovarian antioxidant defense system. This results in elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and oxidative stress, which promote apoptotic pathways and decline ovarian reserve capacity [18–20]. Recent research suggests that CTX impairs ovarian function by stimulating ROS production, iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and pyroptosis. Therapeutic strategies such as iron channel inhibition, anti-pyroptosis agents, and interventions targeting iron metabolism have been shown to reduce oxidative injury in ovarian tissue and preserve ovarian reserve function [21–23].

Intraperitoneal administration facilitates prompt systemic absorption via the highly vascularized peritoneal membrane, leading to elevated bioavailability. This methodological approach is frequently employed in the development of chemotherapeutic-induced POF models. Despite these advantages, variability in CTX dosing protocols—such as single versus fractionated doses—and administration schedules has resulted in diverse modeling strategies. For instance, female murine subjects have been subjected to intraperitoneal injections of 50 mg/kg CTX daily for 14 days, 80 mg/kg for three consecutive days, or a single dose of 90 mg/kg [21, 24, 25]. Jiao [26]contended that prolonged low-dose CTX induces more extensive and sustained ovarian structural damage and functional impairment. They administered 100 mg/kg CTX on Day 1, followed by daily low-dose CTX (20 mg/kg) over 14 days, aiming to rapidly deplete immune cell populations and induce significant immunosuppression within a shortened timeframe. This protocol minimizes immune system rebound and more accurately replicates the cumulative gonadotoxic effects encountered during clinical chemotherapy regimens.

The approach of inducing primary ovarian failure (POF) through chemotherapeutic agents effectively replicates the ovarian damage mechanisms observed in clinical anticancer treatments. It exhibits clinical phenotypes akin to premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), such as primordial follicle depletion and hormonal disruption, which are key pathological features. This model provides a robust foundation for investigating the reproductive toxicology of chemotherapy, screening ovarian protective interventions, and optimizing fertility preservation techniques. Moreover, it offers operational advantages including procedural simplicity, rapid experimental timelines, cost-effectiveness, and ease of replication and standardization. However, it is limited in its inability to mimic POI resulting from genetic, autoimmune, metabolic, or other etiologies [26]. Additionally, individual variation among animal subjects and systemic toxicity from chemotherapeutic agents may cause mortality and confound experimental data. Consequently, vigilant monitoring of animal health indicators during experiments is essential, and adjustments to drug dosing or supportive therapies should be promptly implemented to mitigate systemic toxicity’s impact on study outcomes.

Drug

Tripterygium wilfordii

Tripterygium wilfordii formulations are extensively employed in the management of immune-mediated conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus, owing to their immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties [27]. Their reproductive toxicity primarily involves suppression of autophagy in ovarian tissues and granulosa cells via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, leading to the activation of apoptotic pathways. Furthermore, they alter sex hormone levels, induce ovarian atrophy and senescence, and cause disruptions in estrous cyclicity by inhibiting cAMP/PKA signaling and aromatase activity [28–30].

Yu et al. [27]established a POF model by administering 40 mg/kg/day of Tripterygium glycosides via gavage to female SD rats for 10 weeks. To optimize POI mouse model induction, researchers compared hormone level changes, estrous cycles, and ovarian histology across different administration routes (oral, intraperitoneal, and subcutaneous injection). Results showed elevated serum FSH levels, significantly prolonged estrous cycles, reduced estrous frequency, and decreased ovarian and uterine indices in the subcutaneous injection group. These findings suggest that subcutaneous administration produces more pronounced reproductive toxicity compared to conventional oral delivery [31].

As a traditional Chinese medicinal agent renowned for its efficacy in managing rheumatic and autoimmune diseases, Tripterygium-based formulations should incorporate principles of traditional Chinese medicine alongside contemporary pharmacological and toxicological methodologies in future research. This strategy should employ techniques such as restoring the balance of properties and flavors or harmonizing the yin and yang of visceral zang-fu organs to reduce its toxicity [32].

Hydrocortisone

Within Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), there is no direct equivalent to POF. Clinically, symptoms such as oligomenorrhea, amenorrhea, and infertility are associated with syndromes like kidney deficiency, spleen deficiency, liver qi stagnation, or blood deficiency, often complicated by qi stagnation, blood stasis, or phlegm-dampness accumulation [33].

One experimental study established a POF model in SD rats exhibiting kidney yang deficiency by administering 32 mg/kg hydrocortisone via intramuscular injection into the hindlimbs over a 12-day period [34]. This corticosteroid-induced model aligns with TCM pathophysiological concepts and provides a reference framework for syndrome-based POF research. The methodology is straightforward and pragmatic; however, reproducibility issues, limited existing research, and questions regarding its clinical relevance and translational applicability warrant further validation.

Genetic factors

Genetic determinants contribute to approximately 20–25% of all recognized etiologies of POF, with Turner syndrome being the most common chromosomal anomaly, accounting for 43% of these cases [35]. Considering the role of chromosomal aberrations and gene mutations in the pathogenesis of POF, various genetic mutation models have been utilized to investigate folliculogenesis. The identification of specific gene targets presents significant potential for the development of innovative therapeutic interventions for POF.

Shang et al. [36]identified that Leucine-Rich Repeat Containing 4 (LRRC4) plays a critical role in mediating mitochondrial dynamics—specifically fission and fusion—within ovarian granulosa cells by enhancing K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of YAP. This regulation prevents excessive mitochondrial autophagy, thereby preserving metabolic homeostasis. Their study is pioneering in establishing LRRC4 as a key regulator of folliculogenesis, essential for maintaining ovarian reserve capacity. Deletion of LRRC4 results in hyperactivation of primordial follicles and increased apoptotic loss, contributing to accelerated ovarian reserve depletion and POI. Subsequent research demonstrated that LRRC4 supplementation ameliorates follicular development and restores ovarian function in murine models of POF. This work reveals an innovative LRRC4-mediated pathogenic pathway underlying POI, offering novel therapeutic targets and foundational insights for future clinical interventions. Additionally, another investigation identified calmodulin 2 as expressed in oocytes and granulosa cells; its deficiency impairs ovarian reserve and precipitates premature follicular exhaustion. This discovery provides a promising molecular target for further elucidation of POI pathogenesis and therapeutic development [37].

While gene knockout models are valuable for identifying potential therapeutic targets in POF, their capacity is limited by the inability to concurrently investigate multiple genetic contributors, given the polygenic nature of POF. Moreover, these models entail significant operational complexity and financial costs.

Environmental toxicants and chemical substance factors

PM2.5

PM2.5, a major component of ambient air pollutants, refers to airborne particles with a diameter of less than or equal to 2.5 micrometers. It has increasingly been recognized as an environmental risk factor detrimental to global public health. Recent research has extensively associated chronic exposure to PM2.5 with adverse reproductive outcomes in females. Inhalation of PM2.5 induces inflammatory pathways, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis within ovarian tissue, resulting in hormonal imbalance, follicular atresia, and diminished ovarian reserve [38].

Maternal exposure to PM2.5 results in reduced litter size, low birth weight in offspring, increased adult obesity, heightened anxiety levels, and impaired learning and memory abilities. Furthermore, it accelerates depletion of the primordial follicle pool in offspring by activating the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway, increases accumulation of the lipid peroxidation product 4-HNE, and reduces ovarian reserve in offspring mice via ROS-dependent NF-κB pathways [39–41].

4-Vinyl-1-cyclohexene dicarboxylate

4-Vinyl-1-cyclohexene dicarboxylate (VCD) is isolated and released into the environment during the production of flame retardants, fragrances, pesticides, adhesives, and rubber tire production processes, has been demonstrated to specifically kill primordial follicles, deplete follicular reserves, and induce premature ovarian failure. Its mechanism may involve apoptosis through the KITL/KIT/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and triggering granulosa cell autophagy via the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [42]. Furthermore, maternal exposure to VCD during pregnancy significantly reduces litter size and ovarian reserve, while causing birth defects and impaired fertility in offspring [43].

Surprisingly, one study demonstrated that VCD exhibits dose- and duration-dependent, opposite hormone-like effects on primordial follicle reserve, follicular development, and ovulation induction. Low-dose, short-term (80 mg/kg, 5 days) VCD administration activates primordial follicles via the PTEN-PI3K-FOXO3a signaling pathway, improving follicular atresia and thereby increasing ovarian reserve. Future research could further explore the effects of different VCD concentrations on follicles at various stages, which may positively impact fertility enhancement [44].

Cigarette smoke

Cigarette smoke (CS) encompasses a complex mixture of toxic compounds, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), nitrosamines, heavy metals, alkaloids, and aromatic amines. These constituents contribute to increased ROS generation and impair endogenous antioxidant defense systems, thereby disrupting the redox homeostasis essential for normal folliculogenesis and leading to ovarian reserve depletion. This oxidative and cellular damage persists post-cessation of smoking [45, 46].

Cellular investigations reveal that CS induces dysregulation of multiple oxidative stress (OS)-related gene expressions within granulosa cells surrounding oocytes. Experimental animal studies and in vitro models demonstrate altered mRNA and protein levels of key antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, glutathione peroxidase (GPX), and CAT, indicating compromised antioxidant defenses [47]. Moreover, CS impairs mitochondrial function, promotes apoptosis, and disrupts cytoskeletal integrity in oocytes and granulosa cells [48].

Given the multifaceted toxicant profile of tobacco smoke, most research employs individual chemical exposures. thus, developing models that replicate the complex chemical matrix of cigarette smoke is crucial for elucidating authentic physiological mechanisms. Additionally, accurately representing chemical doses aligned with typical human exposure levels remains a methodological challenge.

Metabolic factors

D-galactose

D-galactose, as a reducing monosaccharide, is oxidized by galactose oxidase into hydrogen peroxide and aldehydes, accompanied by ROS generation. Subsequently, under the catalysis of aldose reductase, it metabolites into galactitol, generating free radicals. Additionally, it promotes the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [49]. These mechanisms collectively perturb the redox homeostasis, inducing oxidative stress that leads to follicular depletion, apoptosis of ovarian granulosa cells, and compromised ovarian function.

Due to its similarity to human ovarian senescence, D-galactose is extensively utilized to establish animal models of POI. Post D-galactose administration, the POI model displays gut microbiota dysbiosis, increased lipid peroxidation markers (MDA), elevated oxidative stress indicators (4-HNE, 8-OHdG, and NTY), markers of cellular senescence (p16 protein), pro-apoptotic factors (Bax, caspase-3), and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β TNF-α), all of which are significantly upregulated [50, 51].

High-fat and high-sugar

Researchers demonstrated that a high-sugar, high-fat (HFHS) diet elevates the risk of tumorigenesis, obesity, and cardiovascular pathology, while also provoking ovarian granulosa cell senescence and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI). This occurs through suppression of endogenous miR-146, activation of the DAB2IP/ASK1/p38 MAPK signaling cascade, and induction of γ-H2A.X phosphorylation [52].

Li et al. [53]successfully induced a POF model by feeding mice a high-fat diet (8 g/kg) and 200 µl of 30% high-fructose solution for two consecutive months. Results demonstrated that the HFHS diet not only caused significant reproductive dysfunction in mice—including lipid metabolism and sex hormone abnormalities, follicular atresia, and irregular estrous cycles—but also induced POF by activating oxidative phosphorylation signaling pathways in the ovarian tissue’s theca cells, granulosa cells, and cumulus cell clusters. Furthermore, reducing granulosa cell PANoptosis and modulating the “gut-ovary axis” to alter the distribution and structure of gut microbiota improved symptoms in HFHS-induced POF mice [54].

Immune factors

Among various autoimmune conditions, the ovaries are frequently targeted as the primary organs of immune-mediated pathology. Approximately 20% of patients with POF have concomitant autoimmune disorders, most commonly affecting the thyroid, adrenal glands, and pancreas. Autoimmune ovarian damage is mediated through multiple immunopathogenic mechanisms, including T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity, autoantibody production by B lymphocytes, and diminished natural killer (NK) cell activity and numbers [55]. In autoimmune POI research, various modeling strategies are utilized: (1) induction of active immunity using zona pellucida 3 (ZP3) peptides to simulate antibody-mediated ovarian destruction; (2) neonatal thymectomy in animal models to elucidate immune tolerance disruption; (3) gene-editing techniques to develop targeted models.

Zona pellucida 3 peptide

The zona pellucida (ZP) constitutes a glycoprotein matrix enveloping mammalian oocytes, functioning as an ovary-specific antigen critical for folliculogenesis and oocyte maturation. ZP glycoproteins are synthesized exclusively by oocytes and include three glycosylated proteins: ZP1, ZP2, and ZP3. As early as 1992, homologous analysis demonstrated approximately 67% sequence similarity between murine and human ZP3 proteins. Immunization of mice with porcine ZP3 (pZP3) induces the production of anti-ZP3 autoantibodies, which bind to ovarian ZP3, resulting in follicular destruction, ovarian insufficiency, amenorrhea, and autoimmune oophoritis [56, 57].

Yin et al. [57]administered subcutaneous injections of 50 nmol/L ZP3 combined with complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) (Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37RA strain, 0.16 mg/mouse) to mice. followed by a second injection of 50 nmol/L ZP3 with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA) (Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37RA strain, 0.16 mg/mouse) two weeks later, establishing an autoimmune-induced POI mouse model. In this investigation, the researchers identified that microRNA-21 facilitates ovarian recovery in POI mice by inhibiting the PTEN/PI3K/AKT/Foxo3a signaling cascade and enhancing CD8⁺, CD28⁻ T lymphocyte populations. These insights may offer novel therapeutic targets for subsequent mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based clinical interventions in humans.

This model is extensively employed to investigate T cell-mediated autoimmune pathogenesis within thymus-intact murine systems. Its strengths include high target specificity, controllability, procedural simplicity, and ovarian tissue morphology that closely parallels human autoimmune POI. Nonetheless, the extended duration of model induction and individual variability in immune responses may affect the reproducibility and stability of the model [58].

Thymectomy

As the primary site for T cell ontogeny, differentiation, and maturation, the thymus functions as a critical central lymphoid organ in humans. Post-thymectomy, there is a reduction in total T lymphocyte count and T cell subpopulations, accompanied by impaired humoral immune responses. This impairment compromises immune surveillance and homeostasis, leading to disrupted immune equilibrium and potential induction of systemic autoimmune responses [59].

In murine models, neonatal thymectomy performed within 2–4 days postpartum (commonly on day 3) induces systemic immunodeficiency, precipitating organ-specific autoimmune phenomena targeting tissues such as the ovaries and thyroid gland. The autoimmune multi-glandular pathology observed in these models recapitulates human autoimmune POI features, including lymphocytic, plasma cell, and mononuclear-macrophage infiltration of ovarian follicles and stroma, ovarian morphological disorganization, and follicular atresia. Therefore, this model provides a valuable platform for investigating the immunopathogenesis of female autoimmune POI and for the development of targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Nonetheless, the technical complexities of establishing this model via thymectomy—requiring advanced surgical skills—pose substantial procedural challenges and are associated with high perioperative mortality rates, thereby limiting its broad application and widespread adoption [58, 60].

Other factors

Hypertension

It remains uncertain whether hypertension contributes to increased ovarian follicular apoptosis and subsequent reproductive impairment. One experimental model demonstrates that salt-sensitive hypertensive rodents (DahlS) develop a chronic hypertensive phenotype characterized by persistently elevated blood pressure. As hypertensive pathology advances, these animals display decreased ovarian volume, extended estrous cyclicity, and heightened apoptosis of granulosa and luteal cells [61]. This model closely reflects the pathophysiological progression seen in humans, providing a clinically relevant platform for investigating hypertensive effects on gonadal function. Currently, research on hypertensive models of POF lacks sufficient depth. Future research should aim to refine these models to improve reproductive health management in hypertensive women and facilitate the discovery of novel therapeutic interventions.

Psychological stress

A clinical investigation demonstrates a strong association between psychological stress and POI. Data reveals that elevated psychological stress levels are linked to reduced antral follicle count (AFC) and AMH concentrations, with stress exhibiting an inverse relationship with ovarian reserve specifically among women with higher educational attainment [62].

By sequentially applying acoustic, photic, and electrical stimulations at varying frequencies to establish a stress-induced animal model, the findings demonstrated that psychological stress markedly decreased biomolecular concentrations—includingβ-endorphin, IL-1, NOS, and GnRH—in the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. This reduction led to decreased levels of E2 in the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis. This disruption impaired the feedback regulatory function of the target gland layer within the HPO axis on the pituitary gland, thereby destabilizing the neuroendocrine-immune biomolecular network and culminating in POF [63].Additionally, a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) paradigm was established over 35 consecutive days, involving alternating daily protocols of water and food deprivation, forced swimming, noise exposure, and foot shock. Results demonstrate that CUMS not only induces behavioral modifications—such as heightened anxiety and depression—but also precipitates ovarian endocrine dysfunction and premature ovarian failure [64].

These findings emphasize the long-term deleterious effects of sustained psychological stress on female reproductive function and underscore the significance of emotional regulation in mitigating endocrine and ovarian pathologies. Moreover, investigation into stress-related impacts on ovarian reserve markers should consider social determinants to facilitate stratified analysis across diverse female populations.

Common evaluation indicators of the POF animal model

According to CALAS guidelines, validation of experimental models for POF necessitates comprehensive evaluation of their behavioral phenotypes, tissue histology, cellular composition, and molecular signatures. While ovarian biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic approach for POF, its invasive nature and potential for ovarian tissue damage make it ethically and practically unsuitable for routine use in human subjects [58]. Current preclinical assessments of POF models predominantly include reproductive performance metrics, hormonal profiles, ovarian morphology, follicle counts, and specific biomarkers. It is important to note that these molecular alterations reflect the functional decline characteristic of POF rather than its initial pathogenic mechanisms.

POF, characterized by diminished ovarian reserve and compromised fertility, is evaluated through reproductive output measures, such as litter quantity. In animal models, impaired reproductive capacity is evidenced by decreased fertility indices, litter sizes, and pup weights [65].

Assessment of ovarian reserve involves both direct histological analysis and indirect biochemical markers. Histologically, parameters such as ovarian weight and volume, follicle counts across developmental stages, corpus luteum number, and estrous cycle duration are indicative of follicular depletion. POF models typically exhibit ovaries with reduced size, lower follicle counts (primordial, primary and sinusoidal), increased atretic and apoptotic follicles, decreased corpus luteum presence, diminished ovulation frequency, and prolonged estrous cycles. Correspondingly, serum hormonal levels serve as indirect measures, with decreased AMH and E2, and elevated FSH and luteinizing hormone (LH) concentrations providing insights into ovarian reserve status and reproductive potential [24, 42].

Pathophysiologically, POF development is associated with elevated oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ROS production. In experimental models, this manifests as increased expression of lipid peroxidation products such as MDA, oxidative stress markers including 4-HNE, 8-OHdG, and NTY, along with cellular senescence indicators like P16 protein, pro-apoptotic factors such as Bax and caspase-3, and inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [16, 17].

Conclusion

The underlying causes of POF are complex and multifactorial, and involve a range of factors such as medical factors, autoimmune responses, genetic mutations or defects, environmental exposures, and metabolic disturbances. These factors not only contribute to the early depletion of the ovarian follicular reserve but also initiate a cascade of physiological disturbances, including hypoestrogenism, a hypergonadotropic state, and a series of long-term health complications. These conditions significantly impair the physical health, mental well-being, and overall quality of life of affected individuals. Consequently, it is crucial to thoroughly understand the underlying mechanisms driving POF and to develop effective strategies for prevention and treatment. Animal models are indispensable in POF research because of their unique advantages, allowing for controlled experimentation and mechanistic studies.

In this review, we systematically categorize and analyze various methods used to establish different POF animal models (refer to Table 1; Fig. 1 for detailed information), aiming to provide a solid scientific foundation for researchers across diverse disciplines. Moving forward, research efforts should prioritize the development of multifactorial, joint models that more accurately reflect the complex etiology of human POF, as well as the creation of more precise, noninvasive evaluation systems for these models.
