From Show Programmes to Data: Designing a Workflow to Make Performing Arts Ephemera Accessible Through Language Models
Clarisse Bardiot, Pierre-Carl Langlais, Bernard Jacquemin, Jacob Hart, Antonios Lagarias, Nicolas Foucault, Aurélie Lemaître-Legargeant, Jeanne Fras

Abstract
Many heritage institutions hold extensive collections of theatre programmes, which remain largely underused due to their complex layouts and lack of structured metadata. In this paper, we present a workflow for transforming such documents into structured data using a combination of multimodal large language models (LLMs), an ontology-based reasoning model, and a custom extension of the Linked Art framework. We show how vision-language models can accurately parse and transcribe born-digital and digitised programmes, achieving over 98% of correct extraction. To overcome the challenges of semantic annotation, we train a reasoning model (POntAvignon) using reinforcement learning with both formal and semantic rewards. This approach enables automated RDF triple generation and supports alignment with existing knowledge graphs. Through a case study based on the Festival d’Avignon corpus, we demonstrate the potential for large-scale, ontology-driven analysis of performing arts data. Our results open new possibilities for interoperable, explainable, and sustainable computational theatre historiography. keywords: Large language models (LLMs), cultural heritage, performing arts, ontology, workflow.



Show programmes constitute one of the most crucial sources for theatre
historians. Despite being often overlooked, in the absence of other
documentation, they frequently serve as the sole evidence that a
performance occurred. Distributed to spectators on the day of the
performance and sometimes hastily printed, these materials were not
intended for preservation. Yet these ephemera, collected across numerous
heritage institutions
[
4
]
, provide invaluable direct data about performances – including
cast and crew details, profession names, venue information, and
last-minute modifications – while simultaneously revealing artistic
networks, editorial strategies, and institutional dynamics.

Although digitization has significantly improved access to these
materials, substantial methodological challenges remain in order to
access their content. Existing databases often reduce show programmes to
minimal metadata, failing to capture the rich contextual information
available in literal transcriptions. Moreover, if show programmes are a
source to numerous databases documenting performances, productions, and
artistic collaborations
[
3
,
8
,
10
,
17
]
, the lack of standardization and interoperability between these
resources limits their potential for comprehensive research in
performance historiography. How can we harness recent advances in
language modelling and ontology-driven data representation to extract,
structure, and analyse the rich historical and social data embedded in
show programmes? This challenge extends beyond technical processing to
address a broader epistemological issue: how can we create the
conditions for large-scale comparative research in performing arts
history by establishing standardised, interoperable methodologies that
could transform the field’s analytical capabilities?

Using the show programmes from the Festival d’Avignon (1947–present) –
one of the world’s most significant performing arts events – this paper
presents a computational workflow that combines large multimodal models
(LMMs) with an extension of the Linked Art ontology for performing arts
to systematically extract and structure this rich historical data.

Our methodology consists of three main stages: first, we segment show
programmes into individual pages through pre-processing; second, we
employ the LLM Claude Sonnet 3.7 to transform pages into text through
images, capturing both conventional printed text and text embedded in
sophisticated layouts that typically challenge standard optical
character recognition (OCR) systems; third, we utilize our performing
arts extension of the Linked Art ontology to train a specialised small
language model based on Pleias – the only EU AI Act compliant’s LLM
trained exclusively on open data – to generate RDF triplets for storage
in a Wikibase infrastructure (Figure

From Pdf to text

With over 2,000 performances staged by international companies since its
inception, the Festival d’Avignon has featured essential artists of the
post-WWII era, including Mnouchkine, Brook, Wilson, Chéreau, Bausch,
Ostermeier, and many others across theatre, dance, performance, and
circus. It serves as an ideal laboratory for examining the evolution of
contemporary performing arts practices and networks.

Our corpus comprises 1,535 show programmes, covering 45 years across two
distinct periods of the Festival d’Avignon’s history (1971-2022), with a
total of 8,408 pages to process. This corpus is exhaustive with few
exceptions: approximately fifty show programmes could not be identified
in the archival collections of the Bibliothèque nationale de France
(BnF) and the Festival d’Avignon. This periodisation reflects both
archival availability—the early years (1947-1970) are currently being
digitised—and exceptional circumstances, as the 2003 festival was
cancelled due to a strike and the 2020 festival due to COVID-19. These
programmes have undergone major evolution in graphics, structure,
content, and format, creating substantial variety in dimensions and
visual content.

Defining an ontology for performing
arts

At first glance, converting theatre programmes into structured data may
appear to be a straightforward task, given that categories such as a
show’s title, author, director, cast, and venue seem clearly defined.
However, these categories often pose significant definitional challenges
and risk anachronisms, particularly regarding performing arts
professions—starting with the very notion of “director”

[
3
]
.
Converting such data while maintaining semantic richness often requires
compromises that risk diminishing content quality. Moreover,
performances represent complex artistic objects involving numerous
individuals in diverse roles, programmed in different venues and dates
and generating various forms of documentation, such as written
publications, photographs, or films. They may also go through different
states, such as works “in progress”. These complexities have impeded
the development of unified ontological models for performance
description, complicating interoperability among existing databases

[
3
,
8
,
10
,
17
]
.
The underlying models of these databases are frequently
poorly documented or remain unpublished, further limiting their
implementation in new projects.

Multiple models have emerged to structure performing arts data, each
presenting distinct advantages and limitations. Currently, two reference
models coexist for the semantic description of cultural universes: the
LRMoo model
[
1
]
, which focuses on written productions while
referring to the intellectual process of creating works of all types,
and emanates from the library environment; and the CIDOC CRM model

[
7
]
,
originating from the museum sector, which initially focused on the
description of museum objects, but opened up in 2014 to less tangible
cultural objects related to intangible heritage

[
13
]
. While the LRMoo model is essentially confined to the
library environment from which it originates—although its very
complete but also very complex expressiveness allows it to address all
areas of culture—the CIDOC CRM has established itself as the reference
in other institutional cultural environments. However, it requires its
users to make a significant investment both in the development of
semantic web techniques and languages, and in learning a rich and
complex model to implement, consisting of more than 80 classes and 160
properties, and whose documentation and examples are more intended for
developers than for cultural content managers.

Specialised frameworks address particular performing arts requirements.
AusStage
[
5
]

adapts CIDOC CRM for Australian performing arts, emphasizing
events, venues, and participants within national cultural heritage
contexts. The Europeana Data Model (EDM) leverages FRBR and CIDOC CRM
for metadata aggregation across European cultural institutions. While
not exclusively focused on performing arts, its modular design and
linked open data alignment demonstrate adaptability for complex cultural
phenomena. The CapData Opera ontology

[
16
]
, developed by the
Réunion des Opéras de France
, employs
Semantic Web technologies to enhance interoperability among opera houses
through schema.org alignment and RDF-based structures. This approach
shares philosophical similarities with Canada’s Linked Digital Future
for the Performing Arts initiative, both promoting online work
discoverability despite different formalizations. However, promising
theoretical foundations, particularly regarding RDF principles and
semantic web potential in performing arts
[
9
]
,
face practical implementation challenges. These models, despite
individual merits, often target specific archives or datasets. While
accommodating collection-specific features and research questions, they
lack genericity and consequently interoperability.

The ontological model we propose for the description of live
performances must meet several imperatives at the conceptual,
operational, and informational levels. To describe the performing arts,
varied dimensions are necessary, such as people, institutions, places
and dates. At a conceptual level, the performing arts are grounded in an
event-based reality: the show. To describe the various aspects of a
performance, we propose a three-level schema (see Figure
4
). At its center
is a specific production (B), conceived as an event-based object. This
refers to the staging of a particular play at a specific venue, within a
given theatre season or festival—for example, Séverine Chavrier’s
staging of William Faulkner’s
Absalom, Absalom!
, at the Festival
d’Avignon in 2024. This production is linked to a conceptual object (A),
which represents a director’s idea for staging a particular work before
this being materialised in a specific production. Object A is therefore
unique to a given director and moment in time, and it may be associated
with multiple instances of object B, since the same show can be
programmed in different venues. We also establish links between the
conceptual object A and external entities that have influenced or
inspired it, such as a specific author or the dramatic text on which the
play is based. Finally, for each production (B), we associate all
corresponding shows (C), including detailed information for each date,
such as ticket prices or cast changes.

Through this three-level schema, we are able to distinguish between the
produced performance and its various representations (shows with unique
dates) as well as the context in which the performance is
programmed—even though these realities all fall under an event-based
characteristic.

heritage institutions and museums, researchers in the performing arts

specificities of the semantic web or the specialised vocabulary used in
data modelling. Despite the complexity of the performing arts field, the
ontology’s structure must thus remain accessible to users without
technical expertise or advanced knowledge in data modelling.

From ontology to policy – training a reasoning model for semantic data
annotation

After the initial design for the ontologies had been drafted, we have
been faced by the typical challenge of any semantic data project: the
“knowledge acquisition bottleneck”, as the “manual encoding of such
information from human experts’ knowledge [is] very slow and
time-consuming”
[
11
]
. The human processing
of 1500 individual programs encompassing thousands of staging events and
associated entities would easily take months.

Instead, we experimented with a new method of structured generation
relying not only on a generalist language model but on a new reasoning
model specially trained for the transformation of Avignon texts into
structured data following on our ontology. While there has been a
renewal of interest in knowledge graph methods in AI research as a
retrieval tool, following on projects like GraphRAG from Microsoft, the
use of generative models for complex semantic data annotation proves
still challenging:

•

LLMs are not pretrained on semantic data. Large dumps of XML and RDF
files are usually missed by web scraping archives like Common Crawl.
Closed-source models like ChatGPT or Claude are not documenting their
training sources. Yet, they display a lack of familiarity with the RDF
format and, even, major ontologies: in our initial tests, Claude
routinely hallucinates Wikidata property names and identifiers.

•

In-context learning is not a reliable solution either. Complex
ontologies cannot really fit into the usable context window: while
language models can in theory process more than 100,000 tokens, this
is the by-product of a late “context expansion” process. In
practice, for tasks requiring a significant amount of accuracy and
precision, the exploitable context window is much lower.

Over the last year, research progress in large and small language models
has been largely driven by the development of “reasoning” or
“thinking” models. All state of the art models currently include a
preliminary step (the “draft”) that allow the models to process more
complex problems and/or perform intermediary actions. Major examples
include the “O” series of models from OpenAI, R1 from DeepSeek

[
6
]
or, most recently, K2 from Moonshot.

For our experiments, we have taken advantage of the parallel development
of a reasoning model for transforming semantic data by Pleias. Pleias is
a French-German AI lab specializing in pre-training and post-training
LLMs on open data. Since February 2025, it has partnered with the
Wikimedia Foundation to develop ethical models on data from Wikimedia
project, which lead to the training of a ‘‘Wikidata’’ reasoning model:
a 350 million transformers able to map any unstructured text in the main
European languages into a Wikidata structured item, using systematically
the 4,000 main properties developed over a decade by the community. The
model was trained using a methodology of ‘‘mid-training’’ at Jean Zay,
the leading GPU public cluster in France
32
32

32

https://www.cnrs.fr/en/press/jean-zay-supercomputer-france-has-increased-its-ai-dedicated-resources-fourfold
,
combining 10 million samples of Wikidata items with synthetic text as
input.

The Linked Art ontology is already significantly aligned on Wikidata,
with most if not all properties having clear Wikidata equivalent. Yet,
our input sources, the digitised Avignon programmes, encoded semantic
information in a very specific way, and we had to empirically introduce
further pragmatic rules to control for a variety of edge cases.

We adapted the Pleias-Wikidata model using the standard recipe for
reasoning models since the release of DeepSeek R1: online reinforcement
learning with GRPO. Concretely, the model is submitted to a series of
exercises and has to come up with a “right” answer. Since we use an
online reinforcement learning approach, the model is continuously
updated throughout training as it solves more exercises. Very crucially,
only the final output of the model is evaluated, not the intermediary
reasoning traces which can evolve significantly throughout the
reinforcement learning process, as the reasoning model tries to come up
with various solutions.

This approach has recently become popular for AI research in verifiable
fields like math or programming, as training dataset frequently include
a grounded non-ambiguous answer. There is yet very little comparable
research in the humanities and other fields where verification cannot
easily be automated. As of mid-2025, we release one of the first ever
examples of an RL-trained reasoning model for a humanities use case.

Semantic data annotation turns out to be a semi-verifiable task, and the
field has actually a very long-standing relationship with symbolic logic
methods. As a result, we came up with a combination of two rewards which
compose a reinforcement learning
policy
:

•

A formal reward: the model should always use exclusively properties
from our pre-defined set, only use them once and not deviate from
it.

•

A soft reward: to assess the correctness of information, we used a
judge model (Gemma 12b). Our approach leverages here the fact that
language models are better at evaluation than generation. We used an
iterate method by first designing a prompt incorporating our ontology
rules. This type of soft verification is partly inspired by the

self-principled critique tuning
from DeepSeek

[
14
]
.

Through the initial experiments, we increasingly came up with a set of
validated examples that subsequently became our
ground truth
. For
the final reinforcement learning training, we simply prompted the judge
model to assess the divergence between the model submitted prompts and
the ground truth. Our final setting included the following training
process:

•

The model is trained over 600 steps.

•

At each step, it receives four different “problems” (different
Avignon programmes).

•

The model generates 8 alternative drafts.

•

Each draft is first filtered by our formal reward. Any draft
containing a property that is not part of our set of predefined is
graded with a zero reward.

•

Once they pass the initial test, the drafts are assessed by the judge
model using a grade notation from 0 to 10.

Figure

shows our final training cycle falls neatly into two separate
phases. In the first phase, the formal reward drops aggressively for generations
that use any of the properties beyond the predefined set. Consequently,
the initial mean grade starts very low but climbs quickly once the model
settles on the expected formal design. During the second phase, nearly
all generations are valid and accepted and training becomes more gradual
and reasoning intensive. At each step, the judge model compares each
data annotation submitted to the ground truth: this is a “soft”
comparison so as to still advantage partial success (like incomplete
titles). The best rated drafts tacitly reward the “winning” reasoning
strategies and, if the training is successful, they will become part of
the internal policy of the model.

We release the first preview of the model on HuggingFace under the name
POntAvignon

33

https://huggingface.co/LLMDH/POntAvignon
.
Due to being built on top of a model primarily trained on Wikidata, the
ontology is immediately compliant with Wikidata and, as shown in
Appendix

,
the reasoning traces explicitly call for the Wikidata properties
IDs. The output is an adapted version of RDF triplets optimized for LLM
generation, with less token repetitions.

The model is still under active development: it only features the
“work” part of the ontology, the one that is fully finalized to this
date. While the data can be immediately transformed into a structured
data representation (either JSON or RDF), it is still a “raw” output
that requires further verification and, crucially, cross-checking with
internal or external statements.

A more ambitious project will be to make it a generic structured data
generation tool for theatre programmes. If it is ever attainable with
current technology, accurate automation would require two major
developments:

•

Increasing the number and the diversity of the sources beyond the
Avignon show programmes, especially in additional languages (as
majority of our sources are in French).

•

Develop internal “tool use” abilities within the model to perform
data reconciliation and, through this, verification. In practice, this
could include built-in connections to Wikidata APIs.

4
Future Work

Our workflow demonstrates the potential of combining large language
models with domain-specific ontologies to transform cultural heritage
materials into structured data while preserving textual integrity and
semantic richness. We used a Large Language Model (Claude) and trained a
Small Reasoning Model (POntAvignon)
34
34

34

https://huggingface.co/LLMDH/POntAvignon

to extract data from historical sources according to a knowledge graph.
This approach limits hallucinations by the models, preserves the
integrity of the data and allows access to the literal transcription of
the text as well as a normalisation of the data which is documented and
allows for alignment with other knowledge graphs. Beyond technical
considerations, our findings suggest that working with LLMs forces a
modular rethinking of research questions. The token limitations inherent
to most models require decomposing complex questions into smaller units.

Processing over 8,000 pages with 99.2% segmentation accuracy and 0.98
Levenshtein ratio for text extraction confirms that our
approach can effectively handle historical
documents. However, the instability of proprietary LLMs presents
significant reproducibility concerns. Model performance may change
following updates, and outputs are non-deterministic by design,
complicating the validation process.

Our extension of the Linked Art ontology has sought to model the
complexity of theatrical events via a three-tier Work–Production–Show
structure. The development involved collaboration between the Linked Art
community, theatre scholars, and cultural heritage institutions. The
alignment with Getty vocabularies and JSON-LD format enhances
accessibility for non-technical users. Nevertheless, additional
controlled vocabularies beyond Getty’s offerings proved necessary,
particularly for theatrical professions. Future work requires systematic
alignment with external authority files (e.g. VIAF, ISNI), development
of missing vocabulary components and enriching the ontology with
domain-specific concepts not yet covered.

Training a specialized reasoning model using the Pleias framework
represents a novel approach to semantic annotation. The combination of
formal rewards (property adherence) and soft rewards (content accuracy)
effectively reduced hallucinations while maintaining quality. The
two-phase training process—initial constraint learning followed by
reasoning refinement—suggests that domain-specific fine-tuning
significantly improves performance. Our work demonstrates ontologies as
foundations for reasoning models in humanities research. Unlike many AI
applications, semantic annotation represents a semi-verifiable domain
with established standards, aligning it with mathematical tasks where
automated verification is possible. However, it is important to stress
that this remains early-stage and experimental work. The underlying
toolkits (e.g. TRL) are still under development, and best practices for
training such models in non-verifiable domains are far from established.
The potential for ontology-driven reasoning models in humanities
research deserves further exploration.

The methodology developed here opens promising avenues for application.
We will present two examples to conclude. First, building on the example
of the Festival d’Avignon, it is possible to extend the corpus to other
venue programmes and potentially to season programmes. Theatre
programmes, too numerous in heritage collections to be subjected to
individualized metadata work, could not only be indexed, but their
entire content would become accessible in a structured manner. New
research questions could then be addressed, such as the impact of the
Festival on an artistic career, the internationalization of the
Festival, or comparisons with other festivals. Second, this approach
paves the way for the creation of
catalogues raisonnés
for
theatre artists, similar to those that exist for visual artists. While
in the field of art history, the creation of such catalogues is well
established, it remains nonexistent in the performing arts, where the
question of touring makes this work particularly complex. Such

catalogues raisonnés
offer the possibility of renewing theatre
historiography.

Appendix D
Final Claude prompt

This image corresponds to a page in a theater program.

You will perform a complete and detailed OCR analysis of this image.

DO NOT generate or use any code, do not use Tesseract or pytesseract ---

only use your internal vision to read the image.

Extract all visible text WITHOUT changing it.

DO NOT summarize, paraphrase, or infer missing text. DO NOT invent

people’s names.

Retain all spacing, punctuation, and formatting exactly as in the image.

You MUST follow the following rules when dealing with these specific

cases:

- All titles are indicated with a preceding \# with no hierarchy

(example: \# Title content). If the title contains line breaks,

recompose the title into one single line.

- Exponents are given as lower case roman characters (example: XVe)

- Footnotes are given as special characters (example: word¹)

- When you detect the number of a page, return it at the start of the

text using the word ‘‘PAGE’’’ followed by the number (example: PAGE 13)

- Do not describe images or logos, simply extract any text within them.

- Do not insert special characters for things like columns and page

separation.

- For distribution: if you detect a table-like structure, typography

variation that could be interpreted as a function/name or name/function

pairing, or a string of points linking a function and name, apply the

following structure:

Element 1: Associated element 1, Associated element 2

Element 2 and Element 3, description: Associated element 3 and

Associated element 4 (Note that in the case of enumeration, there are

NO line breaks, there are commas)

- If you detect any other function/name or name/function pairs that are

separated by a character like \text{\textbar} or /, change the

character to a : (example: element 1 : associated element 1)

- If you detect a completely empty page, or if the page is much too

faint or blurry to confidently transcribe, simply return: [UNABLE TO

TRANSCRIBE]

- Some pages may include texts of different languages, or old languages

(such as old French). It is very important to NOT CHANGE the content,

simply transcribe the letters you see.

Check the overall readability of your transcription and make sure it

reflects the structure of the original document.

The output should be ONLY the transcribed content (DO NOT add comments

like "this is a theatre program" or "I was unable to reliably

transcribe this").
