Clinical outcomes of 27-gauge needle-guided flanged intrascleral haptic fixation using the yamane technique in various indications
Authors: Alper Can Yilmaz, Mevlut Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo describe the clinical outcomes and complications of intrascleral haptic fixation using the Yamane technique in patients with aphakia and intraocular lens (IOL) dislocation.MethodsThis study retrospectively examined 37 eyes of 37 patients who underwent Yamane surgery for aphakia or a dislocated IOL at a tertiary teaching hospital and had a follow-up period longer than 6 months. Demographic data, Yamane indication, ocular comorbidities, and additional surgical procedures performed were recorded. Preoperative and postoperative distance visual acuity (VA), refractive results, intraocular pressure (IOP), central macular thickness measurements, and postoperative complications were recorded.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 68.5 ± 13.1 years, and the mean follow-up period was 9.05 ± 3.36 months. The Yamane indication was aphakia in 24 (64.9%) patients and IOL dislocation in 13 (35.1%) patients. Anterior vitrectomy was performed in 19 patients (51.4%), capsular tension ring extraction in 2 patients (5.4%), IOL extraction in 7 patients (19.0%), and pars plana vitrectomy in 8 patients (21.6%). The mean subjective refraction improved from 8.70 ± 4.83 to -1.36 ± 0.41 diopter (D) at the final visit (p = 0.001). The mean cylindrical refraction was − 1.41 ± 1.05 D in the postoperative period. The mean best corrected VA improved from 0.53 ± 0.22 to 0.15 ± 0.09 logMAR (p = 0.001). Postoperatively, corneal edema was observed in two patients (5.4%), cystoid macular edema in two patients (5.4%), epiretinal membrane in two patients (5.4%), hypotony in one patient (2.7%), elevated IOP in two patients (5.4%), and iris capture of IOL in one patient (2.7%).ConclusionThe Yamane technique has provided favorable visual and refractive outcomes with manageable postoperative complications in patients with aphakia and IOL dislocation, including complex cases requiring additional intervention such as vitreoretinal surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04965-9.

Introduction

Lack of integrity of the lens capsule or zonules prevents intraocular lens (IOL) implantation into the capsular bag or ciliary sulcus. In this case, anterior chamber IOLs, iris-claw IOLs, sutured, or in recent years, sutureless scleral fixation methods, which have come to the fore with new techniques, are used in accordance with the available possibilities and surgical experience [1–3]. It is known that anterior chamber IOLs can lead to serious complications such as corneal endothelial cell loss, bullous keratopathy, iridocorneal angle problems, high intraocular pressure (IOP), chronic inflammation, cystoid macular edema (CME), and retinal detachment [4, 5]. In iris-fixated IOLs, complications such as pupil ovalization, iris trauma, localized iris atrophy, hyphema, vitreous hemorrhage, inflammation, CME, high IOP, and IOL dislocation can occur [4, 5]. Sutured scleral fixation methods can cause suture-related complications such as suture erosion and breakage [6].

The Yamane method, which involves transconjunctival intrascleral haptic fixation and eliminates the need for conjunctival dissection, scleral flap and sutures, was first described in 2017 and has become widely applied over time with several modifications [7]. In this method, the IOL haptic tips are brought out of the eye through the scleral tunnel, flanged with thermal cautery, and then placed into the scleral tunnel. In addition to being a less invasive method compared to traditional methods, it has been reported to yield comparable outcomes in terms of complications as well [8, 9]. With the widespread use of the technique, studies have been reported that demonstrate its effectiveness on visual results and IOL stability [10, 11].

The Yamane technique can be combined with major surgical procedures such as pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) and keratoplasty in patients with aphakic or dislocated IOLs due to a number of reasons such as complicated cataract surgery, trauma, and pseudoexfoliation [12, 13]. The Yamane technique has advantages such as short surgical time, good IOL stabilization, and can be applied with combined surgeries [14]. However, since it is a relatively new technique, it is important to contribute the reliability and efficacy results to the literature. The aim of this study is to evaluate the visual and refractive outcomes and postoperative complications of the Yamane technique, applied alone or in combination with additional surgical procedures such as PPV, in patients with aphakia and dislocated IOLs.

Methods

In this retrospective study, data from 37 eyes of 37 consecutive patients over the age of 18 who underwent intrascleral haptic fixation with 27-gauge (G) needle guidance using the Yamane technique at the Department of Ophthalmology, Gulhane Faculty of Medicine between June 2024 and September 2025 and had at least 6 months of follow-up were examined. Exclusion criteria included a follow-up period shorter than 6 months, incomplete clinical data, trauma-related scleral damage, inflammatory or connective tissue diseases that could cause scleral damage, the presence of uncontrolled glaucoma, retinal detachment, severe macular pathology affecting vision, optic neuropathy, and active intraocular inflammation. All 37 eyes of 37 patients who met the inclusion criteria and completed at least 6 months of follow-up were included in the final analysis. No patients were excluded from the study for any reason after surgery. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (approval number 2025 − 616). Prior to Yamane surgery, patients were given detailed information about the procedure and its associated risks, and their written informed consent was obtained.

All patients underwent ophthalmological examinations before and after Yamane surgery, including refraction measurements, uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) and best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), IOP measurements with pneumotonometry, and anterior and posterior segment examination with slit-lamp biomicroscopy. Demographic data, ocular biometric measurements (axial length, IOL power), Yamane indication, ocular comorbidities, additional surgical procedures performed (anterior vitrectomy, PPV, IOL extraction, capsular tension ring extraction), follow-up periods, and postoperative complications were recorded. Central macular thickness (CMT) was also measured before and after surgery using spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Patients were divided into two groups according to the Yamane indication: aphakia (Group 1) and dislocated IOL (Group 2). The causes of aphakia were also determined (posterior capsular rupture without sulcus support, trauma, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, and zonular dialysis).

All patients and both groups were analyzed by comparing preoperative and postoperative subjective refraction, UCVA, BCVA, CMT, and IOP measurements. In addition, postoperative complications were compared between the two groups.

Preoperative preparation and surgical technique

IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany) was used for IOL power calculation, the SRK/T formula was preferred as the standard, and the Hoffer Q formula was applied to eyes with an axial length shorter than 22 mm. The target refraction was determined as -0.50 diopters (D), calculated based on the position of the IOL within the capsular bag. All patients were implanted with the Sensar AR40e (Johnson & Johnson, Santa Ana, California, USA) three-piece IOL. This hydrophobic acrylic biconvex IOL has a 6 mm optic diameter and a 13 mm total length, and modified C-loop polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) haptics with a 5° angulation.

During surgery, according to the standard technique described by Yamane et al. [7], conjunctival areas 2 mm posterior to the limbus were marked at the 3 and 9 o’clock positions with a sterile surgical pen to create angled sclerotomies. Stable IOP was achieved using an anterior chamber maintainer or with infusion support via a 25G trocar in the inferior temporal region. Corneal side ports were created at the 2 and 10 o’clock positions using a 20G microvitreoretinal blade. Anterior vitrectomy was performed when necessary. Then, a clear corneal incision was made at 12 o’clock using a 2.65 mm keratome, and a three-piece IOL (Sensar AR40e, AMO) was implanted into the anterior chamber using an injector. The trailing haptic was kept outside to prevent the IOL from falling into the vitreous cavity. A transconjunctival angled scleral tunnel was created from the pre-marked areas using a 27G needle, and the leading haptic was inserted into the needle lumen using 25G serrated forceps. The 27G needle has an inner diameter of 0.22 mm, which is large enough to accommodate the haptics of commonly used 3-piece IOLs (0.14–0.17 mm). The IOL haptic was brought out of the eye through this scleral tunnel, and a flange was created at the haptic tip using an ophthalmic cautery device. The trailing haptic, which was left outside, was brought into the anterior chamber. The same procedure was repeated at the marked site located 180 degrees from the first sclerotomy, the trailing haptic was externalized and a flange was created. After the haptics were pushed into the scleral tunnel and secured, the IOL was positioned in its final, stable location (Fig. 1). The 27G needle has an outer diameter of 0.4 mm, which is small enough to allow spontaneous sealing of the angled sclerotomy. Corneal incisions were closed with stromal hydration or, if necessary, with 10 − 0 nylon sutures. At the end of surgery, cefuroxime (Aprokam®) was injected into the anterior chamber; for patients undergoing PPV, a subconjunctival gentamicin/dexamethasone injection was administered.

Dislocated monoblock acrylic IOLs were removed using folding or cutting techniques. In one patient, a PMMA monoblock IOL was removed through a 6.0 mm sclerocorneal incision. In patients undergoing PPV, a 25G sclerotomy was performed using the Constellation Vision System (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX). Trocars were removed after corneal wound integrity was ensured. After wound integrity and leakage control, corneal and transconjunctival sclerotomy incisions were closed without sutures in most cases, although sutures were placed when required. Additional surgical procedures performed besides the Yamane technique were recorded. All surgeries were performed by two experienced surgeons (A.C.Y and M.Y.) using the same standardized technique (see video, supplemental digital content).

Postoperatively, each patient received a topical antibiotic/steroid combination containing 0.5% moxifloxacin and 0.1% dexamethasone (four times daily) and 0.1% nepafenac ophthalmic drops (four times daily) for one month. Data from the last visit of patients with at least six months of follow-up were considered postoperative data, and postoperative complications were recorded. Postoperatively, IOP was defined as hypotony if it was less than 6 mmHg and as elevated IOP if it was greater than 25 mmHg.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk (n < 50) and Skewness-Kurtosis tests were used to determine whether the continuous measurements in the study were normally distributed, and since the measurements showed normal distribution, parametric tests were applied. Descriptive statistics for the variables in the study were expressed as mean, standard deviation, number (n), and percentage (%). Independent samples t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare measurements according to categorical factors. Following the analysis of variance, Duncan’s post hoc test was used to identify different groups. Paired samples t-test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative measurements. The chi-square test was used to determine the relationships among categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to determine the relationships among continuous measurements. P < 0.05 was considered significant, and the SPSS (IBM SPSS for Windows, version 26) statistical package program was used for the analysis.

Results

The study included 37 eyes from 37 patients, 19 (51.4%) of whom were male and 18 (48.6%) were female. The mean age of the patients was 68.5 ± 13.1 years (range, 32–87 years), and the mean follow-up period was 9.05 ± 3.36 months (range, 6.0-16.3 months). Indications for Yamane surgery were aphakia in 24 patients (64.9%) and IOL dislocation in 13 patients (35.1%). In Group 1, the primary causes of aphakia were posterior capsular rupture without sulcus support in 19 eyes, trauma in 2 patients, pseudoexfoliation syndrome in 3 patients, and zonular dialysis. In Group 1, two patients had peripherally located corneal opacities secondary to trauma, two patients had pseudoexfoliation with dropped nucleus, two patients had congenital ectopic pupils, and one patient had pseudoexfoliation with nanophthalmos. In Group 2, two patients had capsular tension ring luxation, six patients had dropped IOLs, and one patient had a stage 1 epiretinal membrane. Anterior vitrectomy was performed in 19 patients (51.4%), capsular tension ring and IOL extraction in 2 patients (5.4%), IOL extraction alone in 5 patients (13.5%), and PPV in 8 patients (21.6%). PPV was performed in 2 patients due to dropped nucleus and in 6 patients due to dropped IOL. Two of the six dropped IOLs were three-piece lenses, and the same IOLs were placed in the anterior chamber and used in Yamane surgery.

Subjective refraction measurements in all patients decreased significantly from the preoperative to the final examination (p = 0.001, from 8.70 ± 4.83 [-2.25 to + 13.25] D to -1.36 ± 0.41 [-0.25 to -2.25] D). This significant difference was observed in both groups (p = 0.001 and p = 0.007, respectively). UCVA improved from 1.60 ± 0.34 (2.00 to 1.00) to 0.40 ± 0.14 (0.80 to 0.10) logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR), and BCVA improved from 0.53 ± 0.22 (1.00 to 0.20) to 0.15 ± 0.09 (0.40 to 0.00) logMAR (p = 0.001, for both). This significant improvement persisted in both groups for both UCVA and BCVA (p = 0.001, for both groups). No significant changes were observed in IOP measurements before and after surgery, in either Group 1 or Group 2 (p = 0.147; p = 0.116, respectively). In 29 eyes (78.4%), the BCVA was better than 0.3 logMAR, and no patient experienced a decrease in BCVA compared to preoperative levels.

Corneal edema developed in two patients (5.4%) after surgery, and in both cases, the corneal edema completely resolved within one week with topical hypertonic saline drops. At six weeks postoperatively, CME developed in two patients (5.4%); one was from the aphakic group, and the other was a patient who had undergone PPV due to a dropped nucleus. One patient was treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory eye drops. Pre-treatment CMT was 554 μm, while at week 4 of treatment, CMT was 223 μm. In the other patient, who did not respond to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory eye drops, complete remission of CME was achieved with a subtenon triamcinolone injection. Pre-injection CMT was 611 μm, while at week four after injection it was 299 μm, and at the last visit it was 217 μm. Neither patient experienced recurrence of CME. Two patients (5.4%) developed epiretinal membranes that did not disrupt the foveal contour during the first year of follow-up. No progression was observed until the last follow-up. In one patient (2.7%) who underwent PPV due to dropped PMMA IOL removal, hypotony (< 6 mmHg) was observed on the first postoperative day due to leakage from the sclerocorneal incision. A sclerocorneal suture revision was performed to restore sealing, and IOP returned to normal. Two patients (5.4%) experienced transient IOP elevation (> 25 mmHg) which was controlled with topical antiglaucomatous treatment within one week after surgery. In one patient (2.7%), iris capture of the IOL was observed on the second postoperative day and treated with a mydriatic agent; it did not recur.

The mean preoperative CMT value was 229.23 ± 38.31 μm, while it was 237.30 ± 44.26 μm at the final examination (p > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant difference was observed between the preoperative and final mean CMT measurements in Group 1 (237.18 ± 41.33 μm vs. 246.13 ± 48.94 μm) and Group 2 (256.21 ± 51.44 μm vs. 263.23 ± 68.56 μm). There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of final mean CMT (p = 0.189) and in the change in mean CMT from baseline to final visit (p = 0.165). Postoperative CMT was not found to correlate with visual acuity (p = 0.647). Postoperative complications and final CMT values are summarized in Table 3. No vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, or IOL dislocation was observed in any patient during the postoperative follow-up.

Discussion

In the present study, patients’ refraction measurements were significantly lower compared to preoperative values, while UCVA and BCVA were significantly better than preoperative values. Combined surgical procedures were performed in 92% of patients, with PPV performed in 21.6%. Despite these complex surgeries, complication rates were acceptable and manageable. None of our patients experienced vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, or IOL dislocation.

In the present study, the Yamane technique was shown to be effective in terms of refractive outcomes after at least 6 months of follow-up. Vural et al.15 evaluated 30 eyes of 30 patients who underwent Yamane surgery and showed significant improvement in subjective refraction measurements (from 10.06 ± 3.10 D to -1.45 ± 0.73 D), consistent with the current study. The IOL power calculation methodology used in the study resulted in an observed myopic shift of -1.36 D in spherical equivalent. Vural et al. [15] reported a myopic shift of -1.45 D in spherical equivalent using a similar methodology.

Predicting the effective lens position in Yamane surgery is difficult. The effective lens position is affected by factors such as sclerotomy-to-limbus distance, haptic cauterization length and haptic retraction depth into the tunnel, which usually results in a hypermetropic shift of 0.7–0.8 D [16]. To avoid myopic shift, it is not recommended to adjust the dioptric power of the 3-piece IOL as in ciliary sulcus implantation [16]. Yuan et al. [17] showed that measurements calculated using the SRK-T formula resulted in a lower mean hypermetropic surprise of 0.21 D compared to those estimated with Holladay 2 and Barrett formulas (0.81D and 0.69D, respectively). They stated that there was no statistically significant difference in estimation errors among the formulas.

Yamane et al. [7], in the first report describing the technique, reported a mean estimation error of -0.21 ± 0.99 D using the SRK-T formula for 4 different IOL models. They also showed a statistically significant difference among different IOL models, with mean refractive errors ranging from − 0.41 D to + 1.08 D. These results indicate that the type of IOL also has an effect on refractive unpredictability. The choice of IOL power calculation formula should be determined based on patient characteristics, surgeon preference, and experience. Ultimately, it is a fact that refractive outcomes are less predictable compared to standard phacoemulsification surgery. As with Yamane, each extracapsular fixation technique has its own sources of refractive variability, and comparative studies exist in the literature that highlight differences in the refractive predictability of different methods [16].

The study showed significant improvements in both UCVA and BCVA. Xu et al. [18] reported that UCVA and BCVA values increased from 1.34 ± 0.75 to 0.48 ± 0.47 and from 0.31 ± 0.28 to 0.16 ± 0.25 logMAR in 75 eyes with at least one year of follow-up. In the last follow-up, they reported that BCVA was better than 0.3 logMAR in 56 eyes (74.7%). In this study, BCVA was better than 0.3 logMAR in 29 eyes (78.4%), and no patient experienced a decrease in BCVA compared to preoperative levels. In another study, the results of surgeries performed with traditional scleral fixation, retropupillary iris-claw IOL and Yamane techniques were compared, BCVA values were reported as 0.08 ± 0.10, 0.08 ± 0.16 and 0.03 ± 0.04 logMAR respectively, and it was shown that the effectiveness of all three techniques was similar [19].

In this study, indications of Yamane were aphakia in 64.9% of patients and IOL dislocation in 35.1%. Kelkar et al. [20] reported Yamane indication in 31 patients as aphakia in 10 (32%) eyes, dislocated crystalline lens in 6 (19%) eyes, and dislocated IOL in 15 (48%) eyes. Guerin et al. [16] reported the aphakia rate as 45% and the IOL dislocation rate as 55%. The relatively high rate of aphakic patients in this study compared to the literature can be explained by the fact that our clinic is both a teaching clinic and a reference center where complex cataract surgeries and vitreoretinal surgical procedures are performed.

It has been reported that the complication rates are lower in the Yamane technique compared to conventional methods [14]. Similar to suture-related complications in conventional methods, haptic-related complications such as haptic breakage, slippage, and disinsertion have been reported in the Yamane technique [4, 21]. It is thought that the surgical technique, the design and material of the IOL used may be related to these complications [22]. Polyvinylidene fluoride haptics are known to have superior shape recovery capabilities compared to PMMA haptics [23]. Cheng et al. [24] reported haptic-related complications in 6 patients (25%) in a 24-patient case series using MA60AC (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX) IOLs with PMMA haptics, and attributed this high rate to the more fragile structure of PMMA haptics. In the study, the Sensar AR40e with modified C-loop and PMMA haptics was used, and no haptic-related complications were observed in any patient. However, the lack of a comparison among IOLs with different characteristics limits our ability to comment on this matter.

Transient corneal edema was observed in two patients (5.4%), possibly related to longer surgical time and additional surgical procedures. Previous reports have indicated that postoperative hypotony occurs in 4.6% to 19.4% of cases [4]. In these studies, postoperative hypotony was more frequently observed in the group that underwent PPV. In the present study, hypotony was observed in one patient (2.7%) who underwent 25G PPV with a sclerocorneal incision. In two patients (5.4%), a transient increase in IOP was observed. In previous studies, this rate has been reported between 2% and 39% [7, 20, 21]. Stem et al. [21] reported this rate as 23% and attributed it to the development of reverse pupillary block due to increased aqueous flow from the vitreous to the anterior chamber after surgery, and recommended prophylactic iridotomy during surgery to avoid this. No patients underwent iridotomy in this study, and no reverse pupillary block was observed in any patient. IOP elevation was associated with residual viscoelastic material.

Retinal detachment is a rare complication in the Yamane technique, with a reported frequency of 0% to 3.8% [4, 25]. In this study, no retinal detachment was observed in any of the patients during the follow-up period. Previous reports have suggested that inadequate vitreous base cleaning at the haptic entry points can lead to traction between the IOL and the vitreous body, which can cause retinal detachment [26].

In the current study, the frequency of CME was 5.4%. In the literature, the frequency of postoperative CME in the Yamane technique has been reported to be between 1% and 21% [7, 21, 27]. Ishikawa et al. [27] compared Yamane and sutured IOL transscleral fixation techniques in terms of the incidence of CME and found them to be 13.0% and 1.9%, respectively, at 3 months. They have shown that the Yamane technique carries a higher risk of postoperative CME compared to the conventional method. However, the prevailing view in the literature is that the use of sutures in scleral fixation increases the frequency of postoperative CME [28]. Based on this, Ishikawa et al. [27] hypothesized that the flanges created in Yamane may be harder and larger than the sutures, and that the part of the IOL flange in the ciliary body may lead to chronic inflammation. There is not yet enough data to confirm this. However, the significant impact of variables such as ocular comorbidities and combined surgical procedures on these rates should not be overlooked [29].

Current literature reports iris capture rates between 8.0% and 12.0% [7, 18]. In the present study, this rate was found to be lower than those reported in the literature (2.7%). Asymmetric scleral tunnel angles, IOL tilt, and IOL optical diameter have been reported to contribute to this complication [7, 30]. During the follow-up period, no IOL decentration was observed in any of the patients; however, this result was based on clinical observation and could not be objectively documented with OCT. Overall, the rates of postoperative complications in the current study were similar to those reported in previous studies (Table 4). Although complication rates were compared descriptively with previous reports, statistical inter-study comparisons were not possible due to significant heterogeneity among the studies.

The limitations of this study include the small sample size, retrospective design, lack of a control group, and insufficient long-term follow-up. The results were limited to a single IOL (AR40e); the absence of different IOL types with varying characteristics affects the generalizability of the results. Parameters such as the length and configuration of the scleral tunnel and the length of haptic cauterization could not be evaluated. To obtain more objective information on the efficacy, safety, and complication rates of Yamane, it is necessary to compare it with conventional scleral fixation methods. It should be kept in mind that rare but clinically significant complications, such as retinal detachment and endophthalmitis, may not be adequately captured in a cohort of this size. Comparing different IOLs and evaluating postoperative IOL tilt and decentration using objective methods such as anterior segment imaging and OCT is important. To overcome these limitations, comparative studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and objective anterior segment imaging techniques are needed.

In conclusion, the Yamane method, a sutureless flanged intrascleral IOL fixation technique using a 27G needle, is an effective surgical method in various patient groups with different indications involving aphakia and IOL dislocation. Favorable outcomes have also been observed in the presence of ocular comorbidities and when performed in combination with surgical procedures such as PPV. However, the relatively small sample size and limited follow-up duration should be considered when interpreting these findings.
