OntoGSN: An Ontology for Dynamic Management of Assurance Cases
Tomas Bueno Momcilovic, Barbara Gallina, Ingmar Kessler, Dian Balta

Abstract
Assurance cases (ACs) are a common artifact for building and maintaining confidence in system properties such as safety or robustness. Constructing an AC can be challenging, although existing tools provide support in static, document-centric applications and methods for dynamic contexts (e.g., autonomous driving) are emerging. Unfortunately, managing ACs remains a challenge, since maintaining the embedded knowledge in the face of changes requires substantial effort, in the process deterring developers—or worse, producing poorly managed cases that instill false confidence. To address this, we present OntoGSN: an ontology and supporting middleware for managing ACs in the Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) standard. OntoGSN offers a knowledge representation and a queryable graph that can be automatically populated, evaluated, and updated. Our contributions include: a 1:1 formalization of the GSN Community Standard v3 in an OWL ontology with SWRL rules; a helper ontology and parser for integration with a widely used AC tool; a repository and documentation of design decisions for OntoGSN maintenance; a SPARQL query library with automation patterns; and a prototypical interface. The ontology strictly adheres to the standard’s text and has been evaluated according to FAIR principles, the OOPS! framework, competency questions, and community feedback. The development of other middleware elements is guided by the community needs and subject to ongoing evaluations. To demonstrate the utility of our contributions, we illustrate dynamic AC management in an example involving assurance of adversarial robustness in large language models.

Introduction

Assurance cases (ACs) represent a widely accepted artifact for documenting, building and maintaining confidence in a property of a system, such as the safety of a rocket
[
42
]
or the security of a software application
[
21

. ACs are defined as a combination of "arguments and evidence intended to demonstrate that a system meets its assurance requirements" (p. 128,
[

). They provide an interface for developers and auditors to verify and validate that there is enough evidence to conclude that the targeted property is achieved. Originally conceived for the safety certification of products, ACs have since proven valuable for assuring more opaque properties and systems such as frontier large language models
[
16

.

However, while much literature has been published on how to create a good AC (cf.
[
30
]
), managing them effectively is still a substantial challenge. Assurance arguments are handled as standalone graphs of statements and documents, separate from active processes or the evidence itself. The tool ecosystem is accordingly centered on visualizing links of natural language statements and documents

To directly support developers in managing their ACs, we present the OntoGSN ontology and the supporting middleware toolkit. As a comprehensive formalization of the Goal Structuring Notation (GSN)

The middleware serves as a complement to existing tools, enabling direct integration with a commonly used software application, and the use of Semantic Web reasoners such as Pellet. Where a graphical user interface for assurance case management is not needed, OntoGSN can be used standalone in an ontology manager or integrated in a graph database.

Related Work

The use of ontologies for managing assurance cases is not a novel idea, but current implementations are limited and publicly unavailable. The Structured Assurance Case Metamodel v1.1
[
23
]
, for example, originally provided mechanisms to link external OWL domain ontologies. The ECSEL AMASS project
[
29
,
35
,
34
]
was an open-source certification platform that supported ontologies based on
Open Services for Lifecycle Collaboration

[
15
]
, which connected assurance cases with artifacts, pioneering the benefit of ontology-driven AC management (cf.
[
14
]
). Denney (2020)
[
12
]
explored the benefits of domain-specific ontologies in their AdvoCATE tool
[
11
]
, which supports GSN cases. More recently, MISRA (2024)
[
20
]
defined custom domain ontologies within SysML to shape assurance cases for automated driving. Unfortunately, the results of these related initiatives have either been deprecated or publicly unavailable.

Similar issues apply to the tools themselves. Software such as ASCE
[
1
]
and AdvoCaTE
[
12
]
are widely used by assurance experts, and provide useful graphical user interfaces. However, these tools closed-source and often proprietary; being specifically geared towards ACs, their adoption by a wider audience and thereby extensibility may be limited. Additionally, the logic and coverage of performed evaluations (i.e., tests or rules) in these tools is not clear.

Related academic resources show promise in addressing some of these issues, and supporting more dynamic ACs development and management. Castillo-Effen et al.
[
7
]
, for example, introduce an algorithmic framework and domain-specific language for managing ACs in the aerospace domain with data; Beyene & Carlan
[
5
]
similarly introduce a the CyberGSN language for enabling features such as digital signatures for cybersecurity ACs. Saswata et al.
[
25
]
provide a triplestore-based framework (i.e., graph database) with patterns that can be instantiated into ACs using domain ontologies. Others, such as Asaadi et al.
[
2
]
, give architectural and development lifecycle recommendations for data-driven applications that are tool-agnostic.

We see the positioning of OntoGSN as highly complementary in following ways. First, the related work contributes with focused representations of GSN that fulfill a particular purpose. We instead provide a complete, comprehensive, and source-driven formalization of GSN that can be integrated into any framework, distilled into smaller models for a particular purpose, or aligned with other AC metamodels. Second, the OntoGSN ontology (alongside supporting tools) is public, free and open-source, thereby allowing easy extension of facts and rules from the domain, and implementation of AC-driven assurance before deciding for more advanced tools such as ASCE. Third, and in addition, this contribution does not aim to substitute state-of-the-art graphical user interfaces, AC management strategies and pattern libraries; it is designed as the middleware that fills the gap between multiple frameworks. Lastly, OntoGSN is a contribution to the assurance and Semantic Web communities, and will be maintained alongside new versions of the GSN standard, but also extended and aligned with other models and less-represented properties in need of assurance (e.g., adversarial robustness).

3
Ontology Design

3.1
Method

The ontology was designed following the guidelines of the NeOn methodology
[
33
]
for the (re)use of non-ontological resources and existing ontologies. The GSN specification, as crystallized in the GSN Community Standard v3
[
3
]
, represents one such non-ontological resource. The standard is the result of many years of iterative community efforts to standardize the documentation of arguments in a structured way. Therefore, early on, it was decided that the normative parts of the standard represent the ground truth, to which any interpretations, guidelines (e.g., Part 2), and supplementary specifications can be added in the future.

Each sentence of the standard was broken down into elements and analyzed with respect to its contribution to the metamodel of GSN. There were two main activities: creating the taxonomy of classes and properties (i.e., the TBox), and creating the rules governing the properties between individuals of classes. Regarding the taxonomy, each sentence of the standard is parsed with the goal of translating the concepts and their relations into semantic triples (i.e., subject-predicate-object statements). This also includes possibilities denoted by qualifiers such as "can". Regarding the rules, the sentences that place conditions or restrictions on the identified elements of triples are translated into logical statements. These sentences were identified based on qualifiers such as "should" or "must", as opposed to qualifiers such as "may".

Given that concepts can be represented in multiple ways, the process of translating text to the ontology involves some degree of interpretation. To mitigate bias, the entire document was parsed three times, each
text-to-ontology
mapping was traced, and the mappings were reviewed by all co-authors and additional early reviewers. Following the "design rationale" paradigm
[
26
]
, all text snippets were included side-by-side with the corresponding ontology statements and rationales in a tabular design document. The document thus served two purposes: 1) provide conceptual version control in a format that allows reviewers to easily (in)validate the mappings; and 2) ensure the ontology designers and reviewers have comprehensively covered the standard.

Technical Details

The ontology was created using the Protégé v5.6.3 ontology editor
[
22

[
37
]
. Annotation and data properties were imported from the following foundational ontologies and vocabularies: Resource Description Framework Schema (RDFS)
[

]
, XML Schema Definition Language (XSD)
[
41
]
, Dublin Core (DC)
[

]
, Schema.org
[

]

[

. Reasoning is based on Semantic Web Rule Language (SWRL)
[

]

[

]
or Drools
[

]
). In this first version of OntoGSN, SWRL provides the simplicity, decidability, and reasoning support that covers most GSN rules.

3.3
Competency Questions

Following the example of
[
10
]
, we drafted personas based on stakeholders or roles, which we perceive to be the intended beneficiaries of our framework. There are three such personas: assurance engineers, domain experts, and auditors. Each is associated with five competency questions (CQ).

We imagine the personas as roles with particular expertise and interests. Assurance engineers want to build a strong GSN assurance case and make sure that the overall case is valid in the face of changes. Domain experts such as security engineers want to ensure that their assurance-relevant controls in applications (e.g., large language models) are still valid (e.g., adversarially robust). Finally, auditors want to verify and validate the controls effectively, and provide targeted evaluations.

Each section of GSN makes the logic behind constraints, propagation, and flagging explicit. To enable the use of this extensive reasoning, we encoded this logic using OWL (for, e.g., class restrictions) and SWRL (for, e.g., flags). We present some illustrative categories of SWRL rules in Table

4.5
Supporting Middleware

Our OntoGSN toolkit consists of two Jupyter notebooks plus a lightweight Python/SPARQL layer and an ASCE bridge.

In
query_manager
notebook, the user begins by loading the OntoGSN OWL ontology (locally or via a remote SPARQL endpoint), binding a “case” namespace for assurance-case individuals, and then running parameterized SPARQL templates (INSERT/SELECT) to add or query GSN elements. For example,
add_evidence.rq
creates a new
gsn:Solution
instance referring to a specified
gsn:Goal
, while
find_all_supported_goals.rq
and
find_all_supporting_evidence.rq
use recursive
gsn:supportedBy+
queries to discover which goals are fully backed by evidence or which solutions support a given goal. Users simply call a helper function, e.g.,
execute_sparql("add_evidence.rq", params)
, to substitute their URI parameters and push the query to the graph.

In the
argument_manager
notebook, each GSN element (Goal, Strategy, Context, Assumption, or Solution) is presented as a mini “form” in a code cell. The user edits a few prewritten Python assignments (for example,
goal_id = "G1"
;
goal_text = "Sensor data is valid"
), runs the cell, and the notebook transparently wraps them in the proper RDF triples (type, label, comment) and issues an INSERT under the hood. Once nodes exist, dedicated “link” cells let the user declare
supportedBy
or
inContextOf
relationships simply by plugging in source and target IDs. This block-based approach hides SPARQL/RDF complexity while still giving full access to the underlying graph.

A separate parser aligns ASCE XML schemas with OntoGSN’s classes and properties, converts ASCE exports into OntoGSN-conformant RDF triples (and can regenerate ASCE XML from an OntoGSN graph), and returns all query results as Pandas DataFrames (or simple Python lists) rendered inline. Because the SPARQL templates live in standalone
.rq
files and the ASCE-mapping logic is modular, you can drop in your own queries—e.g. to summarize confidence annotations, generate a metrics dashboard, or export the entire case as OWL/RDF or back into ASCE XML—while staying entirely within a notebook environment.

5
Assessment and Discussion

OntoGSN was checked at schema- and instance-levels. Regarding schema, using the OOPS!
[
27
]
service, which flagged: missing annotations, domain or range declarations, and inverse relationships, recursive definitions; and untyped classes. Missing assertions and recursion have been addressed, but the issue of untyped classes remains due to how SWRL is implemented in Protégé. Regarding instances, we verified consistency by loading a representative ABox into the Pellet OWL-DL reasoner, confirming that classes remained satisfiable and no logical contradictions arose in inferred class memberships or property assertions. Further, SWRL rules were additionally tested with the Drools reasoner.

5.1
Evaluating on Competency Questions

The competency questions in Section 2 were formalized as SPARQL queries; all are available in the GitHub repository. For instance, the question

Which Goals or Strategies are affected by the Defeater “Jailbreak”?

is realized by selecting all
gsn:Defeater
instances whose
gsn:statement
contains the literal “jailbreak” and then following the
gsn:challenges
property to retrieve the related
gsn:Goal
or
gsn:Strategy
resources. Likewise, to answer

Which bottom-level Goals in the “Robustness” argument lack any supporting Solution?

one locates the
gsn:Argument
whose
gsn:statement
mentions “robustness”, traverses its
gsn:contains+
hierarchy to leaf
gsn:Goal
instances (i.e. those without further
gsn:contains
children), and filters out any goals that appear in a
gsn:supportedBy
relationship. A third example,

Which Solution instances were added more than 180 days ago?

simply filters all
gsn:Solution
resources by comparing their
gsn:published
date-time against a computed cutoff (e.g. “2024-11-14T00:00:00Z”). In each case, the OWL ontology’s classes, properties, and transitive paths directly inform the SPARQL patterns, guaranteeing that queries faithfully satisfy the original competency questions.

5.2
Level of Adoption

As a new contribution, OntoGSN is undergoing several review cycles in different communities. First, we have disseminated OntoGSN with the members and leaders of the SCSC Assurance Case Working Group (i.e., creators and maintainers of GSN) and the Ontology Working Group, who expressed interest in the ontology and tools. Second, we have created an alignment ontology and simple parser for integration with the ASCE tool (academic license), with the aim to develop an add-on for easier linkage between ASCE cases and ontologies. Third, we have separately engaged ontology engineers and domain experts, who provided their comments with respect to ontology quality and usability. These exchanges have motivated the creation of tools that support the adoption and easier interfacing with existing applications.

5.3
Limitations

Several limitations remain in the current version of OntoGSN. First, we do not verify the syntactic correctness or semantic validity of
gsn:statement
s. Second, punning object properties as classes to fit them under
rdf:predicate
in Relationships is not supported due to SWRL conflicts. Third, rules and axioms are not annotated according to their source (
gsn:coreOrExtension
) due to a known Protégé error, and their filtering by source is therefore currently disabled. Finally, while many SWRL rules can (and perhaps, should) be expressed as SPARQL queries
[
28
]
or SHACL constraints
[
40
]
, this translation effort has not been pursued in this version.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

OntoGSN addresses the persistent knowledge-management bottleneck in AC development by providing a machine-readable, standards-compliant, and publicly available formalization of GSN, accompanied by middleware for automated population, querying, and consistency checking. Through an ontology, extensive documentation, and supporting tools, OntoGSN enables engineers to construct, evolve, and evaluate cases with reduced manual effort. Our evaluation—encompassing FAIR principles, OOPS! pitfall analysis, competency-question validation, and community feedback—and the demonstrator exemplify how dynamic, data-driven case management can be realized.

Future work will include three directions: extending tool integrations, broadening framework compatibility, and supporting domain-specific use cases. On the tooling side, one aim will be to integrate OntoGSN with additional AC tools (e.g., AdvoCATE
[
11
]
or CertWare
[
4
]
) and provide support for usage with Python. On the framework side, we plan to formalize and align our approach with existing ontologies and methodologies for dynamic AC management, with the aim to align with the Argument Model Ontology
[
36
]
, Assurance 2.0
[
6
]
and other notations. On the domain side, we aim to improve the interoperability and applicability of OntoGSN for diverse communities, environments and roles.
