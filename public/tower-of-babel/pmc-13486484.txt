Gas capture and selective sensing performance of graphene-like AlNC2 monolayer: a DFT study
Authors: Masoumeh Molaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gas adsorption behavior of a recently proposed graphene-like AlNC₂ monolayer was systematically investigated using density functional theory calculations. The structural stability of the AlNC₂ sheet was confirmed using cohesive energy calculations and phonon dispersion spectrum analysis. The interaction of several environmentally harmful gases including CO, NO, NO₂, SO₂, NH₃, H₂S, and CH₄ with the AlNC₂ surface was examined by calculating adsorption energies, charge density difference, work-function changes, density of states, and recovery times. The calculated adsorption energies indicated strong chemisorption for NO₂, SO₂, NO, and NH₃, while CO and H₂S exhibited moderate interactions. The CH₄ interacted only weakly with the surface. Charge redistribution and work function analysis revealed various acceptor- and donor-type adsorption behaviors depending on the nature of the gas molecule. Noticeable changes in the electronic structure, including band-gap narrowing and the appearance of new states near the Fermi level were observed for strongly adsorbed gas molecules. These finding were confirmed by density of state plots and charge density difference analyses. Recovery time values showed that AlNC₂ exhibits reversible adsorption behavior for moderately adsorbed gas molecules. Moreover, strongly chemisorbed species exhibited difficult desorption at ambient temperatures, indicating the potential of the AlNC2 for gas capture and removal.Keywords: Gas sensing, Density functional theory, AlNC2, Graphene-like structure, Adsorption energySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The design of gas sensors is a topic of interest in today’s scientific world. These sensors can monitor the presence and evaluate the concentration of toxic gases and organic vapors1. Detecting hazardous gases such as H₂S, SO₂, NO₂, and CO due to their harmful effects on the environment2,3 as well as identifying greenhouse gases like CO₂ and CH₄ due to their significant role in climate change, is considered an important challenge4. Climate change, global warming, air pollution, and health concerns have prompted researchers to consider designing more efficient sensors2,5.

Gas sensors typically consist of two main parts for detecting the desired agents: the sensing material and a transducer. The sensing material must be sensitive to the target molecule and lead to a change in its chemical or physical characteristics: electricity, dielectric, magnetic, or optical properties. These changes can be analyzed by a signal produced by the transducer agent6. There are various mechanisms for gas sensing, including the capacitive, electrochemical, optical infrared, gas-chromic, and resistive sensors7. Finding the proper sensing material is the most important challenge in designing a sensor to achieve sensitivity, selectivity, and high performance8. Metal oxide frameworks9, carbon-based materials10, conductive polymers11, and MXenes12 are the most common sensing materials in recent studies.

Carbon-based nanomaterials, as a broad class of materials, are an attractive topic in the gas sensor research field13. Nanomaterials, due to their specific properties such as high surface area, high adsorption capacity, and tunable electronic properties, are commonly used for gas capturing14,15, catalysts16,17, electrode materials18,19, and gas sensing materials. Carbon-based nanomaterials like nanotubes20–22, nanofibers23, quantum dots24,25, fullerene23, and graphene have been studied for gas sensing purposes in theoretical and computational studies due to their distinct physical and chemical properties.

Graphene and its derivatives have attracted significant research attention due to their advantages, such as high electrical conductivity, mechanical strength, and optical properties26. Graphene derivatives have been widely evaluated as gas sensor materials from both computational and experimental perspectives27–29. Aluminum and nitrogen-doped graphene have shown significant effects on selective and strong gas adsorption on graphene30–33. Aluminum-carbon nanomaterials have also shown promising capabilities for gas sensing34,35, suggesting that the presence of aluminum metal alongside carbon provides good adsorption capacity for gases. It is expected that a surface rich in aluminum and nitrogen could adsorb hazardous molecules more effectively than a graphene surface.

The recently proposed graphene-like AlNC₂ monolayer exhibits polar covalent characteristics, due to the coexistence of aluminum, nitrogen, and carbon atoms. Although this material has been studied for hydrogen storage36, its gas-sensing behavior has not been explored. Motivated by the electron-rich C–N sites and the metallic nature of Al atoms, we investigate the potential of AlNC₂ as a sensing material for environmentally harmful gas molecules using DFT.

Computational methods

A 2 × 2 × 1 supercell of AlNC2 containing 9 aluminum atoms, 9 nitrogen atoms, and 18 carbon atoms has been chosen as the sensing material in this study. A vacuum gap of 25 Å in the z-axis at a periodic boundary condition was considered to avoid the interaction between the layers. The electronic structure and optimization calculations for all structures without any symmetry restrictions have been performed using the Quantum-ESPRESSO code37. Generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) as an approximation for the exchange-correlation functional was considered. The wave function in the calculations has been expanded using a basis set with kinetic energy cutoffs of 50 Ry for the wave function and 500 Ry for the charge density. The convergence tests were performed for both the plane-wave cutoff energy and the k-point sampling. The results confirm that the total energy is well converged within a few meV per atom, reported in Tables S1 and S2. Accordingly, a cutoff energy of 50 Ry and a 4 × 4 × 2 k-point grid were adopted in all calculations. The self-consistent field (SCF) convergence threshold was set to 10⁻⁶ Ry for all calculations. All optimization calculations have been done considering spin polarization. The Grimme DFT-D3 method was employed to include the van der Waals interactions. The Brillouin zone was sampled with a Monkhorst-Pack grid of 4 × 4 × 2 k-points for electronic structure calculations.

Results and discussion

Structural optimization and stability

The graphene-like AlNC2 monolayer was recently examined by L. Yuan et al. for hydrogen storage36. In this study, the structure was evaluated for gas sensing. The optimized geometry of AlNC2 is presented in Fig. 1a and the charge density differences plot (CDD) for the surface has been calculated and is depicted in Fig. 1b. The AlNC2 sheet has three nonequivalent rings numbered by 1, 2, and 3. As could be seen in Fig. 1b, the nitrogen and carbon atoms act as relatively electron-rich centers due to their charge acceptance tendency from aluminum atoms and the polar covalent interactions within the Al–N framework. The stability of the monolayer structure was analyzed using phonon frequency calculations. The results, shown in Fig. 1c, indicate the absence of imaginary frequencies, confirming the dynamical stability of the structurewhich is in agreement with the previous study on this sheet36. Additionally, the cohesive energy value of the structure was calculated using Eq. (2) and found to be − 5.77 eV/atom, which is more negative than the cohesive energy of the AlN nanosheet (− 5.08 eV/atom)41. The negative cohesive energy indicates thermodynamic stability, and structures with more negative cohesive energies are generally considered more stable in comparative evaluations42,43. The band gap of the structure was determined to be 1.60 eV, using the HSE06 hybrid functional which is a reliable method for determining band gaps44 aligning well with the findings in the prior study36. Gas adsorption on the AlNC2 surface

In this section, we investigate the adsorption of a series of harmful gases including CO, NO2, NO, H2S, NH3, SO2, and CH4 on AlNC2. Various initial adsorption sites and molecular orientations on the three nonequivalent rings of AlNC2 were systematically examined and fully optimized. The most stable configurations with minimum adsorption energy were then selected for further analysis of sensing properties (Figures S1–S7). As shown in Fig. 2, the most stable sites for CO, NO, NO2, and SO2 are around ring 3 oriented towards the aluminum atoms. These gases form covalant bonds with the surface through interactions between their carbon, oxygen, or nitrogen atoms and aluminum atoms of the sheet. SO2, NO, and NO2 form two covalent bonds with two aluminum atoms in ring 3 andact as charge-acceptor agents due to electron transfer from the Al atoms, as indicated in Table 1. The charge transfer values reported in Table 1 were obtained using Mulliken population analysis. The charge transfer was calculated as the difference between the total Mulliken charge of the gas molecule in the adsorbed state and that of the isolated gas molecule in the gas phase. The negative values indicate the charge transferring from the surface to the gas molecules (Table 1). The charge transfer values for NO, NO2, and SO2 are − 0.55, -0.64, and − 0.85 e, respectively, corresponding to their high adsorption energies of -1.85, 3.35, and − 3.30 eV, respectively. CO is also chemisorbed by forming a covalent bond between the carbon atom and aluminum. In CO, the HOMO is mainly localized on the carbon atom, making it more reactive than oxygen. However, its adsorption energy (-0.71 eV) and charge transfer (-0.05 e) are lower than those of other charge acceptor agents.

Table 1 reports the minimum distances between the gas molecule and the surface. The sminimum adsorption distance of 1.83 Å is observed for SO2 adsorption, where the two oxygen atoms of SO2 form chemisorbed bonds with aluminum atoms at an equal bond distance of 1.83 Å. NO2 also has two similar covalent bond distances (1.92 Å) between its oxygen atoms and the surface. These distances align with the binding energies and charge transfer values. NH3, H2S, and CH4 interact with the surface as charge donor agents with adsorption energy values of -1.26, -0.70, and − 0.23 eV, respectively. The NH3 molecule prefers to create a covalent bond between its nitrogen atom and the aluminum atom of the surface, displaying donor characteristics due to the lone pair on nitrogen in NH3 and vacant orbital in aluminum. The adsorption energy (-1.26 eV) and charge transfer (0.2 e) values for NH3 molecule indicate a stronger interaction with the surface compared to CH4 and H2S. The very low adsorption energy value of CH4 suggests that the AlNC2 is not a significant adsorbent for its sensing. Among the examined gases, the CO, NO, NO2, NH3, and SO2 are chemisorbed, while H2S and CH4 tend to be physisorbed on the surface.

Gas molecules containing oxygen bonded to electronegative atoms such as nitrogen and sulfur (NO, NO2, and SO2) have more tendency to chemisorb onto surface because of the presence of aluminum atoms on the surface. This tendency is due to the creation of polar covalent bond between aluminum and oxygen. In contrast ammonia (NH3) with hydrogen atoms bonded to the electronegative nitrogen atom and a low tendency for hydrogen to accept electrons prefers to donate its lone electron pair to the vacant orbital of aluminum. This results in a moderate adsorption energy of -1.26 eV. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) physisorbs less strongly than ammonia because sulfur has a lower electronegativity than nitrogen. Additionally, methane (CH4) which lacks electronegative atoms only weakly physisorbs.

To further clarify the nature of the interactions, additional analyses have been performed. One of the important criteria is the adsorption distance. It is generally anticipated that if the adsorption distance is smaller than the sum of the corresponding atomic radii, the orbital overlap occurs and indicates the formation of a covalent bond between the gas molecule and the surface45. Therefore, the adsorption distances were compared with the sum of atomic radii (based on standard values in the software) and are presented in Table 2. The results show that for CO, NO, NO₂, SO₂, and NH₃, the adsorption distances are smaller than the corresponding sums of atomic radii which indicate the formation of chemical bonds and chemisorption behavior. In contrast, for H₂S and CH₄, the adsorption distances are larger than the sum of atomic radii, indicating weak interaction and physisorption nature. In the case of H₂S, the adsorption distance is close to the sum of atomic radii, suggesting a weak interaction with a slight tendency toward chemisorption which is further clarified by the ELF analysis.

The work function changes

The work function is the energy required to remove an electron from the Fermi level to the vacuum space of the cell, which could be determined by DFT calculations. The change in the work function () induced by gas adsorption can reveal the charge acceptance or donation nature of the adsorbed gas. If the gas adsorption process involves a transfer of charge from the surface to the gas molecule, it is expected that the work function will increase due to an electron deficiency on the surface after adsorption. Similarly, if the charge is transferred from the gas molecule to the surface, the surface will be exposed to more electron density than before the gas adsorption and results to decrease in the work function.

The work function φ can be calculated using Eq. (3) and the change in work function is determined by subtracting the work function in the presence of gas from the work function of the pristine surface (AlNC2 - AlNC2/gas). The work function values for all gas adsorptions are listed in Table 1. The increase in work function follows the order SO2 > NO2 > NO > CO > NH3 > H2S > CH4, aligning with the trends in charge transfer and adsorption energy. Gases with an acceptor nature will create a charge deficiency on the surface, making electron extraction difficult. Therefore, gases such as CO, NO, NO2, and SO2 will increase the work function of the surface, resulting in a negative . On the other hand, H2S and NH3, which have a donor adsorption nature, will enrich the surface with electrons and so they decrease the work function value. The different values of allow for the selective sensing of gases by AlNC2. The significant changes in work function along with gas insertion result in a considerable shift in band gap energy values, Eg, as can be seen in Table 1. This will be further discussed in the next section using density of states analysis.

Charge density difference analysis

The charge density difference plots for the gas adsorption on the AlNC2 surface, with an isovalue of 0.05 e/Å³, are depicted in Fig. 4. The most pronounced charge density difference is observed for SO2 which is consistent with its highest charge transfer value (0.85 e) and its strong adsorption energy (-3.30 eV). The adsorption of NO2 and NO also exhibits significant charge density difference, although weaker than SO2, in agreement with their relatively high charge transfer values and strong adsorption energies. Red and blue colors indicate electron accumulation and electron depletion regions, respectively.

The red colored regions around SO2, NO, NO2, and CO molecules suggest the charge acceptor nature of these gases, confirming the charge transfer analysis. In contrast, the electron accumulation regions near the surface for NH3 and H2S indicate their donor behavior. The larger redistributed region in the NH3 case is consistent with its higher charge transfer (0.20 e) compared with H2S (0.13 e), as well as its stronger adsorption energy. Finally, CH4 showsno noticeable charge redistribution which is in agreement with its weak adsorption energy and negligible charge transfer.

ELF analysis

To further clarify the adsorption nature, Electron Localization Function (ELF) analysis was performed. The ELF values range from 0 (blue regions indicating delocalized electron density) to 1 (red regions indicating highly localized electrons) as depicted in Fig. 5. ELF values smaller than 0.5 correspond to electron delocalization, while values greater than 0.5 indicate electron localization52. The ELF results reveal that significant localization (ELF > 0.8) is observed for CO, NO, NO₂, SO₂, and NH₃ adsorption which is consistent with their relatively high adsorption energies and considerable charge transfer values. In particular, ELF values in the range of 0.85–0.96 are observed in the bonding regions between the gas molecules and the Al atoms of the surface for the chemisorbed cases. These findings demonstrate the formation of covalent bonds and as the ELF plots show, suggest partial polarization of electron density toward electronegative atoms such as oxygen or nitrogenwhich indicate the presence of polar covalent interactions. However, the separation of the electron cloud in NH₃ adsorption is less pronounced than in the other chemisorbed cases and the electron density around the nitrogen atom is slightly shifted toward the aluminum atom, which is in agreement with the charge transfer from the molecule to the surface. The ELF value in the gas–surface bond region for NH3 adsorption is about 0.85, which is comparable to the electron localization of Al–N bonds in the substrate (ELF ≈ 0.95). This similarity indicates that NH₃ forms a polar covalent bond with the surface, similar to the bonding nature within the AlNC₂ structure. For CO adsorption, a highly localized electron density is observed around the C–Al bond, with an ELF value close to 0.96, confirming the covalent nature of the interaction.

(a–g) Electron Localization Function (ELF) analysis for gas molecules adsorbed on surface (CO, NO, NO2, SO2, NH3, H2S, and CH4), (blue regions indicating delocalized electron density andred regions indicating highly localized electrons).

In contrast, for H₂S adsorption, a weak tendency of electron density redistribution from the sulfur atom toward the aluminum atom of the surface is observed. The ELF value in the interaction region is about 0.64, indicating moderate localization and suggesting a tendency to weak interaction with partial covalent character. This finding is in agreement with its moderate adsorption energy, -0.68 eV. Finally, for CH₄ adsorption, no significant electron localization is observed between the molecule and the surface, confirming its weak physisorption nature.

Recovery time analysis

The reusability of a sensor is primarily determined by its recovery time which refers to the time required for gases to desorb from the sensing material. The recovery time is influenced by both temperature and the adsorption energy values. If the adsorption energy values are in the range of -0.6 to -1.0 eV, it is anticipated that the adsorption will be reversible and the recovery times will be in the range of milliseconds to a few seconds40.

Recovery times for various physisorbed or chemisorbed systems at different temperatures have been calculated using Eq. 4 and are presented in Table 4. The data in Table 4 show that the recovery times for CO, H2S, and CH4 are short, indicating good reusability of the sheet for these gases. Specifically, the recovery time for CH4 which has a weak adsorption energy of -0.23 eV is the minimum value (6.64×s at 300 K). Although rapid recovery is favorable, the very low recovery time may be a result of the weak interaction and low charge transfer and may lead to a lower sensing response. CO and H2S molecules show short recovery times at room temperature, indicating that AlNC2 is well-suited for sensing these gases due to their favorable adsorption energy values and charge transfer values. The recovery time for NH3 at 300 K is long, measured at 1.63×s, indicating a difficult desorption at this temperature. However, as the temperature increases to 500 K, the recovery time for NH3 decreases significantly to 5.33 s. For NO, NO2, and SO2, which are strongly chemisorbed on the sensor surface, the recovery times are considerably high which demonstrates that desorption of these gases at 300 K is not feasible. The desorption process at 300 K for these gases requires chemical agents to remove them. However, as the temperature increases, the recovery times will decrease and make it possible to desorb NO gas at a temperature of 900 K or higher. The stability of the structure at 900 K has been evaluated in a previous study36. However, at 300 K, AlNC2 can be a promising material for capturing NH₃ and strongly adsorbed toxic gases such as NO, NO₂, and SO₂. The significant differences in recovery times originate from the exponential dependence of τ on adsorption energy.

Conclusion

In this study, the gas adsorption behavior of a recently proposed graphene-like AlNC₂ monolayer were systematically investigated using density functional theory calculations. The structural stability of AlNC₂ was confirmed using phonon analysis and cohesive energy calculation. The adsorption characteristics of several environmentally harmful gas molecules (CO, NO, NO₂, SO₂, NH₃, H₂S, and CH₄) on AlNC2 sheet reveal a clear distinction between strong chemisorption and weak physisorption regimes. Gas molecules containing electronegative atoms bonded to N or S (NO, NO₂, and SO₂) exhibit strong chemisorption on Al sites by the polar covalent interaction between the Al atoms and electronegative O atoms. In contrast, CO and H₂S show moderate and reversible adsorption, while CH₄ interacts only weakly with the surface due to its non-polar nature.

Charge transfer analysis and work function changes demonstrate that the combined presence of Al atoms and electron-rich C–N units plays an important role in the adsorption strength and the changes on electronic structure of the AlNC₂ sheet. Strong adsorption induces notable work function changes and band-gap narrowing. These trends were further supported by density of states and charge density difference analyses, which reveal pronounced electronic perturbation near the Fermi level for strongly bound gas molecules. The calculated recovery times indicate that AlNC₂ exhibits dual functionality, enabling reversible adsorption for moderately bound gases such as CO and H₂S, while acting as an efficient capture material for strongly chemisorbed toxic gases, including NO, NO₂, SO₂, and NH₃, at ambient conditions. This behavior highlights the potential of AlNC₂ not only as a sensing-related material but also as a candidate for gas capture and removal applications.

Overall, this study provides a deep understanding of how metal and nonmetal atoms presence in two dimensional materials govern gas adsorption mechanisms. The insights obtained here may guide the rational design of aluminum containing two dimensional materials for gas–surface interaction applications.
