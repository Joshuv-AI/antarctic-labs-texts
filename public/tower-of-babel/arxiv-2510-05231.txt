Hadamard ranks of algebraic varieties
Dario Antolini, Guido Montúfar, Alessandro Oneto

Abstract
. Motivated by the study of decompositions of tensors as Hadamard products (i.e., coefficient-wise products) of low-rank tensors, we introduce the notion of Hadamard rank of a given point with respect to a projective variety: if it exists, it is the smallest number of points in the variety such that the given point is equal to their Hadamard product. We prove that if the variety X X is not contained in a coordinate hyperplane or a binomial hypersurface, then the generic point has a finite X X -Hadamard-rank. Although the Hadamard rank might not be well defined for special points, we prove that the general Hadamard rank with respect to secant varieties of toric varieties is finite and the maximum Hadamard rank for points with no coordinates equal to zero is at most twice the generic rank. In particular, we focus on Hadamard ranks with respect to secant varieties of toric varieties since they provide a geometric framework in which Hadamard decompositions of tensors can be interpreted. Finally, we give a lower bound to the dimension of Hadamard products of secant varieties of toric varieties: this allows us to deduce the general Hadamard rank with respect to secant varieties of several Segre-Veronese varieties.

1.
Introduction

Restricted Boltzmann Machines are a particular type of probabilistic graphical models arising in machine learning as building blocks of deep neural networks. They can be regarded as models for
tensor decompositions
, where tensors are written as
Hadamard products of tensor-rank decompositions
, i.e., as entry-wise products of additive decompositions of decomposable tensors. Motivated by these structures, we investigate broader types of Hadamard product decompositions of tensors. More generally, we introduce the notion of Hadamard rank with respect to a variety and study its properties.

In
[
CMS10
]
, the authors began the study of Restricted Boltzmann Machines from the perspective of algebraic statistics and tropical geometry.
This approach was followed by
[
CTY10
,
MM15b
,
MM17
,
SM18
]
, investigating, in particular, the expressive power and approximation errors of these models. An overview can be found in
[
Mon16
]
.
The size of the tensor, the number of Hadamard factors, and the length of the tensor-rank decompositions in each factor are determined by the
architecture
of the Restricted Boltzmann Machine, namely the number of observable and hidden units and their number of states.
This point of view was considered in
[
Mon16
,
FOW17
,
SM18
,
OV23
]
.
Such decompositions have been called
Hadamard-Hitchcock decompositions
(
\mathbf{r}
-HHD) in
[
OV23
]
, where
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
are the lengths of the tensor-rank decompositions in each Hadamard factor. They have also been considered in the context of data analysis in
[
CGM24
]
.

In parallel to the aforementioned literature, the purely geometric notion of a
Hadamard product of projective varieties
has been considered, see, e.g.,
[
BCK16
,
BC22
,
ABO25
,
MPS25
]
or the book
[
BC24
]
. In a few words, the Hadamard product of two points in projective space is obtained from their coefficient-wise multiplication in a fixed choice of coordinates (see
Equation

2.3
).
It is clear from the definition that this operation is not well-defined everywhere on projective spaces and that it depends on the choice of coordinates.
The Hadamard product of two projective varieties is the Zariski closure of the set of Hadamard products of all pairs of points in the Cartesian product of the two varieties (see
Definition

2.3
).

In the classical algebraic geometry literature on additive decompositions of tensors, the notion of tensor rank has been put in the more general framework of
secant varieties
and
X
-ranks
.
Given a projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
, the
X
-rank
of a point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
is the smallest number of points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\in X
such that
p
lies on their linear span,
p\in\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\rangle
. Geometrically, this is related to the notion of
r
-secant variety
, i.e., the Zariski closure of the set of points of
X
-rank at most
r
. Recall that tensor rank decompositions correspond to the notion of
X
-rank with
X
being a Segre variety (Veronese or Segre-Veronese varieties if we are interested in symmetric or partially-symmetric tensors, respectively).
For a general overview on these definitions, see, e.g.,
[
Lan12
, Section 5.2.1]
.

In this paper, we introduce a multiplicative version of the notion of
X
-rank with the goal of formalizing a geometric framework for HHDs. We define the notion of
Hadamard-
X
-rank
with respect to any projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
. Given a projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
, the
Hadamard-
X
-rank
of a point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
is the smallest number of points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\in X
such that
p
is equal to their Hadamard product (
Definition

2.2
). For example, Hadamard products of tensor rank decompositions are related to the notion of Hadamard rank with respect to Segre-Veronese varieties and to the study of Hadamard products of their secant varieties.

Most of the paper will be focused on Hadamard ranks with respect to
secant varieties
of
toric varieties
. These include Segre-Veronese varieties. Also, since toric varieties are strictly related to statistical models known as
exponential families
, our results on toric varieties extend some of the results of
[
MM17
]
.

Main results and structure of the paper

In
Section

2
, we offer a brief overview on
X
-ranks and secant varieties and we define the notion of
X
-Hadamard-rank with respect to any algebraic variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

In
Section

3
, we study which conditions on the variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
and a point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
guarantee that the
X
-Hadamard-rank of
p
is well-defined or, at least, that the general
X
-Hadamard-rank is finite. For example, in
Corollary

3.9
, as a consequence of a more general result for toric varieties, we deduce that the Hadamard rank with respect to the
r
th secant variety (
r\geq 2
) of a Segre-Veronese variety is finite for any point in the ambient space. This can be rephrased by saying that any partially symmetric tensor admits a partially symmetric
\mathbf{r}
-Hadamard-Hitchcock decomposition for any
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
with
r_{i}\geq 2
. We also show that the Hadamard rank of points with all coordinates different from zero is at most twice the Hadamard rank of a general point (see
Proposition

3.11
).
By means of tropical geometry, we characterize varieties
X
for which the Hadamard-
X
-rank of a general point is finite: these are
concise
varieties (i.e., not contained in a coordinate hyperplane) which are not contained in a proper binomial hypersurface or, equivalently, varieties whose ideal contains no variables and no binomials (see
Corollary

3.40
).

Finally, in
Section

4
, we give a lower bound on the dimension of Hadamard products of secant varieties of a toric variety in terms of dimensions of its higher secant varieties, see
Theorem

4.3
. In
Corollary

4.5
, we rephrase this result in terms of exponential families thanks to their strong relations to toric varieties. This allows us to deduce the actual dimension, and then the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank, for Segre-Veronese varieties.
Theorem

4.3
and
Corollary

4.5
generalize previous results from
[
MM17
]
.

We believe that the present paper sets the ground for future investigations about Hadamard products of secant varieties, Hadamard ranks and Hadamard decompositions with respect to interesting families of varieties.
We list some of them in
Section

5
.

2.
Hadamard ranks of algebraic varieties

Notation
.

If
V
is an
(n+1)
-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, we denote by
\mathbb{P}V
its projectivization. Due to the nature of Hadamard products, we will consider projective varieties with respect to a fixed choice of coordinates. In this case, we will assume a fixed basis on
V
. We will simply write
\mathbb{P}^{n}=\mathbb{P}V
, denoting by
(v_{0}:\cdots:v_{n})
the projective coordinates of the vector with coordinates
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{n})
in the fixed basis.

2.1.
Additive decompositions and secant varieties of algebraic varieties

In 1927, Hitchcock introduced the additive decomposition of a tensor
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}
as a sum of decomposable tensors, i.e.,
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{v}_{i,1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{v}_{i,k}
, where
\mathbf{v}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}+1}
, see
[
Hit27
]
. The smallest
r
for which such a decomposition exists is called the
tensor rank
of
T
. Even earlier, in the 1850s, Sylvester studied additive decompositions of a homogeneous polynomial
F\in{\rm Sym}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
as a sum of powers of linear forms, i.e., a decomposition as
F=\sum_{i=1}^{r}L_{i}^{d}
, where
L_{i}\in{\rm Sym}^{1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, see
[
Syl51
]
. Since homogeneous polynomials can be identified with symmetric tensors, the second decomposition is a symmetric version of the first one. The smallest
r
for which such a decomposition exists is called the
symmetric tensor rank
, or
Waring rank
, of
F
.
More generally, given a partially symmetric tensor
T\in{\rm Sym}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\rm Sym}^{d_{k}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}
, we call the smallest number
r
such that
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{v}_{i,1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{v}_{i,k}^{\otimes d_{k}}

the
partially symmetric tensor rank
of
T
.

The general geometric framework in which studying tensor decompositions and many other additive decompositions is the one of
secant varieties
of projective varieties.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a projective variety, and let
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
.
The
X
-rank
of
p
is the smallest number of points of
X
whose linear span contains the point
p
, i.e.,

\operatorname{rk}_{X}(p):=\min\{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}~:~p\in\langle q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\rangle,\ q_{i}\in X\}.

The
r
th secant variety
of
X
is the Zariski closure of the set of points of
X
-rank at most
r
, i.e.,

\sigma_{r}(X):=\overline{\{p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}~:~\operatorname{rk}_{X}(p)\leq r\}}=\overline{\bigcup_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\in X}\langle q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\rangle}.

The aforementioned additive decompositions of tensors correspond to the notion of
X
-rank with respect to Segre, Veronese and Segre-Veronese varieties, respectively. Given a partition
{\bf d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
of
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
{\bf n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}
, the
Segre-Veronese variety

SV_{\bf d,n}
of rank-one partially symmetric tensors is the image of the map

(2.1)

\begin{matrix}\nu_{\bf d}&:&\mathbb{P}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}&\rightarrow&\mathbb{P}({\rm Sym}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\rm Sym}^{d_{k}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}),\\
&&([\mathbf{v}_{1}],\ldots,[\mathbf{v}_{k}])&\mapsto&[\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\otimes d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}].\end{matrix}

The standard representation in coordinates is the monomial embedding given by

(2.2)

\begin{matrix}\nu_{\bf d}&:&\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{k}}&\rightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{N},&\qquad N=\prod_{i}{n_{i}+d_{i}\choose d_{i}}-1\\
&&({\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{k})&\mapsto&(\cdots:{\bf x}_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots{\bf x}_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}:\cdots)_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n_{i}+1}\\
|\alpha_{i}|=d_{i}\end{subarray}}&\end{matrix}

where
{\bf x}_{i}=(x_{i,0}:\cdots:x_{i,n_{i}})\in\mathbb{P}^{n_{i}}
,
\alpha_{i}=(\alpha_{i,0},\ldots,\alpha_{i,n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n_{i}+1}
, and
{\bf x}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}:=\prod_{j=0}^{n_{i}}x_{i,j}^{\alpha_{i,j}}
.

The case
{\bf d}=\mathds{1}:=(1,\ldots,1)
corresponds to
Segre varieties

S_{\bf n}:=SV_{\mathds{1},\mathbf{n}}
.
The case
k=1
,
i.e.,
{\bf d}=(d)
, corresponds to
Veronese varieties

V_{d,n}:=SV_{(d),(n)}
.

An extensive amount of literature has been dedicated to the study of
X
-ranks of these and many other projective varieties. We refer to
[
BCC+18
]
for a general overview on these topics.

2.2.
Multiplicative decompositions and Hadamard powers of algebraic varieties

In
[
CMS10
]
, Cueto, Morton and Sturmfels developed an algebraic geometry approach to study a statistical model known as the
Restricted Boltzmann Machine
(RBM). The probability distributions arising from such a model can be interpreted as Hadamard products of tensors of prescribed tensor rank, see
[
Mon16
]
. Such multiplicative decompositions of tensors have been considered also in
[
FOW17
,
SM18
,
OV23
]
.

The geometric framework for such multiplicative decompositions is given by
Hadamard products
of algebraic varieties. Once a choice of coordinates is fixed, the coefficient-wise product, known as
Hadamard product
, defines the polynomial map

(2.3)

\begin{matrix}h&:&\mathbb{C}^{N+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{N+1}&\rightarrow&\mathbb{C}^{N+1},\\
&&(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=((x_{0},\ldots,x_{N}),(y_{0},\ldots,y_{N}))&\mapsto&\mathbf{x}\star\mathbf{y}:=(x_{0}y_{0},\ldots,x_{N}y_{N}).\end{matrix}

This extends to a rational map of projective spaces
h\colon\mathbb{P}^{N}\times\mathbb{P}^{N}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}
. Clearly, the latter map is not defined everywhere, e.g., we cannot Hadamard-multiply two distinct projective coordinate points. Also, as already mentioned, the map depends on the choice of a basis. However, in many applications, a natural choice of basis is forced upon us. This happens, for instance, when we consider a tensor representing the joint probabilities of a set of discrete random variables, as in our motivating example of RBMs.

Notation
.

We denote by
\mathds{1}
both the vector with all entries equal to one and the corresponding projective point
(1:\cdots:1)\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
. Note that it is the identity of the Hadamard product. If all coordinates of
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
are non-zero, we write
p^{\star(-1)}
for its Hadamard-inverse, i.e.,
p\star p^{\star(-1)}=\mathds{1}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a projective variety, and let
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
.
The
X
-Hadamard-rank
of
p
is the smallest number of points on
X
whose Hadamard product is
p
, i.e.,

\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(p):=\min\{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}~:~p=q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{m},\ q_{i}\in X\},

or
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(p):=\infty
if
p
cannot be expressed as a Hadamard product of points in
X
.

We write

\eta_{m}(X):=\overline{\{p\in\mathbb{P}^{n}~:~\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(p)\leq m\}}

to indicate the Zariski closure of the set of points having
X
-Hadamard-rank at most equal to
m
.

We denote by
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}
the
generic
X
-Hadamard-rank
. That is the smallest
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
\eta_{m}(X)=\mathbb{P}^{N}
. If such an
m
does not exist, we write
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}:=\infty
.

The definition of
\eta_{m}(X)
is related to the notion of
Hadamard products
of projective varieties introduced in
[
CMS10
]
. We refer to
[
BC24
]
for a recent monograph on the subject and for more references.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be projective varieties. The
Hadamard product
of
X
and
Y
is

(2.4)

X\star Y:=\overline{h(X\times Y)}=\overline{\{p\star q~:~p\in X,\ q\in Y,\ p\star q\text{ exists}\}}.

The
Hadamard powers
of
X
are defined recursively by

X^{\star 0}:=\mathds{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad X^{\star m}:=X\star X^{\star(m-1)}.

Equivalently,

X^{\star m}=\overline{\{q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{m}:q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\in X,\ q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{m}\text{ exists}\}}.

As already mentioned, the Hadamard product of algebraic varieties is highly dependent on the choice of coordinates. If
g\in PGL(N)
, then
X\star Y
can differ from
gX\star gY
. For example, the Hadamard product of a general pair of points is well-defined; however, the Hadamard product of two different coordinate points, such as
(1:0)
and
(0:1)
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, is not defined. See
[
Bal25
]
for Hadamard products of varieties after a generic change of coordinates. Nevertheless, the Hadamard product is well-behaved with respect to the action of the torus of diagonal matrices; see
Lemma

3.13
and its consequences.

Remark 2.4
.

The variety
X\star Y\subset\mathbb{P}V
can be seen also as the linear projection of the Segre product
X\times Y\subset\mathbb{P}(V\otimes V)
on the diagonal coordinates. By continuity, if
X
and
Y
are both irreducible, then
X\star Y
and all powers
X^{\star m}
are irreducible.

Example 2.5
.

It is immediate to see that the
X
-Hadamard-rank of a point can be infinite. For example, since an
embedded toric variety
(see
Definition

3.5
)
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is defined by monomial embeddings and by binomial equations, it is
Hadamard-idempotent
, i.e.,
X^{\star 2}=X
, see e.g.,
[
FOW17
, Proposition 4.7]
. In particular, the
X
-Hadamard-rank of any point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
is either equal to one (if
p\in X
) or it is infinite.

We will see more on the importance of Hadamard-idempotent varieties in the study of the finiteness of
X
-Hadamard-ranks. See
Section

3
.

Remark 2.6
.

We will always assume that
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is
concise
, i.e., it is not contained in any coordinate hyperplane. Indeed, since coordinate hyperplanes are Hadamard-idempotent, if
X
is not concise, then also all its Hadamard powers are not concise. In such a case, we regard
X
in its concise ambient space by forgetting the coordinates of the hyperplanes containing it. Note that this is not the case for degenerate varieties contained in arbitrary linear subspaces: indeed, their Hadamard powers might be no longer degenerate.

Clearly
X^{\star m}\subset\eta_{m}(X)
, but it is not difficult to see that, in general, the inclusion might be strict.

Example 2.7
.

Let
p=(1:2)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
X=\{p\}
. Then,
X^{\star m}=\{(1:2^{m})\}
while
\eta_{m}(X)=\{(1:2^{r})~:~r\leq m\}
.

Example

2.7
immediately highlights where the issue is. We always have that

\eta_{m}(X)=\bigcup_{r=1}^{m}X^{\star r},

but a Hadamard power of
X
might not be contained in the following one. However, for concise varieties, we have that
\dim\eta_{m}(X)=\dim X^{\star m}
, since
\dim X^{\star s}\leq\dim X^{\star(s+1)}
for all
s\geq 1
: indeed, for every
p\in X
with all coordinates different from zero,
p\star X^{\star s}
is isomorphic to
X^{\star s}
and
p\star X^{\star s}\subset X^{\star(s+1)}
. Even if
X
is irreducible,
\eta_{m}(X)
may be a reducible variety and strictly contain
X^{\star m}
. An assumption that guarantees the inclusion between consecutive Hadamard powers and, therefore, the equality
\eta_{m}(X)=X^{\star m}
, is that
\mathds{1}\in X
. Under this assumption,

X\subset X^{\star 2}\subset\cdots\subset X^{\star s}\subset\cdots\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad\eta_{s}(X)=X^{\star s}\text{ for any }s.

The viceversa is not always true, as shown in the following example.

Example 2.8
.

Consider the following points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
:

p=(1:1:2:3),\quad q=p^{\star(-1)}=(6:6:3:2).

Let
L=\langle p,q\rangle
be the line through
p
and
q
. This has equation:

L\colon\qquad\begin{cases}5x_{0}-16x_{2}+9x_{3}=0\\
5x_{1}-16x_{2}+9x_{3}=0\end{cases}.

In
[
ABO25
, Remark 3.1.1]
it is shown that in this case
L^{\star 2}=\langle p^{\star 2},p\star q,q^{\star 2}\rangle
. This is a toric plane in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
:

L^{\star 2}\colon\qquad x_{0}-x_{1}=0.

Since
L^{\star 2}
is linear and
p,q\in L^{\star 2}
, we have that
L\subset L^{\star 2}\subset L^{\star 3}=L^{\star 2}\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{3}
even if
\mathds{1}\not\in L
.

Example 2.9
.

If we consider the
k
-factor
Segre product

S_{\mathds{1}}
, then
\sigma_{2}(S_{\mathds{1}})^{\star m}
is the algebraic variety corresponding to the
Restricted Boltzmann Machine
with
m
binary hidden units and
k
binary observed units, see
[
Mon16
]
. In
[
MM15b
]
, the authors introduced
Discrete Restricted Boltzmann Machines
by allowing non-binary units. From our definitions, this corresponds to Hadamard powers of secant varieties of Segre varieties. With our more geometric interpretation, we might extend the notion of Discrete RBM models to the case of symmetric tensors, partially symmetric tensors or skew-symmetric tensors by looking at Hadamard products of secant varieties of Veronese varieties, Segre-Veronese varieties and Grassmannians, respectively.

Having the latter as guiding example, the general framework we consider is the notion of Hadamard rank with respect to secant varieties. Hence, we generalize the definition of
X
-Hadamard-rank as follows.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a projective variety, and let
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
.
The
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank
of
p
is the smallest number of points on
\sigma_{r}(X)
such that their Hadamard product is equal to
p
, i.e.,

\mathrm{Hrk}_{X,r}(p):=\min\{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}~:~p=q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{m},\ q_{i}\in\sigma_{r}(X)\},

or
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X,r}(p):=\infty
if
p
cannot be expressed as a Hadamard product of points in
\sigma_{r}(X)
.

Analogously, we denote by
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X,r}^{\circ}
the
generic
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank
. That is the smallest
m
such that
\sigma_{r}(X)^{\star m}
is equal to
\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

Note per
Definition

2.1
and
Definition

2.3
that
\sigma_{r}(X)
and
\sigma_{r}(X)^{\star m}
are defined as Zariski closures. In particular, there may exist points
p
whose
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X,r}(p)
is larger than the generic
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X,r}^{\circ}
. Actually, in
Example

3.10
, we will see a case in which the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is finite but there exists an entire locus of points in which the
X
-Hadamard-rank is not even well-defined.

Clearly, the
X
-Hadamard-rank in
Definition

2.2
correspond to the case
r=1
.

Remark 2.11
.

Let
SV_{\bf d,n}
be a Segre-Veronese variety, i.e., the variety of partially symmetric rank-one tensors in
\mathbb{P}({\rm Sym}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\rm Sym}^{d_{k}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1})
. The notion of
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank can be rephrased in terms of decompositions of tensors. If
T\in{\rm Sym}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\rm Sym}^{d_{k}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}
, its
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank, that we denote by
\mathrm{Hrk}_{r}^{\mathbf{d}}(T)
, consists of the smallest number
m
of tensors
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
of partially symmetric rank at most
r
such that
T=A_{1}\star\cdots\star A_{m}
. This general case reduces to the case of decompositions of homogeneous polynomials as Hadamard products of Waring decompositions in the case of Veronese varieties (
\mathbf{d}=(d))
or to the case Hadamard products of tensor-rank decompositions in the case of Segre varieties (
\mathbf{d}=\mathds{1})
.

Inspired by the literature on decompositions of tensors, it is immediate to raise the following questions.

Question A
.

Under which conditions on
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
and
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
is the
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank of
p
finite?
Under which conditions on
X
is the generic
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank finite, i.e.,
\sigma_{r}(X)^{\star m}=\mathbb{P}^{N}
for some
m
?

Question B
.

How can we compute the
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank of a given point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
?
Can we find bounds on the maximal
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank and bounds on the generic

-Hadamard-rank?

3.
On the finiteness of Hadamard ranks

In this section, we focus on
A
. Before approaching it in general, we give an immediate and straightforward proof of the finiteness of
r
th Hadamard ranks with respect to Segre varieties for
r\geq 2
, i.e., finiteness of decompositions of tensors as Hadamard products of rank-
r
tensors. These are the decompositions of tensors corresponding to the Discrete Restricted Boltzmann Machines
[
MM15b
]
and called
r
-HHDs in
[
OV23
]
.

Example 3.1
.

(Finiteness of Hadamard ranks of tensors)
Let
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}
.
The Hadamard rank of the projective point
[T]
with respect to the Segre variety
S_{{\mathbf{n}}}\subset\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1})
is equal to the smallest number of factors of an expression
T=A_{1}\star\cdots\star A_{m}
, where
\operatorname{rk}A_{i}\leq r
.
Extending the idea of
[
FOW17
, Proposition 4.13]
, it is immediate to see that
T
is always the Hadamard product of finitely many tensors of rank at most
r
, for any
r\geq 2
. Let
T=(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}
be a presentation in the chosen basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}
and let
\mathbf{v}_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}:=(t_{0,i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}},\ldots,t_{n_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}})\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}
. Then,

T=\bigstar_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}\left[(\mathbf{v}_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}-\mathds{1})\otimes{\bf e}_{i_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bf e}_{i_{k}}+\mathds{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathds{1}\right].

Clearly, this is far from being an optimal expression.

Remark 3.2
.

The finiteness of generic
r
th Hadamard ranks with respect to the Segre variety for
r\geq 2
, i.e., the fact that the
m
th Hadamard power of the
r
th secant variety of a Segre variety fills the ambient space for some
m
, follows also from
[
MM15b
, Theorem 13]
. Here, the authors give a bound on such an
m
with respect to the size of the tensor and
r
. They do it by showing that Restricted Boltzmann Machines are
universal approximators
, namely that they can approximate arbitrary well any joint probability distribution on the discrete sample space
\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}_{d}
with
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\{1,\ldots,n_{i}\}
. In other words, the model defines a dense subset of the probability simplex with respect to the Euclidean topology. In algebraic geometric terms, this implies that the model is not contained in any proper algebraic subvariety and, therefore, its Zariski closure fills the ambient space. Recall that being a Zariski dense subset over the real numbers is weaker than being Euclidean dense. In
Corollary

3.9
, we extend this result to the case of any partially-symmetric tensor as a corollary of a geometric result on generic
r
th
X
-Hadamard-ranks with respect to embedded toric varieties.

3.1.
On the finiteness of maximum
X
-Hadamard-ranks

We approach
A
by presenting first some particular geometric property on the projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
which guarantees the finiteness of
X
-Hadamard-rank for
any
point in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

In
[
BCK16
]
, Hadamard products of linear spaces have been systematically studied. In particular, they obtain the following useful results about Hadamard powers of lines.

Notation
.

Let
\Delta_{i}\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be the set of points having at most
i+1
non-zero coordinates in the fixed basis.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
L\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a line such that
L\cap\Delta_{N-2}=\emptyset
. Then:

(1)

[
BCK16
, Lemma 2.10]

L^{\star s}=\bigcup_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\in L}(q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{s})
, i.e., the closure in (
2.4
) is not necessary;

(2)

[
BCK16
, Theorem 3.4]

L^{\star s}
is a projective linear space of dimension equal to
\min\{s,N\}
.

Lemma

3.3
allows us to deduce the finiteness of the
X
-Hadamard-rank of any point in the ambient space of
X
under the assumption that
X
contains a line
L
such that
L\cap\Delta_{N-2}=\emptyset
.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
L\subset X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
, where
X
is any projective variety and
L
is a line such that
L\cap\Delta_{N-2}=\emptyset
, then the
X
-Hadamard-rank is finite for any point in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

Proof.

Since
L\subset X
, then
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(p)\leq\mathrm{Hrk}_{L}(p)
for every
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
. By
Lemma

3.3
, we know that
L^{\star N}=\{p~:~\mathrm{Hrk}_{L}(p)\leq N\}=\mathbb{P}^{N}
. Hence,
\mathrm{Hrk}_{L}(p)\leq N
for any
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we extend
Example

3.1
to
r
th
X
-Hadamard-ranks with respect to
toric varieties
. We recall here the definition of toric variety that we will use through the paper: this coincides with what in the algebraic geometry literature is often referred to as an
embedded
toric variety, in contrast to the more general definition of
abstract
toric varieties, see
[
CLS11
, Section 2.3]
.

Notation
.

We write
\mathbb{C}^{\times}:=\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

Given a vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}_{\geq 0}
and a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, we use the notation for monomials
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}:=x_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}.

Definition 3.5
.

An
(embedded) toric variety
is a positive-dimensional projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
defined as the Zariski closure of the image of a monomial map
\varphi_{A}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1}\to\mathbb{P}^{N},\ \mathbf{x}\mapsto(\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{0}}:\cdots:\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{N}})
, where
A=(\alpha_{0}|\cdots|\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
is a matrix with
|\alpha_{i}|=|\alpha_{j}|
for any
i\neq j
.
We will always assume that toric varieties are non-degenerate in the sense that
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for all
i\neq j
. We denote by
\widehat{\varphi}_{A}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
the corresponding affine monomial map when we interpret it as parametrizing the affine cone of
X
in
\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
.

Remark 3.6
.

An embedded toric variety as in
Definition

3.5
is determined by a matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
. Recall that such an embedded toric variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is uniquely determined by the row span of the matrix
A
: indeed, the ideal of the toric variety is uniquely determined by the kernel of the matrix
A
, see, e.g.,
[
CLS11
, Proposition 1.1.9]
. Moreover, since we are considering projective toric varieties given by a homogeneous monomial parametrization, we have that
\mathds{1}\in\text{rowspan}(A)
.
In this way, given a toric variety as in
Definition

3.5
, we will often consider a monomial map induced by a matrix
\bar{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
having the same rowspan of
A
, but with the first row equal to
\mathds{1}
and the first column equal to
(1,0,\ldots,0)
; in this way we get a monomial map
\varphi_{\bar{A}}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1}\rightarrow U_{0}
where
U_{0}
is the affine chart of
\mathbb{P}^{N}
with the first coordinate different from
0
. The images of the two monomial maps
\varphi_{A}
and
\varphi_{\bar{A}}
might be different, but they have the same Zariski closure in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

Notation
.

We denote
[n]:=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.

Example 3.7
.

Consider the Segre variety
S_{\mathbf{n}}
whose usual embedding is given by the map
\nu_{\mathds{1}}
defined in (
2.2
) for
d_{1}=\cdots=d_{k}=1
. This is the monomial parametrization corresponding to the matrix
B_{\bf n}
whose rows are labeled by
\{(j,i_{j})~:~j\in\{1,\ldots,k\},i_{j}\in[n_{k}]\}
, columns are labeled by multi-indices
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}=(i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,i^{\prime}_{k})\in[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k}]
, and
[B_{\bf n}]_{(j,i_{j}),\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}=\delta_{i_{j},i^{\prime}_{j}}
. For example, the matrix defining the Segre embedding of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}
in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
is given by the matrix

B_{1,2}=\begin{pNiceMatrix},

corresponding to the monomial map

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\varphi_{B_{1,2}}:&(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}\times(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{3}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{5}\\
&((a_{1,0},a_{1,1}),(a_{2,0},a_{2,1},a_{2,2}))&\longmapsto&(a_{1,0}a_{2,0}:a_{1,0}a_{2,1}:a_{1,0}a_{2,2}:a_{1,1}a_{2,0}:a_{1,1}a_{2,1}:a_{1,1}a_{2,2}).\end{array}

However, such a matrix

is not full rank. In particular, we can consider a monomial parametrization associated to the matrix
\bar{B}_{\mathbf{n}}
obtained by deleting all the rows corresponding to
(i,0)
, for all
i=1,\ldots,k
, and adding a first row equal to
\mathds{1}
, labeled by

; namely,

\bar{B}_{1,2}=\begin{pNiceMatrix},

corresponding to the monomial map

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\varphi_{\bar{B}_{1,2}}:&(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{4}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{5}\\
&(a_{0},a_{1,1},a_{2,1},a_{2,2})&\longmapsto&(a_{0}:a_{0}a_{2,1}:a_{0}a_{2,2}:a_{0}a_{1,1}:a_{0}a_{1,1}a_{2,1}:a_{0}a_{1,1}a_{2,2}).\end{array}

Note that the map
\varphi_{B_{1,2}}
is surjective onto the Segre variety

\varphi_{\bar{B}_{1,2}}
is equal to the affine chart of
S_{1,2}
given by the subset of points with first coordinate different than zero.

Note that if
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is an embedded toric variety, then
\mathds{1}=\varphi_{A}(\mathds{1})\in X
and the generic point
\mathbf{x}=(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{N})\in X
has pairwise distinct entries, i.e.,
\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
x_{i}&x_{j}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\neq 0
for all

. Therefore, for a generic
\mathbf{x}\in X

3.3
for
\mathbf{x}\in X
generic. Such a line is a secant line to the variety

and, in particular,
L\subset\sigma_{r}(X)
for any

. Therefore, from this observation, together with
Proposition

3.4
, we deduce the following.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
X
be a non-degenerate toric variety

. Then, for any

, the
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank is finite for any point in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

Since Segre-Veronese varieties are toric, we extend

3.1
to partially-symmetric tensors.

Corollary 3.9

(Finiteness of Hadamard ranks of partially-symmetric tensors)
.

Fix any
r\geq 2
. Then, any partially-symmetric tensor
T\in\mathrm{Sym}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{Sym}^{d_{k}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}

.

Even if
Corollary

3.8
gives a positive answer to
A
for interesting varieties related to tensor decompositions, it is restrictive as it requires that the variety contains a special line as in
Proposition

3.4
.

In order to relax the problem, in
Section

3.2
we study finiteness of
generic

X
-Hadamard-ranks. In the process of relaxing the problem, it is natural to ask whether the finiteness of the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank would be enough to guarantee the finiteness of the
X
-Hadamard-rank of
any
point in the ambient space. The following example shows that, for very special points, this is not the case.

Example 3.10
.

If
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
and the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is finite, then there exists a Zariski open set
U\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
such that
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(p)\leq\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}
for all
p\in U
. However, this does not imply that
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(p)<\infty
for all
p\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
. As an example, let
Q=\{x_{0}x_{1}+x_{0}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Then,
Q^{\star 2}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
and
\mathrm{Hrk}_{Q}^{\circ}=2
. However, every point
(a:b:0)
such that
ab\neq 0
cannot be written as a product of two points on
Q
. In fact, suppose that
(a:b:0)=(x_{0}y_{0}:x_{1}y_{1}:x_{2}y_{2})
for some
(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}),(y_{0}:y_{1}:y_{2})\in Q
. Then,
x_{2}y_{2}=0
. Suppose that
x_{2}=0
. Then, since
(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})\in Q
,
x_{0}x_{1}=0
, from which
ab=0
, which is a contradiction. Same if
y_{2}=0
. In particular, for all
a,b\neq 0
, we have that
\mathrm{Hrk}_{Q}((a:b:0))=\infty
.

However, the type of failure that we have illustrated in the latter example cannot occur if we consider points with all coordinates different from zero.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be an irreducible variety with
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}<\infty
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{N})\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
be any point such that
\prod_{i}x_{i}\neq 0
. Then,
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\cdot\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}<\infty
, there exists a non-empty Zariski open set
U\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
such that
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}
for all
\mathbf{y}\in U
. Since, by assumption,
\prod_{i}x_{i}\neq 0
, the coordinate-wise multiplication map
m_{\mathbf{x}}\colon\mathbb{P}^{N}\to\mathbb{P}^{N},\ \mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{x}\star\mathbf{y}
is an isomorphism. Let
\text{Crem}_{N}\colon\mathbb{P}^{N}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N},\ \mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{y}^{\star(-1)}
be the Cremona map. Since
\text{Crem}_{N}
is birational and
m_{\mathbf{x}}
is an isomorphism, both
\text{Crem}_{N}^{-1}(U)
and
m_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}(U)
are non-empty Zariski open subsets. Hence,
m_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}(U)\cap\text{Crem}_{N}^{-1}(U)
is a non-empty Zariski open set. For any
\mathbf{y}\in m_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}(U)\cap\text{Crem}_{N}^{-1}(U)
, we can write
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}\star\mathbf{y})\star\mathbf{y}^{\star(-1)}
. Since
\mathbf{x}\star\mathbf{y},\ \mathbf{y}^{\star(-1)}\in U
, the claim follows.
∎

Interestingly, the previous result can be regarded as a
multiplicative
version of the fact that the maximum
X
-rank is at most twice the generic
X
-rank, see
[
BT15
, Theorem 1]
.

3.2.
On the (in)finiteness of the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank

Here, we consider the relaxed version of
A
by focusing on finiteness of
generic

X
-Hadamard-ranks under the only assumption that
\mathds{1}\in X
. As already mentioned, this implies that

(3.1)

X\subset X^{\star 2}\subset\cdots\subset X^{\star r}\subset\cdots\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}.

Recall that if
X
is an irreducible variety, then
X^{\star i}
is an irreducible variety for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. In particular, we have that the chain of Hadamard powers of
X
in (
3.1
) eventually stabilizes.
We are interested in understanding under which conditions it stabilizes
before
filling the ambient space.

A necessary condition for the finiteness of the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is that
X
is not contained in a proper
Hadamard-idempotent variety
, i.e., a variety
Y
such that
Y^{\star 2}=Y\neq\mathbb{P}^{N}
. Indeed, if
X\subset Y\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{N}
with
Y^{\star 2}=Y
, then
X^{\star m}\subset Y^{\star m}=Y\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{N}
. For example, since they are defined by monomial embeddings, embedded toric varieties are Hadamard-idempotent, see also
[
FOW17
, Proposition 4.7]
.

We give a complete characterization of projective varieties whose generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is infinite: these are the projective varieties contained in Hadamard-idempotent varieties
up to a diagonal coordinate change
.

Notation
.

We denote by
\mathbb{T}\subset PGL(N)
the torus of complex diagonal
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrices with non-zero entries on the diagonal, up to non-zero scaling.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be an irreducible variety. If the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is infinite, then there exists a diagonal invertible matrix
t\in\mathbb{T}
such that
tX
is contained in a proper Hadamard-idempotent irreducible variety.

In order to prove
Proposition

3.12
, we need some preliminary results.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be irreducible varieties and let
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}\subset PGL(N)
be diagonal invertible matrices. Then,
tX\star t^{\prime}Y=tt^{\prime}(X\star Y)
.

Proof.

Since
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}
are diagonal, then, for any
p,q\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
, we have
tt^{\prime}(p\star q)=(tp)\star(t^{\prime}q)
.
This shows that
tt^{\prime}(X\star Y)\subset tX\star t^{\prime}Y
. Moreover, since
t
,
t^{\prime}
and
tt^{\prime}
induce isomorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, we have that
tt^{\prime}(X\star Y)
and
tX\star t^{\prime}Y
are both irreducible. Hence, it is enough to prove that they have the same dimension.

Since
tt^{\prime}
is an isomorphism,
\dim tt^{\prime}(X\star Y)=\dim X\star Y
. Now, for generic points
p\in X
and
q\in Y
,
p
is smooth for
X
,
q
is smooth for
Y
and
p\star q
is smooth for
X\star Y
. Then,
tp
is smooth for
tX
,
t^{\prime}q
is smooth for
t^{\prime}Y
and
(tp)\star(t^{\prime}q)=tt^{\prime}(p\star q)
is smooth for
tX\star t^{\prime}Y
. By Terracini’s Lemma for Hadamard products of algebraic varieties (see
[
BCK16
, Lemma 2.12]
), we get

\displaystyle T_{(tp)\star(t^{\prime}q)}tX\star t^{\prime}Y

\displaystyle=\langle(tp)\star T_{t^{\prime}q}t^{\prime}Y,(t^{\prime}q)\star T_{tp}tX\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(tp)\star(t^{\prime}T_{q}Y),(t^{\prime}q)\star(tT_{p}X)\rangle

\displaystyle=tt^{\prime}\langle p\star T_{q}Y,q\star T_{p}X\rangle=tt^{\prime}T_{p\star q}X\star Y.

Hence,
\dim tX\star t^{\prime}Y=\dim X\star Y=\dim tt^{\prime}(X\star Y)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a proper irreducible variety such that
\mathds{1}\in X
. If the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is infinite, then there exists
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
X^{\star r}\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{N}
is an Hadamard-idempotent subvariety.

Proof.

If the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is infinite, then the chain in (
3.1
) stabilizes, i.e., there exists
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
X^{\star r}=X^{\star(r+1)}=\cdots=X^{\star(2r)}=(X^{\star r})^{\star 2}\neq\mathbb{P}^{N}
. Hence
X^{\star r}\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{N}
is Hadamard-idempotent.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.12
.

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1.
If
X
is not concise, then
X\subset H_{i}
for some
i=0,\ldots,N
, and
H_{i}^{\star 2}=H_{i}
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
X
is concise. Then
X
contains a point
\mathbf{x}=(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{N})\in X
such that
\prod_{i=0}^{N}x_{i}\neq 0
. If
\mathbf{x}=\mathds{1}
, then we conclude by
Lemma

3.14
. If not, we take
t=\text{diag}(x_{0}^{-1},\ldots,x_{N}^{-1})\in\mathbb{T}
and then
\mathds{1}\in tX
. Note that the assumption
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}=\infty
implies that
\eta_{r}(X)\neq\mathbb{P}^{N}
for all
r
: in particular,
\dim\eta_{r}(X)=\dim X^{\star r}<N
for all
r
. Note that, by
Lemma

3.13
,
\dim(tX)^{\star r}=\dim t^{r}X^{\star r}=\dim X^{\star r}
. In particular, we also have that
\mathrm{Hrk}_{tX}^{\circ}=\infty
. Therefore,
tX
satisfies the assumptions of
Lemma

3.14
and this concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition

3.12
underlines the importance of Hadamard-idempotent varieties. As already mentioned, embedded toric varieties and coordinate hyperplanes are Hadamard-idempotent. In
[
BC22
]
, a classification of Hadamard-idempompotent hypersurfaces is given. Here, we employ tools from tropical geometry to extend such result to arbitrary codimension.

3.3.
Hadamard-idempotent varieties and tropical geometry

Tropicalization associates to every algebraic variety a
polyhedral complex
, i.e., a family of polyhedra which is closed under taking faces and taking intersections. This combinatorial counterpart preserves many invariants. We recall some preliminary definitions and results in polyhedral and tropical geometry. For a general exposition, see
[
MS15
]
.

3.3.1.
Structure Theorem of Tropical Varieties

In order to understand tropical varieties, one needs to understand first their building blocks, namely
tropical hypersurfaces
.
These are given by the
normal fan
to a
Newton polytope
1
1

1

Tropical varieties can be defined in a more general setting over fields with
valuations
. Here, we use the trivial valuation.
. Let us recall these definitions.
For a more detailed description, see
[
MS15
, Chapter 2.3]
or
[
Zie12
, Chapter 1-2]
for polyhedral geometry background.

Definition 3.15
.

For a polynomial
f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}}c_{\alpha}{\bf x}^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the
Newton polytope
of
f
is

\operatorname{Newt}(f)=\text{conv}(\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}:c_{\alpha}\neq 0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Example 3.16
.

Given bivariate polynomials
f=x+2y-5,\ g=x^{3}y^{2}+2x^{4}+3xy^{3}+2y-y^{2}+1\in\mathbb{C}[x,y]
, the Newton polytope of
f
is a triangle while the Newton polytope of
g
is a pentagon. The Newton polytope of the 3-variate polynomial
h=x+2y+z-3\in\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In convex geometry, the dual object to a polytope is its
normal fan
. This collection of cones remembers the information about the orthogonal space to the faces of the polytope. Let us recall some definitions.

Definition 3.17
.

A
rational polyhedral cone
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the set of positive linear combinations of a finite set of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, i.e., a set of the form:

\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})=\{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\geq 0\},

for some
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. A
face
of a rational polyhedral cone
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a rational polyhedral cone
C^{\prime}\subset C
such that if
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in C
satisfy
\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}\in C^{\prime}
, then
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in C^{\prime}
. The dimension of a rational polyhedral cone
C
is the dimension of its linear span
\langle C\rangle
. We will write:

\dim C:=\dim\langle C\rangle.

Definition 3.18
.

A
rational polyhedral fan
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a finite collection
\Sigma
of rational polyhedral cones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that:

•

For every rational polyhedral cone
C\in\Sigma
and every face
C^{\prime}
of
C
, we have
C^{\prime}\in\Sigma
;

•

For every pair of rational polyhedral cones
C_{1},C_{2}\in\Sigma
that intersect,
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
is a face of both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

The
support
of
\Sigma
is the set-theoretic union of all its cones
|\Sigma|=\bigcup_{C\in\Sigma}C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
dimension
of
\Sigma
is the maximum dimension of a cone
C\in\Sigma
. A rational polyhedral fan
\Sigma
is
pure
of dimension
d
if every maximal-by-inclusion face
C
of
\Sigma
has dimension
\dim C=d
. The
k
-th skeleton
of
\Sigma
is the polyhedral fan
\Sigma^{\leq k}
made by the cones
C\in\Sigma
of dimension
\dim C\leq k
.

An example of a rational polyhedral fan is given by the following construction.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
P=\text{conv}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})
be a polytope, where
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and we assume for simplicity that
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}
are the vertices of
P
. The (inner)
normal fan
to
P
is the rational polyhedral fan
\mathcal{N}(P)
with rational polyhedral cones

\mathcal{N}_{F}(P)=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})\leq 0\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in\text{conv}(F),\ \mathbf{w}\in P\}

for each face
\text{conv}(F)\subset P,\ \emptyset\neq F\subset\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\}
.

Example 3.20
.

The normal fan of the Newton polytope of
f=x+2y-5
in
Example

3.16
is a 2-dimensional pure polyhedral fan in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with three rays (i.e., 1-dimensional cones)
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
generated by
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0)
,
\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1)
and
\mathbf{e}_{3}=(-1,-1)
respectively, together with three 2-dimensional cones
C_{1}=\text{cone}(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}),\ C_{2}=\text{cone}(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{3}),\ C_{3}=\text{cone}(\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3})
and the zero-dimensional cone
\tau=\{(0,0)\}
. As we shall see, the support of its 1-skeleton (i.e., the union of the rays
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
) is the tropicalization of a generic line
L\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
and it is called a

.

As in the previous example, normal fans of Newton polytopes provide tropical hypersurfaces, as we see in the following definition. Note that, as usually done in the literature, we address tropical geometry in the affine setting. This will allow us anyway to characterize projective varieties with infinite generic Hadamard rank. It will be done by looking at the tropicalization of their affine cones.

Notation
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial. We denote by
Z(f)=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
the corresponding affine hypersurface.

Definition 3.21
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial.
The
tropical hypersurface

\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the support of the
(n-1)
-skeleton of the normal fan of the Newton polytope
\operatorname{Newt}(f)
.

For concise varieties, the previous definition agrees with the usual and more general definition of tropical hypersurfaces using initial ideals or tropical polynomials with respect to the trivial valuation. See
[
MS15
, Proposition 3.1.6]
.

The general definition of tropicalization relies on tropical hypersurfaces.

Definition 3.22
.

Given a concise affine variety
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we define its tropicalization as

\operatorname{Trop}X=\bigcap_{f\in I(X)}\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

where the intersection runs over all polynomials in the ideal of
X
.

We are ready to state one of the main theorems in tropical geometry, whose main consequence is that tropicalization preserves dimensions.

Theorem 3.23

(Structure Theorem of tropical varieties
[
MS15
]
)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a concise irreducible variety. Then,
\operatorname{Trop}X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the support of a pure rational balanced polyhedral fan of dimension
\dim X
.

The combinatorial structure of tropical varieties will be our main tool for investigating Hadamard-idempotent varieties. We recall the necessary definitions.

Notation

\Sigma
.

Definition 3.24

Definition 3.28
.

Let

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\supset\tau\\

.

The

[

]
)
.
and, in particular, the balancing condition will be important in the study of Hadamard-idempotent varieties. The other main tool is the following theorem about the tropicalization of monomial maps

.

Theorem 3.30
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
be a monomial map, i.e.,
\alpha({\bf x})=({\bf x}^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,{\bf x}^{\alpha_{m}})
where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a concise variety and
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be the matrix whose columns are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
. Then

\operatorname{Trop}\overline{\alpha(X)}=A^{T}(\operatorname{Trop}X).

An important consequence regards tropicalizations of Hadamard products of affine varieties
X,Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
.

Corollary 3.31
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be concise affine varieties. Then

\operatorname{Trop}X\star Y=\operatorname{Trop}X+\operatorname{Trop}Y,

where the sum on the right denotes the Minkowski sum.

3.3.2.
Hadamard-idempotent varieties and linear tropical varieties

The starting point of our investigation is the following remark.

Remark 3.32
.

Toric varieties are Hadamard-idempotent. At the same time, consider a monomial embedding
\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{0}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{N}})
as in
Definition

3.5
, whose Zariski closure gives the affine cone of a toric variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
. The tropicalization of this embedding gives a parametrization
\mathbf{x}\mapsto(\alpha_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\cdot\mathbf{x})=A^{T}\mathbf{x}
of the tropicalization of the affine cone of
X
, by
Theorem

3.30
. Hence,
\operatorname{Trop}X
is a linear space.

Building on the previous remark, it is natural to ask if it is always true that the tropicalization of a Hadamard-idempotent variety is
always
a linear space. In the following, we give a positive answer to this question. As in the previous section, we develop our tools in the affine case. We explain how to pass to projective varieties before stating the main result of this section where we classify concise irreducible projective varieties whose Hadamard powers do not fill the ambient space (
Corollary

3.40
).

Lemma 3.33
.

Let
\Sigma
be a polyhedral fan in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose support is a cone but not a linear space. Then,
\Sigma
is not balanced with respect to any weight
w\colon\Sigma^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\sigma=|\Sigma|=\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{d})
. Up to quotienting by the lineality space of
\sigma
, i.e., the largest
linear space contained in
\sigma
, we can suppose that
\sigma\neq\{0\}
and that
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{d}
are linearly independent. If
d=1
, then
\Sigma
is clearly not balanced. If
d\geq 2
, let
\tau=\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{d-1})
. Then,
\tau
is a codimension-1 cone of
\Sigma
and
\text{Star}_{\Sigma}(\tau)
is not balanced. In fact, since
\mathbf{v}_{d}\not\in\tau
, we have that

\pi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v}_{d})\neq\bf 0
and
\text{Star}_{\Sigma}(\tau)^{1}=\{\text{cone}(\pi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v}_{d}))\}
. A single ray is not balanced, hence
\text{Star}_{\Sigma}(\tau)
is not balanced.
∎

Remark 3.34
.

In the proof of
Lemma

3.33
, we use the hypothesis that
\sigma=|\Sigma|
is a cone but not a linear space. In fact, if
\sigma
is a linear space, then it is equal to its lineality space, and then by quotienting we obtain
\{\bf 0\}
and we cannot continue with the proof. A way of writing a linear space as a cone is by taking a basis
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{d}
and writing it as
\text{cone}(\pm\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathbf{v}_{d})
. But then, the set
\{\pm\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathbf{v}_{d}\}
is not linearly independent. Any other generating set (as a cone) will give rise to this problem.

Theorem 3.35
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a concise irreducible affine variety such that
X^{\star 2}=X
. Then,
\operatorname{Trop}X
is a linear space.

Proof.

By
Corollary

3.31
and the Hadamard-idempotent assumption, we know that
\operatorname{Trop}X+\operatorname{Trop}X=\operatorname{Trop}X^{\star 2}=\operatorname{Trop}X
. By induction, for any
k\geq 2
:

(3.2)

k\cdot\operatorname{Trop}X=\underbrace{\operatorname{Trop}X+\cdots+\operatorname{Trop}X}_{k\text{ times}}=\operatorname{Trop}X.

By the
Theorem 3.23

(Structure Theorem of tropical varieties
[
MS15
]
)
.
,
\operatorname{Trop}X
is (the support of) a pure, balanced rational polyhedral fan
\Sigma
of dimension
\dim X
. Let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
be the collection of all rays that appear among a generating set of a cone in
\Sigma
. We shall prove that
\operatorname{Trop}X=\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})
. Take
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\geq 0
. Since
\operatorname{Trop}X
is the support of a polyhedral fan,
\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\operatorname{Trop}X
for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Thus, by
Equation

3.2
, we have that:

\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}}_{\in\operatorname{Trop}X}+\cdots+\underbrace{\lambda_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}}_{\in\operatorname{Trop}X}\in k\cdot\operatorname{Trop}X=\operatorname{Trop}X,

from which we obtain that
\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})\subset\operatorname{Trop}X
. Moreover,
\operatorname{Trop}X\subset\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})
always holds true. In fact, since
\operatorname{Trop}X
is the support of
\Sigma
, for every
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{Trop}X
, there exists a cone
C\in\Sigma
such that
\mathbf{x}\in C
. By definition of the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, there exist
1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\leq k
for which
C=\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{i_{m}})
. This means that
\mathbf{x}\in C\subset\text{cone}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})
. In the end, we obtain that
\operatorname{Trop}X
is a cone. Since
\Sigma
is balanced, by
Lemma

3.33
,
\operatorname{Trop}X
(i.e., the support of
\Sigma
) has to be a linear space.
∎

Notation
.

In what follows, by a binomial we mean a polynomial of the form
a\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}+b\mathbf{x}^{\beta}
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
,
a,b\neq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}
.

Proposition 3.36
.

Let
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a concise irreducible hypersurface. Then,
\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)
is a linear space if and only if
f
is a binomial.

Proof.

If
f=a\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}+b\mathbf{x}^{\beta}
is a binomial, then
\operatorname{Newt}(f)
(
Definition

3.15
) is the line segment with
vertices
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}
and (the support of) its normal fan (
Definition

3.19
) is the tropical hypersurface
\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(\alpha-\beta)\cdot\mathbf{x}=0\}
, which is an hyperplane.

Assume that
\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)
is a linear space. Let
\text{Newt}(f)\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}
be the integer points of
\text{Newt}(f)
; that is, all the possible exponents of monomials that can appear in
f
, i.e.,

f(\mathbf{x})=c_{0}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{0}}+c_{1}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{1}}+\cdots+c_{m}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{m}}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]

for some
c_{0},\ldots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
. By the definition of tropical hypersurface (
Definition

3.21
), we know that
\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)
is (the support of) the
(n-1)
-skeleton of the normal fan of the Newton polytope
\text{Newt}(f)
. Hence, from the assumptions,
\text{Newt}(f)
is a line segment. In particular, we can assume that
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{m}
are the vertices of
\text{Newt}(f)
and that
\alpha_{1}
is the first lattice point after
\alpha_{0}
on the oriented segment from
\alpha_{0}
to
\alpha_{m}
. In other words, we have that
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{0}=j\beta
for all
j
, where
\beta=\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0}
. Then, we can then rewrite

f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{0}}(c_{0}+c_{1}\mathbf{x}^{\beta}+c_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\beta})^{2}+\cdots+c_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\beta})^{m}),

where the second factor can be regarded as a univariate polynomial in
\mathbf{x}^{\beta}
with complex coefficients and, therefore, we can decompose

(3.3)

f(\mathbf{x})=c\ \mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{0}}(\mathbf{x}^{\beta}-\lambda_{1})\cdots(\mathbf{x}^{\beta}-\lambda_{m})

for some
c,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
. In order to conclude the proof it is enough to show that
m=1
. Tne o do that, we employ the irreducibility of
f
. Note that,
Equation

3.3
is a decomposition as Laurent polynomials.

We denote
\alpha_{i}=(\alpha_{i,1},\ldots,\alpha_{i,n})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}).

Let
\bar{\alpha}=(\min_{i=0,\ldots,m}\alpha_{i,j})_{j=1,\ldots,N}
. Since
\operatorname{Newt}(f)
is a segment,
\bar{\alpha}_{j}=\min(\alpha_{0,j},\alpha_{m,j})
. Clearly,
\mathbf{x}^{\bar{\alpha}}\ |\ f
. Since
f
is assumed to be irreducible,
\bar{\alpha}={\bf 0}
. Therefore, we will distinguish two cases: (i)
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha_{0}
and (ii)
\bar{\alpha}\neq\alpha_{0}
.

Case (i).
If
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha_{0}
, then the conclusion is immediate. Indeed,
\beta=\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}
and, by irreducibility,
m=1
.

Case (ii).
Assume
\bar{\alpha}\neq\alpha_{0}
. Since
\alpha_{m}-\alpha_{0}=m\beta
, then for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
\alpha_{m,i}-\alpha_{0,i}=m\beta_{i}
. Note that, for any
i
,
\beta_{i}<0
if and only if
\alpha_{0,i}>0
if and only if
\alpha_{m,i}=0
(the latter equivalence is because
\bar{\alpha}=\bf 0
). In particular,
\alpha_{0}=m\beta^{-}
, where
\beta^{-}=(\max(-\beta_{j},0))_{j=1,\ldots,N}
and, from
Equation

3.3
, we deduce the polynomial decomposition

f(\mathbf{x})=c\ (\mathbf{x}^{\beta+\beta^{-}}-\lambda_{1}\mathbf{x}^{\beta^{-}})\cdots(\mathbf{x}^{\beta+\beta^{-}}-\lambda_{m}\mathbf{x}^{\beta^{-}})

where
\beta^{-},\beta+\beta^{-}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}
. By irreducibility,
m=1
and we conclude.
∎

Corollary 3.37
.

Let
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a concise irreducible hypersurface such that
(\operatorname{Trop}Z(f))^{\perp}\neq\{\bf 0\}
. Then,
f
is a binomial.

Proof.

Let
F=\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)
. Since
F^{\perp}\neq\{\bf 0\}
and
\dim\langle F\rangle\geq N-1
, we have that
\dim F^{\perp}=1
. Hence,
\dim\langle F\rangle=N-1
. Now, suppose towards obtaining a
contradiction that
f
is not a binomial. By
Proposition

3.36
,
F
is not a linear space. In particular, the Newton polytope of
f
cannot be a line segment, hence the support of
f
is not made by aligned vectors. Write
f=c_{0}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{0}}+\cdots+c_{d}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha_{d}}
. Then, we have that there exist
i,j,k
such that
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}
and
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{k}
are linearly independent. Hence, the following two faces of
F
are contained in two distinct hyperplanes of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

F_{ij}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\alpha_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}=\alpha_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}\leq\alpha_{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{x}\ \forall\ell\},\quad F_{ik}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\alpha_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}=\alpha_{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}\leq\alpha_{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{x}\ \forall\ell\}.

This implies that
\langle F\rangle=\mathbb{R}^{N}
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 3.38
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a concise irreducible variety. Then,
\operatorname{Trop}X
is contained in a proper linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
if and only if
X
is contained in a binomial hypersurface of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.

Proof.

If
X\subset Z(f)
and
f
is a binomial, then
\operatorname{Trop}X\subset\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)
and
\operatorname{Trop}Z(f)
is a proper linear subspace by
Proposition

3.36
. For the opposite direction, let
\Sigma=\operatorname{Trop}X
. By
[
MS15
, Theorem 2.6.6]
, every ideal has a finite
tropical basis
. This means that there exists a finite collection
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
of generators of the ideal of
X
such that

\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\Sigma_{s},\quad\Sigma_{i}=\operatorname{Trop}Z(f_{i}).

This implies that

\Sigma^{\perp}=\Sigma_{1}^{\perp}+\cdots+\Sigma_{s}^{\perp}.

Since
X
is irreducible, each
f_{i}
is irreducible. Moreover, since
\Sigma
is contained in a proper linear subspace,
\Sigma^{\perp}\neq\{\bf 0\}
, from which it follows that
\Sigma^{\perp}_{i}\neq\{\bf 0\}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
. Since
\Sigma_{i}
is a tropical hypersurface, by
Corollary

3.37
we know that
f_{i}
is a binomial. Hence,
X
is contained in a binomial hypersurface
Z(f_{i})
.
∎

Remark 3.39
.

The previous results work also in the projective setting. Given a concise projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, denote by
\widehat{X}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
its affine cone. Then, one defines the tropicalization of
X
by quotienting
\operatorname{Trop}\widehat{X}
by the span of
\mathds{1}
in order to work in the
tropical projective space

\mathbb{TP}^{n}
:

\operatorname{Trop}X=\operatorname{Trop}\widehat{X}/\langle\mathds{1}\rangle\subset\mathbb{TP}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\langle\mathds{1}\rangle.

In particular, in the previous results,
\operatorname{Trop}X
is a linear space inside
\mathbb{TP}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\operatorname{Trop}\widehat{X}
is a linear space in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

We conclude this section with the classification of irreducible varieties with infinite generic
X
-Hadamard-rank in the projective setting, i.e., such that Hadamard powers of
X
do not fill the ambient space.

Corollary 3.40
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be a concise irreducible variety. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

the generic
X
-Hadamard-rank is infinite, i.e.,
X^{\star m}\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{N}
for all
m\geq 1
;

(2)

X
is contained in a proper binomial hypersurface;

(3)

the ideal of
X
contains binomials.

Proof.

The fact that (2) is equivalent to (3) is trivial by the fact that taking ideals reverses inclusions.

We prove that (2) implies (1). Assume that
X\subset Y\subsetneq\mathbb{P}^{N}
where
Y
is a binomial hypersurface. Interpreting
Proposition

3.36
in light of
Remark

3.39
,
\operatorname{Trop}Y
is a linear space. Hence, by
Corollary

3.31
,
\operatorname{Trop}Y^{\star m}=\operatorname{Trop}Y+\cdots+\operatorname{Trop}Y=\operatorname{Trop}Y
. Hence,
\dim X^{\star m}\leq\dim Y^{\star m}=\dim Y<N
and
\mathrm{Hrk}_{X}^{\circ}=\infty
.

We now prove that (1) implies (2). Since
X
is concise, then by
Proposition

3.12
, we know that
X\subset tY
, where
t\in\mathbb{T}
is a diagonal invertible matrix,
Y=Y^{\star 2}
and
Y
is concise and irreducible. By
Theorem

3.35
, we know that
\operatorname{Trop}Y
is a linear space. Hence, by
Theorem

3.38
,
Y
is contained in an irreducible binomial hypersurface
Z
. In particular,
tZ
is still an irreducible binomial hypersurface. Since
X\subset tY\subset tZ
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.41
.

We recall that the task of checking computationally whether an ideal in a polynomial ring contains a binomial was studied in
[
JKK17
]
.

4.
Dimensions of Hadamard products of secant varieties

Inspired by
B
and the connection with Restricted Boltzmann Machines already mentioned in
Remark

3.2
, we are interested in the dimensions of Hadamard products of secant varieties.
This allows to compute generic
r
th Hadamard-X-ranks: indeed, recall that the generic
r
th Hadamard-
X
-rank with respect is equal to the smallest
m
for which
\sigma_{r}(X)^{\star m}
fills the ambient space. Since many varieties appearing in tensor decomposition and algebraic statistics are secant varieties of toric varieties, we will focus on them.

Notation
.

In order to ease notation, for any
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
, we write
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X):=\sigma_{r_{1}}(X)\star\cdots\star\sigma_{r_{m}}(X)
.

4.1.
Expected dimensions

Secant varieties of non-degenerate algebraic varieties have a notion of
expected dimensions
which comes from a naïve parameter count. If
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is non-degenerate, then

(4.1)

\exp\!.\dim\sigma_{r}(X)=\min\{N,\ r\dim X+r-1\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\dim\sigma_{r}(X)\leq\exp\!.\dim\sigma_{r}(X).

When the latter inequality is strict we say that
X
is
r
-defective
.

Examples of defective varieties are known among Segre-Veronese varieties since the XIX century. The challenge of providing a complete classification of such defective varieties took the attention of a very broad literature since the late XIX century. Complete results are known for Veronese varieties and some families of Segre-Veronese varieties. We refer to
[
OV25
]
for a recent updated list of known cases.

In the case of Hadamard products of algebraic varieties a notion of expected dimension was introduced in
[
BCK16
]
. In particular, given
X,Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
,

(4.2)

\exp\!.\dim X\star Y=\min\{\dim X+\dim Y-\dim T,\ N\},

where
T\subset(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{N+1}/\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is the highest-dimensional torus acting on both
X
and
Y
. By
[
BCK16
, Proposition 5.4]
,
\dim X\star Y\leq\exp\!.\dim X\star Y
.
In particular, if
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is a toric variety, that is, we have a torus
\mathbb{T}_{X}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{\dim X}
acting on
X
, then, from
Equation

4.2
, we get

(4.3)

\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)\leq\exp.\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1,\ldots,m}\dim\sigma_{r_{i}}(X)-(m-1)\dim X,N\right\}.

If the inequality is strict, we say that
X
is
\mathbf{r}
-Hadamard-defective
.

If we bound
Equation

4.3
by using the expected dimension of
\sigma_{r_{i}}(X)
from
Equation

4.1
, then

(4.4)

\displaystyle\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{\sum_{i=1,\ldots,m}(r_{i}\dim X+r_{i}-1)-(m-1)\dim X,N\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\left(\sum_{i=1,\ldots,m}(r_{i}-1)+1\right)\dim X+\sum_{i=1,\ldots,m}(r_{i}-1),N\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\!.\dim\sigma_{R}(X),\qquad\text{ with }R:=R(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})=\sum_{i=1,\ldots,m}(r_{i}-1)+1.

In the case
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{m}=r
, expecting
Equation

4.4
to be an equality, namely, that
\dim\sigma_{r}(X)^{\star m}=\min\left\{\left(m(r-1)+1\right)\dim X+m(r-1),N\right\}
, we say that the
expected generic
r
th Hadamard-
X
-rank
with respect to a toric variety
X
is

\exp\!.\ \mathrm{Hrk}_{X,r}^{\circ}=\left\lceil\frac{N-\dim X}{(r-1)(\dim X+1)}\right\rceil.

In
Theorem

4.3
, we will show that the dimension of the
R
th secant variety as in
Equation

4.4
of a toric variety
X
is always a lower bound for the dimension of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
. Explicitly, continuing from
Equation

4.4
, we will prove that, given a concise toric variety
X

(4.5)

\dim\sigma_{R}(X)\leq\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)\leq\exp\!.\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)=\exp\!.\dim\sigma_{R}(X).

In the cases where we know that
X
is not
R
-defective, this chain of inequalities is a chain of equalities and allows us to compute dimensions of Hadamard products of secant varieties of
X
.

This is the strategy used in
[
MM17
, Corollary 26]
for restricted binary Boltzmann machines, that is, in the case of
X=S_{\mathds{1}}
being the Segre variety of binary tensors and
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{m}=2
, for any
m
, employing the classification of defective binary Segre varieties
S_{\mathds{1}}
from
[
CGG11
]
. With
Theorem

4.3
, we extend this strategy to any concise toric variety for an arbitrary choice of
\mathbf{r}
.

4.2.
A useful parametrization

We introduce a useful parametrization of (an affine chart) of the Hadamard product of secant varieties of a toric variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
that we will use instead of the classical one.

Let
A=(\alpha_{0}|\cdots|\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
be a matrix giving a monomial parametrization
\varphi_{A}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1}\to\mathbb{P}^{N}
of the toric variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
as in
Definition

3.5
.

4.2.1.
Parametrizations of
r
th secant varieties

The secant variety
\sigma_{r}(X)\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
can be regarded as a linear projection of the toric variety defined by the matrix
A\otimes I_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r(n+1)\times r(N+1)}
, where
I_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r\times r}
is the identity matrix.
Indeed, if
\varphi_{A}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N},\ {\mathbf{x}}\mapsto(\cdots:{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha_{i}}:\cdots)
defines
X
, then
\sigma_{r}(X)
is parametrized by the composition of the maps

(4.6)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\varphi_{A\otimes I_{r}}:&((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times r}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{(N+1)r-1},\\
&({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{r})&\longmapsto&(\cdots:\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{i}):\cdots)_{i=1,\ldots,r};\\
\\
\pi_{r}:&\mathbb{P}^{(N+1)r-1}&\dashrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{N}\\
&((z_{i,0}:\cdots:z_{i,N}))_{i=1,\ldots,r}&\longmapsto&(\cdots:\sum_{i=1}^{r}z_{i,j}:\cdots)_{j=0,\ldots,N}.\end{array}

On the affine side, this corresponds to the usual parametrization of the affine cone of the
r
th secant variety as

(4.7)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes I_{r}}:&((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times r}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}^{N+1},\\
&({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{r})&\longmapsto&\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{2})+\cdots+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{r}).\end{array}

If we pre-compose the above map with the automorphism of the torus
((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times r}
given by

(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{r})\longmapsto(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{r}),

whose inverse is
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{r})\longmapsto(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\star(-1)}\star\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\star(-1)}\star\mathbf{x}_{r})
, we get a parametrization of the affine cone of the
r
th secant variety as

(4.8)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{r}}:&((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times r}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}^{N+1},\\
&({\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{r})&\longmapsto&\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{2})+\cdots+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{r}),\end{array}

namely, as the composition of the monomial map
\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{r}}
, where

\bar{I}_{r}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\mathds{1}\\
\bf 0&I_{r-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
with the affine linear projection corresponding to
\pi_{r}
, which we denote by
\widehat{\pi}_{r}
. As mentioned in
Remark

3.6
, (dense subsets of) toric varieties can be reparametrized in terms of matrices that have the same row-span as the original matrix.
This is the case when we replace
A\otimes I_{r}
with
A\otimes\bar{I}_{r}
.

Example 4.1
.

As a running example we consider the case
\mathbf{r}=(2,3)
that we will use to illustrate the definitions and the proof of
Theorem

4.3
presented below.
Namely, we will compare the dimension of
\sigma_{4}(X)
and the dimension of
\sigma_{(2,3)}(X)=\sigma_{2}(X)\star\sigma_{3}(X)
. In particular, we consider the parametrization of (a dense subset of) the affine cone of the
4
th secant variety of
X
given by

\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{4}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3},\mathbf{y}_{4})=\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{2})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{3})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1}\star\mathbf{y}_{4}).

4.2.2.
Parametrization of Hadamard products of secant varieties

The Hadamard product of secant varieties
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)=\sigma_{r_{1}}(X)\star\cdots\star\sigma_{r_{m}}(X)\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is a linear projection of the toric variety defined by the matrix
A\otimes B_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{(\sum_{k}r_{k})(n+1)\times(\prod_{k}r_{k})(N+1)}
, where
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{r}-\mathds{1}
, i.e.,
r^{\prime}_{k}=r_{k}-1
, and
B_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(\sum_{k}r_{k})\times(\prod_{k}r_{k})}
is the matrix defining the standard monomial parametrization of the Segre variety
S_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
as in
Example

3.7
.
Indeed,
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is parametrized by the composition of the maps

(4.9)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\varphi_{A\otimes B_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}\colon&\prod_{k=1}^{m}((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times r_{k}}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{(N+1)\times(\prod_{k}r_{k})-1},\\
&({\mathbf{x}}_{k,i})_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\ldots,m\\
i=0,\ldots,r^{\prime}_{k}\end{subarray}}&\longmapsto&(\cdots:\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1,i_{1}}\star\cdots\star\mathbf{x}_{m,i_{m}}):\cdots)_{\mathbf{i}\in[r^{\prime}_{1}]\times\cdots\times[r^{\prime}_{m}]};\\
\\
\pi_{\mathbf{r}}\colon&\mathbb{P}^{(N+1)\times(\prod_{k}r_{k})-1}&\dashrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{N}\\
&((z_{\mathbf{i},0}:\ldots:z_{\mathbf{i},N}))_{\mathbf{i}\in[r^{\prime}_{1}]\times\cdots\times[r^{\prime}_{m}]}&\longmapsto&(\cdots:\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[r^{\prime}_{1}]\times\cdots\times[r^{\prime}_{k}]}z_{\mathbf{i},j}:\cdots)_{j=0,\ldots,N},\end{array}

where
[r^{\prime}_{k}]:=\{0,1,\ldots,r^{\prime}_{k}\}
.
On the affine side, this corresponds to the classical parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
given by

(4.10)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes B_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}:&\prod_{k=1}^{m}((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times r_{k}}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}^{N+1},\\
&({\mathbf{x}}_{k,i_{k}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\ldots,m\\
i_{k}=0,\ldots,r^{\prime}_{k}\end{subarray}}&\longmapsto&\left(\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{r^{\prime}_{1}}\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1,i_{1}})\right)\star\cdots\star\left(\sum_{i_{m}=0}^{r^{\prime}_{m}}\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{m,i_{m}})\right)\\
&&&=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[r^{\prime}_{1}]\times\cdots\times[r^{\prime}_{m}]}\left(\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1,i_{1}})\star\cdots\star\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{m,i_{m}})\right).\end{array}

Since
\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1}\star\mathbf{x}_{2})=\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\star\widehat{\varphi}_{A}({\mathbf{x}_{2}})
,
we have that

\mathop{\bigstar}_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i_{k}=0}^{r^{\prime}_{k}}\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{k,i_{k}})\right)=\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{1,0}\star\mathbf{x}_{2,0}\star\cdots\star\mathbf{x}_{m,0})\star\mathop{\bigstar}_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i_{k}=0}^{r^{\prime}_{k}}\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}_{k,i_{k}}\star\mathbf{x}_{k,0}^{\star(-1)})\right).

Using this and the fact that
\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{x}\star\mathbf{x}^{\star(-1)})=\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathds{1})=\mathds{1}
,
we obtain a parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
as

(4.11)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}:&((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times\left(\sum_{k}r^{\prime}_{k}+1\right)}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}^{N+1},\\
&(\mathbf{y}_{0},(\mathbf{y}_{k,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\ldots,m\\
j=1,\ldots,r^{\prime}_{k}\end{subarray}})&\longmapsto&\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0})\star\mathop{\bigstar}_{k=1}^{m}\left(\mathds{1}+\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{r^{\prime}_{k}}\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{k,i_{k}})\right)\\
&&&=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in[r^{\prime}_{1}]\times\cdots\times[r^{\prime}_{m}]}\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0}\star\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{j}}),\end{array}

where

\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{j}}:=\mathbf{y}_{1,j_{1}}\star\cdots\star\mathbf{y}_{m,j_{m}},\text{ with the convention that }\mathbf{y}_{i,0}=\mathds{1}.

Let
\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
be the matrix defined in
Example

3.7
. Observe that (
4.11
) is obtained by composing the parametrization
\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
with the affine linear projection corresponding to
\pi_{\mathbf{r}}
, which we denote by
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathbf{r}}
.
Moreover, as expected, in the special case
m=1
, (
4.8
) and (
4.11
) coincide (up to relabeling of the parameter indices).

Summing up, we have a parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{r}(X)
obtained as
\widehat{\pi}_{r}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{r}}
and a parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
obtained as
\widehat{\pi}_{\bf r}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
.
Note that the matrix
\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
contains the matrix
\bar{I}_{R}
,
R=\sum_{k=1}^{m}r^{\prime}_{k}+1
as a submatrix. This will be crucial for our proof of
Theorem

4.3
in the next section.

Example 4.2
.

Continuing our running example (
Example

4.1
), we consider the parametrization of (a dense subset of) the affine cone of the Hadamard product
\sigma_{(2,3)}(X)=\sigma_{2}(X)\star\sigma_{3}(X)
given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{1,2}}(\mathbf{y}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{1,1},\mathbf{y}_{2,1},\mathbf{y}_{2,2})=\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0})\star(\mathds{1}+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{1,1})\star(\mathds{1}+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{2,1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{2,2}))

\displaystyle=\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0}\star\mathbf{y}_{1,1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0}\star\mathbf{y}_{2,1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0}\star\mathbf{y}_{2,2})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0}\star\mathbf{y}_{1,1}\star\mathbf{y}_{2,1})+\widehat{\varphi}_{A}(\mathbf{y}_{0}\star\mathbf{y}_{1,1}\star\mathbf{y}_{2,2}).

4.3.
On dimensions of Hadamard products of secant varieties of toric varieties

In this section, we prove the following result. As already mentioned, this will be our crucial tool to deduce dimensions of Hadamard products of secant varieties of toric varieties whose secants are known to be non-defective.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
be a non-degenerate toric variety. Let
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{m}
and let
R=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(r_{k}-1)+1
. Then, the dimension of
\sigma_{R}(X)
is always a lower bound for the dimension of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
.

Remark 4.4
.

As mentioned, this generalizes
[
MM17
, Lemma 25]
, which was only for
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{m}=2
. Except for small adaptations, the strategy of the proof is essentially the same.
Note that the discussion of
[
MM17
]
is in terms of
exponential families
.
However, it is well-known that such statistical models are strictly related to embedded toric varieties.
Indeed, the standard exponential parametrization of the exponential family associated with the matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
corresponds to the precomposition of our monomial map
\widehat{\varphi}_{A}
with the map
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n+1}
given by
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})\mapsto(\exp(\eta_{1}),\ldots,\exp(\eta_{n}))
, see also
[
MSUZ16
, Example 2.4]
.
We further observe that the argument of the proof and the statement remain valid when the matrix
A
has real entries.
Using the terminology of
[
MM17
]
, our
Theorem

4.3
can be rephrased as follows.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{A}
be the exponential family associated with the matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
and let
\mathcal{M}_{r}(\mathcal{E})
denote its
r
th mixture model.
Then, the dimension of the
R
th mixture model
\mathcal{M}_{R}(X)
, with
R=\sum_{k}(r_{k}-1)+1
, is a lower bound for the dimension of the Hadamard product of mixture models
\mathcal{M}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{E}_{A})\star\cdots\star\mathcal{M}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{E}_{A})
.

Before getting into the technicalities of the proof, we present our strategy:

(1)

we compute the Jacobian of the parametrization
\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}=\widehat{\pi}_{\mathbf{r}}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
of the affine cone of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
, see (
4.11
);

(2)

we perform a modification to the Jacobian of
\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
that preserves its rank;

(3)

for a general choice of parameters
{\bf Y}=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{R})
, we construct a
1
-parameter family of modified Jacobian matrices of
\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
evaluated at
\mathbf{Y}_{\nu}=(\mathbf{y}^{\nu}_{0},\mathbf{y}^{\nu}_{k,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\ldots,m\\
j=1,\ldots,r_{k}\end{subarray}}
whose limit for
\nu\to 0
tends to the Jacobian matrix of the parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{R}(X)
given by
\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{R}}=\widehat{\pi}_{R}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{R}}
, see (
4.8
), evaluated at the points
\mathbf{Y}
;

(4)

since the rank of matrices is lower semicontinuous and the parameters
\mathbf{Y}
have been chosen generically, this concludes the proof.

Note that point
(3)
exploits the fact that the matrix
\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
contains, as submatrix, the matrix
\bar{I}_{R}
. Indeed, the parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{R}(X)
given by
\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{R}}=\widehat{\pi}_{R}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{I}_{R}}
in (
4.8
) is a truncation of the parametrization of the affine cone of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
given by
\widehat{\Phi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}=\widehat{\pi}_{\mathbf{r}}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{A\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
in (
4.11
), up to higher degree terms.

For convenience of the reader, to illustrate the heavy notations,
Example

4.6
presents a particular instance of the proof looking at the first previously unknown case of
\sigma_{(2,3)}(X)
for
X
being the Rational Normal Curve of
\mathbb{P}^{8}
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.3
.

First of all, we consider the parametrization of
X
given by a matrix
\bar{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
which has the same row-span of
A
, but the first row is equal to
\mathds{1}
and the first column is equal to
(1,0,\ldots,0)
, see
Remark

3.6
.

The (projective) dimension of
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
is

(4.12)

\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)=\max_{\mathbf{Y}\in((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+1})^{\times R}}~\operatorname{rk}{\rm Jac}(\widehat{\Phi}_{\bar{A}\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}})(\mathbf{Y})-1.

Recall the notation
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}-\mathds{1}
and
[r]=\{0,\ldots,r\}
. For
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, we denote by
\bar{A}^{(\ell)}
the matrix
\bar{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times(N+1)}
where the
\ell
th row has all positive entries decreased by one.

The Jacobian of
\widehat{\Phi}_{\bar{A}\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}}
evaluated at the parameters

\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{y}_{0}|\mathbf{y}_{1,1}|\cdots|\mathbf{y}_{1,r_{1}-1}|\cdots|\mathbf{y}_{m,1}|\cdots|\mathbf{y}_{m,r_{m}-1})\in(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{(n+1)\times R}

is a matrix of size
R(n+1)\times(N+1)
whose
h
th column, for
h\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
, has entries:

•

for
\ell=0,\ldots n
,

.

and matrices

Before proving the claim, let us see why this is enough to conclude our proof.
By

4.12

(4.15)

\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)+1\geq\operatorname{rk}{\rm Jac}(\widehat{\Phi}_{\bar{A}\otimes\bar{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}})(\mathbf{Y}_{\nu});

\nu

Then, due to the semicontinuity of the rank,
1
allows us to conclude that, for
\nu

Finally, by construction of the matrix

and by generality of

(4.19)

4.16

4.17

4.4.
General Hadamard ranks

Theorem

4.3
completes the proof of the chain of inequalities from
Equation

4.5
for embedded toric varieties. Under the assumption that
X
is not
R
-defective, the leftmost and rightmost terms coincide. In particular,
\dim\sigma_{R}(X)=\dim\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
. Recall that, in the particular case
\mathbf{r}=(r,\ldots,r)
, this allows us to deduce the smallest
m
for which
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
fills the ambient space, namely, to compute the general
r
th
X
-Hadamard-rank.

Motivated by applications to tensor decompositions and, in particular, to the study of discrete Restricted Boltzmann Machines defined in
[
MM15b
]
, we focus on the case of Segre-Veronese varieties. Recall that a full classification is not yet known, although several complete results are known when restricting to particular families: we refer to the recent survey
[
OV25
]
for more details on the current state-of-the-art and an extended list of references.

A classical general result ensures non-defectivity of
\sigma_{r}(X)
under the geometric constraints that no secant variety is a projective cone, for
r
smaller than a bound depending on the dimension and codimension of
X
; see
[
Adl88
, Corollary 2.3]
. The result has been extended in
[
TBC23
]
to varieties
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
that additionally are invariant under some certain group action. In particular,
[
TBC23
, Corollary 3.5]
, states such an
X
is non-defective for
r\leq\frac{N}{\dim X}-\dim X
and the
r
th secant variety fills the ambient space for
r\geq\frac{N}{\dim X}+\dim X
. A slightly stronger version can be found in
[
Bal24
, Theorem 2.1]
. Combining
[
TBC23
, Corollary 3.5]
with our
Theorem

4.3
, we deduce the following for all Segre-Veronese varieties.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
. Then, the Hadamard product of secant varieties of the Segre-Veronese variety of rank-one partially symmetric binary tensors
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(SV_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{n}})
are not Hadamard-defective if

\sum_{k}(r_{k}-1)+1\leq\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}{n_{i}+d_{i}\choose d_{i}}}{(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k})}-(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k})

and fills the ambient space if

\sum_{k}(r_{k}-1)+1\geq\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}{n_{i}+d_{i}\choose d_{i}}}{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}}+(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}).

In particular, the
r
th generic partially symmetric Hadamard rank in
{\rm Sym}^{d_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\rm Sym}^{d_{k}}\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}+1}
is at most equal to
\Bigl\lceil\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}{n_{i}+d_{i}\choose d_{i}}-(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k})}{(r-1)(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}+1)}\Bigr\rceil
.

Remark 4.8
.

For sake of completeness, we recall a general result on non-defectiveness of toric varieties given in
[
LMR22
, Theorem 2.13]
.
Here, it is proven that if
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
is a toric variety, then
\sigma_{r}(X)
is non-defective for
r<\frac{|P|-m}{\dim X+1}
, where the upper bound is given in terms of the lattice points
|P|
of the polytope defining
X
and the maximal number
m
of lattice points of a hyperplane section of
P
.

As mentioned, we list here a series of results for the most complete families of Segre-Veronese varieties for which a full classification of defective secant varieties is known.

4.4.1.
Veronese varieties, i.e., the case of symmetric tensors

In

5.2.
Identifiability of Hadamard decompositions

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
be an algebraic variety. For any
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
, consider a generic
p\in\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)=\sigma_{r_{1}}(X)\star\cdots\star\sigma_{r_{m}}(X)
:

in how many ways can one write
p=q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{m}
with
q_{i}\in\langle p_{i,1},\ldots,p_{i,r_{i}}\rangle\subset\sigma_{r_{i}}(X)
?

In other words, if we consider the map parametrizing the affine cone of the Hadamard product, namely

\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{r}}\colon\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{X}^{\times r_{i}}\longrightarrow\widehat{\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)};\quad(p_{i,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,m\\
j=1,\ldots,r_{i}\end{subarray}}\mapsto\bigstar_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}p_{i,j}\right),

how does
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}(p)
look like for a general
p\in\widehat{\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)}
?

Clearly, there are actions of permutation groups. If
\mathfrak{S}_{r}
denotes the permutation on
r
elements and
\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{r})\subset\mathfrak{S}_{m}
is the subgroup preserving
\mathbf{r}
, then we consider the map
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{r}}
up to the action of
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathfrak{S}_{r_{i}}\times\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{r})
. We denote by
\widehat{X}^{(\mathbf{r})}
the quotient of
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{X}^{\times r_{i}}
by these permutation groups.

If
X
is a toric variety, we have also the action of
(m-1)
-copies of the full dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}_{X}^{m-1}
:

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m-1})\cdot(p_{i,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,m\\
j=1,\ldots,r_{i}\end{subarray}}:=\begin{cases}t_{i}\cdot p_{i,j}&\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m-1;\\
(t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1})^{-1}\cdot p_{m,j}&\text{ for }i=m.\end{cases}

Following a similar approach to the one considered in
[
OV23
]
, we consider the quotient of
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}(p)
up to the action of all these groups, i.e., we consider the map

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{r}}\colon\widehat{X}^{(\mathbf{r})}/\mathbb{T}_{X}^{m-1}\longrightarrow\widehat{\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)},\ (p_{i,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,m\\
j=1,\ldots,r_{i}\end{subarray}}\mapsto\bigstar_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}p_{i,j}\right).

Since the dimension of
X^{(\mathbf{r})}/\mathbb{T}_{X}^{m-1}
is equal to
\sum_{i}\dim\sigma_{r_{i}}(X)-(m-1)\dim X
, it follows that whenever
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
has the same dimension, namely in the cases in which it is not Hadamard-defective and it is not overfitting its ambient space, then the general fiber of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{r}}
is finite. By overfitting, we mean that parameter count
\sum_{i}\dim\sigma_{r_{i}}(X)-(m-1)\dim X
does not strictly exceed the dimension of the ambient space.
Following the standard literature on secant varieties and additive decompositions, if it is a singleton, then we say that
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(X)
is
generically identifiable
. This rises the following natural question.

Question C
.

Are the cases of known Hadamard-nondefective and not over-fitting Segre-Veronese varieties shown in
Section

4
generically identifiable?

In
[
OV23
]
, it was proved that this is the case for
m
th Hadamard powers of
r
th secant varieties of Segre varieties as long as
mr
is below a numerical bound known as the
reshaped Kruskal bound

[
COV17
]
.
In
[
KYMS+24
]
, a certain type of Hadamard product model is considered where each Hadamard factor corresponds to a subset of
\sigma_{2}(V_{d,1})
(fixed mixture weights). They show that for
d
at least equal to the number of Hadamard factors, this model is generically locally identifiable.

5.4.
Lower bounds on the dimension

In
Theorem

4.3
we extended
[
MM17
, Lemma 25]
to show that the dimension of a Hadamard product of secant varieties of a toric variety
X
is always bounded from below by the dimension of the secant variety of
X
with the same expected dimension.
Although these two types of varieties are quite different in general
[
MM15a
]
, we showed that they have parametrizations whose Jacobians can be related to one another through a certain parameter scaling procedure.
To do this, we used that the matrix
\overline{B}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}
defining the parametrization of
\sigma_{r_{1}}(X)\star\cdots\star\sigma_{r_{m}}(X)
contains as a sub-matrix the matrix
\overline{I}_{R}
defining the parametrization of
\sigma_{R}(X)
. Further characterizing the conditions that allow the application of this parameter scaling procedure in order to lower bound the dimension of one variety by the dimension of another variety would be an interesting future work.

5.5.
Minimal decompositions and universal approximation

In
Section

3
we established upper bounds on the generic Hadamard rank; that is, the minimum number of factors so that the Zariski closure of the Hadamard product of the varieties fills the ambient space.
We also established upper bounds on the Hadamard rank of a point
p
; that is, the minimum number of elements in the Zariski closure of a variety so that their Hadamard product is equal to
p
.
In this direction, another topic of interest is the minimal number of factors that are needed when one does not take Zariski closures. Concretely, what is the minimum
m
so that
p
can be approximated arbitrarily well (in the standard topology of the ambient space of
X
) by a Hadamard product of points in a semi-algebraic set? For instance, we could consider expressions of the form
p_{1}\star\cdots\star p_{m}
, where each
p_{i}
is a convex combination of
r
points in
X
.
This problem is related to the
approximation errors
and
universal approximation
discussed in
[
MR17
]
. Also, can we obtain a semi-algebraic description of a Hadamard product of semi-algebraic sets? This is related to the questions raised in
[
SM18
]
and
[
SSM23
]
about inequalities and the questions raised in
Section

5.3
about the equations that cut the variety of Hadamard powers.
