Acute effects of upper-limb blood flow restriction training on dual-task postural control and physiological correlates in older adults
Authors: Yi-Ching Chen, Yu-Chen Sun, Yi-Ying Tsai, Chen-Guang Zhao, Ing-Shiou Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls represent a major health concern in older adults, underscoring the need for interventions that enhance postural control. This study investigated whether applying blood flow restriction (BFR) during short-term arm ergometry training improves posture–cognition dual-task performance and adaptive changes in cortical–postural coupling.MethodsTwenty-six older adults (12 males, 14 females; 69.1 ± 3.0 years) completed a single 21-minute session of arm ergometry with gender-specific workloads, either combined with BFR at 80% systolic pressure or without restriction (control). Dual-task performance, balance dynamics, and cortico-postural phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) were assessed during concurrent light-pod tapping and stance on an unstable foam surface.ResultsCompared with controls, the BFR condition resulted in greater reductions in center of pressure (COP) area (p = 0.002) and velocity (p = 0.003), indicating improved postural control. Stabilogram diffusion analysis further revealed reductions in critical displacement (CD, p < 0.001) and short-term diffusion coefficient (Ds, p = 0.002), suggesting decreased sway variability and enhanced postural regulation. Phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) analysis showed a significant between-condition difference in the theta band at the frontal region of interest, with greater negative modulation under BFR compared with NBFR (p = 0.016). In contrast, no significant between-condition differences were observed in the alpha or beta PAC (p > 0.05). These findings indicated frequency-specific modulation of cortico–postural coupling associated with upper-limb BFR training.ConclusionArm ergometry combined with BFR is associated with improved dual-task postural performance in older adults. These changes were accompanied by frequency-specific modulation of cortico–postural coupling, evident in the theta band at frontal regions. These findings suggest that upper-limb BFR training may represent a feasible and accessible intervention for improving balance under dual-task conditions in aging populations.

Introduction

Falls represent a major health risk for older adults. Dual-task scenarios that require simultaneous cognitive and motor processing often exacerbate postural instability, reflecting age-related declines in executive function and diminished capacity to allocate neural resources to resolve task interference. Identifying strategies that can cognitively enhance both postural control and cognitive function is a priority in geriatric rehabilitation.

Blood flow restriction (BFR) training has been established as an effective method to increase muscle strength, hypertrophy, and rehabilitation outcomes while relying on low mechanical loads (~ 20–30% 1RM), thereby minimizing joint stress [1, 2]. Its benefits arise from restricted venous return, which increases metabolic stress and elicits adaptations similar to high-intensity resistance exercise. BFR accelerates lactate accumulation [3, 4] and upregulates neurotrophic factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) [5–7]. Beyond peripheral adaptations, these metabolites act as signaling molecules that influence cerebral blood flow, neurotransmitter release, and cortical excitability. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy studies support this view, showing that low-load BFR training increases cerebral blood flow and oxygenated hemoglobin more than exercise without restriction [8, 9]. Collectively, these findings suggest that BFR could serve as a low-cost, non-pharmacological intervention with potential cognitive benefits for both young adults [10, 11] and older populations [12, 13].

Accumulating evidence supports cognitive benefits of BFR. Acute improvements in executive function have been observed following low-load leg-press training with BFR [14], walking with BFR [10], and whole-body vibration combined with leg BFR [13]. Chronic interventions also indicate additive benefits: eight weeks of dual-task training with BFR enhanced Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores in elderly women more than training without BFR [12]. Neural correlates include reorganization of local EEG activity [13] and enhanced EEG–EEG network integration [15], suggesting more efficient neural resource allocation. However, not all findings are consistent. A critical gap is that nearly all prior cognitive studies have examined lower-limb BFR protocols. It remains unclear whether the cognitive effects of upper-limb BFR and associated neural adaptations generalize across limbs. This question is clinically important, as leg resistance exercise may not be feasible for individuals with mobility impairments, whereas arm ergometry is broadly accessible. Furthermore, BFR and/or hypoxia may evoke greater hemodynamic responses in the arms than in the legs, due to higher vascular resistance [16]. Establishing the efficacy of arm BFR is therefore essential for extending its translational applications.

Another unresolved issue is whether BFR modifies cortical–postural coupling during dual-task performance. If BFR enhances executive function, it may optimize allocation of cognitive resources to stabilize posture under challenging conditions (such as dual-task). The frontal cortex is critical for dual-task performance, supporting executive functions such as attentional allocation and interference resolution [17, 18]. Under dual-task postural conditions, increased reliance on frontal regions reflects a shift toward cognitively mediated control. This shift is especially evident in older adults, who exhibit greater prefrontal/frontal recruitment as a compensatory response to reduced automaticity of postural control [19–21] and reduced corticospinal inhibition [22]. Recent evidence also shows that BFR reorganizes cortical activity and cortico-kinematic coupling [13, 15], suggesting that cortical networks adapt to postural demands. Based on this theoretical and empirical background, a prefrontal/frontal region of interest was predefined to capture cortical dynamics associated with executive control during dual-task postural regulation, rather than being determined by data-driven selection.

Accordingly, this study investigated whether short-term arm ergometry with BFR improves posture–cognition dual-task performance in older adults. To capture complementary aspects of postural control, this study examined multiple domains spanning behavioral, dynamical, and neural levels. Center-of-pressure (COP) measures quantified the overall magnitude of postural sway. Stabilogram diffusion analysis (SDA) characterized the dynamical regulation of postural control, while phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) between EEG (Fp1, Fp2, F7, and F8) and COP signals captured frontal executive control over posture dual-task at the neural oscillatory level. These measures provide a multi-level perspective on how prior upper-limb BFR training may influence postural control under dual-task conditions. We hypothesized that upper-limb BFR training would improve postural stability by reducing sway magnitude and variability. The study examined both behavioral outcomes and underlying neural mechanisms, with particular focus on shifts between feedforward and feedback postural control strategies as well as cross-frequency modulation of cortico–postural coupling.

Methods

Participants

This study included 26 older adults (12 males, 14 females; mean age: 69.1 ± 3.0 years) with regular exercise habits. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no known cognitive impairments, fall history, or neurological/musculoskeletal disorders requiring medication. A subset (n = 14) with mild hypertension took antihypertensive medication within one hour prior to testing. The participants’ resting blood pressure for each laboratory visit was measured to ensure systolic and diastolic pressures were below 140/90 mmHg. The study was approved by the University Hospital’s institutional review board (B-ER-110-530), and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental procedures

The present study adopted a balanced-order crossover design. A total of 26 participants were assigned into two counterbalanced conditions and received both interventions within a one-month interval: (1) upper-limb blood flow restriction (BFR) combined with arm-ergometry resistance exercise (BFR condition), and (2) arm-ergometry resistance exercise without occlusion (NBFR condition) (Fig. 1). Participants with odd-numbered identifiers first underwent the BFR condition followed by the control condition one month later, whereas even-numbered participants received the interventions in the reverse order. This design effectively minimized learning effects and order-related bias, thereby enhancing the internal validity of the study. Participants were not informed of the specific study hypotheses.

Each experimental session included three phases: a pre-test, arm-ergometry resistance exercise in either the BFR or NBFR condition, and a post-test. All participants performed arm-ergometry in both conditions. In the BFR condition, adjustable pneumatic cuffs (SAGA Fitness International, Queensland, Australia) were placed bilaterally at the proximal upper arms, aligned with the biceps brachii and deltoid junction, to apply blood flow restriction. Cuff pressure was gradually increased over one minute to 80% of each participant’s resting systolic blood pressure, following standardized procedures [23]. Exercise intensity adhered to Taiwanese guidelines [24] with standardized exercise dosage by gender. The 21-minute training included three 5-minute bouts of steady-load arm cycling (men: 12.7 km/h, 15 W; women: 15.2 km/h, 10 W), with 1-minute warm-up and cool-down phases (men: 12.7 km/h, 10 W; women: 15.2 km/h, 8 W) and 2-minute rest intervals between bouts to minimize fatigue. Participants could stop the session if discomfort occurred. The NBFR condition followed the same protocol, but cuff pressure was set to 10 mmHg.

Pre- and post-test assessments involved a posture-cognition dual-task test. Post-testing was initiated approximately 5 min after cuff removal. Participants stood barefoot on a foam-covered force plate, feet shoulder-width apart, arms relaxed, while performing a light-pod tapping reaction task. Center of pressure (COP) trajectories were recorded during the dual-task. The reaction task used a BlazePod sensor-light system (Play Coyotta Ltd., Aviv, Israel) with three sensor lights arranged in an equilateral triangle at eye level (Fig. 1, lower right plot), adjusted to each participant’s maximum reachable height. Lights displayed five colors (purple, red, yellow, blue, green), with one color (excluding blue or yellow) as the target and blue/yellow as distractors. Participants were instructed to ignore distractors and quickly strike the target light with their dominant hand, positioned at chest level, to minimize off-center errors. Each stimulus lasted 1.5 s within a 2-second presentation; failure to respond within 1.5 s was a missed response. Participants completed two practice trials during the pre-test for familiarization. The formal pre- and post-tests consisted of three 60-second trials.

Instrumentation and measurements

A 40-channel NuAmps amplifier (NeuroScan Inc., El Paso, USA) with Ag-AgCl scalp electrodes recorded scalp voltage variations during the dual-task. Two electrodes on the earlobes enabled offline re-referencing. The scalp was cleaned with alcohol, and conductive gel was applied to reduce impedance. EEG signals were collected from cortical regions (Fp1/2, Fz, F3/4, F7/8, FT7/8, FCz, FC3/4, Cz, C3/4, CPz, CP3/4, Pz, P3/4, T3/4, T5/6, TP7/8, Oz, O1/2) per the international 10–20 system. Horizontal EOG was recorded at the outer canthi of both eyes, and vertical EOG was collected offline above and below the right eye to account for eye movements and blinks. Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ, referenced to linked mastoids. EEG data were acquired with a 0.1–100 Hz band-pass filter at a 1 kHz sampling rate. Simultaneously, a Kistler force plate (Type 9260 A, Switzerland) measured postural sway trajectories during bilateral stance in the pre-test and post-test conditions. Force plate signals were processed via an amplifier (DAQ for BioWare, Type 5695B, Switzerland) and recorded at 1 kHz using BioWare software (Type 2812 A, Switzerland). Pre-test and post-test dual-task outcome measures included scalp EEG, COP trajectory, reaction time (milliseconds), and light-pod tapping accuracy rates.

Data analysis

Analysis of center of pressure (COP)

Two traditional COP metrics, 95% confidence ellipse sway area and mean COP velocity (m_COPv), were analyzed to assess postural stability in pre-test and post-test for both BFR and NBFR conditions. The 95% confidence ellipse sway area represents the area within which 95% of the COP sway data points are expected to lie. Stabilogram diffusion analysis (SDA) of COP

COP trajectory movements were filtered using a 4th-order low-pass Butterworth filter (4 Hz cutoff). Stabilogram diffusion analysis (SDA) modeled postural sway trajectory to random and deterministic components, by describing the power-law relationship between the mean-squared postural variance (< dr²>Δt) and time interval (Δt), expressed as 〈dr²〉Δt = 〈[x(t + Δt) - x(t)]²〉, where 〈•〉 denotes the mean of sway trajectory after removal of its linear trend [25, 26]. Twelve-second segments were used to compute < dr²>Δt for increasing Δt values. Diffusion plots (linear–linear or log–log) plotted < dr²>Δt against Δt (Fig. 2), modeled by two linear regression lines intersecting at a critical point. The critical point represents a paradigm shift in postural control, labelled as short-term and long-term processes. Critical time (CT) and critical displacement (CD) represent the time scale and variation at this point, respectively, reflecting a strategic shift in postural control [27]. Higher CD indicates a shift from open-loop to closed-loop control during larger sways, while higher CT suggests a longer lag for closed-loop engagement [25, 26]. Slopes of the regression lines in the linear–linear plot yield short-term (Ds) and long-term (Dl) diffusion coefficients, parameterizing stochastic control. Short-term (Hs) and long-term (Hl) scaling exponents, derived from log–log plot linear fits, indicate open-loop control (Hs > 0.5, persistent) or closed-loop control (Hl < 0.5, anti-persistent).

Phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) of COP velocity phase and sub-band EEG

EEG data were processed with a zero-phase FIR band-pass filter (1–60 Hz, 60 dB/octave). Blinks were identified using a bipolar EOG channel, and artifacts were removed with NeuroScan 4.3 software (NeuroScan Inc., El Paso, TX, USA) and independent component analysis [28]. The first and last 2 s of EEG and COP data were excluded. Cleaned EEG signals were filtered again using a zero-phase FIR band-pass filter (60 dB/octave) to isolate theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (13–35 Hz) bands, excluding low-frequency movement artifacts (< 4 Hz) and muscle activity (> 35 Hz).

Phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) was used to assess synchronization between the phase of COP velocity (COPv) and the amplitude of sub-band EEG (theta, alpha, beta) across all electrodes (Fig. 3) [27, 29, 30]. The modulation index (MI) quantified PAC by binning COPv phases into 18 bins (Δϕ = π/9) and averaging EEG sub-band amplitudes within each bin [31, 32]. A band-specific phase–amplitude plot was generated for each trial. The MI, based on Kullback–Leibler distance and Shannon entropy, measured the divergence of the phase–amplitude plot, leveraging PAC’s robustness to signal-to-noise ratio and data length variations (Fig. 3). Shuffled coupling values were determined by calculating the MI values between the permuted time series of the phase components of the COPv and the amplitude time series of sub-band EEG in each EEG channel. This shuffling process was repeated 300 times in this study. where MI denotes PAC coupling value, µ denotes the mean and σ denotes the standard deviation (SD) (Fig. 3). Phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) between the COP velocity (COPv) signal and EEG activity in the theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (13–35 Hz) bands was quantified using the normalized modulation index (ZMI). EEG signals were band-pass filtered using zero-phase finite impulse response filters to avoid phase distortion, and the analytic signal was obtained via the Hilbert transform to extract instantaneous phase and amplitude. To reduce potential confounds from volume conduction and broadband signal fluctuations, PAC was computed within channels rather than across channels. Analyses were conducted at hypothesis-driven region-of-interest (ROI) level. A prefrontal/frontal ROI (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F8), the executive control hub, was defined a priori based on its relevance to frontal executive and attentional processes involved in dual-task postural control. PAC strength was averaged across the predefined frontal region of interest to capture executive control-related cortical dynamics. Identical preprocessing and PAC estimation procedures were applied across spectral bands and all experimental conditions to ensure comparability and minimize methodological bias. Changes in PAC (ΔZMI = post–pre) were compared between the BFR and NBFR conditions using paired t-tests. Electrode-level PAC maps were generated to illustrate the spatial distribution of effects and were not used for statistical inference. All signal processing for SDA and PAC variables was performed using MATLAB R2019a (MathWorks, USA).

Statistical analysis

For the posture-cognition dual-task, differences in COP sway area, reaction time (RT), light-pod tapping accuracy, and SDA variables (CT, CD, Ds, Dl, Hs, Hl) between pre- and post-tests were normalized. Differences were calculated as post-test – pre-test, reflecting changes due to arm training. Multivariate Hotelling’s T² tests compared training effects between BFR and NBFR conditions by analyzing the training-related differences (post-test – pre-test) in COP characteristics (m_COPv, COP_Area), light-pod tapping performance (RT, accuracy), and SDA variables (CT, CD, Ds, Dl, Hs, Hl). Post hoc analyses were conducted using paired t-tests with significance determined using Holm’s step-down test to ascertain the level of significant difference. For all post-hoc hypotheses, Holm’s step-down procedure was applied, with ordered p-values compared against adjusted significance thresholds (α/(m − i + 1)). Holm’s correction was used to control the Type I error rate, as it is less conservative than the Bonferroni correction and helps avoid over-correction. Effect sizes were reported for the omnibus multivariate analyses to complement significance testing. A paired t-test compared PAC values (ZMI) in theta, alpha, and beta bands for each EEG channel between BFR and NBFR conditions to identify regions of interest (ROIs) affected by arm training. Subsequently, paired t-tests contrasted average ZMI in these ROIs for each EEG sub-band between the BFR and NBFR conditions.

Results

BFR‑related changes in task performance of the posture-cognition dual‑task

Table 1 presents the results of postural sway and tapping task performance following training with and without BFR. Hotelling’s T² test revealed a significant multivariate effect of BFR on postural sway variables (Wilks’ Λ = 0.679, p = 0.010, η² = 0.32) (Table 1A). Post-hoc analyses showed that sway area (p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.66) and COP velocity (p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = 0.65) were significantly reduced in the BFR condition compared with NBFR. In contrast, no significant multivariate effect was observed for the light-pod tapping subtask (Wilks’ Λ = 0.995, p = 0.940, η² = 0.01) (Table 1B), indicating that reaction time and accuracy did not differ between conditions. Together, these results indicate that adding BFR to arm ergometry training selectively improved postural control without additional benefits in the cognitive-motor subtask.

BFR‑related variations in postural sway dynamics

Table 2 presents Hotelling’s T² statistics comparing normalized changes in SDA variables between pre- and post-tests for the BFR and NBFR conditions. Absolute difference in SDA variables differed significantly between conditions (Wilks’ Ʌ = 0.516, p = 0.025, ƞ2 = 0.48). BFR‑related variations in EEG-COP phase-amplitude coupling

Figure 4 (left panel) shows the topographical distribution of pooled phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) between COPv phase and EEG theta-band amplitude (4–7 Hz) during pre- and post-tests in the BFR and NBFR conditions. Between-condition comparisons of PAC changes (ΔZMI = post–pre) were conducted at the predefined ROI (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F8) (right panel). In the theta band, a significant difference between conditions was observed, with greater modulation under the BFR condition compared with NBFR (t25 = − 2.560, p = 0.016, Cohen’s d = 0.506). Figure 5 (left panel) shows pooled PAC between COPv phase and EEG alpha-band amplitude (8–12 Hz). Between-condition comparisons of PAC changes (ΔZMI = post–pre) were conducted at the predefined ROI (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F8) (right panel). In contrast, there was no significant between-condition differences in the alpha-band ΔZMI (t25 = − 1.627, p = 0.116, Cohen’s d = 0.319). Figure 6 (left panel) shows pooled PAC between COPv phase and EEG beta-band amplitude (13–35 Hz). There was also no significant between-condition differences in the beta-band ΔZMI (t25 = 0.125, p = 0.901, Cohen’s d = 0.025). These findings indicate that BFR-related modulation of PAC was frequency-specific, emerging in the theta band but not in the alpha or beta bands.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that adding BFR to arm ergometry training is associated with greater improvement in the postural component of dual-task performance in older adults compared with conventional ergometry. These benefits were reflected in significant reductions in COP area and velocity, along with decreases in critical displacement (CD) and short-term diffusion coefficient (Ds), indicating improved postural stability. Postural improvements were accompanied by frequency-specific modulation of cortico–postural phase–amplitude coupling (PAC), with a significant between-condition difference observed in the theta band over frontal regions, whereas no significant differences were found in the alpha or beta bands. These findings suggest that upper-limb BFR training may enhance postural performance and is associated with selective neural modulation relevant to dual-task balance control in aging populations.

Improvements in posture–cognition dual-task performance following arm BFR Training

Older adults typically show greater postural sway during dual-tasking, particularly on unstable surfaces, due to diminished executive resources and reduced capacity to manage interference. In the present study, combined arm ergometry and BFR training significantly improved postural stability, as indicated by smaller COP area and lower mean COP velocity. Although whether BFR resistance training directly contributes to cognitive function remains debatable [10, 13], the present results align with our earlier treadmill study using leg BFR, which also showed improved stability under dual-task conditions [15]. Collectively, these findings indicate that BFR, whether applied to the upper or lower limbs, can mitigate dual-task costs and support balance control in aging populations. SDA provided potential mechanistic insights into how BFR may influence postural control. Compared with controls, the BFR condition showed significant reductions in critical displacement (CD) and short-term diffusion coefficient (Ds), indicating changes in the spatial and stochastic characteristics of postural control. The reduction in CD suggests a tighter control boundary for sway excursions, while decreased Ds reflects reduced short-term variability in postural dynamics. These findings were supported by the multivariate analysis, as the Hotelling’s T² test revealed a significant overall effect with a large effect size, indicating that SDA variables collectively captured meaningful changes following the intervention. Because elevated CD and Ds values are typically observed in fall-prone populations [34–36], training-related reduction in CD and Ds in the BFR condition may reflect a shift toward safer and more stable postural control strategies. Interestingly, while postural performance improved, the light-pod tapping subtask did not show additional benefits with BFR compared to non-BFR training. This differs from our previous treadmill-based BFR intervention [15], which improved both postural and cognitive components. One possible explanation is that the higher metabolic demand associated with upper-limb BFR induced greater local fatigue [23], which may have transiently offset potential cognitive benefits. This pattern suggests a potential trade-off, whereby upper-limb BFR may enhance postural control while simultaneously imposing transient fatigue-related constraints on upper-limb task performance. Future studies should further optimize exercise intensity and recovery strategies to balance cognitive and motor outcomes.

Plastic changes in phase-amplitude coupling of posture sway velocity and scalp EEG

A central contribution of this study is the demonstration of plastic changes in cortico–postural phase–amplitude coupling (PAC). PAC between postural sway phase (< 4 Hz) and EEG amplitudes (4–35 Hz) reflects the hierarchical organization of cortico–postural control and offers advantages over traditional coherence measures, which are often confounded by movement artifacts in low-frequency ranges (< 4 Hz) [37]. With the application of surrogate-based normalization [33], PAC serves as a more robust marker of cross-frequency neural modulation during balance control [27, 29, 30]. Notably, PAC-related changes were observed following the intervention. Upper-limb BFR training was associated with a more negative change in theta-band PAC over frontal regions compared with NBFR training (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that older adults may adopt modified postural control strategies following BFR, potentially reflecting altered cognitive engagement of cortical resources during balance regulation.

Low-load resistance training with BFR in the lower extremity enhances cortical activation in the prefrontal [38], frontal [13], and sensorimotor areas [9], indicating improved neurovascular coupling and cortical plasticity. This supports enhanced executive function [10, 39], working memory [13], and cognitive flexibility [40]. Our prior study showed that a single session of leg BFR during treadmill walking improved posture-cognition dual-task performance in older adults, who face age-related executive function decline [15]. BFR treadmill training enhanced dual-task performance and was linked to more star-like EEG–EEG networks in theta and alpha bands, reflecting greater neural economy.

This study extends prior EEG–EEG network research by demonstrating reorganization of cortico–postural coupling, supporting a strategic shift in postural regulation following BFR arm-ergometry training. The observed theta-band PAC modulation over frontal regions suggests altered engagement of cortical resources during postural control. The prefrontal cortex (PFC; Fp1, Fp2) is a key node within the fronto-subthalamic-motor cortical inhibitory network involved in response-conflict processing [41, 42]. Increased prefrontal theta activity during dual-task interference has been interpreted as a neural signature of limited information-processing capacity, particularly in older adults. The theta oscillations in the prefrontal/frontal regions are critically involved in attentional allocation, executive control [41, 42] and conflict solving [43, 44], which are essential for managing cognitive–motor interference during balance tasks. In this study, the reduction in theta-band PAC following training indicates a synchronization change in the coupling relationship between postural sway phase and cortical oscillatory amplitude (Fig. 4). Rather than reflecting precise phase-locking to a specific phase [33], this modulation may indicate a reorganization of temporal interactions between cortical oscillations and postural dynamics, which suggests a more flexible and potentially more efficient organization of cortico–postural interactions following arm-ergometry BFR training. From a systems perspective, these findings may reflect a paradigm shift from a more constrained to a more flexible control regime, enabling more adaptive allocation of neural resources during dual-task postural regulation. This finding is also consistent with a transition toward more automated control, in which cortico–postural interactions become more efficiently organized after BFR training [45, 46].

However, training-related changes in frontal alpha- and beta-band PAC did not differ between the BFR and NBFR groups (Figs. 5 and 6). In contrast to theta-band findings, the absence of group differences in alpha-band PAC may be interpreted in light of the inhibition–timing hypothesis [47], which posits that alpha oscillations play a central role in functional inhibition and temporal gating of neural processing. Therefore, alpha-band PAC may reflect a relatively stable inhibitory scaffold that regulates the timing of cortico-postural interactions, rather than supporting adaptive control processes. Similarly, consistent with the proposed role of beta oscillations in maintaining the status quo of motor control [48], beta-band PAC may reflect preservation of the ongoing motor set and stability of internal models supporting postural regulation. Accordingly, the lack of modulation in alpha- and beta-band PAC suggests that BFR training did not substantially alter inhibitory timing mechanisms or the underlying control architecture for postural regulation, compared with traditional arm-ergometry training. The band-specific modulation of PAC therefore provides a potential mechanistic account of how upper-limb BFR training influences postural regulation, with changes primarily observed in adaptive executive control while fundamental inhibitory and stability-related layers remain preserved.

Methodological concerns.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study involved a relatively small sample size and a single training session; therefore, larger and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the durability and clinical relevance of these findings. Second, although Holm’s step-down procedure was applied to control the family-wise error rate, some post-hoc comparisons did not remain statistically significant after correction and should be interpreted with caution. Although the primary outcome remained robust after correction, future studies with larger samples are warranted to provide more stable estimates of these effects. Third, upper-limb fatigue during BFR ergometry may have influenced light-tapping task performance, potentially masking subtle improvements due to transient fatigue effects. Finally, only healthy older adults were studied; extending this work to fall-prone or clinical populations (e.g., mild cognitive impairment or Parkinson’s disease) will be essential to evaluate the translational potential of this intervention.

Conclusion

In summary, similar to treadmill walking with leg BFR, combining arm ergometry with BFR was associated with improved postural control in older adults during dual-task conditions. BFR was accompanied by reductions in sway magnitude and short-term variability, reflecting changes in the spatial and dynamical characteristics of postural regulation. These changes were accompanied by frequency-specific modulation of cortico–postural coupling, evident in the theta band at prefrontal/frontal regions. These findings indicate that upper-limb BFR training may influence both behavioral and neural aspects of balance regulation, highlighting its potential as an accessible motor–cognition intervention for older adults with temporary mobility limitations who are unable to participate in lower-limb exercise programs.
