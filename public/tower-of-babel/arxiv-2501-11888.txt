Probing negative differential resistance in silicon with a P-I-N diode-integrated T center ensemble Preprint: APS/123-QED
Aaron M. Day, Chaoshen Zhang, Chang Jin, Hanbin Song, Madison Sutula, Alp Sipahigil, Mihir K. Bhaskar, Evelyn L. Hu

Abstract
The T center in silicon has recently emerged as a promising candidate for scalable quantum technologies, due to its telecommunications band optical transition and microwave addressable ground state spin. The immense promise of the T center is driven by its silicon host material; silicon is by far the most mature, manufacturable semiconductor material for integrated photonic and electronic devices. Here, we present the first study of T-centers in an electrical device. We study an ensemble of T centers coupled to a buried lateral P-I-N diode in silicon, observing the T-center’s optical response to static and dynamic electric fields. We utilize the defect’s optical response as a probe of device nonlinearity, observing a phase transition of the carrier density into a stable oscillatory regime characteristic of negative differential resistance. These findings provide fundamental insight into the physics of the T-center for improved quantum device performance and open a promising new direction for defect-based local quantum sensing in semiconductor devices.

A positive bias is then applied to the diode to pass electron and hole current across the ensemble (EL). With the laser turned off, clear T center emission is apparent in both the spatial confocal scan and the spectra (Fig.
2

Weman et al. 1987

While these excited state lifetimes are on the order of PL reported in literature, the difference could be attributed to non-radiative pathways induced from insufficient lattice healing in our flash anneal. We also observe the presence of a slow decay component in the EL which could potentially be explained by the presence of electrically-active intrinsic background defects in the silicon, overlapping spatially and spectrally with the T center.

Finally, the PL-injected optical response of the T centers is characterized under application of a reverse-bias, from 0V to -200V (with diode breakdown occurring at -180V under above-bandgap illumination), in Fig.
2
d. Below diode breakdown, up to a field strength of approximately 400 kV/m, no meaningful changes in emitter ensemble brightness, center wavelength, or linewidth are found. Above diode breakdown, leakage current induces milliwatts of power dissipation across the junction thereby heating the ensemble and quenching the optical emission. We note that these results of T center weak tuning response at high field in an integrated PIN-diode are consistent with the findings at lower field in bulk silicon
Clear et al. 2024
. This is in significant contrast to the G center optical response characterized in similar diode devices, where field-mediated optical charge state modification and stark tuning up to 120 GHz are readily observed

While the observed T center optical stability at large reverse-biased local electric field is a promising finding which offers insight on the defect charge state, the weak frequency response suggests other tuning modalities–ie strain–must be investigated for tuning implementation in quantum networks
Simmons 2024

These findings can be readily implemented for electrically-gated telecom single photon sources in silicon.

I.4
Discussion

I.4.1
Origin of EL

Electroluminescence in a bulk-semiconductor is enabled by a number of mechanisms such as direct impurity ionization under electric field, impact-ionization of impurities through accelerated injected carriers, and direct recombination of injected electrons and holes
Piper and Williams 1955
. Depending on the energy level and excitation pathways, defects in the bandgap can capture these free carriers for promotion to and de-excitation from an excited state. For excitonic defects–the class to which the T center belongs–electroluminescence is less well studied. Nonetheless, the aforementioned mechanisms can apply in our system, whereby free-excitons are formed via (1) injection of electron- and hole-current from the respective N- and P-doped regions
Palm et al. 1996
, and/or (2) impact-ionization of local impurities under field-mediated acceleration of carriers
Eitan et al. 1982
;
Maes et al. 1990
. The formed free-excitons can be then captured and recombine at the defect site driving EL, similar to the proposed method of above-bandgap T center excitation
Xiong et al. 2024a
;
Xiong et al. 2024b
.

I.4.2
Origin of Oscillations

The creation of AC-microwave oscillations under application of a DC-bias is commonly observed in the case of a Gunn Diode. Notably, however, this effect is band structure-dependent and thus only supported in materials like GaAs where an increasing electric field alters the electron population from a low effective mass
\Gamma
valley to a higher effective mass X valley. The two corresponding different average velocities gives rise to clusters of charge that propagate from cathode to anode, and the cycle repeats as new domains form.

However, current density in a semiconductor more generally depends on number density, as well as velocity of both electrons and holes, which can be expressed as:

\vec{J}=q(\mu_{n}{\bf n}+\mu_{p}{\bf p})\vec{E}

(1)

where q is the electron charge,
n, p
are the densities of the free electrons and holes,

\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{p}
are the corresponding mobilities,
and
\vec{E}
is the applied electric field.
n, p
,
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{p}
are all temperature-dependent quantities. The average electron or hole velocity is given by

\vec{v_{n}}=\mu_{n}\vec{E};\vec{v_{p}}=\mu_{p}\vec{E}

(2)

Thus, mechanisms that can create non-monotonic and oscillatory changes in the number density of electrons and holes, as well as in their velocities, might also be expected to produce the signatures of
Negative Differential Resistance
(NDR)–a defining feature of such oscillatory electrical systems. In this way, materials which do not possess the Gunn-type band structure can still permit NDR under certain conditions. Impurity states within the bandgap are well understood to influence the lifetimes of free electrons and holes and hence influence the concentration and dynamics of those free carriers. Indeed, a number of early studies on electrical nonlinearities in semiconductors identified mid-gap defect trap states from Au-doping as the origin of NDR in Si
p^{+}-i-n^{+}
diodes at room temperature, and similar phenomena has been observed in Germanium diode at liquid helium temperature
Lampert and Rose 1961
;
Weber and Ford 1970
;
Deuling 1970
;
Kassing and Dudeck 1975
;
Dudeck and Kassing 1977
;
Jäger et al. 1986
;
Baumann et al. 1986
;
Weman et al. 1989
;
Symanczyk et al. 1991
;
Mayer et al. 1987
.

We thus believe the framework of recombination-induced phase transitions can provide a starting point for the further modeling of the behavior of our T center-integrated lateral p-i-n diodes. In fact, impurity-mediated NDR was described as
recombination-induced nonequilibrium phase transitions
nearly five decades ago
Landsberg and Pimpale 1976
, and the field has since developed a rich understanding of impurity-related chaotic electrical phenomena
Schöll 2001
;
Schöll 2012
. The phase transition model identifies the controlling parameter as
C_{i}-l
, where
C_{i}
is the impact ionization coefficient (which can be tuned via temperature and electric-field), and
l
is the inverse of a characteristic lifetime, e.g. a trapping lifetime. The transition occurs depending on whether
C_{i}-l
is less than, or greater than zero. Thus, this model identifies
C_{i}-l
as the control variable, with the electron concentration as the
order parameter
. We postulate that the high density implantation of carbon ions across the (ultrapure) PIN-junction, or T center ensemble itself, could form the necessary charge traps that mediate such g-r process. The more complex details and the applicability of this model must be further pursued, but there is an important accommodation for a field-induced increase in carrier concentration (or generation,
g
) through mechanisms like impact ionization, and a field-induced decrease in carrier concentration through recombination mechanisms (
r
) that produce charge-state alteration of defect states and changes in trapping lifetimes. In addition to probing fundamental semiconductor physics, the observed non-monotonic field modulation of currents offers new opportunities for the studies of the lifetimes, charge states, and capture cross sections of defect levels, such as those pertaining to the T center in silicon.

Finally, the proposed model for T center EL, whereby capture of free-excitons generated via (1) electron-hole current injection, and/or (2) impact-ionization, purports with the above described phase transition. In our experiments, in the region
without
oscillations, EL may be observed primarily via mechanism (1), whereas for a certain combination of temperature and field-strength, mechanism (2) may dominate and thus the onset of field-mediated impact ionization may satisfy the necessary conditions for the phase transition and thereby observation of oscillations.

II
Conclusion and Outlook

Electric-field instabilities in biased-semiconductors wrought by oscillatory charge generation and recombination (g-r) effects have been studied for many decades
Schöll 2001
;
Schöll 2012
–where the first generalized framework as a non-equilibrium phase transition emerged in the late 1970s
Landsberg and Pimpale 1976
. Great effort has since been dedicated to the study of impurity-related chaotic electrical phenomena in semiconductor devices, reliant on conventional experimental methods of IV-measurements, electron-beam-induced-voltage scanning-electron microscopy
Baumann et al. 1986
, and potential-probe surface conductivity
Symanczyk et al. 1991
. In contrast, we establish the ability of excitonic color centers in silicon to serve as atom-scale probes to the local nonlinear electrical environment–directly mapping the transient electrical nonlinearities onto telecom optical photons thereby enabling optical observation of the semiconductor phase transition.

We have demonstrated the direct probing of nonlinear electrical dynamics inside a silicon PIN-diode by utilizing an embedded T center ensemble. We use the T center’s emission as a probe of the dynamics within our diode. In doing so we first establish and characterize the typical steady-state T center electrical response–finding a weak optical tuning under large reverse-biased electric fields and the existence of T center electroluminescence. We note that the observed weak stark-tuning rate complicates proposals to tune and narrow T centers using electric fields
Simmons 2024
.

We then implement these findings, measuring transient T center carrier capture under electroluminescence and DC-biased photoluminescence. We study the regimes of the behavior, mapping a nonequilibrium phase transition in the electrical nonlinearities which manifest in the emitter optical emission. We find strong agreement between the direct measure of transient electrical current and EL oscillations, whereas in the DC-biased pulsed PL regime we observe phenomena which cannot be detected in the current–suggesting a defect-enhanced sensitivity to a weaker effect in the semiconductor. The extent to which the T centers merely sense the impurity-induced generation-recombination (g-r) oscillatory nonlinearities, as opposed to
cause
them, remains an exciting and open question–as prior investigations of silicon PIN-diode oscillations studied high-purity silicon altered by gold-compensation.

This opens exciting further directions for exploration. Varying the T center ensemble density and material properties (resistivity, purity), could inform whether the trap states introduced by the T centers directly cause the observed electrical nonlinearities, and enable greater control and engineering of their frequency and amplitude. Additionally, further studies are needed to understand the distinction between the two regimes we study–DC-biased PL vs switched-EL–as a unique second-order phase transition is predicted for low-temperature stimulated-exciton creation which may more fully capture our DC-biased PL protocol
Landsberg and Pimpale 1976
.

Our work demonstrates not only the first exploration of electrical-device integration of the silicon T center–an emergent candidate for scalable quantum networking technology–but also utilizes the emitter-device interaction to probe fundamental semiconductor device physics and offer new insight into a decades-old question on chaotic electrical phenomena. Our direct probing of g-r induced phase transitions using a T center ensemble opens new possibilities in the quantum sensing of electric fields, in particular at the intersections with nonequilibrium thermodynamics and chaos theory. Further, the demonstrated ability to simply use a low-voltage DC-source to generate electrical microwave oscillations which transduce directly to amplitude-modulated telecommunications photons opens new technological opportunities in optical communications. Continued exploration and optimization of impurity composition could feasibly enable GHz-frequency modulation of optical photons solely from a DC-bias input, enabling an optical voltage-controlled-oscillator for optical interconnects and low-power radar.

III.1

T centers are formed utilizing a series of carbon-ion implantation, hyrdrogen-ion implantation, and rapid thermal anneals–the exact details of which are given below. In our investigation of emitter synthesis, we found the T center formation to be sensitive to processing conditions, and thus great care is taken in particular in the thermal annealing steps. Additionally, other work has utilized a water-boiling step for T center formation, whereas we do not need this step. Due to the T center processing sensitivity, the steps for diode fabrication (also given in detail below) are assessed and interspersed accordingly to co-form the device and emitter without degradation of each. III.2
Experimental Setup

Experiments are performed on a custom home-built free-space confocal microscope with a Mitutoyo 100x 0.5 NA Near-IR excitation objective, O-band collection optics, and above-bandgap excitation paths (schematic in SI
SI
). Photons are collected and fiber routed to either a spectrometer (Acton Spectra Pro 2750 spectrograph with a Princeton Instruments OMA:V indium-gallium-arsenide nitrogen-cooled photodiode array detector) or O-band optimized superconducting nanowire single photon detectors (SNSPDs, PhotonSpot) housed on the 1 K stage of a dilution refrigerator.

The sample is mounted-on and wirebonded-to an electrically-wired cold-finger with a 16-pin removable electrical insert (see SI
SI
) in a continuous-flow Janis ST-500 cryostat with integrated electrical feed-throughs for temperature sensing/control, and diode electrical biasing. Liquid helium is flowed from a dewar, circulated in the cryostat to reach a base temperature of roughly 6 Kelvin, and recovered in a university network. Temperature sweeps are performed via careful balance of helium flow rate and heating from a LakeShore 321 AutoTuning temperature controller (details in SI
SI
). Steady state electrical-biasing of the diode is performed with a ±210 V Keithley 2400 sourcemeter. Transient electrical switching of the diode is performed with a Rigol IX1 DG 1022 arbitrary waveform generator.

IV.1
Extended Discussion

IV.1.1
Carrier Dynamics

The distinction between EL and PL intensity, and similarly the inefficiency of EL, can be understood by considering the carrier dynamics. At low temperatures, the relative concentration of electrons and holes from the ion-doped phosphorus donors and boron acceptors differs by an order of magnitude due to the thermal ionization of the trap levels, where at 10 K there is a very sparse current density of approximately
1.6\times 10^{2}
electrons/cm
2
/sec, assuming an electric field of
1.2\times 10^{2}
V/cm
SI
. Additionally, the electron and hole mobility should differ at 6K
Ottaviani et al. 1975
;
Canali et al. 1975
. Given these differences in electron and hole density, it is necessary to consider how these dynamics could explain our observed electrically-injected T center emission. It is suggested that T center PL is mediated by capture of free excitons at the defect site
Bergeron et al. 2020
. The electrically-mediated formation of excitons necessary for T center emission could thus be formed via impact ionization
Eitan et al. 1982
;
Maes et al. 1990
, whereby the increased relative electron density could collide with local impurities to ionize electron-hole pairs resulting in T center capture and excitation. Indeed, carbon is uniformly densely implanted in our samples and could serve as such a local impurity
Sugiyama et al. 2012
. Furthermore, the presence of a slow-decay component in the EL lifetime, yet absent from the PL lifetime, could be due to this distinct excitation mechanism of impact ionization, whereby an energetic charge carrier excites a charge neutral defect thus forming a bound exciton with relatively long lifetime–carbon related neutral defect complexes have indeed been investigated as hole traps in silicon
Kamiura et al. 1995
;
Sugiyama et al. 2012
. Although our finding does not exclude the possibility of other emission mechanism, the correlation between the onset of EL and impact-ionization-mediated phase transition provides a solid evidence that the T center emit via a free exciton (FE) capture process.

IV.1.2
Carrier Oscillations

Deep level defects
, with energy levels far removed from the conduction or valence band edges can serve as traps having substantial trapping lifetimes. Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) recombination is a well characterized means of non-radiative recombination
Shockley and Read Jr 1952
;
Hall 1952
. It is not surprising, then that a number of early studies on
p^{+}-i-n^{+}
diodes in Au-doped Si identified NDR in the current-voltage characteristics
Lampert and Rose 1961
;
Weber and Ford 1970
;
Deuling 1970
;
Kassing and Dudeck 1975
;
Dudeck and Kassing 1977
;
Jäger et al. 1986
;
Baumann et al. 1986
;
Weman et al. 1989
;
Symanczyk et al. 1991
. These studies focused on Au-doped high purity Silicon, where the amphoteric Au deep level can serve as both acceptor, in its negatively charged state, and as donor in its positively charged state
Weber and Ford 1970
. As was noted by
Weber and Ford 1970
, non-monotonic current-voltage behavior could be explained by the fact that “the current is limited by recombination kinetics rather than mobilities” where the interplay of electron and hole recombination with the deep-level defect produced the NDR-like behavior.

There are some important additional considerations for our measurements: (1) At the low temperatures for which our data are taken (
<
10K), the hole concentration is an order of magnitude lower than the electron concentration
SI
. Given the larger electron mobility, compared to that of holes, the relative current density is even smaller
Ottaviani et al. 1975
;
Canali et al. 1975
. (2) The low rate of electron injection into the T-center region should provide high sensitivity in mapping out details of carrier capture and trap lifetimes. (3) Photo-excitation, in addition to electrical injection, dramatically changes the concentrations of electrons and holes. In addition, photo-excitation will produce equal concentrations of electrons and holes, yet the overall current density,
\vec{J}
will still consist largely of electrons. It is therefore interesting to trace the forward-biased NDR dynamics as the PL intensity varies. Indeed, we investigate this condition in the supplement and find a suppression of the oscillations at increased PL intensity, whereby the order parameter–electron concentration–is modified by optical injection
SI
.
