Comparative transcriptomic and co-expression analyses of precocious and wild-type Eimeria tenella across developmental stages
Authors: Yang Gao, Xiaojin Li, Yang Yu, Xun Suo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precocious strains of Eimeria tenella are widely used as attenuated live vaccines, yet the transcriptional basis of precocious development remains unclear. Here, we established a genetically stable precocious line (PL) with a pre-patent period 12 h shorter than that of the wild-type (WT) strain. Stage-matched RNA sequencing was performed on sporozoites and merozoites (PL at 96 h and 108 h; WT at 108 h and 120 h). Transcriptomic analysis revealed extensive and stage-dependent transcriptional divergence, with the most pronounced differences identified at the sporozoite stage. Notably, 76 invasion-related genes, including those encoding AMA1, MICs, and RONs, were more highly transcribed in PL sporozoites, which were also significantly enriched in functional programs related to protein synthesis, translation, and macromolecule metabolism. As development progressed, transcriptional profiles between PL and WT became more aligned, though differences remained in the coordinated regulation of cytoskeletal organization and microtubule-related genes during early and late merozoite development. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and hierarchical clustering further confirmed that precocious development is governed by coordinated, stage-specific gene programs rather than isolated gene-level changes. These findings define the transcriptional landscape underlying accelerated developmental timing in E. tenella and provide a molecular framework for optimizing attenuated vaccines.

Introduction

Avian coccidiosis is a major parasitic disease of poultry caused by obligate intracellular apicomplexan parasites of the genus Eimeria, which invade and replicate within intestinal epithelial cells (Blake and Tomley, 2014). Among the seven predominant Eimeria species infecting chickens, Eimeria tenella is one of the most pathogenic and best-characterized, and is widely used as a model to study Eimeria biology (Chapman and Shirley, 2003). Infection with E. tenella causes severe caecal damage, resulting in diarrhea, impaired feed efficiency, reduced weight gain, and increased mortality, thereby imposing a substantial economic burden on the poultry industry (Tomal et al., 2023) Globally, coccidiosis is estimated to cause annual losses exceeding £12.9 billion per year due to reduced productivity and the costs assoiated with prophylactic and therapeutic control (Blake et al., 2025).

Current control of avian coccidiosis relies largely on anticoccidial drugs; however, the widespread emergence of drug resistance (Chapman, 1976) and growing concerns over drug residues have highlighted the limitations of chemotherapeutic strategies. Vaccination therefore represents a more sustainable alternative, and attenuated precocious Eimeria strains—characterized by shortened prepatent periods, reduced pathogenicity, and preserved immunogenicity—are among the most promising vaccine candidates (Price et al., 2015).

The life cycle of Eimeria parasites comprises three major developmental stages: exogenous sporogony of oocysts and endogenous schizogony and gametogony. During endogenous development, precocious strains of E. tenella exhibit accelerated progression through asexual stages, with second-generation schizonts maturing more rapidly, remaining smaller in size, and producing fewer merozoites than those of parental virulent strains (McDonald and Shirley, 1987). These developmental features are associated with a shortened prepatent period and markedly reduced pathogenicity, making precocious strains attractive candidates for attenuated live vaccines (McDonald and Shirley, 2009). Vaccines derived from precocious lines have been implemented for several decades (Johnson et al., 1979), including commercially available products such as TAM™ and Neca™ (Nisseiken Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), and Paracox®-5 (MSD Animal Health, Milton Keynes, UK) (Kawahara et al., 2014) and have become an increasingly important component of coccidiosis control in the poultry industry. Despite extensive morphological characterization of parasites across developmental stages, precocity represents a developmentally regulated phenotype that has received limited transcriptional investigation, and its underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) has become a powerful and widely used approach for profiling gene expression across developmental stages and for identifying differentially expressed genes associated with distinct phenotypes in apicomplexan parasites (Walker et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). In this study, we performed stage-matched transcriptomic analyses of a precocious and a parental wild-type strain of E. tenella. By comparing precocious sporozoites with wild-type sporozoites, precocious 96 h merozoites with wild-type 108 h merozoites, and precocious 108 h merozoites with wild-type 120 h merozoites, our experimental design enabled the identification of transcriptional changes specifically associated with altered developmental timing rather than chronological progression. Together, these analyses provide new insights into the molecular basis of precocious development in E. tenella and establish a conceptual framework for the rational optimization of attenuated live vaccines.

Materials and methods

Animals

Arbor Acres (AA) broilers aged one to six weeks were obtained from Beijing Arbor Acres Poultry Breeding (Beijing, China) for oocyst propagation and precocious line development. Two-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens were obtained from Merial Animal Health Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for merozoites collection. All birds were maintained on a coccidia-free diet with unlimited access to water.

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the China Agricultural University Institutional Animal Welfare and Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection Committee, and the study protocol received specific ethical approval from this committee [approval number: AW22022202-1-1]. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Selection for the precocious line of E. tenella

The procedures for obtaining E. tenella and establishing the precocious line were adapted from a previously published method (Jeffers, 1975). In this work, we isolated oocysts from feces of chickens experimentally infected with the Houghton (H) strain of E. tenella, and for each passage we specifically collected those oocysts that were excreted early in the prepatent period before using them to infect the next group of birds. Through 27 consecutive rounds of selecting these early-shedding oocysts, a precocious line was ultimately established, with the overall selection scheme summarized in Table 1. To evaluate whether the precocious phenotype was stably maintained, the line from passage 27 was subsequently propagated in chickens for three generations without applying any selective pressure. For each generation, three chickens were infected, and the resulting progeny oocysts were harvested from feces between 105–216 h post-infection; sporulated oocysts were then used to inoculate a new set of birds for the subsequent passage. Histological assessment of endogenous development

To characterize differences in endogenous development between the WT and PL, a time-course infection experiment was conducted in which twenty-four chickens were assigned to twelve WT groups and twelve PL groups. infected birds received an oral dose of 1 × 104 oocysts of the corresponding strain. At twelve defined time points post-inoculation (24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108, 120, 132, 144, and 156 h), chickens were euthanized by CO2 inhalation following AVMA guidelines, and their caeca were excised for histological examination. Each caecum was opened longitudinally to remove luminal contents, rinsed briefly with ice-cold PBS, and a ∼1 cm tissue segment was collected. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 µm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. These preparations were analyzed microscopically to document the sequential appearance of endogenous stages and to compare the temporal developmental trajectories of WT and PL parasites.

Isolation and purification of sporozoites

Sporozoites were prepared from 1 × 107 sporulated oocysts. Oocysts were washed repeatedly with PBS to remove potassium dichromate and centrifuged until the pellet appeared white. The cleaned oocysts were resuspended in PBS and disrupted by vortexing with 1 mm glass beads, with the extent of sporocyst release monitored microscopically; vortexing was stopped when approximately 80–90% of sporocysts were liberated. The mixture containing sporocysts and oocyst debris was collected, and residual material adhering to the beads was recovered by PBS washing. The pooled suspension was subjected to 50% Percoll separation to enrich sporocysts and early-released sporozoites, while unbroken oocysts and wall fragments were removed. The debris fraction was reprocessed when necessary to improve recovery. Purified sporocysts were then incubated in excystation buffer (10% [w/v] bile, 90% [w/v] PBS, and 0.75% [w/v] trypsin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane) at 42°C for 1.5 h with intermittent mixing, and excystation efficiency was confirmed microscopically (> 80% release). Following excystation, the suspension was centrifuged and applied to 55% Percoll gradient to separate sporozoites from sporocyst walls (Dulski and Turner, 1988). The sporozoite-enriched fraction was washed with PBS and collected for downstream applications.

Extraction of merozoites

Merozoites were isolated from three 3-week-old SPF chickens orally inoculated with 2 × 105 E. tenella oocysts of either the WT or PL strain. Birds were euthanized at the appropriate developmental stages 108 h and 120 h post-infection for WT, and 96 h and 108 h post-infection for PL. The caeca were collected, opened, and rinsed to remove luminal contents. The tissues were finely minced and digested in merozoite digestion buffer consisting of 0.5% (w/v) sodium taurodeoxycholate (Sigma, St. Louis, USA) and 0.25% (w/v) trypsin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) dissolved in PBS and adjusted to a final volume of 100 mL (Zhou et al., 2010). Digestion was carried out at 42°C on a magnetic stirrer for approximately 1 h, with periodic microscopic monitoring to confirm the release of merozoites. The digested suspension was filtered through four layers of gauze, and the filtrate was collected in 50 mL tubes; residual material on the gauze was rinsed with PBS and refiltered to maximize recovery. The filtrate was centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 10 min, producing three layers: digestion buffer in the upper phase, tissue debris and erythrocytes in the middle, and a compact white pellet of merozoites at the bottom. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, transferred to a 1.5 mL tube, washed twice with PBS, and stored at −80°C until further use.

Transcriptome library preparation and sequencing

RNA was isolated from the sporozoites (SZ) and merozoites (MZ) obtained from WT and PL E. tenella strains using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following manufacturer instructions. The purity, concentration, and integrity of each RNA preparation were evaluated with a Nano 6000 Assay Kit using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 platform (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and only samples that met the required quality thresholds were advanced to library construction. For transcriptome sequencing, three independent biological replicates from each parasite stage and strain combination were used. Libraries were generated with the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), during which cDNA fragments of approximately 250–300 bp were selected and purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, MA, USA). The size distribution of the completed libraries was confirmed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer using DNA 1000 reagents. Clustering of the indexed libraries was performed on an Illumina cBot instrument with the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3, and paired-end sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with the NovaSeq 6000 reagent kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Gene quantification and differential gene expression analysis

High-quality reads were mapped to the chromosome-level E. tenella reference genome pEimTen1.1 (ToxoDB-60_EtenellaHoughton2021, https://toxodb.org/toxo/app/downloads/Current_Release/EtenellaHoughton2021/) using STAR v2.5.3a (Dobin et al., 2012). Gene-level quantification was performed with featureCounts v2.0.6 (Liao et al., 2014), which summarized uniquely aligned reads for each annotated gene. Expression values were subsequently normalized to FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) to correct for variation in transcript length and sequencing depth, thereby enabling meaningful comparisons across samples and developmental stages. The resulting FPKM matrix was used to characterize global transcriptional relationships among samples through principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.42.0) (Anders et al., 2010), which estimates expression differences between groups using a negative binomial model. Significance was determined after adjusting p-values with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p-value (Padj) < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 were classified as differentially expressed.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was conducted using the WGCNA R package (v1.71) (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008) to identify clusters of co-expressed genes and to examine their associations with external sample traits. Genes with similar expression profiles were organized into modules by constructing a weighted correlation matrix and transforming it into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). An appropriate soft-thresholding power was selected to ensure scale-free network topology. Hierarchical clustering based on TOM-derived dissimilarity was then applied to define gene modules. Module eigengenes were correlated with sample traits to highlight biologically relevant modules and potential candidate biomarkers. FPKM expression values were used as input for network construction.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.1) (Yu et al., 2012). Gene length bias was corrected during enrichment testing, and GO terms with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly overrepresented.

RT-qPCR validation

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA purity was assessed with a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, Los Angeles, CA, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 100 to 500 ng of total RNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with random hexamer primers. Quantitative PCR was carried out on a StepOne system using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). To validate transcriptomic expression patterns, primers were designed for 18 selected genes, targeting distinct exonic regions to prevent genomic DNA amplification (Supplementary Data 5). The housekeeping gene small ribosomal subunit 18S (Walker et al., 2015) served as the internal reference. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Results

Establishment of a precocious line of E. tenella

The precocious line of E. tenella was developed through serial in vivo propagation of the parent Houghton (H) strain under selection pressure for early oocyst shedding (Fig. 1A). Over 27 successive passages, the prepatent period progressively shortened from 136 h to 124 h, with notable reductions observed at passages P6, P12, and P19 (Table 1). Histological analysis of endogenous development further revealed distinct differences between the precocious strains (PL) and the wild-type strains (WT). Although no obvious differences were observed during early development, the PL strains exhibited abundant first-generation schizonts as early as 60 h post-infection, whereas only a small number of first-generation schizonts were detected in the WT strains at 72 h. Mature second-generation schizonts appeared at 96 h in the PL strains but were not observed until 108 h in the WT strains, indicating an earlier onset of schizogony in the PL strains. Notably, the second-generation schizonts in the PL strains were markedly smaller in size and contained fewer merozoites compared with those in the WT strains, suggesting a truncated schizogonic cycle and reduced asexual amplification capacity (Fig. 1B).

Comparative transcriptome dynamics in WT and PL of E. tenella

To characterize the transcriptional dynamics underlying the shortened prepatent period of the precocious line PL, RNA-seq was performed on sporozoites and merozoites of PL and WT E. tenella strains. High-quality RNA-seq datasets were obtained for all samples, with consistently high mapping rates and comparable genomic distributions across developmental stages and strains (Table 2). Global correlation analysis revealed high reproducibility among biological replicates and a clear segregation between developmental stages (Fig. 2A), with sporozoite samples clustering distinctly from merozoite samples, indicating that developmental stage is the primary driver of transcriptomic variation. Principal component analysis (PCA) further supported this observation. PC1 explained 79.57% of the total variance and effectively separated sporozoite and merozoite samples, whereas PC2 captured strain-associated differences at the sporozoite stage (Fig. 2B). Notably, PL sporozoites (PL_SZ) were clearly separated from WT sporozoites (WT_SZ) along PC2, suggesting that transcriptional reprogramming occurs earlier in PL even at the sporozoite stage. Co-expression network analysis reveals stage-associated transcriptional modules of E. tenella

To explore gene co-expression patterns across samples, a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed. An appropriate soft-thresholding power was selected to ensure approximate scale-free topology of the network. As shown in Fig. 3A, a soft-threshold power of β = 14 was chosen, at which the scale-free topology fit index (signed R²) reached a plateau above 0.85, while mean connectivity decreased gradually, indicating a biologically meaningful network structure. Using the selected soft-threshold power, genes were hierarchically clustered based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM). Dynamic tree cutting identified multiple distinct gene modules, each represented by a unique color (Fig. 3B). To assess the similarity among identified modules, module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated and subjected to hierarchical clustering. The resulting dendrogram and eigengene adjacency heatmap revealed clear separation among most modules, with several modules showing strong positive correlations within clusters and weaker or negative correlations between clusters (Fig. 3C). This suggests that genes within the same module are highly co-regulated, whereas different modules may represent distinct biological processes. Correlation analysis between module eigengenes and sample groups revealed distinct stage-specific associations among co-expression modules (Fig. 3D). Notably, the blue module showed a strong and highly significant positive correlation with WT_SZ, indicating that genes within this module are specifically upregulated during the sporozoite stage. The brown module was also significantly correlated with PL_SZ samples, suggesting a coordinated transcriptional program associated with the developmental stage preceding sporozoite maturation. In addition to these key modules, several modules displayed stage- or time-specific association patterns. The greenyellow module was preferentially associated with WT_MZ samples at 108 h, whereas the green module showed positive correlations with WT_MZ samples at 120 h. Furthermore, the yellow module exhibited positive associations with PL_MZ samples at 96 h and 108 h, indicating distinct transcriptional signatures linked to PL_MZ developmental progression.

Integrated clustering of differentially expressed genes highlights coordinated transcriptional programs

To examine the global expression patterns of genes differentially expressed between the PL and WT strains, statistically significant DEGs identified from the integrated stage-matched comparisons (conducted with three biological replicates per group) were combined and visualized by hierarchical clustering (Fig. 5A). The resulting heatmap revealed clear and structured expression patterns across developmental stages. Crucially, all three biological replicates within each group clustered together (Fig. 5A, top dendrogram), demonstrating high reproducibility and the statistical robustness of our transcriptomic data. The DEGs were organized into distinct expression modules. One major group of genes exhibited high expression in sporozoites followed by marked downregulation during the merozoite stages. Hierarchical clustering of all DEGs identified across stage-matched comparisons classified these genes into four sub-clusters with distinct expression patterns across PL and WT samples (Fig. 5B). Notably, sub-clusters 1, 3, and 4 exhibited a sporozoite-associated expression pattern, characterized by peak expression in sporozoite samples from both strains followed by decreased expression during the subsequent merozoite stages. Among these, sub-clusters 1 and 4 shared highly comparable trajectories, with the primary difference residing in the magnitude of change; specifically, genes in sub-cluster 4 displayed significantly larger expression amplitudes than those in sub-cluster 1. In comparison, sub-cluster 3 showed a more moderate and heterogeneous decline, with an overall magnitude of change that was less pronounced than those observed in sub-clusters 1 and 4. In contrast, sub-cluster 2 showed a distinct merozoite-associated expression pattern, with low expression in sporozoites and markedly increased expression during merozoite development in both strains. The trend plots further illustrate that within each sub-cluster, the individual biological replicates from the three independent experiments followed highly consistent trajectories, reinforcing the reliability of these transcriptional modules. Overall, these DEGs were organized into expression modules with clear stage-associated patterns, reflecting a coordinated and stage-dependent transcriptional program across the E.tenella life cycle.

GO enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional signatures among the four sub-clusters (Fig. 5C). Specifically, Sub-cluster 1 exhibited robust and statistically significant enrichment (P < 0.05) across all categories, with a strong emphasis on signaling and membrane-related functions; in the Molecular Function (MF) category, genes were primarily associated with catalytic activity acting on proteins, protein kinase activity, and transferase activity, while Biological Process (BP) enrichment was dominated by protein modification process, macromolecule modification, and cellular protein modification process. These signatures were further supported by the Cellular Component (CC) category, where “membrane” exhibited the highest statistical significance. Simultaneously, Sub-cluster 2 was characterized by an extremely significant focus on protein synthesis machinery, where the BP category was dominated by translation, amide metabolic processes, and peptide biosynthetic processes, alongside terms related to proteolysis and gene expression. Consistently, CC enrichment for this module featured ribosomal and ribonucleoprotein complexes, while MF terms were prominently represented by the structural constituent of the ribosome and structural molecule activity. Further functional specialization was observed in Sub-cluster 3, which was significantly enriched in nucleic acid metabolism and intracellular transport activities; the most prominent BP terms included DNA replication and RNA biosynthetic processes, which aligned with MF enrichment in nucleotidyltransferase and RNA polymerase activities. This cluster also featured a strong cytoskeletal component, with CC enrichment in the dynein complex and microtubule cytoskeleton, complemented by MF terms such as ATP-dependent microtubule motor activity and various metal ion binding functions. Lastly, in contrast to the high statistical significance of the other modules, no GO terms in Sub-cluster 4 reached the threshold of P < 0.05; however, the top-ranked BP terms suggested a functional orientation toward transmembrane transport, general transport, and the establishment of localization.

Discussion

Precocious strains of E. tenella are widely used as attenuated live vaccines, yet the transcriptional mechanisms underlying their accelerated development remain poorly understood. In this study, stage-matched transcriptomic analyses of a genetically stable precocious line and the wild-type strain revealed pronounced, stage-dependent transcriptional reprogramming.

The Houghton (H) strain of E. tenella is one of the few well-characterized reference strains used in experimental studies, making it a suitable model for transcriptomic analysis (Chapman and Shirley, 2003). Previous studies have demonstrated that precocious E. tenella lines can be generated through sustained in vivo selection for early oocyst shedding, resulting in shortened prepatent periods, reduced schizont size, and stable attenuation (McDonald et al., 1986; Matsubayashi et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2024). Consistent with these observations, we established a precocious line that exhibited a progressively shortened prepatent period over successive passages. Histological analysis further showed advanced endogenous development, with merozoite-containing schizonts detectable as early as 96 h post-infection and a concomitant reduction in merozoite numbers per schizont. Importantly, the persistence of this phenotype after removal of selection pressure indicates that precocity is genetically fixed, providing a robust model for dissecting the molecular basis of accelerated development.

In this study, we compared the global transcriptional profiles of the precocious line and wild-type E. tenella across key developmental stages to explore the molecular basis underlying the shortened prepatent period of PL. Based on principal component analysis, PL sporozoites were clearly separated from WT sporozoites along PC2, indicating pronounced transcriptional divergence between the two strains at the sporozoite stage. To elucidate the molecular basis underlying this separation, differential expression analysis identified 4,600 genes that were differentially expressed between PL and WT sporozoites. Notably, 76 invasion-related genes were more highly transcribed in PL sporozoites, encompassing genes encoding apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1), microneme proteins (MICs), rhoptry neck proteins (RONs), inner membrane complex proteins (IMCs), myosin, calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs), and surface antigens (SAGs). These genes are known to be involved in parasite invasion (Lourido et al., 2010; Carruthers et al., 1999; Meissner et al., 2002; Mineo et al., 1993; Mineo and Kasper, 1994; Li et al., 2020, 2024; Şahın et al., 2010), suggesting that the early transcriptional divergence observed in PL sporozoites may be functionally linked to enhanced invasive capacity during subsequent parasitism. In addition to the elevated transcription of invasion-related genes, functional enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes between PL and WT sporozoites were significantly enriched in GO terms associated with macromolecule biosynthetic and peptide metabolic processes, specifically including translation and nucleotidyltransferase activity. This enrichment suggests that PL sporozoites possess an enhanced translational capacity and metabolic activity at an early developmental stage. Such transcriptional upregulation of the protein synthesis machinery may enable PL parasites to rapidly produce proteins required for invasion and early intracellular development, thereby facilitating a faster transition into subsequent developmental stages.

During merozoite development, transcriptional divergence between the precocious line (PL) and wild-type (WT) strains was reduced compared with that observed at the merozoite stage, as reflected by the progressively decreased number of differentially expressed genes. Despite this overall convergence, GO enrichment analyses revealed consistent and stage-specific functional differences, with DEGs at both early and late merozoite stages being predominantly enriched in membrane-related components, transferase activity, and vesicle-mediated transport, as well as cytoskeletal and microtubule-associated processes. These processes are fundamental to merozoite morphogenesis, intracellular motility, and nuclear division during schizogony, and play critical roles in apicomplexan parasite replication and developmental progression. Notably, the enrichment of cytoskeleton-related GO terms observed in E. tenella merozoites is consistent with earlier EST-based transcriptome analyses of E. maxima, which identified cytoskeletal components as a dominant functional category during the merozoite stage (Schwarz et al., 2010). Moreover, ultrastructural studies using cryo-electron tomography in Plasmodium falciparum have demonstrated that canonical microtubules are a defining feature of merozoites, further supporting the central role of microtubule organization during this developmental stage (Ferreira et al., 2023).

Integrated clustering showed that transcriptional differences between PL and WT strains are organized into coordinated, stage dependent expression modules rather than uniform changes across the genome. Differentially expressed genes formed distinct sporozoite- and merozoite-associated modules, with similar temporal trajectories but differing mainly in expression amplitudes, indicating that the precocious phenotype arises from quantitative modulation of conserved developmental programs. Functionally, sporozoite-associated clusters were enriched for signaling, protein kinase activity, and protein modification processes, consistent with roles in signal transduction and host cell invasion, whereas merozoite-associated clusters were dominated by processes related to translation, amide metabolic processes, and peptide biosynthetic processes, reflecting the high biosynthetic and metabolic demands of schizogony. Together, these results suggest that accelerated development in PL is driven by coordinated amplification of core biological processes rather than by rewiring of developmental pathways.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we established a genetically stable precocious line of E. tenella and applied stage-matched transcriptomic profiling to disentangle precocity-associated expression changes from chronological progression. Our analyses revealed extensive and stage-dependent transcriptional divergence between the precocious line and wild-type parasites, with the most pronounced differences at the sporozoite stage, characterized by enrichment of macromolecule biosynthetic and peptide metabolic processes and elevated transcription of invasion-associated genes. In contrast, transcriptional differences during merozoite development were comparatively reduced but consistently converged on cytoskeletal and microtubule-related processes, highlighting coordinated regulation of morphogenesis and asexual replication. Co-expression network analysis further demonstrated that precocious development is driven by coordinated, stage-specific gene modules rather than isolated gene-level effects. Together, these findings delineate the transcriptional landscape underlying accelerated developmental timing in E. tenella and provide a molecular framework for understanding attenuation by precocity and for guiding the rational optimization of live-attenuated coccidiosis vaccines.
