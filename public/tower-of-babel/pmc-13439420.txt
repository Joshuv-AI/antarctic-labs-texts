Artificial intelligence and person-centred practice: a critical reflection
Authors: Júlio Belo Fernandes, Brendan McCormack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe introduction of artificial intelligence into healthcare is a reality. Drawing on literature, this critical reflection uses McCance and McCormack’s Person-Centred Practice Framework to examine how artificial intelligence can both advance and undermine the humanisation of care. Through structured dialogue and literature synthesis, we identify opportunities alongside challenges. We argue for context-sensitive implementation that integrates artificial intelligence into everyday practice while safeguarding relational ethics, professional judgement, and core person-centred values.Subject terms: Business and industry, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Scientific community, Social sciences

Introduction

The exponential growth of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in healthcare marks a significant transformation in how care is conceptualized, delivered, and evaluated1,2. AI promises to revolutionize everything from diagnostic precision to treatment personalization and workflow optimization1,3. However, such advancements carry profound ethical, relational, and practical implications4,5.

In parallel, what is often described as a growing international emphasis on the humanization of care, frequently articulated through person-centred approaches6,7, reflects a heterogeneous and evolving set of influences rather than a coherent or unified shift. These include normative commitments promoted by international organisations such as the World Health Organization, professional and disciplinary movements advocating relational and humanistic care, and sustained critiques of technocratic and efficiency-driven models of healthcare.

Importantly, these developments have unfolded within a broader global context where healthcare systems are increasingly influenced by neoliberal logics that prioritise cost-effectiveness, productivity, and standardization8,9. As a result, person-centred care does not simply represent a dominant paradigm shift, but a tension-laden construct: at once institutionalised in policy discourse, variably enacted in practice, and, in some contexts, mobilised as a form of resistance to processes of depersonalisation and managerial control.

It is precisely within this contested and dynamic landscape, rather than within a stable or unified movement toward humanisation, that the introduction of AI must be critically examined.

Despite the rapid expansion of AI research, there remains a limited understanding of how these technologies interact with the humanistic dimensions of care. The current literature often frames AI’s role in binary terms, either as an opportunity for improvement or as a threat to personhood, overlooking the complex tensions between efficiency, and human relationships10,11. In this context, “threats to personhood” are not merely abstract ethical concerns but refer to multiple, overlapping dynamics: the reduction of individuals to data points or predictive profiles; the erosion of relational, moral, and dialogical dimensions of care; and the embedding of efficiency-driven and managerial logics that may displace human judgment and agency. These dynamics are particularly significant within healthcare systems already shaped by neoliberal priorities, where technological optimisation can reconfigure what is valued in care, privileging measurable outputs over lived experience.

Taken together, these tensions reveal a significant gap in current understandings of how AI reshapes not only technical processes but also the relational and ethical foundations of care. This gap underscores the need for critical reflection that can illuminate both the technical and relational transformations AI introduces into care environments.

From this perspective, we offer a critical reflection on the integration of AI into healthcare and explore its potential both to advance and to undermine the humanisation of care, drawing on international literature to ground the analysis. To guide this reflection, the Person-Centred Practice Framework (PCPF)12 was chosen as the analytical lens because it provides one of the most comprehensive and empirically grounded models for understanding the multiple layers through which person-centred practice (PCP) is enacted. Developed to articulate how individual, interpersonal, and organisational factors interact to shape care experiences12, the PCPF captures dimensions that are central to humane healthcare, precisely those most unsettled by the emergence of AI. Our argument synthesises published evidence and conceptual contributions across clinical, organisational, ethical, and policy domains, and, by structuring that synthesis through the PCPF, we examine how AI intervenes within each domain that contributes to the operationalisation of PCP, not only as a technical innovation but as a phenomenon that reshapes meanings, values, and relationships within care systems.

The PCPF (Fig. 1) developed by McCance and McCormack12 offers a multidimensional model to conceptualize, operationalize and evaluate PCP. Its five interrelated domains, macro context, prerequisites, the practice environment, and person-centred processes, which collectively lead to achieving person-centred outcomes, collectively highlight the individual, structural, and organizational requirements necessary to achieve PCP.

Guided by this framing, the paper proceeds to reflect critically on each PCPF domain in turn, drawing on literature regarding AI developments and applications. The analysis explores, first, the macro context in which AI-driven transformations are situated; second, the prerequisites that shape professional competence and values; third, the practice environment, where organisational systems and power dynamics are most visible; and finally, the person-centred processes, where the relational essence of care is enacted. Each section interrogates how AI’s promise to intersect with its paradoxes, revealing how humanistic and technological logics coexist, compete, and co-evolve within contemporary healthcare systems.

The macro context: between strategic promise and policy lag

The macro context of the PCPF refers to the overarching structures that shape healthcare systems: policy frameworks, strategic frameworks, workforce development, and strategic leadership12. At this level, AI integration is frequently driven by economic and operational imperatives, including cost reduction, workforce optimisation, and predictive analytics. These priorities, however, are not ideologically neutral. They reflect broader policy orientations associated with market-driven, efficiency-focused models of healthcare governance, in which value is often defined in terms of productivity, scalability, and measurable outcomes.

Within this macro-level orientation, these priorities become concretely embedded in national and international policy frameworks that guide the development and implementation of AI in healthcare. Frameworks, such as the United Kingdom’s “AI in Health and Care” plan13, the European Commission’s “Fostering a European Approach to Artificial Intelligence”14, and the European Parliament, & Council of the European Union15 “Artificial Intelligence Act” emphasize the role of AI in increasing system efficiency and scalability. In addition, the United States’ National Institute of Standards and Technology “AI Risk Management Framework”16 and China’s “Interim Measures for generative AI”17 further illustrate international orientations shaping macro-level adoption.

While these ambitions can support more responsive and sustainable healthcare systems, they often reflect a technocratic orientation that risks sidelining the relational, ethical, and humanistic dimensions underpinning PCP18. A clear example is the European Parliamentary Research Service 2022 Artificial intelligence in healthcare report19, which highlights AI’s potential to relieve system pressures and improve interoperability and diagnostic precision.

Although the report includes references to the importance of human-centred design and clinician support, the primary emphasis remains on technical performance, standardization, and regulatory conformity. Humanistic concerns such as empathetic communication, patient autonomy, and the preservation of therapeutic relationships, are acknowledged only superficially and remain structurally underdeveloped within these strategic priorities.

Opportunities do exist as AI can help address system-level inequities by identifying underserved populations and detecting health trends at scale20,21. Embedded within public services, such as prevention programmes, population-group monitoring, and resource-allocation workflows, these systems may inform more equitable distribution of resources and support a shift from reactive to predictive and preventive models of care. By analysing patient data for patterns that signal emerging risk, including opportunities for early detection22, AI can align with the proactive orientation of a person-centred philosophy while remaining accountable to local contexts and needs. However, as these systems grow in analytical power, they may begin to shape not only how inequities are identified but also how they are defined. What is measured can determine what becomes visible, and populations or experiences that fall outside dominant data structures may remain unrecognized. Predictive models, while aiming to anticipate needs, could inadvertently reinforce existing social gradients if the data they rely on reproduce historical underrepresentation or structural bias23.

Furthermore, the drive toward prevention and prediction, though aligned with a proactive philosophy, can subtly shift ethical boundaries in care. When risk profiles are continuously generated, the distinction between support and surveillance may become porous, and the promise of personalized prevention could slide into paternalism or behavioural control24. The capacity to foresee patterns of illness can empower systems but also may constrain individual agency if used to categorize rather than to understand.

We must also consider that policymaking often trails implementation, creating regulatory gaps and weak oversight25,26. As predictive capacities scale, strategic frameworks struggle to keep pace, and measurable outputs come to dominate strategic agendas27,28, displacing deliberation on dignity, empathy, and individual agency.

Without a clear understanding of person-centred principles by leaders and health professionals, AI risks being adopted uncritically, reinforcing depersonalized care, surveillance logics, and algorithmic bias, ultimately serving as a tool of technocratic control rather than a driver of humanistic transformation.

To facilitate the integration of AI in ways that align with person-centred values, it is essential to embed these values directly into national policy documents and digital health strategies14,15. This means articulating ethical commitments alongside efficiency goals and ensuring that principles of PCP guide technological advancements. Leadership is critical in this process and must be supported through ongoing professional development that fosters informatics competencies, critical engagement with AI, ethical reasoning, and humanistic, person-centred practice. Strategic leaders should be equipped to ask whether a system is effective and enhances the care experience from the perspective of the person receiving it29.

Co-creation mechanisms must also be institutionalized. This includes creating platforms where people receiving care, caregivers, and health professionals participate in designing, evaluating, and adapting AI tools30. Such participatory processes can safeguard real-world needs and care experiences. To this end, systems should be designed to incorporate a broader range of data, including individual, social and cultural insights1,3. AI tools must be developed to support decisions that are attuned to each individual’s personhood, rather than abstract outcome indicators. One step in this direction is the PREDICT: Breast Cancer decision-support tool, which provides individualized survival estimates based on clinical data and supports shared decision-making around treatment options. While it does not fully capture the richness of personal narratives or contextual realities, it facilitates more collaborative discussions between patients and clinicians by integrating personal values and preferences alongside clinical outcomes31. Such systems have the potential to be further developed into responsive systems that more explicitly incorporate individual narrative as a part of the AI-assisted decision-making process.

Regulatory frameworks must go beyond traditional cybersecurity and data protection concerns to ensure meaningful oversight. This implies a form of governance that is not limited to compliance or procedural control but actively examines how AI systems reshape relationships, power, and responsibility in care. This oversight can occur only when evaluation extends beyond technical validation to include questions of purpose, context, and impact such as who benefits, who is excluded, and how personhood is affected.

New governance structures, such as interdisciplinary cyber-bioethics boards or national commissions on digital health equity, are therefore required to assess not only performance and safety but also the relational, ethical, and experiential implications of AI integration32. In this context, ethical evaluation must extend beyond traditional bioethical frameworks to encompass relational, experiential, and practice-based understandings of care, recognising that AI reshapes not only decisions but the meaning and enactment of human relationships in healthcare. Such governance structures could operate through continuous review processes that combine algorithmic audits with qualitative evidence drawn from users, clinicians, and communities. In this sense, periodic algorithmic audits should be mandated to evaluate bias, transparency, and alignment with person-centred principles33,34, while deliberative forums ensure that oversight remains dialogical and value-driven rather than technocratic.

Importantly, the impact of AI integration should not be measured solely through operational metrics. Person-centred indicators such as user collaboration, perceived dignity, participation in decision-making, and emotional safety must be developed and used as benchmarks for technological implementation35,36. These indicators provide a richer, values-based picture of success and help prevent the reduction of care to a set of technical transactions.

Finally, organizations should implement accessible and responsive user-centred feedback mechanisms that allow individuals to report concerns, share experiences, and raise ethical questions regarding AI use37. Such mechanisms may include digital and non-digital channels like secure online portals, in-app reporting tools, dedicated helplines, and patient or staff liaison offices, through which users can express satisfaction, discomfort, or perceived bias in AI-mediated decisions. Feedback must be triaged and analysed systematically by interdisciplinary teams, ensuring that concerns inform both technical refinement and organizational learning. These processes should be transparent, with clear timelines for acknowledgement, investigation, and communication of outcomes. In this way, accountability structures become relational rather than bureaucratic, enabling timely responses, trust building, and continuous improvement of both the technology and the system in which it operates.

Prerequisites: the human capacities that machines cannot replace

The prerequisite dimension of the PCPF encompasses the personal and professional attributes that healthcare practitioners must bring to their roles to enable PCP12. These include professional competence, developed interpersonal skills, commitment to the job, clarity of beliefs and values, and knowing self. This foundational layer shapes how care is enacted, influencing technical effectiveness, relational quality, and ethical responsiveness.

Integrating AI into healthcare practice introduces opportunities and complexities related to these prerequisites. On the one hand, AI can augment professional competence by providing healthcare professional with real-time access to vast clinical data, decision-support tools, and evidence-based guidelines1,3. This can enhance diagnostic accuracy, personalize treatment plans, and support continuous learning. AI-powered simulations and educational platforms can support the development of clinicians’ capabilities safely and effectively, offering immersive scenarios for honing interpersonal communication and clinical judgment38.

At the same time, much of the debate to date has focused on whether, in practice, such tools make care merely more efficient rather than genuinely person-centred. A promising line of argument is that AI can scaffold person-centredness when it is used to support, not substitute, clinical judgment and dialogue39. For example, in nursing, AI-supported individual medication plans can synthesise a person’s comorbidities, laboratory values, concomitant therapies, lifestyle factors, and stated goals to propose tailored regimens and surface trade-offs in language. Continuous signals (e.g., symptom diaries, wearables) can be translated into dose-adjustment suggestions and early warnings about adverse effects, while interaction checkers and equity-aware dosing heuristics help clinicians anticipate risks in specific subgroups. Crucially, these outputs serve best as conversation prompts: they can structure options, clarify uncertainties, and make explicit the values at stake so that the person’s preferences remain determinative. The same tools, however, may undercut PCP if they optimise primarily for adherence or cost, constrain discussion through opaque recommendations, add alert burden that erodes therapeutic presence, or invite automation bias that displaces reflective judgment. The challenge, therefore, is not simply to deploy AI at the point of decision, but to embed it in ways that protect relational competence, preserve ethical discretion, and keep the person’s narrative central to medication planning40.

However, this very potential to enhance professional competence also introduces new demands on the cultivation and enactment of key prerequisites. As AI systems become embedded in everyday care, practitioners must develop the capacity to navigate an additional layer of epistemic complexity: interpreting algorithmic outputs, judging their reliability, and integrating them with experiential and contextual knowledge41. Professional competence must therefore extend beyond clinical expertise to encompass informatics competencies, a critical understanding of AI’s capabilities and limitations, and the ability to appraise AI-generated recommendations42. Without this, there is a risk that practitioners may either over-rely on automated outputs, potentially diminishing their clinical judgment, or reject AI insights out of mistrust, limiting beneficial collaboration.

The development of interpersonal skills faces new demands in an AI-enhanced environment. The relational space between practitioner and person can be disrupted if technology is perceived as intrusive, depersonalizing, or distancing43. Practitioners must, therefore, develop competencies to integrate AI tools without compromising empathy, active listening, and authentic engagement. This requires technical and informatics competence, alongside reflective practice that continuously examines how technology shapes communication dynamics and the therapeutic relationship, as articulated in contemporary informatics competency frameworks44–46, which emphasise the integration of technical, ethical, and relational dimensions of practice.

Professional identity, ethical accountability, and the ongoing development of values become crucial anchors amidst rapid technological change. These are not individual traits, but dynamic capacities shaped through reflective practice, ethical deliberation, and engagement with professional and organisational frameworks. In this context, reaffirming commitment to personhood and human dignity is not solely a matter of individual intention, but a collective and systemic responsibility, particularly in environments where technological and efficiency-driven pressures risk reducing care to mechanistic processes47.

The relational dimension of care is not only the foundation of what we do as healthcare professionals, it’s also part of who we become. The therapeutic relationship is deeply bidirectional. I (as a healthcare professional) am changed through the encounter with the other. That change, that mutual presence, is fundamental. It shapes our clinical judgment, our compassion, our humanity. When we say that “nurses and care workers need to be emotionally touched by their everyday care experiences,”48 we are also affirming that care is not transactional, it’s transformative. And for that transformation to happen, systems must allow it and value it. Without that commitment we are at risk of designing systems, sometimes under the banner of efficiency or innovation, that actually disables the very conditions that make genuine human encounters possible. We can never remove the importance of the relationship from the equation. If we do, we are not just simplifying care, we are stripping it of meaning.

Clear ethical frameworks, including approaches such as cyber-bioethics, while extending toward broader relational models of digital ethics, can support practitioners in navigating moral dilemmas posed by AI, such as balancing data-driven recommendations with individual preferences or addressing algorithmic biases.

Finally, knowing self is more important than ever in the context of AI-integrated healthcare. Within the PCPF, this element is best understood as part of reflective practice, through which practitioners develop ongoing self-awareness and examine how their values, assumptions, and positionality shape their interactions, decisions, and ethical orientations12. In an era where AI systems increasingly shape clinical pathways and decision-making, reflective practice becomes essential to critically engage with these technologies. This includes recognising one’s own attitudes toward technology, maintaining balanced and responsible use, and identifying implicit biases when interpreting AI-generated data or engaging with algorithm-driven recommendations, particularly given the risk that such systems may reproduce or reinforce societal inequities.

To support this reflective practice, continuous professional development programmes should foster approaches that integrate education in digital ethics, critical appraisal of AI tools, and awareness of cognitive and cultural biases49. Such development can foster deeper self-awareness through reflective practice, encouraging practitioners to ask: How does my relationship with technology shape the way I relate to the people I care for? and How do my beliefs, fears, or expectations influence my use of AI in practice?

In this way, knowing self becomes a safeguard against dehumanization and a guide toward using AI in a way that enhances, rather than replaces, the core human dimensions of care.

To facilitate these adaptations, healthcare organisations and educational institutions must invest in integrated educational approaches that combine clinical and informatics competencies with ethical reflection42. Curricula should be updated to include AI competencies, human-machine collaboration, and person-centred communication in technology-rich contexts. Mentoring and peer support can help practitioners share experiences and strategies for maintaining person-centredness while embracing AI. Such training and mentorship are essential if relational competence, empathy, and ethical discernment are to be retained as core practices in PCP.

The practice environment: culture, power, and organizational infrastructure

The practice environment of the PCPF refers to the contextual conditions in which care is delivered. This includes the appropriate skill mix, effective staff relationships, supportive organizational systems, power sharing, potential for innovation and risk taking and the physical environment that collectively shape the context culture and nature of care interactions12. Integrating AI into this environment presents transformative opportunities and complex challenges that impact how PCP can be realized.

AI offers the potential to influence the skill mix and workload distribution among healthcare teams through scheduling, resource allocation, and task automation50. However, these processes are not inherently neutral or equitable. They are shaped by the data on which they are trained, the organisational priorities embedded in their design, and the power structures governing their implementation. As such, while AI-driven systems may reduce administrative burdens and create opportunities for professionals to focus more on person-centred interactions, they may also reproduce or amplify existing inequities in workload distribution, staffing hierarchies, and role expectations.

Claims that automation will naturally result in balanced team dynamics or improved collaboration therefore require critical scrutiny. In healthcare contexts, scheduling and resource allocation have long been sites of power asymmetry, moral distress, and inequitable burden, and algorithmic systems may reinforce these dynamics if efficiency is prioritised over professional autonomy and well-being. This raises important questions regarding individual agency within increasingly algorithmically mediated environments: How are individual preferences for shifts or working days managed in such environments? What happens when personal issues arise that require last-minute rescheduling?

To maintain a person-centred ethos, AI-based scheduling systems should be built for flexibility: staff must be able to indicate preferred working patterns, set availability, and request specific days off. Additionally, built-in mechanisms that accommodate last-minute changes, such as AI-generated suggestions for coverage or equitable redistribution of shifts, can help respond dynamically to human realities. Managers should retain the final authority to override AI suggestions when equity, or context calls for it. Transparent protocols and feedback loops also ensure that staff feel heard and not subject to overly rigid systems.

Ensuring the effectiveness of human oversight is an ongoing process requiring sustained commitment and support. At the organisational level, blurred lines of accountability between clinical leadership, and information technology make overrides risky; when no one clearly owns the consequences of rejecting an algorithmic allocation, compliance becomes the safest choice. Procurement and vendor terms can penalise deviations from recommended outputs, while performance regimes that valorise throughput cast reflective pauses as inefficiency. Lean staffing and high task intensity further erode the time needed to engage in reflective practice, scrutinise and contest results, turning oversight into aspiration rather than practice51,52. Psychologically, automation bias, authority gradients (especially where systems are institutionally endorsed), cognitive load, and alert fatigue dampen the willingness, and capacity, to question53. Fear of blame for deviating from “what the system said” can produce moral distress and learned helplessness. Economically, the very business case for AI, cost savings and productivity gains, can devalue the human review step as waste; budgets rarely fund the “shadow time” for second looks, audit trails, and reflection, or the ongoing education required to sustain informatics competencies and ethical reasoning at scale54,55.

Against this backdrop, the question is not only whether humans are in the loop, but whether the loop affords real authority52. A human-in-the-loop can safeguard person-centred values only if they possess procedural power to act, if interruptability and contestability are embedded in design, contracts, and performance evaluation. In scheduling, as in clinical decision support, meaningful participation requires the ability to pause or amend algorithmic outcomes without penalty and with institutional backing. However, in highly optimized systems where efficiency is the overriding logic, such discretionary spaces may erode. When algorithms dictate workflow priorities, the human may remain technically “in the loop” but become functionally sidelined, a supervisor of decisions they can no longer meaningfully influence.

This tension invites reflection on both design and governance: how can systems be built so that human intervention does not merely simulate control but embodies it? Should override capacity be an individual responsibility or an institutional mandate shared by teams and leaders? How can organizations recalibrate performance dashboards so that ethically motivated overrides count as quality rather than as delay? And, in environments increasingly measured by narrow metrics, how can acts of ethical resistance, pausing automation to protect relational care, be recognised as legitimate and valuable rather than inefficient? These questions illustrate that the promise of AI to enhance collaboration and reduce burden depends fundamentally on preserving the human capacity to discern, to care, and, when necessary, to say no.

Beyond workload optimization, AI can strengthen communication and shared decision-making by providing accessible, personalized information within the team56. AI-powered decision aids can translate complex medical data into comprehensible formats, enabling individuals to engage more actively in care planning56,57. Yet, the depth of this benefit will be determined by whether organizations invest in the time, professional development, and governance needed for humans to interpret, question, and when warranted, override what the system proposes. Only then can oversight become a living practice rather than a procedural checkbox.

Seen from this angle, the culture around AI becomes as critical as the tools themselves. However, technology does not only enable collaboration; it can also generate new forms of alienation. If AI tools are imposed without adequate preparation and professional development, consultation or adaptation, they may disrupt established workflows and team cohesion58. Staff may feel surveilled, leading to stress, defensiveness, or disengagement, particularly if performance metrics are perceived as punitive or impersonal. Therefore, organizations must foster a culture where AI adoption is collaborative, with frontline workers involved in designing, selecting, and evaluating technologies to ensure they align with human-centred goals59.

Supportive organizational systems are essential to balancing AI’s promise with its risks. This includes investing in robust technology infrastructure, ongoing training, and a culture of safety so staff feel confident in using AI and openly discuss concerns52. Organizations must also ensure equitable access to AI resources across different units and avoid creating disparities that could fracture the workplace culture.

Decision-making power should not become centralized in algorithms or technology vendors60; organizations should promote distributed authority where clinicians, service users, and families co-govern AI tools61. Mechanisms such as ethics committees or advisory panels, including diverse stakeholders, can help maintain accountability and ensure that AI supports rather than overrides human judgment and self-will5,32,33.

AI also introduces new possibilities for innovation and risk-taking within the practice environment. Advanced analytics can identify areas for quality improvement and patient safety in real-time62. However, this potential can only be realized if the organizational culture supports experimentation, learning from errors, and iterative adaptation. Fear of punitive responses to technology-related mistakes may inhibit innovation and creativity63.

Finally, the physical environment plays a decisive role in shaping how AI is experienced in care encounters. The placement of digital interfaces, the layout of consultation spaces, and the visibility of screens influence whether technology supports or disrupts human connection. For instance, positioning monitors so that the practitioner and the person can view information together encourages transparency and shared understanding. In contrast, screen-centred layouts that draw the practitioner’s gaze away from the person may fragment communication and reduce relational presence64,65. Similarly, ergonomic design that minimizes noise, clutter, and intrusive device alerts helps sustain attention and calm within the therapeutic space66. A well-designed environment therefore can act as a mediator between technology and humanity.

Person-centred processes: preserving the relational core

At the heart of the PCPF lie the person-centred processes, the dynamic interactions through which healthcare professionals engage with individuals to deliver care that respects and responds to their unique values, beliefs, and needs. These processes involve engaging collaboratively, authentically, empathetically, and holistically with each person, fostering continuous involvement in the shared decision-making process12. The integration of AI into these deeply human exchanges introduces not a simple balance of opportunities and challenges, but a set of intertwined tensions that reshape what it means to connect, understand, and care.

AI’s promise of personalization clearly illustrates this paradox. By analysing complex datasets, algorithms can identify subtle patterns that help practitioners tailor care plans more closely to each individual’s circumstances, preferences, and life context18. Such data-driven insight can appear to deepen person-centredness by illuminating what matters most to each person. However, the same mechanisms that enable personalization simultaneously extend the reach of surveillance and abstraction. When identity is inferred from data traces, the person risks being redefined through probabilistic profiles rather than lived narratives. The quest for precision can thus flatten individuality, translating stories of experience into categories of prediction.

This interdependence between personalization and reductionism reveals the fragility of authenticity in technologically mediated care. As algorithmic tools suggest optimal pathways, clinicians may feel pressure to privilege what appears efficient or statistically sound, rather than pause for the unstructured dialogue through which meaning and trust are co-created. What is gained in analytic clarity can be lost in relational depth. When practitioners come to rely on AI outputs as surrogates for understanding, the dialogue risks shifting from “What matters to you?” to “What does the system say about you?”.

Engaging authentically is central to PCP, depends on more than access to accurate data; it requires presence, uncertainty tolerance, and responsiveness to context. The presence of technology in the clinical encounter can support these qualities or subtly erode them. Screen-mediated interfaces, decision dashboards, or conversational agents may streamline communication, yet they can also redirect attention away from the interpersonal moment. The task, then, is not to reject technology but to choreograph it, to ensure that digital mediation enhances rather than interrupts the emotional and moral texture of care33,67.

The paradox of AI within person-centred processes lies precisely here: the same systems that promise to make care more individualised can also depersonalise it; the same efficiencies that appear to save time can hollow out the shared space where understanding and trust are built. Navigating this terrain demands reflective practice, a continuous and critical awareness of how every technological gain carries relational and ethical consequences. AI’s integration into PCP will therefore depend less on what it can calculate and more on how practitioners, organisations, and societies choose to interpret and inhabit the spaces between data and dialogue.

While thoughtful examples of integration exist, they are still context-specific and require intentional design. For instance, AI-powered scribing tools such as ambient clinical intelligence have been used to transcribe consultations in real time, allowing clinicians to maintain eye contact and focus fully on the person rather than on a computer screen68. However, their usefulness and effectiveness have yet to be determined.

Similarly, AI-based decision aids can improve shared decision-making by providing evidence summaries, outcome probabilities, and personalized risk assessments that empower individuals to participate actively in their care50,55. Nevertheless, ethical challenges arise when individuals or practitioners lack transparency about how AI-derived recommendations are generated or when complexity makes understanding difficult5,32,33. Maintaining clarity, explaining AI inputs in accessible language, and ensuring informed consent are essential to preserve trust and autonomy.

Being sympathetically present is another dimension vulnerable to technological disruption. AI cannot replace the nuanced emotional intelligence and ethical sensitivity human practitioners bring to moments of vulnerability or distress69. Therefore, AI can assist by alerting clinicians to subtle cues, such as changes in mood or behaviour detected through natural language processing or wearable sensors, enabling timely, empathetic responses70,71. Here, AI functions best as an adjunct, enhancing the human capacity to be fully present40. Its highest contribution is not to speak for the clinician or the person, but to clear space for the encounter itself, to buy back attention, to surface what might otherwise be missed, and then to step aside so that empathetic presence, and shared meaning-making can do the work that no model can.

Working holistically means considering the whole person’s physical, emotional, social, and spiritual dimensions, rather than focusing narrowly on symptoms or diseases12. While AI systems can process and correlate vast datasets, they tend to prioritise what is quantifiable which risks narrowing the scope of care. At the same time, AI’s capacity to integrate multimodal information can personalise care plans and appear to extend a holistic view, linking clinical data with patterns from daily life, preferences, or context. Yet it must be highlighted that the very datafication enabling this personalisation can also expand surveillance and invite algorithmic reductionism: identities risk being redefined by proxies and profiles, and what becomes legible to the system can begin to stand in for who the person is.

To genuinely support holistic practice, AI would need to incorporate diverse sources of data: social determinants of health, family and community contexts, and personal narratives. Even then, one must ask: Can AI truly do this? Or are we, in our enthusiasm, edging toward a dangerous form of anthropomorphism, projecting human capacities like empathy, judgment, and moral intuition onto systems that do not possess consciousness? This feels like a slippery slope, where well-meaning integration of AI may inadvertently contribute to the dehumanisation of care. The relational depth at the heart of holistic, person-centred care cannot be programmed or simulated, it must be lived, felt, and practiced by humans. Therefore, while AI holds tremendous potential to enhance understanding and enrich the informational depth of care, it must never be mistaken for a substitute for human presence, ethical discernment, or relational insight. The role of the healthcare team remains irreplaceable: to interpret AI outputs through a humanistic and ethical framework that safeguards the dignity, individuality, and complexity of each person.

Through this critical reflection, multiple opportunities, challenges, and ethical tensions have been identified across the domains of the PCPF. These findings highlight that the integration of AI into PCP care is not a question of simple benefit or risk but of paradox and balance, where each opportunity introduces new forms of vulnerability. Opportunities and challenges.

While the PCPF provides a powerful lens for examining AI’s influence across the health system, from macro-level policy structures to the direct care-giving context, it predates contemporary algorithmic systems and, as such, reveals certain limitations when applied to them. The framework remains conceptually robust and comprehensive; however, its use in AI-mediated contexts exposes areas that merit critical re-examination. The domains offer a coherent structure for analysing care systems, but the specific constructs were formulated when human actors were the sole agents of care. A genuinely critical reflection must therefore not only employ the PCPF to structure the analysis but also interrogate its adequacy considering this new technological paradigm.

The emergence of AI as a mediating presence in clinical reasoning, communication, and decision-making challenges implicit assumptions such as agency, intentionality, and moral responsibility, and calls for a reconsideration of how the constructs underpinning each domain are operationalised. For example, professional competence must now encompass informatics competencies and the capacity to appraise machine-generated recommendations critically; power sharing must address asymmetries between human judgement and algorithmic authority to keep decision-making dialogical rather than deferential; and authentic engagement requires forms of reflective practice that are attuned to technologically mediated encounters, where presence and empathy face barriers introduced by screens, dashboards, and data visualisations.

The challenge, then, is not to revise the PCPF’s overall architecture, but to expand the meaning and scope of its constructs to include aspects such as digital ethics, data stewardship, and the dynamics of human–AI collaboration. Doing so preserves the framework’s humanistic foundations while extending its relevance to the present and future integration of AI-enabled care. In this sense, the PCPF remains not only a guiding framework but a living theoretical structure, capable of evolving as care itself is increasingly shaped by intelligent technologies.

Conclusion

As healthcare systems face growing pressure to be more efficient, data-driven, and technologically advanced, integrating AI is both opportunity and challenge. Read through the PCPF lens, the promise and peril of AI are co-present: the same capacities that personalise, anticipate, and streamline can also narrow what becomes visible, displace human agency, and thin the relational depth on which PCP depends. This critical reflection has traced those tensions across the framework’s domains and, in doing so, identified research and practice priorities that move beyond binary appraisals toward a programme for humane integration. Empirically, significant gaps remain. We still lack construct-level operationalisations of person-centred competencies under AI-mediated conditions; evidence still privileges technical and operational endpoints, while relational outcomes are rarely primary endpoints. Methodologically, mixed-methods and ethnographic work is needed to illuminate how AI reshapes encounters, roles, and moral responsibility in real settings. From a practice perspective, several fields appear particularly promising, provided person-centred safeguards are in place. In clinical services, decision support, early-warning tools, and intelligent scheduling can buy back time that can be invested in PCP; in public services, pattern detection can guide earlier and fairer interventions if equity auditing, community participation, and clear purpose limitations are embedded.

Education and professional formation likewise have a pivotal role, strengthening informatics competencies, critical appraisal, and communicative capacities so that algorithmic outputs become prompts for dialogue rather than substitutes for understanding.

Ultimately, AI’s promise lies not in replacing human care but in supporting it by enhancing insight, improving access, and enabling more time for human connection. Realizing that promise will require healthcare leaders, clinicians, researchers, and technologists to engage in sustained dialogue about values, power, and purpose. The question is not whether we will use AI in healthcare, but how it will be used and what its impact will be. If guided by relational ethics, equity, inclusivity, and social justice principles, AI can help build a future in which technology amplifies our humanity rather than eclipses it. The challenge before us is not only technical but moral. It is a challenge worth embracing.
