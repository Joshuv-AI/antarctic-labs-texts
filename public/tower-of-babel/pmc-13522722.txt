Variations in the Management of Uncontrolled Gout Between Rheumatologists and Nephrologists: A Retrospective Medical Record Review
Authors: Kenneth Saag, Nana Kragh, Amod Athavale, Bhavisha Desai, Amal Gulaid, Abiola Oladapo, Brittany Smith, Hyon Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the management practices of rheumatologists and nephrologists in treating patients with uncontrolled gout.MethodsUS board‐certified rheumatologists and nephrologists treating patients with uncontrolled gout participated in a web‐based, retrospective medical record audit study, sharing their perceptions of uncontrolled gout management and treatment. Two‐sample t‐tests and chi‐square tests were used to test the differences between specialties and the P values reported reflected statistically significant differences.ResultsA total of 75 nephrologists and 150 rheumatologists extracted medical records of 611 patients with uncontrolled gout. Common comorbidities included chronic kidney disease (CKD) (85%), hypertension (47%), obesity (36%), and diabetes (35%). Rheumatologists frequently prescribed higher doses of allopurinol (29% vs 17%; P = 0.002) and monitored serum urate (SU) levels at every visit (90% vs 75%; P < 0.001) compared with nephrologists. Patients managed by nephrologists had higher rates of emergency department visits (24% vs 13%; P = 0.001) and hospitalization for gout‐related issues (6% vs 1%; P = 0.011) than patients managed by rheumatologists. Both specialties acknowledged a significant burden of current gout treatments among their patients and the need for improved treatment options for uncontrolled gout.ConclusionRheumatologists prescribed higher gout medication doses and monitored SU levels more frequently, whereas patients with uncontrolled gout managed by nephrologists had higher incidence of CKD and greater health care resource utilization. Both groups agreed on the need for improved gout treatments.

INTRODUCTION

An estimated 2% to 6% of patients with gout have uncontrolled gout, characterized by persistently high serum urate (SU) levels (SU ≥ 6.0 mg/dL) and active symptoms of gout, despite the use of oral urate‐lowering therapies (ULTs). 1 The quality of life (QoL) of patients with uncontrolled gout is adversely affected by disease severity, symptoms, and comorbidities. 2 An increase in the number of joints affected by gout is associated with decreased physical mobility, higher pain scores, and reduced QoL. 2 , 3 , 4 Because of this, patients with uncontrolled gout experience high health care resource utilization and a negative impact on QoL. 5 , 6

The treatment of patients with gout is complex. 7 Rheumatologists and nephrologists are both involved in the management of patients with uncontrolled gout and chronic kidney disease (CKD) who are more likely to develop gout. 8 , 9 Limited information is available on the differences and similarities of rheumatologists’ and nephrologists’ management and treatment practices in caring for patients with uncontrolled gout.

The objectives of this study were to assess physician‐reported gout disease burden, treatment experiences, and health care resource utilization among patients with uncontrolled gout. Additionally, we aimed to assess treatment approaches for managing uncontrolled gout and to explore rheumatologists and nephrologists perceptions of unmet medical needs.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study overview and design

We conducted a retrospective medical record review of patients with uncontrolled gout in the United States via a web‐enabled case report form. Rheumatologists and nephrologists participated in an online survey related to their perceptions of uncontrolled gout management and treatment. Fielding took place from March to June 2024. The study was reviewed by an institutional review board (Advarra, Columbia, MD) and deemed exempt.

Study instrument and recruitment

The study instrument consisted of a screener, a physician‐specific questionnaire, and a case report form for the abstraction of patient‐specific information from medical records. Participating physicians were required to be board‐certified in the United States, have between 3 and 30 years of clinical practice experience, and spend at least 50% of their professional time providing direct patient care. They must also have managed at least 25 patients with gout in the past 12 months (including ≥5 patients or ≥10 patients with uncontrolled gout for nephrologists or rheumatologists, respectively), served as the primary decision‐maker for prescribing medications for patients with gout, and have access to patient records that met the study's definition of uncontrolled gout (for more details see Supplementary Table S1). Patients in the current study whose medical records were abstracted had to have been diagnosed with uncontrolled gout for ≥12 months, where uncontrolled gout was defined as having (1) inadequate control of symptoms and failure to normalize SU with ULTs with an SU > 6 mg/dL, (2) a history of >2 gout flares within 12 months, and/or the presence of >1 gout tophus, and/or current diagnosis of gout arthritis, and (3) currently on or with a history of taking medication for uncontrolled gout for ≥12 months (for more details see Supplementary Table S2). Additional information is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Questionnaire and medical record audit

Participating physicians completed an online questionnaire (further details in the Supplementary Methods), which assessed their perceptions of uncontrolled gout management and treatment using a 1 to 7 Likert scale. Scores 1 to 2 indicate low satisfaction or agreement, 3 to 5 indicate moderate levels of satisfaction or agreement, and 6 to 7 indicate higher or strong satisfaction or agreement. Physicians also abstracted demographic and clinical information from patients’ medical records, including demographic information (including race and ethnicity, sex, and date of birth) and clinical information (including symptomology, treatment history and experience, as well as health care resource utilization); data abstracted from patient records are further detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analyses

We summarized overall study data descriptively, with continuous variables presented as means with SDs and categorical variables reported as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons between rheumatologists and nephrologists, statistical hypothesis tests were conducted. We used two‐sample t‐tests for continuous variables, and chi‐square tests were used for categorical variables. When multiple comparisons were performed, we adjusted P values using the false discovery rate correction. P values shown represent statistically significant differences between groups. All statistical analyses were conducted using Q Research Software 5.12.4.0.

RESULTS

Physician demographics

A total of 250 physicians, including 175 rheumatologists and 75 nephrologists, abstracted medical records for 611 patients with uncontrolled gout (Table 1), of which 409 were provided by rheumatologists and 202 provided by nephrologists. Overall, most physicians (68%) were male and practiced in urban areas (72%). Physicians had been in practice for a mean ± SD of 15.7 ± 7.1 years. In the last 12 months, participants managed a mean ± SD of 141 ± 130 patients with gout, 45% of whom had uncontrolled gout. Rheumatologists managed a mean ± SD n of 149 ± 98 patients with gout in the last 12 months, 46% of whom had uncontrolled gout. Nephrologists managed a mean ± SD of 122 ± 184 patients with gout during the same period, with 43% of these patients having uncontrolled gout.

The mean ± SD age at the most recent visit was 54.1 ± 10.5 years (Table 2). The mean ± SD gout disease duration was 6.7 ± 6.2 years, which was slightly shorter for patients managed by rheumatologists (6.4 ± 5.9 years) compared with those managed by nephrologists (7.3 ± 1.3 years) (P = 0.075).

Patient clinical characteristics: initial gout diagnosis

In the 12 months before their initial gout diagnosis, patients had a mean ± SD SU of 9.3 ± 1.3 mg/dL, experienced a mean ± SD of 5.2 ± 2.3 active gout symptoms (including arthritis, visible tophi, swollen joints, painful or inflammation around joints, joint discomfort or stiffness, gout flares, decreased range of motion, mild fever, fatigue, bone spurs, or edema), and had a mean ± SD of 9.6 ± 4.3 gout flares (Table 2). The mean ± SD SU levels were similar between patients managed by rheumatologists (9.2 ± 1.3 mg/dL) and those managed by nephrologists (9.4 ± 1.3 mg/dL) (P = 0.071). Additionally, patients managed by rheumatologists had similar rates of joint symptoms (93% vs 87%, P = 0.102), visible tophi (75% vs 70%, P ≥ 0.999), and gout flares (76% vs 67%, P = 0.114) compared with those managed by nephrologists.

Patient clinical characteristics: most recent visit

Clinical characteristics observed at the most recent visit are provided in Table 2. The mean ± SD time from initial gout diagnosis to the most recent visit was 6.7 ± 6.2 years for overall patients, 6.4 ± 5.9 years for patients managed by rheumatologists, and 7.3 ± 1.3 years for patients managed by nephrologists. Overall, patients had a mean ± SD SU of 8.0 ± 1.1 mg/dL, which was similar, albeit slightly lower than in patients managed by rheumatologists (7.9 ± 1.1 mg/dL) compared with nephrologists (8.2 ± 1.3 mg/dL) (P < 0.001). In the last 12 months, patients reported a mean ± SD of 3.8 ± 2.3 active gout symptoms, including a mean ± SD of 2.6 ± 1.0 gout flares. The mean ± SD number of active symptoms was similar between patients managed by rheumatologist (3.9 ± 2.2 active symptoms) and those managed by nephrologists (3.6 ± 2.5 active symptoms, P = 0.060). Visible tophi was the most common active gout symptom experienced in 90% of patients managed by rheumatologists and 92% managed by nephrologists (P ≥ 0.999). However, patients managed by rheumatologists experienced higher rates of certain gout‐related symptoms compared with those managed by nephrologists (ie, joint symptoms [78% vs 65%, P = 0.009] and gout flares [58% vs 43%, P = 0.002]).

Regarding SU monitoring, 85% of physicians overall measured SU at every visit, with rheumatologists assessing SU more consistently than nephrologists (90% vs 75%, P < 0.001) (Figure 1).

Comorbidities

The most common comorbidities reported by all physicians included CKD, hypertension, obesity, and diabetes (Supplementary Figure S1A). Patients managed by nephrologists had higher rates of renal impairment (97% vs 71%, P < 0.001) and more severe CKD (93% vs 67% of patients with ≥Stage 3 CKD, P = 0.007) than those managed by rheumatologists (Supplementary Figures S1A and B).

Health care resource utilization because of gout

In the last 12 months, the majority of patients had high gout‐related health care resource utilization: 95% attended routine physician appointments; 91% underwent their laboratory testing (including outside routine visits); and 65% had physician visits for medical imaging (Table 3). Additionally, 35% had unplanned urgent care visits, 17% had gout‐related emergency department (ED) visits, and 3% were hospitalized for a mean ± SD of 3.7 ± 5.4 inpatient days (Table 3).

Patients managed by nephrologists had higher rates of hospitalization for gout‐related issues (6% vs 1% of patients, P = 0.011) and trended toward a directionally longer mean ± SD gout‐related hospital stay length (4.4 ± 6.1 vs 1.7 ± 0.6 days, P = 0.471) than patients managed by rheumatologists (Table 3). Physician‐perceived patient QoL

Physicians, regardless of specialty, reported that uncontrolled gout at least moderately impacted patients’ QoL (97%), including their social life (90%–94%), hobbies (93%), family life (89%–93%), health and general wellness (91%–94%), travel and leisure activities (91%–93%), and day‐to‐day lives (91%–92%) (Supplementary Figure S2). Physicians also reported that uncontrolled gout impacted patient's professional life (89%–92%) and work attendance (87%–90%).

Treatment experiences

The majority of patients (72%) were most recently treated with allopurinol (Figure 2A), typically at 300 mg/day, with doses ranging from 50 and 1,500 mg/day allopurinol (Figure 2B and C). A higher proportion of patients managed by rheumatologists received >300 mg/day allopurinol than patients managed by nephrologists (29% vs 17%, respectively, P = 0.002). Febuxostat was prescribed with similar frequency by rheumatologists and nephrologists (24% and 25%, P ≥ 0.999), as was colchicine (18% and 13%, P = 0.957). Only 5% of patients managed by rheumatologists or nephrologists were taking uricase therapy (ie, pegloticase). Nephrologists were twice as likely as rheumatologists to strongly agree that they avoid prescribing treatments with pretreatment requirements such as immunosuppression) (23% vs 12%, P = 0.627) (Figure 3).

For all treatment classes, most patients (59%) were moderately treatment compliant (ie, compliant between 50% and 75% of the time) with their current treatment regimen, including 62% of patients managed by rheumatologists and 53% of patients managed by nephrologists (Supplementary Figure S3A). Regardless of regimen, a higher proportion of patients managed by nephrologists, compared with rheumatologists, had high treatment compliance rates (ie, compliant >75% of the time) (35% vs 25%, P = 0.051). When looking at different treatment classes, more patients taking uricase therapy (57%, Supplementary Figure S3C) were highly compliant compared with patients taking oral ULTs (27%, Supplementary Figure S3B). Among patients on oral ULTs, a higher proportion managed by nephrologists, compared with rheumatologists, were highly treatment compliant (33% vs 24%, P = 0.060). There was no difference in compliance among patients managed by nephrologists or rheumatologists taking uricase therapy (Supplementary Figure S3C). Most rheumatologists and nephrologists, (68% and 56%, respectively, P = 0.380) stated that lack of compliance was likely driven by patient forgetfulness (Supplementary Figure S4).

Most rheumatologists and nephrologists were extremely satisfied with allopurinol, with more rheumatologists expressing satisfaction with allopurinol compared with nephrologists (82% vs 62%, P=0.011). Most physicians also reported being extremely satisfied with febuxostat (76% and 64%, respectively, P = 0.433). Fewer physicians expressed extreme satisfaction with pegloticase monotherapy (38% and 47%, respectively, P ≥ 0.999) or combination therapy (52% and 54%, respectively, P ≥ 0.999). Probenecid had the lowest satisfaction rates among rheumatologists and nephrologists (12% and 20%, respectively, P ≥ 0.999) (Supplementary Figure S5).

Treatment drivers

The primary treatment goals identified by both rheumatologists and nephrologists were to reduce SU, decrease the frequency of severe adverse events, and improve patients’ QoL (Supplementary Figure S6). Rheumatologists placed greater emphasis than nephrologists on ensuring treatment adherence (80% vs 60%, P = 0.037). Additionally, rheumatologists placed greater emphasis than nephrologists on selecting therapies suitable for patients with comorbidities (78% and 64%, respectively, P = 0.435) and on minimizing contraindications (71% and 53%, respectively, P = 0.132).

Among surveyed physicians, 32% reported that their patients were significantly impacted by the burden of current gout treatments, including lifestyle impacts, side effects, and dosing challenges (Figure 3). Many nephrologists (21%) and rheumatologists (15%) strongly agreed that “there is a significant medical need for uncontrolled gout treatments that result in quicker tophi resolution” (P ≥ 0.999). Many rheumatologists (19%) and nephrologists (23%) also strongly agreed with the statement “there is a significant medical need for more efficacious treatments for uncontrolled gout” (P ≥ 0.999). Both specialties highlighted gaps in the standard of care for uncontrolled gout (37% of rheumatologists and 33% of nephrologists, P ≥ 0.999) and agreed on the need for better treatments for uncontrolled gout (20%) and for patients with comorbidities such as chronic liver disease (CLD) (26%), chronic heart failure (CHF) (26%), and CKD (24%) (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

Uncontrolled gout is a progressive and systemic disease characterized by severe pain, disability, long‐term complications, and increased morbidity, and is often associated with comorbidities such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, CHF, CLD, and CKD. 8 , 10 , 11 Because of the complex nature of the disease, rheumatologists and nephrologists are both involved in its management. We described current perceptions of uncontrolled gout management and treatment experiences from two distinct physician specialties: rheumatology and nephrology. The unique perspectives of each specialty on the disease highlight both similarities and differences that may offer valuable insights into current management practices for managing this condition.

Despite currently available treatment options, patients with uncontrolled gout continue to experience a high disease burden, even when adhering to recommended lifestyle changes and medication regimens. 11 We found a high disease burden among patients with uncontrolled gout, even among those that have been on multiple treatments, some for an extended period of time. These burdens are reflected in clinical presentations (eg, high SU levels), frequent active gout symptoms (eg, gout flares, tophi, and joint pain), and challenges with treatment compliance. Overall disease burden is further amplified as the majority of patients in this study presented with comorbidities. The comorbidities in our study generally align with those of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey in 2007 to 2008. 8

The high disease burden of gout and comorbidities leads to increased health care resource utilization for patients with uncontrolled gout. Notably, patients managed by nephrologists experienced higher rates of gout‐related hospitalizations and more frequent ED visits. This may in part be because of the disease burden experienced by patients with uncontrolled gout and severe CKD, which are managed with greater frequency by nephrologists compared with rheumatologists.

Rheumatologists and nephrologists often collaborate and share information on both disease and treatment, as both specialties are important in the management of patients with uncontrolled gout. 12 In the current study, there were many similarities in perceived disease burden and treatment, with primary treatment goals of lowering SU, decreasing severe adverse events, and improving patients’ overall QoL. Almost all physicians from both specialties agreed that patients were at least moderately affected in all aspects of both work and social life because of their uncontrolled gout. Both specialties similarly agreed on the need for more effective therapies to manage uncontrolled gout and its associated comorbidities.

Conversely, there were notable differences between both specialties in their management and treatment of patients with uncontrolled gout. For example, SU was monitored at every patient visit with a greater frequency by rheumatologists than nephrologists, similar to other experiences among rheumatologists. 13 Additionally, although the majority of both rheumatologists and nephrologists treated their patients with allopurinol, a greater proportion of patients managed by rheumatologists received ≥300 mg/day of allopurinol compared with patients managed by nephrologists. The lower allopurinol dosing by nephrologists may relate to the higher incidence of CKD and concomitant diuretic use among these physicians. A concern about a greater risk of allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome in CKD likely explains why nephrologists may be more cautious when prescribing higher doses of allopurinol for patients. The higher frequency of SU monitoring and the increased allopurinol dosing observed among rheumatologists may stem from their greater experience in managing patients with uncontrolled gout.

Another difference between the two specialties was the treatment compliance of patients managed by rheumatologists versus nephrologists. The majority of patients overall were moderately treatment compliant; however, a higher proportion of patients managed by nephrologists exhibited high treatment compliance compared with those managed by rheumatologists for all treatment classes and for oral ULTs. Interestingly, rheumatologists put more value on ensuring treatment adherence, possibly because of the decreased compliance observed by this group of physicians. There was no difference in treatment compliance between the two specialties for uricase therapy, and patients taking uricase therapy were generally more highly treatment compliant compared with those on oral ULTs.

Physicians, particularly nephrologists, stated that they were less likely to prescribe treatment that required a pretreatment such as immunosuppression. Notably, most patients with uncontrolled gout typically take three or more medications to control their gout symptoms. 14 This treatment burden could be reduced if patients’ uncontrolled gout symptoms are properly controlled, thus decreasing the overall impact of treatment burden.

Although both rheumatologists and nephrologists stated that they were highly satisfied with current therapies such as allopurinol or febuxostat, they also expressed that there were significant gaps in today's standard of care for current available treatments for uncontrolled gout. Physicians expressed dissatisfaction with current therapies’ ability to resolve tophi and to reduce gout flares quickly. They also emphasized the need for treatments targeting uncontrolled gout, particularly in patients with comorbidities such as CLD, CHF, and CKD.

Overall, the study findings cannot be generalized beyond the population of physicians and patients included in this study who were not randomly selected. Additional study‐related limitations include (1) the inclusion of only patients currently receiving treatment for gout‐related symptoms, excluding those with uncontrolled gout who were not on medication 6 ; (2) the potential underestimation of disease burden because of survivorship bias, as patients who died from severe complications were excluded as the study focused on recently or currently treated patients; (3) the inability of some physicians to provide insights from their patients’ initial gout diagnosis, given the chronic nature of gout and the potential transition of care; (4) although physicians were asked to extract medical record data from patients who met the criteria and who they had seen most recently, there was no guarantee that patients medical records were chosen according to guidelines; and (5) the overrepresentation of patients with uncontrolled gout in the study sample (whereas 2%–6% of patients with gout are estimated to have uncontrolled gout). 13 , 15 Finally, comparison of treatment patterns between rheumatologists and nephrologists is confounded by the different case mix of these two specialties and among the actual physicians included.

In summary, rheumatologists favored higher medication doses and more frequent SU monitoring, whereas nephrologists managed more patients with CKD with a higher health care resource utilization. Both specialties agreed on the need for improved treatments for uncontrolled gout.
