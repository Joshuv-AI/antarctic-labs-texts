Modeling two-strain competition with reinfection: Mathematical analyses and epidemiological implications
Authors: Jie Bai, Jin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe propose a new epidemic model for the interactions between two different strains associated with the same pathogen. Our focus is how the competition between multiple strains and the impact of reinfection shape the transmission, spread, and progression of the disease. We analyze the dynamical behavior of this mathematical model, especially the existence of various equilibrium points and their stability properties. In addition, we conduct a detailed numerical study, utilizing publicly available COVID data to calibrate the model. Our simulation results reveal complex patterns in the interplay between the emerging and outgoing strains and highlight the importance of effective control for both the primary infection and reinfection routes of the emerging strain.Keywords: Mathematical modeling, Multiple strains, Reinfection, COVID-19

Introduction

Human infections caused by pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites, etc.) frequently involve multiple strains of the same pathogen species. Strains represent genetic variants or subtypes of a pathogenic microorganism that may differ substantially in traits such as immunogenicity, infectivity, virulence, growth rate, and drug resistance. Despite their high prevalence, the importance and clinical relevance of many multiple-strain infections are still unclear (Balmer & Tanner, 2011; Cyranoski, 2021). Mathematical modeling, as a theoretical tool for epidemiological research, could offer valuable insight into the competition, mutualism, and evolution of different strains and improve our understanding of disease dynamics and host-pathogen interactions (He et al., 2025; Hethcote, 2000; Li et al., 2025; Martcheva & Pilyugin, 2006; van den Driessche & Watmough, 2002; Wang, 2020; Wang, 2022; Xavier et al., 2022; Yang & Wang, 2021).

Numerous mathematical models have been published for infectious diseases with multiple strains (Cheng et al., 2021; Fudolig & Howard, 2020; Lazebnik & Bunimovich-Mendrazitsky, 2022; Li et al., 2021; Martcheva, 2009; Martcheva & Pilyugin, 2006; Minayev & Ferguson, 2009; Murall et al., 2014; Poolman et al., 2008; Rong et al., 2007, 2012; Saucedo & Martcheva, 2017; Wang, 2022; Wang & Wang, 2017; Yang & Wang, 2018) For example, Minayev and Ferguson developed a set of relatively simple deterministic models for the transmission dynamics of multi-strain pathogens in order to provide improved biological realism regarding the interaction between epidemiological and evolutionary dynamics (Minayev & Ferguson, 2009). Martcheva proposed a non-autonomous multi-strain epidemic model with periodic coefficients and conducted a detailed analysis, including the well-posedness and stability properties of the solution in general, and the uniform persistence and strain coexistence in particular for a two-strain version of the model (Martcheva, 2009). Rong et al. investigated Hepatitis C virus infection based on a two-strain mathematical model, which incorporated both the wild-type and drug-resistant variants, and examined the effects of backward mutation and liver cell proliferation on the preexistence of the mutant virus and the competition between the variants (Rong et al., 2012). Murall et al. modeled Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection and examined which HPV type interactions and their immune responses produced qualitatively similar patterns frequently observed in patients (Murall et al., 2014).

During the past few years, many modeling studies have been devoted to COVID-19. SARS-CoV-2, the causative agent of COVID-19, has gone through a great number of mutations since its original strain was reported in December 2019. Several variants, such as Alpha (B.1.1.7), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1), Delta (B.1.617.2), and Omicron (B.1.1.529), led to large waves of infection and were classified as ‘variants of concern’ by WHO (World Health Organizationb). Using a two-strain model, Li et al. evaluated the impact of SARS-CoV-2 variants on the pandemic progression and social restoration and estimated related vaccine effectiveness (Li et al., 2022). Massard et al. showed that the Alpha, Beta and Gamma variants were more transmissible than the original viral strain based on a multi-strain modeling study (Massard et al., 2022). Thompson et al. found that a novel variant needs to be more transmissible than previously circulating variants to invade a population, if previous SARS-CoV-2 infection leads to complete cross-reactive immunity (Thompson et al., 2022). Tang et al. proposed a COVID-19 model that incorporated multiple viral strains with both symptomatic and asymptomatic transmission routes and investigated the emergence of the Omicron variant in the US (Gao et al., 2023). Other multi-strain COVID-19 modeling studies include but are not limited to (Arruda et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2023; de León et al., 2022; Khyar & Allali, 2020; Marquioni & de Aguiar, 2021).

Despite these efforts, our quantitative understanding of the infection dynamics involving multiple strains remains insufficient. Empirical studies have shown that compared to the original strain, newer variants could be more transmissible and aggressive, less susceptible to vaccines, and faster in developing drug resistance (Cyranoski, 2021; Diaz Caballero et al., 2023). However, the impacts of such differences on pathogen spread and epidemic/pandemic progression, particularly disease incidence, prevalence, and attack rates, have not been adequately investigated. Meanwhile, infection and recovery from one strain typically conveys immunity for the same strain but does not necessarily protect the host against other strains, especially those newer variants (Özüdoğru et al., 2023). How to quantify the reinfection dynamics due to the mutation and evolution of pathogens, then, is of critical importance toward better understanding their epidemics and endemism and designing more effective control measures.

In the present paper, we propose a mathematical model based on differential equations to describe the interactions between two different strains associated with an infectious disease. The two strains are distinguished by different rates in transmission, recovery, and disease-induced mortality. A special emphasis is placed on the impact of reinfection, where individuals who recover from infection of one strain will be susceptible to the other strain. Additionally, we incorporate vital dynamics that may include migration and natural births and deaths. Combining these factors, this model enables us to study the interplay between multiple transmission modes, competition of different strains, impact of reinfection, and demographic effects.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The model is described in Section 2. Main mathematical results are summarized in Section 3, with detailed proofs provided in the Appendix. Data fitting and simulation results are presented in Section 4, followed by conclusion and discussion in Section 5.

Model formulation

We propose a mathematical model that incorporates the competition between two strains and the impact of reinfection associated with a pathogen of our concern. We divide the human population into the following compartments: the susceptible individuals (denoted by S), the individuals infected by strain j (denoted by Ij), and the individuals recovered from the infection by strain j (denoted by Rj), where j = 1, 2. We assume that recovered individuals in the compartment Rj are protected from reinfection by the same strain j, but are still at risk for reinfection from the other strain.

The model is then described as follows:

Susceptible individuals enter the population at an influx rate Λ and contract the disease from strain 1 and strain 2, respectively, with primary infection rates β1 and β2. Infected individuals from strain 1 and strain 2 have, respectively, recovery rates γ1 and γ2 and disease-induced mortality rates w1 and w2. Individuals recovered from strain-1 infection may contract the disease again from strain 2 with a reinfection rate β~2, and those recovered from strain-2 infection may do so from strain 1 with a reinfection rate β~1. Standard bilinear incidence is used to represent both the primary infection and reinfection. In addition, individuals have a natural death rate μ. All these model parameters are positive constants.

Many empirical studies (Chen et al., 2024; Guedes et al., 2023; Pilz et al., 2022) have found that the reinfection rate is typically lower than the primary infection rate of the same pathogen (particularly, SARS-CoV-2) due to the effects of natural immunity. We thus assume that β~j<βj (j = 1, 2) in our model.

We also emphasize that, for simplicity, out model does not consider the impact of waning immunity. Consequently, the immunity is primarily treated as a static stratification in the sense that individuals recovered from the infection will have permanent immunity against the same pathogen strain, though they are subject to reinfection by the other strain.

We make a comment regarding the epidemiological implication of condition (3.6). The left-hand side of this condition represents the combined effect of cross-strain transmission (with both primary infection and reinfection), normalized by the product of the two recovery rates. The first term on the right-hand side characterizes the effect of strain-1 transmission that includes primary infection and reinfection, normalized by its recovery rate and the disease outbreak risk contributed by strain 1. A similar interpretation applies to the second term on the right-hand side. Note that in a practical sense, the recovery rate is typically much larger than the natural death rate and the disease-induced mortality rate, thus a ≈ γ1 and b ≈ γ2. Overall, condition (3.6) shows that when the normalized cross-strain transmission strength is weaker than the total of the two normalized strain-specific transmission strengths, system (2.1) has a unique positive equilibrium indicating co-existence between the two strains. On the other hand, if the cross-strain transmission effect is sufficiently strong so that condition (3.6) is not satisfied, the dominant strain may completely exclude and replace the other strain, effectively eliminating the co-existence state.

In what follows, we will use numerical simulation to illustrate these mathematical results, and will use a realistic case study to provide deeper insight into the complex dynamics of this model.

Simulation results

Equilibria and stability properties

We first numerically verify our analytical predictions concerning the various equilibrium points and their stability properties, as presented in Section 3. To that end, we run numerical simulation to system (2.1) with a number of different initial conditions, where each initial condition determines a solution that represents an orbit in the phase diagram. We then plot these solution trajectories over time to examine their behavior, which would indicate the stability properties of relevant equilibrium points.

Fig. 1 illustrates the phase diagrams of I1 vs. I2 in four typical scenarios: (a) R01<1, R02<1, where all orbits approach the DFE at I1 = I2 = 0; (b) R01>1, R02<1, where the solutions converge to the strain-1 boundary equilibrium with I1 > 0 and I2 = 0; (c) R01<1, R02>1, where the solutions converge to the strain-2 boundary equilibrium with I1 = 0 and I2 > 0; (d) R01>1, R02>1, where there exists an interior equilibrium with I1 > 0 and I2 > 0 that attracts at least some of the orbits. Note that not all the solution trajectories associated with the given initial conditions are shown in these figures. The observation in Fig. 1(a) confirms the conclusion in Theorem 3.3. Meanwhile, Fig. 1(b) and (c) not only demonstrate Theorem 3.4 but also implies a stronger result that these boundary equilibria are indeed globally asymptotically stable under their respective conditions. Additionally, the parameter setting in Fig. 1(d) satisfies condition (3.6) so that system (2.1) has a unique positive interior equilibrium, which is consistent with Theorem 3.5.

Application to COVID data

During the last few years, several variants of SARS-CoV-2 led to large waves of infection. As a real-world application of our study, we will fit our model (2.1) to SARS-CoV-2 variants data. We consider the US data for the period from late 2021 to early 2022, which represents the transition from the dominance of the Delta strain to the emergence and dominance of the Omicron strain.

Specifically, we focus on the COVID data for the state of Tennessee in the US. We make use of two primary datasets for the period from November 13, 2021 to April 16, 2022: (1) The variants data from CDC (Centers for Disease Control and), which provided weekly reports for the variant proportions in Tennessee; (2) The COVID cases data from Tennessee Department of Health (Tennessee Department of Health), which provided daily reports for the number of new cases and active cases in Tennessee.

We represent Omicron as strain 1 in our model, which includes all its sub-variants such as B.1.1.529, BA.1.1, BA.2, and BA.5 reported in the variants data. Strain 2 in our model mainly contains Delta (B.1.617.2). Additionally, there were a few other strains which had very small proportions out of the total infection during this time period. For simplicity, we aggregate all these variants with Delta into strain 2.

Combining the two datasets described above, we are able to determine the number of active cases associated with strain 1 and strain 2 throughout this period, which will be used to fit the variables I1 and I2 in our model. For parameter calibration, we will fix the values of the natural death rate, population influx rate, recovery rates, and disease-induced mortality rates from published sources (see Section 4.4 for detailed explanations). The values for the transmission rates β1, β2, β~1, and β~2, however, are not available in the literature since they typically vary by locations, populations, specific strains, and time periods. Hence, we will use data fitting to estimate these four transmission rates.

Before we present the fitting results, we conduct an identifiability analysis below for the model parameters.

Data fitting

We now proceed to model calibration and parameter estimation by fitting our model (2.1) simultaneously to the two COVID datasets (variant proportions and cases) described in Section 4.2.

Previous empirical and modeling studies indicated that different strains of SARS-CoV-2, particularly Delta and Omicron, mainly differed by their transmission rates involved in the primary infection and reinfection (Chen et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2023; Guedes et al., 2023; Massard et al., 2022). We will thus focus on the four transmission rates β1, β2, β~1, and β~2, and estimate their values through data fitting. For simplicity, we assume in this study that strain 1 and strain 2 have the same recovery rate γ1 = γ2 = 1/14 per day based on an average recovery period of 2 weeks for COVID-19 (World Health Organizationa), and the same disease-induced mortality rate ω1 = ω2 = 7.5∗10−4 per day based on an average from the estimates of three states in the US (Chen et al., 2024). The US has an average life expectancy of approximately 76 years in 2021 (Arias et al., 2022). Thus, we set the natural birth and death rate μ = 1/(76 years) = 1/(76∗365) per day in our model. In addition, we set the population influx rate as Λ = μ∗N where N = 7.05 × 106 is the population size of Tennessee during 2021-2022 (Wikipedia: Demographics of Tennessee). We note from the fitted values that β1>β~1 and β2>β~2, consistent with our assumption that each primary infection rate is larger than the respective reinfection rate. Also, β1 > β2, indicating that strain 1 (Omicron) was more transmissible and aggressive than strain 2 (mainly Delta) in this time period. This indicates that reinfection of Omicron could play a significant role in shaping the COVID epidemics during this period, whereas the role of Delta reinfection would be mostly minor. These results are consistent with prior empirical findings (Chen et al., 2024; Guedes et al., 2023).

Evaluating equation (3.3) with the fitted parameter values, we obtain R01≈3.5738 and R02≈1.1415 for the reproduction numbers of strain 1 and strain 2, respectively. This clearly shows that R0≈3.5738>1 in Tennessee, a quantification for the persistence of the COVID epidemics. Meanwhile, the fact R01>R02 indicates again that Omicron played a more important role than Delta (and other variants) in the period of our interest.

The basic reproduction number can be used to estimate the herd immunity threshold (HIT), which represents the critical proportion of the host population needed to be immune in order to contain the disease. This threshold may be evaluated by a simple formula (Garnett GP, 2005): HIT=1−1R0. Based on our data fitting result for R0 in Tennessee, we obtain HIT ≈72%. As of November 2021, about 55% of the population in Tennessee received at least one dose of COVID vaccines and about 49% of the population were fully vaccinated (Tennessee Department of Health). These ratios were well below the estimated 72% HIT. This fact, combined with the strong capability of Omicron for immune evasion, may explain the large infection wave during this period. We comment, however, that the actual immunity level of this population may be difficult to assess, given the presence of multiple strains with different infectivity and the limited length of protection conferred by COVID vaccination as well as recovery from the infection.

Using the fitted parameter values, we can easily verify that the co-existence condition (3.6) is indeed satisfied in this case, indicating that there is a unique positive equilibrium. Numerically, we found this equilibrium is approximately located at (S∗,I1∗,I2∗,R1∗,R2∗)≈(1871900,2645,245,4898800,216200). Note that at this equilibrium, the number of strain-1 active cases is more than 10 times larger than that of strain-2 active cases, and the number of strain-1 recovered cases is more than 20 times larger than that of strain-2 recovered cases.

To quantify the contributions of strain 1 and strain 2, as well as the contributions from the primary infection and reinfection, we now compare the incidence and attack rates between these different components of our model.

Fig. 5(a) compares the primary infection incidence of strain 1, represented by β1SI1 in our model, and the reinfection incidence of strain 1, represented by β~1R2I1. Parameter variation

Our estimates of the four transmission rates β1, β2, β~1, and β~2 from data fitting are regarded as the baseline values for these parameters (see Table 1). The sensitivity analysis in Section 4.5 shows that most of these parameters (except the strain-2 reinfection rate β~2) are sensitive. To further demonstrate the impact of the various transmission rates on model outcomes, we conduct parameter variation tests on β1, β2, β~1, and β~2. For each test, we perturb one transmission rate by −50%, −25%, +25%, and +50%, respectively, in reference to its base value, while fixing all other model parameters at their base values. We then run the model and generate hypothetical simulation curves for each state variable on the same time interval from November 13, 2021 to April 16, 2022. Figs. 7–10 display these results (see Appendix B).

We observe from Fig. 7 a strong positive correlation between the strain-1 primary infection rate β1 and the two state variables directly related to strain 1; i.e., the active cases I1 and recovered cases R1. The increase or decrease of β1 leads to substantial changes of I1 and R1 in the same direction. In contrast, the two state variables directly related to strain 2; i.e., the active cases I2 and recovered cases R2, appear to have a negative correlation with β1. When β1 is reduced, I2 and R2 increase due to a weaker contribution from strain 1 (and, thus a stronger contribution from strain 2) toward the overall infection dynamics within the host population. Similarly, Fig. 8 shows a strong positive correlation between the strain-2 primary infection rate β2 and the two strain-2 variables I2 and R2, and a negative correlation between β2 and the two strain-1 variables I1 and R1. The variation of the strain-1 reinfection rate β~1 in Fig. 9 shows qualitatively the same patterns as that of β1 in Fig. 7, though at a lower level of quantitative changes. Finally, the variation results of the strain-2 reinfection rate β~2 in Fig. 10 reinforce our previous observations that β~2 exhibits a very low sensitivity and would have insignificant impact on model outcomes.

Discussion

Through mutation and evolution, many pathogens continuously generate novel strains that may pose a threat to public health. In this article, we have proposed a new mathematical model to investigate the population dynamics of an infectious disease with two simultaneously circulating strains. These strains compete against each other for the ability to transmit and spread within the host population. Our model incorporates the interplay between different strains, the impact of reinfection, and the presence of multiple transmission modes. We have analyzed essential dynamical behavior, including the existence of various types of equilibrium solutions and their stability properties, of this model based on theories of differential equations and dynamical systems. We have paid a special attention to the condition that leads to a unique positive equilibrium representing the co-existence between the two strains, and have conducted a detailed numerical investigation of this co-existence condition. In our simulation study, which is the focus of this paper, we have calibrated our model with real data and carried out a range of numerical simulations. Our work emphasizes a holistic understanding of the complex dynamics involved in multi-strain pathogen transmission.

Using publicly available data, we have demonstrated a real-world application of our model to the COVID epidemics in the US state of Tennessee from November 2021 to April 2022. This period marked the presence of two major SARS-CoV-2 variants, where the Delta strain was outgoing and the Omicron strain was emerging. We have carefully compared and quantified the contribution from each strain and the significance of each transmission route. Our data fitting and numerical simulation clearly showed that Omicron was more transmissible and aggressive, exhibited much higher incidence and attack rate, and had substantially higher risk of reinfection, in comparison to Delta. Meanwhile, we found that both the primary infection and reinfection of Omicron played a significant role in shaping the epidemic wave during this period. On the other hand, the impact of reinfection from Delta appeared to be negligible, though its primary infection route still made a considerable contribution to the overall disease prevalence. These results are consistent with previous empirical findings.

An emerging strain (particularly, Omicron) typically possesses a competitive advantage due to its higher transmission rate, stronger infectivity (reflected by the higher incidence and attack rates from both the primary infection and reinfection), and increased immune evasion, in reference to an outgoing strain (particularly, Delta). The findings from this work highlight the importance of effective prevention and intervention for such emergent pathogen strains. Strategic deployment of vaccines, especially updated vaccines and boosters targeting the most prevalent variants at the time, would be one of the most effective disease control measures. Meanwhile, non-pharmaceutical interventions such as hygiene and sanitation, social distancing, and education campaigns and awareness programs to promote behavioral changes, are also very useful for the management of disease outbreaks. All these approaches can effectively reduce the transmission rates for the circulating pathogens, especially their emergent strains. As illustrated by the hypothetical simulation results in, Figs 7 and 9 a 25% decrease of the strain-1 primary infection rate and reinfection rate would lead to a reduction of about 33% and 16%, respectively, in the disease prevalence. Additionally, the figures show that the reduction of the transmission rates could significantly delay the onset of the infection peak, allowing more time for the management and control of the emergent strain.

There are a few limitations in this study. Our model does not have latent/exposed compartments to represent individuals who are infected but not yet infectious, and does not distinguish asymptomatic and symptomatic infectious individuals. The effect of waning immunity is not considered; instead, our model assumes that recovery from infection confers permanent immunity against the same strain, which may not be realistic. The rationale is to formulate system (2.1) as a parsimonious model with a minimal number of compartments so as to focus on the major motivations of this research - the competition between different strains and the impact of reinfection. Moreover, vaccinated individuals are not explicitly accounted for in this model; instead, the impact of vaccination is reflected (implicitly) by the primary infection and reinfection rates, whose values are fitted from real data. It would be a meaningful future effort to expand the current model and explicitly include additional features such as infection latency, asymptomatic infection, waning immunity, and vaccination. Incorporation of these factors will likely change the dynamical properties of the model, particularly the strain co-existence condition. It may also be possible to extend this modeling framework to represent the interactions between three or more different strains that are concurrently circulating.

For simplicity, we have assumed that all model parameters are constants, though practically these parameters, especially the transmission rates, would most likely change with the progression of the epidemic and the interaction between different strains. Incorporating time-dependent parameters could make the model more realistic, but would also add challenges for model calibration and data fitting. An integration between mechanistic modeling and machine learning may help to overcome such challenges.
