Influence of Dark Matter on the Formation of Biogenic Elements in Early Universe Stars
L. Yıldız, D. Kaykı, M. F. Ciappina

Abstract
We demonstrate that dark matter interactions can profoundly influence stellar nucleosynthesis in the early universe by altering thermodynamic gradients and modifying nuclear reaction rates within primordial stars. Incorporating a dark matter–modified Fermi-Dirac distribution and accounting for localized energy injection from annihilation heating, our model predicts enhanced production of carbon and nitrogen alongside reduced oxygen synthesis. These compositional shifts significantly reshape stellar structure and produce synthetic spectra that closely reproduce the observed characteristics of carbon-enhanced metal-poor (CEMP) stars. Our findings reveal a direct and previously overlooked role of dark matter in driving the chemical evolution of the early cosmos, offering a plausible link between fundamental particle physics and observable astrophysical signatures.



Introduction-
Understanding the formation of biogenic elements such as carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen in first-generation stars remains a central challenge in modern astrophysics. This question lies at the intersection of nuclear astrophysics, stellar evolution, and cosmological structure formation. While standard stellar nucleosynthesis models describe equilibrium burning processes in primordial stars
Heger and Woosley 2002
;
Nomoto et al. 2013
, observations of carbon-enhanced metal-poor (CEMP) stars show elemental abundance patterns that deviate significantly from theoretical expectations
Beers and Christlieb 2005
;
Frebel and Johnson 2015
. In particular, the anomalously high carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) ratios observed in CEMP-no stars—those lacking neutron-capture enhancements—are difficult to reconcile with canonical stellar evolution models
Beers and Christlieb 2005
;
Frebel and Johnson 2015
;
Meynet et al. 2006
. At the same time, the nature of dark matter remains one of the most profound open problems in physics
Bertone et al. 2005
. Dark matter accounts for approximately 85% of the universe’s matter content, yet its fundamental interactions with ordinary (baryonic) matter remain largely unknown and experimentally unconstrained. While its gravitational role in structure formation is well established, the influence of dark matter within stellar interiors—especially during the early universe—has yet to be fully explored
Iocco et al. 2007
;
Spolyar et al. 2008
. Recent theoretical studies suggest that Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) could have been gravitationally captured in the cores of metal-free stars, particularly in overdense mini-halos. Once thermalized, annihilation processes may inject energy into the core, altering thermodynamic conditions and shifting the onset of nuclear burning
Iocco et al. 2007
;
Spolyar et al. 2008
;
Taoso et al. 2008
.
In this work, we examine the possibility that dark matter contributes to stellar evolution not only gravitationally but also thermodynamically. Building upon prior work on dark stars and annihilation heating
Spolyar et al. 2008
;
Freese et al. 2009
, we introduce a framework that incorporates quantum statistical effects—such as modifications to the Fermi–Dirac distribution—and their feedback on nucleosynthetic pathways. By modifying the gravitational potential, statistical occupation functions, and energy generation terms, we explore how dark matter may alter nucleosynthesis in the first stars in the universe—known as Population III stars. Our model reproduces key abundance features observed in CEMP stars and offers a self-consistent explanation for several long-standing anomalies in early stellar chemistry
Heger and Woosley 2002
. We also identify nucleosynthetic outcomes that are sensitive to dark matter profile and annihilation parameters, suggesting that stellar chemical abundances may serve as indirect probes of dark matter microphysics
Ilie et al. 2012
;
Casanellas and Lopes 2011
. By focusing on low-mass primordial stars that may have survived until the present epoch, this study bridges the physics of the early universe with modern observational searches for ancient stellar populations in the Galactic halo
Starkenburg et al. 2017
.

Methods
-To explore the theoretical framework outlined previously, we have developed a robust computational simulation capable of solving the modified stellar structure equations under early-universe conditions. Our numerical model is implemented using a fully parallelized Python-based code, combining high-order numerical integration techniques with adaptive mesh refinement to resolve the strong density and temperature gradients typically found in the interiors of Population III stars. The modified Lane–Emden equation, which incorporates the additional dark matter density term, is solved using a fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator, ensuring high precision in capturing the central density variations without introducing artificial damping in regions of high curvature
Navarro et al. 1997
. To maintain conservation of mass and momentum, spatial discretization of the hydrostatic equilibrium equations is achieved via finite volume methods. In regions where rapid thermodynamic transitions are expected due to dark matter energy injection, an implicit time-stepping scheme is employed to ensure numerical stability and precision. Radiative transfer processes are modeled using a Monte Carlo-based photon transport method, which is particularly effective in the high-opacity environments, typical of stellar interiors
Rogers and Iglesias 1992
. To evaluate the impact of dark matter annihilation on local temperature gradients, we numerically couple the energy source term
Q_{\chi}
with the standard nuclear energy generation rate
\epsilon_{\text{nuc}}
, using an adaptive iterative solver
Freese et al. 2009
. This coupled approach allows for the examination of the feedback loop between energy deposition and nuclear reaction rates. Initial mass profiles, ranging from
M=0.7-1.2M_{\odot}
, were selected to represent low-mass Population III stars, which are believed to have survived into the present epoch. The radial domain of the simulation extends from the stellar center to
R=0.5R_{\odot}
, sufficient to encompass both the convective and radiative zones without significant numerical truncation.
The dark matter density profile is modeled using a generalized Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) function
Navarro et al. 1996
:

\rho_{\chi}(r)=\rho_{0}\left(\frac{r}{r_{s}}\right)^{-\gamma}\left(1+\frac{r}{r_{s}}\right)^{\gamma-\beta}.

This profile is integrated into the hydrostatic equilibrium solver via subroutines designed to compute the local gravitational potential and dark matter–induced pressure enhancements. Nuclear reaction rates are then calculated through a network of temperature-dependent nuclear processes, with a particular focus on the triple-alpha process, in which three helium nuclei fuse to form carbon under high temperature and density conditions. These processes are coupled with the modified Fermi–Dirac distribution that includes the chemical potential shift
\mu_{\chi}
, thereby enabling a full resolution of the change in state occupation probabilities as a function of radial position. The simulation output includes full radial profiles of density, pressure, temperature, luminosity, and elemental abundances. Convergence of the results is confirmed by verifying the invariance of central density and core temperature profiles to within a
10^{-6}
tolerance, ensuring the robustness of the numerical solution. All key results are cross-validated against baseline models that exclude dark matter to isolate the specific effects of dark matter-induced modifications (for more details, see the Supplementary Material (SM)).

Results and discussion-
Here, we explore how dark matter, when accumulated in stellar cores, can alter the internal structure and chemical evolution of stars. Our results indicate that the presence of dark matter in dense stellar environments introduces significant thermodynamic and nucleosynthetic modifications, which may be observable through spectral analysis of ancient stars.

Figure

\rho_{\chi}

0.2\,R_{\odot}

Such a steep central gradient has profound astrophysical implications. The accumulation of dark matter in the core region enhances the local gravitational potential, thereby modifying the hydrostatic equilibrium of the star. This gravitational amplification perturbs the thermal structure of the core, potentially altering temperature and pressure profiles, and influencing key nuclear processes such as hydrogen burning or the onset of helium fusion. In particular, the increased gravitational compression may elevate the central temperature, accelerating nuclear reaction rates and modifying the overall stellar evolution pathway. Additionally, if the dark matter consists of self-annihilating particles, the energy deposited via annihilation could provide a supplementary heat source, further disrupting the conventional balance between radiation pressure and gravity.

This amplification increases the core temperature and density, thereby enhancing the triple-alpha fusion rate, lowering the threshold for helium ignition, and initiating helium burning at an earlier evolutionary stage. As a consequence, the star progresses more rapidly through its nucleosynthetic phases, with an accelerated buildup of carbon and oxygen in the core. These structural and evolutionary changes can influence the star’s luminosity, lifetime, and fate. Notably, these effects are robust and persist across a wide range of stellar masses and initial compositions, indicating that dark matter could play a subtle yet widespread role in shaping stellar evolution.

These changes in elemental distribution represent a key observational signature of dark matter’s influence on stellar interiors, with potential implications for stellar spectroscopy, chemical evolution models, and the interpretation of abundance patterns in ancient stellar populations where dark matter densities may have been higher.

In summary, our results suggest that dark matter does not merely play a gravitational role in the cosmos but can actively shape the evolution and chemical output of stars. By modifying thermal gradients, triggering non-standard nuclear processes, and influencing observable spectra, dark matter offers a new frontier in stellar physics and astrophysical dark matter detection.

Conclusions and outlook-

Our work establishes a comprehensive theoretical and numerical framework that directly connects dark matter microphysics with observable signatures in stellar nucleosynthesis. We demonstrate that dark matter, when gravitationally accumulated in the cores of primordial stars, can profoundly alter thermal gradients, nuclear reaction rates, and equilibrium conditions. These modifications lead to enhanced carbon and helium synthesis, suppressed oxygen production, and significantly reshaped elemental abundance patterns. Our model successfully reproduces spectral features observed in extremely metal-poor halo stars—most notably elevated C/O ratios—that remain unexplained by conventional stellar evolution models
Yoon et al. 2016
;
Frebel and Johnson 2015
.

Unlike previous studies that treated dark matter merely as a background gravitational influence or as an indirect cosmological parameter
Iocco et al. 2007
, our approach integrates dark matter annihilation heating and quantum statistical effects directly into the stellar structure equations. By modifying Fermi–Dirac occupation probabilities and incorporating a localized energy deposition term
Q_{\chi}
, we construct a self-consistent bridge from particle-scale interactions to macroscopic stellar observables. This unified treatment of stellar thermodynamics, nuclear synthesis, and dark matter physics marks a departure from semi-analytical or post-processed models and defines a new class of fully coupled stellar simulations.

Future extensions of this framework include incorporating relativistic structure equations, such as the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) formalism, to explore the role of dark matter in compact stellar remnants
Leung et al. 2011
. Furthermore, varying dark matter masses and interaction cross-sections will allow for systematic exploration of the parameter space in alignment with current and forthcoming observational constraints
Vincent et al. 2013
. By linking cosmology, stellar astrophysics, and quantum field theory, this study opens a promising interdisciplinary path to uncovering the imprints of dark matter in stellar light
Spolyar et al. 2008
;
Taoso et al. 2008
.
