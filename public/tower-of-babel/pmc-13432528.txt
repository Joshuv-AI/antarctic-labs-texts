Unveiling the Microvasculature: The Index of Microcirculatory Resistance and Its Expanding Role in Cardiovascular Care
Authors: Joanna Sohn, Matthew A. Soderstrom, Ariana Ramirez, John‐Henry Dean, Raj Shah, Debanshu Roy, Kenneth Stone, Ariel Vinas, Bryan Ramsey, Charles Lin, Robert Chilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIndex of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) is a cutting‐edge, wire‐based tool that advances the capability assessment of coronary microvascular function. By utilizing distal coronary pressure and mean transit time under maximal hyperemia, IMR delivers consistent, reproducible insights into the microvasculature's dynamic health. IMR has been shown to be of particular use as a diagnostic tool in the assessment of coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) allowing for diagnostic clarification in conditions like ischemia with non‐obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), acute coronary syndrome (ACS), and post‐percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) complications. In addition to its ability to be used as a powerful diagnostic tool, it also serves as a prognostic tool, helping to predict which patients are at elevated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and mortality. With the increasing integration of artificial intelligence into the cath lab, previous sources of error in IMR, such as technical variability and hemodynamic influences, are no longer as significant of an issue and allow for greater precision in the use of this tool. This comprehensive review discusses the physiologic basis of IMR, measurement techniques, and diagnostic and prognostic capabilities of IMR, while highlighting its therapeutic potential and future applications.

The microcirculation is a complex, heterogeneous network of vessels less than ~500 µm in diameter, responsible for gas exchange, nutrient delivery, and metabolic waste removal in the myocardium [1]. While the macrovasculature primarily serves as a low resistance conduit for bulk blood delivery (accounting for only 5%−10% of total coronary resistance), the microvasculature contributes the majority of the coronary resistance (70%−95%), making it the principal determinant of myocardial blood flow under normal conditions [2, 3]. Epicardial stenoses are readily detected on angiography; however, microvascular dysfunction often remains angiographically invisible yet can significantly contribute to ischemia and adverse patient outcomes (Figure 1).

A common clinical scenario highlighting coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) involves a patient with angina or ischemic signs on stress testing who, upon invasive angiography, is found to have no significant epicardial coronary disease. This subset, those with ischemia with non‐obstructive coronary artery disease (INOCA), may represent up to 20% of patients undergoing invasive angiographic evaluation [4, 5] (Figure 2). CMD is thought to be the primary cause of those found to have INOCA. In one representative cohort of 139 patients, comprehensive invasive testing uncovered occult coronary abnormalities in ~76% of this population [4]. Specifically, 44% were found to have endothelial dysfunction (vasospasm with intracoronary [IC] acetylcholine administration), 21% had elevated microvascular resistance (IMR > 25%), 58% had myocardial bridging, while only 23% were found to have entirely normal findings [4]. Data such as these highlight that the majority of patients with INOCA have evidence of CMD accounting for up to 50%−60% of cases and contributing to persistent angina, reduced quality of life, and elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular events [2, 6, 7]. IMR—Physiology and Clinical Importance

IMR is an invasive quantitative measure of coronary microvascular function, derived from Ohm's law (pressure=flow×resistance or resistance=pressureflow). In practice, IMR is calculated as the distal coronary pressure (Pd) multiplied by the mean transit time (Tmn) of a standard IC saline bolus during maximal hyperemia [8]. This allows for isolation of microvascular resistance (reflecting arteriolar tone, structural remodeling, and endothelial function) by accounting for distal pressure and flow independently of epicardial disease [8, 9]. One difficulty in measuring these parameters, however, is that the values can be highly influenced by neurohormonal activation, metabolic demands, and comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia), all of which may alter arteriolar caliber and capillary recruitment, ultimately impacting IMR (Figure 3) [2]. Under hyperemic conditions, a normal IMR < 25 units (in the absence of obstructive coronary disease), IMR values > 25 are generally regarded as indicative of CMD [4]. In the setting of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), an IMR cutoff of > 40 units is often used, where higher microvascular resistance reflects severe microvascular injury (likely due to edema and/or microembolization) and portends worse outcomes [10, 11]. By providing a detailed assessment of the coronary microcirculation, IMR complements traditional epicardial‐focused indices like fractional flow reserve (FFR)/iFR and combined indices like coronary flow reserve (CFR), allowing for more precision in the localization of ischemia and lesions, whether they be epicardial versus microvascular, in patients with anginal symptoms.

Clinical Significance of IMR in Ischemic Heart Disease

Over the past two decades, IMR has emerged as an increasingly useful and reproducible tool in the evaluation of coronary microvascular health. Unlike CFR, which is affected by both epicardial and microvascular factors, IMR specifically targets the microcirculatory component, this is of particular value in the evaluation of entities like INOCA and MINOCA. In patients with INOCA, an elevated IMR > 25 units is diagnostic of CMD and found in up to 40%–60% of INOCA cases [2, 9]. IMR has powerful prognostic ability in these clinical entities. Elevated IMR in MINOCA has been shown to lead to a twofold increased risk of developing heart failure [12]. In conditions like stress cardiomyopathy, transient CMD (IMR > 40) has been shown to contribute to increased apical ballooning, and in myocarditis, IMR reflects inflammatory microvascular injury, with values > 30 units linked to worse left ventricular recovery [12, 13]. IMR‐guided diagnosis in INOCA allows clinicians to initiate targeted therapies (vasodilators, beta‐blockers, or other metabolic agents) to help alleviate anginal symptoms. The CorMicA trial demonstrated that invasive identification of CMD followed by tailored medical therapy (calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, or ranolazine as clinically appropriate) led to significant improvements in anginal symptoms and quality of life compared to the placebo/control group [6]. To effectively guide these tailored therapies, it is crucial to phenotype patients into specific clinical endotypes. While adenosine testing (deriving IMR and CFR) identifies structural microvascular dysfunction, the addition of provocation testing with acetylcholine evaluates for endothelium‐dependent or vasomotor dysfunction (e.g., epicardial or microvascular vasospasm). By combining these invasive assessments, clinicians can accurately categorize patients into distinct diagnoses such as microvascular angina, vasospastic angina, mixed INOCA, or non‐cardiac chest pain (Table 1).

In addition to its ability to aid in prognostication and guidance of therapeutic options in INOCA/MINOCA, IMR is also a powerful tool in obstructive CAD. Elevated IMR values in this setting have been shown to increase the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), reduce event‐free survival in stable CAD, and adverse cardiac remodeling in ACS [9, 10, 14]. While primary percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) are highly successful in restoring angiographically normal coronary flow, CMD may still be present and can blunt myocardial recovery.

Several pivotal studies have evaluated the use of IMR measured immediately post‐primary PCI in the setting of acute ST‐segment myocardial infarction (STEMI) (Table 2) [10, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Notably, these trials demonstrate a strong correlation between elevated IMR and structural microvascular damage. Studies by Carrick et al., De Maria et al., and Maznyczka et al. have utilized cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging to show that higher IMR values accurately predict the presence and extent of microvascular obstruction (MVO) and subsequent myocardial hemorrhage [8, 10, 17]. Furthermore, the AMICRO study highlighted that elevated IMR measured continuously is a strong predictor of lack of left ventricular functional improvement after anterior STEMI [18].

Beyond imaging correlates, this microvascular injury translates directly to worse long‐term clinical outcomes. In patients treated with primary PCI, and post‐PCI IMR value of > 40 units is associated with increased incidence of heart failure and mortality at 1‐2 year follow‐up compared to those with IMR < 40 units [9]. (Figure 4). Another retrospective study again showed an elevated hazard ratio (HR) of 4.5, risk for MACE in STEMI patients [20]. Another large study in those with STEMI again found elevated IMR values to be linked to worse outcomes. Patients with IMR > 40 units were found to have a 2.8‐fold increased risk of mortality, and while IMR was an independent predictor of cardiac death, CFR was not [19]. In addition to ACS, IMR has also been shown to be linked to adverse outcomes in all comers, including stable CAD. A recent meta‐analysis confirmed these findings, with a HR for MACE in patients with elevated IMR of 2.73 (95% CI: 2.16–3.45) across diverse CAD populations, underscoring a consistent threefold increased risk [21] (Figure 5).

This prospective, multicenter, international study investigated the prognostic significance of the Index of Microcirculatory Resistance (IMR) in 253 patients with stable coronary artery disease following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Patients were stratified based on post‐PCI IMR values, with IMR > 40 used as a threshold to define abnormal microvascular function. Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with IMR > 40 had significantly lower event‐free survival, both in terms of all‐cause death and rehospitalization for heart failure. The trial highlighted IMR as a reproducible and independent marker of microvascular dysfunction, providing prognostic value beyond traditional angiographic assessments. Importantly, elevated IMR was associated with poor long‐term outcomes despite successful epicardial revascularization, underscoring the role of microcirculation in influencing post‐PCI prognosis. These findings support the clinical relevance of routine microvascular assessment using IMR in the cardiac catheterization laboratory to improve risk stratification and guide post‐PCI patient management. From a therapeutic standpoint, IMR's role extends to guiding interventions and assessing novel therapies. In the catheterization laboratory, if a STEMI patient had a high IMR value post‐PCI (e.g., > 40 units) may prompt adjunctive interventions to address microvascular dysfunction. For instance, the IC administration of vasodilators like nitroprusside or adenosine can be given to help alleviate microvascular spasm or no‐reflow, acutely reducing IM by ~15%–20%, improving microvascular flow. In some scenarios, elevated IMR may lead to deferred stenting. Studies have shown that in scenarios when FFR is normal but IMR is elevated, deferring PCI has been associated with a lower risk of subsequent MACE compared with immediate stenting [22]. In outpatient settings, IMR guides the selection of anti‐anginal therapies targeting microvascular tone and metabolism. For example, patients with IMR > 25 and CMD benefit from ranolazine or beta‐blockers, which improve angina in ~60%–70% of cases by enhancing microvascular perfusion and reducing oxygen demand [12]. Calcium channel blockers like diltiazem are also efficacious, reducing IMR by ~10%–15% in INOCA patients with vasospastic components [12].

Beyond clinical practice, IMR serves as a valuable surrogate endpoint in clinical trials evaluating novel therapeutic strategies for CMD. Reductions in IMR post‐intervention can signal improved microvascular function and myocardial perfusion. Recently, colchicine has garnered interest: while direct data on IMR are limited, large trials (COLCOT, LoDoCo2) have shown that low‐dose colchicine reduced cardiovascular events in CAD [23, 24]. The benefit derived from low‐dose colchicine is thought to stem from reduced microvascular inflammation leading to improved endothelial function and microvascular health. Ongoing studies are exploring whether these anti‐inflammatory treatments can lower IMR in patients with CMD.

Measurement Methodology in the Catheterization Laboratory

Technical Aspects

Accurate measurement of IMR during cardiac catheterization requires a high‐level technique and standardized protocols (Figure 6). The procedure typically proceeds as follows: 1.Coronary angiography and setup: Diagnostic angiography is first performed to exclude any high‐grade pericardial stenoses (> 50% diameter) that could confound microvascular assessment [9, 25]. A pressure–temperature sensor–tipped guidewire, typically a 0.014‐in. wire (such as Abbott PressureWire X or Boston Scientific COMET), is introduced through a 6 or 7 F guiding catheter to access the target coronary artery. A 6 F guide is sufficient for most IMR measurements, whereas a 7 F guide may provide extra support if concomitant devices are used [9]. Ensuring proper calibration and equalization of the pressure sensor is an essential step. Prior to advancing the wire, the wire is zeroed in and then, with the wire positioned at the ostium, the measured distal coronary pressure (Pd) is confirmed equal (within ±2 mmHg) to the aortic pressure (Pa) read from the guide catheter [9, 26]. This step is crucial, as even minor calibration errors can lead to IMR miscalculations, potentially altering clinical decisions.2.Induce maximal hyperemia: IMR is measured under conditions of maximal hyperemia (vasodilation) to minimize vasomotor tone and isolate microvascular resistance. This is accomplished using intravenous (IV) adenosine administered at a dose of 140 μg/kg/min for at least 2−3 min to achieve a steady‐state hyperemic response [9]. Adenosine reliably provokes maximal arteriolar dilation in > 90% of patients, although it can cause transient side effects like transient flushing, bradycardia (5%–10% of cases), AV block, or hypotension, which generally resolve quickly on cessation [27, 28]. Alternatively, in patients who cannot tolerate IV adenosine e.g., severe asthma, hypotension), an IC adenosine protocol may be used. IC boluses of 60–100 μg for the right coronary artery (RCA) and/or 100–200 μg for the left coronary system induce hyperemia [27, 28]. These bolus injections achieve hyperemia within 5–10 s, with a duration of 20–30 s. Repeated IC boluses can maintain hyperemia for sequential measurements if needed. In select cases, IC papaverine (8–12 mg for RCA, 12–16 mg for LCA) is used to induce a stable hyperemic plateau lasting up to 60 s, particularly in research settings or when adenosine response is suboptimal (e.g., < 5% of cases) [29]. Papaverine, however, carries a small risk (~1%) of ventricular arrhythmias, necessitating continuous electrocardiographic monitoring [29]. The precision of IMR measurement hinges on rigorous adherence to procedural standards. For instance, inaccurate guidewire positioning (e.g., < 5 cm distal to the coronary ostium) can overestimate Pd by 5–10 mmHg, inflating IMR values [9]. Additionally, failure to achieve maximal hyperemia (e.g., if a patient has had recent caffeine or an inadequate adenosine dose), the Tmnhyp will be artificially longer (flow not truly maximal), thus underestimating CFR and IMR by 10%–20% [27, 28]. In cases in which achieving maximal hyperemia is difficult, adjunctive measures like a higher adenosine dose or verifying hyperemia via a Doppler flow sensor (looking for > twofold increase in velocity) can be used. Hemodynamic instability can also impact measurements: for example, if during the measurement the patient develops tachycardia or hypotension (sometimes seen in acute MI patients), the baseline flow conditions change. Operators might postpone measurements until the patient stabilizes or use IC bolus measurements rather than a prolonged adenosine infusion in such cases [32]. Environmental factors are controlled as well—the saline is room temperature; significantly warmer or colder injectate would alter the thermodilution curves. When performed under optimal conditions, IMR has proven reliable and additive in guiding patient management, as discussed earlier. A high IMR result in the cath lab might prompt additional therapy (e.g., IC nitroprusside infusion if IMR is very high post‐PCI), or closer follow‐up and aggressive risk factor management if the patient is at risk for microvascular complications.

Clinical Studies of IMR‐Guided Therapies

Multiple clinical studies have explored therapies to improve microvascular function and the utility of IMR in guiding treatment. The REOPEN‐AMI trial was an open‐label, randomized, placebo‐controlled, multicenter study designed to evaluate whether pharmacologic therapy at the time of PCI could prevent MVO in acute MI. 240 STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI with thrombus aspiration were assigned to receive IC adenosine, nitroprusside, or placebo prior to stenting [33]. The primary endpoint was ST‐segment resolution (STR) > 70% on ECG at 90 min post‐PCI, which was significantly more frequent with adenosine (71% of patients) versus placebo (51%), indicating better microvascular perfusion. Nitroprusside, in contrast, was no significantly better than placebo (54% compared to 51%) [33]. Though there were trends toward reduced MVO and smaller infarct size with adenosine, clinical MACE rates at 30 days did not differ significantly, possibly due to the small sample. Nonetheless, REOPEN‐AMI reinforced that adenosine can enhance microvascular perfusion acutely after MI. Adenosine, a naturally occurring nucleoside with a brief half‐life (< 2 s), offers many benefits in the heart. It powerfully widens small coronary blood vessels by activating A2 receptors, reduces inflammation by targeting neutrophils and preventing platelet clumping, mimics ischemic preconditioning to lessen reperfusion injury, and protects cells from death while potentially encouraging new vessel growth. These effects stem from activating cell surface receptors, which also influence heart electrical activity and pain perception [34]. Notably, adenosine improved STR more than angiographic reperfusion measures, suggesting its benefits extend beyond vessel dilation to include sustained protective effects.

Another study by Ito et al. investigated the cardioprotective effects of IV Nicorandil—an ATP‐sensitive K+ channel opener with nitrate‐like properties—in patients with first anterior STEMI treated with PCI [35]. Patients who received Nicorandil during and after PCI were shown to have significantly better regional left ventricular function recovery: greater reductions in wall motion score (−6 vs. −3) and improvements in regional wall motion (0.7 vs. 0.2 SD/chord) [35]. Left ventricle end‐diastolic volume index decreased in the Nicorandil group (−2 mL/m²) but increased in controls (+4 mL/m²), particularly in early reperfusion cases (< 6 h). In‐hospital complications were lowering with Nicorandil: congestive heart failure (15% vs. 37%), malignant ventricular arrhythmia (5% vs. 20%), pericardial effusion (8% vs. 37%), and no deaths (vs. 10% in controls) [35]. MCE no‐reflow phenomenon was halved (15% vs. 33%), suggesting preserved microvascular perfusion as the mechanism [35]. While this study was not an IMR‐guided study per se, the implication that therapies such as Nicorandil could improve microvascular low and thereby myocardial recovery helped lay the foundation for IMR studies like the one performed by Hirohata et. al. In this unblinded single‐center randomized trial, patients were randomized to either placebo or nicorandil, which was administered just prior to PCI in patients with stable CAD undergoing PCI. IMR was performed post‐PCI and found to be significantly higher in the control group compared to the nicorandil group (IMR 25.4 ± 12.1 vs. 17.9 ± 9.1) [36]. Additionally, cardiac troponin I was found to be significantly more elevated post‐PCI in the placebo group when compared to the Nicorandil group, with 41% compared to 12% having a more than fivefold increase of troponin from baseline [36].

Statin therapy, beyond its lipid‐lowering role, has demonstrated microvascular benefits. Fujii et al. showed that 4 weeks of pravastatin pretreatment before PCI reduced post‐PCI IMR by 5 U, an effect thought to be mediated by statins' anti‐inflammatory properties and enhancement of endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity [37]. Calcium channel blockers, notably in the CorMicA trial, lowered IMR from 32.1 ± 9.6 to 25.4 ± 8.1 U over 8 weeks in vasospastic microvascular dysfunction, alongside an 18‐point improvement in Seattle Angina Questionnaire scores [6]. These data underscore that treating the microvasculature (with anti‐anginals and risk factor control) can measurably improve IMR in the short term, correlating with symptom relief.

More recently, device‐based strategies such as pressure‐controlled intermittent coronary sinus occlusion (PICSO) have shown promise. By intermittently occluding the coronary sinus, PICSO is thought to increase pressure in the microcirculation and drive blood into ischemic territories. In a 2022 report by Gibson et al. applying PICSO after primary PCI in STEMI led to improved microvascular perfusion indices and a trend toward reduced infarct size on MRI [38]. Although not yet widely adopted, such approaches highlight innovative attempts to directly augment microvascular flow.

In addition to pharmacotherapies and device‐based strategies, it's worth noting the interaction between systemic conditions and IMR. Patients with type 2 diabetes often have elevated IMR compared to non‐diabetics (27 vs. 16 in the anterior circulation, p = 0.009), reflecting diffuse microvascular dysfunction from endothelial and small vessel disease [39].

Taken together, the growing body of clinical studies suggests that IMR is dynamic and responsive to interventions. Therapies ranging from pharmacologic agents (adenosine, nicorandil, statins, calcium blockers) to devices (PICSO) and lifestyle changes can all modulate microvascular resistance. Future trials will undoubtedly clarify which strategies produce the most meaningful long‐term improvements in microvascular health and patient outcomes.

Novel pharmacologic classes are under active investigation: Rho‐kinase inhibition with fasudil has been shown to improve microvascular function, lowering IMR and alleviating refractory vasospastic angina, and the potential role of endothelin receptor antagonists in improving IMR is currently under investigation, with the ENDORA‐PCI trial designed to test whether ambrisentan can attenuate microvascular injury during PCI [27, 28].

Limitations and Challenges of IMR

IMR measurement faces technical and practical challenges that can impact its accuracy and clinical usefulness. Pressure wire drift, a common issue in prolonged procedures, can introduce errors of up to 5–10 mmHg in pressure readings, leading to inflated or deflated IMR values and potentially misclassifying microvascular function. Incomplete hyperemia, occurring in ~5%–10% of measurements due to suboptimal adenosine response or patient factors like caffeine intake, may overestimate resistance by 10%–20%, resulting in false‐positive diagnoses of CMD and inappropriate escalation of therapy, such as unnecessary adjunctive interventions or prolonged medical management [28, 40]. Hemodynamic instability, including hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg), tachycardia (> 100 bpm), or arrhythmias, is particularly prevalent in ACS patients (affecting ~20%–30% of cases) and can compromise measurement accuracy by altering coronary flow dynamics, leading to unreliable IMR values that may delay critical decisions like post‐PCI adjunctive therapy or risk stratification [32].

Moreover, the lack of standardized protocols across laboratories—variations in hyperemia induction (e.g., IV vs. IC adenosine), saline injection number (1–5 injections), and cutoff thresholds (e.g., > 25 vs. > 30 for abnormality)—hinders inter‐center comparability and reproducibility, leading to inter‐operative variability [26, 41]. These inconsistencies can lead to discordant interpretations, affecting trial outcomes and clinical guidelines, and contributing to suboptimal patient management, such as under‐ or over‐treatment of CMD [41].

Beyond technical issues, IMR measurement presents challenges related to cost, training, and accessibility, particularly in non‐academic or low‐resource settings. The procedure requires specialized equipment, such as pressure‐temperature sensor‐tipped guidewires (costing $500–$1000 per use) and advanced catheterization laboratory infrastructure, which can add $2000–$5000 to procedural expenses, limiting its feasibility in resource‐constrained environments where reimbursement may not cover these costs [42]. Training requirements are substantial, necessitating interventional cardiologists with expertise in physiologic assessments, often requiring additional fellowship programs or workshops (e.g., 50–100 supervised cases), which are primarily available in high‐volume academic centers and may not be accessible in rural or developing regions.

Accessibility is further hampered in low‐resource settings due to the need for dedicated cath labs and hyperemic agents, resulting in IMR being underutilized outside tertiary hospitals; for instance, the adoption rate was around 50% in European Centers of Excellence, and although adoption outside specialized tertiary settings remains much lower, it potentially exacerbating disparities in CMD diagnosis and management [40]. Addressing these barriers through cost‐effective wire alternatives, telemedicine training, and simplified protocols could enhance global adoption.

To address these challenges, potential solutions are emerging. For pressure wire drift, regular recalibration during the procedure (every 5–10 min) or AI‐assisted drift detection algorithms, which can identify offsets in real‐time with > 95% accuracy, offer promising mitigation strategies [33]. Incomplete hyperemia can be confirmed through adjunctive methods like Doppler flow velocity assessment (> 50% increase) or stable Pd drop monitoring, reducing underestimation errors to < 5% [26]. In cases of hemodynamic instability, patient stabilization with vasopressors or alternative hyperemic agents (e.g., papaverine for adenosine‐intolerant patients) can improve measurement reliability, while delaying IMR assessment until stability is achieved minimizes inaccuracies [32, 43]. To overcome protocol variability, ongoing efforts by societies like the European Society of Cardiology and the American College of Cardiology aim to establish consensus guidelines, including uniform hyperemia protocols and automated software for Tmn calculation, potentially reducing inter‐operative variability to < 5% and enhancing multicenter comparability. AI‐driven tools can further standardize interpretation by automating calibration and integrating hemodynamic data, as demonstrated in models achieving AUC > 0.90 for error detection [44, 45]. By implementing these solutions, the clinical implications of errors—such as misdiagnosis leading to increased angina recurrence (twofold risk) or unnecessary procedures (e.g., 20% higher PCI rates in false‐positives)—can be mitigated, improving overall patient outcomes and facilitating broader adoption of IMR in routine practice.

As the diagnostic and prognostic utility of IMR becomes firmly established, ongoing research must address knowledge gaps surrounding wire‐free physiology, optimized diagnostic thresholds, and targeted interventions. The integration of the IMR with advanced IC imaging modalities, such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), is a significant advancement in the comprehensive evaluation of CAD. This constructive interaction allows for simultaneous assessment of both structural and functional aspects of the coronary vasculature, enabling clinicians to tailor interventions for patients with combined epicardial and microvascular disease. OCT provides high‐resolution imaging (axial resolution of ~10–20 µm) of the coronary vessel wall, identifying microstructural abnormalities such as plaque composition, thin‐cap fibroatheromas, and stent apposition, which are critical for guiding precise interventions IVUS, with its deeper penetration (up to 4–8 mm) and lower resolution (~100–150 µm), complements OCT by offering detailed visualization of vessel remodeling, plaque burden (e.g., percent atheroma volume, typically 30%–50% in significant lesions), and stent expansion. By integrating IMR, which quantifies microvascular function (normal IMR < 25 units, impaired > 25 units), with these imaging modalities, clinicians can differentiate between an epicardial stenosis and microvascular dysfunction, optimizing treatment strategies such as PCI or medical therapy. For instance, studies show that patients with elevated IMR (> 40 units) post‐PCI have a 2.5‐fold increased risk of MACE compared to those with normal IMR, underscoring the prognostic value of combined assessments [46, 47].

Non‐invasive surrogates for microvascular evaluation are expanding access to functional assessment beyond the catheterization laboratory. Computed tomography (CT)‐derived fractional flow preserve (FFR‐CT) and myocardial perfusion imaging via CMR provide physiologic mapping without invasive catheterization. FFR‐CT, with a diagnostic accuracy of ~85% for detecting ischemia (compared to invasive FFR), can estimate microvascular resistance indirectly by modeling coronary flow dynamics [48].

Artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven decision support tools are poised to further enhance the clinical use of IMR by streamlining measurement interpretation and integrating multimodal data. Machine learning algorithms can automate IMR analysis, reducing inter‐operative variability (e.g., from 10% to 15% in manual measurements to < 5% with AI assistance) and integrating IMR values with imaging (OCT/IVUS) and hemodynamic data (e.g., FFR, CFR) [33].

Finally, the incorporation of AI and big data holds promise to personalize and enhance the use of IMR. Machine learning models can integrate IMR readings with patient demographics, lab results, imaging findings, and genomic data to better predict outcomes or responses to therapy. For example, an AI algorithm might identify that a patient with diabetes, an IMR of 28, and a certain inflammatory marker profile has a particularly high risk of future heart failure, prompting more aggressive therapy. AI could also optimize procedural aspects—for instance, guiding the number of injections needed by recognizing when sufficient measurement precision has been achieved, or instantly correcting for any drift in pressure readings. As large data sets of invasive coronary function tests become available, we will better define normal ranges of IMR adjusted for factors like age, sex, or territory, and AI could help derive these nuanced reference values. In the realm of therapeutics, future clinical trials might use AI to stratify which CMD patients (as identified by IMR or CFR) are likely to respond to a given intervention (e.g., a particular vasodilator or anti‐inflammatory drug), moving toward precision medicine in microvascular disease [44, 45].

IMR has established itself as a cornerstone for assessing the coronary microcirculation, with proven diagnostic and prognostic utility. Its integration with other modalities and emerging technologies will likely enhance its accuracy, ease of use, and clinical impact. As we strive to treat ischemic heart disease in a more comprehensive manner, addressing not only the “open pipes” of epicardial arteries but also the “small pipes” of the microvasculature, IMR will play an integral role in guiding this next generation of cardiovascular care.
