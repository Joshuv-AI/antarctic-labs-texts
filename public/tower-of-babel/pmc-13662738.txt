Fatty Acid Oxidation Disorders Detected by Newborn Screening: Clinical, Biochemical and Genetic Spectrum in a Single‐Center Cohort
Authors: Elena Martín‐Hernández, Delia Barrio‐Carreras, María Martín‐Cazaña, Luis Miguel García‐Cárdaba, Carmen Martín‐Fernández, Patricia Pérez‐Mohand, Teresa Hernández Chamorro, Berta Zamora Crespo, Pedro Ruiz‐Sala, Patricia Alcaide Alonso, Pilar Quijada‐Fraile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNewborn screening (NBS) has significantly improved early detection and survival in fatty acid oxidation disorders (FAODs). However, the expanding phenotypic spectrum challenges prognostic stratification, and long‐term morbidity remains incompletely characterized. We conducted an observational, retrospective, single‐center study of patients with FAODs identified through NBS and followed between 2011 and 2024. Clinical, biochemical, genetic, and nutritional data were extracted from medical records. Correlations between biochemical markers, enzymatic activity, and clinical outcomes were analyzed. The study was approved by the Research Committee of the Instituto de Investigación, Hospital 12 de Octubre (Madrid). Sixty‐five patients were included: 42 MCADD, 11 VLCADD, 5 LCHADD, 2 CPT1D, 3 CPT2D, 1 CACTD, and 1 MADD. Median age at last follow‐up was 7.2 years (range 0.4–13.6). Two patients died during follow‐up (overall mortality 3.1%). In MCADD, confirmatory C8 concentrations correlated with residual enzyme activity and metabolic decompensations. In VLCADD, NBS and confirmatory C14:1 levels correlated with enzyme activity but not with clinical severity. VLCADD showed marked clinical heterogeneity, whereas LCHADD patients had the highest disease burden and required the most intensive nutritional management. Overall, 40% of patients experienced at least one metabolic decompensation, occurring significantly more frequently in LC‐FAOD than in MCADD. Neuropsychological manifestations were observed in 16.9% of patients. In summary, NBS markedly improves survival in FAODs, but morbidity remains substantial, particularly in LC‐FAOD. Current biochemical markers are useful for early risk stratification but remain insufficient to predict long‐term clinical severity. These findings support individualized multidisciplinary follow‐up, including specialized nutritional care and systematic neuropsychological assessment.

Introduction

Mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders (FAODs) are a heterogeneous group of autosomal recessive inborn errors of metabolism affecting fatty acid transport or mitochondrial β‐oxidation. Defective fatty acid utilization particularly compromises organs with high energy demand, including liver, skeletal muscle, and heart. During catabolic stress, impaired β‐oxidation may lead to energy deficiency and accumulation of toxic intermediates, causing hypoketotic hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, rhabdomyolysis, hepatopathy, and sudden death [1, 2]. Diagnosis is usually based on characteristic acylcarnitine profiles and molecular confirmation [3].

Medium‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCADD, OMIM #201450) is the most common FAOD in Western countries, followed by very‐long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (VLCADD, OMIM #201475), whereas long‐chain hydroxyacyl‐CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHADD, OMIM #609016), mitochondrial trifunctional protein deficiency (MTPD, OMIM #609015), carnitine palmitoyltransferase deficiencies (CPT1D OMIM #255120; CPT2D OMIM #255110; #600649; #608836), carnitine‐acylcarnitine translocase deficiency (CACTD OMIM #212138), and multiple acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MADD, OMIM #231680) are rarer [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

The incorporation of tandem mass spectrometry into newborn screening (NBS) programs has markedly changed the diagnostic landscape of FAODs by enabling presymptomatic detection and early treatment [9, 10, 11, 12]. Characteristic acylcarnitine markers allow detection of the most clinically relevant disorders, including as principal markers C8 for MCADD, C14:1 for VLCADD, long‐chain hydroxyacylcarnitines for LCHADD/MTPD, elevated free carnitine and diagnostic ratios for CPT1D, and increases in C16 and C18:1 for CPT2D or CACTD [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

NBS has substantially reduced mortality and severe metabolic crises, particularly in MCADD [12, 19]. However, screening has also broadened the recognized phenotypic spectrum, especially in VLCADD, where asymptomatic or mild forms are increasingly identified [4, 7]. In contrast, other long‐chain FAODs (LC‐FAODs) such as LCHADD, MTPD, or CACTD may still be associated with significant morbidity [20, 21, 22].

Nutritional management includes avoidance of fasting and emergency regimens during illness, with dietary fat modification and medium‐chain triglyceride (MCT) supplementation in LC‐FAODs [23, 24]. New therapeutic approaches such as triheptanoin may further improve outcomes in selected patients with LC‐FAODs [25].

Despite advances, long‐term morbidity and prognostic stratification remain challenging, highlighting the need for better prognostic markers.

Objective

To describe the epidemiological, biochemical, genetic, and clinical spectrum of patients with mitochondrial FAODs detected by newborn screening (NBS) and followed at a reference center for inherited metabolic diseases.

Methods

This retrospective, observational, single‐center study included all patients with genetically confirmed mitochondrial FAODs identified through NBS between June 2011 and December 2024, who were followed at the Pediatric Unit for Inherited Metabolic Diseases, Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre (Madrid, Spain). Newborns with abnormal screening results in whom FAOD was not confirmed were excluded.

Data extracted from electronic medical records included demographic and clinical characteristics, biochemical and genetic findings, enzymatic activity, dietary management, anthropometry, neurodevelopmental outcomes, hospital admissions, metabolic decompensations, complications, and mortality.

NBS was performed within the Regional Newborn Screening Program of Madrid. Dried blood spot samples, collected approximately 48 h after birth, were sent to the regional screening laboratory in Hospital Gregorio Marañón, where they were analyzed by non‐derivatized tandem mass spectrometry using NeoBase or NeoBase 2 kits (PerkinElmer) according to the study period. The tandem mass spectrometer used was an Acquity TQD UPLC/MS system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). First‐tier markers, combined markers, and ratios used for FAOD screening included: C0, C2, C6, C8, C10, C10:1, C12, C12:1, C14, C14:1, C14:2, C14OH, C16, C16OH, C16:1OH, C18, C18:1, C18:2, C18OH, C18:1OH, C0/(C16 + C18), (C0 + C2 + C3 + C16 + C18 + C18:1)/citrulline, C14:1/C12, C14:1/C16, C14:1/C2, C16 + C18:1, C16OH/C16, C18OH/C18, C8/C10, and C8/C2. Cut‐off values were periodically reviewed. Abnormal screening results were reanalyzed and, when appropriate, a second dried blood spot sample was requested. Newborns with a positive NBS result were referred, according to demographic criteria, to one of four designated hospitals within the Madrid regional NBS program, including Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre, which is also a National Reference Center for inherited metabolic diseases. Additional information on the [26] is available at the official website (https://www.comunidad.madrid/salud/cribado‐neonatal).

Confirmatory plasma samples were obtained as soon as a positive NBS result became available, at a median age of 7 days (range 3–33). In newborns who developed symptoms before the NBS result was available, biochemical investigations were initiated at the time of clinical presentation.

Confirmatory biochemical testing, including plasma acylcarnitine analysis, was performed at the Centro de Diagnóstico de Enfermedades Moleculares, Universidad Autónoma de Madrid (CEDEM). Enzyme activity in lymphocytes was determined in all patients with VLCADD and in selected patients with MCADD at the same center according to the previously described protocols [27, 28]. Molecular studies were performed at the Department of Genetics, Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre. Diagnostic approaches evolved from Sanger sequencing to targeted next‐generation sequencing panels during the study period. Candidate genes were selected according to biochemical phenotype and HPO terms. Variants were interpreted according to ACMG/AMP guidelines using ClinVar, LOVD, HGMD Professional, population databases and in silico tools [29]. Copy‐number variants (CNVs) were assessed in NGS‐based studies using ExomeDepth (v1.1.10).

Follow‐Up

Symptomatic patients were evaluated as clinically indicated, whereas asymptomatic patients were routinely followed every 3 months during the first year of life, every 4–6 months until 6 years of age, and every 6–12 months thereafter. At each visit, patients were jointly reviewed by a metabolic pediatrician and a specialist dietitian, including clinical evaluation, anthropometry, and dietary assessment.

Anthropometric data were converted to z‐scores using the Spanish Growth Studies 2010 reference charts [30] and Fenton [31] Growth Charts www.ucalgary.ca/fenton.

Routine laboratory monitoring was performed every 6–12 months. Plasma acylcarnitine profiles, vitamins, PUFAs, and liver ultrasound were generally obtained annually. In patients with neurodevelopmental or behavioral abnormalities suggestive of an additional etiology, genetic investigations were expanded.

Patients with LC‐FAOD underwent baseline cardiological evaluation, including echocardiography, with subsequent assessments performed as clinically indicated. Patients with LCHADD underwent regular ophthalmological and neurophysiological evaluations from 6 years of age, or earlier if clinically indicated.

Formal neuropsychological assessment was not performed systematically as part of a prospective research protocol. Patients who developed neurodevelopmental, cognitive, learning, or behavioral concerns during follow‐up were referred to the neuropsychology clinic, where qualitative and quantitative assessments were performed using validated pediatric neuropsychological tests. Autism spectrum disorder and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder were diagnosed by the corresponding specialists according to DSM‐5 criteria. The median age at first neuropsychological assessment was 2.06 years (IQR 1.19–2.95).

Nutritional management initially followed international recommendations and was subsequently individualized according to clinical severity and evolving evidence.

L‐carnitine supplementation was considered in selected patients with hypocarnitinemia, defined as free carnitine concentrations < 6 μmol/L during the first month of life or < 21 μmol/L thereafter.

Metabolic decompensation was defined as hospital admission lasting more than 24 h associated with compatible clinical or biochemical findings. For statistical analyses, both the absolute number of metabolic decompensations and the number of metabolic decompensations per patient‐year of follow‐up were recorded. Because these variables were highly correlated, metabolic decompensations per patient‐year were used in the correlation analyses. Neonatal decompensations occurring before newborn screening results were available were analyzed separately as symptoms before diagnosis and were not considered metabolic decompensations. Likewise, hospital admissions due to intercurrent illnesses without clear evidence of metabolic deterioration were not included in the decompensation analyses. These admissions were managed according to the patient's clinical status, with particular attention to maintaining adequate caloric intake and preventing catabolism. When oral intake was preserved, the usual diet was maintained and supplemented with carbohydrate sources in MCADD and with carbohydrate sources and MCT in LC‐FAOD. In patients with vomiting, diarrhea, or poor oral tolerance, intravenous glucose with electrolytes was administered according to age and clinical requirements, together with treatment of the underlying intercurrent illness.

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons and correlation analyses were performed using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study was approved by the Research Committee of the Instituto de Investigación (TP24/0352), Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre.

Results

Between 2011 and 2024, 862 144 newborns were screened in the Community of Madrid. The incidences of FAODs detected by the Regional Newborn Screening Program were 1:14369 for MCADD, 1:50714 for VLCADD, 1:123163 for LCHADD, 1:862144 for MADD, 1:57476 for primary carnitine deficiency, 1:431072 for both CPT1 and CPT2 deficiencies and 1:862144 newborns for CACTD deficiency. The overall incidence of FAODs detected by NBS was 1:8210 newborns. These patients were referred to one of the four designated hospitals within the Madrid regional NBS program for confirmatory diagnosis, treatment, and follow‐up. The present study includes patients with FAOD managed at Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre, including some referred from other centres. Patients with primary carnitine deficiency were not included.

Cohort Characteristics

The study included 65 patients (52.3% male), with a median age at last follow‐up of 7.2 years (range 0.4–13.6). Diagnoses were MCADD (n = 42), VLCADD (n = 11), LCHADD (n = 5), CPT1D (n = 2), CPT2D (n = 3), CACTD (n = 1) and MADD (n = 1). Overall, 22 patients had LC‐FAOD, 42 MCADD and one MADD (Table 1).

Consanguinity was reported in 16.9% of families. Prematurity was significantly more frequent in LC‐FAOD than in MCADD (31.8% vs. 2.4%; p < 0.001), particularly in LCHADD. Fourteen patients (21.5%) developed symptoms before NBS results were available, with symptom onset occurring within the first days of life in all cases. Most of these newborns had significant perinatal comorbidities, including prematurity, maternal HELLP syndrome, low birth weight, respiratory distress syndrome, or other complications, making it difficult to determine whether the initial manifestations were attributable to the underlying FAOD or to concurrent perinatal conditions. After detailed clinical review, early symptoms were considered likely attributable to the underlying FAOD in five patients (7.7%): one patient with LCHADD who presented with severe neonatal metabolic decompensation and cardiomyopathy, one with MADD, and one with MCADD who developed hypoglycemia requiring intravenous glucose, one with CPT2D who presented with severe hyperammonemia and seizures, and one with CACTD who developed hypoketotic hypoglycemia with lactic acidosis and hyperammonemia. In the remaining patients, symptoms were considered either unlikely or only possibly related to the underlying FAOD (Supplementary Table 1).

Biochemical Findings

In MCADD, the median C8 concentration was 3.48 μmol/L at NBS (IQR 0.72–9.23; range 0.18–26.00; reference < 0.13 μmol/L) and 2.65 μmol/L at confirmatory testing (IQR 0.59–4.48; range 0.21–15.19; reference < 0.25 μmol/L). Additional acylcarnitine species (C6, C10, C10:1) and diagnostic ratios (C8/C10 and C8/C2) at NBS and confirmatory testing are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Disease‐specific acylcarnitines and diagnostic ratios were significantly correlated with C8 concentrations at NBS and confirmatory testing. Residual MCAD enzyme activity showed significant inverse correlations with all major biochemical markers, especially C6 and C8 concentrations at NBS and confirmatory testing. Several confirmatory‐study acylcarnitines were significantly associated with metabolic decompensations per patient‐year. For clarity, Figure 1 illustrates only the correlations involving C8 concentrations, residual enzyme activity, and metabolic decompensations, whereas all correlation analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

Correlations between C8 concentrations, residual MCAD enzyme activity and metabolic decompensations in MCADD.

In VLCADD, the median C14:1 concentration was 2.20 μmol/L at NBS (IQR 1.26–3.17; range 0.78–7.55; reference < 0.37 μmol/L) and 1.44 μmol/L at confirmatory testing (IQR 0.81–1.72; range 0.24–5.00; reference < 0.17 μmol/L). Additional disease‐specific acylcarnitine species (C14, C14:2, C16, C16:1, C18, C18:1 and C18:2) and the C14:1/C12:1 ratio are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Although additional biomarkers were analyzed, C14:1 remained the principal marker and was therefore used for the main analyses.

C14:1 at NBS correlated positively with other disease‐specific acylcarnitines (C14, C16:1, C18:1) and with C14:1 at confirmatory study, and inversely with residual VLCAD enzyme activity. In contrast to MCADD, neither newborn screening nor confirmatory C14:1 concentrations, nor residual enzyme activity were significantly associated with metabolic decompensations per patient‐year (Figure 2).

Correlations between C14:1 concentrations, residual VLCAD enzyme activity and metabolic decompensations in VLCADD.

In LCHADD, the median C16‐OH concentration decreased from 0.94 μmol/L at newborn screening (range 0.40–1.53; reference < 0.06 μmol/L) to 0.37 μmol/L at confirmatory testing (range 0.08–1.75; reference < 0.08 μmol/L). Additional disease‐specific acylcarnitine species and diagnostic ratios for LCHADD, CPT1D, CPT2D, and CACTD are summarized in Supplementary Table 6. Owing to the limited number of patients, no further correlation analyses were performed for these disorders.

Genetic Findings

In MCADD, 17 different ACADM variants were identified. The c.985A>G (p.Lys329Glu) variant was the most frequent, present in 80.9% of patients. Homozygous carriers showed significantly higher C8 concentrations, lower residual enzyme activity, and higher admission rates than patients carrying other genotypes, whereas no significant association with metabolic decompensations was observed (Table 2).

Nutritional Management and Growth

Patients with MCADD received breast milk or mixed feeding during infancy, followed by a healthy diet with avoidance of prolonged fasting. During follow‐up, 25 patients (59.5%) received L‐carnitine supplementation at some point because of secondary hypocarnitinemia, at a mean dose of 46.2 ± 17.7 mg/kg/day.

In LC‐FAOD, dietary treatment was tailored according to clinical severity and included MCT‐enriched formulas, prolonged nocturnal feeding in patients with LCHADD, bedtime snacks rich in complex carbohydrates in selected patients with greater metabolic instability, and particular attention to maintaining adequate protein intake, generally within the upper normal range for age. In one patient with severe LCHADD who developed necrotizing enterocolitis, skimmed breast milk was successfully used for 4 months. In one patient with severe VLCADD, cow's milk protein intolerance and sucrase‐isomaltase deficiency precluded the use of standard MCT‐enriched formulas, contributing to recurrent metabolic decompensations and ultimately requiring gastrostomy feeding and compassionate‐use triheptanoin. Compassionate‐use triheptanoin was also administered to one patient with CPT2D because of recurrent metabolic decompensations despite optimized conventional treatment. During follow‐up, selected patients with hypocarnitinemia received L‐carnitine supplementation at some point, at a mean dose of 18.4 ± 16.5 mg/kg/day.

At the last available follow‐up, patients with LCHADD had significantly shorter permitted fasting intervals than those with VLCADD (p = 0.04) and received a higher proportion of dietary MCT (p = 0.004), reflecting the greater clinical severity of this subgroup (Table 5).

Hospital Admissions, Metabolic Decompensations and Complications

Overall, 26 patients (40.0%) experienced at least one metabolic decompensation. Metabolic decompensations were significantly more frequent in patients with LC‐FAOD than in those with MCADD (68.2% vs. 23.8%; p < 0.001). The highest proportions of affected patients were observed in CACTD (100%), LCHADD (80.0%), and CPT2D (66.7%), whereas most patients with MCADD remained free of metabolic crises.

In MCADD, most patients remained asymptomatic with normal growth and school performance. No cardiomyopathy or myopathy was observed, and only two patients developed mild and transitory hepatic abnormalities. Neuropsychological disorders were identified in 14.3% of patients, including developmental and language delay, attentional difficulties, autism spectrum traits, obsessive behaviors, eating disorders, and impaired social cognition, even in patients with high cognitive abilities.

Among VLCADD patients (Table 3), one patient required highly individualized nutritional management and gastrostomy because of recurrent metabolic instability. One patient (9.1%) presented autism spectrum traits.

In LCHADD, myopathy occurred in 80% of patients and cardiomyopathy in 40%. No patient developed liver involvement, retinopathy, or neuropathy. Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder was identified in two patients (Table 4).

Among carnitine‐cycle disorders (Table 4), one patient with CPT1D presented with Reye syndrome, whereas one patient with CPT2D developed autism spectrum disorder. The patient with CACTD experienced recurrent severe metabolic decompensations and died at 14 months of age.

Overall, heterogeneous neuropsychological manifestations were identified in 16.9% of the cohort, involving multiple cognitive and behavioral domains, including attention, language, learning, executive function and social cognition.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the clinical, biochemical, genetic, and nutritional spectrum of patients with FAODs identified through NBS and followed at a national reference center. As reported in other European cohorts [10, 11], MCADD was the most frequent disorder, although LC‐FAODs accounted for a relatively high proportion of our cohort.

Prematurity and severe neonatal presentation were more frequent among LC‐FAOD patients, particularly in LCHADD, consistent with previous reports [21, 32]. Despite the benefits of NBS, severe early‐onset phenotypes may still manifest before screening results become available. In our cohort, three LC‐FAOD patients presented with severe multisystem disease during the neonatal period, resulting in two deaths in infancy. Mortality among LC‐FAOD patients reached 9%, whereas no deaths occurred in MCADD, yielding an overall mortality of 3.1%, comparable to that reported in other NBS cohorts [11].

The favorable impact of NBS on MCADD prognosis is well established [12, 19, 33] and was confirmed in our cohort, where no deaths occurred and most patients remained asymptomatic with normal growth and school performance. Confirmatory C8 concentrations correlated with residual enzyme activity and metabolic decompensations, supporting previous observations that biochemical severity may improve early risk stratification [27, 34]. Interestingly, confirmatory acylcarnitine concentrations correlated more closely with clinical outcomes than NBS values, suggesting that postnatal biochemical characterization may provide additional prognostic information. Although confirmatory studies are routinely performed for diagnosis, their prognostic value has received little attention and warrants evaluation in larger prospective cohorts.

Homozygosity for the common c.985A>G variant was associated with higher C8 concentrations and lower enzyme activity, consistent with other studies [8, 35]. However, genotype did not completely predict clinical outcome, reinforcing the concept that FAODs identified through NBS represent a broad phenotypic spectrum influenced by multiple genetic and environmental factors.

In VLCADD, C14:1 concentrations correlated with residual VLCAD enzyme activity but did not reliably predict clinical outcome, consistent with previous reports [4, 7, 28]. The c.848T>C (p.Val283Ala) variant was the most frequent ACADVL variant in our cohort, accounting for 31.8% of alleles. This frequency is comparable to that reported in other NBS cohorts [4, 7] and therefore does not suggest population‐specific enrichment. As previously reported, this variant, particularly in homozygosity, is generally associated with residual enzyme activity and a relatively mild clinical phenotype, although metabolic decompensations may still occur. These findings emphasize the limitations of currently available prognostic markers and the need for improved tools for early severity prediction. Recent studies have proposed additional biomarkers, including neonatal C18:2‐carnitine concentrations, which may improve discrimination between mild and severe VLCADD phenotypes [36]. Interestingly, the only patient in our cohort with elevated C18:2‐carnitine concentrations was also the one with the highest number of metabolic decompensations. However, as this observation is based on a single patient, no conclusions can be drawn regarding its prognostic significance.

LCHADD patients showed the highest rates of decompensations, myopathy, and cardiomyopathy, in agreement with previous studies demonstrating persistent long‐term morbidity despite early diagnosis and treatment [21, 23]. Neither retinopathy nor peripheral neuropathy was observed, although the relatively young age of the cohort may have limited the detection of these late‐onset complications.

As NBS has reduced mortality and severe neurological sequelae in FAODs, attention has shifted towards subtle long‐term neuropsychological outcomes. Waisbren et al. [37] described cognitive and developmental impairments in patients with these disorders, while Everard et al. demonstrated that NBS markedly reduces severe neurological sequelae but does not completely prevent milder cognitive and behavioral difficulties. In our cohort, neuropsychological disorders were identified in 16.9% of patients. Attention‐deficit disorders were observed in 6.1%, similar to the general Spanish pediatric population [38], whereas autism spectrum disorder was identified in 4.6%, exceeding the reported prevalence of approximately 1.3% among Spanish schoolchildren [39]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Neuropsychological assessment was not performed systematically but only in patients who developed cognitive, learning, or behavioral concerns during follow‐up. In addition, the relatively small sample size precludes reliable comparisons with the general pediatric population. Potential alternative contributors were also identified: two patients with MCADD had concomitant genetic diagnoses associated with neurodevelopmental features and their neurodevelopmental abnormalities were not considered potentially attributable to MCADD; two patients with LCHADD who developed ADHD had significant perinatal complications, including prematurity and maternal HELLP syndrome; and the patient with CPT2D and autism spectrum disorder experienced severe neonatal metabolic decompensation attributable to the underlying FAOD before NBS results were available. Given the small number of patients and the heterogeneity of these potential contributing factors, no formal association between neuropsychological outcomes and early metabolic events or subsequent decompensations was assessed. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the need for standardized neuropsychological assessment in prospective follow‐up studies to better define the prevalence, spectrum, and determinants of neuropsychological complications in FAOD.

Nutritional management remains the cornerstone of FAOD treatment, particularly in long‐chain disorders. During the study period, recommendations evolved substantially as evidence emerged regarding fasting tolerance, dietary fat composition, protein intake, and alternative energy substrates [20, 24, 25, 32, 40, 41, 42]. Recently published international LC‐FAOD guidelines further emphasize individualized management according to disease severity, clinical course, and metabolic stability [43]. Our cohort reflects this evolving approach, particularly in patients with LCHADD, who required shorter fasting intervals and higher dietary MCT intake because of their greater metabolic instability, and in selected patients with more complex clinical courses, in whom nutritional management included strategies such as skimmed breast milk or compassionate‐use triheptanoin. Although our experience with triheptanoin is limited to two patients (one with VLCADD and one with CPT2 deficiency), both experienced a marked reduction in metabolic decompensations following treatment initiation, in line with recent reports [25, 42, 44]. While our experience is insufficient to define standardized indications, it illustrates how individualized nutritional and pharmacological management may optimize long‐term outcomes in selected patients.

Growth parameters remained within normal ranges at the last available visit, consistent with recent reports [45]. This was achieved through multidisciplinary follow‐up including regular assessment by a specialist metabolic dietitian, enabling individualized dietary adjustments according to growth and metabolic requirements.

The main limitations of this study are its retrospective design, the small number of patients in some disease groups, and the young age of many individuals, which may underestimate late‐onset complications. Its main strength is the long‐term follow‐up of a well‐characterized single‐center cohort managed by the same multidisciplinary team.

Conclusions

Newborn screening has transformed the prognosis of patients with FAOD by enabling early diagnosis and substantially reducing severe morbidity and mortality. However, marked clinical heterogeneity persists, particularly among long‐chain FAOD, highlighting the need for improved prognostic biomarkers and increasingly individualized long‐term management. Our findings illustrate how multidisciplinary follow‐up, integrating clinical, biochemical, nutritional, and neuropsychological assessment, has become essential to optimize long‐term outcomes in this evolving population. Future collaborative longitudinal studies will be crucial to refine risk stratification, validate novel prognostic biomarkers, and evaluate emerging disease‐modifying therapies.
