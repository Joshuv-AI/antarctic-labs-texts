High-speed hyperspectral single-pixel microscopy via line-scan detection with data fusion-based enhanced resolution
Authors: Samuel I Zapata-Valencia, Heberley Tobón-Maya, Cosimo D’Andrea, Jesús Lancis, Enrique Tajahuerce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of hyperspectral microscopy techniques is recognized as an active field of research. Although commercial alternatives are available, these systems remain slow, expensive, and limited in flexibility, indicating clear opportunities for improvement. Coded‑imaging strategies, including single‑pixel imaging (SPI), offer promising alternatives by enabling computational reconstructions with spectral resolution through parallel detection. When combined with conventional cameras via data fusion (DF), these reconstructions can achieve high spatial resolution. However, traditional implementations often rely on spectrometers, which suffer from limited bandwidth and low-speeds acquisition, restricting SPI’s versatility. To overcome these challenges, in this work is developed a hyperspectral single‑pixel microscopy platform (HySPM) assisted by DF. The system employs a customized optical setup that disperses light into spectral components, captured by a high‑speed line‑scan camera in parallel detection. Full Hadamard scans of 64×64 pixels are completed in 0.82 s at 10 kHz. Up to 73 spectral bands are acquired in parallel with 4 nm resolution, without compressive algorithms. Performance is validated through reflected intensity and fluorescence imaging. DF further integrates HySPM with high‑resolution monochrome images, expanding reconstructions from 64×64 to 605×605 pixels while enhancing spatial detail and preserving spectral signatures. HySPM thus provides a scalable, cost‑effective solution that bridges temporal limitations in current hyperspectral implementations.Subject terms: Imaging and sensing, Microscopy

Introduction

Multispectral and hyperspectral imaging has become an active field of research, with applications ranging from food quality control and color inspection to precision agriculture and biomedical imaging1–3. By acquiring spatial information of a specimen across multiple spectral bands, these techniques enable the retrieval of detailed spectral signatures that enhance segmentation, classification, and diagnosis beyond the capabilities of conventional imaging systems2. A variety of approaches have been developed to obtain spatially resolved hyperspectral information. Among them, filter-based spectral scanning4, electronically tunable filters5, Fourier-transform interferometry6, and push-broom line-scanning solutions7 have gained popularity. However, these implementations typically require sequential acquisition over multiple wavelengths or spatial positions, leading to long measurement times and complex optomechanical arrangements, thus requiring long acquisition times and mechanical movements7,8. Commercial implementations of these techniques typically have image frame rates in the order of seconds due to the implemented scans. This represents a gap especially for microscopy related applications. In the microscopic regime, long acquisition times are particularly detrimental9, as they increase susceptibility to motion artefacts and impose limitations due to photodamage, such as photobleaching in fluorescence imaging10,11. These limitations indicate that there is still considerable room for improvement in terms of speed, flexibility, and accessibility of microscopic hyperspectral imaging systems.

In response to these limitations, coded-imaging strategies have emerged as compelling alternatives for multispectral and hyperspectral imaging3,12. Coded-aperture and structured-illumination schemes exploit optical multiplexing combined with computational reconstruction to improve the trade-offs between spatial, spectral resolutions, and acquisition time. In these approaches, spatial or spatio-spectral patterns are imposed on the illumination or detection planes, and the resulting multiplexed measurements are computationally unmixed to recover the underlying hyperspectral datacube. Coded-aperture snapshot spectral imagers, compressive hyperspectral cameras, and structured-illumination systems have demonstrated reduced acquisition times, improved light efficiency, and enhanced robustness to noise when compared with conventional scanning solutions12–14. Nevertheless, most of these architectures still rely on partial scanning in either the spatial or spectral domains, or they require custom optics and specialized detector arrays, which limit their adaptability to standard microscopy configurations and constrain their use in cost-sensitive or photodamage-sensitive environments.

Within the family of structured-illumination techniques, single-pixel imaging (SPI) and, in particular, single-pixel microscopy (SPM) have attracted considerable attention as flexible platforms for multispectral and hyperspectral imaging11,14–16. In SPM, a digital micromirror device (DMD) or related spatial light modulator is used to encode a sequence of structured illumination patterns that are demagnified onto the specimen, while a single-pixel detector collects the integrated intensity for each pattern. The patterns are typically drawn from a selected basis, such as Fourier17,18, Hadamard19–21, or other orthogonal or random ensembles11, and the object is reconstructed by applying the corresponding inverse transform to the measured intensity vector. This architecture decouples spatial encoding from detection, enabling the use of detectors with extended spectral coverage, high sensitivity, or advanced temporal features that are not readily available in conventional array sensors. As a result, SPM has been successfully applied to polarization imaging22,23, fluorescence lifetime measurements11,24, Raman spectroscopy25, phase imaging9,26, and low-light imaging, as well as to multispectral and hyperspectral modalities spanning from the ultraviolet to the terahertz27 and X-ray ranges28. Recent developments have also demonstrated its suitability for spectral heat‑map imaging, enabling spatially resolved thermal-spectral analysis across complex samples29. Moreover, SPI concepts have also been applied to quantum imaging with entangled photons, enabling spectral and spatial reconstructions beyond classical illumination limits30. For hyperspectral SPM, however, the most common implementation employs a spectrometer as the single-pixel detector14,31, recording a spectrum for each projected pattern. In these cases, the overall acquisition time is constrained not only by integration time but also by detector readout speed, internal processing latency, and data transfer bandwidth14. These factors limit the effective spectral scan rate achievable in practice, particularly when high pattern modulation frequencies are required14,32. Often commercial designs rely on narrow entrance slits or fiber coupling geometries, which limit throughput and impose alignment constraints that cannot be optimized for specific applications. These bottlenecks have significantly hindered the rapid implementations of hyperspectral SPM in practical microscopy.

In this work, is presented a hyperspectral single‑pixel microscopy (HySPM) platform that integrates high‑speed single‑pixel detection with a data‑fusion implementation to simultaneously overcome the acquisition‑time and spatial‑resolution limitations of current hyperspectral techniques. The optical layout replaces a conventional spectrometer with a compact, off‑the‑shelf configuration that disperses the sample light into spectral components recorded by a high‑speed line‑scan camera acting as a parallel array of bucket detectors. Synchronous operation of the DMD and line‑scan sensor enables sub‑second acquisition of complete spectral profiles for structured illumination patterns, with integration times on the order of tens of microseconds. Full Hadamard scans with 64×64 spatial patterns are completed in 0.82 s at a 10 kHz pattern rate, while up to 73 spectral bands are acquired in parallel with 4 nm spectral resolution, without reliance on compressive algorithms or mechanical scanning.

Building upon rapid spectral acquisition, the HySPM platform addresses the spatial‑resolution limitations inhere nt to the 64×64 single‑pixel grid. To overcome these constraints, it incorporates a data‑fusion implementation that integrates the low‑resolution hyperspectral datacube with high‑resolution monochrome images from a conventional camera. Related approaches based on pansharpening combine low‑resolution spectral data with high‑resolution panchromatic images to enhance spatial detail while preserving spectral fidelity and have been widely adopted in remote sensing and Raman microscopy33,34. This conceptual framework aligns with the data‑fusion strategy employed in this work. This framework enables hyperspectral reconstructions to be expanded from 64×64 to 605×605 pixels, enhancing spatial detail while preserving the spectral signature provided by the line‑scan detection. Altogether, this combined hardware–software strategy achieves sub‑second, high‑spectral‑resolution acquisitions with improved spatial detail, establishing a scalable and cost‑effective route for hyperspectral microscopy in dynamic, low‑light, and photodamage‑sensitive conditions.

Results

Hyperspectral setup and acquisition

The hardware for the proposed HySPM system is composed of a single-pixel microscope and a line-scan camera coupled with a custom-made spectrometer. Since fast modulation is desired, a DMD is implemented to encode the structured light scanning patterns35. Given that the DMD is a binary modulator, the Hadamard basis ℋ will be used in this work. The basis is also selected taking into account that microscopes often operate under low-signal conditions. In such scenarios, Hadamard-based SPI approaches have shown good performance due to their binary illumination patterns and differential measurement scheme, which enhance noise robustness and avoid quantization error36. To produce images in SPM each pattern Hk(x,y), from the basis is sequentially demagnified onto the sample plane s(x,y), while the resulting light intensity for each pattern, Ik, is collected using a bucket detector. The retrieval of the sample image is performed by computing the inverse Hadamard transformation H−1⋅ over the measured intensity vector37,38. In the hyperspectral case, the high spectral resolution capabilities of the proposed HySPM are achieved by replacing the single-pixel detector with the custom-made spectrometer. In this configuration, the proposed HySPM system allows fast and straightforward acquisition of intensity vectors that encode both spatial and spectral information I(λ,k).

In the system, the light enters into the microscope through two apertures that are used to align the light beam. The light beam hits the DMD, where the structured patterns are encoded, as is shown in the yellow region of the optical system in Fig. 137. The light reflected by the DMD is transmitted through a 4 F system followed by a conventional microscope composed of a tube lens and a microscope objective, as shown in Fig. 1. Between the tube lens and the microscope objective, a beamsplitter or a dichroic mirror is located depending on the imaging application: the beamsplitter is used for reflective imaging, whereas the dichroic mirror is employed for fluorescence imaging. This optical system projects a demagnified image of the structured light patterns onto the sample plane. A focus-tunable lens is located in the aperture plane of the 4 F system for adjusting the focus the patterns onto the sample plane38.

The light reflected or emitted by the sample is collected back by the microscope objective and passes through the tube lens. From this point, a flip mirror allows two detection paths to be selected. In the first path, the magnified image of the sample generated by the microscope is formed into a monochrome digital camera, where a high-resolution snapshot of the sample is recorded. In the second path, the light coming out from the microscope is collected into an optical fiber using an optical fiber collimator. Finally, the optical fiber is coupled with the custom-made spectrometer. There, the light is collimated by a lens and directed onto a transmission diffraction grating, separating the light into its spectral components. The light is focused on the line-scan array sensor by using a lens, as is shown in Fig. 1. It is important to note that, in this configuration, the image of the optical fiber core is formed on the sensor plane with a demagnification factor given by f3/f4. Therefore, the size of the optical fiber limits the spectral resolution of the system39.

For acquiring the sample information with the spectral setup, the output trigger of the DMD is connected to the trigger port of the line-scan camera. Once the optical setup is configured, the set of patterns of the 64×64 Hadamard basis is uploaded to the DMD, with a total of 8192 patterns, including complementary patterns to codify positive and negative values of the Hadamard functions. After projecting the structured light patterns onto the sample and acquiring the intensity coefficients for each spectral band, the sample image is retrieved via inverse Hadamard transformation in parallel for each wavelength. In this way, a set of high-spectral-resolution images is obtained, forming a hyperspectral data-cube.

HySPM characterization

To test the performance of the proposed HySPM system before applying data fusion, both reflective and fluorescent samples were imaged. As a reflective sample, a positive 1951 USAF resolution test target was selected. A Thorlabs HPLS243 plasma white-light source with broad spectral content was used to illuminate the DMD and generate the structured light patterns. For recording the spectral information, the pixels of the line-scan camera were binned every 28 pixels according to the analysis detailed in the Spectral characterization and calibration section, yielding 73 spectral channels with a spectral resolution of 4 nm. In this initial test, 166 mW from the light source were distributed across the active area of the DMD that encodes the 64×64 Hadamard patterns. This value corresponds to the total optical power measured at the DMD plane and is distributed across the full spectral bandwidth of the source. Due to spectral dispersion and the binary nature of the Hadamard patterns, the power reaching the sample is divided among wavelength channels and spatial illumination patterns, resulting in approximately 2 mW per spectral channel at the specimen plane. In addition, the structured illumination patterns are updated continuously at the DMD refresh rate, meaning that no point on the sample is illuminated continuously. This time varying illumination redistributes the optical power over the field of view in time, further reducing the instantaneous irradiance compared to a static, uniformly illuminated field. In these imaging conditions, only the reflective elements of the USAF target, which occupy a small area of the field of view, contribute to the collected signal, while the reflected flux from the glass substrate is negligible. This condition is deliberately chosen to demonstrate the performance of HySPM under low-signal conditions.

The acquired spectral information with the HySPM system is shown in Fig. 2a. In this image, each point along the vertical axis corresponds to one of the 73 spectral channels, while the horizontal axis corresponds to the sample response to the pattern projection in time. In this way, the horizontal axis represents the light response acquired from the sample as it is sequentially illuminated by the Hadamard patterns, alternating between their positive and negative. In (b), a cross section of the vertical axis is illustrated, showing the spectral light-sample response for the first projected pattern. To produce the HySPM image the line-scan camera integration time was set to 50 µs, while the patterns were encoded with the DMD at 10 kHz. Under these conditions, the complete 64×64×73 intensity dataset vector I(λ,k) is acquired in 0.82 s. The spatial dimension of the hyperspectral single‑pixel reconstructions was set to 64×64 pixels as a practical trade‑off between acquisition time and acquired HySPM spatial information. With this size, the full 8192 positive and negative Hadamard patterns can be acquired within a single image of the line‑scan camera, whose maximum lines length is 12,288 lines, where each line corresponds to each projected pattern, as shown in (a). At the operating rates used in this work, a complete 64×64 Hadamard basis can be recorded in less than a second without requiring compressive sampling for the resolution USAF test. Larger the projected pattern dimensions would increase the number of required measurements and therefore the acquisition time, while smaller dimensions would reduce the spatial information available for subsequent data‑fusion processing. The 64×64 basis thus represents a balanced choice that preserves sufficient spatial structure in the hyperspectral data while maintaining sub‑second acquisition times, and it is consistent with common practice in hyperspectral single‑pixel imaging.

The system was further evaluated for fluorescence imaging using a mixture of microspheres, including 1 µm beads (ThermoFisher F13083)40 and 15 µm beads (ThermoFisher F21011)41. The smaller beads exhibit a maximum absorption near 580 nm, leading to an emission peak at 605 nm, whereas the larger beads show a maximum absorption around 515 nm with a corresponding emission peak at 534 nm. For these experiments, the 64×64 Hadamard patterns were encoded with a binning of 2×2 micromirrors, and the DMD active area was illuminated with 21 mW of optical power. The pixels of the line-scan camera were binned every 36 pixels, resulting in a spectral resolution of 5.3 nm, providing a total of 57 spectral channels within the selected range, where only 24 contain the fluorescence response of the sample. Subsequently, the inverse Hadamard basis was computed as previously described, yielding the spectral response of the fluorescent microspheres, as shown in Fig. 3. In the spectral images, two distinct fluorescence spectra can be clearly distinguished: the 15 µm bead exhibits emission across the entire spectral range, whereas the clusters of 1 µm spheres show their spectral response starting from 577 nm. These results confirm that HySPM is capable of retrieving hyperspectral information from fluorescent microscopic samples under low to moderate excitation powers and within acquisition times of the order of one second for a full 64×64 Hadamard basis.

Altogether, the reflective and fluorescent experiments demonstrate that the proposed HySPM configuration can deliver spectrally rich 64×64×73 and 64x64x57 hyperspectral datacubes, respectively, in 0.82 s and 1.02 s of acquisition times. However, the spatial resolution of the reconstructions remains inherently limited by the 64×64 pattern grid, which restricts the visualization of the smallest structures of the resolution target and the fine morphology of the fluorescent beads. This limitation motivates the implementation of the data fusion framework described in the following section, aimed at enhancing spatial resolution while preserving hyperspectral content.

Enhanced HySPM using data fusion techniques

A data fusion model was implemented for combining hyperspectral images with low spatial resolution yLRλ with the high-resolution image yHR recorded by the array camera42. This model considers a hypercube x with a high spectral and spatial resolution, that satisfies the measured spectral information yLRλ=RLx}, where RL{⋅} represents a down sampling operator in the spatial domain. That model also needs to satisfy that yHR=Sx}, where S{⋅} represents a spectral integration operator. where ∣∣⋅∣∣2, is the L2-norm, α, β, γ are regularization parameters, Br⋅ is a blurring operation, and RH{⋅} represents an up-sampling operation in the spatial domain. In this way, the first two terms of the model describe how the energy is physically distributed during the recording process for the same sample. The first term reflects how HySPM acquires the spread the light from the sample over a coarse spatial grid, which integrates and therefore loses high‑frequency spatial details while preserving the spectral distribution of the energy. Conversely, the second term captures how the energy is distributed in the spatial domain with higher spatial fidelity, but at the expense of losing the spectral information. Finally, the last term of the cost function corresponds to an equalization of the sample information, whereby the function prioritizes regions with higher sample density and penalizes those lacking data. Further details regarding the implementation of the data fusion algorithm are provided in the Supplementary Note 1: data fusion model. The data fusion model was solved using automatic differentiation for gradients computation43,44, and the Adam solver for the optimization, ensuring fast and stable convergence45. This optimization yields a solution in which the final energy distribution is reconstructed by combining the complementary information provided by the HySPM measurements and the monochromatic camera, while ensuring that the overall energy is preserved.

Discussion

The HySPM system presented and validated in this work shows a significant decrease in the acquisition time for hyperspectral single-pixel pattern acquisition in microscopy. In reflection mode, the system retrieved 64×64×73 hyperspectral datacubes with 4 nm spectral resolution in only 0.82 s, where less than 2 mW per spectral channel contributed to spectral information acquisition. In fluorescence mode, microspheres were recorded in over 1 s with 24 emission channels at 5.3 nm resolution, confirming that HySPM can operate efficiently across different imaging modalities without reliance on compressive techniques or mechanical scanning.

Beyond acquisition speed, the data‑fusion implementation substantially enhanced spatial resolution. For the USAF resolution chart, reconstructions were expanded from 64×64 to 605×605 pixels, improving lateral resolution from 3.1 to 0.8 µm while preserving spectral fidelity across all channels. In fluorescent bead imaging, the fusion strategy revealed the morphology of individual 1 µm beads that were indistinguishable in the baseline reconstructions, while maintaining the spectral signatures of the emission bands. From a methodological perspective, the proposed data‑fusion framework is closely aligned with classical pansharpening approaches, which enhance spatial detail by combining low‑resolution spectral information with high‑resolution intensity measurements33. This conceptual connection opens the door to future developments, where more advanced pansharpening models or learning‑based fusion strategies could be integrated into the HySPM architecture to further improve spatial resolution and robustness under low‑signal conditions. These results demonstrate that HySPM simultaneously delivers sub‑second, high‑spectral‑resolution acquisitions and high‑fidelity spatial detail, establishing a robust platform for hyperspectral microscopy in dynamic, low‑light, and photodamage‑sensitive conditions.

Altogether, these results confirm that HySPM delivers spectrally rich datacubes in sub‑second scale acquisition times, while the data‑fusion framework enhances the initial hyperspectral single-pixel spatial resolution by a factor of 3.8, for the proposed system, without compromising spectral fidelity. To situate these achievements within the broader landscape of single‑pixel imaging, Table 1 summarizes the pattern acquisition time and spectral performance reported in recent contributions related to this work. Spectrometer-based approaches are the most common methods for multi- and hyperspectral applications, providing a larger number of spectral channels but limiting acquisition times to longer than 1.4 ms14. Pushbroom approaches, while achieving acquisition times similar to the proposed acquisition modality, require precise and synchronized mechanical movement during the acquisition process, which increases system complexity. Other approaches, such as using multiple illumination sources46 and simultaneous spatial and spectral codification approach13, reduce acquisition time by using high-speed single-pixel detectors to acquire more than one spectral component during each pattern projection. However, these approaches are highly demanding for microscopy implementation: the first one, based on different LED illuminations, faces system complexity when the number of spectral channels increases; while the second one, using the simultaneous spatial and spectral codification approach, suffers from significant light losses due to the double pass by the DMD one for the spatial codification and the second for the spectral one. However, the proposed HySPM system exhibits acquisition times in the order of hundreds of microseconds in a motionless approach, with enough spectral channels for reflective inspection and even some fluorescent emission detection microscopy applications.

In summary, in this work, the design and application of a high‑speed hyperspectral acquisition system for single‑pixel microscopy, enhanced through a data fusion implementation has been presented. An off‑the‑shelf line‑scan camera was employed to acquire hyperspectral information in both reflection and fluorescence modalities. The performance of the custom‑made spectrometer, leveraging the high sensitivity of the line‑scan camera, was validated through the acquisition of reflective and fluorescent microscopic samples. The achieved integration time allows full sampling of 64×64 pixel images in approximately 0.8 s for a reflective sample without the use of compression techniques. Finally, the data fusion implementation has substantially enhanced the output of the initial acquisition by effectively combining the high spectral resolution of the line scan camera with the high spatial resolution of the monochromatic array sensor. This integration connects complementary information, and has led to reconstructed images with higher resolution, improved quality and interpretability through the clear identification of sample information, background, and noise. This approach demonstrates the feasibility of obtaining hyperspectral resolved and high spatial resolution microscopy in less than a second for a reflective sample, without compromising acquisition speed, thereby enabling more accurate identification and analysis of fine structural details across spectral channels.

Materials and methods

Hyperspectral single-pixel microscope setup

The optical system described in the results section was built using a Vialux V-7001 DMD, having micromirrors with a side length of 13.67 μm, for codifying structured patterns. The 4 F system was built with two achromatic lenses with 150 mm focal length, a 150 mm focal length lens as tube lens and an EWLD 20x Nikon microscope objective with a 0.4 numerical aperture. The single-pixel microscope was built following the instructions reported in ref. 37. The light was collected using a reflective collimator and an optical fiber with 400 μm core diameter.

The spectral acquisition system was composed of two achromatic lenses in an afocal configuration, with 100 mm and 50 mm focal lengths respectively. The optical fiber was located in the back focal plane of the first lens while the line-scan camera was located in the front focal length of the second lens, for a demagnification ratio of the fiber size over the line camera of 2. The dispersive element is a visible transmission grating with 600 groves/mm. A Basler raL2048-48gm line-scan camera was used for the spectral signal acquisition, and was located at the back focal plane of the second achromatic lens, as was shown in Fig. 1.

The DMD was set to display structured light patterns at 10 kHz for reflection experiments and 8 kHz for the fluorescence experiments. Since the DMD was operated in free‑running mode, it served as the source of the trigger for the camera. The trigger signal is generated when the micromirrors are tilting, taking approximately 32 μs including the transmission delay from the DMD to the line‑scan camera. When the trigger reaches the camera, an additional 6.5 μs are required before the exposure begins, followed by 6.2 μs to complete the acquisition.

Spectral characterization and calibration of the system

For the proposed approach, the spectral resolution is limited by the image size of the optical fiber onto the linear scan camera plane. The smaller the size, the smaller the spectral resolution, but also the less light reaches the sensor. For the previous experiments, a 400 μm diameter core fiber and two lenses of 100 mm and 50 mm of focal length were used for dispersing and focusing the light, respectively. In this way, the image of the core fiber onto the sensor plane has a size of 200 μm of diameter. According to that, to reach the maximum possible spectral resolution while also increasing the collected energy per spectral band and using all the camera pixels for light acquisition, the acquired spectral image information is binned each 28 pixels of the digital camera, achieving a spectral resolution of 4 nm and up to 73 spectral channels for this configuration. This represents the maximum resolvable spectral capability of the system. However, in cases where high spectral resolution is not required and the system operates under low‑power conditions, additional pixel binning can be applied to increase the amount of light contributing to each spectral band. This enhances the signal‑to‑noise ratio, as a larger spectral range of the available light is integrated during the analysis. This strategy was applied to the spectral fluorescence beads, where 36 pixels were integrated for each spectral band.

A Xenon white-light lamp was directly connected into the custom-made spectrometer and eight spectral filters (450 nm, 488 nm, 500 nm, 514 nm, 550 nm, 570 nm, 600 nm and 650 nm), with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 10 nm, were used to characterize the spectral range of the signal acquired with the line-scan camera.

Microbead deposition procedure

Microbeads were prepared and deposited on standard glass microscope slides under sterile conditions. A sterile 1 mL syringe was used to transfer 0.1 mL of the microbead stock suspension into a sterile container. Subsequently, 2 mL of distilled water were added, and the mixture was gently homogenized to obtain a uniform dilution. Using a second sterile syringe, 0.1 mL of the diluted suspension was dispensed onto a clean glass slide. The droplet was allowed to dry naturally at room temperature for approximately one hour, without the use of heating plates or forced convection. After drying, the microbeads adhered directly to the glass substrate, forming a single, planar distribution without immersion in any residual medium.
