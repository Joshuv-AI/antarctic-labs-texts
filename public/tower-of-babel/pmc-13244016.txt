Constrictive Pericarditis Due to Metastatic Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Jai Nagpal, Shreya Arvind, Abhigna Kolupoti, Waseem Farooq, Agnes S Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConstrictive pericarditis is an uncommon but serious condition typically caused by idiopathic inflammation, prior cardiac surgery, or radiation. Malignancy is a rare etiology.Case SummaryA 62-year-old man with a history of tongue squamous cell carcinoma on long-term pembrolizumab presented with dyspnea, orthopnea, leg swelling, and ascites. History of Presentation

A 62-year-old man with a history of tongue squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) with cervical lymph node metastasis presented with progressively worsening exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, and lower extremity edema over a week. Vitals were notable for tachycardia (heart rate: 115 beats/min), tachypnea (respiratory rate: 22 breaths/min), narrow pulse pressure (blood pressure: 138/115 mm Hg), oxygen saturation of 96% on room air, and body temperature of 36.3 °C. Physical examination revealed chronically ill appearance, jugular venous distention with a Kussmaul sign, decreased breath sounds in the left lower lung field, ascites, hepatomegaly, and marked bilateral pitting edema in the lower extremities.

Past Medical History

The patient's history included SCC of the base of the tongue with cervical lymph node involvement, diagnosed 11 years prior and initially treated with chemoradiation including cisplatin and radiation therapy, which achieved remission. He experienced regional recurrence 3 years later and was enrolled in a clinical trial of M7824. This bifunctional anti-PD-L1/TGF-β trap fusion protein was discontinued a year later owing to immune-related liver injury and pericarditis requiring corticosteroids. After resolution of these immune-related adverse events, he was rechallenged with pembrolizumab given disease progression. Current guidelines support immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) rechallenge after resolution of grade 2 adverse events, and class-switching from one ICI to another may reduce the risk of recurrence.1 The patient then received treatment with carboplatin, paclitaxel, and pembrolizumab, followed by the addition of 5-fluorouracil, and then maintenance with pembrolizumab when he presented with the right heart failure symptoms as described above.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included constrictive pericarditis, cardiac tamponade, or congestive heart failure related to pembrolizumab-induced myocarditis.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia, low-voltage QRS complexes, and nonspecific T-wave abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed features consistent with constrictive physiology (Figure 1), including ventricular interdependence with excessive respirophasic variation in transvalvular inflow velocities, abnormal diastolic interventricular septal motion (Videos 1 and 2), and plethoric inferior vena cava. Pulse-wave Doppler showed >25% inspiratory decrease in mitral inflow E-wave velocity with a reciprocal >40% inspiratory increase in tricuspid inflow E-wave velocity. Tissue Doppler imaging demonstrated preserved medial mitral annular e′ velocity (13.9 cm/s) compared with a reduced lateral e′ velocity (8.8 cm/s), consistent with annulus reversus.

Given concern for constrictive physiology, right heart catheterization was performed (Figure 2), which demonstrated an elevated mean right atrial pressure of 19 mm Hg with prominent x- and y-descents. Simultaneous right and left ventricular pressure tracings revealed a classic dip-and-plateau (“square root”) pattern during diastole, along with near-equalization of right and left ventricular end-diastolic pressures (∼20 mm Hg) and respirophasic discordance of ventricular pressures, confirming ventricular interdependence (Figure 2, Table 1).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest demonstrated irregular circumferential pericardial thickening (Figure 3A). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) revealed irregular, nodular, diffuse pericardial thickening of >8 mm on T1-weighted turbo spin-echo sequences (Figure 3B), associated interventricular septal bounce, dilated inferior vena cava, and hepatic veins (Figure 3C). Real-time free-breathing cine imaging demonstrated respirophasic septal shift consistent with ventricular interdependence (Video 3). T2-weighted dark-blood imaging showed increased pericardial signal intensity, consistent with active inflammation (Figure 3D), while phase-sensitive inversion recovery sequences with late gadolinium enhancement demonstrated avid circumferential pericardial enhancement, indicating active inflammatory pericarditis (Figures 3E and 3F).

Management

Constrictive pericarditis with evidence of active inflammation on CMR was initially managed using anti-inflammatory therapy with colchicine and corticosteroids. Differential diagnosis included immune-related pericarditis in the setting of ICI therapy versus malignant involvement of the pericardium. Given high suspicion for cardiac metastasis, positron emission tomography (PET)/CT was performed, which showed fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid nodular pericardial thickening (shown in Figure 4). Thus, the patient underwent pericardiectomy and pericardial biopsy. Intraoperative findings included a thickened, nodular pericardium with tumor invasion into the underlying myocardium, which precluded complete resection. Histopathological analysis of the pericardial tissue confirmed the presence of poorly differentiated carcinoma, consistent with metastatic SCC originating from the tongue.

PET/CT shows FDG-avid left hilar lymphadenopathy (1.6 cm, SUVmax: 4.8) and nodular circumferential pericardial thickening with intense uptake (SUVmax: 9.7), highly suspicious for malignant pericardial metastasis. Outcome and Follow-Up

After the pericardiectomy and pericardial biopsy, the patient remained hospitalized with persistent hypoxic respiratory failure. Despite intensive medical management, including diuretics and anti-inflammatory therapy, his condition continued to deteriorate. The multidisciplinary care team, together with the patient and his family, held a comprehensive discussion regarding prognosis and treatment options. It was determined that prognosis was poor and that additional chemotherapy would not provide meaningful benefit. Based on clinical recommendations and the patient's preferences, the decision was made to transition to hospice care.

Discussion

Constrictive pericarditis most often results from idiopathic inflammation, viral pericarditis, or prior cardiac surgery or radiation; malignancy remains a rare but important etiology.2 Furthermore, patients on immune checkpoint modulating agents such as pembrolizumab are at risk of developing cancer therapy–related pericarditis, which can have a similar clinical presentation.2 In the present case, advanced imaging, including CMR and FDG-PET, revealed marked, irregular pericardial thickening and FDG-avid nodules. Still, these modalities were unable to definitively distinguish between immune-mediated inflammation and malignant infiltration. Final diagnosis was only achieved after pericardial biopsy, which identified poorly differentiated metastatic SCC.

Metastatic cardiac involvement most commonly arises from lung, breast, melanoma, and hematologic malignancies.3 Head and neck SCC rarely metastasizes to the heart or pericardium, but when it does, the clinical course is aggressive.4 In one case report, a patient with SCC of the head and neck with myocardial involvement experienced rapid progression despite treatment and was discharged to hospice shortly after detection of cardiac metastases, findings consistent with poor prognosis in advanced metastatic disease.3

In our patient, the possibility of ICI-induced pericarditis was raised. ICI-related pericarditis typically presents early, days to weeks after 1 or 2 doses of ICI and is often accompanied by other immune-related adverse events.1 The median onset of immune-related adverse events is 28 to 30 days.1 ICI therapy causing constrictive pericarditis is rarely reported in the literature. Generally, constrictive pericarditis can occur along a variable timeline, ranging from months to years to decades after an episode of acute pericarditis. Histopathology, when available, shows lymphocytic infiltration without malignant cells. Response to immunosuppressive therapy (eg, corticosteroids) is common, and discontinuation of ICIs is often required for severe cases, as recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and the American Society of Clinical Oncology.1 In our patient, prolonged pembrolizumab exposure and lack of response to anti-inflammatory treatment made ICI-related pericarditis less likely, raising suspicion for an alternative etiology.

Pericardial metastasis is rarely identified before autopsy. Approximately 30% of patients may develop clinical signs and symptoms, such as dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and/or peripheral edema, often related to pericardial effusion. As the disease progresses, larger effusions can lead to cardiac tamponade and hemodynamic compromise. In rare cases, malignant constrictive pericarditis can produce more overt signs of predominant right-sided heart failure and venous congestion, such as elevated jugular venous pressure, Kussmaul sign, peripheral edema, and ascites.5

Establishing metastasis as the cause of constrictive pericarditis is a challenge, as physical examination and imaging findings can be nonspecific.6 Pericardial fluid cytology is the most commonly used diagnostic tool and has high specificity when malignant cells are identified. However, it is limited by low sensitivity, as approximately 25% of pericardial effusions with benign cytology can be caused by an underlying neoplasm. The possibility of false-negative cytology necessitates consideration of pericardial biopsy to improve diagnostic yield.5,6

In this case, the intense pericardial FDG uptake on PET/CT was suspicious for malignancy but not definitive, as this can also be seen with severe autoimmune pericarditis. In malignant pericarditis with myocardial tumor invasion, the high FDG uptake is present not only on the pericardium but also the myocardium. In benign inflammatory pericarditis, uptake is limited to the pericardium without myocardial involvement. Malignant cardiac and pericardial lesions typically demonstrate higher maximum standardized uptake values compared to benign lesions.7 Although PET/CT provided valuable information suggesting an aggressive malignant process, it has limitations in differentiating ICI-related pericarditis from malignant pericardial involvement, as both demonstrate increased FDG uptake.7 Therefore, tissue diagnosis via pericardial biopsy remained essential for definitive differentiation in this challenging case.

Pericardial metastasis, particularly when associated with constrictive or constrictive-effusive physiology and myocardial invasion, carries a very poor prognosis, with reported median survival of only a few months. Early detection is crucial for more optimal oncologic therapy, which could slow disease progression and improve symptom control.

For patients with established constrictive or constrictive-effusive physiology, surgical pericardiectomy is a definitive form of palliative treatment. However, it has been reported to carry an early in-hospital mortality rate as high as 22%, so careful patient selection with risk-benefit analysis is needed, especially in those with advanced cancer.5,8 For patients with hemodynamic compromise due to cardiac tamponade or significant effusion-related symptoms, pericardiocentesis is first-line therapy. In patients with frequent recurrent symptomatic effusions, a pericardial window can be considered.9 Systemic chemotherapy is used when the primary tumor is treatment responsive. Though reversing an advanced malignant pericardial disease is unlikely, controlling the underlying cancer is associated with better overall symptom control. A multicenter cohort study found that combining systemic chemotherapy with a pericardial window was associated with better survival than chemotherapy alone.9 For patients with effusive recurrence, local intrapericardial therapy with agents like cisplatin and bleomycin has shown success as palliative adjuncts.10 Currently, no standardized treatment guidelines exist for managing cardiac metastasis.2 Diagnosis and treatment must be tailored based on presentation, clinical status, and prognosis.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of considering malignancy as a cause of constrictive pericarditis in patients with cancer. Establishing the diagnosis and etiology of constrictive pericarditis is critical for optimal management.
