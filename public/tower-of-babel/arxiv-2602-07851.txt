Best Approximation Optimal Control for Infeasible Double Integrator and Douglas–Rachford Algorithm
Regina S. Burachik, Bethany I. Caldwell, C. Yalçın Kaya, Walaa M. Moursi

Abstract
We consider the problem of finding (in some sense) the best approximation control for an infeasible double integrator. The control function is constrained by upper and lower bounds that are too tight and thus cause infeasibility. The infeasibility is characterized by a gap function (representing the separation between two constraint sets) whose squared ℒ 2 {\cal L}^{2} -norm is to be minimized to find the best approximation control solution. First, we review the existing results for problems involving a general linear control system. Then, for the infeasible double integrator problem, we present an analytical solution for the bang–bang control with at most one switching. The infinite-dimensional optimization problem is reduced to the problem of solving two algebraic equations in two variables, to compute the switching time and gap function. We discuss numerical approaches to solving the system of equations. Finally, we describe the (relaxed) Douglas–Rachford algorithm for the double integrator problem and carry out numerical experiments to illustrate the implementation of the algorithm and test performance.



Key words
:
Optimal control, Douglas–Rachford algorithm, Infeasible problem, Inconsistent problem, Gap function, Bang–bang control, Double integrator.

Introduction

The simple differential equation
\ddot{y}(t)=u(t)
is called the
double integrator
since the solution
y(t)
is obtained by integrating
u(t)
twice. For a mechanical system,
y(t)
,
\dot{y}(t)
, and
\ddot{y}(t)
denote, respectively, the position, velocity, and acceleration of a unit point mass moving along a straight line on a frictionless plane, and
u(t)
is the force (or control) acting on the point mass, all at a time instant
t
. The double integrator also models the analogous rotational-mechanical and electrical systems
[
21
]
.

Optimal control of the double integrator, which is concerned with finding a control function
u
that minimizes a functional, such as “energy” or duration of time, is simple and yet rich enough to be studied when introducing and illustrating many basic and new concepts or when testing new numerical approaches in optimal control; see, for example,
[
1
,
7
,
19
,
10
,
17
]
. In this sense, the role of the double integrator is similar to that of the Dahlquist test problem, which is widely used for the stability and error analysis of ODE solvers
[
15
,
14
]
.

If there is no constraint on the control
u(t)
, a closed-form analytical solution can be easily written for the
minimum-energy control
of the double integrator. Specifically speaking, the solution for the optimal control
u
minimizing the squared
{\cal L}^{2}
-norm of
u
, subject to the double integrator, can be expressed as an affine function in
t
with coefficients defined by initial and terminal positions and velocities
[
1
]
. This constitutes the building block for cubic splines. However, if the control
u(t)
is constrained (typically by means of upper and lower bounds), then a solution (although known to be a concatenation of constant and affine functions) can only be found numerically.

The Douglas–Rachford (DR) algorithm was first used to find a numerical solution to the control-constrained minimum-energy control of the double integrator in
[
1
]
, and it was shown that the DR algorithm was significantly more efficient (in terms of CPU time and accuracy) than the
first-discretize-then-optimize approach
, in which the optimal control problem is discretized and the resulting large-scale problem is solved by standard optimization software. Later, in
[
7
]
, an extension of the DR algorithm with a “dual step” was also used to solve the dual of the optimal control problem (as well as the primal problem) involving the double integrator, so that the necessary conditions of optimality for the control can be numerically verified.

In real life situations, the control
u(t)
, which typically represents the resources available to practitioners to achieve an optimal outcome, might be overly restricted, making the lower or upper bound (or both) of the control function too tight, thus resulting in an
infeasible
dynamical system and, in turn, an
infeasible
or
inconsistent
optimal control problem. Such infeasible optimal control problems involving general linear ODEs, including the double integrator as a special case, have previously been studied in
[
11
]
. However, only the first-discretize-then-optimize approach was used in
[
11
]
to obtain a
best approximation solution
(such that a measure of infeasibility is minimized). In
[
11
]
, the AMPL–Ipopt optimization suite
[
16
,
22
]
was implemented to solve large-scale optimization problems arising from the time-discretization of various example optimal control problems. One of the novelties of the current paper is the study of the DR algorithm applied to an infeasible optimal control problem in Section
4
. A preliminary application of the DR algorithm to infeasible optimal control problems can be found in
[
13
, Section 6.7]
, where the minimum-energy control of the double integrator and the damped-harmonic oscillator were considered. In
[
13
, Section 6.7]
performance comparisons amongst the relaxed DR algorithm, the Peaceman–Rachford algorithm and the AMPL–Ipopt suite are also included. In the current paper, we study the infeasible optimal control of the double integrator in more detail.

In
[
11
]
, the infeasible optimal control of the double integrator with
|u(t)|\leq a
,
a
a positive constant and specified initial and terminal states is considered where the infeasibility of the problem is caused by
a
being too small. In
[
11
, Theorem 5]
, a complete analytical
best approximation solution
to this problem is presented for the
critically feasible
case with
a=a_{c}
such that the problem is feasible for
a=a_{c}
but infeasible for any
a<a_{c}
. Another novelty of the present paper is the complete analytical best approximation solution to the infeasible optimal control of the double integrator with
a<a_{c}
, as described in Theorem
2
. Incidentally, the proof of Theorem
2
turns out to be more challenging than that of
[
11
, Theorem 5]
.

In
[
11
]
, the constraint set is split into two, namely the
affine set
defined by the general linear dynamics with the specified boundary conditions and the
box
defined by the control constrained by simple bounds. When the intersection of these two sets is empty (notably because of the tight bounds on the control), the optimal control problem becomes infeasible. The
gap
between the two constraint sets is defined as the function that minimizes the
{\cal L}^{2}
-distance between these sets. Then
[
11
, Theorem 3]
states that the best approximation control that belongs to the box is of
bang–bang
type (i.e., the control function switches between the upper and lower bounds) with an unknown number of switchings and provides an expression for the gap function.

Theorem
2
presented in the present paper asserts that the best approximation control is of bang–bang type with at most one switching, and the switching time and the gap function can be found by solving a system of two polynomial equations in two variables. In other words, the switching time can be obtained with very high accuracy; so can the (affine) gap function. We also prove an asymptotic result for the switching time as
a
tends to zero, which might have practical value when
a
is very small. We consider a case study and investigate the solvability (in a reasonable number of iterations) of this system of equations by constructing the colour-coded number of iterations required to reach a solution of these equations when the Newton method and a generalized Newton method are used.

In Section
4
, we consider the same case study and compute the errors for the DR algorithm using the accurate switching times found as described above. Our rationale behind the formulation of the DR algorithm for the double integrator problem here is to use it in the future for problems other than those involving the double integrator, for which an analytical solution cannot be obtained.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide a review of the setting and results in
[
11
]
. In Section
3
, we describe the optimal control problem involving the double integrator and present the results on the bang–bang optimal control, the switching time and the gap function, and carry out a case study. We describe the DR algorithm in Section
4
, and present numerical experiments involving the same case study as in Section
3
. Finally, we provide concluding remarks in Section
5
.

2
Review of the Case With General Linear Dynamics

In this section, we provide a review of the setting and the results in
[
11
]
for the case where the dynamics are given by linear state differential equations in
x
, and
u
appears linearly. Obviously, here the double integrator is a special case.

2.1
The setting

Consider an optimal control problem with the aim of finding a control
u
, which minimizes the functional

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f_{0}(x(t),u(t),t)\,dt\,,

(1)

subject to the differential equation constraints

\dot{x}(t)=A(t)\,x(t)+B(t)\,u(t)\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,

(2)

with
\dot{x}:=dx/dt
, and the boundary conditions

\varphi(x(0),x(1))=0\,.

(3)

The time horizon is set to
[0,1]
, but without loss of generality, it can be taken as any interval
[t_{0},t_{f}]
, with
t_{0}
and
t_{f}
specified. The integrand
f_{0}:{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{n}\times{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m}\times[0,1]\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}_{+}
is continuous. Define the
state variable vector

x:[0,1]\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{n}
with
x(t):=(x_{1}(t)\,\ldots,x_{n}(t))\in{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{n}
and the
control variable vector

u:[0,1]\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m}
with
u(t):=(u_{1}(t)\,\ldots,u_{m}(t))\in{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m}
.
The time-varying matrices
A:[0,1]\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{n\times n}
and
B:[0,1]\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{n\times m}
are continuous. The vector function
\varphi:{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{2n}\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{r}
, with
\varphi(x(0),x(1)):=(\varphi_{1}(x(0),x(1)),\ldots,\varphi_{r}(x(0),x(1)))\in{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{r}
, is affine.

In practical situations, it is realistic to restrict the control function
u
, for example, to impose simple bounds on the components of
u(t)
; namely,

\underline{a}_{i}(t)\leq u_{i}(t)\leq\overline{a}_{i}(t)\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,

(4)

where
\underline{a}_{i},\overline{a}_{i}:[0,1]\to{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}
are continuous, with
\underline{a}_{i}(t)\leq\overline{a}_{i}(t)
, for all
t\in[0,1]
,
i=1,\ldots,m
.

The objective functional in (
1
) and the constraints in (
2
)–(
3
) and (
4
) can be put together to pose the optimal control problem in a concise form as follows.

\mbox{(P) }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{u(\cdot)}&\ \ \displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f_{0}(x(t),u(t),t)\,dt\\[11.38109pt]
\mbox{subject to}&\ \ \dot{x}(t)=A(t)\,x(t)+B(t)\,u(t)\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\ \ \varphi(x(0),x(1))=0\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\ \ \underline{a}_{i}(t)\leq u_{i}(t)\leq\overline{a}_{i}(t)\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,\ \ i=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{array}\right.

We split the constraints of Problem (P) into two sets:

\displaystyle{\cal A}:=\big\{u\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m})\ |\ \exists x\in W^{1,2}([0,1];{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{n})\mbox{ which solves }

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\dot{x}(t)=A(t)\,x(t)+B(t)\,u(t)\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\varphi(x(0),x(1))=0\big\}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle{\cal B}:=\big\{u\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m})\ |\ \underline{a}_{i}(t)\leq u_{i}(t)\leq\overline{a}_{i}(t)\,,\ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,\ i=1,\ldots,m\big\}\,,

(6)

where
W^{1,2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{q})
is the Sobolev space of absolutely continuous functions, namely,

W^{1,2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{q}):=\left\{z\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{q})\,|\,\dot{z}:=dz/dt\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{q})\right\},

endowed with the norm

\|z\|_{W^{1,2}}:=(\|z\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2}+\|\dot{z}\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2})^{1/2}.

In the particular case where the objective function
f_{0}
is quadratic in
x
and
u
, Problem (P) is called a
linear quadratic
(LQ) optimal control problem.
We assume that the control system
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+B(t)u(t)
is
controllable
—see the definition of
componentwise controllability
in Section
2.3
, which is a stronger version of controllability. Then there exists a (possibly not unique)
u(\cdot)
such that the boundary-value problem given in
{\cal A}
has a solution
x(\cdot)
. In other words,
{\cal A}\neq\varnothing
. Also, clearly,
{\cal B}\neq\varnothing
. Recall that
\varphi
is affine, so the constraint set
{\cal A}
is an
affine subspace
. We note that by
[
6
, Corollary 1]
,

{\cal A}\ \ \mbox{is closed}.

(7)

Given that
{\cal B}
is a
box
, the constraint sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
turn out to be two closed and convex sets in a Hilbert space. Using the fact that every sequence that converges in
{\cal L}^{2}
has a subsequence that converges pointwise, it can be directly checked that
{\cal B}
is closed in
{\cal L}^{2}(0,1;{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m})
. It will be convenient to use the expression

B(t)u(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(t)\,u_{i}(t)\,,

where
b_{i}(t)
is the
i
th column of the matrix
B(t)
, interpreted as the column vector associated with the
i
th control component
u_{i}
.

If
{\cal A}\cap{\cal B}\neq\varnothing
, then one has a
feasible
optimal control problem. The
feasibility problem
is posed as one of finding an element in
{\cal A}\cap{\cal B}
, namely:

\mbox{Find\ \ }u\in{\cal A}\cap{\cal B}\,.

(8)

However, if
{\cal A}\cap{\cal B}=\varnothing
then the problem is said to be
infeasible
. Obviously, the feasibility problem in (
8
) has no solution in this case, but in Section
2.4
we will recall the problem of finding (in some sense) a
best approximation solution
and summarize the results obtained in
[
11
]
.

2.2
Problem of minimizing gap

Given the LQ control problem and the definitions of the constraint sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
in (
5
)–(
6
), we consider the case where
{\cal A}\cap{\cal B}=\varnothing
.
A
best approximation pair

(u_{\cal A}^{*},u_{\cal B}^{*})\in{\cal A}\times{\cal B}
is one that solves

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{\cal A}\in{\cal A}\\
u_{\cal B}\in{\cal B}\end{subarray}}\ \ \displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\|u_{\cal A}-u_{\cal B}\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2}\,,

(9)

where
\|\cdot\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}
is the
{{\cal L}^{2}}
norm. Define the
gap
(
function
)
vector
(see
[
3
]
)

v:=u_{\cal A}-u_{\cal B},\,\qquad(u_{\cal A},u_{\cal B})\in{\cal A}\times{\cal B}.

(10)

Here, the
{\cal L}^{2}
-distance
\|u_{\cal A}-u_{\cal B}\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}
is referred to as the
gap
.

Using
u_{\cal A}=v+u_{\cal B}
and the definitions of
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
, the problem of gap-minimization in (
9
) can be rewritten in the format of a standard
optimal control problem
as follows.

\mbox{(Pf) }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{v(\cdot),u_{\cal B}(\cdot)}&\ \ \displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|_{2}^{2}\,dt\\[5.69054pt]
\mbox{subject to}&\ \ \displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(t)(v_{i}(t)+u_{{\cal B},i}(t))\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\ \ \varphi(x(0),x(1))=0\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\ \ \underline{a}_{i}(t)\leq u_{{\cal B},i}(t)\leq\overline{a}_{i}(t)\,,\ \ \mbox{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,1]\,,\ \ i=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{array}\right.

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm. Problem (Pf) is an optimal control problem with two control variable vectors, namely
v
and
u_{\cal B}
, where
v(t):=(v_{1}(t),\ldots,v_{m}(t))\in{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m}
and
u_{\cal B}(t):=(u_{{\cal B},1}(t),\ldots,u_{{\cal B},m}(t))\in{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m}
.

With the introduction of the new unconstrained (or free) control
v
, Problem (Pf) is feasible. We further note that Problem (Pf), having a quadratic objective functional and linear equality and inequality constraints, is convex.

2.3
Componentwise controllability

We call the control system
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+B(t)u(t)

controllable w.r.t.
u_{i}
on
[0,1]
if given any initial state
x(0)=x_{0}
there exists a continuous
i
th component
u_{i}(\cdot)
of the control
u(\cdot)
such that the corresponding solution of

\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+b_{i}(t)u_{i}(t)

(11)

satisfies
x(1)=0
. Then clearly Theorems 1 and 2 cited in
[
11
]
for a more general definition of controllability, with
B(t)u(t)
replaced by
b_{i}(t)u_{i}(t)
, hold for the system in (
11
).

If the control system is time-invariant, i.e.,
A(t)=A
and
b_{i}(t)=b_{i}
, for all
t\in[0,1]
, where
A
and
b_{i}
are constant matrices, then Theorem 2 cited in
[
11
]
simplifies to checking the straightforward
Kalman controllability rank condition
: the control system
\dot{x}(t)=A\,x(t)+B\,u(t)
is controllable w.r.t.
u_{i}
on
[0,1]
if
\rank\,[\ b_{i}\ |\ A\,b_{i}\ |\ \ldots\ |\ A^{n-1}\,b_{i}\ ]=n
.

2.4
Best approximation control

Next, we provide the result
[
11
, Theorem 3]
for the best approximation solution in the set
{\cal B}
, in the case when the constraint sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
are disjoint.

Theorem 1
(Gap Vector and Best Approximation Control in
{\cal B}

[
11
]
)

With the notation of Problem (Pf), assume that
{\cal A}\cap{\cal B}=\varnothing
. Then the optimal gap vector is given by

v(t)=-B^{T}(t)\lambda(t)\,,

(12)

for all
t\in[0,1]
, where the adjoint variable vector
\lambda(\cdot)
solves
\dot{\lambda}(t)=-A^{T}(t)\,\lambda(t)
, with appropriate transversality conditions. Moreover, suppose that
A(\cdot)
and
B(\cdot)
are sufficiently smooth and that the control system
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+B(t)u(t)
is controllable w.r.t.
u_{i}
on any
[t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}]\subset[0,1]
,
t^{\prime}<t^{\prime\prime}
, for some
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then, for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
,

u_{{\cal B},i}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\overline{a}_{i}(t)\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }v_{i}(t)\geq 0\,,\\[2.84526pt]
\underline{a}_{i}(t)\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }v_{i}(t)<0\,.\end{array}\right.

(13)

In other words, such
u_{{\cal B},i}
is of bang–bang type.

The following corollary is restated from
[
11
, Remark 1]
.

Corollary 1
(Best Approximation Control in
{\cal A}

[
11
]
)

Suppose that the hypotheses of Theorem
1
hold. Then the best approximation solution in the set
{\cal A}
is given by

u_{{\cal A},i}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\overline{a}_{i}(t)+v_{i}(t)\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }v_{i}(t)\geq 0\,,\\[2.84526pt]
\underline{a}_{i}(t)+v_{i}(t)\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }v_{i}(t)<0\,,\end{array}\right.

(14)

for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
.

We observe that while the component
v_{i}(\cdot)
, as can be deduced from (
12
), is continuous,
u_{{\cal A},i}(\cdot)
is piecewise continuous.

The following straightforward result is new.

Corollary 2
(Projection of
u_{\cal B}
onto
{\cal A}
)

Suppose that
u_{\cal A}
and
u_{\cal B}
are the best approximation controls. Then
u_{\cal A}
is the projection of
u_{\cal B}
onto
{\cal A}
; namely

u_{\cal A}=P_{\cal A}(u_{\cal B})\,.

(15)

Proof.
From the definition of
v
in (
10
), we have
u_{\cal A}=u_{\cal B}+v
. Substituting next the optimal gap solution in (
12
), one gets
u_{\cal A}=u_{\cal B}-B^{T}(t)\lambda
, where
\lambda(\cdot)
solves
\dot{\lambda}(t)=-A^{T}(t)\,\lambda(t)
, with appropriate transversality conditions. Subsequently, Theorem 3.4 and its proof in
[
6
]
furnish (
15
).

\Box

One further observation is in order for the special but common case of the time-invariant linear control system
\dot{x}(t)=A\,x(t)+B\,u(t)
: if
\rank\,[\ b_{i}\ |\ A\,b_{i}\ |\ \ldots\ |\ A^{n-1}\,b_{i}\ ]=n
, then the best approximation control component
u_{{\cal B},i}
for the infeasible optimal control problem is of bang–bang type as given in (

).

3
A Double Integrator Problem

We consider the following optimal control problem involving the double integrator as a special case of Problem (P).

\mbox{(PDI) }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min&\ \ \displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\,\|u\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2}\\[14.22636pt]
\mbox{subject to}&\ \ \dot{x}_{1}(t)=x_{2}(t)\,,\ \ x_{1}(0)=s_{0}\,,\ \ x_{1}(1)=s_{f}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\ \ \dot{x}_{2}(t)=u(t)\,,\ \ \ \,x_{2}(0)=v_{0}\,,\ \ x_{2}(1)=v_{f}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\ \ |u(t)|\leq a\,,\ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ t\in[0,1]\,,\end{array}\right.

where the bound
a>0
and the boundary values
s_{0}
,
s_{f}
,
v_{0}
and
v_{f}
are specified. Problem (PDI) was previously used in
[
1
,
7
]
to study applications of splitting and projection methods, but only in a feasible setting. The above problem was also studied in
[
11
]
for the infeasible case, i.e. the case where the control bound
a
is small enough to make the constraint set infeasible. However, no splitting and projection methods were implemented in
[
11
]
. In
[
11
]
, the critically feasible case was also studied, namely the case of the smallest
a
for which Problem (PDI) is (still) feasible.
In (PDI), we have
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
and
b=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}
. So,
\rank\,[\,b\ |\ Ab\,]=\rank\,\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}=2=n
, and therefore the control system is controllable.
Recall that the adjoint variables satisfy
\dot{\lambda}(t)=-A^{T}\lambda(t)
, i.e.
\dot{\lambda}_{1}(t)=0
and
\dot{\lambda}_{2}(t)=-\lambda_{1}
, with no boundary (or transversality) conditions imposed in this particular case. It follows that
\lambda_{1}(t)=c_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}(t)=-c_{1}\,t-c_{2}
, for all
t\in[0,1]
, where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are real constants. So, by Equation (
12
) in Theorem
1
, the gap function is simply given by

v(t)=c_{1}\,t+c_{2}\,,

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Consequently,

u_{\cal A}(t)=u_{\cal B}(t)+c_{1}\,t+c_{2}\,.

Combining the above fact with (
13
) in Theorem
1
we obtain

u_{\cal B}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}a\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }c_{1}\,t+c_{2}\geq 0\,,\\[2.84526pt]
-a\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }c_{1}\,t+c_{2}<0\,,\end{array}\right.

(16)

for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
.

Remark 1
(Types of Control According to the Gap Function)

The gap function
v(t)=c_{1}\,t+c_{2}
is affine and thus can obviously change sign at most once. So,
u_{\cal B}
is constant or piecewise-constant, depending on the values of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, as elaborated in the following.

(A)

is to use the Newton method, after setting
r=a
or
r=-a
in (

)–(

).

It is well known that for convergence with Newton one needs to provide an initial guess that is “close enough” to the solution. Graphical
portraits
displaying colour-coded number of iterations to
reach
a solution are useful tools in judging how robust a method is in terms of the choice of the initial iterates. We can perhaps rephrase this concern by asking the following question. How large is the region in which, when an initial iterate (or guess) is chosen, one can get to a solution in a “reasonable” number of iterations?

This question also prompts one to consider variants of the Newton method that may perform better in a larger domain. One such variant is the multivariate generalized Newton method proposed in
[
5
]
, which itself is a generalization of the univariate version in
[
12
]
. While the Newton method solves
f(y)={\bf 0}
, the generalized Newton method is the Newton method that solves
(s\circ f)(y)={\bf 0}
, where the auxiliary function
s
is differentiable and has a differentiable inverse so that the solutions of the equation remain the same, and
s
is chosen in such a way that the Newton method applied to solving
(s\circ f)(y)={\bf 0}
exhibits more desirable convergence properties, notably a larger domain of convergence. Obviously, when
s
is the identity function the generalized Newton method reduces to the Newton method. More recently a Levenberg–Marquardt-type extension of
[
5
]
has also been proposed in
[
9
]
, which, with carefully chosen parameters, potentially enlarges the region further in which convergence is achieved.

Douglas–Rachford Algorithm

To define the Douglas–Rachford (DR) algorithm we require the
proximal operators
of
f
and
g
where the proximal operator, or proximal mapping, of a function
h
is defined by
[
2
, Definition 12.23]
:

{\rm Prox}_{h}(u):=\argmin_{y\in{\cal{L}}^{2}([t_{0},t_{f}];{\rm{I\ \kern-4.30495ptR}}^{m})}\left(h(y)+\frac{1}{2}\|y-u\|^{2}_{{\cal L}^{2}}\right),

(42)

for any
u\in{\cal L}^{2}([t_{0},t_{f}];{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}}^{m})
. It is known that
\operatorname{Prox}_{f}
is firmly non-expansive
[
2
, Proposition 12.28]
. Moreover, if
h=\iota_{C}
then
\operatorname{Prox}_{h}=P_{C}
where
P_{C}
is the
projection operator
onto
C
. Recall that the
Douglas–Rachford operator
is given as

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\operatorname{Id}+\lambda(\operatorname{R}_{\tau g}\operatorname{R}_{\tau f}),

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Id}+2\lambda(\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau g}(2\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau f}-\operatorname{Id})-\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau f}),

where
\lambda\in\;(0,1)
. When
\lambda=1/2
the above operator reduces to the
DR operator introduced in
[
18
]
.
See also
[
2
, Theorem 26.11]
for a more general formula which allows for a variable
\lambda
.

Let
f=\iota_{\cal A}
and
g=\iota_{B}+\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
. Let
\tau>0
and
\gamma:={{\frac{1}{\tau+1}}}\in(0,1)
.
Using
[
2
, Proposition 24.8(i)]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau f}(u)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau\iota_{\cal A}}(u)=P_{\cal A}(u),

(43)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau g}(u)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Prox}_{\tau\iota_{\cal B}}(\gamma u)=P_{\cal B}(\gamma u).

(44)

Using (
43
) and (
44
),

Tx=\operatorname{Id}+2\lambda(P_{\cal A}(2P_{\cal B}(\gamma x)-x)-P_{\cal B}(\gamma x)).

The following convergence result can be found in
[
8
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Theorem 3
(Convergence of the DR Algorithm)

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
and set

T=(1-\lambda)\operatorname{Id}+\lambda\operatorname{R}_{g}\operatorname{R}_{f}.

(45)

Suppose that
Z\neq\varnothing
and that

(\exists h\in\{f,g\})

such that
h
is uniformly convex on
X
.
Then
(\exists\overline{x}\in X)

such that
Z=\argmin(-\left\langle{\cdot},{v}\right\rangle+f+g(\cdot-v))=\{\overline{x}\}
.
Let
x\in X
.
Then the following hold:

(a)

\operatorname{Prox}_{f}T^{n}x\to\overline{x}
.

(b)

\operatorname{Prox}_{g}\operatorname{R}_{f}T^{n}x\to\overline{x}-v
.

4.1
DR Algorithm for the Infeasible Problem (PDI)

The formulas (
46
) and (
47
) below, which can be found in
[
1
, Propositions 1 and 2]
, provide the projections onto
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
. These formulas are in turn going to be used in the DR algorithm (Algorithm
1
) in the following.

(Projection onto
{\cal A}
).

The projection
P_{\cal A}
of
u^{-}\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}})
onto the set
{\cal A}
is given by

P_{\cal A}(u^{-})(t)=u^{-}(t)+c_{1}\,t+c_{2}\,,

(46)

for all
t\in[0,1]
, where

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 12\left(s_{0}+v_{0}-s_{f}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)u^{-}(\tau)d\tau\right)-6\left(v_{0}-v_{f}+\int_{0}^{1}u^{-}(\tau)d\tau\right),

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-6\left(s_{0}+v_{0}-s_{f}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)u^{-}(\tau)d\tau\right)+2\left(v_{0}-v_{f}+\int_{0}^{1}u^{-}(\tau)d\tau\right).

(Projection onto
{\cal B}
).

The projection
P_{{\cal B}}
of
u^{-}\in{\cal L}^{2}([t_{0},t_{f}];{\rm{I\ \kern-5.39993ptR}})
onto the set
{\cal B}
is given by

P_{{\cal B}}(u^{-})(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}a\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }u^{-}(t)\geq a\,,\\[5.69054pt]
u^{-}(t)\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }-a\leq u^{-}(t)\leq a\,,\\[5.69054pt]
-a\,,&\ \ \mbox{if\ \ }u^{-}(t)\leq-a\,,\\[5.69054pt]
\end{array}\right.

(47)

for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]
.

We implemented the DR algorithm for the infeasible problem (PDI) as follows.

Algorithm 1

Douglas–Rachford algorithm

Step 1

(
Initialization
) Choose two parameters
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
and the initial iterate
u^{0}
arbitrarily.
Choose a small parameter
\epsilon>0
, and set
k=0
.

Step 2

(
Projection onto
{\cal B}
) Set
u^{-}=\gamma u^{k}
.
Compute
\widetilde{u}=P_{{\cal B}}(u^{-})
by using (
47
).

Step 3

(
Projection onto
{\cal A}
) Set
u^{-}:=2\widetilde{u}-u^{k}
.
Compute
\widehat{u}=P_{{\cal A}}(u^{-})
by using (
46
).

Step 4

(
Update
) Set
u^{k+1}:=u^{k}+2\lambda(\widehat{u}-\widetilde{u})
.

Step 5

(
Stopping criterion
) If
\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{{\cal L}^{\infty}}\leq\epsilon
, then return
\widetilde{u}
and stop.
Otherwise, set
k:=k+1
and go to Step 2.

5
Conclusion

We have studied the problem of finding the best approximation control for the infeasible double integrator. We have implemented the DR algorithm for the infeasible double integrator and carried out numerical experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the DR algorithm. For the values of
a
not so close to
a_{c}
(where
a_{c}
corresponds to the critically feasible case), the numerical experiments prompt us to use the DR algorithm in the future for those infeasible optimal control problems for which an analytical solution or simplification cannot be obtained. In the future, it would also be interesting to study other operator splitting and projection methods such as the Peaceman–Rachford algorithm (the case where
\lambda\to 1
in the relaxed DR algorithm) and the Dykstra algorithm, for infeasible optimal control problems.
