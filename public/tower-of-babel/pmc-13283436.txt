Comprehensive review and assessment of multi-species splicing variant prediction: task-specific deep learning models and genomic foundation models
Authors: Yinuo Sun, Xiaoyu Wang, Yuheng Jia, Seiya Imoto, Fuyi Li, Chen Li, Jiangning Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlternative splicing generates transcriptomic and proteomic diversity essential for eukaryotic complexity, yet genetic variants disrupting the splicing code underlie numerous human diseases. Deep learning (DL) models and genomic foundation models (GFMs) have achieved outstanding accuracy for predicting splicing variant effects in humans. However, their transferability to non-human species remains poorly understood, limiting applications in agricultural genomics, comparative biology, and non-model organism research, where experimentally validated variant datasets are limited or lacking. In this study, we comprehensively reviewed 35 computational approaches in terms of their architectural characteristics for splicing site and variant prediction and analysis. We systematically benchmarked the performance of 10 representative models for splicing variant prediction across human, rat, pig, and chicken, including four task-specific DL models and six GFMs, using our manually assembled benchmark datasets. Our benchmarking results revealed a substantial cross-species performance decrease (~21%–33% in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve - AUROC) using task-specific models from human to non-human species datasets. We then applied a supervised adaptation to frozen GFM embeddings (DNABERT-2, Evo 2, Genos) by adding a lightweight classifier (i.e. a multi-layer perceptron) and reduced the cross-species performance decrease for rat and pig (8.56%–23.84% in AUROC), while performance on chicken was very close to human (decline within 1%, even exceeding by 0.52% when using the Evo 2 embedding). We proposed several directions to improve the prediction performance of splicing variants, including feature representation transfer and multi-modal fusion integrating global context, universal embeddings, and species-aware conditioning. We hope our comprehensive review and performance benchmarking can provide useful computational insights for further advancement of splicing variant prediction.

Introduction

Alternative splicing (AS) is a major biological process where a precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) can generate multiple transcripts that may ultimately encode protein isoforms with different functions via selective retention or exclusion of exons [1], greatly expanding the coding capacity of eukaryotic genomes [2]. Beyond the contribution to proteomic diversity, AS represents a prominent regulatory tier that impacts almost all aspects of cellular biology, including cell development [3], lineage commitment [4, 5], and adaptation to the environment [6, 7]. This complex process is regulated by a “splicing code” that combines conserved intron–exon boundary signals with a vast array of auxiliary cis-acting sequence elements that are specifically recognized by splicing factors [8, 9]. Disruptions of these exquisite splicing codes by genetic variants (i.e. spliceopathy mutations) are known as a key cause of a variety of human hereditary diseases, including cancers [10]. Spliceopathy mutations can arise from a single-nucleotide alteration that may lead to aberrant splicing events, such as exon skipping, intron retention, or cryptic splice site usage, consequently triggering the inability of functional proteins [11]. Importantly, AS can also be disrupted by pathogenic missense or synonymous mutations in protein-coding regions, which expand the scope of splicing-relevant variants beyond canonical splice sites [12]. Therefore, accurate estimation of the functional impact of genetic variants on AS has emerged as an essential requirement for the diagnosis of genetic disorders, the design of targeted therapy, and the prioritization of pathogenic variants in the context of the extremely large datasets produced by large-scale sequencing strategies, such as whole-exome sequencing [13, 14]. More broadly, AS functions within a wider disease-relevant RNA regulatory landscape that includes other post-transcriptional mechanisms and RNA-informed therapeutic contexts, further underscoring the importance of accurately characterizing sequence variants that perturb RNA processing and transcript outputs [15, 16].

The synergy of high-throughput sequencing and artificial intelligence has ushered in a transformative era for splicing variant prediction, with deep learning (DL) models emerging as state-of-the-art (SOTA) techniques. The evolution of DL-based approaches for AS variant prediction has been remarkable. Task-specific DL models were developed specifically for splicing prediction, starting with the classical convolutional neural networks (CNN) [17]-based tools for splice site recognition (e.g. SpliceRover [18]) and evolving into predictors of variant effects (e.g. SpliceAI [19], MMSplice [20]), followed by context-aware Transformer models [21] such as SpliceTransformer [22]. On the other hand, genomic foundation models (GFMs), which are large-scale models pre-trained on vast genomic sequences, providing embeddings or zero-shot output, have also emerged as powerful potential tools for splicing prediction. With the distinction between specialist and generalist foundation models in medical artificial intelligence (AI) [23], and extending the benchmarking framework proposed for GFMs [24], GFMs can be categorized based on the scope of pre-training and the specific task, including (i) splicing-specialized GFMs (e.g. SpliceBERT [25]) which were pre-trained on curated sequence subsets (e.g. pre-mRNA) specifically for splicing-related tasks (e.g. splice site prediction); and (ii) general-purpose GFMs (e.g. DNABERT [26], Genos [27]), which were pre-trained on whole genomes to learn universal DNA grammar, and can usually be applied to multiple genomic tasks. While predominantly human-centric models established a foundational framework, the paradigm was further advanced by multi-species genomic modeling with GFMs such as DNABERT-2 [28], MutBERT [29], Evo 2 [30], and AlphaGenome [31] by leveraging bi-directional encoding, large-scale pre-training, and advanced architectures to achieve outstanding genomic interpretation, thereby underscoring the immense potential of GFMs in predicting splicing tasks directly from sequences. However, translating this broad genomic competency into precise cross-species splicing variant prediction remains elusive. Multi-species GFMs (e.g. Evo 2), despite their extensive training across phylogenies, often lack the fine-grained sensitivity required for specific variant effect prediction [32]. Meanwhile, these models, largely trained on large human datasets, often have limited transferability to other species [33], due to the following reasons:

Evolutionary differences: Although the splicing core is conserved across species, the surrounding cis-regulatory landscapes and genomic organization are fairly divergent [34] across species. Differences in splicing enhancers/silencers and genomic parameters, such as intron length distribution, suggest that models trained on human gene structure are not necessarily predictive for other species [3, 35].

Data availability: Landmark resources, such as the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) [36] project, offer immeasurably rich data for human genomics, while comparable resources for non-human organisms are limited. Initiatives such as the Farm Animal Genotype-Tissue Expression (FarmGTEx) project [37] have been proposed to address these gaps across domestic species. However, their ambitious timeline extending through 2035 underscores the long-term nature and persistent data scarcity challenge facing cross-species genomics. To date, databases such as RatGTEx [39], FarmGTEx [37], and PlantExp [40] have been published to provide genomic data for a limited number of species, including rat, domesticated animals (e.g. pig and chicken), and plants (e.g. Arabidopsis). Accordingly, DL-based approaches for gene annotation (e.g. Helixer [32] for general eukaryotes) and variant effect prediction [e.g. Genomic Pre-trained Network (GPN) [41] for plants] have been developed for non-human organisms, but systematic evaluations of AS-specific variant prediction across species remain absent.

Despite the emergence of numerous DL-based splicing prediction models and associated reviews, the efforts for targeting cross-species challenges on benchmarking splicing variants remain fragmented. DL-based approaches have been comprehensively reviewed for various genomic applications [42, 43], and more specifically, DL-based methods for AS identification have been summarized [44]. Furthermore, recent benchmarks have evaluated general-purpose GFMs across diverse genomic tasks [24], including multi-species genome region classification (e.g. promoter identification and enhancer prediction [45]) and human variant effect quantification (e.g. pathogenic variant identification [46] and splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTL) association prediction [47]). However, these evaluations focused exclusively on general-purpose foundation models, without assessing task-specific DL splicing prediction models or splicing-specialized GFMs. Moreover, while human sQTL association has been evaluated, cross-species splicing variant prediction remains unexplored. While foundational biological studies have characterized the evolutionary divergence of splicing patterns across vertebrate lineages [35, 48], to our knowledge, no systematic evaluation has been reported to simultaneously benchmark the performance of SOTA approaches for AS variant prediction across species.

In this article, we conducted a comprehensive review of 35 computational approaches, including 22 DL-based models and one specialist GFM for splicing site/variant prediction, and 12 general-purpose GFMs that can be extended for splicing variant prediction. We surveyed existing high-quality genomic databases for various species to highlight the resource disparities that constrain cross-species splicing variant prediction. Given our specific focus on the challenging task of variant effect prediction, where continuous genotype-to-phenotype quantitative splicing metrics remain typically limited for non-human species [49], we formulated our multi-species benchmarking as a binary splice-altering classification task leveraging population-derived sQTLs. Additionally, we comprehensively analyzed model architectures and limitations in predicting splicing variants when applied to our manually curated cross-species datasets. Importantly, we performed a systematic performance benchmarking of 10 representative models (including four task-specific DL models and six GFM-based approaches) across five benchmark datasets, including two human datasets and three non-human datasets from rat, pig, and chicken. Overall, task-specific models performed strongly on human benchmark datasets but still showed a substantial performance decrease in predicting splicing variants in other species, whereas embedding-based adaptation markedly attenuated this performance decline, particularly for chicken. Comparison between the two human datasets further showed that benchmark construction and data quality can influence model performance, suggesting that future progress in non-human splicing variant prediction will depend not only on better models, but also on improved species-specific resources. We finally discussed several potential directions to further improve the performance of multi-species splicing variant prediction.

Overview of computational approaches related to splicing variant prediction

Model evolution for splicing variant prediction

Prior to the emergence of DL techniques, splicing variant prediction relied primarily on statistical methods and traditional machine learning (ML) algorithms, mainly trained on sequence motifs [8, 9]. Position weight matrices were generated to quantify splice site consensus sequences by calculating the log-odds score from position-specific nucleotide frequencies [50], while tools like MaxEntScan [51] scored motif strength based on frequency distributions derived from known splice sites. Traditional ML-based approaches (e.g. Support Vector Machines [52] and Random Forests [53]) extracted a variety of sequence features, such as splice site strength, branch-point position, and exonic/intronic splicing enhancers/silencers, to predict variant effects on splicing. However, the design of the informative features requires extensive domain expertise, and the current feature calculation struggles to capture long-range regulatory interactions or adapt to species-specific splicing patterns [8, 9, 54].

Since 2018, DL techniques have been widely used in various frameworks for splicing site/variant prediction [43], such as SpliceRover [18] and SpliceAI [19], shifting the prediction methodology toward end-to-end learning directly from DNA sequence with no requirement of feature engineering. In this study, we surveyed over 30 DL-based and GFM-based models for splice site prediction, event-specific splicing regulation prediction, transcriptome-based splicing event analysis, and a pre-trained foundation model on genomic data. A chronological landscape demonstrating the development of these methods is provided in Fig. 1A, while Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of our surveyed methods. These models can be categorized into two major groups based on their design and training approach, including task-specific DL models and GFMs (Fig. 1B) for four specific biological tasks (Fig. 1C). Task-specific DL models were designed to solve a particular task [55], such as predicting splicing sites or profiling the effect of variants on splicing; while GFMs build large language models (LLMs), such as bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) [56] and GPT [57], using natural language processing techniques [58] on a vast number of genomic sequences for various predictions of genomic properties and patterns, including splicing site and associated splicing event prediction [25, 26, 59]. We then assessed these models in terms of their targeted species (e.g. human-only versus multi-species), specific task (e.g. splicing site detection versus variant effect prediction), input format (e.g. co-ordinate- versus sequence-driven), and, most importantly, their mechanisms and performance in cross-species splicing variant effect prediction (e.g. direct prediction versus indirect scoring).

Task-specific deep learning models: from sequence to context

Convolutional neural network-based models

To our knowledge, the CNN is the first DL model applied to mining genomic data, from annotation tasks (such as splice site recognition and AS event classification) to variant effect quantification, giving its exceptional ability to learn the hierarchical patterns directly from sequences [17]. Since 2018, DL techniques have been applied to splicing site prediction (e.g. SpliceRover [18], SpliceFinder [60], EnsembleSplice [61], Deep Splicer [62], DeepSplice [63], and Splice2Deep [64]), splice alignment (e.g. minisplice [65] and Splam [66]), alternative splice site selection (e.g. COSSMO [67]), and AS event type classification (e.g. deep splicing code [68], DeepASmRNA [69]). While these foundational approaches have demonstrated the power of convolutional feature extraction, ensemble learning, and multi-signal prediction (e.g. DeepGenGrep [70]), they excel at splice site recognition but are not designed to predict how individual genetic variants alter splicing outcomes—a capability later introduced by tools like SpliceAI [19] and MMSplice [20]. SpliceAI [19], a remarkable CNN-based approach, was published in 2019 to enable not only highly accurate prediction of canonical splicing sites but also robust detection of cryptic splicing activation (e.g. novel exon creation from deep intronic variants and intron retention) triggered by genetic variants using a wide input window (i.e. 10 000 bp) via a deep residual network [71] with dilated convolutions [72], MMSplice [20] was proposed in the same year to predict variant effects on splicing efficiency, delta Percent Splice In (∆PSI), and pathogenicity using a logistic regression model or shallow neural network model in an interpretable modular framework that separately evaluates splice acceptors, donors, exons, and flanking intronic regions. Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD)-Splice [73] was later proposed to translate the splicing variant prediction into clinical practice by integrating splicing scores from DL frameworks (e.g. SpliceAI [19]) into the widely used CADD [74] genome-wide variant annotation framework, thereby enabling clinicians to prioritize potentially pathogenic variants. In parallel, Pangolin [75] employed dilated CNNs to predict quantitative exon inclusion levels to indicate the extent of AS events from DNA sequences by incorporating their evolutionary conservation features from four species (human, rhesus macaque, rat, and mouse) as auxiliary inputs to enhance its predictive accuracy on human splicing variants through multi-species sequence patterns. It is worth noticing that while transformative, these models were predominantly trained and benchmarked on human genomic data, with multi-species information used primarily for feature augmentation to improve predictive performance rather than genuine cross-species generalization. A notable exception is Spliceator [76], a CNN-based approach explicitly trained on over 100 diverse eukaryotic genomes (from human to protists, including fungi and metazoa) to enable genuine multi-species splice site prediction. Beyond the above tools, several other approaches, such as CI-SpliceAI [77], SQUIRLS [78], and AbSplice-DNA [79], combined multiple predictors or added human-specific annotations to enhance the prediction of the impact of clinical variants in splicing.

Context-aware models

A major limitation of sequence-based models is that they do not consider the highly context-dependent nature of splicing regulation, which varies greatly across tissues, organismal developmental stages (e.g. embryonic, fetal, or adult) [4], and species [35, 80]. This limitation became particularly evident by the unsatisfactory performance when performing the prediction of the splicing effect of cross-species variants, where regulatory contexts can differ substantially between organisms [32, 81]. Early efforts to incorporate biological context, such as the model proposed by Leung et al. [82] for tissue-specific splicing patterns in mice and Enhanced Integrated Gradients [83] for tissue-specific regulator identification, laid the conceptual foundations for context-aware variant effect prediction. However, these approaches focused on regulatory pattern recognition (e.g. identifying tissue-specific splicing codes and cis-regulatory motifs) rather than quantifying variant-induced splicing changes. Building upon these studies, SpliceTransformer [22] represented a significant architectural improvement by employing a dual-input transformer architecture [21] that can process both DNA sequence and tissue-type embeddings. Crucially, instead of merely identifying regulatory motifs, SpliceTransformer leverages these learned tissue-specific patterns to accurately quantify how genetic variants alter splicing outcomes differentially across cellular environments. Consequently, SpliceTransformer achieved superior accuracy for human tissue-specific predictions compared to context-agnostic models [22]. The prediction results of SpliceTransformer demonstrated that the same genetic variant could have dramatically different splicing effects depending on the cellular context. Importantly, SpliceTransformer’s multi-species augmentation (i.e. incorporating data from three additional species) was designed exclusively to enhance its predictive performance on human datasets rather than to enable cross-species prediction. This is because SpliceTransformer relies on extensive tissue-matched RNA-seq data, while non-model organisms’ tissue-specific datasets are usually unavailable [37]. Even when these cross-species datasets exist, their molecular signatures and regulatory landscapes can diverge substantially, making direct transfer of context embeddings challenging [35].

Genomic foundation models

Since 2021, inspired by the success of LLMs such as GPT-3 [84], large-scale and pre-trained GFMs have been regarded as the most profound paradigm shift in genomic event prediction [55, 59]. These models were usually pre-trained on massive sequence datasets, learning the fundamental “grammar” of sequences [26], and can therefore be adapted for various downstream applications, including splicing site and variant prediction [85]. In detail, their rich contextual embeddings can be adapted for splicing variant assessment through two main strategies: (i) zero-shot scoring, where variant effects are inferred from embedding distances or likelihood changes without task-specific training; and (ii) embedding-based adaptation, where lightweight classifiers [e.g. multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with identical train/test splits for fair comparison] are trained on concatenated reference-alternative embedding differences (with the GFM parameters frozen/fixed during training) using labelled splicing data. Notably, “zero-shot” refers to the inference paradigm where a pre-trained model makes predictions on new tasks without any task-specific fine-tuning [84, 86, 87]. Similar to LLMs applied in medicine, GFMs can also be broadly grouped into specialized and general-purpose GFMs [88, 89].

Splicing-specialized genomic foundation models

Specialized GFMs aim for prediction tasks of a specific biological domain and are therefore pre-trained on curated domain-specific datasets. The training process is often self-supervised [90] but on a targeted corpus, and the output can be either a direct, task-specific prediction, or a highly specialized embedding. To the best of our knowledge, SpliceBERT [25] is the only splicing-specialized GFM currently available. Crucially, unlike general-purpose GFMs that are pre-trained on entire genomic sequences (including intergenic regions, promoters, and other non-genic elements), SpliceBERT was pre-trained exclusively on over 2 million pre-mRNA sequences from 72 vertebrates, encompassing only exonic and intronic regions of genes. This curated pre-training corpus enables SpliceBERT to learn splicing-specific regulatory patterns directly via the masked language model (MLM) [56] for splice site recognition and splicing-related downstream tasks (e.g. branchpoint identification and variant effect prediction). SpliceBERT provides zero-shot variant effect prediction by computing Kullback–Leibler divergence between reference and alternative sequence representations, quantifying the change in predicted nucleotide distributions around a variant.

General-purpose genomic foundation models

General-purpose GFMs are pre-trained on massive, unlabelled genomic sequences to learn universal DNA representations, rather than being optimized for specific tasks like splicing prediction. The foundational paradigm of general GFMs was established by DNABERT [26], which adapted the BERT to DNA sequences. Pre-trained on the human reference genome (~3 billion nucleotides) using k-mer tokenization, DNABERT demonstrated that self-supervised learning on unlabeled genomic sequences can produce rich contextual embeddings transferable to diverse downstream tasks, such as promoter prediction and splice site detection [26]. Critically, despite being pre-trained on human datasets, DNABERT achieved promising cross-species performance on mouse genomic features [26], providing early evidence that the “grammar” in core regulatory sequence patterns exhibits partial phylogenetic conservation. This pioneering work directly inspired the multi-species expansion embodied in DNABERT-2 [28] and established the blueprint for subsequent general-purpose GFMs, which were typically trained via self-supervised learning (e.g. MLM [56]) on multi-species genomic datasets of varying scale. To further enhance the sensitivity of GFMs to genetic variations, MutBERT [29] introduced a probabilistic pre-training objective that incorporates population allele frequencies, learning mutation-aware representations by processing probabilistic distributions of nucleotides. These models are universal feature extractors: rather than directly predicting variant effects, they generate high-dimensional sequence embeddings that must be interpreted via downstream classifiers. Moreover, general-purpose GFMs have been built using diverse neural architectures and pre-training strategies. Transformer-based models include DNABERT-2 [28] (pre-trained on 135 species, 32.49 billion nucleotides, 117 M parameters via MLM) and the Nucleotide Transformer [59] (pre-trained on 850+ species, including 3202 human genomes, 500 M-2.5B parameters) that leverages self-attention for bidirectional context. Beyond transformers, alternative architectures have also emerged. HyenaDNA [91] employed efficient long-convolution mechanisms to extend context windows beyond 450 kb, Caduceus [92] introduced a bi-directional Mamba (a variant of the Mamba Selective State Space Model) architecture that reflects the reverse complement equivariance inherent in DNA’s double-stranded structure, while generative models (e.g. Evo 2 [30]) used a 7B-parameter StripedHyena architecture with autoregressive next-token prediction (NTP) [57], pre-trained on genomic sequences from over 1000 species across all domains of life including bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes. The latest human-centric GFM, Genos [27], employed a mixture of experts (MoE) [93] architecture via NTP, optimized for million-base pair context modeling and trained exclusively on human reference genome and population-scale variation data. It should be noticed that using the embedding-based strategy, GFMs are not only end-to-end predictors but rather powerful, universal feature extractors that produce rich, contextual embeddings for DNA sequences. To predict variant effects, a downstream interpretation of their embeddings is needed. Exceptionally, some of the general-purpose GFMs provide dual output capabilities not only of generating embeddings but also of zero-shot scoring by computing a difference metric between the outputs for reference and alternative sequences, such as the cosine distance between embeddings (used by Nucleotide Transformer) and the change in sequence log-likelihood score (∆logP used by Evo 2) using unsupervised pre-training.

Among the general-purpose GFMs, a distinct subset of models employed supervised multi-task learning rather than self-supervised pre-training. Unlike self-supervised GFMs, these supervised GFMs were trained directly on experimentally validated functional genomics data to predict multiple genomic features simultaneously, combining general-purpose models (multi-feature, multi-task) with the classification power of supervised learning. A good example is Enformer [47], which was trained on human and mouse functional genomics tracks to predict gene expression from sequences across hundreds of cell types and assays. Notably, Enformer’s primary output is gene expression prediction rather than variant effect scoring, though its generated representations have been adapted for downstream variant interpretation tasks [47]. Another example is AlphaGenome [31], which extended supervised multi-task learning to genomic feature prediction. Trained on human and mouse genomes plus extensive functional genomics data (ChIP-seq, DNase-seq, RNA-seq, and others), AlphaGenome directly outputs probability scores across 11 genomic prediction tasks trained on 5930 human and 1128 mouse functional genomics datasets (tracks) from various cell types and assays, encompassing gene expression (e.g. RNA-seq, CAGE, and PRO-cap), splicing (e.g. splice sites, splice site usage, and splice junctions), chromatin state (e.g. DNase, ATAC-seq, histone modifications, and TF binding), and chromatin contact maps. Critically, AlphaGenome can function as an end-to-end predictor for variant effect scoring using its open-sourced application program interface (API). The U-Net architecture of AlphaGenome theoretically contains intermediate embeddings that could support adaptation to new genomic contexts (e.g. unexplored cell types or non-human species) through transfer learning [94–96].

Compared to GFMs for humans, GFMs customized for non-human species are significantly fewer. Representative models include the GPN [41], AgroNT [97], and GPN-multiple sequence alignment (MSA) [98]. GPN applied causal language modeling to Arabidopsis-specific genomic sequences. In the agricultural domain, AgroNT was pre-trained on 48 edible plant species. Conversely, for vertebrate analysis, GPN-MSA incorporated multiple sequence alignments from 100 species, trained on genome data and primarily designed for variant (not splicing-specific) effect prediction. Notably, dedicated GFMs for livestock and agricultural animals remain lacking. For cross-species splicing variants prediction, self-supervised general-purpose GFMs (e.g. DNABERT-2 [28], Nucleotide Transformer [59], and Evo 2 [30]), while not explicitly trained for splicing prediction, can be adapted for splicing variant prediction through their learned embeddings (i.e. sequence representations). However, supervised multi-task GFMs, such as AlphaGenome [31], explicitly target splicing prediction as one of their tasks, enabling direct variant effect scoring alongside other predicted genomic features. Taken together, the two categories of models have complementary advantages for multi-species splicing variant prediction, and we systematically evaluated the performance of splicing variant prediction using zero-shot and embedding-based strategies across human and cross-species datasets.

Data resources for splicing variant prediction

Robust model development and cross-species prediction of splicing variants rely on reliable and well-annotated databases of splicing-altering variant and splicing regulation events. A summary of current databases containing experimentally validated splicing variants/events of humans and other species is provided in Table 2. Databases of human splicing variants

SpliceVarDB [99] is the most comprehensive and up-to-date human resource that consolidates >50 000 variants from multiple high-throughput assays, clinical databases, and literature. DBASS3/DBASS5 [100] is another database that meticulously offers literature of curated variants causing aberrant 3′ and 5′ splicing. MutSpliceDB [101] harbors mis-splicing variants identified by RNA-seq data, while ClinVar [46] provides clinically annotated variants, among which ~10% are splicing mutations. Beyond variant-level databases, several large-scale resources provide complementary information for splicing analysis. One such example is MAJIQlopedia [102], which catalogs millions of splicing variations from GTEx [36] and RNA-seq datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) [103]. Another example is gnomAD/ExAC [104], which provides population allele frequencies for millions of variants, offering essential context for distinguishing pathogenic from benign splicing variants based on their rarity in healthy populations. Additional specialized databases include OncoSplicing [105], which harbors cancer-related splicing events from TCGA and SpliceDisease [106], which documents ~2,500 disease-linked splicing mutations. While these resources provide valuable biological context, they differ fundamentally from variant-level databases in their utility. For example, the GTEx project [36] serves as the gold standard for human genomics [107]. Complementary resources include SpliceVault [108], a splice junction database derived from GTEx and Sequence Read Archive (SRA) via recount3 [109, 110], ASPicDB [111] [Expressed Sequence Tag (EST)/complementary DNA (cDNA)-derived AS annotations], and GENCODE [112] (reference gene annotation). Hence, we decided to use SpliceVarDB [99] as the primary database for evaluating the best achievable performance on experimentally validated human splicing variants, given that it consolidates over 50 000 experimentally-validated splicing variants from over 8000 genes across multiple sources (e.g. minigene assays, RNA-seq splice junction changes, and/or curated clinical evidence) with precise genomic co-ordinates and alleles. Crucially, SpliceVarDB provides explicit labels to better annotate splicing variants (e.g. splice-altering or normal) and offers easily accessible data formats (e.g. the Variant Call Format; VCF) with its API. This facilitates fair comparison of both sequence-based models (which use fixed-length windows centered on variants) and co-ordinate-based models (which use VCF co-ordinates to extract sequences from reference genome assemblies). Notably, most task-specific DL models (e.g. SpliceAI, Pangolin, SpliceTransformer, and MMSplice) require GTF annotations to identify gene-proximal variants, while GFMs (e.g. AlphaGenome, Evo 2, and DNABERT-2) usually operate solely on genomic co-ordinates or sequences.

Resource of multi-species splicing data

Multi-species splicing variant databases

While human splicing variant databases are well-established, resources for non-human species remain limited. Online Mendelian Inheritance in Animals [113] documents likely causal variants for inherited disorders across ˃540 vertebrate species, including ~1590 variants in domesticated mammals (e.g. dog, cattle, cat, horse, donkey), of which a subset are splice-altering mutations. However, general genomic variant databases (e.g. Mouse Genome Informatics [114], European Variation Archive [115]) lack systematic splicing-effect annotations. Cross-species variant databases have limited quantity, with most species having substantially fewer variants documented compared to human databases, and face challenges in maintaining co-ordinate consistency across evolving genome assemblies [113]. Critically, the scarcity of gold-standard splicing variant annotations for most non-human species makes it challenging to construct experimentally validated benchmarks comparable in scale and quality to human SpliceVarDB [99].

Cross-species transcriptome and splicing event-level resources

In the absence of comprehensive variant-level annotations for non-human species, cross-species transcriptome and sQTL [116] resources provide alternative strategies for benchmark construction to assess the effect of splicing variants. Unlike variant-level databases that directly label each variant as splice-altering or normal, transcriptome databases provide sQTL associations where genetic variants are statistically linked to splicing changes but without experimental validation of individual variant effects. For example, RatGTEx [39] is a species-specific database that provides tissue-resolved expression and sQTLs on a unified genome build for rats (Rattus norvegicus). Broader consortia such as FarmGTEx [37], also harbors transcriptome and sQTL information. FarmGTEx aims to encompass 16 major domesticated species (including 8 mammals, 5 birds, and 3 fish species). Its pilot version has already provided high-quality, tissue-resolved transcriptome, and sQTL datasets for prioritized species, most notably pig (5457 samples across 34 tissues [117] for RNA-seq) and chicken (7015 samples across 28 tissues [118] for RNA-seq). On the other hand, splice event resources catalog splicing events. For example, ASlive [119] catalogs 48 000–151 000 AS events across five major livestock species (cattle, sheep, pigs, horses, and chickens) based on RNA-seq alignments. PlantExp [40] provides millions of AS events from 131 423 samples involving 85 plant species across 24 taxonomic orders, demonstrating the breadth of splicing diversity in plants. Furthermore, specialized plant resources like PastDB [120] (for Arabidopsis), TSTD [121] (for tomato), and PlantSPEAD [122] (covering 70 plant species) offer deeper insights into plant-specific splicing regulation. For evolutionary comparisons, VastDB [123] aggregates AS events across multiple vertebrate species and reports event-level PSI matrices via the VAST-TOOLS pipeline [123], enabling cross-species analysis at the junction/event level. Alternative Splicing Database [124] compiles over 127 000 predicted AS events (ranging from ~2000 to ~54 000 events per species) across nine model organisms based on EST and cDNA alignments, including human, mouse, rat, chicken, fruit fly, zebrafish, rice, Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), and Arabidopsis. Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) catalog [116] provides a harmonized compendium of gene expression, and multiple studies can also serve as a central resource for sQTL analysis. Notably, although multi-species databases exist (e.g. FarmGTEx), no single unified resource currently offers cross-species harmonization that is comparable in consistency and scope to the human eQTL catalog. These resources have enabled comparative splicing analyses across species but require additional processing to characterize variant-level information for model benchmarking.

Benchmarking state-of-the-art methods for splicing variant prediction

Benchmarked methods

We selected 10 SOTA task-specific approaches and GFMs for multi-species splicing variant prediction, including SpliceAI, Pangolin, MMSplice, SpliceTransformer, DNABERT-2, Evo 2-7B, Nucleotide Transformer-v2-500M, Genos-10B, SpliceBERT, and AlphaGenome. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, these methods were prioritized as they are representative frameworks covering a wide spectrum of task alignment, targeted species, architectures (e.g. CNN, Transformer, Hybrid), learning strategies (i.e. task-specific versus GFMs), and input data types (i.e. sequence-based versus co-ordinate-based), thereby enabling a systematic analysis of how these factors impact their predictive performance. All benchmarking datasets for human-specific and cross-species splicing variant prediction were processed independently through our pre-processing and evaluation pipelines (Fig. 2), the comprehensive evaluation framework was demonstrated in Fig. 3, and the complete construction of benchmark datasets, evaluation protocols, and performance metrics was detailed in Supplementary Method S1. As noted in Data resources for splicing variant prediction, although transcriptomic databases (e.g. ASlive and VastDB) provide quantitative splicing metrics such as PSI across various wild-type species, they inherently lack the data from genotype to phenotype. Without the mapping between splicing metrics (e.g. ∆PSI) and variant annotations for animal genomes, benchmarking of splicing variant prediction for non-human species relies on population-derived sQTLs. As sQTL protocols typically report statistical associations (i.e. to identify whether a variant significantly alters splicing across a population) rather than absolute transcript-level ∆PSI for individual variants, we formulated our cross-species performance benchmark as a binary splice-altering classification task. This specifically aligns with the evaluation and zero-shot strategies adopted by SOTA models [30, 31, 75, 125], which benchmark or classify sQTLs (splice-associated variants) from background variants. Five benchmark datasets were constructed as detailed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. Human Data 1 (sourced from SpliceVarDB [99]) contains a high-quality human splicing variant resource, whereas Human Data 2 and benchmark datasets for rat, pig, and chicken (sourced from GTEx [107, 126], RatGTEx [39], PigGTEx [117], and ChickenGTEx [118], respectively) were used for fair sQTL + VCF-based performance comparison using a unified benchmark data construction process.

Experimental results

Prediction performance assessment on human splicing variant data

We first benchmarked the prediction zero-shot performance of eight SOTA approaches for predicting human-specific splicing variants using Human Data 1 (output scores in Supplementary Data S1 and performance matrices in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S4). Compared to predictors using sequences as inputs, predictors using co-ordinates had problems in locating the sequences. For example, SpliceAI, Pangolin, and MMSplice missed 497, 371, and 289 variants (coverage: 97.92%, 98.45%, and 98.79%), respectively, which can be attributed to incomplete or out-of-date gene annotations in specific genomic regions. While the benchmark datasets were the same for all compared methods, some co-ordinate-based models were unable to deliver predictive outcomes of variants that could not be recognized due to annotation gaps. Therefore, each model was evaluated based on the variants that were successfully identified. The coverage values of these models are explicitly reported in Fig. 4A to ensure comparitive transparency. Generally, task-specific models outperformed GFMs. Pangolin and SpliceAI were ranked as the best and second-best models in predicting human splicing variants with an AUROC of 0.9724 and 0.9619, respectively, outperforming the supervised multi-task foundation model AlphaGenome (AUROC: 0.9590), followed by SpliceTransformer (0.9431) and MMSplice (pathogenicity module; 0.9230). In contrast, general-purpose GFMs (including AlphaGenome) exhibited diverse prediction performance, reflecting their underlying architectural paradigms and pre-training objectives. For example, Evo 2 achieved an AUROC of 0.7089, demonstrating that generative log-likelihood scoring retains partial sensitivity to splicing-regulatory alterations despite task-objective mismatch. However, Nucleotide Transformer and SpliceBERT demonstrated a near-random prediction performance with an AUROC of 0.5073 and 0.5163, respectively, suggesting that general language modeling may not work for specific tasks [127, 128], such as splicing site/variant prediction.

Performance evaluation of splicing variant prediction models on human data 1; (A) model coverage demonstrating the percentage of variants successfully identified and scored by each model; lower coverage for some models (e.g. SpliceAI, pangolin) is attributed to their incomplete or outdated gene annotations for specific genomic co-ordinates; (B) receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the comparison of zero-shot prediction performance across eight models; (C) precision-recall (PR) curves for the comparison of precision and recall values; (D) threshold-based metrics comparison demonstrating detailed performance across six classification measures, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and Matthew correlation co-efficient (MCC); metrics were calculated using original default threshold if the model published, otherwise, optimized thresholds were determined by the Youden’s index for each model.

Regarding the performance using default thresholds published (i.e. SpliceAI: 0.2 [19]; SpliceTransformer: 0.27 [22]; Pangolin: 0.2 for loss-of-function variants [75]) and Youden’s Index [129] (Supplementary Table SF5), we observed that these default values, while functional and close to the optimized Youden’s Index thresholds, were calibrated conservatively toward high precision at the expense of sensitivity. For instance, SpliceAI at its default threshold achieved 85.79% recall and 97.07% precision, whereas optimized thresholds improved recall to 87.90% (optimal threshold: 0.11) and 95.79%. SpliceTransformer (optimal threshold: 0.06) showed the same trend with improvements of 3.60% compared to the performance at its default threshold (0.27). Pangolin achieved its best F1-score (0.9353) at threshold 0.05 compared to the published default of 0.2 (F1: 0.9148). These results indicate that default thresholds are calibrated to prioritize high precision (minimizing false positives) over sensitivity, which may be appropriate for clinical diagnostics but sub-optimal for research applications requiring comprehensive variant detection.

Prediction performance assessment for cross-species splicing variants

We selected rat, pig, and chicken for building sQTL + VCF benchmark datasets, respectively, given that there are accessible population-level sQTL resources for these species that can be harmonized into a unified sQTL + VCF benchmark process (Supplementary Table S6). We then examined the predictive power of five models on three species, including rat (28 120 variants), pig (26 358 variants), and chicken (25 000 variants). Among the benchmarked 10 models in the last section, five models were excluded from this direct-score evaluation: Nucleotide Transformer and SpliceBERT were removed due to their poor performance in predicting human splicing variants; MMSplice was also excluded due to its low sequence identification rate using co-ordinates—5.05% for rat, 12.36% for pig, and 14.37% for chicken; and DNABERT-2 and Genos were removed from this section as they do not natively provide zero-shot variant effect scores, but are instead evaluated in the subsequent embedding-based adaptation section. Output scores for each model are displayed in Supplementary Data S3–S5, and performance matrices are shown in Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Table S4.

Cross-species performance evaluation of splicing variant prediction models; (A–D) ROC and PR curves for zero-shot prediction performance on the benchmark datasets of rat, pig, chicken, and human data 2, all constructed using the same sQTL + VCF-based process; (E) cross-species AUROC performance comparison across human data 1, human data 2, rat, pig, and chicken datasets for five models; the results demonstrate that a consistent and performance decline when human-centric models are applied to non-human species, with AlphaGenome exhibiting relatively higher robustness in cross-species generalization compared to other zero-shot approaches.

For a fair cross-species comparison, we benchmarked these five methods on Human Data 2 (Fig. 5D), which was constructed using the same sQTL + VCF-based framework for benchmark data generation for rats, pigs, and chickens. Our results demonstrate that the cross-species performance drop remained evident, but was substantially smaller than the performance decrease when benchmarking these methods on Human Data 1 (sourced from SpliceVarDB). For example, AlphaGenome decreased from an AUROC of 0.8841 on Human Data 2 to 0.6005, 0.6818, and 0.7097 on rat, pig, and chicken, respectively, corresponding to 32.08% [i.e. (0.8841–0.6005)/0.8841], 22.88%, and 19.73% decrease. SpliceAI, Pangolin, and SpliceTransformer showed a similar trend, whereas Evo 2 showed weak prediction on both Human Data 2 and non-human datasets, indicating that its direct zero-shot ΔlogP score did not provide variant discrimination under this benchmark setting. These results confirm that the performance decline in cross-species prediction is obvious, regardless of using either direct splicing variant data from SpliceVarDB (i.e. Human Data 1) or sQTL + VCF-based data (i.e. Human Data 2). Cross-species comparison revealed clear performance differences in different models, with AlphaGenome showing the most robust performance, suggesting that supervised learning may improve the performance in cross-species splicing variant prediction [130, 131], followed by SpliceAI, whereas Pangolin, SpliceTransformer, and Evo 2 generally approached near-random behavior on at least one benchmark dataset.

Threshold analysis also revealed a systematic score distribution shift as a key failure mode in cross-species that human-centric models are functionally unusable for cross-species variant discovery. Our experiments demonstrate that species-specific threshold optimization improved performance to a certain degree (Supplementary Table SF5); it ultimately failed to rescue model performance. Specifically, due to a severe downward shift in score distributions, SpliceAI’s optimal threshold dropped from 0.2 (human) to 0.01 (all three non-human species). However, even with this big adjustment to maximize sensitivity, the resulting F1-scores remained negligible (<0.04) across all species. While Pangolin exhibited similar patterns, in contrast, the performance of SpliceTransformer remained near-random even with optimized thresholds (F1 < 0.03), confirming a fundamental architectural incompatibility. These findings underscore that while threshold recalibration is effective for some task-specific DL models (SpliceAI, Pangolin), it cannot rescue models architecturally dependent on species-specific biological features (SpliceTransformer).

Stratified performance across genomic contexts

To better examine the prediction performance degradation, we stratified model performance by various biological criteria, including distance to the nearest splice site, exonic versus intronic/other location, and GC-content bins (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S8). Given that splicing regulation is strongly dependent on context [132] and that AS evolves diversely across species [133, 134], such stratification provides a useful way to dissect whether cross-species prediction failures are driven by specific genomic contexts. Using these criteria, we further subset our prediction results (including both positive and negative predictions) into various regions, such as regions close to the splice site or exonic/intronic regions, and then calculated the performance using these positive and negative predictions within that region. Our results demonstrate that AlphaGenome remained robust across both exonic and intronic/other variants on Human Data 1 (AUROC 0.9381 and 0.9734), but was notably weaker in Human Data 2, especially for exonic variants (0.7576 versus 0.8640 for intronic/other). Similar shifts were observed for SpliceAI, which decreased from AUROC 0.9419 and 0.9754 in the corresponding Human Data 1 strata to 0.7507 and 0.7699 in Human Data 2. Across rat, pig, and chicken, no single monotonic trend with splice site distance or GC content was observed, suggesting that cross-species zero-shot failure in our benchmark cannot be explained by a single positional or compositional factor alone, but instead reflects broader differences in regulatory context.

Genomic foundation model prediction performance via zero-shot versus generated embedding

GFMs offer two fundamentally different utilities depending on the output: (i) the output can be directly used for evaluating the effect of the splicing variant, such as Evo 2; and (ii) the output of the last second layer is an embedding that can be used for training a downstream classifier for various purposes [24], such as splicing variant prediction (Fig. 3B), Evo 2, and DNABERT-2. Although Genos’s zero-shot output (pathogenicity scoring) is not explicitly aligned with the splicing variant prediction task (as pathogenicity encompasses broader mechanisms beyond splicing [135]), it is still valuable to evaluate whether its embeddings capture splicing variant features across species. In this section, we compare the performance of GFMs in predicting splicing variants in terms of the two utilities above, thereby examining if embedding interpretation could improve the performance of GFMs that failed to predict splicing variants using their outputs directly. To assess whether the embedding adaptation strategy depended strongly on a particular classifier, we additionally compared four lightweight downstream heads, including logistic regression [136], support vector machine (SVM) [52], MLP [137], and XGBoost [138], under the same train/test setting (Supplementary Method S1 and Supplementary Table S9) for all benchmark datasets, respectively. Overall, embedding-based adaptation substantially improved GFM performance relative to zero-shot scoring, particularly for Evo2. MLP and XGBoost improved performance over logistic regression and SVM, but neither was uniformly optimal across all embedding and dataset combinations. In particular, XGBoost performed strongly, especially for Evo2 and several non-human tasks, whereas the MLP remained competitive and performed best in a subset of DNABERT-2 and Genos settings. We retained a two-layer MLP [256, 128] as the default downstream classifier because it provided a simple, fixed, and consistent neural readout across embeddings and benchmark datasets, while the ablation results indicated that the optimal head remains dependent on embeddings and species.

We first tested these GFMs on Human Data 1 (SpliceVarDB) splicing variant prediction. Based on the embedding of DNABERT-2, we trained and tested classifier of MLP on our human splicing variant independent dataset (train:test = 8:2, with 10% of training data reserved for validation). To ensure fair comparison across different GFM embeddings, identical train/test splits (random seed = 42, stratified by label) were applied. Strikingly, the MLP model achieved an AUROC of 0.8810, demonstrating the potential of the combination of the embedding and a non-linear MLP in predicting human splicing variants. As aforementioned, Evo 2 achieved an AUROC of 0.7089 in SpliceVarDB using its output directly. Similarly, we equipped Evo 2 with a two-layer MLP trained on the embedding from Evo 2 and our human independent data. The performance reached an AUROC of 0.8804, which is a 24.19% improvement. Finally, for Genos, whose zero-shot output targets general pathogenicity rather than splicing specifically, we found that the embedding-based adaptation was crucial. By applying the same MLP architecture to Genos’s embeddings, we achieved a competitive AUROC of 0.8589, confirming that Genos’s representations capture rich splicing variant information despite the task misalignment of its pre-trained head. Our analysis reveals that the predictive power of GFMs for splicing variant effect depends on how the generated embeddings are used and that the direct score of GFMs (e.g. the effect of the splicing variant from Evo 2) can be further improved by a re-trained fine-grained model (e.g. an MLP) for splicing variant prediction [139, 140].

Given that Nucleotide Transformer and SpliceBERT exhibited near-random performance in Human Data 1 (AUROC ~0.5000), indicating their representations lack sensitivity to splicing-regulatory signals, we focused embedding-based adaptation on three representative GFMs, applying tailored evaluation strategies based on their architectural characteristics: (i) DNABERT-2: as a BERT-based model without native zero-shot scoring for variants, we evaluated it using embedding-based adaptation (MLP); (ii) Evo 2: we evaluated embedding-based adaptation (MLP) compared to its native zero-shot capability (ΔlogP); and (iii) Genos: we focused on embedding-based adaptation (MLP) because its zero-shot scoring is restricted to human genomic co-ordinates, pre-cluding direct application to non-human species. The MLP was trained using Human Data 2, rat, pig, and chicken datasets, respectively (train:test = 8:2, with 10% of training data reserved for validation). For cross-species comparison (Human Data 2 versus rat, pig, and chicken; Fig. 7A and B), we applied the same structure of MLP classifier for embedding adaptations. Our results again demonstrate that the “embedding + MLP” paradigm exhibited remarkably superior performance in cross-species prediction. Taking the rat data as an example, Evo 2 “embedding + MLP” achieved an AUROC of 0.7366, representing only a 11.49% decrease compared to its performance on human splicing variants (0.8322). Moreover, Evo 2 “embedding + MLP” achieved a 51.94% improvement in predicting rat splicing variants compared to its own output score (i.e. zero-shot; AUROC = 0.4848 on the same test set), suggesting that the ΔlogP score alone is not sufficient for accurate prediction of rat splicing variants. Similarly, DNABERT-2 “embedding + MLP” achieved an AUROC of 0.7112 on the rat test set. Surprisingly, the embedding-based adaptation strategy using Genos, despite being trained using human genomic data, achieved an AUROC of 0.8988 on the chicken test set, very close to its performance in Human Data 2 Genos-MLP prediction (AUROC = 0.8999). This finding demonstrated that supervised embedding adaptation for a specific task (e.g. splicing variant prediction) can compensate for limited phylogenetic coverage in pre-training, highlighting the quality of learned representations and the downstream adaptation strategy, rather than the breadth of pre-training species.

While supervised pre-training models like AlphaGenome often achieve better zero-shot performance due to direct task alignment [140–143], our results suggest that self-supervised objectives (e.g. MLM in DNABERT-2, NTP in Evo 2) capture fundamental sequence patterns such as k-mer distributions and motif co-occurrences [26, 59] - that are evolutionarily conserved [144]. These intrinsic genomic features proved remarkably robust to species domain shifts, enabling effective adaptation even when the pre-training data were phylogenetically limited. One possible explanation for the outstanding performance using Genos embedding on the pig dataset is that the MoE architecture provides modular, compositional representations where different “experts” specialize in genomic contexts [93], which could facilitate generalization for cross-species splicing variant prediction. However, this needs further investigation as the MoE layers of Genos were not analyzed in this study. Overall, these results demonstrate that embedding-based adaptation can markedly reduce the performance drop on non-human species relative to Human Data 2. For rat and pig, the decline remained evident but was substantially attenuated, ranging from 8.56% to 23.84% across DNABERT-2, Evo 2, and Genos depending on the embedding model. Furthermore, the performance of Evo 2 and Genos on chicken was largely preserved, essentially matching their performance on Human Data 2 (Fig. 7A and B).

Performance comparison among traditional machine-learning approaches

To contextualize the performance gap between modern benchmarked models and two traditional references, we additionally evaluated MaxEntScan [51] and the site-only predictor Spliceator [76] across all five benchmarks. As shown in Fig. 7C, both baselines performed only slightly above a random guess across datasets, with MaxEntScan yielding AUROC values of 0.4718–0.5144 and Spliceator reaching only 0.5178–0.5589, while our strongest zero-shot and embedding-based benchmarking models consistently performed better. These comparisons suggest that the value of modern deep-learning and GFM-based approaches lies not in simple splice site recognition alone, but in discovering variant-level signals that remain inaccessible to classical site-only references.

Discussion

Our performance benchmark results reveal that regardless of model structural design or training/testing strategy, most evaluated models showed performance decrease when being tested with data from a different species, reflecting an intrinsic trade-off between task-specific performance optimization and cross-species generalization capacity (visualized in Fig. 7D and detailed in Supplementary Method S2). Hence, we further propose two strategic computational directions for future model construction for splicing variant prediction. First, a “feature representation transfer” [145] technique, which learns a shared feature representation to bridge differences between source and target domains, could be considered to enable cross-species splicing variant prediction. Specifically in cross-species splicing variant prediction, splicing variant patterns can be learned by high-performance human task-specific models projected with new species using GFM embeddings as a universal alignment bridge. By leveraging GFM representations to adjust species-specific genomic shifts, such frameworks could potentially retain the high precision obtained by high-quality human training data when predicting splicing variants in other species lacking sufficient training data [33, 146]. Future efforts should be made to focus on developing standardized “adapter modules” that can be rapidly specialized for new species using modest labelled datasets. Second, multi-modal fusion frameworks could also be considered to transcend current architectural limitations by combining universal sequence representations from GFMs (for evolutionary conservation) with tissue-specific embeddings (for cellular context) and species-aware conditioning, thereby enabling precise splicing variant prediction that is robust to evolutionary divergence while remaining sensitive to tissue-specific regulatory contexts. On the other side, recent resources such as GutUDB [147] further highlight the value of integrated databases in linking genomic, transcriptomic, and phenotypic information across species, although similar splicing-focused resources remain scarce for non-human species. Our results have suggested that benchmark quality strongly influences the predictive ability of the models, while Human Data 2 remained more challenging than Human Data 1, i.e. experimentally validated splicing variants from SpliceVarDB. Meanwhile, embedding-based adaptation substantially mitigated the decrease of cross-species performance on sQTL + VCF-based datasets. Together, efforts to establish species-specific splicing variant databases analogous to human SpliceVarDB [99] or, alternatively, high-quality sQTL databases (e.g. ChickenGTEx [118]) would facilitate efficient adaptation for better prediction performance of splicing variants on non-model organisms.

Conclusion

In this study, we comprehensively reviewed 35 computational approaches related to splicing prediction, covering diverse methodologies from early ML to recent foundation models, and systematically benchmarked 10 SOTA computational approaches for splicing variant prediction, spanning task-specific DL architectures and emerging GFMs. To our knowledge, this work represents the first comprehensive benchmarking analysis quantifying the performance of both task-specific DL models and GFMs in predicting multi-species splicing variants, including human, rat, pig, and chicken, by curating five independent benchmark datasets, including experimentally validated human variants and population-derived sQTLs for human, rat, pig, and chicken, respectively. Our results revealed that while task-specific models achieved outstanding performance in predicting human splicing variants, they all showed decreased performance in cross-species prediction due to regulatory and pattern divergence of splicing variants across species. Conversely, while zero-shot GFM scoring remains limited, we demonstrated that supervised adaptation of GFM-derived embeddings (e.g. Evo 2) via constructing lightweight classifiers (e.g. MLP) significantly improved the performance in cross-species prediction by an average of >50% (relative to zero-shot baseline), for rat, pig, and chicken, respectively. We also summarized a variety of aspects of benchmarked approaches, including model architectures, input modalities, and utilization strategies, and pointed out potential directions for cross-species splicing variant prediction. We anticipate that our analysis will serve as a critical reference to guide future algorithm design and development for cross-species splicing variant prediction.
