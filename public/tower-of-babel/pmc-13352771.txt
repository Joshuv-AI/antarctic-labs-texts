Isolation and characterization of novel bacteriophage, vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8, targeting multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii strains

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health challenge [1]. Without effective interventions, AMR is projected to cause 1.56–2.26 million deaths and to be associated with 6.85–9.65 million deaths worldwide by 2050 [2]. The rising mortality attributed to AMR represents a major public health threat, which demands intensified focus, increased funding, capacity building, research, and development, and the setting of pathogen-specific priorities within the global health community [3]. Addressing AMR is essential for safeguarding public health and ensuring effective disease management. Multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria are a primary driver of this threat [4]. The current therapeutic options for infections caused by MDR pathogens remain limited [5]. Of particular concern, resistance to carbapenems among Gram-negative bacteria has shown the most rapid global increase of all resistance phenotypes from 1990 to 2021, narrowing the therapeutic options and increasing the risk of treatment failure [2].

The rapid emergence and global dissemination of MDR bacteria among ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.) present a formidable challenge to contemporary healthcare. Infections caused by these MDR pathogens substantially elevate the mortality risk in ill patients. The extensive antibiotic resistance shown by these organisms complicates their therapeutic management, while the development of novel antimicrobial agents lags markedly behind the evolution of resistance mechanisms [6]. Particularly concerning is A. baumannii, an opportunistic Gram-negative pathogen. Globally, approximately 45% of A. baumannii isolates are classified as MDR [7] and carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) was designated a “critical priority” pathogen by the World Health Organization (WHO) in the 2024 priority list. CRAB is responsible for severe, high-mortality infections including pneumonia and bloodstream infections, and poses a major challenge in clinical settings.

The global dissemination of CRAB is primarily driven by the expansion of high-risk international clones. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) has been used to clarify the clonality and epidemiology of resistant outbreaks [8]. Sequence types such as ST195, ST208, ST191, and ST369 are associated with heightened virulence, extensive antibiotic resistance, and complex transmission dynamics [9]. These findings underscore the urgent need for innovative antibacterial strategies. Bacteriophage therapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic modality for infections caused by MDR bacteria and other bacterial pathologies [10]. Bacteriophages are viruses that specifically infect bacteria and are the most abundant biological entities on Earth [11]. They can be used as precision antimicrobial “drugs” with a self-amplifying capacity, insofar as they replicated within and lyse susceptible bacterial hosts to release progeny virions. This strict host specificity provides a fundamental rationale for their use in targeted therapies [12]. Notably, in 2016, a 68-year-old patient with an extensive drug-resistant A. baumannii infection successfully recovered after following combination therapy with a cocktail of nine T4-like myoviruses [13]. Phage therapy has also demonstrated marked efficacy in treatment of the severe pneumonia caused by MDR A. baumannii [14, 15]. However, screening for phages that target specific resistant strains such as CRAB remains technically challenging [16, 17]. Their narrow host range limits the development of phages as broad-spectrum therapeutic agents [18], and genomics-driven approaches are consequently required to expand phage resource libraries [19]. Furthermore, A. baumannii phage lysins (e.g., AbLys1, LysP53) have dual functionality—degrading cell walls and stimulating the formation of outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) —offering novel pathways for antibacterial and vaccine development [20].

In this study, a prevalent ST208/KL2-type CRAB strain was used as the host to isolate and purify a lytic bacteriophage from hospital wastewater. The phage was characterized with both phenotypic and genotypic analyses, with particular emphasis on its host range. Its therapeutic potential was evaluated with a combination of in vitro and in vivo antibacterial assays. Our findings contribute both theoretical and material bases for genotype-informed and geographically tailored phage therapies.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Additional strains of K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Salmonella enterica, Listeria monocytogenes, Shigella flexneri, Escherichia coli, enteropathogenic E. coli, and Serratia marcescens were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) or the BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China). The 83 clinically isolated A. baumannii strains maintained in our laboratory were categorized into three distinct groups (designated as the Ab‑series, Ac‑series, and Ad‑series) based on their geographic origins, with all strains within each series originating from the same geographic region. And the standard strains ATCC 19,606 and ATCC 17,978 were used in this study. Lysogeny broth (LB) was prepared by dissolving 10 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L sodium chloride, and 5 g/L yeast extract in Milli-Q H2O and was sterilized with autoclaving. To generate solid or semi-solid medium, LB was supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar or 0.75% (w/v) agar, respectively, before autoclaving.

Phage isolation, propagation and purification

Phages were isolated with the standard enrichment method [21], using MDR A. baumannii strain Ac28 as the host. Environmental samples were collected from the underground sewage of the Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Southwest Medical University. The raw wastewater (100 mL) was transported to the laboratory on ice, centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min to remove cells and particulates, and passed through 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm membrane filters before use. The filtered wastewater (50 mL) was aseptically combined with an equal volume of 2 × LB liquid medium (supplemented with 2 mmol/L CaCl₂) in a sterile 250 mL conical flask. The homogenized mixture was incubated in a temperature-controlled orbital shaker (220 rpm) at 37 °C for 12 h under aerobic conditions. A plaque assay was performed to detect phage particles by spotting filtered supernatant samples onto bacterial lawns and observing plaque formation. To ensure that each isolate contained only one type of bacteriophage, phage purification was performed with three successive rounds of the double agar overlay method [22]. Individual plaques were excised from the top agar layer, suspended in 500 µL of SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO₄, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.01% gelatin), and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The suspension was centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 15 min, filtered through a 0.22-µm sterile membrane, and serially diluted in SM buffer. Each dilution was subjected to a new double agar overlay assay to isolate single plaques. This procedure was repeated for three cycles to obtain clonally pure phage isolates displaying morphological uniformity in plaque size and clarity. For long-term preservation, equal volumes of 50% oil and purified phage suspension were mixed and stored at -80 °C.

Morphological analysis with transmission electron microscopy

Morphological examination of the bacteriophage was conducted with transmission electron microscopy with negative staining, according to established protocols [23]. A 10 µL aliquot of high-titer phage lysate (~ 10⁹ PFU/mL) was applied to glow-discharged carbon-coated 300-mesh copper grids and allowed to adsorb for 10 min. After extensive rinsing with distilled water, the grids were negatively stained with 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid solution, with two applications of 10 s each. The stained grids were air-dried completely and then imaged with a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Determination of optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI)

The MOI was calculated as the ratio of plaque-forming units (PFU) to colony-forming units (CFU) [24]. To identify the optimal MOI, 1 mL of mid-logarithmic-phase host bacteria (~ 10⁸ CFU/mL) was infected with 1 mL of phage lysate at MOIs ranging from 0.00001 to 100. The mixtures were incubated at 37 ℃ with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h. The cultures were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 15 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatants were filtered through 0.22-µm membranes. Phage titers in the filtrates were quantified with the double agar overlay plaque assay. The MOI yielding the highest mean PFU titer was identified as the optimal MOI for phage propagation.

One‑step phage growth assay

The one-step growth assay was performed based on established methods, with appropriate modifications [25]. Bacterial cells were infected with phage at an MOI of 0.1 and allowed to adsorb for 15 min at 37 °C. Unabsorbed phages were removed by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 30 s. The pellet material was resuspended and diluted 2500-fold in pre-warmed LB broth, with this point defined as time zero (T = 0) for the growth cycle sampling. The samples were aseptically collected at predetermined intervals throughout the culture process and filtered through 0.22-µm sterile membrane filters. The phage titer in each filtrate was quantified with the double agar overlay plaque assay. Three independent biological replicates of each sample were processed under standardized conditions. A one-step growth curve was constructed to characterize the viral replication dynamics, and burst size was calculated by quantifying the progeny virions released during the lytic phase [26]. Burst size = N1/N0 (N0: number of bacteria at initial infection; N1: phage titer at the end of the lysis period).

Phage stability assay

Phage suspensions diluted in SM buffer to a titer of 1–5 × 10⁸ PFU/mL were incubated for 1 h at temperatures of 4, 25, 37, 50, 60, 70, and 80 ° C. The residual phage titers were quantified with the double-layer agar assay. SM buffer was adjusted to pH values ranging from 2 to 13 using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Phage suspensions were incubated at each pH for 1 h at 37 °C, after which the viral titers were determined with the double-layer agar assay [23].

Host range determination

The host range of the phage was initially assessed with a standardized spot assay [27]. The host range was evaluated against 83 clinically isolated strains of A. baumannii, A. baumannii ATCC 17978, A. baumannii ATCC 19606, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, etc. Briefly, bacterial lawns were prepared by overlaying LB agar plates with 100 µL of mid-logarithmic-phase bacterial suspension mixed with molten soft agar (0.75% w/v). After solidification, 10 µL aliquots of phage lysate (10⁸ PFU/mL) were spotted onto the designated lawn sectors. The plates were air-dried for 15 min, incubated at 37 ℃ for 18–24 h, and examined for lytic activity based on plaque formation and zone clarity.

Measurement of phage adsorption rate

A log-phase bacterial culture was combined with phage suspension at the optimal MOI and incubated at 37 °C. At specified time points (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 min), 1 mL aliquots of the mixture were collected and immediately centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm sterile membrane, and the titer of the unabsorbed phage in the filtrate was quantified with the double-layer agar assay [28]. The phage adsorption efficiency was calculated as: adsorption (%) = ([initial phage titer − unabsorbed phage titer] / initial phage titer) × 100%.

Phage genome sequencing and analysis

The bacteriophage lysate was treated with 200 U of BenzoNuclease (Novoprotein Scientific Inc., Suzhou, China) and 0.1 mg/mL RNase A (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) to degrade the host nucleic acids. The DNase was subsequently inactivated by heating at 65 °C for 10 min. The phage genomic DNA was extracted with the Qiagen MinElute Virus Spin Kit (Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was quantified with the Equalbit 1 × dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) on a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), and the genome size was assessment with 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. A sequencing library was constructed with the VAHTS® Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd), and its quality was rigorously verified on the Agilent 4200 Bioanalyzer system. After passing quality control, whole-genome sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

To ensure the reliability of the bioinformatic analysis, raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using fastp (v0.20.1) [29] to remove low-quality reads and those with a high proportion of N bases, resulting in clean reads. De novo assembly of the clean reads was performed using metaSPAdes (v3.15.3) [30], considering the environmental origin of the isolate and the potential presence of limited non-target background DNA despite plaque purification, with various k-mer lengths tested to achieve the optimal assembly result. Subsequently, the clean reads were mapped back to the assembled genome sequence using BWA (v0.7.15-r1140) [31] to calculate sequencing coverage.

Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted and functionally annotated with eggNOG-mapper (v5.0.0) [32], with integrated databases including Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy), BiGG Models, and Pfam protein families. A genome circle diagram was generated with the Proksee (https://proksee.ca/) module in CGView.

Virulence genes were predicted with BLAST homology searches against the Virulence Factor Database (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/). Antibiotic resistance genes were identified with ResFinder (v4.1.0) [33]. The phage lifestyle (lytic vs. lysogenic) was predicted with DeePhage (v1.0) [34]. The gene sequence of the phage large terminase subunit was used for the phylogenetic analysis. Reference sequences from the top BLAST hits against the National Center for Biotechnology Information nucleotide database, representing diverse genera within the same family, were included. A multiple sequence alignment was constructed with ClustalW, and a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA (v11.0.13) software, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to evaluate branch support [35]. The taxonomic position of phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 was determined through a combination of genomic and proteomic analyses. First, the complete nucleotide sequence of SWMUZ8 was compared against comprehensive reference datasets, including the INPHARED database (version 14 Apr 2025) and Virus-Host DB (release 231). Nucleotide identity thresholds of ≥ 95% and ≥ 70% were applied to define the same species and genus, respectively. Pairwise intergenomic similarity was calculated using VIRIDIC (v1.1) [36]. To further validate these classifications at the protein level, a network-based clustering analysis was performed using vConTACT3 (v3.1.6) [37]. This tool integrates the target genome into a global protein-sharing network based on shared protein clusters (PCs). Taxonomic assignments (e.g., family and genus) were predicted by identifying the clusters corresponding to established or putative taxa within the protein-sharing network.

In vitro and in vivo activity of phage against drug-resistant (XDR) A. baumannii

To assess the bacteriostatic activity of the phage as previously described [38], strain Ac28 was grown in LB medium to the mid-log-phase and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of the bacterial suspension was aliquoted into each well of a sterile 96-well microplate. To each well, 100 µL of phage suspension (diluted in PBS to achieve MOIs of 0.1, or 1) was added, resulting in a total volume of 200 µL per well. Phage-free bacterial cultures (100 µL bacteria + 100 µL LB) served as the growth controls. A phage-only control group (100 µL LB broth + 100 µL phage suspension) was also included, while sterile LB broth (200 µL) was used as the blank control. The plates were incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking, and bacterial growth kinetics were monitored with the real-time measurement of the optical density of the culture at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) at 60-min intervals for 12 h using a spectrophotometric microplate reader.

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of phage SWMUZ8 was assessed with a Galleria mellonella infection model [39]. Strain Ac28 was grown in LB medium to mid-log-phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5), washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS to a final concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL. Subsequently, 10 µL of the bacterial suspension was injected into the last right proleg of each larva using a Hamilton syringe (n = 10). Following a 30-minute infection period at 37 ℃, therapeutic interventions were performed by injecting 10 µL of phage SWMUZ8 suspension into the last left proleg to evaluate the systemic therapeutic effect and avoid localized interference. Phage doses of 1 × 10⁸ PFU/mL, 1 × 10⁷ PFU/mL, 1 × 10⁶ PFU/mL were administered, corresponding to multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 10, 1, and 0.1, respectively. The infection control group was injected with 10 µL of PBS (infected but untreated); the blank control received 10 µL of PBS without prior infection; and the in vivo protection group was administered 1 × 10⁸ PFU/mL phage SWMUZ8 without bacterial challenge. Survival was monitored at 12-h intervals for 72 h at 37 °C in the dark, with mortality defined as: (1) absence of response to needle tactile stimulation, and (2) complete body melanization. Experimental validity required 100% survival in the blank controls throughout the observation period.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism version 10.3. Differences between experimental groups were assessed with the two-tailed Student’s t test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed to evaluate the significance of temperature and pH stability assays. In vitro lytic activity was analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test was used for survival analyses. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Isolation and characterization of A. baumannii phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8

Wastewater was sampled at the Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Southwest Medical University. Using MDR A. baumannii strain Ac28 (ST208/KL2) as the host, a novel phage was isolated with three consecutive cycles of plaque purification, and was designated “vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8”. After incubation for 8 h on double-layer agar plates, the phage produced uniform, transparent plaques with clearly defined circular margins. The plaques were surrounded by distinct secondary lysis zones, indicative of peripheral bacterial clearance (Fig. 1A). Transmission electron microscopy revealed characteristics of a podovirus (Fig. 1B), with a total virion length of 82.78 ± 1.13 nm, comprising an icosahedral capsid measuring 59.38 ± 2.79 nm in length and 56.69 ± 0.33 nm in width, and a 19.42 ± 3.56 nm tail structure (n = 3).

Characterization of infection kinetics in bacteriophage SWMUZ8

Optimization of the MOI revealed that bacteriophage SWMUZ8 achieved its peak titer (1.3 × 10⁹ PFU/mL) at an MOI of 0.0001—significantly higher than the titers achieved at other MOIs (Fig. 1C). This result suggests that efficient host clearance could even be attained at very low phage-to-host ratios.

Adsorption kinetics assays, which evaluated the phage’s binding affinity for host cells, revealed rapid attachment [40]. Bacteriophage SWMUZ8 achieved over 40% adsorption within 4 min, and more than half the phage particles had adsorbed by 8 min (Fig. 1D).

A one-step growth curve analysis conducted at an MOI of 0.1 revealed the intracellular replication dynamics of bacteriophage SWMUZ8 within its bacterial host [41]. Following a 15-min adsorption and subsequent resuspension in fresh medium, the phage titer remained stable for approximately 4 min. The total latent period was approximately 19 min, calculated from the moment phage was added to include the time for initial adsorption. This was followed by an increase in titer during the burst period, reaching a plateau at 80 min (Fig. 1E). The calculated mean burst size was 16.83 PFU per infected cell.

Assessment of phage stability under thermal and pH conditions

The environmental stability of the bacteriophage was evaluated across a range of temperatures and pHs. The phage maintained stable activity at 4 ℃ and 50 ℃, whereas a marked reduction was observed at 60 ℃, followed by its inactivation at 70 ℃, indicating that the phage is sensitive to thermal stress. Similarly, the phage showed infectivity over a broad pH range (pH 4–10) but lost all activity under acidic (pH < 3) or highly alkaline conditions (pH > 11) (Fig. 1E, F). These findings suggest that the phage can withstand moderate fluctuations in these specific parameters, providing information for storage at 4 °C or transport at ambient temperatures while maintaining its activity.

Susceptibility of ST208 strains to bacteriophage SWMUZ8 and geographic correlation in host range

Bacteriophage SWMUZ8 was evaluated against 83 clinical A. baumannii isolates, the reference strain ATCC 19606, and five non-baumannii bacterial species. The results showed that the phage has a narrow lytic spectrum, specifically lysing A. baumannii strains but remaining inactive against all other microorganisms examined (Table 1). Of the 84 A. baumannii strains tested, 88% (44 of 50) of the ST208 strains were susceptible to the phage, with clear plaques observed in the majority of these isolates. In contrast, all 13 ST195 strains and five ST369 strains were fully resistant to SWMUZ8 (Fig. 2). Additionally, bacteriophage SWMUZ8 lysed all 44 geographically colocalized A. baumannii isolates (originating from the same region as its original host), suggesting a geographic association of its lytic activity.

Genomic characterization and phylogenetic analysis of bacteriophage SWMUZ8

Whole-genome sequencing revealed a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 41,708 bp with a GC content of 39.46% (Fig. 3A). In total, 54 putative protein-coding genes (CDSs) were predicted, over half of which were functionally annotated with HHpred, PHROGs, and BLASTp. These CDSs were categorized into four functional groups: 19 structural/packaging proteins (blue), 21 proteins involved in DNA/RNA processing and amino acid metabolism (orange), two host lysis-associated proteins (red), and hypothetical proteins of unknown function.

The longest CDS (3,099 bp) encoded an internal virion protein containing an endolysin domain. Furthermore no virulence or antibiotic resistance genes were detected in the genome, indicating a low risk of the horizontal transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes and supporting the safety profile of vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 as a therapeutic agent. Based on its DeePhage lifestyle score (0.92; scores ≥ 0.5 indicate lytic activity), the phage was classified as lytic. This phenomenon is consistent with the formation of the plaques on the double-layered plate.

In a phylogenetic analysis based on the large subunit of the terminase protein, vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 clustered closely with phages vB_Aba_SI4 and vB_Aba_ZC2, forming a distinct clade within the genus Friunavirus, family Autographiviridae, and class Caudoviricetes (Fig. 3B). The consistent results from VIRIDIC (Fig. 4) and vConTACT3 (see Additional file 1) support the classification of this phage within the genus Friunavirus and family Autographiviridae. By combining its morphology, genomic features, and phylogenetic position according to the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses nomenclature guidelines, the isolate was named vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8, where “vB” denotes a bacteriophage, “Aba” refers to its host A. baumannii, and “A” indicates membership of the family Autographiviridae.

Lytic activity of phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 against CRAB

To evaluate the inhibitory effect of the phage on the growth of CRAB strains in vitro, lysis profiles were determined over a 12-h period. Statistically significant differences in OD₆₀₀ were observed between the phage-free CRAB control and the phage-treated groups (Fig. 5A). When the MDR host bacterium was cocultured with the phage at MOIs of 0.1 or 1, the OD₆₀₀ values remained at a stable level of approximately 0.1 for the first 5 h, indicating a significant bacteriostatic effect. Notably, a distinct inflection point was observed in the growth curves of all phage-treated groups after 5 h of coculture, followed by an increase in the OD₆₀₀ value.

Therapeutic efficacy of phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 against MDR A. baumannii in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of phage SWMUZ8 against A. baumannii infection, a G. mellonella larval model was challenged with a bacterial inoculum of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. At this dose, larvae in the positive control group (injected with bacteria alone) displayed a 60% mortality rate within 48 h, confirming the successful establishment of a lethal infection model. In comparison, larvae in the negative control group (injected with PBS only) and those receiving a high dose of phage alone (1 × 10⁸ PFU/mL) both showed 100% survival rates, demonstrating the stability of the experimental system and the absence of host toxicity for the phage at high concentrations (Fig. 5B).

Evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 revealed an MOI-dependent protective effect. The highest survival rate (90%) was achieved at an MOI of 10, followed by 80% at an MOI of 1 and 50% at an MOI of 0.1. It is noteworthy that all phage-treated groups showed a certain degree of protection against infection. However, a significant therapeutic effect was only observed at an MOI of 10.

Discussion

The increasing burden of MDR infections has accelerated the development of phage therapy as a promising alternative in this era of escalating antibiotic resistance. This approach offers unique advantages: strict host specificity allows precise pathogen elimination without the disruption of the commensal microbiota (e.g., in the gut); self-amplification allows auto-dosing at the site of infection; and the biofilm-degrading capacity helps eradicate persistent foci, which are often refractory to conventional antibiotics [42, 43]. These properties not only underpin the therapeutic use of phage therapy but also extend to clinical uses [44] and its applicability to diverse fields, such as wastewater treatment [45, 46]. In this study, the lytic phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 was isolated from hospital sewage—a natural reservoir abundant in host bacteria and nutritional substrates that promote phage diversity [47, 48]. A whole-genome analysis confirmed the absence of virulence or antibiotic resistance genes, supporting the phage’s safety profile for therapeutic applications.

The resilience of A. baumannii in nosocomial settings is closely linked to its capsular polysaccharide (CPS) barrier, which functions as a key virulence factor by enhancing its environmental resistance, promoting biofilm formation, and facilitating immune evasion [49]. Among the > 100 identified KL capsule types, KL2 has emerged as particularly relevant due to its widespread prevalence and resistance to imipenem, sulbactam, and tigecycline. Notably, the phage described in this study formed translucent plaques surrounded by halos on bacterial lawns, indicating the presence of a depolymerase capable of specifically degrading CPS [50]. This mechanism is consistent with reports of previous studies. Phage P1068, which targets the K3 capsule type, effectively cleared biofilms and reduced the bacterial load in multiple organs of infected mice [51], whereas phage HZY2308 markedly disrupted the biofilm structure of A. baumannii AB48 [52]. Beyond enzymatic degradation, the precise molecular architecture of surface receptors is a primary determinant of host susceptibility. In A. baumannii strain AB900, both in vitro and in vivo evidence have previously confirmed that resistance to phage øFG02 arises through loss-of-function mutations in genes of the K-locus. Furthermore, these mutations were shown to inhibit the adsorption of phage øFG02, thereby conferring resistance [13, 53]. The differential susceptibility of A. baumannii KL239 to phage APK86 (targeting K86 type capsular polysaccharide) demonstrates that minor variations in L-rhamnose substitution patterns within capsular polysaccharides can critically influence phage recognition [54]. In addition, intact lipooligosaccharide (LOS) outer core impedes phage adsorption, while Lon protease restricts intracellular phage replication, revealing additional layers of phage defense independent of capsule regulation [55]. Moreover, phage-tolerant strains had significantly downregulated expression of the omp genes, which consequently reduced phage adsorption [56]. Furthermore, the phenotypic heterogeneity of A. baumannii presents a significant challenge to phage susceptibility. As demonstrated by previous investigation [57], strains like AB5075 exhibit phenotypic heterogeneity by rapidly switching between virulent opaque (VIR-O) and avirulent translucent (AV-T) states. This transition results in a twofold reduction in capsule thickness in AV-T cells, a modification previously hypothesized to render the host resistant to capsule-dependent bacteriophages. However, recent research on phage Mystique demonstrated that this phage is capable of infecting both VIR-O and AV-T variants of AB5075 [58]. In summary, these findings collectively indicate that the interaction between bacteriophage and bacterial is a complex and multi-dimensional process. In light of these complex surface interactions, the phage infection process involves four critical steps: (i) adsorption to surface receptors, (ii) DNA injection, (iii) transcription/replication and capsid assembly, and (iv) depolymerase-mediated peptidoglycan hydrolysis, followed by the release of progeny virions [59].

This complexity results in a narrow host range—where a single phage can only lyse a limited subset of strains due to the extensive diversity of surface structures among drug-resistant bacterial subtypes [60]. Conventional phage therapeutics faces a dual challenge: personalized approaches require the time-consuming matching of specific phages to bacterial isolates (e.g., in cases of extensively drug-resistant Pseudomonas infections in cystic fibrosis patients [61]), and broad-spectrum phage cocktails may heighten the risk of immunogenic reactions [42]. Consistent with the region-specific treatment strategy proposed by Koncz [18], phage SWMUZ8 exhibited an exceptional lytic rate (100%) against clinical isolates collected from the same geographic region. While Koncz et al. [18] emphasized the validation of phage cocktails, the 100% lytic efficiency of SWMUZ8 against isolates from the same geographic region suggests its potential as a core component for future therapeutic regimens. Using the prevalent CRAB clone ST208/KL2 as the target, the isolated phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 demonstrated not only high specificity for A. baumannii (with no activity against other genera) but also lysed 88% of ST208 strains—efficacy similar to that of phage pIsf-AB 02 (56.3% clearance [62]). Notably, it achieved 100% lytic activity against A. baumannii strains originating from the same geographic region (Fig. 2).

The therapeutic potential of phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 against lethal A. baumannii infection was directly confirmed with a G. mellonella larval model. This finding is consistence with previous animal studies in which the topical application of phage markedly reduced the bacterial burden in wound infections [63], further supporting the potential of phage-based anti-infective approaches. However, a notable paradox was observed. Effective protection in vivo (90% survival at MOI = 10) required a substantially higher dose of phage than the effective protection determined under optimal in vitro propagation conditions or in one-step growth assays. This discrepancy highlights the profound impact of the host environment, including immune barriers, the infection microenvironment, and pharmacokinetics, on phage efficacy and underscores the challenges to future large-scale production [64]. Importantly, all the larvae in the negative control groups (PBS-only and high-dose phage only [1 × 10⁸ PFU/mL]) survived, confirming the safety of the high-concentration phage (Fig. 4B). These results provide a foundation for high-dose treatment strategies. Furthermore, the synergistic effects observed in clinical trials, such as phage-induced antibiotic resensitization and virulence attenuation [61], coupled with novel delivery systems (e.g., nanozyme-encapsulated lysins [65]) that enhance targeting, stability, and bioavailability, offer promising avenues for achieving equally protection (e.g., 90% survival) at reduced doses. Therefore, the clinical translation of this phage will require: (1) the integration of genomic engineering with advanced materials (e.g., antimicrobial hydrogels [66]) to overcome the production challenges associated with high dosing; (2) clarification of the mechanisms underlying key molecular interactions (e.g., CPS-depolymerase activity) to address the efficacy gaps between in vitro and in vivo settings; and (3) the establishment of a region-specific pathogen–phage matching database to standardize the precise recreation of the potent protection observed in models.

Conclusion

In this study, the lytic phage vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8 was isolated from hospital sewage. Its safety profile, together with its depolymerase activity against prevalent KL2-type A. baumannii, underscores its clinical potential. The “genotype–geography” two-dimensional matching strategy effectively overcomes the traditional limitations of the narrow host ranges encountered with phage therapy, achieving 100% lytic activity against ST208 strains from the same region and 88% against geographically distinct isolates. Moreover, the G. mellonella infection model directly validated the in vivo efficacy of vB_AbaA_SWMUZ8, and the demonstrated safety of the high-concentration phage supports the feasibility of high-dose therapeutic strategies. Although this study was limited by the lack of validation in a mammalian model, it provides a foundation for the genotype-guided design of phage cocktails and offers a promising therapeutic alternative against recalcitrant CRAB infections.
