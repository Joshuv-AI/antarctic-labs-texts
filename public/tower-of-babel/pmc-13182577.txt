Rethinking the sphenoparietal sinus: implications for lesser sphenoid wing dural arteriovenous fistulas. Illustrative cases
Authors: Simona Serioli, Silvia Seguini, Luca Quilici, Alessandro Lunghi, Paolo Gritti, Alfonso Ciccone, Carlo Giorgio Giussani, Luigi A. Lanterna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) of the lesser sphenoid wing (LSW) are exceedingly rare, and their angioarchitectural features, natural history, and optimal management remain poorly defined.OBSERVATIONSThe complex anatomy of the sphenoparietal sinus and its controversial relationship with LSW-dAVFs necessitate precise, LSW-specific terminology to guide management. Starting from these premises, 3 patients with LSW-dAVF (Borden type III) underwent microsurgical treatment. Across the authors’ series and 19 papers from the literature, LSW-dAVF showed arterial supply from the middle meningeal artery (89.3%) and the ophthalmic artery (OphtA) (60.7%). Venous drainage typically occurred through a bridging vein originating from the LSW, immediately lateral to the superior orbital fissure, with secondary recruitment of the superficial and deep sylvian veins. An endovascular approach was selected as the first-line treatment in 64% of 28 reported cases, and complete angiographic occlusion was achieved in about one-third of these procedures (endovascular failure rate approximately 67%). In contrast, microsurgical clip ligation resulted in complete obliteration in all surgically treated patients, with no reported mortality.LESSONSThe complex angioarchitecture of LSW-dAVFs favors microsurgical management, as it provides direct access, definitive cure, and low morbidity. Endovascular therapy is limited by the anatomical features of these fistulas, which predispose to incomplete occlusion with the risks associated with navigation of the OphtA. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26129

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) of the lesser sphenoid wing (LSW) represent an exceptionally rare subset of intracranial vascular malformations, with only a small number of cases reported.1–18 These lesions share several distinctive anatomical and pathophysiological features, including frequent recruitment of leptomeningeal draining veins, association with venous varices, and a characteristic arterial supply that commonly involves the ophthalmic artery (OphtA).2–18 Collectively, these features suggest that LSW-dAVFs may demonstrate particularly aggressive clinical behavior, with an elevated risk of intracranial hemorrhage and other neurological complications.

Endovascular therapy is generally regarded as the first-line treatment for most intracranial dAVFs.10,12 However, LSW-dAVFs, similar to ethmoidal dAVFs, may represent an important exception. Embolization via the OphtA carries a well-recognized risk to the central retinal artery, raising concerns about treatment-related visual loss.19 Access via branches of the middle meningeal artery (MMA) is sometimes feasible, but the small caliber and fragile, reticular nature of the distal MMA branches can limit effective penetration of embolic material and reduce the likelihood of complete obliteration.1,20

In contrast, microsurgical disconnection of the LSW-dAVFs offers a direct and definitive solution. A standard pterional approach provides reliable access to the superior orbital fissure (SOF) and adjacent sphenoid wing, allowing for precise identification and interruption of the fistulous draining vein while avoiding the hazards of navigating the OphtA.

In this study, we present 3 surgically treated LSW-dAVFs, detailing their anatomical features, operative nuances, and clinical outcomes. We integrate these data with a systematic review of all published cases of LSW-dAVFs to clarify their anatomy and the role of surgery versus endovascular therapy in this uncommon but clinically significant vascular entity.

Anatomy and Taxonomy

The venous anatomy of the sphenoid region, and in particular the sphenoparietal sinus (SPS) and its relationship to bridging veins draining the superficial middle cerebral vein (SMCV), is complex and has been the subject of a long-standing debate. Clarifying this anatomy is not merely academic; it is essential for understanding the pathophysiology and treatment of dAVFs in this region.

The SPS was first illustrated by Gilbert Breschet21 in 1829 along the free margin of the LSW, draining toward the parietal convexity with the anterior branch of the middle meningeal veins into the superior sagittal sinus. Apart from these anatomical plates, Breschet’s atlas did not provide an explicit textual description of the SPS. In 1852, Cruveilhier22 described it as a collector of diploic and meningeal veins draining into the cavernous sinus (CS). Subsequent authors proposed that the SPS could receive temporal venous blood via bridging veins connecting it with the SMCV.23–25 Other anatomical studies suggested that the SPS could drain not only into the CS, but also in the sphenobasal and sphenoparietal sinuses, venous lakes located lateral to the CS (i.e., paracavernous sinus), considered remnants of the tentorial sinus.26 More recently, the concept that the SMCV may drain into the SPS, and even the existence of the SPS itself, has been questioned. In particular, San Millán Ruíz et al.,27 in a recent anatomical study on 15 human specimens, did not identify connections between the SMCV and SPS and concluded that the two venous structures were independent.

Despite the rigorous design of the aforementioned anatomical study,26 several angiographic and surgical series of the dAVFs have demonstrated clear SPS-SMCV connections.2,4,28 Moreover, recent case reports have described patients with SPS thrombosis and temporal lobe hemorrhage,29,30 a condition that indirectly supports the existence of a connection between the SPS and the SMCV. These apparently discordant findings between clinical and anatomical studies can be reconciled by considering that SPS-SMCV connections may be uncommon variants that are preferentially captured in clinical series (which are biased toward pathology), but easily missed in small anatomical cohorts. Alternatively, a “potential” or “virtual” connection between the SPS and SMCV may become functionally significant only under pathological conditions, such as thrombosis of the drainage pathways between the SPS and CS, leading to rerouting of venous drainage and clinical manifestations.

Because of this complex and still controversial anatomy, the literature on dAVFs in the sphenoidal region is heterogeneous in terminology and inclusion criteria. Some authors use broad anatomical descriptors (“fistula of the sphenoidal region,” “sphenoid wing region,” “middle cranial fossa,” “parasellar region,” and “LSW” or “greater sphenoid wing” [GSW] fistula), whereas others relied on venous-based definitions (“paracavernous” and “SPS”). However, fistulas of the LSW and GSW differ substantially in angioarchitecture,10,28 pial venous recruitment, natural history, and treatment response. For this reason, the terms “LSW fistula” and “GSW fistula” should not be used interchangeably, and these entities must be analyzed separately. Likewise, generic terms such as “parasellar region,” “middle cranial fossa,” or “sphenoid wing region” should be discouraged. Conversely, the term “paracavernous” typically refers to a dAVF of the greater wing that drains lateral to the CS, into what is considered the remnant of the tentorial sinus, and represents a distinct entity. The labels “SPS fistula” and “LSW fistula” can theoretically be synonymous, since the SPS is located along the LSW; however, the term “LSW fistula” may be preferable because 1) the SPS is not always visible on angiography; 2) the fistula may not drain into the sinus, resulting in a non–sinus type fistula, leading to confusion regarding grading; 3) the anatomy and even the existence of the SPS remain debated in the literature; and 4) a purely topographical term unambiguously conveys the regional anatomy and surgical approach.

Scoping Review of the Literature and Our Cases

A scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.31 The electronic search was performed on September 7, 2025, across three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. All English-language publications were screened using combinations of the following key terms: SPS, dAVF, pterional approach, surgery, paraclinoid, sphenoid wing, and SOF. The reference lists of all retrieved articles were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant studies. The search identified a total of 1486 articles. Studies were included based on the anatomical location of the fistula or its venous drainage pathway. Specifically, eligible cases included fistulas explicitly identified by the authors as involving the SPS or dAVFs draining into a bridging vein arising from the LSW, a venous structure receiving blood from the SMCV, or fistulas of the deep sylvian veins (DSVs) or basal vein of Rosenthal (BVR), given the possibility of venous continuity between the DSVs, SPS, and secondary drainage pathways into the BVR. Given the rarity of the condition, case reports and small case series were retained. Borden type was extracted only if explicitly reported by the authors.

In order to maintain a homogeneous anatomical cohort, exclusion criteria were 1) fistulas primarily located in the CS, 2) lesions described only by nonspecific terminology (e.g., “middle cranial fossa fistula”), 3) traumatic and iatrogenic dAVFs, 4) pediatric and syndromic cases, and 5) patients with associated skull base pathologies. Review articles without original data and case series or case reports lacking sufficient details for individual patients were excluded.

Full-text review was performed for all studies meeting the inclusion criteria. The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed according to the PRISMA 2018 recommendations.31 A customized risk of bias questionnaire was developed for the included case reports and case series,32 focusing on five domains (case definition clarity, diagnostic confirmation, completeness of clinical data, intervention description, and outcome reporting) (Supplementary Table 1).

Twenty patients were male (71.4%), with a mean age of 56.7 years (range 27–79 years). Hemorrhagic presentation, including intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) or subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), occurred in 13 patients (46.4%).2–5,7,11,16 Other symptoms reported included seizure (n = 2, 7.1%),11 orbital symptoms (n = 6, 21.4%),6,8,10,12,33,34 and tinnitus (n = 2, 7.1%).2,3,8

Arterial supply most frequently involved feeders from both the internal carotid artery (ICA) and the external carotid artery (ECA). The main ICA feeders arose from the OphtA in 17 cases (60.7%),2–4,6,10,11,15,17,27,33,36–38 with occasional contributions from the meningohypophyseal trunk (n = 4, 14.3%)8,12,35,36 and inferolateral trunk (ILT) (n = 6, 21.4%).4,15,33,35,37 The ECA contributed in most cases, predominantly through the MMA (n = 25, 89.3%).2–8,10,11,12,15–17,27,34–38 The most common venous route involved the SMCV (n = 20, 71.4%). In 13 cases (46.4%), drainage occurred via the deep middle cerebral vein (DMCV) and BVR, while 8 cases (28.6%) showed dual superficial and deep involvement. Among the 21 cases that analyzed venous dilation, a varix was identified in 19 (90.5%).2–6,10,11,12,15,16,27,33,35,37,38 Borden classification was explicitly reported only in 8 cases, all corresponding to high-grade (type III) dAVFs.2,3

Endovascular therapy was attempted as first-line treatment in 18 of 28 patients (64.3%),2,3,6–8,10,11,15,16,27,33–38 achieving complete occlusion in 6 (33.3%), resulting in a failure rate of 67%. Twelve patients subsequently underwent surgical treatment, all achieving complete cure. Among the 22 surgically treated patients, either a pterional or frontotemporal orbitozygomatic approach was used, resulting in complete obliteration in all cases without reported mortality.

Regarding postoperative neurological outcome, among 28 patients with available follow-up data, 26 (92.9%) had no new deficit or deterioration. No patient experienced permanent worsening attributable to surgery. One patient developed transient fourth cranial nerve palsy that resolved completely at the 6-week follow-up, and case 3 in our series achieved a Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) score of 4 due to rebleeding rather than the surgical complications.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 63-year-old male was admitted to the emergency department due to severe ictal headache. Brain CT showed a giant, partially thrombosed left temporal lesion, suggestive of a venous varix (Fig. 2A). Brain edema around the varix, localized SAH, and thinning of the temporal bone in the pterional region due to the remodeling effect of the varix were also documented. A subsequent CT angiography study revealed a dAVF, with a shunt located immediately lateral to the SOF at the level of the LSW/SPS. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) identified the recurrent meningeal artery, ascending pharyngeal artery, MMA, and ILT as the main feeders of the dAVF (Fig. 2B and C). The shunt was located immediately lateral to the SOF, where a dilated bridging vein coming from the LSW secondarily drained arterialized blood into the SMCV and DMCV, tentorial sinus, and left transverse sinus. Considering the risk for endovascular embolization due to the vascular morphology of LSW-dAVF and easy access to the fistula point via the pterional approach, the patient underwent surgery. Intraoperatively, the brain appeared edematous and tense due to recent bleeding and the giant varix mass effect. The reddish bridging vein was easily dissected, then clipped and coagulated, and the surrounding dura was cauterized. FIG. 2.Radiological images obtained in case 1. DSA performed 15 days after surgery demonstrated complete obliteration of the fistula (Fig. 2E and F). Nine years after the surgical intervention, the patient remains in good clinical condition, exhibiting no neurological deficits or functional limitations.

Case 2

The MRI examination, followed by a CT angiography study, revealed an LSW-dAVF on the left side, with the shunt located at the level of the SOF. DSA confirmed the diagnosis of an LSW-dAVF with the main feeders coming from the MMA and recurrent meningeal artery of the OphtA (Fig. 3A–C). Case 3

A 64-year-old male with monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance and multinodular goiter presented with generalized tonic-clonic seizure. In the postictal phase, he was alert, complaining only of headache. Noncontrast head CT revealed a Fisher grade 3 SAH (Fig. 4A). CT angiography and DSA demonstrated a left temporopolar dAVF, supplied by branches of the left OphtA and ECA, with the shunt point located at the level of the SOF and a single, large-caliber venous outflow draining into the BVR and DMSV (Fig. 4B–D). Three venous varices were identified along the tortuous draining vein, the most proximal of which showed focal wall irregularity consistent with a bleb, suggestive of the rupture site. Endovascular embolization was planned for the following day. Several hours later, however, the patient developed sudden-onset severe headache, right hemiplegia, and aphasia in the context of hypertensive crisis. Discussion

Analysis of the anatomical features of LSW-dAVFs revealed a consistent and stereotyped architecture, in which the main feeders frequently arise from the recurrent meningeal artery (originating from the OphtA) and the MMA. In most cases, the fistulas drained into a bridging vein located at the level of the LSW and secondarily into the leptomeningeal veins (SMCV or DMCV), which were markedly dilated and often formed large varices.2–8,10–12,15–17,27,33–38

These peculiar characteristics are consistent with the clinical behavior of LSW-dAVFs and have conditioned the treatment algorithm. In particular, recruitment of the leptomeningeal veins and the development of large varices are consistently considered ominous signs of an aggressive clinical course. The hemodynamic stress, to which the fragile pial veins are exposed, determines a progressive structural modification of the vessel toward the formation of venous aneurysms, explaining the high propensity for hemorrhage.39 Indeed, about 94% of the reviewed cases had large venous varices, and about one-half of the patients presented with cerebral hemorrhage.2–5,7,11,16 This strong relation with fragility and bleeding behavior justified an interventional approach to these fistulas, even when unruptured.

Although the trend in neurovascular surgery favors endovascular interventions, we consistently opted for a microsurgical approach due to the anatomical limitations and risks associated with endovascular access in LSW-dAVFs. In addition, the rate of complete occlusion of dAVFs, via the endovascular route, remained between 60% and 90%.9,40–43 Analyzing the recent results of the CONDOR cooperative study, the complete occlusion rate in high-grade dAVFs was approximately 45%.40 This percentage may be even lower in cases with an unfavorable anatomy, such as dAVFs involving the dural branches of the ICA (e.g., OphtA).

In our review, an endovascular approach was chosen as the first-line therapy in approximately 60% of cases,2,3,8,10,15,16,33–38 yet only one-third of these procedures achieved complete angiographic cure, corresponding to a failure rate of about 67%.

Moreover, although data in the literature remain limited, the risk of complications, whether technical, transient, or permanent, associated with embolization should not be underestimated when compared to surgical treatment in the management of dAVFs.40,41

Our review suggests that, similar to dAVFs of the anterior cranial fossa, these fistulas can be challenging to treat endovascularly, due to the risk of blindness inherent to inadvertent embolization of the central retinal artery and the difficulties in navigating small-caliber MMA vessels.19 This is of relevance in dAVFs in which the OphtA serves as the main, although not exclusive, feeder. Although a venous approach may be feasible, navigating within the venous varices can also present significant limitations.

Observations

Conversely, in this type of dAVF, the surgical approach appears straightforward for several reasons. 1) The surgical target is the bridging vein exiting the SPS, which is consistently located at the middle third of the LSW, a region that is typically accessible. 2) The vein is accessed via the pterional approach, a workhorse of microneurosurgery widely used for a broad spectrum of pathologies. Owing to its versatility, minimal brain retraction, and familiarity to neurosurgeons, this route is characterized by a low rate of permanent neurological worsening directly attributable to the surgical procedure, as evidenced by both our scoping review and institutional case series. 3) Drilling of the lateral sphenoid wing creates a wide surgical corridor, even in the presence of brain swelling. 4) The bridging vein can be safely dissected from the optic and cranial nerves.

Indeed, in both our experience and the cases reported, all dAVFs could be surgically reached, the bridging vein could be safely clipped, and all fistulas were completely and immediately occluded without complications. In addition, the effectiveness of surgery did not differ between those in whom a previous endovascular approach failed and those who underwent surgery as the first-line therapy. This result is concordant with the finding of the CONDOR study, in which surgery achieved an occlusion rate of about 90%.40

Lessons

LSW-dAVFs are rare but distinct vascular malformations exhibiting an aggressive clinical course, including hemorrhage and seizures. Their stereotyped architecture and consistent location of the draining vein make them particularly amenable to surgical treatment. Mainly because o the peculiar anatomy of these fistulas, the surgical management appears to be straightforward and effective with low morbidity and should be strongly considered as first-line therapy for LSW-dAVFs in suitable candidates.
