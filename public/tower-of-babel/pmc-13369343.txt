Heme Iron Polypeptide Resolves Iron-deficiency Anemia in Rats and Mice
Authors: Yue He, Jacob S Shine, Sitara Sriram, Melissa Fraga, Li-Ying Kuo, Jennifer K Lee, Xiaojing Yuan, Iqbal Hamza, James F Collins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDietary iron exists mainly as non-heme iron (NHI) and heme iron (HI). Mechanisms of NHI absorption have been recently revealed, but details of HI absorption remain uncertain. Heme is likely absorbed by receptor-mediated endocytosis, but to date, no receptor (or transporter) has been identified. Within enterocytes, heme may be catabolized by heme oxygenase in the ER, thus liberating ionic iron that then joins the labile cytosolic iron pool. One limiting factor in HI absorption research has been the lack of tractable experimental models. We addressed this shortcoming by establishing a nutritional paradigm that enables investigation of this process using pre-clinical rat and mouse models (JCI Insight. 2025;10(11):e184742). The approach utilized specially formulated diets containing lyophilized porcine RBCs that contained ~ 85% HI and 15% NHI. We have expanded upon these studies here to include heme iron polypeptide (HIP) as an iron source (Proferrin; Colorado Biolabs). HIP, a partially proteolyzed form of hemoglobin, is highly enriched for HI (~ 93% HI, 7% NHI). We again utilized a classic depletion/repletion experimental design in SD rats and C57BL/6 mice to compare outcomes when using three AIN-93-based test diets with ~ 50 ppm total iron: a HIP diet, a NHI diet containing FeSO4 and a HI-enriched diet formulated with porcine RBCs. Outcomes notably showed that all three experimental diets similarly corrected the anemia and normalized erythropoietic biomarkers in iron-depleted rats and mice within the 24-28-day test periods. These results solidify the concept that laboratory rodents are useful in vivo models to study intestinal HI absorption and utilization.Keywords: Heme iron, Heme, Iron supplementation, Intestinal iron absorption, Hepcidin, HAMP

Introduction

Dietary iron includes non-heme iron (NHI) and heme iron (HI); both forms of iron can be utilized by humans [1]. The NHI absorption process has been recently clarified [2], but specific mechanisms of HI absorption remain undefined [3, 4]. Transporters like heme carrier protein 1 (HCP1) and FLVCR were assumed capable of importing or exporting heme molecules across cell membranes [5, 6], but more recent findings showed that HCP1 is a high-affinity folate transporter [7] and FLVCR may mainly function as a choline transporter [8]. Once heme is transported into cells, it may be catabolized by heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), which releases free iron e [9]. HMOX1 was upregulated in iron-deficient rat duodenum [10]; however, an earlier study in mice concluded that HMOX1 was not required for dietary HI assimilation [11]. Heme oxygenase 2 (HMOX2) is not induced by iron depletion like HMOX1 [12], but it co-localizes with endosomes [4], where it could be involved in heme breakdown after import via receptor-mediated endocytosis [13]. Heme transporter HRG1 (SLC48A1) has also been defined as a heme transporter in C. elegans and mice, and it is expressed in human duodenum [14]. HRG1 plays an important role in erythrophagocytosis (EP) in macrophages, where it transports heme out of the phagolysosome into the cytosol for catabolism [15]. The function of HRG1 in the intestine, however, has not been clarified.

A limitation in the field of heme biology has been the lack of tractable genetic models of this process. To address this shortfall, we recently developed rat and mouse models of HI absorption [16]. This work demonstrated that both species could absorb HI from diets formulated with lyophilized porcine red blood cells (RBCs) as the iron source. Lyophilized RBCs contain 85% HI, but also 15% background NHI. We thus felt it was important to do a follow up study using a different HI source that contained lower background NHI. The current study tested heme iron polypeptide (HIP) as an iron source. HIP is produced by partial proteolysis of purified hemoglobin [17], and is an approved supplemental iron formulation that has already been used in human clinical trials [18]. We analyzed HIP obtained from a commercial source and found that background NHI was ~ 7%, which was an improvement from the porcine RBC iron source. Experimental diets containing iron from HIP (93% HI, 7% NHI) were thus utilized in iron depletion-repletion studies in rats and mice, and outcomes were compared to identical experiments using control diets formulated with FeSO4 (100% NIH, 0% HI) or porcine RBCs (85% HI, 15% NHI).

This represents a ~ 5-fold enrichment of HI in the RBC diet and a ~ 15-fold enrichment of HI in the HIP diet (as compared to NHI levels).

Iron Depletion-Repletion Study with Heme Iron Polypeptide in Rats

Our previous study showed that Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats could absorb enough iron to correct iron-deficiency anemia irrespective of whether the diets contained only NHI or mainly HI. In the current study, we utilized a similar study design, but also included a diet sourced with HIP. Iron depletion proceeded first for 2 weeks, and the hemoglobin of these animals dropped from a mean value of 11.25 to 5.68 g/dL (Fig. 1A). Consistent with our earlier studies, a 50 ppm NHI repletion diet made with ferrous sulfate was able to correct the anemia, normalize erythropoietic biomarkers and replete tissue iron levels (Fig. 1). The RBC and HIP diets also corrected the anemia, but iron stores were not fully repleted, as indicated by lower liver, spleen and bone marrow iron levels compared to the rats repleted with the NHI diet (Fig. 2). Dietary HI from HIP was thus absorbed efficiently enough to correct IDA in SD rats. Diets with higher total iron content may have also corrected the iron deficiency, which would be consistent with our previous observation that a HI-enriched RBC diet with 75 ppm total iron was required to correct the anemia and normalize tissue iron stores in iron-depleted rats.

Mouse iron depletion-repletion study with heme iron polypeptide

The iron requirements for growing mice and rats are different; mice require less dietary iron than rats (our unpublished observations). Consequently, it took longer (3 weeks) to deplete iron stores and make mice sufficiently anemic to carry out the repletion study. After consuming the low-iron diet for three weeks, the hemoglobin of experimental mice went down from a mean value of 12.52 to 8.87 g/dL (Fig. 3). Thereafter, mice were switched to repletion diets, including a 50 ppm NHI (control) diet or RBC or HIP (experimental) diets, for 24 days. During the repletion period, hemoglobin was checked every 4 days. Outcomes showed that, like rat study results shown above, all 3 repletion diets successfully corrected the anemia and normalized erythropoietic biomarkers in mice (Figs. 3 and 4) Moreover, also like the rat studies, the RBC and HIP diets did not fully normalize iron stores as effectively as the 50 ppm NHI (FeSO4) diet (Fig. 4).

Discussion

HI absorption and utilization have been described in humans [19, 20]. Historically, pigs, dogs and rats were used as models to study mechanisms of HI absorption [21–24], but molecular details are still lacking. Mice have historically been thought to be poor absorbers of dietary HI, and thus, the innumerable genetic mouse models in existence have not been adequately leveraged to make new discoveries related to HI absorption [11, 25]. HI sources used in earlier studies varied; researchers used meats, hemoglobin, digested hemoglobin and hemin chloride to investigate HI absorption [21, 26–28]. In the current investigation, we sought to compare absorption efficiency/utilization using a recently developed supplemental form of iron, HIP, and compare its efficacy to a physiological source of HI, porcine RBCs. HIP is partially proteolyzed bovine hemoglobin; hydrophobic peptide breakdown products of hemoglobin are thought to make heme more soluble and improve its absorption and utilization [29].

In our previous studies, we demonstrated that both rats and mice were able to utilize dietary HI from lyophilized RBCs to support growth and pregnancy outcomes, to precipitate iron-loading (in a hereditary hemochromatosis model) and to correct iron-deficiency anemia [16]. One pitfall of this previously published work was that the HI source (RBCs) contained 15% background NHI, which undoubtedly contributed to overall iron absorption in these animals. Therefore, in this follow up study, we used a commercial supplement, HIP, as a HI source. Given that HIP is 15-fold enriched for HI and contains only ~ 7% background NHI (Table 1), we hypothesized that it would be an ideal complementary test material to strengthen our claim that laboratory rodents can be useful in vivo models of dietary HI absorption/utilization.

In the current work, rats and mice were both shown to be able to utilize HI from dietary HIP to correct IDA, essentially as effectively as the RBC HI source tested previously. Tissue iron stores, however, were not fully repleted by either HI-enriched diet (RBCs or HIP). We thus postulate that the minimal iron requirement for iron derived from heme is higher than it is for NHI in both species. This seems logical since, unlike NHI, dietary heme has to be processed more extensively to be effectively absorbed and utilized, likely involving the following steps: (1) proteolytic release of heme from associated proteins and interaction between heme and hydrophobic peptides in the lumen of the small intestine; (2) Binding to a cell-surface receptor/transporter and import via endocytosis; and (3) Heme catabolism by HMOX1 (or another enzyme) in either the ER or in endosomes (if in endosomes, iron must then be transported out into the cytosol). Moreover, HI absorption/utilization may be less efficient in mice and rats, but this is not surprising given that rodents and Homo Sapiens diverged from a common ancestor tens of millions of years ago. Irrespective of relative absorption efficiencies, SD rats and C56BL/6 mice can utilize HI as a dietary source, and these new approaches/models can ultimately be leveraged to make new discoveries in this underdeveloped area of research (e.g., using genetically modified animal models).

Methods

Experimental animals and study design

All experimental procedures were approved and conducted in accordance with the University of Florida IACUC. Female SD rats and C57BL/6 mice were either purchased from the Jackson Laboratory or bred in-house at University of Florida Animal Care facilities. Semi-purified, AIN-93-based rodent diets were manufactured by Dyets, Inc (Bethlehem, PA). For iron depletion-repletion studies, rats and mice were weaned on to a low Fe diet for 2 (rats) or 3 (mice) weeks to deplete iron stores and precipitate development of anemia. After the depletion period, animals were fed customized repletion diets until the end of each study (Table 2). During the repletion period, mice and rats were bled every 4 days to check hemoglobin, and the study end point was dictated by the hemoglobin recovery rate in the repletion groups.

Hematological Parameters and Tissue Iron Measurements

Hemoglobin and hematocrit (Hct) were quantified in whole blood. Hemoglobin was measured using a HemoCue 801 analyzer. Blood was transferred to a serum separation tube, and then centrifuged at 12,000 x g for 3 min to separate serum from RBCs. Serum NHI and total iron-binding capacity were measured by a colorimetric assay [30]. TSAT percentage was calculated as [(serum NHI/TIBC) × 100] [31]. Tissue NHI levels were measured by a colorimetric assay, as previously described [32]. Tissue samples were collected fresh and an acid solution containing 3 M hydrochloric acid plus 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid was added to the sample. Tissue samples were digested in the acid solution for 20 h at 65 °C, and then mixed with a chromogen reagent composed of 0.1% (w/v) bathophenanthroline sulfonate (BPS), 1% (v/v) thioglycolic acid and saturated sodium acetate in iron-free water. Samples were then loaded onto a 96-well plate and absorbance was read at 535 nm by a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek). NHI levels were calculated by comparing the absorbance to a standard curve made with a certified iron standard solution (Thermo-Fisher Scientific).

Analysis of HI Sources

Experimental diets contained ferrous sulfate as a NHI source, or lyophilized porcine RBCs or HIP as HI sources. HIP (brand name Proferrin) was purchased from Colorado Biolabs (Lafayette, CO). The target total iron concentration in the diets was 50 ppm. Total iron levels in all diets were determined by ICP-OES (Eurofins), and NHI levels were measured by the colorimetric assay with BPS [32]. For total iron concentration measurements, samples were digested in nitric acid at 95 °C for 20 h, and the supernatants were analyzed by atomic emission spectroscopy (Agilent). Concentrations of total iron were calculated by comparing the absorbance values derived from AES with a standard curve generated from an iron reference solution (Thermo-Fisher). NHI to total iron ratios in lyophilized RBCs and HIP were calculated using NHI and total iron concentrations as [(NHI/total iron) × 100]. To determine NHI/HI ratios, heme quantities were measured in the two source materials by absorption spectroscopy (Soret peak). Briefly, RBCs and HIP were dissolved in 0.5 M NaOH, and the absorbance of the solution was read by a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek) at 400 nm. The concentration of the heme was calculated by comparing the absorbance to a standard curve made with hemin [33].

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

RNA was extracted from frozen tissues with RNAzol RT reagent (Molecular Research Center, Inc.) The RNA concentration was measured by a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo-Fisher). Quantitative PCR was performed following an established protocol, and fold changes of target mRNAs were calculated using the 2-∆∆Ct analysis method [34]. The expression levels of mRNAs were normalized to expression of cyclophilin A (CypA). Primer sequences are listed in Table 3.

Statistical Analyses

Results are shown as line plots presenting mean ± SD, or box-and-whisker plots presenting median, interquartile range, and minimum and maximum values. Statistical analysis was carried out by GraphPad Prism by 1- or 2- way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison post hoc test to compare individual groups. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Curves in Figs. 1a and 3a were generated by JMP Graph Builder to fit the data trends of changes in hemoglobin levels during the repletion phase.
