Comparison of Root Coverage Procedure done with and without Loupes along with CGF—A Clinical Study
Authors: Ramnath Elangovan, Kowsalya Nallathambi, Dinesh C Maganti, Anitha Maganti, Dona Soman, Niranjan Diwekar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The coronally advanced flap (CAF) is a regularly used treatment for gingival recession, which is defined by the apical movement of the marginal gingiva, resulting in root exposure and unsightly crown extension. Treatment approaches have evolved, moving toward less invasive procedures like microsurgery.Materials and Methods:Twenty patients with bilateral Miller’s class I and class II gingival recessions, aged between 18 and 40, were chosen at random from the Adhiparasakthi Dental College and Hospital’s outpatient department in Melmaruvathur. A coin toss was used to split the patient population into test and control groups and groups. The test group received CAF with CGF under 3.5 × magnification using dental loupes, whereas the control group had CAF with CGF membrane using standard surgical techniques. Preoperative and three-month postoperative records were made of clinical measures, such as probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), recession height (RH), recession width (RW), and width of keratinized tissue (WKT).Results:Both groups’ mean postoperative values showed a significant improvement. Significant decreases in RH (3.0 mm to 0.4 mm, P < 0.0001), RW (2.6 mm to 0.3 mm, P < 0.0001), and WKT (4.2 mm to 6.8 mm, P = 0.0002) were seen in the control group. Significant decreases in RH (2.9 mm to 0.2 mm, P < 0.0001), RW (2.9 mm to 0.2 mm, P < 0.0001), and an increase in WKT (4.1 mm to 6.8 mm, P < 0.0001) were also seen in the test group. After three months, the test group had a mean percentage of 93% while the control group had a mean percentage of 87% root coverage.Conclusion:Adequate root coverage was achieved using both traditional and microsurgical methods. Nonetheless, 3.5 × magnification of CAF with CGF membrane resulted in reduced postoperative discomfort, better healing, more surgical accuracy, and improved ergonomics.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis causes the hard and soft tissues that surround teeth to deteriorate. It is characterized by gingival recession, an increase in pocket depth, tooth movement, and a loss of alveolar bone and clinical attachment. The apical displacement of the marginal gingiva that causes root exposure or an unsightly extension of the crown is known as gingival recession. The coronally advanced flap, which Norberg first described in 1926,[1] is the most widely used and reliable method of addressing gingival recession. Gingival recession therapy has changed throughout time, and it now emphasizes the use of minimally invasive methods. One advancement in less invasive surgical techniques is microsurgery. In the discipline of periodontics, microsurgery was first used in 1992.[2] Using a surgical microscope with a magnification of at least 10×, microsurgery is carried out. Magnification enhances visibility at the surgical site and provides ergonomic advantages that enhance operator proficiency.[3] Various methods of regeneration are used, such as growth factors, guided tissue regeneration, and bone grafting. By regulating cell adhesion, attachment, survival, proliferation, chemotaxis, and differentiation, growth factors enhance healing.[4] Since platelets are the first cells to establish hemostasis at the site of injury and release growth factors to promote healing, they are considered autogenous growth factors.[5] The first generation of platelet-derived gel was called platelet-rich plasma, and the second generation is called platelet-rich fibrin.[6]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Twenty patients with bilateral Miller’s class I and class II recessions, aged between 18 and 40, were chosen from the Adhiparasakthi Dental College and Hospital’s outpatient department in Melmaruvathur. The participants gave their agreement after being briefed about the technique and all of the topics. Using the coin toss procedure, the chosen patients were split into two groups: the test site and the control site.

Control site: Coronally advanced flap using conventional surgical approach with concentrated growth factor membrane.

Test location: Coronally advanced flap with concentrated growth factor, magnified 3.5 times using dental loupes.

Before surgery and three months after surgery, the clinical parameters are documented for each surgical site using the University of North Carolina (UNC-15) probe.

CGF preparation

Intravenous blood is drawn from the patient in 10-ml glass-coated plastic tubes without the use of anticoagulant solutions to create a concentrated growth factor prior to the surgical operation. Using a membrane plier, the CGF clot was extracted from the tube and pressed to create membranes with a consistent thickness of 1 mm.

Surgical management

Using a No. 15 Bard Parker blade, crevicular incisions were made on the teeth, exhibiting gingival recession at the control site. A mucoperiosteal flap of full thickness was reflected, and the interdental papilla was de-epithelialized. After planning the exposed root surface, the patients’ CGF membrane was put over the root surfaces, the flap was positioned coronally, and 3–0 (polyglycolide–colactide) sutures were used to fix it [Figure 1].

Coronally advanced flap (control site). (a) Preoperative image, (b) Flap reflection, (c) CGF membrane placement, (d) Coronal advancement and suturing, (e) Periodontal dressing, (f) Postoperative image

In the test site scenario, 3.5 × magnification was used to make crevicular incisions on the teeth without affecting the interdental papillae using a No. 15c microsurgical blade. De-epithelialization occurred in the interdental papilla. Subsequently, a mucoperiosteal flap in its entirety was reflected. After that, the flap was positioned coronally and fastened with 5–0 (polyglycolide–colactide) sutures. The CGF membrane was then applied over the root surfaces [Figure 2].

RESULTS

The difference between the control and test sites’ baseline values was statistically not significant when comparing their mean preoperative values. When comparing the mean postoperative values of several clinical parameters between the test and control sites, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean postoperative values between the two sites. Three months later, there was a substantial increase in the breadth of keratinized tissue and a significant decrease in the height and width of the recession when the mean preoperative and postoperative values of clinical parameters at the control site were compared. There was a substantial increase in the breadth of keratinized tissue and a significant decrease in the height and width of the recession when the mean preoperative and postoperative values of several clinical parameters at the test site were compared [Table 1]. After three months, the average percentage of root coverage was 93% in the test site and 87% in the control site [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

While microsurgery provides benefits, including greater lighting, magnification, and accuracy, conventional root covering procedures are often more invasive. This leads to improved healing via passive main wound edge opposition. Growth factors are tiny proteins that promote biological responses and support angiogenesis, migration, cell proliferation, tissue regeneration, and survival. Rose et al.[7] were the first to identify growth factors in platelets.[1] In connective tissue cells, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) functions as a strong mitogen; TGF-β activates osteoprogenitor cells; insulin-like growth factor encourages osteoblast differentiation; and VEGF facilitates endothelial migration and proliferation, assisting in the formation of angiogenesis.[2]

Because the method was more precise and less intrusive, there was a larger increase in clinical attachment level (CAL) at the test location. Since the coronally advanced flap is a reliable and safe technique, it played a significant role in lowering the recession height. The keratinized tissue exhibited a notable expansion in breadth, which might be attributed to the CGF membrane’s capacity to regulate cellular processes and enhance tissue regeneration and repair. The improved visual acuity and accuracy provided by microsurgery, which makes the treatment less invasive than the traditional approach, may be the cause of the minor increase in mean root coverage at the test location.[3456]

CONCLUSION

Both conventional and microsurgical techniques demonstrated sufficient root coverage within the study’s constraints. However, the coronally advanced flap with concentrated growth factor membrane, when carried out at the test site under 3.5 × magnification, reduced postoperative discomfort, enhanced healing, improved the clinician’s surgical abilities with better ergonomics, and reduced the invasiveness of the surgery. Nonetheless, longer-term randomized clinical control studies have to be conducted to evaluate the advantages of microsurgical treatments.
