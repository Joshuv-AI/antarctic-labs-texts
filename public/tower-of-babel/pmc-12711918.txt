Cell line-matched reference enables high-precision functional genomics
Authors: Luca Corda, Emilia Volpe, Hamza Dallali, Elena Di Tommaso, Alessio Colantoni, Andrea Guarracino, Sai Swaroop Chittoor, Mattia Capulli, Evelyne Tassone, Simona Giunta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Comparative analyses of newly available human genome assemblies highlight extensive variation that peaks at centromeres. Reliance on a single generic reference genome can thus hinder whole-genome analysis of sequencing data derived from laboratory cell lines and limit their accurate genomic manipulation. Here, we demonstrate that using an “isogenomic” diploid reference genome – specific for the experimental cell line – substantially improves the accuracy of genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic analyses and genome editing compared to a non-matched reference. Using our recently generated reference genome of the widely used diploid human cell line RPE-1, we uncover haplotype-specific genetic and epigenetic divergence across all centromeres. Mapping quality of RPE-1 data – DNA- and RNA-seq reads, improves both genome-wide and at highly divergent loci when using the matched RPE1v1.1 reference, resolving haplotype-specific enrichment. For genome engineering experiments, centromeric CRISPR guide RNA efficiency and chromosome specificity are best achieved using the RPE-1 reference. Leveraging high-confidence CUT&RUN read mapping using the matched reference, we define the site of the human kinetochore and identify a wide variation in the position, size and structural organization between haplotypes and chromosomes. This work establishes matched-reference genomics as a powerful framework for high-precision cell biology, calling for the systematic assembly of experimentally relevant cell line genomes.

Introduction

Recent advances in DNA sequencing and genome assembly have led to the generation of high-quality human genomes, including the near-homozygous CHM131 and genomes from individuals of diverse ancestry2,3. These, along with others subsequently added4–6, represent an important step toward a more equitable representation of human genomic diversity3,7. Comparative analyses of the currently available assemblies have revealed an unexpected level of sequence variation3,5,8, including inter-individual divergence3 and population-level variants9–11. Differences within an individual genome pertaining to the two parental alleles12 are just starting to emerge using newly available haplotype-resolved diploid human genome assemblies2,8,13. Early analyses of the maternal and paternal haplotypes in HG002 show haplotype-specific sequence variation, including non-synonymous amino acid changes in almost 48% of all protein-coding genes2, in contrast with previous evidence pointing to low haplotype variation14. Comparison of the two haplotypes of the RPE-1 genome revealed peaks of sequence divergence in centromeric and pericentromeric regions, with an average single nucleotide variants (SNPs density) of 0.6% in centromeres and 0.1% in chromosome arms8. This profound genomic diversity has been shown to underlie phenotypic traits15–17 and significantly affects targeted gene editing and, in turn, clinical outcomes18. The extent of sequence variation highlights the need to reconsider current approaches based on alignment to the commonly-used reference genome for functional studies. Indeed, the analysis of sequencing data from a wide variety of laboratory cell lines is confounded by the ‘reference bias’19, particularly for whole-genome studies including human’s most polymorphic and divergent regions, such as centromeres5,8,20. The centromere consists of repetitive monomers of alpha-satellite DNA hierarchically organized into near-identical reiterating higher-order repeats (HORs) units21–24. While centromeric DNA is rapidly evolving, its essential function in chromosome segregation is epigenetically supported by the centromere-specific histone H3 variant CENP-A as basal layer for kinetochore attachment during cell division25,26. Two features point to the location of the kinetochore binding site within only a small region of the large active HOR: a sudden drop in DNA methylation named centromere dip region (CDR)24 and increased density of CENP-A nucleosomes estimated to reach ~1 every 4 canonical nucleosomes compared to 1 in 20 in the rest of the active domain27. In spite of advances in computational tools and long-read sequencing technologies1,24,28–30, reliable assembly of centromeres for all chromosomes remains a challenge. Telomere-to-telomere (T2T) reference genomes that include centromeres, such as CHM13 and HG002, are derived from cell types that can be hard to grow, manipulate and to use for functional studies and laboratory experiments. Here, we leverage our recent assembly of the diploid human reference genome for the experimentally-amenable cell line RPE-18, and present functional genetic, transcriptomic, genome engineering and epigenetic studies considering haplotype-specific variation (Fig. 1) performed by matching the reference genome with the specific cell line. The retinal pigment epithelial cell line (RPE-1) is one of the most commonly used non-cancer laboratory cell lines, counting thousands of scientific publications31. Because of its stable diploid karyotype and extensive sequencing data publicly available, we chose this cell line to generate a reference-quality, near-complete human diploid genome assembly8 and used it to characterize variation at centromeres. In this work, we show how the use of this assembly as diploid reference genome improves alignments of RPE-1 reads from sequencing experiments compared to any other reference. We describe the approach to match reads with the specific assembly as “isogenomic” reference, where the whole genome is matched between reads (from RPE-1 cells) and the specific reference (RPE1v1.1). We use the isogenomic reference for high-confidence phased epigenetic landscapes enabling the identification of the human kinetochore sites, for designing single guide RNAs that can precisely target highly divergent regions such as centromeres, and for mapping haplotype-resolved transcriptome (Fig. 1). This work establishes a proof-of-concept for an era of functional genomics spurred by matched read-reference genomes. Our findings lay the foundation for a broader effort to assemble genomes of experimentally relevant cell lines—including diploid embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells (ESCs and iPSCs), as well as primary and disease-derived models. Such a catalog of T2T assemblies will enable widespread application of matched reference specific to the experimental datasets for accurate interpretation of haplotype-resolved read mapping, high-resolution epigenetic profiling, and functional genomics at the whole-genome scale, extending our reach to complex regions that have long been omitted from genomic analyses such as centromeres.Fig. 1The diploid reference genome of RPE-1 enables high-precision functional genomic and epigenetic studies.Schematic overview illustrating the utility of the RPE1v1.1 diploid genome in high-throughput sequencing and genome editing experiments (upper panel). Results

Comparative characterization of inter-haplotype variation in RPE-1 centromeres

Previous analyses of a completely phased diploid genome suggest that differences between the two haplotypes of the same individual involve over half of all genes and peaks at centromeres2, which limits meiotic crossover32,33. To further understand the extent and nature of such variation between the two haplotypes of RPE-1, haplotype 1 (Hap1) and haplotype 2 (Hap2), we used our recently assembled RPE1v1.1 reference8 as a resource to study variation across phased haplotypes, especially focusing on the centromere (Fig. 2a, b). In our previous work, direct sequence comparisons revealed a dramatic drop in sequence identity between the two haplotypes for the same chromosome up to ~85%8. To avoid sequence-based alignments that can be confounded by the complex structure of the centromeric regions, we analyzed the organization of live (i.e., active) HOR monomers (LHOR) across centromeres and examined their variation between individual haplotypes. LHOR arrays are regions within which the kinetochore assembles to enable chromosome segregation and exhibit the lowest internal divergence among all HORs. We profiled alpha-satellite HORs using two complementary strategies: a hidden Markov model-based direct mapping approach34 and an indirect method involving the generation of heatmaps of pairwise sequence identity between kb-sized segments within each centromere35 (StainedGlass, see “Methods”). All centromeric LHOR regions displayed variation in organization, haplotype-specific HOR structural variants and/or different length (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1), in spite of overall preservation of the genomic architecture we recently uncovered36. Examples of inter-haplotype structural divergence were observed for chromosomes 9, 11 and X (Fig. 2b). For chromosome 6, both haplotypes were automatically classified as containing only one LHOR; however, the StainedGlass for Hap1 revealed two regions with high internal sequence identity (Fig. 2b). This may imply the insurgence of new active HORs, perhaps through previously proposed layered expansion24, pertaining exclusively to one of the two haplotypes. Other cases of expansion were inferred by looking at variation within the monomer organization. We identified repetitions of the same monomer structure in tandem within one of the LHOR for Hap2 of chromosome 4 and Hap1 of chromosome 19 (Supplementary Data 1). These observations underscore that the centromere of one haplotype may be anchored within a specific type of LHORs, while the other presents structural expansion that retains functionality, reminiscent of metastable epialleles cytologically shown for chromosome 1737. In addition to sequence and structural variation, we found substantial differences in centromere size between haplotypes for the same chromosome, as well as between chromosomes (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 1). Alpha-satellite DNA arrays within RPE-1 range from 0.49 Mb (chromosome 21, Hap2) to 6.24 Mb in size (chromosome 7, Hap1). When considering only the automatically annotated active centromere (LHOR), excluding the flanking divergent region, the range is 0.28 to 5.91 Mb (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 1). This is a wider spread than previously found for CHM1324, with a maximum centromere length of 5 Mb. Next, we calculated relative changes in the length of the active alpha-satellite DNA of centromeres between the two haplotypes of RPE-1 (Fig. 2d). We found a distribution of Hap1 to Hap2 ratio ranging from 0.27 to 5.06, indicating variability between the two parental haplotypes in all chromosomes. Specifically, 8 out of 23 active HORs showed increased size in Hap1 compared to Hap2, with the most pronounced differences observed for chromosomes 21, 7, and 4 (5.06-, 1.85-, and 1.35-fold, respectively). Conversely, 14 out of 23 active HORs showed reduced size in Hap1 compared to Hap2, with the largest reductions found on chromosomes 13, 15, and 11 (3.7-, 1.51- and 1.35-fold, respectively). In chromosomes 1, 2, 12, 14 and 17, the active HOR had approximately the same length in both haplotypes with only subtle variation (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Table 1). This finding is in line with a recent comparison between centromeres of CHM13 and CHM1, showing chromosomes 4, 15 and 21 with the highest divergence in size20. Chromosome 21 has been associated with ensuing chromosome aneuploidy during embryonic development that underlies Down syndrome38, raising questions on the minimal functional size and of the impact of size divergence in centromere function and chromosome segregation. For chromosomes 7 and 15, the biological relevance of size variation remains to be established. Altogether, our data present centromere characterization in a human diploid genome, quantifying the type and extent of the variation between two haplotypes of the same cell.

Read mapping quality improves using the RPE-1 genome compared to non-matched reference

Recent efforts addressed reference bias and its negative impact on read mapping due to individual’s variation3,19,39. Given the inter-haplotype differences within the RPE1v1.1 genome and the inter-genome differences relative to other human assemblies8, we sought to assess the advantage of using the specific reference matched to the cell line’s reads when mapping sequencing data from the same cell line, particularly for regions that show high divergence like centromeres (Fig. 2). To do so, we tested the difference in the quality of reads alignment comparing matched versus non-matched reference genomes. First, we used whole-genome sequencing HiFi long reads generated from RPE-1 cells8 and mapped them against the diploid RPE1v1.1 or against non-matched assemblies, including haploid (CHM131) and diploid (T2T-YAO6 and HG0022) genomes (see “Methods”). These alignments allowed us to assess both the reduction in reference bias when mapping isogenomically and the haplotype-resolved assignment to the correct heterozygous alleles of reads derived from diploid genome sequencing (Fig. 3a). We kept only primary alignments but purposely avoided applying mapping quality filters—commonly used to exclude multi-mapping reads—to preserve information from reads aligning to homozygous regions of the diploid genome, where such filtering would have resulted in the loss of approximately 80% of aligned reads. Mapping coverage and the frequency of the most common and second most common bases aligning at each position (primary and secondary bases, respectively) were visualized using the NucFreq tool40 (Fig. 3b). Alignment of RPE-1 reads to the RPE-1 genome revealed a strong prevalence of primary bases and predominantly uniform coverage, supporting the validity of RPE1v1.1 as specific reference8 (Fig. 3b). Aligning to the CHM13 reference genome resulted in a high frequency of secondary bases as expected for a haploid genome, and revealed several issues with read coverage. These included regions with heterogeneous coverage patterns, abrupt spikes, and areas with complete loss of coverage (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). Centromeric/pericentromeric regions of chromosomes 2, 6, 9 and 17 of CHM13 presented issues in read coverage with respect to RPE1v1.1 (Fig. 3b). The improvements using the matched reference to the reads are found even when comparing to other high-quality but non-matched diploid reference genomes. Mapping the same RPE-1 reads against the T2T-YAO and HG002 diploid genomes, we observed a reduction in coverage evenness and an increase in the frequency of secondary bases, indicating that the usage of the specific diploid matched reference better supports accurate read alignment with haplotype resolution (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). To further address whether the use of the matched reference specific to the cell line improves RPE-1 reads alignment at the whole genome level in addition to the increase in quality within highly divergent loci, we evaluated read alignment in terms of mapping quality (MAPQ, a metric that accounts for how well a read can be mapped to a unique location) and edit distance (NM value, defined as the number of changes necessary to make an aligned read equal to the reference, excluding clipping). To this end, we generated short Illumina reads and long HiFi reads from a batch of RPE-1 cells different from that used to generate the RPE1v1.1 assembly, and mapped them to the two individual RPE1v1.1 haplotypes and to the CHM13 genome. Only primary alignments were kept to gather a single pair of measurements per read. Mapping to the RPE-1 haplotypes yielded lower NM values than using the CHM13 reference genome (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Although all genomes showed an average mapping quality close to 60 (the maximum possible score), we observed a statistically significant reduction in read mapping quality when comparing Hap1 to CHM13 and a significant increase for Hap2 relative to CHM13 (p < 0.0001, Student’s t-test, Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). To explain this result, we then evaluated mapping quality and NM value for each chromosome separately (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 4e). We found that this genome-wide drop was due to the presence of the marker chromosome t(X;10)(Xq28;10q21.2)8, with a decrease in both the NM value (Fig. 3d) and the mapping quality score (Supplementary Fig. 4e). The mapping quality of the other chromosomes, instead, showed a distribution higher or similar when RPE-1 haplotypes were used as a reference compared to CHM13 (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 4e). We then focused exclusively on the RPE1v1.1 regions showing variation relative to CHM13 (highly divergent regions, HDR), which we previously identified using SyRI8,41 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Within the HDRs, MAPQ scores and NM values significantly changed when RPE-1 was used, with a statistically significant increase in mapping quality score comparing Hap1/Hap2 against CHM13 (Fig. 3e, f) and a statistically significant decrease for the NM value comparing CHM13 to Hap1/Hap2 (Fig. 3g, h). Altogether, our results confirm the effects of reference choice on alignments, supporting the use of the specific diploid reference matched to the experimental cell line showing uniquely mapped reads and quality of alignment increase when using a matched reference, and this is especially significant for divergent loci; these improvements represent functional opportunities to uncover haplotype-specific SVs, protein-DNA interaction42 and improve other alignment-based analyses.

Isogenomic reference reveals mapping-induced differences in transcriptome analysis

To assess the impact of using a matched reference genome on transcriptome analysis, we performed a differential expression analysis using bulk RNA-seq data from RPE-1 cells (see “Methods”). Briefly, we aligned the same set of RNA-seq reads from three biological replicates43 to three different genomes: RPE1v1.1 haplotype 1 and 2 (Fig. 4a), and the standard hg38 human reference. We then conducted differential expression analysis by comparing the same biological replicates but analyzed with different genomes used as reference (Hap1 vs. Hap2, Hap1 vs. hg38, and Hap2 vs. hg38, Supplementary Data 2). This approach allowed us to isolate the effects of reference genome choice on apparent gene expression, as any differences observed would be solely due to mapping discrepancies rather than biological variation. Our analysis revealed substantial differences in gene expression profiles that were entirely attributable to the choice of reference genome used for read alignment (Fig. 4b, c, Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Notably, we identified 26 genes that appeared differentially expressed only when comparing RPE-1 haplotypes to hg38 (Fig. 4b, d, Supplementary Fig. 6c). These differences underscore how reference genome choice can drive artificial expression changes. Comprehensive analysis of differential expression patterns and normalized expression levels across all pairwise comparisons revealed intricate mapping biases that may result in apparent upregulation or downregulation of specific genes (Fig. 4e–h, Supplementary Fig. 6d–g). Overall, we observed a large cluster of genes with higher expression in both RPE-1 haplotypes compared to hg38 (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 6e, f), implying better mapping of reads to the matched RPE-1 genomes. Specific genes appeared downregulated in Hap1 compared to Hap2 (Fig. 4e). This suggests haplotype-specific differential expression using phased diploid reference where read mapping finds poorer alignment in Hap1, as evidenced by lower expression levels (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 6d). Interestingly, a subset of genes showed similar expression levels when comparing Hap2 to hg38, indicating comparable mapping attributable to higher sequence conservation within the coding DNA. Centromere-specific sgRNAs designed on the RPE-1 genome capture variation between haplotypes

Next, we tested how applicable was designing single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) for CRISPR-Cas engineering to target the desired region using the CHM13 reference genome compared to using the specific reference matched to the cell line used for the experiment (Fig. 5a). To this end, we took advantage of a recently published study that targeted the centromeres to induce chromosome-specific aneuploidy44. In this work, the selected chromosome-specific sgRNAs were designed on the CHM13 genome and validated using the hg38 reference44. First, we assessed whether these sgRNAs targeted the RPE-1 genome too and calculated the number of binding sites (see “Methods”; Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 3). Globally, we found that 3 (3.94%) out of 76 sgRNAs designed on the CHM13 did not target chromosome-specific LHOR in any haplotype of RPE-1 (Fig. 5b, e). These 3 sgRNAs targeted the LHOR of chromosomes 13 and 18 of CHM13 (13-4, 13-8 and 18-3) with 100% chromosome specificity (Supplementary Data 3). Of the remaining 73 sgRNAs showing binding sites in RPE-1, 72 (94.74% of all sgRNAs) targeted both RPE-1 haplotypes, while 1 (1.32% of all sgRNAs) targeted only Hap1 (Fig. 5b, e). This latter guide had 1797 binding sites on the centromeric LHOR in chromosome 19 (guide # 19-3; Fig. 5e), without off-targets (i.e., binding sites outside the centromere of a specific chromosome and in other chromosomes) in all chromosomes of RPE-1 Hap1, and without any binding site in RPE-1 Hap2 (Supplementary Data 3). To assess haplotype variation, we considered the total number of binding sites of the sgRNAs targeting each RPE-1 haplotype (116725 for Hap1 and 107155 for Hap2; Supplementary Data 3) and observed 24.66% of off-targets for Hap1 (Fig. 5c) and 25.91% for Hap2 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Data 3). These values were similar to those observed in the CHM13 genome (Supplementary Data 3). When comparing the binding sites of the 72 sgRNAs targeting the LHOR of both RPE-1 haplotypes, we found that 4 sgRNAs had less than half binding sites in Hap1 compared to Hap2, all of them targeting the LHOR of chromosome 13 (13-1, 13-3, 13-6 and 13-7). In addition, 61 sgRNAs had a similar number of binding sites in both haplotypes (0.5 <binding sites ratio <2), and 7 sgRNAs had more than double binding sites in Hap1 compared to Hap2 (7-2, 7-3, 14-3, 21-1, 21-2, 21-4 and 21-5), 4 of which targeting LHOR in chromosome 21 with binding sites ratio >4 (21-1, 21-2, 21-4 and 21-5) (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 7a and Supplementary Data 3).

We also analyzed the chromosome specificity, defined as the ratio between the number of binding sites on the LHOR of the target chromosome and the total number of sites across all LHOR44. Although most sgRNAs had a comparable chromosome specificity between CHM13 and RPE-1 haplotypes, we observed a few major chromosome-level differences (Supplementary Fig. 7b and Supplementary Data 3). Specifically, 2 sgRNAs targeting the LHOR of chromosome 13 (13-1 and 13-2) of CHM13 with high chromosome specificity (>0.89) target the LHOR of chromosome 13 of RPE-1 Hap1 with low specificity (<0.10). Similarly, 2 sgRNAs targeting the LHOR of chromosome 21 (21-4 and 21-5) of CHM13 with high chromosome specificity (>0.93) target the LHOR of chromosome 21 of RPE-1 Hap2 with low specificity (<0.16) (Supplementary Data 3).

Altogether, these results confirm the presence of haplotype variation within the LHOR regions and indicate that haplotype-resolved engineering is possible using phased assemblies, such as RPE-1, matched to the corresponding cell line to precisely target specific haplotypes based on sequence specificity.

RPE-1 reference enables high-confidence epigenetic characterization of human centromeres

The improvement in the quality and fidelity of alignment to the matched reference genome compared to using other assemblies implies a change in downstream analyses and results at the whole genome level and especially within divergent loci. We leveraged the possibility to characterize complete epigenetic landscapes of human centromeres in the RPE-1 genome across fully-phased haplotypes, which has been previously unattainable due to a lack of diploid reference matched to experimental models (Fig. 6a). Our system enables us to assess the chromatin enrichment and spread for the centromere-specific H3 histone variant, CENP-A, which epigenetically specifies the functional site of kinetochore formation within the megabase-long centromere LHORs (Fig. 6a, b). A sharp dip in cytosine methylation (5mC) in a specific centromeric region named centromere dip region (CDR) has been recently proposed as the predictive site of the functional domain within the active centromere for kinetochore binding24. Indeed, the haplotype-resolved high-density CENP-A peaks we found using the matched reference overlap with the CDR (Fig. 6). To assess changes in downstream results when using the matched reference, we first compared the alignments of reads across reference genomes. To do so, we selected a publicly available CUT&RUN dataset from RPE-1 cells (GSE132193)45 and a ChIP-seq dataset from CHM131 and mapped the reads against the RPE1v1.1 Hap1 and Hap2 haplotypes and the CHM13v2.0 to simulate matched and non-matched reference for both genomes. CENP-A enrichment landscapes and spread changed depending on the reference genome used to align the reads (Fig. 6c, d, Supplementary Figs. 11, 12, see “Methods”). We found that the use of matched read-reference was the only condition that enabled the detection of a high-density cluster of CENP-A peaks with mapping quality higher than 20 (MAPQ > 20). CHM13 reads aligned to CHM13 reference and RPE-1 reads aligned to RPE1v1.1 marked the exact site of CENP-A enrichment, but not vice versa (Fig. 6c, d, Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). To test whether other chromosome-level genomes were able to identify phased high-density CENP-A peaks, we included hg38, the most utilized human reference genome to date, as well as the diploid Q100 HG002v1.0.1 (maternal and paternal haplotypes)2 and YAO maternal and paternal haplotypes6 (Fig. 6a, b, e, f, Supplementary Figs. 13–15). All the other haplotypes, except the RPE-1 genome matched to the sequencing reads, failed to identify a consensus region for CENP-A enrichment (Fig. 6a, b, e, f, Supplementary Figs. 13–15), underscoring the importance of using the specific genome as reference, and confirming the substantial sequence variation across centromeres from different cell lines, and even between haplotypes within the same cell line (Supplementary Fig. 16a–d).

Identification of the human kinetochore site with haplotype resolution

We then leveraged the RPE1v1.1 assembly8 to capture haplotype-resolved epigenetic landscapes of kinetochore sites within human haplotype-resolved centromeres for all chromosomes using our matched read-reference approach. Having confirmed that the CENP-A cluster marks the human kinetochore mapping within the CDR, we proceeded to characterize the CDR in the RPE1v1.1 reference. Each chromosome showed a different size of CDR and of CENP-A enrichment within the CDR, implying that the precise site of the kinetochore is haplotype and chromosome-specific. We characterized the inter-haplotype variation and found differences in the number and the length of CDRs within the centromere of the same chromosome, ranging from the smallest CDR of 2.3 kb in chromosome 15 Hap2 to 149.9 kb in chromosome 11 Hap2 (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Notably, both haplotypes of chromosomes 1 and X, haplotype 1 of chromosomes 2, 3, 5, 11, 17, 19, and 20, and haplotype 2 of chromosomes 7, 8, 15, and 16 showed more than one CDR (Fig. 6g, h, Supplementary Figs. 8–10, Supplementary Table 2). Next, we addressed the questions whether both CDRs are occupied, if they are occupied heterogeneously within a population, and if two kinetochore sites can co-exist at the same time within the same centromere. To address these points, we assessed the CDR and CENP-A enrichment together with the isogenomic mapping of additional centromeric proteins—CENP-B, CENP-C and CENP-T. The localization of CENP-A, CENP-B, CENP-C and CENP-T within entire phased centromeres assemblies (Fig. 6b, g, h) enabled us to resolve the site of the kinetochore attachment, especially in those chromosomes with more than one CDRs (Fig. 6b, g, h, Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Figs. 8–10) for all chromosomes, except for chromosomes 18 which has incomplete assembly for both haplotypes in our RPE1v1.18 and 13 Hap2 centromere which was excluded because the CDR was not immediately identifiable (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figs. 8–10). We used the statistically significant q-value of the peaks as a proxy for understanding the enrichment of centromeric proteins from filtered and unfiltered reads (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). For chromosomes 1 (Hap1 and Hap2), 3 (Hap1), 5 (Hap1), 7 (Hap2), 8 (Hap2), 11 (Hap1), 15 (Hap2), 16 (Hap2), 17 (Hap1), 19 (Hap1), 20 (Hap1), and X (Hap1 and Hap2), we found more than one CDR enriched in centromeric proteins with colocalization between peaks and CDRs, suggesting that both CDRs may be functional simoultaneously, or functionally equivalent in a population. However, we found that one peak within one of the two or three CDRs always held higher -log10(q-value) consistently for all the centromeric proteins analyzed, suggesting that in spite of the presence of multiple CDRs, one site can be considered the primary kinetochore binding site20 (Fig. 6b, g, h, Supplementary Figs. 8–10). In some cases, such as chromosomes 7 (Hap2), 8 (Hap2), and X (Hap1), there is actually a clear absence of occupancy of the alternative CDRs, regardless of whether peaks were called using filtered or unfiltered reads, indicating that these chromosomes and haplotypes have a single functional site while other CDRs are not marking the kinetochore region (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Figs. 8–10). We noted that the first CDR coming from the p-arm of the chromosome was often functionally occupied, but not always, with examples of functionally active CDR2 and CDR3 as well for specific chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Altogether, our data provide the first detailed report of the haplotype-resolved epigenetic landscapes of the human centromere in a diploid model using our novel approach of matched read-reference intersecting CDR with CENP-A high-confidence binding and additional centromeric factors. We show significant variation due to reference genome choice affecting downstream results and interpretations, highlighting the importance and urgent need for matched references for whole-genome epigenetic analyses, especially when analyzing highly variable and repetitive regions such as centromeres. Using matched read-reference, we identified the exact site of the kinetochore most likely to be functional in vivo at a higher level of precision than previously possible using methylation or other approaches in isolation. Altogether, we provide the assessment of haplotype specificity in kinetochore binding sites and its specification across human chromosomes, and we propose a functional genomic approach to revolutionize epigenetic studies of divergent loci in RPE-1 cells and, in the near future, for more experimental cell lines as reference genomes become available.

Discussion

In this study, we showcase a novel approach that uses RPE1v1.1 as an “isogenomic” reference genome to obtain high-precision alignments of sequencing reads from laboratory cell lines matched to the specific reference assembly for the same line. We demonstrate how using matched read-reference improves sequencing data processing, addresses the long-described reference bias, and provides more reliable mapping, especially within loci that are most affected by sequence divergence between the two haplotypes. Using the isogenomic reference (Fig. 1), we present haplotype-resolved genetic variation (Fig. 2), improvements in coverage and quality of DNA reads mapping (Fig. 3), transcriptomic analysis providing accurate differential enrichment within the specific allele (Fig. 4), and CRISPR guide RNA with better efficiency due to precise selection of binding sites present in the cell line to be engineered (Fig. 5). Ultimately, all these steps result in changes in downstream analyses, results and interpretation, as we have shown for haplotype-resolved enrichment identifying the human kinetochore site at base pair resolution only when the specific reference matched to the reads is used (Fig. 6). As a laboratory that studies human centromeres, the long omitted “dark” regions of our genome, the adoption of matched reference is a necessary step, considering the divergence that we8 and others2,20 have recently reported. We expect that this level of haplotypes-resolved sequence-specific precision will transform technologies like genome editing and engineering, chromosome-specific functional genomics, to assess allele-specific differential expression and phased epigenetic landscapes at whole genome with increasing level of precision18,44,46.

To this end, this approach has enabled us to map the exact site of the kinetochore within the centromeres in both haplotypes (Fig. 6). We have identified high-confidence enrichment of centromere-specific H3 histone variant CENP-A47, DNA-binding protein CENP-B36,48, and Constitutive Centromere Associated Network (CCAN) components CENP-C and CENP-T26 whose precise mapping was not supported by routinely used reference genomes such as hg3849 and CHM131, or even the diploid HG0022 or other available assemblies. Our detailed analysis of centromeres across fully phased haplotypes for the RPE1v1.1 cell line uncovered unprecedented variation in the size, sequence composition and structure of the functional alpha-satellite repeats between haplotypes and chromosomes. In line with the rapid emergence of a new alpha-satellite HOR structure that underlies divergence in centromeres between individuals20, we found variation between haplotypes for all chromosomes, and when comparing with other validated genomes. With this extent of divergence in mind, we demonstrate that RPE-1 reads from a centromeric chromatin immunoprecipitation experiment map differently depending on the reference used, without necessarily reflecting a bona fide biological change. This is noteworthy considering the abundant literature showing epigenetic datasets calling CENP-A localization50–56, and other centromeric proteins57–59, that may be mapped on non-matched reference genomes. Isogenomic mapping using RPE1v1.1 improves alignments across the whole genome and at HDR, for both long and short reads. Thus, reanalyzing previously published datasets1,45 with the isogenomic reference, we found variation in kinetochore between haplotypes in all chromosomes. For instance, the enrichment ratio for chromosomes 2, 7 and X shows higher, equal or lower CENP-A levels between haplotypes, respectively, underscoring striking heterogeneity. These inter-haplotype differences we showcase are in line with recent observations of high kinetochore plasticity between genomes20. Interestingly, the CDR ratio showed lower levels of variation between haplotypes, suggesting that isogenomic mapping identifies the minimal functional size of the kinetochore, which is further narrowed within the domain of CENP-A high-density peaks. Indeed, in this study, we were able to determine which CDR is functional with haplotype specificity by intersecting CDR, CENP-A and other centromere factors mapped with high accuracy and in a haplotype-resolved way. We observed examples of chromosomes where one CDR was occupied by centromeric factors, indicating functional kinetochore localization, while the other was not—hence inactive. For some chromosomes, both CDRs showed centromere proteins’ enrichment—using both unfiltered or MAPQ > 20 filtered reads, suggesting that two CDRs can be functional either at the same time or chosen heterogeneously within the cell population, yet one of the two CDRs displayed higher enrichment for all centromeric factors and in all cases analyzed. This evidence points to the haplotype and chromosome-specific selection of preferred or exclusively used CDR as kinetochore binding sites, which is immediately visible when peak calling is performed isogenomically using matched read-reference.

The enduring quality of hg38 assembly has been celebrated as a valuable reference49; here, we demonstrate a conceptual shift to address the reference bias and sequence variation using matched reference-reads for the next generation of functional genomics. The human pangenome draft3 represents an important step toward a more equitable and fair representation of human genomic diversity3. Our work expands from the idea of representing as many human variants as possible toward the concept of aligning as closely as feasible to the “personalized” reference sequence that incorporates the relevant and exact variation of the model system being used experimentally for pushing the boundaries of data analysis. Altogether, our study opens important opportunities for advancing our understanding of the extent of using a generalized reference’ limitations, the influence on downstream analysis and the biological relevance of improved alignment for transcriptomics, genomic and epigenomic studies of genomes, especially at centromeres and other highly divergent loci. Our proof-of-concept analysis calls for a shift in our modus operandi when analyzing data from cell lines. As more high-quality genome references for commonly used cell lines will continue being generated8,60, we envisage a widespread application of isogenomic reference genomes for faithful multi-omics interpretations and discoveries.

Methods

Cell lines

The hTERT-immortalized RPE (hTERT RPE-1) cells used for the genome assembly and visualization of read coverage pertain to the commercially available RPE-1 cell line (ATCC-CRL-4000, batch 1). To compare NM and MAPQ values, two additional vials of RPE-1 were kindly provided by Teresa Davoli (New York University) (batch 2 and 3). Both batches carry a pInd20 construct for inducible CRISPR-Cas9. RPE-1 cells are near-diploid human cells of female origin with 46 chromosomes. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Corning 10-092-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco 10270-106), 100 μg/ml streptomycin, 100 U/ml penicillin (Euroclone ECB3001D) and 2 mM glutamine (Euroclone ECB3000D). Cells were grown at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

DNA extraction

RPE-1 cells at 70–80% confluence were harvested, and cell pellets were prepared in individual aliquots of 1.5 × 106 cells, frozen in dry ice and stored at −80 °C. High molecular weight (HMW) DNA and ultra-high molecular weight DNA (UHMW) were extracted from dry cell pellets using the Monarch HMW DNA extraction kit for cells and blood (New England Biolabs, NEB T3050L) and for tissue (NEB T3060L). HMW DNA was quantified by Nanodrop and Qubit with a broad range kit (Thermo Scientific Q32850). Native DNA size distribution was assessed using Femto Pulse with the Genomic DNA 165 kb kit (Agilent FP-1002-0275).

Library preparation and sequencing

Sequencing data were obtained using Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) HiFi and Illumina. HMW DNA from 1.5 × 106 cells was used to generate PacBio HiFi libraries with the SMRTbell express template prep kit 2.0 (PacBio 100-938-900). Size selection was performed with Megaruptor 2 (Diagenode) with default settings, and fraction sized 15–20 kb (as determined by Femto Pulse) was sequenced on the Sequel IIe platform with SMRT Cells 8 M (PacBio 101-389-001), using the chemistry 2.0 with 2-h pre-extension, 2-h adaptive loading and 30-h movie collection time, to reach a coverage of 46x and 5x of PacBio HiFi reads in batch 1 and batch 2, respectively. Circular Consensus Sequencing reads were obtained using SMRT-Link (https://github.com/WenchaoLin/SMRTLink) with default parameters. HMW DNA from 1.5 × 106 cells was used to generate paired-end Illumina libraries with the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation kit (Illumina FC-131-1024) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To confirm that tagmentation of HMW DNA produced the expected fragmentation, electrophoretic QC was performed after tagmentation (pre-PCR) on an Agilent TapeStation using D1000 ScreenTape, and after library construction (post-PCR and clean-up) on a TapeStation (High Sensitivity D1000) to verify the final fragment profile and absence of adapter dimers. Sequencing FASTQ files were delivered adapter-free by the provider; the mean insert (fragment) size was 150 bp (batch 3).

Annotation and analysis of centromere repetitive regions

Using the RPE1v1.1 genome assembly generated from a batch of RPE-1 cells cultured in our laboratory8, centromere annotation was done by intersecting the outputs derived from RepeatMasker v4.1.5 (https://github.com/rmhubley/RepeatMasker)61 and HumAS-HMMER_for_AnVIL (https://github.com/fedorrik/HumAS-HMMER_for_AnVIL) to identify the position of the centromeres in the RPE1v1.1 assembly. The HOR monomer annotation was used to predict HOR structural variants (SVs) using the StV script (https://github.com/fedorrik/stv). HOR monomer annotation was used as input to the StV script to identify higher order repeat structural variants (SVs). Haplotype-specific SVs were identified using a custom script that compared, for each chromosome, the SV type annotations between the two haplotypes (Hap1 and Hap2). SV types present exclusively in one haplotype were classified as haplotype-specific (https://github.com/GiuntaLab/RPE1/tree/main/RPE1v1.1_FunctionalGenomics/Figure_2/scripts). RepeatMasker and HumAS-HMMER for AnVIL were used with default parameters. To generate heatmaps showing the variation between centromeres, StainedGlass v6.7.0 (https://github.com/mrvollger/StainedGlass)35 was run with the following parameters: window = 1000 and mm_f = 10000. The start and end coordinates of active alpha satellite HOR were taken from the StV output. In cases with multiple LHORs, the LHOR region considered was defined as the span between the start coordinate of the first centromeric LHOR and the end coordinate of the last centromeric LHOR. The final length of the LHOR regions and the ratio of the length between Hap1/Hap2 were plotted using GraphPad Prism v10.3.1.

Analysis of whole-genome sequencing read alignments

HiFi reads used to generate the RPE1v1.1 assembly were aligned against this reference, CHM13 v2.0, T2T-YAO and HG002 v1.0.1 using minimap2 v2.17-r941 (https://github.com/lh3/minimap2)62,63 with the option -ax map-hifi. Primary alignments, extracted using the view module from the samtools v1.17 suite (https://github.com/samtools/samtools)64 with the option -F 2308, were used to generate NucFreq plots with the NucPlot.py script from the NucFreq v0.1 toolkit (https://github.com/mrvollger/NucFreq). Heterozygous positions were identified using the HetDetection R script65. The output from HetDetection.R was then used to plot the heterozygous ratio as a heatmap with karyoploteR v1.28.0 R package (https://github.com/bernatgel/karyoploteR)66.

The second batch of RPE-1 cells was used to generate PacBio HiFi and Illumina short reads. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed by the provider using the standard preprocessing pipeline implemented for each sequencing platform. Prior to alignment, Illumina reads were randomly downsampled to ~20× coverage. Alignment to the two individual haplotypes of RPE1v1.1 and to CHM13 v2.0 was performed using minimap2 with the option -ax map-hifi for HiFi reads and using BWA-MEM v0.7.17-r1188 (https://github.com/lh3/bwa)67 for Illumina reads. The samtools view command was used to retain only primary alignments. From the resulting BAM files, we extracted the NM value (edit distance) and the mapping quality for each aligned read. These metrics were visualized using bar plots with individual data points generated in GraphPad Prism v10.3.1 to compare their distributions across different reference genomes. Statistical significance of differences in alignment quality between references was assessed with Student’s t-test. For each chromosome in each reference genome, we calculated the total means of NM values from mapped reads. Individual chromosome sums were visualized with dot plots, while the overall distribution of NM value sums per reference was represented using box plots.

Analysis of RNA-seq data

We analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from three biological replicates of RPE-1 cells (SRA accession numbers: SRR23924195, SRR23924196, and SRR23924197), obtained from Papathanasiou et al.43. Raw sequencing data were downloaded using SRA toolkit v3.1.1 with the fastq-dump command. Adapter trimming and quality control were performed using Trim Galore v0.6.10 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore), with Cutadapt v2.868, and FastQC v0.11.9 (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC). MultiQC (v1.24)69 was used to aggregate quality control reports. Trimmed reads were aligned to three different reference genomes: RPE1v1.1 Hap1, RPE1v1.1 Hap2, and the standard human reference genome hg38. Alignments were performed using STAR v2.7.11b70 with default parameters, sorting the alignment by coordinates. For the RPE-1 assemblies, we previously used Liftoff v1.6.3 (https://github.com/agshumate/Liftoff)71 to lift gene annotations from the Ensembl hg38 annotation (version 112)72 to the RPE1v1.1 genome assembly. STAR indices were generated for each reference genome using the corresponding FASTA and GTF files. Aligned reads were quantified using featureCounts v2.0.173 with default parameters for paired-end, restricting the analysis to genes present in each reference. The resulting count matrices were used for differential expression analysis using edgeR v4.0.1674. To isolate the effects of reference genome choice on apparent gene expression, we performed pairwise comparisons between the same biological replicates aligned to different reference genomes (Hap1 vs. Hap2, Hap1 vs. hg38, and Hap2 vs. hg38). Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Among these, genes with log2 fold change (log2FC) > 0 were classified as upregulated, and those with log2FC < 0 as downregulated.

SgRNA analysis and off-targets evaluation

The number of binding sites of 76 centromere-targeting single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) designed on the CHM13 genome and validated on the hg38 genome to induce chromosome-specific aneuploidy44 was evaluated in the RPE1v1.1 genome. We used the CRISPRitz tool75 (https://github.com/pinellolab/CRISPRitz) to search the binding sites of the 76 sgRNAs in the entire RPE-1 genome, followed by the NGG protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence, where N = A, T, C or G. Live HOR annotation information pertaining to the RPE-1 genome was utilized to quantify sgRNA binding sites within the functional centromeric regions across chromosomes, with the intersect module of the bedtools v2.27.1 suite (https://github.com/arq5x/bedtools2)76. Off-target evaluation was conducted by identifying all the sgRNA binding sites located outside the target centromere for each sgRNA in both haplotype 1 and 2 of the RPE-1 genome. To visualize the results, we generated pie charts with GraphPad Prism, as well as a barplot and heatmaps using custom Python scripts.

Analysis of ChIP-seq and CUT&RUN data

Publicly available CUT&RUN raw reads generated from hTERT RPE-1 cells (NCBI BioProject: PRJNA546288)45 and ChIP-seq raw reads generated from hTERT CHM13 cells (NCBI BioProject: PRJNA559484)1 were downloaded to perform the epigenetic analysis of centromeres. Specifically, we downloaded datasets with the following SRA accession numbers: SRR9201843 (CENP-A CUT&RUN, RPE-1), SRR9201837, SRR9201839 and SRR9201844 (CENP-B CUT&RUN, RPE-1), SRR9201840, SRR9201841 and SRR9201845 (CENP-C CUT&RUN, RPE-1), SRR9201837, SRR9201839 and SRR9201844 (CENP-B CUT&RUN, RPE-1), SRR10164102 (CENP-T CUT&RUN, RPE-1), SRR9201838, SRR9201842, SRR9201846 and SRR9201847 (whole-genome sequencing, RPE-1), SRR13278683 and SRR13278684 (CENP-A ChIP-seq, CHM13), SRR13278681 and SRR13278682 (Input, CHM13). Quality control and adapter trimming were performed with TrimGalore, using default parameters. Read alignment was performed against multiple chromosome-level genomes: the RPE1v1.1 phased haplotypes (diploid mapping), T2T-CHM13v2.0 (haploid mapping), hg38 (GRCh38.p14, haploid mapping), the HG002v1.0.1 paternal and maternal haplotypes (diploid mapping) and the YAO paternal and maternal haplotypes (diploid mapping). Bowtie2 v2.4.4 (https://github.com/BenLangmead/bowtie2)77 was used with the following parameters: --end-to-end --sensitive --no-mixed. Alignment files from different replicates, when available, were pooled using samtools merge. Filtered alignment files were produced using samtools view, retaining only reads with a mapping quality (MAPQ) greater than 20, corresponding to a probability of incorrect alignment below 1%. Both for the unfiltered and the filtered alignment files, MACS3 v3.0.1 callpeak module (https://github.com/macs3-project/MACS)78 was used to call broad peaks by comparing immunoprecipitated samples against the corresponding controls, using the parameters -f BAMPE -B -g 3.03e9 -q 0.05; the -g parameter, representing the genome size, was set to 3.00e9 for haploid genomes and to 6.00e9 for diploid genomes. To obtain an enrichment signal for downstream visualization, we used the MACS3 bdgcmp module to compare the *_treat_pileup.bdg and the *_control_lambda.bdg tracks produced by MACS3 callpeak. The -m parameter was set to qpois to calculate, for each genomic bin, the log10 of the Poisson q-value. The karyoploteR v1.34.2 R package (https://github.com/bernatgel/karyoploteR)66 was used to visualize protein enrichment tracks across all chromosomes and genomes with the kpBars function. The coordinates of the broad peaks identified by MACS3 were intersected with those of the centromere dip regions using the bedtools intersect module. For each CDR, the average -log10(q-value) was calculated from the broadPeak file for each centromeric protein and plotted using the ComplexHeatmap v2.24 R package79 (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d).

Evaluation of the haplotype specificity of CENP-A peaks

To assess the haplotype specificity and uniqueness of the genomic region containing CENP-A broad peaks identified on the RPE1v1.1 genome using filtered alignment files, we divided the LHOR regions into non-overlapping 500-nucleotide bins using the GenomicRanges v1.60 R package80. For each haplotype, two FASTA files were created using bedtools getfasta and intersect modules, one containing bins overlapping with at least one peak and the other the remaining bins. The resulting FASTA files were aligned to the other haplotype reference sequence to evaluate sequence similarity using minimap2 with parameters -x asm20 -c --cs=long, generating PAF files, and bowtie2 v2.4.4 (https://github.com/BenLangmead/bowtie2)77 generating BAM files. For the alignments produced by minimap2, percent identity was calculated as (Number of matching bases / Alignment block length) × 100. For those produced by bowtie2, it was calculated as (Alignment block length - NM value) / (Alignment block length) × 100.

DNA methylation calling and CDR detection

The 5-methylcytosine (5mC) profile of the RPE1v1.1 genome was calculated from the Oxford Nanopore Technologies long reads used to assemble the genome8, employing Dorado v0.7.3 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado) with the simplex basecalling model (dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v4.2.0) and parameters --modified-bases 5mC --modified-bases-threshold 0.08 -c 45000 --secondary yes -N 5 --trim all. Dorado directly annotates modified 5mC bases in the SAM/BAM output by including MM (modified base positions) and ML (modification probability) tags during the alignment of reads to the reference genome. The resulting BAM file was then processed using Modkit v0.3.3 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/modkit) with parameters --ref --cpg --bedgraph --filter-threshold 0.80, generating a bedGraph file containing per-base probabilities for 5mC methylation. The genome was partitioned into non-overlapping 5 kb bins using the tileGenome function from the GenomicRanges R package. These genomic bins were then intersected with the bedGraph file produced by Modkit to associate each methylation value with its corresponding bin. For each 5 kb bin, we computed the average methylation probability by aggregating the methylation values of all positions falling within the bin. The resulting average methylation values were then visualized centromere-wide using the kpBars function from the karyoploteR package. The same binning and normalization strategy was applied to the CHM13 genome and to the corresponding methylation profile, using the publicly available track accessible at https://s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/human-pangenomics/T2T/CHM13/assemblies/annotation/regulation/chm13v2.0_CHM13_CpG_ont_guppy3.6.0_nanopolish0.13.2.bw. To determine the CDRs of each centromere, LHOR regions were partitioned into non-overlapping 2.5 kb bins, and bedtools intersect was used to obtain the average methylation score for each of them. CDRs were then manually annotated as regions comprising consecutive bins with an average methylation score below 0.15.
