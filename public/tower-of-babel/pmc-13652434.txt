Feasibility of primary repair in extensive gunshot-induced colon injuries with severe fecal contamination: a case series of six patients and review of literature
Authors: Taha Negahdari, Aidin Darbeh, Mahdokht Azizi, Reza Valiandoost, Raha Rahmani, Mohammadamin Kashefi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The optimal management of penetrating colon injuries, particularly in the presence of high-risk features such as severe fecal contamination, hemodynamic instability, and extensive tissue damage, remains controversial. Although primary repair has gained increasing acceptance, fecal diversion is still frequently performed in complex cases due to concerns regarding anastomotic failure and septic complications. Evidence specifically addressing the feasibility of primary repair in patients with extensive destructive injuries is limited.Case presentation:We report a case series of six patients with shotgun-induced colon injuries characterized by multiple perforations and varying degrees of fecal contamination. All patients met at least one traditionally accepted indication for fecal diversion, including hemodynamic instability, delayed presentation, or extensive injury. Despite these high-risk features, all patients underwent primary repair or resection with primary anastomosis. Postoperative morbidity was limited, with one surgical site infection and one anastomotic leak requiring reoperation. No mortality was observed.Discussion:Our findings are consistent with an evolving body of literature supporting primary repair in penetrating colon trauma. While prior studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes in selected patients, many have excluded those with extensive destructive injuries or severe contamination. In contrast, this series focuses on a high-risk subgroup, suggesting that traditional contraindications may not independently predict adverse outcomes when contemporary trauma care principles and careful patient selection are applied. These findings support a more individualized, physiology-based approach rather than strict adherence to protocol-driven decision-making.Conclusion:Primary repair or resection with primary anastomosis may be a feasible and safe option in carefully selected patients with complex, shotgun-induced colon injuries, even in the presence of traditional high-risk features. Further multicenter studies are warranted to better define selection criteria and validate these findings.

Introduction

The colon is among the most frequently injured intra-abdominal organs in penetrating trauma, ranking second in incidence after the small intestine[1]. Penetrating colon injuries are most commonly caused by firearm projectiles and less frequently by stab wounds from sharp instruments. Among these mechanisms, gunshot-related injuries are associated with greater tissue destruction and higher morbidity, with reported mortality rates ranging from 3% to 16%[2]. Historically, the management of civilian colonic trauma was dominated by routine fecal diversion through colostomy. Primary repair was strongly discouraged due to concerns regarding anastomotic failure in the setting of contamination and physiologic derangement. Over the past several decades, however, this traditional paradigm has been increasingly challenged. Advances in trauma resuscitation, perioperative care, anesthesia, and prehospital systems have contributed to improved outcomes, enabling a re-evaluation of surgical strategies. A substantial body of literature, particularly from high-volume trauma centers in South Africa and the United States, has demonstrated that primary repair of most penetrating colon injuries is safe and associated with acceptable complication rates[5–8].

Despite these developments, the optimal management of high-risk colon injuries remains a subject of ongoing debate[9,10]. Factors such as severe fecal contamination, extensive tissue destruction, delayed presentation, and hemodynamic instability have traditionally been regarded as relative contraindications to primary repair. In particular, gunshot-induced colon injuries frequently involve high-energy transfer, multiple perforations, and significant contamination, raising concerns regarding anastomotic integrity and the risk of intra-abdominal sepsis[11–13]. Although some studies suggest that these adverse factors may not independently predict poor outcomes following primary repair, the available evidence is heterogeneous, and many surgeons continue to favor fecal diversion in such complex scenarios[14].

Furthermore, there is a relative paucity of focused data specifically addressing the feasibility of primary repair in patients with extensive colon injuries accompanied by severe fecal contamination[8,10]. Much of the existing literature either excludes this subgroup or incorporates such patients into heterogeneous cohorts without adequate stratification. Consequently, the generalizability of current recommendations to this high-risk population remains uncertain, particularly in real-world clinical settings where delayed presentation and resource constraints may further influence decision-making.

This case series reports on six patients with extensive gunshot-induced colon injuries and severe fecal contamination who underwent primary repair. The aim of this study is to evaluate the feasibility and short-term outcomes of this approach in a high-risk cohort and to contribute to the limited evidence supporting a more selective, physiologic approach in the management of complex colon trauma.

This case series has been reported in accordance with the PROCESS 2025 criteria[15].

Case series description

A total of six patients with abdominal shotgun injuries involving the colon who were admitted to Shahid Jalil Hospital in Yasuj between December 2023 and April 2025 were included in this study. All patients met at least one of the institutional indications for colostomy (hemodynamic instability, severe fecal contamination, extensive or multiple colon injuries, delayed presentation ≥6 hours, and multiple associated injuries). All procedures were performed by the same institutional trauma surgery team under the supervision of a senior trauma surgeon with 14 years of experience in trauma and emergency surgery. Operative decision-making, patient selection, and perioperative management were discussed within the trauma team and followed a consistent institutional approach, while the specific operative technique was tailored to the intraoperative findings in each patient. All patients were initially assessed and resuscitated according to the advanced trauma life support principles. Perioperative prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics, consisting of ceftriaxone and metronidazole, were administered. Pharmacologic venous thromboembolism prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin) was provided in the absence of contraindications, such as active bleeding. Notably, each patient fulfilled at least one of these criteria, and some patients had more than one indication, for which a colostomy would traditionally be considered mandatory under the institutional protocol.

However, for the purpose of this study, a selected subgroup of patients was included. Patients with confounding factors, such as diabetes mellitus, immunodeficiency, advanced or very young age, or significant comorbidities, were excluded. In addition, patients with associated intra-abdominal injuries involving organs other than the colon were excluded to maintain a more homogeneous cohort. Only those patients who were able to provide informed consent – either at presentation or after initial resuscitation – were enrolled. This careful selection enabled the evaluation of primary repair in a controlled, high-risk setting.

The mean age of the patients was 32 years (range: 20–41 years). Three patients were hemodynamically unstable on admission, whereas the remaining patients were stabilized prior to surgical intervention. The mean time from injury to surgery was approximately 6.5 hours (range: 3–10 hours), with several patients presenting after a delay of 6 hours or more.

All injuries were caused by shotgun pellets, resulting in multiple perforations of the colon. The number of injury sites ranged from 3 to 16. The ascending colon was the most frequently involved segment, followed by the descending and transverse colon. The degree of fecal contamination varied, with three cases classified as severe, two as moderate, and one as mild.

Regarding surgical management, four patients underwent primary repair using a two-layer Lambert suturing technique, while two patients required segmental resection with primary anastomosis due to the extent of tissue damage. Importantly, despite the presence of traditional indications for diversion, none of the patients underwent colostomy at the index operation.

Perioperative management was standardized across all patients. Intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole were administered as prophylactic, broad-spectrum antibiotics in accordance with the institutional trauma protocol. During surgery, bowel viability was carefully assessed, devitalized tissue was debrided when necessary, and gentle bowel handling was emphasized to minimize additional tissue injury. Following primary repair or anastomosis, an intraoperative leak test was routinely performed to confirm repair integrity. No intra-abdominal drains were placed in any patient. Postoperatively, patients underwent close monitoring, including continuous assessment of vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, and oxygen saturation), serial abdominal examinations for pain, distension, guarding, rebound tenderness, and signs of peritonitis, along with routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood counts and serum biochemical analyses. All patients were followed throughout their hospital stay until discharge, during which postoperative recovery and complications were prospectively monitored.

For instance, in one representative case (Case 1), multiple pellet injuries to the ascending colon were managed successfully with primary repair using a two-layer Lembert technique. Postoperative outcomes were generally favorable. The mean hospital length of stay was approximately 6.7 days (range: 4–10 days), and oral intake was resumed between postoperative days 3 and 5. One patient developed a surgical site infection, which was managed conservatively with antibiotics and local wound care. Another patient experienced an anastomotic leak with intra-abdominal abscess formation, requiring reoperation and subsequent colostomy. No mortality was observed in this series. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien–Dindo classification: the surgical site infection was classified as Grade II, while the anastomotic leak requiring reoperation and colostomy was classified as Grade IIIb[16].

Overall, despite the presence of traditional high-risk features warranting colostomy, primary repair or resection with anastomosis was successfully performed in the majority of carefully selected patients, with acceptable complication rates and no mortality. These findings support the feasibility of a selective primary repair approach in colon injuries caused by shotgun trauma.

Discussion

In this case series, primary repair or resection with primary anastomosis was successfully performed in patients with shotgun-induced colon injuries despite the presence of traditional high-risk features that would typically mandate fecal diversion. All patients met at least one established indication for diversion, including hemodynamic instability, severe fecal contamination, multiple or extensive injuries, or delayed presentation; nevertheless, favorable outcomes were achieved in the majority of cases. Postoperative morbidity was limited, with only one surgical site infection and one anastomotic leak requiring reoperation; no mortality was observed. These findings suggest that, in carefully selected patients, primary repair may be a feasible and safe alternative to routine colostomy, even in the setting of complex colon trauma. Importantly, this study suggests that individualized, physiology-based decision-making may be considered when managing penetrating colon injuries, rather than relying solely on traditional protocol-driven criteria.

The findings of the present study are consistent with the evolving body of literature supporting primary repair in penetrating colon trauma. Early randomized evidence, most notably the trial by Stone HH and Fabian TC, demonstrated that primary repair could be performed safely in selected patients, challenging the long-standing preference for routine fecal diversion[4]. Subsequent meta-analyses and systematic reviews have reinforced these conclusions, reporting lower overall and infectious complication rates with primary repair compared with colostomy, without an associated increase in mortality[8,16]. More contemporary studies have further expanded these indications, advocating a nonselective or liberal approach in which most colon injuries can be managed without diversion, even in the presence of traditionally defined risk factors[10,17,18]. For example, studies by Demetriades D and others have shown that factors such as fecal contamination, injury severity, and transfusion requirements may not independently predict poor outcomes following primary repair[19].

However, it is important to note that many of these studies either excluded patients with extensive destructive injuries or included them in heterogeneous cohorts without focused subgroup analysis. Therefore, direct comparisons with the present series should be interpreted with caution. Unlike prior reports, the current study specifically examines a cohort of patients with shotgun-induced colon injuries characterized by multiple perforations and varying degrees of fecal contamination – features traditionally considered high risk. In this context, our findings extend existing evidence by suggesting that, under carefully controlled conditions and appropriate patient selection, primary repair may remain a viable strategy even in cases that would conventionally mandate diversion.

A central concern in the management of penetrating colon injuries is the presence of high-risk features traditionally considered contraindications to primary repair. In the present series, all patients fulfilled at least one such criterion, including hemodynamic instability, severe fecal contamination, delayed presentation, or multiple perforations. Historically, these factors have been associated with an increased risk of anastomotic failure and intra-abdominal sepsis, thereby favoring fecal diversion as the standard approach[20–23]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that these variables may not independently predict adverse outcomes when contemporary trauma care principles are applied. In particular, studies by Demetriades D and colleagues demonstrated that primary repair could be safely performed in patients with penetrating colon injuries, including those with significant contamination and injury severity, without an increase in septic complications[6]. Furthermore, a multicenter trial by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma found no significant difference in outcomes between primary anastomosis and diversion, even in patients with traditionally defined risk factors[19]. A Cochrane systematic review has similarly reported lower overall and infectious complication rates with primary repair compared with colostomy, without an associated increase in mortality[8]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the presence of isolated high-risk features should not automatically preclude consideration of primary repair.

The favorable outcomes observed in this series are likely attributable to careful patient selection and adherence to established surgical principles. Although all patients met institutional criteria for diversion, those with significant comorbidities, immunosuppression, or associated intra-abdominal injuries were excluded, thereby reducing confounding factors known to impair healing. In addition, relatively early operative intervention may have limited the extent of peritoneal contamination and systemic inflammatory response. Prior studies have emphasized that physiologic status at presentation, rather than isolated injury characteristics, is a key determinant of outcome following colon trauma[23,24]. These considerations support the concept that a tailored approach based on patient physiology and intraoperative findings may be more appropriate than strict adherence to traditional exclusion criteria.

An important aspect of the current study is its focus on shotgun-induced colon injuries, a subset that has been relatively underrepresented in the literature. Unlike single high-velocity projectile injuries, shotgun trauma is characterized by multiple low-energy pellets producing numerous small perforations across a segment of bowel. While this pattern may appear extensive, the degree of tissue devascularization is often limited, potentially allowing for successful primary repair. Previous studies of gunshot wounds to the colon have demonstrated favorable outcomes with primary repair in a substantial proportion of patients, even in the presence of multiple injuries[25]. However, few studies have specifically addressed shotgun-related injury patterns. Notably, Jacobson and colleagues, in a series of 58 consecutive patients with penetrating colon injuries managed by primary repair, included only 12% of patients with shotgun-induced injuries; within this subgroup, there were no clinically apparent leaks or fistulae[11]. Our findings suggest that, despite multiple perforations and contamination, primary repair may be feasible in this distinct mechanism of injury when appropriate surgical judgment is applied.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support a more selective and individualized approach to the management of penetrating colon trauma. Routine colostomy for all patients with high-risk features may result in unnecessary stoma formation, which is associated with additional morbidity, psychological burden, and the need for subsequent reversal procedures. Evidence from high-quality studies indicates that primary repair is associated with reduced complication rates and shorter hospital stays compared with fecal diversion[12,26,27]. Contemporary guidelines increasingly advocate for primary repair or resection with anastomosis in the majority of patients, reserving diversion for cases with severe physiologic compromise or nonviable bowel[16]. In this context, the present findings add to the growing body of evidence supporting individualized decision-making in the management of penetrating colon injuries.

The present study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings. Second, the absence of a control group undergoing fecal diversion precludes direct comparison between management strategies. Third, selection bias cannot be excluded, as patients with significant comorbidities and severe associated intra-abdominal injuries were not included. Finally, postoperative follow-up was limited to the in-hospital period, and therefore long-term complications and functional outcomes could not be assessed. Similar limitations have been reported in observational studies of colon trauma[6,10,28].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case series demonstrates that primary repair or resection with primary anastomosis may be feasible and safe in carefully selected patients with shotgun-induced colon injuries, even in the presence of traditional high-risk features such as fecal contamination and delayed presentation. These findings support the growing body of evidence favoring a selective, physiology-based approach to the management of penetrating colon trauma. Larger multicenter studies are needed to further define the subset of patients who may benefit most from this strategy and to validate these findings in more diverse clinical settings.
