Nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients: a quantitative study
Authors: Khulud Almutairi, Reem Saeed Alghamdi, Monirah Albloushi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnd-of-life care is a fundamental component of nursing practice; however, many nursing students report insufficient preparation, emotional discomfort, and a lack of confidence when caring for dying patients. Cultural and religious factors may further influence students’ attitudes toward death and dying, particularly in Saudi Arabia. Understanding these attitudes and their associated factors is essential to improving nursing education and preparing students for compassionate end-of-life care. This study examined attitudes toward caring for dying patients among final-year nursing students at King Saud University and explored the influence of gender, previous death-related experiences, and religious commitment on these attitudes, including religiosity’s potential moderating role.MethodsThis study used a quantitative cross-sectional design. A convenience sample of 318 final-year “Level 8” nursing students completed a self-administered questionnaire, including demographic variables, death-related experiences, a single-item scale measuring religiosity (religious commitment), and the Frommelt Attitudes Toward Care of the Dying Scale (FATCOD-B). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, Pearson’s correlation, and hierarchical multiple regression.ResultsThe students demonstrated neutral to moderately negative attitudes toward caring for dying patients (M = 25.45, SD = 5.95). Male students reported significantly more positive attitudes than female students (p < 0.001); gender accounted for 3.4% of the variance in attitudes (R² = 0.034). No significant associations were found between these attitudes and previous death-related experiences or palliative care education. Religious commitment was not a significant predictor of attitudes when added to the model (ΔR² = 0.003, p = 0.312), and its moderating effect on the relationship between gender and attitudes was not statistically significant (ΔR² = 0.011, p = 0.055); the full model explained 4.8% of the variance (R² = 0.048). However, the descriptive pattern suggested that gender differences decreased at higher levels of religious commitment.ConclusionsFinal-year nursing students exhibited suboptimal attitudes toward end-of-life care, emphasizing the need for enhanced educational strategies. Nursing curricula should incorporate structured and culturally sensitive approaches to address emotional preparedness, communication skills, and reflective learning. Considering gender differences and the potential influence of religiosity may further support the development of effective interventions to improve students’ readiness for end-of-life care.Keywords: End-of-life care, Nursing students, Attitudes toward death, Religiosity, Saudi Arabia, FATCOD-B, Palliative care

Introduction

End-of-life care is a critical component of nursing practice that requires advanced clinical expertise, emotional intelligence, ethical judgment, and effective communication skills. The experience of patient death is inevitable and impactful for nursing students during their clinical training. Exposure to dying patients often evokes anxiety [1], fear [2], and emotional distress [3], particularly when students are unprepared to manage end-of-life situations [2, 3]. Although many undergraduate nursing programs incorporate content related to death, dying, and palliative care, students frequently report insufficient preparation, limited confidence, and discomfort when providing end-of-life care [4, 5]. Additionally, previous studies noted that an insufficient curriculum focus on death and dying results in students feeling unprepared and anxious in practice [1, 4, 5]. Simultaneously, such experiences can promote professional growth, empathy, and reflective learning. García Carpintero-Blas et al. [6] reported that nursing students exposed to end-of-life simulations experienced “emerging emotions” and an enhanced understanding of communication and family roles, demonstrating emotional engagement’s importance in learning. These findings suggest that emotional responses should not be considered solely as barriers but as essential components of professional development.

Understanding nursing students’ attitudes toward death and dying is an important area in nursing education, given that nurses are expected to provide compassionate, competent, and emotionally supportive care to patients and families at the end of their lives [3]. Many nursing students begin their clinical placement with insufficient experience or preparation to provide care for terminally ill patients or manage end-of-life situations [7]. Wang [8] found that undergraduate nursing students often experienced a sense of inadequacy and emotional challenges when caring for dying patients during clinical training. Similarly, Ek et al. [9] noted that students often encounter anxiety, uncertainty, and a lack of confidence in their educational programs owing to limited exposure to death.

Previous studies indicated that nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients are shaped by both their individual experiences and educational factors [8, 10]. Grubb and Arthur found that student nurses’ attitudes were associated with previous experiences of caring for dying patients and the end-of-life education they had received, suggesting that both exposure and formal teaching are important in attitude development [11]. Similarly, Zahran et al. reported that nursing students generally exhibited positive attitudes toward death and caring for dying patients; however, these attitudes varied according to factors such as gender, academic level, and university type [3]. Together, these studies suggest that attitudes are not fixed traits; rather, they develop through educational contexts, personal experiences, and clinical socialization.

Another major area in nursing education is the close relationship between attitudes toward death and students’ death anxiety levels. Gurdogan et al. found a significant relationship between death anxiety and attitudes toward caring for dying patients among nursing students, indicating that students who are more fearful of death may feel less comfortable in end-of-life care situations [12]. Similarly, a systematic review by Jeong et al. identified death anxiety, educational level, knowledge, religion, and previous experience with dying patients as factors influencing attitudes toward caring for dying individuals [13]. Recent evidence has demonstrated that fear of death and death anxiety remain significant issues among nursing students and practicing nurses, influencing their attitudes, emotional responses, and readiness to provide end-of-life care [14, 15].

Religiosity significantly influences nursing students’ attitudes toward death and dying among societies with strong cultural and religious foundations [3]. Previous studies indicate that religious beliefs may lower death anxiety and encourage acceptance of death [16–18]. For example, Iranmanesh et al. found that cultural and religious beliefs significantly influenced nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients [19]. Similarly, Abu Hasheesh et al. reported that higher levels of spirituality were associated with more positive attitudes and less fear when caring for dying patients [20].

Although palliative care has become a global priority, many undergraduate nursing courses do not provide adequate preparation for end-of-life care [21]. Previous studies reported that nursing students frequently experience distress, avoidance, and lack of confidence when caring for dying patients [9, 14].

Regional evidence from neighboring Arab health systems underscores the scope of this gap and the need to examine palliative and end-of-life care attitudes as a distinct construct. In the West Bank, Palestine, Toqan et al. [22] surveyed nurses across 12 hospitals using a cluster random sampling approach and found that palliative care knowledge varied considerably by sociodemographic and professional factors, with knowledge gaps identified as a persistent barrier to quality palliative care delivery. More recently, Awad et al. [23], surveying 260 intensive care unit nurses across West Bank governmental hospitals, reported a similar but more granular pattern: nurses demonstrated insufficient palliative care knowledge (M = 6.6 ± 2.6 out of 20) and low palliative care self-efficacy (M = 23.0 ± 8.1 out of 48), despite reporting comparatively favorable attitudes toward end-of-life care overall (M = 94.1 ± 2.9 out of 150 on the FATCOD scale).

Taken together, these Palestinian findings suggest that favorable general attitudes toward palliative and end-of-life care do not necessarily co-occur with adequate knowledge or confidence (self-efficacy) in delivering it; the three constructs move somewhat independently. This distinction reinforces the value of examining attitudes specifically, rather than knowledge or self-efficacy alone, as a modifiable target for nursing curricula, and is consistent with this study’s theoretical focus on attitudes as a proximal, curriculum-amenable determinant within TRA/TPB (see Theoretical Framework, below). It also situates the present findings among final-year Saudi nursing students within a broader Arab regional pattern: across the Levant and the Gulf, nursing students and practicing nurses alike appear to enter or remain in practice without the full complement of knowledge, self-efficacy, and attitudinal preparation needed for confident end-of-life care. This points to a shared regional need for structured, competency-based palliative care education rather than isolated didactic exposure.

Even where educational interventions have shown potential to improve attitudes, their long-term effectiveness remains uncertain, underscoring the need for more structured and culturally relevant educational approaches. Nursing education in Saudi Arabia has advanced significantly in recent years; nonetheless, there remains a lack of focused research examining undergraduate nursing students’ perspectives on death and dying within the country’s cultural and religious context. Given the influence of Islamic beliefs, cultural values, and educational preparedness, it is essential to examine how Saudi nursing students perceive death and their role in caring for dying patients. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the attitudes of undergraduate nursing students in Saudi Arabia toward death and dying.

Problem statement

Despite the growing emphasis on holistic and patient-centered care, end-of-life care remains one of the most emotionally and ethically demanding aspects of nursing practice [24, 25]. Nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients are critical because they shape their willingness, confidence, and perceived competence in providing compassionate end-of-life care; negative or ambivalent attitudes can lead to emotional avoidance, poor communication, and suboptimal support for dying patients and their families [1, 26].

In Saudi Arabia, cultural norms and Islamic beliefs strongly influence perceptions of death and dying, which may uniquely shape nursing students’ attitudes toward end-of-life care [27, 28]. Gender roles, previous exposure to death-related experiences, and level of religious commitment may be important determinants of these attitudes [20, 29]; however, empirical evidence in the Saudi context remains limited. At King Saud University, one of the largest and longest-established nursing programs in Saudi Arabia, systematic data remain limited regarding final-year nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients and the factors that may facilitate or hinder positive attitudes. In particular, religious commitment’s role, both as a direct influence and as a potential moderator of gender differences, has not been adequately explored [28].

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine final-year nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients and investigate the influence of gender, previous death-related experiences, and religious commitment on these attitudes within the Saudi cultural and religious context. The findings are crucial to inform the design of culturally sensitive, evidence-based educational strategies to better prepare nursing students for end-of-life care.

Study purpose

This study sought to examine attitudes toward caring for dying patients among final-year nursing students at King Saud University, Saudi Arabia. This study aimed to (a) describe the overall attitudes toward caring for dying patients among final-year nursing students; (b) examine differences in attitudes based on gender, previous death-related experiences, and education; (c) examine the relationship between religious commitment and attitudes toward caring for dying patients; and (d) explore whether religious commitment moderates the relationship between gender and end-of-life care attitudes.

Significance of this study

This study makes several important contributions to nursing education and practice in Saudi Arabia. First, it provides empirical evidence on nursing students’ attitudes toward end-of-life care in a major Saudi nursing program. Second, by examining several potential influences on attitudes, including gender, religious commitment, and previous experience, this study provides a perspective on the factors that nursing educators should consider when crafting interventions to improve attitudes toward end-of-life care. Third, examining religious commitment as both a predictor and potential moderator of attitudes recognizes the central role of Islamic faith in Saudi society while empirically testing assumptions about how religion influences professional attitudes. Fourth, the findings can inform curriculum development at King Saud University and other Saudi nursing programs for the development of comprehensive end-of-life care education. Finally, this study adds to the international literature on nursing students’ end-of-life care attitudes and facilitates cross-cultural comparisons that emphasize situation-specific factors requiring adapted educational approaches.

Theoretical framework

This study was informed by the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB [30]) and the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA [31]). These frameworks offer an understanding of the attitudes, social norms, and perceived behavioral control that influence nursing students’ intentions and behaviors regarding the care of dying patients.

Theory of reasoned action

TRA states that intentions are determinants of actual behavior, which are molded by subjective norms and attitudes toward behavior [31]. Attitudes refer to an individual’s positive or negative appraisal of performing a specific behavior. In the context of this study, attitudes pertained to nursing students’ evaluative judgments regarding caring for dying patients, including whether this aspect of nursing care was perceived as positive or distressing. Subjective norms refer to socially influenced perceptions that determine whether an individual engages in a particular behavior. These norms are shaped by the expectations of significant others, including family members, peers, and instructors. Among nursing students, subjective norms may include cultural values, religious teachings, and family traditions associated with death.

Theory of planned behavior

TPB extends TRA by adding a third predictor of behavioral intention: perceived behavioral control, defined as the perception of how comfortable or uncomfortable a behavior is to perform, shaped by past experience and anticipated obstacles [30]. In the context of end-of-life care, perceived behavioral control encompasses nursing students’ confidence in their ability to provide care, communicate with dying patients, and manage their own emotional responses — confidence that is itself shaped by education, clinical exposure, and personal experience with death.

This study drew on TRA and TPB selectively, conceptualizing attitudes — rather than intention or behavior itself — as the variable most directly amenable to change through nursing curricula and therefore the most actionable target for educational intervention at this stage of inquiry. Subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention were not assessed; this was a deliberate scoping decision rather than an oversight, since attitudes represent a necessary antecedent within both models and a reasonable first step before examining the fuller behavioral pathway. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as addressing one determinant within these frameworks — informative for curriculum design targeting attitudes — rather than as a full empirical test of TPB or TRA. Future research should extend this work by measuring subjective norms (e.g., perceived expectations from faculty, peers, and clinical mentors), perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention, to more fully test the applicability of TPB/TRA to end-of-life care behavior among nursing students.

Methods

Design and setting

Study design

A quantitative, cross-sectional survey design was employed to investigate nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients as well as the relationships among gender, religious commitment, and attitudes toward end-of-life care. This approach is suitable for assessing the prevalence of attitudes and examining the correlation between variables at a single point in time [32].

Setting and sample

This study was conducted at the College of Nursing, King Saud University in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The sample comprised final-year “Level 8” nursing students enrolled in the Critical Care Nursing course during the 2024–2025 academic year. By the final semester, these students had completed most of their theoretical coursework and clinical rotations, including experiences in caring for critically ill and dying patients. Convenience sampling was used to recruit the eligible students. The inclusion criteria were Level 8 nursing students at King Saud University who were enrolled in a critical care nursing course and willing to provide informed consent. Of the 319 eligible students enrolled in the course (171 female, 148 male), 318 completed the questionnaire, yielding an achieved response rate of 99.7%.

No a priori power analysis was conducted because recruitment targeted the entire eligible cohort of Level 8 students rather than a sample drawn from a larger population. However, the adequacy of the achieved sample was evaluated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 [33]. For the test of the R² change associated with the Gender × Religious Commitment interaction in the hierarchical regression (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, one tested predictor, three total predictors), a sample of 316 participants would be required to detect a small effect (f² = 0.025); the final sample (N = 318) exceeded this requirement, indicating that the study was adequately powered to detect small effects in the regression and moderation analyses.

Data collection procedure

The data were collected in April 2025. After obtaining approval from the institutional review board, the researcher attended a scheduled Critical Care Nursing course to recruit participants. The students received a brief verbal explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, and of participant confidentiality. Interested students received an electronic survey that included an informed consent form, a demographic questionnaire, a religious commitment question, and the Frommelt Attitudes Toward Care of the Dying Scale-B (FATCOD-B). Participants completed the surveys during the class period, and the surveys were collected immediately afterward. The entire process required less than 20 min. Students were told that participation was voluntary and that choosing not to participate or to withdraw from the study would have no impact on their academic standing or course grades.

Instrument

Data were gathered via a self-administered questionnaire with the following sections: demographic information, questions on witnessing death, caring for dying family members, preparing the deceased, palliative care training, the FATCOD-B scale, and a religious commitment measure. Permission to use the FATCOD-B scale was obtained from Dr. Katherine H. Murray Frommelt prior to data collection.

Demographic questionnaire

The demographic questionnaire included age, gender, academic level, and previous experiences. Other questions were adapted from a previous study on end-of-life care attitudes [34], which included four yes/no questions about participants’ death-related experiences:

Have you received lectures or training about palliative care in your studies?

Have you prepared or assisted in preparing a deceased person?

Have you prepared or assisted in preparing a deceased person?

Frommelt attitudes toward care of the dying scale

The short version of the FATCOD-B was used to evaluate nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients. The FATCOD-B is a brief 9-item instrument derived from the original 30-item FATCOD scale [35, 36]. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). All nine items in the FATCOD-B were negatively worded and reverse-coded during the analysis to ensure that higher scores reflected more positive attitudes toward caring for dying patients. In this study, the tool demonstrated acceptable internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.73).

Religious commitment measure

Religious commitment was assessed using a single item: participants were asked to rate their religious commitment level on a 4-point scale: 1 = “low,” 2 = “average,” 3 = “high,” and 4 = “very high.” This single-item approach to measuring religious commitment was employed in previous studies on nursing students in similar cultural settings and provides a simple assessment of self-reported religiosity [37]. Religious commitment was assessed using a single item asking participants to rate their overall religious commitment on a 4-point scale (low, average, high, very high). This approach was selected for three practical reasons. First, single-item religiosity measures have been used in prior nursing research conducted in comparable Muslim-majority cultural settings, allowing some degree of comparability with existing literature [37]. Second, given that the questionnaire already incorporated a validated 9-item attitude scale alongside demographic and death-experience items, a brief global measure minimized participant burden and survey fatigue within the 20-minute administration window. Third, at the time of data collection, no validated Arabic-language, Islamic-specific religiosity instrument with established psychometric properties in a Saudi nursing student population was readily available, and a single global item offered a transparent, easily interpretable indicator of self-perceived religious commitment.

Nevertheless, this approach carries important limitations addressed further below. Religiosity is a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive (beliefs), behavioral (practice, ritual observance), affective (spiritual experience, comfort derived from faith), and social (community or religious affiliation) dimensions, each of which may relate differently to attitudes toward death and dying. A single item cannot disentangle these dimensions: it is possible that some facets of religiosity, for example, belief in an afterlife or spiritual coping resources, are more strongly related to comfort with dying patients than others, such as religious identity or affiliation alone. Reliance on self-perceived ‘commitment’ also introduces a subjective judgment that may be interpreted differently across respondents and may be more susceptible to social desirability bias than behaviorally anchored items (e.g., frequency of prayer or religious study).

Statistical analysis

First, all nine items were reverse-coded (on a scale of 1 to 5), with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes. IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29) was used to evaluate missing values, outliers, and potential violations of statistical assumptions. This screening identified no missing values: all 318 returned questionnaires were complete, and all analyses were therefore conducted on the full sample (N = 318), with no imputation or case exclusion required. Descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were computed to characterize the sample and assess the FATCOD-B score distribution. Because the FATCOD-B has no validated cut-off scores, total scores were additionally grouped into four descriptive categories (negative, moderately negative, moderately positive, and positive) using the sample mean ± 1 SD. This distribution-based categorization was used solely to describe the spread of attitudes within the sample; the categories are sample-specific, were not treated as clinically meaningful thresholds, and were not used in any inferential analyses, all of which were based on continuous FATCOD-B scores. The 9-item FATCOD-B scale’s internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Group comparisons

Independent-samples t-tests were performed to assess the FATCOD-B scores’ mean differences according to gender and previous death-related experiences, including receiving palliative care lectures, witnessing a dying person, caring for a dying family member, and preparing a deceased body. Effect sizes were determined using Cohen’s d with thresholds of small (0.20), medium (0.50), and large (0.80) effects [38].

Correlation analysis

Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to analyze the relationship between religious commitment levels and the FATCOD-B scores. The correlation coefficient was interpreted using Cohen’s guidelines: 0.10–0.29 as small, 0.30–0.49 as medium, and ≥ 0.50 as large [38].

Hierarchical multiple regression

Before the analysis, the assumption of independence of observations was met through the study design. Continuous variables were mean-centered to reduce multicollinearity when creating the interaction term. Additional regression assumptions were assessed as follows: (a) linearity and homoscedasticity were evaluated through visual inspection of scatterplots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values; (b) normality of residuals was assessed using a normal probability plot (P-P plot) of standardized residuals; (c) multicollinearity was examined using variance inflation factor values, with values < 10 considered acceptable [39]; and (d) influential outliers were identified using Cook’s distance, with values > 1 suggesting potential problems [40].

In Step 1, gender was entered as a dichotomous predictor, with female students as the reference group, reflecting the predominantly female composition of the sample. Because gender is a two-level variable, the specific numeric codes do not affect model fit or significance tests; the coding determines only the sign and interpretation of the coefficient, such that a positive coefficient indicates higher FATCOD-B scores among male students. In Step 2, a mean-centered religious commitment was added to the model. In Step 3, the interaction term (Gender × Religious Commitment) was entered to test moderation. The significance of each step was evaluated using the F-test for R² change (ΔR²), with p < 0.05 demonstrating a significant improvement in model fit. Each regression coefficient was tested for significance using t-tests.

If the interaction term was significant, simple slopes analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between gender and FATCOD-B scores at different religious commitment levels (± 1 SD from the mean [41]).

Ethical considerations

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines established by the King Saud University Institutional Review Board (IRB). Ethical approval was obtained from the IRB of King Saud University (IRB approval number: KSU-HE-25-864) before data collection. Participation in this study was voluntary. Students were informed that they could decline to participate at any time without penalty or impact on their academic standing, grades, or relationships with faculty. The consent form included the study’s purpose, procedures, possible risks and benefits, confidentiality measures, and participants’ rights. All data were stored securely on OneDrive, with only the principal investigator having access, and all electronic files were password-protected. The study was classified as having minimal risk. The potential risk involved mild psychological discomfort from thinking about attitudes toward death and dying. To reduce this, the participants were told that they could skip questions or withdraw at any time. The consent form included contact information for campus counseling services for any participant who felt upset by the survey content.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 318 final-year nursing students participated in the study, comprising 171 females (53.8%) and 147 males (46.2%). The mean age was 21.9 years (SD = 0.83; range = 21–25 years), and all participants were enrolled in Level 8. Most students had prior exposure to palliative care education and death-related experiences: 259 of 318 valid responses (81.2%) reported attending at least one palliative care lecture, 119 of 318 (37.3%) had witnessed a person dying, 90 of 318 (28.2%) had cared for a dying family member, and 60 of 318 (18.8%) had prepared a deceased person’s body (one participant did not respond to these items). Religious commitment was generally high: of 317 valid responses, 6 (1.9%) reported low commitment, 106 (33.3%) average commitment, 129 (40.6%) high commitment, and 77 (24.2%) very high commitment (two responses were missing). A chi-square test of independence indicated that the association between gender and religious commitment was not statistically significant, although it approached significance, χ²(3, N = 317) = 7.74, p = 0.052. This result should be interpreted cautiously, as 2 cells (25.0%) had expected counts below 5, with a minimum expected count of 2.32, violating the assumption required for a valid chi-square test in this configuration. Given this violation, a Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test was conducted as the more appropriate alternative and indicated a statistically significant association between gender and religious commitment, p = 0.047.

Research question 1: What are final-year nursing students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients?

The mean total FATCOD-B score was 25.45 (SD = 5.95; range 9–43). For descriptive purposes only, scores were grouped using the sample mean ± 1 SD: 15.0% negative (< 19.5), 34.5% moderately negative (19.5–25.62), 35.7% moderately positive (25.62–31.4), and 14.4% positive (> 31.4). Because the FATCOD-B has no validated cut-off scores, these sample-specific categories were used solely to aid interpretation of the score distribution and should not be regarded as established thresholds; all inferential analyses used continuous FATCOD-B scores.

At the item level (Table 1), participants reported the greatest discomfort with emotionally demanding and death-related communication tasks: being upset when a patient gave up hope (M = 2.11, SD = 0.96), feeling uncomfortable discussing death (M = 2.46, SD = 1.14), feeling uncomfortable when patients cried (M = 2.59, SD = 1.20), and changing the subject when a patient asked, “Am I dying?” (M = 2.69, SD = 1.24). Students also reported difficulty forming relationships with dying patients (M = 2.93, SD = 1.17) and fear of becoming friends with a dying patient (M = 2.96, SD = 1.29). Comparatively less difficulty was reported for items reflecting reluctance to care for dying patients (M = 3.41, SD = 1.18), discomfort with non-family members discussing death with a dying patient (M = 3.09, SD = 1.12), and feeling like running away when a patient dies (M = 3.29, SD = 1.20). Overall, these item-level findings point to specific emotional and communication challenges rather than a global unwillingness to provide care as the primary source of discomfort in end-of-life situations.

Research question 2: are there significant differences in attitudes between male and female nursing students?

A significant gender difference emerged in FATCOD-B scores. Male participants (M = 26.65, SD = 6.23) reported significantly more positive attitudes toward caring for dying patients than female participants (M = 24.42, SD = 5.52), t(316) = -3.37, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.92, -3.51], Cohen’s d = 0.38 (small effect).

Research question 3: are attitudes associated with previous death-related experiences and palliative care education?

Independent-samples t-tests indicated no significant differences in FATCOD-B scores according to prior exposure to palliative care lectures, witnessing a dying person, caring for a dying family member, or preparing a deceased body (all p > 0.05; Table 2). Mean scores were nearly identical between groups for each comparison, suggesting that these specific prior experiences were not associated with attitudes toward caring for dying patients in this sample.

Hierarchical regression analysis

A three-step hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine whether religious commitment moderated the relationship between gender and attitudes toward caring for dying patients. Continuous predictors were mean-centered prior to computing the interaction term to reduce multicollinearity. Assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity were assessed via inspection of standardized residual scatterplots; normality of residuals was assessed via a P-P plot; multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and VIF values (all tolerances ≥ 0.976; all VIF ≤ 1.024), indicating no problematic collinearity; and no influential outliers were identified (all Cook’s distance values < 1). The Durbin-Watson statistic (1.98) indicated no evidence of autocorrelation among residuals.

In Step 1, The overall model was significant R² = 0.034, F(1, 315) = 1094, p = 0.001, gender was significantly predicted FATCOD-B scores (B = 2.182, SE = 0.660, β = 0.183, t = 3.307, p = 0.001) and explained 3.4% of the variance, ΔR² = 0.034, F(1, 315) = 10.94, p = 0.001, with male students scoring, on average, 2.18 points higher on the FATCOD-B than female students.

In Step 2, religious commitment was added to the model. The overall model remained significant, R² = 0.037, F(2, 314) = 5.98, p = 0.003, but the addition of religious commitment did not significantly improve model fit, ΔR² = 0.003, F(1, 314) = 1.03, p = 0.312. Religious commitment was not a significant predictor of attitudes (B = − 0.422, SE = 0.416, β = − 0.056, t = − 1.014, p = 0.312), while gender remained a significant predictor (B = 2.209, SE = 0.660, β = 0.185, t = 3.345, p < 0.001).

In Step 3, the Gender × Religious Commitment interaction term was added. The overall model remained significant, R² = 0.048, F(3, 313) = 5.26, p = 0.001, but the addition of the interaction term did not significantly improve model fit, ΔR² = 0.011, F(1, 313) = 3.72, p = 0.055. At this step, gender remained a significant predictor (B = 2.204, SE = 0.657, β = 0.185, t = 3.353, p < 0.001), the main effect of religious commitment remained non-significant (B = –0.302, SE = 0.419, β = − 0.040, t = − 0.720, p = 0.472), and the Gender × Religious Commitment interaction did not reach statistical significance (B = − 1.603, SE = 0.831, β = − 0.108, t = − 1.929, p = 0.055). The interaction coefficient’s direction was consistent with smaller gender differences in attitudes at higher levels of religious commitment; however, because this result did not meet the conventional threshold for statistical significance, it is reported as a non-significant coefficient rather than evidence of moderation, and no simple slopes analysis was conducted. Discussion

This study examined attitudes toward caring for dying patients among Level 8 nursing students at King Saud University and explored associations with gender, religious commitment, and death-related experiences. The findings revealed that nursing students generally held neutral to moderately negative attitudes toward caring for dying patients, with significant gender differences favoring male students, and no significant associations for religious commitment or prior death-related experiences. Consistent with the cross-sectional design, all relationships are interpreted as correlational rather than causal.

Overall attitudes toward caring for dying patients

The mean FATCOD score of 25.45 (SD = 5.95) indicated that the nursing students held relatively neutral to moderately negative attitudes toward caring for dying patients, broadly consistent with studies from similar regional contexts. Alwawi et al. reported a comparable FATCOD-B mean of 25.94 among Palestinian nursing students, with 57.6% demonstrating positive attitudes [34]. Abu-El-Noor and Abu-El-Noor likewise documented relatively low levels of positive attitudes in Palestinian samples [42]. Arslan et al. found moderate scores and neutral attitudes among Turkish nursing students [43]. By contrast, studies from Sweden, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom have reported higher FATCOD-B scores and more positive attitudes toward end-of-life care [44, 45]. Because these studies use different FATCOD versions and score ranges, these comparisons indicate relative positioning (lower, moderate, higher within each instrument’s range) rather than strict numerical equivalence.

Within that constraint, Saudi nursing students appear to fall in the moderate range alongside other Middle Eastern and some European cohorts, underscoring the need for stronger palliative and end-of-life care education at King Saud University. Differences between this and higher-scoring Northern European samples may reflect variations in curricular structure, palliative care integration, and cultural openness around death, although these possibilities are inferential rather than directly tested here. Item-level analysis showed the greatest discomfort in intense end-of-life situations, patients crying, discussing impending death, or asking direct questions, mirroring findings reported by Alwawi et al. and Dimoula et al. [34, 46]. This recurring pattern across cultural contexts points to a specific educational need: emotional preparedness and communication skills in end-of-life care, rather than solely knowledge-based content.

Instrument reliability also warrants consideration when interpreting these findings. In the present sample, the 9-item FATCOD-B demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.73). This value is consistent with previous validation studies of the short form: testing the 9-item version among Italian medical students, Loera et al. reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.74 and 0.79 across two administrations [47], and a Polish validation of the 9-item version among undergraduate nursing students reported a value identical to that of the present study (α = 0.73), comparable to the full 30-item Polish version (α = 0.725) [48]. Because coefficient alpha is partly a function of the number of items, somewhat lower values than those reported for the original 30-item FATCOD are expected for brief versions, and values in the low .70s are generally regarded as acceptable for group-level comparisons. Nevertheless, this modest reliability implies greater measurement error at the individual level, which may have attenuated the observed associations; the reported effect sizes should therefore be interpreted as conservative estimates.

Gender differences in attitudes

Male students in this sample reported significantly more positive attitudes than female students. This aligns with Alwawi et al.‘s findings among Palestinian nursing students [34]; however, it contrasts with Dimoula et al. and Zahran et al., who found more positive attitudes among female students in Greek and Jordanian samples [3, 46], and with studies from Indonesia, Palestine, and Europe that reported no gender differences [42, 45, 50]. Taken together, these patterns suggest that gender differences in end-of-life attitudes are context-dependent and likely shaped by how gender roles, emotion norms, and death-related experiences intersect in societies.

Although the present study did not assess death anxiety, emotion-regulation style, or gender-role attitudes directly, a growing regional literature offers plausible frameworks. Studies of medical students in the Arabian Gulf have found higher death avoidance among female students than male students, a pattern that could reasonably impede positive engagement with dying patients [51]. Other work suggests that Saudi women more often use cognitive reappraisal, whereas men tend toward expressive suppression, implying differential emotional engagement with distressing situations that may translate into different perceived burdens in end-of-life care [52]. In addition, Saudi gender-socialization norms that link emotional expressiveness to femininity and emotional composure to masculinity may shape how male and female students perceive and report their comfort with dying patients. Because end-of-life care draws heavily on emotional regulation, empathy, and communication skills, these gender-linked patterns may be best understood through the broader lens of emotional intelligence. Ayed [53] found that among nurses working in a comparably high-stress, emotionally demanding unit (the neonatal intensive care unit), higher emotional intelligence was positively associated with stronger clinical decision-making. This underscores that the capacity to regulate and use emotional information effectively is closely tied to performance in emotionally charged clinical contexts. This parallel strengthens the plausibility that gender differences in emotional regulation style may extend beyond general affect to influence students’ confidence and competence in the specific emotional demands of end-of-life care, although this pathway was not tested directly in the present sample. These pathways, grounded in death-anxiety, emotional regulation, and emotional intelligence perspectives, are best treated as hypotheses for future qualitative and psychometric studies rather than as confirmed mechanisms of the present findings.

The Gender × Religious Commitment interaction approached but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.055), and we therefore refrain from substantive interpretation. The near-significant pattern, narrowing gender differences at the highest levels of religious commitment, may be consistent with literature that describes faith as a coping resource in confronting mortality, particularly for women; however, this remains speculative and untested in our data. Limited variance and measurement error from the single-item religiosity measure likely reduced power to detect moderation effects, reinforcing the need for more granular instruments in future work.

Death-related experiences and educational preparation

In this sample, palliative care lectures, witnessing dying patients, and preparing deceased bodies were not significantly associated with attitudes. This accords with some international studies [49]; however, it diverges from work showing positive associations between structured death-related experiences and more favorable attitudes. The null findings here likely reflect how “experience” and “education” were operationalized rather than indicating that exposure has no impact.

First, brief didactic lectures, especially when unaccompanied by clinical practice, simulation, or structured reflection, may be insufficient to shift attitudes. Smith and colleagues highlight that effective palliative education typically integrates didactic content with simulation, clinical exposure, and guided reflection to support emotional processing and skill development [54]. Although 81.2% of students reported attending a palliative care lecture, we did not capture its duration, content, modality, or integration with clinical practice, limiting our ability to evaluate its educational impact. Second, personal experiences with dying family members are emotionally complex; without reflective support, such experiences can be distressing rather than instructive and may not translate uniformly into more positive professional attitudes [55]. Third, the cross-sectional design cannot capture the dynamic co-evolution of attitudes and experiences across the program; longitudinal studies in Europe have found that attitudes change over time in relation to specific curricular and clinical milestones [56]. Taken together, these considerations suggest that the quality, design, and reflective framing of death-related experiences matter more than their mere occurrence.

Religious commitment and attitudes

Contrary to initial expectations, religious commitment was not significantly associated with attitudes toward caring for dying patients. The wider literature on religiosity and end-of-life attitudes is mixed: Ferri et al. reported minimal impact of religious belief on end-of-life attitudes in an international sample [45], whereas Alkhamees et al. found that stronger religious influence on death attitudes was associated with more negative attitudes toward caring for the dying among Saudi women [57]. A study comparing nurses by religious background reported lower FATCOD-B scores among Muslim participants, without a detailed explanatory analysis [58]. Qualitative work on Muslim healthcare workers describes caring for dying patients as intensifying awareness of mortality, divine judgment, and one’s own eventual death, sometimes generating avoidance and distress [59]. These findings, together with the cultural sensitivity of death in Saudi society [60], suggest that religious belief may simultaneously motivate compassionate care and heighten existential anxiety, producing complex, potentially ambivalent patterns of association.

Methodological factors likely contributed substantially to the null finding in this study. Prior work typically uses multi-item, belief-specific measures that assess religious influence on care-related attitudes and behaviors, whereas we employed a single global item that did not differentiate cognitive, behavioral, or affective dimensions of religiosity. Single-item measures restrict range, introduce measurement error, and attenuate true associations, biasing regression estimates toward the null and reducing power to detect moderation effects. They also cannot indicate which facets of religiosity, organizational practice, private devotion, and intrinsic belief are most relevant to comfort with death and dying. Brief, multidimensional instruments such as the Duke University Religion Index (DUREL) and the Muslim Religiosity-Personality Inventory (MRPI) offer more nuanced assessments of these domains and would allow future studies to identify which specific aspects of religious involvement relate to attitudes toward caring for dying patients.

The final regression model explained only 4.8% of the variance in FATCOD scores, indicating that gender and religious commitment, although statistically detectable, account for a relatively small portion of the attitudinal variation. This aligns with studies where psychosocial factors, such as death anxiety, emotional intelligence, self-efficacy, and depth of palliative care knowledge, show stronger associations with end-of-life attitudes than demographic variables. From an emotional labor and experiential learning perspective, this highlights that how students understand, manage, and reflect on death-related experiences is likely more consequential than their gender or general religiosity alone.

Implications for nursing education

These findings have several implications for undergraduate nursing education at King Saud University and, cautiously, for similar programs in Saudi Arabia. First, the overall neutral to moderately negative attitudes among final-year students suggest that current palliative and end-of-life content is insufficient to produce consistently positive attitudes by graduation. In line with international recommendations [61], palliative care competencies should be integrated longitudinally throughout the curriculum rather than consolidated into isolated lectures. Embedding end-of-life content across clinical courses, with explicit learning outcomes related to communication, emotional coping, and family support, is likely more effective than ad hoc exposure alone.

Second, educational interventions should target the specific attitudinal challenges identified at the item level: discomfort with intense emotional expressions and direct conversations about dying. Simulation-based training that includes scenarios involving crying or distressed patients, structured debriefing, and opportunities to practice responding to questions about death has been associated with improved confidence and attitudes in several settings [54, 62]. Reflection sessions following clinical encounters with dying patients, whether through facilitated small groups, written narratives, or guided debriefings, may help students process distress and transform difficult experiences into learning rather than avoidance [62].

Third, the observed gender difference suggests that educators should be attentive to how male and female students experience end-of-life content, without essentializing or pathologizing either group. Structured opportunities to examine cultural beliefs, gendered emotion norms, and personal responses to death, through mixed-gender small groups or reflective assignments, could foster awareness of how these factors shape attitudes, while empirical work clarifies whether gender differences persist once death anxiety and emotion-regulation styles are measured directly.

Fourth, although religious commitment was not significantly associated with attitudes in this study, the centrality of Islamic teaching to Saudi social and professional identity supports ongoing exploration of how spiritual perspectives can be constructively integrated into palliative education. Curricular approaches that invite discussion of religious meanings of illness, death, and caregiving, while emphasizing compassionate practice and avoiding guilt-based or punitive framings, may help reduce religiously framed death anxiety and support more confident engagement with dying patients.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to an understudied area by examining attitudes toward caring for dying patients among a relatively large cohort of final-year nursing students in Saudi Arabia, using a validated instrument and considering multiple potential correlates: gender, religious commitment, and death-related experience. This single-institution, cross-sectional, convenience-sampled design nonetheless imposes important constraints. The data derive from one large, long-established program, the patterns observed may reflect King Saud University’s specific curricular emphases, faculty attitudes, and clinical placement characteristics, and should not be assumed to represent all Saudi nursing students. Multi-institutional studies across regions and sectors are needed to determine whether these findings are program-specific or more general.

Convenience sampling and the absence of systematically recorded participation rates increase the risk of selection bias; students more comfortable with death or more engaged in coursework may have been more likely to participate. Self-report data collected during scheduled class time and in the presence of course-affiliated staff may be subject to social desirability bias, potentially inflating reported attitudes or religious commitment despite assurances of confidentiality. The coarse measurement of both religious commitment (single item) and palliative education (single binary attendance item) further limits interpretability, particularly for non-significant associations. Future studies should employ validated, multidimensional measures of religiosity such as DUREL or MRPI and more detailed indices of palliative care education that capture duration, content, modality, and integration with clinical practice.

In addition, although a sensitivity analysis indicated that the sample was adequate to detect small effects (f² ≥ 0.025), the observed Gender × Religious Commitment interaction effect (f² ≈ 0.012) fell below this threshold, with an achieved power of approximately 0.48 for the interaction test. The marginally non-significant moderation effect (p = 0.055) should therefore be interpreted with caution, as the study may have been underpowered to detect a very small interaction effect, and larger samples are needed to test this moderation reliably. Finally, the descriptive categorization of FATCOD-B scores was based on sample-specific mean ± 1 SD groupings rather than validated cut-off values; these categories should be interpreted cautiously, and comparisons across studies should be based on mean scores rather than category proportions.

Future research directions

Future work should adopt longitudinal designs to examine how attitudes toward caring for dying patients evolve across the nursing program, in association with specific curricular modules, simulation experiences, and clinical postings. Experimental or quasi-experimental evaluations of targeted educational interventions such as simulation-based training combined with structured reflection would provide stronger evidence for curriculum redesign than cross-sectional associations alone. Studies incorporating validated measures of death anxiety, emotion regulation, emotional intelligence, and self-efficacy, alongside multidimensional religiosity instruments, would allow more precise modeling of how these psychosocial variables interact with gender and cultural context to shape attitudes. Future studies should also capture additional background variables not measured here, such as academic performance (GPA), previous clinical placement in palliative or hospice settings, and family history of terminal illness, as these may provide useful context for interpreting individual variation in attitudes. Qualitative research, particularly focus groups and in-depth interviews, could explore students’ narratives about death, faith, gender norms, and emotional experience, directly testing the hypotheses raised here regarding death avoidance, emotional labor, and religious-professional tension. Multi-institutional studies with documented participation rates and comparators across regions, public/private sectors, and curricular models are needed to determine the extent to which the patterns observed at King Saud University reflect broader trends in Saudi nursing education.

Conclusions

Final-year nursing students at King Saud University generally hold neutral to moderately negative attitudes toward caring for dying patients, with a significant gender difference and no statistically significant association between global religious commitment and attitudes. Within the limits of a cross-sectional, single-institution, convenience-sampled design, these findings highlight that demographic characteristics and coarse religiosity measures explain only a small portion of attitudinal variance, while pointing to the central importance of how students are prepared emotionally and communicatively for end-of-life care. Strengthening palliative and end-of-life content through longitudinal integration, high-quality simulation, structured reflection, and culturally and spiritually sensitive teaching may help ensure that future Saudi nurses are equipped not only with technical competencies but also with the attitudes needed for compassionate care of dying patients.
