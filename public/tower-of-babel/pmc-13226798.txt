Navigating the regulatory landscape of herbal medicines: A global comparison of India, China, Europe, the United States and Japan
Authors: Anil Tukaram Pawar, S.B. Chandrasekar, M.P. Venkatesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of herbal medicines is expanding rapidly worldwide, but regional regulatory systems vary greatly, leading to variations in quality, safety and efficacy. Varying legal frameworks, insufficient quality control and gaps in post-market surveillance create challenges in ensuring the reliability of herbal products. This study aims to analyze and compare the regulations that oversee herbal medicines in five major regions: India, China, Europe, the United States and Japan, highlighting key challenges and proposing recommendations for improving global regulatory alignment. Data were collected from official regulatory websites and scholarly articles. Additionally, the study tracked the progress of related regulations, advancements in scientific research, and the evolving infrastructure in the respective five geographical areas. The study examined regulatory components, including licensing requirements, pre-market validation, quality control and post-market surveillance across these five regions. Regulatory approaches vary significantly: Europe and Japan emphasize pre-market validation and scientific evidence, while the U.S. relies more on post-market monitoring. India and China integrate traditional practices with modern regulatory frameworks. These differences pose challenges for ensuring consistent safety and quality standards globally. Potential solutions involve adopting Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs), harmonizing pharmacopoeial standards and leveraging emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence for quality assurance and supply chain transparency. Achieving global regulatory harmonization is crucial for ensuring the safety and efficacy of herbal medicines while preserving traditional knowledge. Strengthening international collaboration, enforcing standardized quality measures, and integrating scientific validation into traditional systems can enhance consumer confidence and facilitate international trade in herbal medicines.

Introduction

Herbal medicines have been utilized for thousands of years across diverse cultures and civilizations to address various health ailments. They are made from plants and other natural sources. Traditional medical systems, including Ayurveda in India, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in China, Kampo in Japan, and various naturopathic practices in Europe and the United States, hold a significant place in healthcare. With the growing global trend toward alternative and complementary medicine, herbal products have increasingly entered mainstream healthcare systems. Herbal medicines are becoming popular to treat a wide range of illnesses. The global demand for herbal medicines has surged notably, driven by rising consumer interest in natural treatments, heightened awareness of the side effects associated with synthetic drugs, and the increasing integration of traditional medicine into contemporary healthcare. Over the past decade, public interest and acceptance of natural remedies have increased significantly in both developing and developed countries [1].

According to World Health Organization (WHO) reports, herbal medicines are primary means of meeting basic medical needs for over 60% of the global population, including approximately 80% of those residing in developing countries [2]. The global herbal medicine market is projected to grow from USD 169.1 billion in 2023 to USD 279.8 billion by 2028, with compound annual growth rate of 10.6% [3]. However, despite their widespread use and growing popularity, herbal medicines face regulatory challenges. These include issues related to safety, efficacy, quality control and formulations consistency. Unlike conventional pharmaceuticals, which undergo rigorous pre-market testing and regulatory scrutiny, herbal medicines are often subject to lenient standards, which vary significantly from one country to another. This raises concerns regarding the safety and quality of herbal products, along with potential issues of adulteration, contamination and incorrect labelling.

Herbal products are governed by various regulatory categories worldwide, such as natural health products, prescription drugs, over-the-counter products, and complementary and alternative medicines. The regulation of herbal medicines varies across regions, influenced by historical, cultural and scientific contexts. In countries like India, China and Japan, traditional systems of medicine such as Ayurveda, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and Kampo, have shaped the use of herbal remedies for centuries. These systems emphasize a holistic approach, seeking to balance the body, mind and spirit for treating illness. However, as globalization and consumer demand for herbal products have increased, the need for modern regulatory frameworks has become more essential to ensure product safety, efficacy and quality.

Despite the benefits of herbal medicines, inadequate regulation can pose serious health risks. A notable case in the United States in 2003 involved Steve Bechler, a 23-year-old pitcher for the Baltimore Orioles, who collapsed during a spring training session and subsequently died. The medical examiner identified ephedra toxicity as a significant contributing factor to his heatstroke, leading to increased scrutiny and eventual regulatory actions against ephedra-containing supplements. Similarly, in Japan, concerns over herbal medicine safety were highlighted in when several patients developed interstitial pneumonia after taking Sho-saiko-to, a traditional Kampo medicine. Such cases underscore the importance of stringent regulatory oversight and post-market surveillance to identify and mitigate risks associated with herbal products [4].

With the diverse regulatory approaches worldwide, comparing and analyzing the regulatory systems for herbal products across different regions is essential. This review provides a detailed analysis of regulatory frameworks in India, China, Europe, the United States and Japan. It also contextualizes guidelines from Canada, Australia, the Middle East and Africa. This study aims to examine key regulatory components, such as legal frameworks, licensing requirements, quality control measures and post-market surveillance. By exploring the similarities and differences in these systems, it seeks to enhance the understanding of global regulatory landscapes and contribute to potential harmonization efforts that ensure consumer safety while preserving traditional medical knowledge.

Methods

In this study, data were collected from various regulatory databases and official websites for thorough coverage of regulations governing the herbal remedies. Official regulatory websites were visited for each region. These included the Ministry of AYUSH in India, the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) in China, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) in Japan, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in Europe, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States. Additionally, databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar and World Health Organization (WHO) resources were used to gather supporting data for analysis.

Regulatory evolution, scientific advancements and infrastructure development across the five geographical regions were monitored. To find pertinent literature and recommendations, keywords like “herbal medicine”, “herbal medicine regulations”, “herbal regulation”, “herbal regulatory requirements”, “traditional medicine regulations”, “phytopharmaceutical standards”, “traditional medicine safety”, and “GMP herbal products” were used. Relevant case studies were included to illustrate the real-world intricacies and contextual details. The study considered recent revisions in regulatory frameworks and primary sources from official regulatory agencies, highlighting important regulatory elements like safety, efficacy and post-market surveillance. However, non-English sources were excluded.

While the primary focus of this review was on these five major regulatory jurisdictions, regulatory approaches in other regions, such as Africa, the Middle East, Canada and Australia, were also examined through secondary literature, WHO reports, and official regulatory summaries. These regions were reviewed to provide contextual support for the global regulatory discussion and to capture representative regulatory diversity.

Results

A clear understanding of current regulatory frameworks, scientific progress and infrastructure across these regions is crucial for interpreting the results. The regulatory landscape for herbal medicines varies significantly across these regions. Some regions have well-defined policies with stringent quality control measures, while others are still refining their guidelines to align with global standards. Scientific research has been instrumental in driving regulatory changes, influencing policy updates and advancing innovation in herbal drug development. Additionally, the infrastructure supporting regulatory compliance, research and commercialization has evolved at different rates, shaping the availability, acceptance and market integration of herbal medicines. Recognizing these variations helps to contextualize the findings and reveals important trends in global advancements.

India

Herbal medications are incorporated into nearly all officially recognized medical systems in India, including Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, Homeopathy and Naturopathy, with the exception of allopathy. Over 70% of Indians continue to rely on these non-allopathic system of medicines [5]. India recognizes nearly 8,000 herbal treatments across the Ayurvedic, Yoga, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathic (AYUSH) systems [6]. These traditional formulations are produced in India at an approximately 9,000 manufacturing facilities across the country.

In 2025, the Government of India officially expanded the AYUSH acronym to include Sowa Rigpa, recognizing it as a vital traditional system of medicine alongside Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathy. Sowa Rigpa, meaning “Knowledge of Healing” in the Bhoti language, is an ancient medical tradition rooted in the Trans-Himalayan region and is widely practiced in areas such as Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu & Kashmir, Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim and parts of West Bengal [7].

The Ministry of AYUSH regulates herbal products in India under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act of 1940 (D&C Act) and Rules of 1945 (Table 1) [8]. According to Indian regulations, herbal products are primarily categorized into two main types: (1) Classical Ayurvedic medicines, which are manufactured and labeled in accordance with the formulations specified in the authoritative Ayurvedic texts, and (2) Patent or proprietary medicines, which are based on classical formulations but incorporate creativity, innovation, or intellectual input to develop products that differ from traditional medicine [9]. It is pertinent to note that the term “herbal drug” is not a recognized legal term under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, recognizes Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani (ASU) drugs as defined under Section 3(a). The D&C Act covers various aspects of formulation, including composition, manufacturing, labelling, packing, quality assurance and export. Schedule “T" of the Act outlines the guidelines for Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs) in the preparation of herbal medicines. Schedule T of the D&C Act and Rules is a compulsory framework of requirements for GMPs specifically developed for Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani (ASU) drugs. While there are general GMP descriptions, Schedule T includes specific stipulations and enforceable specifications with respect to factory premises and layout, water supply, waste disposal, and materials specifications to maintain hygienic manufacturing. The guidelines require separate areas for raw materials, manufacturing, quality control and final product storage. Manufacturers must validate raw material traceability, properly labeled products and prevent contamination of raw materials and ingredients. Similarly, personnel hygiene and practices, records of batch manufacturing, and quality testing of raw materials and finished products are required. Schedule T ensures that the herbal products entering the marketplace are of consistent quality, safety and efficacy, aligning traditional products with modern GMP standards. Schedule T is a set of requirements that is legally binding on all manufacturers producing products in the AYUSH category, including classical and proprietary medicines, ensuring consumer health and acceptance for products sold domestically and internationally. The implementation of Schedule T demonstrates India’s commitment to integrating traditional knowledge with robust regulatory frameworks.

Under the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules of 1945, classical ASU drugs manufactured strictly as per authoritative texts are generally exempt from pre-market clinical safety and efficacy studies. However, Rule 158B requires that for new ASU drugs, new formulations, new combinations, new indications or safety issues, the licensing authoritycan call for the evidence of safety and efficacy, such as toxicity studies and clinical data. Thus, while traditional medicines rely on historical evidence of their use, there are regulations to ensure that scientific assessment is required when necessary. The classical and patent/proprietary ASU drugs are not generally considered “new drugs” within the traditional allopathic regulatory system and, as such, are not typically required to obtain prior approval from the Drugs Controller General of India (DCGI) for marketing. Phytopharmaceutical drugs, as defined by Rule 122E and subsequent amendments, are within the regulatory scope of the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) and must obtain approval from the DCGI, including approval for clinical trials. Additionally, in the event that an herbal drug is studied for a new therapeutic use or assessed via the new drug route, the rules of the New Drugs and Clinical Trials (NDCT) Rules, 2019 may apply.

In March 2013, the Ministry of AYUSH released the Good Clinical Practice (GCP) Guidelines for ASU Medicines, providing a framework for conducting clinical research. The guidelines covered key areas such as toxicity studies, standardization, ethics and documentation. This document provides general guidance on herbal clinical trial adherence, toxicity studies, standardization needs and GCP compliance across all clinical trials [9]. These guidelines facilitate the evidence-based validation of ASU treatments and complement the statutory regulatory framework.

In 2015, the CDSCO established regulatory guidelines for phytopharmaceuticals. A phytopharmaceutical drug is defined as a “purified and standardized fraction of an extract of a medicinal plant or a part of it that contains at least four bio-active or phytochemical compounds (as determined by a qualitative and quantitative assessment) for internal or external use by humans or animals for the diagnosis, treatment, mitigation, or prevention of any disease or disorder”, excluding parenteral administration. DCGI permission is required for clinical trials of phytopharmaceutical drugs.

The GCP guidelines classify herbal products by their alignment with traditional medicine texts for usage and formulation. The DCGI has set standards for allopathic drugs, which also apply to herbal remedies and medicinal plants intended for clinical evaluation within the allopathic framework, including their potential use in allopathic hospitals. If a plant extract or compound extracted from a plant is to be clinically evaluated for a therapeutic effect not originally documented in traditional texts or if the preparation method differs, it is considered as a new substance or new chemical entity (NCE). The regulatory body requires comprehensive acute, subacute and chronic toxicity data before approving it for clinical studies. A plant extract or chemical that has not been previously used or documented in historical literature should be treated as a new drug, and must undergo all necessary regulatory processes before clinical testing [10].

The main issue facing regulatory bodies is that many herbal medicine products are polyherbal formulations or similar products manufactured at multiple locations and there is a lack of information on the supply chain and quality of the drugs. Many nations have developed pharmacopoeias that establish standardized guidelines for an expanding range of herbal medicines and finished dosage forms to address quality issues [11]. Pharmacopoeias for the herbs used in the majority of widely practiced Indian traditional medical systems have been developed in India. The primary reference books for Indian herbs include the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India, Siddha Pharmacopoeia of India, Homoeopathic Pharmacopoeia of India and Unani Pharmacopoeia of India. The Pharmacopoeia Commission for Indian Medicine and Homeopathy (PCIM&H), functioning under the Ministry of AYUSH, is responsible for providing pharmacopoeias for Indian herbs [12]. Despite the existence of the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act of 1954, India continues to face widespread dissemination of misleading advertisements of herbal and AYUSH products. Thousands of objectionable and deceptive advertisements of AYUSH products, including exaggerated claims of their therapeutic benefits, have been recorded on government platforms and raised by self-regulatory organizations. There have also been references to concerns over advertisements of Ayurvedic and other traditional medicines claiming to provide efficacious treatment for chronic ailments without any scientific rationale. Taken together, these factors indicate a large gap between the existing legislation and its enforcement, which calls for stricter punishment and coordination between the Ministry of AYUSH, CDSCO and consumer protection organizations, as well as the need for evidence-based substantiation of any claims made [14,15].

Case: A study in Hyderabad in 2025 by the ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition evaluated 170 herbal samples and found that more than half, 52.4%, failed to meet the United State Pharmacopoeia standards for microbial limits. In solid herbal formulations like Ashwagandha and Shatavari, 63.6% exceeded the acceptable limits of aerobic bacteria. The pathogens isolated included Staphylococcus at 62.7%, Pseudomonas at 31.8%, and E. coli, which clearly points to the lack of proper manufacturing practices in preventing contamination of herbal drugs [16].

China

The National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), which was renamed from the China Food and Drug Administration (CFDA) in 2018, serves as the regulatory authority for herbal medicines in China. In China, herbal drugs are classified as either functional foods or drugs. The regulatory approval for functional foods falls under the Department of Food Licence, while products related to Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) are regulated by the “Regulations on the Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine” and the “Chinese Pharmacopoeia.” The NMPA requires manufacturers to meet stringent quality, safety and effectiveness requirements before receiving market authorization for their herbal medicines [17]. The NMPA maintains an extensive list of officially recognized herbal ingredients. This list allows for standardized evaluation and approval of herbal medicines, ensuring a degree of consistency and reliability in the market. China has implemented GMP guidelines for the manufacture of herbal medicines. These guidelines ensure standardized manufacturing processes, quality control, raw material management, process validation and safety measures [18]. In China, the term “drugs” covers both chemical and traditional medicines. According to Article 102 of the Drug Administration Law of the People's Republic of China, “drugs” refer to items used for the prevention, treatment and diagnosis of human diseases, as well as for regulating human physiological functions. This broad definition includes Chinese herbal medicines, prepared slices of these herbs, traditional Chinese medicine formulations, chemical drug substances and their preparations, antibiotics, biochemical drugs, radioactive medicines, serums, vaccines, blood products and diagnostic agents. As per the legal provisions, herbal medicinal products must undergo a safety and efficacy evaluation and must be produced in compliance with GMP standards before being sold. The People's Republic of China's 2005 Pharmacopoeia, which establishes the country's standards for herbal medicine, contains 1,146 monographs [19]. This complies with global regulatory requirements to guarantee that all pharmaceutical products, including herbal medicines, meet specific safety and efficacy requirements.

To enhance the global recognition of Chinese herbal products, China has undertaken measures to promote the internationalization of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). A significant milestone in this process was the inclusion of TCM in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) by the WHO in 2019. However, challenges remain, particularly in harmonizing regulatory standards for TCM with those of other countries, where the scientific evaluation of herbal products is often more stringent.

This resulted in over 100 patients developing Aristolochic Acid Nephropathy (AAN), a severe kidney disease that progresses to end-stage renal failure and urothelial carcinoma. Aristolochic acid, found in Aristolochia species, was identified as a potent nephrotoxin and carcinogen. The incident, known as Chinese Herb Nephropathy, highlighted the risks of inadequate quality control and misidentification in traditional medicine. The case highlights the urgent need for strict regulatory oversight, proper botanical classification, and pharmacovigilance in the global herbal medicine industry [22].

European Union

In the European Union (EU), products are categorized as either “Classified as Medicines” or “Classified as Supplements”. Food supplements fall under the category of “Classified as Supplements,” and they are subject to the same regulations as food under Directive 2002/46/EC. The European regulatory framework defines herbal medicinal products, traditional herbal medicinal products, herbal compounds and herbal preparations [23]. The EU consists of member countries, including Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain and Sweden. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) serves as the primary regulatory authority for herbal medicines within the EU, although individual member states have their own regulatory bodies [1]. The EMA's operations are governed by the European Union's Directive 2001/83/EC and Regulation (EC) No 726/2004. To receive market authorization, manufacturers are required to provide scientific evidence demonstrating the safety and efficacy of their herbal medicines.

In the EU, the standard procedure is to evaluate the safety, effectiveness and quality of traditional or herbal medicines before allowing them on the market. The pharmaceutical company must choose the appropriate authorization procedure in order to obtain a marketing authorisation [23]. The various application procedures encompass the centralized procedure, decentralized procedure, mutual recognition procedure and national procedure. The centralized registration procedure involves an assessment managed by the EMA. This procedure is utilized for specific indications, such as neurological or oncological conditions, or for pharmaceutical products of public health importance. Once marketing authorization is granted through the centralized procedure, the pharmaceutical product can be marketed in any of the EU member states. The decentralized registration process applies to certain Member States. In this approach, a Reference Member State leads the assessment, with other Member States (Concerned Member States) involved primarily in verifying the evaluation conducted by the Reference Member State. Once the process is successfully completed, the participating Member States grant marketing permission or registration. If a medicinal product is already approved or registered in one Member State, a mutual recognition procedure based on the existing assessment can be initiated. In this scenario, after a successful process, the participating Member States issue marketing permission or registration. In the national registration procedure, a single national competent authority handles the application, and if successful, only one Member State provides the marketing authorization.

To commercialize an herbal medicinal product in an EU member state, three main regulatory registration types must be adhered to: traditional use registration, well-established use marketing authorization, and stand-alone (full application) or mixed application pathways.

In Article 16a (1) of Directive 2001/83/EC, traditional use registration is outlined. If sufficient safety data and plausible efficacy evidence is provided, clinical studies or tests on safety and efficacy are not required for this type of registration. This process involves evaluating safety and efficacy data primarily from bibliographic sources. The herbal products must have been used for at least 30 years, including a minimum of 15 years within the European Union. They should not be administered by injection and are intended for use without supervision of a medical practitioner. To achieve this, one must apply via national, mutual recognition and decentralized procedures to the national competent authority of a Member State [24].

The use of well-established marketing authorization is outlined in Article 10a of Directive 2001/83/EC. To qualify for this authorization, it must be demonstrated through scientific literature that the active ingredients of the medicinal products have been used for medical purposes within the EU for at least ten years, showing established efficacy and a satisfactory level of safety. This process primarily involves reviewing bibliographic safety and efficacy data. Applicants must submit their request to the relevant national authority of a Member State for national, mutual recognition, or decentralized procedures. For a centralized registration process, the application should be made to the EMA [24].

Article 8 (3) of Directive 2001/83/EC outlines the stand-alone or mixed-type application process. The safety and efficacy data utilized for this registration are obtained either through company-sponsored studies or by combining clinical studies with bibliographical sources. To proceed with this registration, an application must be submitted to the national competent authority of a Member State for national, mutual recognition and decentralized procedures. It is necessary to notify EMA if a centralized procedure is to be used [24].

The European Medicines Agency's Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC) is tasked with compiling and assessing scientific data on herbal substances, preparations and combinations to support the harmonization of the European market. In September 2004, the HMPC assumed the responsibilities previously held by the Working Party on Herbal Medicinal Products of the Committee on Proprietary Medicinal Products. This transition followed the Herbal Directive and Regulation (EC) No 726/2004, which facilitated the registration process for traditional herbal medicinal products across EU member states. The two main tasks of HMPC are establishing EU monographs covering the therapeutic uses and safe conditions of well-established and/or traditional use for herbal substances and preparations, and drafting an EU list of herbal substances, preparations and combinations thereof for use in traditional herbal medicinal products [25].

A key duty of the HMPC is to develop EU herbal monographs that address the safety and effecacy of herbal medicines and substances. To create a monograph, a rapporteur compiles data from public sources and information from national competent authorities. During the monograph preparation process, the Working Party on European Union Monographs and the European Union List, along with the HMPC, review the scientific content. EU monographs provide comprehensive details necessary for the use of a medicinal product containing a specific herbal substance or preparation, including its intended target population, therapeutic uses and safety information such as potential side effects and interactions with other medications [26]. A monograph may classify herbal products or herbal preparations into two distinct groups based on the available evidence. The two categories adhere to either the principle of well-established use or the principle of traditional use. As of 2026, the HMPC has published over 220 herbal monographs.

The European Pharmacopoeia establishes quality standards for herbal medicines in Europe [27]. It provides both general and specific monographs on herbal drugs and herbal drug preparations used in herbal medicinal products within the EU. These monographs cover every aspect of the quality of herbal products and herbal drug preparations, including procedures, examinations, identification, tests and assays for pesticides, heavy metals, aflatoxins and microbial contamination.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has been an important healthcare treatment in China and other Asian countries for centuries. However, registering traditional herbal medicinal products in European market has proven to be a challenge due to strict regulatory requirements. Under the EU's Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive (THMPD) 2004/24/EC, a traditional herbal medicinal product must demonstrate a period of 30 years of traditional use, of which at least 15 years within the EU, to qualify for a simplified registration process. Many TCM products, despite their long history of use in Asia, do not meet this EU-specific requirement. As a result, manufacturers are forced to go through a full marketing authorization process-an expensive, time-consuming procedure requiring extensive scientific data on safety, efficacy and quality.

While these regulations are designed to protect consumers, EU also create significant barriers for traditional herbal medicines that lack a presence in Europe. This raises an important question: how to balance public health protection with cultural preservation of diverse traditional traditions? Finding a balanced approach in which regulatory standards ensure safety without unnecessarily restricting access to beneficial traditional medicines could pave the way for a more inclusive approach to global healthcare framework [21].

USA

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) categorizes botanical products into food, drugs or dietary supplements depending on their intended use or claims. Drugs are substance intended to prevent, diagnose, treat, mitigate, or cure a disease. Botanical products with structure/function claims are regulated as dietary supplements and disease treatment claims make them drugs. According to the FDA, a drug must be marketed only after approval of New Drug Application (NDA) [28].

In the US, some herbal supplements can be considered botanical drugs and approved as prescription drugs using the FDA's New Drug Application (NDA) procedure. To facilitate the development of botanical drugs, the FDA issued its “Botanical Drug Development” guidance (revised in 2016). This document establishes quality, safety and efficacy criteria specific to complex botanical mixtures, but also provides some flexibility in the early stages of development. However, it maintains the same high standards of clinical assessments as conventional drugs. Examples of approved prescription botanical drugs include Veregen® (sinecatechins from green tea for genital warts), Mytesi™ (crofelemer for HIV-associated diarrhea), Filsuvez® (birch triterpenes for wound healing), and NexoBrid® (anacaulase-bcdb for burn care) [29].

Dietary supplements are regulated by the FDA under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA) [30]. According to this regulation, supplements are defined as “products intended to supplement the diet.” Examples of supplements include vitamins, minerals, herbs, enzymes, amino acids, metabolites, extracts or concentrates. A dietary supplement is not permitted to claim to “diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease,” which is a key difference between a drug and a dietary supplement. Notably, manufacturers of dietary supplements are allowed to make particular “structure/function” claims, which often consist of vague statements regarding potential health benefits. In contrast to pharmaceutical medications, which must be demonstrated to be safe and effective before being sold, dietary supplements can be manufactured, distributed, and marketed without prior approval. Additionally, the FDA bears the responsibility of demonstrating that a dietary supplement is unsafe before it can be removed from the market. This is in direct contrast to the pharmaceutical sector, where the manufacturers are required to provide the FDA with proof of a product's safety and efficacy before it can be marketed [31]. The FDA's role in regulating herbal supplements focuses on post-market surveillance, ensuring labelling compliance, and acting against products with safety issues or false claims [32]. This enables the FDA to act if there are safety concerns [33]. The FDA requires herbal supplements to have precise labelling standards, including the components used and the recommended dose. On February 7, 2000, the FDA released a final regulation (docket No. 98N-0044) in the Federal Register outlining how the agency will differentiate between claims about structure/function and disease. The legislation permits claims for non-disease-related advantages (e.g., “muscle enhancement,” “helps you relax”), common, minor symptoms associated with life stages, and health maintenance (e.g., “maintains a healthy circulatory system”). It forbids making explicit or implicit promises about diseases (such as “prevents osteoporosis” or “prevents bone fragility in postmenopausal women”).

The U.S. Pharmacopeial Convention (USP) publishes the Herbal Medications Compendium (HMC), which establishes standards for the herbal ingredients utilized in herbal medicines. Monographs are the main source of standards. A monograph begins with a definition of the herbal ingredient with regard to the monograph title, followed by general information and specification. The standard monograph covers analytical test protocols, acceptance criteria for specified tests and testing for important quality aspects of the herbal ingredient. Using advanced analytical methods and related reference materials, USP's HMC incorporates validated analytical procedures for the tests included in its monographs [34].

The differentiation between herbal drugs and dietary supplements poses a major challenge within the regulatory framework in the United States. To avoid the stringent regulatory requirements for drugs, many herbal products are instead marketed as dietary supplements. This practice raises concerns about the inconsistency, lack of quality control, and insufficient scientific validation for many herbal products available in the market. Current good manufacturing practice (cGMP) regulations were established by the FDA in 2007 to guarantee consistency in the identification, purity, strength, and composition of dietary supplements. As required by cGMP, manufacturers must conduct product quality tests, ensure that products are free of certain contaminants, check that product labels are accurate, and adhere to minimum manufacturing and packing standards. However, because manufacturers are not consistently implementing cGMP rules, these regulations remain insufficient to guarantee the safety of the supplements [35].

In February 2004, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) banned the sale of dietary supplements containing ephedrine alkaloids, with the rule officially taking effect in April 2004. This ban was implemented under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA), which sets different regulatory standards for dietary supplements compared to prescription drugs. One of the most well-known herbal sources of ephedrine alkaloids is Ma Huang, a traditional Chinese herb. While Ma Huang has been used for centuries, its stimulant effects on the nervous system have been linked to serious health risks, including heart attacks and strokes. Due to these concerns, the FDA took decisive action to prohibit its sale, highlighting the challenges of balancing traditional herbal medicine with modern safety regulations [36].

Kratom, an herbal supplement with opioid-like properties, is increasingly used for pain relief and mood enhancement, raising concerns about its safety. A review of kratom exposure cases from the National Poison Data System, including instances where kratom was the sole substance, revealed significant toxicities. Common adverse effects included agitation, rapid heart rate, drowsiness, vomiting, and confusion, while severe cases involved seizures, withdrawal symptoms, hallucinations, respiratory depression, coma, and cardiac or respiratory arrest. These findings highlight the growing public health risk of kratom, emphasizing the need for stricter regulatory oversight to ensure consumer safety [37].

Japan

Traditional herbal medicines in Japan are primarily crude pharmaceuticals originating from natural sources. They are classified into two main categories: Kampo products and non-Kampo crude medical products. Kampo products are formulated using Kampo prescriptions that adhere to Kampo medical principles, while non-Kampo crude medical products contain one or more crude drug substances and are formulated based on traditional folk medicine practices [38]. Kampo drugs are either extracts or dry powders derived from a blend of naturally occurring plant remedies. They have their origins in ancient Chinese medicine and have gradually developed into the Japanese original style over many years. The term “Kampo” translates to “method from the Han dynasty (206 BC to 220 AD)” and signifies its roots in ancient China.

Japan was introduced to Western medicine, or “Rampo” through the Netherlands. Even though Kampo medicine remains the most prescribed herbal medications in Japan, Western herbal medicines are growing in popularity. In Japan, the term “Western herb” is still ambiguous. This word is typically used to refer to foreign-origin herbs that are not used in Kampo treatment. Herbs from other nations are referred to as “seiyohabu,” or “Western herbs,” whereas the plants used in Kampo therapies are known as “shoyaku,” or “crude drugs.”

When herbal products include ingredients not specified in the “List of Raw Materials Exclusively Used as Pharmaceuticals,” they are marketed either as Health and Medicinal Foods or as Dietary Supplements. The official name of the registration guidelines for Health and Medical Foods in Japan is “Food with Health Claims”. Many Western herbs that are available for sale are not included in the official “List of Raw Materials Exclusively Used as Pharmaceuticals,” so they are not classified as drugs. However, they can be marketed as functional (health) foods without the need for a license [39].

There are monographs in the Japan Pharmacopoeia (JP) that describe herbal preparations and medicinal plants. It contains more than two hundred entries of crude drugs and crude drug powders, over twenty-five classical Kampo formulations, and other herbal preparations. Crude drugs not included in the JP are governed under “The Japanese Standards for Non-Japanese Pharmacopoeia Crude Drugs”.

An additional official method for controlling and regulating the quality of over-the-counter (OTC) Kampo medicines is outlined by the “Approval Standards for OTC Kampo Products”. This guideline specifies the conditions under which OTC Kampo medicines should be used and establishes limits on the quantity of each component in these preparations. Unlike prescription formulations, which often contain higher concentrations of active compounds derived from crude plant materials, OTC Kampo medicines typically have no more than 50% of the active components found in prescription formulations. Furthermore, the concentration of active ingredients in OTC medicines can vary depending on the manufacturer. To obtain approval, Kampo formulations must meet specific efficacy standards based on extensive clinical experience and traditional use. Additionally, the same quality and safety standards required for prescription drugs are also mandated for these products [39].

Most Japanese consumers consider natural remedies to be safe and less likely to cause adverse drug reactions (ADRs). However, when the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) announced in early 1990s that the Kampo formulation “Sho-saiko-to” induced interstitial pneumonia, it highlighted that herbal medicines can carry specific risks. Currently, the Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) oversees the procedures for monitoring ADRs [32]. The PMDA is a government agency responsible for ensuring the safety, efficacy, and quality of pharmaceuticals, including herbal medicines. It rigorously enforces quality control procedures to ensure that herbal products adhere to GMP standards [11]. This ensures the consistency of quality and safety. In Japan, there is a strong emphasis on scientific validation, requiring clinical data to support the effectiveness of Kampo medications.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare had announced an association between the Kampo formulation Sho-saiko-to and interstitial pneumonia, a serious lung condition. This finding led to enhanced pharmacovigilance and the implementation of stricter quality control measures for herbal medicines nationwide [40].

In recent years, Japan has continued to monitor and address adverse events associated with herbal medicines. A study published in 2024 analyzed cases of drug-induced interstitial lung disease (DIILD) linked to specific herbal ingredients, including Scutellariae radix (ogon), Bupleuri radix (saiko), and Pinelliae tuber (hange). The research utilized data from the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report (JADER) database to evaluate the onset and characteristics of DIILD associated with these herbs. The findings underscored the importance of vigilant monitoring and regulation of herbal medicines to prevent severe adverse reactions [41].

Other regulatory regions

In addition to the five primary regions analyzed, regulatory data from Canada, Australia, the Middle East and Africa reveal distinct but increasingly structured approaches to the regulation of herbal medicines. Canada regulates natural health products (NHPs), including herbal medicines, through the Natural and Non-prescription Health Products Directorate (NNHPD) under the Food and Drugs Act and Natural Health Products Regulations (NHPR) since 2004. All NHPs require pre-market licensing via risk-stratified Class I-III reviews. The Class I is applicable for low-risk products fully matching NNHPD monographs (10-day review), Class II for minor variations like novel dosages, and Class III for high-risk innovations needing robust evidence. Approved products receive an Natural Product Number (NPN) or Homeopathic Medicine Number (DIN-HM) based on safety, efficacy, and quality assessments using clinical studies, pharmacopoeias, and traditional sources like WHO/EMA monographs. Over 70,000 products and 2,000+ GMP-licensed sites have been authorized in Canada. This regulatory framework is supported by the Natural Health Products Ingredients Database (NHPID) and 250+ monographs covering Traditional Chinese, Ayurvedic, and Western herbs, with post-market surveillance addressing contamination, adulteration, and misleading claims [42].

In the Middle East, Saudi Arabia leads herbal medicine regulation through the National Center for Complementary and Alternative Medicine (NCCAM) under the Ministry of Health, overseeing 75% population use alongside therapies like cupping (68 licensed clinics, 160 practitioners) and honey, with herbal products requiring Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) registration as per the Health Professions Act of 2017 and GMP standards. Complementing this, Oman has registered 14 manufacturers and 60 products since 2008 via dedicated herbovigilance, UAE enforces Federal Law No. 20/1995 with lab testing and Halal certification, Bahrain mandates pharmacy controls treating herbals as pharmaceuticals, and Kuwait requires import licensing under Decree 101/2020 banning narcotic herbs-addressing adulteration and safety gaps in Traditional Arab Herbal Medicine (TAHM) use for diabetes and GI conditions through national pharmacovigilance [28,43].

Australia regulates complementary medicines, including herbal products, through the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) under the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989, requiring entry on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) as either “listed” (AUST L, low-risk with abbreviated pre-market quality/labelling review) or “registered” (AUST R, higher-risk with full safety/efficacy evaluation).​ Most herbals fall under listed medicines containing permitted ingredients from Schedule 14 (herbs, vitamins, homoeopathics), with GMP manufacturing, supported by evidence-based indications from approved lists and post-market surveillance for adverse events. However, certain exemptions are apply to practitioner-compounded formulations and diluted homoeopathic preparations [44,45].

Africa regulates herbal medicines variably across its countries through national agencies or ministries of health, often under general drug laws without dedicated frameworks requiring basic registration in leaders like South Africa (South African Health Products Regulatory Authority [SAHPRA] with full dossier reviews including stability/GMP data), Nigeria (National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control [NAFDAC] mandating safety/quality dossiers plus traditional use evidence), Ghana (Food and Drugs Authority [FDA] requiring product registration with lab testing for contaminants) and Uganda (National Drug Authority [NDA] needing efficacy/safety dossiers for herbal listings), though enforcement varies. Post-market surveillance is weak continent-wide, with open-market sales dominating. WHO African Region: only ∼10% countries have, comprehensive herbal registration, exempting low-risk traditional remedies in places like Ethiopia. WHO African Region reports that only 7% of countries have moderate regulatory capacity, with most needing better standardization, pharmacovigilance and harmonized guidelines despite policy progress [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50]].

Role of WHO guidelines in regulation and quality assurance of herbal medicines

The World Health Organization (WHO) has played a crucial role in defining global benchmarks for the quality, safety and efficacy of herbal medicines. WHO Guidelines on Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) for Herbal Medicines and Guidelines on Good Agricultural and Collection Practice (GACP) for Medicinal Plants are basic references for governmental regulatory authorities around the world. In the GACP guidelines, emphasis is placed on the identification, cultivation and harvesting, and post-harvest procedures for medicinal plants to ensure purity, strength and reproducible quality of raw materials. On the other hand, GMP guidelines emphasize standard operating procedures, quality assurance, hygienic conditions, comprehensive documentation and validation procedures for consistency and safety of finished herbal products. Together, GACP and GMP form a quality assurance system to ensure the traceability and credibility of products from raw materials to the final dosage form.

WHO has developed a system for issuing the Certificate of a Pharmaceutical Product (CoPP) under the WHO Certification Scheme on the Quality of Pharmaceutical Products Moving in International Commerce. Manufacturers that comply with the WHO-GMP program are eligible for CoPPs, which confirm that a specific product is authorized for sale in the exporting country and is produced in compliance with GMP recognized by the WHO. This certification fosters confidence in the quality of the product and facilitates international marketing. India issues CoPP through CDSCO, which is mandatory for exports of herbal and pharmaceutical products. Countries such as Nepal require CoPP, based on WHO-GMP, mandatory for the importation of herbal and pharmaceutical products for manufacturing to global quality standards. Therefore, the use of WHO standards and CoPPs is important in framing the regulatory and quality assurance context for herbal medicines internationally [51].

Post-marketing surveillance and pharmacovigilance

Post-marketing surveillance is vital to guarantee the long-term safety of herbal medicines after they are made accessible to consumers. Unlike synthetic medicines, herbal products have multiple bioactive constituents, which makes the identification of the occurrence of adverse events more difficult. Each region has developed its own pharmacovigilance approach. In India, the Ministry of AYUSH has introduced a program titled Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI) for Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani and Homeopathy (ASU&H) medicines, and these adverse reactions are reported through the All India Institute of Ayurveda to ensure surveillance. China has established a well-organized post-marketing monitoring system to address adverse drug reactions monitoring related to Traditional Chinese Medicines (TCMs) through its National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), which collects and analyzes adverse drug reactions. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) and its Herbal Medicinal Products Committee (HMPC) mandate pharmacovigilance reporting for all approved herbal medicinal products. The US FDA collects adverse event reports, although there is a voluntary component, within its MedWatch system for dietary supplements and botanical drugs. Japan has strict surveillance to evaluate safety through the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), focused on Kampo formulations and single-herb preparations. Harmonizing international systems for pharmacovigilance is an ongoing challenge to ensure safety is monitored consistently and transparently [[52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58]].

Raw material sourcing and quality testing

The quality, effectiveness and safety of herbal medicines are largely depend on the sourcing and testing of herbal raw materials. Factors such as the geographical origin, season of harvest, drying and storage can greatly affect the phytochemical profiles of the herbal raw material. In India, the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API) and Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP) for medicinal plants ensure that the herbal raw material is authentic and pure. In China, the Chinese Pharmacopoeia has strict standards that include botanical identification, fingerprint chromatography and contaminants identification. In Europe, monographs are provided by the European Pharmacopoeia that define identity, purity and content criteria for herbal raw materials. The U.S. Pharmacopoeia (USP) has monographs for botanicals used as dietary supplements and as botanical drugs with an emphasis on microbial testing, heavy metals and pesticide testing. Japan follows the Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP) and the Japanese Standards for Herbal Medicines (JSHM) to ensure quality and standardization of herbal medicine ingredients used in Kampo medicine. Advanced analytical tools (e.g., HPLC, HPTLC, GC-MS, and DNA barcoding) are being applied more vigorously around the world to identify plant sources and species, to detect potential contamination and adulteration, to develop quality products and ensure consumer safety [[59], [60], [61]].

Intellectual property and traditional knowledge protection

The safeguarding of intellectual property (IP) and traditional knowledge (TK) with respect to herbal remedies is a major concern for policy-makers around the globe. India has taken the lead in protecting IP and TK by building the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), which catalogs and translates ancient Ayurvedic, Unani and Siddha texts into a format that patent examiners can access to prevent biopiracy. This effort has prevented a number of unreasonable patent applications concerning Indian herbs. China has formally integrated intellectual property rights regarding TCM into its State Intellectual Property Office (SIPO), allowing for innovation while also protecting traditional formulations. Most places in Europe and the USA rely on patenting based on novelty and inventive step. This creates challenges for licensing registration regarding traditional formulations. Provisions exist under World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) as well as the Nagoya Protocol, to share benefits based on genetic resources, not solely on traditional knowledge. Japan has recognized Kampo medicine as part of its cultural heritage, licensing opportunistic new formulations or compositions or new methods of extraction from herbal components, while not infringing on traditional patent laws. International cooperation and commitment to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) are essential for ensuring that traditional knowledge is acknowledged, respected in its application, and that the advantages of TK return to the communities from where it originates [[62], [63], [64], [65], [66]].

Application of AI in quality management of traditional and herbal medicines

The rapid adoption of digital technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain and big data analytics, is reshaping the regulatory landscape for herbal medicines. These advancements play a crucial role in enhancing traceability, ensuring quality and strengthening pharmacovigilance efforts across the globe. With these technologies, the industry can achieve more reliable tracking of herbal products, improve safety monitoring and ensure product integrity. This shift towards digitalization is not only streamlining regulatory processes but also fostering greater trust in herbal medicine worldwide.

Cultivation practices

The quality of herbal formulations often faces challenges such as adulteration, contamination and variability due to unsustainable cultivation practices. High-quality raw materials form the foundation for ensuring product efficacy and safety, yet many growers lack scientific knowledge and standardized cultivation techniques. AI-powered intelligent agriculture offers viable solutions by using sensor data and predictive modeling to determine optimal conditions, such as temperature, humidity and light, for various herbs. Machine learning aids in identifying patterns, optimizing cultivation techniques and ensuring the traceability of raw materials. Additionally, AI-driven databases enhance the management and monitoring of herbal resources, improving quality consistency and supply chain transparency [67].

Identification of quality markers

Ensuring the quality and safety of herbal formulations is a key challenge, particularly due to their complex composition. The concept of quality markers provides a scientific approach to standardize and control the quality of these formulations. Quality markers are bioactive compounds that reflect the efficacy and safety of herbal products. Machine learning and network pharmacology, enable the identification and prediction of these markers through advanced analytical techniques like chromatography and fingerprinting. These tools provide accurate and efficient quality assessment, facilitating the development of modern, reliable quality control standards for herbal formulations [67].

Processing

The processing of herbal formulations is a critical factor in determining their therapeutic efficacy. AI applications, including deep learning algorithms, have streamlined the identification and standardization of processing methods. AI-driven process control systems enable visualization, real-time monitoring and automation of complex procedures, ensuring consistent quality and optimal therapeutic outcomes. These innovations support standardized preparation methods, reducing variability and enhancing the efficacy of herbal formulations [67].

Quality, authenticity, and standardization of herbal products

Artificial Intelligence (AI) plays a critical role in ensuring the quality, authenticity and standardization of herbal products, addressing challenges like adulteration, contamination and variability. AI-powered spectral analysis, integrated with spectroscopic and chromatographic data, enable differentiation between genuine and adulterated products. Algorithms like Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) analyze unique spectral or chromatographic fingerprints, enhancing product authentication and quality assurance.

Emerging technologies such as CRISPR-Cas diagnostics with multiplex PCR, further enhance product safety by detecting genetic sequences linked to contaminants. These tools enable efficient screening of herbal products for authenticity, genetically modified organisms (GMOs) and pathogenic microorganisms, revolutionizing quality control. AI-driven systems analyzing chromatographic data from HPLC and GC-MS enable rapid identification of contaminated or substituted herbal products, supporting regulatory compliance. With the growing herbal products market, AI offers precise and efficient solutions for product authentication, ensuring consumer safety and confidence in the efficacy of herbal remedies [68].

Notable cases of herbal drug regulations

Ashwagandha

In the U.S., Ashwagandha root is commonly available as a dietary supplement. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) acknowledges its traditional use and indicates that short-term consumption is generally considered safe. The regulation for Ashwagandha in Europe varies across countries. Poland permits its root powder in supplements, restricting daily intake to 3 g and withanolides to under 10 mg. Denmark, however, banned Ashwagandha, citing potential hormonal effects and an unclear safe intake level. Germany has expressed safety concerns, particularly regarding thyroid and sex hormones, but permits its sale with case-by-case evaluations. Sweden acknowledges Denmark's risk assessment but allows local authorities to decide on its use. In India, Ashwagandha is widely used in Ayurvedic medicine. The Ministry of AYUSH regulates its use, ensuring it meets safety and efficacy standards. It is available as a dietary supplement, herbal medicine and Ayurvedic formulation. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) monitors its use in food products, ensuring compliance with safety guidelines. Standardized extracts of Ashwagandha are recommended to avoid contamination and ensure efficacy. Ashwagandha is not recognized under Japan's Foods with Nutrient Function Claims or Foods for Specified Health Uses categories, meaning health claims cannot be made. Import regulations allow a two-month personal supply, while larger quantities require compliance with local laws.

These differences highlight varying national approaches to balancing traditional use with modern safety concerns. Products containing ashwagandha require a more stringent registration process to be marketed legally in China [69].

Kava (Piper methysticum)

Kava (Piper methysticum) has faced global bans and reassessments due to concerns over hepatotoxicity. In 2002, multiple European countries, led by the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM), imposed bans following reports of liver damage, some cases resulting in liver transplants or fatalities. That same year, the U.S. FDA issued warnings about kava's potential hepatotoxic risks but stopped short of an outright ban, instead recommending caution and continued monitoring through post-market surveillance. WHO reviews concluded that traditionally consumed and properly prepared kava posed minimal risk to liver health. The WHO recommended lifting bans while implementing stringent quality control measures. By 2020, some European nations, including Germany and Switzerland, reassessed their stance and lifted restrictions, permitting kava under strict Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) guidelines to ensure product safety and quality [31].

Ephedra

Ephedra, an herb traditionally used in Chinese and Ayurvedic medicine for treating respiratory conditions, gained widespread popularity as a weight loss and energy-boosting supplement. However, increasing concerns over its cardiovascular risks prompted significant regulatory interventions worldwide. In 2004, the U.S. FDA banned ephedra-containing supplements following multiple reports of heart attacks, strokes and fatalities linked to its stimulant effects. EU nations followed with national bans. Despite these prohibitions, China and India continue to permit controlled medicinal use of ephedra in Traditional Chinese Medicine and Ayurveda, strictly limiting it to prescribed doses and prohibiting its inclusion in dietary supplements. In Japan, ephedra is designated as a prescription-only drug, significantly restricting its accessibility [70].

Herbal medicines, like all treatments, should be judged by both their benefits and risks. A risk-benefit analysis examines safety, dosage, side effects and effectiveness to determine whether a product is worth using. The cases of Ephedra, Kava, and Ashwagandha illustrate the variation in regulations. Ephedra was banned due to serious heart risks, while Kava was initially restricted but later allowed under controlled use. Ashwagandha, despite strong evidence of its safety and benefits, has faced bans based on selective risk assessments that ignore its medicinal value. Instead of outright bans, regulators should set clear dosage guidelines, ensure quality control, and educate consumers. The focus should be on safe, informed use, allowing people to benefit from herbal medicines while managing potential risks.

Discussion

The role of regulatory bodies in herbal medicine is crucial for ensuring the safety, quality and effectiveness of these products. With the growing popularity and global demand for natural therapies, the control of these regulatory bodies is critical for ensuring product qualities and protecting consumer health. Regulatory agencies establish criteria and procedures that herbal medicine manufacturers must follow [71]. These agencies assist in identifying possible dangers and ensuring that only safe items access the market by performing thorough reviews and assessments. This protects customers from potentially harmful impacts and health risks. Herbal medication quality can vary greatly depending on factors such as sourcing, processing and manufacturing techniques. To maintain consistent product quality, regulatory bodies adopt GMP and quality control procedures. Many regulatory agencies make decisions based on scientific evidence and clinical data. This evidence-based approach contributes to the efficacy of herbal medicines and also increases consumer trust in the products [72]. Herbal medicines are frequently comprised of complicated formulas and natural substances that vary in strength and composition. Regulatory agencies seek to standardize herbal products, assuring consistency in each dose. These agencies prohibit false or misleading claims and safeguard customers from deceptive marketing tactics by regulating the herbal medicine sector. This contributes to the industry's integrity and fosters confidence between producers and customers. Regulatory authorities attempt to maintain traditional knowledge while incorporating contemporary scientific data in places where herbal medicine is deeply rooted in cultural practices. This helps in the preservation of the cultural legacy related to herbal treatments. Even after herbal medicines are on the market, regulatory agencies continue to monitor them. Post-market surveillance aids in the detection of new safety concerns or adverse responses, enabling quick action to protect public health [73].

A major distinction across regions lies in the balance between traditional knowledge and evidence-based practices. India and China have rich and old herbal medicinal traditions that are strongly ingrained in their civilizations. Herbal regulations are overseen by India's Ministry of AYUSH and China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), which depend largely on Ayurvedic and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) systems, respectively [74]. Kampo's medical principles in Japan originated from ancient Chinese medicine. The PMDA in Japan is tasked with ensuring the safety, effectiveness, and quality of pharmaceuticals, including Kampo as well as non-Kampo herbal medicines. These authorities acknowledge the historical usage of specific plants and emphasize traditional knowledge in product evaluation. This method may generate public health concerns regarding the absence of modern scientific evidence for some natural medications. While traditional knowledge can provide useful insights, it must be combined with modern scientific research to ensure consumer safety and worldwide trust in these therapies. Collaboration between traditional practitioners and scientific researchers may help to overcome this gap and develop evidence-based herbal therapy guidelines [75].

Unlike China and India, Europe and the US rely on evidence-based assessments for regulating herbal medicines. Both the FDA (US) and the EMA (Europe) require the manufacturers to submit scientific evidence proving the safety and efficacy of herbal medicines. From the aspect of public health, this emphasis on evidence-based assessments provides a more solid platform for ensuring the quality and safety of herbal medicines [76]. Regardless of the regulatory approach, manufacturing quality herbal products remains a central public health concern across all regions. The GMP rules have been established in China and Europe for herbal medicine production, to ensure consistent manufacturing methods and quality assurance [77]. The GMP is also recognized in India for the manufacturing of some herbal products. The PMDA in Japan and the FDA in the US enforce quality control methods to ensure that herbal products meet GMP requirements. GMP implementation is critical from a public health perspective in reducing the risk of contamination, adulteration and uneven potency in herbal medicines. These principles enhance product dependability and lowers the chance of unpleasant reactions. Disparities in GMP compliance between locations, on the other hand, may result in differences in product quality, necessitating worldwide harmonization to maintain uniform standards.

Beyond manufacturing standards, regulatory systems also differ substantially in their approach to pre-market approval and post-market surveillance. Herbal medicines are categorized as dietary supplements in the United States, and producers do not need FDA pre-market approval. Instead, post-market monitoring is used to check product safety and quality. In contrast, India, China, and Europe often need pre-market approval before herbal medications can be marketed. From the perspective of public health, the lack of pre-market approval in the United States raises concerns about potentially dangerous herbal products reaching consumers [78]. While post-market monitoring can detect unfavourable effects, the pre-market review is critical in preventing dangerous products from entering the market in the first place. Harmonizing legislation to incorporate rigorous pre-market evaluations while retaining efficient post-market surveillance might improve consumer protection worldwide. In addition to approval processes, standardization and labelling play a crucial role in ensuring consumer safety and informed use.

Standardization of herbal medicines is critical for ensuring consistent quality and efficacy. Europe has introduced the Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive (THMPD), which requires herbal components to comply with approved monographs. Similarly, India and China have national pharmacopoeias that provide rules for standardization [79]. Standardized herbal medicines, from the perspective of public health, give a better guarantee of product quality and safety. Labelling that is clear and accurate is especially important in alerting customers about the composition, dosage and potential hazards of herbal products. Uniform labelling standards can equip consumers with the knowledge they need to make educated decisions and prevent potential damage. Regional regulatory discrepancies can influence the worldwide commerce of herbal medicines [23].

Canada's system strikes a great balance like Europe's EMA or Japan's Kampo with mandatory licensing, but more flexible than India's traditional exemptions or the U.S.'s post-market DSHEA approach. Backed by 70,000+ products, TCM/Ayurvedic monographs, NHPID, and GMP, it tackles contamination better than fragmented African setups. Under-reporting of adverse events remains a hurdle, but NNHPD's shift to oversee all OTC drugs enchances regulatory efficiency and supports the development for global standards that protect consumers without compromizing the traditional knowledge. The Middle East's pharmacy-centric, registration-focused model bridges Canada's pre-market rigor and India's traditional flexibility, effectively limiting adulteration via Halal/GMP mandates, unlike fragmented African systems, though harmonization across Gulf states lags behind Europe's EMA framework. Oman's herbovigilance aligns with global pharmacovigilance trends, enhancing safety for traditional TAHM while supporting market growth through regulated distribution.

Australia's two-tier system (listed for low-risk, registered for high-risk) smartly combines Canada's strict pre-checks with U.S. after-market monitoring. It ensures GMP-compliant quality for herbal medicines and allows traditional use claims, while high-risk products undergo full Europe-style reviews, unlike India's exemptions. ARTG supervision and recalls keep safety high while speeding market access. Africa's uneven registration system falls short of Australia's two-level ARTG checks or Canada's strict NNHPD reviews. It mixes U.S.-like after-sales monitoring with India's easy exemptions but skips Europe's strong proof requirements. Major countries support GMP and traditional claims despite fake products and contamination risks, but inconsistent enforcement undermines safety, unlike Australia's approved ingredient list. The WHO advocates for better coordination to enhave safety and consumer confidence.

These regional differences become even more pronounced when considering supply-chain complexity and emerging public health concerns. For example, India treats traditional and proprietary herbal medicines differently, with clinical trials mainly required for modern phytopharmaceuticals. China requires strict testing for new herbal formulations, but gives some exceptions to classical traditional recipes. Europe insists on solid scientific evidence for new products but allows simpler approval for herbal medicines used for decades. The US has strict rules for herbal medicines classified as drugs, but it is more lenient with dietary supplements. Japan focuses on traditional Kampo medicines, requiring strong proof of safety and effectiveness.

When it comes to supply chains, India is still improving transparency and quality controls. China, Europe and Japan have strong systems to ensure the quality and safety of herbal products through strict manufacturing standards. The US has challenges due to the large and complex dietary supplement market. India is improving safety monitoring and voluntary trial guidelines, while China, Europe and Japan require safety data and closely monitor for adverse effects. The US emphasizes monitoring supplements once they are on the market. Understanding these differences helps to understand the challenges of regulating herbal medicines globally. It also highlights opportunities to work together to improve quality, safety and access to effective herbal therapies worldwide.

In addition to regulatory oversight, clinical research has become increasingly important in validating herbal medicines. Globally, the landscape of clinical trials on herbal medicines has expanded significantly in recent years, reflecting increasing interest in validating the safety and efficacy of traditional therapies. According to Koonrungsesomboon et al. (2024), a descriptive analysis of herbal medicine clinical trials identified over 1500 studies conducted between 2019 and 2022, with the majority employing randomized, double-blind, parallel designs and using capsules as the most common dosage form. These trials were predominantly conducted in Asian countries, highlighting regional expertise and interest in herbal therapies [80].

Despite this growth, several methodological and regulatory challenges continue to limit the strength of clinical evidence. The integration of herbal medicines into modern clinical practice faces significant challenges due to a lack of standardized clinical trial protocols. Ensuring batch-to-batch consistency of active constituents is difficult because of variations in plant material and preparation methods, while creating suitable placebos that match the color, taste and smell of herbal drugs complicates the blinding process. Furthermore, inconsistent and often fragmented regulatory frameworks across countries hinder the approval and execution of clinical trials, leaving insufficient high-quality clinical data to substantiate the efficacy and safety of herbal products. To overcome these challenges, adherence to standardized clinical protocols, implementation of stringent quality control measures and harmonization of international regulatory standards are essential. Such measures will enhance the reliability of clinical evidence, facilitate global acceptance, and support the safe and effective integration of herbal medicines into modern healthcare systems [81].

Leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) can help by enhancing post-market surveillance, predicting herb-drug interactions and ensuring supply chain transparency through blockchain-based tracking. AI-driven pharmacovigilance can detect adverse events more efficiently, strengthening consumer protection. A centralized regulatory framework incorporating AI and big data analytics could accelerate approvals while preserving traditional knowledge. A structured approach, where well-documented traditional remedies require less regulatory scrutiny than new formulations, would balance safety with accessibility. Standardizing regulations through global collaboration will be key to fostering trust, ensuring quality and facilitating international trade in herbal medicines. Harmonizing international rules will allow the free movement of herbal medicines across borders while ensuring that goods meet stringent quality standards. Collaboration among regulatory bodies facilitates information sharing and reciprocal acceptance of authorized herbal products, enhancing global confidence in traditional medicines [82]. Finally, long-term regulatory success depends on strong post-marketing surveillance, quality raw materials and protection of traditional knowledge.

Robust post-marketing surveillance, standardized sourcing of raw plant materials and protection of traditional knowledge all influence the proper use of herbal medicines based on their quality, safety and efficacy. Globally, pharmacovigilance systems have differences based on cultural and healthcare systems considerations; India and the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI) investigates the safety of ASU drugs; China engages the National Medical Product Administration (NMPA) to ensure safety in TCM; in Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) requires reporting for herbal products; the USA also collects voluntary MedWatch reports to monitor herbs; and Japan monitors the safety of Kampo and single-herb preparations through the PMDA. The quality of raw materials is governed by both a set of country-specific standards, like India's Ayurveda, Siddha, and Unani medicine (ASU) guidelines and Good Agricultural Practices (GACP) for plant materials, the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2015), the European Pharmacopoeia (2011), the United States Pharmacopoeia (USP), and Japan's Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP) and Japanese Society of Herbs and Medicine (JSHM) standards, and pharmaceutical manufacturing methods, such as chemical analysis with HPLC, HPTLC, GC-MS, and DNA barcoding. The protection of traditional knowledge and traditional intellectual property is ensured through mechanisms such as Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) in India, the State Intellectual Property Office (SIPO) in China, and multinational international regulations and agreements, including the WIPO agreements and the Nagoya Protocol. The integration and harmonization among all of these systems are critical for ensuring the quality and safety of herbal medicines and equitable benefit sharing.

Challenges, solutions and recommendations for herbal drug regulations

Several contemporary challenges complicate the global regulation of herbal medicines. Widespread adulteration with undeclared pharmaceutical substances such as corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors and weight-loss agents poses serious health risks and undermines consumer trust. In parallel, the rapid growth of online sales, cross-border e-commerce and social-media-based marketing allows many products to bypass national regulatory controls, quality testing and labelling requirements, creating enforcement challenges. Furthermore, the regulation of complex herbal mixtures presents scientific challenges, such as multiple bioactive constituents, potential synergistic effects and batch-to-batch variability, are not adequately addressed by conventional regulatory frameworks.

In recent years, the increasing global demand for herbal drugs and formulations has highlighted the need for harmonized regulatory frameworks to ensure safety, efficacy and quality while facilitating international trade. To establish a globally unified framework for herbal medicine regulation, it is essential to tackle key challenges through collaborative and strategic approaches.

Conclusion

The regulatory systems for herbal medicines in India, China, Europe, the United States and Japan are varied and complicated, undermining the establishment of uniform safety, quality and efficacy standards. Europe and Japan adopt rigorous pre-market approval of herbal medicines based on evidence from the scientific literature, while the United States focuses on post-market surveillance of herbal medicine to monitor safety and quality. India and China combine traditional knowledge of herbal medicine with modern regulatory frameworks. There is an urgent need for global regulatory harmonization to encourage the safe and effective use of herbal medicines while maintaining respect for the preservation of traditional medical knowledge. A globally coordinated regulatory framework would aim to harmonize and establish recognized standards for Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP), evidence-based validation processes and a single pharmacopoeial standard. Such alignment would enhance product consistency, increase consumer confidence, and promote international trade of herbal products.

Beyond the five primary regions examined, regulatory approaches in Canada, Australia, the Middle East and Africa reflect different levels of regulatory development. Canada and Australia have adopted risk-based, pre-market licensing systems that effectively balance traditional use with scientific evaluation. In the Middle East, herbal medicine regulation is becoming increasingly structured through centralized registration processes, enforcement of Good Manufacturing Practices and the integration of Halal certification. In contrast, many African countries, despite widespread reliance on traditional medicine, continue to face challenges related to fragmented regulatory frameworks and limited resources, underscoring the need for stronger capacity building, improved pharmacovigilance and greater regional harmonization.

The global harmonization can be facilitated by formulating a Global Herbal Medicine Regulatory Consortium that allows existing regulatory agencies, researchers and traditional medicine practitioners to work together. A tiered approval system that balances well-documented traditional use with the need for clinical evaluation of new formulations would be better suited to address the variability of herbal products. Artificial Intelligence can be employed to strengthen safety monitoring, predict herb-drug interactions and potentially provide transparency in the supply chain through blockchain technology to support regulatory supervision. Additionally, an appropriate approach should address intellectual property rights and protect traditional knowledge for equitable benefit sharing if herbal medicines are to be a trustworthy part of global healthcare. Education for the public and healthcare providers is an important part of responsible use of herbal medicines and increase awareness of their risks and benefits. Thus, creating a regulatory environment that merges wisdom from tradition and scientific rigour on global scale will require collaboration with both international inter-governmental agencies and civil society in order to ensure robust, transparent and inclusive framework for the presence of herbal products in healthcare globally.
