Single-Stage Repair of Complex Congenital Aortic Arch Pathology With Dissection and Aneurysm in an Adult
Authors: Nouman Ahmad, Xuliang Chen, Yingjie Huang, Jiawei Li, Yaowei Wang, Qian Zhang, Lingjin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplex congenital aortic arch anomalies in adults are rare and may present abruptly when complicated by dissection or aneurysm, posing significant surgical challenges.Case SummaryA 42-year-old man presented with sudden-onset chest and back pain. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated aortic coarctation, right aortic arch with an aberrant left subclavian artery arising from Kommerell diverticulum, and associated arch and descending thoracic aortic aneurysm with dissection. Because of the absence of suitable landing zones for endovascular repair, a single-stage anatomical repair via median sternotomy was performed, including hemiarch reconstruction, proximal descending thoracic aortic replacement, and bilateral subclavian artery reconstruction. Postoperative recovery was complicated by bleeding requiring re-exploration and pulmonary infection, both successfully managed.DiscussionSimultaneous management of multiple congenital arch anomalies with dissection is rarely reported and highlights the feasibility of single-stage repair.Take-Home MessageSingle-stage repair can provide definitive treatment in carefully selected patients with complex anatomy.Key words: aberrant left subclavian artery, aortic dissection, coarctation of the aorta, Kommerell diverticulum, right aortic arch, single stage open repair

History of Presentation

A 42-year-old man presented with sudden-onset chest and back pain. He denied dysphagia, dyspnea, or other respiratory symptoms. On physical examination, vital signs were stable, and no significant abnormalities were noted. Cardiac examination revealed no murmurs or signs of heart failure.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of diabetes mellitus and stable schizophrenia. There was no previous diagnosis of cardiovascular disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the abrupt presentation of chest and back pain, the differential diagnosis included acute aortic syndrome (aortic dissection or intramural hematoma), thoracic aortic aneurysm rupture, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, and esophageal pathology.

Investigations

Electrocardiography showed a normal sinus rhythm without St-T wave abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function without valvular abnormalities or pericardial effusion.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography revealed complex congenital and acquired aortic pathology (Figure 1, Video 1). Findings included coarctation of the aorta distal to the right common carotid artery (diameter 13.9 mm), a right aortic arch with an aberrant left subclavian artery arising from a Kommerell diverticulum (diameter 28.2 mm), aortic dissection involving the aortic arch, and aneurysmal dilation of the aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta.

The descending thoracic aorta demonstrated an S-shaped configuration, with a left-sided proximal segment and right-sided distal segment. The first arch branch was the left common carotid artery, followed by the right common carotid artery anteriorly. The right subclavian artery originated from a dilated segment of the arch, whereas the aberrant left subclavian artery arose from the Kommerell diverticulum. Compression of the esophagus and trachea was also observed.

Management (Operative Technique)

A single-stage surgical repair via median sternotomy was planned to address all pathological components. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established using ascending aortic, right femoral arterial, and bicaval venous cannulation to maintain continuous perfusion of the heart, upper body, and distal organs. Systemic cooling was performed to 25 °C.

The descending thoracic aorta was exposed through the right pleural cavity and replaced with a 24-mm single-branched Dacron graft (Maquet vascular graft) routed beneath the right lung hilum. The aortic arch was reconstructed distal to the right common carotid artery, encompassing the coarctation, dissection, aneurysm, and Kommerell diverticulum.

The right subclavian artery was reimplanted into the graft branch, and the left subclavian artery was reconstructed using an 8-mm conduit anastomosed to the ascending aorta. The Kommerell diverticulum and aneurysmal tissue were excised. After rewarming, the patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass. Key procedural steps are shown in Video 2.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The immediate postoperative course was initially stable. However, 12 hours after transfer to the intensive care unit, increased mediastinal drainage prompted surgical re-exploration, during which hemostasis was achieved.

The patient subsequently developed a pulmonary infection requiring tracheostomy and antibiotic therapy. After appropriate treatment and supportive care, the patient recovered well and was discharged.

At 3-month follow-up, computed tomography angiography demonstrated complete exclusion of the aortic pathology and satisfactory anatomical reconstruction (Figure 2).

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare and highly complex combination of congenital aortic arch anomalies—coarctation of the aorta and right aortic arch with an aberrant left subclavian artery arising from a Kommerell diverticulum—complicated by aortic dissection and aneurysmal degeneration.

From an embryologic perspective, this rare anatomical configuration may result from abnormal regression and persistence of components of the primitive aortic arch system. A right aortic arch with an aberrant left subclavian artery typically arises from regression of the left fourth arch proximal to the left subclavian artery, with persistence of the right fourth arch and right dorsal aorta. The Kommerell diverticulum represents a remnant of the left dorsal aorta. The coexistence of aortic coarctation further suggests abnormal development of the aortic arch and ductal tissue, contributing to this complex anatomy.1 Such developmental variations may explain the unusual combination observed in this patient.

In this case, mild tracheal compression was observed on preoperative computed tomography imaging; however, the patient remained asymptomatic, without respiratory distress, stridor, or recurrent pulmonary infections. Bronchoscopy was not performed because of the absence of clinical indications and the invasive nature of the procedure. Although chronic airway compression may predispose to tracheomalacia, computed tomography imaging demonstrated preserved airway patency. Similarly, mild esophageal compression was noted without dysphagia or aspiration symptoms, and additional evaluation, such as esophagogastroduodenoscopy or esophagography, was not pursued. Furthermore, invasive airway or esophageal evaluation was approached cautiously, as these procedures may provoke vagal reflexes and hemodynamic instability in patients with complex aortic pathology. Careful perioperative airway assessment remains important in such cases, particularly when long-standing compression is present.

Current management options for the Kommerell diverticulum and associated arch anomalies include open surgical repair, hybrid procedures, and thoracic endovascular aortic repair, depending on anatomical characteristics and patient-specific factors. Contemporary practice generally favors endovascular or hybrid strategies when adequate proximal and distal landing zones are available.1,2

In this patient, the coexistence of coarctation, aneurysmal Kommerell diverticulum, arch dissection, and complex supra-aortic branching anatomy precluded safe endovascular treatment due to the absence of suitable landing zones. In addition, staged procedures were considered suboptimal because of the risk of residual pathology and interval complications.

A single-stage open repair via median sternotomy allowed comprehensive exposure of the aortic arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta, facilitating complete excision of diseased segments and anatomical reconstruction of the supra-aortic vessels. Although technically demanding, this strategy enabled definitive correction of all pathological components in a single procedure.

The need for postoperative re-exploration for bleeding and pulmonary infection highlights the complexity and potential morbidity associated with extensive open aortic surgery. Therefore, this approach should be reserved for carefully selected patients and performed in centers with expertise in complex aortic reconstruction.

Conclusions

In selected patients with complex congenital and acquired aortic arch pathology, single-stage anatomical repair through median sternotomy can provide definitive treatment when endovascular or staged strategies are limited by anatomical constraints.
