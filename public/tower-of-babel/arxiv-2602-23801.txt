Abstract
. Finding spanning structures with many distinct colours in properly edge-coloured graphs is a central theme in extremal combinatorics. A classical result of Andersen shows that every proper edge-colouring of the complete graph K n K_{n} contains a Hamilton cycle with n − O ⁡ ( n 1 / 2 ) n-O(n^{1/2}) distinct colours. In the bipartite setting, the analogous question for perfect matchings is closely related to permutations in Latin squares. In this paper, we investigate how a Dirac-type minimum degree condition forces colour diversity in spanning structures. For every constant 1 / 2 < c ⩽ 1 1/2<c\leqslant 1 , we prove the following. • Every properly edge-coloured graph G G on n n vertices with δ ⁡ ( G ) ⩾ c ​ n \delta(G)\geqslant cn contains a Hamilton cycle with at least c ​ n − O ⁡ ( n 1 / 2 ) cn-O(n^{1/2}) distinct colours. • Every subset of an n × n n\times n Latin square with at least c ​ n cn cells in each row and each column contains a permutation with at least c ​ n − O ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ) cn-O(n^{2/3}) distinct symbols. Both bounds are best possible up to the error term.



1.
Introduction

Given a properly edge-coloured graph, a natural and long-studied problem is to find a spanning structure whose edges all receive different colours, commonly referred to as a
rainbow
structure. More generally, when a fully rainbow structure does not exist, one seeks spanning structures that maximise the number of distinct colours they contain. In this paper we investigate how minimum degree conditions force colour diversity in spanning structures. This line of research has been particularly active for Hamilton cycles in graphs and perfect matchings in bipartite graphs, two central spanning structures in extremal combinatorics.

1.1.
Hamilton cycles in graphs

In 1980, Hahn
[
Hah80
]
conjectured that every properly edge-coloured

K_{n}
with
n\geqslant 5
contains a rainbow Hamilton path.
This conjecture was disproved by Maamoun and Meyniel
[
MM84
]
, who
constructed infinitely many counterexamples.
This shifted attention to the weaker but more robust question of how many
distinct colours can be guaranteed on a Hamilton cycle. A foundational result in this direction is due to Andersen
[
And89
]
, who proved that properly edge-coloured complete graphs contain
almost rainbow Hamilton cycles.

Theorem 1.1

(Andersen)
.

For all sufficiently large
n
, every properly edge-coloured
K_{n}

contains a Hamilton cycle with at least
n-\sqrt{2n}
distinct colours.

Balogh and Molla
[
BM19
]
later improved the error term, proving that every properly edge-coloured
K_{n}
contains a Hamilton cycle with at least
n-O(\log^{2}n)
distinct colours.

More recently, rainbow Hamiltonicity has been investigated in general
graphs.
Coulson and Perarnau
[
CP20
]
established a rainbow Dirac theorem under
a global boundedness condition on colour classes.
Peng and the fifth author
[
PY24
]
subsequently studied proper
edge-colourings and showed that if each colour appears on at most
n/8

edges, then every Dirac graph contains a Hamilton cycle with
n-o(n)

distinct colours.
Without any boundedness assumption, they obtained a universal bound of

n/4-o(n)
colours.

These results highlight a difficulty: unlike the complete
graph, a properly edge-coloured Dirac graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
may use only
cn+O(1)
colours. In particular, even the total number of colours available may be far below
n
, so a rainbow Hamilton cycle cannot in general be expected.
As a result, techniques that rely on an abundance of colours in
K_{n}
do
not directly extend to the min-degree setting. Motivated by this difficulty, the following conjecture was proposed in
[
PY24
]
.

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
PY24
]
)
.

Every proper edge-colouring of an
n
-vertex graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant n/2
contains a Hamilton cycle with at least
n/2-o(n)
distinct colours.

We verify this conjecture for all graphs with min-degree
cn
with
c>1/2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1/2<c\leqslant 1
and let
n
be sufficiently large.
If
G
is an
n
-vertex graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
, then every properly
edge-coloured
G
contains a Hamilton cycle with at least

cn-O(\sqrt{n})
distinct colours.

This bound is asymptotically optimal up to the
O(\sqrt{n})
term. Our result may also be viewed from the perspective of
resilience
.
In general, resilience-type problems ask to what extent a global graph property
persists under local perturbations, and they have been studied extensively in
extremal and random graph theory.
From this viewpoint, Andersen’s theorem treats the extremal case of the complete
graph, while Theorem
1.3
shows that the “nearly all available colours appear on a Hamilton cycle” phenomenon is stable under arbitrary edge deletions that preserve the Dirac condition.

The core technical contribution is a new structural result for properly edge-coloured Dirac graphs: the existence of a
spanning linear forest with few components that already uses a
near-maximal number of colours.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
1/2<c\leqslant 1
and let
n
be sufficiently large.
Every properly edge-coloured
n
-vertex graph
G
with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn

contains a spanning linear forest with at most
\sqrt{n}
components and at
least
cn-4\sqrt{n}
distinct colours.

Our construction builds on the classical backtracking algorithm of
Andersen
[
And89
]
and Alon, Pokrovskiy, and Sudakov
[
APS17
]
, but new ideas
are needed in the Dirac setting to compensate for the potential scarcity of
colours.
The key new ingredient is a rigidity phenomenon for maximal colourful
linear forests. Roughly speaking, once such a forest cannot be extended while preserving
its colour set, many of its edges acquire a form of global uniqueness:
their colours cannot be replicated elsewhere without destroying maximality.
This structural constraint allows us to iteratively expose many new
vertices and colours, leading to a linear growth of the set of reachable
colours.
This rigidity mechanism appears to be specific to the Dirac setting and may be of independent interest.

It is tempting to combine absorption with recent results on long rainbow paths.
For example, Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy, and Yepremyan
[
BFM+25
]

showed that every properly edge-coloured graph
G
with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
contains a rainbow
path of length
cn-o(n)
. However, long colourful paths (or linear forests) need not be compatible with Hamilton
cycles: even in Dirac graphs there exist linear forests with
cn
edges such that no
Hamilton cycle contains more than a
(2c-1)
-fraction of their edges, as the following proposition shows. This obstruction explains why one must carefully control the global structure of the forest rather than merely maximize its size.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
1/2<c<1
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large. There exists a graph
G
on
n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
and a linear forest
F
in
G
with
cn
edges such that every Hamilton cycle in
G
contains at most
(2c-1)n
edges of
F
.

1.2.
Perfect matchings in bipartite graphs

Another well-studied direction concerns perfect matchings in properly edge-coloured
K_{n,n}
.
This problem is equivalent to a classical question concerning permutations and transversals in Latin squares.
An
n\times n
Latin square is an
n\times n
array filled with
n
symbols such that each symbol appears exactly once in every row and every column.
A permutation in a Latin square is a selection of
n
cells such that no two cells share the same row or column.
If, in addition, the symbols appearing in these cells are all distinct, then such a permutation is called a transversal.
The existence of transversals in Latin squares was highlighted by a conjecture of Ryser
[
Rys67
]
, which asserts that every Latin square of odd order contains a transversal.
Subsequently, Brualdi and Stein
[
BR+91
,
Ste75
]
formulated a stronger conjecture, proposing that every Latin square of order
n
admits a partial transversal of size at least
n-1
.

A sequence of works has established the existence of large partial transversals in Latin squares.
Brouwer, A. de Vries, and Wieringa
[
BdVW78
]
proved that every Latin square of order
n
contains a partial transversal of size
n-O(\sqrt{n})
.
Hatami and Shor
[
HS08
]
improved this to
n-O(\log^{2}n)
.
Keevash, Pokrovskiy, Sudakov, and Yepremyan
[
KPSY22
]
reduced the error term to
n-O(\log n/\log\log n)
.
Very recently, Montgomery
[
Mon23
]
proved the existence of transversals of size
n-1
for every sufficiently large
n
.

Our next result finds almost maximally colourful perfect matchings in bipartite graphs, which may be viewed as a natural min-degree analogue of the Ryser–Brualdi–Stein problem.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
1/2<c\leqslant 1
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large. Every properly edge-coloured balanced bipartite graph
G
on
2n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
contains a perfect matching with at least

cn-O(n^{2/3})
distinct colours.

Equivalently,
Theorem

1.6
reads as follows. Every subset of an
n\times n
Latin square with at least
cn
cells in each row and column contains a permutation with at least
cn-O(n^{2/3})
distinct symbols.

Our proof of Theorem
1.6
is motivated by the work of Gyárfás and Sárközy
[
GS14
]
, who proved that every properly edge-coloured graph
G
with
\delta(G)\leqslant n/2
contains a rainbow matching of size
\delta(G)-o(n)
.
However, a matching of size
\delta(G)-o(n)
is not necessarily extendable to a perfect matching, and there does not always exist a perfect matching that contains all but
o(n)
edges of such a matching, as shown below.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
1/2<c<1
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large. There exists a balanced bipartite graph
G
on
2n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
and a matching
M\subset G
with
cn
edges such that every perfect matching in
G
contains at most
(2c-1)n
edges of
M
.

2.
Colourful Hamilton cycles in Dirac graphs

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. We first prove the existence of a spanning linear forest in a properly edge-coloured Dirac graph that uses almost as many distinct colours as possible (see Proposition
2.1
), which directly implies
Theorem
1.4
. Then combining with absorption method for Hamilton cycles, we prove Theorem

2.1.
Colourful path-forests

In this subsection we prove the key structural proposition for path-forest, as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
1/2<c\leqslant 1
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
the following holds. Let
\delta=\delta(n)
and
t=t(n)
satisfy
2c<t\delta<\delta^{2}n/2
. If
G
is a graph on
n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
and
\chi
is a proper edge-colouring of
G
, then
G
contains a spanning linear forest
F
consisting of
t
vertex-disjoint paths and containing at least
(c-\delta)n
distinct colours.

Proposition
2.1
directly implies Theorem
1.4
, by choosing
t=n^{1/2}
and
\delta=4n^{-1/2}
.

The proof proceeds by contradiction. Consider a spanning linear forest with
t
components that maximises the number of distinct colours. Suppose that this forest uses less than
(c-\delta)n
distinct colours, we study how unused colours can be propagated into the forest via its path endpoints. This leads to an iterative expansion of a family of colour sets
C_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq C_{t}
, where at each step new colours are “reached” through coloured neighbourhoods of an endpoint. The key observation is that maximality forces a strong rigidity: each such reachable vertex contributes a globally unique colour among the forest edges, since any collision would allow a backtracking modification that strictly increases the number of colours. Using the minimum degree condition and properness of the colouring, this forces the size of reachable colour sets to grow linearly, ultimately contradicting the maximality of the forest.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
where
1/2<c\leqslant 1
, equipped with a proper
edge-colouring
\chi
.
Fix an integer

By Dirac’s theorem,

contains a Hamilton cycle, and hence
\mathcal{F}\neq\varnothing
for all
t\leqslant n/2
. Choose a spanning linear forest

that maximises the number of distinct colours appearing on its edges. Suppose that
F_{0}
uses fewer than
(c-\delta)n
distinct colours.

Write
F_{0}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}P_{i}
, where

P_{i}=v_{1}^{(i)}v_{2}^{(i)}\cdots v_{m_{i}}^{(i)}

are vertex-disjoint paths. For a vertex

1\leqslant i\leqslant t

to its predecessor on the path.
We refer to
v_{j-1}^{(i)}
as the

and write

.

Let

C_{0}
be the set of colours that do not appear in

.
We now define an increasing sequence of colour sets

For each

, let

(1)

C_{i}:=\Big\{f(x)\;:\;x\in N_{C_{i-1}}\!\big(v_{1}^{(i)}\big)\setminus\{v_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{1}^{(t)}\}\Big\}\;\cup\;C_{i-1},

.

The key observation is the following lemma. Whenever a vertex

. This rigidity is the core mechanism that drives the growth of the colour sets

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

and
C_{i}
,

Suppose that the number of colours in

Then for every

.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on

.
The idea is that any colour collision of the type described in the

.

Since the colours of edges incident to

\displaystyle|N_{C_{0}}(v_{1}^{(i)})\setminus\{v_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{1}^{(t)}\}|\geqslant cn-(c-\delta)n-t\geqslant\frac{\delta}{2}n,

which implies that

, hence introduces a new colour.
Removing

Thus

Suppose for contradiction that there exist vertices

\chi(v_{1}^{(i)}x)\in C_{i-1}\setminus C_{0}

\chi(v_{1}^{(i)}x)=f(x_{1})

where

.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this subsection we complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
by turning the
colourful linear forest obtained in Proposition
2.1
into a
Hamilton cycle.
Our argument follows the standard
absorption method
introduced
in
[
RRS06
]
. The strategy is as follows.
First, we construct a short
absorbing path

A
with the property that any
small set of vertices can later be incorporated into
A
while preserving its
endpoints.
Second, we choose a small
reservoir
set
R
that allows us to connect many
vertex-disjoint paths.
After removing
A\cup R
, we apply Proposition
2.1
to the
remaining graph to obtain a spanning linear forest with many colours.
Finally, we use vertices from
R
to merge all paths into a single cycle and then
absorb the remaining vertices via
A
.
Throughout this process, the number of distinct colours on the colourful forest is preserved.

We begin with the absorbing lemma, which is Lemma 3.1 from
[
PY24
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(Absorber for Hamilton cycles)
.

For every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
and
0<d<2^{-10}\varepsilon
, there exist constants

C=C(d,\varepsilon)
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(d,\varepsilon)
such that the following holds
for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
and all integers

with
(\log n)^{2}\leqslant m\leqslant n/C
.
If
G
is an
n
-vertex graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant(1/2+\varepsilon)n
, then there
exists a path
A\subset G
with

|V(A)|\leqslant Cm

such that for every set
U\subset V(G)\setminus V(A)
with
|U|\leqslant dm
, there
exists a path
A_{U}\subset G
with the same endpoints as
A
and

V(A_{U})=V(A)\cup U
.

Next we state the reservoir lemma, which will be used to connect the components
of a linear forest, which is Lemma 3.3 from
[
PY24
]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Reservoir for Hamilton cycles)
.

For every
0<d,\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
and
C>1
, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(d,\varepsilon,C)

such that the following holds for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
and all integers

(\log n)^{2}\leqslant m\leqslant\varepsilon n/(2d)
.
If
G
is an
n
-vertex graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant(1/2+\varepsilon)n
and

W\subset V(G)
satisfies
|W|\leqslant Cm
, then there exists a set

R\subset V(G)\setminus W
with
|R|=dm
such that

|N(x)\cap N(y)\cap R|\geqslant\varepsilon|R|

for every pair of vertices
x,y\in V(G)
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
, equipped with a proper
edge-colouring
\chi
.
Set

\varepsilon=(c-1/2)/2,\qquad d=2^{-11}\varepsilon,\qquad m=2^{12}\varepsilon^{-2}n^{1/2}.

Applying Lemma
2.3
to
G
, we obtain an absorbing path

A\subset G
with
|V(A)|\leqslant Cm=Dn^{1/2}

for some constant
D=D(\varepsilon)
, such that any vertex set

U\subset V(G)\setminus V(A)
with
|U|\leqslant dm
can be absorbed into
A
.

Next, we apply Lemma
2.4
with
W=V(A)
to obtain a
reservoir set

R\subset V(G)\setminus V(A)\quad\text{with}\quad|R|=dm=2\varepsilon^{-1}n^{1/2}

such that

(5)

|N(x)\cap N(y)\cap R|\geqslant\varepsilon|R|\geqslant 2n^{1/2}

for all
x,y\in V(G)
.

Let
G^{\prime}=G\big[V(G)\setminus(V(A)\cup R)\big]
,
and let
\chi^{\prime}
be the restriction of
\chi
to
E(G^{\prime})
.
Note that
\chi^{\prime}
is a proper edge-colouring of
G^{\prime}
and

\delta(G^{\prime})\geqslant cn-|V(A)|-|R|\geqslant cn-(D+2\varepsilon^{-1})n^{1/2}.

Applying Theorem
1.4
to
G^{\prime}
, we obtain a spanning linear forest

F\subset G^{\prime}
with at most
n^{1/2}
components and at least
cn-(2D+4\varepsilon^{-1}+4)n^{1/2}

distinct colours.

We now merge the components of
F
and the path
A
into a single cycle using
vertices from
R
.
Write
F=P_{1}\cup\cdots\cup P_{T}
, where
T\leqslant n^{1/2}
and each
P_{i}
has endpoints

\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
.
Let
x_{0},y_{0}
be the endpoints of
A
, and set
x_{T+1}=x_{0}
.
By (
5
), for each
0\leqslant i\leqslant T
the vertices
y_{i}
and
x_{i+1}

have at least
2n^{1/2}
common neighbours in
R
.
Since
T+1<2n^{1/2}
, we can greedily choose distinct vertices

z_{i}\in N(y_{i})\cap N(x_{i+1})\cap R\quad(0\leqslant i\leqslant T).

Adding the edges
\{y_{i}z_{i},z_{i}x_{i+1}:0\leqslant i\leqslant T\}
produces a cycle
H
with

|V(H)|\geqslant|V(F)|+|V(A)|\geqslant n-|R|.

Importantly, this step only adds edges without deleting any edges from
F
, so
H
retains at least as many distinct colours as
F
.

Finally, let
U=V(G)\setminus V(H)
and we have
|U|\leqslant|R|\leqslant 2\varepsilon^{-1}n^{1/2}
.
By Lemma
2.3
, we obtain a path
A_{U}
having the same endpoints with
A
and

V(A_{U})=V(A)\cup U
.
Replacing
A
by
A_{U}
in
H
yields a Hamilton cycle in
G
with at least

cn-O(n^{1/2})
distinct colours.
∎

3.
Colourful perfect matchings in Dirac bipartite graphs

In this section we prove Theorem
1.6
. Let
G
be a balanced bipartite graph on
2n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
and equipped with a proper edge-colouring. We first construct a matching in
G
with
n-o(n)
edges that uses
cn-o(n)
distinct colours (see Proposition
3.1
), and then extend this matching to a perfect one retaining most of its colours.

3.1.
Colourful near-perfect matchings

In this subsection, our goal is to construct a near-perfect matching with a near-optimal number of colours, as follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
1/2<c\leqslant 1
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
the following holds. Let
t=t(n)\geqslant n^{2/3}
. If
G
is a balanced bipartite graph on
2n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
and
\chi
is a proper edge-colouring of
G
, then
G
contains a matching
M
with at least
n-t
edges using at least
cn-8t
distinct colours.

The proof proceeds by contradiction. Let
G
be a balanced bipartite graph on
2n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
where
1/2<c\leqslant 1
, equipped with a proper
edge-colouring
\chi
.
Fix an integer
t
, and let

\mathcal{M}=\big\{M\subset G:\;M\text{ is a matching with $n-t$ edges}\big\}.

By Dirac’s theorem,
G
contains a perfect matching, and hence
\mathcal{M}\neq\varnothing
for all
t\leqslant n-1
. Choose a matching
M\in\mathcal{M}
that maximises the number of distinct colours appearing on
its edges. Suppose that
M
uses fewer than
cn-8t
distinct colours.

Denote
R:=V(G)\setminus V(M)
, and
|R|=2n-2e(M)=2t.

Let
C(M)
be the set of colours appearing in
M
and denote the leftover colours by
C_{0}:=\{\chi(e):e\in E(G)\}\setminus C(M)
. Fix an arbitrary vertex
v\in R
, we have

(6)

\displaystyle N_{C_{0}}(v)\subset V(M),

where
N_{C_{0}}(v)=\{u:uv\in E(G){\rm\ and}\ \chi(uv)\in C_{0}\}
.
Indeed, otherwise there is an edge
vv^{\prime}
with
v,v^{\prime}\in R
whose colour lies in
C_{0}
; then we could add
vv^{\prime}
to
M
and delete an edge of
M
with a repeated colour (if necessary) to obtain a matching in
\mathcal{M}
using more colours, contradicting the maximality of
M
.
Let
C(N(v))
be the set of colours appearing in the edges incident to
v
. As
N_{C_{0}}(v)\subset V(M),
we have

(7)

\displaystyle|N_{C_{0}}(v)|=|N(v)|-|N_{C(M)}(v)|\geqslant|N(v)|-|C(M)|\geqslant cn-(cn-8t)=8t.

The key observation is that, using
C_{0}
and (
7
), we can iteratively construct a sequence of colour sets and, more importantly, a collection of rainbow matchings of growing size. After sufficiently many iterations, this forces
M
to contain a rainbow sub-matching with more than
cn
edges, contradicting the assumption
|C(M)|\leqslant cn-8t
. To be precise, we present the following key lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}
be the matching with maximal number of colours and let
C_{0},R
be defined as above. Suppose the number of distinct colours in
M
is at most
cn-8t
. Then for any integer
\ell
satisfying
cn/4t<\ell<t^{1/2}/2
, there exist matchings

H_{1}\subset H_{2}\subset...\subset H_{\ell}\subset M,

together with a sequence of colour sets
C_{1}\subset C_{2}\subset...\subset C_{\ell}
defined by

C_{i}:=C_{0}\cup C(H_{i}),\quad\text{where}\ C(H_{i})=\{\chi(e):e\in E(H_{i})\},\qquad 1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell,

(set
H_{0}=\varnothing
and
C_{-1}=\varnothing
) such that the following holds.

(i)

For each
0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
,
H_{i}
is rainbow, that is,

\chi(e)\neq\chi(e^{\prime})

for any distinct
e,e^{\prime}\in H_{i}
.

(ii)

For each
0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
,
e(H_{i})=i\cdot 4t.

(iii)

For each
0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
and every edge
xy\in H_{i}
,

(8)

\displaystyle\text{either}\qquad N_{C_{i-1}}(x,R)=\varnothing\qquad\text{or}\qquad N_{C_{i-1}}(y,R)=\varnothing.

Moreover,

(9)

\displaystyle|N_{C_{i-1}}(x,R)|+|N_{C_{i-1}}(y,R)|\geqslant 8t^{1/2},

where
N_{C}(v,U)
denote the set of
C
-neighbours of
v
in
U
.

(iv)

For every
0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
and every vertex
v\in R
,

(10)

\displaystyle N_{C_{i}}(v)\subset V(M)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|N_{C_{i}}(v)|\geqslant(i+2)\cdot 4t.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
i
. For the base case
i=0
, (i)–(iii) vacuously hold, and (iv) follows from (
6
) and (
7
). Now let
i\geqslant 1
and assume that the conclusions of this lemma hold for all indices
j<i
. We first prove the following trace-back argument which is the core of this proof.

Claim 3.3
.

For each
0\leqslant j<i
, the following holds.

(1)

If there exists an edge
xy
with
x,y\notin V(H_{j})
and
\chi(xy)\in C_{j}
, then there exists a matching
H^{\prime}
with
V(H^{\prime})\subseteq R\cup V(H_{j})\cup\{x,y\}
,
xy\in E(H^{\prime})
and
e(H^{\prime})=e(H_{j})+1
, containing
1
more colour in
C_{0}
than
H_{j}
;

(2)

If there exist two vertex-disjoint edges
xy,zw
with
x,y,z,w\notin V(H_{j})
,
\chi(xy)\neq\chi(zw)
and
\chi(xy),\chi(zw)\in C_{j}
, then there exists a matching
H^{\prime\prime}
with
V(H^{\prime\prime})\subseteq R\cup V(H_{j})\cup\{x,y,z,w\}
,
xy,zw\in E(H^{\prime\prime})
and
e(H^{\prime\prime})=e(H_{j})+2
, containing
2
more distinct colours in
C_{0}
than
H_{j}
;

(3)

If there exists two vertex-disjoint edges
xy,zw
with
x,z\notin V(H_{j})
and
yw\in E(H_{j})
,
\chi(xy)\neq\chi(zw)
and
\chi(xy),\chi(zw)\in C_{j}
, then there exists a matching
H^{\prime\prime}
with
V(H^{\prime\prime})\subseteq R\cup V(H_{j})\cup\{x,y,z,w\}
,
xy,zw\in E(H^{\prime\prime})
and
e(H^{\prime\prime})=e(H_{j})+1
, containing
2
more distinct colours in
C_{0}
, and losing at most
1
colour in
C(H_{j})
.

Proof of claim.

We apply a trace-back method, based on the iterative conditions (
9
) and (
8
). Without loss of generality we just consider
j=i-1
.

For (1), assume
\chi(xy)\in C_{i-1}
. By the definition of
C_{i-1}
, if
\chi(xy)\in C_{0}
, then simply set
H^{\prime}=\{xy\}\cup H_{i-1}
. Otherwise, there exists a minimal
m_{1}
with
1\leqslant m_{1}\leqslant i-1
and an edge
x_{1}y_{1}\in H_{m_{1}}\setminus H_{m_{1}-1}
such that
\chi(x_{1}y_{1})=\chi(xy)
. We then perform a trace-back process. First, add the edge
xy
. By (
9
) and (
8
) for
m_{1}
, there exists at least
8t^{1/2}

C_{m_{1}-1}
-edges with one end
x_{1}
or
y_{1}
(say
x_{1}
) and another end in
R
. We can then pick
z_{1}\in R
such that
\chi(x_{1}z_{1})\in C_{m_{2}}\setminus C_{m_{2}-1}
for some
0\leqslant m_{2}<m_{1}
.
Remove
x_{1}y_{1}
and add
x_{1}z_{1}
. If
m_{2}=0
, then we are done; otherwise perform the same process on
x_{1}z_{1}
as we did on
xy
. At each step we remove one matching edge and add a new disjoint edge say
x_{s}z_{s}
, maintaining the matching property. The point is that
\chi(x_{s}z_{s})\in C_{m_{s+1}}
and
m_{s}
is decreasing in
s
. After at most
i<t^{1/2}/2
steps, we introduce a colour from
C_{0}
, producing a matching
H^{\prime}
as required.

For (2), we run a two-round trace-back process. Given
xy
and
H_{i-1}
, by (1) we can get a matching
H^{\prime}
with the mentioned property and avoid the vertices
z,w
and keep the edge of the same colour with
zw
(since the process has at most
i<t^{1/2}/2
steps and swap one edge each step, meanwhile
|R|=2t>t^{1/2}
and we have at least
8t^{1/2}
choices for each step). We then run the same process for
H^{\prime}
and
zw
as in (i). Note we can run at most
i<t^{1/2}/2
steps to finally introduce a colour from
C_{0}
(each step we have enough choices to avoid the chosen edges with ends in
R
and removed edges from the initial
H_{i}
), to get a matching
H^{\prime\prime}
with required properties. Moreover, in the second round we choose the final
C_{0}
-edge with a colour different from the one obtained in the first round; this is possible since at each step forbidding one colour rules out at most one candidate neighbour (by properness of the colouring), while we always have at least
8t^{1/2}
choices.

For (3), we first remove
yw
from
H_{i-1}
. Then we run a two-round trace-back process for
xy,wz
and
H_{i-1}\setminus\{yw\}
as in (2).
∎

We now construct the desired
H_{i}
via some matching
H_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq M
such that
H_{i-1}\subseteq H_{i}\subseteq H_{i}^{\prime}
always holds. Let
H^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq M
be a maximal sub-matching such that every
xy\in H^{\prime}_{i}
satisfies (
9
) for
i
(with respect to
C_{i-1}
).
Note
H_{i-1}\subset H^{\prime}_{i}
, since each edge in
H_{i-1}
satisfies (
9
) for
i-1
, and
C_{i-2}\subset C_{i-1}
.

First we prove for every edge
xy\in H^{\prime}_{i}
, either

(11)

\displaystyle N_{C_{i-1}}(x,R)=\varnothing\qquad\text{or}\qquad N_{C_{i-1}}(y,R)=\varnothing.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists an edge
xy\in H^{\prime}_{i}
such that both
N_{C_{i-1}}(x,R)
and
N_{C_{i-1}}(y,R)
are non-empty. As
H_{i}^{\prime}
satisfies (
9
) for
i
, we can find two edges
xw,yz
such that
w,z\in R
,
\chi(xw)\neq\chi(yz)
, and
\chi(xw),\chi(yz)\in C_{i-1}
. If
xy\notin H_{i-1}
, then as
H_{i-1}\subset H_{i}^{\prime}
we must have
x,y\notin V(H_{i-1})
and we can apply Claim
3.3
(2) to
H_{i-1}
and the two edges
xw
and

to get a matching
H^{\prime\prime}
with
V(H^{\prime\prime})\subseteq R\cup V(H_{i-1})\cup\{x,y,z,w\}
and
e(H^{\prime\prime})=e(H_{i-1})+2
, containing
2
more distinct colours in
C_{0}
than
H_{i-1}
. Set
M^{\prime\prime}=H^{\prime\prime}\cup M\setminus(H_{i-1}\cup\{xy\})
, and remove one edge in

with repeated colour. Observe then

and
|C(M^{\prime\prime})|\geqslant|C(M)|+2-1=|C(M)|+1
, which conflicts with the colour maximality of

. If

, applying Claim

(3) to
H_{i-1}
and
xw
,

gives a conflict as well.

Now we show
e(H^{\prime}_{i})\geqslant i\cdot 4t
. To do this, we consider

and

where the first equality holds since

) for

. Hence we have

(12)

\displaystyle|E_{C_{i-1}}(R,V(M))|\geqslant|R|\cdot\min_{v\in R}|N_{C_{i-1}}(v,V(M))|\geqslant|R|\cdot(i+1)\cdot 4t.

On the other hand, assume

. We partition

to upper bound

-edges between

and

,

where the first inequality holds since either

(

. Hence combining the assumption

, we have

\displaystyle|E_{C_{i-1}}(R,V(M))|

\displaystyle\leqslant|E_{C_{i-1}}(R,V(H^{\prime}_{i}))|+|E_{C_{i-1}}(R,V(M\setminus H^{\prime}_{i}))|

\displaystyle<|R|\cdot 4t\cdot i+8t^{1/2}n<|R|\cdot 4t\cdot(i+1),

which conflicts with (

Therefore

, and we can select

, and meanwhile (iii) also holds for

) and

. It remains to check (i) and (iv) for

.

For (i), we claim that each colour in

. If

) for

, fix one such

than

. Then let

, a contradiction. If

, applying Claim

(1) to
xy
and

gives a conflict similarly as well.

Finally we check (iv) for

. Fix an arbitrary vertex
v\in R
. We first show

Suppose to the contrary that there exists a

. If
\chi(vz)\in C_{i-1}
, applying Claim

(1) to

, we can find a matching

, which contains 1 more colour in

than

. Set

(and delete one edge of a repeated colour) to obtain a matching in

with one more colour than

, a conflict. Otherwise

, which means there exists an edge

such that

. Then by (iii) for

we can find one

-edge with one end

and another end

(say

). Applying Claim

(1) for

) for

.

. Note that

, we have

where

by (ii) for

. This completes the proof.
∎

Based on Lemma
3.2
, we can now prove Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Assume
M
is a matching in

with the maximal number of distinct colours, which is at most

. Choose an integer

, there exist a rainbow matching

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

The following lemma completes the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large and
t=t(n)<\varepsilon n/4
. Let
G
be a bipartite graph with two parts
X,Y
that
|X|=|Y|=n
, and
\delta(G)\geqslant(1/2+\varepsilon)n
, and let
M\subset G
be a matching with
e(M)\geqslant n-t
. Then there exists a perfect matching
M^{\prime}\subset G
such that

|E(M^{\prime})\cap E(M)|\geqslant n-2t.

Proof.

For every pair
x\in X,y\in Y
that
x,y\notin V(M)
, we claim there exists at least
2\varepsilon n-2t
edges
ab\in M
such that
ay,bx\in E(G)
.
Indeed, by the minimum degree condition,
x
is adjacent to a set
Y^{\prime}\subseteq V(M)\cap Y
of size at least
(1/2+\varepsilon)n-t
. Let
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
be the set of vertices matched to
Y^{\prime}
by
M
, then
|N(y)\cap X^{\prime}|\geqslant 2\varepsilon n-2t.
Then all edges in
M
incident to
N(y)\cap X^{\prime}
are as desired.

Now for each unmatched vertices
x,y\not\in V(M)
, we can greedily swap an edge
ab
as above with edges
ay,bx
to complete
M
to a perfect matching
M^{\prime}
. As we swap at most
n-e(M)\leqslant t
edges in
M
during this process,
|E(M^{\prime})\cap E(M)|\geqslant n-2t,

as required.
∎

Applying the above lemma to the colourful matching we obtained from Proposition
3.1
with
t=O(n^{2/3})
, we obtain a perfect matching
M^{\prime}
of
G
with at least
cn-8t-2t=cn-O(n^{2/3})
.

4.
Non-extendable linear forests and matchings

In this section, we prove Propositions
1.5
and
1.7
.

Proof of Proposition
1.5
.

Let
V(G)=A\cup B
be a partition with
|A|=(1-c)n
and
|B|=cn
.
Let
G[A]
be an independent set, let
G[B]
be a clique, and include all edges
between
A
and
B
.
Note that

\delta(G)=|B|=cn
.

Now let
F
be any spanning linear forest in
G[B]
(so
V(F)=B
), for example a
collection of vertex-disjoint paths covering
B
.
Consider any Hamilton cycle
H
in
G
.
Since
A
is independent,
H
cannot contain an edge with both endpoints in
A
.
Thus every vertex of
A
must be incident in
H
to two edges going from
A
to

B
, and consequently
H
uses exactly
2|A|=2(1-c)n
edges between
A
and
B
.
As
H
has
n
edges in total, it follows that
H
contains at most

n-2|A|=n-2(1-c)n=(2c-1)n

edges with both endpoints in
B
.
In particular, since
E(F)\subseteq E(G[B])
, we have
|E(H)\cap E(F)|\leqslant(2c-1)n
,
as required.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.7
.

Let
G
be a balanced bipartite graph with parts
X
and
Y
, where
|X|=|Y|=n
. Let
X_{1}\subseteq X
and
Y_{1}\subseteq Y
satisfy
|X_{1}|=|Y_{1}|=cn
, and set
X_{2}:=X\setminus X_{1}
and
Y_{2}:=Y\setminus Y_{1}
.
Define
G
by including all edges between
X_{1}
and
Y
, and all edges between
X_{2}
and
Y_{1}
, while adding no edges between
X_{2}
and
Y_{2}
. Notice then
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
.

Let
M
be a perfect matching between
X_{1}
and
Y_{1}
, so
e(M)=cn
. Now consider any perfect matching
M_{1}
in
G
.
Since there are no edges between
X_{2}
and
Y_{2}
, in order for every vertex in
X_{2}
to be covered by an edge of
M_{1}
, each vertex of
X_{2}
must be matched to a distinct vertex in
Y_{1}
. Consequently, the edges of
M
incident to these vertices of
Y_{1}
cannot belong to
M_{1}
. Therefore,
M_{1}
can contain at most

cn-|X_{2}|=cn-(1-c)n=(2c-1)n

edges of
M
, as required.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we prove that in any properly edge-coloured Dirac graphs, one can find an almost maximally colourful Hamilton cycle (perfect matching). We believe that the conclusions in
Theorems

1.3
and
1.6
hold already at
c=1/2
. It would be interesting to settle this case.

Beyond the existence of a single colourful Hamilton cycle, it is natural to ask whether many such cycles exist. In the uncoloured setting, Christofides, Kühn and Osthus
[
CKO12
]
showed that every
n
-vertex graph
\delta(G)\geqslant(1/2+\varepsilon)n
contains at least
n/8
edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles; see also
[
MPS19
,
KKKO20
]
for related results. Motivated by these results, we propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
1/2<c\leqslant 1
. If
G
is a graph on
n
vertices with
\delta(G)\geqslant cn
, then every proper edge-colouring of
G
contains at least
n/8
Hamilton cycles, each using at least
cn-o(n)
distinct colours.

Another natural direction is to study other colourful spanning structures.
In the complete graph setting, Montgomery, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov
[
MPS20
]
showed that any tree on
n-o(n)
vertices can be embedded in a properly edge-coloured
K_{n}
with all edges receiving distinct colours.
For Dirac graphs, however, embedding arbitrary almost-spanning trees is impossible without further restrictions.
Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
KSS01
]
proved that every
n
-vertex tree with maximum degree at most
cn/\log n
embeds into any graph of minimum degree at least
(1/2+\varepsilon)n
.
This motivates the following colourful analogue.

Conjecture 5.2
.

Let
1/2<c\leqslant 1
. Suppose that
G
is a graph on
n
vertices with minimum degree

\delta(G)\geqslant cn
. Then there exists a constant
d>0
such that for every tree
T
on
n
vertices with
\Delta(T)\leqslant dn/\log n
, any proper edge-colouring of
G

contains a copy of
T
using at least
cn-o(n)
distinct colours.
