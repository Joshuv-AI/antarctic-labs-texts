When Benign Is Not Benign
Authors: Silvana Di Maio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Cardiac myxomas (CMs) are the most common primary cardiac tumors in adults, accounting for approximately 50% of benign cardiac neoplasms, with an estimated incidence of 0.5 to 1 case per million population per year. They typically present as a solitary mass in the left atrium, attached to the interatrial septum, with a narrow stalk and lobulated contours.1

Although CMs do not have a pathognomonic clinical presentation, their typical manifestations include a triad of intracardiac obstruction, embolic events, and constitutional symptoms, which may vary according to tumor location, size, and mobility.1 Importantly, serious complications often arise even in the absence of preexisting symptoms. Studies have suggested that CMs may produce growth factors and inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-6, which is thought to contribute to systemic inflammatory manifestations such as fever, fatigue, and elevated inflammatory markers.2 In addition, chronic inflammation and immune-mediated mechanisms may support a pro-angiogenic and proliferative microenvironment, potentially contributing to mass growth and persistence.

Despite their rarity and generally favorable prognosis after surgical excision, selected cases challenge this reassuring paradigm, revealing a far more complex clinical course characterized by recurrence, infection, inflammatory activation, embolic events, and difficult long-term management.1,3

Familial CM represents approximately 10% of cases and frequently presents with multiple tumors at atypical sites.4 In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, the authors present a remarkable case of recurrent multifocal cardiac myxoma associated with Carney complex, highlighting the unpredictable and potentially aggressive nature of these tumors.3

Carney complex is a rare autosomal dominant syndrome, most commonly associated with pathogenic variants in the tumor suppressor gene protein kinase A type-1 regulatory subunit linked to chromosome 17q24. It is characterized by spotty skin pigmentation, endocrine abnormalities, and multiple neoplasms, including recurrent CMs. Compared with sporadic forms, syndromic myxomas tend to occur at younger age, are more frequently multicentric, show a male predominance, and carry a substantially higher risk of recurrence.3,4

Although histologically nonmalignant, syndromic CMs may exhibit clinically aggressive behavior, including repeated recurrence and multicentric growth, requiring repeated interventions and prolonged surveillance rather than isolated surgical treatment. In this context, the traditional distinction between benign and malignant cardiac tumors may become clinically less meaningful than the actual biological behavior of the lesion itself.3,4

Secondary recurrence frequently develops years after the initial operation.5 Proposed mechanisms include incomplete primary excision, familial predisposition, spontaneous or surgical implantation of tumor fragments into the myocardium, and the presence of pretumoral lesions in different myocardial sites. Such mechanisms likely reflect the complex and incompletely understood biology underlying syndromic CMs.5

Embolization occurs more commonly in CMs presenting with irregular surfaces, particularly those with papillary or villous projections, larger sizes, and in left-sided location due to the high blood flow dynamics. Of note, tumor infections can make the mass more friable and prone to systemic embolization.1

This case underscores the challenges of managing histologically benign tumors that may follow a complex clinical course. Accordingly, serial multimodality imaging and lifelong follow-up should be considered integral components of disease management.3,4 Genetic testing and family screening are also essential, as early recognition may guide preventive surveillance in asymptomatic relatives and reduce morbidity and mortality in affected patients.3,4

Beyond the remarkable clinical presentation, the present report further emphasizes the evolving role of multimodality imaging, which increasingly contributes to the assessment of embolic propensity, disease progression over time, and differential diagnosis in challenging scenarios, including possible superimposed infection.1,3

Nevertheless, several uncertainties remain. Predictors of recurrence are still poorly understood, genotype-phenotype correlations remain limited, and imaging markers associated with aggressive behavior have not yet been systematically validated. Ultimately, future studies integrating imaging, molecular biology, inflammatory profiling, and genetic characterization may help refine risk stratification and identify patients at highest risk for challenging disease behavior.
