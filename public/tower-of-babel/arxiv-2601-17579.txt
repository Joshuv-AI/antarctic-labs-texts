Abstract
We consider fractional variants of divergence form problems with highly oscillatory local coefficients. We characterise the convergence of these coefficients by means of classical H H -convergence covering the local behaviour of the fractional divergence form problem and weak- ∗ \ast convergence on the complement caused by the nonlocality of the differential operators. The results are further described in the light of nonlocal H H -convergence as introduced in [Waurick, Calc Var PDEs, 57, 2018] and certain Schur topologies. Applications to symmetric coefficients and a homogenisation problem for a fractional heat type equation are provided.

Introduction

In the modelling of phenomena in mathematical physics it has been apparent that nonlocal equations, that is, equations that take into account the behaviour of particles in a certain region and not only at a single point, provide more accurate descriptions in various cases. A prominent example in electromagnetism is the so-called nonlocal response theory; see
[
27
, Chapter 10]
.
In this theory, the material coefficients are represented by convolution kernels, whereas the associated differential operators remain classical.
In contrast, nonlocal equations can also be formulated using fractional differential operators, such as the fractional Laplacian, which replace classical derivatives with nonlocal ones. For a probabilistic interpretation, applications to a wide range of physical systems, and the numerical treatment of such operators, we refer to
[
14
,
22
]
. Furthermore, fractional derivative terms have been shown to improve predictions for certain physical phenomena; specifically, models involving fractional derivatives often outperform traditional ansätze by requiring fewer terms to achieve greater accuracy
[
7
]
.

While several fractional derivatives are commonly used in the one-dimensional setting and the theory is well-standardised
[
30
,
35
]
, the situation in higher dimensions is significantly more diverse (see
[
39
]
and the references therein). Recently, however, the Riesz fractional gradient was rigorously established in
[
38
,
39
]
. By identifying it as a canonical higher-dimensional generalisation, this work has sparked significant interest in the framework, as seen in
[
5
,
15
,
18
,
28
,
40
]
. Our contribution follows this approach.

In nonlocal equations modelling physical phenomena the coefficients might be highly oscillatory in order to take material variations appropriately into account. In these cases, compared to classical equations, computations are more involved not only due to the nonlocal nature of the equations but also due to the variations of the coefficients on a smaller scale. Hence, the set-up asks for what is called homogenisation, i.e., to find a somewhat optimal approximation of highly oscillatory coefficients by non-oscillatory ones.
The majority of the literature addresses the homogenization of Lévy-type operators, where the oscillations are contained within a scalar kernel describing the jumps of the underlying processes (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
6
,
8
,
26
,
36
]
). The techniques employed range from probabilistic methods to those tailored for partial differential equations. Notably, the work
[
8
]
(see also
[
6
]
) represents the first attempt to adapt the notion of
H
-convergence to nonlocal problems, which is the central theme of our contribution.
In 2018, a more comprehensive theory for local differential operators and nonlocal coefficients has been found which operator-theoretically captures this quest of finding non-oscillatory coefficients. Indeed, in
[
45
]
, the notion of nonlocal
H
-convergence has been coined, which, if restricted to local coefficients, coincides with classical homogenisation approaches that are in turn covered by
H
-convergence. It is the purpose of the present text to study homogenisation problems for nonlocal differential equations with local coefficients and fractional derivative operators, building upon the theory introduced in
[
15
]
.

{}_{{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\langle\grads F,f\rangle_{{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}=-{}_{{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\langle F,\divs f\rangle_{{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})},\quad f\in{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

and

{}_{{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\langle\divs G,g\rangle_{{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}=-{}_{{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\langle G,\grads g\rangle_{{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})},\quad g\in{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

By (
7
) this definition is clearly an extension of the operators given in Definition
2.1
, while the continuity of operators
\grads
and
\divs
ensures that the weak fractional operators map
{\cal T}^{\prime}
into
{\cal T}^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, the definition is consistent with the previous comment on bounded Lipschitz functions. Indeed, if
F
is bounded and Lipschitz continuous, then
F\in{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, while
[
20
, Proposition 2.8]
ensures that
\grads F
, i.e., the above weak fractional gradient, is given by (
5
). An analogous statement holds for the operator
\divs
.

Although the theory of fractional operators
\grads
and
\divs
is rather new, the fractional Laplacian operator
(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}
is well-established in the literature.
Hence, we do not state its precise definition, but just refer to
[
31
,
39
]
for the definition of
(-\Delta)^{s}
for
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle

on
{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

(note that by
[
39
, Definition 4.5, Theorem 4.7 and Section 6]
this definition coincides on the space
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
with the standard one given e.g. in
[
21
, Section 3]
).

In the local setting (
s=1
) we have
-\dive\nabla=-\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the classical (local) Laplacian. This important relation is retained for the fractional operators.

Theorem 2.3

For any
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
it holds

(a)

-\divs(\grads F)=(-\Delta)^{s}F\,,\quad F\in{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\,,

(b)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1-s}{2}}\grads F=\grads\,(-\Delta)^{\frac{1-s}{2}}F=\nabla F\,,\quad F\in{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

While the first identity in the above theorem is provided in
[
39
, Theorem 5.3]
for
{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, its straightforward extension to
{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is deduced directly from the duality argument used to define the weak fractional differential operators.
The version of the second identity in Theorem
2.3

(see
[
39
, Theorem 5.3]
and
[
28
, (2.8)]
) adjusted for the Sobolev spaces was heavily used in the proof of the compactness result of
H^{s}
-convergence in
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
.

One might be tempted to invert the fractional Laplacian in Theorem
2.3
(b) and get:
\grads=\nabla(-\Delta)^{\frac{s-1}{2}}
.
This indeed holds, but only for smooth functions
[
39
, Section 5]
, and it is more commonly expressed in terms of Riesz potentials
I_{\alpha}
, which, for
\alpha\in\langle 0,d\rangle
and a measurable function
f:{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, is defined by (cf.
[
24
, Section 6.1.1]
)

I_{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{\mu_{1-\alpha}}{d-\alpha}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}dy\,,

where
\mu_{1-\alpha}
is given in Definition
2.1
.
Then in
[
18
, Section 2.3]
the following is shown:

Theorem 2.4

For any
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and any
\psi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

we have

\grads\varphi=\nabla I_{1-s}\varphi=I_{1-s}\nabla\varphi\quad\hbox{and}\quad\divs\psi=\dive I_{1-s}\psi=I_{1-s}\dive\psi\,,

where for vector-valued functions (here
\nabla\varphi
and
\psi
)
the Riesz potential
I_{1-s}
is set to act componentwise.

Therefore, as discussed in
[
18
]
, either of these expressions leads to the same notion of fractional differential operators.

Let us close this discussion by mentioning another equivalent definition of operators
\grads
and
\divs
in terms of the Fourier transformation. Either using Theorem
2.3
or
2.4
, one easily gets (see
[
38
, Theorem 1.4]
and
[
40
, Section 4]
) the following result.

Theorem 2.5

For any
f\in{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
g\in{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
we have

\widehat{\grads f}(\xi)=i\xi|\xi|^{s-1}\widehat{f}(\xi)\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{\divs g}(\xi)=i|\xi|^{s-1}\xi\cdot\widehat{g}(\xi)\,,

where
\hat{u}
denotes the Fourier transform of
u
, given by

\hat{u}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}u(x)dx
for all
\xi\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
For vector-valued functions, the Fourier transform is used componentwise.

To conclude, all three options addressed here, namely, Definition
2.1
, Theorem
2.4
, and Theorem
2.5
, yield equivalent definitions of fractional differential operators (cf.
[
18
,
38
]
).

2.2
Fractional Sobolev spaces

For the analysis of fractional diffusion problems and
H^{s}
-convergence, we shall need fractional Sobolev spaces.
In this paper, we focus exclusively on the case
p=2
, where
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})={\rm W}^{s,2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Here, the former denotes the Bessel potential spaces and the latter the Sobolev–Slobodeckij spaces (see
[
25
, Section 1.3.1]
).
We will continue to use
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
to denote these spaces and use any of two equivalent norms.

Using Theorem
2.5
it is an easy exercise to see that
the norm

\|u\|_{s}:=\left(\|u\|^{2}_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}+\|\grads u\|^{2}_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\right)^{1/2}\,,\quad u\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\,,

is equivalent with the norm of
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
for functions in
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
(here it is more convenient to use the norm of the Bessel potential spaces which, by the Plancherel theorem, reads:

\varphi\mapsto\|(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}\hat{\varphi}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}
).
Hence, since
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is dense in
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
,
we have that the above norm
\|\cdot\|_{s}
, when extended to
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
via Definition
2.2
, is an equivalent norm on
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, i.e.
{\sf Cl\thinspace}_{\|\cdot\|_{s}}{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})={\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
This is in fact rigorously proven in
[
38
, Theorem 1.7]
for any integrability exponent
p\in\langle 1,\infty\rangle
.

Remark 2.6

It is important to notice that the first identity in Theorem
2.5

can be extended for
f\in{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

Remark 2.7

Let us make a short historical comment on the recent development of the fractional Sobolev spaces used here.
In
[
38
]
, for
p\in\langle 1,\infty\rangle
, the following norm

\|\varphi\|_{s,p}:=\Bigl(\|\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}^{p}+\|\grads\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}^{p}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}}\,,\quad\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\,,

is introduced and then
{\rm X}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is defined as the completion of the normed space

({\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),\|\cdot\|_{s,p})\,.

Then it was shown (
[
38
, Theorem 1.7]
) that
{\rm X}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})={\rm H}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, where
{\rm H}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is the Bessel potential space with the integrability exponent
p
(cf.
[
25
, Section 1.3.1]
).

On the other hand, in
[
18
]
another space
is introduced:

{\rm S}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):=\Bigl\{f\in{\rm L}^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):\grads f\in{\rm L}^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\Bigr\}\,,

where here
\grads
denotes the weak derivative in the sense of Definition
2.2
.
Then for any
p\in[1,\infty]
the space
({\rm S}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),\|\cdot\|_{s,p})
is a Banach space
[
18
, Proposition 3.20]
(where again we consider the extension of
\grads
in the definition of

\|\cdot\|_{s,p}
).

In order to prove that
{\rm X}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
{\rm S}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
coincide, it is sufficient to show that
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is dense in
{\rm S}^{s,p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
This was done in
[
18
, Theorem 3.23]
for
p=1

and then extended for
p\in[1,\infty\rangle
in
[
10
, Theorem A.1]

(see also
[
28
, Theorem 2.7]
). Of course, for
p=\infty
the claim does not hold.

Therefore, both variants of fractional spaces coincide with Bessel potential spaces.

Since in this work we are interested in the study of the suitable boundary value problems on
\Omega\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, associated to the introduced fractional operators, we will primarily focus on a closed subspace of
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
which contains an information about the boundary condition on
\Omega
.
More precisely, following
[
15
]
, for
U\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
open
we define

{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U):={\sf Cl\thinspace}_{\|\cdot\|_{s}}{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(U)\,,

i.e. the closure of
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(U)
in
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
Of course,
{\rm H}^{s}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})={\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
The space
{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)
is a separable Hilbert space, with inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)}=\int\limits_{U}uv\,dx+\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\grads u\cdot\grads v\,dx\,,

(8)

where we have used the fact that functions in
{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)
are equal to zero a.e. in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus U
.
Of course, for any
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(U)
the mapping

{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\ni u\mapsto\varphi u\in{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)
is linear and continuous.

Moreover, we denote the dual of
{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)
by
{\rm H}^{-s}(U)
, i.e.,

{\rm H}^{-s}(U):=\left({\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)\right)^{\prime}\,.

Then the following embeddings hold

{\cal D}(U)\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{\rm H}^{s}_{0}(U)\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{{\rm L}^{2}(U)}\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{\rm H}^{-s}(U)\hookrightarrow{\cal D}^{\prime}(U)\,.

If
U={{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, we can include also the spaces
{\cal T}
and
{\cal T}^{\prime}
to get:

{\cal D}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{\cal T}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\overset{\rm dense}{\hookrightarrow}{\rm H}^{-s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\hookrightarrow{\cal T}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\hookrightarrow{\cal D}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),

where
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
is considered as the pivot space, as usual.

Remark 2.8

From (
8
) it is clear that a sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
in
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
weakly converges to
u\in{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
if and only if

u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}u
and
\grads u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\grads u
.

Let us briefly comment on some properties of (weak) fractional operators
\grads
and
\divs
related to the above fractional Sobolev spaces.
It is easy to see that the restriction of
\grads
, given in Definition
2.2
, to
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is a continuous linear operator from
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
to
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
.
Of course, the same applies if we replace
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
by

{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
.

On the other hand, the restriction of
\divs
to
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}

is a continuous linear operator from
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
to

{\rm H}^{-s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
Furthermore, since
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)\subseteq{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
implies
{\rm H}^{-s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\subseteq{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, we can define
\divs_{\Omega}:{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
by the following relation

{}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle\divs_{\Omega}G,g\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}=-\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}G(x)\cdot\grads g(x)\,dx,\quad g\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega).

(9)

Now, for
G\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}

(\divs G)|_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}=\divs_{\Omega}G.

In the sequel we will use
\divs
to denote both operators. However, we want to emphasise that although
\divs_{\Omega}G=\divs_{\Omega}F
is equivalent to
(\divs G)|_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}=(\divs F)|_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}
, it does not imply that
\divs G=\divs F
on
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Note here that the inclusion
{\rm H}^{-s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\subseteq{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
is not an embedding since
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
is not dense in
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
in general.
This distinction will be important for some comments that we will make in Section
4
.

Remark 2.9

Let us relate the space
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
to the existing theoretical framework in the literature.

Recall that the Sobolev-Slobodeckij norm on an open set
U\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
is given by

\|u\|_{{\rm W}^{s,2}(U)}:=(\|u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(U)}}^{2}+[u]_{s,U}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
where the term

[u]_{s,U}:=\left(\int\limits_{U}\int\limits_{U}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is the so-called Gagliardo or Slobodeckij seminorm of
u
.
Given the equivalence of the norms
\|\cdot\|_{{\rm W}^{s,2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and
\|\cdot\|_{s}
, utilising
\|\cdot\|_{{\rm W}^{s,2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{s}
in the definition of
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
yields the same space.
In particular,
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
coincides with the space
\widetilde{\rm W}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
given in
[
9
, Definition 2.1]

and
{\rm H}^{s}_{\Omega}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
in
[
17
]
.
Consequently, we can deduce conclusions about our space
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
from the corresponding properties of the spaces discussed above, a relationship we will leverage in the subsequent analysis.

It is worth mentioning that in general we only have the following strict inclusions:

\displaystyle{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\subsetneq{\rm W}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\subsetneq\bigl\{u\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}:u=0\ a.e.\ \hbox{in}\ {{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega,\ [u]_{s,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}<\infty\bigr\}\,,

where
{\rm W}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
is defined as the closure of
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)

with respect to the weaker norm

\|u\|_{{\rm W}^{s,2}(\Omega)}=(\|u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}^{2}+[u]_{s,\Omega}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

while the space on the right-hand side of the second inclusion coincides with the spaces
{\rm S}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
and
{\rm H}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
as used in
[
28
]
and
[
5
]
, respectively.
However, for sufficiently smooth
\Omega
(e.g. Lipschitz),
the equality holds in both inclusions (see e.g.
[
9
, Appendix B]
,
[
17
, Appendix]
and
[
25
, Section 1.3]
).

In order to prove some results on
H^{s}
-convergence, we shall need additional properties of fractional Sobolev spaces. For their compactness result regarding
H^{s}
-convergence, the authors of
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
relied on several properties of the fractional Sobolev spaces
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
listed in their preliminary Section 2. Specifically, certain conclusions required the continuity of the Riesz potential for
{\rm L}^{p}
spaces, which imposed the requirement
1-s<\frac{d}{2}
. However, since
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
, this condition is automatically met for
d\geq 2
, which explains the restriction used in
[
15
]
. Since our aim is to address phenomena appearing in the one-dimensional setting (see Section
4
), we shall now concisely show that all the necessary properties still hold for
d=1
. This immediately implies that
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
can be generalised to the regime
d\geq 1
.

Namely, the arguments provided for Proposition 2.6 (continuity of the Riesz potential), Proposition 2.8 (Poincaré inequality) and Proposition 2.9 (Rellich–Kondrašov theorem) from
[
15
, Section 2]
require the condition
d\geq 2
. We now present a single theorem that encompasses the version of these statements valid also for
d=1
.
For a clearer presentation we use
X\lesssim_{a}Y
to indicate that there exists a constant
C>0
that depend only on
a
such that
X\leq CY
.

Theorem 2.10

Let
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle,

d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
open and bounded.

(a)

(Continuity of the Riesz potential)

I_{1-s}:{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)\to{\rm H}^{1}_{\rm loc}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is linear and continuous.
Moreover, for any
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
we have

\nabla(I_{1-s}u)=\grads u
a.e. in
\Omega
.

If
s>1-\frac{d}{2}
, then the statement still holds if we replace the space
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
by
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

(b)

(Poincaré inequality)
For any
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
we have:

\|u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}\lesssim_{d,s,\Omega}\|\grads u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\,.

(10)

(c)

(Rellich–Kondrašov theorem)

{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded into
{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}
.

Proof.

(a)

The case
s>1-\frac{d}{2}
is covered in
[
15
, Proposition 2.6]
(in fact the stronger version with the space
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is shown).
Let us then consider the case
s\leq 1-\frac{d}{2}
. Note that since
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
, this is possible only if
d=1
.
Hence, the case
s\leq 1-\frac{d}{2}
reduces to
s\in\langle\left.0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
,
d=1
, and we shall use the notation
\Ds
instead of
\grads
(just to indicate that the object is scalar-valued).
For
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
by Theorem
2.4

we have
\|\DD I_{1-s}\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}=\|\Ds\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}
.
Thus, by linearity of the Riesz potential it is left to estimate the
{\rm L}^{2}
-norm of
I_{1-s}\varphi
.
Let us define
\frac{1}{p}:=1-\frac{s}{2}
. Since
s\in\langle\left.0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
,
we have
p\in\langle\left.1,\frac{4}{3}\right]
and
\frac{1}{p}>1-s
. Hence, we can apply the standard continuity result for the Riesz potential (known as the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev theorem on fractional integration) (see e.g.
[
24
, Theorem 6.1.3]
and
[
32
, Section 4.2, Theorem 2.1]
):

\|I_{1-s}\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}})}\lesssim_{s}\|\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})}\,,

where
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-(1-s)=\frac{s}{2}
.
Next, take
O\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
open and bounded.
Then using the facts that
p<2<q
and
{\rm supp\,}\varphi\subseteq\Omega
, we obtain

\|I_{1-s}\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{2}(O)}\lesssim_{s,O}\|I_{1-s}\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{q}(O)}\lesssim_{s}\|\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})}=\|\varphi\|_{{\rm L}^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{s,\Omega}\|\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}\,.

Hence, for any
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and any
O\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
open and bounded, it holds

\|I_{1-s}\varphi\|_{{\rm H}^{1}(O)}\lesssim_{s,O,\Omega}\|\varphi\|_{s}\,.

Since
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
, the claim follows.

(b)

Via a standard density argument, it suffices to consider
u\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. The result then is a consequence of the standard Poincaré inequality in terms of the Gagliardo semi-norm (see Remark
2.9
and
[
9
, Lemma 2.4]
):

\|u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}\lesssim_{d,s,\Omega}[u]_{s,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\;,\quad u\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)\,.

Indeed, one just need to use the following relations (
[
2
, Proposition 2.8]
):

[u]_{s,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}^{2}=C_{d,s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(-\Delta)^{s}u\,dx=C_{d,s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u\divs\grads u\,dx=C_{d,s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\grads u|^{2}\,dx\,,

where the first equality is a standard one (see e.g.
[
21
, Proposition 3.6]
), the second is due to Theorem
2.3
(a),
while the last one follows from the fractional integration by parts formula (
7
).
Since the value of the positive constant
C_{d,s}
is irrelevant for our purposes, for simplicity we keep it in an implicit form (note that it can be expressed using the constant
\mu_{s}
in Definition
2.1
); we also refer to
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
.

(c)

By virtue of Remark
2.9
, the claim follows immediately from
[
9
, Theorem 2.7]
; alternatively,
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
provides a more direct proof.

\blacksquare

In conclusion, we state two results that will be fundamental for the analysis in Section
5
, where the condition that the coefficients are fixed outside
\Omega
will be omitted.
The first one concerns compactness of the fractional gradient on the complement of
\Omega
: When
s=1
, we get
\nabla u=0
a.e. in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega\eqqcolon({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}

for any
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Hence, the mapping
u\mapsto\nabla u|_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}}
is the zero operator.
On the other hand, for
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
and
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
, we do not have any information on the support of
\grads u
. However, although the mapping

u\mapsto\grads u|_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}}
is no longer trivial, we know that it is compact.

Theorem 2.11
(
[
28
, Lemma 2.12]
)

Let
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
. For any open set
\Omega_{1}\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{1}
the mapping

{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)\ni u\mapsto\grads u|_{\Omega_{1}^{c}}\in{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{c})}}

(11)

is compact.

The second result is an extension of the property from
[
28
, Lemma 4.3]
.

Lemma 2.12

Let
\Omega\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
be open and bounded. For any
x^{0}\in\Omega\cup({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exists
\varphi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\grads\varphi(x^{0})=\lambda
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
\Omega=B(0,1)
and
\lambda=\beta e_{1}
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
For
x^{0}\in\Omega
, the statement is proved in
[
28
, Lemma 4.3]
.
Let
x^{0}\in({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}
and consider non-trivial functions
\psi,\theta\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\langle-1,1\rangle)
supported in
\left\langle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right\rangle
. We further require that
\theta\geq 0
, and that
\psi
is an odd function satisfying
\psi(x)\geq 0
on
\langle 0,1\rangle
. With these functions, we define
\varphi
as

\varphi(x):=\frac{|x^{0}-x|^{d+s+1}}{|x|^{d+s+1}}\psi(x_{1})\theta(x_{2})\cdots\theta(x_{d}),\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Obviously
\varphi(x^{0})=0
, while from the properties of
\psi
and
\theta
, and the fact that for
x\in B(0,1)
we have
|x^{0}-x|>|x^{0}|-1>0
, it follows that
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(B(0,1))
. Then the first component of the fractional gradient of
\varphi
at
x^{0}
reads

\displaystyle(\grads\varphi(x^{0}))_{1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{s}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{(\varphi(x^{0})-\varphi(y))(x^{0}_{1}-y_{1})}{|x^{0}-y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy

\displaystyle=-\mu_{s}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{\psi(y_{1})\theta(y_{2})\cdots\theta(y_{d})(x^{0}_{1}-y_{1})}{|y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy

\displaystyle=-\mu_{s}x^{0}_{1}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{\psi(y_{1})\theta(y_{2})\cdots\theta(y_{d})}{|y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy+\mu_{s}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{y_{1}\psi(y_{1})\theta(y_{2})\cdots\theta(y_{d})}{|y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy.

Since
\psi
is odd the first integral in the above equation vanishes. As
\psi
and
\theta
are not identically zero and
y_{1}\psi(y_{1})
and
\theta
are non-negative, the second integral yields

(\grads\varphi(x^{0}))_{1}=2\mu_{s}\int\limits_{\{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}:y_{1}>0\}}\frac{y_{1}\psi(y_{1})\theta(y_{2})\cdots\theta(y_{d})}{|y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy\eqqcolon\gamma>0.

Similarly, for
j\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
, we have

(\grads\varphi(x^{0}))_{j}=-\mu_{s}x^{0}_{j}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{\psi(y_{1})\theta(y_{2})\cdots\theta(y_{d})}{|y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy+\mu_{s}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{y_{j}\psi(y_{1})\theta(y_{2})\cdots\theta(y_{d})}{|y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy=0,

where we used that both integrands are odd functions with respect to
y_{1}
. Hence,
\grads(\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\varphi)(x^{0})=\beta e_{1}
, so that
\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\varphi
is the desired function.

\blacksquare

2.3
Fractional compensated compactness

In the classical analysis of partial differential equations, the theory of compensated compactness plays a significant role. It dates back to the work of Murat and Tartar in the second half of the twentieth century
[
33
,
34
,
42
,
43
]
.
Specifically, they proved the div-curl lemma, a well-known result providing sufficient conditions under which the product of two weakly convergent sequences converges to the product of their limits. Using fractional integration by parts and the fractional Leibniz rule, we are able to prove a special case of the div-curl lemma in the fractional setting, where one sequence is in the form of
\grads v_{n}
(hence, curl-free) and another sequence has strongly convergent fractional divergence (
\divs u_{n}
strongly converges in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
). We refer to a recent survey
[
16
]
, where this property and two more variants of mixed local-nonlocal div-curl lemmas were proved in the loc-spaces setting.

First, recall the Leibniz rule for fractional gradients (the analogue statement holds for fractional divergence), which can be found in the literature in several forms, depending on the considered function spaces, see e.g.
[
20
, lemmas 2.4,2.5]
,
[
19
, Lemma 3.2]
,
[
28
, Lemma 2.11]
. Here we state the version which is suitable for our analysis.

Proposition 2.13
(Fractional Leibniz rule)

Let
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
and
u\in{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Then,
\varphi u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and

\grads(\varphi u)=\varphi\grads u+u\grads\varphi+\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,u),

where the remainder term
\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,u)
is given by

\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,u)(x):=\mu_{s}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\frac{(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))(u(x)-u(y))(x-y)}{|x-y|^{d+s+1}}\,dy,\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Moreover,
\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,\cdot):{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
is a linear and continuous map which satisfies

\|\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,u)\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\lesssim_{d,s}\|\varphi\|^{1-s}_{{\rm L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\varphi\|^{s}_{{\rm L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}.

Proposition 2.14
(Fractional compensated compactness)

Let
\Omega\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
be open and bounded, and let
(u_{n})_{n}
in
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and
(v_{n})_{n}
in
{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. For some
u\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and
v\in{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
assume

v_{n}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}v,\quad v_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}v,

u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}u,\quad\divs u_{n}\xrightarrow{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\divs u.

Then

u_{n}\cdot\grads v_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\cal D}^{\prime}(\Omega)}u\cdot\grads v.

Remark 2.15

Note that by Theorem
2.10
(c) and Remark
2.8
the assumptions on
v_{n}
can be replaced by a stronger requirement

(v_{n})_{n}\text{ in }{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad v_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}v.

Proof.

For
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, using the fractional Leibniz rule we get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}(u_{n}\cdot\grads v_{n})\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}u_{n}\cdot(\varphi\grads v_{n})\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}u_{n}\cdot\grads(\varphi v_{n})\,dx-\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(u_{n}\cdot\grads\varphi)v_{n}\,dx-\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}u_{n}\cdot\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,v_{n})\,dx.

For the first term, we have

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}u_{n}\cdot\grads(\varphi v_{n})\,dx=-\,{}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle\divs u_{n},\varphi v_{n}\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}-\,{}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle\divs u,\varphi v\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}.

(12)

Regarding the second term, since
u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}u
,
\grads\varphi\in{\rm L}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
v_{n}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}v
, we get

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(u_{n}\cdot\grads\varphi)v_{n}\,dx\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(u\cdot\grads\varphi)v\,dx.

(13)

Moreover, from Proposition
2.13
we have that
v_{n}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}v
implies
\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,v_{n})\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,v)
, which together with
u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}u
gives

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}u_{n}\cdot\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,v_{n})\,dx\longrightarrow\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}u\cdot\grads_{\rm NL}(\varphi,v)\,dx.

(14)

Using (
12
), (
13
) and (
14
), and applying the fractional Leibniz rule once more, this time to
\varphi
and
v
, we obtain

\int\limits_{\Omega}(u_{n}\cdot\grads v_{n})\varphi\,dx\longrightarrow\int\limits_{\Omega}(u\cdot\grads v)\varphi\,dx,

which concludes the proof.

\blacksquare

We conclude this section by giving an auxiliary result which is an extension (to the fractional setting) of the classical characterisation of the dual Sobolev space. This property will be significant for the analysis of the one-dimensional homogenisation problem.

Lemma 2.16

For
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, there exists
g\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
such that
f=\divs g
in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, and the mapping
f\mapsto g
is a linear isometry
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}\longrightarrow{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
.

Proof.

From the Poincaré inequality it follows that
\|\cdot\|_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}
and
\|\grads\cdot\,\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}
are equivalent norms on
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
, implying that
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space when endowed with the (equivalent) inner product given by

\langle v,u\rangle=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\grads v\cdot\grads u\,dx,\quad u,v\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega).

Furthermore, the Riesz representation theorem implies that for a given
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
there exists a unique
v\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
such that

{}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle f,u\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\grads v\cdot\grads u\,dx,\quad u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega),

(15)

with
f\mapsto v
being an isometric isomorphism
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}\longrightarrow{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
. Having in mind the definition of
\divs
on
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
, we conclude

f=\divs(-\grads v)\quad\text{on }{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega).

Hence,
g=-\grads v\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
is the desired function, and
f\mapsto g
is clearly a linear isometry
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}\longrightarrow{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
.

\blacksquare

2.4
Fractional diffusion: well-posedness

For
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
we consider the fractional diffusion problem as follows.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}\grads u)=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(16)

Here, we assume
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, where, let us recall,
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
denotes the class of all matrix-valued functions
{\bf A}:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
satisfying

\displaystyle{\bf A}(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\alpha|\xi|^{2},\quad

\displaystyle\text{for every $\xi\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}$ and for a.e. $x\in U$},

(17)

\displaystyle{\bf A}(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\frac{1}{\beta}|{\bf A}(x)\xi|^{2},\quad

\displaystyle\text{for every $\xi\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}$ and for a.e. $x\in U$}.

(18)

We say that
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
is a
weak
or
variational solution of the problem
(
16
)
if for any
v\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)

\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\bf A}(x)\grads u(x)\cdot\grads v(x)\,dx={}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle f,v\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}\,.

Proposition 2.17

Let
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. For any
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
there exists a unique weak solution

u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
of (
16
). Moreover, the solution
u
satisfies the following estimate

\|\grads u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\|f\|_{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}\,.

(19)

The well-posedness result presented in
[
15
, Lemma 2.12]
is provided for
d\geq 2

by means of the Lax–Milgram theorem. However, the case
d=1
is obtained analogously, since the necessary properties have been established for this setting in Subsection
2.2
(see Theorem
2.10
).
A discussion of well-posedness for more general nonlocal problems can be found in
[
23
]
. Note that in the present setting and, quite generally also for a nonlinear setting, as it was shown in the main result of
[
44
]
, the only requirement for well-posedness is a mere closed range inequality as for instance provided by the (fractional) Poincaré inequality and an invertibility condition satisfied by the coefficients. The former has been exemplified above and the latter is implied by the coercivity condition on the conductivities.

satisfying

As we have mentioned, the proof of

.

Theorem 3.1
(
[

)

Let
{\bf A}_{0}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. For any sequence
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}

{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})

Furthermore, the limit matrix function
{\bf A}
is unique, i.e., if for
{\bf A},\tilde{{\bf A}}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
it holds that

{\bf A}_{n}\text{ $H^{s}$-converges to }{\bf A}\text{ in }\Omega

and

{\bf A}_{n}\text{ $H^{s}$-converges to }\tilde{{\bf A}}\text{ in }\Omega,

then

{\bf A}(x)=\tilde{{\bf A}}(x)\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

The compactness obtained above follows directly from the central theorem of
H
-convergence, which provides both compactness and metrisability. For ease of reference, we summarise these results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
(
[
3
, Thm. 1.2.16 and Prop. 1.2.24]
)

There is a metric
d_{H}
on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
that induces
H
-convergence. Moreover,
({\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega),\tau_{H})
is compact, where
\tau_{H}
is the topology induced by
d_{H}
.

Now, let us discuss implications of the results presented in Theorem
3.1
and clarify how they imply equivalence between
H
- and
H^{s}
-convergence, stated in (

∗

‣
1
).

Proof of (

∗

‣

).

From the proof of
[

we see that

-convergence implies
H^{s}
-convergence, i.e., if a sequence
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}

{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)

, then, for every fixed
{\bf A}_{0}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

-converges to

Remark 3.3

20
) and the fact that
u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}u
, we get

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n}\cdot\grads u_{n}\,dx={}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle f,u_{n}\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}\to{}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle f,u\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}.

(24)

On the other hand, the weak formulation for the problem (
23
) implies

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\bf A}\grads u\cdot\grads u\,dx={}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle f,u\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)},

which together with (
24
) gives the desired result.

\blacksquare

Theorem 3.7
(Metrisability)

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a dense countable family in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
,
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
,
0<\alpha\leq\beta
. We define a metric on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
by

d_{s}({\bf A},{\bf B})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\frac{\|u_{n}-v_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}+\|{\bf A}\grads u_{n}-{\bf B}\grads v_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}}{\|f_{n}\|_{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}},

(25)

where
u_{n},\,v_{n}\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
are unique solutions of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}\grads u_{n})=f_{n}&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u_{n}=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf B}\grads v_{n})=f_{n}&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
v_{n}=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.\,

Then
d_{s}
is indeed a metric on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
generating the
H^{s}
-topology on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
.

Before we turn to the proof of this statement, we quickly provide an elementary lemma comparing (weak) convergences in embedded spaces.

Lemma 3.8

Let
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}_{1}
be Hilbert spaces with
\mathcal{H}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}
continuously. If
(v_{n})_{n}
in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is bounded and (weakly) convergent in
\mathcal{H}
to some
v\in\mathcal{H}
. Then
v\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v
in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Proof.

As
(v_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, we find a weakly convergent subsequence with limit
w\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
. As
\mathcal{H}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}
is continuous, it is weakly continuous, and hence this subsequence is weakly convergent in
\mathcal{H}
to
w
but also to
v
, by assumption. Hence,
v=w\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and the subsequence principle ensures that
(v_{n})_{n}
itself is weakly convergent in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
v
.

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

From the Poincaré inequality (
10
) and the a priori estimate (
19
), we get

\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}+\|{\bf A}\grads u_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\lesssim_{d,s,\Omega,\beta}\|\grads u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\|f_{n}\|_{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)},

(26)

and analogous estimate holds for
{\bf B}
and
v_{n}
. Hence, we can conclude that
d_{s}
is well-defined, i.e.,
0\leq d_{s}({\bf A},{\bf B})<\infty
, for all
{\bf A},\,{\bf B}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
.
Since symmetry and triangle inequality are trivially satisfied, in order to prove that
d_{s}
is a metric, it remains to show that
d_{s}({\bf A},{\bf B})=0
implies
{\bf A}={\bf B}
a.e. on
\Omega
. (Note that on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\Omega

{\bf A}={\bf B}={\bf A}_{0}

For an arbitrary

define

, and denote by

the unique solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf B}\grads v)=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
v=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.\,

Take a subsequence of
(f_{n_{k}})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
f_{n_{k}}\longrightarrow f
in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
. Then

is the unique solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs\bigl({\bf A}\grads(u-u_{n_{k}})\bigr)=f-f_{n_{k}}&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\

\end{array}\right.\,

and by estimate (
26
), applied to

u_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}u\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf A}\grads u_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}{\bf A}\grads u.

Similarly,

v_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}v\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf B}\grads v_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}{\bf B}\grads v.

Since

, it follows that

{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}
and
{\bf A}\grads u_{n_{k}}={\bf B}\grads v_{n_{k}}

{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
. Therefore,
u=v
and
{\bf A}\grads u={\bf B}\grads v
, which further implies

({\bf A}-{\bf B})\grads u=0\quad\,\rm{a.e.\,in}\,\,\Omega.

Moreover, if we denote by
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega

{\bf A}-{\bf B}

. Therefore,

holds in
\tilde{\Omega}
, for any

.

Let

be arbitrary. By Lemma
2.12

\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
there exists

such that

Using this we obtain
{\bf A}={\bf B}
in
\tilde{\Omega}.

It is left to prove that the convergence in this metric is indeed
H^{s}
-convergence. Let
({\bf A}_{m})_{m}
be a sequence in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
which
H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
, and let

be the solutions to

respectively. By the definition of
H^{s}
-convergence, for every

,

u_{n}^{m}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}u_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf A}_{m}\grads u_{n}^{m}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}}{\bf A}\grads u_{n},\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

The above convergences and the compact embedding from Theorem

u_{n}^{m}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}}u_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf A}_{m}\grads u_{n}^{m}\xrightarrow{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}{\bf A}\grads u_{n},\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty.

This, together with the uniform convergence of the series in the definition of

, implies

.

Conversely, let
({\bf A}_{m})_{m}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
and
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
be such that

d_{s}({\bf A}_{m},{\bf A})\longrightarrow 0.

Let
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
and choose a subsequence of
(f_{n_{k}})
such that
f_{n_{k}}\to f
in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
.
Furthermore, let
u,\,u^{m},\,u_{n_{k}},\,u_{n_{k}}^{m}\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)

\displaystyle-\divs({\bf A}\grads u)

\displaystyle=f\,,

\displaystyle\qquad-\divs({\bf A}_{m}\grads u^{m})

\displaystyle=f\,,

\displaystyle-\divs({\bf A}\grads u_{n_{k}})

\displaystyle=f_{n_{k}}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad-\divs({\bf A}_{m}\grads u_{n_{k}}^{m})

respectively. Since

Since

and, uniformly with respect to

4
The one-dimensional case

The homogenisation in the classical setting, when
s=1
, and one space dimension (
d=1
) is rather simplified as the
H
-limit of a sequence coincides with its harmonic mean. This is mainly due to the fact that in one dimension operators
{\rm div}
and
\nabla
coincide, i.e.,
{\rm div}=\nabla=\frac{d}{dx}
, and thus, from
{\rm div}F=\frac{dF}{dx}=0
, for
F\in{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}
, we conclude
F={\rm const}
.
Indeed, for
A_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
,
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
and
f\in{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega)
,
using the above properties, the one-dimensional, static, diffusion problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\frac{d}{dx}\Big(A_{n}\frac{d}{dx}u_{n}\Big)=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u_{n}\in\rm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\\
\end{array}\right.

reduces to

A_{n}\frac{d}{dx}u_{n}=c_{n}-g(x),

where
g\in{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}
is such that
f=\frac{dg}{dx}
and
(c_{n})_{n}
is a bounded sequence in
{\mathbb{R}}
. This further implies strong
{\rm{L}}^{2}
-convergence of a subsequence
\sigma_{n}=A_{n}\frac{d}{dx}u_{n}
, which is a key step in the deduction that the
H
-limit is the harmonic mean
\underline{A}
of
A_{n}
, i.e., the inverse of the weak-
\ast
limit of
\frac{1}{A_{n}}
(for more details, see
[
3
, Sec. 1.2.3]
).

By Remark
3.3
, the same result holds for
H^{s}
-convergence in the one-dimensional case.
More precisely, for
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
, let
\Ds
denote operators
\divs
and
\grads
in the one dimensional setting (for
d=1
they coincide). Then, the corresponding one-dimensional, static, fractional diffusion problem is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Ds(A_{n}\Ds u_{n})=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u_{n}=0&{\rm in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

(31)

where
(A_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
. Let
A^{s}
denote the
H^{s}
-limit of the sequence
(A_{n})_{n}
, and let
A

be the
H
-limit of the restricted sequence
(A_{n}|_{\Omega})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
. Since
H
- and
H^{s}
-limits coincide a.e. on
\Omega
, we infer
that
A^{s}=A=\underline{A}
a.e. on
\Omega
. Consequently, the
H^{s}
-limit
A^{s}
is given by

A^{s}(x)=\begin{cases}\underline{A}(x),\quad x\in\Omega\\
A_{0}(x),\quad x\in\Omega^{c}\,.\end{cases}

Remark 4.1

Although both
H
- and
H^{s}
-limits (for
d=1
) are equal to
\underline{A}
on
\Omega
, there are some essential differences between the classical (
s=1
) and the fractional case (
s\in\langle 0,1\rangle
). Specifically, in the fractional case, we are unable to obtain strong
{\rm{L}}^{2}
-convergence (up to a subsequence) of the fluxes
\sigma_{n}^{s}:=A_{n}\Ds u_{n}
. We will elaborate on that in more detail.

For
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, Lemma
2.16
implies that there exists
g\in{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
f=\Ds g
. Then, problem (
31
) reduces to

\Ds(A_{n}\Ds u_{n}+g)=0\quad\text{in }{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}.

(32)

The same is true in the classical (
s=1
) case, just with
\frac{d}{dx}
instead of
\Ds
. However, in contrast to the classical case, where we can conclude that the expression in the brackets above is a sequence converging to a constant, in the fractional case the relation (
32
) is not sufficient to provide a strong convergence (up to a subsequence) of
(\sigma^{s}_{n})_{n}
in
{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
. More precisely, neither
\Ds F=0
implies
F=const.
, nor from
\Ds F_{n}=0
we can conclude the strong convergence of
F_{n}
, as we shall see in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.2

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be open and bounded.
Then there exists a bounded sequence
(F_{n})_{n}
in
{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\setminus\{0\}
such that, for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Ds F_{n}=0
in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, and
(F_{n})_{n}
does not have a strongly convergent subsequence. Consequently, the operator
\Ds:{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
has an infinite-dimensional kernel.

Proof.

Since
\Omega
is bounded, there exists
M>0
such that
\Omega\subseteq[-M,M]
. Let
w\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Omega)
be such that
{\rm supp\,}w\subseteq\langle M,\infty\rangle
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}w(x)\,dx=0
, i.e.
\widehat{w}(0)=0
.

If we define the sequence
w_{n}(x):=w(x-n)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
(w_{n})_{n}
in
{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Omega)

and
\widehat{w}_{n}(0)=\widehat{w}(0)=0
, since the Fourier transform of
w_{n}
reads

\widehat{w}_{n}(\xi)=e^{-in\xi}\widehat{w}(\xi)
. Moreover, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define
F_{n}
by

\widehat{F}_{n}(\xi):=\frac{\widehat{w}_{n}(\xi)}{i\xi|\xi|^{s-1}}=e^{-in\xi}\widehat{F}(\xi),\quad\widehat{F}(\xi):=\frac{\widehat{w}(\xi)}{i\xi|\xi|^{s-1}},\quad(\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}).

Let us first prove that
(F_{n})_{n}
is a bounded sequence in
{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.
Since
|F_{n}|=|F|
, it is sufficient to prove that
F\in{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.
From
w\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Omega)
, we have that
\widehat{w}
is a Schwartz function and, by definition of
w
,
\widehat{w}(0)=0
. This in addition implies

\widehat{w}(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(|\xi|)
, when
\xi\to 0
, hence there exist
\delta>0
and
m>0
such that
|\widehat{w}(\xi)|\leq m|\xi|
, when
0<|\xi|<\delta
. We have

\|\widehat{F}\|^{2}_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}=\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{|\widehat{w}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2s}}\,d\xi=\int\limits_{-\delta}^{\delta}\frac{|\widehat{w}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2s}}\,d\xi+\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\delta,\delta)}\frac{|\widehat{w}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2s}}\,d\xi\,.

For the first integral in the above sum we apply the above mentioned estimate of
\widehat{w}
around the origin:

\int\limits_{-\delta}^{\delta}\frac{|\widehat{w}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2s}}\,d\xi\leq 2m^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\delta}\xi^{2-2s}\,d\xi=2m^{2}\frac{\delta^{3-2s}}{3-2s}<\infty,

since
s<1
. Further on, for the second integral, it holds

\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\delta,\delta)}\frac{|\widehat{w}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2s}}\,d\xi\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{2s}}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\delta,\delta)}|\widehat{w}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{2s}}\|\widehat{w}\|^{2}_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}<\infty.

By the Plancherel theorem, it follows that
F\in{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, implying that
(F_{n})
is a bounded sequence in
{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, i.e.,

\|F_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}=\|F\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}<\infty
.

Let us now prove that
\Ds F_{n}=0
in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
. Using the Fourier transform property from Theorem
2.5
, we get
\widehat{\Ds F_{n}}(\xi)=i\xi|\xi|^{s-1}\widehat{F}_{n}=\widehat{w}_{n}
in
{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, which implies
\Ds F_{n}=w_{n}
a.e. in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Now, for every
\varphi\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
, since the supports of
\varphi
and
w_{n}
are mutually disjoint, it holds

{}_{{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}}\langle\Ds F_{n},\varphi\rangle_{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}=\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}}\Ds F_{n}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}}w_{n}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=0.

It remains to prove that
(F_{n})_{n}
does not have a strongly convergent subsequence. Notice first that, for any
\varphi\in{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, we have that
\widehat{F}\,\overline{\widehat{\varphi}}\in{\rm L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, and by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma it holds

\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}}\widehat{F}_{n}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\varphi}}(\xi)\,d\xi=\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-in\xi}\widehat{F}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\varphi}}(\xi)\,d\xi=\mathcal{F}\{\widehat{F}\,\overline{\widehat{\varphi}}\}(n)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

which implies
\widehat{F}_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}0
,
n\to\infty
.

If
(F_{n_{k}})_{k}
is a subsequence such that, for some
F^{\ast}\in{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
,
F_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}F^{*}
, then, from
\widehat{F}_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}0
it follows that
F^{*}=0
, which is in contradiction with
\|F_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}=\|F\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}}\neq 0
.

The existence of a bounded sequence
(F_{n})_{n}
in
\ker(\Ds)
which does not have a strongly convergent subsequence implies that the unit ball in
\ker(\Ds)
is not a compact set. Hence,
\ker(\Ds)
has infinite dimension.

\blacksquare

It is worth emphasising that the above lemma provides an intrinsic property of the (nonlocal) fractional derivative. It indicates that the operator
\Ds:{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
is more complex, compared to the classical derivative
\DD=\frac{d}{dx}:{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}\to{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega)
where we have
\dim(\ker(\DD))=1
. This illustrates why, in the one-dimensional fractional setting, there are some difficulties in deriving strong convergence of the fluxes
\sigma^{s}_{n}=A_{n}\Ds u_{n}
.

Remark 4.3

If we consider
\Ds:{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{\rm H}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}})
, then from the assumptions
F\in{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
and
\Ds F=0
in
{\rm H}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}})
, using the interaction with the Fourier transformation from Theorem
2.5
, we obtain
F=0
a.e. in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence, when
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}
, we have the same conclusion as in the local,
s=1
case.

5
H^{s}
-convergence on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

Although in the analysis of fractional diffusion problems we are looking for the solutions in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
, the nonlocality of the fractional gradient requires for the coefficients to be defined on the whole space
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. In
[
15
]
, the authors proposed a way to overcome this difficulty by considering the class
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
, with coefficients fixed outside of
\Omega
. As we can see from previous results, this approach is effective and, by connecting fractional and classical operators as well as fractional and classical Sobolev spaces, it gives a solid foundation for the development of the homogenisation theory in the fractional setting.

In this section, we expand this approach by considering the coefficients from
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, without imposing any additional conditions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\Omega
.

Remark 5.1

Here, and in the rest of the paper, we assume that the domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is open and bounded, and
that the Lebesgue measure of its boundary is equal to zero. This assumption on
\Omega
is crucial for our arguments, as it guarantees that the space
{{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
can be isomorphically decomposed as the direct sum:

{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega)}\oplus{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)}\,.

Moreover, we also have that
{\sf Cl\thinspace}_{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\partial\Omega)={{\rm L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
, which is in fact equivalent to the previous decomposition. Both identities are readily verified.
The decomposition above permits us to effectively divide the convergence analysis on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into two independent parts:
\Omega
and
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega
.

Let us close this remark by noting that we could equivalently require that
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is open, bounded and Jordan measurable
[
29
, Prop. 10.5.1]
.
In particular, the standard assumption that
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain implies that its boundary has zero measure.

A key ingredient for obtaining
H^{s}
-compactness in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is Theorem
2.11
.
Roughly speaking, the main idea is to consider separately cases when we are on
\Omega
and on
\Omega^{\text{c}}
, and to combine obtained results for
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
, strong convergence in the complement (
11
) and properties of weak-
\ast
convergence.

Remark 5.2

As already mentioned, for
U\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
open, the bounds in the definition of
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
are chosen in such a way that this set is closed under the
H
-limit. Let us verify that
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
is also closed under the arithmetic and harmonic mean of a sequence, i.e., for a sequence
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
we have
\overline{{\bf A}},\underline{{\bf A}}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
, where
\overline{{\bf A}}
is a weak-
\ast
limit of (a subsequence of)
({\bf A}_{n})
and
\underline{{\bf A}}
is the inverse of a weak-
\ast
limit of (a subsequence of)
{\bf A}_{n}^{-1}
.
Since
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
is equivalent to
{\bf A}^{-1}\in{\cal M}\left(\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{1}{\alpha};U\right)
, it suffices to show that inequalities (
17
) and (
18
) are preserved under the weak-
\ast
limit.

The expression on the left-hand side of the first inequality (
17
) in the definition of
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;U)
is linear in
{\bf A}
, and thus it is also weakly-
\ast
continuous, meaning that this inequality is preserved on the weak-
\ast
limit.

Regarding the second inequality (
18
), the left-hand side is similar as above, while the expression on the right-hand side is slightly more complicated, as it is a composition of linear functions and a quadratic function
u\mapsto u^{2}
,
{{\rm L}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\longrightarrow{{\rm L}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
. However, we can use weakly-
\ast
sequential lower semi-continuity of this function on
{{\rm L}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
to conclude that this inequality is also preserved in the weak-
\ast
limit.

Now we are able to prove
H^{s}
-compactness for the class
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, and to give (unique) representation of the
H^{s}
-limit via
H
-limit on
\Omega
and weak-
\ast
limit on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega
.
We first establish the uniqueness of the
H^{s}
-limit.

Proposition 5.3

The
H^{s}
-limit within
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is unique. More precisely,
if a sequence
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

H^{s}
-converges to both
{\bf A}
and
{\bf A}_{\infty}
(in
\Omega
), where
{\bf A},{\bf A}_{\infty}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, then
{\bf A}={\bf A}_{\infty}
a.e. in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

Proof.

Let
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
be a sequence in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
such that it

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
and
{\bf A}_{\infty}
in
\Omega
, where
{\bf A},{\bf A}_{\infty}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

Following the lines of the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 3.3]
, where uniqueness of the
H^{s}
-limit in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega,{\bf A}_{0})
is shown, we obtain

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\bf A}\grads\psi\cdot\Phi\,dx=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\bf A}_{\infty}\grads\psi\cdot\Phi\,dx,\quad\text{for every }\psi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\text{ and }\Phi\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})},

which implies

{\bf A}(x)\grads\psi(x)={\bf A}_{\infty}(x)\grads\psi(x),\quad\text{ for a.e. $x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}$ and for all $\psi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)$}.

This step differs from the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 3.3]
only in the use of Lemma
2.12
(a refined version of
[
28
, Lemma 4.3]
) to obtain

{\bf A}(x)={\bf A}_{\infty}(x),\quad\text{ for a.e. }x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

This concludes the proof of uniqueness.

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Assume that
{\bf A}_{n}|_{\Omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf A}|_{\Omega}
and
{\bf A}_{n}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}\xrightharpoonup{\ *\ }{\bf A}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}
, and let us show:

{\bf A}_{n}\text{ $H^{s}$-converges to ${\bf A}$ in $\Omega$}.

(33)

Let
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
and denote by
u_{n}
the unique weak solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n})=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u_{n}=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(34)

Since
(u_{n})_{n}
and
({\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n})_{n}
are bounded in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
, respectively, there exist joint subsequences (which we do not relabel),
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and
\sigma\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
such that

u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}u\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}\sigma,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Define

{\sf g}_{n}:={\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n}-{\bf A}\grads u,\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

It is enough to prove that
({\sf g}_{n})_{n}
weakly converges to zero in
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
, as then

\sigma={\bf A}\grads u\quad\text{a.e. in }{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

which further gives (
33
).

Since
({\sf g}_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
, we can test the weak convergence with functions from
{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Moreover, the assumptions on
\Omega
and Remark
5.1
imply that it suffices to consider functions
\varphi=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
, where
\varphi_{1}\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
\varphi_{2}\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Then, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi\,dx=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi_{1}\,dx+\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi_{2}\,dx.

Since
{\rm supp\,}\varphi_{1}\subseteq\Omega
, following the arguments given in the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi_{1}\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi_{1}\,dx=0.

Furthermore, since
\varphi_{2}\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, there exists open and bounded
\Omega_{1}\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{1}
and
{\rm supp\,}\varphi_{2}\subseteq({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{\text{c}}
. Hence, from Theorem
2.11
and assumption
{\bf A}_{n}|_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}\xrightharpoonup{\ \ast\ }{\bf A}|_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}
, it follows

{\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}(({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{c};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}{\bf A}\grads u,

which further implies
{\sf g}_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}(({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{c};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}0
, and we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi_{2}\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{c}}{\sf g}_{n}\cdot\varphi_{2}\,dx=0\,,

completing the argument.

Now, assume that
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
in
\Omega
, and define sequences
({\bf B}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
and
(\tilde{{\bf A}}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)
by

{\bf B}_{n}:={\bf A}_{n}|_{\Omega}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{{\bf A}}_{n}:={\bf A}_{n}|_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}.

From compactness of the
H
-convergence (Theorem
3.2
), it follows that there exist
{\bf B}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
and a subsequence
{\bf B}_{n^{\prime}}
, such that
{\bf B}_{n^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf B}
. Furthermore, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem implies compactness of the weak-
\ast
convergence, which, together with closedness of the weak-
\ast
limit in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)
(Remark
5.2
), gives the existence of
\tilde{{\bf A}}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)
and a subsequence
\tilde{{\bf A}}_{n^{\prime}}
(chosen jointly with the subsequence of
{\bf B}_{n}
) such that
\tilde{{\bf A}}_{n^{\prime}}\xrightharpoonup{\ \ast\ }\tilde{{\bf A}}
.
Thus, the above proved statement (
H
-convergence on
\Omega
and weak-
\ast
convergence on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega
imply
H^{s}
-convergence) and the fact that the measure of the boundary of
\Omega
is zero imply

{\bf A}_{n^{\prime}}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H^{s}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad\hat{{\bf A}}:=\begin{cases}{\bf B},\quad\text{on }\Omega\\
\tilde{{\bf A}},\quad\text{on }({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}\end{cases}.

Now, from the uniqueness of the
H^{s}
-limit provided by Proposition
5.3
, we conclude
{\bf A}=\hat{{\bf A}}
a.e. on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. In particular,
{\bf A}|_{\Omega}={\bf B}
and
{\bf A}|_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}=\tilde{{\bf A}}
. Hence,

{\bf A}_{n^{\prime}}|_{\Omega}={\bf B}_{n^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf B}={\bf A}|_{\Omega}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf A}_{n^{\prime}}|_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}=\tilde{{\bf A}}_{n^{\prime}}\xrightharpoonup{\ \ast\ }\tilde{{\bf A}}={\bf A}|_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}.

(35)

Furthermore, since both the
H
-limit and the weak-
\ast
limit do not depend on the chosen subsequences, we conclude that (
35
) holds for the whole sequence
{\bf A}_{n}
.

\blacksquare

From Theorem
1.3
, the compactness of the
H
-convergence and the weak-
\ast
convergence, we have that the compactness of
H^{s}
-convergence on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
follows directly. More precisely, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 5.4

For any
({\bf A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
there exists a subsequence
({\bf A}_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
such that
({\bf A}_{n_{k}})

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
.

6
Properties of
H^{s}
-topology on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

In this chapter, we present several fundamental properties of
H^{s}
-convergence on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. We begin with a Žikov type lemma, a fundamental tool in the homogenisation theory, see
[
47
, Theorem 5.2]
, where a similar statement was formulated for symmetric coefficients and
H
-convergence.

Proposition 6.1
(Žikov type Lemma.)

Let
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
and
{\bf v}\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
.
Assume that
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is such that

{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\ H^{s}\text{-converges to }{\bf A}^{\rm T}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),

and denote by
(u_{n})_{n}
the sequence in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
of solutions to

-\divs({\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v}))=f.

(36)

Then, there exists a subsequence of
(u_{n})_{n}
, which we do not relabel, such that

u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}u\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad{\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}{\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v}),

where
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
is the unique solution to

-\divs({\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v}))=f.

Proof.

By denoting
\tilde{f}_{n}=f+\divs({\bf A}_{n}{\bf v})\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, the sequence of problems (
36
) reduces to

-\divs({\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n})=\tilde{f}_{n}.

For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the above problem is well-posed and has a unique solution
u_{n}\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, since
{\bf A}_{n}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and since
f
and
{\bf v}
are fixed, we have that
(\tilde{f}_{n})_{n}
is a bounded sequence in
{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
. Hence, from the Poincaré inequality (
10
) and the a priori estimate (
19
), it follows that
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and
({\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v}))_{n}
is bounded in
{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
. Let
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and
{\bf q}\in{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
be such that (by passing to subsequences) we obtain

u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}u\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}{\bf q}.

By proving that
{\bf q}={\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})
, a.e. on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, we get the desired result. Having in mind Remark
5.1
and Theorem
1.3
, we shall consider separately the cases
x\in\Omega
and
x\in({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}}
.

First, let
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and let
\Omega_{1}\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
be an open and bounded set such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{1}
and
{\rm supp\,}\varphi\subseteq({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{\text{c}}
.
From the
H^{s}
-convergence of
{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}
to
{\bf A}^{\rm T}
, by Theorem
1.3
it follows that
{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\xrightharpoonup{*}{\bf A}^{\rm T}
on
({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}}
. This, together with
u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}u
and Theorem
2.11
, implies

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}}}{\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\cdot\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{\text{c}}}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\cdot{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega_{1})^{\text{c}}}(\grads u+{\bf v})\cdot{\bf A}^{\rm T}\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}}}{\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})\cdot\varphi\,dx\,.

Since
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{\text{c}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
was arbitrary, we conclude
{\bf q}={\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})
a.e. on
({\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)^{c}
.

It remains to prove
{\bf q}={\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})
a.e. on
\Omega
. Let
w_{0}\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
and define
g\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
by
g:=-\divs({\bf A}^{\rm T}\grads w_{0})
. We take
(w_{n})_{n}
in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
to be the sequence of solutions to

-\divs({\bf A}^{\rm T}_{n}\grads w_{n})=g.

Then, from the
H^{s}
-convergence of
{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}
to
{\bf A}^{\rm T}
and the choice of
g
, by Theorem
1.3
we get that

w_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)}w_{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\grads w_{n}\xrightharpoonup{{{\rm L}^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}}{\bf A}^{\rm T}\grads w_{0}.

Now, let
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. We have

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi{\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\cdot\grads w_{n}\,dx=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\cdot{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\grads w_{n}\,dx,

and by letting
n\to\infty
, and applying the fractional compensated compactness result, on the left-hand side we get

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi{\bf A}_{n}(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\cdot\grads w_{n}\,dx=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,{\bf q}\cdot\grads w_{0}\,dx,

while on the right-hand side

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi(\grads u_{n}+{\bf v})\cdot{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\grads w_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\grads u_{n}\cdot{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\grads w_{n}\,dx+\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,{\bf v}\cdot{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}\grads w_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\grads u\cdot{\bf A}^{\rm T}\grads w_{0}\,dx+\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,{\bf v}\cdot{\bf A}^{\rm T}\grads w_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,{\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})\cdot\grads w_{0}\,dx.

Hence, for every
\varphi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we get

\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,{\bf q}\cdot\grads w_{0}\,dx=\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi{\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})\cdot\grads w_{0}\,dx,

which implies

({\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})\cdot\grads w_{0})(x)=({\bf q}\cdot\grads w_{0})(x),\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

(37)

Since the set of all Lebesgue points of
({\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})-{\bf q})
, denoted by
\tilde{\Omega}
, is contained in the set of all Lebesgue points of
({\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})-{\bf q})\cdot\grads w_{0}
, for any
w_{0}\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
(and since
w_{0}\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
was arbitrary), we get that (
37
) holds in
\tilde{\Omega}
for any
w_{0}\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
By taking a suitable
w_{0}\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
for each
x\in\tilde{\Omega}
and applying Lemma
2.12
, we conclude

{\bf q}(x)={\bf A}(\grads u+{\bf v})(x),\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

\blacksquare

From Žikov’s lemma, by taking
{\bf v}=0
, we immediately obtain transpose invariance of the
H^{s}
-limit. More precisely, the following is valid.

Corollary 6.2

Let
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, and
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Then

{\bf A}_{n}

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
if and only if
{\bf A}_{n}^{\rm T}

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}^{\rm T}
.

Moreover, as a consequence of the fractional compensated compactness result and due to the local nature of weak-
\ast
convergence, the energy convergence property stated in Proposition
3.6
and the locality property from Proposition
3.4
remain to hold for sequences
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
in
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
In addition, there exists a metric on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
that generates
H^{s}
- topology on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
To be precise, let
d_{\ast}
be the weak-
\ast
metric on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)
, and let
d_{H}
be the
H
-metric on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
. Define
d:{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to[0,\infty\rangle
such that, for
{\bf A},{\bf B}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
,

d({\bf A},{\bf B})=d_{H}({\bf A}|_{\Omega},{\bf B}|_{\Omega})+d_{\ast}({\bf A}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega},{\bf B}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega}).

(38)

Since both
d_{H}
and
d_{\ast}
are metrics on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;\Omega)
and
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\sf Cl\thinspace}\Omega)
, respectively, we get that
d
is a well-defined metric on
{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Furthermore, by Proposition
5.3
and Theorem
1.3
, we get

{\bf A}_{n}\ H^{s}\text{-converges to }{\bf A}_{\infty}\quad\iff\quad d({\bf A}_{n},{\bf A}_{\infty})\to 0.

7
Schur topology

In this section only, the generic codomain of function spaces will be
\mathbb{C}
.

Given local coefficients
{\bf A}\in{\rm L}^{\infty}(U)^{d\times d}
for a measurable
U\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we can also consider them as a bounded operator
{\bf A}\in L({\rm L}^{2}(U)^{d})
by mapping
v\in{\rm L}^{2}(U)^{d}

to
{\bf A}(\cdot)v(\cdot)
. Applying, e.g., the Helmholtz decomposition, one can show that
{\rm L}^{\infty}(U)^{d\times d}\subsetneq L({\rm L}^{2}(U)^{d})
, and that, in general, the local homogenisation approaches fail if possibly nonlocal coefficients, i.e., elements of
L({\rm L}^{2}(U)^{d})
, are considered (cf., e.g.,
[
45
]
for details). In
[
45
]
, the following approach to homogenisation of such nonlocal coefficients was introduced and later refined in
[
46
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space (linear in the second argument),
\mathcal{H}_{0}
a closed subspace, and
\mathcal{H}_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Then, we can identify a bounded operator

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n})=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u_{n}=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega\\
\end{array}\right.

(43)

converges weakly in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
to the solution
u
of the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}\grads u)=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(44)

We shall prove that
G^{s}
- and
H^{s}
-convergence are equivalent on
{\cal M}^{\textnormal{sym}}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. To this end, we require the following additional result.

Lemma 8.2

Let
{\bf A},{\bf B}\in{{\rm L}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})}
be symmetric matrix functions and
U\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
be an open set. Assume that for all
\xi\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

{\bf A}(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf B}(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq 0,\quad\text{ for a.e. }x\in U

and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\bf A}(x)\grads\psi(x)\cdot\grads\psi(x)\,dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\bf B}(x)\grads\psi(x)\cdot\grads\psi(x)\,dx,\quad\psi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(U)\,.

(45)

Then

{\bf A}(x)={\bf B}(x),\text{ for a.e. }x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Proof.

The identity
{\bf A}(x)={\bf B}(x)
for a.e.
x\in U
was established in
[
15
, Lemma 4.2]
. To obtain the corresponding equality on
U^{\text{c}}
, we employ the same methodological approach as in the aforementioned result. More precisely, it is enough to notice that for every

\varphi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and for every
x_{0}\in U^{\textrm{c}}
there exist
r_{0}\in\langle 0,\infty\rangle
and
x_{1}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
such that for every
r\in\langle r_{0},\infty\rangle
we have

\varphi_{x_{0},x_{1},r}(x):=\varphi(r(x-x_{0})-x_{1})\in{\rm C}_{c}^{\infty}(U).

Moreover, from
s
-homogeneity and translational invariance of the fractional gradient, we obtain

\grads\varphi_{x_{0},x_{1},r}(x)=r^{s}\grads\varphi(r(x-x_{0})-x_{1}).

The statement now follows by applying the same steps as in the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 4.2]
.
\blacksquare

Proposition 8.3
(The equivalence between
H^{s}
- and
G^{s}
-convergence)

Let
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
be a sequence in
{\cal M}^{\textnormal{sym}}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Then
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
if and only if
{\bf A}
is symmetric and
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}

G^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
.

Proof.

If
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}

H^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
, then by Proposition
5.3
and Corollary
6.2
,
{\bf A}
is symmetric and, therefore,
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
also
G^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}
.

On the other hand, assume that
{\bf A}_{n}

G^{s}
-converges to
{\bf A}\in{\cal M}^{\textnormal{sym}}(\alpha,\beta;{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Then, for every
f\in{{\rm H}^{-s}(\Omega)}
, the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
of solutions of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}_{n}\grads u_{n})=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u_{n}=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega\\
\end{array}\right.

(46)

converges weakly in
{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
to the solution
u
of the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf A}\grads u)=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(47)

According to Corollary
5.4
, there exists a subsequence of
({\bf A}_{n})_{n}
that
H^{s}
-converges to a limit
{\bf B}
, which is symmetric by virtue of Corollary
6.2
. Consequently, the limit function
u
of the corresponding subsequence of solutions
(u_{n})_{n}
of (
46
) is the unique solution in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\divs({\bf B}\grads u)=f&\rm{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&{\rm in}\,\,{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(48)

Subtracting the two homogenised equations (
47
) and (
48
), we obtain

-\divs({\bf A}-{\bf B})\grads u=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

which in a weak formulation reads

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}({\bf A}-{\bf B})\grads u\cdot\grads v=0,\quad v\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega).

By the arbitrariness of
f
, this identity also holds for any
u\in{\rm H}_{0}^{s}(\Omega)
. Thus, using Lemma
8.2
it follows that
{\bf A}={\bf B}
a.e. in

, which concludes the proof.

\blacksquare

9
Conclusion

In this note, we discussed homogenisation problems in the context of fractional diffusion with general non-periodic highly oscillatory conductivities in divergence form. The spatial derivatives are provided by bounded domain variants of operators given on the whole space by their Fourier symbols. In order to capture non-periodic settings, as it is done classically, the corresponding version of
H
-convergence was used, called
H^{s}
-convergence here which was introduced in
[
15
]
under a different name. The main results of the present research characterises
H^{s}
-convergence in terms of
H
-convergence and convergence in the weak-* topology. Moreover, these results are put into perspective of the notion of the Schur topology (two seemingly different Schur topologies were found that both characterise
H^{s}
-convergence). As a result, a homogenisation problem for the corresponding fractional heat equation has been solved. The final application provides a perspective to symmetric coefficients and characterises the suitable variant of
G
-convergence by mere energy convergence.

The results are based on compactness results, Poincaré-type inequalities, and a suitable version of Žikov’s lemma recently used for the description of local
H
-convergence in terms of nonlocal
H
-convergence, i.e., convergence with respect to an appropriate Schur topology.

In comparison to earlier research, the improvement concerns the incorporation of the one-dimensional case, the possibility of introducing coefficients oscillating on the whole of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, the complete description of
H^{s}
/
G^{s}
-convergence in terms of known, local topologies, the characterisation in terms of the Schur topology, and their application to a time-dependent partial differential equation.
