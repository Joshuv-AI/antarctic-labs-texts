Kinetic analysis and characterization of the simultaneous reduction–sintering of NiO–Fe2O3 nanoprecursors for ferronickel alloy production
Authors: K. S. Abdel Halim, A. A. El-Geassy, M. I. Nasr, Mohamed Ramadan, A. Hussein, Abourehab Hammam, Abdulaziz S. Alghamdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ni–Fe alloys with varying nickel contents (5%, 10%, 20%, and 30%) were successfully synthesized through a novel simultaneous reduction–sintering of nano-sized NiO–Fe2O3 precursors. Non-isothermal reduction was performed in hydrogen gas up to 1000 °C at varying heating rates (10–25 °C/min), followed by sintering in pure argon atmosphere at temperatures between 1000 °C and 1300 °C. Thermal analyses (TG–DTA), X-ray diffraction, and scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray analysis identified complex, multi-step reduction reactions and phase transformations influenced by reduction temperature, heating rate and precursor composition. Kinetic analyses indicated a dual control mechanism dominated initially by combined gaseous diffusion and interfacial reactions, transitioning to interfacial chemical reactions at higher temperatures. Sintering characteristics of compacts derived from precursors containing 5–30% NiO–Fe2O3 were examined at temperatures from 1000 to 1300°C for 60 min in pure argon. Increased Ni content promoted the formation of intermetallic phases (Fe7Ni3 and Fe3Ni), enhancing compositional homogeneity and reducing grain boundary visibility. Higher sintering temperatures notably improved grain growth, densification, intermetallic formation, reduced porosity, and increased micro-hardness. It was found that the combination of 20–30 wt% NiO with reduction at 900 °C followed by sintering at 1300 °C for 60 min yielded the best overall performance of synthesis Ni–Fe alloys. This optimization is making these materials more suitable for structural and functional applications where high strength and density are required.

Introduction

Nickel–iron (Ni–Fe) alloys have attracted considerable attention due to their superior magnetic, mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties, making them ideal for numerous advanced industrial and engineering applications1–3. They offer good thermal shock resistance, high corrosion resistance, and high mechanical strength, making them highly attractive for various technological applications, including magnetic materials, electronics, energy storage, catalysis, and structural components4–7. The characteristics of these alloys depend significantly on their microstructure and composition8–10. Ferronickel alloys are widely used in steel industry (stainless steel, alloy steels, and heat-resistant steels), electronic devices, turbines, and energy applications (SOFC batteries). Furthermore, ferronickel alloys can be alloyed with other metals such as Cu, Al, Ti, and Mn to produce different grades of alloys with advanced applications11–16.

Conventionally, Ni–Fe alloys are synthesized through traditional metallurgical techniques such as casting, mechanical alloying, and electrodeposition. However, these methods often face challenges such as compositional inhomogeneity, grain growth, and undesired phase transformations, which can adversely affect alloy properties17–20. The current work developed powder treatment methods to fabricate Ni–Fe alloys from nano-sized metal oxides by novel technology depends mainly on reduction and sintering processes using environmentally friendly hydrogen gas instead of polluting fossil fuels. Such an efficient alternative technique offers enhanced control over the alloy’s microstructure and homogeneity at the nanoscale level21–25. However, the powder treatment methodology with hydrogen contributes to worldwide techniques replacing utilization of fossil carbon in steel making processes that is currently causing considerable CO2 pollution to the environment26,27. Among the emerging technologies, the use of hydrogen as an alternative reducing agent has garnered particular attention as a promising pathway for cleaner steel production. Accordingly, ferronickel alloys can be synthesized through the hydrogen reduction of nickel oxide–iron oxide composites. This reduction-based approach offers a simple and cost-effective method for producing various ferroalloys, including Ni–Fe, Ni–Cr–Fe, and Ni–Co–Fe systems14,15. Recent developments have highlighted the advantages of reduction-sintering techniques using nano-sized oxide precursors, enabling better control over phase purity, grain size, and microstructural uniformity. Such methods offer improved control of the alloy’s physical properties, reducing typical defects associated with conventional synthesis routes. The composition of the produced alloys is often a key element to control the mechanical, chemical and physical properties of the synthesized alloys.

The reduction of Fe2O3/NiO composites involves a series of complex, stepwise reactions that proceed through the formation of various intermediate oxides and metal ferrites28,29. The reduction mechanism and kinetics are governed by various factors including temperature, particle size, precursor composition, and heating rates. Previous studies demonstrated that the nanoscale dimensions of oxide precursors significantly enhance the reaction kinetics due to higher surface area and improved gas–solid interaction30–33. Although the reduction technique is well established for producing metallic materials, the use of nano-sized metal oxides as starting precursors offers the potential to synthesize ferroalloy particles with unique physical and mechanical properties, enhancing their applicability in advanced technologies such as energy systems, sensors, and electronics. By optimizing key process parameters such as temperature, Ni content, and sintering time, high performance ferronickel materials with uniform microstructures can be obtained. These tailored materials show promise for incorporation into advanced systems aimed at producing liquid energy carriers, conducting particle separation, oxygen generation, low-CO2 power generation, and advancing hydrogen technologies14.

This study investigates the simultaneous reduction and sintering of nano-sized NiO–Fe2O3 precursors to synthesize Ni–Fe alloys with varying nickel contents. The work systematically examines the effects of heating rate, precursor composition, and sintering temperature on reduction kinetics, phase evolution, and the structural and mechanical characteristics of the resulting alloys. A fundamental kinetics study was conducted to assess the reduction mechanisms of nano-sized NiO–Fe2O3, aiming to identify optimal conditions for the effective utilization of nanomaterials in alloy fabrication.

Materials and experimental techniques

In this study, Ni–Fe alloys containing 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30% Ni were synthesized via simultaneous reduction-sintering of nano-sized NiO–Fe2O3 precursors. The samples were prepared by thoroughly mixing predetermined molar ratios of nano-sized NiO and Fe2O3 powders. Non-isothermal reduction of these precursors was conducted under hydrogen gas flow up to 1000 °C using various heating rates. Subsequent sintering of reduced precursors occurred at 1000, 1100, 1200, or 1300 °C for 60 min in pure argon atmosphere. Detailed descriptions of raw materials, reduction, and sintering procedures follow.

Raw materials

Analytical grade NiO and Fe2O3 powders (purity > 99.8%, grain size < 20 nm) were utilized. NiO nanoparticles were obtained from Guangzhou Hongwu Material Technology Co., Ltd., China, and Fe2O3 was sourced from BDH, UK. Precursors for Ni–Fe alloys with varying Ni contents were prepared by mixing calculated molar ratios of NiO and Fe2O3 in an agate mortar followed by ball milling to ensure homogeneity. The oxide precursor mixtures were ball-milled for 6 h at 200 rpm in a planetary ball mill using a ball-to-powder weight ratio of 10:1. Alumina balls were employed, and the milling was performed under ambient conditions. The mechanical activation during milling may have promoted limited solid-state reactions at the particle interfaces, which explains the minor presence of the NiFe2O4 phase observed in the milled powders. The raw materials and resulting precursors underwent physical and chemical characterization prior to their use in reduction-sintering process.

Reduction of precursors

Nano-sized NiO, Fe2O3, and mixed NiO–Fe2O3 precursors were nonisothermally reduced in pure hydrogen up to 1000 °C using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG-DTA, STA-504, USA). The recorded TG profile was used to monitor the reduction reactions, while the DTA profile was used to monitor the phase transformations during reduction reactions. Samples (~ 100 mg) were placed in alumina crucibles (d = 6 mm, h = 8 mm) alongside Al2O3 reference powder. The system was purged initially with pure argon gas for 15 min, then heated to 1000 °C under a 30 ml/min hydrogen flow. The reduction experiments conducted using the TG–DTA apparatus were performed at heating rates of 10, 15, 20, and 25 °C/min to establish the kinetic model for the non-isothermal reduction process. The preliminary reduction tests indicated that 30 ml/min of H2 gas was sufficient to overcome the gas boundary layer effect around the sample. The reduction reaction was continued till the sample reached a constant weight showing no further mass loss could be detected. At the end of experiment, the sample was cooled down to room temperature in a purified Ar gas, then kept for the subsequent measurements. Simultaneous reduction-sintering processes

Simultaneous reduction–sintering experiments were conducted to synthesize Ni–Fe alloys from mixed oxide precursor compacts. Uniformly shaped cylindrical compacts were prepared by thoroughly mixing nano-sized NiO–Fe2O3 powders. To 100 g of precursor, 6% distilled water was added and blended in an agate mortar to facilitate compaction. Approximately 2 g of the moistened mixture were pressed into a stainless-steel cylindrical mold (Ø 10 mm × 12 mm) using a Sealey bench-type hydraulic press (Suffolk, UK) under a 10 kN load, producing a pressure of ≈127 MPa. The compacts were then dried at 120 °C for 24 h. In the simultaneous reduction–sintering process, the precursor compacts were initially nonisothermally reduced in hydrogen using a computer-controlled horizontal tube furnace equipped with silicon carbide heating elements. The samples, placed in fused alumina boats, were positioned in the center of the furnace’s hot zone. After purging with purified argon for 15 min, the gas was switched to hydrogen (500 ml/min), and the temperature was ramped to 900 °C at 10 °C/min. The compacts were held at this temperature for 10 min to complete the reduction step. Subsequently, sintering was carried out by raising the furnace temperature to predetermined levels (1000, 1100, 1200, and 1300 °C) under a continuous flow of purified argon. Each compact was maintained at the target sintering temperature for 60 min before being cooled to room temperature at a rate of 10 °C/min to prevent micro-crack formation.

Characterization of materials

Raw materials, along with partially and fully reduced precursors and sintered compacts, were subjected to comprehensive physical and chemical characterization. X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical Empyrean, Eindhoven, Netherlands) was employed to identify the phases formed during the non-isothermal reduction and subsequent sintering processes. The morphology and grain structure of the samples were examined using a scanning electron microscope equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SE-EDAX; JEOL JSM-5410, Tokyo, Japan). Micro-hardness measurements of the sintered phases were performed using a Vickers micro-hardness tester (Shimadzu HMV-2, Japan). Additionally, total porosity, pore size distribution, and both bulk and apparent densities were determined using a high-pressure mercury intrusion porosimeter (MIP; PoreSizer 9320, Micromeritics Instrument Corp., USA).

Results and discussion

Thermal treatment of raw materials

The TG-DTA technique was employed to monitor the thermal behavior of pure Fe2O3, pure NiO, and 5–30% NiO–Fe2O3 precursors. The thermal reduction was carried out up to 1000 °C in a continuous flow of purified hydrogen gas. The thermogravimetric (TG) profiles were used to calculate the reduction degree at various temperatures, while the differential thermal analysis (DTA) profiles provided insights into phase transformation behavior. Reduction experiments were conducted at heating rates of 10, 15, 20, and 25 °C/min. Figure 2a–d shows the TG and DTA profiles for nano-sized NiO, Fe2O3, 5% NiO– Fe2O3, and 30% NiO–Fe2O3 precursors, respectively, at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The TG curve for pure NiO (Fig. 2a) reveals an initial 7% weight loss at around 120 °C due to moisture removal. As the temperature increased to approximately 380 °C, NiO was reduced to metallic Ni in a single-step reaction, resulting in a 20.5% weight loss. The corresponding DTA profile shows an endothermic peak between 120 and 150 °C (moisture release), followed by a broad exothermic peak at 380–420 °C attributed to the reduction reaction NiO + H2 → Ni + H2O. Figure 2b displays the thermal behavior of pure Fe2O3. The TG curve indicates an initial dehydration step at 120–150 °C, followed by sequential reductions: Fe2O3 to Fe3O4 at 430 °C (3% weight loss), Fe3O4 to wüstite (FexO) at 600 °C (6.9% weight loss), and FexO to α-Fe at 780 °C (20.6% total weight loss). No further weight loss was observed up to 915 °C, where the α-Fe to γ-Fe transformation occurs22. The DTA profile confirms these transitions, showing an exothermic peak at 430 °C for Fe2O3 to Fe3O4 and three subsequent endothermic peaks: Fe3O4 to FexO (570–620 °C), FexO to α-Fe (780 °C), and α-Fe to γ-Fe (≥ 915 °C). While the reduction to α-Fe involves oxygen loss, the α-Fe to γ-Fe transition reflects lattice rearrangement from a body-centered cubic to a face-centered cubic structure. Figures 2c and d illustrate the TG–DTA profiles for the 5% and 30% NiO–Fe2O3 precursors. The TG curves reveal three distinct reduction stages: initial reduction of NiO to Ni and Fe2O3 to Fe3O4 below 380 °C, a second stage at ~ 420 °C, and a final step around 730 °C corresponding to the reduction of FexO to α-Fe. The DTA profiles show that reduction behavior depends on the NiO content. For both precursors, an exothermic peak was observed below 420 °C, attributed to simultaneous reduction of NiO and Fe2O3. Endothermic peaks at 615 °C and 720 °C correspond to the reduction of Fe3O4 to FexO and FexO to α-Fe, respectively. Effect of reduction reaction conditions

Pure metal oxides (NiO, Fe2O3) and mixed NiO–Fe2O3 precursors containing 5–30% NiO were nonisothermally reduced in hydrogen up to 1000 °C. The effects of heating rate and precursor composition were thoroughly investigated to elucidate the reduction reaction mechanisms. Reduction experiments were conducted at heating rates of 10, 15, 20, and 25 °C/min. Figure 5 illustrates the reduction extent as a function of temperature for the various precursors (5%, 10%, 20%, and 30% NiO–Fe2O3) under different heating conditions. In all cases, the reduction proceeded in two distinct stages, with reduction rates increasing at lower heating rates. The extent and behavior of each step were strongly dependent on the NiO content in the precursor. The first reduction stage began between 320–350 °C and continued up to approximately 420–500 °C. This stage corresponded to the reduction of NiO to Ni and the partial reduction of Fe2O3 to magnetite (Fe3O4). Sintering characteristics

Precursors containing compacts made from nano-sized particles containing 5%, 10%, 20% and 30% NiO–Fe2O3 were thoroughly mixed for 6 h in a ball mill followed by compact formation. These precursor compacts underwent non-isothermal reduction up to 900 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in flowing hydrogen, serving as reference samples for subsequent sintering experiments. Figure 13a and b illustrate XRD patterns and SEM images, respectively, of the 20% NiO–Fe2O3 compacts reduced at 900 °C. The experimental XRD d-spacings and peak intensities were carefully compared with the standard values from the PDF database to confirm phase identification. The observed shifts in diffraction peaks are consistent with Vegard’s law, indicating the formation of a continuous Fe–Ni solid solution in which the lattice parameter varies linearly with increasing Ni content. As the sintering temperature increased, the rate of solid-state diffusion was enhanced, promoting the growth and formation of intermetallics. Similarly, increasing the Ni content in the compacts led to higher molar fractions of intermetallic phases. Based on the phase analysis presented in Table 3, the solid solution (Fe,Ni) was identified as the predominant phase, together with stable α-Fe and minor intermetallic phases Fe7Ni3 and/or Fe3Ni. The coexistence of the solid solution and α-Fe indicates partial homogenization, which is typical of solid-state sintering systems where diffusion distances limit complete alloying. The proportion of intermetallic phases increased with increasing Ni content. This XRD phase profile reflects a diffusion-controlled reduction–sintering mechanism. The emergence of intermetallic phases with increasing Ni concentration and temperature suggests localized diffusion-controlled reactions, where Ni-enriched regions undergo ordering transformations at intergranular boundaries. This composition-dependent transformation aligns with the Fe–Ni binary phase diagram, in which higher Ni concentrations promote intermetallic ordering at elevated temperatures. Notably, no free nickel phase was detected in any of the XRD patterns, confirming that Ni was completely consumed in forming alloy phases.

When the Ni content increases to 30 wt% Ni at the same temperature (Fig. 18b), Ni still fails to homogenize fully with Fe. Although Ni still forms Ni-rich islands, diffusion has partially homogenized these islands into the Fe matrix. Consequently, the Ni-rich zones are finer and more evenly spaced, as shown by higher but narrower Ni peaks, while Fe intensity dips correspondingly at those locations. Overall, the Ni:Fe intensity ratio along the scan closely matches the nominal 30:70 composition. This finer Ni dispersion is expected to improve hardness, mechanical integrity, and magnetic permeability relative to the coarse Ni clusters observed after sintering at 1200 °C. The results indicate that total porosity consistently decreased with increasing sintering temperature across all compositions. At any given temperature, compacts with higher Ni concentrations exhibited enhanced grain sintering and densification, resulting in lower porosity levels. For example, the 5% NiO–Fe2O3 compact exhibited a reduction in porosity from 32.15% at 1000 °C to 14.32% at 1300 °C. This trend is attributed to enhanced particle bonding, densification, and grain coalescence at elevated temperatures. Additionally, higher Ni content led to lower porosity at each sintering temperature. At 1300 °C, porosity decreased from 14.32% in 5% NiO–Fe2O3 to only 8.07% in 30% NiO–Fe2O3. The improved sintering performance with increased Ni content may be due to nickel’s enhanced diffusivity and its role in promoting grain rearrangement and bonding. This suggests that Ni content plays a significant role in promoting compact densification during sintering.

Similarly, micro-hardness measurements revealed a parallel trend to porosity. Values increased with both sintering temperature and Ni content. For instance, the micro-hardness of the 30% NiO–Fe2O3 compact increased from 240 Hv at 1000°C to 417 Hv at 1300°C. This is a direct consequence of the improved densification and formation of intermetallic phases, such as FeNi3 and Fe₇Ni3, which contribute to mechanical strengthening. In contrast, lower Ni content and lower temperatures resulted in reduced hardness due to the presence of more porous and less compacted structures. In general, the enhancement in hardness can be attributed to the improved structural integrity and grain bonding induced by higher thermal energy and nickel content. Overall, the data confirm that both Ni content and sintering temperature significantly influence the microstructure and mechanical performance of the Ni–Fe alloys, leading to denser materials with lower porosity and higher hardness.

Conclusions

This study successfully synthesized Ni–Fe alloys from nano-sized particles of NiO–Fe2O3 precursors through controlled simultaneous reduction-sintering methods. Reduction behaviour demonstrated significant dependency on heating rate, temperature, and precursor composition, proceeding in multiple steps with distinct phase transformations. Kinetic modeling revealed a transition from combined gaseous diffusion and interfacial chemical reaction control at lower temperatures to purely interfacial chemical reaction mechanisms at higher temperatures. The kinetic analysis provided insights into the complex interplay between diffusion dynamics and reaction kinetics, underscoring the critical role of composition in alloy formation. These findings contribute to optimizing production parameters for tailored properties in Ni–Fe alloy applications.

On the other hand, the present work examined the sintering characteristics of Ni–Fe compacts derived from precursors containing 5–30% NiO–Fe2O3. Initial compacts were reduced in hydrogen at 900 °C, producing FeNi3, Fe0.6Ni0.4, and α-Fe phases with limited grain growth. Sintering experiments conducted at temperatures of 1000–1300 °C in purified argon atmosphere revealed that increasing Ni content enhanced the formation and molar ratios of intermetallic phases such as Fe7Ni3, and Fe3Ni, reducing the presence of metallic iron. SEM-EDAX analyses confirmed improved homogenization and reduced grain boundary visibility at higher Ni levels. Increasing sintering temperature led to more pronounced grain growth, densification, and enhanced intermetallic formation, reducing porosity and increasing micro-hardness significantly (≈343 Hv for 20% Ni and ≈417 Hv for 30% Ni at 1300 °C). Optimization of Ni content and sintering temperature effectively governs the phase composition, morphology, and mechanical properties of Ni–Fe alloys. The combination of 20–30 wt% NiO with reduction at 900 °C followed by sintering at 1300 °C for 60 min yielded the best overall performance. These conditions produced dense, homogeneous, and high-strength Ni–Fe alloys derived from NiO–Fe2O3 nanoprecursors through the powder technology route.

The present work lays a solid foundation for future research in the Ni–Fe alloy system. The established reduction–sintering correlation and kinetic modeling can be further extended to explore nanostructured or doped Ni–Fe systems, mechanical alloying routes, or additive manufacturing–based sintering under controlled atmospheres. Moreover, the quantitative relationships between composition, reduction kinetics, and mechanical properties provide valuable input for thermodynamic simulations and predictive process optimization in subsequent studies. The results can be utilized in designing next-generation Ni–Fe alloys with tailored microstructures and multifunctional performance suitable for energy, magnetic, and structural applications.
