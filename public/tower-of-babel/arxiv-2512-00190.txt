On the nullspace of split graphs
Daniel A. Jaume, Victor N. Schvöllner, Cristian Panelo, Kevin Pereyra

Abstract
We study the nullspace of the adjacency matrix of split graphs, whose vertex set can be partitioned into a clique and an independent set. We introduce the clique-kernel , a subspace that decides whether clique vertices lie in the support of a kernel eigenvector, and we prove that its dimension is at most one. This yields the the formula nl ⁡ ( S ​ p ) = nl ⁡ ( R ) + dim ( Cker ⁡ ( S ​ p ) ) , \nul(Sp)=\nul(R)+\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}), which fully describes the nullity of a split graph in terms of the biadjacency submatrix R R . We also analyze unbalanced split graphs through the concept of swing vertices and characterize the structure of their kernel supports. Furthermore, we study the behavior of the nullspace under Tyshkevich composition and derive a closed formula for the determinant. These results provide a unified algebraic framework for understanding when a split graph is singular and how its combinatorial structure determines its nullspace.

Introduction

The study of spectral properties of graphs, particularly the nullspace of the adjacency matrix, has emerged as a fundamental area in algebraic graph theory with significant implications for both theoretical mathematics and applied sciences. A graph
G
is said to be
singular
if its adjacency matrix
A(G)
is singular, that is, if the dimension of the nullspace of
A(G)
is at least one. The
nullity

\nul(G)
of a graph
G
is the multiplicity of zero as an eigenvalue of
A(G)
or, equivalently, the dimension of the nullspace of
A(G)
. Understanding the structure of singular graphs and characterizing their nullspace has been a subject of intensive investigation since 1957 when von Collatz and Sinogowitz posed the problem, see
[
20
]
.

In her seminal work
[
16
]
Sciriha introduced the notion of core vertices (those vertices whose entries in a kernel eigenvector are nonzero) and laid the foundations for the study of singular graphs. This approach led to a systematic characterization of singular graphs in terms of minimal configurations and core structures. Subsequently, numerous researchers have contributed to this framework, including studies on nut graphs (core graphs of nullity one)
[
15
]
, maximal core sizes
[
17
]
, and the relationship between core and Fiedler vertices
[
1
]
. Jaume and Molina, see
[
11
]
, presented a decomposition of trees in terms of the supported vertices, which turn out to be equivalent to the classical Edmond-Gallai decomposition, see
[
12
]
.

Graphs whose vertex set can be partitioned into a clique and an independent set were first singled out by Földes and Hammer in 1977,
(see
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
), under the name

contain an induced copy of

,

or

(
[
7
]
).
The term “split” was coined to stress that the vertex set is split into two sets whose induced subgraphs are as dissimilar as possible.
Subsequent monographs of Golumbic
[
8
]
and Mahadev–Peled
[
13
]
embedded the class into the wider hierarchy of perfect graphs and made the notion standard in graph theory. Many NP-hard problems that are intractable for general graphs admit efficient algorithms when restricted to split graphs
[
9
]
. Moreover, split graphs can be recognized in linear time, and classical problems such as finding maximum cliques and maximum independent sets can be solved efficiently on this class

The spectral properties of split graphs have received considerable attention. Threshold graphs, which form a proper subclass of split graphs characterized by the absence of induced
P_{4}
,
C_{4}
, and
2K_{2}

[
13
]
, have been extensively studied from a spectral perspective. Sciriha
[
18
]
investigated the spectrum of threshold graphs and established that singular threshold graphs possess kernel eigenvectors with at most two nonzero entries. Recent work by Panelo, Jaume, and Machado Toledo
[
14
]
on the core-nilpotent decomposition of threshold graphs further illuminates the algebraic structure of these important graph families.

Despite the rich literature on both singular graphs and split graphs, a comprehensive study of the nullspace structure specifically for split graphs has been lacking. The block structure of the adjacency matrix of a split graph
Sp
,

A(Sp)=\begin{pmatrix}J-I&R\\
R^{t}&0\end{pmatrix},

where

is the all-ones matrix,
I
is the identity matrix, and
R
encodes adjacencies between the clique
K
and independent set
S

. This structure allows us to decompose the nullspace problem into more manageable components and to establish clear relationships between the rank properties of
R
and the nullity of
Sp

In this paper, we provide a thorough investigation of the nullspace of split graphs, establishing several new results that characterize when a split graph is singular and describing the structure of its kernel eigenvectors. Our main contributions include:

1.

A complete characterization of the support of kernel eigenvectors (
Section

3
), showing that for many split graphs, the support lies entirely within the independent set
S
. We establish necessary and sufficient conditions for when clique vertices can appear in the support.

2.

A detailed analysis of
unbalanced
split graphs (
Section

4
), i.e., those whose s-partition is not unique. We prove that the nullspace structure depends critically on whether the set of swing vertices (those which can belong to either part of an s-partition) forms a clique or an independent set.

3.

For split graphs with supported clique vertices (
Section

5
), we prove that the nullity equals
\nul(R)+1
when the support intersects the clique non-trivially, and we provide explicit formulas relating kernel eigenvectors to the adjugate matrix when
\nul(Sp)=1
(
Section

6
).

4.

Applications to the Tyshkevich composition (
Section

7
),
demonstrating how our nullspace characterizations extend to composite structures.

5.

An explicit determinant formula for split graphs (
Section

8
) expressed in terms of the matrix

using the Schur complement and the matrix determinant lemma, which facilitates computational verification of singularity.

Our results extend and complement existing work on singular graphs by exploiting the special structure of split graphs. While Sciriha’s general theory
[
16
]
applies to arbitrary graphs, the block decomposition of split graph adjacency matrices enables us to obtain more precise characterizations. Furthermore, our treatment of unbalanced split graphs and swing vertices reveals phenomena that have not been systematically explored in the literature on singular graphs.

When studying split graphs one is naturally led to investigate the column space of the biadjacency matrix

Lemma 3.1

Let

Therefore,

(
\Rightarrow

(
\Leftarrow

The relationship between the sizes of the clique and independent set in a split graph has implications for its singularity. The following result establishes simple combinatorial criteria that guarantee when a split graph must be singular based solely on the cardinalities of its s-partition. In particular, we show that an imbalance in favor of the independent set always forces singularity, while in the balanced case, the singularity of the entire graph reduces to the singularity of the biadjacency matrix. This provides a bridge between the graph’s global spectral properties and the local structure encoded in the matrix
R

.

Let

(2)

(

Regularity conditions within the clique or independent set impose strong constraints on the nullspace structure of a split graph. This theorem reveals how degree homogeneity among clique vertices confines the support entirely to the independent set, while degree homogeneity among independent vertices forces a specific linear dependence in the kernel of
R
. These results demonstrate how local symmetry conditions—all vertices in one part having identical degrees—propagate to global spectral properties, offering combinatorial tools to control the location of support vertices and the structure of the nullspace.

Theorem 3.6

Let

be a connected split graph.

1.

If

2.

they are all mutually adjacent (each has degree
|K|-1
within
K
). Thus, there exists
d\geq 0
such that
|N(x)\cap S|=d
for all
x\in K
.

Since
Sp
is connected and
S
is nonempty (as
Sp
is a split graph with
|K|\geq 1
and connected), we must have
d\geq 1
. Therefore, each row sum of
R
equals
d
, so:

\sum_{j=1}^{|S|}R_{*j}=d\unovec,

which shows that
\unovec\in\im(R)
. The result follows from
Theorem

3.2
(5).

(2)

The condition
\deg(x)=\deg(y)
for all
x,y\in S
means that all vertices in
S
have the same number of neighbors in
K
(since vertices in
S
have no neighbors in
S
). Thus, there exists
d\geq 0
such that
|N(x)\cap K|=d
for all
x\in S
.

By connectedness and the fact that
K
is nonempty, we have
d\geq 1
. Therefore, each column sum of
R
equals
d
, which means each row sum of
R^{t}
equals
d
, so:

\sum_{j=1}^{|K|}R^{t}_{*j}=d\unovec,

which shows that
\unovec\in\im(R^{t})
. By
Lemma

3.1
, this implies
\nulo(R)\subset\nulo(J)
. ∎

The following corollary provides a sufficient combinatorial condition for nonsingularity that arises from a perfect “neighborhood covering” of the clique by the independent set.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph. If
K=\mathop{\dot{\bigcup}}\limits_{v\in S}N_{Sp}(v)
, then
Sp
is nonsingular.

Proof.

The condition
K=\dot{\bigcup}_{v\in S}N(v)
means that the neighborhood sets
\{N(v):v\in S\}
form a partition of
K
. This implies that each vertex in
K
has exactly one neighbor in
S
, so every row of
R
contains exactly one nonzero entry (which is 1). Consequently, all vertices in
K
have the same degree in
Sp
, since each has degree
|K|-1
within
K
plus exactly 1 neighbor in
S
, giving
\deg(u)=|K|
for all
u\in K
. By
Theorem

3.6
(1), this implies
\Supp(Sp)\subset S
. Then by
Theorem

3.4
(3), we have
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}=\{0\}
, and by
Theorem

3.2
(3),
\nul(Sp)=\nul(R)
.

Now, consider an arbitrary vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
and suppose
Rx=0
. Since each row of
R
has exactly one nonzero entry, the equation
R_{i*}x=0
implies that for each
i\in K
, if
v\in S
is the unique neighbor of
i
, then
x_{v}=0
. As the neighborhoods cover all of
K
and are disjoint, this means every
v\in S
appears as the unique neighbor of some
i\in K
, so
x_{v}=0
for all
v\in S
. Therefore,
x=0
and
\nul(R)=0
.

Hence,
\nul(Sp)=\nul(R)=0
, so
Sp
is nonsingular.
∎

4
Unbalanced split graphs

A split graph
Sp(K,S)
is said to be
balanced
if
|K|=\omega(Sp)
and
|S|=\alpha(Sp)
. Otherwise, we say that
Sp
is
unbalanced
(or not balanced).
It is known that (see
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
8
]
):

1.

Sp
is balanced if and only if it has a unique s-partition (thus, unbalanced split graphs are exactly those with two or more s-partitions);

2.

Sp
is balanced if and only if
\omega(Sp)+\alpha(Sp)=|Sp|
.

3.

Sp(K,S)
is unbalanced if and only if

(|K|,|S|)\in\{(\omega(Sp),\alpha(Sp)-1),(\omega(Sp)-1,\alpha(Sp))\}.

4.

Sp
is unbalanced if and only if
\omega(Sp)+\alpha(Sp)=|Sp|+1
.

We say that a vertex
w
of a split graph
Sp
is
swing
if there exist s-partitions
(K,S),(K^{\prime},S^{\prime})
for
Sp
such that
w\in K
and
w\in S^{\prime}
. Let
W(Sp)
be the set of all swing vertices of
Sp
. Then, it follows that
Sp
is balanced if and only if
W(Sp)=\varnothing
. However,
Sp\setminus W(Sp)
may not be balanced in general.
It is easy to see that
w\in W(Sp)
if and only if there are s-partitions
(K,S),(K^{\prime},S^{\prime})
for
Sp
such that
w\in K
,
w\in S^{\prime}
and

K\setminus\{w\}=N_{Sp}(w)=K^{\prime},

(1)

(|K|,|S|)=(\omega(Sp),\alpha(Sp)-1),\ (|K^{\prime}|,|S^{\prime}|)=(\omega(Sp)-1,\alpha(Sp)).

If
Sp
is a split graph, let:

1.

\mathcal{K}(Sp)
be the set of all cliques
K
of
Sp
such that
(K,S)
is an s-partition for
Sp
, for some
S
;

2.

\mathcal{S}(Sp)
be the set of all independent sets
S
of
Sp
such that
(K,S)
is an s-partition for
Sp
, for some
K
;

3.

K^{*}(Sp)
be the set of vertices that are “always clique” in
Sp
, i.e.,
K^{*}(Sp)=\bigcap(K\in\mathcal{K}(Sp))
;

4.

S^{*}(Sp)
be the set of vertices that are “always independent” in
Sp
, i.e.,
S^{*}(Sp)=\bigcap(S\in\mathcal{S}(Sp))
.

Then, by the definition of swing vertex, it is clear that

V(Sp)=K^{*}(Sp)\ \dot{\cup}\ W(Sp)\ \dot{\cup}\ S^{*}(Sp).

(2)

The concept of swing vertex lies implicitly among several works on split graphs. But, as far as we know, the term “swing” first appears explicitly in
[
21
]
(page 8). However, there the definition of swing vertex is slightly different: a vertex
w
is swing in
Sp(K,S)
if

N_{Sp}(w)=K\setminus\{w\}.

(3)

Although (
3
) agrees with (
1
), we think there is an issue with (
3
): it depends on the s-partition. To see that, consider the split graph
Sp=abc\approx P_{3}
with the s-partitions
(K,S)=(\{a,b\},c)
and
(K^{\prime},S^{\prime})=(a,\{b,c\})
. By our definition,
W(Sp)=\{a,c\}
, which is an invariant of
Sp
. However, using (
3
), we observe that: 1) in
Sp(K,S)
,
a
is swing but
c
is not; 2) in
Sp(K^{\prime},S^{\prime})
,
c
is swing but
a
is not. For this reason, we decided to modify (
3
). As we have seen above, this choice allows us to immediately obtain the useful vertex-decomposition (
2
).

Next theorem is a translation to our setting of a known result that characterizes the s-partitions of an unbalanced split graph.

Theorem 4.1

(Cheng, Collins, Trenk,
[
3
]
, page 2348)
.

Let
Sp
be an unbalanced split graph and let
\mathcal{KS}
be the collection of all the s-partitions for
Sp
. Then, we have the following (for simplicity, we will use
W,K^{*},S^{*}
instead of
W(Sp),K^{*}(Sp),S^{*}(Sp)
).

1.

If
W=\{w\}
, then

\mathcal{KS}=\{(K^{*}\cup w,S^{*}),(K^{*},S^{*}\cup w)\};

moreover,
K^{*}\cup w
is the unique maximum clique of
\mathcal{K}(Sp)
and
S^{*}\cup w
is the unique maximum independent set of
\mathcal{S}(Sp)
.

2.

If
W
is a clique of size
\geq 2
, then

\mathcal{KS}=\{(K^{*}\cup W,S^{*})\}\cup\{(K^{*}\cup(W\setminus\{w\}),S^{*}\cup w):w\in W\};

moreover,
K^{*}\cup W
is the unique maximum clique of
\mathcal{K}(Sp)
and
|S^{*}\cup w|=\alpha(Sp)
.

3.

If
W
is an independent set of size
\geq 2
, then

\mathcal{KS}=\{(K^{*},S^{*}\cup W)\}\cup\{(K^{*}\cup w,S^{*}\cup(W\setminus\{w\})):w\in W\};

moreover,
S^{*}\cup W
is the unique maximum independent set of
\mathcal{S}(Sp)
and
|K^{*}\cup w|=\omega(Sp)
.

The following proposition is a refinement of
Theorem

4.1
, because, essentially, tells us that “
\mathcal{K}(Sp)
” and “
\mathcal{S}(Sp)
” can be replaced with “
Sp
” in
Theorem

4.1
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
Sp
be an unbalanced split graph. Then,

1.

\mathcal{K}(Sp)
contains all the maximum cliques of
Sp
.

2.

\mathcal{S}(Sp)
contains all the maximum independent sets of
Sp
.

Proof.

(1).

Let
K
be a maximum clique of
Sp
. Since
Sp
is unbalanced, we know that
\omega(Sp)+\alpha(Sp)=|Sp|+1
, and so
\alpha-1=|Sp|-\omega=|V(Sp)\setminus K|
. Let
S
be a maximum independent set of
Sp
. Since
|K\cap S|\leq 1,|S|=\alpha
and
|V(Sp)\setminus K|=\alpha-1
, it must be
S=(V(Sp)\setminus K)\dot{\cup}w
, for some
w\in K
. Consequently,
V(Sp)\setminus K
is an independent set of
Sp
. Thus,
(K,V(Sp)\setminus K)
is an s-partition for
Sp
, which means that
K\in\mathcal{K}(Sp)
.

(2).

Let
S
be a maximum independent set of
Sp
. Then,
S
is a maximum clique of the graph
\overline{Sp}
(the complement of
Sp
), which is clearly split and unbalanced. From (1), we know that
S\in\mathcal{K}(\overline{Sp})
, which means that
V(\overline{Sp})\setminus S
is an independent set of
\overline{Sp}
. Hence,
V(Sp)\setminus S
is a clique of
Sp
, which shows that
S\in\mathcal{S}(Sp)
.

∎

Surprisingly,
Theorem

4.2
does not hold in general for balanced split graph. In fact, every balanced
Sp(K,S)
with an independent vertex of degree
|K|-1
is a counterexample (
P_{4}
is the “simplest” one). In other words, a balanced split graph
Sp(K,S)
has always a unique s-partition, but
K
might not be the only maximum clique of
Sp
. An analogous phenomenon can be observed for maximum independent sets. The following result clarifies this behavior of the balanced split graphs.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a balanced split graph. Then, we have the following.

1.

K
is the only maximum clique of
Sp
if and only if
Sp
does not contain an independent vertex of degree
|K|-1
.

2.

S
is the only maximum independent set of
Sp
if and only if
Sp
does not contain a clique vertex
x
such that
|N_{Sp}(x)\cap S|=1
.

Proof.

(1).

If
x
is a vertex of
Sp
of degree
|K|-1
, then
x
must be independent (otherwise,
x
would be swing). Now, observe that
N(x)\cup x
is a maximum clique of
Sp
, different from
K
.

For the converse, suppose that
K^{\prime}
is a maximum clique of
Sp
, different from
K
. Since
|K^{\prime}\cap S|\leq 1
, it must be
K^{\prime}=(K\setminus\{w\})\dot{\cup}\{x\}
, for some
w\in K
and
x\in S
. Since
x\in K^{\prime}
, we have
K\setminus\{w\}\subset N(x)
. Since
x\in S
,
x
is not adjacent to any vertex of
S
, which means that
N(x)\subset K
. If
w\in N(x)
, then
N(x)=K
and hence
x\in W(Sp)=\varnothing
. This absurdity implies
N(x)=K\setminus\{w\}
, i.e.,
\deg(x)=|K|-1
.

(2).

If
x
is a vertex of
Sp
such that
N(x)\cap S=\{w\}
for some
w
, it is clear that
x\in K
and
(I\setminus\{w\})\dot{\cup}\{x\}
is a maximum independent set different from
S
.

For the converse, suppose that
S^{\prime}
is a maximum independent set of
Sp
, different from
S
. Since
|K\cap S^{\prime}|\leq 1
, it must be
S^{\prime}=(S\setminus\{w\})\dot{\cup}\{x\}
, for some
w\in S
and
x\in K
. Since
x\in K
,
K\setminus x\subset N(x)
. Since
x\in S^{\prime}
,
x
is not adjacent to any vertex of
S\setminus\{w\}
. Notice that
w\in N(x)
(otherwise,
x
would be swing). Therefore,
N(x)=(K\setminus x)\dot{\cup}\{w\}
, which means that
|N(x)\cap S|=1
.

∎

The following theorem states that in unbalanced split graphs, supported vertices must lie in the intersection of all the maximum independent sets. This has significant implications for understanding the nullspace structure of unbalanced split graphs.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
Sp
be an unbalanced split graph. Then,
\Supp(Sp)
is contained in the intersection of all the maximum independent sets of
Sp
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary s-partition
(K,S)
for
Sp
such that
|S|=\alpha(Sp)
. The existence of such is guaranteed by

4.1
. Then, by
Theorem

4.1
, there exists a vertex

such that

, which means that

. Since
\unovec\in\im(R)
, we conclude by

. Therefore,
\Supp(Sp)
is contained in the intersection of all the maximum independent sets in

(2).
∎

A
threshold
graph is a graph that can be constructed from
K_{1}
by repeatedly applying either of the following two operations: 1) addition of an isolated vertex; 2) addition of a universal vertex. Thus, each unlabeled threshold graph of order

b_{i}\in\{0,1\}

“add an isolated vertex" and

“add a universal vertex". Threshold graphs are characterized as those graphs that do not contain induced subgraphs isomorphic to

,

or

(
[

]

,
C_{4}
or

(
[
2
]
, page 14), it is clear that threshold graphs are split. If

is the binary sequence associated with a threshold graph

, it is easy to see that

(K,S)=(\{v\in V(Sp):b_{v}=1\},\{v\in V(Sp):b_{v}=0\})

is an s-partition for

. Then,

, which shows that

is unbalanced. The following corollary is a refined version of a result that can be found in
[

]
.

Corollary 4.5
.

If

Proof.

Follows from the previous discussion and from

.

Two vertices

in a graph

N_{G}(u)\setminus v=N_{G}(v)\setminus u.

It is easy to check that “to be twins” is an equivalence relation over

, and the equivalence classes

are cliques or independent sets of

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
Sp
be a split graph. If

, then
[w]=W(Sp)
.

Proof.

If
W(Sp)=\{w\}
, the inclusion
W(Sp)\subset[w]
is obvious. If
|W(Sp)|\geq 2
, let
w^{\prime}\in W(Sp)\setminus\{w\}
. Since
w
and
w^{\prime}
are swing, there are s-partitions
(K,S),(K^{\prime},S^{\prime})
for
Sp
such that

w\in S,\ w^{\prime}\in S^{\prime},\ |S|=\alpha(Sp)=|S^{\prime}|,\ N(w)=K,\text{ and }\ N(w^{\prime})=K^{\prime}.

Suppose that
K\neq K^{\prime}
. If
x\in K\setminus K^{\prime}
, then
x\in S^{\prime}
. Hence,
x\in W(Sp)
, and so
K^{\prime}=N(x)=K\setminus\{x\}
. Now, observe that
S^{\prime}=S\dot{\cup}\{x\}
: this is impossible because

and
S^{\prime}
are maximum independent sets. Therefore,

, which means that
w^{\prime}
and

are twins. We have just proven the inclusion

.

To prove the reverse inclusion, consider a vertex

with

is swing, there is an s-partition

and

. Then,

.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

be twin vertices of a graph

Since

\sum_{i\in N(v)\setminus\{u\}}x_{i}=\sum_{i\in N(u)\setminus\{v\}}x_{i}.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a connected split graph such that
K=\bigcup_{v\in S}N_{Sp}(v)
. If
\rank(R)=1
, then
R=J
. Moreover, all the independent (clique) vertices of

are pairwise twins.

Proof.

Since
Sp
is connected and has no isolated vertices in

, all the columns of
R
are nonzero. Since
K=\bigcup_{v\in S}N(v)
, all the rows of
R
are nonzero. Moreover, since
\rank(R)=1
, all the columns of

must be scalar multiples of each other. Since all columns are nonzero

for all

for all

. Therefore,

.

Since

for all

5
Split graphs with supported clique vertices

In this section we examine the structure of the nullspace when clique vertices are involved; this scenario gives rise to rich yet highly constrained linear dependencies. We prove that the subspace of kernel vectors with support in the clique—the clique-kernel —is at most one-dimensional. This foundational result leads to a precise dichotomy: the overall nullity of the split graph is either equal to the nullity of its biadjacency matrix
R
, or exactly one greater. This establishes a complete and computable classification of the nullity based purely on the dimension of this key subspace, tightly linking the combinatorial presence of supported clique vertices to the algebraic property of the graph’s nullity.

The following result is crucial as it shows that when clique vertices appear in the support, they do so in essentially a one-dimensional way, which has important implications for the structure of the nullspace.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph such that
|K|\geq 2
. If
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}\neq\{0\}
, then
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=1

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}\neq\{0\}
, it is clear that
R\neq 0
. Let
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}\}
be a basis for the subspace
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}
. For each
i\in[r]
, we have

(I-J)b_{i}=Rc_{i},

for some
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
. Then,
(b_{i},c_{i})^{t}\in\nulo(Sp)
by
Theorem

3.2
, and hence
b_{i}\notin\nulo(J)
by item (4) of
Theorem

3.2
(since
\unovec^{t}b_{i}\neq 0
).

Now, assume that
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})\geq 2
. Then we can write:

\frac{b_{1}}{\unovec^{t}b_{1}}-\frac{Rc_{1}}{\unovec^{t}b_{1}}=\unovec=\frac{b_{2}}{\unovec^{t}b_{2}}-\frac{Rc_{2}}{\unovec^{t}b_{2}}.

Rearranging gives:

\frac{b_{1}}{\unovec^{t}b_{1}}-\frac{b_{2}}{\unovec^{t}b_{2}}=\frac{Rc_{1}}{\unovec^{t}b_{1}}-\frac{Rc_{2}}{\unovec^{t}b_{2}}\in\im(R).

The left-hand side belongs to
\nulo(R^{t})
since
b_{1},b_{2}\in\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}\subset\nulo(R^{t})
. Therefore,

\frac{b_{1}}{\unovec^{t}b_{1}}-\frac{b_{2}}{\unovec^{t}b_{2}}\in\nulo(R^{t})\cap\im(R)=\{0\},

so

\frac{b_{1}}{\unovec^{t}b_{1}}=\frac{b_{2}}{\unovec^{t}b_{2}},

which implies
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are linearly dependent, contradicting our assumption. Hence,
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=1
.
∎

Now we can show that the appearance of clique vertices in the support is an "all-or-nothing" phenomenon in a one-dimensional sense, which has significant implications for understanding the structure of kernel vectors in split graphs.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph such that
|K|\geq 2
. If
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}\neq\{0\}
, then
\Supp(Sp)\cap K\neq\varnothing
if and only if
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=1
.

Proof.

We obtain the desired result by combining item (3) of
Theorem

3.4
with
Lemma

5.1
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

If
Sp(K,S)
is a split graph with
\Supp(Sp)\cap K\neq\varnothing
, then

\nul(Sp)=\nul(R)+1.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{(0,x)^{t}:x\in\nulo(R)\}
. Since
\mathcal{X}\subset\nulo(Sp)
by
Theorem

3.2
(2), we can extend a basis
B_{\mathcal{X}}
for
\mathcal{X}
to a basis for
\nulo(Sp)
to span those kernel vectors
(x_{K},x_{S})^{t}
with
x_{K}\neq 0
.

We know from
Theorem

5.2
that
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}=\langle z_{0}\rangle
, where
z_{0}=(I-J)^{-1}Ry
, for some
y
. Since
\nulo((I-J)^{-1}R)=\nulo(R)
and
z_{0}\neq 0
, such a vector
y
belongs to
y_{0}+\nulo(R)
, where
y_{0}
is fixed and
y_{0}\notin\nulo(R)
.
Then, by items (1) and (2) of
Theorem

3.4
, every vector
s\in\nulo(Sp)\setminus\mathcal{X}
has the form

s=c\binom{z_{0}}{y_{0}}+\binom{0}{x_{0}},

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
and some
x_{0}\in\nulo(R)
.
Therefore,
B_{\mathcal{X}}\dot{\cup}\{(z_{0},y_{0})^{t}\}
is a basis for
\nulo(Sp)
.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

If
Sp(K,S)
is a split graph with
|K|\geq 2
, then
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})

\leq 1
and

\nul(Sp)=\begin{cases}\nul(R),&\text{if }\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=0\\
\nul(R)+1,&\text{if }\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.1
,
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})\leq 1
. If
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=0
, then by
Theorem

3.4
(3),
\Supp(Sp)\subset S
, and hence
\nul(Sp)=\nul(R)
by
Theorem

3.2
(3). If
\dim(\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)})=1
, then by
Theorem

5.2
,
\Supp(Sp)\cap K\neq\varnothing
, and hence
\nul(Sp)=\nul(R)+1
by
Theorem

5.3
.
∎

Lemma 6.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

If
A
is a square matrix, then
\adj(A)\!\neq\!0
if and only if
\nul(A)\!\leq\!1
.

While this is a standard result (see
[
10
]
), we include a concise proof here as this lemma plays a crucial role in our characterization of split graphs with nullity one, and we wish to keep our presentation self-contained.

Proof.

Let
n
be the order of
A
. The fact that
\adj(A)
has at least a nonzero entry means that
A
contains a nonzero minor of order
n-1
. This is equivalent to
\rank(A)\geq n-1
, because
\rank(A)
is, by definition, the maximum size of an invertible submatrix of
A
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
A
be a square matrix. If
\nul(A)=1
, then
\rank(\adj(A))=1
and every column of
\adj(A)
is a vector of
\nulo(A)
.

This also is a standard result in linear algebra; we include the proof for completeness.

Proof.

Let
n
be the order of
A
and let
\adj(A)=[a_{1}\mid\ldots\mid a_{n}]
, where
a_{i}
denotes the
i
-th column. Since
\det(A)=0
, the fundamental identity
\det(A)I=A\adj(A)
implies that

0=A\adj(A)=[Aa_{1}\mid\ldots\mid Aa_{n}].

So,
a_{i}\in\nulo(A)
for all
i\in[n]
, from which we deduce that
\rank(\adj(A))\leq 1
, because
\nul(A)=1
. On the other hand, we know by
Lemma

6.1
that
\rank(\adj(A))\neq 0
. Therefore,
\rank(\adj(A))=1
.
∎

If
A
is a symmetric matrix, recall that
\adj(A)
is symmetric as well.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
G
be a graph with
\nul(G)=1
and
\Supp(G)=[s]
, for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, we have the following:

1.

G-v
is singular if and only if
\adj(G)_{vv}=0
;

2.

\adj(G)_{uv}\neq 0
if and only if
u,v\in\Supp(G)
;

3.

v\in\Supp(G)
if and only if
G-v
is nonsingular;

4.

If
(x,0)^{t}\in\nulo(G)
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\setminus 0
, then

x_{v}^{2}=\det(G-v)\det(G-u),

for each
v\in\Supp(G)
and some fixed
u\in\Supp(G)
.

Proof.

(1)

By removing row
v
and column
v
from
A(G)
we obtain
A(G-v)
. Since
\adj(G)_{vv}=(-1)^{v+v}\det(G-v)=\det(G-v)
, we get the desired equivalence.

(2)

Let
\nulo(G)=\langle z\rangle
and
s=|\Supp(G)|
. First, we label
V(G)
using the ordering
(\Supp(G),V(G)\setminus\Supp(G))
. By this labeling,
z=(x,0)^{t}
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
x_{v}\neq 0
for all
v\in[s]
. Then, by
Lemma

6.2
, we can write

\adj(G)=\begin{pmatrix}Z&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

where

Z=[x\mid c_{2}x\mid\ldots\mid c_{s}x]=x(1,c_{2},\ldots,c_{s}),

for some nonzero constants
c_{2},\ldots,c_{s}
. So, we see that
\adj(G)_{uv}\neq 0
if and only if
u,v\in\Supp(G)
.

(3)

It follows from (1) and (2).

(4)

Since
A(G)
is symmetric,
\adj(G)
is symmetric, so
Z
must be symmetric. From the structure of
Z=x(1,c_{2},\ldots,c_{s})
, symmetry implies that
c_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{1}}
for all
i\in[s]
. Therefore,
Z=x_{1}^{-1}xx^{t}
.

Fix
u\in\Supp(G)
as the first vertex in our ordering, so
x_{1}=\adj(G)_{uu}=\det(G-u)
. Then for each
v\in\Supp(G)
:

\det(G-v)=\adj(G)_{vv}=Z_{vv}=x_{1}^{-1}x_{v}^{2}=\det(G-v_{1})^{-1}x_{v}^{2}.

Rearranging gives
x_{v}^{2}=\det(G-v)\det(G-u)
. ∎

The former result allow us to prove that for split graph with nullity one all the “interesting” kernel structure comes from the clique-support phenomenon, not from
R
itself.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph such that
\Supp(Sp)\cap K\neq\varnothing
, with
|K|\geq 2
. Then,
\nul(Sp)=1
if and only if
\nulo(R)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

5.3
, since
\Supp(Sp)\cap K\neq\varnothing
, we have
\nul(Sp)=\nul(R)+1
. Therefore,
\nul(Sp)=1
if and only if
\nul(R)=0
.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix such that
n\geq 2
,
\nul(A)=1
, and
A_{*j}\neq 0
for all
j\in[n]
. If
z\in\nulo(A)\setminus 0
and
z
has no zero entries, then every set of
n-1
columns of
A
is a basis for
\im(A)
.

Proof.

Since
z\in\nulo(A)
has no zero entries, for each
k\in[n]
we have
A_{*k}=-\frac{1}{z_{k}}\sum_{j\neq k}z_{j}A_{*j}
, so each
B_{i}=\{A_{*j}:j\neq i\}
spans
\im(A)
. Since
\rank(A)=n-1=|B_{i}|
, each
B_{i}
is a basis for
\im(A)
.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with
|\Supp(A)|\geq 2
,
\nul(A)=1
, and
A_{*j}\neq 0
for all
j\in[n]
. If
i\in\Supp(A)
, define
B_{i}=\{A_{*j}:j\in\Supp(A),j\neq i\}
. Then,

B=B_{i}\cup\{A_{*j}:j\notin\Supp(A)\}

is a basis for
\im(A)
, for each
i\in\Supp(A)
.

Proof.

From
Lemma

6.5
, we know that
B_{i}
spans the subspace
\langle\{A_{*j}:j\in\Supp(A)\}\rangle
. With this in mind, it is clear that
B
spans
\im(A)
. Since
|B|=n-1=\rank(A)
, we can finally conclude that
B
is a basis for
\im(A)
.
∎

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
G
be a graph without isolated vertices. If
\nul(G)=1
and
s\in\Supp(G)
, then

B_{s}=\{A(G)_{*j}:j\neq s\}

is a basis for
\im(G)
.

This theorem provides a very concrete and useful result: for any graph with nullity 1 and no isolated vertices, you can obtain a basis for the column space by simply removing any one column corresponding to a supported vertex. This is computationally useful and reveals the highly structured nature of the adjacency matrix in this case.

Proof.

Since
G
has no isolated vertices,
A(G)_{*j}\neq 0
for all
j\in V(G)
. If
\Supp(G)=\{u\}
for some
u
, then
\nulo(G)=\langle e_{u}\rangle
. But,
0=A(G)e_{u}=A(G)_{*u}
, contradicting that all the columns of
A(G)
are nonzero. Therefore,
|\Supp(G)|\geq 2
, and so we can apply
Lemma

6.6
.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph such that
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}\neq\{0\}
, and let
P
be the submatrix of
R
of size
|K|\times|\rank(R)|
whose columns form a basis for
\im(R)
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the set of vertices associated with the columns of
P
. If
Sp^{\prime}(K,S^{\prime})
is the split graph whose biadjacency matrix is
P
, then

\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}=\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp^{\prime}\right)}

and
\nul(Sp^{\prime})=1
.

Proof.

Since
P
is obtained by selecting a basis for
\im(R)
, we have
\im(P)=\im(R)
(hence,
\im((I-J)^{-1}P)=\im((I-J)^{-1}R)
) and

\nulo(P^{t})=\nulo(R^{t})
. Therefore,
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}=\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp^{\prime}\right)}
. Since
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}\neq\{0\}
, by
Theorem

3.4
(3), we have
\Supp(Sp^{\prime})

\cap K\neq\varnothing
. Since
\nulo(P)=\{0\}
, applying
Corollary

6.4
to
Sp^{\prime}
gives
\nul(Sp^{\prime})=1
.
∎

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph such that
\operatorname{Cker}{\left(Sp\right)}=\langle z\rangle
, for some
z\in\mathbb{R}^{|K|}\setminus 0
. Assume that the first
\rank(R)
columns of
R
are linearly independent and let
P
be the submatrix of
R
obtained by deleting the last
|S|-\rank(R)
columns of
R
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the set of vertices associated with the columns of
P
. If
Sp^{\prime}(K,S^{\prime})
is the induced split subgraph of
Sp
whose biadjacency matrix is
P
, then we have the following:

1.

\Supp(Sp^{\prime})\cap K=\Supp(Sp)\cap K
,
\Supp(Sp^{\prime})\cap S^{\prime}\subset\Supp(Sp)\cap S^{\prime}
.

2.

For each
s\in\Supp(Sp^{\prime})
, then

B_{s}=\{A(Sp)_{*j}:j\in V(Sp^{\prime})\setminus s\}

is a basis for
\im(Sp)
.

3.

\rank(Sp)=|V(Sp^{\prime})|-1=\rank(R)+|K|-1
.

4.

If
y_{0}
is the unique vector such that
(I-J)z=Py_{0}
, and
y_{v}
is the unique vector such that
Py_{v}=R_{*v}
for each
v\in W=V(Sp)\setminus V(Sp^{\prime})
, then

B=\{(z,y_{0},0)^{t}\}\cup\{(0,-y_{v},e_{v})^{t}:v\in W\}

is a basis for
\nulo(Sp)
.

Proof.

(1)

The equality
\Supp(Sp^{\prime})\cap K=\Supp(Sp)\cap K
can easily be derived from
Lemma

6.8
. To obtain that
\Supp(Sp^{\prime})\cap S^{\prime}\subset\Supp(Sp)\cap S^{\prime}
, recall that, by item (2) of
Theorem

3.4
and item (1) of
Theorem

3.2
, there exists a (unique) vector
y_{0}
such that
(I-J)^{-1}Py_{0}=z
. Then,

\Supp(Sp^{\prime})\cap S^{\prime}=\{u\in S^{\prime}:(y_{0})_{u}\neq 0\}.

On the other hand, we have
(I-J)^{-1}R(y_{0},0)^{t}=z
as well, which means that
(z,y_{0},0)^{t}\in\nulo(Sp)
. Hence,

\{u\in S^{\prime}:(y_{0})_{u}\neq 0\}\subset\Supp(Sp)\cap S^{\prime}.

(2)

Let
M
be the
|V(Sp)|\times(|V(Sp^{\prime})|-1)
matrix obtained from
A(Sp)
by deleting its last
|S|-\rank(R)
columns and the column
s
. Since
\rank(M)
is the size of its largest invertible submatrix, we have that
\rank(M)=|V(Sp^{\prime})|-1
because
M
contains
A(Sp^{\prime}-s)
, which is nonsingular by
Lemma

6.8
and
Theorem

6.3
. Hence,
B_{s}
is a linearly independent set.

Obviously,
\langle B_{s}\rangle\subset\im(Sp)
. Since
s\in\Supp(Sp)
by (1), there exists
x\in\nulo(Sp)
such that
x_{s}\neq 0
and
x_{v}=0
for all
v\in V(Sp)\setminus V(Sp^{\prime})
(recall the proof of (1)). Therefore, we can solve for
A(Sp)_{*s}
from the equality

0=\sum_{j\in V(Sp)}x_{j}A(Sp)_{*j}=x_{s}A(Sp)_{*s}+\sum_{j\in V(Sp^{\prime})\setminus s}x_{j}A(Sp)_{*j},

and show that
A(Sp)_{*s}\in\langle B_{s}\rangle
. For each
v\in V(Sp)\setminus V(Sp^{\prime})
, it is clear by definition of
P
that
A(Sp)_{*v}
can be written as a nontrivial linear combination of elements in
\{A(Sp)_{*j}:j\in S^{\prime}\}
(if
s\in S^{\prime}
, recall that
A(Sp)_{*s}\in\langle B_{s}\rangle
). Hence,
\langle B_{s}\rangle=\im(Sp)
.

(3)

By (2),
\rank(Sp)=|B_{s}|=|V(Sp^{\prime})|-1
. Moreover,
|V(Sp^{\prime})|=|S^{\prime}|+|K|
and
|S^{\prime}|=\rank(R)
, by the definition of
Sp^{\prime}
.

(4)

Since

0=P(-y_{v})+1R_{*v}+\sum_{j\in W\setminus v}0R_{*j}=R(-y_{v},e_{v})^{t},

we see that
(-y_{v},e_{v})^{t}\in\nulo(R)
. Note that
\{(-y_{v},e_{v}):v\in W\}
is clearly a basis for
\nulo(R)
(recall that
|W|=\nul(R)
). Consequently, we get the desired result by repeating the same argument used in the proof of
Theorem

5.3
. ∎

Clearly, the vector
z
constructed in
Theorem

6.9
can always be chosen with integer coordinates such that
\gcd\{z_{i}:z_{i}\neq 0\}=1
. In such a case, observe that
z
becomes, up to multiplication by
-1
, an interesting algebraic invariant of split graphs with supported clique vertices.

7
Tyshkevich composition

Understanding how the nullspace behaves under graph operations is a natural and important question. In this section, we analyze how the nullspace of a split graph interacts with the Tyshkevich composition, a well-known product that builds a larger graph by fully joining the clique of a split graph to an arbitrary second graph. We establish fundamental results showing how the singularity of the composition is controlled by the properties of its components, providing a powerful tool for constructing graphs with prescribed nullity and for understanding the propagation of kernel vectors through this structured gluing.

If
Sp(K,S)
is a split graph and
G
is a graph disjoint from
Sp
, the
Tyshkevich composition

Sp\circ G
of
Sp
and
G
is defined as the graph whose vertex set is

V(Sp\circ G)=V(Sp)\cup V(G),

and whose edge set is

E(Sp\circ G)=E(Sp)\cup E(G)\cup\{xy:x\in K,y\in V(G)\}.

This operation was introduced by R. Tyshkevich in
[
19
]
. In Figure
6
we can see an example of this composition.

This operation is particularly relevant for studying the nullspace of split graphs, as it allows us to build more complex split graphs from simpler components while potentially controlling spectral properties.

Sp
G

Figure 6:
Tyshkevich composition of
Sp\approx P_{4}
and
G\approx C_{4}
.

The Tyshkevich composition preserves (and potentially amplifies) the singularity coming from the biadjacency matrix
R
of the split graph.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph, and let
G
be a graph. Then,

\nulo(Sp\circ G)\supseteq\{(0,z,0)^{t}:z\in\nulo(R)\}.

In particular, if
\nulo(R)\neq\{0\}
, then
Sp\circ G
is singular.

Proof.

Let
H=Sp\circ G
. By using the ordering
(K,S,V(G))
for
V(H)
, we see that

A(H)=\begin{pmatrix}J-I&R&J\\
R^{t}&0&0\\
J^{t}&0&A(G)\end{pmatrix}.

Now, observe that
A(H)(0,x_{S},0)^{t}=(Rx_{S},0,0)^{t}
. Therefore, if
x_{S}\in\nulo(R)
, then
(0,x_{S},0)^{t}\in\nulo(H)
.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
and
Sp^{\prime}(K^{\prime},S^{\prime})
be split graphs with
|K|=|S|
and
|K^{\prime}|=|S^{\prime}|
. Then,
Sp\circ Sp^{\prime}
is nonsingular if and only if
Sp
and
Sp^{\prime}
are both nonsingular.

Proof.

Since
(K\cup K^{\prime},S\cup S^{\prime})
is an s-partition for
H=Sp\circ Sp^{\prime}
, we can label
V(H)
following the ordering
(K,K^{\prime},S,S^{\prime})
, and write

A(H)=\begin{pmatrix}J-I&P\\
P^{t}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P=\begin{pmatrix}R&J\\
0&R^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
|K\cup K^{\prime}|=|S\cup S^{\prime}|
, we know by item (2) of
Theorem

3.5
that
A(H)
is nonsingular if and only if

\det(P)=\det(R)\det(R^{\prime})\neq 0,

because
P
is block-triangular. Hence,
H
is nonsingular if and only if
R
and
R^{\prime}
are both nonsingular. Now, apply once again item (2) of
Theorem

3.5
to conclude that this occurs if and only if
Sp
and
Sp^{\prime}
are both nonsingular.
∎

8
Determinant of split graphs

Thanks to the block structure of their adjacency matrices, split graphs admit explicit combinatorial determinant formulas that can be obtained with the Schur complement and the matrix-determinant lemma.

In this section, we establish a precise formula for
\det(Sp)
(i.e.,
\det(A(Sp))
) in terms of the biadjacency matrix
R
, providing a direct computational method to determine the singularity of a split graph and revealing how the determinant is influenced by the interplay between the clique and the independent set.

Theorem 8.1

(Schur complement)
.

Let

M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}

be a block matrix, where
A
and
D
are square matrices (of possibly different sizes). If
A
is invertible, then

\det(M)=\det(A)\det(D-CA^{-1}B).

Theorem 8.2

(Matrix determinant lemma)
.

If
M
is an invertible matrix and
u,v
are vectors, then

\det(M+uv^{t})=(1+v^{t}M^{-1}u)\det(M).

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
Sp(K,S)
be a split graph with
|K|=k\geq 2
. If

A(Sp)=\begin{pmatrix}J-I&R\\
R^{t}&0\end{pmatrix},

and
r=R^{t}\unovec
, then

\det(Sp)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)\det\left(R^{t}R-\frac{rr^{t}}{k-1}\right).

Furthermore, if
\nulo(R)=\{0\}
, we have

\det\left(R^{t}R-\frac{rr^{t}}{k-1}\right)=\left(1-\frac{1}{k-1}r^{t}(R^{t}R)^{-1}r\right)\det(R^{t}R).

Proof.

We apply the Schur complement (
Theorem

8.1
) with

A=J-I
,
B=R
,
C=R^{t}
, and
D=0
:

\det(Sp)=\det(J-I)\det(0-R^{t}(J-I)^{-1}R)=

(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)\det(-R^{t}(J-I)^{-1}R).

Using the fact that
(I-J)^{-1}=I-(k-1)^{-1}J
, we have:

R^{t}(J-I)^{-1}R=-R^{t}(I-J)^{-1}R=-R^{t}\left(I-\frac{1}{k-1}J\right)R=

-R^{t}R+\frac{1}{k-1}R^{t}JR.

Note that
R^{t}JR=(R^{t}\unovec)(\unovec^{t}R)=rr^{t}
. Therefore:

\det(Sp)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)\det\left(R^{t}R-\frac{rr^{t}}{k-1}\right).

For the second part, if
\nulo(R)=\{0\}
, then
R^{t}R
is invertible, and we apply the matrix determinant lemma (
Theorem

8.2
) with
M=R^{t}R,u=\frac{r}{1-k}
and
