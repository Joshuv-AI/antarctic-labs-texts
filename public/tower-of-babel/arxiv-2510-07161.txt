Integrating Domain Knowledge into Process Discovery Using Large Language Models
Ali Norouzifar, Humam Kourani, Marcus Dees, Wil van der Aalst

Abstract
Process discovery aims to derive process models from event logs, providing insights into operational behavior and forming a foundation for conformance checking and process improvement. However, models derived solely from event data may not accurately reflect the real process, as event logs are often incomplete or affected by noise, and domain knowledge, an important complementary resource, is typically disregarded. As a result, the discovered models may lack reliability for downstream tasks. We propose an interactive framework that incorporates domain knowledge, expressed in natural language, into the process discovery pipeline using Large Language Models (LLMs). Our approach leverages LLMs to extract declarative rules from textual descriptions provided by domain experts. These rules are used to guide the IMr discovery algorithm, which recursively constructs process models by combining insights from both the event log and the extracted rules, helping to avoid problematic process structures that contradict domain knowledge. The framework coordinates interactions among the LLM, domain experts, and a set of backend services. We present a fully implemented tool that supports this workflow and conduct an extensive evaluation of multiple LLMs and prompt engineering strategies. Our empirical study includes a case study based on a real-life event log with the involvement of domain experts, who assessed the usability and effectiveness of the framework.

Introduction

Event data recorded by information systems provides a valuable foundation for process mining, enabling organizations to gain insights into their operations and identify opportunities for improvement. In this context, a variety of automated techniques are applied to discover descriptive models that represent and explain observed process behavior
[
5
]
. To evaluate the quality of these discovered models with respect to a given event log, conformance checking metrics such as
fitness
,
precision
,
generalization
, and
simplicity
are commonly used
[
11
]
. These models serve as a starting point for various types of analysis, including the detection of deviations, performance evaluation, and process enhancement.

As illustrated by the Venn diagram in Fig.
1
, the central objective of process discovery is to derive a model (represented with
M
) that reflects the behavior recorded in the event log (represented with
L
). However, the broader goal is to approximate the
actual
underlying process (represented with
P
), which is typically unknown. This poses a challenge: the event log may not fully capture the real process, i.e., there may be parts of the process in
P-L
that are never observed, and it may include traces that deviate from the expected behavior due to noise or exceptional circumstances, i.e., elements in
L-P
. As a result, evaluating the discovered model solely based on the event log can be misleading, since the log represents only a partial and potentially imperfect view of the actual process, particularly when these models are intended for conformance checking and process improvement.

:

), and the actual process (

).

In addition to event data extracted from information systems, organizations often have access to valuable resources such as domain experts, process documentation, and other forms of
domain knowledge

[
32
]
. While this knowledge cannot be directly leveraged by traditional process discovery techniques, it provides complementary insights into how the actual process operates. However, such resources typically remain underutilized during model discovery. Incorporating domain knowledge into the process discovery task presents several challenges. For example, domain experts possess deep understanding of their processes but usually express their knowledge in natural language, which is difficult to formalize. Similarly, textual process documentation, despite its richness, poses integration challenges due to its unstructured nature. In this work, we address these limitations by enabling the direct incorporation of domain knowledge into process discovery using Large Language Models (LLMs). The recent emergence of powerful LLMs, such as GPT-4
[
29
]
and Gemini
[
4
]
, which demonstrate strong capabilities in natural language understanding and reasoning over textual inputs, offers promising opportunities for advancing tasks related to text interpretation.

This paper builds upon the original idea of incorporating domain knowledge, transformed into formal rules via an LLM within a specific process discovery technique, namely the IMr framework
[
28
]
. IMr is an inductive mining-based approach that recursively identifies the process structure that best represents the underlying process, using both an event log and a set of input rules
[
27
]
. The framework systematically explores alternative process structures and leverages the provided rules to prune the search space, thereby eliminating suboptimal candidates. While the notion of rules can be broadly interpreted, this work adopts the Declare rule specification language as a representative formalism
[
24
]
. Declarative rules are particularly suitable for this purpose due to their alignment with human reasoning and their well-defined logical semantics, which are supported by a rich body of literature. Nonetheless, expecting end users to manually define such rules is often unrealistic, motivating the use of LLMs to bridge this gap.

Overview of the interactive framework, showing how the LLM, domain expert, and backend services collaborate to support rule extraction and process discovery.

Our proposed method goes beyond a simple chatbot by integrating multiple interacting components. Figure
2
illustrates the interconnections among the key entities in the framework: domain knowledge (represented by the red box), the large language model (blue box), and the system components (green boxes), which include several predefined services responsible for managing communication and executing algorithms. Domain knowledge can be injected into the discovery process either prior to the initial model construction or after a preliminary model has been discovered and reviewed by domain experts. In both scenarios, an LLM, configured through prompt engineering, acts as an intelligent assistant that interfaces with available resources. The LLM engages in dialogues with the domain expert to acquire process descriptions and to ask clarification questions when ambiguities arise. The services autonomously handle tasks such as generating task descriptions, managing information about event logs, validating LLM outputs, and extracting rules from them, without requiring direct intervention from the domain expert. Meanwhile, the domain expert can interact with the system to perform actions such as initiating process discovery, reviewing statistical summaries, and selecting plausible rules based on domain knowledge.

The main contributions of this paper, in comparison to our previous work
[
28
]
, are summarized as follows:

(1)

Framework Redesign for Transparency and Automation:

We redesigned the framework to incorporate several dedicated backend services responsible for executing predefined algorithms. These services enhance automation and reliability, while also promoting more active involvement of the domain expert. This design reduces the risk of opaque or unreliable rule communication, thereby improving the transparency and explainability of the LLM-generated rules.

(2)

Tool Development and Interactive User Interface:

We developed a fully functional tool that implements the proposed framework. It features a user-friendly web interface that allows users to run the entire pipeline by selecting an LLM model and providing an API key. The tool supports interactive exploration of results and enables users to input process descriptions at different stages. This interactivity facilitates iterative refinement of the extracted rules and discovered models.

(3)

Extended Evaluation with Multiple LLMs and Prompting Strategies:

We conducted a comprehensive quantitative evaluation involving several LLMs and prompt engineering strategies. The evaluation assesses the models’ ability to extract declarative rules from natural language descriptions, comparing their outputs against a human-labeled ground truth.

(4)

Evaluation Metrics for Accuracy and Robustness:

We designed and measured several evaluation metrics to compare different configurations. These metrics include recall, precision, the frequency of error-handling cycles, and the failure rate when no valid rule set can be generated after multiple correction attempts.

(5)

Assessment of LLM Behavior Under Ambiguity:

Since clarification dialogues are expected in our method when input is ambiguous, we evaluated how different LLMs handle such cases. The analysis focuses on whether the models appropriately engage in clarification and how effectively they recover from incomplete or unclear input.

Related Work

Traditional process discovery methods primarily rely on event data to construct process models
[
5
]
. The overarching goal is to generate models that not only fit the observed behavior well (i.e., exhibit high fitness), but also restrict behavior not present in the log (i.e., ensure high precision), allow for plausible but unseen behavior (i.e., achieve good generalization), and maintain a level of simplicity that makes them easy to interpret and understand
[
11
]
.

Event data is not the only resource available for process discovery. In many real-world scenarios, domain knowledge, such as expert insights and process documentation, can be leveraged as an additional input across various process mining tasks
[
32
]
. This knowledge can be utilized prior to discovery, for instance, to filter the event log
[
15
]
. Some discovery techniques, such as the one proposed in
[
27
]
, are designed to incorporate domain knowledge in the form of declarative rules alongside the event log, thereby guiding the discovery process toward models that better reflect expert expectations. The approach presented in
[
14
]
introduces an interactive framework in which users provide low-level input by manually placing transitions and places, supported by visual guidance. The method in
[
33
]
begins with an initial model discovered from a user-selected subset of variants and iteratively refines it by incorporating additional variants to incrementally update the model.

Declarative constraints are widely used in the literature to represent process models that characterize the allowed behavior in the business processes
[
13
]
. Despite their strong formal semantics, these constraints are often interpretable in natural language, making them accessible to domain experts
[
3
]
.
Building on the IMr discovery technique proposed in
[
27
]
, the framework proposed in
[
28
]
leverages large language models to assist domain experts in translating textual process descriptions into declarative constraints. The extracted rules are then integrated into the discovery approach, enabling the generation of imperative process models that align more closely with domain knowledge by combining both the event log and the derived constraints.

Some studies focus on repairing process models after the discovery phase, aiming primarily to enhance alignment between the discovered models and the corresponding event logs, rather than explicitly incorporating domain knowledge
[
31
,
16
]
. The main objective of such approaches is to improve the conformance between the model and the log. In contrast, our work not only integrates domain knowledge expressed in natural language prior to the discovery phase, but also enables the incorporation of textual feedback from domain experts. This allows for refining the discovered process models in accordance with expert expectations and domain-specific requirements.

The paper
[
35
]
offers a comprehensive survey of NLP techniques used for extracting business rules from textual sources. The transformation of natural language process descriptions into declarative constraints was explored in earlier works
[

,

. Later the approaches like
[

showed that the extraction of such rules with LLM is more effective by comparing the results of traditional NLP methods with LLM extracted rules.
More recently,
[
25

proposed a fully data-driven pipeline based on gradient boosting to extract business rules from natural language. Similar to declarative constraints, these rules capture logical relationships between activity instances derived from text.

The paper
[
2
]
explores how natural language processing techniques support various levels of business process management, including the transformation of textual descriptions into formal models, verification of model completeness, and conversational process guidance. With the rise of LLMs, NLP has become more scalable and effective. LLMs have been applied to diverse process mining tasks. The study in
[
34
]
investigates their integration across the BPM lifecycle, from identification and discovery to analysis, redesign, implementation, and monitoring. It showcases application scenarios such as extracting processes from unstructured documentation, generating models from communication logs, and facilitating conversational interfaces for interactive process analysis.

From a system-level perspective, LLMs are increasingly integrated into conversational BPM systems to improve transparency and user engagement. The paper
[
12
]
shows how LLM-powered chatbots can transform traditional BPM systems into AI-augmented ones that proactively assist users, provide recommendations, and enhance explainability.
The paper
[
7
]
proposes ProcessGPT, a generative AI-based framework designed to enhance business process management. It leverages large language models to support both automation and augmentation in data-centric and knowledge-intensive processes.
The paper
[
6
]
investigates how large language models can support and improve the semantic quality of business process models.

The use of LLMs for process modeling has received increasing attention, primarily focusing on the extraction of formal models from natural language texts. The advent of LLMs has substantially advanced efforts to bridge the gap between unstructured domain knowledge and formal process representations. The study
[
21
]
surveys methods for deriving process models using chatbots and examines how LLM-powered conversational services can assist in the creation and refinement of models through natural language interaction with domain experts. Several other works demonstrate the capability of LLMs to transform textual descriptions into process artifacts, including process models
[
26
,
22
,
20
]
. In contrast to these approaches, our method retains the event log as the primary source of information and augments the discovery process by integrating textual process knowledge.

The study in
[
8
]
surveys various prompt engineering strategies tailored for process mining applications. Recent research also investigates prompt engineering and automated prompt correction to enhance the robustness of LLMs. The paper
[
10
]
highlights prompt engineering as a promising approach for applying LLMs in BPM, emphasizing the use of task-specific prompts to guide pre-trained models without requiring fine-tuning. A foundational contribution in this context is GPT-3
[
9
]
, which demonstrated that LLMs can perform diverse NLP tasks in few-shot, one-shot, or even zero-shot settings by relying solely on prompt-based instructions. The paper
[
36
]
proposes the automatic prompt engineer method, where one LLM generates and scores candidate prompts to autonomously select the most effective one, achieving performance comparable to human experts. In a related direction,
[
17
]
explores the use of LLMs for generating user-centered explanations of business processes. Their situation-aware explainability framework combines multiple services to tailor explanations to the user’s perspective and context.

The paper
[
30
]
provides a comprehensive survey of self-correction techniques for LLMs. It categorizes existing methods into three main stages, training-time, generation-time, and post-hoc correction, and examines how feedback (either internal/self-generated or external) can be leveraged to address issues such as hallucination.
The paper
[
19
]
critically evaluates the self-correction capabilities of LLMs, particularly in reasoning tasks. It introduces the concept of intrinsic self-correction, where models attempt to improve their own responses without any external feedback.
Techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, and feedback loops are used to enhance model reliability and interpretability in BPM contexts.

(3)

occurs after

.

(4)

.

(5)

\phi(r)=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\mid\sigma\vDash r\}.

Since declarative constraints are expressed as rule-based constructs, their quality can be evaluated using standard metrics such as
support
and
confidence
. In our framework, these metrics are computed and reported to the domain expert, providing statistical insights that assist in assessing the relevance and reliability of the extracted rules with respect to the given event log.

Definition 4
(Support and Confidence)

Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
be an event log. For a given rule
r\in\mathcal{R}
, we define the following quantities:

(1)

\#_{\mathit{activated}}(L,r)
: the number of traces the constraint
r
is activated in
L
,

(2)

\#_{\mathit{satisfied}}(L,r)
: the number of traces
r
is satisfied in
L
,

(3)

\#_{\mathit{traces}}(L)
: the total number of traces in the log
L
.

Based on these quantities, the following metrics are defined:

(1)

Support:
The proportion of traces satisfying the constraint relative to the total number of traces in the log:

\mathit{Support}(L,r)=\frac{\#_{\mathit{satisfied}}(L,r)}{\#_{\mathit{traces}}(L)}.

(2)

Confidence:
The proportion of traces satisfying the constraint among all traces with an activation of the constraint. To prevent division by zero, the denominator is lower-bounded by 1:

\mathit{Confidence}(L,r)=\frac{\#_{\mathit{satisfied}}(L,r)}{\max(1,\#_{\mathit{activated}}(L,r))}.

L_{1}

support(L_{1},r_{1})=\frac{2}{5}

(2)

support(L_{1},r_{2})=\frac{1}{5}
,
confidence(L_{1},r_{2})=\frac{1}{2}

(3)

support(L_{1},r_{3})=\frac{2}{5}
,
confidence(L_{1},r_{3})=\frac{2}{5}

IMr Process Discovery

Figure

provides an overview of the IMr framework, and Algorithm

[
27

. The IMr framework adopts a divide-and-conquer strategy for process discovery, recursively identifying process structures by partitioning the behavior observed in the event log.
The algorithm begins with the following inputs:

(1)

an event log
L\in\mathcal{L}
, capturing observed process executions,

(2)

a predefined set of declarative rules
R\in\mathcal{R}
, which guide and constrain the discovery process, and

(3)

a parameter
sup\in[0,1]
, which regulates the penalization of missing behavior during model discovery.

This framework assumes that the set of rules
R
is provided as input.
However, when domain knowledge is available in textual form, manually extracting these rules is not straightforward. In the following section, we describe how such rules can be automatically derived from natural language inputs.

The first step of IMr is to search for base cases, i.e., check whether the event log consists of only a single activity. In such cases, the process model consists of just that one activity. If no base cases are found, IMr employs the directly-follows graph as an intermediate representation, formally defined below, to determine a suitable process structure at each recursion step.

Definition 5
(Directly-Follows Graph)

Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
be an event log. The DFG of this event log denoted as
\mathcal{G}(L)=(\Sigma,E)
, is constructed, where:

(1)

\Sigma=\{a\mid a\in\sigma,\,\sigma\in L\}
represents the set of activities, and

(2)

E=[(\sigma(i),\sigma(i+1))\mid\sigma\in L^{\prime},\,1\leq i<|\sigma|]
, with
L^{\prime}=[\langle\triangleright\rangle.\sigma.\langle\square\rangle\mid\sigma\in L]
, denotes the multiset of edges capturing directly-follows relationships. Here, artificial start (
\triangleright
) and end (
\square
) nodes are added, ensuring completeness in the graph representation.

At each recursion step, the algorithm extracts a directly-follows graph from the event log. Its task is to partition the set of activities into two subsets,
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, based on a selected cut operator, i.e., sequence, exclusive choice, concurrent, or loop type, that defines the behavioral relation between the activities in these subsets.

Definition 6
(Binary Cut)

Let
L{\in}{\mathcal{L}}
be an event log.
\mathcal{G}(L){=}(\Sigma,E)
is the corresponding DFG. A binary cut
(\oplus,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})
divides
\Sigma
into two partitions, such that
\Sigma_{1}{\cup}\Sigma_{2}{=}\Sigma
,
\Sigma_{1}{\cap}\Sigma_{2}{=}\emptyset
, and
\oplus{\in}\{\rightarrow,\times,\wedge,\circlearrowleft\}
is a cut type operator.
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
is the universe of binary cuts we can generate with the set of nodes
\Sigma
.

Given a set of rules
R
, the algorithm leverages these rules to prune the space of all possible cuts
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
, retaining only those that do not violate any of the specified constraints. Since there are numerous ways to partition the set of activities using different cut operators, the space of potential cuts can be large. The function
\mathit{explore}(\mathcal{G}(L),R)
is responsible for identifying the subset of candidate cuts that are consistent with the rules in
R
.

Constraint violation in a cut is a key concept that guides the process discovery algorithm by eliminating candidate cuts which, regardless of the chosen process structure in the next recursion, result in traces that violate at least one rule in
R
. We formally define the notion of constraint violation in the context of cuts as follows.

Definition 7
(Constraint Violation)

Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
be an event log, and let
\mathcal{G}(L)=(\Sigma,E)
denote the directly-follows graph extracted from
L
. Let
R
be a set of rules. A cut
c=(\oplus,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})\in\mathit{explore}(\mathcal{G}(L),R)
is said to
violate
a constraint
r\in R
, denoted by
c\nvDash r
, if the following condition holds:

for every process tree
M\in\mathcal{M}_{c}
, where

\mathcal{M}_{c}=\{\,\oplus(M_{1},M_{2})\mid M_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma_{1}},\;M_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma_{2}}\,\},

there exists a trace
\sigma\in\phi(M)
such that
\sigma\nvDash r
. In other words, none of the process models induced by the cut
c
can satisfy the constraint
r
for all their traces. Here,
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma_{1}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma_{2}}
represent the sets of all possible process trees over activity sets
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, respectively.

For example, consider the cut

\displaystyle c=(

\displaystyle\rightarrow,\{\texttt{A-created},\texttt{Doc-checked}\},\{\texttt{Hist-checked},\texttt{A-accepted},\texttt{A-rejected},

\displaystyle\texttt{A-canceled}\})

and the rule

r_{2}=\mathit{NotSuccession}(\texttt{Doc-checked},\texttt{Hist-checked}).

We have
c\nvDash r_{2}
because all process models that can be generated from this cut allow for at least one trace where
Doc-checked
occurs before
Hist-checked
, thereby violating
r_{2}
.

Cost functions are used to evaluate the quality of each candidate cut by considering the number of deviating edges and estimating missing edges with respect to a given threshold
\mathit{sup}
. For example, in the case of a sequence cut, any edge from
\Sigma_{2}
to
\Sigma_{1}
is considered a deviation and contributes to the cost. Additionally, edges from
\Sigma_{1}
to
\Sigma_{2}
are expected; their absence results in a penalty based on the
\mathit{sup}
parameter. A higher
\mathit{sup}
value enforces stricter requirements on the presence of such expected edges, while
\mathit{sup}=0
implies that missing edges are ignored and only deviations are penalized.
In each recursion step, the algorithm selects the cut with the minimum cost, splits the event log accordingly, and recursively processes each resulting sub-log until only base cases remain.

4
Domain-Enhanced Process Discovery with LLMs

The core idea of the proposed framework in this paper is to utilize domain knowledge to generate a set of rules
R
which serves as input for the IMr framework. This can be done before starting the discovery by encoding process descriptions as rules, or after the process discovery by having a domain expert review the process model and provide feedback. Engaging in interactive conversations with LLMs in both scenarios helps address uncertainties and improve the quality of the extracted rules.

Figure

These algorithmic components are referred to as
services
throughout the paper. Each service is depicted as a green box, with its inputs shown on the left and outputs on the right.

Overview of the proposed framework for LLM-assisted process discovery.

The orange elements represent input assets and system outputs that are stored internally and are thus enclosed within the green area. Dashed arrows indicate natural language messages exchanged either between services and the LLM, or between the services and domain experts. This section provides a detailed explanation of the framework’s components and their interactions.

4.1
Message coordination and LLM task

In our framework, multiple components can generate messages that are communicated to the LLM as part of the prompt. To distinguish the origin of each message, we define a set of roles corresponding to the entities responsible for message creation. Specifically, we define the set of roles as
Role=\{\mathit{domain\mathchar 45\relax expert},\,\mathit{assistant},\,\mathit{service}\}
, where:

(1)

\mathit{domain\mathchar 45\relax expert}
refers to the human user who possesses domain-specific knowledge and interacts with the system to guide or refine the process discovery task.

(2)

\mathit{assistant}
denotes the large language model, which engages in interactive reasoning, asks clarification questions, and extracts rules based on user input and contextual information.

(3)

\mathit{service}
refers to automated system components that operate independently of the domain expert. These components perform predefined functions, generate structured messages, or process inputs to produce outputs such as error notifications, statistical summaries, or process models.

This role classification enables clear attribution of each message within the interaction flow, thereby supporting transparency, accountability, and traceability throughout the process.

Definition 8
(Message Structure)

Let
\mathcal{W}
be the set of all words and punctuation symbols in natural language, and let
\mathcal{W}^{*}
denote the set of all finite-length natural language texts. Let

Role{=}\{\mathit{domain\mathchar 45\relax expert},\,\mathit{assistant},\,\mathit{service}\}

be the set of roles. A message is defined as a pair
m=(role,w)\in Role\times\mathcal{W}^{*}
, consisting of a role identifier and its associated text. We define
\mathcal{M}=Role\times\mathcal{W}^{*}
as the universe of all possible messages.

Any message generated by the LLM, the domain expert, or one of the services is forwarded to the service manager that determines the appropriate downstream service to handle it. If a message needs to be sent to the LLM, the system constructs the corresponding prompt as described in Section
4.2
, and forwards it to the LLM. Once a response is received, it is routed back to the service manager for further processing.

Definition 9
(LLM Task Interface)

An
LLM task
is a function

\mathit{LLM}:\mathcal{M}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}^{*}

that maps a sequence of role-tagged messages to a natural language response generated by the LLM.

4.2
Services

Upon receiving a message, the service manager inspects its content and makes one of the following decisions:

(1)

If the message is intended for the LLM, the service manager invokes the prompt generation service to enrich and prepare the input.

(2)

If the message is a JSON-formatted response generated by the LLM, it is forwarded to the error checking service for validation.

(3)

If the message contains extracted rules and corresponding evaluation metrics based on the event log, the user is prompted to review the rules and select those that are logically consistent and suitable for inclusion in the discovery process.

4.2.1
Prompt Generation

The prompt generation service is responsible for enriching the message with additional context, including the task description, the list of activities extracted from the event log, and the latest set of rules selected by the user (or an empty set if no rules have been generated or selected yet). The prompt generator also appends a history of previous interactions to provide contextual continuity. The output of the prompt generator is a sequence of messages

Prompt=\langle M_{0},M_{L},M_{R}\rangle\cdot M_{H}\cdot\langle M\rangle\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
composed of the following components:

Task Description

M_{0}=(\mathit{service},W_{0})
such that
W_{0}\in\mathcal{W}^{*}
is a task description that defines the LLM’s objectives.
To ensure reliable constraint extraction, the LLM is assigned the role of an assistant specifically responsible for identifying declarative constraints from input text. Its behavior is anchored through role-based prompting, which explicitly instructs the model to extract constraints, return them in strict JSON format, and request clarification in cases of ambiguity. A deterministic output specification is enforced by defining a rigid JSON structure and explicitly prohibiting explanations, markdown, or non-conforming formats, thereby reducing hallucinated outputs and ensuring compatibility with automated parsers.

The prompt includes a specific set of supported constraint templates (e.g.,
AtMost1
,
Precedence
,
CoExistence
) while forbidding unsupported constructs such as disjunctions and logical operators.
Few-shot learning is implemented by the inclusion of labeled examples for each constraint type, as well as two extended examples consisting of multi-sentence textual descriptions with activity names and their corresponding expected sets of rules.
These examples serve to operationalize the semantics of each constraint and support generalization across diverse phrasings. The prompt is designed to support multi-turn dialogue by instructing the LLM to ask clarification questions when input is incomplete or ambiguous, thereby improving robustness.
Additionally, error prevention strategies are embedded through repeated formatting instructions and examples, which help minimize malformed JSON and other common output issues.

An abstracted version of the task description message is as follows:

Each time a prompt is passed to the LLM, it returns a response

M_{H}=M_{H}\cdot\langle M\rangle\cdot\langle(\mathit{assistant},A)\rangle
.

4.2.2
Rule Validation

The declarative constraints are extracted from the LLM’s response, which is expected to be a well-formed JSON object. The LLM is explicitly instructed to return only the constraints in a predefined JSON format, using a controlled vocabulary and without any additional text or explanation.
Following extraction, the constraints are subjected to a validation process to ensure their syntactic and semantic correctness. This includes verifying that each constraint matches the expected template structure, such as checking that the template identifier is valid and that the correct number of activity parameters is provided. Furthermore, each referenced activity label is checked against the set of activities present in the event log to ensure consistency.

If any issues are detected during validation, an error-handling loop is triggered. A diagnostic message is generated that describes the nature and location of the error, and a new prompt is constructed to inform the LLM of the issue. This allows the LLM to revise its output and produce a corrected version of the constraint set.

4.2.3
Other Services

If the LLM produces a set of rules in valid JSON format and the error validation returns no issues, the rule evaluation service computes the support and confidence of each extracted rule. The resulting statistics are then presented to the domain expert, who performs a final review and selects the rules that align with domain knowledge. This additional validation step provides an extra layer of safety, allowing the user to identify any illogical or implausible rules and, if necessary, revise their own explanations or resolve ambiguities, particularly when the statistical evidence contradicts their initial assumptions.

After the rules are extracted and the desired ones are selected, the domain expert can invoke the process discovery service, as described in Section
3.2
. The service generates a process model, which the domain expert can then review and validate. Based on this evaluation, the expert may provide further feedback or instructions to refine the model if necessary.

4.3
Domain Expert

The involvement of the domain expert in our framework can occur at two key stages: (1) prior to the discovery of a process model, where domain knowledge is introduced proactively, and (2) after an initial model is discovered, where expert feedback is used to refine and validate the model. Each interaction scenario supports different use cases and contributes to the overall quality of the discovered constraints.

Business Context Before Discovery

In this setting, the domain expert introduces the actual business process to the LLM before any model is generated. This input may include a general description of the process, relationships between specific activities, or even declarative constraints formulated in natural language. Such knowledge may originate from formal process documentation, institutional know-how, or operational experience. By providing this context upfront, the domain expert can guide the discovery process and reduce the likelihood of irrelevant or misleading model structures.

Feedback Integration After Discovery

A more common use case involves engaging the domain expert after the initial process model has been discovered. In this scenario, the model is presented to the expert for review, initiating an interactive dialogue aimed at improving its quality. The domain expert is expected to assess the model in terms of accuracy, completeness, and alignment with real-world process behavior. If discrepancies or implausible behaviors are identified, the expert provides written feedback describing the issues and clarifying the intended process semantics. Based on this feedback, the LLM is prompted to adjust the set of declarative constraints to better reflect the actual process.

Interactive Clarification and Refinement

To support deeper understanding and refinement, the framework enables the LLM to express uncertainty and actively seek clarification. When ambiguities or gaps are detected in the input text, the LLM is encouraged to formulate follow-up questions, which are presented to the domain expert. The responses to these questions play a critical role in improving the precision of the extracted constraints. This interactive loop fosters a collaborative refinement process in which the LLM and domain expert jointly contribute to building a high-quality, context-aware process model.

After the successful extraction of rules, a service computes the
support
and
confidence
values for each rule with respect to the given event log. These metrics are then presented to the domain expert, who can decide whether to include or exclude individual rules based on this feedback. The numerical values serve as an important indicator of how well the extracted rules align with the actual event data, helping to identify potential compliance issues or inconsistencies between textual descriptions and observed behavior.

Once the expert selects the desired subset of rules through the interactive interface, the selected rules are passed to the IMr process discovery framework. There, they are used to guide the discovery of a process model that is not only data-driven but also informed by domain knowledge and aligned with expert expectations.

6
Evaluation

Our evaluation focuses on answering several key questions. First, we assess how well large language models can extract declarative rules from natural language input. Another objective of our evaluation is to test the interactive behavior of the framework. Specifically, we analyze whether the LLM correctly engages in clarification when the provided domain input is ambiguous or underspecified. For this, we intentionally introduce vague textual descriptions and examine the LLM’s responses.
Finally, we conduct a case study evaluation using real-life event logs from UWV, the Dutch employee insurance agency. In this case, we had the opportunity to collaborate directly with domain experts to elicit and incorporate domain knowledge into the discovery process.

6.1
Prompting Strategies and LLM Comparison

The proposed integration of large language models relies on receiving textual input from the user, with the LLM tasked with extracting declarative process constraints from this input. The user-provided text is not restricted to a single sentence, it may span multiple sentences or an entire paragraph.
This part of the evaluation investigates how different prompting strategies and LLM configurations influence the extraction of constraints. Our objectives are as follows:

(1)

Quantitative Evaluation:
We introduce a set of evaluation metrics to quantify the quality of LLM responses, enabling a systematic comparison of results across different prompting strategies and model configurations.

(2)

Model Performance Comparison:
We compare several state-of-the-art LLMs to assess whether their performance differs significantly in extracting declarative constraints from natural language inputs.

(3)

Effect of Few-Shot Prompting:
We evaluate whether augmenting the task description with a few examples (few-shot prompting) improves the quality of extracted constraints compared to using only the task description (zero-shot prompting).

(4)

Input Granularity Analysis:
We examine whether there is a notable difference in LLM performance when prompts contain one sentence per desired constraint versus a full paragraph describing multiple constraints.

Evaluation Metrics

The proposed LLM-based method then attempts to extract rules from the input set of text blocks
I
, and its performance is assessed by comparing the results against the annotated ground truth using standard information retrieval metrics. For each
w\in I
, we consider a ground truth set of rules and the set of rules extracted by LLM as:

(1)

\mathit{ground\mathchar 45\relax truth}(w)
returns the set of declarative rules manually identified as correct for a given text block.

(2)

\mathit{LLM\mathchar 45\relax rules}(w)
returns the set of declarative rules extracted by the LLM for a given text block.

We define the following evaluation metrics:

(1)

Recall
measures the proportion of ground truth rules that are correctly extracted by the LLM. It penalizes omissions:

\mathit{recall}=\frac{\sum_{w\in I}\left|\mathit{ground\mathchar 45\relax truth}(w)\cap\mathit{LLM\mathchar 45\relax rules}(w)\right|}{\sum_{w\in I}\left|\mathit{ground\mathchar 45\relax truth}(w)\right|}

(2)

Precision
measures the proportion of extracted rules that match the ground truth. It penalizes hallucinated or incorrect rules:

\mathit{precision}=\frac{\sum_{w\in I}\left|\mathit{ground\mathchar 45\relax truth}(w)\cap\mathit{LLM\mathchar 45\relax rules}(w)\right|}{\sum_{w\in I}\left|\mathit{LLM\mathchar 45\relax rules}(w)\right|}

(3)

Error Rate
is defined as the fraction of prompts that triggered at least one error, thereby requiring activation of the error-handling mechanism.

(4)

Failure Rate
denotes the fraction of prompts for which the LLM failed to produce any valid rule after a maximum of 10 error-correction iterations, resulting in termination without output.

Comparison Between S2S and PAR Input:

Although both the
S2S
(sentence-to-sentence) and
PAR
(paragraph-level) configurations use the same set of sentences and are evaluated against the same ground truth, the results reveal meaningful differences in performance. In the
S2S
setup, where each sentence is processed individually in a separate prompt, we observe an average improvement of approximately
8% in recall
. This suggests that presenting each sentence in isolation enables the LLMs to focus more precisely on the intended meaning and correctly extract the associated declarative constraint, leading to higher rule coverage.

Conversely, the
PAR
configuration—where multiple sentences are concatenated and processed jointly in a single prompt—achieves
around 4% higher precision
on average. This improvement likely stems from the richer context available to the LLM and the reduced number of prompt invocations, which together help limit the generation of spurious or overly general rules not present in the ground truth. These findings indicate a trade-off:
S2S
favors completeness (higher recall), while
PAR
favors precision. Depending on the application, whether maximizing coverage or minimizing false positives is more critical, one configuration may be more suitable than the other.

Handling Ambiguities

To evaluate the LLMs’ ability to handle ambiguous input, we manually constructed a set of 12 sentences that intentionally contain vague or underspecified information. The objective of this experiment is to assess whether the LLMs appropriately enter a question-and-answer mode—seeking clarification from the user—or whether they attempt to generate declarative rules despite lacking essential domain knowledge. For each event log, we created 4 such ambiguous sentences, as detailed below:

(1)

S1:
(BPIC17)
Validating an application occurs at most two times.

Reason:
The constraint template
AtMost2
, which restricts the number of occurrences of an activity to two, is not included in the instruction prompt. As a result, the LLM lacks the necessary schema to map this statement to a valid declarative constraint.

(2)

S2:
(BPIC17)
Each time an offer is created, it is sent via mail only.

Reason:
The activity
O_Sent (mail only)
does not exist in the BPIC17 event log. Instead, the log contains
O_Sent (online only)
and
O_Sent (mail and online)

(3)

S3:
(BPIC17)
The loan application process has three possible outcomes that cannot coexist for a specific application.

Reason:
While the sentence refers to mutually exclusive outcomes, it does not explicitly mention the corresponding activity labels. Without these, the LLM cannot infer the specific constraints (e.g.,
NotCoExistence
) that need to be derived from the statement.

(4)

S4:
(BPIC17)
In certain rare situations, it has been observed that the cancellation of an application may be followed by the creation of another offer.

Reason:
The sentence uses hedging language (e.g., “may", “in rare situations") which introduces vagueness. Declarative process constraints require definitive assertions about behavioral regularities—statements must be clearly framed as either expected or forbidden. The phrase “conflicting actions” is ambiguous. Without a precise definition or enumeration of the conflicting activities, the LLM cannot reliably map the statement to a specific constraint type such as

or
NotSuccession

(7)

The use of hedging language (e.g., “in some cases", “may not be expected") makes the intent unclear. Declarative constraints require definitive behavioral assertions, and such probabilistic or conditional language cannot be directly encoded in the constraint formalism.

To assess the usability of our proposed method, we conducted the case study in several stages. As an initial step, we asked a domain expert to describe the claim-handling process based on their knowledge, without being exposed to any discovered process model. The textual explanation provided by the expert is shown below and serves as the basis for constraint extraction. Given its strong performance in both generating high-quality rules and effectively handling ambiguities, as discussed earlier, the OpenAI o3 model was selected to extract declarative constraints from the expert’s input.

Our process is designed to handle claim applications within an insurance agency, where applicants request various types of benefits, and our system processes these applications through a structured workflow. There are three types of blocking mechanisms: Block Claim 1, Block Claim 2, and Block Claim 3, each with corresponding resolutions: Unblock Claim 1, Unblock Claim 2, and Unblock Claim 3. The process begins once a claim is received. Block Claim 1 occurs when initial issues are detected, leading to necessary corrections before the claim is unblocked and accepted, resulting in the client receiving between one to three payments. Block Claim 2 is applied when the claim is deemed ineligible for receiving any payments, resulting in rejection and potentially an objection from the client. Block Claim 3 is triggered if there is missing information, such as incorrect bank account details, after a claim is accepted; the client is notified to provide the correct details, and the claim is unblocked upon receipt of this information. Additionally, clients may file an objection after receiving payments, leading to the withdrawal of the claim and repayment of the received money to the agency.

7
Conclusion

Event logs extracted from information systems are not the only source of information relevant to process discovery. Incorporating domain knowledge remains an underexplored yet essential dimension in process mining, particularly for producing models that align more closely with real-world operations. This paper proposed a novel framework that leverages LLMs to extract declarative constraints from natural language descriptions provided by domain experts. These constraints serve as supplementary input to guide the discovery of process models that are not only behaviorally sound but also better aligned with expert understanding and organizational context.

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the framework, assessing its performance across multiple configurations and LLMs. These included variations in prompt design (e.g., with or without examples), input granularity (sentence-level vs. paragraph-level prompting), and the LLM’s handling of ambiguous inputs. The results demonstrate the feasibility and potential of using LLMs for this task, showing promising accuracy in rule extraction and robustness in dealing with incomplete or ambiguous input. Furthermore, a case study using a real-life event log from UWV, complemented by direct interaction with domain experts, demonstrated the practical usability of the framework in real-world settings.

Our framework introduces several key innovations: it enables the encoding of domain knowledge into formal rules, supports iterative and interactive refinement through expert feedback, and integrates seamlessly with the IMr discovery algorithm to balance data-driven learning with knowledge-guided modeling.
Ultimately, this work lays the foundation for a more human-in-the-loop paradigm in process discovery, where domain expertise and automated analysis of event data complement one another to produce interpretable, and accurate process models.
