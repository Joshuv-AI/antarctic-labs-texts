Genomic analysis of a clinical Mycolicibacterium houstonense isolate reveals dual escalation in adaptability and virulence
Authors: Ailing Xu, Nifang Pan, Bibo Zhang, Xuan Liu, Yuefeng Sheng, Yimai Deng, Zhenghao Wu, Shuyan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycolicibacterium houstonense is prone to being misidentified as other mycobacteria, particularly M. fortuitum. This study characterized the genome of a clinical M. houstonense isolate and delineated its phylogenetic relationship with M. fortuitum to provide a basis for accurate diagnosis and management of infections caused by this species.MethodsGenome sequencing was used to determine the taxonomic status, virulence profile, and resistance traits of the clinical isolate, while comparative genomics was employed to delineate its evolutionary relationship with M. fortuitum.ResultsThe clinical isolate was definitively identified as M. houstonense. Genomic analysis revealed a unique repertoire of virulence and resistance genes. Its genome harbors 590 virulence genes, many of which encode T7SS effectors. In addition to resistance genes commonly found in nontuberculous mycobacteria, it also carries M. tuberculosis-related resistance determinants, enhancing its resilience to disinfectants, antiseptics, and antimicrobials commonly used in clinical settings. Phylogenomic analysis indicates that M. houstonense and M. fortuitum drive reciprocal divergence, reinforcing their environmental adaptability.ConclusionGenomic analysis is poised to serve as a robust tool for the identification of M. houstonense. The clinical isolate in our study suggests a potential dual escalation of M. houstonense in adaptability and virulence, underscoring the need for stricter clinical monitoring and environmental surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04637-6.Keywords: Mycolicibacterium houstonense, Mycolicibacterium fortuitum, Virulence, Antimicrobial resistance, Comparative genome analysis

Introduction

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) comprise all members of the genus Mycobacterium, excluding the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and M. leprae. NTM, also referred to as environmental mycobacteria, is widely prevalent across diverse ecological niches, encompassing natural environments like soil, water, and dust. While the majority of NTM are saprophytic and do not cause disease, certain species have been implicated in human infections, leading to a range of clinical manifestations [1, 2]. In recent years, diseases caused by NTM have been on the rise rapidly, emerging as one of the significant public health issues that pose a threat to human health [3–5]. NTM can be classified into slowly growing mycobacteria (SGMs) and rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGMs) based on their growth rate. M. avium and M. intracellulare are the most common pathogenic SGMs of NTM. The clinically relevant RGMs include M. fortuitum, M. abscessus, M. smegmatis, M. chelonae, etc [6–8]. M. houstonense, a species of the third biovariant complex of M. fortuitum, was originally isolated from a facial wound of a patient in Houston, Texas. Its biochemical profile is acid-fast, Gram-positive, sorbitol-positive, and pleomorphism [9]. M. houstonense is intrinsically resistant to macrolides due to the presence of the erm gene [10]. However, the mechanisms underlying its resistance to other anti-tuberculosis drugs and antibiotics remain unknown.

In this study, we sequenced M. houstonense (designated as MH_CS1) isolated from a patient with gluteal infection. The patient was a 56-year-old woman. The abscess on her left buttock emerged after she had received 5 times intramuscular injections at a local community health service center to treat pruritus. The injected drugs included ceftriaxone and triamcinolone acetonide injection. The patient self-administered cephalosporin antibiotics, but the swelling and pain remained unrelieved. Subsequently, intravenous penicillin was administered at a local hospital, but the symptom worsened, with increased erythema, fever, and local exudation. Since the draft genome of M. houstonense ATCC 49403T was released in 2016, no additional assembled genome of this species has been deposited in public databases. This knowledge gap has left microbiologists without a reliable reference framework for species identification, drug susceptibility interpretation, or hospital outbreak tracing. We sequenced a clinical isolate of M. houstonense, expanding the public genomic dataset for this species. Moreover, this endeavor not only elucidated the virulence characteristics and resistance profiles of the clinical isolate of M. houstonense but also provided an evidence-based scientific reference for optimizing clinical pharmacotherapeutic strategies. Additionally, M. houstonense is frequently misidentified as M. fortuitum in routine clinical practice, risking inappropriate antimicrobial therapy. To address this, we performed comparative genomic analyses of both species to delineate their evolutionary relationship and genetic differences. This provided a foundation for establishing a simple method to distinguish the two species among NTM.

Materials and methods

Study design

We reported a 56-year-old woman who developed an abscess at the site of intramuscular injections administered at a community health service center. Empirical self-medication with oral antibiotics and subsequent intravenous antibiotic therapy at a local hospital were accompanied by clinical deterioration. Both primary and secondary immunodeficiencies were ruled out prior to treatment initiation. The patient underwent two debridement procedures with thorough drainage to eliminate abscess contents, coupled with six months of intensive antimicrobial therapy. Subsequent close monitoring for local extension or systemic dissemination revealed no evidence of disease spread or lymph-node involvement. Complete resolution of the abscess was confirmed during outpatient follow-up. Mass spectrometry identified the causative pathogen as an NTM. Given the emergence of NTM infections as a pressing public-health concern, coupled with the distinct antimicrobial susceptibility profiles exhibited by different species, we subjected the isolate to whole-genome sequencing to achieve precise species identification. In this study, we aimed to delineate—at the genomic level—potential factors underlying the increasing incidence of NTM-associated disease and to elucidate the genetic basis for the severe clinical presentation observed in this patient.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval for the collection of Mycolicibacterium houstonense hospital isolates and background clinical data was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, Changshu NO.1 People’s Hospital, Jiangsu, China, with approval number L2025037.

Isolate and DNA extraction

Approximately 1 mL of purulent secretion was collected from the patient and inoculated onto a Columbia blood agar plate (Zhengzhou Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd). The plate was incubated at 35 ℃. After 48 h, circular, white, and dry colonies appeared on the blood agar plate. Single colonies were selected for further purification and cultivation. Purified colonies were selected using a sterile inoculation loop and two smears were prepared. One smear was subjected to Gram staining, while the other was subjected to acid-fast staining.

A single colony was picked using a sterile inoculation needle and inoculated into 200 mL of Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium. The culture was incubated at 35 ℃ in a shaking incubator at 150 revolutions per minute (rpm) until it reached the logarithmic growth phase. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃. Genomic DNA was extracted from the purified bacteria using the Bacteria/Fungi DNA Extraction Kit (Magnetic Bead Method) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted genomic DNA was quantified, and high-quality DNA was utilized for library preparation and subsequent sequencing.

Mass spectrometry detection

An appropriate amount of purified bacterial colonies was selected from the blood agar plate using a sterile inoculation needle and evenly distributed onto the target plate. One microliter of 70% formic acid was added and allowed to dry at room temperature. Subsequently, 1 µL of matrix solution was added. After drying at room temperature, MALDI-TOF MS identification was performed from the colony with VITEK-MS system (bioMérieux, Saint-Laurent, Canada) using the in vitro diagnosis (IVD) knowledge database V3.2 [11].

Genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was collected and purified. The Covaris system was used for genomic DNA fragmentation, and a genomic sequencing library was constructed. Using the Illumina sequencing platform, DNA samples that passed quality control were used to construct fragments with an insert size of 400 bp for PE150 paired-end sequencing. The raw sequencing data was 166.66× sequencing coverage. Quality control was performed on raw data to obtain clean data, and multiple scaffolds were finally assembled. The genome sequencing and assembly were performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. The MH_CS1 genome assembly sequence was annotated using Prokka v1.14.5 [12] with default parameters.

Phylogenomic and comparative genomic analyses

The average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were calculated using FastANI v1.33 [13] to determine the taxonomic status of the isolates at the genomic level. The results were visualized and plotted with the R package pheatmap. The pan-genome analysis was performed on the gff3 files annotated by Prokka using Roary v3.13.0 [14]. The minimum percent identity by BLASTp for Roary software was set to 95%. The data results were visualized using R v4.1.2.

To investigate the evolutionary relationship between the strains isolated in this study and M. fortuitum, we used Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) to detect single nucleotide variants (SNVs). High-quality SNVs were obtained using VCFtools v0.1.13 [15]. The maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree based on high-quality SNVs was constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.12 [16]. The best model for tree was selected using the ModelFinder program [17]. The ultrafast bootstrap was repeated 1,000 times. The phylogenetic tree was visualized through the iTOL website (https://itol.embl.de/) [18].

Results

Identification of a clinical isolate of M. houstonense

To identify the isolate, the patient’s abscess drainage fluid was inoculated onto Columbia blood agar plates and incubated. The colony was round, small, white, and relatively dry. The bacterial cells of the isolate were weakly Gram-positive, with a slender, straight or slightly curved shape, some exhibited distinctly visible branches. The strain was identified as M. fortuitum by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) technology with the VITEK MS (a fully automated rapid microbial mass spectrometer by bioMérieux) (Figure S1). However, based on the concatenated sequences comparison analysis of 31 housekeeping genes (dnaG, frr, infC, nusA, pgk, pyrG, rplA, rplB, rplC, rplD, rplE, rplF, rplK, rplL, rplM, rplN, rplP, rplS, rplT, rpmA, rpoB, rpsB, rpsC, rpsE, rpsI, rpsJ, rpsK, rpsM, rpsS, smpB and tsf) [19], the strain isolated in this study belonged to M. houstonense (Fig. 1). A multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the 16–23S rRNA intergenic spacer region (ITS) has been developed to differentiate NTM species [20]. Thus, we compared the 16–23S rRNA ITS in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, and found that the strain in this study had the highest consistency of 99.02% with M. houstonense (Table 1).

Genomic features and ANI analysis for confirmation of the M. houstonense isolate

To clarify the taxonomic position of the isolation in this study, we conducted genome sequencing. The assembly has a genomic size of 6,375,595 bp with a G + C content of 67.2%. The sequenced genome of this study has an N50 value of 231,098 bp, N90 size of 61,826 bp and uncalled bases (N) rate of 0.055%, indicating the high quality of this assembly. The number of coding sequences (CDSs) was 6169, accounting for 92.65% of the genome. The predicted numbers of rRNAs, tRNAs, and sRNAs were 4, 54, and 29, respectively. An overview of the strain genome is shown in Fig. 2.

The ANI values of the strain obtained in this study and 63 strains downloaded from the NCBI database for the genus M. fortuitum (62) and M. houstonense (1) were used to assess the overall genome similarity (Fig. 3 and Table S1). The results indicated that the highest ANI value between the strains in this study and M. houstonense downloaded from NCBI was 98.43%, while the ANI values with M. fortuitum ranged from 85.92% to 86.12%. According to previous research reports, the ANI threshold for strains considered to belong to the same species is 95% [13]. Therefore, the strain isolated in this study was identified as M. houstonense (designated as MH_CS1).

Virulence profile and the resistance traits of the M. houstonense isolate

The virulence factor database (VFDB, http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs) is a comprehensive and integrated web-based database for managing information on pathogen virulence factors [21]. The analysis of virulence genes indicated that the MH_CS1 genome contained 590 virulence genes (Fig. 4A). Among them, there were many effectors encoding genes of the Type VII secretion system (T7SS), which is a specialized secretion apparatus primarily found in Gram-positive bacteria, and is also known as ESX (essential for secretion) system. It has been found that the ESX system is crucial for mycobacterial growth and virulence [22]. The MH_CS1 genome not only contained the ESX-1 (mycP1, eccA1, eccB1, eccCa1, eccCb1, eccD1, eccE1, espG1, espH, espI, espR, espL, PE35 and PPE68) virulence factors found in RGM, but also included the espK gene associated with SGM, which might be one of the reasons for the enhanced virulence of MH_CS1. Moreover, the MH_CS1 genome contained ESX-3 virulence-related genes, such as eccA3, eccB3, eccC3, eccD3, eccE3, PPE4, PE5, mycP3, esxG, esxH, and espG3. The ESX-3 is involved in iron and zinc acquisition, as well as regulation of host immunity. Currently, ESX-5 has uniquely been reported in slow-growing and pathogenic species, primarily involved in immune regulation and nutrient uptake [23]. However, the genome of MH_CS1 in this study contained two ESX-5 related genes, mycP5 and cyp143. This might be attributable to the strain experiencing environmental stressors. In MH_CS1 genome, we did not find ESX-2 (associated with SGM) and the ancient ESX-4 (Table S2).

Through annotation using the comprehensive antibiotic resistance database (CARD), it was found that the MH_CS1 strain harbors a variety of genes encoding bacterial resistance to disinfectants and antiseptics as well as to antimicrobials. Among the detailed annotated genes, the highest were associated with “disinfecting agents and antiseptics”, followed by resistant genes to fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, cephalosporins, penams, aminoglycosides, macrolides, phenicol antibiotics, rifampin and so on (Fig. 4B). Among them, the gene sequences related to rifamycin, phosphonic acid, tetracycline, isoniazid-like, aminoglycoside, pyrazine, and peptide antibiotics showed a high degree of alignment consistency, exceeding 90% (ranging from 90.6% to 97.3%). Particularly, the 97.3% identity between gene5790 and the rifampicin resistance-associated gene rbpA suggested a robust resistance of MH_CS1 to rifamycin. Additionally, we annotated in MH_CS1 genome the drug resistance-related genes commonly found in M. tuberculosis, such as mutations in iniA, iniC, pncA, and ribD, which conferred resistance to isoniazid, ethambutol, pyrazinamide, and para-aminosalicylic acid, respectively. Interestingly, in addition to the presence of drug resistance genes associated with M. tuberculosis, MH_CS1 exhibited resistance genes analogous to those identified in Planobispora rosea (79.8%), Nocardia farcinica (70.8%), M. leprae (74.0% to 91.2%), and Mycolicibacterium smegmatis (94.7%) (Table S3 and Table 2). The complex virulence and resistance genes of MH_CS1 indicated that the strain had strong environmental adaptability, which might explain why the strain differed from previously reported isolates of M. houstonense and M. fortuitum and contributed to the exacerbation of the patient’s condition.

Comparative genome analysis of the isolate of M. houstonense and M. fortuitum

M. houstonense was previously known as the third biotype of M. fortuitum [9]. To clarify the evolutionary relationship between MH_CS1 and M. fortuitum, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on genome SNVs. The results indicated that MH_CS1 and ATCC 49403T were located at the terminal end of the evolutionary branch and originated from the differentiation of M. fortuitum, which was consistent with the findings in existing literature. According to the phylogenetic relationships, a strain isolated from Mozambique belonged to cluster 1. Cluster 2 was composed of a strain isolated from Felis catus in Brazil and eight strains isolated from humans in Ethiopia. The remaining strains clustered into cluster 3, which included 5 strains of M. fortuitum isolated from China and the MH_CS1 isolated in this study (this strain in red font in Fig. 5). Notably, the five M. fortuitum strains isolated from China displayed a clear sequential evolutionary pattern, reflecting the diversity and complexity of these Chinese isolates. Except for isolate_4, the other four Chinese M. fortuitum isolates (isolate_1, MF GZ001, ATCC 35855 and isolate_2) diverged earlier than the strain isolated in this study (Fig. 5). This suggested that M. fortuitum and M. houstonense facilitated each other’s differentiation and enhanced their adaptability to the environment.

To retrieve more insights into the genome of this study, we compared the strain with the Mycobacterium species that were found to be closely related according to our phylogenetic analyses above, namely M. houstonense ATCC 49403T, M. fortuitum 1165541.1, M. fortuitum W4, M. fortuitum E2981, M. fortuitum E3377, M. fortuitum GA-0871, M. fortuitum isolate_4, M. fortuitum E2378 and M. fortuitum NS-7455. Egy-CU-AM1 and Egy-CU-AM2 were not included in the analysis of this part due to the number of scaffolds exceeding 200. We considered that the genome assembly of these two strains was incomplete. Through the pan-genome analysis, 9 strains shared 14,528 genes (Fig. 6). Compared with the 8 strains of M. fortuitum, MH_CS1 had 4,825 unique genes, of which 4,400 (91.2%) were hypothetical proteins. Antibiotic resistance genes and virulence genes account for 17.6% (75/425) of the remaining genes with defined functions suggested that MH_CS1 was more likely to survive and had stronger pathogenicity than 8 strains of M. fortuitum in hospital environment. Among them, some were the resistance and virulence genes that had been identified in M. tuberculosis, such as glpK, gatD, and glnA [59–61]. Moreover, the unique genetic determinants of MH_CS1 include genes associated with bacterial metabolic processes such as pxpA, ackA, acsA and fdhA. Some metabolic genes could indirectly affect bacterial antibiotic resistance and virulence (Table S4). For example, the absence of gdhA affected the adaptability of Streptococcus pneumoniae to high temperatures, thereby affecting its virulence [62]. The results indicated that MH_CS1 developed extensive antimicrobial resistance through a variety of mechanisms under the stress of environmental pressures.

Discussion

Currently, the incidence and prevalence of NTM infection in some countries and regions have even surpassed those of tuberculosis. The rising trend may be attributed to improved laboratory culture techniques, greater clinical recognition, an aging population, expanding immunosuppressed cohorts, long-term use of antimicrobials and immunosuppressants, increased environmental exposure to NTM, and potential human-to-human transmission [2, 3, 5, 63–65]. M. fortuitum is a relatively common pathogenic NTM species [4, 5, 66]. In this study, we isolated an NTM strain from the purulent secretion of a 56-year-old patient, which was initially identified as M. fortuitum by MALDI-TOF MS. However, based on the comparison of 16–23S rRNA ITS and the results of genome sequencing, the strain was ultimately confirmed to be M. houstonense. Our findings underscore that M. houstonense is frequently misidentified as M. fortuitum in routine clinical diagnostics, a discrepancy that may lead to inappropriate antimicrobial therapy and compromise patient outcomes.

In recent years, MALDI-TOF MS continues to be valued for microbial identification and clinical diagnosis due to its speed, sensitivity and cost-efficiency. While identification accuracy of MALDI-TOF MS is dependent on the comprehensiveness and currency of the reference mass spectral database. Rare or novel bacterial strains that not included in the database can lead to misidentifications. Moreover, this technique is limited to distinguish between highly similar bacterial species, as it relies on mass spectra of whole-cell proteins, which may show minimal differences for closely related taxa [67]. Genome sequencing reveals the complete genetic information of a strain and allows for accurate species identification. With the increasing popularity and decreasing cost of next generation sequencing (NGS), it is expected to play an increasingly important role in the accurate identification of M. houstonense and other NTM. In this study, findings from 16 to 23S rRNA ITS alignment were consistent with those of ANI analysis. Given this consistency, we recommend the 16–23S rRNA ITS as a reliable tool for identifying this group of mycobacteria.

NTM are widely present in natural environments, the water system in hospitals, such as faucets, humidifiers, dialysis equipment, etc., is a potential “storage facility” for them. Among extrapulmonary manifestations related to NTM, cutaneous infections occur occasionally in hospital settings and are frequently observed following surgical procedures, cosmetic interventions, acupuncture, and acupoint injections. M. fortuitum is regard as an NTM associated with skin and soft tissue infections, while M. houstonense has been linked to similar infections but with potentially different transmission routes (e.g., contaminated water or medical devices) [9, 68, 69]. Previous studies have indicated that there is no difference in drug resistance between M. houstonense and M. fortuitum [9, 70]. However, the MH_CS1 isolate in this study harbors the erm gene, indicating a strong potential for resistance to macrolide antibiotics, whereas M. fortuitum is typically susceptible to these agents. To further explore the pathogenicity and drug resistance characteristics of MH_CS1, the resistance and virulence genes in the genome were finely analyzed using CARD and VFDB. The results showed that MH_CS1 carried not only the virulence genes common to RGM but also the virulence genes of M. tuberculosis. This indicated that the virulence of this strain might be stronger than that of RGM in natural environments. The strain harbors 80 resistance genes to disinfectants and antiseptics [71] as well as many resistance genes to antimicrobials that are commonly used in clinical practice (e.g., macrolides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, and β-lactams). This might be the result of long-term evolution and selection in hospital environment. The majority of resistance genes predicted in MH_CS1 have already been expressed and shown to confer phenotypic resistance in M. fortuitum and other mycobacterial species (Table 2). This strongly suggests that MH_CS1 is predisposed to express a clinically relevant resistance phenotype, thereby potentially undermining the therapeutic efficacy of guideline-recommended antimycobacterial regimens.

In addition, based on the phylogenetic tree of 62 M. fortuitum strains and MH_CS1, we found that the M. fortuitum strains isolated in China were clustered in different clades and had an evolutionary order. This demonstrated the complex and diverse genomes of M. fortuitum strains isolated in China. Moreover, MH_CS1 and ATCC 49403T evolved later than isolate_1, MF GZ001, ATCC 35855, and isolate_2, but earlier than isolate_4. This implies the potential existence of synergistic evolutionary mechanisms among RGMs. These results elucidate the close phylogenetic affinity between M. houstonense and M. fortuitum, thereby providing novel insights into the deep evolutionary underpinnings of pathogenicity within the RGMs.

The antimicrobial resistance and virulence traits encoded by the genome of strain MH_CS1 may contribute to the enhanced environmental adaptability of M. houstonense, potentially increasing its clinical relevance. This dual escalation in adaptive capacity and virulence collectively contributes to an ever-growing threat to human health, particularly among immunocompromised populations. Such trends thus warrant heightened vigilance in clinical monitoring, environmental surveillance, and the development of targeted intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Genomic characterization of M. houstonense isolates facilitates accurate clinical identification and clarifies the species’ evolutionary history. Our findings reveal reciprocal enhancement of environmental adaptability between M. houstonense and M. fortuitum, advancing our understanding of nontuberculous mycobacterial diversity and adaptive evolution, and providing a genomic basis for optimized infection prevention and control strategies.
