Chemophenetic Analysis of Ten Wild‐Growing East Adriatic Helichrysum italicum (Roth) G. Don (Gnaphalieae, Asteraceae) Populations
Authors: Milica Trajković, Pedja Janaćković, Milan Gavrilović, Uroš Gašić, Ivana Vitasović Kosić, Nemanja Rajčević
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe essential oil (EO) and phenolic composition of Helichrysum italicum (Roth) G. Don from natural populations in east Adriatic were analyzed using gas and liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry. In EOs of the studied populations, 146 compounds were identified, representing, on average, 97.3% of the total oil composition. The essential oils were dominated by sesquiterpenes. γ‐Curcumene was among the most abundant compounds in all samples. Twelve compounds were present in concentrations greater than 5% in at least one of the ten populations. In the extracts, 73 phenolic compounds were identified, belonging to four classes. Compound classes included phenolic acids, flavonoid glycosides, flavonoid aglycones, pyranones, and related compounds. Arzanol was the most prominent peak across all samples, and pyranones and related compounds were the most abundant. No correlations between EO and flavonoid aglycone composition and the studied environmental parameters were found, whereas phenolic acids and glycosides showed correlations with temperature and precipitation.Keywords: chemophenetics, environmental parameters, immortelle, phenolics, volatile compounds

Introduction

The genus Helichrysum Mill. (Asteraceae family) includes approximately 600 accepted species distributed worldwide [1, 2]. Helichrysum italicum (Roth) G. Don, commonly known as immortelle, is a typical Mediterranean plant that grows in arid areas of Southern Europe on alkaline, dry, sandy, and poor soils [3]. It is an aromatic shrub, 30–70 cm high [4], with elongated, linear leaves. The underside of leaves is silvery due to tomentose and glandular hairs [5]. The capitula is composed of numerous bright yellow flowers with a strong, curry‐like aroma [6]. Their essential oil (EO) is located in glandular hairs on flower petals, sepals, bracts, and stem leaves [7].

According to the Plants of the World database (https://powo.science.kew.org/), five subspecies were previously recognized within H. italicum: H. italicum subsp. italicum (Roth) G. Don (widespread in Italy, Croatia, eastern Mediterranean coast of France and Corsica, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Greece—mainly Aegean islands and Cyprus), subsp. microphyllum (Willd.) Nyman (endemic to Crete), subsp. siculum (Jord. & Fourr.) Galbany, L. Sáez & Benedí (endemic to Sicily) [8], subsp. ericoideum (Fiori) Georgiado and subsp. pseudolitoreum (Fiori) Bacch., Brullo & Mossa. Herrando‐Moraira et al. [9]. provided a taxonomic revision of the whole H. italicum complex. The Majorcan Mountain populations of H. italicum subsp. microphyllum is now considered as H. massanellanum Herrando, J. M. Blanco, L. Sáez & Galbany; H. italicum subsp. tyrrhenicum (Bacch., Brullo & Giusso) Herrando, J.M.Blanco, L.Sáez & Galbany is now sixth subspecies and it comprises populations of H. italicum subsp. microphyllum from Corsica, Sardinia, Majorca coastline, and Dragonera islet, while subsp. microphyllum is restricted to the island of Crete.

Numerous scientific studies, summarized in Ninčević et al. [3]. confirm the health‐promoting properties of immortelle. Due to the large number of subspecies and genotypes, along with the influence of environmental conditions (e.g., climate and soil type), there is significant variation in EO quality [10, 11]. A wide range of variations in the composition of the EO of immortelle has been identified [12, 13, 14, 15]. Although its EO is highly valued, there is no official quality assessment standard. However, there are two main types of H. italicum EO on the market: Corsican (rich in oxygenated monoterpenes, especially neryl acetate) and Balkan (with dominant sesquiterpenes γ‐curcumene and ar‐curcumene, and monoterpene α‐pinene) [16]. Researchers who studied the chemical composition of H. italicum natural populations in Croatia found significant variability in the EO. This diversity is attributed to the influence of geographical origin [17], environmental factors, growth stage [18], genetic factors [19], and processing methods [20]. Among phenolic compounds, numerous chemical subgroups have been identified in H. italicum, including flavonoids, acetophenones, coumarins, phenolic acids, and their derivatives [21]. Several previous studies have shown that flavonoids are the major part of phenolic compounds present in the extracts of H. italicum [22, 23].

Most of the studies regarding phytochemistry of H. italicum are focused on bioactivity, while variability was rarely directly studied. Our aim was to investigate variability in the composition of EOs and extracts from different populations from Croatia and Montenegro. Additionally, environmental parameters that could potentially influence the variability of the studied specialized metabolites were also analyzed.

Results and Discussion

Essential Oil Composition and Variability

The composition of EOs of the studied populations is shown in Table 1. In EOs of the studied populations, 149 compounds were detected, of which 146 were identified, representing an average of 97.3% of the total oil composition. An average of 64 compounds were detected per population, of which 62 were identified. The EOs were dominated by sesquiterpenes (56.0%–75.1%). A high percentage of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons characterized the EOs of populations HI1, HI2, HI4, HI5, HI7, and HI8, while EOs of HI6, HI9, and HI10 were dominated by oxygenated sesquiterpenes. In EOs of population HI3, percentages of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and oxygenated sesquiterpenes were about equal. The most abundant components were α‐pinene (HI1), γ‐curcumene (HI2, HI8, and HI9), neryl acetate (HI3), β‐selinene (HI4, HI5, and HI7), ledol (HI6), and 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudesmol (HI10). γ‐Curcumene was among the most abundant compounds in all samples (5.4%–17.2%). Twelve compounds were present in concentrations greater than 5% in at least one of the ten populations. The most abundant compounds were α‐pinene, γ‐curcumene, ar‐curcumene, (E,Z)‐2,6‐dodecadienal‐1‐al, β‐selinene, α‐selinene, neryl acetate, ledol, rosifoliol, 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudesmol, selin‐11‐en‐4‐α‐ol, and (E)‐nerolidyl isobutyrate.

Principal component analysis (PCA) separated all samples into two groups (Figure 1a). The first two eigenvectors accounted for 58.1% of the total variability. Most of the variability is attributed to four components: ledol, α‐pinene, γ‐curcumene, and 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudosmol. On the first axis, population AC_Stoja (HI6) is clearly separated from the other populations, characterized by a high abundance of ledol and a lower percentage of γ‐curcumene and α‐pinene. Due to differences in percentages of 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudesmol, β‐selinene, and neryl acetate, the rest of the populations formed three clusters on the second axis. Populations Orjen (H1), Kukljica (H2), and Pula (H8) differed from the rest in the lowest percentages of 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudesmol and the highest percentages of γ‐curcumene and α‐pinene. On the other hand, populations Brsač and Kali had the highest levels of 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudesmol, positioning them on the opposite end of the second axis.

Hierarchical cluster analysis (Euclidean distances, UPGMA) supports the separation of AC_Stoja (HI6) from all other samples due to the high presence of ledol, and Orjen (HI1) from all others due to a high presence of α‐pinene. In contrast, all other populations are separated into distinct clades due to differences in the abundance of several components (Figure 1b).

Multivariate tests of EO composition revealed high variability within the sample, separating geographically proximate populations (Figure 1). For example, two geographically proximate populations, Kukljica (HI2) and Kali (HI3), exhibited different primary components, and their EO profiles differed substantially. The same can be said for samples from Istria (HI6–HI9), which showed even greater variability in a small geographic area. The differences in EO profiles cannot be attributed solely to environmental factors, suggesting the presence of putative chemotypes in the studied area. Since each sample is a pooled sample of individuals from a single locality, one may assume that differences between closely spaced sampling locations could be due to differences in chemotype ratios within each sample.

Results of literature search on immortelle EO composition are presented in Table 2. The classification of H. italicum chemotypes was generated by Aćimović et al. [15]. Depending on the main constituents, the authors identified ten chemotypes of H. italicum, namely (1) high neryl acetate chemotype (50.5%–83.4%), (2) moderate neryl acetate chemotype (19.5%–48%), (3) neryl acetate + ar‐curcumene (3.9%–20.3% and 0.8%–14.5%, respectively), (4) ar‐curcumene + γ‐curcumene (17.9%–28.6% and 12%–22%, respectively), (5) γ‐curcumene (13.6%–27.7%), (6) high α‐pinene chemotype (25.2%–53.5%), (7) moderate α‐pinene (5.6%–20%), (8) juniper camphor (25.3%–45.1%), (9) β‐selinene (11.6%–38%) and (10) italidiones chemotype.

According to this classification, HI1 belongs to a moderate α‐pinene chemotype, which was also recorded in Croatia [5] and Bosnia and Herzegovina [12, 25]. Sample HI2 can be assigned to the γ‐curcumene chemotype, which was also present in samples from Montenegro and Portugal, as well as in some samples from Serbia [22, 34, 35]. Sample HI7 belongs to the β‐selinene chemotype, which is also found in a sample from Montenegro [33]. However, some samples did not fit into any of the previously described chemotypes. For example, HI3 and HI10 had dominant 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐eudesmol; HI4 and HI5 β‐selinene (but lower than 11.6%); HI6 had ledol as a dominant compound, while HI8 and HI9 had γ‐curcumene as a dominant compound (but lower than 13.6%). 5‐epi‐7‐epi‐α‐Eudesmol and ledol, while previously detected, were never dominant compounds in H. italicum EO [30, 32].

Phenolic Composition and Variability

LC‐MS analyses identified 73 phenolic compounds belonging to four classes: phenolic acids, flavonoid glycosides, flavonoid aglycones, and pyranones and related compounds. Since there are no available standards for most compounds, only the total areas under the curves were used in the analysis, which do not correspond to quantities. However, within the same chemical class, the relative ratios of compounds could approximate the relative abundances of their components. Arzanol (56) was the most dominant peak in all samples. Of the eight identified phenolic acids, dicaffeoylquinic acid (7) was the highest peak in all samples. Nine flavonoid glycosides were identified, and the most dominant were based on quercetin and kaempferol. Of the 15 flavonoid aglycones found, three were the most prominent in all samples: isorhamnetin (23), hispidulin (28), and acacetin (32). The most numerous were pyranones and related compounds (cf. Table S1).

For multivariate analysis of phenolics from ethyl acetate extracts, data were log‐transformed (log(x+1)). PCA scatter showed separation of samples into several groups. The first two eigenvectors account for 40.7% of the total variability, with eight compounds contributing the most. Interestingly, four of these compounds were flavonoids (11, 14, 15, and 20), while the remaining were pyranones and related compounds (Figure 2a). What is more interesting, the class of compounds with the lowest areas in the sample (11, 14, and 15) contributed the most to the separation of the samples. Two flavonoids (compounds 14 and 20) separate populations into three groups. Samples Pariževačka and Banjole had the highest abundance of 14, and populations Kali, Brseč, and Pula had the highest amounts of compound 20. The rest fell somewhere between these two groups.

On the other hand, Unije separated from the rest based on the highest amounts of 15, while AC Stoja separated on the highest abundances of 11 and several pyrananes (52, 59, 63, and 65). Since the flavonoid glycosides contributed most to sample variability, PCA using only the flavonoid glycosides was also performed (Figure 2b). Among the eight identified phenolic acids, six (3–8) have been previously reported for H. italicum [36, 37, 38]. Compounds 1 and 2 were reported so far only for Helichrysum arenarium (L.) Moench [39].

Nine flavonoid glycosides have been identified in our study. Six compounds were reported in H. italicum [37, 40, 41], though in these reports, they were specified as glucosides, while we were only able to identify them as hexoses. The remaining compounds are reported in other members of Helichrysum [42]. Among fifteen flavonoid aglycones detected, eleven were previously found in H. italicum [21, 36]. Other compounds were found in different members of the Helichrysum genus [43, 44, 45, 46].

In this study, we identified forty‐one pyranones and related compounds. Among these, twelve compounds were previously found in H. italicum [21, 36, 37]. Compounds 34–41, 43–45, 47–49, 51‐‐54, 60–63, 65–70, and 72 were reported in other Helichrysum species [43, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52].

Correlation Between Environmental Parameters and Phytochemistry of Immortelle

PCA analysis of 19 bioclimatic parameters (Figure S1) shows a clear separation of localities: Orjen and Brseč on one side and Pariževačka glavica on the other, from the rest. On the first axis, separation was primarily driven by the mean temperature of the coldest month (BIO6). The highest winter temperatures were in Unije, while the lowest were in Orjen and Pariževačka glavica. On the second axis, populations Unije, Brseč, and Orjen were separated from all others due to higher values of bioclimatic parameters related to precipitation (BIO12–BIO19). Köppen‐Geiger climate types show three distinct climates, roughly corresponding to the bioclimatic parameter‐based separation. Populations HI2–HI5 have a hot‐summer Mediterranean climate type (Csa), while population Orjen (HI1) has a warm‐summer Mediterranean climate type (Csb). The remaining populations have a humid subtropical climate (Cfa).

Multivariate analysis of bioclimatic parameters revealed groupings of localities that differed from the EO composition, thereby confirming the greater influence of genetics in determining the EO profile. To analyze the correlation of the EO profile with bioclimatic and geographic proximity, Mantel and partial Mantel tests were performed. Tests did not reveal any statistically significant correlation between the EO profile and bioclimatic parameters, even after accounting for geographical proximity. However, a moderate but unsupported correlation (R = 0.3, p = 0.17) was found between the EO profile and geographic proximity. This suggests that geographically closer populations tend to have more similar EO profiles. Given the pollination system of this taxon, the EO profile may reflect the genetic structure of the studied populations. On the other hand, the sampling strategy and the EO analysis limit the ability to verify such a claim. Additionally, since EOs primarily mediate short‐term ecological interactions (e.g., pollinator attraction), and since these interactions can vary sharply across microclimates and local habitats, the volatile profile is oftentimes highly sensitive to local ecological factors and its dynamics. This makes EOs excellent markers for understanding localized intraspecific variability, and signals the chemophenetic value of EOs. Sharp differences in the EO profiles, as indicated by dominant compounds, suggest that the genetic makeup of the individuals is more responsible for the EO profile differences, than just short‐term interaction with the environment. Considering this plant is perennial, a multi‐year study of the same populations/individuals would give a better understanding of the EO variability, and how much of it is attributable to the environment, and how much to the genotype of the individuals.

Although several individual phenolics showed correlations with bioclimatic parameters, the Mantel and partial Mantel tests did not detect significant associations between the phenolic profile and bioclimatic parameters or population geographic location. On the other hand, the linear correlation test revealed associations between total phenolic acid and flavonoid glycoside contents and several environmental factors. For example, latitude showed a strong negative correlation with the content of these two groups (R = −0.8 and −0.6, respectively, p < 0.01), whereas longitude showed a positive correlation (R = 0.8 and 0.7, p < 0.01). This means that the further south and the further east the population lay, the more phenolic acids and flavonoid glycosides it contained. This was also true for the altitude (R = 0.6 and 0.7, p < 0.01). Several bioclimatic parameters were also correlated with total phenolic acid and glycoside content. Phenolic acids were positively correlated with BIO3 (isothermality) and BIO15 (precipitation seasonality), while glycosides negatively correlated with BIO1 (annual mean temperature), BIO6 (min. temperature of coldest month), BIO10 (mean temperature of warmest quarter), and BIO11 (mean temperature of coldest quarter). Total aglycone and total pyranone contents did not correlate with any environmental parameters. This suggests that the flavonoid aglycones and possible pyranones may have taxonomic value in the study of immortelle. Moreover, pyranones are very important as attracting dispersers, defensive agents, and signaling molecules for growth, etc. their synthesis and presence are predominantly genetically determined, which highlights their chemophenetic value. At the same time, phenolic acids and flavonoid glycosides may be modified by environmental parameters, making them suitable for optimizing growing conditions to achieve higher yields of these bioactive compounds. Phenolic acids and glycosides, similar to EOs, have a very strong ecological function since they function primarily in defense against systemic abiotic stressors, such as UV radiation, drought, and soil composition. These environmental pressures tend to be stable over longer periods, so correlation with the studied environmental parameters is probably not an artifact.

Regarding soil type, most of the populations grow on either redzina or cambisols. Eutric cambisols were the most abundant soil type, and all of the putative EO chemotypes could be found growing on them. This suggests that soil type does not significantly influence EO or phenolics profile. Two‐way hierarchical cluster analysis shows that remote populations can share similar phenolic/EO profile, while close populations can have significantly different profiles, attesting to a limited influence of the environmental factors on general composition, though a more complex relationship cannot be excluded (Figures 3 and 4).

Conclusions

This study provided an integrated analysis of the EO and phenolic composition of H. italicum from natural populations in Croatia and Montenegro, contributing to a clearer understanding of phytochemical variability within this species. The results confirmed known chemical diversity, both in volatiles and phenolics, indicating that H. italicum represents a metabolically highly variable taxon, while also adding more information (i.e., additional compounds). Essential oil profiles revealed clear chemotypic differentiation among populations, whereas several samples exhibited atypical compositions, suggesting that existing chemotype classifications may not fully reflect the species' chemical complexity. Phenolic compounds also showed considerable variability; however, their distribution patterns were not always consistent with EO chemotypes. Statistical analyses showed no correlation between EO and flavonoid aglycone composition and the studied environmental parameters, although other phenolics showed correlations with temperature and precipitation. This indicates that volatile and nonvolatile metabolites of immortelle are influenced by different ecological factors. These findings are important for taxonomy and understanding intraspecific variability.

Isolation of Essential Oils

The EOs were obtained from dry aerial parts (5 g). The plant material was chopped and subjected to simultaneous hydrodistillation‐extraction using a Lickens Nickerson‐type apparatus for 2 h. The volatiles were collected in dichloromethane (VWR, Germany) and stored in amber glass vials at 4°C until GC/MS analysis.

GC‐FID and GC/MS Analyses

The GC‐FID and GC/MS analyses were carried out with an Agilent 7890A apparatus equipped with a 5975C mass‐selective detector (MSD), a flame ionization detector (FID), and a DB‐5 MS fused‐silica cap. column (30 m 0.25 mm i.d., film thickness 0.25 mm). The oven temp. was programmed linearly rising from 60° to 300° at 3°/min; injector temp., 220°; detector temp., 300°; transfer‐line temp., 240°; carrier gas, He (1.0 mL/min at 210°, constant pressure mode); injection vol., 1 mL; split ratio, 10 1. Electron impact mass spectra (EI‐MS; 70 eV) were acquired over the m/z range 30–550. Library search, mass spectral deconvolution, and extraction were performed using the software NIST AMDIS (Automated Mass Spectral Deconvolution and Identification System) version 2.64.113.71, with the retention index (RI) calibration data analysis parameters set to the strong level and a 10% penalty for compounds without an RI. The RIs were experimentally determined using the standard method, which involved the retention times (tR) of n‐alkanes injected after the EO under the same chromatographic conditions. The linear RI was calculated for all compounds using the following formula: LRI = 100 × (trs—trn)/(trn + 1–trn) + 100 × n. The search was performed against our in‐house library, which contains 4972 spectra. The relative amounts of the identified compounds were calculated from their GC peak areas.

Extracts

Air‐dried, ground aerial parts (5 g per population) were extracted with 200 mL of ethyl acetate (VWR, Germany). All the samples were ultrasonicated for 30 min in an ultrasonic bath at 25°C. Each extract was filtered through a Whatman No. 1 filter paper after 24 h. The filtrates were evaporated to dryness with a rotary vacuum evaporator and stored at 4°C until use. For metabolite profiling, all prepared extracts were dissolved in MeOH at 3 mg/mL. Extract yields are presented in Table S2.

The LC/MS Metabolic Profile

The metabolite profile of the extracts was analyzed using LC/MS (Thermo Scientific Vanquish Core HPLC system) coupled with the Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (San Jose, CA, USA). The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was equipped with an ESI source operating in negative ionization mode. Liquid chromatography system was equipped with a Hypersil GOLD C18 analytical column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 µm particle size). The compounds of interest were eluted with ultrapure water +0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (MS grade) + 0.1% formic acid (B): 5% B in the first min; 5%–95% B from 1 to 10 min; 95% B from 10 to 12 min; 5% B until 15 min. Full scan MS were monitored from 100 to 1500 m/z with Orbitrap resolution set to 60 000 FWHM, while data‐dependent MS2 experiments were conducted at an Orbitrap resolution of 15 000 FWHM with normalized collision energy of CID set to 35%. All the other chromatographic and mass spectrometric parameters were applied as previously described in the literature [45]. LC/MS data were evaluated using R Studio (version 2023.09.1, build 494) software. Peak picking was performed using enviPick R package and peak correspondence across samples was performed using the density method available in xcms R package [46]. The compounds were identified based on their chromatographic behavior and mass spectra, with those of standard compounds, and also by comparison with literature data on previously identified compounds in various Helichrysum species. Data acquisition was carried out using an Xcalibur data system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA).

Environmental Data and Statistical Analyses

Univariate (Pearson's correlation test) and multivariate statistical analyses (Principal Components Analysis, Hierarchical Cluster Analysis, Mantel test, and partial Mantel test) were performed using Past 5.3 [53] was used for all statistical analyses. For EO data, only components present at >0.5% were included; those that were not normally distributed or correlated with one another were excluded. For phenolics, a log transformation (log(x+1)) was applied for multivariate analyses. The bioclimatic data (temperature, precipitation) for all ten studied localities were obtained from the WorldClim 2.0 set of global climate layers, with an accuracy of approximately 1 sq km [54] using the QGIS software (QGIS Development Team, 2015). Climate types from Present and future Köppen‐Geiger climate classification maps with a 1‐km resolution were also used [55].
