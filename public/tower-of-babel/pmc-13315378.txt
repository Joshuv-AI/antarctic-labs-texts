Pediatric parotid mucoepidermoid carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Gashaw Arega, Abdurrhman Kedir Hamza, Leul Adane, Mesay Tilahun, Fitsum A Gemechu, Adino Melkamu Gobez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalivary gland neoplasms are rare, comprising less than 5% of pediatric head and neck tumors, with mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) being the most common malignant type. MEC in the parotid gland is exceedingly rare in children. Histologically, MEC tumors consist of squamous cells, mucus-secreting cells, and lymphoid infiltration, reflecting their complex cellular composition. This case report presents a 9-year-old female with a one-year history of progressive, painless swelling in the left cheek, accompanied by facial nerve palsy. Imaging studies revealed a heterogeneously enhancing mass in the left parotid gland with features suggestive of malignancy. Surgical resection was performed, and histopathologic examination confirmed MEC with perineural invasion. The patient subsequently received adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Post-surgery, she demonstrated good healing, and with no significant complications from the adjuvant therapy. Literature review described in this case report provides insights into the prevalence, diagnostic approaches, histopathological characteristics, treatment modalities, and prognostic factors for MEC, offering a comprehensive understanding of this uncommon pediatric malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05054-5.Keywords: Salivary Gland, Parotid Gland, Mucoepidermoid Carcinoma, Surgery, Adjuvant Therapy

Introduction

Salivary gland neoplasms are rare in children, comprising less than 5% of pediatric head and neck tumors [1]. Pleomorphic adenoma is the most common, with over 80% being benign; among malignant cases, half are mucoepidermoid carcinomas [1, 2]. Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) of the parotid gland is particularly rare in children [3]. High-grade tumors, including adenoid cystic carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma, and high-grade MEC, have a higher risk of distant metastasis [4]. Independent prognostic factors for MEC of the parotid gland include Tumor stage, pathologic grade, and distant metastasis [5] A study analyzing 967 pediatric patients with major salivary gland cancers from the National Cancer Database (2004–2018) showed that pediatric salivary tumors, predominantly affecting the parotid gland, had a favorable prognosis, with an overall 5-year survival rate of 94.9%, including 90% for the 0–5 years age group and 96% for the 11–15 years age group [6].

Case presentation

A 9-year-old female patient presented with a progressive, painless swelling of the left cheek of one year’s duration. This was associated with left-sided facial drooping, difficulty puffing her cheeks, incomplete eye closure, drooling, tearing from the left eye, and weakness of the left cheek. Her past medical and family histories were unremarkable. On physical examination, she appeared comfortable with stable vital signs. Local examination revealed a firm, mobile, non-tender mass in the left parotid region measuring approximately 2.5 × 3 cm, with intact overlying skin and no discoloration (Fig. 1). Neurological examination demonstrated ipsilateral facial nerve palsy. Preoperative facial nerve dysfunction was assessed clinically; however, formal grading using the House-Brackmann scale was not documented, limiting objective comparison of functional outcomes. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

Clinical photograph showing a mass in the left parotid region (circled in red)

Initial laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, serum electrolytes, renal function tests, and liver enzymes, were within normal limits. Contrast-enhanced CT of the head and neck was selected as the initial imaging modality due to its availability and ability to rapidly evaluate osseous involvement, regional extension, and nodal status. Pre- and post-contrast CT scans demonstrated an avidly enhancing mass in the posterior aspect of the left parotid gland, measuring 3.0 × 2.9 × 2.4 cm, with a central necrotic component and extension toward the gland’s tail. The lesion caused medial compression of the retromandibular vein and loss of normal fat planes with the overlying skin and the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. No radiologically enlarged or suspicious cervical lymph nodes were identified, and there was no evidence of bone erosion, stylomastoid foraminal involvement, or intracranial extension (Fig. 2). Preoperative MRI was not performed, as CT imaging was considered sufficient for surgical planning in this setting; however, the absence of MRI limited detailed assessment of soft tissue and perineural extension. A chest CT scan showed no evidence of distant metastasis.

Pre (A) and post-contrast (B) axial images at the level of the parotid gland demonstrating heterogeneously hypo-attenuating left parotid tumor (A, red arrow) with peripheral enhancement and central non-enhancing component (B, orange arrow). The tumor has no clear fat plan with the skin and anterior part of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (B, red zigzag arrow)

The patient underwent total parotidectomy with en bloc resection of the tumor, including the involved segment of the facial nerve. No elective cervical lymph node dissection was performed, as there was no clinical or radiological evidence of cervical lymph node involvement preoperatively. Histopathologic examination of the surgical specimen revealed features consistent with high-grade mucoepidermoid carcinoma. The tumor was composed predominantly of solid nests of squamous epithelial cells with mildly pleomorphic nuclei, intracellular bridges, and eosinophilic cytoplasm, along with scattered mucous cells and intermediate cells within a fibrous stroma. Two surgical margins were positive, and perineural invasion was identified (Fig. 3A-C). Surrounding lymph nodes were negative for malignancy, and postoperative MRI demonstrated no residual mass. The surgical site healed well, leaving a minimal linear scar (Fig. 4).

The patient subsequently received adjuvant radiotherapy, with a total dose of 66 Gy delivered in 33 fractions of 2 Gy each, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy with cisplatin-gemcitabine. Chemotherapy consisted of cisplatin 75 mg/m² administered on day 1 and gemcitabine 1,000 mg/m² administered on days 1 and 8, repeated every 21 days for a total of six cycles. The patient’s family was counselled regarding potential adverse effects, including the risk of hearing loss, prior to initiation of therapy. The patient tolerated treatment without significant adverse effects.

At the most recent follow-up, 1 year and 4 months after completion of treatment, the patient remains under regular surveillance, with no clinical or radiological evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis. The facial nerve palsy persists, and although facial nerve reconstruction was offered, the patient’s parents declined further surgical intervention. To date, the patient has not developed significant treatment-related adverse effects, including hearing loss.

Discussion

Salivary gland neoplasms are uncommon in both adult and pediatric populations, accounting for approximately 1% of all malignancies, with fewer than 5% occurring in individuals younger than 20 years [7, 8]. Among malignant salivary gland tumors, mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) is the most frequent histologic subtype in children, yet it remains a rare entity overall [9, 10]. The parotid gland and hard palate are the most commonly affected sites, typically presenting as a painless mass with or without facial nerve involvement [11].

The present case is notable for two uncommon features in the pediatric population: very young age at presentation and facial nerve palsy at initial diagnosis. Facial nerve dysfunction is rarely reported in pediatric MEC. In a series of 16 pediatric MEC cases, none presented with facial nerve palsy at diagnosis [12]. When present, facial nerve involvement is generally considered a marker of aggressive disease and higher histologic grade, with important prognostic implications [13]. The early presence of facial nerve palsy in our patient raised concern for high-grade malignancy, which was subsequently confirmed on histopathological examination.

Radiologic evaluation plays an important role in assessing tumor extent and guiding management. While MRI is preferred for evaluating soft tissue and perineural spread, contrast-enhanced CT remains a valuable modality, particularly in resource-limited settings, for assessing tumor size, regional extension, and cervical lymph node status [14]. In our case, CT findings demonstrated features suggestive of malignancy, including central necrosis and loss of normal fat planes, with no radiologic evidence of nodal or distant metastasis.

Histologically, MEC arises from pluripotent cells of the excretory ducts and is composed of variable proportions of mucous, intermediate, and epidermoid cells [15–17]. Tumors are classified as low-, intermediate-, or high-grade based on architectural pattern, cytologic atypia, and cellular composition [9]. High-grade MEC, as seen in this patient, is characterized by solid growth patterns, fewer mucous cells, increased cellular pleomorphism, and aggressive behavior, including perineural invasion and positive surgical margins [15, 18]. These features are associated with a poorer prognosis compared with low-grade tumors [19]. Although molecular characterization such as CRTC1–MAML2 fusion analysis may provide additional prognostic information, it was not performed in this case due to resource limitations.

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for MEC, with the extent of surgery guided by tumor grade and stage [20]. High-grade MEC typically warrants aggressive surgical management, often followed by postoperative radiotherapy to reduce locoregional recurrence [21, 22]. Adjuvant chemotherapy may be considered in high-risk cases, including high-grade tumors and those with positive surgical margins [21, 23]. In our patient, the presence of high-grade histology, perineural invasion, and positive margins supported the use of adjuvant chemoradiotherapy in accordance with international treatment protocols.

Pediatric patients with MEC generally have favorable outcomes, with reported five-year survival rates exceeding 90%, although recurrence rates remain higher in high-grade tumors [21]. At 1 year and 4 months of follow-up, our patient remains free of disease recurrence or metastasis, despite persistent facial nerve palsy. This clinical course aligns with existing literature, which highlights the importance of early diagnosis, appropriate risk stratification, and multimodal treatment in achieving disease control.

Conclusions

Mucoepidermoid carcinoma is the most common malignant tumor of the parotid gland in children, typically presenting as a painless parotid or palatal swelling, with facial nerve involvement occurring in more aggressive cases. This report describes a single patient, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as illustrative rather than generalizable. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of management, with outcomes influenced by tumor location, histological grade, clinical stage, and adequacy of surgical intervention. Prompt and accurate diagnosis is essential, and mucoepidermoid carcinoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric patients presenting with parotid gland enlargement.
