Dynamic feedback BacGuard anchors microbial metabolism to host symbiosis in real-time ulcerative colitis therapy
Authors: Zeyuan Jin, Yaqi Zhang, Zilong Zhong, Zipeng Shen, Lei Huang, Haijun Hu, Xiping Chen, Wenxing Liu, Lanjuan Li, Changyou Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe escalating global burden of ulcerative colitis (UC) underscores the limitations of conventional anti-inflammatory therapies. Although multi-omics insights have propelled gut microbiota modulation to the forefront of therapeutic innovation, current strategies relying on probiotics or fecal transplants remain constrained by empirical designs due to the lack of spatiotemporal precision and real-time monitoring of microbial metabolic vitality in situ. In this study, BacGuard, a metabolically orthogonal microgel platform, was developed to unify the probiotic surveillance and guided dynamic dose regulation with spatially targeted microbiota-associated metabolic modulation. Our core design featured a β-xylosidase-activated chemiluminescent probe (XOS-CL) that was conjugated with xylooligosaccharide-based hyperbranched polymers (HBXOK) and orally delivered via microgels. Thereby, this system enabled real-time monitoring of probiotic abundance and metabolic activity in the colon through enzyme-responsive signaling, while simultaneously promoting short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production via redirected bacterial metabolic flux. This dual-action system created a self-reinforcing therapeutic loop and optically quantifying the microbial activity in a dynamic manner. By resolving the causal disconnects between enzymatic activity, microbiota proliferation, and host interactions, the BacGuard bridged the diagnostic metrics to functional therapeutic outcomes. Anchoring both sensing and treatment to microbial metabolic flux, our platform reimagined the precision gut ecosystem engineering, establishing an image-guided dynamic dose regulation framework that actively preserved the microbiota-host symbiosis through in-time and function-adaptive modulation.Keywords: β-Xylosidase monitoring, Chemiluminescence, Ulcerative colitis, Microgels, Gut microbiota

Introduction

The global incidence of ulcerative colitis (UC), a chronic inflammatory bowel disease, has risen consistently over the last two decades. This debilitating condition is now constituting a growing public health concern due to its substantial impact on patient quality of life [1]. The current standard treatments, including 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) [2], corticosteroids [3], immunomodulators [4] and biologic agents [5], are fundamentally anti-inflammatory in nature, with their primary focus remaining on the treatment and management of disease symptoms, particularly through suppressing chronic inflammation [6]. Notably, multi-mics studies have unveiled increasingly intricate associations between the gut microbiota and human health, thereby shifting research focus toward elucidating its mechanistic roles [7,8]. Based on these discoveries, this paradigm evolution is now translating into clinical practice, with microbiota-targeted interventions emerging as a transformative diagnostic and therapeutic frontier [[9], [10], [11]].

Gut microbiome has emerged as a central regulator of human health, orchestrating critical functions from immune education to metabolic homeostasis [12,13]. Moreover, its dysbiosis is now implicated in a spectrum of disorders spanning inflammatory bowel diseases [14], metabolic syndrome [15], and even neurological conditions [16,17]. Despite this recognition, current microbiome-modulating approaches such as probiotics [18,19], prebiotics [[20], [21], [22]], and fecal microbiota transplantation [22] remain largely empirical, suffering from inconsistent clinical outcomes due to their inability to account for the dynamic, individualized nature of microbial ecosystems [23]. This therapeutic gap underscores an urgent need for precision tools capable of not only interrogating but also actively modulating microbial communities with spatiotemporal resolution.

The gut microbiota engages in dynamic metabolic interplay with the host, where dysbiosis-induced metabolic reprogramming directly perturbs host physiology through microbial-derived metabolites, thereby underscoring the necessity to assess microbial functional vitality in vivo. To address this challenge, current investigative paradigms rely on three principal approaches: targeted quantification of functional metabolites (e.g., fecal butyrate levels via GC-MS) [24], metagenomic sequencing to profile taxonomic composition and functional gene potential [25,26], and spatial multi-omics platforms to resolve microbe-host spatial co-localization and interactions [27]. However, their inability to monitor microbial metabolic vitality in situ and in real-time represents a pivotal translational gap, because this functional parameter is central to defining pathological dysbiosis. Critically, early spatiotemporal detection of aberrant microbial activity could preempt irreversible host damage by enabling timely intervention, thereby positioning the development of tools to resolve metabolic dynamics within native microenvironments as an urgent priority for precision microbiome therapeutics.

Although the enzyme-responsive chemiluminescent probes (e.g., adamantylidene-dioxetane derivatives) [28] have enabled detection of inflammatory [29] and oncogenic signals [30,31], and the microgel systems have facilitated localized cargo delivery [32], these technologies have evolved in parallel and never converge into a unified functional loop. Although recent advances have expanded their applications from mammalian systems to pathogenic bacteria detection in vitro [[33], [34], [35], [36]], their potential for quantification of probiotic metabolic vitality in situ remains unexplored. Critically, controlling bacterial metabolic output requires precise regulation of cellular activity, yet this information is fundamentally lost in bulk assays that measure abundance alone while lacking spatial and functional resolution. β-Xylosidase, a bacteria-specific enzyme, cleaves β-1,4-glycosidic bonds to depolymerize xylooligosaccharides (XOS) into monosaccharides [37,38], which is a critical step fueling microbial synthesis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [39] that are the key metabolites maintaining gut microbiota and immunity homeostasis [40,41]. However, current methodologies fail to establish causal links between the enzymatic activity, subsequent probiotics proliferation, and their functional interplay with the host in vivo. Collectively, this gap highlights the urgent need for viability monitoring in situ [[42], [43], [44]], especially for developing the targeted bacterial modulation strategies and investigating the adaptive alteration of materials in vivo in a real-time manner.

To bridge this divide, we move beyond the incremental improvement of individual material components to engineer a system-level innovation, BacGuard, a metabolically orthogonal microgel platform that uniquely integrates real-time probiotic monitoring with targeted metabolic modulation (Scheme 1). Specifically, the BacGuard represents a chemiluminescent microgel whose signal is switched on specifically by microbial β-xylosidase. The key innovation is that the system integrates a β-xylosidase-activatable probe (XOS-CL) with an antioxidant xylo-oligosaccharide-based polymer (HBXOK) to form a self-reinforcing therapeutic circuit, wherein enzymatic cleavage simultaneously triggers chemiluminescent reporting and releases metabolic substrate for SCFA production. From the perspective of theranostic, this platform operates through enzyme activity-dependent optical signaling, enabling spatiotemporally resolved tracking of probiotic abundance and functional vitality, while simultaneously reprogramming bacterial metabolism via HBXOK channeling to amplify SCFA synthesis. The self-reinforcing interplay between detection and intervention establishes an image-guided adaptive dosing validation framework, where therapeutic efficacy becomes both optically quantifiable and dynamically optimizable. Crucially, by anchoring both surveillance and intervention to the same microbial metabolic flux, the BacGuard transcends conventional monofunctional approaches, establishing a new paradigm for adaptive gut ecosystem engineering that actively preserves microbiota-host symbiosis through real-time, function-guided modulation.

BacGuard for real-time gut microbiota surveillance and adaptive microbiota-associated metabolic modulation to sustain host symbiosis. Following oral delivery, BacGuard targets the colon where XOS-CL is specifically cleaved by probiotic β-xylosidase, enabling spatiotemporal quantification of enzyme activity via chemiluminescence. Simultaneously, XOS-CL degradation fuels microbial metabolism through carbon source provision, driving orthogonal expansion of SCFA-producing probiotics via competitive exclusion of pathobionts. This dual-action mechanism restores mucosal niche occupancy, rebalances immune-microbiota crosstalk, and enhances epithelial barrier resilience.

Synthesis of XOS-CL

A novel chemiluminescent probe, XOS-CL, was synthesized by conjugating a β-xylosidase-responsive leaving group with a 1,2-dioxetane probe. The convergent approach [28,45] for synthesis and structure characterization of the chemiluminescence probe, XOS-CL, is included in the Supporting Information (Figs. S1–S11).

Synthesis of XOS-MA and HBXOK

XOS-MA was synthesized from XOS and methacrylic anhydride (Fig. S12). TK-diamine and XOS-MA were used to synthesize the HBXOK. Briefly, 1 g XOS-MA and 50 mg TK-diamine were dissolved in DMF, and then reacted at 60 °C under the protection of a N2 gas flow for 8 h. The methyl tert-butyl ether was used for precipitation of the target compound. The precipitate was subsequently dried under vacuum to afford HBXOK as the light-brown solid (Fig. S13). The derivative HBXOK-FITC was prepared through covalent conjugation of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) to the terminal amine moiety of HBXOK under alkaline condition.

Characterization for chemiluminescence property of XOS-CL

The emission spectrum of XOS-CL was characterized by fluorescence spectroscopy (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instruments, UK), while the chemiluminescence intensity in other experiments was measured by a microplate reader (INFINITE M200PRO, TECAN, USA).

The XOS-CL (final concentration, 50 μM) was incubated with β-xylosidase working solution (final concentration, 0.15625-10 U/mL), whose luminescence intensity was monitored at different time points using a microplate reader for kinetics profile. Besides, the chemiluminescence intensity corresponding to Bifidobacterium concentrations ranging from 104 to 107 CFU/mL was examined, with the data also recorded using a microplate reader. The reaction products of XOS-CL were characterized by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS, AB Sciex Triple TOF 5600+, AB Sciex, USA).

Interfering substances were co-incubated with XOS-CL (final concentration, 50 μM) for 1 h, before the luminescence intensity was measured. The concentration was maintained at 1 U/mL for enzymes, 1 mM for sodium cholate, 0.9% NaCl, and 4 mM for potassium ions.

Imaging experiments with the probe XOS-CL were performed by incubating bacterial suspensions of Bifidobacterium adolescentis and Lactobacillus brevis (108 CFU/mL) with XOS-CL (final concentration, 20 μM) at 37 °C in dark for 2 h. The samples were then transferred to confocal-specific culture dishes, before the distribution of the probe within bacterial cells was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM900, Zeiss, Germany) with the 63× oil immersion lens.

Fabrication and Characterization of BacGuard

BacGuard microgels were fabricated from HBXOK-CL and sodium alginate via electrospaying. Alginate is regarded as safe and undergoes complete colonic dissolution under physiological conditions [46], and xylo-oligosaccharides exhibit a comparable safety profile [47]. Therefore, systematic repeat-dose studies were planned to verify complete clearance and any immune response. The HBXOK-CL was synthesized by a facile coupling reaction, and characterized by chemiluminescence assay and high-performance liquid chromatography (Figs. S14 and S15). Conversely, the microgels prepared from xylo-oligosaccharides and sodium alginate are designated as XOS by referring to the method in our previous work [48]. Following optical mode of fluorescence microscopy (IX81, Olympus, Japan) characterization of BacGuard hydrated morphology, the particle diameter was quantified by ImageJ. The morphology of lyophilized BacGuard was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU8010, HITACHI, Japan). The distribution of FITC-labeled HBXOK within microspheres was examined by CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

In vitro degradation of BacGuard

BacGuard (100 mg/mL) was incubated with simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.2, containing 2000 U/mL pepsin), simulated small intestinal fluid (pH 6.8, containing 100 U/mL trypsin), and simulated colonic fluid (pH 7.0, containing 108 CFU/mL Bifidobacterium) at 37 °C and 120 rpm for 3 h, 6 h and 12 h. Changes in the morphology of BacGuard were observed using an optical microscope.

Cytotoxicity of BacGuard

Caco-2 cells (ATCC) were propagated in DMEM containing 10% FBS and dual antibiotics (penicillin 100 U/mL, streptomycin 100 μg/mL), with routine incubation at 37 °C under a 5% CO2-controlled humidified environment. The biocompatibility of BacGuard was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Beyotime, China). BacGuard microgels (20 mg/mL) were incubated in DMEM under the conditions of 37 °C and 120 rpm shaking to obtain the extract, which was then diluted to various concentrations. Caco-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 8 × 103 cells per well, and co-cultured with the extract at different concentrations. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% (v/v) CCK-8 reagent. After incubation for 2 h in dark, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Modulation of MNK-3 function

The MNK-3 cells were cultured in the same condition as Caco-2 cells. To investigate the modulation of MNK-3 cells, FICZ (an agonist for upregulating IL-22) was served as the positive control while CH-223191 (an antagonist for downregulating IL-22) as the negative control [49]. Caco-2 cells were seeded into 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well) at first. The cells were cultured for 24 h and then stimulated with 2 μM FICZ (TargetMol, USA), 1 μM CH-223191 (MCE, USA), and 0.5 mM, 1 mM and 2 mM butyric acid overnight. IL-22 in the supernatant was measured using an ELISA kit.

Antioxidative effect of BacGuard

DCFH-DA (Beyotime, China) was the primary choice to evaluate the antioxidative capacity. After seeded into 24-well plates with a density of 5 × 104 cells/well, the Caco-2 cells were cultivated at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were then exposed to LPS (1 μg/mL) overnight. Subsequently, the XOS group and HBXOK group were treated with 500 μg/mL microgels containing xylooligosaccharides and HBXOK for 8 h, respectively. After the medium was changed into the fresh medium containing DCFH-DA (10 μM), the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in dark. The medium was then removed, and the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (10 μg/mL) for 10 min. Finally, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS and examined under a fluorescence microscope (IX81, Olympus, Japan).

Protection effect of BacGuard for gut barrier

Caco-2 cells cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 were passaged and seeded at 2 × 105 cells/mL. The upper chamber of Transwell was rinsed with serum-free medium, before it was filled with 0.2 mL cell suspension. Meanwhile, the lower chamber was filled with 0.8 mL complete medium. After 12 h, cell attachment was checked by inverted microscopy. The medium was changed every two days. Cell growth, morphology, and confluence were monitored on 3, 5, and 7 d. On day 21, cells formed a uniform monolayer. Trans - epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured using a TEER meter (RE1600, Beijing Hongtai, China) after replacing the medium with HBSS. A TEER value over 400 Ω cm2 indicates successful model establishment [50].

The experimental procedure involved first removal of the culture medium from the Transwell apical chamber. The control group received fresh complete medium, while other groups were treated with medium containing 1 μg/mL LPS. For the XOS and HBXOK groups, 1 mg/mL additional compounds were supplemented before overnight stimulation. 0.2 mL of FD-4 solution (1 mg/mL in PBS) was then added to the apical chamber, while 0.8 mL PBS was added to the basolateral chamber. The plates were then incubated for 3 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. TEER was finally measured to assess the barrier integrity, and the basolateral solution was collected for fluorescence intensity measurement using a microplate reader to determine FD-4 permeability. This protocol evaluates both the inflammatory effects of LPS and the protective potential of XOS and HBXOK on intestinal barrier function.

DSS-induced mice model

C57BL/6 male mice at 8 w of age were sourced from the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences Experimental Animal Center. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2025-025) and adhered to the ethical guidelines for animal research. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: Control, DSS, XOS, and BacGuard, and underwent a 7-day environmental adaptation protocol in SPF-certified containment facilities (specific-pathogen-free conditions). Subsequently, the Control group received sterile water, while the experimental groups were administered with a 3% DSS solution [51,52]. On days 2, 4, 6, and 8, mice in the XOS and BacGuard groups were treated with xylo-oligosaccharides-loaded microgels and BacGuard (HBXOK-loaded microgels) at 200 mg/kg (suspended by 200 μL sterile water) via oral gavage. The control and DSS group mice received the same volume of sterile water. After 5 d, all mice were given sterile water, and were euthanized on day 8 for analysis. Daily recordings included stool consistency, bleeding (the occult blood within fecal was assessed using a fecal occult blood reagent set) and body weight changes. The calculation criteria of disease activity index (DAI) scores is summarized in Table S2, [48].

IVIS imaging in vivo

The position and distribution of microgels at different time points in the gastrointestinal tract after oral administration can be referred to the findings of previous research [48]. Therefore, in this study, we utilized imaging in vivo to track the distribution of Cy5.5-labeled BacGuard within the intestinal tract at 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h. Prior to imaging experiments in germ-free mice in vivo, we established an assay to determine the respective survival rates of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus following gastric passage in vitro. The Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus strains (108 CFU/mL) were anaerobically incubated in PBS and SGF at 37 °C for 1 h, and their respective survival rates were determined by plate counting. To demonstrate the feasibility of BacGuard for detection of probiotics in the gastrointestinal tract in situ, three germ-free mice were designated as Control, Lac, and Bifido. The Lac and Bifido mice were administered with 100 μL of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium suspensions via gavage (at 108 CFU/mL), respectively, while the Control mouse received 100 μL of PBS [53,54]. The BacGuard was then administered by oral gavage, followed by CL imageing by using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS, Lumina LT, PerkinElmer, UK) in a bioluminescence mode, with an open filter and a 60 s acquisition time.

A salient feature of gut microbiota imbalance in a colitis setting is the reduction in both the abundance and vitality of probiotics. To evaluate the feasibility of the BacGuard in probiotic changes in an inflamed milieu, a UC model of C57BL/6 mice was utilized. The mice were randomly assigned to three groups. (n = 3). To induce ulcerative colitis, mice in the DSS and BacGuard groups were administered with a 3% DSS solution for 5 d following a 1-week acclimation period, after which they received sterile water for the last 2 d. The Control group mice were given sterile water throughout the experiment. In the BacGuard group, on days 2, 4, 6, and 8, bioluminescent imaging was performed using the IVIS with consistent parameters 2 h after oral administration of BacGuard. Quantification of the chemiluminescent (CL) intensity facilitated the assessment of the inflammation severity. Meanwhile, the stool was collected for 16S rRNA analysis.

To verify that monitoring of the probiotic-associated enzymatic signal in situ can guide dynamic dose adjustment and enable precision therapy, we added three subgroups based on the preceding experiments. From day 0 to day 4, all mice received the same BacGuard regimen established earlier. On day 4, the animals were stratified into mild, moderate, and severe groups according to the chemiluminescence intensities in situ. Beginning on day 5, the gavage doses were re-titrated to high, medium, and low levels (high, medium, and low groups) for the severe, moderate, and mild groups, respectively. Luminescence imaging was then repeated on days 6 and 8 to track signal evolution.

H&E staining, immunofluorescence staining, PAS staining and immunohistochemistry

Colons were collected on day 8, and their length was recorded at first. Then, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was used to fix the colon tissue samples. After dehydration in ethanol, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices. The histopathological features were analyzed via Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining. Additionally, MUC2 immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining analysis for tight junction protein were conducted. The colon tissue sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and rinsing, followed by antigen retrieval using the heat-induced epitope repair method and blocking with goat serum. They were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight, followed by staining with biotinylated secondary antibodies. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed by using a commercial horseradish peroxidase-DAB chromogenic system (Agilent Technologies, USA) for target visualization. Fluorescent labeling was achieved through secondary antibody conjugates. Histopathological evaluation of H&E, PAS, and MUC2-stained sections was conducted via high-resolution digital slide scanning (NanoZoomer S360, Hamamatsu, Japan), whereas fluorescence signals were captured using a multispectral imaging platform (Pannoramic SCAN Ⅱ, 3DHISTECH, Hungary).

qRT-PCR assessment

Total RNA was isolated from colon tissue using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) following the instructions. PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan) was applied to synthesize cDNA via reverse transcription. qRT-PCR experiment was conducted on a 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the specific primers (Sangon Biotech, China). Relative quantification was determined using the 2-ΔΔCT method. β-Actin was used for normalization. The primer sequences used in the experiment are listed in Table S3.

Serum ELISA

Mouse blood samples were gathered and subjected to centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min to obtain serum. The levels of IL-17, IL-1β, and TNF-α were assayed using the Bio-Plex Pro™ Mouse Cytokine Th17 Panel A 6-Plex (Bio Rad, USA). For the detection of IL-22 and LPS concentrations, the Mouse IL-22 Precoated ELISA Kit (DAKEWE, China) and Mouse LPS ELISA Kit (JONLNBIO, China) were employed correspondingly.

Flow cytometry analysis of leukocytes isolated from lamina propria

Leukocyte isolation from colonic lamina propria was conducted through digestion protocol. Briefly, intestinal tissue fragments (0.5 cm segments) were subjected to enzymatic dissociation in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with collagenase VIII (300 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) and DNase I (150 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C with gentle agitation for 90 min. The resulting cell suspension was sequentially filtered through 100 μm cell strainer, centrifuged (400 ×g, 5 min, RT), and subjected to density gradient separation using 40% Percoll (1000 ×g, 20 min). Prior to immunophenotyping, cells were pre-treated with anti-CD16/32 (1:100 dilution) to block nonspecific Fc receptor binding. Leukocytes for ILC3 population analysis were then stained with antibodies targeting CD45 Brilliant Violet 605 (dilution 1:100), RORγt PE (dilution 1:50), and Lineage Percp-cy5.5 Cocktail (dilution 1:50). Live and dead cells were differentiated using the LIVE/DEAD Fixable Dead Cell Stain Kits. The samples for ILC3 cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Cytoflex, Beckman, USA).

Microbiome analysis

Mouse feces were gathered individually on day 8 and preserved at −80 °C. Microbial DNA was extracted from the feces using the Fecal Genome DNA Extraction Kit (AU46111-96, BioTeke, China) according to the instructions and quantified with Qubit (Invitrogen, USA). The 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR with the universal primer pair 341F/805R (341F: 5′-CCTACGGNGGCWGCAG-3′; 805R: 5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). PCR products underwent purification with AMPure XT Beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA), quantification with Qubit, and quality assessment using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer alongside Illumina library quantitative kits. Qualified PCR products were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE250). Raw sequences underwent processing to eliminate sequencing primers using cutadapt (v1.9) and were merged using FLASH (v1.2.8). Low-quality reads were trimmed with fqtrim (v0.94) to obtain high-quality clean tags. Chimeric sequences were removed using Vsearch software (v2.3.4). DADA2 was employed for denoising and generating amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Species annotation was conducted by aligning sequences with the QIIME2 plugin feature-classifier, utilizing the SILVA and NT-16S databases. Alpha and beta diversities were calculated in QIIME2, with bacterial taxonomy based on relative abundance. The Wilcoxon test identified differentially abundant genera (P < 0.05). Additionally, LDA effect size (LEfSe) analysis was performed (LDA≥3.0, P value < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple comparison test, unless otherwise specified in the figure legends. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Design and detection capability of XOS-CL

XOS-CL is the meticulously designed derivative of Schaap's dioxetanes. This novel chemiluminescence probe could monitor the activity of β-xylosidase via the mechanism shown in Fig. 1a. The emission peak of XOS-CL in the presence of β-xylosidase activation was determined at 554 nm, indicating the highly accessible of detection process (Fig. 1b). The chemiluminescence kinetics of XOS-CL exhibited a peak signal at 1 h, after which the signal was stabilized into a plateau (Fig. 1c). The measurements conducted at the peak (for maximum sensitivity) or during the plateau (for optimal stability) would significantly improve the detection accuracy. Notably, the observed changes in the chemiluminescence intensity showed a reproducible regime regardless of the enzyme concentration. Building upon these findings, the correlation between the probe's chemiluminescence intensity and enzymatic activity was systematically examined, revealing a strong linear relationship across a dynamic range of 0.015625-1 U/mL (Fig. 1d). The LOD was calculated to be 2.19 × 10−3 U/mL, indicating excellent detection capability for trace amounts of β-xylosidase. Double-logarithmic linear regression revealed a significant power-law relationship between luminescence intensity and bacterial concentration over the range of 104 to 107 CFU/mL (Fig. 1e). The HPLC-MS analysis unequivocally identified four reaction fragments that corresponded with the theoretical predictions (Fig. 1f and Fig. S16). Notably, the detection of xylose molecules provided direct evidence that β-xylosidase-mediated cleavage of glycosidic bonds triggered the chemiluminescence activation. Selectivity evaluation confirmed the XOS-CL specific response to β-xylosidase with a signal amplification approaching 3 orders of magnitude (∼103-fold) over background, while remaining non-responsive to ionic, pH, or other enzymatic interferents (Fig. 1g and Fig. S17). Next, it is essential to validate the feasibility of the probe in imaging bacteria and to assess their activity. CLSM observation showed that the luminescence of the bacteria overlapped with their profiles, thereby proving that the probe functions as a detector in situ by detecting the bacterial intracellular β-xylosidase (Fig. 1h). Conversely, for bacteria devoid of the enzyme, no discernible luminescence signal was observed (Fig. S18), thereby underscoring the remarkable selectivity of XOS-CL probe. In summary, the XOS-CL facilitates reliable probiotic detection by monitoring β-xylosidase activity, offering a functional approach to assess viable microorganisms.

Behavior of BacGuard at physiological conditions

Microgel stability serves as a critical determinant of functional performance. The BacGuard microgels (231 ± 11 μm) had a monodisperse spherical morphology in their swollen state, and maintained structural integrity post-drying, as evidenced by less than 5% variation in diameter (Fig. 2a and Fig. S19), enabling practical storage and transportation. Cross-sectional imaging also revealed homogeneous distribution of HBXOK within the matrix of BacGuard (Fig. 2b). As a component of the BacGuard, the HBXOK core comprised of xylo-oligosaccharide fragments serves as a selective carbon source for probiotics (e.g., Bifidobacterium spp.), whereas cannot be effectively utilized by the host. Consequently, these fragments are subjected to orthogonal digestion by the gut microbiota in the colon, a process that is pivotal in driving the adaptive alterations of the materials (Fig. 2c). Stimulated gastric fluid (SGF pH = 1.2) with 2000 U/mL pepsin, stimulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH = 6.8) with 100 U/mL trypsin and stimulated colon fluid (SCF, pH = 7.0) with Bifidobacterium at 108 CFU/mL were established to mimic sequential digestive phases. Structural analysis demonstrated the maintained spherical architecture integrity in SGF and SIF, whereas significant morphological disintegration in SCF within a short period of time (Fig. 2d), validating the primary hypothesis. Furthermore, with prolonged incubation, the BacGuard disintegrated in SIF, but remained stable in SGF (Fig. S20). Meanwhile, cell viability assay demonstrated outstanding biocompatibility, establishing a robust basis for subsequent in vivo applications (Fig. 2e).

Performance of BacGurad In vitro

As both a biomarker of UC severity and a critical therapeutic target for mucosal healing, the BacGuard was evaluated with respect to protective effects on gut epithelial function. The gut epithelial barrier dysfunction is originated principally from oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis. Although glycan chains of HBXOK maintained host-pathway orthogonality, their incorporation reduced cellular oxidative burden compared to XOS (Fig. 3a and b). This protection is correlated directly with the redox-modulating capacity of the thioketal group. Given that the colonic epithelial barrier requires both morphological and functional soundness, a confluent monolayer model was employed. The oxidative stress compromised the barrier function significantly, manifesting as drastically reduced electrical resistance and consequent hyperpermeability that was remedied significantly by the HBXOK (Fig. 3c and d). Although the LPS and XOS groups promoted considerable basolateral extravasation of macromolecules (FD-4), the therapeutic administration of HBXOK effectively counteracted this breach (Fig. 3d), thus affirming its barrier-fortifying properties.

Monitoring capability of BacGuard In vivo

To systematically evaluate the capability of BacGuard under varying probiotic conditions, imaging studies in vivo were conducted utilizing both C57BL/6 mice with induced ulcerative colitis (Fig. 4a) and germ-free mice inoculated with defined bacterial suspensions.

Relative abundance of e) Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in stool. n = 5. f) Guided by the chemiluminescence intensity measured on day 4, the mice were stratified into three groups and the gavage dose was adjusted accordingly for in vivo imaging of the probiotics. g) Representative in vivo chemiluminescence images of mice on days 4, 6, and 8. h) Quantification of in vivo chemiluminescent intensity from different dosage groups performed by ROI analysis. n = 3. ns, no significant; ∗p < 0.01.

Based on the in vitro survival rates of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in SGF (Fig. S21), the germ-free mouse imaging assay clearly distinguished the probiotic-supplemented groups (Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) through robust chemiluminescent signals, while the control group (BacGuard only) displayed negligible background activity (Fig. S22), confirming the capacity of BacGuard for probiotic monitoring in vivo. Longitudinal evaluation in the UC model revealed dynamic chemiluminescent patterns, where the DSS-treated mice showed complete signal extinction during acute inflammation that is indicative of profound probiotic depletion. In contrast, the BacGuard intervention maintained detectable signals throughout the experimental timeline (Fig. 4b). Although an initial decline in intensity took place by day 2, the chemiluminescent recovery was stabilized by day 8, reaching to a level comparable to that of the untreated control and significantly stronger over the DSS-challenged ones (Fig. 4c). Upon disintegration of the BacGuard matrix, the encapsulated HBXOK-CL reacts with probiotic-derived β-xylosidase to emit a detectable light signal (Fig. 4d). Supporting evidence emerged from fecal microbiome analysis, which showed substantial enrichment of bacteria Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in the BacGuard-treated mice compared to the inflammatory group (Fig. 4e), further validating the BacGuard ability to preserve probiotic activity under pathological conditions. Having established the BacGuard capability for monitoring of probiotic activity dynamics in vivo, its broader utility in assessing therapeutic outcomes would be explored. Crucially, demonstration of the BacGuard capacity to elucidate the intricate interplay between gut barrier integrity and microbial ecology will require comprehensive experimental validation.

Beyond the initial validation, a pivotal question remains: can fluctuations in chemiluminescence intensity serve as a quantitative guide for dynamic dose modulation, thereby fulfilling the promise of precision therapy? Day 4, as the acute phase of inflammation, is a pivotal turning point when the intestinal microenvironment undergoes drastic alterations. Even under identical BacGuard treatment, individual mice exhibited pronounced variability: those with severe disease displayed markedly reduced luminescence, whereas milder cases retained strong signals (Fig. 4f). Consequently, mice were stratified into three groups based on luminescence intensity, and gavage doses were adjusted accordingly starting on day 5. In the severely affected animals, the weak luminescence on day 4 foreshadowed a substantial suppression of both the abundance and enzymatic activity of β-xylosidase-expressing probiotics (Fig. 4g). Quantitative analyses confirmed that after dose adjustment, probiotic recovery in all three groups was robust and converged toward levels observed in healthy controls (Fig. 4h). These striking results demonstrate that BacGuard-derived luminescence not only reflects probiotic abundance and activity within the colon, but also functions as an indicator of pathological progression, enabling dynamic, individualized dose optimization for precision theranostics.

Therapeutic capacity of BacGuard In vivo

The disease-mitigating efficacy of BacGuard is originated fundamentally from its orthogonal modulation of microbial metabolic pathways, which drives functional adaptation within the gut ecosystem. IVIS imaging revealed negligible residual microgels in the intestinal tract 24 h after oral administration (Fig. S23), indicating rapid clearance of BacGuard and a reduced risk of retention-related safety concerns. The excellent biosafety of the materials was further assessed through comprehensive blood biochemical analysis that collectively contributed to the evidence for its safety (Fig. S24). Primary evaluation of its therapeutic potential was conducted through systematic assessment of host pathophysiological restitution metrics. During the experiment, the most significant recovery indicators included resolution of rectal bleeding, normalization of colon length, body weight as well as decreased disease activity index (DAI) scores, which showed a moderate negative correlation with chemiluminescence intensity (Fig. 5a–e and Equation S(1)). Comparative analysis reveals that the BacGuard treatment produced more substantial therapeutic effects than XOS administration. Histopathological analysis demonstrates striking contrasts between the experimental groups. While DSS administration induced severe colonic structural disruption with extensive inflammatory infiltration, both the XOS and BacGuard interventions preserved near-normal tissue morphology comparable to those of the healthy controls (Fig. 5f). Higher-magnification examination and scores of H&E-stained sections reveal the superior mucosal integrity and goblet cell preservation in the BacGuard-treated specimens compared to the XOS-administered ones (Fig. 5g and Table S4), suggesting the stronger protective efficacy of BacGuard against oxidative damage. These morphological observations collectively indicate that the BacGuard treatment provides enhanced histological protection during inflammatory challenge.

From the perspective of molecular level, the gut barrier function exhibits intricate interplay with inflammatory mediators and tight junction protein expression. Analysis reveals significant downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-17 at both the transcriptional and translational levels (Fig. S25 and Fig. 6a–c). Notably, the reparative cytokine IL-22, typically induced during severe inflammatory challenge, was recovered to the baseline level comparable to that of the healthy control (Fig. S26), demonstrating the accelerated resolution in the BacGuard-treated mice and enhanced inflammatory restoration capacity. Serum LPS quantification (Fig. 6d), which serves as a sensitive indicator of bacterial translocation and gut barrier compromise, coupled with immunofluorescence and expression analysis of tight junction proteins (Fig. 6e), revealed differential restorative patterns between the intervention groups. While the XOS administration showed partial recovery of intestinal integrity, the BacGuard treatment achieved near-complete restoration of ZO-1 and occludin expression patterns (Fig. 6f–h), with spatial distribution characteristics indistinguishable from the non-inflamed controls, indicating superior barrier reparative potential at both molecular and structural levels.

Furthermore, 16S rRNA analysis of the gut microbiota provided compelling evidence for the orthogonal metabolic strategy's selective impact on bacterial communities, with clear correlations to observed tissue repair outcomes (Fig. 7). The alpha diversity metrics revealed enhanced microbial abundance and evenness in the BacGuard group (Fig. 7a and b), as demonstrated by elevated Shannon and Simpson indices. Moreover, the BacGuard-treated specimens approached a Pielou's evenness index of 1, indicating optimal species distribution stability (Fig. 7c). Beta diversity analysis through principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) further demonstrated distinct clustering patterns, where the XOS groups exhibited closer proximity to DSS-induced dysbiotic communities, and the BacGuard intervention restored a microbial architecture indistinguishable from the healthy control (Fig. 7d). Collectively, these findings establish the BacGuard's capacity to maintain a homeostatic microbial state structurally and functionally analogous to the undisturbed microbiota. Taxonomic profiling at the phylum level revealed two distinct compositional patterns: Firmicutes dominated the microbial communities in both the BacGuard-treated and control groups, whereas Verrucomicrobiota prevailed in the DSS-induced and XOS-supplemented specimens (Fig. 7e and Fig. S27). Further stratification to the family level identified a pathological expansion of Akkermansiaceae in the DSS and XOS groups (Fig. 7f and g). Although the Akkermansia muciniphila is generally regarded as a promising probiotic candidate, its aberrant proliferation in this context is correlated with the dysregulated innate immune responses, particularly group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) dysfunction [55]. Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to induce a robust IgE response in mice fed with a fiber-deficient diet [56]. Another research indicated that this bacterium may promote pathogen infection by compromising the mucus barrier and altering the microbiota environment. However, it exerts protective effects through host response modulation under conditions of adequate dietary fiber or hyaluronan treatment [57]. Notably, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) confirmed the absence of such pathological Akkermansia enrichment under the BacGuard modulation, suggesting its capacity to maintain microbial homeostasis while preventing opportunistic bacterial overgrowth (Fig. 7h).

Comprehensive analyses substantiate the BacGuard dual efficacy in promoting colonic tissue repair and maintaining gut microbiota homeostasis, mechanistically linked through its orthogonal metabolic regulation (Fig. 8a). Fecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiling revealed significant enrichment of acetate, propionate, and butyrate in the BacGuard-treated cohorts, indicating the enhanced probiotic metabolic activity that corroborates longitudinal with the chemiluminescent imaging tendency (Fig. 8b). Coculture assays in vitro further validated the BacGuard's capacity to selectively stimulate probiotic proliferation (Fig. 8c and d). As pivotal innate lymphoid cells, ILC3s orchestrate mucosal repair in DSS-induced ulcerative colitis [58,59]. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated minimal RORγt+ (green)/CD3− (pink) ILC3 populations in colonic tissues of Control group (Fig. 8e). Strikingly, BacGuard treatment drove an ILC3 reconstitution pattern that closely mirrored that of healthy controls, whereas DSS and XOS groups exhibited pathological deviations, a phenotype further validated by flow cytometric quantification of lamina propria ILC3s (RORγt+) at the resolution phase on day 8 (Fig. 8f). Notably, DSS-challenged mice displayed pronounced ILC3 infiltration on day 8, reflecting sustained immune activation. In contrast, the BacGuard induced only transient ILC3 expansion during active repair on day 5 (Fig. S28), with populations returning to homeostatic baselines upon epithelial restitution on day 8 (Fig. 8f).

SCFA-induced IL-22 production is known to ameliorate UC and positively regulate the immune microenvironment [60,61]. Accordingly, SCFA-driven activation of MNK-3 cells (a validated ILC3 line) at the host-microbiota interface was associated with enhanced IL-22 secretion (Fig. 8g), which correlates with improved gut barrier integrity. Histochemical analysis with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed abundant mucin secretion throughout the colonic epithelium, and subsequent immunohistochemical (IHC) examination indicated significant upregulation of MUC2 (the predominant gel-forming mucin in intestinal secretions), suggesting increased goblet cell activity (Fig. 8h). Concurrent upregulation of mucin expression was also observed, consistent with functional restoration and flourishing of goblet cells (Fig. S29). These changes may help establish a mucosal niche conducive to probiotic colonization and prevention of bacterial translocation.

In summary, the BacGuard system innovatively integrates a β-xylosidase-activated chemiluminescent probe (XOS-CL) to enable spatiotemporal imaging of probiotic metabolic activity in the gut, overcoming the limitations of conventional abundance-based detection. By combining XOS-CL with the metabolically tunable polymer HBXOK, our BacGuard establishes a positive feedback loop that links enzyme activity sensing, substrate release, and metabolic enhancement. This dual-function system was associated with increased SCFA production and enhanced anti-inflammatory effects, correlating with restoration of mucosal niche occupancy, rebalancing of immune–microbiota interactions, and improved colonic epithelial barrier integrity.

Over the past decade, the majority of gut-directed microgels have been engineered either to deliver small-molecule or biologic cargos that scavenge ROS and dampen inflammatory signaling, or to protect and deliver live probiotics so that they survive gastric transit and engraft within the colon. While both modalities achieve symptomatic relief, neither offers real-time feedback on therapeutic efficacy. The BacGuard departs from these paradigms by exploiting β-xylosidase, a metabolic pathway exclusive to probiotics and orthogonal to host physiology. This enzymatic activity is absent in mammalian cells but abundant in beneficial commensals, ensuring that the chemiluminescent signal is generated solely by the intended microbial population. Consequently, unlike existing approaches that focus on host-centric signaling or symptom relief, the BacGuard dynamically monitors and modulates microbiota function in real time, offering a precise tool for guiding the dynamic treatment adjustment.

Furthermore, the reliance of BacGuard on a single metabolic product restricts its universality. Future iterations could incorporate multiplexed optical reporters for tryptophan catabolites or bile-acid profiles to capture a more comprehensive metabolic network [[62], [63], [64]]. These optimizations could enhance its clinical potential by improving tissue penetration and expanding the range of metabolic outputs. Nonetheless, our BacGuard introduces a novel paradigm for gut disease management by emphasizing the microbiota as a critical therapeutic target.

Conclusion

The development of BacGuard represents a transformative advance in microbiome engineering, establishing an image-guided dynamic dose regulation framework that seamlessly integrates real-time probiotic monitoring with reinforced metabolic modulation. The β-xylosidase-responsive chemiluminescent probe (XOS-CL) achieved sensitive detection of probiotic activity in vivo. In the case of probiotic abundance, a secondary effect of the overall system occurred, resulting from the XOS fermentation and the antioxidant environment related to HBXOK. This promoted SCFA production, forming a looping on microbial metabolism to immune regulation and barrier restoration. By capitalizing on the enzymatic specificity of XOS-CL, this system achieved spatiotemporal tracking of microbial activity while orthogonally enhanced beneficial metabolic outputs that bridge the critical gap between microbial detection and intervention. The self-reinforcing design ensured that the therapeutic efficacy was dynamically optimized through continuous optical feedback, surpassing the limitations of conventional monofunctional strategies. Nonetheless, mechanistic studies of the SCFA-ILC3-IL-22-MUC2 axis will require approaches such as IL-22 neutralization and FFAR2/3 gene knockout to establish more rigorous causal evidence.

Beyond its immediate application in gut microbiome regulation, the metabolic orthogonality of BacGuard offers a scalable blueprint for engineering host-microbe interfaces in diverse physiological contexts. Its dual sentinel-guardian functionality not only addresses current challenges in microbiome-based therapies but also opens new avenues for developing adaptive and evidence-driven interventions.

For clinical translation, the BacGuard faces several practical challenges. Scaling up production will require coordinated optimization at both the synthetic and processing levels. Microgel manufacture could be advanced by establishing a microfluidic platform compatible with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards and a rigorous quality control framework, which would help ensure batch-to-batch consistency and scalability. Meanwhile, given that the current biosafety data are preliminary, comprehensive and systematic validation of the integrated system remains essential before clinical deployment. Nonetheless, this work highlights the potential of enzyme-activated materials for microbiome management and symbiotic therapeutics, suggesting a promising direction for future research.
