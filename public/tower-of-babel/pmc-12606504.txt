Protective and risk factors for eating disorders: protocol for a prospective cohort study of children at familial high risk (BLOOM)
Authors: Mie Sedoc Jørgensen, Amalie Schousboe, Edoardo Pappaianni, Lea Steinstad, Charlotte Eisemann, Mathilde Klitgård, Nadia Micali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aetiology of eating disorders (EDs) is complex, involving an interplay of genetic and environmental factors. Children at familial high risk (FHR) for EDs demonstrate increased risk of developing EDs themselves compared with children born to parents without EDs, though the majority remain unaffected. The BLOOM study is an FHR study that aims to identify biological, neurocognitive and psychosocial risk and protective pathways to EDs. Results of this study will help identify trait markers of EDs, inform targeted preventive intervention strategies and clarify how to mitigate genetic risk of EDs.Methods and analysisThis prospective cohort study will enrol 510 children aged 8–10 years, including 360 at FHR for developing EDs (comprising 120 children each from mothers with anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge ED) and 150 children not at FHR. Participants will be recruited over 2 years and followed up every 2 years. Data collection will include dual X-ray absorptiometry scans to evaluate body composition, saliva samples for genotyping, neurocognitive testing, self and parent-reported questionnaires and semistructured diagnostic interviews of psychopathology and well-being. Optional data collection will include fasting blood samples, stool samples and brain MRI scans. Mothers and secondary caregivers will complete questionnaires regarding family functioning and their mental health.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (p-2023-14826) and the Regional Ethics Committee of Health Research of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-23043100). Written consent will be obtained from all participants. Results will be disseminated at scientific conferences, in peer-reviewed publications, PhD dissertations and to relevant stakeholders.

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs), including anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN) and binge ED (BED), are severe mental disorders that can lead to persistent and pervasive consequences on both physical and mental well-being.1 2 EDs have one of the highest mortality rates among mental disorders, second only to opioid abuse.35 Approximately 1 in 10 individuals will experience an ED over the course of their lifetime,6 although this estimate is likely to be an underrepresentation of the true prevalence.7

EDs typically onset during adolescence and early adulthood,6 8 9 and the prevalence in young people has notably increased within recent years, particularly in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic.10 11 The onset of EDs is inextricably linked to significant and complex biopsychosocial milestones that characterise a crucial developmental period, that is, adolescence, including onset of puberty, brain structural and functional changes, individuation from family, pursuit of independence and the development of romantic relationships, sexuality and personality.12 13 Accordingly, researchers have pointed to this developmental period as a ‘sensitive period’ for the emergence of mental health symptoms, as well as a ‘window of opportunity’ for early detection and intervention.13 Children and adolescents often present with prodromal symptoms, microphenotypes or subsyndromal symptoms that can differ from those seen in adults, necessitating tailored approaches for detection and intervention.12 13 For example, Stice et al14 found that compensatory weight control behaviours often emerge before AN and BN onset, while binge eating emerges prior to BED. Cognitive symptoms, such as weight and shape overvaluation, fear of weight gain and feelings of fatness, were also identified as significant predictors for specific ED onset.14 Delaying detection and intervention until full-syndrome ED risk prolongs and exacerbates the disorder and increases the likelihood of chronicity. Currently, the duration of untreated EDs remains unacceptably high, with estimates based on a systematic review of available studies suggesting an average of 30 months for AN, 53 months for BN and 67 months for BED, despite retrospective studies suggesting that a shorter duration of untreated ED is associated with a greater likelihood of remission.15

Aetiology of EDs

EDs arise from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors. Recent large-scale population-based studies using nationwide healthcare registers and multigenerational genealogical data have provided refined heritability estimates, indicating moderate genetic contributions to EDs, with heritability estimates of 36% for AN, 39% for BN and 30% for other EDs.16 Familial aggregation analyses further demonstrate that individuals with a first-degree relative diagnosed with an ED have, on average, a threefold increased risk of receiving an ED diagnosis themselves, emphasising the substantial role of genetic factors in familial transmission.16 Additionally, genetic correlations suggest notable overlap between EDs and certain psychiatric disorders, such as obsessive–compulsive disorder and autism.16

In addition to genetics, a range of other factors have been found to contribute to ED development, including behavioural mechanisms and neurocognitive processing, early life experiences, personality traits, comorbid mental health disorders, sociocultural influences and other biological factors.17 18 Consequently, EDs are thought to arise from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental risk factors, though the exact mechanisms and associated trait markers are still not well-known.

Conversely, certain resilience factors may help protect against the development of EDs. Clinical research has highlighted self-compassion, self-esteem and body appreciation as potential protective factors.1921 However, how these factors exert a protective role and whether additional resilience factors exist remains unclear.

To guide clinical and public health decisions on the early detection and prevention of EDs, prognostic models incorporating risk and resilience factors, along with neurocognitive and genetic factors, and biomarkers are needed. However, studying risk and protective factors in individuals with full-syndrome EDs presents challenges: observed differences from healthy comparisons may reflect either underlying trait markers or the long-term effects of EDs, making it difficult to distinguish true precursors from disorder-related ‘scars’. Consequently, examining risk and protective factors is best conducted in pre-onset samples, such as individuals at familial high risk (FHR) for EDs.

FHR studies

Studying individuals at FHR of EDs, such as those with a family history of EDs, offers valuable insights into the development of these disorders. FHR studies focus on a biological relative, often offspring, of individuals with a disorder. FHR studies aim to identify biological and psychological markers that clarify risk and protective pathways crucial for early detection and prevention. The FHR design has been effectively used across several psychiatric disorders, including depression, bipolar disorder and psychosis.2224 A unique strength of FHR studies is the ability to track children from a young age, before symptoms emerge, thus providing a more representative picture of the general population compared with other high-risk groups that generally include older children with elevated symptoms (eg, those at clinical or ultra-high risk). Additionally, studying children within the same age range allows for more precise conclusions about age-specific markers, in contrast to studies with broader age spans, which may yield findings that do not apply consistently across all ages.

FHR studies of EDs

Children at FHR of EDs—those born to mothers with an ED—have an elevated risk of developing an ED themselves, though estimates vary across studies depending on methodology and sample characteristics. Earlier research suggested up to a 10-fold increased risk,25 whereas more recent large-scale register-based studies, which focus on clinically diagnosed parents and children, indicate a more moderate threefold increased risk among individuals with a first-degree relative diagnosed with an ED.16 This variation highlights the importance of study design and diagnostic criteria in determining familial risk estimates. Despite the elevated risk, most of the children do not develop an ED, making them an ideal population for longitudinal studies on risk and protective factors,2527 as well as trait markers associated with specific EDs. Yet, FHR studies in the ED field remain very limited.

Smaller studies28 29 suggest that children of mothers with AN may exhibit poorer visuomotor skills, planning and abstract reasoning. To date, our team has conducted the only two available FHR studies for EDs, aside from a study by Dahlin et al,30 which focused exclusively on AN. Our recent preliminary findings31 32 indicate that children at FHR for EDs demonstrate poorer cognitive flexibility, greater cognitive rigidity on tasks and distinct functional connectivity patterns in resting-state neural networks. Female offspring of mothers with EDs also displayed differences in white matter integrity (ie, structural connectivity) and grey matter volume compared with controls, along with increased psychopathological symptoms.31 While these factors may represent potential ED markers, the small sample size warrants caution and replication in larger samples. Despite these limitations, our preliminary findings highlight that FHR studies in EDs are both feasible and likely to provide important insights.

Leveraging the strengths of the FHR design, and expanding on our previous studies, the BLOOM study (Beskyttelses- og risikofaktorer for spiseforstyrrelser: et familiestudie) aims to identify behavioural, cognitive, biological and neural markers in a large cohort of offspring of mothers with EDs, compared with children of parents with no psychiatric disorders. These findings will provide a basis for clarifying a range of potential biological and psychological markers of FHR for ED status, allowing investigation of pathophysiological pathways. Importantly, we will be able to study resilience in individuals with high genetic risk and track this over time, providing a unique perspective on biopsychosocial resilience pathways for EDs. Insights from BLOOM may drive the development of targeted early interventions and prevention strategies, ultimately mitigating the healthcare and societal costs associated with EDs.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The BLOOM study is a prospective longitudinal cohort study examining children at FHR for EDs. We plan to recruit 510 children aged 8–10 years, along with their mothers and, if possible, secondary caregivers. Our target is 360 children at FHR for developing EDs, divided into three groups of 120 children each, based on maternal diagnoses of AN, BN and BED. Additionally, 150 children of parents without any current or past psychiatric disorder will serve as a control group. Enrolment will occur over a 2-year period. This protocol describes the baseline wave of the BLOOM study. Follow-up waves are likely to be planned every 2 years. Further protocols will describe subsequent follow-up waves in detail as they are implemented.

The decision to focus on ages 8–10 is based on several factors. At this age, most children have begun school, marking a significant developmental milestone. This transition introduces heightened cognitive, academic, social and practical demands on the child. From a neurocognitive perspective, this period is characterised by rapid cognitive development, particularly in the frontal lobes.33 At the same time, the early development of the child is not too distant to be recalled by the parent being interviewed. Importantly, examining risk factors and markers for EDs necessitates studying healthy offspring at FHR of ED before onset of the disorder.

Recruitment and procedures

Eligible participants will be identified through several sources. First, a list of potential participants residing in Region Zealand or the Capital Region of Denmark will be drawn from the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register, the Central Person Register, the National Patient Register (LPR), the Research-Oriented National Patient Register (LPR3_F), the Psychiatric National Patient Register and the Medical Birth Register (MFR). Cases will include children aged 7–9 years at the time of extraction whose mothers have a registered ED diagnosis (ICD-10: F50.0, F50.1, F50.2, F50.3, F50.8) but who themselves have no ED diagnosis. Controls will be matched on age and selected based on the absence of any psychiatric diagnosis in both parents (ICD-10: F01–F99; ICD-8: 290–315) and no ED diagnosis in the child (ICD-10: F50.0, F50.1, F50.2, F50.3, F50.8). All children must be alive and residing in either the Capital Region of Denmark or Region Zealand at the time of extraction. Eligible mothers will receive an email in their personal inbox (E-boks), which is used for public communication in Denmark. They will receive a folder that briefly describes the project and an invitation to participate. If the participants do not reply within a few weeks, we will contact them by phone, if possible. Second, participants may be referred directly from the inpatient or outpatient clinics at the Department of EDs at the Mental Health Centre Ballerup or the Centre for EDs Clinic in Region Zealand. Further recruitment will be conducted through public advertisement. This is necessary because certain EDs, such as BED and Avoidant and Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID), are not identifiable in Danish health registers due to the continued use of ICD-10. Public advertisement also allows us to reach individuals who may not have received a formal diagnosis but are still eligible and interested in participating.

After initial contact, the research team will invite all families to an in-person information meeting about the study (day 0). Parents who agree to their child’s participation will sign informed consent. In instances of divorced parents who both retain custody, consent must be obtained from both parents. Parents will be informed that participation is voluntary and that they can withdraw their consent at any time. Children will be informed about the study in an age-appropriate manner, without any reference to EDs. An information leaflet tailored to the understanding of the age of the children has been developed to complement the leaflet provided to parents. Additionally, children will have the opportunity to ask questions directly to the research staff during experimental day 1. If a child expresses any reservations about participating, they will not be included in the study.

After obtaining informed written consent, families will be invited to participate in experimental day 1. If they express interest in the optional MRI scan and are deemed eligible, they will be scheduled for an MRI scan.

Recruitment for the baseline wave started in June 2024 and will conclude on 31 December 2026. Participants will be followed longitudinally, with assessments planned every 2 years. At present, funding is secured for follow-up assessment after 2 years (2026–2028).

Maternal psychopathology

To determine ED diagnoses, treatment history and presence of other mental disorders, mothers will be interviewed by trained research staff with the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders–Research Version (SCID-5-RV).35

Sociodemographic information

Mothers and secondary caregivers will provide the following sociodemographic and health-related information: native language, level of education, ethnicity, marital status, whether the child lives with both parents, employment status and type, smoking and substance use status and history.

Experimental day 1

Participants (child with at least one parent) will arrive at the Mental Health Centre Ballerup in the morning. The child will be examined with validated and age-appropriate instruments and questionnaires. All assessment instruments are sensitive to small changes and, therefore, suitable for follow-up. The assessments are described below.

Neurocognitive tests

Cognitive function

Five tasks from the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery36 will be employed; the Motor Screening Test, which assesses a general assessment of sensorimotor deficits; the Multitasking Test which evaluates executive functions, specifically cognitive flexibility and cognitive control; a Rapid Visual Information Processing task, which provides a measure of sustained attention; a Spatial Working Memory Task, which requires retention and manipulation of visual spatial information; and a Stop Signal Task, which measures impulse control and inhibitory response.

Intellectual functioning

Reynolds Intellectual Assessment Scale (RIAS37) will be used to measure intellectual functioning through measures of Verbal Intelligence, Nonverbal Intelligence, Verbal Memory and Nonverbal Memory. General Intelligence and Memory Indexes will be calculated.

Biological assessments

Biological measures and physical health

A clinical evaluation of anthropometry (height and weight) and pubertal status will be performed by a trained staff nurse. Pubertal status will be assessed using Tanner stages of sexual maturation.38 39

Saliva samples

Saliva samples from the child will be obtained to extract DNA for genotyping. The children will be asked to fast for 30 min before providing the sample.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scan

The child will be scanned with a dual-energy X-ray (DXA) Hologic Horizon scanner40 to obtain data on body composition, for example, body fat, fat-free mass and muscle mass. DXA scanning will be performed by trained staff.

Structured lunch

The children will be offered a standardised meal, representative of a typical Danish school lunch, consisting of predefined meal components: two pieces of ryebread, two slices of chicken breast, a slice of cheese, butter and a glass of milk. For those with food allergies or dietary restrictions (eg, vegetarian, vegan, halal, kosher), suitable alternatives will be offered. Before and after lunch, we will assess hunger using The Hunger-Satiety Scale41 and anxiety levels using a Visual Analogue Scale ranging from 0 (no anxiety) to 10 (extreme anxiety). Following the meal, we will calculate the total intake of calories and macronutrients.

Self-report questionnaires

Children, mothers and secondary caregivers will additionally complete a series of questionnaires. For an overview, see table 2.

Optional assessments

Families can consent to optional assessments outlined below.

Blood sample

A blood sample will be collected fasting at the start of the day. Given the age of the participants, it is optional for the child to provide a blood sample. If the child wants to participate, a blood sample of 22 mL will be collected, and the child will be provided breakfast afterwards. The sample will be stored in a biobank and used for the analysis of various blood markers, including hormones (eg, oestradiol, testosterone, T4, T3), metabolic (HbA1c, triglycerides, cholesterol, HDL, LDL and glucose) and inflammatory (C reactive protein, cytokines) markers and appetite hormones (eg, ghrelin, AgRP).

Stool sample

Stool samples from the child are collected to characterise the composition of their gut microbiome. Standard instructions and questions are provided to families prior to collection. Stool samples can be collected at home or at our lab. A minimum of 1 sample of about 5 g is collected (up to three tubes) with the help of a collection tray and/or 1–3 specialised collection tubes with an in-tube spoon attached.

MRI scan

A subset of ~100 participants (including both FHR and HC), who volunteer for the MRI session, will undergo a brain MRI scan on a Siemens Prisma 3 Tesla MRI scanner at the Danish Research Centre for Magnetic Resonance (DRCMR). Before the experimental session begins, a short test run with a mock scanner will be suggested, which is intended to familiarise the child with the experimental procedure. The MRI examination protocol will include acquisition of: (1) an anatomical MP2RAGE structural scan; (2) an anatomical T2 scan; (3) a diffusion-weighted image scan with a multiband sequence and (4) functional volumes during 8 min of resting-state (resting-state fMRI). Physiological recordings (respiration and heart rate) that will be used during data analysis for signal cleaning will be made with ad hoc physiological recording device and adapted sensors.

Register-based data

We will collect data from the following registers: the Danish Civil Registration System, the Danish Psychiatric Central Register, the Danish MFR, the IDA Database and the Children Register. The register-based data will be used to explore clinical and sociodemographic factors, including age, sex, marital status, ethnicity, children, education, employment status and income. Additionally, we will obtain information on any mental health diagnosis of the mother and biological father. Furthermore, we will seek consent from mothers and fathers to access their and their child’s blood spots (if the family does not consent to the child providing a saliva sample) from the Danish National Biobank to genotype trios.

Add-on studies and substudies

Throughout the study period, add-on or substudies may be conducted.

Honoraria and economic compensation

Children will be awarded a gift card of DKK700–DKK1000 (≈£85–£120) and parents DKK400–DKK600 (≈£50–£70) for time and costs incurred in participating in the study. Reasonable transportation costs will likewise be covered.

Data analysis

Data analysis plan

The children will be categorised into two primary diagnostic groups: those at FHR for ED (N=360), and those at no FHR (N=150). To examine potential differences based on maternal ED diagnosis, children will also be grouped by maternal diagnosis: AN (N=120), BN (N=120) and BED (N=120), and compared with a control group of children (N=150). We will conduct secondary post hoc analyses investigating the effect of maternal symptoms (eg, restriction, binge eating) and duration of illness on child outcomes.

Descriptive analyses will be performed using χ² tests, t-tests and Mann-Whitney U-tests, as appropriate, to examine differences in demographic characteristics. For other outcomes, analyses will employ ANOVA/ANCOVAs with diagnostic group as the predictor. All analyses will be stratified by sex.

For each neurocognitive task, participants who show lack of engagement—defined as being visibly distracted during the task, taking significantly longer than expected to complete it or failing to complete it—will be excluded from the analysis. Additionally, participants who exhibit extreme performance deviations (beyond ±3 SD from the mean) will be excluded from the analysis. Subsequently, between-group comparisons will be conducted to assess differences in performance.

Body composition data will be analysed using ANCOVA to assess mean differences between the two groups, adjusting for age and z-BMI. Comparisons will be made based on these parameters. Participants exhibiting excessive movement during DXA scans will be excluded from the analyses.

Genotyping will be used exclusively to compute ED and related polygenic risk scores available at the time of data analyses for the child, mother and secondary caregiver. Polygenic risk scores will be used as a regressor (continuous variable) to explore associations with psychological/neuropsychological/biological data within groups.

Questionnaire data will be analysed using a range of statistical methods: mixed models, logistic regressions, t-tests and ANOVA for normally distributed data. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests such as Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis and Friedman tests will be employed.

MRI images will be preprocessed using gold-standard software and analysed with appropriate statistical methods, either parametric or non-parametric tests. We will extract and compare between groups structural volumes (grey, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid volumes), cortical thickness, gyrification, indices of white matter integrity, expressed by fractional anisotropy, radial, axial and mean diffusivity indices. From a functional point of view, resting-state networks and seed-based functional connectivity will be calculated and compared between children at FHR of EDs and controls.

All analyses will be corrected for multiple comparisons using a statistical threshold of 0.05 where applicable.

Sample size calculation

We will conduct analyses stratified by sex and maternal ED diagnosis; hence our sample size needs to be adequately powered to achieve this aim. Given our preliminary findings31 of a mean difference of 38 (SD=90) between high-risk ED offspring and controls on the Attention Switching Task (now renamed the Multitasking Test) score, a sample size of at least N=50 in each group will allow us to achieve >80% power with an alpha=0.05 to detect a difference between girls at FHR for AN high-risk and controls. For continuous outcomes, an ANOVA with 4 groups and a total sample size of 250 (female children only) allows detection of a small effect size (0.3) with 87% power, with an alpha=0.05. Our sample size is in line with that of other previously conducted studies that had the same FHR design.42

Longitudinal studies require accounting for participant attrition. Our calculation includes an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 10% over time. This rate is consistent with similar studies,43 which experienced a 10% dropout rate 4 years after initial evaluation.

Stool sample analysis

The stool specimens are processed and analysed as follows: bacterial and viral DNA extraction, MinION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) and taxonomy analyses. The DNA will be isolated with the Bead-Beat Micro AX Gravity kit (A&A Biotechnology, Gdynia, Poland, Cat. # 106-100 M1), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Near full-length 16S rRNA gene amplicon is determined using MinION, a nanopore-based sequencing technique. The Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) based sequencing sequences of the near full-length 16S rRNA gene amplicons by targeting the V1–V8 hypervariable region. It will also be possible to bioinformatically access the prokaryotic virus species (bacterial phages). In selected cases, we will isolate specific members of the microbiome using standard microbiology methods.

Genotyping

DNA will be extracted from saliva samples and dried blood spot samples, which were collected during routine neonatal screening for phenylketonuria44 and stored on filter paper. A single 3 mm punch will be taken from each filter paper sample for laboratory work. The genotyping of the samples will be done with a modified version of the Illumina Global Screening Array, which involves the removal of rare variants and clinically relevant mutations listed by the American College of Medical Genetics. Therefore, the protocol will not involve comprehensive mapping of the genome.

Retention of study participants

A participant who drops out after providing informed consent but does not participate in experimental days 1 or 2 will be replaced. Once included, all participants will be retained in the study, and follow-up data will be collected unless consent for participation is explicitly withdrawn.

Data handling and storage

All participant data will be registered and kept in an electronic case report form (eCRF) within a secure data management platform designed for non-commercial clinical research (REDCap). Each participant will have a dedicated eCRF for data collection. All questionnaires will be entered into REDCap. Data from SCID and RIAS will be manually entered into the eCRFs by research staff. The DAWBA will be stored by Zitelab, a company specialising in digital mental health assessments.1 45 Only designated research personnel will have encoded access to the eCRFs. The principal investigator and research staff are obliged to handle all participant data with confidentiality in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation and Data Protection Act. The primary investigator is responsible for all completed eCRFs.

Anatomical MRI images will be stored in the Capital Region’s Radiology System (PACS). MRI data will be securely stored in the IT system at the DRCMR (Hvidovre Hospital), which is approved for handling personal data. DRCMR will only provide access to MRI data to staff involved in the project.

Biological samples, including saliva, serum and stool samples, will be stored in a research biobank. At the study’s conclusion, biological material will be transferred to the Danish National Biobank. All biological samples will be registered in a pseudo-anonymised database and stored under optimal storage conditions.

At the study’s conclusion, investigators will extract data for analyses on primary and secondary outcomes. Study data will be published in pseudonymous form to ensure participant privacy.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (p-2023-14826) and the Regional Ethics Committee of Health Research of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-23043100). Results, regardless of outcome, will be published through PhD dissertations, peer-reviewed scientific journals, newspapers and presented at scientific conferences. The study will adhere to the Vancouver Declaration and good publication practice guidelines.

Data statement section

If publishing in peer-reviewed international journals is not achieved, the research report will be deposited in a public online repository, such as osf.io. Furthermore, a lay summary of the findings will be written and distributed to interested study participants.

Expected risks

Overall, this study is associated with low risk of side effects and discomfort. The research staff is trained to manage participant discomfort, such as anxiety. If any results indicate serious health concerns or unmet treatment needs, the family will be guided on how to access appropriate care. In urgent cases, with parental consent, researchers may contact appropriate authorities to ensure immediate support for the child or family.

Blood tests are generally low risk and not associated with significant discomfort, though there may be slight bruising or, rarely, infection around the sampling site. To minimise discomfort, blood samples will be collected in a single session, and an EMLA patch can be applied. If redness, swelling or signs of infection occur at the blood collection site, participants will be advised to contact the study staff for treatment. The study will not analyse rare or clinically significant genetic variants. Therefore, results from saliva and dried blood spots analyses will not impact participants. Additionally, as samples will be anonymised and not linked to the individual participants, no incidental findings will be reported. Faecal collection may cause mild discomfort, but adherence to standard hygienic principles will ensure that it poses no significant risk. DXA scans generally pose minimal risk, with radiation exposure equivalent to less than 4 days of background radiation. MRI is likewise considered non-invasive and safe, and the researchers will ensure that participants have no metal objects or implants, such as pacemakers or insulin pumps, beforehand. Possible side effects of MRI include dizziness, nausea, vertigo and electro-gustatory effects. In the case of incidental findings on an MRI scan, that is, structural/tissue abnormalities, a board-certified radiologist will assess the findings, and the parent or primary care physician will be informed if further investigation is needed.

Patient and public involvement

Focus group interviews were conducted with mothers with lifetime EDs in the design phase of the study. The mothers provided critical feedback on the aims and design of the study.46 Their insights and suggestions were incorporated into the design of the current study. This process ensured that the study framework was informed by the perspectives of those with direct lived experience of EDs. All participants in the current study will receive an evaluation form on completion (available on request), and any relevant feedback will be used to make necessary adjustments to the study.
