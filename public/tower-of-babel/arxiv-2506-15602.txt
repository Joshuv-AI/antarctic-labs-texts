Estimate Hitting Time by Hitting Probability for Elitist Evolutionary Algorithms
Jun He, Siang Yew Chong, Xin Yao

Abstract
Drift analysis is a powerful tool for analyzing the time complexity of evolutionary algorithms. However, it requires manual construction of drift functions to bound hitting time for each specific algorithm and problem. To address this limitation, general linear drift functions were introduced for elitist evolutionary algorithms. But calculating linear bound coefficients effectively remains a problem. This paper proposes a new method called drift analysis of hitting probability to compute these coefficients. Each coefficient is interpreted as a bound on the hitting probability of a fitness level, transforming the task of estimating hitting time into estimating hitting probability. A novel drift analysis method is then developed to estimate hitting probability, where paths are introduced to handle multimodal fitness landscapes. Explicit expressions are constructed to compute hitting probability, significantly simplifying the estimation process. One advantage of the proposed method is its ability to estimate both the lower and upper bounds of hitting time and to compare the performance of two algorithms in terms of hitting time. To demonstrate this application, two algorithms for the knapsack problem, each incorporating feasibility rules and greedy repair respectively, are compared. The analysis indicates that neither constraint handling technique consistently outperforms the other.

Introduction

Hitting time is an important metric to evaluate the performance of evolutionary algorithms (EAs), referring to the minimum number of generations required for an EA to find the optimal solution. Drift analysis is one of the strongest tools used to analyze the hitting time of EAs
[
1
,
2
]
and different drift analysis methods have been developed over the past two decades
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. In drift analysis, a drift function is constructed to bound the hitting time, but is manually tailored for each specific problem
[
5
]
.

To overcome this limitation, the linear drift function for elitist EAs was proposed
[
10
]
, which combines the strength of drift analysis with the convenience of fitness level partitioning. Given fitness levels
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
from high to low, a lower bound on the hitting time from
S_{k}
to
S_{0}
(where
1\leq k\leq K
) is expressed as the following linear function.

\frac{1}{\displaystyle\max_{X\in S_{k}}p(X,\cup^{k-1}_{j=0}S_{j})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{\displaystyle\max_{X\in S_{\ell}}p(X,\cup^{\ell-1}_{j=0}S_{j})},

(1)

where the term
p(X,\cup^{\ell-1}_{j=0}S_{j})
represents the transition probability from
X\in S_{\ell}
to higher levels
S_{0}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell-1}
and
c_{k,\ell}\in[0,1]
is a linear coefficient. Similarly, an upper bound on the hitting time from
S_{k}
to
S_{0}
is expressed as the following linear function.

\frac{1}{\displaystyle\min_{X\in S_{k}}p(X,\cup^{k-1}_{j=0}S_{j})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{\displaystyle\min_{X\in S_{\ell}}p(X,\cup^{\ell-1}_{j=0}S_{j})}.

(2)

The above drift functions are a family of linear functions used to bound the hitting time for elitist EAs. While the calculation of transition probabilities is straightforward, determining the coefficients is more complex.
The primary research question is how to effectively calculate the coefficients for tight linear bounds. Several methods for computing these coefficients have been proposed
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
10
]
. However, more efficient techniques are needed to determine coefficients for tight lower bounds, particularly on multimodal fitness landscapes with shortcuts
[
10
]
. While the linear bound coefficient has been interpreted in terms of visit probability
[
13
]
, a general method for computing this visit probability is still lacking.

This paper aims to develop an efficient method for computing the coefficients in linear bounds (
1
) and (
2
). The method makes two significant contributions to the formal development and application of drift analysis. First, it reinterprets a coefficient as a bound on the hitting probability, which is the probability of reaching a fitness level for the first time. Consequently, the task of estimating hitting time is transformed into estimating hitting probability.

Secondly, drift analysis of hitting probability is introduced to estimate the hitting probability or linear bound coefficients. Although the method is termed “drift analysis”, it focuses on calculating hitting probabilities but not on hitting times. Hence, it is entirely different from drift analysis of hitting time
[
5
]
. The method provides a new way to compute linear bound coefficients and introduces new explicit expressions for these coefficients. This greatly simplifies drift analysis because it allows direct estimation of hitting time using explicit formulas.

Comparing the performance of different EAs is crucial for empirical research. Since the proposed method can estimate both lower and upper bounds of hitting time, it provides a useful tool for theoretically comparing the performance of two EAs in terms of hitting time. The application is demonstrated through a case study that compares two EAs for the knapsack problem incorporating feasibility rules
[
14
,
15
]
and solution repair
[
16
,
17
]

Recently, He and Zhou

-paths jump functions, naming the coefficient

and Type-

[

]
.

To address the shortcut issue, He and Zhou
[
10
]
proposed drift analysis with fitness levels and developed the general Type-
c_{k,\ell}
linear bound, though this bound still necessitates recursive computation. Drift analysis with fitness levels has unified existing fitness level methods

,
12
,

The study of hitting probability has received limited attention in the theory of EAs, with only a few studies available. The term hitting probability has been used in various contexts. He and Yao

]
and Chen et al.

).

Preliminaries

III-A

Th Markov Chain for the Elitist EA

Consider an EA designed to maximize a function
f(x)
, where
f(x)
is defined over a finite set. The EA generates a sequence of solutions
(X^{[t]})_{t\geq 0}
, where
X^{[t]}
represents the solution(s) at generation

. We model the sequence by a Markov chain
[
23
,
24
]
. Let
X
denote a state,
S
the state space, and
S_{\mathrm{opt}}
the set of optimal solutions. Throughout this paper, we assume that the Markov chain
(X^{[t]})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies three key properties.

1.

Convergent
: Starting from any
X\in S
, the chain can reach the optimal set

with probability
1
.

2.

Homogeneous
: The transition probability
p(X,Y)
from
X
to
Y
is independent on
t
.

3.

Elitist
: Fitness values do not decrease: for any
t\geq 0
,
f(X^{[t+1]})\geq f(X^{[t]})
, where
f(X)
is the fitness of
X
.

,

For any pair of

Given the Markov chain

to

(where

p(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\mbox{if }k<\ell,\\
\in[0,1]&\mbox{{if }}k\geq\ell.\end{array}\right.

(3)

Let
[i,j]
denote the index set
\{i,i+1,\ldots,j-1,j\}

The transition probability from

In the fitness level method

The minimum and maximum values of the transition probability

\displaystyle r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\min_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}r(X_{k},S_{\ell}),

\displaystyle r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\max_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}r(X_{k},S_{\ell}).

Similar notation applies to the minimum and maximum values of the transition probability

III-D

Hitting Time and Hitting Probability

Given the Markov chain
(X^{[t]})_{t\geq 0}
and a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\cdots,S_{K})
, we introduce the concepts of hitting time and hitting probability of reaching
S_{\ell}
(where
0\leq\ell\leq K
) based on the textbook
[
28
]
.

Definition 1

Assume that the Markov chain
(X^{[t]})_{t\geq 0}
starts from
X_{k}\in S_{k}
, the first
hitting time
from
X_{k}
to

\tau(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\inf\{t\geq 0,X^{[t]}\in S_{\ell}\}.

The

h(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\Pr(\tau(X_{k},S_{\ell})<\infty).

The

h(X_{k},Y_{\ell})=\Pr(X^{[\tau(X_{k},S_{\ell})]}=Y_{\ell},\tau(X,S_{\ell})<+\infty).

The following proposition adapts

for the hitting probability

to

is composed of two parts: (i) the probability of transitioning from

to an intermediate state

to

Proposition 1

For any
0\leq\ell\leq k\leq K
,

satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h(X_{k},S_{k})=1.\\
h(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\sum^{k}_{i=\ell}\sum_{Y_{i}\in S_{i}}p(X_{k},Y_{i})h(Y_{i},S_{\ell}).\end{array}\right.

The following proposition modifies
[
28
, Theorem 1.3.5]
for the mean hitting time

. This mean time from
X_{k}
to leaving
S_{k}
is composed of two parts: (i) the probability of transitioning from

to any intermediate state

Proposition 2

For any

,

satisfies

m(X_{k},\overline{S_{k}})=1+\sum_{Y_{k}\in S_{k}}p(X_{k},Y_{k})m(Y_{k},\overline{S_{k}}).

III-E

Lower and Upper Bounds on Hitting Time

Assuming that the Markov chain
(X^{[t]})_{t\geq 0}
starts from
X
, the mean hitting time

from
X
to the optimal set

is always abbreviated as

.
If

, then

is called a lower bound of

. Conversely, if

, then

is called an upper bound of

.

Asymptotic notations such as

are used to differentiate between tight and loose bounds, as described in the textbook
[
29
]
. Let

represent the dimension of the search space. The mean hitting time

is a function of

differs from the actual mean hitting time

by only a constant factor, meaning

for an upper bound and

for a lower bound.

Drift Analysis Using Linear Drift Functions

The main results of drift analysis using linear drift functions

Given a fitness level partition

,

d(X_{k})=\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{[0,k-1]})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})},

where coefficients

satisfy

c_{k,\ell}\leq\min_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}\left\{r(X_{k},S_{\ell})+\sum^{k-1}_{j=\ell+1}{r}(X_{k},S_{j})c_{j,\ell}\right\}.

(6)

Then for any
k\geq 1
and

, the drift

\Delta d(X_{k})=\sum^{K}_{i=0}\sum_{Y_{i}\in S_{i}}p(X_{k},Y_{i})[d(X_{k})-d(Y_{i})]\leq 1,

and the mean hitting time

(2) Let a drift function

,

and for any

and any

Then for any

IV
Estimate Hitting time by Hitting Probability

This section explains the linear coefficient of a linear bound as a bound on the hitting probability.

IV-A

Exact Hitting Time

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
, the hitting time can be intuitively derived as follows. Assume that the Markov chain
(X^{[t]})_{t\geq 0}
starts from
X_{k}\in S_{k}
. Intuitively, the hitting time from
X_{k}
to
S_{0}
is equal to the time that the chain stays at all non-optimal levels
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
. The staying time at level
S_{\ell}
is equal to the hitting time from
S_{\ell}
to leaving
S_{\ell}
, multiplied by the hitting probability from
S_{k}
to entering
S_{\ell}
. This observation is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
, the mean hitting time from
X_{k}
to the optimal set
S_{0}
(where
1\leq k\leq K
) is equal to

m(X_{k})=\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\sum_{X_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}h(X_{k},X_{\ell})\,m(X_{\ell},\overline{S}_{\ell}).

Proof:

Let
\tau
be the time that the chain spends at level
S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{k}
and
\tau_{\ell}
be the time period between when the chain entering
S_{\ell}
and leaving
S_{\ell}
. Then

\tau=\tau_{1}+\cdots+\tau_{k},

and

m(X_{k})=\mathrm{E}[\tau_{1}]+\mathrm{E}[\tau_{2}]+\cdots+\mathrm{E}[\tau_{k}].

(9)

Once the chain enters the level
S_{\ell}
, it will not return to a level lower than
S_{\ell}
. The mean hitting time

\begin{split}\mathrm{E}[\tau_{\ell}]=\sum_{X_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}h(X_{k},X_{\ell})m(X_{\ell},\overline{S}_{\ell}),\end{split}

(10)

where
h(X_{k},X_{\ell})
is the hitting probability from
X_{k}
to
X_{\ell}
and
m(X_{\ell},\overline{S}_{\ell})
is the mean hitting time from
X_{\ell}
to leaving
S_{\ell}
.
Inserting (
10
) into (
9
), we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

IV-B

Lower and Upper Bounds on Hitting Time

Theorem
1
gives the exact hitting time. But in most cases, it is impossible to calculate the exact hitting time. Therefore, it is necessary to estimate their lower and upper bounds. The following theorem gives lower and upper linear bounds on hitting time based on the hitting probability.

Theorem 2

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and a fitness level
k:0<k\leq K
,

(1) The mean hitting time from
X_{k}
to the optimal set
S_{0}
(where
1\leq k\leq K
) is lower-bounded by

m(X_{k})\geq\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\frac{h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}.

(11)

(2) The mean hitting time from
X_{k}
to the optimal set
S_{0}
(where
1\leq k\leq K
) is upper-bounded by

m(X_{k})\leq\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\frac{h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}.

(12)

Proof:

1) First, we estimate a lower bound on
m(X_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})
. Let
X^{\sharp}_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}
such that
m(X^{\sharp}_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})=m_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})
. According to Proposition
2
, we have

\displaystyle m_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})=

\displaystyle m(X^{\sharp}_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{Y_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}p(X^{\sharp}_{\ell},Y_{\ell})m(Y_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1+p(X^{\sharp}_{\ell},S_{\ell})m_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}}).

Then we get

\displaystyle m_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})\geq\frac{1}{1-p(X^{\sharp}_{\ell},S_{\ell})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p(X^{\sharp}_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}\geq\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}.

(13)

Second, we estimate a lower bound on the mean hitting time
m(X_{k})
to the optimal set.
According to Theorem
1
,

\displaystyle m(X_{k})=

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\sum_{Y_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}h(X_{k},Y_{\ell})m(Y_{\ell},\overline{S}_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})m_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{\ell},\overline{S_{\ell}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\frac{h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}\quad\mbox{(by \eqref{equ:MeanTimeLowerBound})}.

Then we get Inequality (
11
).

2) The proof is similar to the first part.
∎

The above theorems provide an explanation of the linear bound coefficients. The hitting probability
h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
is a lower bound coefficient, and
h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
is an upper bound coefficient. Any
c_{k,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
is a lower bound coefficient, while any
c_{k,\ell}\geq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
an upper bound coefficient. Since
h(X_{k},S_{k})=1
, the coefficient
c_{k,k}
is always assigned to
1
.

The terms viscosity
[
12
]
, visit probability
[
13
]
, and coefficient
[
10
]
fundamentally study the same subject: the probability of visiting fitness levels. Their distinctions can be characterized as viscosity for Type-
c
time bounds, visit probability for Type-
c_{\ell}
bounds, and coefficient for Type-
c_{k,\ell}
bounds. Hitting probability is adopted in the paper because it is a standard term used in Markov chains
[
28
]
.

V
Drift Analysis of Hitting Probability

This section develops drift analysis of hitting probability to compute the coefficients in a linear drift function.

V-A

Drift Function

Computing exact values of hitting probabilities is challenging. Instead, a new drift analysis method is proposed to estimate their bounds. Given a level
S_{\ell}
(where
1\leq\ell\leq K
), a drift function
c(X,S_{\ell})
is used to approximate the hitting probability
h(X,S_{\ell})
. It is designed specifically for elitist EAs based on fitness level partitioning.

Definition 2

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
, and a level
S_{\ell}
(where
1\leq\ell\leq K
), a
drift function

c(X,S_{\ell})
is a function
S\to[0,1]
such that for any
X_{k}\in S_{k}
,

c(X_{k},S_{\ell}):=c_{k,\ell}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }\ell>k,\\
1&\mbox{if }\ell=k,\\
\in[0,1]&\mbox{if }\ell<k,\end{array}\right.

(14)

where
c_{k,\ell}
is called coefficient in the paper.

The above drift function makes use of two observations that (i) the hitting probability from a lower level to a higher level is equal to
0
, and (ii) the hitting probability to the same level is equal to
1
. Note that there is no need to approximate the probability of hitting the optimal set, because in a converged EA, this probability is equal to
1
. Hence, we assign
c_{k,0}=1
.

Definition 3

Based on the transition probability, the standard
drift
from
X_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
(where
1\leq\ell<k\leq K
) is

\displaystyle{\Delta}c(X_{k},S_{\ell}):=

\displaystyle\sum^{K}_{i=0}\sum_{Y_{i}\in S_{i}}p(X_{k},Y_{i})\left(c_{k,\ell}-c_{i,\ell}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{k,\ell}-\sum^{k}_{i=\ell}p(X_{k},S_{i})\,c_{i,\ell}.

(15)

Based on the conditional transition probability, the
conditional drift
from
X_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
is

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{k},S_{\ell}):=

\displaystyle\sum^{K}_{i=0}\sum_{Y_{i}\in S_{i}}r(X_{k},Y_{i})\left(c_{k,\ell}-c_{i,\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{k,\ell}-\sum^{k-1}_{i=\ell}r(X_{k},S_{i})\,c_{i,\ell},

(16)

The above definitions makes use of the facts:
p(X_{k},S_{i})=0
for
i>k
,
r(X_{k},S_{i})=0
for
i\geq k
, and
c_{i,\ell}=0
for
i<\ell
.

V-B

Drift Conditions

The following theorem provides drift conditions to determine that a drift function is a lower or upper bound on the hitting probability.

Theorem 3

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
, a drift function (
52
) and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
,

(1) If for any
\ell<j\leq k
and any
X_{j}\in S_{j}
, the conditional drift

\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{j},S_{\ell})\leq 0
, equivalently, the coefficient

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}\leq\min_{X_{j}\in S_{j}}\left\{r(X_{j},S_{\ell})+\sum^{j-1}_{i=\ell+1}r(X_{j},S_{i})c_{i,\ell}\right\},

(17)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

(2) If for any
\ell<j\leq k
and any
X_{j}\in S_{j}
, the conditional drift

\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{j},S_{\ell})\geq 0
, equivalently, the coefficient

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}\geq\max_{X_{j}\in S_{j}}\left\{r(X_{j},S_{\ell})+\sum^{j-1}_{i=\ell+1}r(X_{j},S_{i})c_{i,\ell}\right\},

(18)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\geq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

Proof:

1) For
j:\ell<j\leq k
, let
X^{\sharp}_{j}\in S_{j}
such that
h(X^{\sharp}_{j},S_{\ell})=h_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.
By Proposition
1
,

\displaystyle h_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{\ell})=

\displaystyle h(X^{\sharp}_{j},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{j}_{i=\ell}\sum_{Y_{i}\in S_{i}}p(X^{\sharp}_{j},Y_{i})h(Y_{i},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum^{j}_{i=\ell}p(X^{\sharp}_{j},S_{i})h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{i},S_{\ell}).

Moving the term
i=j
from the right-hand sum to the left, we obtain

\displaystyle h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})\geq

\displaystyle\sum^{j-1}_{i=\ell}\frac{p(X^{\sharp}_{j},S_{i})}{1-p(X^{\sharp}_{j},S_{j})}h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{i},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{j-1}_{i=\ell}r(X^{\sharp}_{j},S_{i})h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{i},S_{\ell}).

(19)

We prove that for any
\ell<j\leq k
,
c_{j,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
by induction. For
j=\ell+1
, by (
17
) and then (
19
) (note that
c_{\ell,\ell}=h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{\ell},S_{\ell})=1
), we get

\displaystyle c_{\ell+1,\ell}\leq

\displaystyle r(X^{\sharp}_{\ell+1},S_{\ell})\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{\ell+1},S_{\ell}).

We make an inductive assumption that for
j\geq\ell+1
,

c_{j,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell}).

(20)

By the condition (
17
), the assumption (
20
) and (
19
), we get

\displaystyle c_{j+1,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{j}_{i=\ell}r(X^{\sharp}_{j+1},S_{i})c_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{j}_{i=\ell}r(X^{\sharp}_{j+1},S_{i})h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{i},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq h_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j+1},S_{\ell}).

We prove that
c_{j+1,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j+1},S_{\ell})
for
j+1
. Thus, by induction, we complete the proof.

2) The proof is similar to the first part.
∎

Interestingly, the recursive formulas (
17
) and (
18
) in the above theorem are identical to that in Proposition
3
, despite being derived through entirely different proofs. But Theorem
3
makes a new contribution: drift analysis of hitting probability. A lower bound coefficient is a lower bound on the hitting probability, while an upper bound coefficient is an upper bound on the hitting probability. A drift function is used to bound the hitting probability, and the drift condition determines whether the drift function serves as a lower or upper bound.

Type-
c
and Type-
c_{\ell}
bounds
[
10
]
can be rewritten in terms of necessary and sufficient drift conditions. A detailed analysis is given in the supplementary material. The drift conditions in Theorem
3
are based on pointwise drift. Average drift
[
30
]
can be used to handle random initialization.

V-C

Direct Calculation and Alternative Calculation

The coefficients in Theorem
3
are determined recursively. He and Zhou
[
10
]
gave explicit expressions for calculating the coefficients as follows.

Corollary 1

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
,

(1) Let a drift function (
52
) (denoted by
c^{\min}(X,S_{\ell})
) satisfies that for
X\in S_{j}
,
c^{\min}_{\ell,\ell}=1
and for any
\ell<j\leq k
,

\displaystyle c^{\min}_{j,\ell}=

\displaystyle\ r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell<j_{1}<j}r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{j_{1}})\,r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j_{1}},S_{\ell})+\cdots

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell<\ell+1<\cdots<j-1<j}\,r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{j-1})\cdots r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{\ell+1},S_{\ell}).

Then
c^{\min}_{j,\ell}\leq h_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{\ell}).

(2) Let a drift function (
52
) (denoted by
c^{\max}(X,S_{\ell})
) satisfies that for
X\in S_{j}
,
c^{\max}_{\ell,\ell}=1
and for any
\ell<j\leq k
,

\displaystyle c^{\max}_{j,\ell}=

\displaystyle\ r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell<j_{1}<j}r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{j_{1}})\,r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j_{1}},S_{\ell})+\cdots

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell<\ell+1<\cdots<j-1<j}\,r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{j-1})\cdots r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{\ell+1},S_{\ell}).

Then
c^{\max}_{j,\ell}\geq h_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(X_{j},S_{\ell}).

Proof:

(1) By applying induction, it is straightforward to confirm that for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c^{\min}_{j,\ell}
satisfies

c^{\min}_{j,\ell}=\sum^{j-1}_{i=\ell}r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{i})c^{\min}_{i,\ell}\leq\sum^{j-1}_{i=\ell}r(X_{j},S_{i})c^{\min}_{i,\ell}.

Consequently, according to Theorem
3
.(1), for any
\ell<j\leq k
, we have

c^{\min}_{j,\ell}\leq h_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{\ell}).

(2) The proof is similar to the first part.
∎

Each term in
c^{\min}_{j,\ell}
or
c^{\max}_{j,\ell}
denotes the conditional probability of reaching
S_{\ell}
along a path originating from
S_{j}
. The hitting probability is obtained by summing these conditional probabilities over all possible paths connecting
S_{j}
to
S_{\ell}
.

In Theorem
3
, coefficients are computed recursively in the direction from
\ell+1
to
k
:
c_{\ell+1,\ell},c_{\ell+2,\ell},\ldots,c_{k,\ell}
. They can also be computed recursively in the opposite direction from
k-1
to
\ell
:
c_{k,k-1},c_{k,k-2},\ldots,c_{k,\ell}
, as shown below.

Theorem 4

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
,

(1) Let a drift function (
52
) satisfy that for any
\ell<j\leq k
,

\displaystyle{c}_{j,\ell}\leq\sum^{K}_{i=0}{c}_{j,i}\,r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{i},S_{\ell})=\sum^{j}_{i=\ell+1}{c}_{j,i}\,r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{i},S_{\ell}),

(21)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

(2) Let a drift function (
52
) satisfy that for any
\ell<j\leq k
,

\displaystyle{c}_{j,\ell}\geq\sum^{K}_{i=0}c_{j,i}\,r_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(X_{i},S_{\ell})=\sum^{j}_{i=\ell+1}c_{j,i}\,r_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(X_{i},S_{\ell}),

(22)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\geq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

Proof:

(1)
By applying induction to (
21
), we can establish that
c_{j,\ell}\leq c^{\min}_{j,\ell}
. By the above corollary, we arrive at the desired conclusion.

(2) The proof is similar to the first part.
∎

Note: The sums in (
21
) and (
22
) do not contain the terms
i\leq\ell
and
i>k
, since for
i\leq\ell
,
r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{i},S_{\ell})=0
, and for
i>k
,
c_{i,\ell}=0
.

V-D

Lower Bound Coefficients Using Paths

For multimodal fitness landscapes, there are multiple paths from one fitness level to another. For example, in Fig.
1
, there are
11!
paths from
S_{12}
to
S_{1}
. To calculate coefficient
c_{k,\ell}
, it is sufficient to use one path
P[\ell,k]
from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
, rather than using all paths from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
. For example, in Fig.
1
, coefficient
c_{12,1}
can be estimated using a longer path
S_{12}\to\cdots\to S_{2}\to S_{1}
, or a shorter path
S_{12}\to\cdots\to S_{8}\to S_{1}
. In this case, two
c_{12,1}
values can be generated, however, it suffices to utilize any one of them.

The following theorem uses a path to obtain the lower bound coefficient
c_{k,\ell}
. If vertex
j\in(\ell,k]
is not on the path
P(\ell,k]
, then we directly assign the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}=0
.

Theorem 5

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
, let
P{[\ell,k]}
be a path from
k
to
\ell
. Let a drift function (
52
) satisfy that for any
j\in(\ell,k]\setminus P{(\ell,k]}
,
c_{j,\ell}=0
, and for any
j\in P(\ell,k]
,

c_{j,\ell}\leq\min_{X_{j}\in S_{j}}\left\{r(X_{j},S_{\ell})+\sum_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}r(X_{j},S_{i})\,c_{i,\ell}\right\}.

(23)

Then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

Proof:

For any
j\in(\ell,k]\setminus P{(\ell,k]}
, since
c_{j,\ell}=0
, the conditional drift
\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{j},S_{\ell})\leq 0
. For any
j\in P{(\ell,k]}
, the conditional drift
\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{j},S_{\ell})\leq 0
by (
23
). According to Theorem
3
.(1), we get the conclusion.
∎

The coefficient computation in Theorem
5
is recursive.
The following corollary provides a non-recursive way to compute the coefficients.

Corollary 2

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
,
let
P{[\ell,k]}
be a path from
k
to
\ell
.
Let a drift function (
52
) satisfy that for any
j\in(\ell,k]\setminus P{(\ell,k]}
,
c_{j,\ell}=0
, and for any
j\in P(\ell,k]
,

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in P(\ell,j]}r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{i},S_{P[\ell,i)}),

(24)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\leq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

Proof:

From (
24
), we get

\displaystyle\min_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}c_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in P(\ell,j)}r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{i},S_{P[\ell,i)}),

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle=r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{P[\ell,j)})\min_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}c_{i,\ell}.

(25)

For any
j\in P{(\ell,k]}
,

\displaystyle\min_{X_{j}\in S_{j}}\left\{\sum_{i\in P{[\ell,j)}}r(X_{j},S_{i})\,c_{i,\ell}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \sum_{i\in{[\ell,j)}}r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{i})c_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ r_{\scriptscriptstyle\min}(X_{j},S_{P{[\ell,j)}})\min_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}c_{i,\ell}=c_{j,\ell}\quad\mbox{(by \eqref{equ:Cor1Condition})}.

According to Theorem
5
, we get the conclusion.
∎In (
24
), the transition probability
r(X_{j},S_{P[\ell,j)})
represents the transition from
X_{j}
to the path
P[\ell,j)
.

V-E

Upper Bound Coefficients Using Paths

Intuitively, it seems impossible to obtain an upper bound on the hitting probability using a path, since the transition probability of going from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
is not less than that of going from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
via a path
P{[\ell,k]}
. Counterintuitively, the following theorem surprisingly establishes an upper bound on the hitting probability using a path. If vertex
i\in(\ell,k]
is not on the path
P(\ell,k]
, we simply assign the coefficient
c_{i,\ell}=1
.

Theorem 6

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
, let
P{[\ell,k]}
be a path from
k
to
\ell
.
Let a drift function (
52
) satisfy that for any
j\in(\ell,k]\setminus P{(\ell,k]}
,
c_{j,\ell}=0
, and for any
j\in P(\ell,k]
,

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}\geq\max_{X_{j}\in S_{j}}\left\{r(X_{j},S_{[\ell,j)\setminus P{(\ell,j)}})+\sum_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}r(X_{j},S_{i})c_{i,\ell}\right\},

(26)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\geq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

Proof:

For any
j\in(\ell,k]\setminus P{(\ell,k]}
, since
c_{j,\ell}=1
, the conditional drift
\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{j},S_{\ell})\geq 0
. For any
j\in P{(\ell,k]}
, the conditional drift
\tilde{\Delta}c(X_{j},S_{\ell})\geq 0
by (
26
).
According to Theorem
3
.(2), we get the conclusion.
∎

The coefficient computation in Theorem
6
is recursive.
The following corollary provides a non-recursive way to compute the coefficients.

Corollary 3

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
and two levels
\ell,k:1\leq\ell<k\leq K
, let
P{[\ell,k]}
be a path from
k
to
\ell
. Let a drift function (
52
) satisfy that for any
j\in(\ell,k]\setminus P{(\ell,k]}
,
c_{j,\ell}=0
, and for any
j\in P(\ell,k]
,

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}=\sum_{i\in P(\ell,j]}r_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(X_{i},S_{[\ell,i)\setminus P(\ell,i)}),

(27)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient
c_{j,\ell}\geq h_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{j},S_{\ell})
.

From (
27
), we get

\displaystyle\max_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}c_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in P(\ell,j)}r_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(X_{i},S_{[\ell,i)\setminus P(\ell,i)}),

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle\max_{X_{j}\in S_{j}}\left\{r(X_{j},S_{[\ell,j)\setminus P{(\ell,j)}})+\sum_{i\in P{(\ell,j)}}r(X_{j},S_{i})c_{i,\ell}\right\}

, we get the conclusion.

In (

), the conditional probability

to those vertices in
[\ell,j)
but not on the path
P(\ell,j)
.

VI
Comparison of Two Algorithms

This section demonstrates the use of the new drift analysis method to compare two EAs for the knapsack problem.

VI-A

Comparison of Two EAs

In computer experiments, the performance of two EAs is assessed using a benchmark suite that includes both easy and hard problems
[
31
]
. Similarly, theoretical studies should compare EAs using a benchmark suite.

In this paper, several instances of the knapsack problem are designed to serve as a benchmark suite for comparison. They represent both easy and hard scenarios. The knapsack problem is selected due to its NP-complete nature and real-world application. Unlike traditional benchmarks such as OneMax and LeadingOnes
[
12
,
13
]
, the knapsack problem is subject to a constraint. Various constraint-handling techniques have been employed in EAs, such as feasibility rules
[
14
,
15
]
, the penalty method
[
32
]
, solution repair
[
16
,
17
]
.

When comparing two EAs, A and B, on a benchmark, we employ the following ratio to compare their hitting times.

{compare}(\mbox{A,B})=\frac{\mbox{mean hitting time of A}}{\mbox{mean hitting time of B}}.

VI-B

The Knapsack Problem

The knapsack problem is described as follows. There are
n
items, each with a specific weight
w_{i}
and value
v_{i}
. The goal is to select a subset of these items to include in the knapsack, ensuring that the total weight does not exceed the capacity of the knapsack
C
while maximizing the overall value. For the
i
th item, let
b_{i}=1
indicate that the item is included in the backpack, and
b_{i}=0
indicate that the item is not included in the backpack. The knapsack problem can be expressed as a constrained optimization problem. Let
x=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
.

\displaystyle\max f(x)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}v_{i}b_{i}

\displaystyle\mbox{subject to }\sum^{n}_{i=1}w_{i}b_{i}\leq C.

(29)

The first EA is the (1+1) EA using feasibility rules, which is described in Algorithm
1
. The (1+1) EA is chosen because it serves as a common baseline in the theoretical analysis of EAs
[
12
,
13
]
. Its purpose is to avoid complex calculations of transition probabilities, allowing a focus on the analysis itself.

Algorithm 1

VII
Conclusions

This paper addresses the computation of coefficients within linear drift functions for elitist EAs. First, it provides a new interpretation of linear bound coefficients, where each coefficient represents a bound on the hitting probability of a fitness level. This transforms the task of estimating hitting time into that of estimating hitting probability. Second, it proposes a new method called drift analysis of hitting probability, which allows estimating linear bounding coefficients separately through different paths. This method significantly improves existing drift analysis by constructing general and explicit linear drift functions.

The proposed method can estimate both lower and upper bounds on the hitting time. It provides a useful tool to compare the hitting time of two EAs. To demonstrate this, it is applied to compare two EAs with feasibility rules and greedy repair for solving the knapsack problem. The results show that neither constraint handling technique consistently outperforms the other across various instances. However, in certain special cases, using greedy repair can significantly reduce the hitting time from exponential to polynomial.

Future research will aim to extend this framework to the analysis of more combinatorial optimization problems, including vertex cover and maximum satisfiability problems. However, there are inherent limitations to the linear drift function. Specifically, it may not provide tight time bounds for fitness functions that do not follow a level-based structure. Also, it is not available for non-elitist EAs.

1&\mbox{if }\ell<k,\end{array}\right.

(52)

When the coefficient
c_{k,\ell}=c
, the Type-
c_{k,\ell}
bound degenerates into the Type-
c
bound.

Corollary 4
(Type-
c
lower bound)

Let the coefficient
c_{k,\ell}=c
in the drift function (
52
). Then for any
\ell<k
and
X_{k}\in S_{k}
, the drift

{\Delta}c(X_{k},S_{\ell})\leq 0
is equivalent to

c\leq\min_{k>\ell}\min_{\ell\geq 1}\;\min_{X_{k}:p(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})>0}\frac{p(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})}.

(53)

Proof:

It is sufficient to analyze the case
p(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})>0
.
From the drift condition, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}&{\Delta}c(X_{k},S_{\ell})\leq 0.\\
\Leftrightarrow&c\leq r(X_{k},S_{\ell})+\sum^{k-1}_{j=\ell+1}r(X_{k},S_{j})c.\\
\Leftrightarrow&c\leq\frac{r(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{r(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]}}=\frac{p(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})}.\end{array}

Then we obtained the desired conclusion.
∎

VIII-B

Advantage of the Type-
c_{k,\ell}
Bound Over the Type-
c_{\ell}
Bound

In most cases, the Type-
c_{k,\ell}
bound is tighter than the Type-
c
and Type-
c_{\ell}
bounds. Let us prove this claim.
Since a linear bound is determined by a set of coefficients, the comparison of two linear bounds is based on Pareto dominance.

Definition 4

Given a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\cdots,S_{k})
and an EA, a linear lower bound with coefficients
\{c_{k,\ell}\}
(where
1\leq\ell<k\leq K
) is considered tighter than another bound with coefficients
\{c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}\}
if
c_{k,\ell}\geq c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
for all
({k,\ell})
, and
c_{k,\ell}>c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
for some
({k,\ell})
. Similarly, a linear upper bound with coefficients
\{c_{k,\ell}\}
is considered tighter than another bound with coefficients
\{c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}\}
if
c_{k,\ell}\leq c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
for all
({k,\ell})
, and
c_{k,\ell}<c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
for some
({k,\ell})
.

The theorem below states that the Type-
c_{k,\ell}
lower bound is tighter than the Type-
c_{\ell}
lower bound.

Theorem 7

Given an elitist and convergent and EA, and a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\cdots,S_{K})
,
For any
1\leq\ell<k
, let
c^{*}_{\ell}
denote the best coefficient
c_{\ell}
in the Type-
c_{\ell}
lower bound such that

c^{*}_{\ell}=\min_{\ell<i\leq k}\;\min_{X_{i}:p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})>0}\frac{p(X_{i},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})}.

(62)

Let
c^{*}_{k,\ell}
denote the coefficient in the Type-
c_{k,\ell}
lower bound such that

\displaystyle c^{*}_{k,\ell}=\min_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}\left(r(X_{k},S_{\ell})+\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{k-1}r(X_{k},S_{j})\,c^{*}_{j,\ell}\right).

(63)

Then the coefficients
c^{*}_{k,\ell}
Pareto dominate
c^{*}_{\ell}
, meaning:

1.

For any
i=\ell+1,\ldots,k
,
c^{*}_{i,\ell}\geq c^{*}_{\ell}
.

2.

If for some
i
,

\displaystyle\min_{X_{i}:p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})>0}\frac{p(X_{i},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})}>c^{*}_{\ell},

(64)

then
c^{*}_{i,\ell}>c^{*}_{\ell}
.

Proof:

According to the definition of conditional probability
r(X_{k},S_{\ell})
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{p(X_{i},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})}=\frac{r(X_{i},S_{\ell})}{r(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})}.

(65)

Inserting the above equality to (
62
), we get

\displaystyle c^{*}_{\ell}=\min_{\ell<i\leq k}\;\min_{X_{i}:p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})>0}\frac{r(X_{i},S_{\ell})}{r(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})},

and then for an
i
,

\displaystyle r(X_{i},S_{\ell})\geq r(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})\;c^{*}_{\ell}.

(66)

Inequality (
66
) is strict if (
64
) is true.
Utilizing Inequality (
66
), we can employ induction to demonstrate that
c^{*}_{k,\ell}
Pareto dominates
c^{*}_{\ell}
.

Since the conditional probability
r(X_{\ell+1},S_{[0,\ell]})=1
, it is trivial to get for
j=\ell+1
,

\displaystyle c^{*}_{\ell+1,\ell}=r(X_{\ell+1},S_{\ell})=\frac{r(X_{\ell+1},S_{\ell})}{r(X_{\ell+1},S_{[0,\ell]})}\geq c^{*}_{\ell}.

(67)

The inequality is strict if (
64
) is true.

Assume that for
j=\ell+1,\cdots,i-1
,
c^{*}_{j,\ell}\geq c^{*}_{\ell}
.
Inserting this to (
63
), we have

\displaystyle c^{*}_{i,\ell}\geq r(X_{i},S_{\ell})+\sum^{i-1}_{j=\ell+1}r(X_{i},S_{j})c^{*}_{\ell}.

(68)

Combining (
66
) with the above inequality, we get

\displaystyle c^{*}_{i,\ell}\geq r(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell]})\;c^{*}_{\ell}+r(X_{i},S_{[\ell+1,i-1]})c^{*}_{\ell}=c^{*}_{\ell}.

(69)

Inequality (
68
) is strict if (
64
) is true. By induction, we complete the proof.
∎

S_{k}

(70)

Let
