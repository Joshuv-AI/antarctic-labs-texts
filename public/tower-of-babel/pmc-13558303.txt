Clinical characteristics and temporal trends of pediatric gastrointestinal foreign body ingestion: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Yuta Takeuchi, Seiichiro Inoue, Yuki Muta, Akio Odaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe aimed to evaluate temporal changes in the clinical characteristics and management of pediatric gastrointestinal foreign body ingestion.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients aged < 15 years who presented with suspected foreign body ingestion between January 2016 and June 2025. Patients were divided into early (≤ 2020) and late (≥ 2021) periods. Clinical characteristics, foreign body type, time to presentation, and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsOverall, 586 patients were included, of whom 371 underwent imaging and 187 had confirmed foreign bodies. Removal was required in 52 cases. The removal rate was higher in the late period despite fewer detected cases. Button batteries were the most common foreign bodies and the only category showing an increase in the number of removal cases. Cigarette- and electronic cigarette-related ingestion was more frequent in the late period. Patients in the late period tended to present earlier after foreign body ingestion, with foreign bodies more frequently located in the esophagus or stomach. Age was not associated with the increased removal rate.ConclusionsTemporal changes in pediatric foreign body ingestion were observed, with increased removal rates in the late period. These findings may be associated with an increased proportion of high-risk button batteries and relatively earlier presentation after ingestion.Keywords: Foreign body ingestion, Button battery, Esophageal foreign body, Magnet ingestion, Foreign body removal

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion is a common cause of emergency department visits in children, particularly among infants and toddlers who explore their environment orally [1, 2]. Most ingested foreign bodies spontaneously pass through the gastrointestinal tract without intervention. However, a subset of foreign bodies can cause serious complications that require urgent medical management. Button batteries and multiple magnets are of particular concern. Button batteries can cause rapid tissue injury through local electrical current generation and alkaline injury, leading to mucosal necrosis, perforation, and even life-threatening complications within a short period [3, 4]. Similarly, the ingestion of multiple magnets can result in pressure necrosis, fistula formation, and bowel perforation when the magnets are attracted to the intestinal walls [5, 6]. Therefore, early diagnosis and appropriate intervention are essential in high-risk cases. In recent years, changes in lifestyle and consumer products have influenced patterns of foreign body ingestion in children. The widespread use of lithium-button batteries in household electronic devices and increased availability of high-power magnets have contributed to an increase in severe cases [7, 8]. In addition, emerging hazards such as electronic cigarettes and related products have been increasingly recognized as potential sources of ingestion, particularly in younger children [9]. Despite these concerns, the clinical characteristics and temporal trends of pediatric foreign body ingestion vary across regions and institutions. Furthermore, factors such as age, sex, and time to presentation may influence both the detection rates and the need for intervention. However, comprehensive analyses integrating these factors are limited. The present study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, management, and temporal trends of pediatric gastrointestinal foreign body ingestion at a single tertiary center. We focused on differences according to sex, age, and time period, particularly between the early (≤ 2020) and late (≥ 2021) periods to evaluate temporal changes in clinical characteristics and management.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary pediatric surgical center. We reviewed the medical records of patients aged < 15 years presenting with suspected gastrointestinal foreign body ingestion between January 2016 and June 2025.

Diagnosis and initial assessment

Foreign body ingestion was diagnosed based on clinical history, physical examination, and imaging findings. Imaging, primarily radiography, was performed when foreign body ingestion was suspected. Foreign body detection was defined as the identification of foreign objects in imaging studies.

Management strategy

Management strategies included observation, endoscopic removal, manual extraction when feasible, and surgical intervention. Removal was performed when the foreign body was considered high risk or unlikely to pass spontaneously. Surgical intervention was defined as an operative procedure performed under general anesthesia and was distinguished from endoscopic removal. Management decisions were primarily made by the attending pediatric surgeons, and no major change in the decision-making process or institutional protocol occurred between the early and late periods.

Indications for removal

The decision to remove an ingested foreign body was primarily made by the attending pediatric surgeons on the basis of the type, size, anatomical location, time since ingestion, symptoms, and radiographic findings. All foreign bodies located in the esophagus were considered for prompt removal. For foreign bodies located in the stomach, removal was performed whenever feasible when the object was sharp or potentially harmful, including button batteries. However, management of sharp objects was individualized according to the estimated balance between the risk of endoscopic removal and the risk of observation. For example, metallic components of electronic cigarettes were managed conservatively when the anticipated risk of removal was considered greater than the risk of spontaneous passage. Multiple magnets, or magnets ingested together with metallic objects, were also considered for early removal whenever endoscopic retrieval was feasible because they may attract across different bowel loops and cause pressure necrosis or perforation. Removal was additionally considered when patients had symptoms, when a foreign body remained in the same location on serial imaging, or when spontaneous passage was considered unlikely. In contrast, asymptomatic patients with low-risk foreign bodies that had progressed beyond the stomach were generally managed conservatively with clinical and radiographic follow-up.

Study design

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate temporal changes in the clinical characteristics and management of pediatric foreign body ingestion. Patients were divided into two groups based on the study period: early (January 2016–December 2020) and late (January 2021–June 2025). Comparisons between these groups were performed to assess the differences in detection rates, foreign body types, and management outcomes. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to sex (male vs. female) and age (≤ 1 year vs. ≥2 years).

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research of Saitama Medical Center, Saitama Medical University (No. 2025 − 125). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study, and information regarding the study was disclosed using an opt-out procedure.

Outcome measures

The outcomes were: detection rate of foreign bodies, removal rate among detected cases, distribution of foreign body types, time to presentation, and anatomical location of foreign bodies.

Button battery characteristics

For cases involving button battery ingestion, battery composition, diameter, anatomical location at presentation, and management were reviewed from the medical records when available. In this cohort, alkaline batteries were approximately 10 mm in diameter, whereas lithium batteries were approximately 20 mm in diameter.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, and continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Results

Patient flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 586 patients with suspected foreign body ingestion were included in this study. The mean age was 2.09 years. Among these patients, 371 (63.3%) underwent imaging, and foreign bodies were detected in 187 patients, corresponding to 50.4% of those who underwent imaging and 31.9% of the overall cohort. Removal was required in 52 patients (27.8% of detected cases), and only one patient required surgical intervention in the early period. No deaths occurred during the study period. Multiple foreign bodies were identified in 11 patients (Fig. 1).

Comparison of clinical outcomes by period, sex, and age

When comparing the early period (≤ 2020) with the late period (≥ 2021), the number of detected foreign bodies decreased in the late period. However, both the absolute number and proportion of cases requiring removal increased, resulting in a significantly higher removal rate in the late period (Fig. 2). The imaging rates were comparable between boys and girls (Table 1). The detection rate was higher in boys than in girls (P < 0.01), whereas the removal rate among patients with detected foreign bodies was higher in girls than in boys (Table 1). Age-based analysis showed that detection rates were higher in patients aged ≥ 2 years, whereas removal rates were similar between age groups (Table 1). These sex- and age-based analyses were performed to assess whether demographic factors might have contributed to the temporal difference in removal rates. Foreign body type differed by age group: button batteries were most common in children aged ≤ 1 year, whereas toys were predominant in children aged 2–4 years. Magnets and food-related foreign bodies were more frequent in older than in younger children. Button batteries were the most common indications for removal in younger children, whereas coins were the most common in older children. No clear association was observed between age distribution and the increased removal rate in the late period.

Distribution and temporal trends of foreign body types

The most common foreign bodies were button batteries (n = 82), toys (n = 80), metals (n = 52), magnets (n = 51), and coins (n = 48) (Fig. 3). Temporal analysis revealed variability in foreign body types across the study period. Notably, cigarette/e-cigarette ingestion increased in the late period despite an overall decrease in total cases (Fig. 3). Metallic components of electronic cigarettes were managed conservatively because the anticipated risk of endoscopic removal was considered greater than the risk of observation. All metallic components of electronic cigarettes passed spontaneously without symptoms or complications. Button batteries were the only category showing an increase in the number of removal cases in the late period (Fig. 3).

To further characterize button battery ingestions, we reviewed battery composition, approximate diameter, and management according to study period. Although the total number of button battery ingestions decreased from 47 cases in the early period to 35 cases in the late period, the proportion requiring removal increased from 12.8% (6/47) to 37.1% (13/35). Lithium batteries increased from 1 case (2.1%) in the early period to 5 cases (14.3%) in the late period, whereas the remaining cases involved alkaline batteries. Alkaline batteries were approximately 10 mm in diameter, whereas lithium batteries were approximately 20 mm in diameter. All five lithium battery cases in the late period underwent removal. The single lithium battery case in the early period had already progressed to the small intestine at presentation and was managed conservatively. Among the 63 button battery cases managed without removal, no clinically significant complications, including gastrointestinal perforation, bleeding, or stricture, were observed during follow-up. In the late period, one patient developed esophageal mucosal injury caused by a lithium button battery, which was removed endoscopically. Among magnet ingestions, removal was performed in four cases. Early removal was selected for multiple magnets or magnets associated with metallic objects when endoscopic retrieval was feasible. One patient in the early period required surgical intervention because magnets had adhered across separate bowel loops, resulting in marked bowel-wall compression with impending perforation. The remaining magnet cases were managed according to the number, location, and interval change on serial radiographs.

Time to presentation and temporal changes

Among patients with known time from ingestion to presentation, the mean time to presentation was shorter in the late period than in the early period (206 vs. 329 min), although the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, among removal cases, the mean time to presentation was shorter in the late period (213 vs. 510 min), although this difference was also not statistically significant (Table 2; Fig. 4).

Relationship between time to presentation and anatomical location

We next examined the relationship between time to presentation and the anatomical location of the foreign body. Foreign bodies identified within 120 min after ingestion were more commonly located in the esophagus or stomach, whereas those identified after a longer interval were more frequently located in the duodenum or more distal bowel. When time to presentation was examined according to foreign body type, button battery ingestions tended to present within 120 min more frequently than other types of foreign body ingestion.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated temporal differences in pediatric gastrointestinal foreign body ingestion by comparing the early (≤ 2020) and late (≥ 2021) periods. We found that the removal rate increased in the late period despite a decrease in the number of detected cases, suggesting changes not only in the frequency but also in the clinical characteristics and management of foreign body ingestion.

A notable finding was an increase in the number of removal cases in the late period. This trend may be partially explained by changes in the clinical characteristics and management of button battery ingestion. Button batteries can cause rapid and severe tissue injury through local electrical current generation and alkaline injury, often requiring urgent intervention [3, 4]. Previous studies have emphasized that button battery ingestion is associated with a higher risk of complications and a greater likelihood of requiring removal than ingestion of other foreign bodies [5, 6].

The distribution of foreign body types in our cohort differed in part from that reported in other countries. In recent studies from the United States and Europe, coins have remained among the most commonly ingested foreign bodies in children [1, 2, 9], whereas button batteries were the most frequently detected foreign body in our cohort. This difference may reflect regional variation in household products, caregiver awareness, healthcare-seeking behavior, and referral patterns. These findings support the need for region-specific surveillance and prevention strategies rather than assuming that foreign body ingestion patterns are uniform across settings.

Although the total number of button battery ingestions decreased in the late period, button batteries were the only category showing an increase in the number of removal cases. The proportion of button battery cases requiring removal increased from 12.8% (6/47) in the early period to 37.1% (13/35) in the late period. In addition, lithium batteries were identified more frequently in the late period than in the early period (5 vs. 1 case), and all five lithium battery cases in the late period underwent removal. Larger lithium batteries are clinically important because of their potential for esophageal impaction and severe tissue injury. Therefore, the increased proportion of lithium batteries may have contributed to the higher removal rate in the late period. However, the number of lithium battery cases was small, and this association should be interpreted cautiously. Although no clinically significant complications were observed among button battery cases managed without removal in this cohort, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that removal can be safely delayed. Most non-removed batteries had already progressed beyond the stomach or were judged to be at low risk based on their anatomical location and clinical course. In contrast, esophageal button batteries and gastric batteries with a high risk of injury require prompt assessment and removal according to established management recommendations.

In addition to button batteries, we observed an increase in cigarette and electronic cigarette-related ingestion in the late period. This finding is noteworthy, because these items are not traditionally considered common foreign bodies in the pediatric population. The increasing availability and use of electronic cigarettes in households may have contributed to this trend [9, 10]. Previous reports have highlighted the potential toxicity of nicotine-containing products in children, emphasizing the need for heightened awareness and preventive measures [11, 12]. Our findings suggest that emerging household products should be recognized as potential hazards for pediatric ingestion. In Japan, consumer safety agencies issued public warnings regarding button battery ingestion in 2014, and packaging designed to reduce the risk of accidental ingestion was introduced by the domestic battery industry in 2018. These initiatives may have increased caregiver awareness and contributed to earlier medical evaluation after suspected ingestion. However, the present study was not designed to assess the direct effect of these individual measures. We selected 2020 as the cutoff because it provided two clinically comparable observation periods and enabled assessment of more recent changes in the clinical presentation and management of pediatric foreign body ingestion. Further multicenter studies using interrupted time-series analyses will be needed to evaluate the impact of specific public health initiatives and packaging changes in Japan.

Although foreign body types differed according to age, removal rates were similar between age groups, suggesting that age alone is unlikely to explain the observed temporal changes. Age is known to influence the type and risk of foreign body ingestion [1, 2]. However, the observed temporal changes may instead reflect changes in caregiver behavior, healthcare-seeking patterns, and clinical decision making. Increased awareness of the risks associated with certain foreign bodies, particularly button batteries, may have led to earlier presentations and proactive intervention strategies. We also observed an association between time to presentation and the anatomical location of foreign bodies. Although the mean time from ingestion to presentation was shorter in the late period, the difference was not statistically significant. Foreign bodies identified soon after ingestion were more commonly located in the esophagus or stomach than in the distal intestine. This may have contributed to the higher removal rate observed in the late period, as foreign bodies located in proximal sites were more accessible for intervention. Early medical evaluation remains important to prevent distal migration and reduce complications, particularly for high-risk objects such as button batteries and magnets [7, 8, 13]. Our findings underscore the importance of prompt medical evaluation for suspected foreign body ingestion, especially when high-risk foreign bodies are suspected.

Collectively, these results highlight the importance of public awareness and early intervention in the management of pediatric foreign body ingestion. Over the past decade, various educational efforts have been undertaken to improve the recognition and prompt medical consultation for suspected ingestion. Our findings suggest that these efforts may have contributed to a tendency toward earlier presentations in the late period, thereby increasing the likelihood of timely intervention. However, the concurrent increase in the number of high-risk foreign bodies highlights the need for continuous and enhanced preventive strategies. From a clinical perspective, our findings emphasize the importance of rapid assessment and management, particularly in cases involving high-risk objects. Caregivers should be educated to seek immediate medical attention when foreign body ingestion is suspected, especially in cases involving button batteries and magnets. In addition, healthcare providers should maintain a high index of suspicion and perform prompt imaging when indicated.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated temporal changes in the management of pediatric foreign body ingestion, characterized by a higher removal rate in the late period despite fewer detected foreign bodies. This change may have been influenced by the increased proportion of lithium button batteries and relatively earlier presentation after ingestion, which may have allowed intervention before distal passage. Continued public education, rapid medical evaluation, and prompt imaging when indicated are essential to reduce complications associated with pediatric foreign body ingestion.
