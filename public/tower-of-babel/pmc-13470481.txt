Adaptive super-twisting sliding mode control for robust tracking of wearable PAM-driven robotic manipulator
Authors: Solomon Ferede Ezez, Daniel Gosaye Tesfaye, Tofik Kemal Mohammed, Manivannan Raman, Chala Merga Abdissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPneumatic Artificial Muscle (PAM)-actuated rehabilitation robots provide a safe and compliant alternative to traditional rigid motor-driven systems, mimicking the inherent viscoelasticity of human muscle. However, precise trajectory tracking is a difficult control problem due to their highly nonlinear pressure–force–contraction relationship, multibody leg dynamics, friction effects, and external disturbances. This paper proposes an Adaptive Finite Time Super-Twisting Sliding Mode Control (ASTSMC) strategy for robust tracking of a leg rehabilitation manipulator capable of performing hip/knee flexion-extension exercises. The human leg is represented as a planar 2-DOF system that includes the hip and knee joints. The Euler–Lagrange formulation is used to derive a comprehensive nonlinear dynamic model that includes viscous–Coulomb friction and antagonistic PAM torque generation. The controller employs a second-order sliding mode law to ensure a continuous control signal, significantly suppressing high-frequency oscillations. Additionally, in order to manage unknown lumped uncertainties without knowing the upper bounds beforehand, an adaptive gain law is integrated. Finite-time convergence of the tracking errors is confirmed by stability analysis using the Lyapunov method. Simulation results in MATLAB/Simulink validate that the proposed ASTSMC achieves superior trajectory tracking accuracy and robustness against parameter variations compared to standard sliding mode control schemes. The substantial reduction in chattering improves patient comfort and safety, proving that the proposed framework could be applied for advanced robotic neurorehabilitation in clinical settings.Keywords: Pneumatic artificial muscle, Rehabilitation robot, Adaptive super-twisting sliding mode control, Trajectory tracking, Chattering reductionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The field of physical therapy and neurorehabilitation has experienced a significant change, moving from labor-intensive manual exercises to the incorporation of advanced robotic systems intended to enhance motor function recovery. This shift is driven by the clinical observation that the human brain and spinal cord possess a remarkable capacity for adaptation and neural reorganization following injury, provided that specific movements are practiced consistently and repetitively1. The dosage and length of therapy sessions are often restricted by the physical strain that traditional rehabilitation techniques place on therapists. In order to improve motor outcomes and promote neuroplasticity in patients with stroke, spinal cord injuries, or aging mobility decline, robotic systems, especially lower-limb rehabilitation robots, provide quantifiable, repetitive, and task-specific training2,3. Neural and intelligent control strategies have further enhanced the effectiveness of such rehabilitation systems4.

Central to the development of these systems is the choice of actuation. Conventional rehabilitation robots have historically relied on hydraulic systems or electric motors with rigid gearing5. While these actuators offer high precision and high torque density, they are inherently non-compliant, which poses significant safety risks in human-robot interaction. Rigid actuators require complex, high-bandwidth force-feedback loops to ensure that the robot does not exert excessive force on a patient’s limb, particularly during involuntary muscle spasms6. This limitation has led researchers toward the adoption of soft actuation technologies, among which Pneumatic Artificial Muscles (PAMs), specifically the McKibben-type muscle, have emerged as a premier candidate for wearable and assistive devices7.

Pneumatic Artificial Muscles offer a unique set of advantages that make them ideal for medical rehabilitation. Their key features include a high power-to-weight ratio, simple structure, and compliance. Since PAMs operate based on the compressibility of air, they exhibit a natural elasticity similar to that of human skeletal muscles8. This soft characteristic ensures increased safety and comfort for patients, as the robot can easily yield to unexpected movements9. However, transitioning from rigid to soft actuation introduces certain complexities. The relationship between compressed air pressure, contraction length, and contracting force in a PAM is highly nonlinear, hysteretic, and time-varying. Consequently, precise trajectory tracking requires the use of nonlinear control techniques10.

Recent work on pneumatic artificial muscle (PAM) actuators has produced a wide range of robust control approaches designed to address nonlinearities, hysteresis, uncertainties, and input constraints. Many designs are based on sliding mode control (SMC) combined with observers or intelligent approximators11,12. Intelligent hybrid and learning-based SMC frameworks have also been explored to enhance tracking performance in complex robotic systems13. Integral and terminal sliding mode variants further enhance robustness and finite-time convergence while reducing chattering effects14,15. In parallel, adaptive and learning-based control methods have been developed to handle parametric uncertainties and time-varying dynamics in PAM systems16. Adaptive super-twisting strategies have also shown improved robustness for uncertain robotic manipulators17.

In addition to sliding mode-based strategies, Active Disturbance Rejection Control (ADRC) has emerged as a powerful alternative for handling uncertainties and disturbances in rehabilitation robotics. ADRC employs extended state observers to estimate and compensate for internal dynamics and external disturbances in real time, enabling robust performance without requiring an accurate system model. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ADRC in lower-limb exoskeleton systems, including differential flatness-based ADRC combined with impedance control for enhanced human–robot interaction18, as well as observer-based ADRC frameworks for PAM-driven rehabilitation devices19. Neural-enhanced ADRC approaches have also been proposed to further improve disturbance estimation and adaptability in nonlinear robotic systems20.

Furthermore, enhanced ADRC strategies have been successfully applied to trajectory tracking control of robotic rehabilitation systems under time-varying and nonlinear conditions21. While these approaches provide strong disturbance rejection capabilities, they typically rely on observer tuning and may not explicitly guarantee finite-time convergence of tracking errors. In contrast, higher-order sliding mode techniques, such as the proposed Adaptive Super-Twisting Sliding Mode Control (ASTSMC), offer inherent robustness with finite-time convergence properties and continuous control action. This distinction highlights the relevance of ASTSMC as an alternative robust control framework for wearable PAM-driven rehabilitation systems.

Another important trend is the integration of SMC with fuzzy or neural approximators to estimate unknown nonlinearities and disturbances online. Adaptive fuzzy SMC and related intelligent control schemes have demonstrated improved robustness and reduced tracking error compared with conventional methods22,23. Neural-network-based and fuzzy-enhanced SMC approaches further improve disturbance rejection and adaptability24,25. These hybrid controllers have been successfully applied to PAM-driven robotic systems under input saturation, dead zones, and external disturbances26–28. In a related development, adaptive fuzzy sliding mode control has been shown to effectively reduce chattering while preserving robustness in highly nonlinear and underactuated systems such as the inverted pendulum6.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain for wearable multi-DOF PAM-driven rehabilitation systems. Many existing approaches are limited to single-DOF systems or simulation-based validation and do not fully address joint coupling, safety, and comfort requirements in human–robot interaction14,15,29. Furthermore, some intelligent SMC methods introduce high design complexity and may still suffer from residual chattering13,22. Fixed-time and terminal sliding mode approaches show promising convergence but often lack validation in wearable rehabilitation scenarios17,24,25,27,28.

Motivated by these challenges, this work proposes a finite-time Adaptive Super-Twisting Sliding Mode Control (ASTSMC) strategy for a multi-DOF wearable PAM-driven lower-limb rehabilitation system. The proposed controller combines robustness, reduced chattering, and adaptive gain tuning without prior knowledge of disturbance bounds30–33. Lyapunov stability theory is employed to guarantee finite-time convergence of tracking errors under uncertainties and disturbances. Simulation results demonstrate improved tracking performance and reduced chattering, highlighting the suitability of the proposed approach for safe and compliant rehabilitation applications.

System modeling

Mechanical architecture of the PAM-actuated rehabilitation leg

Wearable pneumatic artificial muscle actuation.

The proposed rehabilitation manipulator utilizes a wearable Pneumatic Artificial Muscles (PAMs) as a compliant actuator for assisting lower-limb motion during therapeutic training. PAM-driven actuators are particularly suitable for human-robot interaction applications due to their lightweight construction, intrinsic compliance, and high force-to-weight ratio. These characteristics are particularly advantageous in medical robotics, as they facilitate safe and natural human–robot interaction, a critical requirement for effective lower-limb physical therapy34–36.

A McKibben-type PAM consists of an inner elastomeric bladder surrounded by an inextensible braided mesh sleeve and terminated by rigid end fittings. When compressed air is supplied into the bladder, internal pressure causes radial expansion. However, because the braided fibers maintain nearly constant length, radial expansion results in axial shortening of the actuator. Consequently, pneumatic energy is transformed into a mechanical contraction force along the muscle axis37,38.

Due to this unique architecture, a PAM produces a strictly unidirectional tensile force along its longitudinal axis. As the internal volume increases, the muscle shortens, creating a powerful pulling motion as in Fig. 1. Because the elastomeric bladder and mesh cannot provide structural rigidity under internal vacuum or atmospheric pressure, the actuator is incapable of generating a compressive or pushing force38.

Unidirectional tensile actuation of a pneumatic artificial muscle (PAM).

The strictly contractile nature of PAMs dictates the mechanical design of the overall robotic system. To achieve bidirectional joint motion, such as knee or ankle flexion and extension, the actuators must be integrated into specific mechanical arrangements39.

Antagonistic actuation mechanism

To achieve bidirectional rotational motion, each joint of the rehabilitation manipulator is actuated using an antagonistic pair of PAMs that mimics the functional behavior of human muscle groups such as the quadriceps and hamstrings. One actuator functions as the agonist muscle producing motion in one direction, while the opposing actuator generates motion in the reverse direction.

Both muscles are connected to the joint through a pulley transmission mechanism. When pressure inside one muscle increases, contraction occurs, and joint rotation is produced. Simultaneously, the opposing muscle elongates. Reversing the pressure distribution reverses joint motion as in Fig. 2. Furthermore, simultaneous pressurization of both muscles enables modulation of joint stiffness without producing motion, providing an inherent impedance regulation capability highly desirable in rehabilitation systems37–39.

Antagonistic PAM actuation for bidirectional joint motion.

This setup requires four PAMs per leg (two for the hip and two for the knee), totaling eight PAMs for a bilateral rehabilitation system5. The use of antagonistic pairs offers several functional benefits beyond bidirectional torque:

Controller design

To achieve finite-time trajectory tracking for the 2-DOF PAM-driven leg manipulator under parameter uncertainties and actuator nonlinearities, an adaptive super-twisting sliding mode controller (ASTSMC) is designed. The joint torque generated by antagonistic PAM pairs is used as the control input, and the adaptive gains are updated online to ensure robustness and reduce chattering41–43.

In the presence of persistent disturbances, it is important to ensure that the adaptive gains remain bounded and do not exhibit windup behavior. The proposed adaptive law incorporates multiple safeguards to prevent unbounded gain growth. First, the inclusion of leakage (decay) terms ensures that when the sliding variable enters the boundary layer (), the gains decrease exponentially, preventing unnecessary accumulation. Second, the boundary layer regulates the adaptation process by limiting gain updates to regions where the tracking error is significant, thereby attenuating adaptation near convergence. Third, the adaptive scaling factor is constructed from bounded signals, which inherently constrains the rate of gain increase.

In addition to these theoretical properties, practical implementation includes explicit gain saturation to ensure that the adaptive gains remain within physically meaningful limits. As a result, even under sustained disturbances, the gains increase only transiently and settle to bounded values, avoiding instability and excessive control effort. This behavior is consistent with the simulation results, where the adaptive gains adjust in response to disturbances but do not diverge.

Results and discussion

Simulation setup

This section presents the simulation studies conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Adaptive Super-Twisting Sliding Mode Controller (ASTSMC) for the PAM-driven 2-DOF rehabilitation manipulator. Simulations are performed using the nonlinear dynamic model developed in the System Modeling Section, which incorporates manipulator dynamics, antagonistic pneumatic actuation of artificial muscles, gravitational effects and friction disturbances.

The performance of the proposed ASTSMC is evaluated through comprehensive MATLAB/Simulink simulations and compared with classical Sliding Mode Control (SMC) and Super-Twisting Sliding Mode Control (STSMC). Three distinct simulation scenarios are considered: sinusoidal trajectory tracking (Rehabilitation Mode), robustness against sudden step changes (Step Mode), and performance under external disturbances and parameter variations.

The mechanical, actuator, and controller parameters used in the simulation study are summarized in Table 2. These parameters correspond to typical values reported for PAM-driven rehabilitation manipulators and were selected to represent a realistic lower-limb assistive system.

In addition, the computational complexity of the proposed ASTSMC controller is low, involving only basic algebraic operations for the equivalent control, super-twisting terms, and adaptive gain updates. For the considered 2-DOF system, the total computational load is modest and compatible with typical real-time control rates (e.g., 1 kHz). This indicates that the proposed controller is suitable for real-time implementation on standard embedded platforms.

Step tracking performance (constant reference)

To evaluate the transient response and steady-state tracking performance of the controllers, constant reference trajectories are employed for both hip and knee joints. This setup is analogous to a step-response test commonly used in control system analysis, enabling a clear assessment of convergence speed, overshoot, and steady-state error.

While constant references do not fully represent practical rehabilitation movements, they provide a useful benchmark for comparing control performance under well-defined conditions. More realistic time-varying trajectories are considered in the subsequent section.

Another noticeable advantage of the ASTSMC is its ability to achieve significant chattering reduction.. The control input results further highlight the superiority of the ASTSMC approach. As shown in Fig. 9, the classical SMC control torque is characterized by high-frequency switching, and hence, chattering is significant. Although STSMC reduces this effect, some fluctuations remain. The ASTSMC significantly suppresses chattering, resulting in smoother control signals with lower oscillation amplitude and faster stabilization. This leads to more efficient torque utilization and reduced mechanical stress on the system, demonstrating the effectiveness of the adaptive mechanism in improving both control smoothness and overall system performance.

Conclusion

This study presented a robust control framework for trajectory tracking of a 2-DOF pneumatic artificial muscle (PAM)-driven lower-limb rehabilitation manipulator. An adaptive super-twisting sliding mode control (ASTSMC) strategy was developed to address the inherent nonlinearities, hysteresis effects, and parametric uncertainties associated with PAM actuators. By integrating a nonlinear dynamic model based on the Euler–Lagrange formulation with an adaptive high-order sliding mode controller, the proposed approach ensures finite-time convergence of tracking errors while maintaining robustness against external disturbances and modeling inaccuracies.

A key contribution of this work lies in the development of an improved low-chattering adaptive super-twisting algorithm with bounded adaptive gain laws and a smoothed switching mechanism. These enhancements effectively mitigate chattering phenomena while preserving the strong robustness properties of sliding mode control. The inclusion of a differentiator-based disturbance estimation and adaptive gain tuning further improves control performance without requiring prior knowledge of disturbance bounds. Simulation results demonstrated that the proposed ASTSMC approach provides superior trajectory tracking accuracy and reduced chattering compared to conventional sliding mode strategies. The controller maintains stable and consistent performance under varying operating conditions, highlighting its suitability for PAM-driven rehabilitation systems where compliance and safety are critical.

For future work, the model can be extended to a more realistic 3-DOF lower-limb configuration that includes hip, knee, and ankle joints to better represent human gait dynamics. Additionally, experimental validation on a physical PAM-driven rehabilitation platform is necessary to further assess real-time performance, robustness, and clinical applicability of the proposed control strategy.
