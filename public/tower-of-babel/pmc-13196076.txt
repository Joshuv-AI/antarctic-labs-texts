A 3D skeletal muscle system for disease modelling and secretome profiling of Duchenne muscular dystrophy
Authors: Mariam Zouhair, Marianna Cosentino, Ludovica Apa, Desiree Genovese, Sasha Marco Maria Di Iorio, Carmine Nicoletti, Domenico Liguoro, Giorgia Nanni, Simone Dinarelli, Alessandro Palma, Caterina Boccia, Laura Forcina, Silvia Sideri, Rita Mancini, Monica Ballarino, Antonio Musarò
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe X-linked genetic disease characterized by progressive muscle degeneration, exhaustion of the muscle stem cell pool, and extensive fibrotic remodelling, ultimately leading to loss of function and reduced quality of life. Although conventional cultures and mouse models have provided valuable insights into the pathogenesis of DMD, their mild phenotypes and prolonged disease progression require large sample sizes and lengthy experimental timelines. In turn, the field lacks experimental models that recapitulate the complexity of the dystrophic muscle phenotype in vitro for disease modelling or drug screening.MethodsWe developed a three-dimensional (3D) construct of dystrophic skeletal muscle using a scaffold-free approach, starting from primary cells isolated from the mdx4cv mouse strain, a widely used model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). To assess the pathological fidelity of the DMD 3D model, we conducted a thorough morphological and functional characterization. Taking advantage of the controlled and isolated nature of the system, we explored the paracrine role of the derived muscle-extracellular vesicles (EVs), investigating their potential contribution to disease progression.ResultsThe heterogeneous 3D skeletal muscle model of DMD faithfully reproduced the hallmark pathological features observed in patient-derived muscle tissue, including progressive muscle degeneration, fibrotic remodelling, and defective regenerative capacity. Furthermore, it enabled mechanistic investigations of muscle-derived EVs, revealing their ability to propagate both regenerative and catabolic signals.ConclusionsThis 3D model provides a physiologically relevant and reproducible tool for studying the molecular mechanisms underlying DMD and evaluating potential therapeutic interventions, reducing the use of animal models. Its capacity to replicate key aspects of the muscle pathology holds significant potential for identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, with broad implications for translational research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13395-026-00423-8.Keywords: Modelling Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD), Skeletal muscle tissue engineering, Three-dimensional (3D) in vitro models, Muscle-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), Skeletal muscle microenvironment

Introduction

Skeletal muscle is a complex and dynamic tissue essential for voluntary movement, metabolic regulation, and whole-body homeostasis [1]. Its remarkable regenerative capacity relies on coordinated interactions among different cell populations, including satellite cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and resident immune cells, embedded within an intricate three-dimensional architecture [2, 3]. However, this regenerative potential is progressively compromised in chronic disease settings, giving rise to muscle degeneration and functional decline [4]. Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), the most prevalent and severe form of inherited myopathy, is caused by mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes dystrophin [5, 6]. This large cytoskeletal protein links the intracellular actin cytoskeleton to the sarcolemma and extracellular matrix (ECM) via the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC), stabilizing muscle fibers during contraction [7]. The absence of dystrophin leads to repeated cycles of myofiber damage, chronic inflammation, impaired regeneration, and fibrotic remodelling, ultimately resulting in loss of ambulation and premature death [8, 9]. Despite extensive research, a critical need remains for physiologically relevant and experimentally tractable models to dissect early DMD mechanisms and evaluate therapeutic interventions. Within this context, traditional in vitro systems, primarily based on two-dimensional (2D) cultures of immortalized or primary myoblasts, fall short in recapitulating the complexity of mature muscle tissue, as they lack the mechanical context and the multicellular interactions essential for muscle homeostasis and pathology [10]. In contrast, in vivo models can faithfully reproduce many aspects of DMD but are limited by systemic confounders, ethical concerns, cost, and low experimental throughput. Moreover, practical constraints surrounding human muscle biopsies restrict their utility, particularly in longitudinal or mechanistic studies [11].

To address these limitations, three-dimensional (3D) muscle engineering strategies have gained attraction as in vitro systems that mimic key features of native muscle in a controlled environment. Recent advances include hydrogel-based myobundles, scaffold-supported constructs seeded with immortalized or stem cell–derived myoblasts, and bioprinted muscle tissues, many of which exhibit remarkable structural and functional maturation [12–16]. Moreover, several studies have demonstrated that specific technical strategies can substantially increase the physiological complexity and long-term stability of engineered muscle tissues. These include innervated skeletal muscle models that support functional neuromuscular junction formation and motor neuron–dependent contractile responses [17–19], as well as the incorporation of endothelial cells to promote a vessel-like network to enhance nutrient and oxygen delivery during prolonged in vitro maintenance [20, 21]. While these models support functional readouts such as contractility and force generation, they often rely on exogenous scaffolds, exhibit limited cellular heterogeneity, or require complex fabrication procedures [15, 22]. Instead, the X-MET (eX vivo Muscle Engineered Tissue) model represents a complementary scaffold-free platform that prioritises endogenous cellular heterogeneity, self-organisation, and experimental accessibility over maximal architectural maturation [23]. It exhibits spontaneous contractility, aligned myofibers, and responsiveness to stimuli, while preserving satellite cells, non-myogenic support cells, and developing vascular-like structures, enabling regenerative responses and long-term in vitro maintenance [23–25].

These characteristics make X-MET a physiologically responsive platform particularly suited for investigating early and intermediate stages of muscle organization, degeneration, and regeneration. By maintaining both intrinsic and microenvironment-dependent features, X-MET enables detailed interrogation of early degenerative changes, impaired satellite cell activation and behaviour, and local cell–cell communication, processes that are otherwise difficult to isolate in vivo. Collectively, these properties [25, 26] highlight X-MET as an accessible and versatile system for studying key events in skeletal muscle pathogenesis, modelling regenerative processes, and exploring mechanisms underlying muscle diseases such as muscular dystrophy.

In this study, we adapted the X-MET system to model DMD by generating constructs from the mdx4cv mouse, a refined variant of the classical mdx strain harbouring a nonsense mutation in exon 53 of the Dmd gene, characterized by a more severe dystrophic phenotype and reduced revertant fiber frequency [27, 28]. Our goal was to establish and validate a 3D dystrophic muscle model that mirrors the early and progressive morphological and functional alterations characteristic of DMD. We examined the organization of the sarcolemma, contractile properties, and the expression of molecular markers associated with degeneration, fibrosis, and failed regeneration [29, 30]. By comparing wild type and dystrophic X-METs over time, we aimed to track the pathological evolution in vitro and assess how well the model replicates known features of DMD muscle biology.

As skeletal muscle is increasingly recognized as an active secretory organ, extracellular vesicles (EVs), membrane-bound carriers of bioactive molecules, have emerged as important mediators of intercellular communication during both homeostasis and disease [31]. These nanostructures carry diverse cargo, including proteins, RNAs, and lipids, and are thought to contribute to the regulation of inflammation, fibrosis, and regeneration in dystrophic muscle. However, the precise and direct role and composition of muscle-derived EVs in DMD remain largely unexplored. The X-MET system, by enabling the study of isolated muscle tissue in a controlled environment, offers a unique opportunity to examine muscle-specific secretory activity and the potential contribution of EVs to disease progression. Thus, embedding the X-MET system into DMD research could lead to the identification of new therapeutic targets and shed light on the pathological mechanisms mediated by EVs.

Methods

Experimental model

Wild type (WT) C57Bl/6J mice and mdx4cv (MDX) mice, both 4 weeks old, were used for the experiments in the study, in accordance with Italian Law on the Protection of Animals. Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled (22 °C) room with a 12-h light/dark cycle. The research project was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the animal facilities of the National Institute of Health-Italy (No. 82945.56/2020-PR).

Generation of ex vivo muscle-engineered tissue (X-MET)

X-MET constructs were generated from wild type (WT) and mdx4cv (MDX) mice, a murine model of DMD, through mechanical and enzymatic dissociation of hindlimb muscles (Gastrocnemius (GN), Tibialis Anterior (TA), Soleus (S), Extensor Digitorum Longus (EDL), and Quadriceps), using the Skeletal Muscle Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), as previously described [23–25]. Following dissociation, cell suspensions were filtered using a 70-µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 15 min. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in Growth Medium (GM) composed of Dulbecco Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), 20% horse serum (HS), 3% chick embryo extract, 25 mM HEPES, 4 mM L-glutamine, 0.1% gentamicin, and penicillin/streptomycin. To enrich the myogenic population, cells underwent two sequential 30-minute pre-plating steps. Myoblast-enriched fractions were then seeded at a density of 40,000 cells/mL, a value defined by the same number of satellite cells for both wild type and MDX cultures, onto 35-mm tissue culture dishes pre-coated with type I collagen (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. After 5–6 days of culture, myoblasts were induced to differentiate using a Differentiation Medium (DM) composed of DMEM, 2% horse serum, 25 mM HEPES, 4 mM L-glutamine, 0.1% gentamicin, and penicillin/streptomycin. Once differentiated, the resulting monolayer of primary myotubes and non-myogenic cells was gently detached (delaminated) and transferred to silicone-coated culture dishes (Sylgard, Dow Corning, Midland, MI, USA). Constructs were then pinned using 0.20 mm stainless steel minutien pins (Austerlitz INSECT PINS®) to allow mechanical tension and tissue maturation. Approximately 15 X-METs were obtained from each mouse and randomly allocated to biomechanical, morphologic or molecular analyses. In total, 3–4 wild type and 3–4 mdx4cv mice were used per condition. For each experiment in the present study, a minimum of five different constructs derived from different mice were used to ensure a statistically robust analysis.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA from X-MET constructs was extracted using TriReagent™ (Sigma-Aldrich). The double-stranded cDNA was obtained following the retrotranscription of 1 µg of RNA per sample, using the QuantiTec Reverse Transcription Kit (QIAGEN). Relative quantitative PCR was performed on ABI PRISM 7500 SDS (Applied Biosystems, USA), using premade 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM)-labelled TaqMan assay, Myogenic Differentiation 1 (MyoD1), Myogenin (MyoG), Troponin T1 (Tnnt1), NADPH oxidase 2 (Nox2, also known as Cybb), Nitric oxide synthase-2 (Nos2), Glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (Gclc), Interleukin 1b (Il1β), Tumor necrosis alpha (Tnfα), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (Cxcl1), Integrin alpha M (Itgam, also known as CD11b), Arginase 1 (Arg1), Collagen type I alpha 1 chain (Col1a1), Perilipin 5 (Plin5), Adiponectin (Adipoq), Embryonic Myosin Heavy Chain (Myh3/EmbMHC). Hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (Hprt) expression was used as the endogenous control for normalization. To assess the expression of microRNAs (miR-1, miR-206, miR-133a, miR-21, miR-24, miR-31, and miR-34c), the extracted RNA was retrotranscribed using the microRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Relative quantitative PCR was performed on ABI PRISM 7500 SDS (Applied Biosystems) as described above, and the relative quantitative RT-PCR sample value was normalized for the expression of U6 snRNA.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescence analysis was conducted either on whole X-MET samples fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 h or 20 μm transverse cryosections of OCT-preserved X-MET, with sections fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min. Fixed samples were washed in PBS with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.2% Triton X-100, preincubated for 1 h in 10% goat or donkey serum at room temperature (RT), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: Myosin 4 Monoclonal Antibody (MF20) (Sigma-Aldrich), Embryonic Myosin Heavy Chain (Embryonic MHC) (Abcam), Myosin Heavy Chain 7 (MYH 7) (Hybridoma Bank) and Myosin Heavy Chain 2b (MYH 2b) (Hybridoma Bank), or with conjugated antibodies: alpha-actinin (α-ACTIN) (Mylteny Biotech), dihydroethidium (DHE) (Thermo Fisher), and phalloidin (Thermo Fisher). X-METs were then washed in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 and eventually incubated with a secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor, Life Technologies) for 45 min at RT. Nuclei were stained with HOECHST (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were captured with a Nikon DS-Fi1 camera (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) mounted on a Zeiss Axiovert optical microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For each experimental condition, three to five independent experiments were performed.

Histological analysis

X-MET samples embedded in OCT were sectioned at 8–10 μm and mounted on glass slides. Sections were air-dried for 10 min on ice and then fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min at RT. Then, the sample slides were stained following a standard Masson’s Trichrome protocol to identify areas of fibrosis. All analyses were performed using Zeiss Confocal software (Zen 3.0 Blue edition). For each experimental condition, three to five independent experiments were performed.

Measurements of engineered tissue contractility

We conducted several functional tests to assess the biomechanical properties of the X-MET model under different conditions, aiming to validate it as a suitable model to study the functional component of muscle wasting in vitro. We evaluated the frequency of the tissue’s spontaneous contraction by using an optical tracking algorithm developed in LabView 2019 (National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA) [32, 33]. Images of the X-MET models were acquired at 300 fps for 10 s every 24 h from delamination and for 7 consecutive days. In the post-processing analysis, the images were correlated by the algorithm in a region of interest (ROI) of 600 × 90 pixels placed in the center of the construct [34]. The spontaneous contraction frequency was then computed through the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The use of the FFT allowed obtaining the amplitude of the signal components at each analysed frequency, thus finding the main signal component that characterizes the tissue’s contraction. On the 7th day, we measured the spontaneous contraction force of the X-MET models and the force generated by the constructs during an electrical stimulation protocol constituted by one twitch test with a 1ms single pulse stimulation to measure the kinetic contractile parameters. For all tests, each X-MET was connected at one end to a microforce transducer (Kronex AE801) [25, 26, 35], while the opposite end was kept fixed. During the electrical stimulation protocol, two electrodes connected to a pulse stimulator (Aurora Scientific Inc. 701B) were positioned parallel to the construct. The specific spontaneous force was obtained by normalizing the measured force to the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the tissues, which was optically calculated using a custom Matlab software [32]. For the entire duration of the functional tests, the X-MET models were maintained at a temperature of 37 °C by using a temperature control plate (Okolab s.r.l., H401).

Evans blue dye

X-METs, pinned in a silicon-coated dish, were treated with 1% Evans Blue dye (EBD) (Sigma Aldrich) (w/v) in PBS (pH 7.5) filtered using Millex®-GP 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Evans Blue is a non-permeating dye, used for checking cell viability and plasma membrane damage. Images were obtained using an Axio Imager A2 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microimaging, Inc.) and processed by ZEN2 software (Blue Edition). EBD-derived fluorescence was evaluated through the ImageJ software on at least five fields chosen arbitrarily from each sample.

Atomic force microscopy

The elasticity measurements were conducted using a FlexAFM (Nanosurf, Liestal, CH), allocated onto an IX80 inverted optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, JPN), equipped with the probe holder to operate in a fluid environment and under controlled temperature (37 °C). The samples were maintained pinned into a petri dish covered with a layer of PDMS to hold the pins in position. During the entire measurement, the samples were not adhered to the PDMS surface. The measurements were carried out in physiological buffer by using MLCT-BIO tips from Bruker, with elastic constants (calibrated in air, with the thermal tune method before starting each experiment) that follow in the range 0.0065 ± 0.0005 N/m. Each sample has been measured by means of force volume maps of 16 × 16 force curves, at a speed of 3 force curves per second, in several different areas of 100 microns squared each. A prior manual selection (by visible inspection) of the force curves has been done by using homemade Matlab routines to exclude perturbing signals from the analysis. The stiffness calibration value (also called detector sensitivity) has been obtained from acquiring force curves on the same petri dish prior to the FC acquisitions. Thus, the force curves were analysed using the FC_analysis software, whose complete functioning is described in Dinarelli, Girasole, and Longo 2018 [36]. The Young’s modulus value was obtained by fitting the curves to a Sneddon-modified Hertzian model [37] by considering the Poisson ratio of the samples as 0.5 and a conical tip with an opening half-angle of 35°. The statistical and graphical elaborations have been carried out by using the software Origin (OriginLab, Northampton, MA).

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry analysis was performed after the initial phase of primary cell isolation and on wild type and MDX X-MET constructs at 7 days. Cells derived from wild type and mdx4cv mice or from X-MET constructs were filtered, blocked, and labelled with antibodies diluted to a final concentration of 0.005 mg/mL in PBS supplemented with 2% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). Samples were incubated at RT for 45 min with gentle intermittent shaking to ensure optimal binding. The following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies were used for staining: anti-mouse ITGA7-AF647 (AbLab), anti-mouse CD31-PECy7 (eBioscience), anti-mouse Sca1-FITC (Miltenyi Biotech), anti-mouse CD45-PE (BD Pharmingen), and anti-mouse PDGFR-BV510 (BD Pharmingen). After incubation, the samples were washed with 500 µL of 2% FBS/PBS and centrifuged at 600 × g for 15 min. The stained cells were then resuspended in 700 µL of 5% FBS/PBS containing SYTOX™ Blue Dead Cell Stain at a 1:2000 dilution. Finally, colour compensation was performed. FACS gating strategies benefited from Lanzure et al. [38]. A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired with a CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Milan, Italy) instrument equipped with three lasers (488 nm, 405 nm, and 638 nm) and 9 detectors. Cytometer quality control was assessed daily using CytoFLEX Daily QC Fluorospheres (Beckman Coulter). Data were collected using CytExpert 2.2 software (Beckman Coulter) [39]. FCS files were analyzed using CytExpert version 2.2 software.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA (> 30 µg) was extracted from MDX and wild type X-MET constructs at day 7 of differentiation for RNA sequencing analysis. As part of quality control, RNA concentration was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), while RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm the absence of degradation. RNA was sent to the Institute of Applied Genomics (Udine, Italy) for deep mRNA sequencing. cDNA libraries were processed according to the standard Illumina protocol and sequenced with the HiSeq2500 (4-plex run, 1 × 50 bp reads, about 30 M reads/sample). Reads were aligned to the UCSC mm10 version of the mouse genome using Tophat2 (v2.1.1), quantified with HTSeq-count (v0.5.4p5). Differential expression analysis was performed in R (v3.5.1) using DESeq2 (v1.20.0). Genes were considered differentially expressed with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05.

Isolation of Extracellular Vesicles (EVs) from X-MET Culture Media

EVs were isolated from the culture media of X-MET constructs using the Miltenyi Pan EV Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with minor modifications to optimize yield and purity for our specific samples. X-MET constructs were incubated for 48 h in an EV-free differentiation medium to ensure that the collected media were devoid of contaminating EVs from the serum or other supplements. The conditioned media were collected and centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min to remove cell debris and large apoptotic bodies. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 2000 g for 20 min to eliminate remaining cell debris and larger vesicles. The pre-cleared supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to further ensure the removal of any remaining larger particles. The isolated EVs were aliquoted and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analyses, ensuring minimal freeze-thaw cycles to maintain their integrity.

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance was determined by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA, and unpaired t-test with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between groups were determined by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, Student T-Test and Bonferroni’s test. Asterisks denote statistically significant differences. Statistical significance was set to a P value < 0.05.

Results

MDX X-MET derived from dystrophic progenitors display progressive cell and functional impairment

To model Duchenne muscular dystrophy in a physiologically structured in vitro context, we generated three-dimensional muscle constructs using the X-MET system [23]. Primary skeletal muscle cells were isolated from hindlimb muscles of wild type (WT) and mdx4cv (MDX) mice at approximately one month of age, a stage characterized by active regeneration in dystrophic muscle. To assess the cellular composition of the starting populations, we performed fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis. MDX-derived cell preparations contained a significantly higher proportion of lineage-negative (CD45⁻/CD31⁻) cells expressing Sca-1 compared to wild type samples (6.93% vs. 3.83%) (Fig. 1A). This increase is consistent with an expansion of the fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) population (4,46% wild type, 7,92% MDX), as validated by PDGFRpos expression (Fig. 1A), thereby supporting the presence of an enlarged stromal compartment in MDX-derived muscles compared to wild type controls [38]. Similarly, the proportion of Sca-1⁻/ITGA7⁺ satellite cells, the principal myogenic progenitors [40], was also elevated in MDX samples (50.56%) relative to wild type (40.11%) (Fig. 1A). This cellular profile reflects the dystrophic muscle’s increased regenerative activity and a dysregulated repair environment at the time of isolation, indicating that MDX-derived cells are seeded with a pathologically primed cellular milieu influenced by the in vivo microenvironment.

These cells were used to generate dystrophic X-METs, whose morphological, molecular, and functional properties were compared with those generated from wild type mice [25].

To determine in vitro time points corresponding to stages of pathological progression observed in vivo, we conducted a time-course analysis of X-MET contractile behaviour every 24 h from delamination (3D construct formation). Dystrophic constructs initially exhibited elevated spectral amplitude in the Fourier Transform analysis (Fig. 1B), suggesting a higher rhythmicity in contraction, while wild type X-METs showed a gradual remodelling and a steady increase in spectral amplitude, which stabilized around 1 m/m² RMS amplitude and persisted for up to 20 days, as previously reported [23]. In contrast, MDX X-METs initiated contractions within 24 h of delamination, reaching 0.5 m/m² RMS amplitude on day 1, but rapidly lost rhythmic contractile activity by day 7. Thereafter, MDX X-MET constructs underwent progressive degenerative changes, characterized by a pronounced decline in contractile properties. These alterations impaired tissue integrity and reduced the reliability of analyses at later time points (e.g., day 20).

To characterize the cell composition of the constructs at an early stage of functional divergence, we performed FACS analysis on wild type and MDX X-METs after 7 days in culture (Fig. 1C). MDX exhibited a marked increase in CD45⁻/SCA1⁺/PDGFR⁺ cells (62.99%) compared with wild type controls (0.12%). Consistent with previous reports, this substantial expansion of the FAP compartment suggests that the dystrophic microenvironment actively promotes sustained FAP proliferation during construct maturation, thereby recapitulating key aspects of disease progression [38]. Moreover, Sca-1⁻/ITGA7⁺ satellite cells were detectable in both groups but appeared reduced in MDX X-METs compared to wild type X-METs (1,94% in MDX X-METs vs. 2,88% in WT X-METs), consistent with an impaired myogenic compartment within the dystrophic environment. Collectively, these findings indicate that the MDX X-MET in vitro model not only reproduces hallmark dystrophic features but also progressively mirrors the functional and cellular alterations characteristic of in vivo disease progression. In addition, these findings establish day 7 as a robust time point at which dystrophic features are recapitulated in vitro, capturing both cell dysregulation and functional decline.

Molecular and morphological deficits in MDX X-METs recapitulate key features of dystrophic muscle

To define the pathological features of MDX X-MET constructs at day 7, a stage that marks functional decline and mirrors disease progression, we conducted comprehensive molecular and morphological analyses. Gene expression profiling (Fig. 2A) confirmed, as expected, the complete absence of DMD transcripts, reflecting the underlying genetic deficiency. Interestingly, by day 7, the expression of early myogenic regulators (MyoD1, MyoG) and structural genes (Myh4, Tnnt1, Myh7) markers was significantly downregulated compared to wild type (Fig. 2A), coinciding with functional loss and defective assembly of contractile proteins required for fast (Myh4), slow (Myh7), and regulatory (Tnnt1) fiber function. Immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 2B) corroborated the transcriptional findings. Specifically, MDX constructs exhibited a marked reduction in MYH 2b and MYH 7 positive fibers compared to wild type ones (Fig. 2B-C). Although both fast- and slow-myosin fibers were decreased in MDX X-METs, slow-myosin fibers were relatively more preserved than fast-myosin fibers, indicating a selective susceptibility of fast-twitch fibers in the dystrophic context. These results are consistent with previous reports in MDX muscle, where fast-twitch fibers are preferentially affected, leading to a relative enrichment of slow/oxidative fibers [41, 42]. Furthermore, these molecular and morphological changes coincide with the reductions observed at this stage in both whole MDX X-MET and single-fiber cross-sectional area. Qualitative analysis of F-actin-stained constructs (Fig. 2D) suggested an overall reduction in construct size and fiber density in MDX X-MET construct compared to wild type 3D system. These observations were quantitatively confirmed by the immunofluorescence staining for MF20 and laminin (Fig. 2E), which revealed a marked reduction in both the overall size of the constructs and the cross-sectional area of individual MF20-positive fibers (Fig. 2F–G). Taken together, these results show that dystrophic MDX constructs are unable to sustain proper fiber maturation and structural integrity, exhibiting marked defects at both the tissue and individual fiber levels that recapitulate hallmark features of dystrophic muscle pathology.

Dystrophic X-METs recapitulate the cascade of primary degenerative events in dystrophic muscle

To assess whether dystrophic constructs reproduce early pathogenic events, we first examined sarcolemma integrity. Evans Blue Dye (EBD) uptake was markedly increased in MDX X-METs compared to wild type, consistent with the membrane fragility and increased permeability that initiates pathology in DMD (Fig. 3A and C). These microtears in the sarcolemma facilitate intracellular calcium influx, a pathological driver of muscle degeneration in DMD [8]. Indeed, semiquantitative analysis using Indo-1 AM revealed significantly elevated cytosolic calcium concentrations in dystrophic constructs (Fig. 3C, right graph), supporting the presence of calcium overload at the cellular level, which might explain the enhanced contraction in the early stages of our in vitro dystrophic system (Fig. 1B). This was associated with signs of oxidative stress, as indicated by intense DHE staining (Fig. 3B and D) increased expression of the NADPH oxidase subunit Cybb (Nox2) and Nos2, and a concomitant compensatory upregulation of the antioxidant enzyme Gclc (Fig. 3E). These changes resemble the in vivo cascade, where Ca²⁺ overload and Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) production occur immediately downstream of membrane instability. Along with causing oxidative damage, Nox2-derived ROS also trigger pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, which in turn promote stress-responsive cytokines [9]. We thus examined, by qPCR, the expression of cytokines and myeloid-associated transcripts. Dystrophic constructs showed increased levels of Il1β, Tnfα, Cxcl1, Itgam (Cd11b), and Arg1, pointing to the emergence of an inflammatory-like profile despite the absence of systemic immune infiltration (Fig. 3E). To provide a more comprehensive molecular framework for MDX X-MET, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing on both wild type and MDX X-METs on day 7 (Fig. 3F). The transcriptomic profile confirmed the presence of key dystrophic signatures, including enrichment in pathways related to oxidative stress, cytoskeletal remodelling, energy metabolism, and muscle regeneration (Fig. S1), with the major significantly enriched category involving inflammatory signalling events (Fig. 3F). Although no systemic immune recruitment occurs in this in vitro model, the transcriptional profile likely reflects the activity of resident CD45⁺CD11b⁺ populations (Fig. S2), indicating that intrinsic oxidative stress is sufficient to drive an inflammatory-like activation in dystrophic skeletal muscle, suggesting that muscle-resident cells intrinsically modulate stress and injury responses, including the activation of resident monocytes. Together, these results demonstrate that dystrophic constructs capture a sequence of early events characteristic of DMD muscle in vivo, where membrane fragility initiates oxidative stress that in turn promotes an inflammatory environment [8, 9].

Regeneration and fibrotic remodelling in dystrophic constructs

Having established that dystrophic constructs recapitulate early stress responses, we next examined whether they also display the maladaptive regenerative and fibrotic remodelling that drives DMD progression. The X-MET system retains a pool of satellite cells that contribute to tissue homeostasis, offering a platform to study their dynamics in vitro (Fig. 1C) [23]. In MDX constructs, we observed an early upregulation of embryonic Myosin Heavy Chain (eMHC), accompanied by increased Myh3 gene expression, indicative of a transient activation of regenerative programs in response to initial damage (Fig. 4A-C). This was followed by a rapid depletion of the progenitor pool (Fig. 1A and C), reflecting the progressive regenerative collapse characteristic of dystrophic muscle in vivo [43]. Concomitantly, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed increased fibrotic deposition in MDX X-METs compared to wild type (Fig. 4D and F). Fibrosis was further confirmed at the molecular level by upregulated expression of fibro-adipogenic markers Tgfβ1 (Fig. 4E), a master regulator of fibrosis, and Col1a1, a principal extracellular matrix component, along with upregulation of Plin5 and an upward trend in Adipoq (Fig. 4G), suggesting early fibro-adipogenic skewing. This fibro-adipogenic drift further supports the notion that non-myogenic cells in the construct undergo fibro-fatty conversion under dystrophic conditions, contributing to extracellular matrix remodelling and tissue stiffening. These features underscore the model’s capacity to mirror the shift from regeneration to fibrosis typical of late-stage DMD. Thus, MDX X-METs not only model the early membrane stress cascade but also capture its downstream consequences on the balance between regeneration and fibrosis, providing an integrated in vitro representation of dystrophic pathology and a robust and controllable platform for dissecting muscle-intrinsic mechanisms of DMD pathogenesis and for evaluating interventions aimed at stabilizing or restoring muscle homeostasis.

3D X-METs provide a direct measure of dystrophic muscle contractility

Mechanical instability and impaired force generation are hallmark features of DMD, driven by the inability of dystrophic muscle to withstand stress associated with contraction [44]. To evaluate whether MDX X-MET recapitulates these deficits, we performed functional and biomechanical assessments at day 7, when pathological phenotypes are most pronounced. We first measured the spontaneous contraction force and the kinetic contractile parameters during a twitch test. Compared to wild type constructs, MDX X-METs generated lower peak-to-peak forces on average (0.32 vs. 1.50 mN) (Fig. 5D and A) and exhibited a reduction in contraction frequency (0.72 vs. 0.84 Hz), consistent with compromised contractile output. Dystrophic X-METs also showed a higher time-to-peak value compared to wild type (317 ms vs. 86 ms) and delayed half-relaxation time (480 ms vs. 120 ms), indicative of impaired excitation-contraction (E-C) coupling (Fig. 5E and B). These functional impairments closely mirror those observed in vivo in dystrophic muscle, where sarcolemma fragility and aberrant calcium dynamics contribute to reduced force production and delayed relaxation [45]. To further probe biomechanical alterations, we assessed tissue elasticity using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM). MDX X-METs displayed a markedly lower Young’s modulus (0.036 ± 0.09 kPa) (Fig. 5F) compared to wild type constructs (3.7 ± 2.3 kPa) (Fig. 5C), reflecting a pronounced decrease in stiffness. This mechanical softening is consistent with previously reported values in dystrophic tissue [46] and suggests a loss of structural integrity. Such changes likely arise from combined deficits in the extracellular matrix (ECM), cytoskeletal architecture, and myofiber organization. Importantly, functional readouts such as force generation, contraction dynamics, and elasticity represent key outcome measures when evaluating therapeutic efficacy in vitro. These provide a direct window into the physiological performance of engineered tissues and offer a robust, quantifiable endpoint to assess whether candidate interventions improve muscle strength, coordination, and structural resilience. Together, these results confirm that dystrophic X-METs functionally and mechanically recapitulate core features of DMD pathology, underscoring the utility of this system for translational applications in a controlled in vitro environment.

X-MET enables molecular and functional profiling of extracellular vesicles in dystrophic muscle

Skeletal muscle regeneration following injury depends on tightly regulated intercellular communication involving proteins, cytokines, lipids, and non-coding RNAs. A growing body of evidence supports the role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in orchestrating these signals during both homeostasis and disease [47–49]. Leveraging the physiological relevance of the X-MET system, we analysed EVs directly secreted by healthy and dystrophic 3D muscle tissues under identical culture conditions. EVs were isolated from the culture media of wild type and MDX X-METs using a standardized magnetic bead-based protocol (Miltenyi Pan-EV kit, Miltenyi Biotech), and their size distribution was measured by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis. Both populations fell within the typical size range for exosomes (WT: 84.8 ± 16.4 nm; MDX: 80.4 ± 22.4 nm) (Fig. 6A) [50]. The concentration of EVs released by MDX X-METs was significantly higher (1.62E + 10 vs. 1.09E + 10 EVs/mL; Fig. 6A), consistent with in vivo observations in dystrophic muscle [51]. This heightened release suggests that dystrophic myofibers actively engage in paracrine signalling, potentially contributing to disease propagation. Notably, inhibition of EV release has been shown to attenuate muscle damage in vivo [52], further underscoring their implication in pathogenesis.

Thus, to explore the molecular cargo of dystrophic EVs, we performed qRT-PCR analysis for key muscle-enriched miRNAs (myoMiRs), known to regulate regeneration and fibrosis. EVs isolated from MDX X-METs exhibited higher levels of miR-206, miR-133a, miR-1, and miR-34c, while miR-29c was markedly downregulated compared to wild-type-derived EVs (Fig. 6B). These data confirm the in vivo reports linking myoMiR dysregulation to impaired satellite cell dynamics, ECM remodelling, and excessive autophagy [53, 54]. Moreover, we observed upregulation of miR-223, which is associated with the inflammatory response and macrophage polarization [51]. Notably, EVs isolated from whole hindlimb muscles showed a similar overall miRNA expression pattern, with wild type and MDX EVs displaying distinct cargo profiles closely mirroring those of X-MET–derived EVs (Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings indicate that the X-MET 3D model faithfully recapitulates disease-associated alterations in muscle EV miRNA cargo, providing a robust, physiologically relevant platform for directly interrogating muscle-derived EV signaling under pathological conditions. To functionally assess the impact of dystrophic EVs, we treated wild type X-METs with MDX-derived EVs (1 × 10⁸ particles/mL), added from the first day of culture and replenished at each media change. Confocal imaging confirmed rapid uptake of fluorescently labelled EVs within the cytoplasm of treated X-METs (Fig. 6C). We then evaluated both molecular and structural outcomes in dystrophic EVs-conditioned wild type X-MET (X-MET WT + EVs MDX) structure. After 7 days, these X-METs exhibited increased expression of embryonic myosin heavy chain (eMHC), confirmed by staining and relative quantification (Fig. 6D and E), indicative of the onset of regeneration. No significant increases in fibrotic index were observed in the X-MET WT + EVs MDX compared to control WT X-MET (Fig. 6F, compared with Fig. 4D). In line with a regenerative response, Myh7 and Myh4 expression were preserved, while Utrn levels were upregulated (Fig. 6G), likely reflecting a compensatory program driven by the miRNA cargo of MDX EVs. This supports the idea that EVs can promote myogenic activation and remodelling, even though the major loss of myosin content in dystrophic constructs is primarily linked to intrinsic mechanical fragility rather than paracrine signalling. However, treated wild type X-METs also showed elevated Lc3 expression (Fig. 6G), suggesting autophagy activation, possibly driven by decreased miR-29c in MDX EVs, which normally suppresses lysosomal protease expression and modulates fibrosis [55]. These dual effects, activation of both regenerative and catabolic pathways, highlight the complex signalling conveyed by dystrophic muscle-derived EVs. Together, our findings demonstrate that the X-MET system not only enables the isolation and profiling of disease-relevant EVs but also allows mechanistic dissection of their functional consequences in a reductionist yet physiologically relevant setting. Thus, given the emerging role of EVs as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets, this in vitro system offers a valuable tool for translational investigation.

To assess whether dystrophic EVs alter the contractile behaviour of healthy skeletal muscle tissue, we measured the spontaneous contractions in wild type X-METs treated with MDX-derived EVs. Untreated wild type X-METs displayed, as expected [23–25], a rhythmic contraction, reflective of coordinated excitation-contraction (EC) coupling. In contrast, EV-conditioned X-METs showed a lower peak-to-peak amplitude of the contraction force, on average, and a loss of rhythmic contractile activity (Fig. 7A-C). These changes suggest a partial impairment of EC coupling, potentially resulting from the pathologic impact of dystrophic EVs and their cargo composition. Further analysis will be necessary to fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying this contractile dysregulation. Nonetheless, this finding underscores the capacity of the X-MET system to reveal subtle functional phenotypes resulting from pathological extracellular cues.

Discussion

In this study, we established and validated a scaffold-free, three-dimensional skeletal muscle system (X-MET) as an in vitro system to model key features of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). By employing primary cells derived from dystrophic mdx4cv mice, we successfully reproduced key pathological features of dystrophic muscle, including impaired myogenic differentiation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling, and progressive disruption of contractile activity. Compared to a conventional culture system, the X-MET system offers several advantages, including the morphological complexity typical of skeletal muscle and the possibility of monitoring contractile properties in a 3D in vitro system. Notably, we demonstrated that this model preserves functional outputs, including spontaneous and stimulated contractions, thereby allowing molecular and structural defects to be linked to physiological consequences. By enabling controlled manipulation of environmental variables and cellular components, this 3D muscle system holds promise for mechanistic studies of disease progression and for evaluating candidate therapies in a reductionist yet biologically meaningful setting, making it well-suited for therapeutic testing and functional phenotyping.

In particular, the ability of X-METs to maintain endogenous regenerative potential and cellular heterogeneity allowed the identification of early hallmarks of dystrophic progression. Consistent with the regenerative state of juvenile dystrophic muscle in vivo [29, 56], MDX-derived X-METs exhibited elevated expression of embryonic myosin heavy chain (eMHC), increased numbers of FAPs, and persistent cytoskeletal disorganization. These features point to a dysregulated attempt at repair, consistent with reports of asynchronous regeneration in DMD [57]. The modest yet detectable increase in ECM deposition, captured histologically and by gene expression analyses, further supports X-MET’s capacity to uniquely reflect early fibrotic cues that accompany repeated cycles of degeneration and regeneration [58].

The major advantage of the X-MET model lies in its functional fidelity. X-METs generated contractile activity without external scaffolds or artificial patterning, offering an experimentally accessible readout of muscle performance. Functional assessment revealed that MDX X-METs, as well as wild type X-MET treated with dystrophic extracellular vesicles (EVs), displayed abnormal contraction dynamics, specifically, irregular and lower-amplitude contractions, suggesting defective excitation–contraction (EC) coupling. These alterations precede overt histological degeneration and thus serve as sensitive indicators of early dysfunction. Importantly, functional outputs, such as contraction frequency and rhythm, are key parameters for evaluating therapeutic efficacy, as force restoration remains the goal of DMD treatment [59, 60].

Beyond its structural and contractile phenotypes, this study highlights the model’s potential to interrogate non-cell-autonomous mechanisms driving disease progression. We focused on the paracrine effects of skeletal muscle-derived EVs, which are increasingly recognized as modulators of intercellular communication in dystrophic muscle [49]. Consistent with in vivo findings [49], we observed significantly higher EV release from MDX X-METs. These vesicles were enriched in muscle-specific microRNAs (myoMiRs) such as miR-206, miR-1, and miR-133a, factors implicated in myogenesis and pathological remodelling [51, 61], and exhibited a downregulation of miR-29c, a known repressor of proteolytic enzymes [54]. Notably, miRNAs such as miR-1, miR-206, and miR-133 can target Ca²⁺-handling proteins, thereby influencing muscle contractility and calcium homeostasis [62, 63]. The up-regulation of the inflammatory response-relevant miR-223 suggests that dystrophic muscle activates an endogenous compensatory mechanism to interfere with inflammatory cytokines that, in in vivo models, are recruited by the circulation, colonizing the dystrophic muscle, and contributing to further damage to the muscle.

Exposure of wild type X-METs to MDX-derived EVs induced a dual cellular response: upregulation of regenerative markers, such as eMHC, alongside increased expression of autophagy-related genes (Lc3, Ctsl) and loss of rhythmic contraction. This complex outcome suggests that EVs simultaneously carry compensatory and deleterious signals, a finding in line with previous studies showing context-dependent effects of dystrophic secretome [52]. Importantly, conditioned wild type X-METs showed limited fibrotic changes and were able to counteract EV-induced damage. These results underline the suitability of X-MET for studying early, reversible phases of DMD pathology, as well as for parsing out the contribution of muscle-intrinsic secretory pathways to disease evolution.

Despite its advantages, the X-MET system presents certain limitations. While the mdx4cv model recapitulates aspects of DMD such as chronic regeneration and altered secretome composition, it does not fully reproduce the severity of human disease. The EV characterization was also restricted to a small panel of miRNAs; integrating proteomic and lipidomic analyses will be crucial to fully elucidate the functional cargo and signalling effects of muscle-derived EVs. Finally, while our model allows for the study of muscle-intrinsic degeneration and regeneration, the absence of immune cell recruitment limits our ability to fully capture the inflammatory environment that shapes DMD pathology. At present, it remains unclear how inflammatory processes manifest or evolve in this in vitro context, and this represents an important direction for future characterization [29, 64]. Nevertheless, the X-MET system revealed that dystrophic skeletal muscle can intrinsically activate a compensatory mechanism by stimulating satellite cells and other endogenous cell types, including resident macrophages, to counteract muscle degeneration and promote regenerative processes. However, regeneration remains impaired in dystrophic X-MET, underscoring the model’s ability to reproduce critical aspects of in vivo dystrophic pathology. Furthermore, our study strengthens the evidence that muscle fibers produce, express, and release cytokines, miRNA, and other myokines, which exert autocrine, paracrine, or endocrine effects.

In conclusion, we present a robust, functional, and experimentally tractable 3D model of DMD using scaffold-free X-MET constructs. This system captures key features of dystrophic pathology, including aberrant regeneration, early fibrotic remodelling, and functional deterioration. Furthermore, it enables mechanistic investigations of muscle-derived EVs, revealing their capacity to propagate both regenerative and catabolic signals. These insights lay the foundation for using X-METs as a screening tool for candidate therapeutics, particularly those aimed at restoring functional output and modulating the dystrophic secretome. By bridging the gap between reductionist cell culture and complex in vivo models, X-METs represent a valuable addition to the preclinical landscape of DMD research.
