Limit Theorems for the Volume of Random Projections and Sections of ℓ p N \ell_{p}^{N} -balls
Joscha Prochno, Christoph Thäle, Philipp Tuchel

Abstract
Let 𝔹 p N \mathbb{B}_{p}^{N} be the N N -dimensional unit ball corresponding to the ℓ p \ell_{p} -norm. For each N ∈ ℕ N\in\mathbb{N} we sample a uniform random subspace E N E_{N} of fixed dimension m ∈ ℕ m\in\mathbb{N} and consider the volume of 𝔹 p N \mathbb{B}_{p}^{N} projected onto E N E_{N} or intersected with E N E_{N} . We also consider geometric quantities other than the volume such as the intrinsic volumes or the dual volumes. In this setting we prove central limit theorems, moderate deviation principles, and large deviation principles as N → ∞ N\to\infty . Our results provide a complete asymptotic picture. In particular, they generalize and complement a result of Paouris, Pivovarov, and Zinn [A central limit theorem for projections of the cube, Probab. Theory Related Fields. 159 (2014), 701-719] and another result of Adamczak, Pivovarov, and Simanjuntak [Limit theorems for the volumes of small codimensional random sections of ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} -balls, Ann. Probab. 52 (2024), 93-126]. Keywords . Asymptotic theory of convex bodies, central limit theorem, high-dimensional probability, functional limit theorem, large deviation principle, ℓ p N \ell_{p}^{N} -ball, moderate deviation principle, random projection, random section, stochastic geometry MSC . Primary 52A23, 60F05, 60F10; Secondary 46B09, 52A22, 60D05.

The motivation for the present paper stems at first from a result obtained by Paouris, Pivovarov, and Zinn who proved the following central limit theorem in
[
66
]
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
let
{\mathbb{B}}_{\infty}^{N}=[-1,1]^{N}
be the unit
N
-dimensional cube and assume that
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\leq N
. Denote by
\mathbb{G}_{m,N}
the Grassmannian manifold of
m
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which is equipped with the Haar probability measure
\mu_{m,N}
, and suppose that
(E_{N})_{N\geq m}
is a sequence of independent random subspaces such that
E_{N}\in\mathbb{G}_{m,N}
has distribution
\mu_{m,N}
. Define for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
a random variable

Z_{N}:=\mathrm{vol}_{m}\big({\mathbb{B}}_{\infty}^{N}\big|E_{N}\big),

where
{\mathbb{B}}_{\infty}^{N}\big|E_{N}
denotes the orthogonal projection of
B_{\infty}^{N}
onto the linear subspace
E_{N}
. Then

\frac{Z_{N}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[Z_{N}]}}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\textup{d}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{d}}}{{\rightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution. At its core, the proof is based on the Gaussian projection formula

\mathrm{vol}_{m}(G{\mathbb{B}}_{\infty}^{N})=\det(GG^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{vol}_{m}\big({\mathbb{B}}_{\infty}^{N}\big|E_{N}\big)

with a standard Gaussian matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
having independent columns
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
(
G^{*}
denotes the transpose of
G
) and the fact that
GB_{\infty}^{N}
is a random zonotope (i.e., the Minkowski sum of the random line segments
[-g_{i},g_{i}]
). The latter allows a representation of the volume of the Gaussian projection essentially in terms of a
U
-statistic (or a special case of Minkowski’s theorem on mixed volumes of convex sets, to put it in convex geometry parlance), which assures that one can apply Vitale’s central limit theorem for Minkowski sums of random convex sets
[
78
]
; one should note, however, that both the Gaussian projection as well as the determinantal part contribute to the asymptotic normality, which requires a careful and delicate analysis (we refer to
[
66
]
for more information).

Another motivation for this paper comes from the work of Adamczak, Pivovarov, and Simanjuntak
[
2
]
. In contrast to what has been discussed before, these authors study for general
\ell_{p}
-balls in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
0<p\leq\infty
limit theorems for volumes of random hyperplane sections; see Section
2

{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{N}
denotes the
\ell_{p}
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and if
(H_{N})_{N\geq 1}
is a sequence of independent random elements of the Grassmannian
\mathbb{G}_{N-1,N}
of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, they show that there is a sequence
(c_{N})_{N\geq 1}
such that

N^{3/2}\Bigg(\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{N-1}({\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{N}\cap H_{N})}{\mathrm{vol}_{N-1}({\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{N-1})}-c_{N}\Bigg)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\textup{d}}\xi

where
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{p}^{2})
and both,
(c_{N})_{N\geq 1}
as well as
\sigma_{p}^{2}
, are given explicitly (see
[
2
, Theorem 3.4]
). A similar result is also obtained for the volumes of the sections with a random subspace of a fixed codimension
m\in\mathbb{N}
if
0<p<2
(see
[
2

). The proof of this result is based on probabilistic formulas for volumes of sections of convex bodies developed by Nayar and Tkocz
[
64
]
and Chasapis, Nayar, and Tkocz
[
14
]
. They in turn allow a representation of the volume of
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{N}\cap H_{n}
in terms of determinants involving auxiliary random variables. Together with a novel reverse Hölder-type inequality, this eventually paves once again the way to applying limit theorems for classical U-statistics.

Its is immediate from the discussion above that the proof in
[
66
]
is tailored for the
\ell_{\infty}
-structure of the cube and cannot be used to obtain a similar central limit result for general
\ell_{p}
-balls. Similarly, it seems that the approach taken in
[
2
]
cannot be modified to deal with random sections of fixed dimension, for example. In particular, complementing the central limit theorem for the projections of the cube (and more generally for
\ell_{p}
-balls) by moderate and large deviation principles for random projections and sections requires a completely different approach with different tools. The aim of this paper is to provide a complete picture in a 4-fold way:

(i)

We consider simultaneously random projections and random sections with random linear subspaces of fixed dimension.

(ii)

We cover the full range of limit theorems from the central limit perspective over moderate deviation principles to large deviation principles.

(iii)

We study limit theorems for random sections and projections of the general class of
\ell_{p}^{N}
-balls.

(iv)

We investigate general geometric functionals of random sections and projections, which includes the volume as a special case. As some of our functionals are related to convex bodies, the convexity of the sections and projections is relevant. This explains the restriction to
p\geq 1
as opposed to
p>0
in
[
2
]
.

2.2
Functional results

The main theorems presented in the previous section are, in fact, corollaries of more general theorems that we prove. To see how they relate to the main results for the volume, we refer to Section
4.7
.

In order to state the results, we require some additional notation. We shall denote by
M^{*}
the transpose of a matrix
M
. For
N\geq m
the Stiefel manifold

\mathbb{V}_{m,N}:=\Big\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}:MM^{*}=\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Big\}

carries a unique Haar measure, also called uniform distribution, denoted by
\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
which is invariant under left and right multiplication with elements from the orthogonal group. Further, elements from the Stiefel manifold correspond, up to an orthogonal transformation, to an orthogonal projection (see for instance
[
65
, Proposition 2 and its proof]
). In more detail, we have
VC=V{\big|_{E}}(C\big|E)
, where
E=\operatorname{Range}(V^{*})
. Then, if
x\in E
, we can write
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}
, where
v_{i}
are the orthonormal rows of
V
. Observe
V{\big|_{E}}x=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
and hence
V{\big|_{E}}
can interpreted as a function that maps the orthonormal basis
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
onto the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In particular, for any convex body
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have that

\mathrm{vol}_{m}(VC)\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{vol}_{m}(C\big|E),

where
V\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
and
E\sim\mu_{m,N}
. The support function of a convex body
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is given by

h(C,u):=\sup_{x\in C}\langle x,u\rangle,\hskip 10.00002ptu\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}.

If
0\in C
, then the function
\rho(C,\,\cdot\,):{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\to[0,\infty)
,

\rho(C,u):=\sup\big\{r\in[0,\infty)\,:\,ru\in C\big\}

is called the radial function of
C
. Both, the support and radial function, determine the convex body
C
uniquely. The random functional sequences in the following theorems are transformations of radial or support functions of the projected or intersected
\ell_{p}^{N}
-balls; see Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
.
Let us also recall the following representation for the volume of a convex body in terms of its support function, which can be found, e.g., in
[
74
, p. 151]
. It says that for any convex body
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
0\in C
,

\mathrm{vol}_{m}(C)=\kappa_{m}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\rho(C,u)^{m}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u),

(3)

where we write
\sigma:=\sigma_{m-1}
for the normalized spherical Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
.

We start with the following invariance principle dealing with the weak convergence of random continuous functions on
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
that are sums over functions of columns of the Stiefel manifold. In the following
{}_{2}F_{1}
denotes the Gaussian hypergeometric function (see
[
1
, Chapter 15]
) and for random variables
\xi,\xi_{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, taking values in some metric space, we shall write
\xi_{N}\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\xi
or
\xi_{N}\xrightarrow{d}\xi
to denote weak convergence of the corresponding distributions. Moreover, let us recall that
G(u)
,
u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
, is said to be a Gaussian process indexed by
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
if and only if for any finite collection
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
,
(G(u_{1}),\dots,G(u_{k}))
is a multivariate Gaussian random variable.

Theorem 2.2

(Functional CLT for sums over the Stiefel manifold)
.

Let
V_{N}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
be uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
for each
N\geq m
. Let
q\in[1,\infty)\setminus\{2\}
. Then we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigg(\Big|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle\Big|^{q}-\mathbb{E}\Big[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle|^{q}\Big]\Bigg)\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}Z(\,\cdot\,),

where
the random variables take values in
(C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
, and
Z
is a centered Gaussian process indexed by
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
whose covariance structure is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z(u)Z(v)]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}\Big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}v_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2}+q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}u_{k}u_{\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}}{\pi}{}_{2}F_{1}(-q/2,-q/2,1/2,\langle u,v\rangle^{2})-\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}}{\pi}\Big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}v_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2}+q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}u_{k}u_{\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}\Big)

for
u,v\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
with
u\not=v
, and

\mathbb{E}[Z(u)^{2}]=\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+q)}{\sqrt{\pi}}-\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}}{\pi}\Big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}^{4}+q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}u_{k}^{2}u_{\ell}^{2}\Big)

for
u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
.
The same holds true if we replace
\mathbb{E}[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle|^{q}]
in the process by its asymptotic equivalent
\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]
, where
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

We also state the corresponding functional MDP and LDP results.

Theorem 2.3

(Functional MDP for sums over the Stiefel manifold)
.

Let
V_{N}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
be uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
for each
N\geq m
. Let
q\in[1,2)
. Let
\beta_{N}
be a sequence of real numbers with
\beta_{N}\to\infty
and
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. Then the sequence

\Bigg(\frac{1}{\beta_{N}\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigg(\Big|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle\Big|^{q}-\mathbb{E}\Big[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle|^{q}\Big]\Bigg)\Bigg)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies an MDP with speed
\beta_{N}^{2}
on
(C(\mathbb{S}^{m-1}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and good rate function given in Remark
4.12
. This remains true if we replace
\mathbb{E}[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle|^{q}]
in the process with
\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}],g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Theorem 2.4

(Functional LDP for sums over the Stiefel manifold)
.

Let
V_{N}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
be uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
for every
N\geq m
. Then, for every
q\in[1,2)
, the sequence

\Bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle\Big|^{q}\Bigg)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies an LDP with speed
N
on
(C(\mathbb{S}^{m-1}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
and rate function
I^{\prime}_{LDP}:(C(\mathbb{S}^{m-1}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})\to[0,\infty]
given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{LDP}}^{\prime}(f)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\,\inf\Big\{I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}(\mu)\,:\,(f^{q}(u_{1}),\ldots,f^{q}(u_{k}))=F_{k}(\mu)\Big\},

where
I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}:\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to[0,\infty]
is the strictly convex rate function

I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}(\nu):=\begin{cases}H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes m})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Trace}\big(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\mathscr{C}(\nu)\big)&:\quad\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\mathscr{C}(\nu)\text{ is positive semidefinite },\\
+\infty&:\quad\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

The following provides an interpretation for all of the above convergence results. Let
E_{N}\in\mathbb{G}_{m,N}
be distributed according to the uniform distribution
\mu_{m,N}
and let
\mathbb{B}_{2}^{m}(E_{N})
be the
m
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball in

. Then we have the following almost sure convergence in the Hausdorff distance (for background information on the space of convex bodies and the notion of Hausdorff distance, we refer to
[
73
, Chapter 1.8]

\displaystyle d_{H}\Big(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\big|E_{N}),\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2q}}}\Gamma\big(\frac{q+1}{2}\big)^{1/q}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{m}(E_{N})\Big)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0

(4)

for
p\in(1,\infty]
and

\displaystyle d_{H}\Big(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap E_{N}),\frac{\pi^{\frac{1}{2p}}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{p+1}{2})^{1/p}}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{m}(E_{N})\Big)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,

(5)

for

. These limit theorems show that projections and sections of appropriately rescaled
\ell_{p}^{N}
-balls in high dimensions are close to Euclidean balls of appropriate radii within the corresponding subspaces. The result for the volume of sections was already shown in
[
56
, Section 5]
and the case of projections and
p=\infty
can be found in
[
45
, Theorem 1.1]
. This convergence can also be deduced using the Dvoretzky–Milman theorem
[
23
]
as is shown, for instance, in
[
8
, Corollary 7.24]
). To see this, one needs to combine the value for the Dvoretzky dimension for
\ell_{p}^{N}
-balls (see
[
23
, Example 3.1]
or
[
8
, Theorem 7.31 and Equation (5.63)]
) with the Borel–Cantelli lemma exactly as in
[
45
, Remark 1.2]
. We provide an alternative self-contained proof for this convergence in Section
4
that is aligned with our methods of proof used throughout this work. In this light, our main results can be seen as characterizations of the above convergences in different ways, including the speed of convergence and fluctuations.

3
Preliminaries and more notation

In this section, we provide a number of definitions, notation, and standard results that appear throughout the paper.

3.1
Notation related to
\ell_{p}
-spaces

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we denote the
\ell_{p}^{N}
-norm of
x
by

\|x\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|x_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}&:\quad p\in[1,\infty),\\
\max\left\{\left|x_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|x_{N}\right|\right\}&:\quad p=\infty.\end{cases}

The corresponding
\ell_{p}^{N}
-spaces are defined as
\ell_{p}^{N}:=(\mathbb{R}^{N},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{p})
and the closed unit ball in
\ell_{p}^{N}
is

\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}:=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,:\,\|x\|_{p}\leq 1\Big\}.

If
p=2
, we typically write
\|\,\cdot\,\|:=\|\,\cdot\,\|_{2}
and
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,:\,\|x\|=1\big\}
for the Euclidean unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We denote by
q
the Hölder conjugate of
p
, which is defined by the relation

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1

and where we use the convention that
\frac{1}{\infty}:=0
, implying that
q=1
is the Hölder conjugate of
p=\infty
.

3.2
Asymptotic notation and notation from probability theory

For two sequences
(\alpha_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers, we write
\alpha_{N}\sim\beta_{N}
if
\frac{\alpha_{N}}{\beta_{N}}\to 1
, as
N\to\infty
. For a random element
X
and a probability measure
\mu
, the notation
X\sim\mu
means that
X
is distributed according to
\mu
. Further, we denote
\overset{d}{=}
and
\xrightarrow{d}
for equality and convergence in distribution, respectively, and we indicate by
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
the centred Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
\Sigma
and by
\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
the standard Gaussian distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix. The symbol
\sigma
will always denote the normalized spherical Lebesgue measure on the Euclidean unit sphere.

3.3
Brackground from the theory of large deviations

For a topological space
\mathbb{X}
, a function
I:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
is said to be a rate function if all sublevel sets are closed, i.e., if
\{x\in\mathbb{X}:f(x)\leq y\}
is closed in
\mathbb{X}
for every
y
. A rate function is called good if the sublevel sets are additionally compact. A large deviation principle is defined as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables taking values in a topological space
\mathbb{X}
. The sequence is said to satisfy a
large deviation principle (LDP)
with
speed

s_{N}
, where
s_{N}\to\infty
, and
rate function

I:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
if and only if

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}[X_{N}\in O]

\displaystyle\geq-\inf_{x\in O}I(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all open }O\subseteq\mathbb{X},

(6)

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}[X_{N}\in C]

\displaystyle\leq-\inf_{x\in C}I(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all closed }C\subseteq\mathbb{X}.

(7)

Let
\beta_{N}\to\infty
with
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. We say that the sequence
(\beta_{N}X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a
moderate deviation principle
(MDP) if and only if it satisfies an LDP with speed
\beta_{N}^{2}
.

We say that
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a
weak LDP
if it satisfies the definition of an LDP with the exception that the upper bound (Equation (
7
)) only holds for compact sets instead of all closed sets. A closely related concept is that of exponential tightness.

Definition 3.2
.

A sequence of random variables
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
on a topological space
\mathbb{X}
is
exponentially tight
with respect to the speed
s_{N}
if and only if for every
a>0
there exists a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{X}
such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}[X_{N}\in\mathbb{X}\setminus K]\leq-a.

If nothing else is mentioned, we assume
s_{N}=N
. An important property of exponential tightness is that a sequence of random variables satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function if and only if it is exponentially tight with respect to
s_{N}
and it satisfies the corresponding weak LDP (
[
20
, Lemma 1.2.18]
). Tuples are exponentially tight exactly if each component is exponentially tight as the next lemma shows.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are exponentially tight sequences of random variables on the metric spaces
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, respectively. Then, the sequence
(X_{N},Y_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is exponentially tight on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}
equipped with the product topology.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
. By assumption there exist compact sets
K_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
and
K_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{Y}
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[X_{N}\in K_{1}^{c}\big]

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon,

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[Y_{N}\in K_{2}^{c}\big]

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon.

Now, define the set
K=K_{1}\times K_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}
, which is compact in the product topology (by Tychonoff’s theorem, see
[
16
]
). We have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[(X_{N},Y_{N})\in K^{c}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\Big(\mathbb{P}\big[(X_{N},Y_{N})\in(K_{1}^{c},\mathbb{Y})\big]+\mathbb{P}\big[(X_{N},Y_{N})\in(\mathbb{X},K_{2}^{c})\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\max\Bigg\{\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[(X_{N},Y_{N})\in(K_{1}^{c},\mathbb{Y})\big],\,\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[(X_{N},Y_{N})\in(\mathbb{X},K_{2}^{c})\big]\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon,

by Proposition
[
20
, Lemma 1.2.15]
, which proves exponential tightness.
∎

We present a method that can be used to transfer an LDP for one sequence to another sequence if they are exponentially equivalent.

Proposition 3.4

(Exponential equivalence,
[
20
, Theorem 4.2.13]
)
.

Assume
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are sequences of random variables on the metric space
\mathbb{X}
which is equipped with the metric
d
and assume that
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function
I
. If
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are
exponentially equivalent
, i.e.,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[d(X_{N},Y_{N})>\epsilon\big]=-\infty

for every
\epsilon>0
, then
(Y_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with the same speed and the same rate function.

The following is a standard tool that allows us to continuously transform an LDP into another.

Proposition 3.5

(Contraction principle,
[
20
, Theorem 4.2.1]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
be a continuous function and
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}
be Hausdorff topological spaces. Let
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables that satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{X}
with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I
. Then, the sequence
(f(X_{N}))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{Y}
with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
J:\mathbb{Y}\to[0,\infty]
,

J(y)=\inf_{x\in f^{-1}(\{y\})}I(x).

By convention, we set
J(y)=\infty
, whenever
f^{-1}(\{y\})=\emptyset
. If the context is clear, we may write
f^{-1}(y)
instead of
f^{-1}(\{y\})
.

One prominent results in large deviation theory is Cramér’s theorem. Similar to other limit theorems as the central limit theorems or the laws of large numbers, it characterizes the behavior of means of i.i.d. random variables.

Proposition 3.6

(Cramér’s theorem,

(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

N
and rate function
\Lambda^{*}
given by the
Fenchel–Legendre transform

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(\langle\lambda,x\rangle-\Lambda(\lambda)\big),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(8)

The following is the analog for the MDP case.

Proposition 3.7

(MDP for empirical means in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
[
20
, Theorem 3.7.19]
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\beta_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\Big)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

We introduce the notion of projective limits. A
projective system

\left(\mathbb{Y}_{j},p_{ij}\right)_{i\leq j}
consists of Hausdorff topological spaces
\mathbb{Y}_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{Y}_{j}

of this system is given by

\mathbb{Y}:=\Big\{y=\left(y_{j}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\prod_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Y}_{j}\,:\,y_{i}=p_{ij}\left(y_{j}\right),\forall i<j\Big\}.

We denote by
p_{j}:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{Y}_{j}
for

the canonical projection, i.e., for

, we have
p_{j}(y)=y_{j}
in the notation above. The following theorem by Dawson and Gärtner allows inferring an LDP on

\mathbb{Y}_{j}
for each

.

Proposition 3.8

(Dawson and Gärtner,
[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{Y}
be the projective limit of the projective system

\left(X_{N}\right)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

\mathbb{Y}

satisfies an LDP with speed

and rate function

given by

3.4
Hadamard differentiability, the delta method, and a functional CLT

Let us introduce the notion of Hadamard differentiability, which arises from a concept of directional derivative for maps between Banach spaces; in our situation we shall be interested in the Banach space
(C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
of continuous functions on
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
. This concept will appear in an infinite-dimensional version of the delta method.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathbb{X}})
and
(\mathbb{Y},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathbb{Y}})
be real normed spaces and consider a map
\Phi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
. We say that
\Phi
is
Hadamard differentiable
at
x\in\mathbb{X}
if and only if there exists a map
\textup{d}\Phi(x):\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
, which we call the derivative of
\Phi
, such that for all (directions)
h\in\mathbb{X}
and all sequences
(h_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
h_{N}\to h
, and all sequences
(t_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
t_{N}\to 0
and
t_{N}\neq 0
, we have

\Bigg\|\frac{\Phi(x+t_{N}h_{N})-\Phi(x)}{t_{N}}-\textup{d}\Phi(x)[h]\Bigg\|_{\mathbb{Y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

It follows directly from the definition that the map
\textup{d}\Phi(x)
is always continuous.
The next proposition is a generalized delta method.

Proposition 3.10

(Delta method for infinite-dimensional spaces,
[
70
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
X_{N}
be random variables with values in a normed vector space
\mathbb{X}
such that

b_{N}(X_{N}-\mu)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}Z

for some
\mu\in\mathbb{X}
, some sequence
b_{N}\to\infty
, and some random variable
Z
taking values in
\mathbb{X}
. Further, assume that
\phi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
, where
\mathbb{Y}
is some other normed vector space, is Hadamard differentiable at
\mu
with derivative
\textup{d}\Phi(\mu)
. Then we have

b_{N}(\phi(X_{N})-\phi(\mu))\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\textup{d}\Phi(\mu)[Z].

If
Z
is Gaussian and
\textup{d}\Phi(\mu)
is linear, then
\textup{d}\Phi(\mu)[Z]
is also Gaussian (see, e.g.,
[
70
]
).

A similar delta method exists for large deviations and we only state a special case.

Proposition 3.11

(Delta method for large deviation principles,
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{Y}
be a normed vector spaces,
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
open and
\Phi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{Y}
be Hadamard differentiable in
\mu
with Hadamard derivative
\textup{d}\Phi(\mu)
. Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be random variables with values in
\mathbb{D}
. Further, assume that

b_{N}(X_{N}-\mu)

satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
for some sequence
b_{N}\to\infty
. Then

b_{N}(\Phi(X_{N})-\Phi(\mu))

satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I^{\prime}:\mathbb{Y}\to[0,\infty]
,

I^{\prime}(x)=\inf_{\textup{d}\Phi(\mu)[y]=x}I(y).

Finally, we need a central limit theorem for random variables having values in function spaces.

Proposition 3.12

(Functional central limit theorem,
[
30
]
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{X},d)
be a compact metric space and let
(X(u))_{u\in\mathbb{X}}
be a real-valued and centered random process on
\mathbb{X}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}X(u)^{2}<\infty
for all
u\in\mathbb{X}
. Furthermore, suppose there exist a square-integrable random variable
L
such that

|X(u)-X(v)|\leq Ld(u,v),\hskip 20.00003ptu,v\in\mathbb{X}.

Finally, assume that

\int_{0}^{1}\log\big(\operatorname{Covering}(\mathbb{X},d,\epsilon)\big)\,\textup{d}\epsilon<\infty,

where
\operatorname{Covering}(\mathbb{X},d,\epsilon)
denotes the covering number of
\mathbb{X}
with respect to the metric
d
and radius
\epsilon
. Then there exist a centered Gaussian process
Z=(Z(u))_{u\in\mathbb{X}}
on
C(\mathbb{X})
with
\mathbb{E}[Z(u)Z(v)]=\mathbb{E}[X(u)X(v)]
for all
u,v\in\mathbb{X}
, and such that for a sequence of independent random elements
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
, all having the same distribution as
X
, the convergence in distribution

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\textup{d}}Z

holds on the space
(C(\mathbb{X}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
.

4.1
General functional results in large deviation theory and weak convergence

The following lemma allows us to deduce a weak LDP for a sequence of random continuous functions if each collection of finite evaluation tuples satisfy an LDP. We state it in more generality than needed as it might be of independent interest.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f_{i}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
be a continuous function from a separable topological space with countable dense subset
\mathbb{U}
to a Hausdorff topological space for each
i\in\mathbb{T}
where
\mathbb{T}
is another Hausdorff topological space. Assume that, for each collection
\{u_{1},...,u_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{U}
and a sequence of random variables
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
taking values in
\mathbb{T}
, the sequence of tuples

\left(f_{X_{N}}(u_{1}),...,f_{X_{N}}(u_{k})\right)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{Y}^{k}
with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
J_{k}:\mathbb{Y}^{k}\to[0,\infty]
. Then, the sequence of functions
(f_{X_{N}})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a weak LDP on the space
(C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
with speed
s_{N}
and rate function

I:\{f_{i}:i\in\mathbb{T}\}\to[0,\infty],\hskip 10.00002ptI(f)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}J_{k}(f(u_{1}),...,f(u_{k})),

where
\{u_{1},u_{2},...\}=\mathbb{U}
.

Proof.

Let
\{u_{1},u_{2},...\}=\mathbb{U}
be an enumeration of the dense countable subset. Define the projective system
(\mathbb{Y}_{k},p_{\ell k})_{\ell\leq k}
by

\mathbb{Y}_{k}:=\big\{(f_{i}(u_{1}),...,f_{i}(u_{k}))\,:\,\hskip 5.0pti\in\mathbb{T}\big\}

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
with the product topology and for
\ell\leq k
the projection maps

p_{\ell k}(f_{i}(u_{1}),...,f_{i}(u_{k}))=(f_{i}(u_{1}),...,f_{i}(u_{\ell})).

Next, define

\mathbb{Y}:=\big\{(f_{i}(u_{1}),f_{i}(u_{2}),...)\,:\,i\in\mathbb{T}\big\},

also equipped with the product topology and projection maps

p_{k}:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{Y}_{k},\hskip 10.00002ptp_{k}(f_{i}(u_{1}),f_{i}(u_{2}),...)=(f_{i}(u_{1}),...,f_{i}(u_{k}))

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. These maps are continuous and
p_{i}(y)=p_{ij}(p_{j}(y))
for all
y\in\mathbb{Y}
,
i\leq j
holds. By assumption,
(p_{k}(f_{X_{N}}(u_{1}),f_{X_{N}}(u_{2}),...)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
J_{k}
. By the Dawson–Gärtner Theorem (Proposition
3.8
), the sequence
(f_{X_{N}}(u_{1}),f_{X_{N}}(u_{2}),...)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I^{\prime}:\mathbb{Y}\to[0,\infty],I^{\prime}(x)=\sup_{k}J_{k}(p_{k}(x))
.

Now, note that the restriction on a dense subset uniquely determines the function. More precisely, if
g,f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
are continuous and
f\big|_{\mathbb{U}}=g\big|_{\mathbb{U}}
then
f=g
. To see this, assume that there is
x\in\mathbb{X}
such that
f(x)\not=g(x)
. Since
\mathbb{Y}
is Hausdorff and the functions are continuous, there are disjoint open sets
U_{1},U_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{Y}
(with
f(x)\in U_{1}
and
g(x)\in U_{2}
) such that
f^{-1}(U_{1})\cap g^{-1}(U_{2})\subseteq\mathbb{X}
is open and non-empty. Since
\mathbb{U}
is dense, there is
y\in\mathbb{U}\cap f^{-1}(U_{1})\cap g^{-1}(U_{2})
and by assumption
f(y)=g(y)
. But at the same time
f(y)\in U_{1}
and
g(y)\in U_{2}
which is a contradiction because these sets are disjoint. That means
f=g
.
Consequently, the map

(f_{i}(u_{1}),f_{i}(u_{2}),...)\mapsto f_{i}\in C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}),\hskip 10.00002pti\in\mathbb{T},

is a well defined continuous injection. Here, the space
C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence. This topology is not metrizable but Hausdorff (as a product of Hausdorff spaces). The contraction principle (Proposition
3.5
) yields an LDP for
(f_{X_{N}})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
on the space
C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
with speed
N
and rate function

I:C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})\to[0,\infty],\hskip 10.00002ptI(f)=I^{\prime}(f(u_{1}),f(u_{2}),...).

Since the topology of uniform convergence is finer than the topology of pointwise convergence (which is Hausdorff),
[
20
, Corollary 4.2.6]
leads to the weak LDP on
(C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
which is precisely the desired statement.
∎

The next result gives a similar statement in the context of weak convergence.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
\mathbb{X}\subseteq C(K)
, where
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is compact. Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be random elements with values in
\mathbb{X}
. Suppose
\rho
is a metric on
\mathbb{X}
that makes
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
tight. If for any collection
\{u_{1},\ldots u_{k}\}\subseteq K
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

(X_{N}(u_{1}),\ldots,X_{N}(u_{k}))\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}(X(u_{1}),\ldots,X(u_{k}))

for some random element
X
in
(C(K),\rho)
. Then
X_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\textup{d}}X
with respect to
\rho
.

Proof.

A (necessary and) sufficient condition for
X_{N}\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}X
is that each subsequence
X_{N^{\prime}}
contains a further subsequence
X_{N^{\prime\prime}}
with
X_{N^{\prime\prime}}\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}X
. Indeed, assume that there is a bounded continuous function
f
and a subsequence
X_{N^{\prime}}
such that

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(x)\mathbb{P}_{N^{\prime}}(\textup{d}x)-\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(x)\mathbb{P}(\textup{d}x)\Big|>\epsilon

for some
\epsilon>0
and all
N^{\prime}
where
\mathbb{P}_{N^{\prime}}
and
\mathbb{P}
are the push-forward measures for
X_{N^{\prime}}
and
X
, respectively. Then, this subsequence would contain no further converging subsequence.

Now, take any subsequence
X_{N^{\prime}}
. By assumption, this sequence is tight with respect to
\rho
. By Prokhorov’s theorem, there is a further subsequence
X_{N^{\prime\prime}}
and some element
Y
in the closure of
\mathbb{X}
such that
X_{N^{\prime\prime}}\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}Y
. This implies

(X_{N^{\prime\prime}}(u_{1}),\ldots,X_{N^{\prime\prime}}(u_{k}))\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}(Y(u_{1}),\ldots,Y(u_{k}))

by the continuous mapping theorem. It is left to show that
X=Y
. By taking a subsequence in the assumption, we have that for any collection
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in K
,

(X_{N^{\prime\prime}}(u_{1}),\ldots,X_{N^{\prime\prime}}(u_{k}))\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}(X(u_{1}),\ldots,X(u_{k})).

By uniqueness of the limit, we get
Y(u)=X(u)
for all
u\in K
and hence
X=Y
.
∎

4.2
Volume and support function representation

The following lemma shows that the support function of a randomly projected
\ell_{p}^{N}
-ball involves a sum over random functions depending on columns of a random element in the Stiefel manifold.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty]
,
q
be its Hölder conjugate and
m\leq N\in\mathbb{N}
. For an element
V_{N}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
, the support function of
N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}V_{N}\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}
satisfies the identity

\displaystyle h(N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}V_{N}\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p},u)^{q}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{q},\hskip 10.00002ptu\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}.

(9)

Proof.

Note that
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}
. We have

h(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}V_{N}\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p},u)=\sup_{x\in N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}V_{N}\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p}}\langle x,u\rangle=N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p}}\langle V_{N}x,u\rangle=N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p}}\langle x,V_{N}^{*}u\rangle=N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\|V_{N}^{*}u\|_{q}.

This can be written as

N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\|V_{N}^{*}u\|_{q}=N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle v_{i},u\rangle|^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

A rearrangement of the terms proves the claim.
∎

We also show a representation for the volume of a random section of an
\ell_{p}
-ball. We see that this involves a similar sum in the integrand as in the support function of the projection.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
p\in(1,\infty]
. Further, let
V_{N}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
be a random element distributed according to the Haar probability measure on
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
and
E
be a random subspace distributed according to
\mu_{m,N}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{m}\Big(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap E)\Big)\overset{d}{=}\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{p}\Big)^{-\frac{m}{p}}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u).

Proof.

Let
\widehat{E}=\operatorname{Range}(V_{N}^{*})
be the range of the linear operator
V_{N}^{*}
. Then we note that due to the uniqueness of the Haar measure on
\mathbb{G}_{m,N}
,
\widehat{E}\in\mathbb{G}_{m,N}
has distribution
\mu_{m,N}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{vol}_{m}\big(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap E)\big)\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{vol}_{m}\big(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap\widehat{E})\big).

For
F\in\mathbb{G}_{m,N}
let us write
\mathbb{S}_{F}^{m-1}
and
\sigma_{F}
for the unit sphere and the normalized spherical Lebesgue measure in
F
. For the distance of projections of
\ell_{p}^{N}
-balls to one-dimensional subspaces we have the identity

\displaystyle\rho\big(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap F,\theta\big)=\rho\big(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap\operatorname{span}(\theta),\theta\big)=\Big\|\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{p}}\Big\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\|\theta\|_{p}}

(10)

for
\theta\in\mathbb{S}_{F}^{m-1}
. Using (
3
) in the subspace
\widehat{E}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{m}\big(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap\widehat{E})\big)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\widehat{E}}^{m-1}}N^{\frac{m}{p}-\frac{m}{2}}\rho\big(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap\widehat{E},u\big)^{m}\,\sigma_{\widehat{E}}(\textup{d}u).

Let
A
be distributed according to the Haar probability measure on
O(N)
. We can view
V_{N}
as the first
m
columns of
A
(which is also distributed according to Haar measure). Using this construction, we see that
A
maps the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, into the rows of
V_{N}
and hence
A(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})=\mathbb{S}_{\widehat{E}}^{m-1}
. By first applying the transformation
A
to the integral and then using (
10
), we get

\displaystyle\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\widehat{E}}^{m-1}}N^{\frac{m}{2}-\frac{m}{q}}\rho\big(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap\widehat{E},u\big)^{m}\,\sigma_{\widehat{E}}(\textup{d}u)=\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}N^{\frac{m}{p}-\frac{m}{2}}\rho\Big(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\cap\widehat{E},Au\Big)^{m}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}N^{\frac{m}{p}-\frac{m}{2}}\Big\|V_{N}^{*}u\Big\|_{p}^{-m}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)=\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{p}\Big)^{-\frac{m}{p}}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u).

This completes the argument.
∎

4.3
Calculation of Hadamard differentials

We calculate the Hadamard derivative of
f\mapsto f^{\alpha}
. This will be needed to transform the limit theorems for the random sums of non i.i.d. elements to the volume of the random section and projection.

Lemma 4.5
.

The map
\Phi:(C(K),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})\to(C(K),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
,
\Phi(f)=f^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and some compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is Hadamard differentiable at any point
f
such that
f(x)\not=0
for all
x
with derivative
\textup{d}\Phi(f)[h]=\alpha f^{\alpha-1}h
.

Proof.

We need to show that

\frac{1}{t_{N}}(\Phi(f+t_{N}h_{N})-\Phi(f))\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\textup{d}\Phi(f)[h]

for
t_{N}\to 0
and for any
h,h_{1},h_{2},\ldots\in C(K)
such that
h_{N}\to h
in
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty}
. Using
(1+x)^{\alpha}=1+\alpha x+o(x^{2})
for
x\to 0
and the fact that
f
is bounded on
K
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Big(\Phi(f+t_{N}h_{N})-\Phi(f)\Big)=\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Big(f^{\alpha}\Big(1+t_{N}\frac{h_{N}}{f}\Big)^{\alpha}-f^{\alpha}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Big(f^{\alpha}\Big(1+\alpha t_{N}\frac{h_{N}}{f}+o(t_{N}^{2}h_{N}^{2})-1\Big)\Big)=\alpha f^{\alpha-1}h_{N}+f^{\alpha}o(t_{N}h_{N}^{2})\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\alpha f^{\alpha-1}h=\textup{d}\Phi(f)[h].

Here
f_{N}=o(h_{N})
means that
\frac{f_{N}}{h_{N}}\to 0
with respect to
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty}
.
∎

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a convex body. By
[
73
, Page 54 and Equation (1.52)]
, we have

\rho(C,x)=\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\frac{h(C,u)}{\langle x,u\rangle}.

Remember that
\rho(C,\,\cdot\,)
is the radial function of
C
. The map
\Phi:(C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})\to(C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
with

\displaystyle\Phi(f)=\Big(x\mapsto\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\frac{f(u)}{\langle x,u\rangle}\Big)

(11)

is Hadamard differentiable in any constant
\mu>0
as the next lemma shows. Together with Proposition
3.11
and Proposition
3.10
we see that a limit theorem for the support function holds in the same way for the radial function.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
M\in(0,\infty)
let
f:{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\to(0,\infty)
,
x\mapsto M
be a positive constant function. The map
\Phi
defined by (
11
) is Hadamard differentiable at
f
with derivative
\textup{d}\Phi(f)[h]=h
.

Proof.

Going with the definition, let
t_{N}\to 0
and
h_{N}\to h
in the uniform norm on
C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})
. Notice that for any
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
, using the definition in (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(f+t_{N}h_{N})(x)-\Phi(f)(x)}{t_{N}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Bigg(\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\frac{M+t_{N}h_{N}(u)}{\langle x,u\rangle}-\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\frac{M}{\langle x,u\rangle}\Bigg)

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Bigg(\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\frac{M+t_{N}h_{N}(u)}{\langle x,u\rangle}-M\Bigg)

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Bigg(\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\frac{M+t_{N}h_{N}(u)-\langle x,u\rangle M}{\langle x,u\rangle}\Bigg)

(14)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\langle u,x\rangle>0,u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}M\frac{1-\langle x,u\rangle}{t_{N}\langle x,u\rangle}+\frac{h_{N}(u)}{\langle x,u\rangle}

(15)

\displaystyle=M\frac{1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}{t_{N}\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}+\frac{h_{N}(u_{N})}{\langle x,u_{N}\rangle},

(16)

where
u_{N}:=u_{N}(x)\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
is the sequence of minimizers, which exists since the infimum is attained at some
u_{N}\not=0
. Further, it holds that
u_{N}\to x
, since otherwise
\frac{1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}{t_{N}\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.

We want to show that
1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle=o(t_{N})
uniformly over all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
. Assume now that for some
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
,
\langle x,u_{N}\rangle\leq 1-ct_{N}
for some
c\in(0,1)
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
big enough along a subsequence. Then for
N
being in the subsequence,

\displaystyle M\frac{1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}{t_{N}\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}+\frac{h_{N}(u_{N})}{\langle x,u_{N}\rangle}\geq M\frac{c}{1-ct_{N}}+\frac{h_{N}(u_{N})}{1-ct_{N}}.

It is easy to see that
M\frac{c}{1-ct_{N}}+\frac{h_{N}(u_{N})}{1-ct_{N}}>h_{N}(x)
for
N
large enough in that subsequence since
M\cdot c\in(0,\infty)
, which is the value attained at
u=x
by the function over which the infimum in (
12
) is taken. Hence, this is a contradiction to
u_{N}
being the minimizer. We have thus shown that indeed
1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle=o(t_{N})
uniformly over all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
.

In particular, this implies that for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
, we have
\|u_{N}-x\|_{2}^{2}=2(1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle)=o(t_{N})
. Thus, since

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}|h_{N}(u_{N})-h(x)|\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}|h_{N}(u_{N})-h_{N}(x)|+\|h_{N}-h\|_{\infty},

combining that
1-\langle x,u_{N}\rangle=o(t_{N})
uniformly over all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
with the uniform continuity of
h_{N}
and the uniform convergence of
h_{N}
to
h
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{N}}\Big(\Phi(f+t_{N}h_{N})-\Phi(f)\Big)=M\frac{o(t_{N})}{t_{N}(1-o(t_{N}))}+\frac{h_{N}(u_{N})}{1-o(t_{N})}=M\frac{o(1)}{1-o(t_{N})}+\frac{h_{N}(u_{N})}{1-o(t_{N})}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}h

in the uniform norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty}
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.4
Computations of moments and other preliminary results

The next lemma gives a moment computation that will be used frequently throughout the rest of this paper and a justification to change an
N
-dependent normalization to a constant one.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
q>-1
,
v_{i}
be a column of
V_{N}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
for fixed
m\leq N
and
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]=\frac{2^{\frac{q}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}

and

\sup_{u\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\sqrt{N}|\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{q}])|\to 0

as
N\to\infty

Proof.

By using symmetry we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|g|^{q}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{q}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}\,\textup{d}x=2(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{q}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}\,\textup{d}r=\frac{2^{\frac{q}{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{1+q}{2}\Big).

Consider
\mathbb{E}[|\langle v_{i},u\rangle|^{q})]
. By rotational invariance of the Haar measure on
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[|\langle v_{i},u\rangle|^{q})]
=
\mathbb{E}[|v_{i,1}|^{q})]
, where
v_{i,1}
is the first entry of
v_{i}
. So the statement does not depend on
u
or
m
. The random variable has the same distribution as the first coordinate
U_{1}
from a vector
U
uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. It is well known that
U_{1}^{2}
is beta distributed with parameters
\frac{1}{2}
and
\frac{N-1}{2}
. Using the moments of the beta distribution and its relation to the gamma function,

\mathbb{E}[|v_{i,1}|^{q})]=\mathbb{E}[(U_{1}^{2})^{\frac{q}{2}}]=\frac{\operatorname{Beta}(\frac{q+1}{2},\frac{N-1}{2})}{\operatorname{Beta}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{N-1}{2})}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{N-1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{N+q}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{N-1}{2})}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{N+q}{2})\sqrt{\pi}}.

By Stirling’s formula, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\Big(\frac{N^{\frac{q}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{N+q}{2})}-2^{\frac{q}{2}}\Big)=\frac{\sqrt{N}N^{\frac{q}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{N}}\big(\frac{N}{2e}\big)^{\frac{N}{2}}(1+O(\frac{1}{N}))}{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{N+q}}\big(\frac{N+q}{2e}\big)^{\frac{N+q}{2}}(1+O(\frac{1}{N+q}))}-\sqrt{N}2^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+O(\frac{1}{N}))}{(1+O(\frac{1}{N+q}))}\Big(1+\frac{q/2}{N/2}\Big)^{-N/2}(2e)^{\frac{q}{2}}\sqrt{N+q}-\sqrt{N}2^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle\sim e^{-\frac{q}{2}}(2e)^{\frac{q}{2}}\sqrt{N+q}-\sqrt{N}2^{\frac{q}{2}}=2^{\frac{q}{2}}(\sqrt{N+q}-\sqrt{N})\to 0,

which yields the claim.
∎

The following computations will be used in the proof of the central limit theorem and, more precisely, for the calculation of the variance.

Lemma 4.8
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
,
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}),v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
and
q>-1
. Then

1.

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{i}]=u_{i}\frac{2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}=u_{i}\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q}],

2.

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}g_{i}^{2}]=\frac{2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})(1+qu_{i}^{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}=(1+qu_{i}^{2})\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q}]

3.

For
i\not=j
,

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}g_{i}g_{j}]=u_{i}u_{j}\frac{q2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}=qu_{i}u_{j}\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q}]

4.

For
u\not=v
,

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]=\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}}{\pi}{}_{2}F_{1}(-q/2,-q/2,1/2,\langle u,v\rangle^{2})

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
denotes the Gauss hypergeometric function.

5.

For
u=v
,

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}]=\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+q)}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the fact that

(\langle g,u\rangle,\langle g,v\rangle)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}(u_{i},v_{i})\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\begin{pmatrix}1&\langle u,v\rangle\\
\langle u,v\rangle&1\end{pmatrix}\Big)

holds for any
u,v\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
. It is well known that for any joint Gaussian distribution also a conditional Gaussian distribution between the components holds. In this case,

\langle g,u\rangle\big|\langle g,v\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(\langle u,v\rangle\langle g,v\rangle,1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}).

We combine this with the law of total probability and the fact that

\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q}]=\frac{2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}

in each case (see Lemma
4.7
). For the first one just choose
v
as the vector that has only a one at the
i
-th entry. This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle\mathbb{E}[g_{i}|\langle g,u\rangle]]=u_{i}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}]=u_{i}\frac{2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}},

which is what we wanted to show. For the second one,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}g_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}\mathbb{E}[g_{i}^{2}|\langle g,u\rangle]],

and again using the conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}[g_{i}^{2}|\langle g,u\rangle]=\mathrm{Var}(g_{i}|\langle g,u\rangle)+\mathbb{E}[g_{i}|\langle g,u\rangle]^{2}=1-u_{i}^{2}+u_{i}^{2}\langle g,u\rangle^{2},

which leaves us with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}\mathbb{E}[g_{i}^{2}|\langle g,u\rangle]]

\displaystyle=(1-u_{i}^{2})\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}]+u_{i}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q+2}]

\displaystyle=(1-u_{i}^{2})\frac{2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}+u_{i}\frac{2^{q/2+1}\Gamma(\frac{q+3}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

This simplifies to the desired expression using the property
\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
for any
x>0
.

We continue similarly for the third one. This time we will need that similar as above

(g_{i},g_{j},\langle g,u\rangle)\sim\mathcal{N}\Bigg(0,\begin{pmatrix}1&0&u_{i}\\
0&1&u_{j}&\\
u_{i}&u_{j}&1\end{pmatrix}\Bigg),

which gives

(g_{i},g_{j})\big|\langle g,u\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}\Bigg(\langle g,u\rangle(u_{i},u_{j}),\begin{pmatrix}1-u_{i}^{2}&-u_{i}u_{j}\\
-u_{i}u_{j}&1-u_{j}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg).

Now continue with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}g_{i}g_{j}]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}\mathbb{E}[g_{i}g_{j}|\langle g,u\rangle]]

and using the conditional distribution from above

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{i}g_{j}|\langle g,u\rangle]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}[g_{i},g_{j}|\langle g,u\rangle]+\mathbb{E}[g_{i}|\langle g,u\rangle]\mathbb{E}[g_{j}|\langle g,u\rangle]

\displaystyle=-u_{i}u_{j}+u_{i}\langle g,u\rangle u_{j}\langle g,u\rangle=u_{i}u_{j}(\langle g,u\rangle^{2}-1),

which gives the final result

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}\mathbb{E}[g_{i}g_{j}|\langle g,u\rangle]]

\displaystyle=u_{i}u_{j}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}(\langle g,u\rangle^{2}-1))]=u_{i}u_{j}(\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q+2}]-\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q}])

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{\sqrt{\pi}}(2^{q/2+1}\Gamma(\frac{q+3}{2})-2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2}))=u_{i}u_{j}\frac{q2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}},

using again
\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
in the last step. We are left with the fourth expectation. The case
u=v
follows simply from the rotational invariance:

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{2q}]=\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{2q}]=\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+q)}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

The case
u\not=v
is a bit more involved. We start as in the other cases with

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}|\langle g,v\rangle]].

Calculating the inner expectation
\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}|\langle g,v\rangle]
involves the
q
-th absolute moment of a non-centered Gaussian random variable (with mean
\langle u,v\rangle\langle v,g\rangle
and variance
1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}
). By
[
79
]
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}\Big|\langle g,v\rangle\Big]=(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})^{q/2}2^{q/2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}{}_{1}F_{1}\Big(-q/2,1/2,-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}\langle g,v\rangle^{2}}{2(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})}\Big),

where
{}_{1}F_{1}
denotes Kummer’s hypergeometric function (see
[
1
, Chapter 15]
). By definition of this function we have

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{1}\Big(-q/2,1/2,-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}\langle g,v\rangle^{2}}{2(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})}\Big)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(-q/2+n)}{\Gamma(-q/2)}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(n+1/2)}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\big(\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{2(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})}\big)^{n}\langle g,v\rangle^{2n}.

This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}|\langle g,v\rangle]=(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})^{q/2}2^{q/2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\Gamma(-q/2)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(-q/2+n)}{\Gamma(n+1/2)}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\Big(\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{2(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})}\Big)^{n}\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q+2n}]

since this sum converges absolutely. Now we can use the central moment to conclude that the above is the same as

(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})^{q/2}2^{q/2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(-q/2)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(-q/2+n)}{\Gamma(n+1/2)n!}\Big(-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{2(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})}\Big)^{n}2^{n+q/2}\Gamma\big(\frac{q+2n+1}{2}\big).

By writing out the definition of the Gauss hypergeometric function we see

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(-q/2+n)}{\Gamma(n+1/2)n!}\big(-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{2(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})}\big)^{n}2^{n+q/2}\Gamma\big(\frac{q+2n+1}{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{q/2}\Gamma(-q/2)\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(-q/2,\frac{q+1}{2},1/2,-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}\Big),

which yields

\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]=\frac{(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})^{q/2}2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}{}_{2}F_{1}(-q/2,\frac{q+1}{2},\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}})}{\pi}.

After a Pfaff transformation (see
[
1
, Equation 15.8.1]
), we end up with

\frac{(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})^{q/2}2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}{}_{2}F_{1}(-q/2,\frac{q+1}{2},\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}{1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}})}{\pi}=\frac{2^{q}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})^{2}}{\pi}{}_{2}F_{1}(-q/2,-q/2,1/2,\langle u,v\rangle^{2}),

as desired.
∎

The expectation in the next lemma will be needed later in the calculation of the variance for the volume.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p,q\geq 1
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}|\langle g,u\rangle|^{p}|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)\sigma(\textup{d}v)\Big]=\frac{2^{\frac{p+q}{2}+1}\Gamma(\frac{m+p+q}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1+p}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1+q}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{m}{2})}{m\pi\Gamma(\frac{m+p}{2})\Gamma(\frac{m+q}{2})}.

Proof.

By changing to spherical coordinates, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|g\|^{p}\big]

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|x\|^{p}e^{-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}}\,\textup{d}x=m\kappa_{m}(2\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{m+p-1}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}\,\textup{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{m\kappa_{m}2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}}{\pi^{\frac{m}{2}}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{m+p}{2}\Big)=\frac{m2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\Gamma(\frac{m+p}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{m}{2})}.

We use
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
and the result above to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}|\langle g,u\rangle|^{p}|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)\sigma(\textup{d}v)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\|g\|_{2}^{p+q}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big|\Big\langle\frac{g}{\|g\|_{2}},u\Big\rangle\Big|^{p}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)\Big)\Big(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big|\Big\langle\frac{g}{\|g\|_{2}},u\Big\rangle\Big|^{q}\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{1+p}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{m}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}m\Gamma(\frac{m+p}{2})}\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{1+q}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{m}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}m\Gamma(\frac{m+q}{2})}\mathbb{E}\big[\|g\|_{2}^{p+q}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{p+q}{2}+1}\Gamma(\frac{m+p+q}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1+p}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1+q}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{m}{2})}{m\pi\Gamma(\frac{m+p}{2})\Gamma(\frac{m+q}{2})},

which completes the proof.
∎

The following is an elementary Lipschitz estimate that we will need in order to apply Proposition
3.12
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
q\geq 1
and
s_{N}\geq 0
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Further, let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be compact and
\sup_{u\in K}\|u\|_{2}\leq C
for some
C\in(0,\infty)
. Then, we have

\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle x_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle x_{i},v\rangle|^{q}\Big|\leq\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}qC^{q-1}\|x_{i}\|_{2}^{q-1}\Big)\|u-v\|_{2}

all
u,v\in K
.

Proof.

Clearly, the composition of Lipschitz functions is again Lipschitz with the Lipschitz constant for the composition being the product of the respective Lipschitz constants of the functions. Since
u\mapsto|\langle x,u\rangle|
has Lipschitz constant
1
,
x\mapsto x^{q}
has Lipschitz constant
qa^{q-1}
on the interval
[0,a]
, and
|\langle x,u\rangle|\leq C\|x\|_{2}
, we find that

\Big||\langle x_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-|\langle x_{i},v\rangle|^{q}\Big|\leq qC^{q-1}\|x\|_{2}^{q-1}\|u-v\|_{2}.

Consequently,

\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle x_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle x_{i},v\rangle|^{q}\Big|\leq\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}qC^{q-1}\|x_{i}\|_{2}^{q}\Big)\|u-v\|_{2},

as desired.
∎

4.5
Proof of the central limit theorem and the moderate deviation principle

In this section we prove the functional results Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
. We now provide an outline for a general method of proof for Theorem
2.2
. The sequence in the theorem is a sum of random functions depending on columns of a random element in the Stiefel manifold. Every column has the same distribution as
(G_{N}G_{N}^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{i}
, where
G_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
is a standard Gaussian matrix with i.d.d.  columns
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
. Using a Taylor expansion, we can separate
(G_{N}G_{N}^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
from
g_{i}
and reduce this problem to sums over i.d.d. random functions to which we can apply known limit theorems. These can be transformed back to the original sequence which proves the desired statement. The proof of the MDP follows similar steps using the corresponding theorem in the moderate deviations regime.

We start with a central limit theorem for case of i.i.d. random function that will be used later.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\geq 0
,
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be compact and
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
g_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
. Then the sequence of random functions
\Phi_{N}:K\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\Phi_{N}(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle g_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{i},u\rangle|^{q}]),

converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian process
Z_{1}
indexed by
K
with

\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(u_{1})Z_{1}(u_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[(|\langle g_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}])(|\langle g_{i},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{i},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}])]

for all
u_{1},u_{2}\in K
.

Proof.

We check the assumptions of Proposition
3.12
. We find a constant
c>0
such that
K\subseteq c\mathbb{B}_{2}^{m}
. The summands are centered and

\mathbb{V}[|\langle g_{i},u\rangle|^{q}]=\mathbb{E}\Big[(|\langle g_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{i},u\rangle|^{q}])^{2}\Big]<\infty.

Lemma
4.10
for
N=1
gives the Lipschitz condition. Finally, the covering number with respect to a radius
\varepsilon>0
, denoted by
\operatorname{Covering}(\mathbb{B}_{2}^{m},\|\,\cdot\,\|,\epsilon)
, is upper bounded by
(\frac{2}{\epsilon}+1)^{m}
for any
k
according to
[
77
, Corollary 4.2.13]
. Thus,

\int_{0}^{1}\log\operatorname{Covering}(c\mathbb{B}_{2}^{m},\|\,\cdot\,\|,\epsilon)\,\textup{d}\epsilon=m\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(1+{2c\over\varepsilon}\Big)\,\textup{d}\varepsilon<\infty.

We can now apply Proposition
3.12
to conclude that the sequence of random processes
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges, as
N\to\infty
, in distribution with respect to the topology of uniform convergence to a centered Gaussian process
Z_{1}(u)
,
u\in K
with

\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(u_{1})Z_{1}(u_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[(|\langle g_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}])(|\langle g_{i},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{i},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}])]

for all
u_{1},u_{2}
.
∎

For
0<\delta<1
, define

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}(m,\delta):=\Big\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}:1-\delta\leq\lambda_{i}\leq 1+\delta\text{ for all eigenvalues }\lambda_{i}\text{ of }M,M\text{ symmetric }\Big\}.

(17)

Since
M
is symmetric the eigenvalues are real. Let
M\in\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)
. Then,
\det(M)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}>0
,
M
is positive definite and any norm of
M
is bounded. Further, since eigenvalues are continuous in the entries of the matrix, the set is closed and hence,
\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)
is compact. We define

\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)\ni G_{N,\delta}:=\begin{cases}G_{N}&:\quad\frac{1}{N}G_{N}G_{N}^{*}\in\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)\\
\operatorname{Id}_{m}&:\quad\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

We want to find the weak limit of the sequence of processes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}])

(18)

in the uniform norm indexed by
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
. By Lemma
4.7
,
v_{i}\overset{d}{=}(G_{N}G_{N}^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{i}
and Slutsky’s theorem, this sequence satisfies the same weak convergence as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(|\langle(\frac{1}{N}G_{N}G_{N}^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]\Big),

where
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
are all independent. It is therefore sufficient to establish the central limit theorem for the latter sequence. Let

S^{m}_{+}:=\Big\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\,:\,M\text{ is positive definite and symmetric}\Big\}.

It is well known that the set
S^{m}_{+}
is open in the set of symmetric matrices. Further, with the notation from above we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\Phi\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{N}G_{N}G_{N}^{*}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{2}},u,g_{i}\Big)-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]\Big),

where
\Phi:S^{m}_{+}\times{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\Phi(M,u,g_{i}):=|\langle Mg_{i},u\rangle|^{q}.

To shorten notation, let
F_{N}=(\frac{1}{N}G_{N}G_{N}^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. We show some properties about
\Phi
and
F_{N}
that we will need later on. A
m\times m
matrix
M\in S^{m}_{+}
has entries
(m_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{m}
and
m_{i,j}=m_{j,i}
due to symmetry. For
q>1
,
\Phi
is continuously differentiable in all points (since
x\mapsto|x|^{q}
has continuous derivative
q|x|^{q-2}x
in
x\not=0
) with

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{i,j}}\Phi(M,u,g)=\begin{cases}q|\langle Mg,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle Mg,u\rangle(g_{j}u_{i}+g_{i}u_{j})&:\quad i\not=j\\
qu_{i}|\langle Mg,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle Mg,u\rangle g_{i}&:\quad i=j,\end{cases}

due to the symmetry of
M
. We remark that in the case
q=1
the function is almost everywhere continuously differentiable in
M
. We will need

\displaystyle e_{i,j}(u)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{i,j}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m},u,g)]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}qu_{i}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{j}]+qu_{j}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{i}]&:\quad i\not=j\\
qu_{i}\mathbb{E}[|\langle Mg,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle Mg,u\rangle g_{i}]&:\quad i=j.\end{cases}

Further, we know by the strong law of large numbers and the continuous mapping theorem that
F_{N}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\operatorname{Id}_{m}
. By the multivariate central limit theorem (
[
12
, Theorem 11.10]
), we know

\sqrt{N}\Big(\frac{1}{N}G_{N}G_{N}^{*}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Big)\xrightarrow{d}Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

where
\Sigma
is a
m^{2}\times m^{2}
matrix with entries
\Sigma_{(i,j),(k,\ell)}
given by the covariance of
g_{1,k}g_{1,\ell}
and
g_{1,i}g_{1,j}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{1,k}g_{1,\ell}g_{1,i}g_{1,j}]-\mathbb{E}[g_{1,k}g_{1,\ell}]\mathbb{E}[g_{1,i}g_{1,j}]=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}[g_{1,k}^{4}]-\mathbb{E}[g_{1,k}^{2}]^{2}=2&:\quad i=j=k=\ell\\
\mathbb{E}[g_{1,k}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[g_{1,\ell}^{2}]=1&:\quad i=k\not=j=\ell\text{ or }j=k\not=i=\ell\\
0&:\quad\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

Since
\Psi(M):=M^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is a differentiable map in
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
on the space of symmetric matrices, the delta method (Proposition
3.10
) implies that

\sqrt{N}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m})\xrightarrow{d}-\frac{1}{2}Z,

since by matrix calculus we know
\textup{d}\Psi(\operatorname{Id}_{m})[Z]=-\frac{1}{2}Z
(combine, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
61
, Theorem 8.3]
to
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
using the chain rule). Note that almost surely,
\Phi(\,\cdot\,,u,g_{i})
is differentiable in a neighborhood of
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
. We do a Taylor expansion of
\Phi
in
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
with integral remainder (see
[
24
, Theorem 2]
). This means

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m},u,g_{i})-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k}^{m}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m})_{k,\ell}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle g_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k}^{m}(\sqrt{N}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}))_{k,\ell}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\textup{d}t.

(19)

Next, we show that

\displaystyle Z^{k,\ell}_{N}(\,\cdot\,)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\textup{d}t\xrightarrow{a.s.}c_{1}^{k,\ell}(\,\cdot\,)

(20)

for some deterministic element
c_{1}^{k,\ell}\in C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})
. First, consider the version of
Z_{N}
that restricts
F_{N}
to the matrix
F_{N,\delta}:=\big(\frac{1}{N}G_{N,\delta}G_{N,\delta}^{*}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
which is defined on a compact set around
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
in the space of symmetric positive definite matrices:

\widehat{Z_{N}}^{k,\ell}(u):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(F_{N,\delta}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\,\textup{d}t.

Let
\epsilon>0
and notice
\mathbb{P}(\|Z^{k,\ell}_{N}-\widehat{Z_{N}}^{k,\ell}\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(\frac{1}{N}G_{N}G_{N}^{*}\not\in\mathbb{K}(m,\delta))\to 0
. This mean if
\widehat{Z_{N}}^{k,\ell}\xrightarrow{a.s.}c_{1}^{k,\ell}
, then
Z_{N}^{k,\ell}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}c_{1}^{k,\ell}
.
For
M\in\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)
, consider the pair

\displaystyle\Big((u,M)\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(M-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\textup{d}t,\frac{1}{N}(G_{N,\delta}G_{N,\delta}^{*})\Big),

(21)

which is an element in
C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\times\mathbb{K}(m,\delta))\times\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)
. This is an empirical mean over i.i.d.  random elements in a separable Banach space. Further, we have that

\mathbb{E}\big[\|(u,M)\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(M-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\textup{d}t\|_{\infty}\big]\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[q\|(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+tM)g\|^{q-1}g_{\ell}]\textup{d}t<\infty.

By the strong law of large number for separable Banach spaces (
[
57
, Corollary 7.10]
), we have convergence to the pair

\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[(u,M)\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(M-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),u,g_{i})\textup{d}t\Big],\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Big)

in the space
C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\times\mathbb{K}(m,\delta))\times\mathbb{K}(m,\delta)
in the sense of the Bochner integral. The continuous mapping theorem applied to the function
(f,M)\mapsto f(\,\cdot\,,M^{-\frac{1}{2}})
implies

\widehat{Z_{N}}(\,\cdot\,)^{k,\ell}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m}+t(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),\,\cdot\,,g_{i})\textup{d}t\Big]=\mathbb{E}[\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}\Phi(\operatorname{Id}_{m},\,\cdot\,,g_{i})]=e_{k,\ell}(\,\cdot\,),

which is what we wanted to show.

By Lemma
4.11
, we have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle g_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]\xrightarrow{d}Z_{1}
with

\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(u_{1})Z_{1}(u_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{1},u_{1}\rangle\langle g_{1},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}.

Further, we know from above that

\sqrt{N}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m})\xrightarrow{d}Z_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

By Prohorovs theorem (
[
46
, Theorem 23.2]
) both sequences are tight which implies that the pair

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle g_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}],\sqrt{N}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m})\Big)

is also tight (even without independence). For any
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
the multivariate central limit theorem implies that

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle g_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}],\ldots,|\langle g_{i},u_{k}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}],\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}G_{N}G_{N}^{*}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Big)

converges weakly to a centered multivariate Gaussian vector with its corresponding covariance matrix on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. The two individual central limit theorems together with tightness and Lemma
4.2
imply the weak convergence of

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle g_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}],\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}G_{N}G_{N}^{*}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Big)\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2})

since the limit is determined by the projections. Here the tuple
Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is a Gaussian process (all projections are Gaussian) indexed by
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\cup\{1,\ldots,m\}^{2}
. The covariance is given by

\mathbb{E}[Z(s)Z(t)]=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(s)Z_{1}(t)]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{1},u_{1}\rangle\langle g_{1},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}&:\quad s,t\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\\
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(s)Z_{2}(t)]=\mathbb{E}[(|\langle g_{1},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])g_{t_{1}}g_{t_{2}}]&:\quad s\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}},t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{2}\\
\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}(s)Z_{2}(t)]=\Sigma_{s,t}&:\quad s,t\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{2}.\end{cases}

The delta method applied to
(f,M)\mapsto(f,M^{-\frac{1}{2}})
gives the weak convergence of

\sqrt{N}\Big(\big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle g_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}\big),F_{N})-(\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}],\operatorname{Id}_{m})\Big)=\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle g_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]),\sqrt{N}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m})\Big)

to the tuple
Z=(Z_{1},-\frac{1}{2}Z_{2})
with covariance

\mathbb{E}[Z(s)Z(t)]=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(s)Z_{1}(t)]=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g_{1},u_{1}\rangle\langle g_{1},u_{2}\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}&:\quad s,t\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\\
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(s)(-\frac{1}{2})Z_{2}(t)]=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(|\langle g_{1},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])g_{t_{1}}g_{t_{2}}]&:\quad s\in{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}},t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{2}\\
\mathbb{E}[(-\frac{1}{2})Z_{2}(s)(-\frac{1}{2})Z_{2}(t)]=\frac{1}{4}\Sigma_{s,t}&:\quad s,t\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{2}.\end{cases}

Writing
e(u):=(e_{k,\ell}(u))_{k,\ell=1}^{m}
and
Z_{N}(u)=(Z_{N}(u)^{k,\ell})_{k,\ell}^{m}
, Slutsky’s theorem provides the joint convergence of

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle g_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]),\sqrt{N}(F_{N}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}),Z_{N}\Big)\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\Big(Z_{1},-\frac{1}{2}Z_{2},e(\,\cdot\,)\Big).

Finally, by the continuous mapping theorem, we obtain the weak convergence

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])\xRightarrow[N\to\infty]{}Z(\,\cdot\,):=Z_{1}(\,\cdot\,)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k}^{m}Z_{2}^{k,\ell}e_{k,\ell}(\,\cdot\,).

Notice that the right-hand side is Gaussian (see, e.g.,
[
70
, Page 3]
). We use Lemma
4.8
to show that the covariance is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z(u)Z(v)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(u)Z_{1}(v)]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k}^{m}\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(u)Z_{2}^{k,\ell}e_{k,\ell}(v)]+\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(v)Z_{2}^{k,\ell}e_{k,\ell}(u)]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\geq\ell}\sum_{i\geq j}e_{k,\ell}(u)e_{i,j}(v)\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}^{k,\ell}Z_{2}^{i,j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}qu_{k}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{k}](\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}g_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}qv_{k}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,v\rangle g_{k}](\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}g_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}(qu_{k}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{\ell}]+qu_{\ell}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{k}])\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}g_{k}g_{\ell}]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}(qv_{k}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,v\rangle g_{\ell}]+qv_{\ell}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,v\rangle g_{k}])\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q}g_{k}g_{\ell}]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}q^{2}u_{k}v_{k}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{k}]\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,v\rangle g_{k}]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}q^{2}u_{k}v_{k}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,u\rangle g_{\ell}]\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,v\rangle|^{q-2}\langle g,v\rangle g_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{q}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]((1-qv_{k}^{2})\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])+v_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]((1-qu_{k}^{2})\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-2q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}u_{k}u_{\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}^{2}v_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt+q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}u_{k}u_{\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}\Big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}v_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2}+q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}u_{k}u_{\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}\Big).

The explicit formulas in Lemma
4.8
and Lemma
4.7
yield the claim.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{N}\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}])

(k,\ell)

and

otherwise. Applying the contraction principle (Proposition

) to

Remark 4.12
.

In this remark we give the explicit form of the rate function in a comprehensive way. The rate function
J:C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})\to[0,\infty]
is defined by

\displaystyle J(f)=\inf_{F^{-1}(f)=(Z_{1},Z_{2},c_{1})}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\langle\widehat{u},\mathcal{C}_{k}^{-1}\widehat{u}\rangle}{2},

where
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is the covariance matrix of

\Big(\Big(|\langle g,u_{1}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{q}],\ldots,|\langle g,u_{k}\rangle|^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]\Big),gg^{*}-\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Big),

where
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
,
g^{*}
is its transposition, and
\widehat{u}=(Z_{1}(u_{1}),\ldots,Z_{1}(u_{k}),Z_{2})
. Here the infimum runs over
Z_{1}\in C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})
and
Z_{2}\in\overline{S^{m}_{+}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
\overline{S^{m}_{+}}
being the space of symmetric and positive semi-definite matrices. Further, the constant matrix
c_{1}
is given by

(c_{1})_{k,\ell}=\mathbb{E}[\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}m_{k,\ell}}|\langle Mg_{i},u\rangle|^{q}],

where the derivative is taken in
\overline{S^{m}_{+}}
. The linear function
F:C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})\times\overline{S^{m}_{+}}\times C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})^{m\times m}\to C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}})
is defined by

F(f,(M_{1,1},M_{1,2},\ldots,M_{m,m},h_{1,1},h_{1,2},\ldots,h_{m,m})=f-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k}^{m}M_{\ell,k}h_{\ell,k}.

4.6
Proof of the LDP

In this section, we prove the large deviation principle. We make use of the Sanov-type result
[
52
, Theorem 2.8]
for columns of random elements in the Stiefel manifold and combine this with the explicit form of the support function.

Remember the notation from the statement of the main results. We denote by
\mathscr{M}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
equipped the
q
-Wasserstein topology, which is defined as the coarsest topology that makes integration of continuous functions bounded by
x\mapsto c(1+\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}^{q})
a continuous operation, where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
c>0
is some constant.

The following result is also proven in
[
52
, Theorem 2.8]
and states an LDP for the empirical measure of random elements from the Stiefel manifold.

Lemma 4.13
.

For all
N\geq m
, let
V_{N}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})
be independently and uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
. Then, the sequence of random measures

\mathscr{L}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\sqrt{N}v_{i}}

satisfies, for
N\to\infty
, an LDP on
\mathscr{M}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
for any
0<q<2
with speed
N
and strictly convex rate function
I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}:\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to[0,\infty]
,

I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}(\nu)=\begin{cases}H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes m})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Trace}(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\mathscr{C}(\nu))&:\quad\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\mathscr{C}(\nu)\text{ positive semidefinite },\\
+\infty&:\quad\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Here
\mathscr{C}
is the covariance operator introduced in Equation (
2
).

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We study the sequence

X_{N}(\,\cdot\,)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}

of random functions on
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
. Let
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
be a subset of an enumeration of a dense subset in
{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
and let
F^{(k)}:\mathscr{M}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

F^{(k)}(\mu)=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}\mu(\textup{d}x),\ldots,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u_{k}\rangle|^{q}\mu(\textup{d}x)\Big).

This function is continuous and hence, the contraction principle (Proposition
3.5
) applies. We have

F^{(k)}(\mathscr{L}_{N})=\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q},\ldots,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u_{k}\rangle|^{q}\Big),

where we recall

\mathscr{L}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\sqrt{N}v_{i}}.

This sequence satisfies an LDP with speed
N
by Lemma
4.13
for each
k
. Lemma
4.1
implies the weak LDP for
X_{N}(\,\cdot\,)
with speed
N
and rate function
I^{\prime}_{\mathrm{LDP}}:(C({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})\to[0,\infty]
,

I_{\mathrm{LDP}}^{\prime}(f)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\inf\{I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}(\mu):F(\mu)=(f(u_{1}),\ldots,f(u_{k}))\}

where
I_{\mathrm{Stiefel}}
is the rate function from Lemma
4.13
. For the full LDP, notice that for
L>0
the set of
L
-Lipschitz functions bounded by
L
, denoted by
\operatorname{Lip}_{L}({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}},[0,L])
, is compact by Arzelà-Ascoli’s theorem (see
[
49
, Chapter 7, Theorem 17]
). Further,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},u_{1}\rangle|^{q}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\sqrt{N}v_{i}\|_{2}^{q}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\sqrt{N}v_{i}\|_{2}^{q}\mathscr{L}_{N}(\textup{d}x)

and the Lipschitz constant of
X_{N}
is upper bounded by

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}q\|\sqrt{N}v_{i}\|_{2}^{q}\mathscr{L}_{N}(\textup{d}x)

almost surely by Lemma
4.10
. Hence, by
[
20
, Lemma 1.2.15]
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log(\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\not\in\operatorname{Lip}_{L}({\mathbb{S}^{m-1}},[0,L])))

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log(\mathbb{P}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}q\|\sqrt{N}v_{i}\|_{2}^{q}\mathscr{L}_{N}(\textup{d}x)\geq L)),\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log(\mathbb{P}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\sqrt{N}v_{i}\|_{2}^{q}\mathscr{L}_{N}(\textup{d}x)\geq L))\Big\}.

Since
\mathscr{L}_{N}
satisfies the LDP with speed
N
and
q<2
the same holds for both integrals in the last expression as those are continuous transformations of
\mathscr{L}_{N}
in the
q
-Wasserstein topology. The LDP implies exponential tightness. This also makes
(X_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
exponentially tight in the uniform topology which concludes the proof.
∎

4.7
From functional limit theorems to the volume

Our goal is now to deduce the corresponding results for the volume of random sections and projections of
\ell_{p}
-balls. The radial or support function of the random projection or random section, which is itself a convex body, is a continuous (or Hadamard differentiable) transformation of the functional results (Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
). The contraction principle (Proposition
3.5
) or the delta method (Proposition
3.11
and Proposition
3.10
) allow us to transfer the results to the volume. In fact, the LDP result can be deduced similarly for any continuous functional from the support or radial function. For the MDP and CLT, similar results can be obtained for any Hadamard differentiable map from the support or radial function. Also the support and radial functions for projections and sections satisfy the same weak convergence and the same MDP results (see Lemma
4.6
).

The map from the radial function to the volume
f\mapsto\kappa_{m}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}f^{m}(u)\sigma(\textup{d}u)
is Hadamard differentiable. This is because of Lemma
4.5
, since linear functions are Hadamard differentiable and the composition of Hadamard differentiable functions is again Hadamard differentiable.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

By Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
4.3
the normalized support function of
V_{N}{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{N}
satisfies the LDP with speed
N
. The map
\Phi
, defined in Equation (
11
), as well as the map

f\mapsto\kappa_{m}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}f^{m}(u)\,\sigma(\textup{d}u)

are continuous, implying that its composition, i.e., the map to the volume of the projection, is also continuous. For the sections case, apply the continuous transformation given by Lemma
4.4
. The contraction principle (Proposition
3.5
) yields the claim for both cases.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A
.

We apply the delta method (Proposition
3.10
) to the function
f\mapsto f^{\frac{m}{q}}
using Lemma
4.5
and the process in the statement of Theorem
2.2
. By the representation of the support function (Lemma
4.3
), this means that

{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\ni u\mapsto\sqrt{N}\Big(h(N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}V_{N}\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p},u)^{m}-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{\frac{m}{q}}\Big)

converges weakly to a centered Gaussian process
\widehat{Z}
on the sphere with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}(u)\widehat{Z}(v)]=\frac{m^{2}}{q^{2}}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{\frac{2m}{q}-2}\mathbb{E}[Z(u)Z(v)]

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{q^{2}}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{\frac{2m}{q}-2}\Big(\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}\Big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}v_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2}+q^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}u_{k}u_{\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}\Big)\Big).

By Lemma
4.6
and Proposition
3.10
the same conclusion holds if we replace the support with the radial function. Now, we apply the function
f\mapsto\kappa_{m}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}f(u)\sigma(\textup{d}u)
. The continuous mapping theorem implies that

\sqrt{N}\Big(\mathrm{vol}_{m}(N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{N}\big|E_{N}))-\kappa_{m}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{\frac{m}{q}}\Big)

converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian random variables with variance

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\Big[\kappa_{m}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\widehat{Z}(u)\sigma(\textup{d}u)\Big]=\kappa_{m}^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\widehat{Z}(u)\sigma(\textup{d}u)\Big)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\kappa_{m}^{2}\frac{m^{2}}{q^{2}}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{\frac{2m}{q}-2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\mathbb{E}[|\langle g,u\rangle\langle g,v\rangle|^{q}]-\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}\Big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}v_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2}\Big)\sigma(\textup{d}u)\sigma(\textup{d}v).

Notice that the last part involving
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}u_{k}u_{\ell}\sigma(\textup{d}u)
vanishes due to symmetry. We can calculate the integral using Lemma
4.9
and
\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}u_{k}^{2}\sigma(\textup{d}u)=\frac{1}{m}
,
1\leq k\leq m
, which gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\Big[\kappa_{m}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}}\widehat{Z}(u)\sigma(\textup{d}u)\Big]

\displaystyle=\kappa_{m}^{2}\frac{m^{2}}{q^{2}}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{\frac{2m}{q}-2}\Big(\kappa_{m}^{2}\frac{2^{q+1}\Gamma(q+m/2)\Gamma(\frac{1+q}{2})^{2}\Gamma(1+\frac{m}{2})}{m\pi\Gamma(\frac{m+q}{2})^{2}}-\kappa_{m}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]^{2}\big(1+\frac{q^{2}}{2m}\big)\Big).

Simplifying this and using Lemma
4.7
for exact value of
\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]
gives

\frac{m\pi^{\frac{qm-m}{q}}\big(2^{q/2}\Gamma(\frac{q+1}{2})\big)^{\frac{2m}{q}}\big(4\Gamma(1+m/2)\Gamma(m/2+q)-(2m+q^{2})\Gamma(\frac{m+q}{2})^{2}\big)}{2q^{2}\Gamma(1+m/2)^{2}\Gamma(\frac{m+q}{2})^{2}}.

This concludes the case for projections. The section case can be done analogously by changing the roles of
p
and
q
and the maps
f\mapsto f^{\frac{m}{q}}
and
f\mapsto f^{-\frac{m}{p}}
(see Lemma
4.4
). This gives

\frac{m\pi^{\frac{pm+m}{p}}\big(2^{p/2}\Gamma(\frac{p+1}{2})\big)^{-\frac{2m}{p}}\big(4\Gamma(1+m/2)\Gamma(m/2+p)-(2m+p^{2})\Gamma(\frac{m+p}{2})^{2}\big)}{2p^{2}\Gamma(1+m/2)^{2}\Gamma(\frac{m+p}{2})^{2}}

for the variance.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B
.

By Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
2.3
, the sequence

\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\beta_{N}}\Big(h(N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}V_{N}\mathbb{B}^{N}_{p},\,\cdot\,)^{q}-\mathbb{E}[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},\,\cdot\,\rangle|^{q}]\Big)\Bigg)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies an LDP with speed
\beta_{N}^{2}
and rate function from Theorem
2.3
. We can also replace
\mathbb{E}[|\langle\sqrt{N}v_{i},(\,\cdot\,)\rangle|^{q}]
by
\mathbb{E}[|g|^{q}]
by Lemma
4.7
as the sequences are exponentially equivalent. By Proposition
3.11
and Lemma
4.6
, we have the same result for the radial function. As in the proof of Theorem
A
we apply this to the Hadamard differentiable map to the volume. The rate function transforms according to the delta method in Proposition
3.11
(remember that
J_{2}
was defined in Equation (
22
)):

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{MDP}}^{\diamondsuit}(x)=\inf_{\textup{d}\Phi_{\diamondsuit}(\mu_{\diamondsuit})[y]=x}J_{2}(y)

(23)

where
