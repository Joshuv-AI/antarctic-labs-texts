Spin-driven enantioselective regulation of cyclooxygenase-2 activity for rheumatoid arthritis therapy via chiral gold nanohelices
Authors: Jiao Yan, Lai Liu, Zhihao Chen, Qian Zhao, Xiaoqing Han, Yanjing Wang, Zhengzhi Mu, Xingbo Wang, Panpan Song, Yaqing Kang, Weijie Lu, Ai-Min Guo, Qing-Feng Sun, Haiyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electron-spin dynamics represent an additional dimension in enzymatic catalysis, where most regulatory strategies focus on modulating active-site chemistry. Here, we present a spintronic approach that employs chiral gold nanohelices (CAu) as electron spin polarizers to enantiospecifically modulate cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) activity for rheumatoid arthritis intervention. Exploiting the chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect inherent to both COX-2 and CAu, we demonstrate that left-handed CAu (Lh-CAu) enhances, whereas right-handed CAu (Rh-CAu) suppresses COX-2 catalytic efficiency via spin-dependent electron transfer at the chiral nanoparticle-enzyme interfaces. To achieve targeted modulation in complex biological settings, we engineer molecularly imprinted CAu (CAu@MIP) for selectively regulating COX-2 in inflammatory cells and collagen-induced arthritis murine model (male DBA/1 J mice). Treatment with Rh-CAu@MIP significantly reduces prostaglandin E2 secretion and mitigates joint inflammation, achieving therapeutic efficacy comparable to conventional COX-2 inhibitors. Our findings introduce electron spin polarization as an orthogonal mechanism for enzymatic regulation, offering a bioelectronic strategy for inflammation-targeted therapy.

Introduction

Regulating enzyme activity is a central mechanism for controlling diverse biological processes and has underpinned numerous therapeutic strategies1–4. Current approaches primarily target substrate access to enzyme active sites via small-molecule screening, which have yielded many successes but remain laborious, time-consuming and often enzyme-specific5–8. These limitations motivate to search for orthogonal regulatory strategies that transcend traditional chemical modulation. Electron transfer, a critical step in many enzymatic reactions, and its rate strongly influences catalytic efficiency9–12. Enhancing electron transport rate has been widely reported to improve the catalytic efficiency13–15. Spin-polarized electrons, with spins aligned parallelly, exhibit reduced scattering and longer coherence lifetimes, enabling more efficient transport with higher activity15–18. Therefore, manipulation of electron spin polarization may offer a nonclassical and potentially generalizable strategy for modulating enzyme activity.

Chiral materials provide an alternative paradigm in generating highly spin-polarized electrons because of chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS)19–25 (Fig. 1a). When electrons propagate across two adjacent chiral systems with matching CISS profiles, spin orientation is preserved, leading to efficient transport. In contrast, a mismatch in CISS necessitates spin-flipping, which consumes additional energy and disrupts electron flow (Fig. 1b). Given the intrinsic chirality of enzymes, modulating electron spin polarization at the nano-bio interface may enable to regulate enzymatic activity in a chirality-dependent manner26–30. Indeed, injecting spin-polarized photons to enzymes using circularly polarized light (CPL) has been shown to enantioselectively change phosphoglycerate kinase activity31. Here, we explore this concept using cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), a chiral heme-peroxidase that catalyzes the oxidation of arachidonic acid (AA) to prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a key mediator of inflammation and validated therapeutic target in rheumatic arthritis (RA)32–34. We synthesized chiral gold nanohelices (CAu)—left-handed (Lh-CAu) and right-handed (Rh-CAu)—to enantioselectively modulate COX-2 activity via spin-selective electron transport: Lh-CAu enhanced the COX-2 activity owing to the identical CISS to COX-2, while Rh-CAu inhibited it because of the opposite CISS to COX-2. This stereospecific effect is attributed to the spin-selective compatibility between COX-2 and CAu enantiomers. To enable targeted enzyme modulation in complex biological settings, we further engineered molecular imprinted CAu (CAu@MIP), which selectively binds COX-2 and enriches chiral interface interactions. In activated macrophages and collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model, Rh-CAu@MIP treatment significantly reduces PGE2 production and alleviates inflammation, showing efficacy comparable to conventional COX-2 inhibitors (Fig. 1c). Our work establishes electron-spin polarization as an additional dimension of enzymatic regulation, offering a spintronic biointerface platform for precision modulation of inflammatory enzymes. It also broadens the conceptual toolkit for bioelectronic therapeutics and sheds light on spin–chirality coupling in biological catalysis.

Results

Activity of COX-2 regulated by spin-polarized electrons

Spin-polarized electrons exhibit enhanced catalytic efficiency owing to reduced scattering and prolonged active lifetime15,17,18,35,36. To examine whether COX-2 activity can be modulated by electron spin, we measured cyclic voltammetry (CV) of arachidonic acid (AA) oxidation on a custom electrochemical setup (Fig. 2a). A ferromagnetic Ni plate, coated first with Au nanoparticles to improve conductivity and enzyme loading, served as the working electrode. Applying an external magnetic field (MF) through the Ni substrate spin-polarizes the injected electrons, with polarity (north vs. south) determining the electron-spin orientation16,37,38. In the absence of MF (w/o MF), the CV curve exhibited an asymmetric profile with a cationic oxidation peak current at ~1.28 V (Ep) with a corrected peak current (Ip) of 2.03 ± 0.08 μA but without any obvious corresponding anionic reduction peak, indicating the irreversibility of the oxidation of AA (Fig. 2b, c). Under MF with the north pole of the magnet facing the working electrode (MF-N), Ip decreased to 1.07 ± 0.27 μA, whereas under the south pole orientation (MF-S), Ip increased to 2.52 ± 0.22 μA (Fig. 2b–d). These opposite shifts confirm that COX-2 catalytic activity is sensitive to the spin polarization of the incoming electrons. To determine whether the observed MF-dependent effects on AA oxidation arise from COX-2-mediated spin-selective electron transport, rather than a direct influence of spin-polarized electrons on AA itself, we performed control CV measurements on Ni electrodes coated with gold nanoparticles but without COX-2 modification (Fig. S1a). The CV curve in w/o MF showed an Ip of 1.85 ± 0.12 μA, and both values of Ip under MF-N and MF-S decreased, but did not show the bidirectional modulation observed in the COX-2-coated system (Fig. S1b–d), which is probably because the Lorentz force under MF increases the transmission dissipation of electrons and inhibits the oxidation of AA39. Therefore, the introduction of COX-2 as a catalyst for AA oxidation altered the electric response to MF, with inhibition under MF-N and promotion under MF-S, a typical feature of the CISS effect of chiral molecules, as evidenced by the circular dichroism (CD) spectra of COX-2 (Fig. 2e). To further validate intrinsic spin selectivity, we modeled electron transport through COX-2 as an α-helical protein and computed spin-resolved conductances using a tight-binding Hamiltonian40. (Fig. 2f). The calculated spin-up conductance (GN) and spin-down one (GS) diverge substantially across the whole energy spectrum, yielding highly large spin polarization (Fig. 2g, h). This behavior arises from the chiral structure-induced spin-orbit coupling, and holds for different α-helical protein of various sequences (Fig. S2), indicating that electrons of one specific spin orientation will preferentially transmit through COX-2. Taken together, these results confirm that the COX-2 catalytic activity in AA oxidation is enantioselectively modulated by the spin state of injected electrons (Fig. 2i, j). The AA oxidation by COX-2 has been studied to occur via two active domains: cyclooxygenase (COX) site embedded in short α-helical segments containing Tyr385 and Trp387, and the heme-containing peroxidase (POX) site, via an intramolecular electron transfer process41,42. The catalytic cycle starts at the POX site, where heme Fe3+ is oxidized by peroxide (ROOH) to form radical Intermediate I (Fe4+=O Por•+), which extracts one electron from Tyr 385 via Trp387, producing Tyr385• that abstracts a hydrogen atom from AA to form AA•. AA• then reacts with O2 to yield prostaglandin G2 (PGG2). PGG2, as a peroxide, donates an electron back to regenerate POX heme Fe3+, being reduced to prostaglandin H2 (PGH2), which is subsequently isomerized to PGE2 (Fig. 2i, j). CISS effect of CAu to generate spin-polarized electrons

To explore the potential of CAu as spin polarizers, we synthesized Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu, and racemic (RAC-CAu) nanohelices by helical overgrowth on Au nanorods using L-, D-, and RAC-cysteine as chiral inducing agents43–47. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images showed that Lh-, Rh- and RAC-CAu possess comparable dimensions in length (L), transverse width (W) and helical pitch (P) (Lh-CAu: L = 93.6 ± 12.2 nm, W = 32.7 ± 2.5 nm, P = 21.3 ± 2.2 nm; Rh-CAu: L = 96.9 ± 11.2 nm, W = 30.8 ± 3.1 nm, P = 22.7 ± 3.7 nm; RAC-CAu: L = 87.9 ± 13.3 nm, W = 28.4 ± 2.4 nm, P = 24.5 ± 2.5 nm) (Fig. 3a–c). And all three CAu variants shared similar zeta potentials of +20 ~ + 22 mV (Fig. S3) and a localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) maximum absorption at 760 nm (Fig. 3d). However, their chiroptical responses revealed pronounced differences. CD spectra revealed mirror-image signals for Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu, with characteristic Cotton effects at 530 and 729 nm, while RAC-CAu exhibited negligible CD signals (Fig. 3e). According to the CISS mechanism, chiral structures can preferentially transmit electrons of a specific spin orientation, functioning as spin filters. To test this, we deposited each Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu, or RAC-CAu onto a ferromagnetic Ni plate to form working electrodes and recorded CV profiles of the robust redox couple of Fe(CN)64− to Fe(CN)63− under MF (Fig. 3g). In this configuration, electrons are injected from the Ni surface toward CAu, the transport efficiency of spin-polarized electrons from the Ni electrode to CAu will strongly depend on the CISS effect of CAu. Under w/o MF, all CAu variants exhibited similar CV profiles (Fig. S4). However, when with MF, clear chirality-dependent shifts appeared in the CV curves of Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu, and these shifts changed to opposite directions with MF reverted (Fig. 3h, i). In contrast, RAC-CAu showed no significant changes with or without MF (Fig. 3j), resembling the behavior of achiral Au nanospheres (Fig. S5). It indicates that spin-polarized electrons induced by MF-S can selectively traverse Lh-CAu (spin matched), while those induced by MF-N cannot traverse Lh-CAu (spin mismatched). For Rh-CAu, the situation is opposite (Fig. 3k). These bidirectional CV shifts unambiguously demonstrate that Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu generate spin-polarized electrons with opposite spin orientations.

To substantiate these findings, we developed a theoretical model simulating electron transport through CAu-like helical geometry, which was constructed by etching a helical groove from achiral gold cylinders, and the cross sections at different positions of the remaining CAu resemble major sectors of different notch orientations (Figs. S6 and 3l–n). The calculated spin-resolved conductance results showed that, for Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu, GN with spin-up electrons injected was different from GS with spin-down electrons injected, and Gw/o with spin-unpolarized electrons injected fell between them (Fig. 3o, p). While for RAC-CAu, no observable difference existed between GN and GS (Fig. 3q). These numerical results demonstrate that both Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu exhibit strong but opposite spin selectivity, where the spin polarization was reversed between Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu (Fig. 3r). Further current (I) calculation also revealed the similar behaviors of IN, IS and Iw/o of CAu (Fig. 3s–u), as demonstrated in their GN, GS, and Gw/o. Moreover, the spin selectivity of Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu is robust against variations in helical pitch, radius, and structural defects (Figs. S7–12), revealing pronounced spin polarization of both systems in the presence of geometric or surface irregularities, ionic screening, or surface functionalization with different substances. These theoretical results align well with the experimental data shown in Fig. 3h–j, confirming that both Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu function as effective spin polarizers, generating spin-polarized electrons with opposite orientations depending on chirality.

Enantioselective modulation of COX-2 by CAu

Building on our demonstration that COX-2 activity is sensitive to electron spin and that CAu can generate spin-polarized electrons, we tested whether CAu enantiomers could directly modulate COX-2 catalysis. We first employed a resorufin-based fluorescence assay, in which signal intensity scales linearly with COX-2 activity. Incubation of COX-2 with Lh-CAu increased activity modestly to 105.2 ± 5.0% relative to enzyme alone (set to 100%), whereas Rh-CAu and RAC-CAu reduced activity to 52.0 ± 2.5% and 62.3 ± 3.0%, respectively (Fig. 4a, b). These results demonstrate clear enantioselective modulation of COX-2 activity by CAu. To corroborate the fluorescence findings, we immobilized COX-2 directly on each CAu variants, forming Lh-CAu@COX-2, Rh-CAu@COX-2 and RAC-CAu@COX-2, respectively, and measured the CV profiles of AA oxidation on a Pt electrode (Fig. S13). Consistent with the fluorescence data: Lh-CAu@COX-2 increased Ip from 2.07 ± 0.1 μA (COX-2 alone) to 2.38 ± 0.13 μA, whereas Rh-CAu@COX-2 and RAC-CAu@COX-2 reduced Ip to 1.42 ± 0.06 and 1.61 ± 0.07 μA, respectively (Fig. 4c, d, and S14). Furthermore, we experimentally and theoretically studied the modulation of COX-2 activity with varied enantiomeric excess (ee) of CAu. The results revealed an approximately linear correlation between the relative modulation of CAu on COX-2 and ee (Fig. S15), demonstrating that the CAu can regulate COX-2 activity in an enantioselective and quantitatively predictable manner. And the theoretical results consistently showed good linear relationship between current and ee values (Fig. S16). Mechanistic dissection: cooperative CISS-driven electron transport underlines the enantioselective modulation of CAu to COX-2 activity

Chiral nanomaterials can influence enzyme activity through three possible mechanisms: (i) nanozymatic property28,48–50, (ii) active site change27,51,52, or (iii) electron transfer29,31. To pinpoint which mechanism drives the enantioselective regulation of CAu to COX-2 activity, we performed the following studies: first, we studied the possible mechanism (i), the nanozymatic property of CAu. For the electrochemical studies without COX-2, CV curves of AA oxidation obtained with electrodes coated with Lh-, Rh- or RAC-CAu all showed enhanced Ip, which was independent on the CAu chirality albeit of different enhancement times (Fig. S17), indicating that all CAu variants exhibit weak COX-2-like enzymatic properties. Since this enhancement did not depend on the chirality of CAu, we exclude intrinsic nanozymatic activity (mechanism i) as responsible for enantioselective regulation of COX-2 activity.

Regarding mechanism (ii), in which CAu enantiomers may enantioselectively interact with the active sites of COX-2, or mechanism (iii), in which CAu enantioselectively change the electron transport process, we investigated the influence of MF on CAu@COX-2 catalyzed oxidation of AA by measuring CV curves, as only the electron transport process instead of the interaction with the active site of COX-2 will be changed by MF. Therefore, a nonmagnetic Pt plate instead of magnetic Ni plate was cast by Lh-CAu@COX-2, Rh-CAu@COX-2 and RAC-CAu@COX-2 to form the working electrodes with MF applied (Figs. 4e and S18). Using nonmagnetic electrodes ensures that any MF-dependent response arises from CISS-induced spin polarization within the CAu@COX-2 system, rather than from spin-polarized injection by the electrode. Since MF can perturb spin transport through the Peierls phase to alter electron spin evolution and transport efficiency53,54, it allows us to selectively probe CISS-mediated electron transport while excluding active-site effects. The CV curves showed that for all these CAu@COX-2, the application of MF-N suppressed the AA oxidation, while MF-S promoted it to a certain degree (Figs. S19 and 4f–i). As RAC-CAu lacks the CISS effect, the influence of MF on RAC-CAu@COX-2 likely originates from the CISS effect of COX-2. While RAC-CAu@COX-2 showed inhibited COX-2 activity, it may relate to the passivation of active sites due to the adsorption of CAu (Figs. 4d, h, i), although such adsorption caused negligible changes in the secondary structure of COX-2 (Figs. S20 and S21). Therefore, it turns out that mechanism (ii) is excluded from the enantioselective regulation of COX-2 activity, while mechanism (iii) is implicated.

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, we simulated the electron transport by injecting unpolarized electrons from a nonmagnetic electrode into CAu@COX-2 constructs and the conductance was calculated for electrons ejected from all the terminals of these COX-2. (Fig. 4j). Spin-resolved conductance calculations revealed that for all these CAu@COX-2, GS > GN across a broad energy range, and the conductance without MF Gw/o fell between GS and GN (Fig. S22). Further calculations on the current–voltage characteristics of all these CAu@COX-2 were plotted in Fig. 4k–m, showing that IS was the largest, followed by Iw/o and IN (Fig. 4n), demonstrating the electron transport efficiency varied with MF directions for various CAu@COX-2. All the above results further confirm the critical role of the mechanism (iii) in enantioselective regulation of COX-2 activity.

Taken together, CAu influences COX-2 activity through three additive contributions: (i) the nanozymatic enhancement of CAu, which is weak and achiral (Fig. S17); (ii) active-site passivation by surface adsorption of CAu, yielding chirality-independent inhibition (Fig. 4h); (iii) cooperative CISS filtering at the CAu-COX-2 interface, which contributes to the enantioselective regulation of COX-2 activity (Fig. 4i, n). Notably, although nanozymatic activity (mechanism i) and active-site passivation (mechanism ii) clearly contributes to overall activity modulation, it does not account for the chirality-dependent differences between Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu. Remarkably, spin-polarized electron transfer via CISS can modulate the passive inhibition by CAu adsorption. Thus, the relative contributions of the three mechanisms were quantified for each CAu sample via a simple sum model and are presented in the radar plot (Fig. 4o). This visualization highlights how CISS selectively modulates COX-2 activity beyond the additive effects of catalytic activity and active-site passivation, providing a quantitative framework for the enantioselective regulation.

Cellular inflammation regulation

Inhibiting the expression and activity of COX-2 has been proven effective in alleviating inflammation34,52. Given that CAu can enantioselectively modulate COX-2 activity via spin-polarized electron transfer, to assess the biomedical potential of our spintronic platform, CAu was utilized to regulate COX-2 activity on inflammatory cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated RAW 264.7 macrophages (Fig. 5a). To improve the specific recognition of COX-2 and minimize the nonspecific interaction with various biomolecules, CAu was molecularly imprinted against COX-2 through dopamine-based molecule imprinting process, forming CAu@MIP (Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP, and RAC-CAu@MIP), with TEM images shown in Fig. 5b–d, hydrodynamic sizes at the range of 111.0–118.9 nm (Fig. S23), and zeta potentials of −10.1 ~ − 13.5 mV (Fig. S24). Both experiments and theoretical simulations demonstrate that CAu coated with the MIP (CAu@MIP), maintains high spin selectivity comparable to that of the pristine CAu without the MIP (Figs. S25 and S26). All these CAu@MIP exhibited minimal cytotoxicity against LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells as assessed by CCK assay (Fig. S27). Cy5.5-labeled Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP, and RAC-CAu@MIP (Lh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, Rh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, and RAC-CAu@MIPCy5.5) were efficiently internalized by inflammatory cells without significant difference, as confirmed by confocal fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry (Figs. 5e, f, and S28). Subsequently, the expression and activity of COX-2 were assessed by Western blot (Fig. 5g,h) and PGE2 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), respectively (Fig. 5i). LPS stimulation significantly increased the expression of COX-2 (Fig. 5g), confirming the presence of inflammation in inflammatory cells. Treatment with Lh-CAu@MIP increased the expression of COX-2 to 118.3%, whereas treatments with Rh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP reduced the expression to 18.4 and 41.7%, respectively. Celecoxib, a known COX-2 inhibitor, reduced the expression of COX-2 to 7.1%, in terms of COX-2 activity (Fig. 5h). Consistently, following LPS stimulation, the secretion of PGE2 increased to 334.0 ± 20.4 pg mL−1, compared to 50.10 ± 1.9 pg mL−1 without LPS stimulation (Fig. 5i). Further treatment with Lh-CAu@MIP slightly increased PGE2 level to 345.7 ± 39.6 pg mL−1, while treatments with Rh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP decreased the levels to 98.0 ± 1.8 and 163.9 ± 12.3 pg mL−1, respectively. As a positive control, treatment with celecoxib decreased PGE2 level to 126.4 ± 13.7 pg mL−1. Notably, Rh-CAu@MIP showed a lower PGE2 level than celecoxib, while the statistical analysis resulted in a P > 0.05, possibly due to the limited sample size. Additionally, the expression of CD86, a surface mark indicative of inflammatory responses, was evaluated by flow cytometry. LPS stimulation could increase the CD86-expressed cells to 65.2% compared to 12.3% without LPS stimulation (Fig. 5j). This percentage increased to 79.9% by further treatment with Lh-CAu@MIP, but decreased to 47.8 and 60.0% following treatments with Rh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP, respectively. Furthermore, the typical inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) were assessed by ELISA (Figs. S29 and S30). Treatment with Lh-CAu@MIP notably increased the levels of both cytokines, while treatments with Rh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP reduced these levels, similar to the effect of celecoxib. To uncover how CAu@MIP variants reprogram macrophage inflammation at the molecular level, we performed integrated proteomic and untargeted metabolomic profiling of LPS-stimulated cells treated with Lh-, Rh-, or RAC-CAu@MIP or celecoxib. Hierarchical clustering analysis of AA metabolism-associated proteins and metabolites was conducted to identify distinct regulatory patterns across the treatment groups. Rheumatic arthritis therapy in vivo

Building on the potent enantioselective COX-2 activity regulation with CAu observed in vitro, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of CAu@MIP in the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model. Six to eight-week-old male DBA/1J mice were immunized to establish CIA-induced RA model, in which typical RA symptoms, including erythema and swelling in the limb, were observed54–56. To detect the targeting ability of CAu@MIP to the inflammatory joints where COX-2 is highly overexpressed, the metabolism profiles of CAu@MIP were studied using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system, where intravenously injected Cy5.5-labeled CAu@MIP (CAu@MIPCy5.5) accumulated at joints and ankles at 4 h post-injection, peaking at 12 h and dissipating at 48 h. In comparison, Au@DOPCy5.5, which was Cy5.5-labeled and dopamine-coated Au NPs but without molecular imprinting against COX-2, showed weak fluorescence signal at 6–24 h post-injection, while Cy5.5-labeled dopamine (DopCy5.5) showed no signal at the inflammatory sites (Figs. 6a and S31). Thus, to investigate the therapeutic effect of CAu@MIP, RA mice were divided into 5 groups receiving PBS, celecoxib, Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP, respectively, through intravenous injection every other day for consecutive 12 days (Fig. 6c). During the treatment, RA severity was evaluated by the thickness and erythema of the ankle or paw of the mice and scored56,57. Rh-CAu@MIP group showed the continuous and significant decrease in the thickness and erythema of the paw, and RAC-CAu@MIP group showed moderate improvement, but the Lh-CAu@MIP group had minimal effects similar to the PBS group (Figs. 6d–f and S34).

At the end of treatment, the paws of mice were collected for further evaluation of the therapeutic effects by micro-CT imaging, histological analysis and immunohistochemical analysis. Micro-CT images showed that Rh-CAu@MIP effectively impeded the bone erosion in the small joints of paws and ankles, and remarkably reduced the bone porosity of ankle joints and metatarsophalangeal joints, and Lh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP partially alleviated the bone damage, whereas PBS resulted in severe bone destruction (Fig. 6g). The pathological sections of bone joints revealed that severe inflammation (Fig. 6h, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining), narrow articular cavity, formation of pannus (Fig. 6h, Masson’s trichrome staining) and damaged cartilage (Fig. 6h, safranin O staining) were observed in PBS group (Fig. 6h). These symptoms were mildly mitigated in the RAC-CAu@MIP group. Conversely, the celecoxib and Rh-CAu@MIP group exhibited marked improvements, with enlarged articular cavity, reduced inflammation and clear bone-cartilage separation. Since PGE2 is the direct effector of inflammation induced by COX-2 and its expression reflects the COX-2 activity, the immunohistochemical staining of PGE2 in the limbs indicated that Rh-CAu@MIP effectively reduced the expression of PGE2, similar to celecoxib (Fig. 6i). Additionally, systemic inflammatory markers, such as TNF-α and IL-1β levels in the plasma, were significantly reduced in the Rh-CAu@MIP and celecoxib groups, aligning with normal levels. This further confirmed a systemic reduction in inflammation (Figs. S35 and S36).

To assess the biocompatibility of CAu@MIP, the body weight of RA mice was continuously monitored, showing minimal variation across groups (Fig. S37). Comprehensive blood routine and biochemical analysis revealed most hematological parameters within the normal ranges (Fig. S38). Liver function markers (alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)) along with blood urea nitrogen (BUN) indicated no significant toxicity associated with Lh-CAu@MIP (Fig. S39). In addition, H&E-stained sections of major organs revealed no discernible difference compared to the PBS group (Fig. S40). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CAu@MIP exhibit excellent biocompatibility and promising enantioselective therapeutic efficacy in RA.

Discussion

In this study, we explored a fundamentally distinct mechanism for enzyme regulation via electron spin polarization. Rather than altering enzyme conformation or targeting catalytic sites, our approach leverages the CISS effect to control electron transport efficiency across enzyme-nanomaterial interfaces. Lh-CAu, possessing spin orientation aligned with COX-2, facilitated spin-consistent electron transport and preserved enzymatic activity. In contrast, Rh-CAu, with an opposite spin configuration, imposed energy-intensive spin flipping and thereby suppressed electron transport, resulting in substantial inhibition of COX-2 activity. Importantly, this spin-mediated regulation was retained in vivo when CAu@MIP was constructed for COX-2-specific targeting, enabling site-specific delivery and selective enzyme inhibition. Rh-CAu@MIP achieved potent anti-inflammatory effects in the RA mouse model, comparable to the clinical COX-2 inhibitor celecoxib, yet with a mechanistically orthogonal mode of action. These findings suggest that spin state can serve as a key regulatory element for enzymatic function.

Our findings define a third, dynamic paradigm for enzyme regulation, centered on spin-dependent electron transport, complementing conventional structure-based or charge-based approaches. This work introduces a conceptual shift: enzyme activity can be modulated not solely through static structural engagement, but via the spin dynamics of electron flow at the molecular level. The integration of spintronics with enzymology not only expands our understanding of bioelectronic interactions but also opens the door to precision biocatalysis and next-generation therapeutics. The demonstrated in vivo efficacy and selectivity of CISS-based modulation underscore its translational promise, particularly in redox-related pathologies. Beyond COX-2, the principle of spin-selective electron transport may also apply to other oxidoreductases that rely on multistep electron relay pathways. Preliminary experiments with Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu on laccase and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) revealed enzyme-dependent, enantioselective modulation of catalytic activity, indicating broader potential applicability (Fig. S41). However, as redox catalysis involves multiple coupled factors, definitive attribution of such effects to CISS requires systematic, enzyme-specific studies. While such exploration lies beyond the present scope, our COX-2 system provides a robust framework, highlighting CISS as a promising generalizable mechanism for redox enzyme regulation. As such, this study establishes a conceptual basis for spin-engineered nanomedicines and broadens current understanding of spin-biological interfaces.

Method

Ethical statement

All animal studies were conducted at the Changchun Institute of Applied Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CIAC-CAS). Experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Committee for Animal Research of CIAC-CAS (no. 2021-08).

Materials

Chloroauric acid (HAuCl4·3H2O, ≥47.8%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, 99.99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥96%), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 96%), L-cysteine (L-Cys, 99%), D-cysteine (D-Cys, 99%), potassium ferricyanide (K3Fe(CN)6, 99.5%), potassium ferrocyanide (K4Fe(CN)6, 99.6%), 10-acetyl-3,7-dihydroxyphenoxazine (ADPH, 98%), cyclooxygenase (COX-2, >90%), acetic acid (AR, 99.8%), dopamine (98%), Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 92.5–100.5%), acetone (99.5%), perchloric acid (HClO4, 70–72%), ethanol (95%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, ≥96%), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), acetonitrile (99.9%), sodium perchlorate (NaClO4, 95%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, ≥98%) was purchased from Acros Organics. Arachidonic acid (AA, ≥90%), 4-mercaptobenzoic acid (≥ 95%) and hydroquinone (HQ, 99%) were received from Aladdin. All chemicals were used as supplied by the manufacturers without additional purification.

Characterization

Electrochemical measurements were conducted using a Chi 660E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua, China). The morphology and primary size of nanomaterials were examined by transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL-1400, Japan). Fluorescence emission spectra were acquired on an Agilent Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer (USA). Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of nanomaterials were detected on a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 analyzer (Malvern, UK).

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were acquired using a circular dichroism spectroscopy (JASCO 1500, Japan). The CD spectra of COX-2 was measured with the following parameters: scan range, 180–900 nm; scanning speed, 50 nm min−1; data pitch, 3 nm; band width, 2 nm; 3 repeats. The CD spectra of CAu were obtained with the following parameters: scan range, 200–900 nm; scanning speed, 100 nm min−1; data pitch: 0.5 nm; band width, 5 nm; 3 repeats.

Preparation of CAu, CAu@COX-2 and CAu@MIP

CAu with different chiralities, including Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu and RAC-CAu, were prepared via chiral thiol-mediated overgrowth on gold nanorods (GNRs).

GNRs were prepared first as the seed for the growth of CAu. Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu and RAC-CAu were synthesized by adding L-/D-/RAC-Cys during the overgrowth process, respectively. The detailed experimental procedures were described as follows:

First, Au seed was synthesized by reducing HAuCl4 (0.1 mL, 25 mmol L−1) with freshly prepared ice-cold NaBH4 (600 μL, 10 mmol L−1) in an aqueous CTAB solution (10 mL, 100 mmol L−1). The light brown seed was formed after 3 min vigorous stirring, and the solution was kept still with cap-opened at 30 °C for 3 h to allow the unreacted NaBH4 oxidized by oxygen in air.

Second, GNRs were prepared by adding 160 μL of well-prepared seed solution into a growth solution: HQ (500 μL, 0.1 mol L−1) was added into a mixed aqueous solution of CTAB (10 mL, 100 mmol L−1), HAuCl4 (200 μL, 25 mmol L−1), AgNO3 (70 μL, 0.1 mol L−1). The reaction was taken for 12 h at 30 °C to obtain GNRs. The resulting GNRs were centrifuged twice (14,000 × g, 5 min) and redispersed in an aqueous solution containing 10 mmol L−1 CTAB for further synthesis of CAu.

Finally, for the synthesis of Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu or RAC-CAu, 60 μL of 10 mmol L−1 L-Cys or D-Cys or 60 μL of an equal amount mixture of L-Cys and D-Cys (30 μL each, 10 mmol L−1) was added to 10 mL of GNR aqueous solution (0.1 nmol L−1, Abs400 nm = 0.2) stabilized with 10 mmol L−1 CTAB, respectively, and sit still at 30 °C for 3 h. Then, AgNO3 (70 μL, 10 mmol L−1), HAuCl4 (56 μL, 25 mmol L−1) and ascorbic acid (32 μL, 100 mmol L−1) were quickly added sequentially and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was allowed to grow at 70 °C for 1 h, during which the solution color gradually changed from light red to bluish purple. The solution was centrifuged twice (14,000 × g, 5 min) to obtain Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu or RAC-CAu.

For preparation of CAu@COX-2, 0.1 mL of 2 mg mL−1 COX-2 was added to 10 mL of 0.5 nmol L−1 Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu or RAC-CAu (Abs400 nm = 1.0) aqueous solution, respectively, followed by ultrasonication in a water bath for 15 min and incubation at 30 °C for 12 h. Then, unbound COX-2 was removed by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 5 min) to obtain Lh-CAu@COX-2, Rh-CAu@COX-2, RAC-CAu@COX-2, respectively.

Then, for the preparation of CAu@MIP, CAu@COX-2 synthesized by the above method was dispersed in 10 mL of deionized (DI) H2O, and the pH was adjusted to 8.5 using 1 mol L−1 NaOH. Then, 500 μL of 2 mg mL−1 dopamine was added to 10 mL of CAu@COX-2 dispersion under vigorous stirring to react for 2 h. After that, the dopamine-coated CAu@COX-2 was centrifuged (14,000 × g, 5 min) and washed five times with a 3% (v/v) acetic acid solution containing 0.1% (w/v) SDS, and then washed thoroughly with distilled water 3 times.

Cy5.5-labeled CAu@MIP (CAu@MIPCy5.5, including Lh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, Rh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, and RAC-CAu@MIPCy5.5) were prepared via electrostatic interactions between Cy5.5 and the dopamine shell. In detail, 10 μL 5 mg mL−1 Cy5.5 (DMSO) was added into 5 mL dispersion of Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP and RAC-CAu@MIP (equivalent to 3 mg mL−1 Au), respectively. After ultrasonicated for 5 min in a water bath, the mixture was sit still for 3 h for efficient modification, then followed by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 5 min) for 4 times to remove unbound Cy5.5 to obtain CAu@MIPCy5.5. The loading concentration of Cy5.5 on CAu@MIPCy5.5 was calculated to be ~1.25 μg mL−1 by measuring the fluorescence spectra of Cy5.5, which showed obvious emission peak around 690 nm.

Electrochemical measurements setup

The electrochemical measurements were performed in a typical three-electrode cell using an electrochemical workstation (Chi 660e, Shanghai, China). Ni or Pt plate (size 1 cm × 1 cm), an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) electrode, and a Pt wire were used as the working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively. Prior to use, Ni or Pt plates were cleaned in boiling acetone and ethanol for 20 min, and then used as the working electrode to remove the oxides on the surface with the potential set at −1.0 V in 1 mol L−1 HClO4 to run for 20 min, then washed with acetone and ethanol again. All the glassware used in the electrochemical experiments were carefully cleaned with piranha solution (H2SO4:H2O2 = 7:3 (v:v)). A permanent magnet of a field strength H = 1.2 T was placed close to the working electrode for investigating the influence of MF. The field strength on the Ni or Pt surface was measured to be 0.8 T using a digital Gauss meter.

CV curves of AA oxidation without COX-2

The well-cleaned Ni plate was spin-coated with ~8 nm Au nanoparticles (NPs) using a vacuum spin coater (Shenzhen Kejing, VTC-100B), followed by rinsing with DI H2O to remove unadsorbed Au NPs. Then the Au NP-deposited Ni plate was used as the working electrode for CV measurements. Acetic acid containing 20% (w/v) acetonitrile and 0.5 mol L−1 NaClO4 was used as the electrolytes. After purging N2 for 30 min to displace the air in electrolytes, 10 mmol L−1 AA was added, and the CV curves were measured using the following parameters: initial potential, −0.4 V; final potential, 1.6 V; scan rate, 50 mV s−1. To study the influence of external MF, a permanent magnet was placed close to the Ni plate outside the electrochemical measurement cell. The MF direction was reversed by simply flipping the magnet.

All electrochemical measurements involving AA oxidation were independently repeated three times under identical conditions. Specifically, each Ni (or Pt) working electrode was prepared as a single large film and then cut into three electrodes of equal size. Freshly prepared AA solution was evenly divided for the three replicates. For each sample, only the first cycle of the cyclic voltammogram (CV) was analyzed to avoid surface passivation or accumulation effects. The original CV curves obtained from three independent experiments were averaged as the final CV curves shown. Then the positive semi-cycle of CV curves were baseline fitted and subtracted to generate background-corrected CV curves for peak current determination and comparison. Peak currents from the background-corrected positive semi-cycle of CV curves obtained from the three independent measurements were used to calculate the mean ± standard deviation (SD), which are represented as error bars in all relevant figures. This protocol was applied consistently across all AA oxidation-related CV measurements unless otherwise stated.

CV measurement of AA oxidation with COX-2

Following deposition with Au NPs, the Ni plate was immersed in 2 mg mL−1 COX-2 at 4 °C with shaking for 12 h to adsorb COX-2 onto the surface, and then washed with DI H2O. To check if COX-2 was successfully modified on Au NP-deposited Ni plate, the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) was measured before CV measurements. The measurement parameters were set to the same as that case without COX-2 modification.

CISS effect of CAu

In order to better modify CAu on Ni plate, the well-cleaned Ni plate was first immersed in 10 mmol L−1 4-mercaptobenzoic acid at 4 °C for 12 h. After washing with DI water, the Ni plate was immersed in a 5 mg mL−1 solution of Lh-CAu, Rh-CAu, RAC-CAu or achiral Au NPs for 3 h. The EIS spectra were measured to confirm the successful deposition of Au NPs on the Ni plate. Then, the CAu-modified Ni plate was used as the working electrode for CV measurement. 4 mmol L−1 K4[Fe(CN)6] and K3[Fe(CN)6] were used as the redox pair, and 0.1 mol L−1 KOH was employed as the supporting electrolyte for CV curve scanning. The measurement parameters were set as: initial potential, −0.1 V; final potential, 0.5 V; scan rate, 50 mV s−1. For each condition, at least three independent experiments were conducted, and the final stable CV curves for each experiment were employed for analysis.

Enantioselective regulation of CAu on COX-2 activity

The well-cleaned Pt plate was immersed in a solution of Lh-CAu@COX-2, Rh-CAu@COX-2, and RAC-CAu@COX-2, respectively, at 4 °C for 12 h. Then, the Lh-CAu@COX-2-, Rh-CAu@COX-2-, or RAC-CAu@COX-2-deposited Pt plate was used as the working electrode to measure the CV curves of AA oxidation. The measurement parameters were set as: initial potential, −0.4 V; final potential, 1.6 V; scan rate, 50 mV s−1. To study the influence of external MF, a permanent magnet was placed close to the Pt plate outside the electrochemical measurement cell. The MF direction was reversed by simply flipping the magnet.

Determination of enzyme activity regulation effects of CAu

CAu with varied ee values (100%, 80%, 40%, 20%, 0, −20%, -40%, −80%, and −100%) was obtained by mixing different concentration Lh-CAu and Rh-CAu. Then CAu ([Au0] = 100 μg mL−1) with varied 7 ee values were pre-incubate with 2 mg mL−1 enzyme (COX-2, laccase or LDH) at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, the mixture of CAu and enzymes were employed for enzyme activity determination with the corresponding kit according to the product description. Cyclooxygenase 2 Inhibitor Screening Kit (Beyotime, Lot#S0168), micro laccase assay kit (Acmec, Lot# AC10346) and lacate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity assay kit (Acmec, Lot# AC10186) were employed for determining the activity of COX-2, laccase and LDH, respectively.

Cell culture

RAW 264.7 murine macrophages purchased from ATCC were employed for in vitro experiments and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (10 U mL−1) and streptomycin (10 μg mL−1) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were passaged routinely and used during the logarithmic growth phase. To establish an inflammatory phenotype, RAW 264.7 cells were exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng mL−1) for 12 h prior to subsequent treatments, as specified for individual experiments.

Cytotoxicity assessment of CAu@MIP

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells per well) and allowed to stabilize and grow overnight. Then, the medium was changed into 100 μL of fresh DMEM containing 0, 25, 50, 100, 200 μg mL−1 (calculated in [Au0]) of Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP, or RAC-CAu@MIP, respectively. After co-culture for 24 h, 10 μL cell counting kit 8 (CCK8, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) reagent was added into each well for 2 h of incubation at 37 °C. Then, the 96-well plates were used for measuring the absorbance at 450 nm by microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, USA). Wells without cells were applied as blanks.

Endocytosis and distribution of CAu@MIP in macrophages

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates pre-placed with coverslips (3.0 × 105 cells per well) and stimulated with 100 ng mL−1 LPS for 6 h. Then, the medium was replaced by DMEM containing CAu@MIPCy5.5 (Lh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, Rh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, and RAC-CAu@MIPCy5.5) ([Au0] = 100 μg mL−1) and co-cultured for 12 h. Then, after washing with PBS, cells were fixed by 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde, followed by staining (FITC-labeled wheat germ agglutinin (FITC-WGA) (2 μg mL−1), 15 min, for cell membrane; Hoechst 33,342 (100 nmol L−1), 3 min, for nuclei). For the endocytosis study, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD AccuriTM C6 plus flow cytometer, USA). The data were analyzed by FlowJo v10.0. For the distribution study of nanoparticles in cells, the coverslips were used for fluorescence imaging under a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCP SP8, USA).

COX-2 expression analyses

COX-2 expression levels in inflammatory cells were analyzed by Western blotting. RAW 264.7 cells seeded in 6-well plates with a density of 3.0 × 105 cells per well were first stimulated with 100 ng mL−1 LPS for 6 h. Then, the medium was replaced by DMEM containing Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP, RAC-CAu@MIP ([Au0] = 100 μg mL−1) or celecoxib (100 nM), respectively, for an additional 12 h of co-culture at 37 °C. Following treatment, cells were harvested and lysed on ice with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, CAT#P0013B) containing protease inhibitors. After centrifugation (14,000 × g, 15 min), the supernatant was collected, and the total protein concentration for each sample was determined using a BCA assay. Proteins were loaded in equal amounts, and resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE and then electronically transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with a blocking buffer (5% skimmed milk in TBST solution) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with a primary antibody against COX-2 (Abcam, Ab28,672) at 4 °C overnight. Membranes were then washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature before enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) imaging, which were recorded using a Tanon 2500 imaging system (Shanghai, China). The uncropped blot images are provided in the Source data file.

Cell inflammatory phenotype determination by flow cytometry

After cells co-cultured with Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP, RAC-CAu@MIP ([Au0] = 100 μg mL−1) or celecoxib (100 nM) for 12 h, respectively, the cell pellets were collected by centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min) and then stained with antibodies of FITC-anti-F4/80, PE-anti-CD86, and APC-anti-CD206 (BioLegend, Cat# 12,3107, 10,5007, 14,1708) according to the instructed conditions. Flow cytometric analysis was then conducted using a flow cytometer (Accuri C6 Plus, BD Biosciences, USA). Cells were first gated on forward scatter (FSC) versus side scatter (SSC) to exclude debris and doublets, ensuring analysis of viable single cells. A total of 10,000 FITC-positive events were randomly collected for each sample. FITC+ cells were subsequently analyzed for fluorescence intensity in the PE and APC channels to determine the proportion of CD86+ (M1-like) and CD206+ (M2-like) macrophages. Figure S42 refers to the PBS group exemplifies the gating strategy for all samples.

Cytokines PGE2, TNF-α, and IL-1β quantification by ELISA

After the indicated treatments, culture supernatants from RAW 264.7 cells were collected and clarified by centrifugation to remove cellular debris. The concentrations of PGE2, TNF-α, and IL-1β were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (PGE2: Abcam, Ab287802; TNF-α: BioLegend, 430901; IL-1β: BioLegend, 575109). Absorbance signals were recorded using a microplate reader, and cytokine levels were calculated based on standard curves measured for each assay.

Proteomics and untargeted metabolomics analysis

RAW 264.7 cells subjected to the same treatments as described in Section ‘COX-2 expression analyses’ were harvested by centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min) and washed twice with PBS. Cell pellets were stored at −80 °C until further proteomic and untargeted metabolomic analysis.

For proteomics, frozen cell pellets were ice lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were then measured using a BCA assay. For each sample, 100 μg protein was digested via the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol. The resulting peptides were desalted and vacuum-dried. Peptide analysis was conducted on a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved on a C18 analytical column (150 μm × 15 cm, Evosep) with a linear gradient of 5–35% acetonitrile for 120 min with a flow rate of 300 nL min−1. Raw data were processed using Proteome Discoverer™ Software 2.4 against the UniProt mouse protein database. The false discovery rate was controlled below 1% at both peptide and protein levels, with a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm and fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was used for relative protein quantification. To focus on inflammation-related pathways mediated by arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism, differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified by a fold change ≥1.5 and p < 0.05 (t-test). AA metabolism-related proteins were then curated by cross-referencing DEPs with known pathway databases, including: KEGG pathway: mmu00590 (arachidonic acid metabolism); Reactome pathway: “arachidonic acid metabolism” and “biosynthesis of eicosanoids”; Gene Ontology (GO) terms related to: “arachidonic acid metabolic process” (GO:0019369) and “eicosanoid biosynthetic process” (GO:0006690). The final list included key enzymes, such as PTGS2, PTGS, PTGES, PTGES2, PLA2G4A, PLA2G6, PTGS1/2, PTGES, PTGES2, and ALOX5 family members involved in AA processing. The expression profiles of these AA metabolism-related proteins in different groups were then subjected to hierarchical clustering analysis.

For metabolomics, cell pellets were extracted in pre-chilled methanol: acetonitrile:water (2:2:1, v/v/v), vortexed, and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles. After centrifugation (14,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), the supernatants were collected, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 80% methanol. Metabolite profiling was acquired using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was performed using a HSS T3 column under both positive and negative ionization modes. Raw data were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corp.) for peak alignment, deconvolution, and normalization. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching MS/MS spectra to public databases, including HMDB, METLIN, and KEGG. Metabolites associated with AA metabolism were identified based on KEGG annotations and manual curation of key intermediates and eicosanoids, including: arachidonic acid (AA), PGE₂, PGA1, PGA2, PGD2, PGJ2, 15d-PGJ2, PGF2α, LTB4, and 20-HETE. Then, the expression data of the curated AA-associated metabolites in different groups were used for subsequent hierarchical clustering analysis.

Both proteomic and metabolomic clustering heatmaps were generated using the Bioinformatics Online Platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn, last accessed: Dec 10, 2024). Prior to clustering, all values were Z-score normalized across samples. Hierarchical clustering was obtained using Euclidean distance and the complete linkage method; color gradients were used to represent relative abundance (blue: low, red: high).

Animal

DBA/1J mice (male, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Only male mice were used in this study to align with the well-established protocols for the CIA-induced RA model. All animals were maintained under a specific pathogen-free conditions at around 20 °C, with 40–60% relative humidity, on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and with free access to food and water.

Establishment of CIA-induced RA mouse model

Male DBA/1J mice (~15 g, 6–8 weeks old) were employed for building the RA model by the CIA method. Briefly, each DBA/1J mouse was subcutaneously immunized by injection of 100 μL of homogeneously emulsified mixture of 50 μL of type II bovine collagen (2 mg mL−1) and 50 μL of Freund’s Complete Adjuvant (CFA) containing mycobacterium tuberculosis (2 mg mL−1) at the base of the tail. On day 21, 100 μL homogeneously emulsified mixture of 50 μL type II bovine collagen (2 mg mL−1) and 50 μL Freund’s Incomplete Adjuvant (IFA) containing mycobacterium tuberculosis (2 mg mL−1) was subcutaneously administered into the mice at the base of the tail as a booster injection to induce arthritis. On day 28, mice with red and swelled paws and joints were observed, suggesting the successful establishment of the CIA mouse model.

Biodistribution analysis

Biodistribution of CAu@MIP in RA mice was investigated using Cy5.5-labeled CAu@MIP (CAu@MIPCy5.5). DOPCy5.5 and CAu@DOPCy5.5 were also prepared for comparison. In detail, for DOPCy5.5, 5 mg dopamine hydrocholoride powder was added to 10 mL H2O with pH pre-adjusted to 8.5 under vigorous stirring, after reaction for 1 h, 20 μL of 5 mg mL−1 Cy5.5 dissolved in DMSO was quickly added, and continued to react for 2 h, and the obtained greyish solution was centrifuged in 14,000 × g for 30 min to settle down the dopamine nanoparticles (DOPCy5.5). CAu@DOPCy5.5 was prepared using the similar protocol to CAu@MIPCy5.5, but skipping the step of COX-2 adsorption. Lh-CAu was dispersed in 10 mL of deionized H2O, and the pH was adjusted to 8.5 using 1 mol L−1 NaOH. Then, 500 μL of 2 mg mL−1 dopamine was added under vigorous stirring and allowed to react for 2 h. Then the dopamine-coated CAu (CAu@DOP) obtained by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 5 min) for 3 times and redispersed in deionized H2O, and the concentration of CAu@DOP was adjusted to ~15 nM (equivalent to 3 mg mL−1 Au). Then, 10 μL of 5 mg mL−1 Cy5.5 (DMSO) was added into 5 mL dispersion of Lh-CAu@Dop, followed by 5 min ultrasonication in water bath and sit still for 3 h before centrifugation (14,000 × g, 5 min) for 4 times to remove unbound Cy5.5 to obtain CAu@DOPCy5.5.

RA mice were intravenously injected with 100 μL of DOPCy5.5, Lh-CAu@DOPCy5.5, Lh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, Rh-CAu@MIPCy5.5, RAC-CAu@MIPCy5.5 (equivalent to 3 mg mL−1 Au or 1.25 μg mL−1 Cy5.5), and then the mice were imaged on an in vivo fluorescence imaging system (VISQUE InVivo Smart-LF, Viewworks, Korea) at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post injection. For ex vivo imaging, mice were similarly treated as described above, and at 12 h post-injection, mice were euthanized, and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and limbs were harvested for fluorescence imaging.

Therapeutic treatment

RA mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5 per group) and intravenously administered with 100 μL of PBS, Lh-CAu@MIP, Rh-CAu@MIP, RAC-CAu@MIP ([Au0] = 3 mg mL−1, 20 mg kg−1), and celecoxib (100 nmol L−1) every other day for 2 weeks. During the treatment, the hind paw thickness of each mouse was monitored using a digital caliper, and arthritis severity was assessed using a clinical scoring system. Four paws of each mouse were scored every other day (score: 0, normal with no paw swelling or erythema; 1, slight erythema and swelling; 2, moderate swelling with edema; 3, pronounced edema and reduced joint mobility; 4, severe edema and joint stiffness). The maximum possible score for each mouse was 16.

Micro-CT imaging

After 12 days of treatment under different conditions, the hind limbs of RA mice were collected and fixed using 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde. The collected hind limbs were scanned in 12 μm isotropic resolution and analyzed by a 3D Cone Beam Nemo Micro-CT (NMC-200, PINGSENG, China). 3D reconstructions of the tissues were conducted with the FDK algorithm using the Avatar software (version 1.7.2, PINGSENG, China).

Histopathological analysis of joints

After 12 days of treatment under different conditions, limb tissues of RA mice were collected at the end of 2 weeks of treatment, fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde, followed by decalcification with 10% (w/v) EDTA for 3 weeks. Then, the embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 30 μm-thickness slices for H&E, Masson, and safranin O/Fast Green staining. Representative images were obtained using an optical microscopy (Mshot MF53-M, China).

Immunochemical analysis of PGE2 in arthritis

At the end of 12 days of treatment under different conditions, the hind limbs of mice in all groups were collected, fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin for sectioning into 10 μm-thick slices. Prior to antibody staining, heat-mediated antigen retrieval was applied in citrate buffer solution for 10 min under the microwave, then endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide solution for 10 min in the dark. Sections were then incubated with 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS buffer for 10 min to minimize nonspecific binding, followed by incubation with a PGE2-specific primary antibody (antibodies-online, CAT# AA281,384) at 4 °C overnight. After thorough washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied to incubate at room temperature for 10 min, and immunoreactivity was visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogenic substrate, with hematoxylin counterstaining. Stained sections were dehydrated in ethanol and xylene, mounted with neutral balsam, and examined under an optical microscopy (Mshot MF53-M, China).

Cytokines measurement in serum

After 12 days of treatment under different conditions, blood samples were retrieved from the retro-orbital plexus of RA and normal mice, respectively, and then centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min to separate the serum, which was used for the detection of the concentrations of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β using specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Biocompatibility analysis

After 12 days treatment, blood and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) were collected. Blood samples were used for hematological and biochemical analysis and key parameters including liver function markers (ALT, AST) and kidney function markers (creatinine, blood urea nitrogen) were measured. Major organs were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm-thickness slices for H&E staining. Representative images were captured using an optical microscope (Mshot MF53-M, China).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and GraphPad Prism (v8.0) was used for all statistical analyses. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for comparisons among multiple experimental groups. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, with the specific number of replicates (n) indicated in the figure legends.

Theoretical calculation

Theoretical models of COX-2, CAu and CAu@COX-2 were respectively constructed and positioned between two electrodes to investigate their spin-selective electron transport properties. Spin-resolved conductance spectra and current–voltage (I–V) characteristics were calculated using a model Hamiltonian framework. The electronic transport behavior of each system was analyzed by applying the Landauer–Büttiker formalism in conjunction with the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) method. Full details on model construction, Hamiltonian formulation, and parameter selection are provided in the Supplementary Information.
