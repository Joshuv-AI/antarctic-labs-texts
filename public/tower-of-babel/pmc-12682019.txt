Protocol for developing a mouse model of gastric cancer following Helicobacter pylori eradication and chronic ethanol feeding
Authors: Faisal Aziz, Zigang Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHelicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection drives gastric cancer through chronic inflammation. Here, we present a protocol for establishing a mouse model that induces persistent gastritis and tumorigenesis through chronic ethanol feeding after H. pylori eradication, recapitulating persistent gastritis and precancerous and cancerous phenotypes. We describe steps for H. pylori infection, triple-drug eradication therapy, and ethanol administration. Subsequent tissue collection, histopathology, and molecular analyses assess inflammation and tumor development, enabling investigation of how post-eradication inflammation and alcohol exposure synergistically promote gastric carcinogenesisFor complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Aziz et al.1

Before you begin

This protocol provides a robust framework for studying gastric inflammation, tumor progression, and preclinical drug testing in mice. By combining H. pylori infection with sustained alcohol exposure, it models post-eradication gastric cancer development more accurately than previous approaches. This system allows investigation of host–bacterial interactions, immune responses, and gene expression changes associated with gastric disease. Compared to existing models2,3,4; the protocol follows NIAAA guidelines for chronic ethanol administration, ensuring reproducibility, translational relevance and addressing prior limitations in alcohol exposure duration and dosing.5

We describe a stepwise method for developing a mouse model of alcohol-induced gastric cancer, covering bacterial strain selection, animal numbers, housing, equipment, and technical considerations. C57BL/6J-219 mice are selected for their genetic background and ethanol preference, enabling consistent induction of gastritis and tumorigenesis. The protocol, requiring minimal technical expertise, includes proper controls and chronic alcohol administration via drinking water. Histological, molecular, and biochemical analyses assess tumor burden, genomic stability, and metabolic changes under controlled housing conditions.

Innovation

This protocol presents a significant methodological advancement by establishing a novel experimental framework that integrates H. pylori infection, eradication, and chronic ethanol exposure to model persistent gastritis and gastric carcinogenesis in mice. Unlike conventional H. pylori infection models that terminate after bacterial clearance or maintain chronic infection, this design sustains post-eradication inflammation through controlled ethanol administration, closely mimicking the chronic gastric injury observed in patients who develop cancer despite successful eradication therapy. Existing models often require persistent infection, chemical carcinogens, or genetic modifications to induce tumors, limiting their clinical relevance. In contrast, this approach uniquely decouples the initiator (H. pylori) from the promoter (ethanol), allowing targeted investigation of how alcohol sustains a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment even after bacterial clearance. By adhering to NIAAA guidelines for chronic ethanol administration, the protocol ensures translational relevance to human drinking patterns.

Key innovations include optimized procedures for synchronized H. pylori infection and antibiotic eradication, followed by chronic ethanol feeding that reproducibly induces lesions progressing from atrophic gastritis to carcinoma. The workflow provides detailed, stepwise methods for histopathological evaluation, inflammatory marker quantification, and molecular profiling, enabling comprehensive assessment of host–pathogen and host–alcohol interactions. Its modular design further allows integration with genetic knockouts, microbiome modulation, or therapeutic interventions. By replicating the clinically relevant post-eradication gastric environment under alcohol exposure, this system overcomes limitations of short-term or infection-dependent models, providing a robust and adaptable preclinical platform for investigating inflammation-driven gastric carcinogenesis and testing preventive or therapeutic strategies.

Expected outcomes

The risk of developing gastric cancer is affected by multiple environmental and lifestyle factors, notably infection with H. pylori and prolonged alcohol intake.1,23 H. pylori bacteria colonize the gastric mucosa, causing inflammation that elevates the risk of developing gastric cancer.24,25,26 While eradication of H. pylori is a crucial preventive measure, some individuals still develop gastric cancer due to persistent inflammation driven by lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption.27,28,29,30 The impact of alcohol intake on the advancement of gastric cancer, both during active H. pylori infection and following its eradication, remains to be experimentally determined. However, numerous epidemiological and clinical studies suggest that alcohol consumption contributes to persistent gastric damage, ultimately leading to cancer development.16 Gastric cancer progresses through multiple stages and is a slow-developing disease, making its study particularly challenging. Therefore, there is an urgent need for an alcohol-associated gastric cancer mouse model infected with H. pylori to facilitate a more rapid and comprehensive investigation of disease progression. We established a fast-progressing mouse model by infecting C57BL/6J-219 mice with H. pylori, a bacterium responsible for human stomach infections, alongside chronic alcohol intake (Figures 1, 2, and 3). This model enables the study of the prolonged impact of ethanol consumption following H. pylori eradication, focusing on its contribution to sustained gastritis and gastric tumor formation.1,2,31 This model was meticulously developed to accurately replicate the progression of gastric cancer in humans resulting from alcohol consumption combined with H. pylori infection. Gastric tumors primarily develop in the corpus and antrum of the stomach. Additionally, precancerous changes, including hyperplasia, metaplasia, and dysplasia of the epithelium, are often observed in non-tumor areas. Due to the variability in tumor size and number among animals, we typically use more than 25 mice per group. In studies involving drug treatment, the timeline for gastric tumor development may be altered. Our results demonstrate that ongoing alcohol intake sustains gastritis even after H. pylori has been eradicated, promoting the development of precancerous lesions and, ultimately, gastric tumors—unlike in alcohol-free controls. Additionally, mice subjected to both alcohol exposure and H. pylori infection show a greater frequency of gastric cancer. In contrast, eliminating H. pylori slows disease progression, lowers tumor occurrence, and mitigates inflammation. Our study addresses a critical gap in gastric cancer animal modeling. Building on our earlier findings that chronic alcohol consumption synergizes with H. pylori infection to promote gastric tumorigenesis via IL-10 inhibition,32 we now present a novel mouse model demonstrating that continuous alcohol exposure, even after successful H. pylori eradication with triple-antibiotic therapy, can drive gastric cancer development. Whereas our prior model illuminated the initiation phase of tumorigenesis under sustained infection, it did not capture the clinically relevant post-eradication context. The present model replicates gastric cancer progression following eradication in the setting of ongoing alcohol use—a scenario paralleling human populations where alcohol remains a risk factor despite bacterial clearance. Compared to existing systems, the advantages of this approach are clear. Conventional H. pylori-only or alcohol-only models often require additional carcinogenic stimuli to achieve cancer, and combined infection–alcohol models have largely focused on tumorigenesis during persistent infection. By contrast, our protocol uniquely incorporates bacterial eradication before endpoint analyses, enabling investigation into why gastric cancer persists in some patients after eradication. This framework aligns with epidemiological data: long-term cohort studies reveal that gastric cancer risk remains elevated for over a decade after eradication (SIR ≈ 1.20 up to 12 years), with risk declining only after approximately 13 years.1,29,33,34 Clinically, post-eradication gastric cancer occurs most frequently in patients with prior gastric ulcers,35 further underscoring the translational relevance of our model. Furthermore, this model shortens the timeline for gastric cancer progression, providing an effective tool for gastroenterologists and oncology researchers to explore how H. pylori infection and alcohol intake interact in driving tumorigenesis. It also enables the identification of novel pathways and genes involved in gastric tumorigenesis. Most importantly, this model closely mimics human gastric cancer development, making it highly relevant for translational research.

Limitations

When considering the use of our mice model, it is important to acknowledge several limitations. First, the model involves multiple endpoints to assess inflammation and various stages of cancer development. Blood collection is also required to check H. pylori titers, confirming both the initial infection and successful eradication. These additional steps may increase the complexity and duration of the experiment. Second, our protocol involves chronic alcohol treatment, which differs from other mice models that administer alcohol via gavage. This treatment results in a longer experimental duration but offers a more realistic representation of gastric diseases. Third, our data indicates early infiltration of immune cells during alcohol and H. pylori infection, allowing for the exploration of acquired immune responses. Although this indicates a hastened onset of gastritis pathology, it is essential to acknowledge that the model may not entirely reflect the prolonged progression characteristic of chronic gastritis in humans. We also acknowledge important limitations of this mouse model is that ethanol metabolism differs significantly between mice and humans: mice process ethanol much faster, and primarily rely on alcohol dehydrogenase pathways (e.g., higher hepatic ADH activity), whereas humans increasingly use the microsomal ethanol oxidizing system (MEOS) involving CYP2E1, especially after chronic exposure.36 These metabolic distinctions may alter tissue exposure to acetaldehyde and oxidative stress. Additionally, while murine pathology recapitulates key features of gastric carcinogenesis—chronic gastritis, metaplasia, dysplasia—it does not fully reflect the heterogeneity and complex progression stages seen in human gastric cancer. Accordingly, our model should be viewed as a mechanistic and proof-of-concept system rather than a direct surrogate for human disease. Future validation using human gastric organoids or humanized mouse models will be essential to confirm and extend translational insights. Another limitation of our study is the use of pooled RNA samples at certain endpoints. Pooling was applied to reduce inter-cage variability and generate representative profiles of disease stages, as we published in alcohol-associated gastric cancer models.1 To preserve reproducibility, all animals underwent histological confirmation of lesions, molecular findings were validated in at least five individual samples per group, and assays included both biological and technical replicates. While this strategy balanced efficiency with reliability, we acknowledge that independent RNA sequencing from single animals—or single-cell transcriptomics—would provide greater resolution into inter-individual heterogeneity and remains an important direction for future refinements. Empirical evidence also supports pooling as a cost-effective approach that can maintain statistical power when within-group variance is high.37 Overall, while our mice model provides valuable insights into gastric diseases and immune responses, researchers should consider these limitations when interpreting results and designing experiments. Future studies may aim to refine the model to better mimic the chronic properties of human chronic gastritis.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Some mouse strains exhibit a genetic predisposition that makes them particularly vulnerable to H. pylori infection, resulting in an increased mortality risk. (Consideration of mouse selection and strain, step 25).

Potential solution

A possible approach in such scenarios is to lower the dose or dilute the concentration of the H. pylori suspension. This adjustment is particularly important in chronic gastritis or cancer models, where repeated administrations may exceed the animals’ tolerance threshold.

Problem 2

Some mouse strains may inherently have a low disposition to develop infection, making the induction process less effective or yielding weak inflammatory or cancerous symptoms. (Consideration of mouse selection and strain, step 25).

Potential solution

To counter this, researchers can escalate the dose of H. pylori agents, increasing the likelihood of an effective infection. This ensures that even resistant strains can develop a robust inflammatory or cancerous response.

Problem 3

Contamination may occur due to inadequate sterilization of media or improper aseptic techniques during the preparation of bacterial cultures. (Culture of Hparagraph. pylori, steps 20–31).

Potential solution

It is essential to sterilize culture mediums according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and work in a sterile environment, such as near a flame hood, to minimize the risk of contamination. This practice helps maintain the integrity of the culture.

Problem 4

Cage effects and microbiota differences can introduce variability, leading to inconsistent results across experimental groups. (Animal housing, step 34–37).

Potential solution

To reduce variability, researchers should use littermate controls and cohousing animals, which standardizes the genetic background and controls microbiota variability. This step helps ensure that the results are more reproducible across the experimental population.

Problem 5

Incorrect positioning of the mouse or using a gavage needle that is too large for the animal may hinder successful gavage. (Administration of H. pylori by oral gavage, steps 78–83).

Potential solution

Make sure the mouse’s body is aligned properly with its neck fully extended. Use a rounded-tip gavage needle to gently move the tongue forward. If the needle remains problematic, switch to a smaller ball-tip needle for easier insertion.

Problem 6

Incorrect needle positioning can result in leakage of the gavage solution back into the mouth. (Administration of H. pylori by oral gavage, steps 78–83).

Potential solution

Ensure the gavage needle is correctly positioned and inserted sufficiently into the lower esophagus to avoid reflux or regurgitation of the administered liquid.

Problem 7

Insufficient oxygen supply may result in inadequate anesthesia for the animal. (Euthanasia and sample collection, step 97–99).

Potential solution

Replace the oxygen or isoflurane supply to ensure proper anesthetic depth during experiments or procedures.

Problem 8

Incision sites that are too close to capillaries can cause excessive bleeding during procedures. (Stomach sample preparation, step 100).

Potential solution

Applying pressure with cotton swabs for approximately one minute should help control the bleeding. If this doesn’t stop bleeding, use a cautery device to close the blood vessels and stop further blood loss.

Problem 9

Using improper tools or techniques during dissection can lead to delays or inaccurate procedures. (Stomach sample preparation, steps 101–105).

Potential solution

Use curved-end forceps, which are ideal for precise dissection of tissues, and ensure that the appropriate tools are used to prevent delays or mishandling.

Problem 10

Insufficient adhesion to ITO-coated glass slides or irregular matrix distribution during coating may lead to tissue loss. (Histological analysis, steps 129).

Potential solution

Apply a poly-L-lysine coating to the slides, which helps improve tissue adherence. Also, clean any defective spray needles and ensure proper matrix distribution to avoid tissue loss during deparaffinization.

By addressing these tissues with appropriate interventions, researchers can significantly improve experimental outcomes, reduce animal suffering, and ensure more reliable data.
