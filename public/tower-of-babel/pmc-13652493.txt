Peripheral ossifying fibroma mimicking oral Kaposi’s sarcoma in an immunosuppressed patient with Behçet’s disease: a case report

Introduction

Kaposi’s sarcoma (KS) is an HHV-8-associated vascular neoplasm occurring in classic, endemic, AIDS-related, and iatrogenic forms[1–3]. Oral involvement occurs in up to 65% of HIV/AIDS patients, with the hard palate (95%) and gingiva (23%) being the most frequently affected intraoral sites[2]; in 22% of cases, oral lesions are the initial site of manifestation[2,4].

Several benign reactive gingival lesions – pyogenic granuloma (PG), peripheral giant cell granuloma, and peripheral ossifying fibroma (POF) – may clinically mimic oral KS[2,5]. In patients with known systemic KS, newly detected oral lesions carry a high risk of diagnostic anchoring bias – a cognitive error whereby clinicians over-rely on a prior diagnosis and fail to revise their reasoning when contradictory evidence emerges – making HHV-8 (LANA-1) immunohistochemistry indispensable[9–11].

We report a rare case of POF mimicking oral KS in an immunosuppressed patient with Behçet’s disease and established cutaneous KS, in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[12].

Case presentation

Patient information

A 57-year-old man was referred to the Department of Oral Surgery in June 2024 for a gingival lesion. He had been under follow-up for Behçet’s disease since childhood, with recurrent oral aphthous ulcers (more than three episodes per year), genital ulcer scars, and occlusive retinal vasculitis requiring intravenous corticosteroid boluses followed by long-term oral corticosteroid therapy (initial dose 1 mg/kg/day, tapered to 15 mg/day), combined with colchicine 1 mg/day and calcium/vitamin D supplementation. Kaposi sarcoma (KS) was diagnosed in October 2022 following biopsy of violaceous nodules on both feet; histopathology confirmed spindle-cell proliferation with positive immunostaining for HHV-8. Serology for HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C was negative. He was a non-smoker and did not consume alcohol or use recreational drugs, and he had no relevant family history.

Clinical findings

Dermatological examination revealed violaceous angiomatous nodules on the feet and in the interdigital spaces, with lower-limb edema, and scattered violaceous macules on the hands and trunk, consistent with cutaneous KS. No cervical lymphadenopathy was detected. Intraoral examination revealed a well-demarcated, painless, firm, pink nodular lesion on the lingual aspect of the anterior mandibular gingiva (Fig. 1A) that was non-ulcerated and non-bleeding on manipulation and had been slowly evolving over 6 years. Dental plaque and calculus were present in the anterior mandibular region. Panoramic radiography showed no bone involvement (Fig. 1B). Diagnostic assessment and interpretations

Given the coexistence of confirmed cutaneous KS and prolonged corticosteroid-induced immunosuppression, oral KS was the primary hypothesis. However, four features were atypical: (1) pink rather than violaceous coloration; (2) firm rather than compressible consistency, with no spontaneous bleeding; (3) indolent 6-year evolution inconsistent with the progressive course of oral KS; and (4) absence of bone involvement.

The differential diagnoses considered were PG, peripheral giant cell granuloma (PGCG), benign salivary gland tumor, fibrous epulis, POF, and oral KS. Although PG, PGCG, and benign salivary gland tumor were considered less likely, the features remained insufficiently specific for a definitive diagnosis, making histopathological examination essential.

Intervention

Preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis (amoxicillin 2 g orally 1 hour prior) was administered, given the patient’s iatrogenic immunosuppression. Complete surgical excision was performed under local anesthesia (2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine) using a cold-blade technique. The lesion was excised en bloc with healthy margins to the periosteum (Fig. 2A). Local irritants were addressed by ultrasonic scaling. Hemostasis was achieved by compression; no sutures were required. The specimen was fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histopathology. The procedure was performed by a resident under direct senior supervision in the outpatient oral surgery department of the Faculty of Dental Medicine in Rabat. Gross examination revealed a whitish tissue fragment measuring 1.5 × 1.0 × 0.6 cm (Fig. 2B). Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated a benign fibrocellular proliferation of spindle-shaped fibroblasts within a collagenous stroma without cytological atypia and with focal mineralized deposits (bone trabeculae and cementum-like material) and a chronic inflammatory infiltrate. Histology allowed systematic exclusion of differential diagnoses: absence of lobular capillary vascular proliferation excluded PG; absence of multinucleated giant cells excluded PGCG; mineralized deposits excluded a fibrous epulis; and absence of ductal or acinar structures excluded a benign salivary gland tumor. No KS features – spindle-cell fascicles, slit-like vascular spaces, or extravasated erythrocytes – were identified. HHV-8 LANA-1 immunohistochemistry, performed on deparaffinized sections using the DAKO EnVision K5007 system on the Autostainer Link 48 platform, demonstrated absence of HHV-8 expression, definitively excluding oral KS. The definitive diagnosis of POF was established on histopathological and immunohistochemical grounds.

Follow-up and patient perspective

Postoperative follow-up at 1 week, 1 month, and 6 months showed progressive mucosal healing without complications. At 6 months, the site showed complete healing with restoration of normal gingival architecture, no recurrence or adverse events, and no complications per the Clavien–Dindo classification.

The patient reported complete satisfaction with management and outcome, and significant relief upon confirmation that the lesion was benign and unrelated to his known KS. He reported no functional discomfort, pain, or eating difficulties after healing and noted that the reassurance of the histopathological diagnosis positively affected his overall well-being.

Discussion

In HIV-negative patients on long-term corticosteroids, HHV-8 reactivation is driven by T-cell-mediated immunosuppression and dysregulation of viral oncoproteins.

The rare association between Behçet’s disease and KS reflects both disease-related immune dysregulation and iatrogenic immunosuppression as independent facilitators of HHV-8-driven tumorigenesis[13,14]. Corticosteroid-induced KS in autoimmune conditions, while uncommon, is increasingly reported, reinforcing the need for vigilance when new lesions appear[14].

Oral KS manifests as pink to violaceous macules, plaques, or nodules, but these features are not pathognomonic. Among its mimickers, PG, PGCG, and POF share overlapping presentations. A retrospective analysis of 138 cases showed that bleeding tendency, color, consistency, and growth kinetics may orient the clinician, though histopathological confirmation remains mandatory[15]. PG is soft, highly vascular, and bleeds readily, reflecting its lobular capillary hemangioma histology[16]. PGCG characteristically shows a bluish-purple discoloration from its vascular component and extravasated erythrocytes, which may resemble the violaceous hue of KS[5]. POF presents as a firm, slow-growing, pink nodular mass from the interdental papilla, consistent with its fibro-osseous reactive nature[6–8]. In this case, the established diagnosis of cutaneous KS created a powerful cognitive prior directing reasoning toward mucosal KS – a textbook illustration of diagnostic anchoring bias[9]. Four features were discordant with oral KS: pink coloration, firm consistency, absence of spontaneous bleeding, and a 6-year indolent evolution. Overriding this cognitive anchor was critical, as premature diagnostic closure may lead to unnecessary oncologic workup, empirical chemotherapy, or harmful modification of an established immunosuppressive regimen[9,17].

Histologically, POF shows fibrocellular proliferation with bone trabeculae, cementum-like deposits, and dystrophic calcifications arising from periodontal ligament cells[6,8]. Local irritants – dental plaque and calculus – are well-recognized contributors[6,18], providing an HHV-8-independent pathogenic explanation. KS, by contrast, shows spindle-cell fascicles forming slit-like vascular spaces with extravasated erythrocytes and hemosiderin, and is consistently HHV-8 positive[10,11]. HHV-8 LANA-1 immunohistochemistry is the diagnostic gold standard[10,11], and its negative result was decisive: it confirmed POF, established that the oral lesion was unrelated to systemic KS, and avoided unnecessary oncologic investigation[2,19].

Accurate diagnosis carries major management implications. Oral KS may require modification of the immunosuppressive regimen, intralesional chemotherapy, or systemic agents such as liposomal doxorubicin or paclitaxel[2,14]. In Behçet’s disease, any corticosteroid modification must be weighed against the risk of disease flare and ocular deterioration, making diagnostic precision particularly consequential. POF, by contrast, is managed by complete surgical excision to the periosteum with elimination of local irritants, as performed here. Reported recurrence rates of 8–20% necessitate long-term surveillance[18,20]; none was observed at 6 months.

With expanding indications for immunosuppression across rheumatologic and inflammatory conditions, clinicians will increasingly encounter patients in whom HHV-8-related malignancies coexist with unrelated oral pathologies. Maintaining a broad differential and systematically applying histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses, rather than relying on clinical gestalt shaped by prior diagnoses, is essential to evidence-based care.

Strengths include rigorous clinicopathological and immunohistochemical correlation, explicit discussion of anchoring bias as a preventable cognitive pitfall, and full SCARE 2025 compliance. Limitations include the single-case design precluding generalization, the 6-month follow-up being insufficient for long-term conclusions, and the absence of complementary molecular analysis, such as HHV-8 viral load quantification.

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the importance of a histology-driven diagnostic approach when evaluating oral lesions in immunosuppressed patients with Behçet’s disease and KS. Early biopsy, combined with HHV-8 immunohistochemistry, is essential to establish the correct diagnosis and prevent unnecessary oncologic investigations.
