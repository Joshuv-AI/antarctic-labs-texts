Photoacoustic and ultrasound Imaging of placental functions in gestational diabetes: From diagnosis to precision interventions
Authors: Yingru Liu, Yufei Wu, Xinmei Gao, Qikun Zhang, Xuanyu Jin, Zhengman Lin, Xinyi Yang, Rui Zheng, Lijia Du, Xiuming Zhang, Shiyang Chang, Qiangzhou Rong, Lixian Wang, Chulhong Kim, Zhifen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is rising, with adverse maternal–fetal outcomes. Exercise is first-line therapy, but current evaluations rely on glycemic and anthropometric metrics, missing dynamic maternal–placental–fetal adaptations. This review synthesizes imaging evidence on high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and resistance training (RT) in GDM. Multimodal imaging—photoacoustic imaging, Doppler ultrasound, and power Doppler—provides quantitative information on placental hemodynamics, vascularization, and fetal adaptation. HIIT may improve vascular function, while RT may induce structural remodeling, though GDM-specific evidence is limited. Photoacoustic imaging provides spatially resolved tissue oxygenation estimates, complementing ultrasound-based placental assessment. We propose imaging endpoints as key secondary outcomes in GDM trials. Hybrid photoacoustic and ultrasound (PA/US) imaging may integrate hemodynamic and oxygenation information. Coupled with continuous glucose monitoring, this framework may support comprehensive efficacy evaluation and individualized exercise prescription, highlighting that improved glycemic control does not necessarily reflect enhanced placental function.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Placental function, Multimodal imaging, Photoacoustic imaging, Ultrasound imaging, Exercise intervention

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy in women with previously normal glucose metabolism. It is typically diagnosed during the mid-second trimester (24–28 weeks of gestation) using an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) [1]. The global prevalence of GDM has been rising markedly, paralleling increases in advanced maternal age and obesity. According to the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 16.7% of pregnancies worldwide are affected by hyperglycemia, with GDM accounting for over 80% of these cases [2]. GDM not only elevates the risk of perinatal complications such as gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, macrosomia, and neonatal hypoglycemia [3,4], but also significantly increases the mother's long-term risk of developing type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Furthermore, through the mechanism of "intrauterine metabolic programming," GDM can adversely affect the offspring's long-term metabolic health [5].

The primary goal of GDM management is to achieve effective glycemic control while ensuring adequate fetal growth and development. Dietary intervention and exercise prescription are first-line treatments, with insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents considered only when lifestyle modifications are insufficient for glycemic control. Numerous studies have confirmed that regular exercise significantly improves insulin sensitivity, reduces fasting and postprandial glucose levels, and contributes to improved pregnancy outcomes [6,7]. Recent systematic reviews and network meta-analyses further indicate that structured exercise interventions during pregnancy can reduce the incidence of GDM, demonstrating efficacy even in overweight and obese populations [8], with supervised or group-based programs showing greater efficacy than individual interventions [9]. However, a significant limitation persists in the efficacy evaluation of existing prenatal exercise studies: most rely on discrete-time-point glycemic measures or weight changes as primary endpoints. This approach fails to capture glucose variability and the complex interactions within the maternal–placental–fetal axis.

The placenta plays a central role in the pathogenesis of GDM and subsequent maternal-fetal outcomes. Maternal metabolic disturbances can alter the fetal growth environment by affecting placental vascular development, perfusion efficiency, and nutrient transport function. Consequently, relying solely on glycemic parameters or weight as outcome measures is insufficient to fully assess the true biological effects of exercise interventions in GDM. Imaging technologies, particularly ultrasound (US) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), enable non-invasive, dynamic, and quantitative assessment of placental hemodynamics, vascularization, and maternal body composition distribution. These techniques provide a critical link between exercise interventions, placental functional changes, and pregnancy outcomes.

It is important to note that placental dysfunction cannot be fully explained by structural abnormalities alone, but rather reflects an imbalance among vascular remodeling, perfusion distribution, and oxygen exchange. Conventional Doppler US enables assessment of vascular resistance in the uterine artery, umbilical artery, and fetal circulation; however, these measurements primarily reflect large-vessel or global hemodynamic status and are limited in capturing regional heterogeneity in perfusion and oxygenation within the intervillous space and placental microcirculation. In recent years, advances in US-based imaging and photoacoustic imaging (PAI) have opened new avenues for functional placental assessment. Techniques such as three-dimensional power Doppler (3D-PD), ultrafast Doppler, shear-wave elastography (SWE), and microvascular imaging can provide additional insights into placental vascularization, tissue stiffness, and microcirculatory flow. In contrast, PAI leverages endogenous optical absorption of hemoglobin to enable spatially resolved estimation of tissue oxygen saturation (sO₂), thereby complementing conventional US by providing direct information on placental oxygenation [10,11].

International guidelines universally recommend aerobic exercise for pregnant women, although earlier iterations often limited this recommendation to low-impact activities (e.g., "walking, slow cycling") [12]. In recent years, high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and resistance training (RT) have gained increasing attention as components of prenatal exercise prescriptions due to their time efficiency, potent metabolic stimulus, and adaptability. Studies have shown that HIIT, through its alternating pattern of high-intensity bouts and recovery periods, can significantly improve cardiorespiratory fitness, enhance skeletal muscle glucose transport, and promote mitochondrial biogenesis within a relatively short timeframe [13,14]. RT, by increasing skeletal muscle mass, improving muscle quality, and modulating myokine secretion, demonstrates clear potential for reducing fasting and postprandial glucose levels and decreasing insulin requirements [15]. Emerging evidence suggests the feasibility of HIIT during pregnancy in terms of blood pressure, cardiorespiratory fitness, and safety [16], while randomized controlled trials in GDM patients confirm that RT can improve glycemic control and delivery outcomes [17]. Nonetheless, a systematic synthesis of imaging endpoints used in these studies is lacking, limiting comparisons between exercise modalities and the generalizability of findings.

Therefore, this review adopts a functional imaging-based perspective to summarize the primary imaging techniques and quantitative metrics used to assess the effects of exercise interventions in GDM. Particular attention is given to Doppler US, 3D-PD, placental elastography, MRI/magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), and emerging photoacoustic and ultrasound (PA/US) approaches, with a focus on their potential to characterize maternal metabolic remodeling and placental perfusion, vascularization, and oxygenation in the context of HIIT and RT. While providing essential biological context, this article emphasizes the unique value of imaging endpoints in interpreting exercise-induced placental and maternal adaptations. Furthermore, it proposes a research framework for incorporating imaging parameters as key secondary endpoints in future GDM exercise intervention trials, aiming to inform the deepening of mechanistic research and the optimization of clinical trial design (Fig. 1).

The role of multimodal imaging techniques in metabolic and placental functional assessment of GDM

Imaging techniques serve as important tools for linking exercise interventions to pregnancy outcomes by enabling non-invasive, reproducible, and quantitative assessment of the structural and functional status of the maternal-placental-fetal unit. Compared with traditional metabolic markers, imaging endpoints more directly capture alterations in fat distribution, placental perfusion, and fetal hemodynamics, offering a perspective for understanding the biological effects of different exercise modalities in GDM. From a placental perspective, imaging assessment in GDM should not be limited to fetal growth and vascular resistance, but should instead capture key aspects of placental function, including perfusion efficiency, tissue microenvironment, and oxygen transport. Accumulating evidence suggests that major pregnancy complications share convergent pathophysiological features, such as impaired vascular remodeling, heterogeneous perfusion, and disrupted oxygen homeostasis. Recent reviews highlight the complementary roles of US and PAI in placental disorders, with US providing structural and hemodynamic information and PAI offering spatially resolved information related to tissue oxygenation. Together, these modalities support a shift from morphology-based assessment toward a more integrated, function-oriented evaluation of the placenta [11]. These imaging modalities are at different stages of clinical translation. Conventional Doppler ultrasound is routinely used in obstetric practice, whereas 3D power Doppler and MRI provide additional structural or functional information but are used more selectively in clinical or research settings. In contrast, placental PAI and PA/US remain emerging techniques, with current evidence derived mainly from animal models and ex vivo human studies. The following sections therefore discuss these modalities in the context of their current level of clinical validation.

PAI

Principle of PAI

PAI is an innovative biomedical imaging technique integrating the high optical contrast of optical imaging and the deep penetration capability of US. Based on the PA effect discovered by Bell in 1880 [18], PAI achieves non-invasive, multi-scale visualization of tissue structures, vascular features, metabolic and molecular information (Fig. 2A and B). In the past two decades, it has been widely applied in cancer diagnosis, cardiovascular research, neuroscience, placental examination and image-guided treatment [19,20].

When short pulsed lasers irradiate biological tissues, endogenous chromophores absorb light energy and trigger a slight transient temperature rise (below 1 °C). Local thermal expansion then produces broadband US signals captured by ultrasonic transducers. The whole process includes four steps: laser excitation, photon absorption, thermoelastic expansion, and acoustic signal collection for image reconstruction [24].

Unlike conventional optical imaging techniques, which suffer from strong photon scattering in tissue, PAI relies on ultrasonic detection. Because acoustic scattering is orders of magnitude weaker than optical scattering, PAI achieves significantly greater imaging depth while preserving optical absorption contrast [22].

PAI Modalities

To address different imaging scales and biological applications, several PAI modalities have been developed (Fig. 2C), each characterized by distinct spatial resolution, imaging depth, and field of view (FOV) in Fig. 2D.

(1)Optical-Resolution Photoacoustic Microscopy (OR-PAM)OR-PAM achieves micron-scale imaging by tightly focusing the excitation laser beam. In OR-PAM, the lateral resolution is determined primarily by the optical focal spot rather than the acoustic detector. Consequently, OR-PAM provides exceptionally high spatial resolution, typically ranging from 1 to 10 μm, enabling visualization of individual capillaries and even single red blood cells [[25], [26], [27]]. However, due to optical scattering, imaging depth is generally limited to approximately 1 mm beneath the tissue surface. OR-PAM is therefore particularly suitable for imaging superficial microvascular networks, skin microcirculation, tumor angiogenesis, and cerebral cortical vasculature [28,29].

Acoustic-Resolution Photoacoustic Microscopy (AR-PAM)

AR-PAM extends imaging depth by weakly focusing the excitation light while tightly focusing the US detector [[30], [31], [32]]. In this configuration, spatial resolution is determined by the acoustic focus rather than optical focusing. Compared with OR-PAM, AR-PAM sacrifices some spatial resolution but significantly increases penetration depth, typically reaching 3–10 mm. The lateral resolution usually ranges between 30 and 100 μm. AR-PAM occupies the mesoscopic imaging regime and is widely used for imaging deeper vascular structures, lymphatic networks, skeletal muscle perfusion, and tumor microenvironments.

Traditional PAM systems (OR-PAM and AR-PAM) rely on motorized scanning stages for point-by-point raster scanning, and have been limited by their slow imaging speeds [33]. This limitation has restricted their ability to capture rapid biological processes such as changes in blood flow and sO₂. Recent technological breakthroughs have led to the development of high-speed PAM technologies that greatly improve temporal resolution and high-speed imaging capabilities. These innovations enable detailed visualization of biological structures and functions at unprecedented speeds, broadening the potential applications of PAM. This review summarizes these technological advancements and discusses the ongoing and prospective applications of high-speed PAM in both biomedical and preclinical settings [34,35].

PAM has emerged as a powerful tool for functional imaging by simultaneously providing high-resolution structural, hemodynamic, and metabolic information. Its ability to quantify endogenous optical absorbers, particularly hemoglobin, enables label-free assessment of tissue oxygenation, blood perfusion, and vascular dynamics across a wide range of biological systems [32,49].

In neuroscience, PAM has become an important modality for mapping cerebral hemodynamics and oxygen metabolism. Two major strategies have been developed for quantitative sO2 imaging. The first employs a single-wavelength approach that exploits the distinct photoacoustic responses of hemoglobin under picosecond and nanosecond laser excitation to quantify sO2 (Fig. 4A and B) [46,50]. By eliminating wavelength switching, this strategy minimizes wavelength-dependent optical attenuation while significantly improving imaging speed and penetration depth. The second strategy combines ultrafast functional PAM with stimulated Raman scattering to generate dual-wavelength excitation at 532 and 558 nm. Together with image registration and deep learning-based spectral unmixing, this platform enables accurate quantification of oxy- and deoxyhemoglobin distributions and high-resolution visualization of whole-brain oxygen dynamics (Fig. 4D) [50,51]. Beyond vascular imaging, the integration of near-infrared genetically encoded calcium indicators (iGECIs) with PAM has enabled simultaneous imaging of neuronal activity and cerebral hemodynamics through the intact skull. When combined with fluorescence microscopy, this multimodal platform achieves lateral and axial resolutions of approximately 3 μm and 25–50 μm, respectively, allowing comprehensive in vivo investigation of neurovascular coupling.

The capability of PAM for functional hemodynamic imaging has also been demonstrated in whole-animal models. Taboada et al. developed a high-resolution PAM system to visualize red blood cell distribution in living glassfrogs (Fig. 4C), enabling quantitative mapping of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) throughout the circulatory system [52]. This work highlights PAM's unique ability to provide non-invasive, deep, and quantitative measurements of systemic oxygen transport and vascular physiology in vivo.

Beyond neuroscience, PAM has shown broad applicability in the investigation of major organ systems. In acute kidney injury, label-free PAM simultaneously quantifies total hemoglobin concentration (CHb), blood sO2, and blood flow velocity within peritubular capillaries (Fig. 4E), providing direct assessment of regional oxygen metabolism and microvascular dysfunction [53]. In liver disease, PAM enables high-resolution visualization of hepatic microvascular architecture, tissue oxygenation, and molecular alterations associated with disease progression (Figs. 4F and 4G) [54]. Importantly, quantitative analysis of photoacoustic signals has enabled estimation of liver function reserve (LFR) through empirical mathematical modeling. In alcoholic liver disease (ALD) models, PAM revealed a prolonged indocyanine green (ICG) half-life, demonstrating its potential for non-invasive assessment of hepatic functional reserve.

PAM has also been extended to ophthalmology and systemic vascular physiology. Fig. 4H illustrates high-resolution imaging of the rat eye, where the iris root vessels and limbal microvasculature are clearly resolved in the photoacoustic maximum-amplitude-projection (MAP) image [55]. Furthermore, the ultra-wide-field DC-PAM system enabled simultaneous imaging of the ears of two mice during repeated hypoxia challenges, with one mouse serving as the control [47]. The corresponding sO2 maps (Fig. 4I) clearly distinguished arteries, characterized by higher oxyhemoglobin levels, from veins enriched in deoxyhemoglobin under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, demonstrating the capability of PAM for longitudinal monitoring of systemic oxygen dynamics.

In dermatology, PAM provides non-invasive, label-free assessment of cutaneous vascular remodeling. As shown in Fig. 4J, PAM clearly visualizes the dermal and subdermal vascular networks in patients with psoriasis, enabling quantitative evaluation of vessel density, vascular morphology, inflammation, and other disease-associated biomarkers without the need for exogenous contrast agents or invasive biopsy. These capabilities highlight the growing potential of PAM as a quantitative imaging platform for disease diagnosis, therapeutic monitoring, and precision medicine.

Overall, the combination of micron-scale spatial resolution, endogenous contrast, and quantitative functional imaging positions PAM as a versatile technology for investigating vascular physiology and organ function across multiple biomedical applications, ranging from neuroscience and ophthalmology to nephrology, hepatology, and dermatology. Future integration with artificial intelligence, multimodal imaging, and high-speed volumetric acquisition is expected to further expand its translational potential for both preclinical research and clinical practice.

PACT, also referred to as photoacoustic tomography (PAT), is designed for deep-tissue and organ-scale imaging [22,57,58]. Unlike PAM, PACT illuminates a large tissue volume with broad-field laser pulses while detecting photoacoustic signals using multi-element US arrays configured in linear, circular, spherical, or hemispherical geometries. Volumetric images are reconstructed using tomographic algorithms, including back-projection and model-based inversion. PACT typically achieves imaging depths of several centimeters (>7 cm in favorable conditions) with spatial resolutions of 100–500 μm, depending on the detector bandwidth and imaging geometry.

Beyond structural imaging, multispectral PACT exploits wavelength-dependent optical absorption to distinguish endogenous and exogenous chromophores, enabling quantitative assessment of physiological and molecular biomarkers, including total hemoglobin (HbT), oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO2), HbR, blood sO2, lipids, water, and targeted contrast agents. These capabilities have established PACT as a powerful platform for functional and molecular imaging, bridging anatomical visualization with physiological and biochemical characterization.

Recent technological advances have substantially improved the imaging performance and clinical potential of PACT. Menozzi et al. developed three-dimensional diffractive acoustic tomography (3D-DAT), which integrates diffractive photoacoustic tomography (DPAT) and diffractive ultrasound tomography (DUST) for volumetric PA/US imaging with near-isotropic spatial resolution, high imaging speed, and a large field of view (Fig. 5A) [59]. The system provides near-isotropic spatial resolution, a large field of view, high imaging speed, and improved quantitative accuracy for deep-tissue imaging.

For breast imaging, Li Lin et al. introduced single-breath-hold PACT (SBH-PACT), enabling volumetric imaging of the entire human breast within approximately 15 s (Fig. 5B) [60]. The system achieves a penetration depth of ~4 cm with an in-plane resolution of 255 μm and a 10 Hz imaging frame rate. Three-dimensional reconstruction effectively minimizes respiratory motion artifacts and clearly visualizes tumor-associated angiogenesis, demonstrating promise for early breast cancer detection, particularly in women with dense breast tissue.

To expand the field of view while preserving high spatiotemporal resolution, Wang et al. developed three-dimensional panoramic PACT (3D-PanoPACT) (Fig. 5C) [61]. This platform integrates structural, functional, and dynamic imaging to visualize vascular networks and organ morphology from the organ to whole-body scale. Maximum-intensity projection (MIP) and depth-encoded reconstructions reveal comprehensive three-dimensional vascular architecture throughout the entire mouse trunk.

For neuroimaging, Chen et al. developed a dual-frequency fiber US transducer array with a sheet-shaped acoustic focus for multispectral PACT (Fig. 5D). The system achieves a detection sensitivity of ~5.2 Pa, a penetration depth of ~1.2 cm, and a spatial resolution of ~70 μm within the mouse cerebral cortex [62]. Its dual-frequency response enables accurate mapping of cerebral vasculature and quantitative measurement of whole-brain blood sO₂, providing a promising alternative for functional brain imaging and disease diagnosis.

More recently, Huynh et al. developed a fast all-optical PACT system based on a Fabry–Perot US sensor (Fig. 5E) [63]. By integrating parallel optical readout, high-repetition-rate laser excitation, and compressed sensing, the system reduced volumetric acquisition times from minutes to seconds or even hundreds of milliseconds. It enables high-resolution three-dimensional imaging of microvasculature to depths approaching 15 mm while capturing dynamic tissue perfusion and other hemodynamic processes. Human wrist imaging resolved dermal microvessels with reconstructed lateral and axial resolutions of approximately 45 μm and 35 μm, respectively, while simultaneously visualizing larger vessels such as the radial artery. These advances highlight the strong potential of fast PACT for applications in cardiovascular medicine, oncology, dermatology, rheumatology, and other clinical fields.

Overall, recent innovations in detector design, reconstruction algorithms, and high-speed data acquisition have significantly advanced PACT from static structural imaging toward real-time, quantitative, and whole-organ functional imaging. These developments continue to accelerate its translation into preclinical research and clinical diagnostics.

In summary, PAI represents a unique hybrid imaging modality that combines optical contrast with ultrasonic resolution and penetration. Through its diverse imaging modalities, including OR-PAM, AR-PAM, and PACT, PAI provides multiscale structural, functional, and molecular information, making it one of the most promising biomedical imaging technologies for both preclinical research and future clinical applications.

Photoacoustic and Ultrasonic Dual-Modal Imaging

The integration of US and PAI has emerged as a powerful dual-modal imaging strategy by combining the deep anatomical imaging capability of US with the high optical contrast and functional sensitivity of photoacoustics [[64], [65], [66], [67]]. This complementary approach enables simultaneous visualization of tissue morphology, vascular architecture, hemodynamics, and molecular information within a single imaging platform. Recent technological developments have substantially accelerated the clinical translation of US–PA imaging. Huang et al. developed a high-speed dual-modal imaging (HDMI) system integrating PAI with ultrasound computed tomography (USCT) for rapid whole-breast imaging (Fig. 6A) [68]. The system performs dual-modal imaging at 10 Hz, completes a whole-breast scan in 12 s, and achieves imaging depths of up to 5 cm, enabling simultaneous visualization of breast anatomy and vascular architecture without ionizing radiation or exogenous contrast agents, thereby demonstrating strong potential for early breast cancer diagnosis.

To further expand multimodal capabilities, Park et al. developed a clinical triple-modal US–photoacoustic–fluorescence imaging system based on a transparent US transducer (TUT) array (Fig. 6B) [69]. The handheld probe integrates US, PAI, fluorescence imaging, and optical imaging into a compact platform, providing real-time visualization of tissue morphology, blood vessels, and lymphatic vessels. The system successfully guided lymphovenous anastomosis microsurgery, highlighting the clinical potential of multimodal imaging for image-guided microsurgical procedures.

For endoscopic applications, Kim et al. introduced a miniaturized dual-modal PA/US endoscopy probe using a TUT to achieve coaxial optical and acoustic alignment within a 1.8-mm-diameter device compatible with standard endoscopic channels (Fig. 6C) [70]. The system delivers commercial-grade US together with high-resolution PAI over extended working distances. In vivo imaging of the rat rectum simultaneously visualized the rectal vascular network in photoacoustic images and surrounding anatomical structures in US images, while depth-encoded photoacoustic images resolved superficial epithelial capillaries and deeper vascular networks, demonstrating its potential for minimally invasive gastrointestinal imaging and image-guided endoscopic interventions.

Despite these advances, challenges remain, including limited optical penetration in highly scattering tissues, increasing system complexity, and the demand for high-speed data acquisition and processing. Continued progress in laser technology, transparent and high-sensitivity transducers, artificial intelligence-assisted reconstruction, and probe miniaturization is expected to further enhance imaging performance and accelerate the widespread clinical adoption of US–PA imaging across oncology, cardiovascular medicine, regenerative medicine, and precision surgery.

PA/US Imaging for Placental Function

The placenta is a temporary specialized vascular organ responsible for oxygen, nutrient and waste exchange to sustain fetal growth, and placental dysfunction often leads to serious pregnancy complications like preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction and stillbirth. In vivo placental evaluation remains challenging owing to its intricate microvascular structure, dynamic blood perfusion and dual maternal-fetal blood supply. Although conventional US and MRI can display macroscopic anatomy and perfusion, they fail to sensitively detect microvascular oxygenation and metabolic heterogeneity at high resolution. As a hybrid imaging technique combining optical contrast and ultrasonic penetration, PAI enables non-invasive functional monitoring of placental physiological status.

PA/US imaging toward functional assessment of placental oxygenation

As mentioned above, multispectral PAI enables quantitative mapping of blood sO2, providing spatially resolved estimates of placental sO₂, which serve as a surrogate for oxygen transport. However, transabdominal sO₂ quantification is influenced by light attenuation, spectral coloring, tissue depth, motion, maternal adiposity, and placental location, and is not a simple direct measurement of metabolism. As oxygen delivery is fundamental to placental function and fetal development, this capability offers insights beyond conventional structural or hemodynamic imaging. Consistent with this, multispectral PAI has revealed significantly reduced placental oxygenation in preeclampsia models, supporting placental hypoxia as a key mechanism of disease pathogenesis [11].

Several representative studies highlight the rapid development of placental PAI. Zhu et al. developed an implantable placenta window that enables longitudinal intravital photoacoustic and fluorescence imaging throughout mouse pregnancy (Fig. 7A) [48]. Combined with ultrafast functional photoacoustic microscopy (UFF-PAM), the platform continuously monitored placental vascular development and oxygenation with unprecedented spatiotemporal resolution, while revealing placental responses to alcohol exposure, cardiac arrest, and inflammation. The integration of fluorescence imaging further enabled visualization of viral gene delivery and molecular transport across the placental barrier at single-cell resolution, providing a powerful tool for investigating placental physiology and the origins of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

To facilitate clinical translation, Maneas et al. developed two three-dimensional PAI systems for visualization of chorionic vasculature in human placentas (Fig. 7B) [71]. Volumetric imaging resolved superficial and subsurface fetal vessels to depths of approximately 7 mm in both normal-term and twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS)-treated placentas. The systems also distinguished laser-ablated tissue from surrounding vasculature, highlighting the potential of PAI to guide fetoscopic laser photocoagulation by improving the identification of residual vascular anastomoses and supporting more complete dichorionization during TTTS treatment.

Whole-body functional placental imaging was demonstrated by Basak et al., who developed a volumetric PAT platform capable of imaging approximately 4-cm cross-sections of pregnant mice in real time (Fig. 7C) [72]. Multispectral imaging enabled simultaneous visualization of maternal tissues, placenta, and embryos while quantitatively mapping HbO2, HbR, and sO₂ during oxygen-breathing challenges. The system further demonstrated the placental barrier function by monitoring ICG transport, illustrating the capability of PAT for non-invasive assessment of fetal-maternal oxygen exchange and embryo development.

In summary, preclinical studies have further demonstrated the capability of PAI for longitudinal monitoring of placental physiology. PA/US imaging has been used to dynamically quantify regional oxygenation under normoxic, hypoxic, and hyperoxic conditions, revealing the intrinsic oxygen-buffering capacity of the placenta and the heterogeneous oxygen distribution across placental compartments [73]. In models of placental dysfunction, decreased placental oxygenation has been consistently associated with impaired perfusion, oxidative stress, and inflammation. These observations are particularly relevant to GDM, where abnormal vascular remodeling and metabolic dysregulation play central pathological roles. Recent developments in volumetric PAI, including spherical-view PAT, further extend these capabilities by enabling non-invasive, three-dimensional visualization of placental oxygen dynamics throughout gestation. Collectively, these advances position PAI as a unique platform for bridging placental vascular structure with functional oxygen metabolism.

It is important to emphasize that the studies summarized above represent a spectrum of translational readiness. The mouse placenta window study and whole-body PAT in pregnant mice are preclinical proof-of-concept investigations. The ex vivo human placental imaging study demonstrates feasibility in human tissue but does not represent in vivo clinical imaging. To date, no study has directly applied PA/US imaging to evaluate placental functional responses to exercise interventions in women with GDM. The clinical application of PA/US imaging for assessing placental oxygenation and perfusion in vivo remains an active area of investigation rather than an established clinical tool. Future studies are needed to validate these approaches in pregnant populations, particularly in the context of GDM.

The value of imaging endpoints for safety and efficacy in exercise interventions

Regarding safety, a systematic review involving 4641 pregnant women provides robust evidence supporting the safety of prenatal exercise. Neither acute nor chronic exercise adversely affects fetal heart rate or utero-placental blood flow, and regular exercise may reduce umbilical artery flow resistance [74,75]. These findings support the implementation of exercise interventions in GDM and highlight Doppler USas a reliable tool for monitoring hemodynamic safety.

For efficacy, imaging techniques enable a shift from global or weight-based outcomes to organ-specific metabolic assessment. MRI and MRS can quantify reductions in visceral adiposity and hepatic or pancreatic lipid deposition, reflecting underlying metabolic improvements. Meanwhile, US-derived placental blood flow indices, combined with MRI-based fetal body composition analysis, allow integrated evaluation of maternal, placental, and fetal responses. Within this framework, imaging endpoints facilitate comparison of different exercise modalities, such as the effects of HIIT on fat reduction and placental perfusion, or RT on lean mass and fetal body composition.

Overall, imaging approaches provide objective and quantifiable evidence to both ensure safety and characterize efficacy, thereby supporting more individualized exercise prescriptions during pregnancy. Future studies in GDM may further incorporate advanced modalities such as PA/US imaging as exploratory endpoints to interrogate placental function beyond conventional glycemic measures. Key questions include whether metabolic improvements are paralleled by changes in placental perfusion and oxygenation, and whether distinct exercise modalities produce differential functional phenotypes. Integrating such imaging-based endpoints may help move the field from empirically guided recommendations toward mechanism-informed and precision-based intervention strategies.

Imaging-based assessment of maternal–fetal adaptations to exercise in GDM

Different exercise modalities (e.g., HIIT vs. RT) may preferentially remodel distinct imaging phenotypes: the former is more likely to manifest as perfusion- and hemodynamics-related changes, whereas the latter is more often associated with body composition- and fat distribution-related remodeling. However, this conceptual distinction is currently based on evidence derived predominantly from healthy pregnancies, animal models, and non-pregnant T2DM populations. Direct validation in GDM-specific cohorts is lacking, and the proposed differential imaging phenotypes should be considered a testable hypothesis rather than an established finding. This section outlines exercise-induced adaptive changes based on imaging evidence within the maternal–placental–fetal unit, with a focus on the potential differential imaging characteristics associated with various exercise modes.

Imaging evidence of exercise-induced placental hemodynamic adaptation

Placental function is a key determinant of pregnancy outcomes, and its hemodynamic status can be dynamically and non-invasively assessed using imaging modalities such as Doppler US. Current evidence suggests that regular exercise interventions, particularly structured aerobic exercise, HIIT, and RT, can induce favorable adaptations in placental hemodynamics without compromising fetal perfusion safety. It is important to recognize that Doppler-derived indices largely reflect vascular resistance and cannot, by themselves, establish whether placental oxygen delivery is effectively improved. Exercise-induced adaptations may involve coordinated changes in hemodynamics, microvascular perfusion redistribution, and oxygen utilization. Incorporating PA/US imaging into future studies may therefore help resolve whether observed increases in perfusion translate into meaningful improvements in oxygenation. This distinction is critical for separating true functional adaptation from compensatory hemodynamic responses, and for interpreting the physiological relevance of exercise-induced changes in the placenta.

Regarding long-term effects, a randomized controlled trial showed that, compared with controls, pregnant women participating in a supervised moderate-intensity exercise program showed a decrease in the z-score of the umbilical artery pulsatility index (UAPI) throughout gestation, while key parameters such as the uterine artery pulsatility index (PI) and middle cerebral artery PI remained stable [76]. Regular exercise selectively reduces vascular resistance on the placental-fetal side without triggering compensatory redistribution of fetal cerebral perfusion, reflecting a physiologically optimized pattern of placental blood flow regulation.

Acute imaging responses to exercise, several studies have shown that following moderate-to-high intensity aerobic exercise (approximately 60–84% of heart rate reserve) in healthy pregnant women, the uterine artery PI, resistance index (RI), and systolic-to-diastolic ratio (S/D) transiently decrease, indicating immediate improvement in maternal-side placental perfusion post-exercise [77]. Similarly, in regularly exercising healthy pregnant women, a single session of HIIT resistance circuit training resulted in significant reductions in umbilical artery S/D, PI, and RI, reflecting an acute decrease in vascular resistance within the fetal-placental circulation after high-intensity exercise [78]. These imaging changes are typically transient and reversible and are not accompanied by fetal heart rate abnormalities, further supporting the hemodynamic safety of exercise during pregnancy.Notably, most imaging evidence comes from healthy pregnant women, with limited data from GDM cohorts. Although these findings offer valuable insights into physiological adaptations to exercise, caution is warranted when extrapolating them to GDM populations, and future studies are needed to confirm whether similar hemodynamic responses occur in the setting of glucose intolerance.

Imaging evidence of placental vascularization and perfusion preservation under RT

Beyond traditional spectral Doppler parameters, 3D-PD US provides a more comprehensive assessment on placental vascularization. Evidence suggests that even during high-load RT exercises like bench press near one-repetition maximum, the placental vascular flow index (VFI) does not decrease and may even increase in regularly exercising pregnant women [79]. This indicates that, with professional guidance and appropriate exercise selection, heavy-load RT does not compromise overall placental perfusion and may instead induce transient flow enhancement. A large study showed that following a training regimen involving heavy-load resistance exercises like sumo deadlifts, bench press, and incline bench press, uterine artery PI significantly decreased, with a low incidence of adverse fetal events [80]. Imaging-detectable cardiovascular adaptation in the maternal–fetal unit

Postpartum placental analyses show that compared to sedentary women, regularly exercising pregnant women (regardless of intensity) exhibit marked upregulation of both mRNA and protein expression of Angiopoietin-1 (ANG1) in placental tissue (increased by 20-fold and 10-fold, respectively), without elevation of markers for hypoxia, endoplasmic reticulum stress, or oxidative stress [81]. This indicates that exercise directly stimulates the placenta to promote placental vascular network development, and this process is physiological rather than stress-induced, suggesting exercise induces "physiological vascular remodeling." Evidence from animal models shows that in mice with diet-induced maternal obesity, exercise intervention reverses obesity-induced reductions in placental labyrinth zone vascular density and significantly upregulates the expression of various angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF, APLN/Apelin) [82]. At the signaling pathway level, exercise activates the phosphorylation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)—which is suppressed by obesity in the placenta—thereby regulating downstream pathways including the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and insulin signaling, collectively promoting placental anabolism and angiogenesis [82]. This suggests that AMPK may act as a key regulator mediating exercise-induced improvements in placental function. Recent research on the impact of physical activity during pregnancy on placental structural adaptation also notes that moderate exercise may compensate for transient reductions in uterine blood flow during exercise by increasing villous and placental vascular surface area [83].

Pregnancy itself is accompanied by significant cardiovascular remodeling, characterized by increased cardiac output and hemodynamic load. In this context, imaging studies provide important approaches for assessing the impact of exercise on maternal cardiac function and fetal circulatory adaptation. Previous research indicates that regularly exercising pregnant women show better recovery of cardiac systolic and diastolic function, as assessed by imaging, during pregnancy and postpartum compared to sedentary women, suggesting exercise training induces favorable cardiovascular functional adaptations [84]. While some studies delve into exercise-related molecular and transcriptional changes, within the imaging framework, the clinical value lies in the ability of echocardiography and hemodynamic parameters to directly reflect improvements in maternal cardiac load and efficiency due to exercise intervention, providing objective evidence for evaluating safe windows for exercise in high-risk pregnancies.

In summary, current imaging evidence demonstrates that exercise interventions during pregnancy induce maternal-fetal blood flow and cardiovascular adaptive changes directly quantifiable by US and related imaging techniques. These changes are characterized by reduced placental flow resistance, maintained or enhanced vascularization, and benign remodeling of maternal cardiac function. These findings provide a solid basis for incorporating imaging endpoints into the core assessment framework of GDM exercise intervention studies.

Considerations for trial design: imaging endpoints in GDM exercise intervention research

Why imaging endpoints should be elevated from secondary to core outcome measures

In previous exercise intervention studies for GDM, efficacy evaluation has primarily relied on glycemic measures (e.g., fasting glucose, HbA1c, and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) parameters) and weight control outcomes. Traditional self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) only provides discrete-time-point glucose values, failing to capture 24-hour glycemic variability, which limits precise assessment of GDM management effectiveness. In recent years, CGM technology provides an effective solution. CGM provides continuous measurement of core metrics including time in range (TIR), time above range (TAR), time below range (TBR), mean blood glucose (MBG), mean amplitude of glucose excursions (MAGE), and coefficient of variation (CV), thereby enabling comprehensive assessment of glycemic control quality. A systematic review notes [85] that CGM is significantly superior to SMBG in detecting hyperglycemic and hypoglycemic events and is more closely related to clinical decision-making and pregnancy outcomes, serving as a more precise predictor for insulin therapy. Future studies should prioritize CGM core metrics (e.g., TIR, TAR, nocturnal MBG) as primary endpoints for evaluating intervention efficacy [86,87] and incorporate patients' baseline metabolic characteristics (e.g., HbA1c level) in the design [88] to identify subgroups that respond best to different exercise modalities, supporting personalized intervention strategies. However, these endpoints largely reflect the final outcomes of systemic metabolic status and often fail to directly reveal the immediate or structural impacts of exercise intervention on key organs within the maternal-fetal system, particularly the placenta and cardiovascular system. In this context, imaging technologies, especially Doppler US, 3D power Doppler, and MRI, demonstrate clear advantages. These techniques can quantitatively assess placental perfusion, vascular resistance, and maternal cardiovascular function status in a non-invasive and repeatable manner, thereby capturing subclinical functional changes during exercise intervention that may not yet be reflected in glycemic changes but could be predictive. Elevating imaging parameters from auxiliary observational variables in traditional studies to core or key secondary endpoints in GDM exercise intervention research has clear scientific rationale and clinical value. This perspective is further supported by recent advances in biomedical imaging, particularly PA/US-based functional imaging, which has demonstrated strong potential for non-invasive assessment of vascular structure, perfusion, and oxygenation. In placental research, such technologies may help define whether an intervention modifies only systemic metabolic indices or truly improves placental functional status at the level of perfusion–oxygenation coupling [11,89].

Recommended core imaging endpoints for future research

Based on current evidence, clinical feasibility, and reproducibility, imaging endpoints for future GDM exercise intervention studies can be categorized into the following three tiers: placental and uteroplacental circulation indices, quantitative assessment of placental vascularization, and maternal cardiovascular function assessment.

Placental and uteroplacental circulation indices (core imaging endpoints)

Recommended core imaging endpoints include uterine artery PI and RI, umbilical artery PI, RI, and S/D, as well as the cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) [[76], [77], [78]]. These indices are widely used for assessing placental vascular resistance and fetal perfusion status and have been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes such as GDM, gestational hypertension, and fetal growth restriction [15,90]. However, as noted in Section 3.1, Doppler-derived parameters reflect macrovascular resistance rather than direct measures of oxygen delivery, microvascular function, or placental metabolic status. They are best interpreted as part of a multimodal assessment, ideally combined with other functional measures such as 3D power Doppler vascularization indices and, where feasible, oxygenation-sensitive imaging. In exercise intervention studies, longitudinal follow-up of these indices pre- and post-intervention helps assess the trajectory and temporal characteristics of different exercise modalities' impact on placental hemodynamics.

Quantitative assessment of placental vascularization (advanced imaging endpoints)

Key secondary endpoints include 3D-PD US parameters such as the vascularization index (VI), VFI, and shear wave elastography. Compared to traditional spectral Doppler parameters, 3D power Doppler quantifies placental vascular network density and perfusion levels at a holistic level [79,91], making it more suitable for comparing the differential impact of various exercise modes (e.g., HIIT vs. RT) on placental vascularization patterns. Where feasible, novel functional US/PAI may be incorporated to assess placental microflow and oxygenation [48], with emerging evidence demonstrating its capability for real-time visualization of placental oxygenation and hemodynamics in vivo [11].

Furthermore, the study by Park, Geonho et al. [92] reported a wearable ultrasound patch (UPatch) for continuous, autonomous fetal monitoring (Fig. 8). Placed on the maternal abdomen for continuous surveillance at the placental cord insertion site, the UPatch can acquire continuous flow spectra during fetal and maternal movement without requiring a sonographer, reducing operator dependency and clinical workload. The study validated the UPatch's accuracy in 62 participants, covering various vessel depths, orientations, and placental locations. Based on Bland-Altman analysis, the UPatch results showed substantial agreement with clinical-grade equipment. Moreover, using the UPatch for continuous fetal heart rate and umbilical cord flow monitoring in 52 pregnant women generated longitudinal data enabling differentiation of transient fluctuations from persistent flow abnormalities, effectively distinguishing high-risk from healthy pregnancies. In the future, UPatch data streams could be integrated with other physiological metrics from the fetus and mother for a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the dynamic interactions between fetus and mother [93]. Applying big data analytics to correlate these data streams with postnatal outcomes could provide novel insights into the mechanisms of fetal complications, driving continuous advancements in clinical diagnosis and intervention strategies.

Maternal cardiovascular function assessment (supportive imaging endpoints)

Echocardiography-assessed parameters such as cardiac output and diastolic function, as well as hemodynamic response characteristics during exercise or stress (e.g., the relationship between heart rate and functional indices), can serve as supportive imaging endpoints. These parameters help assess the impact of exercise intervention on maternal cardiovascular adaptability from an imaging perspective and can provide objective evidence for stratifying exercise intensity and managing safety in high-risk pregnancies [84].

Imaging-informed design of exercise intervention trials

Personalization is central to the success of exercise interventions. Prescription should be closely tailored to the pregnant woman's pre-pregnancy BMI, baseline insulin resistance, exercise habits, and gestational age. In clinical application, careful consideration of exercise mode and intensity is required to avoid a one-size-fits-all approach [94]. Incorporating real-time fetal heart rate monitoring and related physiological parameter tracking during exercise sessions helps objectively and dynamically record exercise intensity and maternal-fetal physiological responses, thereby enhancing both study data reliability and patient adherence while ensuring safety [95,96]. For mechanistic exploration, beyond classic metabolic and inflammatory pathways, future research may further investigate the potential impact of HIIT and RT on the gut microbiota composition and function in GDM patients [90], offering supplementary explanations for exercise intervention effects from the microbiota-gut axis perspective. Such mechanistic studies are better suited as biological context supporting imaging outcome changes, rather than serving as direct trial endpoints. With advancing technologies, the application prospects of imaging and digital tools in exercise intervention research are expanding. Evidence suggests that artificial intelligence prediction models based on CGM data can accurately forecast short-term glycemic trends [97]. Future efforts could attempt to integrate such models with exercise intervention strategies to provide more targeted exercise advice for pregnant women, shifting GDM management from reactive to proactive control. Simultaneously, the rapid development of wearable US, functional US, and micro-flow imaging technologies provides new possibilities for continuous, low-burden monitoring of placental and fetal blood flow during exercise or daily activities [93,98]. These technologies have the potential to reduce operator dependency and capture dynamic physiological changes that traditional static examinations may miss, supporting high-frequency data acquisition in real-world settings. In terms of trial design and data analysis, future studies could concurrently collect imaging endpoints, CGM metrics (e.g., TIR, MBG), and traditional metabolic markers. This would allow investigation into whether improvements in placental blood flow or vascularization precede glycemic improvements and the mediating or moderating role of imaging changes in predicting maternal-neonatal outcomes. Integrating and analyzing such multimodal data holds promise for constructing more explanatory and clinically meaningful models of exercise intervention effects.

From a study design perspective, PA/US imaging is unlikely to be appropriate as a primary endpoint in early clinical studies, but may serve as a mechanistic or exploratory imaging outcome. Transabdominal placental PA/US imaging also faces several challenges that currently limit clinical translation. Optical attenuation and spectral coloring through the maternal abdominal wall and other overlying tissues can affect both imaging depth and sO₂ estimation. These effects may be more pronounced with increased maternal adiposity or a posteriorly located placenta. Respiratory and fetal motion can introduce additional variability, while separation of maternal and fetal vascular signals remains challenging. In addition, differences in acquisition settings, reconstruction methods, spectral correction, and calibration may limit quantitative comparisons across systems. Further technical development and standardized acquisition and analysis protocols will therefore be needed before placental PA/US can be used as a reproducible clinical endpoint. In practice, small, well-characterized cohorts—such as those with anterior placentation, adequate acoustic windows, and good follow-up adherence—may be selected for longitudinal imaging. Serial measurements incorporating Doppler indices, 3D-PD parameters, elastography, and PA-derived sO₂ can then be integrated with CGM data, inflammatory and oxidative stress markers, and maternal–fetal outcomes to enable correlation analyses. This strategy reflects current technical constraints while allowing for the identification of candidate imaging biomarkers and preliminary effect size estimation, thereby informing the design of subsequent larger-scale clinical studies [99,100]. Future clinical translation will therefore require improved light-delivery strategies, optimized wavelength selection, motion correction, spectral correction, and standardized calibration and analysis procedures.

Focus on efficient exercise modalities in pregnancy: HIIT and RT

Among various forms of prenatal exercise intervention, HIIT and RT have received increasing attention in recent years due to their advantages in metabolic regulation, cardiovascular adaptation, and body composition remodeling [74]. Compared to traditional moderate-intensity continuous aerobic exercise, these two modalities possess distinct characteristics in their mechanisms of action, adaptation kinetics, and potential imaging phenotypes, providing a useful framework for exploring the "exercise---imaging---outcome" relationship.

HIIT

HIIT refers to an intermittent exercise pattern involving short bursts of exercise performed at or near intensities approaching or exceeding the lactate threshold (e.g., 85–95% of maximum heart rate), alternating with periods of low-intensity activity or passive rest [101,102]. In general and T2DM populations, HIIT has been shown to be more effective than traditional moderate-intensity continuous training in improving cardiorespiratory fitness, improving insulin sensitivity, and reducing HbA1c [103]. In pregnant populations, recent HIIT studies have primarily focused on safety and feasibility endpoints. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that an 8-week prenatal HIIT program significantly increased maximal oxygen uptake and reduced exercise blood pressure in pregnant women, with no observed serious maternal-fetal adverse events [16].

From the perspective of metabolic and body composition regulation, HIIT may offer the following advantages: First, HIIT can enhance the expression and translocation of GLUT4 in skeletal muscle and promote mitochondrial biogenesis, thereby enhancing skeletal muscle glucose uptake and utilization efficiency. Second, due to the "excess post-exercise oxygen consumption" (EPOC) effect following high-intensity exercise, the body maintains a higher metabolic rate for an extended period post-exercise, which may help reduce postprandial glucose fluctuations. Third, HIIT can promote reductions in body weight and fat mass by decreasing adipocyte size, inhibiting the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) from adipose tissue, and enhancing muscle glucose utilization. Existing research suggests HIIT may also influence weight management by modulating gut microbiota structure (e.g., increasing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio) [104]. Although direct evidence from GDM populations is lacking, this provides indirect support for its potential metabolic benefits. Implementing HIIT during pregnancy still faces several challenges. Elevated estrogen levels during pregnancy can lead to connective tissue laxity and reduced joint stability, making high-impact movements more likely to induce musculoskeletal injury. Furthermore, high-intensity intermittent exercise may cause transient increases in core temperature and relative hypoxemia, which theoretically could affect embryonic development in early pregnancy [104,105]. Previous studies also indicate that heat stress can alter placental blood flow distribution, suggesting excessive heating carries potential risks. HIIT protocols vary considerably across studies in terms of interval duration, work-to-rest ratios, and specific exercise modalities, making it difficult to establish a unified, generalizable prescription suitable for pregnancy or the GDM population at present.

RT

RT enhances muscle strength and size by working against body weight or external resistance (e.g., resistance bands, dumbbells, machines). For most pregnant women, professionally assessed and individually designed RT has shown good safety and feasibility. Regarding glucose metabolism regulation, RT can activate anabolic pathways within muscle cells (e.g., the mTOR signaling pathway), stimulating hypertrophy and potentially hyperplasia of type II muscle fibers, thereby increasing skeletal muscle glucose storage capacity [106]. Energy stress such as exercise activates the AMPK signaling pathway, increasing GLUT4 expression in muscle and fat cells, which facilitates the transport of more extracellular glucose into cells, leading to a reduction in blood glucose levels.

Regarding inflammation and oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 are implicated in the immunometabolic homeostasis during pregnancy. They induce insulin resistance directly or indirectly—TNF-α by interfering with insulin signaling and IL-6 by contributing to systemic inflammation. Serum levels of both are elevated in women with GDM and are associated with poor glycemic control and adverse pregnancy outcomes. A study by Pedro Acosta-Manzano et al. [107] found that after 8 weeks of resistance band training (3 sessions per week, 30 min per session), serum TNF-α levels decreased by 18%-22% and IL-6 levels by 15%-17% in women with GDM. Additionally, RT can enhance maternal antioxidant enzyme activity (e.g., SOD, GSH-Px) and reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. At the placental tissue level, RT is associated with significant reductions in ROS and lipid peroxidation products [90], which helps protect trophoblast cell insulin receptor function and mitigate "placenta-derived" insulin resistance. Nevertheless, implementing RT has certain limitations. Postural changes, increased weight, and joint laxity during pregnancy may limit the feasibility of high-load training. Incorrect technique or excessive load can increase the risk of low back and pelvic discomfort. Therefore, RT programs should adhere to basic principles such as starting with low loads, progressive overload, avoiding the Valsalva maneuver and supine positions, and incorporating professional guidance and fetal heart rate monitoring when necessary.In summary, HIIT and RT exhibit complementary characteristics in terms of metabolic response kinetics, body composition regulation, and potential risk profiles: the former leans more towards rapid metabolic activation and functional adaptation, while the latter focuses on structural improvements and long-term, stable benefits (Fig. 9). Precisely for these reasons, these two exercise modalities have become the most representative and research-worthy subjects in current discussions on prenatal exercise interventions, particularly for GDM management, and also provide a solid foundation for subsequent imaging-based analysis of their biological effects. Based on these characteristics, the metabolic and circulatory adaptations induced by HIIT and RT should theoretically manifest as distinguishable imaging phenotypes at different organ levels (Fig. 10). Core metabolic effects of HIIT and RT: providing a mechanistic basis for imaging phenotype selection

Although mechanistic evidence in pregnant populations—particularly those with GDM—remains relatively limited, systematic reviews and meta-analyses from general and T2DM populations suggest: HIIT tends to trigger rapid metabolic adaptations related to "energy stress---mitochondrial and vascular function," while RT tends to produce structural, sustainable metabolic benefits through "muscle mass/quality---basal metabolic capacity" [103,[108], [109], [110], [111], [112], [113], [114], [115]]. Regarding glycemic control, HIIT can improve HbA1c and fasting glucose over relatively short periods by enhancing skeletal muscle glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation via IRS1–PTP-1B regulation, AMPK-PGC-1α activation, and GLUT4 translocation [111,112,114,115]. RT primarily improves basal metabolism and long-term insulin sensitivity mainly through mTOR-mediated muscle hypertrophy and increased type II fiber and GLUT4 capacity [110,116]. At the level of inflammation and oxidative stress, HIIT tends to exert anti-inflammatory effects via pathways such as reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress and improving mitochondrial quality control [[117], [118], [119], [120]]. RT emphasizes maintaining or increasing skeletal muscle mass, promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory myokines, and improving endothelial function, thereby providing a more stable, lower-risk anti-inflammatory background [120]. Combined training may yield complementary or synergistic effects and suggests that different exercise modes may correspond to different priority pathways for imaging phenotype changes (perfusion/flow resistance-related vs. body composition/fat distribution-related) [110,121]. Extrapolation to pregnant women with GDM requires caution, as pregnancy-specific hormonal, metabolic, and hemodynamic changes may modify these pathways. The "imaging expression" of pathogenetic clues—from molecular pathways to quantifiable imaging endpoints

The core aim of this review is to translate the metabolic benefits of HIIT and RT into organ/tissue phenotypes measurable by imaging: for example, reductions in maternal visceral fat, improved placental perfusion, decreased vascular resistance, and fetal body composition remodeling. Mechanisms in this article aim to propose a closed-loop hypothesis of "imaging endpoint – mechanistic explanation – trial design," enabling more precise evaluation and replication of exercise intervention studies (and providing mediating variables for outcome prediction models). Within this framework, PA/US imaging provides a means to bridge hemodynamic changes with tissue-level oxygen metabolism. While Doppler-derived indices reflect alterations in vascular resistance and flow distribution, they do not establish whether oxygen delivery is effectively improved. A more meaningful indication of functional improvement arises when increased perfusion is accompanied by restoration of local sO₂ or reduced oxygenation heterogeneity. Accordingly, PA-derived sO₂, measures of oxygenation heterogeneity, and perfusion–oxygen coupling metrics may define a set of imaging-derived functional phenotypes that warrant further investigation in the context of exercise interventions in GDM (Fig. 11).

Imaging the direct metabolic effects: focusing on GLUT4 and PGC-1α hubs, translating to "muscle/fat" imaging phenotypes

The "direct metabolic effects" of exercise on improving glycemia and insulin sensitivity can be summarized as being centered on enhanced skeletal muscle glucose uptake capacity. At the mechanistic level, muscle contraction promotes the translocation of GLUT4 from intracellular storage sites to the plasma membrane and T-tubules, increasing glucose uptake. During higher-intensity exercise, skeletal muscle hyperemia and capillary recruitment further reduce uptake resistance, potentially making glucose phosphorylation a rate-limiting step [123] (Fig. 12).

Schematic representation of GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake. (A) Molecular signaling pathways mediating contraction-induced GLUT4 translocation to the cell membrane. (B) GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells. (adapted from [123]).

Concurrently, ROS, as exercise-induced signaling molecules, participate in regulating GLUT4 translocation and can also activate pathways promoting GLUT4 expression, thereby contributing to more persistent metabolic sensitization following exercise training [124]. Furthermore, PGC-1α, the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, can be upregulated through exercise-induced epigenetic modifications and is associated with improvements in mitochondrial quantity and function [125,126].

These mechanisms can be translated into two types of observable imaging phenotypes: ① Body composition and fat distribution remodeling: Reduction in visceral fat and ectopic fat deposition (e.g., hepatic/pancreatic fat), corresponding to quantifiable metrics such as visceral fat volume and hepatic/pancreatic fat fraction via computed tomography (CT)/MRI (including MRS). ② Indirect evidence of perfusion and metabolic adaptation: Improved tissue perfusion and metabolic substrate utilization post-exercise can form a mechanism-phenotype linkage with functional imaging (e.g. perfusion-related MRI indices, or synergistic changes with US hemodynamic indices), providing an explanatory framework for the differential effects of HIIT/RT.

Imaging the indirect metabolic effects: the adipose tissue inflammation-oxidative stress axis, translating to "visceral fat/flow resistance/placental phenotypes"

Key features of GDM include enhanced oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation. Enhanced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome accompanied by upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18 can further impair insulin signaling and exacerbate insulin resistance [127]. Simultaneously, maternal obesity and GDM can suppress placental vascular development, alter nutrient transport, and induce mitochondrial oxidative dysfunction [82,128]. Therefore, when evaluating the intervention effects of HIIT/RT, merely observing glycemia is insufficient. It is essential to incorporate placental blood flow and function as a key intermediary component within the same experimental framework. Quantifying visceral fat via CT/MRI can serve as an indirect surrogate endpoint for inflammatory load. Doppler indices of the uterine and umbilical arteries (PI, RI, S/D) and the CPR, which reflect vascular resistance and blood flow redistribution, can be used to identify inflammation-related changes in perfusion efficiency and fetal compensatory responses. If placental volume/vascularization indices (3D US vascularization indices) are collected alongside changes in flow resistance, this enables linking "structural adaptation" with "functional improvement" into a testable chain. Incorporation of PA-derived sO₂ extends the conventional structure–perfusion framework to include oxygenation as a key functional dimension. This extension is particularly important in GDM, where early placental alterations may not be reflected by changes in vascular resistance, but instead arise from impaired oxygen exchange efficiency or increased oxidative stress.

HIIT is more likely to induce changes in flow resistance indices (PI/RI/S/D, UAPI z-score) and perfusion-related parameters via the "energy stress → improved vascular function and perfusion" pathway. RT is more likely to manifest as improvements in body composition and basal metabolism through the "muscle mass/metabolic capacity → sustained metabolic sensitization" pathway, indirectly promoting placental vascularization and structural remodeling via long-term adaptation. Combined HIIT and RT training may simultaneously drive both "functional perfusion improvement (short-term) + vascular structural remodeling (mid-to-long term)," potentially offering greater advantages for maternal-neonatal outcomes.

Beyond the placental-maternal-fetal circulation, exercise during pregnancy may also activate cardioprotective signaling and hemodynamic adaptation in the mother [129], and influence the fetal cardiac transcriptional regulatory network [130]. In imaging trial design, it is recommended to include maternal/fetal cardiac function US as exploratory endpoints, collected in parallel with placental hemodynamic indices, to assess the potential mediating role of "circulatory adaptation" in improving maternal-neonatal outcomes.

Overall, systematically incorporating imaging endpoints into the trial design of GDM exercise intervention research enhances the precision and organ specificity of efficacy evaluation but also provide objective evidence for the safety assessment and personalized application of different exercise modalities. Imaging should not merely serve as a tool for outcome verification but should become a key bridge connecting exercise intervention, metabolic improvement, and maternal-fetal outcomes.

Conclusion

Exercise research in GDM is moving from assessing efficacy to defining how interventions should be evaluated and individualized. Imaging modalities provide objective measures of placental adaptation and enable linkage between metabolic changes and pregnancy outcomes. Integration of PA/US imaging with Doppler, vascularization, elastography, and CGM data may further extend this framework by incorporating oxygenation as a functional dimension. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that PA/US imaging is still an emerging modality for placental assessment, and its application within GDM exercise trials remains preliminary. The proposed imaging-derived placental functional phenotypes require systematic validation in GDM-specific cohorts prior to any clinical translation. This approach supports a transition toward imaging-defined placental functional phenotypes and highlights that glycemic improvement does not necessarily reflect improved placental function.
