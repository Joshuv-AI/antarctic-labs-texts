Champions and early adopters in an implementation-effectiveness study of shared decision making: implications for interpersonal strategies
Authors: Jennifer L. Ridgeway, Megan E. Branda, Shubhangi Bagewadi, Victor Montori, Hugh Jackson, Vivek Nautiyal, Ashlee Dagoberg, Yvonne L. Gomez, Hooman Gharai, Charanjit S. Khurana, Kelly Louks, Derek L. Gravholt, Victor M. Montori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough recommended, shared decision making (SDM) is not widely adopted for primary prevention of cardiovascular (CV) disease. Champions may influence peer adoption if they are enthusiastic and skilled. We describe adoption of an SDM conversation aid for CV risk reduction (CV Prevention Choice) by champions and early adopters in a pragmatic implementation trial and consider their influence on uptake in clinics.MethodsWe embedded the SDM tool in the electronic health record in three U.S. health care systems. Eight of their 15 affiliated clinics deployed a champion implementation strategy. Adopters used the SDM tool in ≥ 2 routine clinical encounters. Superusers had ≥ 20 encounters. Penetration was the proportion of adopters among eligible clinicians. Adoption and penetration were assessed in the usual care (early adoption), active implementation, and maintenance implementation phases. Thematic analysis methods were used to analyze qualitative data from individual interviews and periodic reflections.ResultsBetween May 2021 and January 2025, 37 interviews with clinicians and other staff and 65 periodic reflections were completed. Among 176 clinicians, 55% (n = 97) used CV Prevention Choice at least once and 42% (n = 76) adopted it. Champions and other opinion leaders gave presentations and demonstrated their practice, but targeted individual persuasion and group dialogue were less common, especially when champions appreciated peers’ preferences. Champions used the tool more frequently (median = 93, IQR 53–150) than non-champions (median = 16, IQR 4–50), although only 4 of the top 20 users were champions. Clinics with champions had higher penetration rates (50% vs 29%) and lower rates of one-time tool use than clinics without champions (17% vs 33%).ConclusionChampions, superusers, and other opinions leaders may influence peer adoption of clinical innovations by sharing their experience, but group discourse may be needed to build the case for uptake of innovations. Superusers may be impactful in clinics without formal champions.Trial registrationThe protocol described herein was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov on 22 June 2020 (No. NCT04450914).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00977-0.

Contributions to literature:• Champions and early adopters may influence peer adoption of an SDM tool, but they need to practice it in routine care to develop self-efficacy.• Better definition of champions and other interpersonal strategies is needed to leverage superusers who are not identified as influential champions, but who may have capacity to promote innovation uptake and use.• Champions may share their experience with individual clinicians or groups of peers, but group discourse may be needed to debate the relative merits of using an SDM tool compared to continuing their usual practice.

Background

The increasing burden of cardiovascular (CV) disease [1, 2] can be attributed in large part to the suboptimal implementation of prevention strategies and uncontrolled Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) risk factors in many adults. Shared decision making (SDM) is a method of care by which patients and clinicians work together to address the patient’s situation, including one where treatment decisions may be made [3]. The process involves developing a shared understanding of the aspect of the patient situation that demands action (i.e., the risk of ASCVD) and considering what actions this situation demands (i.e., whether to initiate or intensify the use of preventive interventions) in light of their potential benefits (i.e., ability to reduce ASCVD risk), harms, and burdens, arriving through collaborative deliberation to an agreed-upon course of action [4–7]. In the context of CV disease, SDM is guideline-recommended for primary prevention of CV risk [8].

Despite its recommendation, SDM has not been widely adopted in preventive care settings [9]. Barriers include clinician beliefs that patients do not want to be involved in decision making and lack of formal SDM training, as well as organizational factors like lack of time and resources for SDM and challenges incorporating SDM into routine workflows (e.g., team-based care, use of EHR) [10, 11]. Implementation strategies are methods or techniques that support adoption, implementation, or sustainability of an evidence-based practice (EBP) and address barriers to successful implementation and sustainability [12–15]. Educational outreach and training strategies, for example, may support SDM adoption by increasing clinician knowledge about the goals of SDM or their confidence engaging patients in SDM. SDM tools such as conversation aids that summarize evidence and portray visual depictions of risks and treatment options can also effectively support SDM in clinical encounters by providing information about the patient situation (e.g., patient’s estimated ASCVD risk) and the effect of different treatment decisions [16, 17]. Integration of tools such as conversation aids in the electronic health record (EHR) may ameliorate barriers related to workflow integration. Interpersonal strategies (i.e., ones that rely on relationships between individuals) may be needed to influence clinician attitudes, though, including concern that SDM takes too much time or interferes with clinician autonomy.

Clinical champions are people who promote an innovation among their peers, and as such, they can play a role in overcoming resistance [18]. In the Expert Recommendations for Implementation Change (ERIC) taxonomy of implementation strategies [14], the champion strategy includes activities such as building relationships, facilitating knowledge and skills, and communicating need for the practice change [18]. There is some evidence that champions are associated with increased uptake of healthcare innovations [19, 20], including in the context of CV disease [21], although definitions of champions are varied and may be interchanged with “opinion leaders” and other related titles.

The growing evidence base suggests that champions may be most effective when they are engaged and committed, persuasive and credible, and strong communicators, and when they are embedded in the clinical setting where the practice change is taking place [19, 22, 23]. Enthusiasm and belief in the initiative have also been cited as important characteristics of effective champions, and having personal positive experience with the innovation might be especially important for helping others form positive attitudes about it [19, 23]. Self-efficacy delivering the innovation may also positively impact performance if champions are better able to facilitate knowledge development among others and troubleshoot problems [18].

Champions may perform essential functions in various stages of implementation, but during early stages [18], when the innovation is new to the setting, it may be difficult to identify individuals who will be sufficiently enthusiastic about it to serve as champions, and few may be skilled in using the innovation. When champions are not experienced users, they may have limited influence on peers or lack the self-efficacy necessary to effectively promote its use.

We developed an SDM conversation aid to support clinical discussions about CV preventive care (CV Prevention Choice), implemented it in the EHR of three U.S. health systems [24] and evaluated tailored implementation strategies in a hybrid design pragmatic trial. Adoption of CV Prevention Choice was the primary outcome [25]. All three health systems identified clinical champions to support at least one of their clinics. This report describes adoption among clinics with identified champions and considers the effect of champions and of early adopters on CV Prevention Choice uptake in clinics. We address two central questions related to clinicians and clinics: 1) Did designated clinical champions adopt CV Prevention Choice early and were they high users; and 2) Did clinics with champions or other early adopters have high rates of penetration and overall use of CV Prevention Choice? Answers to these questions provide insights into whether champions are enthusiastic adopters, use the tool enough to likely develop self-efficacy in its use, and influence peer adoption.

Methods

Setting and participants

This work was conducted as part of a hybrid implementation-effectiveness study in three U.S. health systems, the objective of which was to increase the routine use of SDM for primary CV prevention (the Shared Decision Making for Individualized CV Prevention [SDM4IP] study) [25]. Each health system integrated CV Prevention Choice in their version of the Epic EHR [24] and targeted four to six of their primary or preventive care clinics to receive tailored implementation strategies, have their patient encounters assessed for CV Prevention Choice use, and participate in research activities (e.g., clinician surveys). All clinicians who provided preventive care to adult patients in the selected clinics were eligible to participate. In addition to central study staff (principal investigators with expertise in implementation science and SDM, statistician, analyst), our research team included a co-investigator and implementation facilitator from each health system. Co-investigators were all practicing clinicians in a clinic, but as a health system co-investigator, they provided leadership for all of the clinics in their health system. Facilitators had non-clinical roles but extensive experience in the clinical settings. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB#20-002772).

Study design

SDM4IP was a hybrid design pragmatic trial with both implementation and effectiveness aims and stepped-wedge cluster randomization at the health system level [25]. Implementation outcomes were organized using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) implementation evaluation framework [26, 27]. This report focuses on adoption of SDM, which was considered an EBP based on the 2019 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines strong (Class I) recommendation of SDM for primary CV prevention [8, 14].

CV Prevention Choice use during a clinical encounter was the proxy for clinician adoption of SDM. While conversation aid use is not a perfect indication of whether SDM occurred, it is a surrogate measure of the extent to which clinicians used information about personalized CV risk—populated within the electronic workflow—to engage patients in a conversation about their estimated risk and of the treatment options to address this threat during a routine clinical encounter. CV Prevention Choice was designed for clinicians to use it with patients during the clinical encounter [28]. It displays 100-people pictographs of the patient’s estimated 10-year ASCVD risk and of the expected change in this risk after starting or stopping preventive interventions, including diet, exercise, smoking cessation, and preventive medications (e.g., statins, antihypertensives). This represents a trigger for a conversation about the reasons for implementing one or more preventive interventions, including their potential for reducing ASCVD risk given their potential downsides. The conversation continues until patient and clinician agree on a plan of care that is commensurate with the level of threat (ASCVD risk), is capable of sufficiently reducing that risk to justify the intervention’s harms and costs, and is feasible in the life of the patient [28]. Consistent with the pragmatic study design [29], CV Prevention Choice was available to all clinicians who wanted to use it in a clinical encounter during the study period; the study team neither required clinicians to use the SDM tool nor deployed study procedures demanding its use in particular encounters.

There were four study phases: pre-implementation (study start-up), usual care, active implementation, and maintenance implementation. The usual care phase began with integration of CV Prevention Choice in the EHR and included passive monitoring of its use. Integration of CV Prevention Choice in the EHR was considered a marker of usual care because that condition mimics that of many clinicians in similar settings, who have tools related to CV risk in their EHR. The start of the active implementation phase indicated the start of active implementation strategies (beyond EHR integration) being deployed in the health system. Finally, active implementation strategies were stopped for the maintenance implementation phase, although those that were routinized in the settings and did not rely on the study team or the health system co-investigator or facilitator were allowed to continue.

Implementation strategies deployed in the active implementation phase were based on results of formative evaluation and selected by the health system co-investigator and facilitator. As previously described, formative evaluation included interviews, surveys, site visits, and periodic reflections [25]. Determinants identified during formative evaluation were organized by domains of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) framework [30] (e.g., tension for change, adaptability, access to knowledge and information, and relative advantage) and mapped to implementation strategies in the ERIC taxonomy [14]. Candidate strategies were presented to the health system co-investigator and facilitator for final selection using feasibility and impact criteria.

The health system co-investigator and facilitator also chose which of their clinics would receive each strategy. Some strategies, like educational outreach, were deployed across all clinics in the health system. Other strategies, like visual reminders on clinician computers and identification of clinical champions, were targeted to specific clinics. The clinical champions strategy was selected in response to key determinants identified as important in all four health systems, most importantly innovation relative advantage and adaptability. More specifically, the strategy was aimed at helping clinicians see how CV Prevention Choice could support their conversations with patients and do so in a way that had advantages over the standard risk calculators (i.e., estimations of the level of threat only) available in the EHR or on smartphones. It supplemented other educational outreach in some clinics, with the champion strategy selected for its potential to influence peers.

Champion selection and preparation

Although the champion strategy was considered for all 15 clinics, it was implemented in only eight. Champions were selected by health system co-investigators and facilitators based on peer respect, communication skills, familiarity with local context and organizational culture, and experience with quality improvement or practice change. The primary reason for not deploying the champion strategy in a clinic was the inability to identify a clinician willing to serve in the role. Although designed to be pragmatic and minimally burdensome (less than one hour per month), most clinicians declined due to time considerations. Health system co-investigators and facilitators prepared champions using guidance from the study team, including meeting with selected champions to orient them on how best to use CV Prevention Choice to support SDM. All champions had the same exposure to CV Prevention Choice as their peers in the clinics during the usual care phase. The facilitator in one health system (HS1) requested a 30-minute meeting between the identified champions in HS1 clinics, during which champions asked study team leadership about CV Prevention Choice use cases. During the active implementation phase, health system co-investigators and facilitators were asked to share with the champions information they could use in their communications with peers, including any updates to CV Prevention Choice and monthly reports on clinic-level and clinician-level adoption.

Data collection and analysis

Data for findings presented in this manuscript are detailed below. They include structured data from the EHR, as well as qualitative data from interviews and periodic reflections. EHR data were analyzed using SAS software v9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Qualitative data were entered into NVivo software (Lumivero, Version 14) to facilitate queries for analysis.

EHR data

Data collection. Encounter-level data, including patient demographic and clinical information, were abstracted monthly and transferred to the study team for analysis. Encounter data included a clinician identifier, and those were coded to identify designated champions.

Clinician-level data analyses. Adoption was analyzed among all clinicians with eligible encounters during the study period (i.e., encounters with a patient who would be eligible for a conversation about CV primary prevention during a scheduled visit with a physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant) regardless of the reason for the visit. Clinicians were categorized by type: 1) “Non-adopters” include those who never used CV Prevention Choice during the study period; 2) “One-time users” include those who used CV Prevention Choice a single time but then discontinued use (i.e., they tried it but did not adopt it); and 3) “Adopters” include those who used CV Prevention Choice in at least two clinical encounters. Superusers were adopters who used CV Prevention Choice ≥ 20 times.

Adopters were further categorized by timing of clinician first use, using a system guided by categories of innovation adoption adapted from Diffusions of Innovations theory [31] and organized into the usual care, active implementation, and maintenance implementation phases, as detailed below:Usual care phase (EHR integration only): Innovators were individuals who used CV Prevention Choice for the first time in the first half of usual care, while early adopters used it for the first time during the second half of the usual care phase. Innovators are thought to be risk-tolerant people who actively seek information and new ideas. Early adopters are similarly engaged with early innovations, but they may take more calculated risks and encourage adoption among peers.Active implementation phase (active implementation strategies in use): The early majority used CV Prevention Choice for the first time in the first half of the active implementation phase, while the late majority used it for the first time in the second half. Individuals in the early majority group may adopt earlier than average, while the late majority may be somewhat more cautious than average individuals in the group.Maintenance implementation phase (removal of active implementation support): Laggards used CV Prevention Choice for the first time during the maintenance phase. Laggards are generally thought to be more traditional, adopting after the innovation is established.

Clinic-level data analyses. Penetration is the integration of an innovation in a setting (i.e., its widespread use among all eligible people). In this study, it was assessed as the proportion of clinicians in the clinic who adopted CV Prevention Choice among all eligible clinicians in the practice. Discontinuation was assessed as the proportion of clinicians who used CV Prevention Choice one time among clinicians who used the tool at least once (i.e., one-time users). Penetration and discontinuation were assessed by clinic and among clinics with and without a champion strategy and with and without other (non-champion) innovators and early adopters who went on to be super users, overall and by phase.

Interviews and periodic reflections

Data collection. Periodic reflections, which are lightly structured prompts for guided discussion [32], were conducted at monthly online meetings of the study team and the health system facilitators, which were recorded and transcribed. Facilitators were given the option of completing their reflection via a separate recording link outside of the meeting to bolster completion during absences. Monthly reflection prompts were developed by the study team and included topics related to implementation determinants. Reflections were captured in transcripts generated by the video-conferencing software, and transcripts were reviewed against the recording and corrected, as needed.

Individual interviews were conducted with eligible clinicians and other clinic staff at the end of the active implementation period; co-investigators and facilitators were also interviewed during the usual care phase. Individuals were identified and recruited to interviews using a combination of identifiers in the EHR data and names and emails provided by the health system facilitator. The interview guide was developed using constructs from CFIR [30] and Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [33], and interviews were conducted by a member of the study team either in-person or using video-conferencing software. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Data analysis. Qualitative data were independently coded by two members of the study team using a codebook based on constructs from CFIR (e.g., innovation design and relative advantage, relational connections, and opinion leader motivation and capabilities) and NPT (e.g., communal specification and appraisal), as well as codes representing ERIC strategies. After discussion and reconciliation of coding discrepancies, data were entered into NVivo, queried by code, and summarized using methods of thematic analysis. For this report, analysis included review of coded text relevant to champions, opinion leaders, and peer influence.

Data integration. Quantitative and qualitative data were first analyzed separately and then compared to expand understanding of the phenomenon and identify ways in which the data converge or diverge [34]. A joint display, organized around key constructs, facilitated data integration and interpretation [34, 35]. More specifically, we used the Pillar Integration Process, which works from the outside of the table inward, to list and match data and then build a central column for inferences about patterns and possible explanations [36].

Results

Over the study period, 97 clinicians (55% of all clinicians) used CV Prevention Choice in a clinical encounter, and 76 clinicians (42%) were considered adopters (HS1: n = 25, 41%; HS2: n = 24, 36%, HS3: n = 27, 52%). While all HS1 sites had a designated champion, one site (n = 19 clinicians) stands out for having 5 super users among 8 adopters, indicating widespread penetration and high utilization at the clinic level. In addition to a champion (who was an early adopter and a superuser), that site was also home to a co-investigator who regularly championed CV Prevention Choice and worked with the facilitator and others on tailored strategies such as education and reminders. In the clinic with the lowest penetration (HS1: 1, n = 5/13, 22%), the champion was not a superuser.

In HS2, there was only one clinic with a designated champion. The champion was an early adopter and one of the highest users in the study. The champion was not the only super user in that clinic though; 7 of the other 11 adopters in that clinic were super users, and there were no clinicians who used CV Prevention Choice only one time. The champion and one other superuser in that clinic were the only ones who adopted early; the other six superusers adopted during active implementation. Among the remaining five clinics in HS2, penetration ranged from 17% to 31%, with a total of six superusers across clinics. In one of those clinics, three of 13 clinicians adopted the tool (23% penetration), two of whom were superusers. In another clinic, 5 of 16 clinicians adopted the tool (31% penetration), including three superusers. Two of the three remaining clinics (Clinics 1 and 3 in Fig. 2) had high proportions of clinicians who tried but discontinued use after a single encounter. These clinics had no early adopters or superusers. The HS2 co-investigator was not embedded in any of the participating health systems but did engage clinicians in several of the clinics, using both virtual and in-person presentations and at-the-elbow demonstrations.

In HS3, three of five clinics achieved ≥ 50% penetration, including a preventive cardiology clinic in which 75% of clinicians adopted the tool. Four of five clinics had multiple superusers, and two ranked among the highest clinics in overall tool use. The timing of adoption in HS3 differed from that observed in HS1 and HS2. Specifically, in HS3, 37% of adopters were innovators (n = 10), 26% were early majority adopters (n = 7), and 33% were late majority adopters (n = 9), indicating a distribution skewed toward the later part of the active implementation phase compared to the other health systems. In the two clinics with the highest tool use, one and two clinicians adopted very early (i.e., innovators), respectively, followed by additional superusers who adopted during the active implementation phase (four in one clinic and three in the other).

Qualitative data. Between May 2021 and January 2025, 37 interviews and 65 periodic reflections were completed. Interview participants included 32 clinicians (including 5 champions and 3 co-investigators) and five other health system staff. Qualitative findings are described below. A diagram portraying interpersonal processes, guided by constructs from NPT, is included in Appendix 1.

Theme 1: Champion and opinion leader role. Findings highlight what facilitators and co-investigators expected from champions, as well as the perceived benefits of champions and other opinion leaders in promoting CV Prevention Choice and influencing adoption. This included giving formal and informal group presentations to promote the relative advantages of CV Prevention Choice over other risk calculators and sharing their experience using it to support SDM, including integration into time-constrained visits. In some cases, the champion and opinion leaders (e.g., co-investigators, division chairs) worked together, but opinion leaders more often served as advocates in group forums. Clinicians did not often describe group presentations as forums for group appraisal. While most champions were enthusiastic about how their patients benefited from CV Prevention Choice and they provided examples of patient cases, interviewees described their peer interactions in broad terms and gave few examples of things they did to specifically influence peer behavior.

Theme 2: Champion selection and engagement. Findings also provide insights into challenges identifying and engaging champions. Most champions were identified and recruited by health system co-investigators and facilitators because they were viewed as technology adopters (e.g., one champion had expertise in and a position on an institutional committee on clinical informatics) and were considered influential among peers. As busy clinicians, though, they often lacked time to serve in the champion role for this study, especially if they also held other leadership roles in the organization. In response, some facilitators suggested that future strategies could leverage other roles in the clinic that were respected among clinicians but did not carry clinical responsibilities, such as practice managers. Co-investigators visited or interacted virtually with participating clinics across their health systems, and two of three also practiced in one of the study clinics. The third co-investigator was a preventive cardiologist and had relationships with the primary care clinicians in his health system, but he did not practice in the same settings. Interview participants repeatedly noted the value of in-person interaction; this task was generally performed solely by the co-investigator in clinics without a champion.

Theme 3: Champions as peers. Although they were selected for their potential to influence peers, some champions reported challenges when clinician peers had a preferred conversation tool (e.g., a risk calculator on their smartphone) that was ingrained in their practice or practiced using a particular workflow that did not work well with CV Prevention Choice (e.g., ordered lab tests necessary for the personalization of risk after the visit). Reasons for non-adoption were sometimes portrayed as legitimate and based on individual preferences, rather than described as things that should be addressed to impact behavior change. None of the interviewed champions reported using the monthly data reports on clinician adoption to target one-on-one peer interactions, but they did describe how sharing their experiences might convince a non-adopter of the tool’s value or compatibility with workflows.

Discussion

This study advances understanding of early adoption among clinical champions and their impact on clinic-level penetration. Prior research has established key champion characteristics, such as influence, persuasiveness, credibility and enthusiasm [21, 22], and champion roles including advocating, motivating, promoting, mentoring, troubleshooting, and educating [18, 37, 38]. Identification of early adopters may be one way to determine who is enthusiastic about the innovation and able to share knowledge and experience with peers. In the parent implementation trial, co-investigators and facilitators identified clinical champions based on their enthusiasm for CV Prevention Choice and their perceived standing as influential among their peers. However, CV Prevention Choice was newly introduced to their clinics for this study. Thus, the work presented here investigated both adoption among champions and the impact of champions on clinic penetration.

Six of the eight champions in this study adopted CV Prevention Choice early, and all but one champion went on to become a superuser. However, while clinics with champions had high penetration and low non-adoption, champion tool use varied substantially from 11 to 239 encounters. In addition to potentially indicating less enthusiasm for adoption, variable champion uptake is a critical indicator of knowledge and self-efficacy. Champions need relevant knowledge and skills to develop self-efficacy and fulfill the role of demonstrating innovation use [18, 39], including developing users’ skills and troubleshooting problems. Individuals with real-world experience using CV Prevention Choice were likely best able to speak with peers about why and how to use it in practice. It is notable that large shares of clinicians went so far as to use CV Prevention Choice in a clinical encounter with a patient but then did not use it again. Prior research has identified champion activities like audit and feedback as a method of reinforcing behaviors [38].

The Shea conceptual model further extends self-efficacy to performing the role of champion [18]. Our findings may highlight important nuances in self-efficacy as it relates to champion roles, e.g., educating versus influencing. Specifically, our qualitative findings raise questions about how champions are expected to reinforce change. We did not find that champions used data reports to identify and target non-adopters, for example, and while champions were willing to share their experiences with peers, they hesitated to reinforce adoption if they perceived that peers were using other effective approaches to engage patients in conversations about CV risk. Champions may not adequately address clinician barriers to SDM adoption, including preferences of existing practices, if they do not perceive that as part of their role or they feel unprepared to influence behavior. A recent systematic review of champion training and education found few studies considered the content of behavior change [40]. Bartels and colleagues used the Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement (ADKAR®) change management framework to consider champion preparation in two phases, the first related to the practice change and the second adoption of the champion role [41]. Our findings suggest this second phase is critical, particularly in the context of SDM, which many clinicians perceive they practice routinely in situations that call for it and champions may find it more difficult to articulate the need for the practice change [42]. Consistent with NPT, this lack of difference between what is currently routinized and what needs to be normalized reduces the effort to enact individual and collective work to normalize the innovation. Tailored training in interpersonal influence and how champions can engage peers in a way that also respects colleagues’ autonomy may be needed.

Prior research calls for better understanding not only of intrinsic champion characteristics but also of ways in which champions can be developed and supported [21]. A handful of studies used role-play and role-modeling in champion training [40], which may be especially useful for practicing ways to influence adoption of SDM. Delivering training to busy professionals in low-touch pragmatic trials is a critical implementation science challenge, though. Collective reflection and joint training may also help foster shared understanding [43], but again, these may raise questions about the feasibility of champion strategies in real-world contexts. In this study, local implementation teams were not always successful recruiting clinicians as champions, primarily due to limited time. Future research should rigorously evaluate the workload, benefits, and costs of the champion role, including opportunity costs [20].

Clear definitions of champions and other influential users may also be needed [20], as conflation with other terms (e.g., opinion leader) and other strategies (e.g., facilitation) may limit efforts to delineate the purpose of the champion role and evaluate it as a distinct strategy [19, 38]. Our study suggests that individuals who influence peers may not have formalized champion roles in all cases, though. Some clinics may have had high adoption because they also had high shares of superusers, whether they were designated as champions or not. Those clinicians may have influenced peer use without a formal role in the study. Similarly, clinicians who declined a formal role may still exhibit champion-like behaviors or actions. Future research should consider methods to identify latent champions, i.e., superusers who were not identified a priori for their potential to influence peer adoption, as well as ways to engage them in interpersonal strategies. Other methods, such as social network analysis, may better identify influential leaders or trusted colleagues, regardless of formal clinical or research roles, as well as formal and informal communication and advice relationships [44–46]. Additional case-based methods may also provide insights into contextual factors that play a key enabling role for champion performance, including organizational culture and resources [43].

The analyses presented here do not establish a causal connection between champions and peer adoption, but they highlight the importance of implementation as a social process in context and of collective work as mechanisms for action, particularly as conceptualized in NPT [47]. Our qualitative findings suggest some champions talked about CV Prevention Choice in group settings and modeled their practice for their peers. Group discussions wherein clinicians engage in group dialogue about the innovation may be a critical component of behavior change. This type of group reflection and sensemaking, as proposed in implementation theories like NPT, may help clinicians appraise the innovation’s value. A recent realist evaluation presented seven potential context-mechanism-outcome (CMO) configurations of champions in primary care. Our use of NPT, which presented CMO coding categories in its qualitative coding manual released in 2022, may further the development of testable configurations by contributing to the understanding of mechanisms based on collaborative work and group resources, such as work to sustain a community of practice and to appraise innovations [47]. Improved conceptual framing of interpersonal strategies as causal pathways may also better support evaluation of whether they influenced outcomes as intended, including the role of champion mechanisms like collective efficacy [48].

Our findings may also be consistent with interpersonal theories like Diffusion of Innovations, where some individuals adopt in later stages after seeing peers adopt it. Our assessment of time to adoption somewhat mirrors the proposed bell curve in Diffusion of Innovations. In this study, there were high proportions of clinicians adopting early (i.e., usual care, when CV Prevention Choice was in the EHR but other strategies, including champions, were not yet deployed), which may indicate that some clinics had more innovators and early adopters than expected. Still, 46% of adopters used CV Prevention Choice for the first time in the active implementation phase, meaning that nearly half of adopters may have adopted the tool after strategies such as education, reminders, or peer encouragement from champions or opinion leaders were deployed. For clinics without a champion, where most adoption was in the active implementation phase, superusers may have influenced adoption. These findings may point to the need for study teams to articulate how interpersonal strategies like champions may change from pre-implementation to implementation and sustainment periods, the former being more about communicating innovation need and raising awareness and the latter being focused on innovation trialability and persistence [18].

There are limitations to this work. First, the pragmatic use of EHR data to assess adoption minimized participant burden but also limited our ability to evaluate clinician characteristics associated with peer influence. Second, not all champions completed an interview, which may bias findings. Finally, although health systems identified and recruited champions using study team guidelines, champion selection required judgment. Similarly, the study team provided recommendations for preparing and engaging champions, but implementation teams followed these recommendations to variable extents. While this approach is consistent with the development and deployment of strategies that could operate in real-world conditions outside of a study context, it complicates assessment of the champion role.

Conclusions

Most formal champions adopted the SDM tool early and likely used it enough to bolster proficiency with it even though it was new to all clinicians. Clinics with champions had higher adoption and lower discontinuation, but additional attention may be needed to those who trialed the SDM tool once but then abandon its use. Superusers may be impactful in clinics with and without formal champions. Champions and other opinions leaders may influence adoption by sharing their experience, but group discourse may be needed to build the case for the relative advantage of adopting innovations such as the one in this study, which may represent one of many options clinicians have to support SDM conversations.
