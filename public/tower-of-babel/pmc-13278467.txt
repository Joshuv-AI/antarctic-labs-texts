Quantitative 3D histology reveals localized immune remodeling during early pancreatic cancer progression
Authors: Ashley L. Kiemen, Valentina Matos, Cristina Almagro-Pérez, André Forjaz, Alicia M. Braxton Hickman, Lucie Dequiedt, Jeeun Parksong, Courtney D. Cannon, Xuan Yuan, Sarah M. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPancreatic cancer features a dense, immune-excluded stroma whose origins remain unclear. In this work, we extended CODA, a cellular-resolution three-dimensional (3D) histology pipeline, to map inflammation around more than 1,000 pancreatic precancers in large human pancreas specimens. Bulk analyses reproduce prior associations between overall inflammation and precancer burden, fibrosis, and acinar dropout, implicating ductal obstruction and stromal remodeling in early immune changes. Crucially, 3D mapping reveals that inflammation around individual precancers is highly heterogeneous, with immune hotspots and cold spots interchanging over tens of microns. Hotspots are found around regions of higher-grade dysplasia and ductal obstruction and are enriched for regulatory T cells and macrophages, indicating focal emergence of immunosuppression at the precancer stage. Integration with spatially resolved DNA sequencing implicated mutation in inflammation. These results position 3D mapping as a framework to identify rare sites of active microenvironmental priming and highlight focal immunosuppressive niches as candidate sites for early interception.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a deadly cancer, with a historically dismal 5-year survival rate of 8%.1 One reason for this recalcitrance is the dense, immunosuppressive stroma that comprises the pancreatic tumor microenvironment and inhibits the ability of therapeutic agents, including immunotherapies, to incite an effective host immune response.2-7 To uncover the origins of the pancreatic cancer microenvironment, groups have turned to the premalignant setting. Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) is the most common precursor to PDAC. Emerging evidence suggests that PanINs are more numerous and genetically diverse than previously believed, indicating that most lesions will not progress to cancer in a person’s lifetime.8,9 This raises the question of which microenvironmental features may reflect a PanIN’s risk of progression.

Early immunological work in the precancer setting used genetically engineered mouse models to demonstrate that pancreatic inflammation increases during tumorigenesis, starting at the precancer stage.10 The inflammation surrounding low-grade PanINs is thought to be enriched for pro-inflammatory cells, such as CD8+ T cells, then shift toward immune suppression with increased regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) as lesions progress to high-grade dysplasia and cancer.10-17 Supporting the idea that immunosuppression arises late in tumorigenesis, work in organ donors found that PanINs contain a distinct microenvironment with a heterogeneous mix of CD4+ T cells, myeloid cells, and fibroblasts and a rare incidence of the Tregs more commonly seen in surgically resected samples.9 However, other work suggests that Tregs also play a role in tumor suppression,18 indicating that the role of these cells around PanINs is as of yet unclear. As our current understanding of the precancer microenvironment differs greatly from that of PDAC, the origins of the unique pancreatic cancer stromal environment remain elusive.

Here, we sought to profile the spatial distribution of inflammation in PanINs across 48 large, cm3-sized human pancreas samples. By extending CODA, a software for cellular-resolution three-dimensional (3D) imaging,8,11,19-26 to co-register multiplex serial histology images, we integrated deep-learning-detected pancreatic anatomical structures with immunohistochemically defined immune cell subtypes. Further, we introduce the concept of using large 3D quantitative maps of tissues and agnostic quantification to pinpoint rare, biologically meaningful regions for further profiling, in this case to identify unique immunosuppressive hotspots around pancreatic precancers.

Taken together, our data suggest that 3D, spatially resolved analysis is critical for understanding the evolving and spatially heterogeneous role of inflammation in the pancreas in response to pancreatic precancers and ductal obstruction. By pinpointing highly inflamed loci, we identified unique immunosuppressive niches surrounding regions of higher-grade dysplasia, fibrosis, and PanIN-associated acinar atrophy. Finally, by correlating our data with spatially resolved DNA sequencing, we found that clonal origin, in addition to 3D structure, may impact immune response. Our findings indicate that the shift from a pro-inflammatory to an immunosuppressive state arises in heterogeneous patches during the precancer stage.

RESULTS

3D reconstruction of the pancreatic immune microenvironment at single-cell resolution

We assessed 48 cm3-scale samples of grossly normal human pancreas tissue surgically resected in response to various pancreatic abnormalities, excluding samples from patients who underwent surgical resection for pancreatitis (Figure 1A; Table S1; see STAR Methods). Samples were serially sectioned, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) every third slide, and digitized.8 To characterize immune infiltration, two samples were selected for immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Figure 1B). Every third slide was stained with CD45 to label leukocytes. In one sample, the remaining sections were dual stained with CD3/FOXP3 to label T cells and Tregs.

We adapted CODA’s nonlinear image registration for application to datasets with multiple stains (Figure 1C; Video S1). CODA registers images by maximizing pixel cross-correlation and has been shown to outperform other registration algorithms in several parameters.19 In the case of registration of similarly stained image pairs (H&E to H&E or IHC to IHC), structures in the images are colored similarly (Figure S1A). However, for registration of multi-stained datasets, structures on adjacent sections may show variable staining patterns—for example, collagen stains pink in H&E but is light gray in most IHC images, reducing pixel-to-pixel correlation. We tested three workflows to determine the optimal procedure: (1) registration of color images, (2) registration of the hematoxylin channel images, and (3) registration of the H&E images, followed by serial integration of the IHC images (Figure S1B). By comparing target registration error and tissue warp, we found that the third method yielded the best results (Figure S1C).

We next used semantic segmentation27 to label ten structures in the pancreas at a 2-μm resolution: PanIN, normal ducts, acinar tissue, islets of Langerhans, vasculature, nerves, fat, stroma, immune aggregates, and non-tissue (Figure 1A). When evaluated on an independent testing dataset, the model performed with an accuracy of 95.1% (Figure S2A). Color deconvolution was used to de-mix the hematoxylin from the remaining stains, and nuclear coordinates were generated.19 In IHC, the antibody signal in the area surrounding each nucleus was determined, and k-medoids clustering was used to distinguish CD45+ and CD45− cells or CD3+/FOXP3−, CD3+/FOXP3+, and CD3−/FOXP3− cells (Figure 1D). Validation against manual counts revealed average precision and recall of 0.89 and 0.88 (Figure S2B). Finally, as previously described,19 we adjusted our cell counts to account for over- or undercounting due to serial sectioning (Figure S2C). Integration of image registration, segmentation, and immune cell detection enabled 3D visualization of pancreas structures and the immune microenvironment.

After noting the high density of leukocytes in the pancreatic stroma, we hypothesized that we could accurately estimate CD45+ cell density from an H&E-based measure of stromal cell density, as has been done by previous groups (Figure 1E).28,29 We extracted these data from the two samples containing H&E and IHC images. Using R2 and root-mean-square error (RMSE), we tested linear, power, and exponential fits to determine that a power law well approximated CD45+ cell density. To confirm that our model accurately estimated PanIN inflammation, we performed three checks. First, we compared IHC-measured with H&E-measured PanIN inflammation in the two samples containing H&E and IHC staining, revealing a close match. Second, we compared the IHC-measured PanIN inflammation in the two samples with H&E and IHC staining to the H&E-measured PanIN inflammation in the 46 samples with only H&E staining, revealing similar distributions between the two cohorts and demonstrating the accuracy of our power-law model. Finally, we stained CD45 IHC in four independent samples to test the prediction accuracy of our algorithm, achieving comparable prediction scores (an RMSE of 7.43 in testing compared to an RMSE of 7.15 in training; Figure S3).

Together, this workflow enabled us to reconstruct 48 cm3-sized samples to study the pancreas immune microenvironment. Next, we assessed correlations between pancreas structure and inflammation at microanatomical (μm) and bulk (cm) scales to map immune patterns around pancreatic precancers.

PanINs are the most inflamed structures in the normal pancreas and display a heterogeneous immune response in 3D

We compared bulk inflammation to patient demographics (Figure 2A), identifying significantly higher inflammation in samples surgically resected from the pancreatic head compared to those resected from the pancreatic tail. We found elevated but non-significant inflammation in the pancreases of individuals diagnosed with PDAC compared to those diagnosed with pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs), non-pancreatic cancers, and other abnormalities necessitating pancreatic resection. We found no significant difference in inflammation between individuals based on age or sex.

We quantified the average immune cell density in the 150-μm vicinity of eight major pancreatic structures to identify associations with inflammation (Figure 2B). We found that PanIN lesions were the most inflamed structure, followed by normal pancreatic ducts and stroma. Acinar lobules, islets of Langerhans, and fat were consistently the least inflamed structures, with the exception of one sample that contained extensive acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM) and so greater inflammation in the exocrine tissue (Figure S4A).

To understand the immune cell density surrounding PanIN lesions, we generated 3D immunomaps (Figure 2C; Videos S2 and S3), where the 3D structure of the PanIN was overlaid by the local immune cell density. We define local immune cell density as the number of immune cells within a 150-μm radius around local regions of a PanIN, normalized by the amount of tissue in that radius. We exclude the non-tissue space in the density calculation to control for the ductal lumens. A radius of 150 μm was chosen because it roughly corresponds to the thickness of the periductal stromal layer in a moderately sized pancreatic duct. These 3D immunomaps revealed that both large and small PanIN lesions exhibit striking immune heterogeneity, possessing both immune hotspots and cold spots. We quantified this heterogeneity in immune cell density around PanINs in two ways: orthogonally away from the PanINs and tangentially along the 3D external surface of the PanINs.

First, we compared the radial immune cell density around PanIN lesions in 2D and 3D (Figure 2D). In the graphs, each thin gray line represents the radial density around each 2D instance of the PanIN as it appears in the histology. The dashed blue line represents the bulk average of these 2D calculations, and the solid black line is the true, 3D radial immune cell density. This calculation demonstrates that random calculation of inflammation around a single 2D instance of a 3D structure can result in dramatic over- or underestimation of the true immune response in a tissue and that even the bulk average of 2D measurements fails to recapitulate true 3D information. Finally, we computed the “length scale” of inflammation at PanINs, or the distance one would need to travel across the surface of a PanIN for the local inflammation to change by 25%, 50%, or 100% (Figure 2E). To equally consider the length scale of immune heterogeneity around large and small PanINs, we plotted 1,000 randomly selected points per lesion. We found that inflammation changes rapidly, on average changing 50% within 40 μm, or 10 histological sections, suggesting that conclusions about the hot or cold nature of a lesion may be misleading if determined through the assessment of a single 2D slide.

Pancreatic inflammation correlates with global tissue architecture but is locally heterogeneous

We next investigated the hypothesis that a higher incidence of PanIN correlates with higher levels of inflammation. We compared inflammation to pancreatic structure across microanatomical and bulk length scales (μm vs. cm scales). To compute the local correlation, we determined the cellular composition within the 150-μm radius around each of the 1,476 PanINs, as visualized in the sample 3D heatmap renderings (Figure 3A; Video S4). To compute bulk correlation, we determined the 3D cellular composition in each of the 48 samples, as visualized in the sample z-projections (Figure 3B). We then determined the correlation coefficient of local or bulk inflammation to each local or bulk tissue structure (Figure 3C).

Remarkably, while local inflammation correlated moderately with stromal and acinar composition, bulk inflammation correlated strongly with many pancreatic structures, including stroma, acini, PanIN, normal duct, vasculature, and nerves. Locally, we identify no correlation between PanIN and inflammation (Figure 3D), most likely explained by the intense heterogeneity in immune infiltrate around PanINs (see Figure 2C). Conversely, in bulk, we identify a strong correlation between overall PanIN cellular composition and inflammation (Figure 3E). When we consider this information in the context of the patient diagnosis, we see a non-significant trend of more PanIN and higher overall inflammation in tissue from patients diagnosed with PDAC compared with PanNET or other pancreatic abnormalities (Figure S4B). Analysis of a larger cohort could further explore the link between PanIN density and inflammation in the context of pancreatic cancer.

These data quantitatively confirm the works that identified an association between PanIN and increasing pancreatic inflammation.9,10 Further, they expand on these works by suggesting that the associated inflammation is patchy around the precancers and correlates equally strongly with acinar atrophy, suggesting a link between pancreatic precancers, ductal obstruction, and inflammation. This finding is supported by our identification of regions of highly inflamed acinar lobules that are upstream but physically separated from large PanINs (Figure S4C).

PanINs feature distinct immune hotspots and cold spots that correlate with precancer grade, ductal obstruction, and transition to an immunosuppressive state

We found that bulk pancreatic inflammation is highly correlated to bulk PanIN and acinar content, suggesting that inflammation may be in response to both neoplastic presence and ductal obstruction and acinar atrophy (Figure 3C). To better understand the precancer-associated immune cells, we developed an algorithm to identify the local extremes around the PanINs, which we term immune hotspots and cold spots. In each of the 48 3D samples, we pinpointed the coordinates of the ten hottest and ten coldest locations on a PanIN (Figure 4A). For each region of interest (ROI), the algorithm outputs the corresponding histology from our serial dataset (see sample hotspot histology in Figure S5 and sample cold spot histology in Figure S6). Review revealed more higher-grade dysplasia (PanIN-2 or PanIN-3), ADM, and reactive stroma surrounding the PanIN hotspots and large lumens or dilated pancreatic ducts associated with the PanIN cold spots. These correlations were present independent of the underlying patient diagnosis of PDAC, PanNET, non-pancreatic carcinoma, or other pancreatic abnormalities requiring resection. To confirm these results, we quantified the tissue composition in these regions of interest, revealing significantly more acini, islets of Langerhans, and PanIN surrounding the hotspots and significantly more stroma and ductal lumen surrounding the cold spots (Figure 4B). The decreased composition of acini and increased composition of islets around the immune hotspots both indicate acinar atrophy, as islet accumulation has previously been proposed as an effect of exocrine dropout.30 These results provide further evidence linking pancreatic inflammation to PanIN progression, ductal obstruction, and atrophy, though it remains unclear whether inflammation associated with progression is a separate process from inflammation associated with obstruction.

To determine whether these immunological extremes reflected the evolution of the precancerous stroma into the immunosuppressive microenvironment associated with advanced disease, we applied an imaging mass cytometry (IMC) panel to label distinct immune cell types in a subset of three 3D pancreatic samples (Table S2). We identified sections containing a PanIN immune hotspot, a secondary, non-hotspot PanIN, and a non-neoplastic duct of similar radius and compared the IMC-derived immune cell densities around these regions (Figures 4C and 4D). Overall, when comparing inflammation around PanINs to inflammation around normal ducts, we found that PanINs are more inflamed. When we considered the hotspot and non-hotspot PanINs separately, we found generally higher density of all immune cell types around the hotspots. These hotspots were particularly notable for a relatively higher presence of immunosuppressive cell types, such as FOXP3+ Tregs and CD163+, CD206+, and Arg1+ macrophages.

PanIN size correlates with changes in the presence of Tregs

Following our immune hotspot analysis, we sought to better understand the heterogeneity of the immunosuppressive landscape around low-grade PanINs in 3D. As it has been previously noted that the relative abundance of FOXP3+ T cells increases during the progression of low-grade PanINs to high-grade dysplasia and pancreatic cancer, we hypothesized that regions with high FOXP3+-to-CD3+ T cell ratios may indicate advanced disease.12,14 We serially stained a single sample alternately with H&E, CD45 to label leukocytes, and a dual stain to label CD3+ T cells and FOXP3+ Tregs. This sample contained 34 spatially independent PanIN lesions. We created 3D immunomaps that revealed differences in local cell densities between CD45+, CD3+, and FOXP3+ (Figure 5A). We computed the correlation coefficient between local CD45+, CD3+, and FOXP3+ cell density around all 34 PanINs in the sample to find a high correlation between CD3 and CD45 and a moderate correlation between FOXP3 and CD3 (Figure S7A). This suggests that CD3+ T cells make up a relatively homogeneous proportion of immune cells around a PanIN, while the proportion of FOXP3+ Tregs is spatially variable.

We computed the density of immune cells in each structure, finding similar results to the calculation shown in Figure 2B: PanIN is the most inflamed structure in the pancreas, followed by normal pancreatic ducts and stroma (Figure 5B). Considering each of the 34 PanIN lesions in the 3D sample separately, we computed the volume of each PanIN, as well as its average CD45+, CD3+, and FOXP3+ cell density. We find that PanIN lesions possess a range of immune cell densities (Figure 5C). For each PanIN, we similarly calculated the ratios of CD3+ to CD45+ cells, FOXP3+ to CD45+ cells, and FOXP3+ to CD45+ cells (Figure 5D). We found that some PanINs possess a higher ratio of FOXP3+ to CD3+ T cells than others, suggesting that the percentage of immunosuppressive cells differs around spatially separate PanINs.

To determine which PanINs contain higher densities of Tregs, we compared the immune cell density and composition between small and large PanINs, using 0.01 mm3 as a cutoff (Figure 5E). We found that larger PanINs contain slightly higher densities of CD45+ and CD3+ cells and much higher levels of FOXP3+ cells than smaller PanINs. Additionally, while the ratio of CD3+ to CD45+ was not related to PanIN volume, we found significantly higher ratios of FOXP3+ to CD45+ cells and FOXP3+ to CD3+ cells in larger PanINs (see bar plots in Figure 5E and scatterplots in Figures S7B and S7C). This suggests that Tregs exist in higher density and make up a larger percentage of the immune infiltrate around more developed PanINs compared to smaller PanINs. Figure 5F shows the sample histology of small and large PanINs that present dramatically different immune cell densities and ratios. Volumes and immune cell density information are presented in the table in Figure 5G.

PanIN inflammation differs based on somatic mutation

Previous work using 3D mapping of surgically resected pancreata found that PanINs are extremely common. Through the incorporation of spatially resolved DNA sequencing, it was observed that anatomically distinct PanINs often possess distinct somatic KRAS mutations. Finally, it was noted that large PanINs often possess multiple KRAS mutations within the same lesion, possibly as the result of multiple point mutations or of independent clones colliding within the ductal system.8

To determine whether any of the immune heterogeneity in the PanINs could be explained by differences in somatic mutation, we calculated the immune cell density around 38 PanIN lesions and compared this to their measured mutational data. We determined statistical significance between mutation groups containing at least 3 PanINs from at least 2 separate samples (Figure 6). We found no difference in the inflammation of PanINs with KRAS G12D, G12R, or G12V mutations but found that lesions containing a GNAS or KRAS Q61H mutation were more inflamed in comparison. The increased inflammation around GNAS-mutant precancers may be explained by the noted higher inflammation around intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) compared with PanIN, as GNAS mutations are associated with IPMN.13 This may suggest that the increased inflammation in IPMNs compared to PanINs is partly due to the prevalence of GNAS mutations in IPMN, and not to lesion size, as the lesions analyzed here were physically small enough to meet the clinical definition of PanIN.31

To understand whether inflammation is different in large, polyclonal lesions compared to single clones, we further compared predicted inflammation around each lesion to the number of somatic KRAS mutations per lesion and found no significant difference. This implies that the number of KRAS mutations does not impact the relative inflammation of a lesion, although we note that our sample size of PanIN regions with one KRAS mutation (n = 69) greatly outweighed the number with two (n = 10) or three (n = 3) mutations.

DISCUSSION

This work represents a powerful extension of quantitative 3D imaging methods to profile the heterogeneity of inflammation in human tissue. We reveal novel patterns in inflammation in early pancreatic carcinogenesis that span multiple length scales—from bulk changes to organ structure at the multi-cm scale to rapid shifts in local immune cell density at the μm scale. When we analyze these samples in bulk and neglect spatial information, our analysis is consistent with previous work.9,10 However, when we resolve our data in 3D, we identify important novel structural correlations to inflammation.

Pancreatic inflammation indeed correlates strongly with PanIN burden but equally strongly with acinar dropout and stromal density, highlighting the understudied role of ductal obstruction and fibrosis in the evolution of the premalignant immune microenvironment. This correlation may explain the successes of works to non-invasively detect features such as pancreatic fibrosis, pancreatitis, lobulocentric atrophy, and PanINs using techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)32-39 and suggests that further development of imaging-based screening for adverse changes to the pancreatic microanatomy is warranted. Further, this correlation provides strong evidence that supports the proposed existence of a positive feedback loop between PanIN initiation, ductal obstruction and ADM, and inflammation.33,40-43

Immune response to PanINs is extremely heterogeneous in 3D, with 50% decay in local immune cell density within 10 serial sections (40 μm). Within this heterogeneity, there exist distinct immunological hotspots comprising immunosuppressive Tregs and macrophages and surrounding regions of higher-grade dysplasia, acinar atrophy, and reactive stroma. These focal regions may represent the early evolution of the precancer microenvironment to the immune-excluded microenvironment that will dominate the pancreatic landscape at the invasive cancer stage. As previous work has shown that immune cells play an active role in stimulating malignant transformation in pancreatic cancer,44,45 we hypothesize that these regions may be sites of active immune remodeling in response to higher-grade dysplasia or physical ductal obstruction, demonstrating the power of 3D to identify rare, biologically significant events. Important future work could decouple the role of inflammation in ductal obstruction and acinar atrophy from its role in PanIN progression, as these may be separate processes.

Interestingly, we find that somatic mutation may also play a role in the immune infiltrate around PanINs. We find that PanINs with GNAS or KRAS Q61H mutations, which are believed to be rare in PanINs, are more inflamed compared to PanINs with KRAS G12D, G12R, and G12V mutations. GNAS is a common mutation in IPMNs, which have been shown to be more highly inflamed than PanINs.13 One explanation for the higher inflammation we observed here could be that these small GNAS-mutant lesions are the beginnings of IPMNs.31 Another explanation is that these mutations may incite a specific host-immune response that inhibits progression to cancer, which may explain why GNAS and KRAS Q61H are less common in PDAC than KRAS G12 mutations.46,47 Systematic validation of these findings in a larger cohort is an important next step for determining mutation-specific differences in response to precancers.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of this study is our focus on surgical resections. Work in organ donors suggests that the microenvironment around PanINs in healthy pancreata differs from that of tissue taken from patients diagnosed with PDAC. Future extension of this 3D analysis platform to the organ donor pancreas is crucial to determining whether PanIN immune hotspots are a universal feature of precancers or arise only in the context of overt pancreatic disease. We note that our dataset contained 18 grossly normal adjacent samples from patients with PDAC compared to 30 samples from patients with other pancreatic diseases. All samples contained immune hotspots with similar features, including a correlation to higher-grade dysplasia, reactive stroma, and acinar atrophy, indicating the hotspots exist outside the context of PDAC. A second limitation of this work is our focus on lymphocytes and macrophages. Recent work has identified fibroblasts as a major population present around PanINs.9,11 In PDAC, CAF-defined microenvironmental subtypes have been linked to patient survival.48 Quantitative mapping of fibroblast density in 3D space is an important area for future research. Finally, while research using retrospective human tissue, such as that performed here, is important in clarifying the true precancer microenvironment around PanINs, a limitation of this work is the lack of mechanistic studies. Future mechanistic work to determine the relevance of immune hotspots and the role of Tregs in PanINs is necessary to contextualize these findings in pancreatic cancer progression.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Samples of grossly normal human pancreas tissue were harvested from the normal-adjacent tissue to a mass of clinical interest following surgical pancreatectomy. Tissues containing significant fibrosis, atrophy, or cancer in the normal adjacent region, or coming from patients diagnosed with acute or chronic pancreatitis, were excluded. The 48 samples analyzed here came from patients undergoing surgery for treatment of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, serous cystadenomas, distal common bile duct adenocarcinomas, metastatic carcinomas from other organs, mucinous cystic neoplasms, tubulovillous adenomas of the duodenum, ampullary tumors, and lymphoepithelial cysts. These tissues were first described in a paper investigating the abundance and genetic heterogeneity of PanIN in grossly normal human pancreases.8 Further information regarding these samples is present in Table S1.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Johns Hopkins Hospital and all patients included here consented for their tissue to be used for research.

METHOD DETAILS

Computational resources

All analyses were performed on a local workstation running 64-bit Windows 11, equipped with an Intel Core i9 (12th generation) CPU, 128 GB DDR4 RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM).

Tissue processing

Samples were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, and serial sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Every third section was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and digitized at 20x magnification. In some samples, the intervening unstained slides were discarded or used for other purposes. In a subset of samples, the intervening tissue was cut onto plus slides, where it was integrated for labeling of immune cells using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Here, two large (>cm3) pancreas samples were 3D reconstructed using serial labeling of leukocytes using CD45 and used for training the CD45+ cell prediction algorithm. Slides from four additional samples were stained with CD45 to independently test the accuracy of the algorithm. One of the samples also contains labels for T cells using CD3 and regulatory T cells using FOXP3. The remaining 46 samples contain serial H&E staining for mapping of gross anatomical pancreas structures and cell counts.

CODA 3D reconstruction of pancreas microanatomy from H&E images

Samples were 3D reconstructed using CODA,19 resulting in visualizable and quantifiable maps of the human pancreas. The CODA workflow consists of four steps: image registration, nuclear detection, tissue multi-labelling, and visualization. Openslide software was used to save reduced-size copies of all tissue images, corresponding to 2μm/pixel using nearest neighbor interpolation.49 For a pair of images, the registration was calculated through maximization of 2D cross correlation of pixel intensity to all images and corrected for tissue rotation, translation, folding, splitting, and stretching. The CODA cell detection algorithm was used to quantify the cellularity of components via detection of 2D intensity peaks in the hematoxylin channel of the H&E images. Deep learning semantic segmentation was used to create microanatomical labels from histological images. Using annotations on a subset of histological images, the trained algorithm labeled, to a resolution of 2 μm, ten structures in histological images of the pancreas: islets of Langerhans, normal ductal epithelium, vasculature, fat, acinar tissue, collagen, PanIN, nerves, immune cell aggregates, and non-tissue whitespace with per class precision and recall of >90%. The image registration, cell detection, and tissue segmentation are integrated to create 3D reconstructions of pancreas microanatomy at a large scale (up to multi-cm3), while maintaining cellular resolution.

Registration of H&E and IHC images

For samples containing a mix of H&E and IHC images, the CODA image registration workflow was adapted to enable accurate and smooth integration of multiplex datasets. Similar to the original CODA registration,19 sections were registered with a two-step process, with the central image of each case serving as a reference. Images were downsampled to a resolution of 8 μm/pixel. In a global rigid alignment step, the cross-correlation of a pair of whole slide images was maximized through the determination of rotation and translation values. Then, in a local elastic step, images were cropped into patches (250 × 250 pixels), and rigid registration was applied to each region. Finally, displacement fields were interpolated from the grid of local registration data to generate maps to correct for non-uniform deformation between the images.

To optimize our H&E to IHC registration workflow, we tested three approaches: (1) registration of all raw, color images using the original CODA workflow, (2) registration of the hematoxylin channel of all images using the original CODA workflow, and (3) registration first of all H&E images using the original CODA workflow followed by serial integrative registration of the raw, color IHC images to the registered H&E images. The accuracy of registrations was assessed by calculating target registration error (TRE), change in tissue area from unregistered to registered images (Δarea), and maximum per-image magnitude of nonlinear displacement (tissue warp). Using 100 manually annotated fiducial landmarks on 50 pairs of images, TRE was calculated by determining the Euclidean distance between pairs of fiducial points in unregistered and registered images. To screen for shrinking or expanding of tissue sections caused by the registration process, Δarea was measured by calculating the %change in tissue area from unregistered to registered images. Finally, to screen for erroneous stretching of tissue regions during the nonlinear step of the registration process, the tissue warp was determined by calculating the difference between the maximum and minimum translations in the nonlinear displacement matrix. While this number is expected to be greater than zero (as zero means the image was only rigidly transformed), very large values imply non-biological stretching of the tissue.

Detection of immune cell coordinates from IHC images

The previously described CODA cell detection algorithm was adapted to detect positive cells in IHC-stained images.19 First, the hematoxylin and antibody stain channels were isolated using color deconvolution. For each image, the pixels containing tissue were isolated by finding regions of low green channel intensity and high red-green-blue channel standard deviation. One hundred k-medoids clusters were calculated to represent the optical densities of the tissue-containing pixels. The most common, blue-favored clusters were averaged to define the hematoxylin channel, and, for the CD45-stained images, the most common, brown-favored clusters were averaged to define the CD45 channel. The third channel was defined as the complement of the average of the hematoxylin and CD45 channels. For the dual-stained CD3/FOXP3 stained images, the most common, red-favored clusters were averaged to define the CD3 channel, and the most common, black-favored clusters were averaged to define the FOXP3 channel. These optical densities were used to deconvolve the IHC images into their respective stains. To minimize variations in staining hue and saturation among images, histogram equalization was employed using the deconvolved channels of the first image of each sample as the reference. Next, CODA cell detection was used to generate nuclear coordinates from the hematoxylin channel of the images. The intensity of the antibody channels was determined at each nuclear coordinate. Joint histograms of the normalized intensities of the detected nuclei in the different channels were built. First, a joint histogram of the hematoxylin channel and the CD45 channel of the first IHC image of each sample was built to separate CD45+ and CD45− cells. Similarly, a joint histogram with three channels (hematoxylin, CD3, and FOXP3) of the first dual-IHC stained image was generated to separate T cells and Treg cells from the rest of cell types. In both cases, K-medoids partitioning50 was used to establish the separation thresholds.

To assess immune cell detection accuracy, four images of each CD45-stained image were randomly selected from each sample. Adjacent images were selected for the sample containing CD3/FOXP3 staining. In this way, the same tissue region was assessed for all the immune cell types. From these images, tiles of 1 mm × 1 mm were extracted, containing hundreds of immune cells each. Cells were manually annotated. A manually labeled cell was considered equivalent to an automatically identified cell if the coordinates were within 5 μm of each other. The 5 μm was selected as the radius, as this was determined to be half of the average radius of cytoplasm in the images. Finally, immune cell detection accuracy was assessed by measuring the precision and recall within each validation tile.

Construction of 3D matrices for the calculation of tissue metrics

The integration of image registration, cell detection, and tissue multi-labelling allowed the construction of digital maps of tissue and cellular components. This reconstruction resulted in 3D matrices corresponding to each sample reconstruction. A 3D matrix containing tissue labels, a matrix containing nuclear coordinates generated from the H&E images, and a 3D matrix containing nuclear coordinates generated from the IHC images were obtained for the samples. Matrices were created at a resolution of 12 × 12 × 12 μm.3 These matrices were used to perform all calculations shown in this manuscript. Calculations were performed in MATLAB 2022b.

Calculation of volumes, cell counts, and cell densities

The volumes of each PanIN were calculated by summing the number of voxels in the tissue type matrix labeled as PanIN and converting from units of voxels to micron.3 The number of cells of different structures was determined by dot multiplying the cell coordinate matrix by the tissue type matrix and summing the result. Cell counts determined via dot multiplication were corrected using the factors described in the cell detection section above. The number of immune cells surrounding a PanIN was determined using the tissue and cellular data matrices. The MATLAB function bwdist was used to determine the area of 150 μm surrounding each PanIN, and the number of immune cells located within that area was determined via dot multiplication of the distance matrix with the immune cell matrix.

Calculation of local immune cell density and creation of 3D heatmap renderings

Local immune cell density was determined by counting the number of immune cells within a given distance of a voxel in the cell coordinates matrices and normalizing by the number of voxels labeled as tissue (and not whitespace) in the tissue type matrix. To visualize local immune cell densities, PanIN were first 3D rendered using the MATLAB functions isosurface and patch. The local immune cell density map was overlaid on this patch using a determined colormap (with black corresponding to low density values and a brighter color corresponding to high density values).

Estimation of 3D CD45+ cell density from 3D stromal cell density

In two 3D samples containing H&E and IHC-stained sections, we compared the coordinates of detected CD45+ cells to those of all nuclei located in the H&E images. Initially, we calculated the immune cell density within local spheres of 150-μm radius. Next, the number of stromal cells was determined by dot multiplying the H&E cell matrix with a stromal mask. This mask was derived from the tissue type matrix label generated through deep learning 3D segmentation of each sample. The stromal cell density was then measured using the same 150-μm radius spheres.

From the two 3D pancreas matrices containing H&E and CD45+ cell data, we extracted CD45+ and stromal cell densities for 500,000 voxels each, resulting in a combined dataset of 1,000,000 points. Using this combined dataset of 1,000,000 voxels, we employed 5-fold cross-validation to evaluate linear, exponential, and power fits. We assessed the performance of these models using R2 and root mean squared error metrics. The resulting functions enabled the estimation of 3D CD45+ cell density based on 3D stromal cell density.

Quantification of PanIN inflammatory heterogeneity

For all 1,476 PanIN in the 48 3D pancreas samples, 1,000 starting points were randomly chosen. Moving across the surface of each PanIN in 12 μm intervals, we measured the change in immune cell density with distance to determine the distance necessary for the inflammation to change 25%, 50%, and 100%. This was repeated for all 1,476 PanIN and plotted as a histogram.

Calculation of 2D and 3D radial immune cell densities

The MATLAB function bwdist was used to calculate the 2D or 3D radial distance of pixels in the tissue type matrix to a defined PanIN. For iterative distances away from the PanIN, the immune cell matrix was dot multiplied to determine the number of immune cells occupying a radial shell around the PanIN in 2D or 3D space. This number was normalized by the area or volume of the shell to determine the immune cell density at that distance from the PanIN. This calculation was performed starting at the external edge of the PanIN to a distance of 2 mm into the tissue. This radial data was visualized as a line (for calculation in 3D) or a series of lines (for calculation over a collection of 2D images) to show decay of immune cell density from the PanIN into the surrounding pancreas.

Calculation of 3D local tissue density

Tissue density was determined by calculation of the number of voxels of a certain tissue type contained in a local sphere of 150-μm radius. This number was normalized by the number of tissue pixels (excluding nontissue whitespace) contained in the sphere. Correlation coefficient and p-values were determined using MATLAB 2022b.

Detection of immune hotspots and cold spots around PanINs

Using the tissue label matrix, the location of all PanIN in each 3D sample was determined. The immune cell density at each PanIN voxel was computed through dot multiplication with the power-law adjusted stromal cell density matrix. The voxel with the highest immune cell density (a “hotspot”) was identified, and a 0.5 × 0.5 mm2 histological image centered on that coordinate was output, along with the tissue composition at that region of interest. All voxels within 0.5 mm of the hottest location in 3D space were eliminated, such that the next hotspot would be a minimum of 0.5 mm away from any previous hotspot. The next hottest location was found, and this process was repeated until ten hotspots were output. The process was repeated to identify the ten PanIN locations with the lowest immune cell density (“cold spots”) in each 3D sample.

IMC staining and acquisition

Slides were cut from the FFPE blocks onto slides. The slides were first baked for two hours at 60°C, dewaxed in xylene wash, and rehydrated in an alcohol gradient (100%, 95%, 80%, 70% EtOH in Maxpar H2O). The slides were then washed with Maxpar Water, then incubated in an Antigen Retrieval Agent (Dako) at a temperature of 105°C for 1 h. Slides were blocked with 3% BSA in Maxpar PBS for 45 min. Selected antibodies were conjugated in-house, diluted to a concentration ranging from 0.25 mg/mL to 0.5 mg/mL, then aliquoted for use. The slides were stained with the final antibody cocktail (detailed in Table S2) overnight at 4°C. Slides were subsequently washed with Triton X- in Maxpar PBS, then washed in Maxpar PBS. For DNA labeling, Cell-ID Intercalator-Ir (Fluidugm) was diluted at 1:400 in Maxpar PBS and stained. As a tissue counterstain, Ruthenium tetroxide 0.5% Stabilized Aqueous Solution (Polysciences) was diluted at 1:2000 in Maxpar PBS and stained. A final wash was performed in Maxpar Water. Images were acquired with a Hyperion Imaging System (Standard BioTools) at the Johns Hopkins Mass Cytometry Facility. Following acquisition, stacks of multi-layered ome.tiff images were exported, and representative images were generated utilizing MCD Viewer (Standard BioTools).

Immune cell mapping using imaging mass cytometry

To quantify the resulting images, each channel of the ome.tiff file was exported as a separate TIF file. First, nuclear coordinates were generated using the CODA cell detection algorithm on the DAPI channel. Next, the intensity of each antibody channel was measured at each nuclear coordinate. Nuclei with an intensity greater than 50% of the maximum intensity were counted as positive cells. To exclude the non-periductal stroma region, image masks were manually generated for each region of interest. The immune cell counts were converted to immune cell density through normalization by the periductal stroma area in mm2. Immune cell densities were compared across conditions (inflamed PanIN ROIs, randomly selected PanIN ROIs, and normal duct ROIs) using a bar graph.

Comparison of PanIN inflammation to somatic mutation

We identified the 38 PanINs that originally underwent genomic analysis in Braxton et al.8 For each of these PanINs, between one and five regions were laser capture microdissected and sequenced based on the number of histology slides spanned by the PanIN. We identified the regions of each PanIN in our digital 3D matrices that corresponded to each microdissected region and computed the predicted mean inflammation. For each PanIN region, we logged the mean inflammation and the associated somatic mutations. We compared this data using bar plots.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks version R2023b). Unless otherwise specified in the figure legends, significance was assessed with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. For within- and between-cohort comparisons, summary statistics reported include the median, mean, standard deviation, and interquartile rang. No additional statistical tests were applied.
