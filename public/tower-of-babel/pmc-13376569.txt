Oral delivery of enterocyte-mimetic butyrate-loaded nanoparticles repairs the gut barrier and inhibits growth of digestive system tumors
Authors: Guocheng Xie, Zhenzhen Fu, Yifan Zhang, Ziqiang Xu, Kaikai Xue, Huijuan Dai, Feng Wu, Sisi Lin, Jinyao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenesis, progression, and therapeutic response of numerous digestive system tumors are associated with reduced intestinal barrier integrity. Here, we find that oral delivery of enterocyte-mimicking butyrate-loaded nanoparticles (EMBNs) capable of repairing the gut barrier can inhibit the occurrence, growth, and metastasis of digestive system tumors. EMBNs are prepared via loading butyrate into electrostatically complexed and sodium triphosphate-stabilized chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanoparticles, which are further coated with cell membranes extracted from enterocytes. The presented membrane receptors adsorb lipopolysaccharide and zonulin, known promoters of intestinal barrier impairment, while the loaded butyrate strengthens the function of the intestinal barrier. Mechanistically, the inhibition of autophagy in enterocytes and the promotion of a healthy microbial composition contribute to the repair of the gut barrier. Oral ingestion of EMBNs suppresses the disturbance of the liver immune microenvironment via alleviating the disruption of the intestinal barrier, achieving potent inhibition against hepatocellular carcinoma and hepatic metastasis of colonic carcinoma in dextran sulfate sodium-induced model in male mice. The reinforcement of the gut barrier spotlights a strategy to prevent and treat digestive system tumors.Subject terms: Nanoparticles, Drug delivery, Drug development

Introduction

Intestinal barrier dysfunction is known as an important driver of various types of cancer1,2. Compromise of the intestinal barrier can induce immune dysregulation due to uncontrollable translocation of microorganisms and their pathogenic factors, causing chronic inflammation and cancerization3,4. Increasing evidences point out that the occurrence, metastasis, and treatment outcome of numerous digestive system tumors are closely associated with decreased intestinal barrier integrity5,6. For instance, the increase of intestinal barrier permeability often occurs prior to the development of colorectal cancer2,7. Additionally, the liver translocation of bacteria caused by decreased intestinal barrier integrity facilitates the formation of colorectal liver metastasis8. On the other hand, patients with oncologic diseases are always at a high risk of damage to the intestinal barrier, as the frequent use of antibiotics and chemoradiation9,10, subsequently leading to suboptimal treatment outcomes. Thus, the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity is regarded as a potential gatekeeper to intervene the course of cancer11. Conventionally, immunosuppressive small molecules and macromolecular biologicals have been employed to remit inflammations, which in turn protects the intestinal barrier from impairment12. More recently, oral probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation have been utilized to directly inhibit pathogens or positively modulate microbial composition for repairing the intestinal barrier13,14. As an alternative strategy, we have used oral delivery of immune activators into Peyer’s patches (PPs) to elicit mucosal immunity15, which mediates the regulation of the gut microbiome to strengthen the intestinal barrier16. However, these solutions solely function on individual targets and are incapable of comprehensively repairing the gut barrier. More importantly, previous strategies lack the ability to eliminate microbiota dysbiosis-derived products, which are continuously released and can cause severe damage to the intestinal barrier.

Zonulin and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are two of the most common gut microbiota dysbiosis-derived products17,18. Upgraded levels of zonulin and LPS are correlated with increased intestinal barrier permeability in patients with different diseases, particularly digestive system tumors, such as liver and colorectal cancer19,20. As the sole physiological modulator, zonulin regulates the opening of tight junctions to allow the transport of nutrients in the intestine, while its upregulation can lead to tight junction disassembly and subsequent uncontrolled systemic influx of pathogens and bacterial toxins19,21. LPS is released from the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria by shedding or bacterial lysis. Under normal physiological conditions, baseline LPS levels do not induce significant intestinal epithelial damage. However, previous studies report that in pathological conditions such as colitis, altered gut microbial communities promote the production of diverse LPS variants, which exert varying effects on proinflammatory cytokine production and exacerbate intestinal barrier defects22,23. In addition to provoking inflammation, LPS disrupts the expression and distribution of tight junction proteins, further compromising gut barrier function22,24. The removal of circulating LPS has been shown to accelerate clinical improvement in IBD patients25,26. Therefore, timely removal of zonulin and LPS is critical for the preservation of intestinal barrier integrity. Unfortunately, to the best of our knowledge, whether the repair of the intestinal barrier and the removal of major intraluminal pathogenic triggers could inhibit digestive system tumors remains rarely explored.

Here, we show that oral enterocyte-mimicking butyrate-loaded nanoparticles (EMBNs) can repair the intestinal barrier by eliminating zonulin and LPS and supplementing butyrate. EMBNs are prepared via physical encapsulation of butyrate into electrostatically complexed and sodium triphosphate-stabilized chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanoparticles, which are further wrapped with plasma membranes extracted from intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells. Given the inheritance of native membrane receptors from enterocytes, EMBNs can bind zonulin and LPS to remove their destructive effects on the gut barrier. Meanwhile, the delivery of butyrate is capable to upgrade the expressions of tight junction proteins of occludin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and promote the secretion of mucins, which reinforces the integrity of the intestinal barrier (Fig. 1A). We demonstrate that the mechanism of EMBNs in protecting the intestinal barrier is the inhibition of LPS-induced autophagy of enterocytes and the reconstruction of a healthy microbial structure. With these characteristics, EMBNs not only restrain the translocation of intraluminal microorganisms and their pathogenic factors, but also suppress the disturbance of liver immune microenvironment in murine models of intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by dextran sulfate sodium (DSS). During in vivo assessment, oral administration of EMBNs achieves effective inhibition of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) occurrence and growth as well as potent suppression of the hepatic metastasis of colon cancer in DSS mice. We further find that the protection of intestinal barrier integrity by EMBNs inhibits the production and translocation of LPS and zonulin, subsequently abolishing their suppression effects on antitumor immune responses (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our work discloses how the integrity of the gut barrier can be maintained by oral biomimetic nanomedicines under unfriendly environmental alterations or external assaults. We anticipate that EMBNs-mediated maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity can be applied as an appealing strategy for preventing and treating digestive system tumors.

Results

Design, preparation, and characterization of EMBNs

Cell membranes are embedded with various ligands and receptors on the surface, which regulate a number of biological functions27. Coating with plasma membranes extracted from a specific cell type has become a versatile approach to modify nanoparticles, which can be endowed with an array of cell membrane associated functions28,29. We have previously developed a type of regulatory T (Treg) cell membrane-coated nanoparticles that successfully suppress inflammatory responses by directly interacting with multifaceted overactive immune cells30. In the intestine, intraluminal LPS or zonulin can interact with Caco-2 cells through corresponding receptors expressed on the surface and subsequently induce cell injury24,31. Inspired by this specific interaction, Caco-2 cell membranes (CCMs) were extracted to coat nanoparticles, which we speculated could adsorb and remove excessive LPS and zonulin in the gut. Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid that is one of the crucial energy substrates for intestinal epithelial cells, is known to strengthen the intestinal barrier by facilitating the expression of tight junction proteins, such as occludin and ZO-132,33. It can also promote the secretion of mucus by goblet cells, which contributes to maintaining the homeostasis of the intestinal barrier and microbiota32. Moreover, in addition to gut mucosal immunity, butyrate also affects immunity at extra-intestinal sites, such as the liver34,35. However, supplementation with butyrate is always suffered from rapid metabolism under the stimulation of enzymes and acids in the gastrointestinal tract, which largely restricts its pharmaceutical effect36,37. Thus, we loaded butyrate into nanoparticles and expected a controlled release in the intestinal fluid to improve its effectiveness. With respect to the selection of nanoparticles, anionic hyaluronic acid was chosen to self-assemble with cationic chitosan by electrostatic interaction, given their natural source and favorable biocompatibility as well as the ability of formed nanocomplexes to protect payloads from digestive degradation38,39. The resulting nanoparticles were further stabilized with sodium triphosphate to extend the release profile of loaded butyrate.

To prepare EMBNs, hyaluronic acid was mixed with sodium triphosphate at a molar feed ratio of 0.2:1, and the obtained solution was added dropwise into a mixture containing 0.6 mg/ml chitosan and 2.5 mg/ml butyrate. We first confirmed the appropriate ratio of nanoparticles to cell membranes by laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, there were few uncoated butyrate-nanoparticles (BNs) and free CCMs observed when the concentration of cell membranes was set at 0.42 mg/ml. The formed BNs were suspended with CCMs, followed by physical extrusion through a porous polycarbonate membrane to generate EMBNs. We confirmed the expressions of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), the receptors of LPS and zonulin, respectively, on CCMs and EMBNs by western blot (Supplementary Fig. 3). Representative transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showed a bare sphere nanoparticular structure for BNs and a typical core-shell nanostructure of cell membrane-coated nanoparticles after fusing with CCMs (Fig. 1B). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurement suggested that in contrast to BNs, the zeta potential of EMBNs was decreased from 32.4 ± 3.5 to 10.2 ± 0.8 mV due to coating with negatively charged CCMs (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, the average sizes of BNs and EMBNs were 268.1 ± 8.7 and 379.4 ± 13.3 nm, respectively (Fig. 1D). The size distribution of these nanoparticles was further calculated by TEM images. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the average sizes of BNs and EMBNs were 197.9 ± 52.9 and 330.1 ± 69.0 nm, respectively. The smaller size obtained from TEM images compared to DLS measurements could be explained by the complete dehydration of nanoparticles during sample preparation. The formation of BNs and successful coating with CCMs were further confirmed by LSCM and flow cytometric analysis. To facilitate these measurements, rhodamine B-labeled hyaluronic acid was used to prepare BNs, which were wrapped with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) marked CCMs. Colocalization of rhodamine B (red) and FITC (green) signals in confocal images of EMBNs indicated the presence of CCMs on coated BNs (Fig. 1E). Flow cytometry histograms depicted ~10-times higher mean fluorescence intensity of FITC for EMBNs compared to that of BNs (Fig. 1F). The quantitative analysis illustrated that the coating efficiency of EMBNs reached up to near 84.1% (Fig. 1G). We evaluated the stabilities of BNs and EMBNs in simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), which have been commonly used to assess the stability of oral formulations40,41. Similar to those in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), there were no significant size fluctuations after incubation with SGF and SIF for up to 24 h, respectively (Fig. 1H, I and Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating favorable stabilities of these nanoparticles. The release of butyrate was studied by dispersing EMBNs in SIF, and the concentration of butyrate in free form was detected using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). As shown in Fig. 1J, the use of sodium triphosphate could improve the stability of the nanoparticles, suggested by the significantly retarded release of the loaded butyrate. Meanwhile, to measure the effects of LPS and zonulin binding on the release of butyrate, CCMs were preincubated with LPS and zonulin. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 6, the release profile of butyrate between bEMBNs prepared with LPS and zonulin-treated CCMs was similar to that of EMBNs, indicating that LPS and zonulin binding did not affect butyrate release. We also examined the distribution of EMBNs following oral ingestion by an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). The nanoparticles were prepared by using cyanine5.5 (Cy5.5)-labeled hyaluronic acid. Mice gavaged with PBS were set as a control. The fluorescence signals of mice were captured at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 24 h after oral administration by IVIS. Tissues of major organs, blood, and feces were also collected 24-h post-gavages for fluorescence detection. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 7, the fluorescence signals were mainly detected in the intestine and feces rather than the blood and major organs, indicating that the nanoparticles across the gastrointestinal tract were negligible. To verify intraluminal and circulatory butyrate concentrations, mice were gavaged with equivalent free butyrate, BNs, and EMBNs, respectively. The blood and luminal contents were collected 1, 4, 6, and 12 h after administration. Butyric acid, the ionized form of butyrate, in serum and luminal contents was isolated and measured by gaschromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) as described elsewhere42. Butyric acid was not detected in the serum, which might be ascribed to its rapid metabolism. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, the EMBNs group showed durable butyrate concentrations in the intestinal contents throughout the 12‑h post‑administration period.

Ability of EMBNs to eliminate LPS and zonulin

To study whether the coating of CCMs could bind LPS, EMBNs without butyrate were mixed directly with LPS dissolved in cell culture medium. After incubation for 24 h, the mixture was centrifuged, and the concentration of free LPS in the supernatant was determined by limulus amebocyte lysate assay (LAL). As disclosed in Supplementary Fig. 9A, LPS adsorption by BNs was limited. In contrast, the concentration of residual LPS was decreased with the increase of CCMs content, and near 78% of LPS was removed with CCMs content increasing to 1 mg/ml (Fig. 2A). To examine the ability to eliminate zonulin, Caco-2 cells were challenged by LPS, which can induce zonulin secretion43. The level of free zonulin in cell supernatant was found to be 925.3 ng/ml, which was reduced continuously with the increase of CCMs content and decreased to 774.9 ng/ml at a CCMs content of 1000 μg/ml (Fig. 2B). Similar to LPS, the adsorption of zonulin by BNs was negligible (Supplementary Fig. 9B). To further confirm the potency to adsorb LPS and zonulin, coated nanoparticles were added directly to the culture medium of LPS-stimulated Caco-2 cells. It has been reported that LPS and zonulin can induce cell death, which is accompanied by increased cell permeability22,44. Expectedly, both cell permeability and percentage of dead Caco-2 cells were decreased with the increase of CCMs content, supporting the reduced levels of LPS and zonulin (Fig. 2C, D). The addition of coated nanoparticles with a CCMs content of 1000 μg/ml showed 1.5- and 6.8-times decrements in cell permeability and dead cell percentage, respectively. We next evaluated the effectiveness of EMBNs in vivo. Mice were fed with 4% w/v DSS drinking water for 7 days and then orally delivered with PBS, BNs, CCMs, and EMBNs, respectively. At 7-day post administration, the concentrations of LPS and zonulin were analyzed by LAL assay and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), respectively. Elevated LPS level in circulation is one of biomarker of intestinal barrier dysfunction45. Compared to normal mice, DSS stimulation induced a significant increment in LPS and zonulin production (Fig. 2E–G and Supplementary Fig. 10). CCMs were unable to decrease the levels of both LPS and zonulin, while BNs only inhibited the upregulation of LPS in plasma. Given that the levels of LPS and zonulin in vivo were affected by the complex interplay among production, host-mediated clearance, and systemic translocation, the limited in vivo effects of CCMs and BNs may be attributed to their inability to simultaneously modulate gut dysbiosis and remove LPS and zonulin. Different from BNs and CCMs, EMBNs could significantly reduce the levels of LPS in plasma and zonulin in the colon and ileum. The similar level of LPS in BNs and EMBNs groups could be explained by the clearance of LPS by the liver following systemic absorption. More importantly, the level of LPS in plasma was positively associated with the levels of zonulin in plasma and the tissues (Fig. 2H–J). LPS has been demonstrated to induce an increased release of zonulin, which in turn promotes the translocation of LPS17,43.

Protective effects of EMBNs on the intestinal barrier

Having confirmed their ability to sustainably release butyrate and to efficiently eliminate LPS and zonulin, we then evaluated whether EMBNs could protect the intestinal barrier from impairment under environmental stimulation. Caco-2 cell monolayer, an in vitro model of intestinal epithelial barrier46, was developed to test the effectiveness of EMBNs to maintain gut barrier integrity. The LPS-stimulated monolayer was incubated with PBS, BNs, and EMBNs, and the level of zonulin was measured and plotted in Fig. 3A. Interestingly, compared to PBS, both BNs and EMBNs could decrease zonulin expression, while only EMBNs were able to maintain the integrity of the epithelial monolayer, as significantly decreased permeation of FITC-dextran was observed in the transwell experiment (Fig. 3B, C). Increased intestinal permeability is characterized with abnormal expression and/or distribution of tight junction proteins47. Similar to PBS group, the expressions of ZO-1 and occludin, two crucial tight junction proteins, appeared normal after treatment with EMBNs (Supplementary Fig. 11). Although occludin expression in the BNs group was also high, the effect of BNs on ZO-1 was negligible. This could explain the limited effect of BNs on inhibiting increased intestinal permeability as ZO-1 is required for the assembly of both adherens and tight junctions to regulate cell permeability48. In addition to tight junction disassembly, intestinal barrier injury is also associated with dysregulated death of intestinal epithelial cells49. The percentage of dead cells was measured by live and dead cell staining to evaluate the protectiveness of EMBNs on Caco-2 cells. After 24-h LPS stimulation, the percentages of apoptotic and necrotic cells were increased, while only treatment with EMBNs was able to effectively inhibit excessive death of Caco-2 cells in both forms of apoptosis and necrosis (Fig. 3D, E and Supplementary Fig. 12A–C). The similar effect of BNs and EMBNs against cell apoptosis induced by LPS might be attributed to the relatively light damage induced by LPS. Thus, the application of butyrate was sufficient to protect the cells from injury. With exposure time prolonged to 48 h, treatment with EMBNs enabled significantly decreased percentages of necrotic cells compared to those of BNs (Supplementary Fig. 12D–G). The lower apoptotic cell ratios in cells incubated with LPS for 48 h than 24 h might be ascribed to the enhanced resistance of Caco-2 cells against LPS after a relatively long-term stimulation. As reported, LPS is able to accelerate the proliferation of Caco-2 cells after 48 h of incubation50. The ability of EMBNs to maintain the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier in vitro in the presence of external stimuli could be explained by the synergistic effect of butyrate supplementation as well as the elimination of LPS and zonulin.

We further evaluated the effect of EMBNs on protecting the intestinal barrier in vivo. To develop an intestinal barrier impairment model, mice were administered with 4% w/v DSS water for 7 days and then gavaged with 0.2 ml of PBS, BNs (0.24 mg butyrate/mouse), CCMs (0.08 mg/mouse), and EMBNs (0.24 mg butyrate/mouse and 0.08 mg CCMs/mouse), respectively. Healthy mice were used as a control. As expected, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining proved that mice suffered from DSS stimulation showed severe edema and rupture in the muscular layer and mucosal inflammatory injury of the colon (Fig. 3F). Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (AB/PAS) staining indicated that mucus shown in blue was rarely produced by goblet cells (Fig. 3G). Meanwhile, treatment with BNs or EMBNs could increase the expression of mucin Muc2 mRNA, suggesting the beneficial effect of the loaded butyrate (Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, ZO-1 and occludin were disrupted as suggested by labeling with anti-ZO-1 and anti-occludin antibodies (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 14). These structural alterations in the intestinal barrier eventually caused increased intestinal permeability (Fig. 3I). Strikingly, intestinal barrier integrity was protected by treatment with EMBNs, as supported by negligible alterations both in the structures including muscular layer, goblet cells, and tight junction proteins and in the permeability of the gut barrier. Particularly, the structure of colon tissue, the expression levels of mucus and tight junction proteins, and intestinal permeability in mice treated with EMBNs were much similar to those in healthy mice (Fig. 3F–I). Despite increased expression of occludin, BNs were incapable of preventing the decrement in ZO-1 expression (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 14). Moreover, mice administered with BNs presented an increased intestinal permeability (Fig. 3I), which was ascribed to the contribution of the binding between the transmembrane protein occludin and the cytoplasmic scaffolding protein ZO-1 to the regulation of the intestinal barrier48. Additionally, as the breakdown of intestinal barrier integrity, intestinal bacteria were translocated from nearby PPs and mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) to the blood and the liver (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. 15A–D). It was worth mentioning that abnormal accumulations of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria in the liver disrupted its immune microenvironment, as confirmed by a significantly elevated percentage of Treg cells, which has been reported to promote tumor growth51. In contrast to PBS, BNs, and CCMs groups, the protection of the intestinal barrier by EMBNs effectively avoided the accumulation of intestinal bacteria in the liver and thus circumvented the influence of translocated microorganisms on liver immunity (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Fig. 15E). Note that the breakdown of the intestinal barrier leads to the translocation of bacteria to the liver, which further contributes to an altered immune state and various liver diseases52. It has been reported that the intestinal barrier regulates immune balance in the liver via IL-10-producing macrophages, which are known to play an important role in maintaining gastrointestinal homeostasis53. Mice with a macrophage-selective deletion of IL-10Rα can develop markedly enhanced colitis compared to wild-type controls, which is associated with increased mucosal barrier disruption54. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, the percentage of IL-10+ macrophages in the MLNs was increased by EMBNs compared to the other groups. Taken together, EMBNs played an important role in intestinal barrier protection by regulating tight junction expression, mucus production, cell death, and immune response, which suppressed gut microbial translocation and the alteration of the liver immune microenvironment.

The mechanisms underlying the protective effect of EMBNs

Autophagy, an intracellular degradation pathway, is required to maintain antimicrobial defense, epithelial barrier integrity, and mucosal immune response55,56. Dysregulation of autophagy disrupts various components of the intestinal barrier, which results in an abnormal immune response and consequent inflammation55,56. It has been reported that increased LPS level was indicated to induce autophagy in enterocytes57. We next studied whether EMBNs could suppress LPS-induced autophagy in Caco-2 cells. As evidenced by increased autophagosomes in TEM images shown in Fig. 4A, a concentration of 20 μg/ml LPS significantly induced autophagy in Caco-2 cells after 24-h incubation. In contrast, autophagosomes were rarely found in cells co-incubated with EMBNs (equiv. 1 mM butyrate), while co-culture with BNs containing equivalent butyrate showed a limited effect. We further used monodansylcadaverine to quantitatively analyze the autophagy in Caco-2 cells. Consistent with the TEM result, EMBNs effectively inhibited LPS-induced autophagy, as reflected by largely decreased fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4B). 3-Methyladenine (3-MA), a widely used inhibitor of autophagy, was used to verify the role of autophagy activation in EMBN-mediated protective effect. As plotted in Supplementary Fig. 17, autophagy in Caco-2 cells was suppressed in response to LPS stimulation in the presence of 3-MA. Remarkably, the suppression of autophagy abolished the protective effect of EMBNs. It has been documented that reactive oxygen species (ROS) production is closely involved in the regulation of autophagy58. To measure the ROS level, these Caco-2 cells were further stained with 2′-7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), a sensitive cell-permeable redox probe for the detection of ROS. As claimed in Fig. 4C, D, the activation of autophagy upgraded the intracellular ROS level, which was effectively inhibited by co-incubation with EMBNs. Collectively, EMBNs protected the intestinal barrier by suppressing LPS-induced autophagy in enterocytes.

Given that the microbiota is also an important factor that shapes the gut barrier59, we hence turned our attention to evaluate the underlying mechanism of EMBNs in repairing the gut barrier via positively modulating the microbial structure. We first evaluated the effects of EMBNs on bacteria by monitoring the growth kinetics of different bacterial strains in medium supplemented with gradient EMBNs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, EMBNs exhibited a concentration-dependent biphasic regulation of bacterial growth. Specifically, EMBNs at a low concentration (≤87.5 μg/ml) could enhance bacterial proliferation. After daily administration of EMBNs for 7 days, DSS mice were euthanatized and the intraluminal content of the intestine was collected for 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing. Healthy mice without any treatments or DSS mice treated with PBS, BNs, and CCMs were used as controls, respectively. The differences in bacterial communities were visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) in operational taxonomic unit (OTU) and genus level, respectively, in which each point represents a sample and a shorter distance between two points indicates a smaller difference in bacterial composition. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19A, B, both of the points belonging to the PBS and BNs groups were distant from those of the healthy mouse group, indicating that DSS induction led to a significant alteration in microbial composition and treatment with BNs lifted limited efficiency in recovering microflora balance disturbed by DSS. Although butyrate promoted mucus secretion, which is involved in the modulation of the gut microbiota32, the disturbance of bacterial composition caused by zonulin- and LPS-induced inflammations could not be prevented by BNs. In contrast, EMBNs restored the balance of the gut microbiota in DSS mice effectively, as implied by a close distribution of the points between EMBNs and the healthy mouse group. Similar results were obtained in hierarchical clustering analysis, which was visualized as a heatmap showing a closer relationship between healthy mice and the EMBNs group rather than other control groups (Fig. 4E). The absolute abundance of bacteria in the family, genus, and order levels was selected to monitor the changes in microbial community under different treatments (Supplementary Fig. 19C–E). It was found that bacterial species associated with intestinal and liver inflammation or even HCC development, such as Peptostreptococcaceae, Erysipelotrichales, and Enterobacteriales, were increased after DSS stimulation (Fig. 4F, G and Supplementary Fig. 19F). While bacteria with benefits for the gut and liver, including Alistipes, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and Muribaculaceae, were decreased significantly (Fig. 4H, I and Supplementary Fig. 19G). Alistipes and Muribaculaceae are correlated with the production of short-chain fatty acids, which play an important role in the maintenance of gut homeostasis60,61. Decreased production of short-chain fatty acids contributes to hepatic fibrosis, a risk factor for HCC development62. Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group is positively correlated with the production of antimicrobial peptides, while negatively correlated with oxidative stress and inflammation63. Evidently, not only the application of EMBNs could inhibit the abundance of the mentioned adverse bacteria, but also promote the abundance of those beneficial bacteria. These data verified that both the suppression of autophagy in enterocytes and the promotion of a balanced gut microbiota composition contribute to the effectiveness of EMBNs in repairing the gut barrier under environmental stimulations (Fig. 4J).

Tumor inhibition enabled by EMBNs-mediated repair of the gut barrier

HCC, one of the most common malignant cancers with a high rate of morbidity and mortality64. Despite the epidemiology of HCC has changed during the past decade with the help of hepatitis B vaccination, other risk factors for HCC are constantly on the rise65. Increments in intestinal barrier permeability and hepatic accumulation of microbiota have been observed in HCC patients66,67. Thus, we investigated whether the protection of the intestinal barrier by EMBNs could inhibit HCC development. The preventive effect of EMBNs on HCC was first evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, mice were given DSS drinking water for 7 days, in which PBS, BNs, CCMs, and EMBNs were administered daily by oral gavage. Afterward, H22 cells were inoculated in the left lobe of the liver. Encouragingly, 7 days after inoculation, we observed superior inhibition of HCC occurrence for mice dosed with EMBNs, which was not appeared in all other inoculated groups. This was evidenced by fewer tumor lobes and a smaller tumor area occurred in the liver sampled from the EMBNs group (Fig. 5B–D). Notably, the size of the spleen was decreased in inoculated mice without treatment, but was recovered after treatment with EMBNs (Supplementary Fig. 20A). The spleen is well regarded as a gatekeeper of systemic immunity, where immune responses play an immune surveillance role that protects against tumor development68. Clinically, spleen atrophy is commonly observed in infections69. As expected, in addition to the MLNs and the liver, there were more translocated gut microbiota in the spleen of inoculated mice without treatment. In good agreement with the prevention effect, treatment with EMBNs could greatly suppress the translocation of intestinal bacteria, which was attributed to the less compromised gut barrier (Fig. 5E–G). Translocation of gut microbiota has been reported to disorder the homeostasis of liver immunity and cause severe hepatic injury or even HCC pathogenesis and progression5,70. We therefore evaluated EMBNs-mediated modulation of the liver immune microenvironment in the inoculated mice. Compared to normal mice, there were increased infiltrations of T-helper cell 1 (Th1) and T-helper cell 17 (Th17) in the MLNs and the liver of inoculated mice without treatment (Fig. 5H–J and Supplementary Fig. 20B). Among these CD4+ T subtype cells, an increased level of Th17 cells has been demonstrated to correlate with rapid tumor growth and poor survival in HCC patients71,72. Implementation with EMBNs not only inhibited the increases of CD4+ T cell subgroups in the liver, but also improved the immune landscape of the MLNs. EMBNs were also able to intervene the alterations of CD8+ T cells and macrophage cells. In addition to increased infiltration of M1 macrophages, EMBNs promoted the percentages of IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells, a subset of CD8+ T cells that are responsible for the eradication of tumors (Fig. 5K, L). These results verified that EMBNs-mediated protection of intestinal barrier integrity could maintain normal immune microenvironments in the liver and MLNs to prevent HCC.

We further assessed the value of EMBNs in treating HCC. Based on a previously reported therapeutic setting73, H22 cells were implanted into the left liver lobe after 7 days of DSS induction, and mice were orally administered with PBS, BNs, CCMs, or EMBNs daily for 14 days (Fig. 6A). After treatment, mice dosed with EMBNs showed significantly fewer and smaller tumor nodules in the liver than those in the other treatment groups (Fig. 6B–D). Expectedly, mice received with PBS displayed a decrease in the size of the spleen, which was recovered effectively by EMBNs treatment (Supplementary Fig. 21A). Consistent with the effects on liver immunity presented in the prevention schedule, increased infiltration of Th1 cells in both the MLNs and the liver were observed in the inoculated mice without treatment (Fig. 6E–G and Supplementary Fig. 21B). Previous studies have reported that an increase in Th1 cells in the liver may not necessarily confer antitumor effects74. Compared to corresponding non-tumor regions, the levels of Th17 and Th1 cells are significantly increased in the tumors of patients with HCC. Although Th1 polarization in the IFN-γ-dominant condition induces T-cell immune responses, it also enhances the immunosuppressive activity of tumor-associated macrophages. The percentages of IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells and M1 macrophages in the liver were increased in mice treated with EMBNs (Fig. 6H, I). Briefly, these results implied the potential of EMBNs to inhibit the growth of HCC.

In addition to the involvement in the occurrence of liver cancer, the translocation of intestinal bacteria is also related to hepatic metastasis8,75. Particularly, the liver is the most common metastatic site of colon cancer, which is one of the three most common cancers worldwide and the major cause of death from colon cancer8,76. It has been noted that liver metastasis of colon cancer is associated with the translocations of intestinal bacteria and LPS76. Thus, we further studied the efficacy of EMBNs to prevent hepatic metastasis by inoculating colorectal carcinoma CT26 cells in the spleen after disrupting intestinal barrier integrity via DSS and different treatments. As depicted in Fig. 7A, 7 days after inoculation, mice were euthanized to visualize the metastatic tumor. In comparison to inoculated mice without DSS stimulation, more distinct metastasis was observed in the liver of PBS-treated mice, which were developed with DSS-induced compromised intestinal barrier (Fig. 7B, C). Meanwhile, treatment with EMBNs achieved notable suppression of metastatic tumors compared to other treatment groups. The alleviated metastasis was further supported by decreased liver weight (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, a single cell suspension of the liver was prepared to assess the variation of the immune microenvironment. Comparable to normal mice, the levels of CD4+ subgroup cells in the liver and MLNs remained unchanged in inoculated mice without DSS stimulation. However, significant alterations in the landscape of immune cells were found in the liver and MLNs, particularly for Treg and Th1 cells in PBS-treated DSS mice, which were characterized by intestinal barrier injury and gut microbiota dysbiosis. The alterations of CD4+ subgroup cells were reversed under the treatment of EMBNs, contributing to the inhibition of cancer metastasis (Fig. 7E–J). In sum, EMBNs demonstrated the potency to inhibit the liver metastasis of colon cancer.

We next evaluated the role of the intestinal barrier in EMBNs-mediated inhibition of tumor metastasis. Mice were treated with 4% (w/v) DSS in drinking water for 7 days, followed by oral gavage of PBS (0.2 ml), BNs (0.24 mg butyrate/mouse), CCMs (0.08 mg/mouse), or EMBNs (0.24 mg butyrate/mouse and 0.08 mg CCMs/mouse). After DSS induction, mice were inoculated with CT26 cells and daily administered with PBS, BNs, CCMs, or EMBNs for 14 days. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22A, B, EMBNs-treated mice exhibited the lowest plasma levels of LPS and zonulin, indicating improved intestinal barrier integrity. Additionally, we measured plasma D-lactic acid, a microbial metabolite that only enters circulation upon intestinal barrier dysfunction77. Clearly, EMBNs-treated mice displayed the lowest D-lactic acid level (Supplementary Fig. 22C). The reduced levels of these markers associated with compromised intestinal barrier function were accompanied by histological improvements. As evidenced in Supplementary Fig. 22D-H, EMBNs effectively mitigated DSS-induced goblet cell depletion and restored tight junction protein expression. The protection of intestinal barrier function by EMBNs contributed to enhanced anti-tumor immune responses. Specifically, EMBNs treatment induced a significant expansion of IFNγ+CD8+ T cells, which showed increased production of TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin (Supplementary Fig. 23A–D). Beyond CD8+ T cell modulation, EMBNs also promoted the polarization of IFNγ+CD4+ T cells, but reduced Treg cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 23E, F). Furthermore, EMBNs enhanced dendritic cell activation and promoted M1 macrophage polarization (Supplementary Fig. 23G–I). Taken together, EMBNs-mediated gut barrier protection orchestrated an immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment, contributing to tumor inhibition.

Key elements for effective suppression of tumor growth by EMBNs

To further elucidate the importance of each component of EMBNs to antitumor efficacy, DSS-treated Balb/c mice were inoculated with H22 cells and daily administered with PBS, BNs, CCMs, EMNs (CCM-coated nanoparticles without butyrate), SMBNs (SK-MEL-1 cell membrane-coated BNs) and EMBNs for 21 days, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 24). The expressions of TLR4 and EGFR on SK-MEL-1 cell membranes (SKMs) were negligible (Supplementary Fig. 25). As expected, EMBNs showed the most potent ability to inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 8A–C). The antitumor efficacy of EMBNs was associated with the effective initiation of both adaptive and innate immunity. The alterations of immune cells in the spleen were measured by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 26). As depicted in Fig. 8D, E, the EMBNs group presented the maximum percentages of IFNγ+CD4+ T cells and IFNγ+CD8+ T cells. In contrast, all other treatments failed to increase the IFNγ+CD4+ and IFNγ+CD8+ T cell populations. Additionally, EMBNs could promote the polarization of macrophages to an M1-like phenotype, while all other treatments showed a limited effect on regulating macrophages (Fig. 8F). In addition to serving as a primary energy source for colonocytes to maintain intestinal homeostasis, butyrate is able to regulate immune functions by affecting the migration, activation, proliferation, and cytokine expression of immune cells78,79. As observed, BNs were able to increase natural killer (NK) cells compared to the PBS group. The increased NK cells were further enhanced by EMBNs, showing a higher percentage than that of BNs (Fig. 8G). The limited effects of BNs might be associated with the immunosuppression effects of zonulin and LPS, as which are found to be associated with liver damage80. Meanwhile, butyrate plays a double-edged sword role against tumors and is able to suppress HCC by promoting NK cell infiltration. However, in murine models with innate immune deficiencies, prolonged butyrate administration paradoxically increases hepatic inflammation, liver fibrosis, and HCC markers, highlighting the context-dependent nature of its therapeutic effects80. In contrast, EMBNs effectively decreased the plasma levels of zonulin and LPS (Fig. 8H, I). Notably, the levels of zonulin and LPS were negatively correlative with the percentages of IFNγ+CD4+ T cells, IFNγ+CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and M1 macrophage (Figs. 8J and 9A–H). Taken together, the combination effect of BNs-mediated immune modulation and the sequestration of LPS and zonulin by Caco-2 cell membranes contributed to the antitumor efficacy of EMBNs. The gut microbiota is one of the important modulators of immune cell dynamics81. To confirm whether the gut microbiota was involved in the EMBNs-mediated protective effects, we further investigated the antitumor effects and immune responses in antibiotic-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 27). As depicted in Fig. 9I, J and Supplementary Fig. 28, EMBNs failed to inhibit tumor growth, which was associated with invalid responses of the immune system. The percentages of IFNγ+CD4+ T cells, IFNγ+CD8+ T cells, and NK cells in the EMBNs group were similar to those in the PBS group (Fig. 9K–M). Interestingly, EMBNs could still promote the polarization of macrophages (Fig. 9N). These results indicated that the gut microbiota was involved in EMBNs-mediated antitumor efficacy by affecting the activation of the immune system, particularly the adaptive immune response.

Safety and flexibility of EMBNs

Coating nanoparticles with cell membranes has emerged as a promising technology for disease treatment28,82. To examine the potential of EMBNs for further translation, we first evaluated the toxicity and safety of EMBNs after long-term exposure in healthy mice. Mice were daily dosed with EMBNs for 7 days, and both PBS and BNs were separately applied as controls. The body weight of mice was monitored, and blood routine and histology of major organs were conducted. EMBNs exhibited a favorable biosafety as the treated mice maintained the same rate of body weight gain to the PBS group (Fig. 10A). It was worth noting that negligible fluctuation in the numbers of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), and platelets (PLT) was observed after treatment with EMBNs (Fig. 10B–D). Additionally, H&E staining of the major organs indicated no morphological changes visualized after treatment, supporting the satisfactory safety of EMBNs for oral delivery (Fig. 10E). Meanwhile, the components of hyaluronic acid and chitosan for preparing EMBNs are materials approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for applications in food and medicine83,84. Many clinical trials and human clinical studies associated with the application of butyrate to treat different diseases are also in progress78,85. In addition to CCMs, cell membranes with TLR4 and EGFR could be acquired from human colon epithelial cells. As confirmed by Western blot, the expressions of TLR4 and EGFR on cell membranes were observed in NCM460 cells, a typical human colon epithelial cell line (Supplementary Fig. 29). We validated the flexibility of EMBNs by coating BNs with NCM460 cell membranes (NCMs). Consistent with the effects of EMBNs, the obtained nanoparticles (termed NMBNs) could effectively remove LPS and zonulin, contributing to the protection of the intestinal barrier (Supplementary Fig. 30). As shown in Fig. 10F, G and Supplementary Fig. 31, NMBNs significantly protected Caco-2 cells from LPS-induced injury. The percentage of dead cells in the NMBNs group was ~5.6-fold lower than that of the PBS group (Fig. 10G). Moreover, the destruction of tight junction proteins induced by LPS was significantly reduced by NMBNs (Fig. 10H–J). Collectively, these results demonstrated favorable safety and flexibility of EMBNs, which ensure the potential for further translation.

Discussion

In summary, we report the preparation of EMBNs that can supplement butyrate and eliminate zonulin and LPS to restore and maintain the homeostasis of the gut barrier. EMBNs are fabricated through physical loading of butyrate into electrostatically interacted and sodium triphosphate-stabilized chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanocomplexes, which are further coated with CCMs. By means of the reservation of native membrane receptors from Caco-2 cells, EMBNs are able to bind zonulin and LPS to restrain their negative influences on the gut barrier. The release of encapsulated butyrate is beneficial for enhancing the expression of tight junction occludin and ZO-1 proteins and promoting the production of mucins, which can strengthen the integrity of the gut barrier. The protective effect of EMBNs on the intestinal barrier is associated with the suppression of autophagy activation in enterocytes and the facilitation of a balanced microbial composition. Under environmental insults, EMBNs potently restrict the translocation of intraluminal bacteria and derived pathogenic factors and also largely resist the disturbance of liver immune microenvironment. During in vivo evaluation in mice with DSS-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction, following oral administration, EMBNs exhibit effective inhibition against HCC and hepatic metastasis of colon cancer. The gut microbiota is involved in the EMBNs-mediated antitumor effects by mainly affecting the adaptive immune response. For future perspective, despite the presence of a favorable biosafety in mice, a systematic biosafety study of these biomimetic nanoparticles, particularly in large animals, is necessitated. Moreover, the loading content of butyrate, dosage, and administration frequency need to be optimized. We will explore the use of EMBNs-endowed maintenance of gut barrier homeostasis as a versatile strategy for preventing and treating other digestive system tumors and beyond. In short, this work discloses how gut barrier integrity can be reserved by oral biomimetic nanomedicines under environmental insults to prevent and treat tumors.

Caco-2 cells were cultivated in 150 mm cell culture plates supplemented with DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2/95% air atmosphere. After reaching 80–90% confluence, the cell monolayers were rinsed with cold PBS twice after removing the cell culture medium. Afterward, the cells were scraped off with a rubber policeman and centrifuged at 4 °C, 10,800 × g for 30 min. Then the pellets were suspended with the Potter-Elvehjem Tissue Homogenizer in a buffer (pH 7.5) containing 500 mM mannitol and 10 mM hepes. After incubation with CaCl2 (10 mM) for 15 min on ice, the homogenates were centrifuged at 4 °C, 2000 × g for 15 min to separate aggregated membranes. Then the supernatants were further centrifuged at 4 °C, 21,000 × g for 1 h to collect cell membranes. The cell membranes were stored at −80 °C after the protein concentrations were measured by BCA. The absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy neo2, BioTek, USA, or Infinite M200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland).

Preparation and characterization of EMBNs

Chitosan (0.6 mg/ml), hyaluronic acid (1.3 mg/ml), and sodium triphosphate (0.5 mg/ml) were dissolved in deionized water and filtered with 0.22 µm filters. A volume of 100 µl sodium triphosphate was dropped into 2 ml of hyaluronic acid under stirring for 10 min. Then 0.5 ml of the resulting solution was added dropwise to 1.2 ml of chitosan solution with 22.5 mM butyrate under stirring for 10 min. The resulting mixtures were then held at room temperature for 30 min before use. The resulting mixtures were then incubated with Caco-2 membranes (1.4 mg/ml) in a volume ratio of 7:3 (V/V) for 1 h. The morphology of BNs and EMBNs was measured by TEM. 10 µl of BNs or EMBNs was loaded onto a Formvar/Carbon film with a 300-mesh grid. After 30 min, the remaining liquid was absorbed with filter paper, and the Formvar/Carbon film was rinsed with distilled water twice. Then, the samples were observed by TEM (Tecnai G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) after drying. The size and zeta potential of BNs and EMBNs dispersed in PBS were determined using DLS (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern, UK). BNs coated with FITC-labeled CCMs were measured by flow cytometry (CytoFlex, Beckman Coulter, USA). BNs consisting of rhodamine B-labeled hyaluronic acid and FITC-labeled CCMs were imaged by a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS-SP8, Leica, Germany).

Stability of EMBNs

SGF was prepared by dissolving NaCl (2 g) and pepsin (3.2 g) in 1000 ml of deionized water, and the pH was adjusted to 2.0 with a dilute HCl solution. SIF was prepared by dissolving 6.8 g of KH2PO4 in 250 ml of deionized water. This was followed by the addition of 200 ml of water, and 10 g of trypsin. The pH of the mixture was then adjusted to 6.8 with 0.2 M NaOH. The volume was made up to 1000 ml with water. Equal amounts of BNs and EMBNs were incubated with 1 ml of SGF, SIF, and PBS at 37 °C, respectively. After incubation for a predetermined time interval, the sizes of BNs and EMBNs were measured by DLS (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern, UK).

Encapsulation efficiency and in vitro release of butyrate

To assess the encapsulation efficiency of butyrate, the supernatants were collected after centrifugation of EMBNs at 3000 × g for 10 min. For the measurement of butyrate release, SIF was prepared by dissolving KH2PO4 (6.8 g) in 250 ml of deionized water, followed by the addition of 200 ml of deionized water, and trypsin (10 g). EMBNs were added to SIF with a concentration of 3 mg/ml in the dialysis tube. The dialysis tube was then introduced into a cylinder containing release medium, which was stirred at 300 rpm using a magnetic stir bar. Butyrate release was assessed by intermittently sampling the contents of the outer media. Replace with a fresh buffer immediately after sampling. The amount of butyrate in the sample was analyzed by HPLC (1260 Infinity II, AGILENT, China). HPLC was run using 20% acetonitrile as mobile phase A and 80% phosphoric acid 0.1% v/v as mobile phase B. Flow rate was set to 1 ml/min, and column temperature was 30 °C. The injection volume was 20 µl. The total run time was 10 min per sample.

LPS and zonulin binding studies

To quantify LPS and zonulin removed by EMBNs, 1 EU/ml LPS or the cell supernatant collected from Caco-2 cells stimulated with 10 μg/ml LPS was mixed with varying amounts of CCMs content (0, 31.3, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 µg/ml). After incubating for 24 h at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected after centrifugation of the mixture at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The LPS and zonulin levels in the supernatant were quantified by using the LAL assay and zonulin ELISA kits, respectively.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from colon tissues using a HiPure RNA Mini Kit. The concentration of RNA and optical density at 260/280 were first measured by a Nanodrop ND1000 Spectrophotometer. Then, the RNA was transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA reverse transcription kit with a gDNA wiper. qRT-PCR was conducted in a total reaction volume of 20 μl according to the SYBR Green detection protocol using a Real-Time PCR System. The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to normalize data. The following primer sets were used for amplifications: β-actin-Rv: TCACGCCATCCTGCGTCTGGACT, β-actin-Fw: CCGGACTCATCGTACTCCT, Muc2-Rv: CATAGAGGGCCTGTCCTCAGG, Muc2-Fw: TGTGGCCTGTGTGGGAACTTT.

Western blot

The protein concentration was determined by BCA. Then, a given amount of CCMs, NCMs, SKMs, and EMBNs was mixed with loading buffer and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. Afterward, the protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After incubating with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h, the membranes were washed with TBST 3 times and incubated with primary antibody (anti-TLR4 and anti-EGFR, 1:200 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the PVDF membranes were incubated with horse radish peroxidase (HRP) secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrates by a chemiluminescent detection system (ChemiDoc XRS+ System, Bio-Rad, USA).

Cell permeability

Caco-2 cells (5 × 105) were seeded into a 12-well Transwell® plate (Corning, Inc., Corning, USA). The cells were stimulated with LPS (10 μg/ml) and treated with BNs or EMBNs with butyrate in 2 mM for 24 h. Then FITC-dextran (500 µl, 100 μg/ml) was added to the apical chamber. The medium in the bottom was replaced with 500 µl PBS. After 2 h of incubation, 300 µl of PBS from the bottom chamber was transferred to a fluorescence measurement plate and measured with a microplate fluorometer (Infinite M200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland) with an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm.

Measurement of cell death

Caco-2 cells (5 × 105) were seeded into 12-well plates. After reaching 80-90% confluence, the Caco-2 monolayer was under stimulation of LPS (10 μg/ml) as well as treatment with PBS, BNs, NMBNs, or EMBNs with butyrate in 2 mM for 24 and 48 h, respectively. Then the cells were washed with PBS for two times, and the cells were incubated with PBS containing Annexin V-FITC or Hoechst 33342 and PI at 37 °C for 15 min under dark conditions. Finally, the dead cells were visualized and quantity by LSCM and flow cytometer, respectively.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

The 16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis were conducted at Biomarker Technologies. The total DNA was extracted from colonic content and sequenced by building a sequencing library on Illumina HiSeq 2500. The Illumina platform targeted the V3-V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene. Reads at a 97.0% similarity were used to obtain OTU through cluster analysis using the Usearch software. The valid data were used for subsequent analysis after filtering data where the species abundance was less than 0.005%. The taxa were selected based on the analysis in family and genus levels without FDR correction. The results were stored in FASTQ (referred to as fq) format file, and data analysis was performed on BMKCloud. Beta diversity was analyzed with PCoA. Microbial composition analysis based on family, genus, and order levels, respectively.

Lymphocytes isolation and flow cytometric analysis

Lymphocytes were isolated from the spleen, liver, and MLNs, respectively. The cells were resuspended with 500 μl of PBS after centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cells (~1 × 106 cells/ml) were firstly stained with antibodies (1:200 dilution) against surface markers of CD4+ T cells (CD45-BV421, CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5 or APC-Cy7, CD4-FITC or PE, and CD25-PerCP-Cy5.5 or PE-Cy7), CD8+ T cells (CD45-BV421, CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5 or APC-Cy7 and CD8-APC or PE), NKs (CD3-APC-Cy7 and CD49-PerCP-Cy5.5), and macrophages (CD11b-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD86-APC or PE-Cy7, and F4/80-PE), respectively. For the measurement of intracellular markers (CD206-Alexa Fluor 647 or FITC, IL-4-APC, IFN-γ-PE-Cy7, Foxp3-PE or Alexa Fluor 647, and IL-17A-PE, Perforin-FITC, Granzyme B PerCP-Cy5.5, TNF-α-APC), the cells were first fixed and permeabilized with 1 × Perm buffer. After antibody incubation and PBS rinsing, the cells were resuspended in 300 μl of PBS and measured with flow cytometry (LSRFortessa, BD Biosciences, USA). Immunofluorescence

Caco-2 cells seeded on carrying sheet glasses or slides of colon were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and washed with PBS for 3 times. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum in PBS after antigen retrieval treatment. The samples were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-ZO-1 and anti-occludin, 1:50 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. Then, the samples were washed with PBS for 3 times and incubated with secondary antibodies (1:300 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. Images were visualized using LSCM after sealing carrying sheet glass or tissue slides with an antifade solution containing DAPI.

Analysis of autophagosomes by TEM

Caco-2 cells with 90% confluence were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde after 24-h co-incubation with 20 μg/ml LPS and PBS, BNs, and EMBNs, respectively. The cells were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. Then, cells were fixed with 2% osmic acid and 3% potassium ferrocyanide for 30 min in the dark. After 3 times washing with PBS, cells were incubated with 1% dithiomethylhydrazine for 30 min in the dark. Then, the cells were fixed again with 2% osmic acid for 30 min and stained with 2% uranyl acetate at 4 °C overnight. After dehydration with ethanol in different concentrations, the samples were embedded with Epon812. Finally, ultra-thin samples were prepared for TEM observation (FEI Talos L120C, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Analysis of autophagosomes by monodansylcadaverine

Caco-2 cells in 96-well plates were exposed to DMEM containing 20 μg/ml LPS and PBS, BNs, and EMBNs, respectively. The concentration of butyrate in these nanoparticles was set at 1 mM. After 24-h treatment, cells were washed with 1 × assay buffer and incubated with monodansylcadaverine for 1 h. Then, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader with an excitation wavelength of 355 nm and an emission wavelength of 512 nm.

Measurement of ROS

Caco-2 cells in the 96-well or confocal dish were exposed to 20 μg/ml LPS and then treated with PBS, BNs, and EMBNs, respectively. The concentration of butyrate in the used nanoparticles was set at 1 mM. After 24-h treatment, cells were washed with culture medium 3 times and incubated in 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. LSCM captured the images.

Measurement of butyric acid

Male mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were randomly assigned to three experimental groups. Each mouse received 0.3 mL of sodium butyrate, BNs, or EMBNs via oral gavage. At predetermined time points (1-, 4-, 6-, or 12-h post-gavage), the mice were euthanized. Serum was isolated from blood samples by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C for later analysis. Intestinal contents from the entire gut were collected into sterile microcentrifuge tubes and homogenized with 500 μl of 1× PBS. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 1 ml of the supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of diethyl ether and 10 μl of hydrochloric acid. After vigorous vortexing and phase separation, the upper ether layer was transferred to a new tube. Anhydrous sodium sulfate was added to remove residual water, and the extract was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane before GC-MS analysis (Shimadzu GCMS-QP2020 NX). Specifically, chromatographic separation was performed on an SH-Polar D capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; P/N: R221-75981-30) using helium as the carrier gas. The oven temperature was initially held at 50 °C for 2 min, ramped to 150 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min, and subsequently increased to 220 °C at 20 °C/min, followed by a final hold for 5 min. Butyric acid was characterized and identified by comparing the acquired mass spectra against the NIST reference mass spectral library. All data acquisition and analysis were performed using GC-MS solution software.

Distribution of EMBNs

Balb/c mice (male, 6–8 weeks old) were orally administered with 0.2 ml of PBS or EMBNs. EMBNs were prepared by using Cy5.5-labeled hyaluronic acid. The fluorescence signals of nanoparticles were captured in mice under anesthesia at the designated time intervals (0.5, 1, 1.5 and 24 h). Then, the major organs, blood, and feces were collected for the measurement of fluorescence intensity by IVIS (IVIS Lumina II, Caliper Life Sciences, USA).

Induction of intestinal barrier injury

After 7 days of adaptation, male mice were classified into 5 groups randomly, including the control, PBS, BNs, CCMs, and EMBNs groups. Mice were administered with 4% w/v DSS in drinking water and gavaged with 0.2 ml PBS, BNs, CCMs, and EMBNs, respectively. Control mice were given ddH2O only. During the 7 days of induction, the body weight of mice was monitored. On day 8, mice were sacrificed, and samples were collected.

Intestinal permeability

Male mice were gavaged with FITC-dextran at a dose of 400 mg/kg body weight. Blood samples were collected at 4 h post-administration. Plasma samples were collected by centrifugation of blood samples (2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C). Then, the fluorescence intensity of the FITC-dextran in plasma was determined using a microplate fluorometer with an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. Serially diluted FITC-dextran was used to establish a standard curve.

Construction of orthotopic and metastatic murine tumor models

H22 and CT26 cells were cultured in 1640 and DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, respectively, at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air. Control mice were free of any treatments. After 7 days of treatments of ddH2O and PBS or 4% w/v DSS along with PBS, BNs, CCMs, or EMBNs, 3–5 × 105 H22 cells were injected into the left lobes of the liver to establish the murine orthotopic hepatoma model. Samples were collected 7 days after inoculation. In the treatment schedule, the mice were administered PBS, BNs, CCMs, or EMBNs for another 7 days after tumor cell inoculation. Similarly, CT26 cells in 5 × 105 were injected into the spleen to establish liver metastases of murine colon adenocarcinoma. Samples were collected on day 8. To assess the effects of the gut microbiota on EMBNs-mediated antitumor efficacy, mice were provided with an antibiotic cocktail as describe elsewhere86. The antibiotic cocktail consisted of ampicillin (0.08 mg/ml, A610028), metronidazole (0.08 mg/ml, A600633), neomycin (0.08 mg/ml, A610366), and vancomycin (0.04 mg/ml, A600983) for the whole course. The antibiotic-containing water was renewed every 3–4 days to maintain efficacy. In accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines, animals should be euthanized if they show ≥20% body weight loss from baseline, a persistent hunched posture, or an inability to reach food and water.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and IBM SPSS Statistics 19. Data points were excluded only when identified as statistical outliers by the standardized boxplot-based analysis using Tukey’s method. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots, with non-normality defined as p < 0.05. When continuous variables follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution, data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). The results were analyzed using a one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test with the least significant difference (LSD) test among the three groups. The differences between the two groups were analyzed by Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data were either logarithmically transformed or expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Differences between medians were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for pairwise comparisons or the Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple comparisons, followed by post-hoc analysis with Dunn’s test. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The linear association between two variables was measured by the Pearson correlation coefficient. Experiments were independently replicated at least twice.
