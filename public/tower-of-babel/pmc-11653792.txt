Behaviors and attitudes towards mobile learning during course work and scientific research among dental, medical and non-medical undergraduates: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhihang Song, Xinyu Wen, Zhaoping Li, Xiaoyu Han, Lifeiyan Lai, Jun Yang, Fei Yu, Zhihe Zhao, Peilin Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMobile learning is a valuable supplement to traditional education methods, but the current research on mobile learning is still insufficient. This study aimed to assess dental undergraduates’ behaviors and attitudes towards mobile learning during course work and scientific research and to compare these findings to those of medical and non-medical students.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among undergraduates at Sichuan University, China. Participants completed an online questionnaire covering demographic information, mobile learning behaviors in course work and scientific research, and attitudes towards mobile learning.ResultsA total of 849 valid responses were collected, including 290 dental students, 307 medical students, and 252 non-medical students. The average behavior scores for using mobile devices in course work and scientific research were 3.02 and 3.09 (out of 5), respectively. Smartphones were the most frequently used devices for course work across all majors, while laptops predominated for scientific research. Mobile device preferences for various learning activities were similar across all majors, with minor variations. Over 80% of dental students reported using mobile learning for more than one hour daily for course work, and they had the highest proportion of daily mobile learning exceeding 90 min. Overall, students demonstrated positive attitudes towards mobile learning, with average attitude scores of 3.58, 3.66, and 3.47 (out of 5) for dental, medical, and non-medical students, respectively.ConclusionResult show that dental undergraduates used mobile learning extensively and exhibited positive attitudes towards it for both course work and scientific research. This study highlights mobile device preferences and their alignment with various academic activities, which can inform the strategic development of educational technologies in dental curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06417-8.

Introduction

The advent of information technology has reshaped the educational landscape, with mobile devices such as smartphones, tablets, and laptops becoming increasingly prevalent in undergraduate learning processes [1, 2]. Mobile learning, which utilizes portable electronic devices across various settings, has garnered considerable scholarly attention due to the flexibility, portability, and connectivity these devices offer [3–5]. This facilitates a more dynamic and contextualized approach to learning [6]. Advances in both hardware and software have expanded the capabilities of mobile learning and enriched students’ educational experiences [7].

Studies have demonstrated the heightened popularity of mobile learning among dental and medical undergraduates, who frequently confront considerable academic pressures and rely on mobile technology to facilitate their learning [8, 9]. Mobile learning is posited to enhance the study process within healthcare education [10], and it has been observed to transform the manner in which medical students engage with and assimilate knowledge [11, 12]. This transformation is particularly significant given the need for dental students to acquire professional knowledge, honed operating skills, and adept patient management abilities [13]. The incorporation of mobile technology into the learning process is vital as it supports the development of competencies essential to the medical and dental professions, suggesting that mobile learning is a valuable adjunct to traditional educational approaches.

Mobile learning behavior encompasses the context and frequency of students’ use of mobile devices for educational purposes. Mobile learning attitude, on the other hand, reflects students’ disposition toward mobile learning practices, ranging from positive acceptance to negative resistance. Despite the growing adoption of mobile learning, there remains a gap in the literature regarding the comparative behaviors and attitudes of dental and medical undergraduates toward this mode of learning [14, 15]. Additionally, the current state of knowledge suggests that there has been no research conducted on mobile learning activities in scientific research, and further investigation emergence is imperatively warranted. Participation in research is crucial for the development of academic and professional skills, particularly among dental students [16, 17], and mobile learning has the potential to enhance this process.

This study aimed to assess dental undergraduates’ behaviors and attitudes towards mobile learning during course work and scientific research and to compare these findings to those of medical and non-medical students. The mobile learning behaviors and attitudes of undergraduates in scientific research activities were brought into focus for the first time. By identifying potential obstacles and exploring potential strategies, this study aimed to enhance the quality of dental education through the strategic application of mobile learning.

Materials and methods

A comprehensive cross-sectional survey was conducted to compare the behavior and attitude toward mobile devices among dental, medical, and non-medical students to provide a broader perspective on the adoption of mobile learning.

The sample size was determined based on the number of undergraduate students in West China school of stomatology (about 750) and calculated by single population proportion formula (Formula 1) with a predetermined margin of error of 5% and a confidence level of 95%. This cross-sectional survey was conducted using an online questionnaire. During the questionnaire design process, our survey drew on several existing research studies [18–21]. Following comprehensive discussions among all authors, we created an initial version of the questionnaire. We then randomly selected undergraduates from Sichuan University to participate in a pilot test. Based on their feedback, we refined certain question options and wording. The final version questionnaire identified common aspects and scenarios encountered by undergraduates, ensuring the relevance of the questions to the target population. At the beginning of the questionnaire, the consent approval would be obtained from participants firstly. The questionnaire was structured into four distinct sections:

(i)Demographic information: This section contained basic demographic details, including gender, academic grade, and major. The internal consistency and reliability of the questionnaire were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, a robust measure of internal consistency [16]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the three sections exceeded 0.8, with sections ii, iii, and iv yielding values of 0.899, 0.998, and 0.920, respectively. These results indicate a significant level of reliability and credibility of the questionnaire. According to our internal test, responses to the questionnaire were considered invalid if completion time was less than 2 min.

We investigated undergraduate students’ behaviors and attitudes towards mobile learning and their perception of its benefits and limitations. To quantitatively assess mobile learning behaviors and attitudes, a five-point Likert scale scoring system was established as follows: “always” (using the device in every applicable situation or instance) or “strongly agree” were assigned a score of 5, “frequently” (using the device in the majority of applicable situations) or “agree” were assigned a score of 4, “sometimes” (using the device in about half of the applicable situations) or “normal” were assigned a score of 3, “occasionally” (using the device in a minority of applicable situations) or “disagree” were assigned a score of 2, and “never” (never using a mobile device for the activity) or “strongly disagree” were assigned a score of 1. The total, mean scores, and standard deviations were utilized to characterize usage patterns across different participant groups.

Data collection

This cross-sectional survey was conducted among undergraduate students at Sichuan University in China. The questionnaires were hosted on an online platform, wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), and disseminated via social media, particularly WeChat. Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous. The consent approval form participants were obtained at the beginning of the questionnaire. The data collection phase spanned from March 10th to April 1st in 2024. A total of 1,420 questionnaires were collected preliminarily. To ensure the integrity and reliability of the data, we excluded incomplete and invalid questionnaires, yielding 849 valid questionnaires. Invalid questionnaires were characterized by a pattern of uniform response selection across all items.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (IBM, USA, version 23.0.0). To evaluate the validity of the questionnaire, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was computed. The normality of the numerical data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the data were not normally distributed, nonparametric statistical methods were employed to analyze the variance in the scores. For comparisons between two independent groups, such as the domains of course work versus scientific research, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. For comparisons among multiple groups, including assessments involving different types of mobile devices, the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05 for all tests conducted.

Results

Participants’ behavior in mobile learning

Our study preliminarily collected a total of 1,420 questionnaires. After a thorough examination, any incomplete and invalid responses were excluded, yielding 849 valid questionnaires and an effective response rate of 59.78%.

The 849 valid respondents comprised 290 dental students, 307 medical students, and 252 students from non-medical disciplines, as detailed in Table 1. The gender distribution across the sample was 315 males to 534 females, approximating a 1:1.7 ratio.

Among the 290 dental students, 133 (45.9%) had participated in scientific research, as had 155 (50.5%) of the medical students and 79 (31.3%) of the non-medical students. This indicated a greater level of engagement in scientific research activities among dental and medical students than among their non-medical peers.

Mobile learning behavior in course work

Figure 1 delineates the average total scores associated with the usage of mobile devices in mobile learning across different student groups. Medical students achieved the highest total score for mobile learning behaviors in course work (p = 0.001). In the realm of course work, smartphones were the most frequently utilized devices by all students, with tablets ranking second in preference and laptops being the least preferred option.

Table 3 presents the three most prevalent scenarios in which students used mobile devices during their course work activities. The preferences of dental and medical students regarding the top three course work activities on different mobile devices were the same. Interestingly, non-medical students exhibited a similar frequency of device usage to their medical and dental peers, despite the differences in their course curricula. Minor variations existed. Tablet use was most pronounced for classroom note-taking, information retrieval, and self-directed learning in the non-medical group. However, for dental and medical groups, the predominant uses of tablets were note-taking, self-directed learning, and accessing course materials.

The survey revealed the device preferences of dental students. They primarily used smartphones for class sign-in (4.14 ± 0.93), photo-taking and recording (3.83 ± 1.07), and information retrieval (3.56 ± 1.16). Tablets were favored for notetaking during class (3.86 ± 1.40), self-learning (3.79 ± 1.37), and information retrieval (3.52 ± 1.36). Laptops were less preferred, with higher scores for information retrieval (3.16 ± 1.32), self-directed learning (3.10 ± 1.25), and seminar participation (2.91 ± 1.39).

Mobile learning behavior in scientific research

Figure 2 illustrates that laptops were the predominant device used by all three groups of undergraduate students for scientific research. This suggested that portability was not a primary concern for students in research contexts. The usage frequencies of smartphones (21.77 ± 6.94) and tablets (19.69 ± 9.09) in scientific research for dental students were significantly lower compared to other majors. Conversely, the score of laptop usage among dental students (36.10 ± 6.84) was the highest across all majors, indicating a strong preference for dental students.

Daily time investment in mobile learning by participants

Figure 3 presents the distribution of daily mobile learning time as a pentagonal chart, expressed as a percentage of students across different majors. The results indicated that a significant proportion (81.7%) of dental students devoted over one hour daily to mobile learning for course work. The dental students had the highest proportion of daily mobile learning exceeding 90 min in all groups (p<0.01). Specifically, 17.6% of the dental students spent 90–120 min on course work, and 43.1% dedicated more than two hours daily, highlighting the essential role of mobile learning in their educational regimen. In comparison, 12.4% of medical students and 16.7% of non-medical students spent 90–120 min daily on mobile learning; 33.2% of medical students and 31.7% of non-medical students dedicated more than two hours to course work activities.

In the context of scientific research, the distribution of the daily mobile learning time of dental students differed from that of other majors. Compared to their medical and non-medical peers, more dental students reported a daily mobile learning duration of less than 30 min, or 90–120 min (p < 0.05).

Participants’ attitudes towards mobile learning

On average, dental, medical, and non-medical students reported attitude scores of 3.58 ± 0.53, 3.66 ± 0.57, and 3.47 ± 0.53, respectively, out of a maximum of 5 points, demonstrating a generally positive attitude towards mobile learning.

Concerning the perceived drawbacks of mobile devices, the most commonly reported drawbacks overall include distractions from irrelevant information on smartphones, the additional cost of tablets, and issues with charging and battery life for laptops. There were some differences in students’ opinions across different majors. Dental students identified the lack of professional image processing and data analysis software as a notable limitation for smartphones and tablets and expressed concerns about the potential for visual and physical strain associated with prolonged laptop use. Medical students reported that distractions from unrelated content diminished the effectiveness of smartphones and tablets, and they highlighted the constraint of limited battery life for laptops. Non-medical students predominantly cited issues related to distractions from irrelevant information on smartphones, the lack of specialized software on tablets, and the financial burden of owning laptops.

Discussion

This study is the first to assess the behaviors and attitudes towards mobile learning in course work and scientific research among dental, medical, and non-medical undergraduates. In the digital era, understanding the current application of mobile technology in learning processes is essential for enriching teaching methods, refining pedagogical approaches and enhancing teaching efficiency. Our survey revealed that smartphones are the most frequently utilized mobile devices for course work by undergraduate students across all majors, while laptops are predominantly used for scientific research. The preferences for the most frequent activities associated with different devices are similar among dental, medical, and non-medical students, with only minor variations.

The amount of daily time students allocate to mobile learning reflects its significance in their academic lives. For course work, more than four out of five dental students devoted more than one hour daily to mobile learning, and they had the highest proportion of daily mobile learning exceeding 90 min. A greater proportion of dental students reported daily mobile learning times of less than 30 min or 90–120 min for scientific research than did their medical and non-medical peers. Notably, over half of the participants in our study engaged with mobile devices for more than one hour daily, indicative of their significant integration into their academic routines for course work and research. The scores of attitudes suggest a broadly positive attitude toward mobile learning among undergraduates. Generally, undergraduates from all majors commonly undertake mobile learning daily and hold positive attitudes towards using mobile technology in both course work and scientific research.

The observed greater engagement of dental and medical students in scientific research compared to other majors’ highlights pronounced research endeavors. This phenomenon is consistent with findings from a study conducted among their counterparts in Pakistan, which demonstrated their strong interest in research activities [22]. One plausible explanation for this inclination toward research is the encouragement from higher education institutions for undergraduates to engage in scientific research, as well as the competitive admission criteria for postgraduate programs in dental and medical fields. Many institutions have initiated research projects for dental undergraduates to foster and enhance their academic capacities [23]. A study conducted in the United Kingdom reported that 98% of the involved medical undergraduates recognized the benefits of research experience, with 90% having already gained research experience [24]. Similarly, a study involving 1022 Chinese medical undergraduates revealed that 48.48% of the participants engaged in scientific research, resulting in enhanced scientific thinking and basic research capacities [25]. In China, it is a widely adopted criterion for undergraduate students majoring in dentistry or medicine to possess a scientific research background or tangible research outcomes when applying for postgraduate studies. On a global scale, a robust research profile is frequently valued as a critical advantage for graduate program applicants across various universities.

In the context of mobile learning behaviors during the course work, our findings indicate that smartphones are the most preferred devices among participants from different majors. The participants also demonstrated similar preferences regarding the devices most frequently used for certain activities. Smartphones were predominantly utilized for check-ins, class recordings, and information searches, likely due to their real-time capabilities, rapid processing, and quick access to information. Additionally, the mobility of smartphones facilitates easy sharing and communication without the constraints of time and space. This supports the findings of another study in which smartphones were considered a supportive educational tool that positively impacts students’ knowledge, skills, confidence, and attitudes [26].

However, this study also identified drawbacks associated with smartphone use in learning. The most significant issue reported by participants was distraction from irrelevant information. Similar problems have been reported in other studies, particularly distractions from social media [27]. Interestingly, only a few participants in those studies felt personally affected by these distractions [28]. This discrepancy may be due to a lack of self-awareness regarding the impact of distractions. Problematic smartphone use, characterized by an inability to control usage leading to negative outcomes and impulse control issues, may underlie social media distractions [29, 30]. Interventions could include training in cognitive-behavioral techniques aimed at raising students’ awareness about the consequences of problematic smartphone use and teaching them effective self-regulation strategies. Additionally, the development of technological solutions, such as applications with built-in pause features or focus modes, could support students in managing their smartphone use during educational activities. These interventions could help create an environment that promotes focused and efficient learning [22].

In terms of scientific research, our study indicates that students, irrespective of their major, exhibit a common preference for laptops over smartphones and tablets. This preference diverges from that observed in course work, where the portability of mobile devices is often prioritized. In scientific research, the laptop’s utility is predominantly attributed to its superior functionality and ability to augment academic collaboration, which is essential for intricate tasks such as data analysis, article composition, and online presentations and discussions. Accordingly, the results indicated that the predominant research activities conducted on laptops were literature searching, reading, and writing academic papers.

However, the study also revealed that laptops have drawbacks, including issues related to charging and battery life. Participants expressed concerns about the lack of sufficient charging outlets when using laptops in various locations. Utilizing high-capacity batteries often leads to an increase in the weight of the devices. Given that high portability is considered a fundamental aspect of mobile learning [31], one potential avenue for improvement is to enhance laptop portability through technical innovations such as high-capacity batteries and the use of lightweight materials. This enhancement could bridge the gap between the preferred functionalities of laptops and the mobility offered by smartphones and tablets in mobile learning contexts.

The overall attitudes of undergraduates toward mobile learning were affirmative, with the majority of respondents recognizing the advantages of mobile learning in both course work and scientific research. This positive perception aligns with existing studies conducted among students in London, Kuwait, and Turkey [18, 32, 33]. Perceived usefulness, ease of use, and self-efficacy are important factors contributing to positive attitudes [34, 35]. Positive attitudes have been linked to the effective utilization of mobile learning and can improve students’ study performance [33, 36]. A quasi-experimental study conducted among dental students showed that 93.5% of the participants held a positive attitude toward mobile learning, and mobile learning may improve their achievement [20].

Understanding the drawbacks of mobile learning is essential to maximizing its effectiveness and ensuring its successful integration into educational frameworks. The drawbacks associated with mobile learning can be categorized into technical issues, user proficiency, and instructor attitudes [37]. Addressing technical challenges necessitates the solving of limitations such as screen size, battery life, device memory, and network connectivity [38]. Students must be equipped to judiciously leverage mobile learning based on a comprehensive understanding of its functionalities, advantages, and limitations [14]. Educators, in turn, should integrate mobile learning with traditional pedagogical approaches in a balanced manner and provide guidance on the effective use of mobile devices.

This study is subject to several limitations that may influence the interpretability of the results. First, with the exception of architecture, non-medical undergraduate majors are typically structured as four-year courses. Consequently, the number of fifth-year non-medical undergraduates is relatively small. This factor may have contributed to an imbalance in the representation of various academic years. Second, the reliance on self-assessment questionnaires might introduce recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the responses. Last, the generalizability of the findings may be constrained since the study was conducted at a single university. Future research would benefit from a collaborative, multi-institutional design, encompassing multiple regions or even countries. This approach would significantly mitigate the potential for bias in research findings. Although this study may not represent the entire community of undergraduates, it still offers valuable information to better comprehend the behaviors and attitudes of undergraduates of health profession students toward mobile learning. Further research is needed to address or mitigate the drawbacks of mobile learning, making it a more robust and beneficial component of the teaching process.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the significant integration of mobile learning into the academic lives of undergraduates, particularly in dental and medical education. Our findings underscore the value of mobile learning as a versatile tool for both curricular and research purposes. Most participants recognized its merits and held positive attitudes towards its adoption. This research offers valuable insights for educators, enabling them to understand undergraduates’ mobile learning behaviors and attitudes better and to refine teaching methods accordingly.
