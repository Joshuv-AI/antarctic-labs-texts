Synergistic effects of two bacteriophages with distinct infection patterns and broad host specificity against multidrug-resistant Salmonella Typhimurium and their potential applications in the poultry industry
Authors: Youbin Choi, Jeongkuk Park, Anna Kang, Eunsol Seo, Daniel Junpyo Lee, Seon-hui Son, Ki Beom Jang, Woo Kyun Kim, Darae Kang, Younghoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractControlling Salmonella Typhimurium in poultry requires effective alternatives to conventional antimicrobials. This study evaluated the efficacy of a two-phage cocktail composed of S. Typhimurium-infecting phages SLAM_phiST45 and SLAM_phiST56 and investigated its mechanistic basis. Host-range profiling of 61 phages against 10 S. Typhimurium strains revealed two major host-associated clusters (swine-human vs. poultry). Notably, the cocktail exhibited broad host ranges that crossed these clusters. The cocktail showed clear synergy, supported by complementary receptor usage and reduced emergence of phage-resistant mutants. In egg-based food application assays, the cocktail achieved rapid bacterial suppression even at an MOI of 1 and fully eliminated S. Typhimurium under refrigerated conditions. In a chick infection model, the cocktail improved growth performance, reduced intestinal and systemic bacterial loads, alleviated intestinal lesions, and restored inflammatory and barrier-related gene expression. While the overall gut microbiota structure remained stable, subtle alterations in low-abundance taxa indicated that phage treatment may cause minor off-target shifts. Overall, this study demonstrates that the phage cocktail is a potent and practical biocontrol tool capable of reducing Salmonella in both food matrices and live birds. These findings support its application in poultry production and highlight the importance of monitoring microbiome-level impacts in future phage-based interventions.Keywords: Bacteriophage cocktail, Broad host range, Distinct infection mechanisms, Poultry industry, Salmonella Typhimurium

Introduction

Salmonella is a leading foodborne pathogen often linked to poultry products and poses a serious zoonotic threat. Nontyphoidal serovars such as S. Enteritidis, S. Typhimurium and S. Gallinarum are frequently isolated from chickens and can infect humans through contaminated meat and eggs (Kim et al., 2025; Oh, et al., 2025; Raza, et al., 2024). In the United States alone, salmonellosis causes on the order of 1.35 million illnesses and hundreds of deaths annually (Punchihewage-Don, et al., 2024). As global chicken production continues to rise, controlling Salmonella in poultry is critical for public health and food safety.

The widespread misuse of antibiotics in animal agriculture has exacerbated this problem, leading to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Salmonella strains (Alcaine, et al., 2007). Resistance to critical antibiotics (e.g. fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins and macrolides) has been increasing in non-typhoidal Salmonella, leaving fewer effective treatments (Marchello, et al., 2020). Consequently, stricter regulations now limit antibiotic use in poultry, yet resistant strains persist on farms. These trends underscore the urgent need for alternative control strategies against MDR Salmonella in poultry (Rajashekara, et al., 2000).

Bacteriophage (phage) therapy has re-emerged as a promising alternative to antibiotics (Jo, et al., 2024). Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, phages are highly specific “guided missiles” that infect only particular bacterial hosts (Choi, et al., 2024). Virulent phages replicate in and lyse their targets, proliferating at the site of infection without harming unrelated microbiota. The specificity and self-amplifying nature of phages allow effective targeting of pathogens even at low doses, and phages can penetrate biofilms and reduce collateral damage to beneficial bacteria (Chan, et al., 2013; Gordillo Altamirano and Barr, 2019).

To overcome the narrow host range of individual phages, phage cocktails, which are mixtures of multiple phages, have been developed (Gu, et al., 2012; Pereira, et al., 2016). Combining phages broadens the overall host range and makes it much harder for bacteria to evolve resistance, since simultaneous mutation of multiple receptor sites would be required (Kim, et al., 2024). In fact, designing cocktails with phages that bind distinct bacterial surface receptors has proven especially effective in suppressing MDR pathogens. For example, an “ABCD-type” cocktail of four Salmonella phages, each recognizing a different LPS receptor on the cell surface, achieved prolonged bactericidal activity and much greater efficacy in food and animal models than single-phage treatments (Chen, et al., 2025). These synergistic cocktails can dramatically reduce Salmonella on foods and in vivo while mitigating resistance emergence.

Given these advantages, phage cocktails are a compelling tool for Salmonella control in poultry. In the present study, we focus on two S. Typhimurium-infecting phages, SLAM_phiST45 (hereafter phiST45) and SLAM_phiST56 (hereafter phiST56), which were selected from 61 screened Salmonella phages based on their complementary host ranges, strong lytic activity, and enhanced bacterial suppression when combined. Specifically, phiST45 showed broad lytic activity against most tested strains, whereas phiST56 effectively lysed strains that were less susceptible to phiST45, thereby expanding the combined host range of the cocktail. We aim to evaluate the efficacy of the phiST45 + phiST56 cocktail both in vivo (in live chicks) and in food matrices, and to carefully assess its host range and impact on the gut microbiota. The high host specificity of phages suggests minimal collateral effects, and indeed prior work has shown phage treatment causes only minor shifts in poultry gut flora (Choi, et al., 2025). By testing this cocktail in controlled experiments, we seek to demonstrate its potential for reducing MDR Salmonella in poultry production while preserving microbial balance.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and bacteriophages

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium strain used in this study, KVCC-BA0000422, was obtained from the Korea Veterinary Culture Collection (KVCC, Korea) and originally isolated from a diseased chicken at a commercial poultry farm in Korea. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted for genomic characterization of the bacteria.

Briefly, a single colony was selected, suspended in 1 mL of sterile water, and genomic DNA was extracted using the HiGene™ Genomic DNA Prep Kit (BioFACT, Korea) (Kang, et al., 2025). DNA libraries were prepared according to the Oxford Nanopore Technologies ligation sequencing kit protocol (SQK-LSK109) and sequenced using the MinION Mk1B platform equipped with an R9.4.1 flow cell. Reads longer than 1,000 bp were assembled de novo into a single contig using Flye v2.9.4 (Kolmogorov, et al., 2019), and gene annotation was conducted with Prokka v1.14.6 (Seemann, 2014). Functional gene analysis and sequence alignment were performed using ClustalW in MEGA X v10.2.6 (Kumar, et al., 2004), and nucleotide identity was assessed using NCBI BLASTn (Lee, et al., 2016). Circular genome map was generated using the CGView Server available through Proksee.ca (Stothard and Wishart, 2005). The assembled genome was designated S. Typhimurium SLAM_SAT01 (hereafter SAT01) and deposited in NCBI GenBank under the accession number JBSKFX000000000.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of SAT01 was performed using the disk diffusion method. Briefly, SAT01 was cultured, adjusted to the appropriate bacterial density, and evenly spread onto TSB agar plates. BD BBL™ Sensi-Disc™ antimicrobial susceptibility test discs were then placed on the agar surface, and the plates were incubated at 37°C (Lalitha, 2004). The tested antibiotics included ampicillin, penicillin, imipenem, meropenem, kanamycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and vancomycin. After incubation, inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters. The results were interpreted according to the CLSI M100™ 35th edition criteria for Salmonella and Shigella spp., where applicable (Blosser, 2024).

Two S. Typhimurium-infecting phages were used in this study. PhiST45 was isolated using SAT01 as the host strain, whereas phiST56 was isolated using S. Typhimurium KVCC-BA1600008 and was shown to exert synergistic inhibitory effects on the growth of SAT01 when applied together with phiST45. The genome sequences of the two phages have been deposited in the NCBI GenBank under accession numbers PP948674.1 and PP948675.1, respectively.

Host range determination

Three hundred microliters of each overnight bacterial culture was mixed with 4 mL of 0.4 % TSB soft agar and immediately overlaid onto a prepared Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; BD Difco, USA) agar plate (Khan Mirzaei and Nilsson, 2015). The plates were allowed to solidify at room temperature for approximately 5 min. Subsequently, 10 µL of phage suspension diluted to 10⁶ plaque-forming units (PFU)/mL was spotted onto the surface of the solidified overlay and incubated at 37°C for 24 h to assess lysis or plaque formation. Lytic activity was classified as clear lysis, turbid lysis, or no lysis based on the appearance of the spotted area. The combined host range of phiST45 and phiST56 was determined by considering a strain as covered when clear lysis was observed with at least one of the two phages. A total of 10 Salmonella strains were evaluated. Seven livestock-associated strains were obtained from the KVCC, including two pig-derived strains (BA1900016 and BA2000161) and five chicken-derived strains (BA1600008, BA0000422, BA0000423, BA0000474, and BA0000482). The remaining three strains were of human origin, consisting of SL1344, ATCC14028, and NCCP16207. A total of 61 pre-isolated Salmonella phages were screened, comprising 27 phages isolated using pig-derived Salmonella strains, 29 phages isolated using chicken-derived strains, and 5 phages obtained using human-derived strains.

Functional annotation and classification

Genomic DNA was extracted from DNase- and SDS-treated, filter-sterilized PEG-precipitated phage stocks using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK109) and sequenced on an R9.4.1 flow cell using the MinION Mk1B platform. Reads longer than 40,000 bp were assembled into complete phage genomes using Flye v2.9.1.

Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using RAST (Aziz, et al., 2008), Phanotate (McNair, et al., 2019), and Prodigal (Hyatt, et al., 2010), and ORFs detected by at least two programs were retained for further analysis. Predicted ORFs were functionally annotated using BLASTP searches against the NCBI non-redundant protein database. tRNA-encoding genes were identified using ARAGORN v1.2.41 (Laslett and Canback, 2004). Circular genome maps of phiST45 and phiST56 were generated using CGView based on the completed genome annotation results (Stothard and Wishart, 2005).

The taxonomic positions of phiST45 and phiST56 were determined using whole-genome-based comparative analyses. First, the complete genome sequences of each phage in FASTA format were uploaded to VIPTree to generate viral proteomic trees based on genome-wide sequence similarity. Closely related phages identified from the VIPTree analysis were used to infer the family-level taxonomic placement of phiST45 and phiST56 (Nishimura, et al., 2017). Subsequently, representative ICTV-classified phages belonging to the related genera were collected and analyzed together with phiST45 and phiST56 using VIRIDIC (Moraru, et al., 2020). Pairwise intergenomic similarity values generated by VIRIDIC were used to support genus-level classification.

One-step growth curve

One-step growth assays were performed to determine the latent period and burst size of phiST45 and phiST56. Briefly (Hyman and Abedon, 2009), the host bacterial culture was grown in TSB at 37°C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.2. Each phage was added at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 and allowed to adsorb for 5 min without shaking. After centrifugation and filtration to remove unadsorbed phages, samples were collected every 5 min and phage titers were determined by plaque assay.

Thermal and pH stability

For thermal stability analysis, phage suspensions were diluted to 1 × 10⁹ PFU/mL in SM buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgSO₄; pH 7.5) and incubated at different temperatures for 1 h. For pH stability analysis, phage suspensions were mixed with SM buffer adjusted to different pH values and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, viable phage titers were determined by plaque assay (Choi, et al., 2025).

Liquid inhibition assay

An overnight culture of SAT01 was inoculated at 1 % into fresh TSB medium and grown until reaching an OD₆₀₀ of 0.3 corresponding to approximately 1 × 10⁸ colony-forming units (CFU)/mL. The culture was then treated with either a single phage or a phage cocktail at an MOI of 1. All treatment groups were incubated at 37°C with shaking at 160 rpm, and optical density was measured every 3 hours to monitor growth inhibition (Shin, et al., 2011). The experiment was terminated when the OD₆₀₀ of all treatment groups exceeded 0.5.

Transmission electron microscopy

The lysate stocks of phiST45 and phiST56 were concentrated using polyethylene glycol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) precipitation and subsequently purified through CsCl gradient ultracentrifugation. The purified phages were dialyzed overnight in SM buffer and subsequently used for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis. Briefly (Bandara, et al., 2012), 10 μL of phage suspension diluted to 1 × 10⁹ PFU/mL was placed onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb for 5 min. The grid was then negatively stained with 5 μL of 2 % uranyl acetate for 5 s. Phage morphology was examined using EF-TEM (LIBRA 120, Carl Zeiss, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV and a magnification of 200,000 ×.

Deletion of phage receptor genes

To investigate the infection patterns of phiST45 and phiST56, the porin gene ompC and the TonB-dependent receptor gene btuB were deleted in SAT01 using the E. coli CRISPR/Cas9 system based on the pCas and pTargetT plasmids, following previously described protocols (Jiang, et al., 2015). PCR was performed using AccuPower® PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Korea), and amplified fragments were purified with the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

For construction of gene-specific editing plasmids, primers were designed to generate upstream and downstream homologous arms of approximately 300 bp and 500 bp, respectively, flanking each target gene. The two arms were fused via overlap PCR and ligated into XbaI/HindIII-digested pTargetF. The ligated plasmids were transformed into DH5α, and positive colonies were screened by colony PCR. A second round of cloning was then performed to insert the gene-specific N20 sequence, and correct insertion was validated through PCR and sequencing.

The finalized pTargetT-Δtarget plasmids were used for CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing in Salmonella following previously described methods. The primer sequences used for homologous arm construction, N20 insertion, and mutant verification are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The ΔbtuB, ΔompC, and wild-type strains were cultured separately and plated using the double-layer agar method to compare the PFU of phiST45 and phiST56.

Bacteriophage insensitive mutant frequency assay

The frequency of bacteriophage-insensitive mutant (BIM) formation was evaluated for phiST45, phiST56, and their cocktail. SAT01 overnight cultures were inoculated at 1 % into fresh medium and grown to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.2. The culture was then mixed with either individual phages or the cocktail at an approximate MOI of 100 and incubated statically at 37°C for 20 min to allow phage adsorption. The mixture was subsequently combined with 4 mL of TSB soft agar and poured onto TSA plates, followed by incubation at 37°C for 24 h. BIM frequency was calculated as the ratio of surviving colonies in the cocktail-treated group relative to those observed in each single-phage treatment (O'flynn, et al., 2006).

Food application

Overnight-grown SAT01 was inoculated into 5 mL of fresh TSB medium at 1 % and incubated until the culture reached an OD₆₀₀ of 0.3. The culture was then diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; LPS Solution, Korea) to adjust the final concentration to 1 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. Fresh eggs laid on the same day (local supermarket, Seoul) were aseptically separated into egg white and yolk. Each fraction (9 mL) was dispensed into sterile tubes, followed by inoculation with 1 mL of the diluted SAT01 suspension to achieve an initial bacterial concentration of 1 × 10⁴ CFU/mL. After gentle mixing at refrigeration temperature for 20 min, phiST45, phiST56, or a phage cocktail were added at an MOI of 1, and samples were incubated at either 4°C or 25°C. CFU enumeration was performed for 30 days under 4°C conditions and for 6 days at 25°C. At each sampling point, 1 mL of the sample was collected, appropriately diluted, and plated onto Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD; BD Difco, USA) agar plates to determine CFU (Yi, et al., 2021).

Animals and experimental design

All animal procedures followed institutional guidelines and were approved by the Jeonbuk National University IACUC (Approval No. JBNU 2022-091). A total of 100 one-day-old unsexed broiler chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery (Harim, Korea) and randomly assigned to five treatment groups, each consisting of five pens with four birds per pen. The brooding temperature was maintained at 34°C on day 1, reduced to 32°C by day 7, and further lowered to 28°C from day 8 until study completion. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. From day 1 to day 12, birds were fed a commercial starter diet (Happy Chick Starter, Korea), which, according to the manufacturer (as-fed basis), contained 19.5 % crude protein, 3.0 % crude fat, 5.5 % crude fiber, 8.0 % ash, 0.8 % calcium, 0.8 % methionine + cystine, 0.9 % phosphorus, and 2,920 kcal/kg metabolizable energy. Major ingredients included grains, oilseed meals, cereal by-products, meat and bone meal, limestone, dicalcium phosphate, amino acids, trace minerals, enzyme additives, vitamins, choline chloride, propionic acid, and added fat/molasses.

Five treatment groups were established: BL (non-infected control), SA (SAT01-infected), P1 (phiST45 pretreated + SAT01-infected), P2 (phiST56 pretreated + SAT01-infected), and PC (phage cocktail pretreated + SAT01-infected). All birds were euthanized on day 12 via cervical dislocation, and tissues and intestinal contents were collected. Body weights were recorded on days 1, 7, and 12, and feed intake and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for days 7-12. For analyses, one bird per pen (five per group) with median body weight was randomly selected at necropsy. Metagenomic analysis of cecal contents was performed for the BL, SA, and PC groups only.

qPCR-based quantification of Salmonella in intestinal contents

Approximately 500 mg of luminal content from the cecum, ileum, and jejunum was collected and homogenized using a bead-beating method. Total genomic DNA was then extracted from each sample with the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The relative abundance of Salmonella was quantified by SYBR Green–based quantitative PCR (qPCR), using the Salmonella invA gene as the target and the universal 16S rRNA gene as an internal reference for total bacterial load. Relative Salmonella levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. The primer pairs used were as follows: 16S rRNA gene (forward, 5′-CGGCAACGAGCGCAACCC-3′; reverse, 5′-CCATTGTAGCACGTGTGTAGCC-3′) and invA (forward, 5′-GCTGCTTTCTCTACTTAAC-3′; reverse, 5′-GTAATGGAATGACGAACAT-3′) (Heymans, et al., 2018).

Enumeration of Salmonella in liver and spleen

Liver and spleen tissues (0.1 g each) were aseptically collected from selected birds and homogenized in 1 mL of PBS using a bead-beating method. The homogenates were filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer to remove residual debris (Yu, et al., 2025). Filtrates were serially diluted in PBS and plated onto XLD agar. Plates were incubated at 37°C overnight, and colonies characteristic of Salmonella were enumerated to determine CFU per gram of tissue.

Histological assessment of distal ileum

Distal ileum tissues were collected and rinsed with PBS buffer. The samples were fixed in 4 % neutral-buffered formalin, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. Tissue sections were then prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin using standard histological procedures. Under light microscopy, villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured, and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio was calculated for each sample (Hashemitabar and Hosseinian, 2024).

Colonic tight junction integrity and cytokine expression

Large intestine tissues were collected from the region extending from the cloaca to the cecum. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized with the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the RealHelix™ [Green] qPCR Kit (Nanohelix, Korea) to quantify mRNA expression of tight junction proteins and cytokines. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Expression of inflammatory cytokine genes, including interleukin-1 beta (IL1B), interleukin-6 (IL6), interleukin-8 (IL8), interferon gamma (IFNG), and interleukin-10 (IL10), as well as tight junction–associated genes such as claudin-1 (CLDN1), claudin-4 (CLDN4), occludin (OCLN), zonula occludens-1 (TJP1), and mucin 2 (MUC2), was examined to evaluate inflammatory responses and epithelial barrier function (Heidari, et al., 2016; Song, et al., 2017). Primer sequences used in this analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Cecal microbiome analysis

Cecal contents from the BL, SA, and PC groups were collected for intestinal microbiome analysis using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Approximately 500 mg of each cecal sample was homogenized via bead-beating, and genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified using primers 515F (5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), which contain Illumina adapter overhang sequences. Amplicons were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq platform (Sanigen, Anyang, Korea) using a paired-end 2 × 300 bp strategy (Sergeant, et al., 2014).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.3.0). In general, differences among treatment groups were evaluated using two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. All in vitro experiments, including food-application assays, were conducted in triplicate unless otherwise specified. For the animal experiment, five biological replicates per treatment group were used unless stated otherwise, with one bird of median body weight selected from each of the five pens per group. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied for all analyses, and data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean.

Results

Genomic characterization of SLAM_SAT01 as a pathogenic and multidrug-resistant strain

The complete genome of SAT01 measured 4,793,452 bp and encoded 4,543 predicted coding sequences. Comprehensive genomic characterization confirmed the presence of SPI-1 and SPI-2, together with an extensive repertoire of invasion- and secretion-associated genes dispersed throughout the chromosome. Additional virulence- and adaptation-related loci, including mgtC, mgtB, ttrR, ttrS, ttrB, ttrC, and pagN, were also identified. Multiple type III secretion system effectors such as sopB, sopD, sopE2, sifA, and pipB, along with several fimbrial operons (e.g., fimA clusters), were detected. Genes implicated in intracellular survival and immune modulation (e.g., ompR, sodC), secretion systems (hcp), stress response (clpB, dnaK, ibpA/B, htpG), and toxin-antitoxin maintenance systems (relE-relB) were likewise present. Collectively, these genomic attributes demonstrate that SAT01 possesses a diverse and functionally rich array of virulence-associated determinants linked to epithelial invasion, intracellular persistence, and stress adaptation (Fig. 1A).

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing further confirmed the multidrug-resistant profile of SAT01. Disk diffusion assays showed that SAT01 was resistant to ampicillin, penicillin, imipenem, kanamycin, tetracycline, and vancomycin, whereas it was susceptible to chloramphenicol. Meropenem produced an inhibition zone of approximately 20 mm and was interpreted as intermediate according to the CLSI M100™ 35th edition criteria for Salmonella and Shigella spp. The inhibition zone diameters for imipenem and meropenem were approximately 14 mm and 20 mm, respectively. These phenotypic results support the classification of SAT01 as a multidrug-resistant S. Typhimurium strain and provide additional evidence for its reduced susceptibility to carbapenem antibiotics (Fig. 1B).

Genomic characterization and classification of SLAM_phiST45 and SLAM_phiST56

Whole-genome sequencing was performed to further characterize phiST45 and phiST56. The genome of phiST45 was 111,044 bp in length, with a G+C content of 40.1 %, and contained 130 predicted ORFs and 29 tRNA genes. Among the predicted ORFs, 55 ORFs were functionally annotated. The genome of phiST56 was 87,026 bp in length, with a G+C content of 38.8 %, and contained 104 predicted ORFs and 26 tRNA genes, of which 52 ORFs were functionally annotated. The annotated ORFs of both phages were categorized into functional modules, including structural proteins, lysis-related proteins, replication and metabolism proteins, packaging and assembly proteins, transcription regulation proteins, and hypothetical proteins. Genome annotation identified lysis-related genes in both phages, including genes encoding holin-, endolysin-, lysin-, and spanin-like proteins. In contrast, no lysogeny-related genes, such as integrase or repressor genes, were detected in either genome. In addition, no known virulence-associated genes or antibiotic resistance genes were identified (Supplementary Fig. 1A and B).

To clarify the taxonomic positions of the two phages, genome-based classification was performed using VIPTree and VIRIDIC. VIPTree analysis showed that phiST45 was most closely related to Salmonella phage SH9, which belongs to the genus Epseptimavirus. Therefore, phiST45 was further compared with 20 ICTV-classified Epseptimavirus phages using VIRIDIC. phiST45 showed the highest intergenomic similarity to Epseptimavirus S116, with 93.9 % similarity, indicating that phiST45 belongs to the genus Epseptimavirus but likely represents a distinct species within this genus. Similarly, VIPTree analysis showed that phiST56 was most closely related to Salmonella phage BPS17W1, a member of the genus Felixounavirus. VIRIDIC analysis with nine ICTV-classified Felixounavirus phages showed that phiST56 had the highest similarity to Felixounavirus D12, with 93.7 % intergenomic similarity, suggesting that phiST56 belongs to the genus Felixounavirus but may represent a distinct species (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B).

Functional and morphological characterization of SLAM_phiST45 and SLAM_phiST56 and their synergistic cocktail effects

Host range analysis was performed by screening 61 S. Typhimurium phages against ten Salmonella strains (Fig. 2A). PhiST45 produced clear lysis in eight of the ten strains, while the remaining two strains exhibited turbid lysis. In contrast, phiST56 showed strong lytic activity specifically against these two strains. Notably, SAT01, which was used as the challenge strain in the liquid inhibition, food application, and in vivo assays, was the original host bacterium of phiST45, whereas phiST56 showed relatively weak lytic activity against SAT01. Thus, phiST45 and phiST56 exhibited complementary lytic spectra against the tested Salmonella strains. When the host ranges of the two phages were combined, all ten tested strains were covered by at least one of the two phages. Based on these results, phiST45 and phiST56 were selected for subsequent cocktail experiments.

In the liquid inhibition assay, the growth of strain SAT01 was compared between the phiST45 single-treatment group and the phage cocktail group (Fig. 2B). PhiST45 alone effectively inhibited bacterial growth for up to 3 hours; however, regrowth occurred by 6 hours, with OD600 values exceeding 0.5. In contrast, the phage cocktail maintained significantly lower bacterial density for up to 15 hours compared with phiST45 alone (p < 0.0001).

TEM analysis revealed that phiST45 exhibited a siphovirus morphology, with a non-enveloped head (75 ± 6 nm in diameter, n = 8) and a long, non-contractile tail (182 ± 6 nm, n = 8) (Fig. 2C). PhiST56 displayed a myovirus morphology, characterized by a non-enveloped head (59 ± 7 nm in diameter, n = 9) and a contractile tail (115 ± 10 nm, n = 9) (Fig. 2D).

Successful deletion of the target receptor genes was confirmed by PCR before the receptor mutant infection assays. The ΔbtuB and ΔompC mutants showed PCR amplicon sizes distinct from those of the corresponding wild-type loci, confirming successful deletion of the target genes (Fig. 2E). In infection assays using receptor mutant strains, phiST45 showed no significant differences in PFU counts among the wild-type, ΔbtuB, and ΔompC strains (Fig. 2F). In contrast, phiST56 produced PFU levels comparable between the wild-type and ΔbtuB strains, but exhibited an approximately one-log reduction in the ΔompC strain (Fig. 2G).

The BIM assay showed that the BIM frequency for phiST45 was approximately 2.5-fold higher under single-phage treatment than under cocktail treatment, whereas phiST56 exhibited a roughly 12.5-fold higher BIM frequency when applied alone (Fig. 2H).

Biological characterization of SLAM_phiST45 and SLAM_phiST56

The thermal stability, pH stability, and one-step growth kinetics of phiST45 and phiST56 were evaluated. In the thermal stability assay, both phages remained stable from −20°C to 60°C, whereas phage titers decreased at 70°C and were not detected at 80°C. PhiST45 showed an approximately 3-log reduction at 70°C, while SLAM_phiST56 showed an approximately 1-log reduction (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B).

In the pH stability assay, both phages were stable over a broad pH range but were not detected at pH 2 and pH 13. At pH 3, phiST45 showed an approximately 1.5-log reduction, whereas phiST56 was largely maintained (Supplementary Fig. 3C and D).

One-step growth analysis showed that both phiST45 and phiST56 had a latent period of approximately 20 min. The estimated burst sizes of phiST45 and phiST56 were approximately 140 and 98 PFU/infected cell, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3E and F).

Evaluation of phage cocktail efficacy in egg white and egg yolk under different storage temperatures

Food application assays were conducted separately in egg white and egg yolk. Depending on storage temperature, Salmonella counts were measured every five days for 30 days at 4°C, and every two days for 6 days at 25°C. Across all experimental conditions, phiST56 (P2 group) showed minimal inhibitory activity against strain SAT01; therefore, the analysis focused on comparing phiST45 (P1 group) with the phage cocktail (PC group).

In egg white stored at 4°C, both P1 and PC groups exhibited more than a 2-log reduction relative to the SA group by day 5 (Fig. 3A). A significant difference between P1 and PC emerged from day 15 onward (p < 0.0001), and by the end of the experiment, no detectable Salmonella remained in the PC group. In egg yolk, both P1 and PC groups similarly showed more than a 2-log reduction versus the SA group by day 5 (Fig. 3B). From day 20, the PC group displayed significantly lower bacterial counts than the P1 group (p = 0.0035), and by day 30, most Salmonella cells were eliminated in the PC group.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a cocktail composed of two lytic phages, phiST45 and phiST56, exerts a pronounced synergistic effect in controlling Salmonella in poultry. Previous reports have already highlighted the advantages of phage cocktails; for instance, even at a relatively high MOI of 10⁴, single phages showed limited suppression of Salmonella in milk and lettuce, whereas cocktail treatment achieved significant and durable inhibition (Chen, Wang, Zhang, Zhang, Liu, Zhao, Li, Hu, Lv, Luo, Xu, Petersen, Cai, Sicheritz-Pontén, Clokie and Liu, 2025). In contrast to these earlier studies, our work shows that the phiST45 + phiST56 cocktail markedly outperformed individual phages even at an exceptionally low MOI of 1, achieving rapid suppression of S. Typhimurium and completely eliminating detectable bacteria under refrigerated conditions. Although a broad MOI optimization assay was not performed, MOI of 1 was selected because clear single-phage activity and synergistic cocktail-mediated suppression were observed at this relatively low initial phage input. Future studies comparing a wider range of MOIs and dosing regimens will be necessary to define the optimal application dose under food matrix and in vivo conditions. To provide mechanistic evidence for this synergy, we generated mutant strains of the phiST45 host bacterium SAT01 by knocking out either the butB or ompC genes and compared their PFU titers with those of the wild-type strain (Kim and Ryu, 2011; Parent, et al., 2014). The results revealed that phiST56 utilizes ompC as a co-receptor and displays an infection pattern distinct from that of phiST45. BIM analyses further demonstrated that combined treatment significantly reduced the emergence of phage-resistant mutants, providing additional support for the synergistic interaction between the two phages. These findings align with previous studies showing that cocktails composed of phages targeting diverse host receptors and those yielding low BIM frequencies tend to exhibit enhanced bacterial suppression (Bai, et al., 2019; O'flynn, et al., 2004; Shin, et al., 2012b).

S. Typhimurium is a zoonotic serovar with an exceptionally broad host range, infecting humans, poultry, and swine (Mun, et al., 2025; Song, et al., 2025a, 2025b). Thus, phages effective in chickens may hold promise for cross-species application. However, in our host-range assays involving 61 isolated Salmonella-infecting phages tested against 10 S. Typhimurium strains of poultry, swine, and human origin, we observed that host-range patterns clustered strongly by species, with swine and human strains grouping together and poultry strains forming a separate cluster. This separation may be explained by host source-associated differences in bacterial surface structures involved in phage adsorption. Even within the same S. Typhimurium serovar, variations in phage receptors, such as LPS/O-antigen structures, outer membrane proteins, and flagella, can influence phage adsorption and host specificity (Kim and Ryu, 2012; Shin, et al., 2012a). Therefore, the distinct clustering of poultry-derived strains may reflect strain-level receptor variation shaped by host-associated adaptation, although the precise molecular basis requires further investigation. Notably, among phages infecting swine- or human-derived bacteria, none could clearly lyse more than three of the five poultry-derived strains. This variability in host range, even within the same serovar, is consistent with previous observations regarding the host specificity of lactic acid bacteria (Johnson, et al., 2023; Keresztény, et al., 2024; Li, et al., 2024; Ren, et al., 2019). Nonetheless, the two phages selected for this study, phiST45 and phiST56, both originally isolated using chicken-derived strains, exhibited remarkably broad host ranges, successfully lysing all swine- and human-derived S. Typhimurium strains tested. Such cross-species activity holds substantial practical value, given the frequent interspecies transmission of Salmonella. In swine production systems, reducing intestinal colonization is critical for minimizing contamination during slaughter and distribution (Wall, et al., 2010). Previous studies have reported that phage treatment significantly decreases Salmonella shedding in pigs, and our results suggest that prophylactic phage administration in poultry may yield comparable benefits (Thanki, et al., 2023).

One of the most important considerations in antimicrobial strategies is whether treatment perturbs the normal gut microbiota (Pandey, et al., 2024; Zhong, et al., 2025). In this study, the phage cocktail selectively targeted Salmonella while largely preserving overall microbial diversity and community structure, closely resembling that of the uninfected control group. Notably, however, two genera with relatively low abundances (1–4 % of the total community), Eisenbergiella and Anaerotruncus, were significantly reduced in the PC group compared with the SA group. These shifts were not associated with any pathological outcomes and instead appear to represent an ecological adjustment occurring as the microbiota re-stabilizes following pathogen clearance. Although these taxa accounted for only a small fraction of the total community, their reduction may still be biologically relevant because low-abundance bacteria can contribute to specific metabolic or ecological functions within the gut ecosystem. In previous poultry studies, Anaerotruncus has been described as a butyrate-producing bacteria involved in maintaining intestinal energy balance and mucosal homeostasis, and its abundance was shown to increase following dietary prebiotic supplementation (Gao, et al., 2022). Likewise, Eisenbergiella has been reported to rise after Bacillus subtilis probiotic administration and has been linked to improved feed efficiency and reduced feed conversion ratios (Gong, et al., 2021; Jeong, et al., 2025). Although no overgrowth of harmful non-target bacteria or overt dysbiosis was observed, the microbiota did not fully revert to the level of the blank control, and subtle alterations were detected in low-abundance genera with potential beneficial roles. These findings suggest that phage treatment may induce minor unintended shifts in community composition. Such observations diverge from earlier claims that phages exert virtually no off-target microbiome effects and underscore the need for more refined investigations into phage-microbiota interactions.

Beyond pathogen reduction, the phage cocktail substantially improved host health outcomes. By day 5 post-infection, SA group exhibited marked reductions in body weight and feed intake, whereas PC group maintained growth performance comparable to BL group. FCR also worsened only in the SA group, while PC birds retained normal efficiency. These improvements mirrored reductions in Salmonella colonization both in the intestine and systemically, as PC birds showed significantly lower bacterial loads in the ileum than the single-phage groups, and dissemination to the spleen and liver was suppressed only in the PC group (Zhang, et al., 2012). Histopathological and molecular analyses further underscored the protective effects of the phage cocktail. The SA group exhibited classic Salmonella-associated lesions, including villus blunting, villus fusion, and crypt abnormalities, whereas PC birds retained intestinal morphology similar to healthy controls (Shao, et al., 2013). Pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL1B, IL6, IL8, IFNG) were elevated in the SA group but returned to baseline in PC birds. Conversely, genes related to intestinal barrier integrity, including IL10, MUC2, and TJP1, were highest in the PC group, with TJP1 and MUC2 showing significantly elevated expression compared even with the blank group. These results indicate that the cocktail effectively mitigated inflammation and preserved mucosal barrier function (Criado-Mesas, et al., 2021; Duangnumsawang, et al., 2022).

Collectively, the phiST45 + phiST56 cocktail exhibited clear synergistic advantages over single-phage treatment, demonstrating excellence in pathogen clearance, tissue protection, immune modulation, and microbiota stability. These findings strongly support the use of phage cocktails as a safe and practical biocontrol strategy for managing Salmonella in poultry production. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vivo challenge employed a single MDR S. Typhimurium strain, and further evaluation against additional S. Typhimurium field isolates, other Salmonella serovars, and diverse poultry-associated isolates is required. Second, although receptor involvement was inferred, the precise receptor-binding mechanisms of both phages remain to be fully elucidated. Combining phages with distinct receptor targets is expected to delay resistance development, but confirmation will require detailed genetic and biochemical analyses. Third, our assessment of food safety applications focused solely on liquid environments, and follow-up studies should examine efficacy on solid matrices such as chicken skin or eggshell surfaces. Nevertheless, previous poultry studies have shown that phage treatment can reduce recoverable Salmonella from poultry products and carcass rinse samples (Higgins, et al., 2005), and that phage administration through drinking water can reduce Salmonella shedding and eggshell surface contamination in laying hens (Thanki, et al., 2026). Consistent with these findings, our in vivo experiment showed that prophylactic oral administration of the phiST45 + phiST56 cocktail significantly reduced Salmonella levels in the cecum, ileum, jejunum, spleen, and liver after oral challenge. In addition, both phages remained stable at −20°C and up to 60°C for 1 h, providing preliminary information on their tolerance to freezing and moderate heat exposure. However, direct validation on solid matrices and under industrial processing conditions remains necessary. Fourth, microbiome analysis was focused on the BL, SA, and PC groups because these groups represented the key biological comparison needed to evaluate whether Salmonella-induced microbiota disruption could be restored by the optimized phage cocktail. The single-phage groups were not included in the microbiome analysis because the primary objective was to assess the microbiota-level impact of the final cocktail formulation, which showed superior pathogen reduction and host protection compared with individual phages. However, future studies comparing the microbiome effects of single-phage and cocktail treatments would provide additional insight into phage-specific ecological effects. In addition, large-scale application in poultry farms will require optimization of phage formulation, storage stability, delivery through drinking water or feed, dosing schedules, and application frequency under commercial farm conditions. Therefore, future field-scale validation is necessary before practical implementation. Despite these limitations, the present study, together with existing literature, provides compelling evidence that phage cocktails represent a promising tool for controlling MDR Salmonella in livestock industries.

Conclusion

This study shows that the phiST45 + phiST56 phage cocktail is a highly effective approach for controlling S. Typhimurium in poultry. Host-range assays across 61 phages and 10 strains revealed two major species-associated clusters (swine–human vs. poultry), and the two phages used here uniquely spanned both groups. Their complementary receptor usage and reduced BIM frequencies further confirmed a genuine synergistic interaction. The cocktail also achieved rapid and complete pathogen suppression at an exceptionally low MOI of 1, outperforming previous phage applications that required high titers. Although overall gut microbiota structure remained largely stable, subtle shifts in low-abundance taxa indicated that phage therapy may introduce minor, unintended alterations. Overall, the phiST45 + phiST56 cocktail effectively eliminated Salmonella in both food matrices and live birds while improving host health and minimizing resistance development. These findings highlight its strong potential as a practical biocontrol tool in poultry production, while underscoring the need to continue monitoring microbiome-level impacts.

Data Availability Statement

Whole-genome sequencing data for S. Typhimurium SLAM_SAT01, SLAM_phiST45, and SLAM_phiST56 have been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under accession numbers JBSKFX000000000, PP948674.1, and PP948675.1, respectively. The 16S rRNA gene sequencing data generated from cecal content samples of the three groups (BL, SA, and PC) have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession number PRJNA1291055. These datasets are publicly available through the NCBI database.
