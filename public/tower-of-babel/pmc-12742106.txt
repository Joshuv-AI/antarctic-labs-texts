Comparative efficacy and acceptability of non-invasive brain stimulation and acupuncture for depression: protocol for a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Yadi Li, Xia He, Xinmin Deng, Jianlong Zhou, Min Shi, Yanyan Tian, Zhuoling Li, Dongdong Yang, Xuhong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepression is a significant global health burden, affecting over 300 million people worldwide and contributing substantially to disability and healthcare costs. Despite the widespread use of antidepressants, many patients experience limited efficacy or adverse effects. Non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) and acupuncture have emerged as promising non-pharmacological interventions for depression. However, the heterogeneity of treatment protocols and the lack of direct comparisons limit the development of optimal treatment strategies. This study seeks to perform a network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare and rank the comparative efficacy and acceptability of NIBS and acupuncture interventions for depression. This review is expected to inform evidence-based decision-making by comparatively evaluating efficacy and acceptability; any clinical implications will depend on the certainty and consistency of the evidence.Methods and analysisA comprehensive literature search will be conducted across 14 academic databases and registries (PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Embase, OVID, Scopus, ProQuest, CNKI, Wanfang, CBM, VIP, ICTRP, ChiCTR and ClinicalTrials.gov) from inception to May 2025. Eligible studies will include randomised controlled trials assessing NIBS and/or acupuncture for depression. Two reviewers will independently conduct study selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Primary outcomes will include depressive symptom severity and acceptability (measured by all-cause discontinuation). Secondary outcomes will include response rate and adverse events. At the time of protocol submission, the literature search had been substantially completed, while study selection and data extraction had not yet commenced. Pairwise meta-analyses will be executed applying Review Manager V.5.4 and Stata V.17.0. A Bayesian NMA will be conducted via the GeMTC package in R V.4.2.2, and Stata will also be used for additional statistical analyses and visualisation. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses will explore sources of heterogeneity, and the certainty of evidence will be appraised using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not warranted since the study does not involve any confidential or private patient records. The results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420250651706.

Introduction

Depression is a pervasive and debilitating mental health disorder that profoundly impairs quality of life and imposes a substantial burden on society, individuals, families and society.13 Worldwide, approximately 322 million people, representing 4.4% of the global population, are affected,4 making depression the leading cause of disability even before the COVID-19 pandemic.57 During 2020, the pandemic exacerbated this public health crisis, with major depressive disorder cases surging by an estimated 27.6% due to social isolation, economic hardship and disruptions in healthcare delivery.8 Beyond its profound human cost, depression exacts a toll exceeding $1 trillion annually in lost productivity and healthcare expenditures, driven by higher unemployment, increased disability claims and reduced workplace performance.9 Moreover, over 10% of individuals with depression experience suicidal ideation, and lifetime suicide rates among this population range from 3.8% to 7.8%.10 11

Currently, first-line treatments for depression depend largely on antidepressant medications, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs).12 Although these agents are effective for many patients, up to one-third fail to achieve adequate symptom relief, and a substantial number discontinue therapy because of adverse effects such as sexual dysfunction, weight gain and gastrointestinal upset.1316 Such challenges have prompted growing interest in non-pharmacological interventions that may improve clinical outcomes while offering a favourable tolerability profile. Among these, non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) and acupuncture have gained prominence as two promising alternatives, offering distinct mechanistic advantages.

NIBS techniques, including transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and related modalities, demonstrate both efficacy and excellent tolerability in the treatment of depression.1724 By safely modulating cortical excitability, these interventions induce durable changes in synaptic plasticity2527 and normalise activity within brain circuits implicated in mood regulation,28 29 effects that correlate with significant reductions in depressive symptoms.

Parallel to these advancements, acupuncture has been increasingly investigated as a non-pharmacological approach for depression, particularly in regions where it is widely practised. Some clinical trials have reported symptomatic improvements and suggested fewer adverse events compared with pharmacotherapy.3034 Mechanistically, acupuncture is thought to exert its antidepressant effects through multiple pathways, including modulating central monoaminergic neurotransmission (serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine),3538 reducing stress responses,3941 suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines4244 and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis hyperactivity,4547 and enhancing BDNF-mediated neuroplasticity.4850 Taken together, these findings highlight acupuncture’s potential relevance as a non-pharmacological intervention for depression, warranting further rigorous investigation to clarify its efficacy and safety.

Despite their different modes of action, growing evidence suggests that both NIBS and acupuncture may alleviate depressive symptoms through overlapping final common pathways, including modulation of monoaminergic neurotransmission,353851 enhancement of neuroplasticity252748 and regulation of brain network activity,28 29 54 55 etc. This converging biological plausibility provides a rationale for their joint evaluation within a unified framework. Furthermore, from a clinical perspective, both NIBS and acupuncture are frequently considered comparable non-pharmacological options, especially for patients who exhibit intolerance or inadequate response to conventional antidepressant medications. Thus, directly comparing their efficacy and acceptability addresses a pressing need in clinical decision-making.

Although accumulating evidence suggests the potential efficacy of both NIBS and acupuncture for depression, no high-quality systematic review yet directly compared these two interventions or their combined effects within a unified analytical framework. Moreover, the lack of head-to-head or integrated data across these non-pharmacological treatments has left decision-makers without strong evidence to guide the selection of the most effective and acceptable options. Network meta-analysis (NMA), recognised as an advanced evidence synthesis methodology in clinical guidelines,56 fills this gap by synthesising both direct and indirect evidence through an observational approach that enables the simultaneous comparison and ranking of multiple interventions across trials.57 58 By pooling data from a broad range of randomised controlled trials (RCTs), NMA provides enhanced statistical power and a more integrated assessment of relative treatment effectiveness and tolerability compared with traditional pairwise meta-analyses, though its conclusions depend on the validity of transitivity and consistency assumptions.

Given these gaps in existing literature and the rising importance of non-pharmacological interventions, this protocol outlines a systematic review and NMA to compare and rank the effectiveness and acceptability of NIBS and acupuncture in treating depression. By synthesising evidence from RCTs and rigorously addressing study heterogeneity, this study aims to generate a hierarchically organised evidence base that will guide clinicians and decision-makers in selecting the most effective and well-tolerated non-pharmacological interventions for depressive disorders.

Search strategy

The search strategy will be methodically designed to ensure comprehensiveness and reproducibility, adhering to the standards outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions,60 and reported according to the PRISMA-NMA guidelines.59

Electronic searches will be independently conducted by two reviewers (YL and XH) from the inception to May 2025. The search will cover both English-language (PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Embase, OVID, Scopus and ProQuest) and Chinese-language (CNKI, Wanfang, CBM and VIP) databases. In addition, registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov, International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) and Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) will be examined to locate ongoing or unpublished trials. No restrictions will be imposed regarding country or publication type.

The search strategy will include terms related to depression (eg, ‘depression,’ ‘depressive disorder’), NIBS (eg, ‘non-invasive brain stimulation,’ ‘repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation,’ ‘transcranial direct current stimulation,’ ‘transcranial alternating current stimulation’) and acupuncture (eg, ‘acupuncture therapy,’ ‘manual acupuncture,’ ‘electroacupuncture’). Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms will be combined to guarantee a thorough search.

Search strategies will be tailored to each database, with representative examples provided in online supplemental appendix 1. When full-text versions of studies are unavailable, we shall reach out to the first or corresponding author to obtain the complete manuscript.

At the time of protocol submission, the literature search had been substantially completed across all planned databases. However, study selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessment had not yet commenced. These steps will be conducted in accordance with the methods described in this protocol.

Study selection

Two reviewers (YL and XD) will screen titles and abstracts of all retrieved records separately, according to the predefined inclusion criteria. Studies not clearly aligning with the predefined criteria will be excluded. If both reviewers independently assess a study as potentially eligible based on title and abstract, the full text will be retrieved for further evaluation.

For the title and abstract screening, studies will be screened based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria defined in the protocol. Any studies that do not clearly meet the criteria will be excluded. Only studies that meet the inclusion criteria at this stage will proceed to full-text review.

For full-text screening, if both reviewers independently assess the full-text study as meeting the inclusion criteria, the study will be included in the review. Studies not meeting the inclusion criteria after full-text review will be excluded. Discrepancies between reviewers will be addressed via deliberation. If consensus remains unattainable, the third reviewer (XH) will be engaged to mediate. In cases where disagreement persists, a consensus meeting will be held with all team members, where each reviewer will present their rationale. Final decisions will be made based on group consensus and documented accordingly. Study records

To ensure the reliability of included studies, we will carefully examine potential integrity issues such as data fabrication, duplicate publications and retracted articles. The identification process will involve cross-referencing trial registrations (eg, ClinicalTrials.gov, ICTRP) with published reports to detect discrepancies, searching for retractions or errata in trusted databases (eg, PubMed, Retraction Watch), and using tools like CrossRef and PubMed to identify duplicate publications.

This approach will ensure that only the most reliable and representative data is included in the review.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies will be independently evaluated by two reviewers (XD and MS) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool, with systematic adjudication of risk judgments across its five pre-specified domains: (1) randomisation process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of the outcome and (5) selection of the reported result.79

Risk of bias assessment will be conducted at the study level, meaning each study will receive an overall risk-of-bias judgement. This approach represents a pragmatic choice, as the domains most critical to a trial’s internal validity (eg, the randomization process and missing outcome data) are typically inherent to the overall study design. Consequently, a study-level judgement coherently captures the foundational integrity of the evidence that each study contributes to the NMA.

Based on these domain-specific assessments, each study will subsequently be assigned an overall risk-of-bias judgement of ‘low risk of bias’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk of bias’. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer (YT) will be consulted to help reach an agreement. All risk of bias judgments, along with the supporting justifications and any resolution of discrepancies, will be documented in detail in the data extraction form to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Data synthesis

Pairwise meta-analysis

Pairwise meta-analyses will be executed applying Review Manager (RevMan) V.5.4 and Stata V.17.0 software. Comparisons will be conducted across studies sharing similar interventions and outcome measures. For dichotomous outcomes (eg, response rate), ORs with 95% CIs will be computed. For continuous outcomes (eg, HAMD scores), standardised mean differences (SMD) or mean differences (MD) will be applied, contingent on the reported outcome metrics. Statistical significance is set at p<0.05.

Heterogeneity across studies will be evaluated using both the I² statistic and the Q test. The choice between a fixed-effect or random-effects model will depend on the extent of heterogeneity: if I² <50% and p>0.10, a fixed-effect model will be applied; otherwise, a random-effects model will be used to account for variability among studies.80 81

Network meta-analysis

A Bayesian NMA will be implemented using the GeMTC package (R V.4.2.2) via the Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling framework. This approach is chosen because it enables estimation of the full posterior distribution of treatment effects, allows incorporation of prior information when appropriate and provides probabilities of treatment rankings, making it particularly suitable for complex networks with sparse direct comparisons by combining both direct and indirect evidence in a coherent framework.

Intervention nodes will be defined according to intervention modality. NIBS will be represented by separate nodes for TMS, rTMS, tDCS, tACS and TBS, rather than being aggregated into a single ‘NIBS’ category. Acupuncture will be represented by separate nodes for manual acupuncture, electroacupuncture, scalp acupuncture and auricular acupuncture. Combination therapies will be assigned to dedicated nodes (eg, rTMS and manual acupuncture).

In the network, control conditions will be treated as distinct nodes and will not be merged a priori: (1) sham NIBS, (2) placebo acupuncture and (3) treatment as usual/conventional care (TAU). Where available, TAU will serve as the preferred common comparator to enhance transitivity across trials. However, we acknowledge that the content of TAU may vary across studies, potentially impacting the assumption of transitivity. To evaluate and mitigate this, we will extract detailed descriptions of TAU interventions and implement sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. We will also prespecify sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of findings if sham NIBS and placebo acupuncture are combined vs kept separate. Active pharmacological or psychotherapeutic comparators (eg, SSRIs/SNRIs, CBT) will be represented as independent nodes; add-on designs (eg, rTMS and SSRI compared with SSRI alone) will be modelled with explicit ‘add-on’ nodes as noted above.

The NMA will assess both dichotomous and continuous outcomes. Dichotomous data will be summarised as OR, and continuous data as SMD or MD, both with 95% CI. For multiple-arm trials, all eligible arms will be included, and the correlation between effect estimates within the same study will be accounted for in the Bayesian arm-based model to avoid unit-of-analysis errors. Where multiple reports of the same trial are identified, data will be merged, prioritising the most comprehensive or recent report, and discrepancies will be resolved by consulting trial registries or contacting the original authors for clarification.

For all primary analyses, the network will be fitted under a random-effects consistency model, given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across trials. A fixed-effect NMA will be conducted only as a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of the results.

To comprehensively evaluate the validity of NMA assumptions, we will rigorously assess the following:

Transitivity

In an NMA, transitivity refers to the assumption that the distribution of potential effect modifiers is similar across treatment comparisons, ensuring the validity of indirect comparisons.82 To proactively protect this assumption, we designed the network with clinically and methodologically coherent node definitions, including distinct nodes for each intervention modality (eg, rTMS, tDCS, manual acupuncture, electroacupuncture) and, critically, for each type of control condition (eg, sham NIBS, placebo acupuncture). This strategy avoids the invalid presumption of equivalence between controls with different mechanisms and participant expectations, thereby ensuring that all indirect comparisons are routed through clinically plausible pathways. To evaluate the transitivity assumption, we will compare the distribution of prespecified effect modifiers (eg, age, baseline severity of depression, treatment duration) across all treatment comparisons. Continuous variables will be assessed using ANOVA and categorical variables using χ2 tests. Imbalance will be considered meaningful if the SMD exceeds 0.1 or if p<0.05. A balanced distribution will be interpreted as no evidence of intransitivity.

Inconsistency

Inconsistency in NMA describes a statistical disagreement between direct and indirect evidence for the same treatment comparison within the network.83 We will evaluate inconsistency using both local and global approaches. Locally, we will employ the node-splitting method to compare effect estimates from direct and indirect evidence for each comparison84; a p value <0.05 will indicate substantial inconsistency. Globally, we will use a design-by-treatment interaction model to test for overall inconsistency across the network.

Heterogeneity

In the context of an NMA, heterogeneity refers to variation in treatment effects between studies that compare the same pair of interventions.85 This variability may arise from clinical diversity (eg, differences in patient populations, intervention protocols or outcome measurements) or methodological diversity (eg, differences in study quality or design). A Bayesian random-effects model will be employed for all primary analyses to explicitly account for the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity, particularly between NIBS and acupuncture trials. We will quantify between-study heterogeneity using the between-study variance (τ²) and its 95% prediction intervals for each pairwise comparison. Standard pairwise meta-analyses and forest plots will be generated for all possible direct treatment comparisons to visualise effect sizes and assess heterogeneity.

If violations of any assumptions are detected, we will conduct sensitivity or subgroup analyses to identify potential sources and determine the robustness of our findings. Specifically, we will perform a pre-specified sensitivity analysis to examine the impact of our approach to control conditions by testing an alternative model where ‘sham NIBS’ and ‘placebo acupuncture’ are merged into a single node.

To assess model convergence, the Potential Scale Reduction Factor will be monitored; values converging near 1 imply sufficient convergence. Model fit will be determined using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), and the model with the smaller DIC will be selected.

To rank the comparative efficacy and acceptability of different interventions, the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) will be calculated.86 A higher SUCRA value indicates a greater probability that an intervention is among the most effective or acceptable. In line with guidance from the Cochrane Handbook, treatment ranking metrics will be interpreted as probabilistic summaries rather than definitive clinical hierarchies. Rankings can be influenced by the amount and quality of evidence, risk of bias, between-study heterogeneity and network incoherence. Accordingly, rankings will be presented alongside relative effect estimates and their uncertainty and considered together with certainty-of-evidence assessments, rather than used as stand-alone indicators of superiority.

Stata V.17.0 will complement the Bayesian analysis through a six-step quantitative workflow: (1) evidence network visualisation using ‘network plot’ with edge weights scaled by inverse variance to map all direct comparisons; (2) global inconsistency testing for closed loops via design-by-treatment interaction models with Wald test (α=0.10), to identify discrepancies between direct and indirect evidence; (3) relative effects synthesis using ‘network forest’ to generate forest plots with pooled OR/SMD and 95% CI; (4) league table presentation via ‘network league’ to output heatmap-style matrices of pairwise comparisons; (5) treatment ranking with ‘network rank’ to compute SUCRA values and (6) publication bias assessment through ‘network funnel’ with contour-enhanced funnel plots and Egger’s test for asymmetry. All outputs will be cross-verified against R/GeMTC results to ensure robustness.

Publication bias and small-study effects will be assessed using funnel plots when ten or more studies are available for an outcome.87 Statistical tests such as Egger’s and Begg’s will be applied to examine funnel plot asymmetry. Additionally, a network evidence map will be constructed to visualise the strength, distribution and connectivity of the evidence across interventions.

The entire review and NMA process will adhere to the PRISMA-NMA guideline to ensure transparency, reproducibility and methodological rigour.

Handling of a disconnected network

A recognised methodological challenge in NMA is the risk of a disconnected network, which arises when interventions form isolated clusters without common comparators, preventing indirect estimation of relative effects.88 To mitigate this risk, our inclusion of frequently used common comparators (such as sham NIBS, placebo acupuncture and TAU) is anticipated to serve as connecting hubs, thereby maximising network connectivity.

In the event that a disconnected network persists, we will focus on the largest connected component for the main analysis, using the most robust set of interventions with available comparisons. For interventions in disconnected components, we will conduct separate pairwise meta-analyses, providing quantitative estimates where possible. If no statistical comparisons are feasible, we will employ a narrative synthesis to compare findings from disconnected components qualitatively, addressing clinical and methodological similarities and differences.

Additionally, sensitivity analyses will be performed by sequentially removing the weakest bridging links to assess the stability of indirect estimates and the impact of disconnected components on overall conclusions. This approach ensures a comprehensive synthesis of evidence, even when full statistical integration of all interventions is not possible.

Additional analyses

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis will be conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates, particularly for outcomes exhibiting moderate to high heterogeneity (eg, I² >30%). This will involve comparing the results obtained from random-effects and fixed-effect models. If the number of included studies permits, further sensitivity analyses will exclude high-risk-of-bias studies to assess the influence of methodological quality on the overall findings.

Subgroup analysis

In an NMA, the validity of the transitivity assumption requires that participants across different trials are jointly randomisable. To evaluate this, we will conduct subgroup analyses on outcomes exhibiting substantial inconsistency to explore underlying causes of heterogeneity. Subgroup stratifications will be based on prespecified clinical and methodological characteristics, including participant gender (male vs female), age group (young, middle-aged, elderly), severity of depression (mild, moderate, severe), patient setting (inpatients vs outpatients), type of intervention (eg, rTMS, tDCS, manual acupuncture, electroacupuncture), treatment duration (eg, 4 weeks, 8 weeks) and treatment frequency (eg, daily, every other day, three times per week). Where data permit, we will further apply network meta-regression to formally test the impact of these variables. These analyses aim to clarify whether treatment effects differ across subpopulations or intervention protocols, thereby strengthening the assessment of transitivity and the robustness of our conclusions.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence will be evaluated using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework (https://cinema.ispm.unibe.ch/), which adapts the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation methodology specifically for NMA.8991 CINeMA assesses evidence confidence by examining six key domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence. Based on these assessments, the overall evidence quality will be classified as high, moderate, low or very low.

Patient and public involvement

This protocol does not involve patient or public participation in its development.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not warranted since the study does not involve any confidential or private patient records. The results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

Depression presents a significant burden on individuals and healthcare systems due to its high prevalence, recurrent course and association with functional impairment.9294 Despite the widespread use of conventional therapies, including antidepressant medications, a substantial portion of patients either fails to achieve an adequate therapeutic response or encounters severe adverse effects, which hinder long-term adherence.1316 In light of these challenges, non-pharmacological interventions such as NIBS and acupuncture have emerged as promising alternatives, demonstrating effectiveness and good tolerability in managing depression.172430 However, the lack of direct comparative studies between these modalities complicates the formulation of clear clinical guidelines and recommendations for treatment selection.

This protocol is designed to systematically review the existing literature and apply NMA to evaluate and rank the comparative efficacy and acceptability of various NIBS modalities and acupuncture in the treatment of depression. By synthesising direct and indirect evidence from RCTs, this review is expected to yield comparative estimates and probabilistic rankings that may support clinical decision-making, contingent on the certainty and coherence of the network. The results are expected to help clinicians better understand the relative benefits of different non-pharmacological treatments, thus guiding the development of personalised therapeutic strategies.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, limiting the search to English- and Chinese-language publications may introduce language bias, potentially narrowing the scope of the evidence and excluding relevant studies published in other languages. Future reviews should consider including studies published in multiple languages to ensure a more comprehensive analysis. Second, the exclusion of grey literature may have introduced publication bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of treatment effects, as this literature often includes null or negative findings. This decision was a trade-off made to prioritise the methodological detail and rigour of peer-reviewed publications. Third, studies with incomplete outcome data or insufficient methodological details were excluded. Although contacting the original investigators for missing information is a recommended practice to minimise selection bias, this was not practically feasible within the scope and timeline of the present review. As a result, some potentially eligible studies may have been omitted, which could affect the comprehensiveness of our findings. Future research would benefit from undertaking such efforts to obtain unpublished data and mitigate this risk. Fourth, clinical heterogeneity may arise from variations in acupuncture and NIBS protocols, such as differences in stimulation parameters, treatment duration, frequency and techniques, which may affect the comparability of interventions. Additionally, while SUCRA and related ranking measures offer a convenient overview of the relative performance of interventions, they have important limitations. Rankings are model-based probabilities that may be unstable when evidence is sparse or inconsistent, and they are sensitive to heterogeneity, incoherence and risk of bias across the network. Consequently, rankings should not be over-interpreted as definitive ‘best-worst’ lists; rather, conclusions will be based primarily on relative effect sizes, uncertainty (CIs/CrIs) and the certainty of evidence, with rankings used only as complementary summaries.
