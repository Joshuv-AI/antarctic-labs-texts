Optimal local basis truncation of lattice quantum many-body systems
Peter Majcen, Giovanni Cataldi, Pietro Silvi, Simone Montangero

Abstract
We show how to optimally reduce the local Hilbert basis of lattice quantum many-body (QMB) Hamiltonians. The basis truncation exploits the most relevant eigenvalues of the estimated single-site reduced density matrix (RDM). It is accurate and numerically stable across different model phases, even close to quantum phase transitions. We apply this procedure to different models, such as the Sine-Gordon model, the φ ^ 4 \hat{\varphi}^{4} theory, and lattice gauge theories, namely Abelian U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) and non-Abelian SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) , in one and two spatial dimensions. Our results reduce state-of-the-art estimates of computational resources for classical and quantum simulations.



In the study of QMB systems, the dimension
d
of the single-site local Hilbert space plays a crucial role in determining the computational cost and feasibility of both classical and quantum simulations.
Although paradigmatic spin models such as the Ising or Heisenberg Hamiltonians can operate with a minimal local basis
d\!=\!2
, most physically relevant systems exhibit large or unbounded local spaces arising from different physical mechanisms.
In these scenarios, basis truncation strategies become crucial for enabling numerical and experimental simulations.

From a computational perspective, an increase in the local dimension directly affects memory requirements and algorithmic complexity
Eisert 2013
.
In the realm of tensor network (TN) algorithms
Orús 2014
;
Montangero 2018
, such as Matrix Product States (MPS)
Schuch et al. 2008
;
Schollwöck 2011
;
Paeckel et al. 2019
, Projected Entangled Pair States (PEPS)
Verstraete et al. 2006
;
Schuch et al. 2007
;
Verstraete et al. 2008
;
Corboz et al. 2018
;
Cirac et al. 2021
;
Vanderstraeten et al. 2022
, or Tree Tensor Networks (TTN)
Silvi et al. 2019
, the cost of tensor storage and contraction scales with the local dimension, often quadratically or worse.
These challenges are not limited to classical simulations.
On quantum hardware, while qudit-based quantum processors have significantly improved in gate fidelity and scalability
Ringbauer et al. 2022
, a large local dimension
d
would require as many transitions between the internal levels, which are hard to engineer and not always available.
Alternatively, encoding large local Hilbert spaces into qubits substantially increases the computational cost by extending interaction ranges and introducing genuine many-body terms into the Hamiltonian
Setia et al. 2019
, which results in deeper circuit depths.

This work proposes a general and adaptive truncation scheme based on single-site RDM estimation, which naturally identifies the most relevant local states from the QMB ground-state or low-lying eigenstates.
By construction, the resulting local basis is optimal under local transformations.
This provides a fundamental pre-processing step for efficient classical or quantum simulations.

Large local Hilbert spaces appear in a wide range of physical contexts.
In bosonic lattice models, such as the Bose-Hubbard model, local site occupations are in principle unbounded due to bosonic statistics.
However, strong interactions and low fillings, when working with finite energy bandwidths, naturally suppress high-occupancy states
Hanada et al. 2023
, making truncated local Fock spaces both physically justified and commonly used in both exact diagonalization (ED) and density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) approaches
Kühner and White 1999
;
Jaksch et al. 1998
.
Important exceptions arise in systems with infinitely many states at finite bandwidth, such as electronic bound states in atoms.
Similarly, lattice gauge theories (LGT)
Kogut and Susskind 1975
;
Rothe 2012
with continuous gauge groups such as
\mathrm{U(1)}
,
\mathrm{SU(2)}
, or
\mathrm{SU(3)}
require infinite-dimensional gauge link Hilbert spaces.
Truncated formulations, such as quantum link models (QLM)
Chandrasekharan and Wiese 1997
;
Horn 1981
;
Orland and Rohrlich 1990
;
Brower et al. 1999
, Casimir-energy cutoffs
Cataldi et al. 2024
;
Calajó et al. 2024
;
Rigobello et al. 2021
;
Rigobello et al. 2023
;
Calajó et al. 2025
;
Cataldi et al. 2025a
;
Magnifico et al. 2025
;
Cataldi et al. 2025b
,
finite subgroups
Ercolessi et al. 2018
;
Magnifico et al. 2020
;
Haase et al. 2021
;
Mariani et al. 2023
, digitization of gauge fields
Hackett et al. 2019
, and fusion-algebra deformation
Zache et al. 2023
, are essential for numerical treatment
Bañuls et al. 2017
;
Magnifico et al. 2025
and experimental realization on quantum platforms
Byrnes and Yamamoto 2006
;
Martinez et al. 2016
;
Bañuls et al. 2020
;
Mathis et al. 2020
;
Davoudi et al. 2020
;
Mazzola et al. 2021
;
Kan et al. 2021
;
Zohar 2021
;
Pomarico et al. 2023
;
Bauer et al. 2023a
;
Bauer et al. 2023b
.
The reliability of such truncations is often justified in strong coupling or low-energy limits
Buyens et al. 2017
.
Large local spaces also arise in electron-phonon systems
Jeckelmann and White 1998
;
Macridin et al. 2018
, such as the Holstein and SSH models, which involve fermions coupled to local harmonic oscillators, resulting in an infinite local basis.
Tractable subspaces are obtained via phonon cutoffs based on coupling strength and occupation statistics
Bonča et al. 1999
;
Franchini et al. 2021
.
A similar task is faced in multi-orbital, impurity, and molecular systems due to spin, orbital, and charge configurations
Georges et al. 1996
, as well as in quantum chemistry from vibrational and rotational modes requiring energy-based truncation or mode reduction
Baiardi and Reiher 2020
.

Despite their differences, all these systems highlight a common challenge: identifying and retaining the physically relevant subspace of a large or infinite local Hilbert space.
While energy cutoffs, symmetry constraints, or system-specific intuition are often used, all these truncation strategies are typically model-dependent and may fail near criticality or in strongly entangled regimes.

Existing local basis optimization (LBO) methods are typically described as techniques that adaptively truncate or transform the large local Hilbert space into a smaller, more relevant effective basis during DMRG sweeps
Stolpp et al. 2021
;
Stolpp et al. 2020
;
Guo et al. 2012
.
In contrast, this work proposes a different strategy: a pre-processing step in which the RDM is estimated using both ED, MF, and TN calculations.
This makes our approach independent of the specific problem or TN architecture, and directly applicable to quantum simulation.

In

In

\mathrm{U(1)}

\mathrm{SU(2)}

I
Local basis optimization scheme

The fundamental steps towards an optimal truncation of a large local basis for a generic QMB system are sketched in
Fig.

1
.
We consider a QMB theory defined on a
D
-dimensional spatial lattice
\Lambda
, with
N
sites
\mathbf{n}\!=\!(n_{1},\dots n_{D})
equally spaced by lattice spacing
a>0
and links identified by positive unit vectors
{\bf\it\mu}_{k}
with
k\in\quantity{1\!\dots\!D}
.
Assuming the theory degrees of freedom to be originally described with a local Hilbert space of size
d=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}
, there might exist (in principle) a smaller and numerically exact (up to a certain precision) basis which can be obtained (
a posteriori
) by computing the single-site RDM.
In practice, if
d
is significantly large, any classical or quantum simulation of the problem would be limited to small lattice sizes, preventing access to such an exact basis.
We then need to find an
optimal
basis out of the available resources we can access.

(i) RDM estimation.—
For a fixed value of the Hamiltonian parameters, we initially focus on the smallest non-trivial lattice size where we can isolate a single site in the bulk of a translational invariant portion of the system (e.g., three sites in a nearest-neighbor 1D system).
To isolate a single site, we exploit three approaches: (
i
) by using a
k
-site cluster mean-field (MF) approach; (
ii
) through ground-state TN simulations with maximal bond dimension
\chi
on the smallest available lattice size; (
iii
) with ED methods on the largest available translational invariant lattice.
Once we access the ground state estimate of the problem, we compute the RDM
\varrho_{i}
of the
i^{\rm{th}}
site in the bulk of the system.
If the wave function ansatz lacks translational invariance, as in the case of small-size TTN calculations, an efficient basis can still be extracted by computing the mean RDM over the system size.
By diagonalizing
\varrho_{i}
, we then extract and sort in descending order the corresponding
d
eigenvalues
\quantity{\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{d}}
and the associated
d
-dimensional eigenstates
\quantity{\vb{v}_{1}\dots\vb{v}_{d}}
.

(ii) Basis truncation.—
Then, we set a finite and controllable tolerance
\varepsilon
which selects the first
k
eigenstates
\quantity{\vb{v}_{1}{\dots} \vb{v}_{k<d}}
such that
\lambda_{j}{\geq}\varepsilon
,
\forall j=1{\dots}k\!<\!d
.
Out of these eigenstates, we build a projector
\hat{P}_{d,k}
which projects the single-site Hamiltonian operators
\hat{O}
in the Hilbert subspace:
\hat{O}^{\prime}_{k,k}\!=\!\hat{P}_{k,d}^{\dagger}\hat{O}_{d,d}\hat{P}_{d,k}
.
The optimal Hamiltonian obtained using the truncated operators
\hat{O}^{\prime}_{k,k}
allows for simulating the original model with a smaller local Hilbert space and exploring larger system sizes.

E_{d}^{\max}

.

(iii) Basis augmentation.—
Such a procedure strongly relies on the scaling of eigenvalues in the single-site RDM, which in turn varies across different regimes of the zero-temperature phase diagram depending on the distance to any critical point of the system.
In their proximity, some long-range modes might not appear in the single-site
\varrho_{i}
obtained from too-small-lattice simulations.
To verify the reliability of this LBO protocol, especially when expanding the local Hilbert space into the
\varrho_{i}
-basis fails to yield a good ground-state approximation, we propose an
augmentation
scheme (see
Fig.

1
(e)).
We select powers of single-site non-diagonal Hamiltonian operators
\hat{O}_{i}
and iteratively apply each of them to the
k
most relevant eigenstates
\quantity{\vb{v}_{1}{\dots} \vb{v}_{k}}
of
\varrho_{i}
,

\hat{O}_{i}\quantity(\vb{v}_{1},\dots,\vb{v}_{k})=\quantity(\vb{v}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\vb{v}_{k}^{\prime}),

(1)

searching for other relevant states with a small overlap with the selected largest
k
ones.
Specifically, for a chosen residual tolerance
r>0
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\prime}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\braket{\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{v}_{\ell}}}\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\right\rVert_{2}>r,

(2)

we extend the existing basis
\quantity{\vb{v}_{1}\dots\vb{v}_{k}}
to
\quantity{\vb{v}_{1}\dots\vb{v}_{k}, \vb{v}_{i}^{\prime}}
and apply the Gramm-Schmidt algorithm to it to obtain an orthonormal spanning set.
If none of the obtained states is found to be orthonormal to the eigenstates of
\varrho_{i}
, the truncation scheme is already optimal; otherwise, the projector
\hat{P}
is augmented from
k
to
k+\delta k
with
\delta k
new states satisfying
Eq.

2
.

II
Scalar Field Theories

As a first application, we implement the protocol on two paradigmatic scalar field theories: the sine-Gordon model
Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov 1997
;
Zamolodchikov 1995
;
KLASSEN and MELZER 1993
;
Calliari et al. 2024
and the
\hat{\varphi}^{4}-
model
Loinaz and Willey 1998
;
Sugihara 2004
;
Milsted et al. 2013
.
Throughout the work, we work in the units where
\hbar\!=\!c\!=\!1
.
To obtain the corresponding discretized Hamiltonians, the
D
-spatial dimensions are regularized (see
Appendix

A
) on the lattice
\Lambda
.
The obtained lattice field operator
\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}
and its conjugate momentum
\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{n}}=a\,\partial_{t}\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}
satisfy the equal-time commutation relation:

[\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}(t),\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}{(t)}]=i\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\qquad\forall\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\in\Lambda

(3)

and can be rewritten in terms of real-space realizations of creation and annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger},\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}\right),

(4)

with
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}\ket{0}=0
, and
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}
,
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}
fulfilling the canonical commutation relation
[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}},\hat{b}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
whereas all the other commutation relations are zero.

II.1
Sine-Gordon model

The sine-Gordon (sG) model in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
is a well-known example of an integrable relativistic quantum field theory.
Due to its integrability, the model represents an ideal test ground for our LBO protocol.
The corresponding lattice Hamiltonian (in units of the lattice spacing
a=1
) reads:

\Ham\!=\!\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\Lambda}\quantity[\frac{\mom[\vecsite][2]}{2}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\qty[\qty(\field[\vecsite+\latvec]\!-\!\field[\vecsite])^2\!-\!\frac{m_{0}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\cos(\beta\field[\vecsite])]],

(5)

The dimensionless coupling
\beta^{2}\!>\!0
identifies the different phases of the models
Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov 1997
.
Namely, at
\beta^{2}\!=\!8\pi
, the model exhibits a Berezinsky-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) transition, which separates a gapless (and renormalizable) Luttinger liquid phase in
\beta^{2}\!>\!8\pi
from a gapped phase in
\beta^{2}\!<\!8\pi
supporting massive single-particle excitations.
The continuous theory exhibits no divergences in the regime of
\beta^{2}\!<\!4\pi
, while being super-renormalizable in
4\pi\!<\!\beta^{2}<8\pi
.
The model has a
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
symmetry
\hat{\varphi}\rightarrow\hat{\varphi}\!+\!2\hat{\pi}n/\beta
, where
n
is an arbitrary integer.

Throughout this work, we study the model in the parameter regime
0\!<\!\beta\!<\!1
, where the symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is spontaneously broken.
In this
broken
phase, the theory has infinitely many groundstates
\ket{0_{n}}
, characterized by a finite, nonzero expectation value of the order parameter, which in the sG theory corresponds to the vertex operator and is analytically known
Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov 1997
and reads:

\begin{split}\langle e^{i\beta\hat{\varphi}}\rangle=&\frac{(1+\xi)\pi\Gamma(\frac{1}{1+\xi})}{16\sin(\pi\xi)\Gamma(\frac{\xi}{1+\xi})}\quantity[\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+\xi}{2})\Gamma(1-\frac{\xi}{2})}{4\sqrt{\pi}}]^{-\frac{2}{1+\xi}}m^{\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi}},\end{split}

(6)

where
\xi\!=\!\beta^{2}/(8\pi\!-\!\beta^{2}),

while
m\!=\!2M\sin(\pi\xi/2)
is the mass of the lightest bound state of the particle associated with
\hat{\varphi}
, expressed in terms of the soliton mass
M
, which is analytically known
Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov 1997
;
Calliari et al. 2024
(see
Eq.

17
in
Section

A.1
).

The need for a large local Hilbert space becomes particularly significant in the
broken
phase of the theory (see
Fig.

2
(a)), where higher field excitations are required to mitigate truncation effects caused by the growth of the order parameter
\braket{e^{i\beta\hat{\varphi}}}
.
Close to criticality, additional excitations are needed to capture large local fluctuations.

II.2
The
\hat{\varphi}^{4}
theory

A similar analysis can be performed for the
\hat{\varphi}^{4}-
theory.
This model has been extensively studied using numerical simulations, including Monte Carlo techniques
Loinaz and Willey 1998
, DMRG
Sugihara 2004
, and TN algorithms
Milsted et al. 2013
;
Delcamp and Tilloy 2020
;
Vanhecke et al. 2022
;
Kadoh et al. 2019
.
The discretized lattice Hamiltonian reads

\Ham=\sum_{\mathbf{n},k}\quantity[
\frac{\mom[\vecsite]^{2}}{2}
+ \frac{\qty(\field[\vecsite+\latvec[k]]\!-\!\field[\vecsite])^{2}}{2}
+ \frac{\mu_{0}^2}{2} \field[\vecsite][2]
+ \frac{\lambda}{4!} \field[\vecsite][4]
],

(7)

The Hamiltonian is invariant under the discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}\rightarrow-\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}
, and the continuum limit displays an ultraviolet divergence that can be absorbed by redefining the mass parameter as

\mu_{R}^{2}=\mu_{0}^{2}-\delta\mu^{2},

where
\delta\mu^{2}
is a counter-term to subtract the divergence.

The
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}

\hat{\varphi}^{4}
-theory exhibits a second-order quantum phase transition in the
2D
Ising universality class
Caginalp 1980
;
Dash and Harrington 1976
, driven by the spontaneous breaking of its
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.
This transition separates a
symmetric
phase from a spontaneously
broken
phase and is controlled by the ratio of couplings
\lambda/\mu_{R}^{2}
.
The order parameter is given by the expectation value of the field operator
\hat{\varphi}

\braket{\hat{\varphi}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\Lambda}\braket{\hat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}}=A(\lambda)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu_{R}^{2}}-\frac{\lambda_{c}}{\mu^{2}_{R,c}(\lambda)}\right)^{\beta(\lambda)},

(8)

where
A(\lambda)
is a constant for given coupling
\lambda
.
In the regime where
\braket{\hat{\varphi}}\neq 0
, the model is in the broken phase, and when
\braket{\hat{\varphi}}=0
, the model is in the symmetric phase.
The critical coupling
(\lambda/\mu_{R}^{2})_{c}
and the critical exponents are defined in the continuum limit
N\rightarrow\infty,\lambda\rightarrow 0
.
To illustrate the phase diagram at finite system size, in
Fig.

3
(a) we show the scaling of the order parameter as a function of the bare mass
\mu^{2}_{0}
, for fixed coupling
\lambda=0.6
, obtained from MPS simulations on a
N\!=\!64
-site chain with bond dimension
\chi\!=\!100
.

Similar to the Sine-Gordon model, the challenges of a large local Hilbert space become most severe in the
broken
phase, where the expectation value
\braket{\hat{\varphi}}
increases with the power of
\beta(\lambda)
.
Consequently, the number of Fock levels required in the truncation must increase

This can be seen in
Fig.

3
(b–c), where we examine the convergence of the ground-state energy as a function of the local dimension.
We find that the optimal basis, obtained from an 8-site MF calculation, achieves a convergence rate comparable to the exact basis one obtained with an
N\!=\!64
-site chain.
As shown in
Fig.

3
(d), the deeper in the broken phase, the smaller the ratio
\mathcal{R}\!=\!d_{\text{opt.}}/d_{\text{Fock}}
and therefore, the larger the advantage of the LBO protocol.

II.3
Basis augmentation

Whenever the local basis obtained from the RDM, computed via MF or TN methods, fails to capture all relevant local modes (typically due to the lack of long-range entanglement in these calculations), the basis augmentation protocol described in
Section

I
can be employed.
In this section, we demonstrate its application in the
broken
phase of the
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}

\hat{\varphi}^{4}
-theory, which, as discussed in
Section

II.2
, particularly benefits from the LBO protocol.

The augmentation provides a systematic way to assess the quality of the estimated RDM and improve the local basis.
By applying local, non-diagonal operators of the theory to the
k
most significant RDM eigenstates, the local basis is extended to include modes typically missed in MF or finite-size TN calculations.
This procedure enriches the basis with both lower- and higher-energy local modes surrounding the initially captured ones (see
Fig.

1
(d)), replicating the effect of increasing the Fock space.

The application of the method is illustrated in
Fig.

4

The augmentation is performed by iteratively applying an operator from the set of local operators appearing in the Hamiltonian
\{\hat{\pi}_{i}^{2},\hat{\varphi}_{i}^{2},\hat{\varphi}_{i}^{4}\}
.
After the application of each operator, we assess, through
Eq.

2
, whether the resulting states are sufficiently orthogonal (up to a chosen tolerance
r\!=\!10^{-8}
) to the previous optimal basis.
If this condition is satisfied, the optimal basis is extended and the Gram-Schmidt algorithm is applied.
We observe that, close to the critical point (
\mu_{0}^{2}=-0.6,\lambda=0.6
), the convergence in energy with the optimal basis saturates, even when including eigenstates with small eigenvalues (below
\!<\!10^{-16}
).
This indicates that the 2-site MF estimate of the RDM fails to approximate the exact RDM.
In contrast, the proposed augmentation method leads to continued improvement in the ground-state energy, reaching the target accuracy
\Delta\!=\!10^{-8}
.
The convergence behavior is comparable to that achieved using the exact basis.
All results are obtained from MPS simulations on an
N\!=\!64
-site chain at bond dimension
\chi\!=\!100
.

III
Lattice Gauge Theories

We test the LBO protocol on two LGTs: the
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
SU(2) Yang-Mills LGT with flavorless dynamical matter
Calajó et al. 2024
;
Cataldi et al. 2025a
;
Calajó et al. 2025
and the
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
pure quantum electrodynamics (QED)
Felser et al. 2020
;
Magnifico et al. 2021
;
Magnifico et al. 2025
(without dynamical matter).
In both cases, we adopt the
Kogut-Susskind
formulation
Kogut and Susskind 1975
, while dynamical matter is described using
staggered fermions

Wilson 1974
.
The gauge degrees of freedom, which in principle belong to an infinite local Hilbert space, are decomposed in the eigenbasis of the Casimir (electric or irrep basis) and truncated in terms of a fixed Casimir eigenvalue.
To locally enforce Gauss’s law (in both U(1) and SU(2) cases), we employ the dressed-site scheme
Silvi et al. 2014
;
Magnifico et al. 2025
;
Cataldi et al. 2024
, in which each fermionic matter site is fused with its neighboring bosonic gauge half links, resulting from splitting each local gauge field, into a (bosonized) basis consisting solely of gauge-invariant combinations of the fields.
For details, see
Appendix

C
.
To measure convergence with respect to the local dimension, we measure the Casimir operator.

III.1
(1+1)D SU(2) Yang-Mills LGT

The
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
SU(2) Yang-Mills LGT with flavorless dynamical matter reads (in units of lattice spacing)
Calajó et al. 2024
;
Calajó et al. 2025
;
Cataldi et al. 2025a
:

\begin{split}H_{\rm{SU(2)}}^{\rm{1D}}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\quantity[i\hpsi^{\dagger}_{\vecsite,\alpha}\hat{U}^{\alpha\beta}_{\vecsite,\vecsite+\latvec[x]}\hpsi_{\vecsite+\latvec[x],\beta}+\hc]\\
&+m\sum_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}(-1)^{\mathbf{n}}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}\hat{\psi}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}+\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}+{\bf\it\mu}_{x}}\,.\end{split}

(9)

Here, fermionic matter degrees of freedom
\hat{\psi}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{n},\alpha}}^{\vphantom{dagger}}
represent are located on the lattice sites
\mathbf{n}
and obey the anti-commutation relation

\{\hat{\psi}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{n},\alpha}}^{\vphantom{dagger}},\hat{\psi}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{n}^{\prime},\beta}}^{\dagger\,}\}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}},

where
\alpha
and

are color indices.
The gauge degrees of freedom, residing on the lattice links, are described by the parallel transporter
\hat{U}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}^{\vphantom{dagger}\alpha\beta}

\hat{E}^{2}
, which corresponds to the electric energy (no magnetic term is present in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}

The first two terms of

9

Using ED methods, we simulate a
N\!=\!10
-site chain with periodic boundary conditions (PBC) and 10 incremental truncations of the

gauge field, from
j_{\max}\!=\!\hskip-0.7pt{\frac{1}{2}}\hskip-0.7pt

d\!=\!6

Calajó et al. 2024
) to
j_{\max}\!=\!5
(with local dimension
d\!=\!42

We compute the ground-state model phase diagram for a grid of couplings

(a).

In the absence of the magnetic term, any fluctuation of the gauge field irrep is obtained via the hopping term, which in turn is dominant only for small
m
.
Therefore, the model undergoes a transition in
m
that can be detected through local observables, such as the Casimir (but also via particle densities).
The small-
m

j_{\max}
and hence on the local dimension
d
.

Fixing
g^{2}=0.1

As shown in
Fig.

Essentially, the electric basis proves to be the optimal local basis for such
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
non-Abelian LGT at any regime of the mass and gauge coupling.
Indeed, at the ground-state level, the irreps fusing in the Gauss Law are not mixed and contribute to the energy in ascending order with the corresponding Casimir, which is reflected in the single-site RDM.
Moreover, as shown in

III.2
(2+1)D pure QED LGT

To further investigate the local basis optimization scheme in LGTs, we consider QED in two spatial dimensions, the minimal example where magnetic effects contribute and can affect the mixing of gauge link irreps.
For simplicity, we focus on the pure theory without dynamical matter.
The corresponding
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
pure QED Hamiltonian reads:

\hat{H}_{\rm{QED}}^{\rm{2D}}=\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},k}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}_{k}}-\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\sum_{\square}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{U}_{\square}+\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{\square}).

(10)

Here, in addition to the electric term, expressed in terms of the Casimir operator
\hat{E}^{2}
, we have a magnetic term expressed by the square plaquette operator
\hat{U}_{\square}
:

\hat{U}_{\square}=\hat{U}_{\mathbf{n},+{\bf\it\mu}_{x}}\hat{U}_{\mathbf{n}+{\bf\it\mu}_{x},+{\bf\it\mu}_{y}}\hat{U}_{\mathbf{n}+{\bf\it\mu}_{y}+{\bf\it\mu}_{x},-{\bf\it\mu}_{x}}\hat{U}_{\mathbf{n},+{\bf\it\mu}_{y}},

(11)

where
{\bf\it\mu}_{x}
and
{\bf\it\mu}_{y}
denote the lattice unit vectors spanning the plaquette’s plane.

Similarly to the SU(2) case, we adopt the dressed site formulation with gauge link truncation based on incremental energy cutoffs in the Casimir and consider sequential spin-discretizations of the gauge fields, ranging from
j=1
(with a local dressed-site basis of
d\!=\!19
sites
Felser et al. 2020
) to
j=9
(with
d\!=\!4579
).
With ED methods
Cataldi 2024
, we simulate a lattice
\Lambda\!=\!3\!\times\!2
in PBC on a grid of couplings
g^{2}\in\quantity[10^{-2}, 10^{1}]
.
In this range of the couplings, the model in
Eq.

10

g

g

As shown in the
Fig.

5

.

As shown in

At the same time, in

) is composed of highly entangled single-sites, preventing significant truncations of the local basis (see

This motivated the need for an alternative
magnetic

Promising proposals have been addressed in the Abelian

In this perspective, the LBO scheme proposed in this work can provide an alternative solution.
Indeed, in the
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}

j_{\max}\!=\!3
(with local dimension

5299

This suggests that, starting from a single-site electric basis, we can project the Hamiltonian onto an

and obtain a more efficient representation of the local degrees of freedom with a drastic reduction of computational cost.
At small-
g

5299
to
\sim\!250
).
At intermediate

A systematic exploration of the resulting computational gains will be addressed in future work.

IV
Improved resource estimate of classical and quantum computing

In this section, we discuss the practical implications of our LBO scheme for classical simulations across different TN architectures, as well as for digital quantum computing.

IV.1
Resource estimate for classical simulations

The LBO procedure previously developed applies broadly across ED, TN, MF, and cluster-MF simulations, being entirely problem-independent.
Although the computational complexity of a given TN ansatz may vary, reducing the local dimension
d
enhances efficiency in all cases, resulting in significant runtime improvements.

For instance, for the MPS-based DMRG algorithm, among the most accurate methods for one- and quasi-one-dimensional QMB systems, the computational cost for ground-state calculations scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(Nd\chi^{3}\!+\!Nd^{2}\chi^{2}\right)
or
\mathcal{O}\left(Nd^{3}\chi^{3}\right)
, depending on whether single- or 2-site updates are used during the optimization sweeps
Schollwöck 2011
;
Chan et al. 2016
.

In higher dimensions, DMRG algorithms on PEPS, the natural generalization of MPS, scale as
\mathcal{O}\left(Nd^{2}\chi^{8}\right)

Eisert 2013
;
Vanderstraeten et al. 2022
.
This limits simulations to relatively small bond dimensions
\chi\!\approx\!10
.
Here, an
optimal
local basis is even more critical, particularly for bosonic systems, as it can render simulations feasible that would otherwise be computationally prohibitive.
As for binary TTN architecture, ground-state searches scales with
\mathcal{O}\left(Nd^{2}\chi^{2}+N\chi^{4}\right)
, regardless of the
spatial dimension
Silvi et al. 2019
.

An optimal basis is also crucial in TN algorithms for time evolution.
In TEBD (time evolving block decimation)
Vidal 2003
;
Vidal 2004
, the local dimension scales at least quadratically in any matrix product operator operation on MPS
Paeckel et al. 2019
.
There, the LBO acts as a projection on each physical leg of the MPS, mapping it to a smaller basis (see also
Brockt et al. 2015
).
In TDVP (time-dependent variational principle)
Haegeman et al. 2011
, the LBO scheme applies directly as for DMRG
Schröder and Chin 2016
.

More generally, while the scaling with
d
can be either leading or sub-leading with respect to the bond dimension
\chi
, reducing
d
consistently leads to substantial performance improvements, particularly in systems where
d
is very large.
This is especially relevant for LGTs, where local dimensions as large as
d\geq 10^{6}
are possible depending on the truncation scheme
Magnifico et al. 2025
.
Basis optimization thus emerges as a universally applicable strategy for reducing computational costs across all TN ansätze, enabling simulations of large-scale QMB systems.

IV.2
Resource estimate for quantum computing

In this section, we estimate the impact of our LBO protocol on digital quantum simulations of field theories.
A typical digital quantum simulation involves three main tasks:
(i)
preparing an initial state,
(ii)
simulating the time evolution,
(iii)
and measuring final observables.
Our method affects all these steps by providing a scheme to find an optimal realisation for the field configurations.
For simplicity, we focus on the representation in terms of qubits, but there exist proposals to simulate field theories with qudit systems
Kurkcuoglu et al. 2021
, which would be similarly affected.

A state of the lattice field theory is represented by assigning to each lattice site a register of qubits, which truncates the unbounded field variable to a maximum value
\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{max}}
and discretizes it with resolution
\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}}
.
This low-energy subspace at lattice site
\mathbf{n}
can then be represented as the eigenspace of the discrete field operator
\tilde{\Phi}

Macridin et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023

\tilde{\Phi}\ket{\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}}=\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}\ket{\tilde{\varphi_{\mathbf{n}}}},\qquad\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}\,\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}}

(12)

where
\mathbf{n}\!=\!-\!\tfrac{N_{\tilde{\varphi}-1}}{2},\tfrac{N_{\tilde{\varphi}-1}}{2}\!+\!1,\dots,\tfrac{N_{\tilde{\varphi}-1}}{2}

and

\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}}\!=\!\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N_{\tilde{\varphi}}\mu_{0}^{2}}}.

To prepare a Gaussian state
\ket{\psi}
of the
free

\hat{\varphi}^{4}
-model (
\lambda\!=\!0
), a field cut-off

\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{max}}=\sqrt{\frac{|\lambda|E}{\mu_{0}^{2}\epsilon}}
is sufficient to achieve fidelity of
1\!-\!\epsilon

Jordan et al. 2012
.
The discrete conjugated momentum field operator
\tilde{\Pi}
is then obtained, through the discrete Fourier transform
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
as

\tilde{\Pi}=\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{-1},

defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{\tilde{F}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\tilde{\varphi}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=-M}^{M}e^{\frac{2\pi i}{N_{\tilde{\varphi}}}}\ket{\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}}\bra{\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}},

(13)

where
M\!=\!\frac{N_{\tilde{\varphi}}-1}{2}
is the range of summation.
The momentum eigenstates
\ket{\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{n}}}
of the discrete momentum operator
\tilde{\Pi}
are then constructed by applying the Fourier transform to the eigenstates of the discrete field operator:

\ket{\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{n}}}\!=\!\mathcal{\tilde{F}}\ket{\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}}.

The states generators of the field operators
\hat{\varphi},\hat{\pi}
of the free
\hat{\varphi}^{4}
-theory (restricted to the lowest

N_{b}
bosonic excitation) are then identical to the
N_{b}
bosonic excitation of the restricted operators
\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\Pi}
up to an error
\epsilon
, which decreases exponentially with
N_{\tilde{\varphi}}

Macridin et al. 2022
.
Hence, the larger the truncation
\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{max}}
and the finer the field discretization, the better the agreement between the bosonic excitation generated by the discrete fields
\tilde{\Phi}
and
\tilde{\Pi}
and that of the free
\hat{\varphi}^{4}

To store the local field configuration
\ket{\tilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{n}}}
, a qubit register of size

Jordan et al. 2012

n_{q}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log_{2}\left(\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{max}}/\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}}\right)\right)

N_{b}

\mathcal{\epsilon}
of the low-energy subspace of the discretized field operators for

The accuracy

improves exponentially with increasing cutoffs

\mu_{0}^{2}\!<\!3

However, the most significant reduction in the number of qubits per lattice site is achieved in the unbroken phase, for
\mu_{0}^{2}\!>\!3

For instance, at
\mu_{0}^{2}\!=\!8

An alternative approach to quantum simulation is the variational method, exemplified by the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA)
Farhi et al. 2014
and the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
Peruzzo et al. 2014
.
For example, VQE approximates the Hamiltonian ground state by preparing a parametrized quantum state with a variational quantum circuit and iteratively optimizing its parameters via classical feedback to minimize the measured energy expectation value.
Representing a local bosonic degree of freedom with
d\!>\!2
requires multiple qubits, which induces intrinsically long-range interactions in the Hamiltonian and leads to increased circuit depth. Alternatively, it can be encoded in qudits, but engineering as many transition levels as
d
is quite demanding already for
d\sim 10

.
In both scenarios, the proposed LBO protocol can improve the efficiency and accuracy of the VQE procedure.

V
Conclusions

We proposed a scheme for constructing an optimal basis that enables an effective description of the target theory.
The method relies on estimating the single-site RDM, obtained from ED, TN, MF, and cluster MF calculations on small translational-invariant systems.

This approach has been first applied to two paradigmatic scalar field theories: the sine-Gordon model in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
and the
\hat{\varphi}^{4}
-theory in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
and
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
.
The benefits of basis optimization are particularly pronounced in symmetry-broken phases, where conventional expansions in the Fock basis typically demand large local Hilbert spaces and thus substantial computational resources.
The method proves especially advantageous in two spatial dimensions, opening the door to applying TN techniques to bosonic field theories in higher dimensions, where standard approaches are limited by computational cost.

We further extended the method to Abelian and non-Abelian LTGs in
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
and
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
scenarios, confirming that the electric basis built from incremental spin-irreps of the gauge fields provides an optimal single-site description, particularly in one dimension.
In
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
, we investigated the decay of the single-plaquette RDM eigenvalues across different values of the coupling
g^{2}
.
Our results show that the optimal dimension of the single-plaquette Hilbert space is significantly smaller than the one required in the original dressed-site formulation of the LGT.
These findings indicate that expressing the Kogut–Susskind Hamiltonian in a plaquette basis provides a more compact and efficient representation of the local degrees of freedom, especially in the small-
g^{2}
regime.

Finally, we explored the advantages of the LBO protocol for digital quantum simulation of the
(2\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}

\hat{\varphi}^{4}
-theory.
We demonstrated that the optimal basis significantly reduces the number of qubits required to encode local field variables throughout the phase diagram.
This reduction is particularly valuable in the current NISQ era
Preskill 2018
, where quantum resources are limited, and provides the most efficient encoding of local degrees of freedom on a quantum device.

Appendix A
Models

In this section, we provide additional information about the sine-Gordon (sG) and
\hat{\varphi}^{4}-
theories in the continuum; specifically, we give details about their respective renormalization procedure, necessary to extract the properties of the continuum theories from the lattice Hamiltonians in
Eqs.

5
and
7
.

A.1
The sine-Gordon theory

The energy density of the continuum quantum sG model in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
is defined as follows
Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov 1997
;
Roy et al. 2021
;
Pallua and Prester 2001
:

\mathcal{H}_{\text{sG}}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\pi}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{x}\hat{\varphi}\partial_{x}\hat{\varphi}-\frac{m_{0}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\cos(\beta\Op{\varphi}),

(15)

where
m_{0}^{2}
is the mass parameter and
\beta^{2}
is a dimensionless coupling constant.
The sG model can be interpreted as a deformation of a free conformal field theory (CFT), where the mass term is treated as a perturbation
KLASSEN and MELZER 1993
.
When evaluated relative to the CFT vacuum, the energy density remains finite and is given by
Zamolodchikov 1995

E_{0}(M)=\frac{\braket{\Ham}_{\text{sG}}-\braket{\Ham}_{\text{CFT}}}{V}=-\frac{M^{2}}{4}\tan\left(\frac{\pi\xi}{2}\right),

(16)

where
M
is the renormalized soliton mass, and
\xi
is a parameter depending on the coupling
\beta^{2}
, defined as
\xi\!=\!\beta^{2}/(8\pi\!-\!\beta^{2})
.
The formula for the soliton mass
M
is

Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov 1997
;
Calliari et al. 2024

M=b\tilde{\Lambda}\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{\xi}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{1+\xi}{2})}\left(\frac{m_{0}^{2}(1+\xi)\Gamma(\frac{1}{1+\xi})}{16\xi\Gamma(\frac{\xi}{1+\xi})(b\tilde{\Lambda})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1+\xi}{2}}.

(17)

Here
\tilde{\Lambda}=\pi/a
is the UV-cut-off and
b=e^{C}/2
, a parameter depending on the renormalization scheme.

An alternative way to study the quantum sG model is to use the fact that there exists a spin chain lattice regularization in the form of the XXZ spin chain with periodic boundary conditions in a transverse magnetic field
KLASSEN and MELZER 1993
.

A.2
\hat{\varphi}^{4}-
theory

The classical Hamiltonian density of the
\varphi^{4}-
model in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\varphi^{4}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{x}\varphi\partial_{x}\varphi+\mu_{0}^{2}\varphi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\varphi^{4}.

(18)

Here,
\pi
is the canonical momentum conjugate to the field
\varphi
, defined by

\pi=\partial_{t}\varphi.

Both fields
\varphi
and
\pi
are dimensionless, while
m
and
\lambda
have units of energy.
The theory is symmetric under the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
transformation:
\varphi\rightarrow-\varphi
.
For
\mu_{0}^{2}>0
, the potential

U(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}^{2}\varphi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\varphi^{4}

has a unique minimum at
\varphi=0
, preserving
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.
For
\mu_{0}^{2}<0
, we can rewrite the potential by adding a constant shift
\Delta U=\frac{3}{2}\frac{|\mu_{0}^{2}|^{2}}{\lambda}
as

\displaystyle U(\varphi)+\Delta U=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\left(\varphi^{2}-\frac{6|\mu_{0}^{2}|}{\lambda}\right)^{2},

(19)

which has degenerate minima at
\varphi=\pm\sqrt{6|\mu_{0}^{2}|/\lambda}
. The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry therefore interchanges the two vacua, resulting in symmetry breaking.
More importantly, this classical calculation estimates how the local dimension scales as one proceeds further into the broken phase.

After quantization of the
\varphi^{4}
-theory, as described in
Section

II.2
, the quantum field theory has to be renormalized; this can be done by redefining the mass parameter.
The counter-term
\delta\mu_{0}^{2}
is defined as
Loinaz and Willey 1998
;
Sugihara 2004

\displaystyle\delta\mu_{0}^{2}(\mu_{0}^{2})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}\sqrt{\mu_{0}^{2}+4}}F\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4}}\right).

(20)

Here,
F
is the elliptic integral of the first kind

\displaystyle F\left(\alpha,m\right)=\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}\sin(x)}}\diff x.

(21)

The counter-term is derived from the only divergent Feynman diagram of the theory in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
, depicted in
Fig.

8
.

Figure 8:
Divergent diagram of the
\hat{\varphi}^{4}
theory in
(1\!+\!1)\mathrm{D}
.

The necessity for renormalizing the theory by subtractions comes from the choice of a real-space realization of the Hamiltonian.
In contrast, in Fock space, the theory can be regularized by choosing the normal-ordered Hamiltonian
Glimm and Jaffe 2012
.

Appendix B
Numerical methods

In this section, we review the three numerical tools adopted to extract the local RDM of the given QMB Hamiltonians and provide the corresponding local basis: exact diagonalization (ED), k-side cluster mean-field (MF) theory, and tensor network (TN) methods.

B.1
k-side MF theory

To formulate the
k
-site MF problem, we define the
k
-site MF state as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(a,b)}\!=\!\left[\bigotimes_{-\infty}^{n-1}\ket{\psi(a)}\right]\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi(b)}\!\otimes\!\left[\bigotimes_{n+k}^{\infty}{\psi(a)}\right]\!\in\!\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d},

(22)

where
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
denotes an infinite lattice system with local Hilbert space dimension
d
at each site. The states
\ket{\psi(a)}
and
\ket{\psi(b)}
are normalized vectors defined on the
k
-site subsystem
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes k}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
.
Here, the parameters
a
and
b
indicate that the corresponding states are variational.

The
k-
side MF estimation for the many-body ground-state minimizes then
\mathrm{min}_{\psi}\braket{\psi|H|\psi}
, under the constraint that

\ket{\psi}
is normalized.
To achieve that, we formulate the Lagrange function

\displaystyle L=\langle\psi|\Ham|\psi\rangle-\lambda\left(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle-1\right)

(23)

and impose

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bra{\psi(b)}}=0,

(24)

which is minimized if
\ket{\psi(b)}
is an eigenvector of an effective Hamiltonian (for
k\!=\!2
, see
Fig.

10
).
We start the algorithm by sampling a random state
\ket{\psi(a)}\!\in\!\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes k}
, calculate
\ket{\psi(b)}
, then
\ket{\psi(a)}\!\leftarrow\!\ket{\psi(b)}
and repeat to minimize with respect to
\ket{\psi(b)}
until convergence in consecutive ground state energies
|E_{k}\!-\!E_{k-1}|\!<\!\epsilon
for a chosen value
\epsilon>0
.

An example of a 2-side MF state is depicted in
Fig.

9
.

Figure 9:
MF state for
k=2

Taking a two-sided MF state as an ansatz, the effective Hamiltonian with nearest-neighbour interaction can then be depicted as in
Fig.

.

H=\sum_{i}h_{i,i+1}
with nearest neighbour two-body operators
h_{i,i+1}
.

B.2
Tensor Network

To study the ground-state properties of the models, we employ a TN technique, such as matrix product states (MPS)
Fannes et al. 1992
and tree tensor network (TTN)
Shi et al. 2006
states.
A general quantum many-body (QMB) state is defined on a lattice
\Lambda
with
N=|\Lambda|
sites.
A state
\ket{\psi}
of this quantum system lives in the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\bigotimes_{i\in\Lambda}\mathcal{H}_{i},

where
\mathcal{H}_{i}
denotes the local Hilbert space at site
i
, each of dimension
d
.
The full state
\ket{\psi}
can be expanded as

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\psi_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\ket{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}},

(25)

where
\{\ket{i}_{j}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{j}
.
A complete description of the quantum state
\ket{\psi}
thus requires tracking
d^{N}
complex coefficients, which becomes computationally unfeasible for large
N
.

However, many low-energy QMB states exhibit limited entanglement.
This behaviour is captured by the entanglement area law, which states that the entanglement entropy of a subsystem scales with the size of its boundary rather than its volume.
When the area law holds, an efficient representation of
\ket{\psi}
as an MPS exists
Eisert et al. 2010
,

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\Tr\left[A_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots A_{N}^{i_{N}}\right]\ket{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}},

(26)

where the number of parameters scales polynomially with the system size.
In
Eq.

26
, for each physical index
i_{j}
, the matrix
A_{j}^{i_{j}}
has shape
D_{j-1}\times D_{j}
. In the case of open boundary conditions, the boundary matrices reduce to row and column vectors, such that
D_{1}=D_{N}=1
.
In one-dimensional systems with gapped local Hamiltonians, where locality refers to interactions between neighbouring sites, the area law has been proven analytically.
For higher-dimensional QMB systems, no general proof exists; however, extensive numerical evidence supports the validity of the area law under the assumption of local interactions
Eisert et al. 2010
;
Masanes 2009
;
Kastoryano et al. 2019
.
To estimate the ground state and low-lying excitations, we apply the density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) algorithm

Schollwöck 2005
;
Verstraete et al. 2008
.
All the TN simulations performed in this work have been obtained using the open-source TN library Quantum TEA
Baccari et al. 2025a
;
Baccari et al. 2025b
.

Appendix C
Dressed-site formulation of Hamiltonian Lattice Gauge Theories

The Gauss operator for a compact Lie gauge group
G
can be written as

\hat{G}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\nu}=\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\nu}+\hat{q}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\nu}+\sum_{\mu}\left[\hat{L}_{{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}^{\nu}+\hat{R}_{\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{\mu},\mathbf{\mu}}^{\nu}\right],

(27)

where
\hat{L}_{{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}^{\nu}
and
\hat{R}_{{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}^{\nu}
are the independent generators of the left and right gauge transformations, located at the respective ends of the link
({\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}})\in\Lambda
. The index
\nu
labels the generators, with
\nu\in\{1,\dots,\dim(\text{G})\}
.
In the Abelian
U(1)
case, the left and right generators coincide and reduce to the electric field,

\hat{E}_{{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}\!=\!\hat{L}_{{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}\!=\!\hat{R}_{{\mathbf{n},{\bf\it\mu}}}.

In
Eq.

27
,
\hat{q}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\nu}
denotes the background charge, which is set to zero in the present work, while

\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\nu}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\hat{\psi}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{T}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\nu}\hat{\psi}_{\mathbf{n},\beta}

represents the matter charge at lattice site
\mathbf{n}
, with
\{\hat{T}^{\nu}\}_{\nu=1}^{\dim(\text{G})}
denoting the generators of the gauge group.

To locally enforce Gauss’s law in LGT, as discussed in
Section

III
, we employ the so-called dressed-site formalism.
This approach reduces a non-Abelian constraint to an Abelian one, as we will see in this section.
Although dressed sites typically have larger local dimensions than those arising from other truncation schemes, this approach applies to all compact gauge groups.
It preserves the locality of interactions, making the framework particularly well-suited for TN simulations.
First, the parallel transporter is decomposed into left and right degrees of freedom, referred to as left (L) and right (R) rishon operators.
This is achieved by embedding the attached link Hilbert space as
Silvi et al. 2014

\ket{jmn}\;\to\;\ket{jm}_{L}\otimes\ket{jn}_{R}.

The parallel transporter then takes the form

\hat{U}\to\sum_{k}\zeta^{L(k)\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},+{\bf\it\mu}}\zeta^{R(k)\beta\,\dagger}_{\mathbf{n}+{\bf\it\mu},-{\bf\it\mu}}\,\,.

(28)

Physical configurations are those in which the left and right rishons are in the same irreducible representation.
This construction thus yields an Abelian link symmetry on the TN level
Silvi et al. 2014
;
Rigobello et al. 2023
, regardless of whether the gauge symmetry of the theory is Abelian or non-Abelian.

Now, the local gauge generators act only on the matter site at
x
and its
2D
neighboring rishons in the lattice
\Lambda
.
The matter site and the surrounding rishon degrees of freedom are fused into a composite site, and Gauss’s law becomes a constraint that reduces the dressed-site Hilbert space to the Hilbert space of physical configurations, the states transforming under the singlet

\mathcal{H}_{dress}=\text{ker}\,\hat{G}_{\mathbf{n}}^{a}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\text{matt}}\otimes\left(\mathcal{H}_{\text{rish}}\right)^{\otimes 2D}.

(29)

The gauge-singlet basis states of
\mathcal{H}_{\text{dress}}
are then expanded, the Clebsch–Gordan
Wigner 2012
decomposition in terms of matter and rishon bases.
