Uniform-in-Time Estimates on the Size of Chaos for Interacting Particle Systems
Pengzhi Xie

Abstract
For any weakly interacting particle system with bounded kernel, we give uniform-in-time estimates of the L 2 L^{2} norm of correlation functions, provided that the diffusion coefficient is large enough. When the condition on the kernels is more restrictive, we can remove the dependence of the lower bound for diffusion coefficient on the initial data and estimate the size of chaos in a weaker sense. Based on these estimates, we may study fluctuation around the mean-field limit.



1
Introduction

1.1
Overview

Consider the first order interacting particle system on the torus with i.i.d. noise

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}^{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(X_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{j})\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2\sigma}\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}W_{t}^{i},\\
&X_{t}^{i}|_{t=0}=X_{0}^{i}\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
X_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
for
i=1,\dots,N
are the positions of particles at time
t
. Here
W^{i}
’s are i.i.d. Brownian motions and
X_{0}^{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\rho_{0},\;i=1,\dots,N
. Then, at least formally, the joint distribution of
(X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{N})
, denoted by
f_{N}
, satisfies the Liouville equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f_{N}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{div}_{x_{i}}\bigg(f_{N}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(x_{i},x_{j})\bigg)=\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{x_{i}}f_{N},\\
&f_{N}|_{t=0}=\rho_{0}^{\otimes N}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

Then exchangeability of the particles is equivalent to symmetry of
f_{N}
in its
N
variables and we may study
f_{[j],N}
, the
j
-marginal of
f_{N}
, where
[j]=\{1,\dots,j\}
. That is,

f_{[j],N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})=\int\dots\int f_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{j+1}\dots\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{N}.

Here and thereafter we put the index set on the subscript of some function
h
to emphasize the variables involved in it. For any
P\subset\mathbb{N}\cup\{*\}
with
|P|<\infty
, and any
h_{P}:(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, define

\displaystyle S_{k,l}h_{P}:(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\Big(S_{k,l}h_{P}\Big)(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\bigg(K(x_{k},x_{l})h_{P}(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)\bigg).

Moreover, if
*,k\in P
and
k\neq*
, denote

\displaystyle H_{k}h_{P}:(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P-\{*\}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\Big(H_{k}h_{P}\Big)(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P-\{*\})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\left(\int K(x_{k},x_{*})h(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{*}\right).

Then the evolution of
f_{[j],N}
’s is described by the BBGKY hierarchy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{[j],N}-\sigma\sum_{k=1}^{j}\Delta_{x_{k}}f_{[j],N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{j}S_{k,l}f_{[j],N}=-\frac{N-j}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{j}H_{k}f_{[j]\cup\{*\},N}.

(1.3)

When the number of particles
N
is large, we can seek for averaged behavior of one typical particle in the system. For example, consider the
1
-marginal
f_{[1],N}
, one may expect that it is close to the solution to the McKean-Vlasov equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho-\sigma\Delta\rho+\mathrm{div}\bigg(\rho\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K(x,x_{*})\rho(x_{*})\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{*}\bigg)=0

(1.4)

with initial data
\rho_{t}|_{t=0}=\rho_{0}
.

Correction to such a mean-field approximation is usually based on the so-called correlation functions
g_{[j],N}
[
Due21
,
HCR23
,
BD24
]
, see also
[
PS17
,
PPS19
,
BDJ24
,
DHM24
]
for its variants. Roughly speaking,
g_{[1],N}
equals exactly the
1
-marginal
f_{[1],N}
and all the
g_{[j],N}
’s are small for
j\geq 2
. That is, we want to estimate
g_{[j],N}
to determine the
size of chaos
. It is proved in
[
Due21
,
BD24
]
that for second order particle systems, the correlation function with
j
variables is of order
\frac{1}{N^{j-1}}
. However, the kernel there is required to be smooth enough. The paper
[
HCR23
]
tried to conduct asymptotic expansion of the correlation functions and consider kind of
L^{2}
bounds of them on finite time intervals, for first order diffusive particle system with bounded kernels. However, it is not clear whether such asymptotic expansion converges.

1.2
Main Results

Given all the marginals
f_{[j],N}
’s, define the
m
particle correlation function by

g_{[m],N}:=\sum_{\pi\vdash[m]}(-1)^{|\pi|-1}(|\pi|-1)!\prod_{P\in\pi}f_{P,N}.

Thanks to the combinatorial identity

\sum_{\sigma\leq\pi}(-1)^{|\sigma|-1}(|\sigma|-1)!=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1,\quad&|\pi|=1,\\
&0,\quad&|\pi|\geq 2,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

we can express
f_{[j],N}
’s in terms of
g_{[m],N}
’s

f_{[j],N}=\sum_{\pi\vdash[j]}\prod_{P\in\pi}g_{P,N},

where
\pi\vdash[m]
means that
\pi
is a partition of the set
[m]
and
\sigma\leq\pi
means that
\pi
is a refinement of the partition
\sigma
. Our first result shows that, under the same setting as
[
HCR23
]
, we can decide the exact
L^{2}
size of chaos uniformly-in-time.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the kernel
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Let
f_{[j],N}
’s be solution to BBGKY hierarchy (
1.3
) with chaotic initial data and let
g_{[m],N}
’s be the corresponding correlation functions. Then there exist constants
C=C(\|K\|_{L^{\infty}},\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}})
and
C_{0}=C(\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}})
such that, if
\sigma>C
, then for all
N
the following quantitative estimate of the
L^{2}
size of chaos

\|g_{[m],N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{dm})}\leq\frac{C_{0}(m-1)!}{m^{2}N^{m-1}}

holds for all
m=1,\dots,N
and all
t\geq 0
.

Remark 1.2
.

If we estimate, e.g., the
L^{2}
size of chaos properly, then we can derive propagation of chaos provided that the stability of McKean-Vlasov equation (
1.4
) has been established, see Section
5
.

Remark 1.3
.

Theorem
1.1
is essentially a result for
small initial data
, as the lower bound of
\sigma
depends on
\rho_{0}
. Such assumption on
\sigma
appears in
[
LLF23
]
as well as in Section 8 of
[
Due21
]
, and is stronger than that in
[
BD24
]
.

To overcome the drawback mentioned above, we have to consider some framework weaker that
L^{1}
since we have the apparent bound
\|\rho_{0}\|_{1}=1
and thus we can replace the constant
C_{0}
in
1.1
by some universal constant, say
10
. See Section
3
and Section
4
.

Recall that propagation of chaos means that for any
j
, for any time
t
, the
j
-marginal of the joint distribution converges weakly to
\rho^{\otimes j}(t)
. As its name suggests, converging weakly is weaker than converging in
L^{1}
norm, thus we need to find some norm compatible to weak convergence. There are several equivalent conditions for weak convergence of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
or
\mathbb{T}^{D}
. By definition, we say a sequence of probability measures
\mu_{n}
on
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{T}^{D}
converges weakly to some
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{D})
if for any
\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{D})
,

\displaystyle\int\varphi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{n}\to\int\varphi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\mu,\;n\to\infty.

(1.6)

When we take the domain
\mathbb{D}
to be the torus
\mathbb{T}^{D}
, by Stone-Weierstrass theorem, the trigonometric polynomials,
\mathrm{span}\{e^{2\pi ik\cdot x};k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\}
, are dense in
C_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{D})
. Thus we only need to check (
1.6
) for every
\varphi\in\mathrm{span}\{e^{2\pi ik\cdot x};k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\}
. In other words, weak convergence is equivalent to pointwise convergence of Fourier modes for probability distributions on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{D}
. Thus the norm we ought to study is some weighted
l^{\infty}
norm of Fourier coefficients.

We consider the original
l^{\infty}
norm in this paper. For a measurable function
h
on
\mathbb{T}^{D}
, define

\|h\|_{\hat{l}^{\infty}}:=\|\hat{h}\|_{l^{\infty}}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}|\hat{h}(k)|,

and our second result concerns on the size of chaos under
\hat{l}^{\infty}
norm.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that the kernel
K
satisfies that the Fourier modes of
K(x,y)
is summable, i.e.

\|\hat{K}\|_{l^{1}}:=\sum_{\eta,\lambda}|\hat{K}(\eta,\lambda)|<+\infty

and that
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. With the same notations as in Theorem
1.1
, there exists a constant
C=C(K)
such that, if
\sigma>C
, then for all
N
the following quantitative estimate of the
\hat{l}^{\infty}
size of chaos

\|g_{[m],N}(t)\|_{\hat{l}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{dm})}\leq\frac{2(m-1)!}{m^{2}N^{m-1}}

holds for all
m=1,\dots,N
and all
t\geq 0
.

With such kind of size of chaos as in Theorem
1.4
, we may prove a central limit theorem for empirical measure of the system (
1.1
). Propagation of chaos implies that
X_{i}
’s are “almost” independent, so we may expect that they behaves like i.i.d. random variables. In particular, some central limit theorem would hold. Let

\mu_{N}^{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}^{t}}

be the empirical measure, then CLT means that for fixed test function
\phi
, the distributions of random variables

\int\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{N}^{t}

approximates to some normal distribution.

Corollary 1.5

(Uniform-in-Time CLT)
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1.4
, there exists a constant
C=C(K)
such that for
\sigma>C
, for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, for any time
t
such that the quantity

\int\phi^{2}\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}-\left(\int\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}\right)^{2}+\int\phi\otimes\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}b_{t}

does not vanish, where
\rho_{t}
is solution to the McKean-Vlasov equation (
1.4
) with initial data
\rho_{0}
and
b_{t}
is defined in Section
5
, we have

i)

(Central Limit Theorem)There exists some constant
C_{1}
depending on
\phi
such that the random variable

Y_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\phi,N}}\Bigg(\int\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{N}^{t}-\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{N}^{t}\bigg)\Bigg)

satisfies

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N^{1/7}},

for
N
large enough (independent on
t
), where
\sigma^{2}_{\phi,N}=Var\left(\int\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{N}^{t}\right)
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

ii)

(Convergence of Variance)As
N\to\infty
, the variance
N\sigma^{2}_{\phi,N}
converges to

\int\phi^{2}\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}-\left(\int\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}\right)^{2}+\int\phi\otimes\phi\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}b_{t}

which we mentioned above, uniformly in
t
.

Remark 1.6
.

In fact, for some first order systems with singular interaction, Central Limit Theorem can also be derived. Examples include the point-vertex dynamics
[
JW18
,
WZZ23
]
and the equilibrium of Coulomb interactions
[
Ser23
]
. Despite these results, we present a proof of the Corollary above to show that CLT for interacting particles systems would hold when the size of chaos were estimated properly.

Remark 1.7
.

To our best knowledge, there are many early works on Central Limit Theorem for interacting particle systems, such as
[
BH77
,
Mél96
,
Lan09
]
, without providing convergence rate. The paper
[
WZZ23
]
states that a CLT result similar to that in
[
Mél96
]
holds when the relative entropy between the
N
-particle distribution and the
N
-tensor product of limit distribution is about
O(1/N)
, which has been justified for the point-vortex model in
[
JW18
]
. In
[
Due21
]
and
[
BD24
]
, CLT with convergence rate
O(1/N^{1/2})
(
O(1/N^{1/3})
, resp.) was estabilshed for particle systems with smooth kernel. However, the kernels considered here are not smooth, so we can only get the rate
O(1/N^{1/7})
.

Preliminaries

In this section we recall some preliminary results. Equivalent hierarchy concerning correlation functions has been derived in
[
HCR23
]
. The utility of Fourier modes plays a key role in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, so we list some basic notations here. Finally, we cite the Abstract Bootstrap Principle from
[
Tao06
]
.

2.1
Hierarchy for Correlation Functions

Here and thereafter we shall omit the subscript
N
of marginals and correlation functions. Since we can express marginals in terms of correlation functions and vice versa, we may get the evolution of
g_{[m]}
’s from BBGKY hierarchy
[
HCR23
]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{[m]}-\sigma\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Delta_{x_{k}}g_{[m]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{N-m}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m}H_{k}g_{[m]\cup\{*\}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{W\subset[m]-\{k\}}\frac{m-1-|W|}{N}H_{k}g_{W\cup\{k,*\}}g_{[m]-\{k\}-W}

\displaystyle-\frac{N-m}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{W\subset[m]-\{k\}}H_{k}g_{W\cup\{k\}}g_{[m]\cup\{*\}-W-\{k\}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{W\subset[m]-\{k\}}\sum_{R\subset[m]-\{k\}-W}\frac{m-1-|W|-|R|}{N}H_{k}g_{W\cup\{k\}}g_{R\cup\{*\}}g_{[m]-R-W-\{k\}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{m}S_{k,l}g_{[m]}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1,k\neq l}^{m}\sum_{W\subset[m]-\{k,l\}}S_{k,l}g_{W\cup\{k\}}g_{[m]-\{k\}-W}.

(2.1)

In fact, the hierarchy (
2.1
) is equivalent to BBGKY hierarchy (
1.3
). The derivation of (
2.1
) is omitted here.

2.2
Fourier Transform on Torus

Since all the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
take value on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we may write any function
h_{P}
in its Fourier modes

h_{P}(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)=\sum_{\xi_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d};\alpha\in P}\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}.

Also, we may write the kernel
K
as

K(x,y)=\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)e^{2\pi i(\lambda\cdot x+\eta\cdot y)}.

Then

\displaystyle\Big(S_{k,l}h_{P}\Big)(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\bigg(K(x_{k},x_{l})h_{P}(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\Bigg(\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)e^{2\pi i(\lambda\cdot x_{k}+\eta\cdot x_{l})}\sum_{\xi_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d};\alpha\in P}\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\Bigg(\sum_{\xi_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d};\alpha\in P}e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,\xi_{l}-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k,l\})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d};\alpha\in P}e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}(2\pi i\xi_{k})\cdot\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,\xi_{l}-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k,l\})

for
k\neq l
and

\displaystyle\Big(S_{k,k}h_{P}\Big)(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d};\alpha\in P}e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}(2\pi i\xi_{k})\cdot\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k\}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\Big(H_{k}h_{P}\Big)(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\Bigg(\int K(x_{k},x_{*})h(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{*}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\Bigg(\int\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)e^{2\pi i(\lambda\cdot x_{k}+\eta\cdot x_{*})}\sum_{\xi_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d};\alpha\in P}\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{*}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{x_{k}}\Bigg(\sum_{\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P-\{*\}}e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P-\{*\}}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{*\})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P-\{*\}}e^{2\pi i\sum_{\alpha\in P-\{*\}}\xi_{\alpha}\cdot x_{\alpha}}(2\pi i\xi_{k})\cdot\sum_{\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{*\}).

To represent the Fourier modes of
S_{k,l}h_{P}
and
H_{k}h_{P}
, we may define

\hat{S}_{k,l}\varphi(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P):=\sum_{\lambda,\eta}(2\pi i\xi_{k})\cdot\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\varphi(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,\xi_{l}-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k,l\}),

\hat{S}_{k,k}\varphi(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P):=\sum_{\lambda,\eta}(2\pi i\xi_{k})\cdot\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\varphi(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k\}),

\hat{H}_{k}\varphi(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P-\{*\}):=\sum_{\lambda,\eta}(2\pi i\xi_{k})\cdot\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\varphi(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k,*\}).

For any function
h_{P}
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}h_{P}(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{k}=0

for some
k\in P
, we may define
|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}h_{P}
by

\bigg(|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}h_{P}\bigg)^{\wedge}(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|2\pi\xi_{k}|^{-1}\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P),\qquad&\xi_{k}\neq 0,\\
&0,\qquad&\xi_{k}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Then
|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}
is a linear operator on certain subspace of
L^{2}((\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P})
. In particular, we have

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}S_{k,l}
(
|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}H_{k}
) is a bounded operator from
L^{2}((\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P})
to
L^{2}((\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P})
(
L^{2}((\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P-\{*\}})
, resp.) with operator norm no bigger than
\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}
, for any suitable index set
P
.

Proof.

We may prove the boundedness of
|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}H_{k}
. The case of
|\nabla_{k}|^{-1}S_{k,l}
is similar. For any
P
such that
*,k\in P
and
k\neq*
, for any
h_{P}\in L^{2}((\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P})
, by Plancherel’s identity,

\displaystyle\||\nabla_{k}|^{-1}H_{k}h_{P}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P,\xi_{k}\neq 0}\left|\frac{1}{|2\pi\xi_{k}|}\hat{H}_{k}\hat{h}_{P}(\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P-\{*\})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P,\xi_{k}\neq 0}\left|\sum_{\lambda,\eta}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\varphi(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k,*\})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\alpha};\alpha\in P}\left|\sum_{\lambda,\eta}\hat{K}(\lambda,\eta)\varphi(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{k}-\lambda,-\eta;\alpha\in P-\{k,*\})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{P-\{*\}}}\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K(x_{k},x_{*})h_{P}(x_{\alpha};\alpha\in P)\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}x_{*}\Bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|h_{P}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

∎

2.3
Bootstrap Method

To deal with the nontrivial dependency in (
2.1
), we need the so-called bootstrap principle, which can be seen as a more general version of Gronwall’s inequality. Here we present such principle in the form of Proposition 1.21,
[
Tao06
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(Abstract Bootstrap Principle)
.

Let
I
be a time interval, and for each
t\in I
we have two statements, a “hypothesis”
\mathbf{H}(t)
and a “conclusion”
\mathbf{C}(t)
. Suppose we can verify the following four assertions:

(a)

(Hypothesis implies conclusion) If
\mathbf{H}(t)
is true for some time
t\in I
, then
\mathbf{C}(t)
is also true for that time
t
.

(b)

(Conclusion is stronger than hypothesis) If
\mathbf{C}(t)
is true for some
t\in I
, then
\mathbf{H}(t^{\prime})
is true for all
t^{\prime}\in I
in a neighbourhood of
t
.

(c)

(Conclusion is closed) If
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
is a sequence of times in
I
which converges to another time
t\in I
and
\mathbf{C}(t_{n})
is true for all
n
, then
\mathbf{C}(t)
is true.

(d)

(Base case)
\mathbf{H}(t)
is true for at least one time
t\in I
.

Then
\mathbf{C}(t)
is true for all
t\in I
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be the set of times
t\in I
for which
\mathbf{C}(t)
holds. Properties
(d)
and
(a)
ensure that
\Omega
is non-empty. Properties
(b)
and
(a)
ensure that
\Omega
is open. Property
(c)
ensures that
\Omega
is closed. Since the interval
I
is connected, we thus see that
\Omega=I
, and the claim follows.
∎

2.4
Cumulants and Normal Approximation

In the proof of CLT in Section
5
, it is convenient to apply the method of cumulants (
[
DJS22
]
, see also
[
SS12
]
). For any random variable
X
with its characteristic function smooth enough, define its
m
-th cumulant
\kappa_{m}(X)
by

\kappa_{m}(X):=(-i)^{m}\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}^{m}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{\mathrm{d}}^{m}t}\log\mathbb{E}e^{itX}\bigg|_{t=0}.

In fact, we can express the
m
-th cumulant in terms of the
j
-th moments with
j\leq m
.

\kappa_{m}(X)=\sum_{\pi\vdash[m]}(-1)^{|\pi|-1}(|\pi|-1)!\prod_{B\in\pi}\mathbb{E}[X^{|B|}].

Conversely,

\mathbb{E}[X^{j}]=\sum_{\pi\vdash[j]}\prod_{B\in\pi}\kappa_{|B|}(X).

It is easy to verify that for all
m\geq 3
, the
m
-th cumulant of standard normal distribution vanishes. So for some standardized random variable
X
(i.e.,
\mathbb{E}X=0,\mathrm{Var}(X)=1
) with decaying cumulants, we may expect that the distribution of
X
is close to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. To be precise, we have the following Berry-Essen type bound (Theorem 2.4 in
[
DJS22
]
)

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
\mathbb{E}X=0,\mathrm{Var}(X)=1
and there exists
\gamma\geq 0,\Delta>0
such that

|\kappa_{m}(X)|\leq\frac{(m!)^{1+\gamma}}{\Delta^{m-2}},\;m\geq 3,

then there exists some constant
C>0

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)|\leq\frac{C}{\Delta^{1/(1+2\gamma)}},

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
