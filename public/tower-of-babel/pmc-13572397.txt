Synthesis, characterization and comparative study of Zn, Fe, and Cu MOFs-impregnated polysulfone mixed-matrix membranes for CO2/N2 gas separation
Authors: Yinglian Qin, Ghulam Rasool, Muhammad Sarfraz, Hamid Raza, Muneerah Alomar, Maryam Al Huwayz, Shili Li, Adeolu A. Adediran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Decarbonization from fossil-combusted flue gas is crucial for recovering carbon sources and mitigating global warming risks. Composite membranes made of polysulfone (PSF) were created by doping Fe, Cu, and Zn metal-organic frameworks (MOFs). Synthesized MOF nanomaterials and membranes were characterized using advanced techniques to assess their structural, crystalline, morphological, thermal and decarbonization properties. Chemical characteristics of materials assessed through FTIR spectroscopy corroborated formation of MOFs nanocrystals. Crystalline phases of prepared MOF-based materials were established by XRD patterns. SEM-EDX micrographs indicated decent surface topography, uniform nanofiller distribution and compatible filler-matrix adhesion. Characteristic temperatures and improved thermal stability of membranes were inferred by TGA thermograms. Decarbonization efficiency of produced membranes determined by single- and mixed-gas permeation experiments using Gas Permeation rig indicated CO2 permeability improvement of about 2, 2.2 and 3.8 along with CO2/N2 permselectivity enhancement of 1.6, 1.5 and 2.1 for Cu, Zn and Fe based composite membranes, respectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-51315-2.

Introduction

Ever-increasing release of greenhouse gas, mainly constituting CO2, has led to global efforts to develop energy-efficient and cost-effective CO2 reduction technologies1. Cutting down on human-caused carbon dioxide emissions and reducing atmospheric concentrations of greenhouse gases has become widely recognized as one of the most demanding environmental tests of contemporary era. One strategy for lowering these dangerous CO2 emissions is known as carbon capture and storage (CCS) as schematically shown in Figure S12.

Despite advances in several technologies and techniques, removing CO2 from flue gas streams remains an important problem. Finding carbon-capture materials that have both good separation performance and minimal capital cost is just as crucial as developing novel methods. Current amine-based absorption processes strive from high energy penalties, solvent losses, and equipment corrosion issues3. Alternatively, membrane-based gas separation technology has appeared as a promising approach attributed to its less energy consumption, small footprint, and ease of scaling up4. Particularly, incorporating porous nanofillers (e.g., graphene oxide (GO), zeolites, metal-organic frameworks into polymeric membranes to fabricate mixed-matrix membranes (MMMs) have extensively been studied to encounter the permeance-selectivity compromise of pure polymer membranes5–8. From the comparison made in the previously published work, membrane technology is superior to older technologies as shown in Figure S29–11.

As wonder materials, graphene and graphene oxide have also permeated all scientific disciplines. Graphene’s oxidized compound, also known as graphene oxide, may be thought of as chemically functionalized building blocks enabling the production of novel porous graphene materials, is one of their most appealing features but the precise regulation of pore shape and pore size is crucial12. On the other hand, zeolites are also well known for their versatility as a framework. Physical and hydrothermal stability, non-toxic nature, improved surface areas, interchangeable cations, and economic benefits all contribute to their broad use. Even though zeolite applications frequently rely on their microporous properties, they might place diffusional restrictions on large reactant and product molecules13. Activity of zeolites for CO2 separation from flue gas is delineated in Figure S314.

MOFs have emerged to be a diversified class of crystalline materials due to their extraordinarily high porosity with upto 90% void volume accompanied with interior surface areas surpassing 5000 m2/g. These unique features make MOFs attractive for prospective uses in clean energy, especially as storage medium for gases and as elevated adsorbent materials to suit various separation requirements. In addition to these properties, MOFs are extremely intriguing because their organic and inorganic structural components are extraordinarily variable. The importance of further applications in catalysis, thin-film devices, membranes, and biological imaging is on the rise. The very wide surface areas, configurable pore diameters, and programmable internal surface characteristics of MOFs are well-known15. Several processes involved in CO2 capture including adsorption, storage and membrane-based separation are using MOFs16. Also, MOFs are being used to separate CO2 from hydrocarbons as highlighted in Figure S417.

MOFs are constructed from metal-containing nodes (e.g., Zn, Fe, Cu) interlinked by organic ligands via strong coordination bonds18. One of the widely investigated MOF for gas separation applications is based on M-BDC (where M = Zn, Fe, Cu)19. Nevertheless, the realization of MOFs potential in large-scale gas separation membranes remains a daunting challenge. The main hurdle is the poor MOF/polymer interfacial compatibility, which leads to non-ideal particle dispersion, aggregation, and interfacial defects in MMMs20. Polysulfone, polyether sulfone, polyimide, polycarbonate, polyacrylate, and poly-acetylene are important polymers employed in gas-purification membranes. Amorphous structure coupled with high glass-transition temperature (Tg) suggesting better permeability and selectivity ratio are the selection criteria for these polymers. The resultant MMMs exhibit impaired separation performance and mechanical strength compared to expectations from pristine MOFs. Additionally, the high sensitivity of common MOFs to humid flue gases and liquid solvents hinders their applications in post-combustion carbon capture and other related processes21. Pictorial representation of CO2 from flue gas via membrane process is shown in Figure S522.

To address the above separation process and material synthesis challenges, we have conducted systematic study to correlate the physiochemical properties of Zn, Fe, Cu BDC MOFs (in terms of chemical compositions, water stability, pore textures, particle size/morphology) to their dispersion within polysulfone matrix and the ultimately resultant N2/CO2 separation performances. With the chemical structure depicted in Figure S6, polysulfone exhibits superior electrical characteristics, strength, stiffness, and creep resistance at room temperature23. It can be utilized for ultra-filtration, microfiltration, and gas separation systems because of its innate chemical resistance. It makes it easier to manufacture membranes with regulated pore sizes24–27.

Our discovery in this study not only provides mechanistic insights into Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-MOF/PSF interfacial interaction but also is of importance for expeditiously tailor-designing robust and high-performance MOF/polymer membranes for industrial gas separations. Additionally, the humidity and solvent stability problems of some well-known MOFs such as Zn/Fe/Cu-BDC are referred to as more hurdles for real-world applications. To overcome these barriers, we seek to clarify the structures and separations performance relationship of MOF/polymer membranes in terms of connecting their intrinsic physiochemical properties for Zn/Fe/Cu-BDC MOFs with physical dispersion behaviors into polysulfone polymer matrix as well as final CO2/N2 separation performance. This knowledge is essential not only for the understanding of complex MOF/polymer interactions but also for the rational design of stable and high-performance gas separation membranes. At present, data available from utilizing micro-sized Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-BDC MOFs in polysulfone resin to separate CO2 from N2 also implies comparative studies.

It is expected that the loading of Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-BDC MOF nanoparticles in polysulfone matrix will significantly improve its carbon capture performance, which could be attributed to their high surface areas with extraordinarily strong affinity toward CO2 molecules. It is essential to improve the poor interaction between nanofiller particles and polymer chains at their interfaces for better carbon-separation performance of hybrid membranes. It will permit us to fully exploit the advantages of Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-BDC MOFs and enhance separation properties for prepared membranes. In the present work, Zn, Fe, and Cu-BDC MOFs are used to prepare polysulfone (PSF) based composite membranes. The goal is to improve their decarbonization efficiency, thermal stability and ensure even dispersal of the fillers in polymer matrix.

Several features can upgrade the decarbonization performance of polymeric membranes. The properties that must be considered include the type of solvent and filler loading, the choice of filler types, degassing and sonication steps, curing temperatures during fabrication, and most importantly the polymer matrix used. We currently cannot control them all at once. Our attention is therefore devoted to the study of the effect of Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-BDC MOF nanofillers concentration on gas separation properties.

Experimental

Materials

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Merck), copper (II) nitrate trihydrate (Merck), iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (Uni-chem) and terephthalic acid (BDC) (Sigma-Aldrich) were utilized for concocting Fe-, Cu- and Zn-BDC MOFs. Polysulfone pellets (E6020P grade supplied by ULTRASON) were used to synthesize membranes; this grade exhibited 92,000 g/mol molecular weight, 230 °C Tg, and 0.95 g/cm3 bulk density. Nanocrystals of Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-based MOFs - concocted by solvothermal method - correspondingly rendering reddish brown, greenish blue and white colored solids were used as fillers for preparing composite membranes. 99.1% pure N, N’-dimethylformamide (DMF), represented by chemical formula HCON(CH3)2 having 73.1 g/mol molar mass was purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

Synthesis of Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-BDC MOFs

Nanocrystals of Fe-, Cu-, and Zn-BDC MOFs were concocted through solvothermal method utilizing DMF as organic solvent as pictorially described in Figure S7. The synthesis commenced with the preparation of separate solutions of zinc nitrate hexahydrate, copper (II) nitrate trihydrate, and iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate, which served as the metal nodes for their respective MOF structures. Stoichiometric quantities of 0.5 mmol of each metal salt and 0.5 mmol of BDC were thoroughly dissolved in 5 mL of DMF in individual vials28. The solutions were then subjected to sonication at 40 °C for half an hour to ensure homogeneity.

After sonication, the solutions were transferred to separate 100 mL of Teflon-lined autoclaves and placed in an oven. The temperature increased at a rate of 2 °C per minute until reaching 100 °C, at which point the temperature was maintained for 24 h. Subsequently, the solutions were allowed to cool at the same rate of 2 °C per minute until attaining 25 °C and were left undisturbed at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) for an additional 12 h. The resulting precipitates were filtered, washed multiple times with pure DMF, and dehydrated at 25 °C.

MOFs activation

The as-synthesized MOFs were activated by removing the solvent molecules occupying the pores using the solvent exchange method. As-synthesized MOFs were soaked in methanol and stirred continuously at 25 °C for 24 h to allow the replacement of DMF with the more volatile methanol. The process was repeated three times by replacing fresh methanol at intervals of 24 h. This resulted in the residual DMF being removed from the membrane pores, thereby activating the active sites MOF nanocrystals, thus enhancing their adsorption performance, and improving the reliability of the material for further applications and characterization.

Preparation of composite membranes

The clean polysulfone membrane and 10 wt% loadings of M-BDC MOF nanofillers, with M = Fe, Cu and Zn, were synthesized via a phase inversion method29. The base polymer pellets were initially dried in a vacuum oven at 60–70 °C for the period of 4 h to remove absorbed moisture and gases as PSF is known to be hygroscopic. Furthermore, a polymer solution with good homogeneity was prepared by dissolving 1 g of dried PSF pellets into 5 ml of N, N’-dimethylformamide solvent with incessant stirring for 24 h at elevated temperature in a beaker covered to avoid evaporation of the solvent. The solution obtained was placed in an ultrasonic bath (40 °C) for 30 min to remove trapped gases and impurities.

The homogeneous casting solution was afterwards coated on a spotless glass plate by using a doctor blade at a height of 100 μm, and the cast film was immediately submerged into a water bath to cause phase inversion for making anisotropic dense membrane with thin selective layer. Once coagulated, the membrane was dried gradually for 7–8 h under covered condition to ensure proper evaporation of solvent.

M-BDC MOF-PSF MMMs were prepared as follows; the method was repeated additionally containing 10 wt% M-BDC MOF nanofillers (100 mg MOF in 900 mg PSF) incorporated 5 ml of DMF solvent before the membrane casting. Schematics of film preparation method are pictorially delineated in Figure S8.

The well-controlled fabrication process allowed the preparation of uniform morphology and good-dispersed filler PSF and M-BDC MOF-PES MMMs for further characterization and performance testing in the field of membrane separation.

Characterization of MOF nanocrystals

Detailed understanding of mesoporous MOF nanocrystals is essential for establishing correlation between their structure and properties, as well as their ability to tailor absorption function for practical application. Multi-technique analysis is applied to clarify intrinsic features of these porous nanomaterials, such as crystallinity, porosity, surface area, and chemical composition.

Powder x-ray diffraction (P-XRD) is the fundamental practice to identify crystallinity and phase purity of MOFs in addition to obtaining structural parameters30,31. In complement to P-XRD, SC-XRD analysis gives detailed insight into atomic-level disposition and coordination environments of atoms in the MOF structure32,33.

Spectroscopy methods such as FTIR34, Raman spectroscopy35, and NMR spectroscopy36 provide valued information on chemical composition, bonding, and local environments in MOF structures. Thermal analysis techniques (TGA, DSC, etc.) allow the estimation of thermal stability and decomposition behavior of MOF crystals37,38.

SEM39 and TEM40 provide visual information on MOF morphology, particle size, and structural properties at different magnification scales. EDX41,42 can be employed to measure the chemical composition and oxidation state of constituting elements of MOFs.

A full understanding of the structural, compositional, and functional properties of MOFs is provided using a combination of these characterization methods which aids their rational design and optimization for myriad applications.

Characterization of membranes

Structure-property relationships of synthesized doped membranes are explored using advanced characterization methods. Gas permeation tests offer information about membranes’ permeability and selectivity, which are crucial factors in separation performance43–45. FTIR investigation provides the chemical composition of the membrane and functional groups present on it46. In optical microscopy, the study of physical properties, like crystallization, morphology, and multiphase structure can be performed as well as surface defects or modifications47. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) is an efficient technique for evaluating the thermal stability of polymer components and quantifying volatile or moisture present in the membrane48. Scanning electron microscopy in conjunction with EDX analyzes surface morphology, cross-section structure and elemental composition of membranes in detail49. During the assessment of membrane capacity, the dynamic process associated with the performance of membranes is especially relevant50. Hitachi S-4300SE/N SEM instrument was helpful in investigating surface morphology and nanofillers dispersion in polymer matrix.

FTIR (JSCO) operated in 400–4000 cm− 1 helped assess chemical structure and information on type of functional groups on concocted samples. TGA 851 (Mettler Toledo) instrument was employed to secure thermal properties. CO2 gas permeability and CO2/N2 permselectivity were reckoned via gas permeation unit functioned in constant volume (variable pressure) mode.

Gas transport quantification

The gas permeability system comprises various components essential for conducting permeation experiments. It consists of cylinders as a source of gases, gas pressure-regulating valves, pressure gauges to monitor pressure of feed gas, a small gas tank to receive permeate, and a vacuum pump to evacuate reservoir. Most crucial component is steel cell that houses membrane during gas permeation process (see Figure S9). Pressure-driven permeation measurements for single gases are performed using a gas permeation apparatus. All membrane specimens had a 5.1 cm2 active permeation area. Prior to conducting permeation measurements, the membrane sheets were subjected to heat treatment at 160 °C (with a heating/cooling rate of 16 °C/h) to ensure the removal of any residual water content, organic residues, and/or volatile compounds.

During the permeation experiments, the pure gas was introduced to the membrane mounted in a temperature-controlled cell made of stainless steel, with the upstream surface of the membrane exposed to incoming feed gas stream. Pressure of gas stream exiting the cell was measured using a pressure gauge.

The gas permeation measurements provide the gas permeability of a specific gas through the membrane structure. Furthermore, these experiments are a convenient way to study the effect of additives and fillers on the transport resistance during transport of different gases through membranes.

In a gas permeation evaluation setup, the membrane is placed within a sealed chambered cell, and transporting gas is introduced to membrane surface at predetermined pressure. This setup allows for the determination of the membrane’s selectivity and permeability towards these gases. Employing a single gas permeation cell, the permeability and separation performance (perm-selectivity) of transporting selected gases through the membranes are reckoned. Results and discussion

MOFs characterization

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis represented in Fig. 1 was conducted to elucidate the structural characteristics and functional groups in Fe-, Cu- and Zn-BDC MOFs. These spectra were acquired in wavenumber range of 4000 –400 cm− 1, providing valuable insights into the MOF structures’ bonding nature and coordination environment.

Fig. 1FTIR spectra of nanocrystals of Fe-BDC, Cu-BDC and Zn-BDC MOFs.

FTIR spectra of nanocrystals of Fe-BDC, Cu-BDC and Zn-BDC MOFs.

Fe-BDC MOF’s broadband at 3428 cm− 1 is ascribed to O-H stretching of coordinated water or hydroxyl groups as shown in Fig. 1. The 1667 cm− 1 and 1583 cm− 1 peaks represent asymmetric and symmetric stretching of carboxylate groups coordinated to Fe3+ ions, respectively. Peak at 1381 cm− 1 corresponds to C-O stretching vibration. Fingerprint region below 1000 cm− 1 shows characteristic peaks for Fe-O stretching and aromatic ring deformations. Notably, the relative intensities and slight shifts in peak positions compared to Cu-BDC reflect different coordination environment and metal-ligand interactions in Fe-BDC structure.

FTIR spectra of Cu-BDC MOF registered in Fig. 1 exhibits several distinctive peaks indicative of organic linker and metal-ligand interactions. The broadband centered at 3566 cm-1 can be ascribed to O-H stretching vibrations of coordinated or adsorbed water molecules. The 1663 cm-1 and 1579 cm-1 peaks correspond to the carboxylate groups’ asymmetric and symmetric stretching modes (COO-) coordinated to the Cu2+ centers, respectively. The sharp peak at 1391 cm-1 is assigned to the C-O stretching vibration, while the bands in 1250 –1000 cm-1 range are characteristics of the C-H in-plane bending modes of the aromatic rings. The low-frequency region (below 800 cm-1) shows peaks related to the aromatic rings’ Cu-O stretching vibrations and out-of-plane bending modes.

In this regard, the Zn-BDC MOF spectrum (Fig. 1) has unique characteristics when compared with the Cu-BDC and Fe- BDC spectra. Wide band observed at 3156 cm− 1 can be attributed to O-H stretching vibrations of water molecules coordinated solvent or structural hydroxyl group. The strong absorptions at 1586 cm− 1 and 1378 cm− 1 are associated with the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of carboxylate groups bonded to Zn2+ ions, respectively. The characteristic peak at 1502 cm− 1 is assigned to the stretching C = C vibration of aromatic rings. In the fingerprint of both samples below 1000 cm− 1, peaks corresponding to Zn-O stretching vibration and aromatic ring deformation are observed. The spectral discrepancies between Zn-BDC, particularly differences in peak position and relative intensity, are indicative of the special coordination of geometry and metal-ligand interactions present for the Zn MOF framework.

XRD

The XRD shapes shown in Fig. 2 were utilized for the structural characterization of three types of MOFs which all consist of individual metal cages connected by linker BDCs. The MOFs are abbreviated as Fe-MOF, Cu-MOF, and Zn-MOF which represent the metal ions [Fe (III), Cu (II) and Zn (II)] incorporated within their frameworks.

Fig. 2XRD patterns of: (a) Fe-BDC MOF, (b) Cu-BDC MOF, and (c) Zn-BDC MOF.

XRD patterns of: (a) Fe-BDC MOF, (b) Cu-BDC MOF, and (c) Zn-BDC MOF.

The crystalline diffraction pattern for the Fe-MOF, having lower intensity peaks, is presented in Figure 2a. Some strong peaks are found at 2θ = 8.3318°, 9.8664°, 10.6135°, 17.9233°, 19.8012° and 25.1752°. The peak intensities and positions are subtly different to Cu-BDC (shown in Figure 2b) due to the change in metal-center, or more probably altered unit cell parameters. However, the lower intensities of the peaks and broader reflections imply a low completion level of long-range structural order, which can be associated with the presence of defect sites or amorphous component in Fe-MOF.

The Cu-MOF (Fig. 2b) also possesses a series of sharp peaks within 2θ range of 5° to 50°, suggesting its good crystallinity structure. The characteristic peaks were observed at 2θ = 8.3116°, 9.8462°, 10.5938°, 17.9031°, 19.7810°, and 25.1532° positions. The strong peak at 9.8462° indicates a preferable crystallographic plane of Cu-BDC MOF.

For the Zn-MOF (Figure 2c), well-defined sharp Bragg reflections in the diffractogram indicate that a crystalline framework structure is present. The most intense peaks were detected at 2θ values of about 11.8049°, 12.1280°, 16.9338°, 23.7186°, 25.1194° and 25.4350°. The highest peak at 11.8049° is assigned to the principal crystallographic plane of Zn-MOF. The pattern is like the well-known MOF-5 (Zn4O(BDC)3) framework, indicating a closely related topology.

The XRD diffraction patterns of the as-prepared MOF materials are shown in Fig. 2. Well-defined and intense Bragg reflections in the Zn-MOF and Cu-MOF patterns suggest that highly crystalline frameworks with long-range structural order are formed. Comparison of the XRD pattern showed that Cu-MOF is more crystalline than Fe-MOF, which may be related to structural defects and/or amorphous film formation.

Crystallite sizes were calculated following an in-depth peak profile analysis from the FWHM values of the most intense diffraction peaks. The estimated crystallite size for Fe-BDC MOF was 53.94 nm. For the Cu-BDC MOF, crystallite size was reported as 50.30 nm. The Zn-BDC MOF had a higher crystallite size of 96.56 nm, in four significant figures.

Synchrotron X-ray powder diffraction patterns of synthesized MOFs [Fe-BDC (Fig. 3), Cu-BDC (Fig. 4) and Zn-BDC (MOF-5) (Fig. 5)] closely matched experimental literature data and confirmed successful synthesis and structural integrity. In Fe-BDC, it was observed that diffraction peaks at 2θ = 6.98°, 8.64°, 17.37° and 21.21° are analogous to the four diffractions of Fe-BDC-DMF, indicating that a characteristic MIL-101 topology framework structure has been reserved.

SEM analysis of Fe-BDC MOF

The scanning electron microscopy images of the Fe-BDC MOF (Fig. 6) demonstrate a unique morphology characterized by layered structures and hierarchical surface textures. SEM images of Fe-BDC MOF under various resolution are shown in Figure S12.

TGA-DSC analysis of Fe MOF

The thermogravimetric analysis and the differential scanning calorimetry of Fe-BDC MOF indicate a complex thermal behavior related to its hierarchical structure and composition, as shown in Fig. 7. A multi-step weight loss is evident in the TGA curve, an initial release of surface-adsorbed and coordinated solvent molecules (≈ 5% mass loss) occurring at 100 °C, followed by a gradual decay of the organic framework (≈ 30% mass decrease) from 100 to 300 °C. A larger scale of weight loss (≈ 20%) appeared when temperature rose up from 300 to 500 °C that was probably due to primary decomposition of MOF lattice.

The fifth steep weight loss at 500–600 °C suggests the complete collapse of the framework, corresponding to ~ 70% mass loss. In the meantime, DSC curve would give information on the energetics of those processes because it manifests an initial endothermic peak related to solvent evaporation following with a broad exothermic region (100–400 °C) resulting from slow decomposition of organic linker and/or crystalline phase transition. The strong exothermic peak of 500–600 °C can be attributed to the last phase decomposition of MOF.

TGA-DSC analysis of Cu MOF

The TGA curve of the Cu-BDC MOF is shown in Fig. 8, which indicates its thermal stability and decomposition behavior from 25 to 600 °C. The profile indicates a multi-stage weight loss process, which is associated with structural complexity of MOF. A plateau, which is quite stable should be noticed at 200 °C with negligible weight loss that the solution has no loose solvent molecules. A drastic weight loss of 20% in this range (200–250 °C) and an endothermic peak prominent in DSC curve may be attributed to release of coordinated solvent molecules as well as decomposition of the organic linker.

The apparent weight loss occurs in the temperature range of 250–400 °C, and a sharp drop of initial weight (upto 35%) occurs at higher temperature (say 450 °C) indicates main decomposition of the framework. The broad exothermic peak observable in the temperature range of 250–400 °C designates complex decomposition reactions and phase transformations. A last exothermic event occurring from 450 to 600 °C with slight weight loss refers to the crystallization of copper oxide remains. This thermal curve also shows that the Cu-BDC MOF has good stability in the temperature region < 200 °C, suggesting its feasibility in gas separation applications under mild conditions.

TGA-DSC analysis of Zn MOF

The TGA and DSC curves of Zn-BDC MOF, with its complex thermal response due to the high structural complexity, are accessible in Fig. 9. The TGA behavior of the system (Fig. 9) revealed a moderate weight loss (~ 3%) below 100 °C, which is attributed to surface-adsorbed water or residual solvent, as supported by an initial endothermic peak in the DSC trace. A second stage of weight loss (≈ 10%) at 100–200 °C with an intense endotherm indicates loss of coordinated solvent molecules or beginning framework breakdown.

From 200 to 400 °C, a slow weight loss (≈ 10%) and a broad exothermic peak in the DSC curve suggest that filling vacancies at the MOF structure and decomposition of organic linker increased with temperature. The sharp weight loss (≈ 35%) occurring in the range of 400–500 °C with an intense exothermic peak corresponds to the main collapse of flexible lattice and transition to ZnO phase. The last step (500–600 °C) is characterized by a residual mass ≈ 42% indicating insignificant additional weight loss and likely refers to stable zinc oxide species. This thermal profile reveals moderate stability of Zn-BDC-MOF until 200 °C which is a key factor for this MOF to be used at elevated temperatures.

Relative thermal stability of MOFs

The relative thermal stability of Zn-BDC, Cu-BDC, and Fe-BDC MOFs was demonstrated through comparative TGA analysis (Fig. 10). The Cu-BDC MOF demonstrates the highest thermal stability, almost 100% of mass is retained before degradation at 200 °C that followed by a breakneck weight loss showing solvate cliff structure. Zn-BDC MOF shows intermediate stability, with gradual weight loss starting at 100 °C, consistent with stepwise guest molecule removal and framework decomposition.

Fig. 10TGA (a) and DTG (b) curves of Fe, Cu and Zn MOFs.

TGA (a) and DTG (b) curves of Fe, Cu and Zn MOFs.

Fe-BDC MOF demonstrates the lowest thermal stability, with continuous weight loss throughout heating, suggesting a more flexible structure or weaker metal-ligand interactions. The final residual masses at 600 °C follow the order Cu-BDC (≈ 39%) < Fe-BDC (≈ 44%) < Zn-BDC (≈ 37%), corresponding to metal oxide residues. Differences in thermal behavior are attributed to variations in metal-ligand bond strengths, framework architectures, and guest molecule incorporation. The observed thermal stability trend is Fe-BDC > Cu-BDC > Zn-BDC.

Based on contact angle measurements provided in images shown in Fig. 20, the surface properties of pristine polysulfone membrane and composite membranes impregnated with 10% loadings each of the three MOF nanoparticles in relation to their potential performance in CO2/N2 gas separation applications can be interpreted. The virgin polysulfone membrane (Fig. 20a) shows a contact angle of 77.2° (mean of 77.8° and 76.6), implying moderately hydrophobic nature of the surface. It is of interest for gas separation that hydrophobicity can be useful to mitigate competitive water adsorption, a common challenge in membrane separation process under real working conditions with humidity.

When 10% Fe-MOF is incorporated in the polysulfone matrix (Fig. 20b, the contact angle reduces to about 74.5° (mean of 74.6° and 74.4°). This hydrophilicity increment slightly indicates that the Fe-MOF implants a few polar groups on the membrane surface. In case of CO2/N2 separation, this type of material nature could increase the adsorption of CO2 and thereby potentially CO2/N2 selectivity, since CO2 has a higher quadrupole moment than N2.

The 10% Cu-MOF/PSF MMM (Fig. 20c) has the maximum contact angle among all doped membranes at about θ = 83.8° (average of 83.1° and 84.5°). An increased hydrophobicity would be beneficial in the CO2/N2 separation due to reducing water competition and even possibly promoting higher diffusion of non-polar gases in the membrane.

Lastly, the 10% Zn-MOF/PSF MMM (Fig. 20d) exhibits a contact angle of about 81.1° (average of 80.5° and 81.7°), which is slightly higher than the pristine membrane but lower than the Cu-MOF doped membrane. This moderate increase in hydrophobicity could provide a balance between enhanced gas diffusion and selective CO2 adsorption.

As shown in Fig. 21, the incorporation of MOFs into PSF matrix significantly improved CO2 permeability and CO2/N2 selectivity compared to that of pristine PSF counterpart. The unfilled PSF membrane showed a CO2 permeability of about 26 Barrer and a CO2/N2 selectivity of ca. 23, which is in good agreement with literature data for pristine PSF membranes. Compared to pristine PSF membrane, the enhanced CO2/N2 selectivity of metal-BDC membranes can be attributed to shared contributions of precise molecular sieving via polymer matrix and superior adsorption capabilities of porous MOF nanofillers. Owing to the presence of selective functional groups on them, these metallic-BDC MOFs help improve preferential CO2 transport through MMMs structure. Other influencing parameters promoting CO2 permeation and blocking N2 transport include size-selective molecular sieving, facilitated transport through functionalized sites, preferential CO2 adsorption, and optimized interfacial interactions.

The introduction of MOFs resulted in significant enhancement of membrane performance, and uplifted CO2 permeability fell in the range of 52–98 Barrer (depending on MOF type) with a N2 permeability ranging from ∼1.1–2.1 Barrer. The Fe-MOF/PSF MMM rendered best trade among all MMMs tested (CO2 permeability of 98 Barrer) and most significant CO2/N2 selectivity (49), showing a good balance of gas diffusion rate and molecular sieving ability. Cu-MOF/PSF and Zn-MOF/PSF membranes also exhibited improved CO2/N2 selectivity of 36 and 34 respectively, with corresponding CO2 permeabilities of 52 and 56 Barrer.

Mixed-gas permeation results

CO2 and N2 permeability as well as CO2/N2 permselectivity data presented in Fig. 22 indicate permeable and selective behavior of a 50:50 N2:CO2 gas mixture while permeating through the membranes. As compared to single-gas permeation results, both the CO2 permeability and CO2/N2 real selectivity through the membranes show a slight decline as shown in Fig. 22. This marginal reduction in separation properties can be attributed to the reasonable adsorption of N2 molecules, thus impeding CO2 permeation. Metallic-based frameworks with open-metal sites or polar functional groups generally render high affinity for quadrupolar CO2 molecules as compared to nonpolar N2 molecules, thus leading to higher CO2 solubility. Prepared MOFs are selected based on their specific pore diameters to allow CO2 (kinetic diameter = 3.3 Å) to pass while restricting N2 (kinetic diameter = 3.64 Å), enhancing the diffusivity-based selectivity. Furthermore, effect of concentration polarization can also result in decreased permselectivity of both the gases.

Single- and mixed-gas permeation results demonstrate a key role of MOFs chemistry and morphology in modulating gas transport properties. This higher performance of Fe-MOF/PSF hybrid membrane compared to other counterparts is due to the stronger interaction with CO2 molecules, because of the metal center’s affinity, and a better interfacial compatibility and dispersion in PSF matrix, resulting in a synergistic enhancement of selective permeability. The present results suggest that MOFs- and metalated MOFs-based MMMs can indeed be considered as promising candidates in advanced decarbonization technology and emphasize the design of tailored fillers to break conventional permeability-selectivity trade-off.

Conclusion

Three types of MOFs were synthesized via solvothermal method by reacting different metal salts (Cu, Zn, Fe) with BDC organic linker. Thorough characterization of activated MOF nanocrystals provided deep insights into their structural, crystalline, morphological, thermal and phase properties. 10% weight loading of activated MOF nanocrystals were impregnated into polysulfone matrix to fabricate mixed-matrix membranes for efficient carbon capture applications. Concocted membranes showed effective performance of CO2/N2 separations.

The gas transport properties of MOFs-loaded membranes were significantly different from pure PSF counterpart; all the MMMs created with MOFs showed enhanced CO2 permeability in contrast to pristine PSF membrane. The most significant increase was obtained in the case of Fe-BDC MOF-doped MMM followed by Zn- and Cu-BDC MOF-filled MMMs. The same was also true for CO2/N2 selectivity, whereby Cu-BDC was the most selective among all the MMMs followed by Zn-BDC, though at a small margin. Remarkably, while the Fe-BDC MMMs showed outstanding gains in permeabilities, it was neither as good as its Cu- or Zn-counterparts in terms of selectivity, although it was better compared to pure PSF. It is, therefore, obvious that the role of metal center choice in BDC MOF is of utmost importance in achieving tailor-made membrane performance which could be beneficial for carbon capture technologies and improvement in greenhouse gas mitigation strategies.
