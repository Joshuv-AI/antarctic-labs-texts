Guselkumab as a Treatment Adjunct for Pyoderma Gangrenosum While Maintaining Clinical Remission of Ulcerative Colitis
Authors: Rohan Moghe, Alex J. Mathew, David Choi, David T. Rubin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis which can present as an extraintestinal manifestation of ulcerative colitis (UC). There is significant overlap in the pathophysiology of both diseases, including interleukin (IL)-23 overexpression. Guselkumab, a selective IL-23 inhibitor, has been shown to be an effective treatment option in UC and has successfully treated PG in case reports. However, there is limited literature on concomitantly treating PG and UC. We present a case in which guselkumab contributed to the treatment of PG in a patient with UC, while also maintaining clinical remission of UC.

INTRODUCTION

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the rectum and colon with complex etiology, often associated with extraintestinal manifestations.1 The pathophysiology of UC includes genetic factors, gut microbial dysregulation, and adaptive immune activation mediated primarily by effector T-cell (Th2 and Th17) response.2 Cytokines interleukin (IL)-17 and IL-23 have been shown to contribute to UC pathophysiology.2–4 Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a neutrophilic dermatosis that occurs as an extraintestinal manifestation in approximately 1.5% to 3.0% of patients with UC.1,5,6 IL-23, which aids in Th17 differentiation and proliferation, is often overexpressed in PG, leading to inflammation.7 Targeted IL-23 inhibitors have been shown to be effective in treating IL-23-mediated dermatologic conditions, including PG.8

Guselkumab is an IL-23 inhibitor and has been shown, in case reports, to be an effective option in treating PG.9–11 In the QUASAR trial, guselkumab was a safe and effective option for the treatment of UC and has become an increasingly common treatment choice.12 Given IL-23 overexpression in both PG and UC, guselkumab is a reasonable treatment option. To our knowledge, no cases describe guselkumab as a treatment adjunct for PG while maintaining clinical control of UC symptoms.

CASE REPORT

We present the case of a 24-year-old woman who developed a painful left lower extremity PG lesion in May 2025 (Figure 1).

She was diagnosed with UC at age 15, presenting with abdominal pain and rectal bleeding, and colonoscopy demonstrated pancolitis (E3 Montreal Classification), with a Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES) of 2. She initially responded to mesalamine, 6-mercaptopurine, and infliximab. She remained in remission on infliximab until 2022, when treatment was discontinued due to insurance-related reasons. In 2023, she was briefly switched to upadacitinib (45 mg/d) and then tacrolimus, which were both not tolerated. Repeat colonoscopy showed active inflammation and pseudopolyps (MES 1–2). At this time, she restarted infliximab, but cost again limited access.

Her symptoms returned in late March 2025, at which time her fecal calprotectin (FCP) was >8,000 mcg/g and no recent intestinal ultrasound or colonoscopy was on file. Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI) was 7. She then received guselkumab, with intravenous (IV) loading (200 mg) planned for weeks 0, 4, and 8 as per QUASAR induction dosing.12

Approximately 2 weeks after the first dose of IV guselkumab, she noticed a small, painful nodule on the tibial surface of her left leg. She did not have known trauma to the region, although she had developed similar lesions in the past. The lesion progressed with pain and purulent discharge, and it was incised and drained in the emergency department. She was discharged on trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole with a diagnosis of sebaceous cyst with abscess. Wound cultures obtained at this initial visit came back negative, excluding infection. She ultimately saw dermatology as an outpatient, where she was diagnosed with PG based on wound morphology, negative cultures, and history of UC. She was directly admitted for further management. She had no symptoms of colitis at that time, although on admission her white blood cell count was 18.3 × 103/μL, FCP was 1,073 mcg/g, and her C-reactive protein (CRP) was 109 mg/L. She was treated with IV cyclosporine (3 mg/kg divided twice daily dosing) and IV corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 40 mg daily), and she later transitioned to oral medications in addition to nonadherent Telfa, loose dressings, and clobetasol ointment. Biopsy was deferred, as the severity of her wound began to improve over the course of her hospitalization (Figure 2), and she was discharged on cyclosporine and a prednisone taper, with healing, but persistently active lesions.

After a 4-week interruption due to hospitalization, guselkumab was continued through the remainder of the loading phase (200 mg IV at weeks 4 and 8), and she completed a steroid taper in the first few weeks after discharge. She discontinued cyclosporine after 2 weeks due to insurance issues. In clinic follow-up in July (after full loading of guselkumab), the PG lesion appeared smaller. The patient continued to be in clinical remission from her UC (SCCAI of 0). She received maintenance dosing of 200 mg subcutaneous every 4 weeks subsequently. By September, her UC and PG were both completely controlled, and she reported no significant side effects to guselkumab. Her CRP in November 2025 was <3 mg/L.

DISCUSSION

This case is the first, to our knowledge, to show guselkumab maintaining clinical remission in UC while aiding in PG treatment. Although the patient experienced partial improvement of her PG lesions with prednisone and cyclosporine, sustained healing of her PG was achieved with guselkumab. Given her history of refractory UC and insurance constraints, guselkumab proved to be an effective adjunct in her treatment of both conditions, and the patient remains in clinical remission of her UC on guselkumab monotherapy as of her most recent follow-up.

IL-23 expression occurs in both diseases, and an association between the 2 has been described.3,4,7 In randomized controlled trials, corticosteroids, cyclosporine, and infliximab have been shown to successfully treat PG, and case reports demonstrate successful treatment of isolated PG with IL-23 inhibitors.7,13,14 Case reports have also demonstrated the efficacy of various biologics in treating both UC and PG; specifically, tumor necrosis factor-α blockers alone and concurrently with integrin receptor antagonists.15–17 However, an IL-23 inhibitor has not been previously described to treat PG to resolution while inducing and maintaining clinical remission; we believe this case report fills an important gap in the existing literature.

Of note, the interpretation of guselkumab alone treating the PG is confounded by the additional use of corticosteroids and short-term cyclosporine. The progressive healing of the skin and persistent control of both skin and bowel symptoms off cyclosporine supports its effective role in this case. It is worth noting that the PG lesion appeared within 2 weeks of her first guselkumab infusion. However, the patient had developed similar lesions in the past, before any guselkumab exposure. This PG lesion likely developed in the context of worsening systemic inflammation and possible pathergy after incision and drainage, as evidenced by persistently high inflammatory biomarkers, at the time, though the lesion improved following guselkumab initiation.
