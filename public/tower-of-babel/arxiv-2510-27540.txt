Linear Convergence and Error Bounds for Optimization Without Strong Convexity
Kira van Treek, Javier F. Peña, Juan C. Vera, Luis F. Zuluaga

Abstract
Many optimization algorithms—including gradient descent, proximal methods, and operator splitting techniques—can be formulated as fixed-point iterations (FPI) of continuous operators. When these operators are averaged, convergence to a fixed point is guaranteed when one exists, but the convergence is generally sublinear. Recent results establish linear convergence of FPI for averaged operators under certain conditions. However, such conditions do not apply to common classes of operators, such as those arising in piecewise linear and quadratic optimization problems. In this work, we prove that a local error-bound condition is both necessary and sufficient for the linear convergence of FPI applied to averaged operators. We provide explicit bounds on the convergence rate and show how these relate to the constants in the error-bound condition. Our main result demonstrates that piecewise linear operators satisfy local error bounds, ensuring linear convergence of the associated optimization algorithms. This leads to a general and practical framework for analyzing convergence behavior in algorithms such as ADMM and Douglas–Rachford in the absence of strong convexity. In particular, we obtain convergence rates that are independent of problem data for linear optimization, and depend only on the condition number of the objective for quadratic optimization.

Introduction.

A wide range of iterative numerical methods to solve optimization problems such as the gradient descent, the proximal point, proximal gradient, the Douglas-Rachford and the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), among many others, have in common that they can be stated by
iteratively applying an appropriate continuous operator
F:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to a given starting point. Namely, starting with an initial guess
x_{0}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, these methods iteratively apply the operator
F

x_{k+1}=F(x_{k})
, which is known as

(FPI). The aim of these methods is to obtain a sequence
x_{k}

Notice that the continuity of
F
implies that when such

F(x)=x.

We will denote the set of fixed-points associated to an operator

\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}:=\{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:F(x)=x\}.

When solving optimization problems,
F
is chosen such that the set
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}
is related to the set of optimal points for the optimization problem, in such fashion that any
x\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}
can be used to construct an optimal point.

(Gradient descent for unconstrained convex optimization)

Since the convergence of FPI is asymptotic, the iterative process is halted after a finite number of iterations
k
, once an iterate that is sufficiently close to a fixed point is achieved. This is determined when
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})
, the (Euclidean) distance to a fixed-point, is sufficiently small. Since this distance cannot be directly computed, a practical stopping criterion is to ensure that the residual, given by
\|F(x_{k})-x_{k}\|=\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|
, is sufficiently small. Thus, it is crucial to demonstrate that the operator
F
satisfies a
(Lipschitz) error-bound
condition that relates the distance to the fixed-points to the residual. This relationship ensures that the proposed stopping criterion for the iterative process is sound. If the operator does not satisfy such an error bound, the algorithm cannot guarantee a near-optimal solution after stopping.

Contracting operators satisfy a global error-bound condition and the corresponding algorithm converges linearly. Under strong convexity and smoothness assumptions all the operators in
Table

1
are contracting (see,
[
1
]
and
Section

4.1
). Indeed, the proofs of the linear convergence of these algorithms under such assumptions, which are scattered around in the literature
[
2
,
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
, and references therein]
, implicitly (and sometimes explicitly) use the contracting property of the corresponding operator.

It may seem logical to concentrate on designing contracting operators for optimization. However, an important consideration arises: Banach’s fixed-point theorem ensures the existence of a unique fixed point. Consequently, when multiple optimizers are present, it is reasonable to expect that the corresponding operator will not be contractive, as we should anticipate having more than one fixed point in such cases. In particular, in the nonstrongly convex case, it is important to consider operators beyond the contracting ones.

A weakened version of contraction is nonexpansion. An operator
N
is said to be
nonexpansive
if for all
x,y\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\|N(x)-N(y)\|\leq\|x-y\|.

(2)

Nonexpansive operators might not have any fixed points (e.g. translations); and when they do, the FPI sequence might not be convergent (e.g. rotations). A standard technique for finding fixed points of nonexpansive operators is to use the Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iteration, which involves applying the FPI to an
averaging
of the nonexpansive operator.

Given
0<\alpha<1
, an operator
F
is said to be
\alpha
-averaged
, or simply
averaged
, if it is defined as:

F(x)=(1-\alpha)x+\alpha N(x)

for some nonexpansive operator
N
. It is important to note that the set of fixed points remains unchanged by the averaging operation; that is,
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}=\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{N}
.
As stated in
Theorem

1
below, the FPI of averaged operators always converges to a fixed point if one exists. However, in general, this convergence is only sublinear, and error bounds are not guaranteed.

Example 2

(Gradient descent is an averaged operator)
.

Consider the gradient descent operator
F_{GD}(x)=x-\lambda\nabla f(x)
. By definition,
F_{GD}
is non-expansive if and only if for all
x
and
y
,

\|x-y\|^{2}\geq\|F_{GD}(x)-F_{GD}(y)\|^{2}=\|x-y\|^{2}-2\lambda(x-y)^{\intercal}(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))+\lambda^{2}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}.

Which is equivalent to,

(x-y)^{\intercal}(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2},

which is in turn equivalent to
f
being
\tfrac{\lambda}{2}
-smooth.
Now, we turn our attention to when is
F_{GD}
an averaged operator. Given
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We write the gradient descent operator as
F_{GD}(x)=x-\lambda\nabla f(x)=(1-\alpha)x+\alpha F_{GD}^{\alpha}(x)
, where
F_{GD}^{\alpha}(x):=(x-\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}\nabla f(x))
. Then
F_{GD}
is
\alpha
-averaged if and only if
F_{GD}^{\alpha}(x)
is non-expansive, which is equivalent to
f
being
\tfrac{\lambda}{2\alpha}
-smooth.

Theorem 1

(Convergence of averaged operators)
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
F:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an
\alpha
-averaged operator, such that

has fixed-points. Given
x_{0}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
x_{k+1}=F(x_{k})
for

. Then

(i)

x^{*}=\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}
exists and
F(x^{*})=x^{*}

[
6
, Thm. 5.14]
.

(ii)

The rate of convergence of
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\to 0
is of order
O(1/\sqrt{k})

[
7
]
.

In practice, parameters are chosen so that the fixed-point operators associated with various optimization algorithms are averaged. As discussed in detail in Section
4.1
, all the fixed-point operators associated with the algorithms listed in Table
1
are averaged, except for the Peaceman-Rachford operator. As elaborated in Section
4.1.2
, the Douglas-Rachford operator is the averaged version of the Peaceman-Rachford operator.

Theorem

1
is a fundamental result applicable to a wide array of optimization algorithms. However, it merely offers sublinear convergence and does not provide any specific error bounds. Recently, this result has been strengthened by showing that FPI of any averaged operator converges linearly to its fixed-points when the operator satisfies an error-bound condition, paving the way for generic techniques covering large classes of algorithms and/or models. In
[
8
]
this type of result is established under a

global
error-bound condition. However, this global error bound condition limits the applicability of the result, as piecewise linear operators typically satisfy only local error bound conditions. Such operators commonly arise in important classes of optimization problems that are not strongly convex, including linear and quadratic optimization. In
[
9
]
, the global error-bound condition is weakened to a local error-bound condition. However, the result requires the existence of a unique fixed-point which again limits the applicability of the result to the cases discussed above.

Contributions.

Here, we begin by enhancing the results in
[
9
,
8
]
by using a local error-bound condition similar to the one used in
[
9
]
with no extra assumptions beyond the averageness of the operator. We provide both upper and lower bounds for the associated linear rate of convergence, as well as bounds not only for the convergence in
distance
form (which coincides with the one given in
[

), but also in
sequence
form. More specifically, we show (
Theorem

2
and

)
that for an averaged operator

, the linear convergence of the associated FPI is equivalent to the following (
local
) error-bound condition for a suitable

:

\begin{gathered}\text{There exists $K_{F}>0$ such that }\\
\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq R\text{~implies~}\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq K_{F}\cdot\|F(x)-x\|.\end{gathered}

(3)

In particular, if the error-bound condition (
3
) holds for some
\alpha
-averaged operator
F
with

\mathcal{F}_{F}\neq\emptyset
, then the sequence of points
x_{k+1}=F(x_{k})
for
k=0,1,\dots
, generated by the FPI of
F
starting from some initial iterate

, converges linearly to

at a rate that can be bound in terms of the constant
K_{F}
in the error-bound condition (
3
) as follows

\operatorname{dist}(x_{k+1},\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq\rho\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}),\text{ and }\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq\left(1-\tfrac{1+\rho}{2}(1-\rho)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|,

where
\rho=\left(1-\tfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha K_{F}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Above, in the left, the linear convergence is stated in

form, while in the right, it is given in

form. The equivalence between the error-bound condition and the linear convergence of averaged operators can be exploited (

3
) to obtain the following upper and lower bound relationships between the constants defining the error-bound condition and the linear convergence of FPI for an

-averaged operator

1-\tfrac{1}{\tilde{K}_{F}}\leq\tilde{\rho}_{F}\leq\sqrt{1-\tfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha\tilde{K}_{F}^{2}}}\text{ and }\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha(1-\tilde{\rho}_{F}^{2})}}\leq\tilde{K}_{F}\leq\tfrac{1}{1-\tilde{\rho}_{F}},

where
{\tilde{\rho}_{F}}
and
\tilde{K}_{F}
denote the tightest constants such that the linear convergence and the error-bound condition respectively hold.

With these results established, our main result (
Theorem

4
) is to show that a wide range of optimization algorithms, when applied to a broad class of optimization problems, satisfy the error-bound condition (
3
) and therefore, achieve linear convergence. This result provides a powerful and generic framework for proving the linear convergence of optimization algorithms without relying on strong convexity assumptions. The algorithms we consider encompass, but are not limited to, gradient descent, proximal point methods, proximal gradient, Douglas-Rachford, and ADMM. Our analysis includes various fundamental types of optimization problems, notably linear optimization (LO) and convex quadratic optimization (QO). Furthermore, we cover least squares and its adaptations, such as LASSO and elastic net, along with support vector machines (SVM).

The error-bound condition guarantee presented in

We begin by providing a useful result about averaged operators.

Lemma 1
.

Let

From the characterization of

The result then follows by using the fact that
F(\hat{x})=\hat{x}
.
∎

5
).

To finish, we prove the upper bounds for the rates
\rho
and
\hat{\rho}

5
).
Let
\theta:=\smash{\tfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha K_{F}^{2}}}
. Clearly,
0<\theta

), and by (

\theta<1

0\leq u<1\text{ implies }(1-u)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\leq 1-\tfrac{1}{2}u.

(12)

By (

\rho\leq 1-\tfrac{1}{2}\theta

Theorem

2
allows us to recast the problem of proving and estimating the rate of linear convergence as the problem of computing the constant
K_{F}
in the generic error-bound condition (
3
).
In Section
4
, we will illustrate this by estimating in forthright fashion, the rate of linear convergence of different optimization algorithms to minimize well-known classes of functions.

By looking closely to the proof of
Theorem

2
, one can see that we have proven a stronger statement than the linear convergence of the FPI. We prove that if the error-bound condition (
3
) holds for
R>0
then the distance to the set of fixed-points decreases linearly when
F
is applied to any point in
\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq R\}
.
Next, in

we prove the converse of this statement, showing that the error-bound condition is necessary to obtain this form of linear convergence.

Proposition 3

(Linear convergence implies error bound)
.

Let
F:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be such that
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}\neq\emptyset
. Let
R>0

and
\rho_{F}<1
be such that

\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq R\text{ implies }\operatorname{dist}(F(x),\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq\rho_{F}\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}).

(13)

Then, the error-bound condition (
3
) holds for
R
, with constant
K_{F}=\smash{\tfrac{1}{1-\rho_{F}}}

Proof.

Let
x
be such that
\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})\leq R
holds. Let
\overline{x},\hat{x}\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}
be such that
\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})=\|x-\overline{x}\|
and
\operatorname{dist}(F(x),\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})=\|F(x)-\hat{x}\|
. Then,

\displaystyle\|x-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x-\hat{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x-F(x)\|+\|F(x)-\hat{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x-F(x)\|+\rho_{F}\|x-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle(\text{by~\eqref{eq.lc}})

Rearranging terms, we obtain,

\operatorname{dist}(x,\{x:F(x)=x\})=\|x-\bar{x}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho_{F}}\|x-F(x)\|.

(14)

∎

Theorem

2
and
Proposition

3
relate the rate of convergence
\rho_{F}
of the FPI to the error-bound condition constant
K_{F}
. Using these results we can upper and lower bound on the convergence rate in terms of the error-bound constant and viceversa. We state these bounds in
Corollary

1
and show that both sides of the bounds can be tight on Examples
3
and
4
below.

Corollary 1

(Error-bound condition and linear convergence bounds)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
F:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a continuous
\alpha
-averaged operator with
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}\neq\emptyset
.
Let
R>0
and define
\tilde{\rho}_{F}\in[0,1]
and
\tilde{K}_{F}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
as

\tilde{\rho}_{F}=\sup_{x\notin\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(F(x),\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})}{\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F})}\text{ and }\tilde{K}_{F}=\sup_{x\notin\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,\{x:F(x)=x\})}{\|F(x)-x\|},

the tightest constants such that both the linear convergence result (
13
), and the error-bound condition (
3
) hold. We have the following equivalent relations,

1-\frac{1}{\tilde{K}_{F}}\leq\tilde{\rho}_{F}\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha\tilde{K}_{F}^{2}}}\text{ and }\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha(1-\tilde{\rho}_{F}^{2})}}\leq\tilde{K}_{F}\leq\frac{1}{1-\tilde{\rho}_{F}}.

The next examples illustrate the tightness of the bounds on
Corollary

1
.

Example 3

(Convergence rates and error bounds for Gradient descent)
.

Consider gradient descent applied to the optimization problem
\min_{x}f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
. Note that
\nabla f(x)=x
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous. Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. The gradient descent operator for this problem is given by
F_{GD}(x)=x-\lambda\nabla f(x)=(1-\lambda)x
.
In fact,
F_{GD}
is a contraction with constant
\beta=1-\lambda<1
and unique fixed-point
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{GD}}=\{0\}
. The fixed-point iteration of
F_{GD}
converges at a global linear rate
\tilde{\rho}_{F_{GD}}=1-\lambda
to this fixed-point. Next, we compute
\tilde{K}_{F_{GD}}
. For any
x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{GD}})=\|x-0\|=\frac{1}{\lambda}\|x-F_{GD}(x)\|.

Hence,
F_{GD}
satisfies the global error bound with
\tilde{K}_{F_{GD}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}
. In particular,
\tilde{\rho}_{F_{GD}}=1-\frac{1}{\tilde{K}_{F_{GD}}}
.

Example 4

(Convergence rates and error bounds for a rotation)
.

Let
N_{\theta}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
denote the rotation by the angle
\theta
for some fixed
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. Let
F_{\theta}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(x+N_{\theta}(x))
be the corresponding half-averaged operator. Notice that
0
is the unique fixed point for
F_{\theta}
. Thus, for any
x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
we have
\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{\theta}})=\|x\|
.

Standard trigonometric identities imply that,
\|F_{\theta}(x)\|=\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)\|x\|
and
\|F_{\theta}(x)-x\|=\sin(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)\|x\|
.
Therefore,

\tilde{K}_{F_{\theta}}=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{\theta}})}{\|F_{\theta}(x)-x\|}=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|x\|}{\sin(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)\|x\|}=\frac{1}{\sin(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)}.

On the other hand,

\tilde{\rho}_{F_{\theta}}=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(F_{\theta}(x),\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{\theta}})}{\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{\theta}})}=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|F_{\theta}(x)\|}{\|x\|}=\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta).

It follows that

\tilde{\rho}_{F_{\theta}}=\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)=\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta)}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\tilde{K}_{F_{\theta}}^{2}}}.

3
Piecewise linear fixed-point operators.

In order to specialize the results in
Theorem

2
and
Proposition

3
to
fixed-point operators associated with optimization algorithms, we now focus on
the class of piecewise linear fixed-point operators which, as discussed later in Section
4
, arise ubiquitously in optimization.

Specifically, we show in
Theorem

4
that if an operator
F
is piecewise linear, then
F
satisfies the error-bound condition (
3
) by showing that piecewise linear operator
I-F
satisfies a
relative Hoffmann
bound
[
10
]
.
To show this formally, we use the following notation and terminology.

Definition 1

(Compatible collection)
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be nonempty polyhedra and
F_{i}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\;i=1,\dots,k
be affine operators. Then we say that
(F_{1},P_{1}),\dots,(F_{k},P_{k})
is a compatible collection if the following two conditions hold:

(i)

\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=P_{1}\cup\cdots\cup P_{k}
,

(ii)

F_{i}(x)=F_{j}(x)
for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and all
x\in P_{i}\cap P_{j}
.

Given a compatible collection
(F_{1},P_{1}),\dots,(F_{k},P_{k})
, we shall write
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}F_{i}|P_{i}
to denote the piecewise linear operator defined via

x\in P_{i}\mapsto F_{i}(x)
. Note that every piecewise linear operator
F
is of the form
F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}F_{i}|P_{i}
for some compatible collection
(F_{1},P_{1}),\dots,(F_{k},P_{k})
.

In what follows, given an operator
G:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
we will denote the
set of zeros
of the operator by
G^{-1}(0):=\{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:G(x)=0\}
.
Theorem

4
below relies on the following Hoffman-type constant.

Definition 2

(Relative Hoffman constant
[
10
]
)
.

Let
P\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
G:P\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an affine operator on
P
satisfying
G^{-1}(0)\cap P\neq\emptyset
. Then Hoffman’s Lemma
[
11
,
25
]
implies that

H(G|P):=\sup_{x\in P\setminus G^{-1}(0)}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,G^{-1}(0)\cap P)}{\|G(x)\|}<\infty

(15)

with the convention that
H(G|P)=0
when
P\subseteq G^{-1}(0)
.
We call
H(G|P)
the Hoffman constant of
G
relative to
P
. In the case when
P=\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we use the notation

H(G):=H(G|\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n});

that is
H(G)
is the standard (i.e., non-relative) Hoffman constant.

Theorem 4
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be nonempty polyhedra and
F_{j}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\;j=1,\dots,k
be affine operators such that
(F_{1},P_{1}),\dots,(F_{k},P_{k})
is a compatible collection. Let
F=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}F_{j}|P_{j}
.
If
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}\neq\emptyset
then the error-bound
condition (
3
) holds for the operator
F
when
R
small enough and

K_{F}=\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,k\}:P_{j}\cap{\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}}\neq\emptyset}H(I-F_{j}|P_{j}).

Proof.

Define the operators
G:=I-F
and
G_{j}:=I-F_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
. Note that
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}\neq\emptyset
implies that
G^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
. Let
H:=\max_{j:P_{j}\cap G^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset}H(G_{j}|P_{j})
. Proving the statement of the Lemma is equivalent to showing that there exists

R>0
such that for all
x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\operatorname{dist}(x,G^{-1}(0))\leq R\text{ implies }\operatorname{dist}(x,G^{-1}(0))\leq H\|G(x)\|.

(16)

To prove this, first consider any
1\leq j\leq k
such that
G_{j}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Let
x\in P_{j}
. We have by (
15
),

\operatorname{dist}(x,G^{-1}(0))\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,P_{j}\cap G^{-1}(0))\leq H(G_{j}|P_{j})\cdot\|G_{j}(x)\|\leq H\cdot\|G(x)\|.

(17)

Now, consider any
1\leq j\leq k
such that
G_{j}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{j}=\emptyset
. We have

\epsilon_{j}:=\inf_{x\in P_{j}}\|G_{j}(x)\|>0
. Taking
R<H\epsilon_{j}
we have,

x\in P_{j}\text{ and }\operatorname{dist}(x,G^{-1}(0))\leq R\text{ implies }\operatorname{dist}(x,G^{-1}(0))\leq\tfrac{R}{\epsilon_{j}}\cdot\|G_{j}(x)\|\leq H\cdot\|G(x)\|.

(18)

Combining (
17
) and (
18
) we obtain equation (
16
) for any
R\leq H\cdot\min_{j:P_{j}\cap G^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset}\epsilon_{j}
.
∎

4
Piecewise linear-quadratic optimization.

In this section, we leverage our main results, specifically
Theorem

2
in combination with
Theorem

4
, to demonstrate that a wide range of optimization algorithms, when applied to the class of
piecewise linear-quadratic
(PLQ) optimization problems, satisfy the error-bound condition and achieve linear convergence (see
Proposition

5
). The algorithms considered include gradient descent, proximal point, proximal gradient, Douglas-Rachford, and ADMM.

By PLQ optimization, we refer to the class of problems where the objective is a piecewise linear-quadratic convex function
[
26
, Def. 10.20]
whose feasible set is a polyhedron. This class encompasses various fundamental types of optimization problems, including linear optimization (LO) and convex quadratic optimization (QO). Additionally, it includes least squares and its variations, such as LASSO and elastic net, as well as support vector machines (SVM).

Furthermore, we refine our results for the ADMM (equivalently Douglas-Rachford) algorithm to establish error bounds and linear convergence rates for its application to LO (
Section

4.3
) and QO (
Section

4.4
) problems. For LO, we obtain an error-bound constant
K_{F_{DR}}=1
, which translates into a linear rate of convergence
\rho_{F_{DR}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, independent of the problem’s data (
Theorem

6
). For QO, we derive an error-bound constant and a linear rate of convergence, both of which depend solely on the condition number of the data matrix defining the problem’s quadratic objective (
Theorem

7
).

To derive these results, we will first introduce some relevant notation and definitions.

4.1
Preliminaries.

In what follows, we use standard notation and results from
[
27
,
26
,
28
]
.
We denote the set of extended real numbers by
\operatorname{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
.
The identity matrix is denoted by
I
.
For any
A\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, we denote by
\operatorname{rank}(A)
the column rank of
A
. We say
A
has full rank if
\operatorname{rank}(A)=n
.
For any
A\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\operatorname{rank}(A^{\intercal})=m
, we denote the Moore-Penrose or pseudo inverse as
A^{\dagger}:=A^{\intercal}(AA^{\intercal})^{-1}
. For any proper differentiable function
f:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
, the gradient of
f
, denoted
\nabla f
, is the vector of partial derivatives of
f
; that is
\nabla f(x)=(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x),\cdots,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}(x))
, and its Fenchel dual (or Fenchel conjugate), denoted
f^{*}
, is given by
f^{*}(x)=\sup_{u\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(x^{\intercal}u-f(u))
.

Let
\mu\geq 0
.
A function
f:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
is
\mu
-strongly convex if
f(x)-\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^{2}
is convex.
In particular, convex functions are 0-strongly convex.
We denote the class of proper, closed,
\mu
-strongly convex functions,
f:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
, by
\Gamma_{\mu}
.

The
proximal
operator is a fundamental concept in optimization that generalizes the notion of a projection.
Formally, given
f\in\Gamma_{\mu}
, the proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{f}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, is defined by

\operatorname{prox}_{f}(x):=\operatorname{argmin}_{u\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{f(u)+\frac{1}{2}\|u-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(19)

As it is usual, given
\gamma>0
, we will consider computing the proximal operator on the
scaled
function
\gamma f
, denoted
\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which using (
19
) is equivalent to

\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)=\operatorname{argmin}_{u\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{\gamma f(u)+\frac{1}{2}\|u-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(20)

If
f\in\Gamma_{0}
then the proximal mapping is defined everywhere and the following
Moreau decomposition
holds. For any
x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)+\gamma\operatorname{prox}_{\tfrac{1}{\gamma}f^{*}}(\tfrac{1}{\gamma}x)=x.

(21)

Let
\delta_{S}
denote the
indicator function

over a closed and convex set
S\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We have,

\operatorname{prox}_{\delta_{S}}(x)=\Pi_{S}(x):=\operatorname{argmin}_{u\in S}\|u-x\|^{2},

the projection of
x
onto
S
.
The proximal operator lays the foundation for proximal methods, a class of algorithms encompassing, for instance, the Douglas-Rachford algorithm, and it is a standard tool for solving nonsmooth, constrained, large-scale, or distributed optimization problems.

To simplify the presentation, it is useful to introduce the
reflector
operator. Formally, given
\gamma>0
and
f\in\Gamma_{\mu}
, the reflector operator
\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of the scaled function
\gamma f
is defined by

\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x)=2\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)-x,

(22)

or equivalently by the equation

\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(x+\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x)).

(23)

Both the proximal and the reflector operators are nonexpansive operators. To see this, it is useful to introduce the notion of
subdifferential
. The subdifferential of
f\in\Gamma_{0}
, at
x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, is the set
\partial f\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of all subgradients of
f
at
x
. That is

\partial f(x)=\{u\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:f(y)-f(x)\geq u^{\intercal}(y-x)\text{ for all }y\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\}.

Let
\mu\geq 0
. For all
f\in\Gamma_{\mu}
, the subdifferential of
f
is
monotone
.
That is, for all

(v-u)^{\intercal}(y-x)\geq 0\text{ for all }x,y\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\,u\in\partial f(x)\text{ and }v\in\partial f(y).

(24)

The proximal operator (
20
) is related to the subdifferential as follows

u=\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)\text{ if and only if }\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(x-u)\in\partial f(u).

(25)

This relationship allows us to characterize the fixed-points of the proximal operator. Namely, from (
25
), it follows that

x\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}}\text{ if and only if }0\in\partial f(x)\text{ if and only if }x\in\operatorname{argmin}f(x).

(26)

Using (
23
), the condition (
25
) can be stated in terms of the reflector operator. That is

u=\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x)\text{ if and only if }\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}(x-u)\in\partial f\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+u)\right).

(27)

Let
x,y\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be given. From (
27
) and the monotonicity of the subdifferential (
24
) we obtain

\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}(x-\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x))-\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}(y-\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(y)))^{\intercal}(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x))-\tfrac{1}{2}(y+\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(y)))\geq 0
or, equivalently,

\|\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x)-\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(y)\|^{2}\leq\|x-y\|^{2}.

(28)

That is, the reflector is nonexpansive. Further, from (
22
), it follows that
\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}
is
\tfrac{1}{2}
-averaged.

4.1.1
Douglas-Rachford Algorithm.

Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)+g(x)

(29)

where
f,g\in\Gamma_{0}
. Since the class
\Gamma_{0}
includes indicator functions over convex sets, problem (
29
) can be used to represent constrained convex optimization problems.
Algorithm
1
describes the Douglas-Rachford (DR) algorithm
[
29
,
30
,
24
]
for solving problem (
29
).

Algorithm 1

Douglas-Rachford

1:

Pick
w_{0}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\gamma>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

x_{k+1}:=\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(w_{k})

4:

y_{k+1}:=\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma g}(2x_{k+1}-w_{k})

5:

w_{k+1}:=w_{k}+2\alpha(y_{k+1}-x_{k+1})

6:

end

for

Observe that the update in Step
2
of Algorithm
1
can also be written as the fixed-point iteration

w_{k+1}:=F_{DR}(w_{k}),

(30)

where the fixed-point
Douglas-Rachford
operator
F_{DR}:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is defined as follows

F_{DR}(w)=w+2\alpha(\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma g}(2\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(w)-w)-\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(w)).

(31)

Then, using (
22
), we have that

F_{DR}(w)=(1-\alpha)w+\alpha\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma g}(\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w)).

(32)

From (
32
), we have that
F_{DR}
is
\alpha
-averaged, as the reflector is nonexpansive and the class of nonexpansive operators is closed under composition. Therefore, by
Theorem

1
, the DR algorithm converges sublinearly to a point in
\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{DR}}
. Notice that
w\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{DR}}
if and only
\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma g}(\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))=w
, which by (
27
) is equivalent to
\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}(\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w)-w)\in\partial g(\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w)+w))
. It also follows from (
27
), that
\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}(w-\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))\in\partial f\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(w+\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))\right)
and therefore,

w\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{DR}}\text{ is equivalent to }0\in\partial g\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(w+\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))\right)+\partial f\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(w+\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))\right).

(33)

As a result, the optimal solutions of (
29
) can be characterized using the fixed-points of operator
F_{DR}
; by (
33
) and (
23
),

\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)+g(x)=\{\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(w):w\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{DR}}\}.

(34)

4.1.2
Peaceman-Rachford Algorithm.

Note that if we purposely violate the domain restriction on

in Algorithm

. Thus, the fixed-point operator associated to the Peaceman-Rachford algorithm is

F_{PR}(w)=\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma g}(\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))=w+2(\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma g}(2\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(w)-w)-\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(w)).

(35)

From the discussion in
Section

4.1.1
, it follows that
F_{PR}
is nonexpansive but not averaged. Further, it also follows that
w\in\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F_{PR}}
if and only
\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma g}(\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(w))=w
. Thus, the optimality conditions (

) and (

) also hold for

. However, our main results,

,

, and

4.1.3
ADMM.

Consider the optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min_{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}},y\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{2}}}&f(x)+g(y)\\
\operatorname{st.}&Ax+By=c,\\

\operatorname{dist}(x,G_{J}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{J})=\operatorname{dist}(x,G_{J}^{-1}(0)).

∎

Now we prove
Theorem

6
.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

Let
J\subseteq[m]
be such that
G_{J}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{J}\neq\emptyset
.
Let
M=2\alpha A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}
and
v=2\alpha(A_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}-\gamma c)
.
From (
48
), we have that
G_{J}(x)=Mx-v
for any
x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then, by
Lemma

3
,

\displaystyle H(G_{J}|P_{J})

\displaystyle\leq H(G_{J}):=\sup_{x\notin G_{J}^{-1}(0)}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,G_{J}^{-1}(0))}{\|G_{J}(x)\|}=\sup_{x:Mx\neq v}\frac{\|M^{\dagger}(Mx-v)\|}{\|Mx-v\|}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\neq 0}\frac{\|M^{\dagger}w\|}{\|w\|}=\|M^{\dagger}\|=\|(2\alpha A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})^{\dagger}\|=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}\|.

By considering the singular value decomposition
A_{J}
one obtains that
\|A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}\|=1
. Hence, we conclude that

H(G_{J}|P_{J})\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}
for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
. Since (
48
) and (
49
) establish that the piecewise linear operator
I-F_{ADMM}
for problem (
45
) can be expressed in terms of the compatible collection
\{(F_{J},P_{J}):J\in\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}\}
, it follows from
Theorem

4
that the error-bound
condition (
3
) holds for the operator
F_{DR}
when
R>0
small enough and

K_{F_{DR}}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}
. The lower bound
K_{F_{DR}}
follows from (
4
) as
\rho_{F_{DR}}\geq 0
. This, together with the fact that
F_{DR}
is averaged allow us to apply
Theorem

2
to obtain
\rho_{F_{DR}}\leq 1-2\alpha(1-\alpha)
. In particular the statement for ADMM follows by setting
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

4.4
ADMM/DR for Quadratic Optimization.

Next, we analyze the Douglas-Rachford algorithm and its corresponding fixed-point operator applied to QO. From the discussion in the last paragraph of
Section

4.1.3
, the ADMM algorithm and corresponding operator are equivalent to setting the parameter
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
in the Douglas-Rachford algorithm.

Throughout this section, we fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
0<n\leq m
. We also fix
Q\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
a non-zero symmetric positive semidefinite matrix,
c\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
A\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, and
b\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Consider the linearly constrained (convex) quadratic optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}Qx+c^{\intercal}x\\
&Ax\leq b,\end{array}

(52)

for which we assume a solution exists; that is
\{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:Ax\leq b\}\neq\emptyset
.

Problem (
52
) is a particular instance of (
29
) with
g(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}Qx+c^{\intercal}x
and
f(x)=\delta_{X}(x)
, where

X:=\{x\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:Ax\leq b\}

is the feasible region of problem (
52
). In this case,

\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)=\Pi_{X}(x)
and
\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma g}(x)=(\gamma Q+I)^{-1}(x-\gamma c)
. Consequently,
\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma f}(x)=2\Pi_{X}(x)-x
and
\operatorname{ref}_{\gamma g}(x)=2(\gamma Q+I)^{-1}(x-\gamma c)-x
. Thus, from (
32
), it follows that the fixed-point DR operator for the linearly constrained quadratic optimization problem (
52
) is given by

F_{DR}(x)=x+2\alpha(\gamma Q+I)^{-1}\big(2\Pi_{X}(x)-x-\gamma c\big)-2\alpha\Pi_{X}(x).

(53)

Thus, using the same tools and similar notation to the one introduced in Section
4.3
,
it follows that for any
J\in\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}
and
x\in P_{J}
,

\displaystyle I-F_{DR}(x)

\displaystyle=G_{J}(x):=2\alpha\Pi_{J}(x)-2\alpha(\gamma Q+I)^{-1}\big(2\Pi_{J}(x)-x-\gamma c\big)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\big(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}-(\gamma Q+I)^{-1}(I-2A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})\big)x

\displaystyle\qquad-2\alpha\big((\gamma Q+I)^{-1}(2A_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}-\gamma c)-A_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}\big)

\displaystyle=M_{J}x-v_{J},

(54)

where

M_{J}=2\alpha(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}-(\gamma Q+I)^{-1}(I-2A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})),

(55)

and

v_{J}=2\alpha((\gamma Q+I)^{-1}(2A_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}-\gamma c)-A_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}),

(56)

and as in the linear programming case,

I-F_{DR}=\bigcup_{J\in\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}}G_{J}|P_{J}.

(57)

That is, the piecewise linear operator
I-F_{DR}
can be written in terms of the compatible collection of affine operators and nonempty polyhedra
\{(F_{J},P_{J}):J\in\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}\}
defined above. Thus, specializing
Theorem

4
, we can use
Theorem

2
. In particular, in
Theorem

7
, we obtain that the error-bound condition constant for the DR operator
F_{DR}
and the convergence rate of the DR algorithm depends only on the condition number
\kappa^{+}(Q):=\tfrac{\lambda_{\max}(Q)}{\lambda_{\min}^{+}(Q)}
of the matrix
Q
defining the problem’s quadratic objective (see (
52
)), where
\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\cdot)
is the smallest positive eigenvalue.
Notice, however, that the value of
R
for which error-bound condition and the convergence rate are valid might depend on the problem data.

Theorem 7
.

Assume (
52
) has a feasible solution. Let
0<\alpha<1
and let
\gamma>0
be such that
\gamma_{0}:=\gamma\lambda_{\max}(Q)<1
.
Then the error-bound condition (
3
) holds for the Douglas-Rachford operator
F_{DR}
(
53
) with

K_{F_{DR}}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\frac{1+\gamma_{0}}{(1-\gamma_{0})\gamma_{0}}(\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0}),

(58)

when
R>0
is sufficiently small. Consequently, the Douglas-Rachford algorithm (Algorithm
1
) converges linearly with rate

\rho_{F_{DR}}\leq 1-\frac{2\alpha(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma_{0})^{2}\gamma_{0}^{2}}{(1+\gamma_{0})^{2}(\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0})^{2}}.

(59)

In particular, for the ADMM operator
F_{ADMM}
(
42
) and the ADMM algorithm (Algorithm
2
) we obtain

K_{F_{ADMM}}\leq\frac{1+\gamma_{0}}{(1-\gamma_{0})\gamma_{0}}(\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0})\text{ and }\rho_{F_{ADMM}}\leq 1-\frac{(1-\gamma_{0})^{2}\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2(1+\gamma_{0})^{2}(\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0})^{2}}.

Remark 1

(Compact expression for
K_{F_{DR}}
and
\rho_{F_{DR}}
)
.

Set
\gamma
such that
\gamma_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then the upper bound on
K_{F_{DR}}
in (
58
) can be written as:

K_{F_{DR}}\leq\tfrac{3}{\alpha}\kappa^{+}(Q)

and then the linear convergence rate
\rho_{F_{DR}}
in (
59
) satisfies

\rho_{F_{DR}}\leq 1-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{18\kappa^{+}(Q)^{2}}.

In order to prove
Theorem

7
, we first state an auxiliary result in
Lemma

4
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
J\in\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}
. Let
M_{J}
and
G_{J}
be defined by (
4.4
)-(
56
).
Assume
G_{J}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{J}\neq\emptyset
, where
P_{J}
is given by (
47
). Then

(i)

\operatorname{Null}(M_{J})\subseteq\operatorname{Null}(Q)\cap\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})
.

(ii)

G_{J}^{-1}(0)\subseteq P_{J}
.

Proof.

Note that from the definition of
\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}
, it follows that
A_{J}
is full row rank; that is
\operatorname{rank}(A_{J}^{\intercal})=|J|
. Let

L_{J}:=(\gamma Q+I)M_{J}=2\alpha(\gamma Q(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})+A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})
,
and

l_{J}:=(\gamma Q+I)v_{J}=2\alpha((I-\gamma Q)A_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}-\gamma c)
.

To prove
(i)
, let
w\in\operatorname{Null}(M_{J})=\operatorname{Null}(L_{J})
. Write
w=w^{N}+w^{\perp}
, for some
w^{N}\in\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})
and
w^{\perp}\in\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})^{\perp}
. Then we have

L_{J}w^{N}=2\alpha(\gamma Q(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})+A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})w^{N}=2\alpha\gamma Qw^{N},

(60)

and

L_{J}w^{\perp}=2\alpha(\gamma Q(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})+A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})w^{\perp}=2\alpha w^{\perp}.

(61)

The latter follows from the fact that
\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})^{\perp}=_{J}^{\intercal})
, and thus

A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}w^{\perp}=w^{\perp}
.

Note that by (
60
) and (
61
)

0=L_{J}w=L_{J}(w^{N}+w^{\perp})=2\alpha(\gamma Qw^{N}+w^{\perp}).

(62)

Thus,
w^{\perp}=-\gamma Qw^{N}
. By the orthogonality of
w^{N}
and
w^{\perp}
we have

0=(w^{N})^{\intercal}w^{\perp}=-\gamma(w^{N})^{\intercal}Qw^{N}.

By positive semidefiniteness of
Q
we obtain
Qw^{N}=0
. By (
62
),
0=L_{J}w=2\alpha w^{\perp}
, and consequently
w^{\perp}=0
.
Hence
w=w^{N}\in\operatorname{Null}(Q)\cap\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})
.

To prove
(ii)
, let
w_{0}\in G_{J}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{J}
. As
\gamma Q+I
is nonsingular,
G_{J}^{-1}(0)=\{w\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:L_{J}w=l_{J}\}=w_{0}+\operatorname{Null}(L_{J})
. From
(i)
,
\operatorname{Null}(L_{J})=\operatorname{Null}(M_{J})\subseteq\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})
. Thus,
G_{J}^{-1}(0)\subseteq P_{J}+\operatorname{Null}(A_{J})=P_{J}
.
∎

Now we provide the proof of
Theorem

7
, which is analogous to the proof of
Theorem

6
, the core of the proof is to bound the value of an appropriate Hoffman constant.

Proof of
Theorem

7
.

Let
J\subseteq[m]
be such that
G_{J}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{J}\neq\emptyset
. Then

\displaystyle H(G_{J}|P_{J})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{w\in P_{J}\setminus G_{J}^{-1}(0)}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(w,G_{J}^{-1}(0)\cap P_{J})}{\|G_{J}(w)\|}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\notin G_{J}^{-1}(0)}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(w,G_{J}^{-1}(0))}{\|G_{J}(w)\|}

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:subGzP.QP}\ref{it:subGzP.QP.two}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{+}_{\min}(M_{J})},

where
\sigma^{+}_{\min}(\cdot)
is the smallest positive singular value.

To bound
\sigma^{+}_{\min}(M_{J})
, observe that:

2\alpha I-(\gamma Q+I)M_{J}=2\alpha(I-\gamma Q)(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}).

Since
\gamma\lambda_{\max}(Q)<1
, the matrix
I-\gamma Q
is invertible, and we can write:

I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}(I-\gamma Q)^{-1}(\gamma Q+I)M_{J}=(I-\gamma Q)^{-1}.

Let
r=\operatorname{rank}(M_{J})
, then
\sigma^{+}_{\min}(M_{J})=\sigma_{r}(M_{J})
. Using singular value bounds, we get:

\sigma_{\max}(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sigma_{r}((I-\gamma Q)^{-1}(\gamma Q+I)M_{J})\geq\sigma_{r}((I-\gamma Q)^{-1})=\frac{1}{1-\gamma\lambda_{r}(Q)}.

(63)

From
Lemma

4
(i)
, it follows that
r=\operatorname{rank}(M_{J})\geq\operatorname{rank}(Q)
. Thus,

\lambda_{r}(Q)\geq\lambda_{\min}^{+}(Q)
which implies

\frac{1}{1-\gamma\lambda_{r}(Q)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma\lambda_{\min}^{+}(Q)}=\frac{\kappa^{+}(Q)}{\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0}}.

(64)

Since
\sigma_{\max}(I-A_{J}^{\dagger}A_{J})\leq 1
, it follows that:

\sigma_{r}((I-\gamma Q)^{-1}(\gamma Q+I)M_{J})\geq\frac{2\alpha\gamma_{0}}{\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0}}.

On the other hand, by sub-multiplicativity of singular values:

\sigma_{r}((I-\gamma Q)^{-1}(\gamma Q+I)M_{J})\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma\lambda_{\max}(Q)}\cdot(1+\gamma\lambda_{\max}(Q))\cdot\sigma_{r}(M_{J}).

(65)

Combining (
63
)-(
65
)

\frac{2\alpha\gamma_{0}}{\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0}}\leq\frac{1+\gamma_{0}}{1-\gamma_{0}}\sigma_{\min}^{+}(M_{J}).

Therefore,

H(G_{J}|P_{J})\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}^{+}(M_{J})}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\frac{1+\gamma_{0}}{(1-\gamma_{0})\gamma_{0}}(\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0}).

Since
I-F_{DR}
is affine over
P_{J}
, for each
J\in\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}
,
Theorem

4
implies the error-bound condition holds for
F_{DR}
with:

K_{F_{DR}}=\max_{J:P_{J}\cap\operatorname{\mathcal{F}}_{F}\neq\emptyset}H(G_{J}|P_{J})\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\frac{1+\gamma_{0}}{(1-\gamma_{0})\gamma_{0}}(\kappa^{+}(Q)-\gamma_{0}).

Finally, since
F_{DR}
is
\alpha
-averaged,
Theorem

2
implies (
59
).
In particular the statement for ADMM follows by setting
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

5
Final Remarks.

Throughout the article, we have looked at some of the most popular optimization algorithms. However, it is clear that our results apply to an even wider range of optimization algorithms with similar properties when formulated in fixed-point iteration form. For example, consider the
plug-and-play
algorithms that are used in image processing
[
34
,
35
]
, in which new algorithms are created by replacing the proximal operator in the proximal gradient method or in the second update of ADMM by a simpler linear transformation referred as a
linear denoiser
. Under appropriate conditions on the linear denoiser, the fixed-point operator associated with these algorithms is averaged
[
34
, Thm. 3.5 and 3.6]
. Thus our main results can be used to study the convergence of these algorithms.

Our results in Section
4.4
for quadratic optimization imply that one can scale the variables such that
Q
is well-conditioned, without worrying about the effect on the conditioning of the linear constraints to improve the efficiency of the algorithm and the quality of the solution, as the convergence rates are independent of the data defining such linear constraints.
