Introduction

In the U.S., an estimated 84 million adults likely have obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), but only about 6 million are formally diagnosed and treated - (Boers et al., 2025). Men, older adults, and Black Americans have a higher prevalence of sleep apnea. Risk factors such as obesity, age, and abnormal breathing structures may be significant contributors in the development of sleep apnea. Individuals who exhibit the early symptoms of OSA are sent to a Sleep Lab for diagnosis, but many cases remain undiagnosed and untreated.

There are three types of sleep apnea, i.e., central sleep apnea (CSA), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and mixed sleep apnea (MSA). Central apnea occurs when the brain fails to send signals to the muscles that control breathing, leading to pauses in breathing. Obstructive apnea, which is the most common type, occurs when the airway collapses during sleep due to factors such as excess weight, enlarged tonsils, or a soft palate. Mixed apnea is a combination of OSA and CSA, where both airway obstruction and central control issues contribute to breathing pauses (Liu et al., 2022).

Neuronal activity moves into various sleep stages, measured through electroencephalography (EEG) which is part of traditional overnight polysomnography. Polysomnography records brain waves, oxygen level in the blood, and heart rate and breathing during sleep (Mayo Clinic, 2025). There are several major sleep electroencephalography features (slow-wave activity, sleep spindles, K-complexes) identified in obstructive sleep apnea (Gu et al., 2023). Historically, sleep has been identified as rapid eye movement (REM) or non-REM (NREM) sleep. NREM has corresponded to neural activation waveforms where the negative component of the waveform is seen after 100 msec (N1), 200 msec (N2) and 300 msec (N3) (Berry et al., 2012). The N3 component is considered the deepest stage of sleep, characterized by slow waveforms as low frequency is less than 2 Hz (Berry et al., 2012). Slow-wave activity (SWA) is quantified as the spectral power density of the delta frequency (0.75–4.5 Hz) within the overall EEG waveform during sleep. SWA is measured along the frontal cortical regions and is generally thought to play a critical role in sleep homeostasis and maintenance of sleep adequacy. Studies have shown a strong correlation between SWA and indices of hypoxemia in REM sleep (Appleton et al., 2019; Heinzer et al., 2001). Dominant SWA occurrences during OSA suggest disrupted sleep homeostasis during a sleep cycle, causing fragmented sleep states (Ondze et al., 2003). SWA patients with upper airway resistance syndrome had similar respiratory disturbance as well as patients with comorbid insomnia (Guilleminault et al., 2001; Paramo-Brando et al., 2020). Manifestations of sleep-disordered breathing may be caused by sleep architectural changes (Younes et al., 2022; Malhotra et al., 2020). Electroencephalograph activity reflects neural mechanisms contributing to OSA, such as those involved in upper airway control (McSharry et al., 2013; Subramanian et al., 2013).

Sleep spindles are EEG events defined within the alpha frequency (11–15-Hz) and are characteristic of N2 sleep (Berry et al., 2012; Weiner and Dang-Vu, 2016). They are also closely associated with maintenance of sleep stability (Fernandez and Luthi, 2020). Lower spindle occurrences were observed within a large OSA patient multicohort study in the frontal, central and parietal regions (Purcell et al., 2017; Schonwald et al., 2012). K-complexes are also a characteristic of N2 sleep where the K-complex waveforms occur over 0.5 s exhibiting a negative sharp wave followed by a positive component that is separate from the background EEG (Berry et al., 2012). K-complex waveforms are predominant in frontal and prefrontal derivations and are thought to have a cortical origin from the thalamus (Reda et al., 2021). Airflow constriction tends to cause K-complex waveforms (Nguyen et al., 2016). A study with patients with OSA had significantly larger positive waveform amplitudes relative to controls (Sun et al., 2017).

Sleep apnea events transition into a series of short cycles, called A-phases which can be observed in the human EEGs during NREM sleep. These events can be classified in three groups (A1, A2, and A3) according to their spectral contents and are thought to play a role in the transitions between the different sleep stages (Arce-Santana et al., 2020). A1-phases are defined by high-amplitude low-frequencies occurring over 80% of the total A-phase duration, followed by low-amplitude high frequencies occupying the remaining 20%. The A2-phase is comprised of fast and slow frequencies. The A3-phase features low amplitudes with high frequencies occurring over 50% of the phase duration (Avila et al., 2025). A-phases refers to the stimulation phase of a Cyclic Alternating Pattern (CAP) in sleep, where sleep instability is associated with sleep apnea. During an apnea event, a person's breathing stops or becomes shallow, which triggers a compensating internal stimulation response in the brain. This can be observed on an electroencephalogram (EEG) as a shift from a stable sleep state to an A-phase brain activity pattern (Avila, 2020).

Several approaches have been applied toward the automatic classification of apnea events. A hypothesis that blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) is indirectly related to the electrical behavior of the cortex, where electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings have been considered to provide information in the diagnosis of the obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) syndrome. Power spectral density waveforms focus on where the most power is located across the frequency spectrum, which is used to portray the frequency content of SpO2 and EEG recordings. Applying forward stepwise logistic regression procedures enabled spectral feature sets of SpO2 and EEG recordings, providing 91% sensitivity, 83.3% specificity and 88.5% accuracy in characterizing apnea states (Alvarez et al., 2009). Transient, apnea-induced hypoxemia relating to cortical electrocortical activity has been tested on patients with severe obstructive sleep apnea (Svanborg and Guilleminault, 1996). Electroencephalographic recordings were acquired to perform spectral array analysis. EEG recordings were timed exactly to respiratory events, where nonrapid eye movement (NREM) apneas were correlated to an increase of amplitude within the theta band. Polysomnography EEG electrodes, C3-A2 and C4-A1 were applied to capture apnea events from a cohort comprised of children where Fast Fourier transform (FFT) routines were applied during data processing which found theta amplitudes to be higher post apnea event (Bandla et al., 2000). A novel sleep apnea detection system using EEG data acquisition implemented frequency variation methodology. Band-pass filtering is applied to remove high and low frequencies and focus on the frequency of interest. Baseline correction was applied to eliminate components with pseudo-interference frequency using Hilbert–Huang transformations. The system then detects the duration of the obstructive sleep apnea state from the variation of Hilbert spectrum frequency (Hsu and Shih, 2011).

The approach considered in this study features dominant frequency attributes found during apnea events. Non-linear band-pass filtering is applied to isolate the frequencies associated with the apnea event. Hilbert Transformations are applied to the filtered signal where amplitude and phase components of the signal are decomposed, amplitude and phase lock values are calculated and placed into an equation that demarcates apnea events within a cohort's sleep state. The application of this equation is tested across an apnea cycle featuring the different types of apnea events, i.e. OSA, CSA and MSA. Sensitivity, precision/specificity and accuracy scores are applied to each apnea event. Through this approach, hypoxemia events that relate to sleep apnea cycles can be isolated throughout an EEG time series.

Methods

PSG data and preprocessing

Processing apnea data sets involves focusing on the Polysomnographic (PSG) electrodes that correspond to neurological data acquisition from the electrodes that cover the scalp of the individual. Pair-wise comparisons are processed between neurological electrodes that cover EEG (C3-A2), and (C4-A1) locations on the scalp – Fig. 1. To avoid spurious detection of locking due to noise and small oscillation coupling, we initially focused on a filtering range that produced the most dominant apnea event signals. EEG dataset

Data sets were gathered online from the ‘St Vincent's University Hospital, Dublin’, which consists of PSG recordings from subjects required to be older than 18 years of age, with no known cardiac disease or autonomic dysfunction, and not taking medication known to interfere with heart rate (https://physionet.org/content/ucddb/1.0.0/, datasets: ucddb002.rec - ucddb0023.rec: March 1, 2025). Twenty-five subjects (21M, 4F) were selected (age: 50 ± 10 years, range 28-68 years; BMI: 31.6 ± 4.0 kg/m2, range 25.1-42.5 kg/m2; AHI: 24.1 ± 20.3, range 1.7-90.9). The dataset has a continuous annotation scheme signifying respiratory events where the onset time of sleep is provided for each recording, i.e., HYP – Hypopnea, C – Central, O – Obstructive, or M – Mixed. Moreover, the onset time of every apneic activity is documented along with the activity duration. The database contains overnight polysomnograms from adult subjects with suspected sleep-disordered breathing (Goldberger et al., 2000).

To find the most effective frequency band characterizing apnea episodes, we first calculated the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) of the EEG signals within the time series that featured a sample sleep apnea occurrence. FFTs showed peak power at ∼4 Hz where apnea activity had occurred. We then determined the dominant frequency of apnea oscillations across all patients, which was between 4 and 7.5 Hz, labeled as the theta frequency range. Consequently, band-pass filtering was implemented on the dataset to include this nominal frequency range that described apnea activity (4-7.5 Hz; transition bandwidth = 1Hz; N = 200). Filtering was implemented in the EEGLAB MATLAB toolbox (http://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab/) (Delorme and Makeig, 2004) using the Parks-McClellan optimal finite impulse response (FIR) filter design method (McClellan and Parks, 1973).

Analytical Amplitude Method

The Discrete Hilbert transform provides the imaginary part, xˆ [n] of the signal representing an estimate of the output of the activity of inhibitory neurons (Freeman, 2004a). The sum of squares of the real and imaginary parts of the signal produces the instantaneous power required by the excitatory and inhibitory neurons in local areas of the cortex from dendritic currents (Freeman, 2004a). The analytic amplitude is calculated through the square root of the sum of the real and imaginary parts of the signal. The Analytical Amplitude A(n) provides the main tuning of the apnea threshold selection. The Analytical Phase Φ(n) enables fine tuning of the location of the apnea threshold selection. A(n) and Φ(n) are utilized in equations (4), (5)) to measure the level of phase and amplitude synchrony between EEG signals from paired electrodes using a sliding window of n = 2000 data points.

The synchrony levels are termed Phase Lock Value (PLV) and Amplitude Lock Value (ALV) and are determined using electrodes in the areas of the scalp where apnea events are located. One electrode is designated as the working electrode and the other is designated as the reference electrode. The ALV measurement is used in conjunction with the PLV to identify the apnea event. The degree of amplitude and phase locking between two channels determines the similarity between the two amplitudes. The apnea event methodology is as follows. PLV and ALV are calculated between the working and reference electrodes. The sliding window acts as a filtering method to sum a larger group of points to calculate the PLV and ALV. The PLV/ALV values represent synchronous neural populations, where two distinct areas across the cortex move in lock step to each other. The ALV/PLV values rise from values between zero to one denoting the amount of synchrony in amplitudes/phase.

During nonrapid eye movement (NREM) apneas, the theta band amplitude increased, starting on average 13 s after the apnea onset (Svanborg and Guilleminault, 1996). Sleep apnea EEG detection using amplitude-based analysis involves measuring changes in signal strength, particularly focusing on increases (high amplitude) in low-frequency theta waves (Zhao et al., 2021). Slow wave sleep (SWS), is considered the deepest stage of sleep, and is characterized by an abundance of slow waves of low frequency (<2 Hz) and high amplitude (>75 μV; Berry et al., 2012). Freeman, specifically in studies with rabbit olfactory bulbs and other sensory areas, found that brain activity operates in short, discontinuous bursts (frames), analogous to cinema, where amplitude changes represent the content of the stimulus, specifically internal stimulus from breathing cessation (Freeman, 2004b). Therefore, apnea events found in the amplitude changes are based on internal stimulus, and phase changes represent discontinuous bursts due to brain activity. This activity is quantified as squaring the power or amplitude of the signal and adding the phase part of the signal to signify the frames that carry the content of the internal stimulus. The ALV values provide focused areas where an apnea event may occur, and PLV values provide a fine-tuning mechanism to lock into the time interval of apnea events. The square of ALV(t) plus PLV(t) provides the upper and lower bounds of the apnea events, where ALV(t) squared plus PLV(t) is calculated over a sliding window across the time series. When the ALV(t) and PLV(t) values rise at the same time series location, this crossing represents a precise location of apnea events. The equation represents the dominance of the amplitude component on apnea waveforms, described as the Amplitude Waveform (AW(t)).

A threshold value is selected to determine when the EEG signal activity corresponds to apnea/non-apnea states. The accumulated amplitude and phase lock values of the filtered signals are compared against that threshold and so the corresponding epoch is marked by the algorithm as apnea or non-apnea states. False negatives (FN) refer to the detection of a patient with a non-apnea state when they are in an apnea state, and false positives (FP) refer to the detection of a patient with an apnea state when they are in a non-apnea state. Discussion

EEG signal processing approaches that involve amplitudes and frequency changes have utilized the dominant power within a frequency spectrum to relate to apnea events. Initial research in apnea events have shown that dominant power within a frequency spectrum isolated the apnea event, as well as pre- and post-apnea states. Paired with logistic regression, the changes in the slope of the frequency spectrum provide a manner to see the changes in frequency as apnea events emerge and disperse (Alvarez et al., 2009). Once the dominant frequency is isolated, usually around the theta range of neural activity, signal decomposition was considered. Hilbert-Huang transformations (HHT) decompose a signal into intrinsic mode functions (IMF) where IMFs obtain instantaneous frequency data. This approach is implemented after band-pass filtering is applied, demonstrating fast changes within the frequency component can locate apnea events (Hsu and Shih, 2011).

Recently, the amplitude component of the apnea state EEG waveform has been considered to identify apnea events. Delta wave amplitudes are a signature of NREM sleep states. Utilizing signal processing together with neural network classifications, amplitude changes can be found within a distinct frequency range (Dey et al., 2017). Additionally, rapid changes in signal amplitudes associated with apnea events have been found in EEGs using deep learning models, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) (Zhang et al., 2025). The study by Zhang et al. (2025) using electrocardiogram (ECG) with a convolutional neural network module with large convolutional kernels (LKCNN) had implemented leave-out cross-validation at a ratio of 4:1 and averaged the results 5 times, which had a sensitivity and specificity of 69.22%, and 96.21%, respectively. In a study by Barnes et al. (2022), an implementation using CNN to classify apnea events performed with an accuracy of 69.9% (Barnes et al., 2022). In a study by Tapia-Rivaz, Estevez, 2020, featuring detection of EEG data with sleep spindles and K-complexes, convolutional and recurrent neural networks were implemented for sleep EEG event detection (Tapia-Rivas et al., 2024). Both sleep spindle and K-complex detection had a mean F1-score of at least 80.9% and 82.6%, respectively. While these performance metrics demonstrate a similar range of values to the Amplitude Waveform approach, these are indirect comparisons due to the differences in methodology.

The approach described in this paper considers several previous implementations to classify apnea events. Isolating the frequency bandwidths and then decomposing the EEG signal into its amplitude and phase components, we see that the influence of the amplitude component has shown to be the dominant value to locate apnea events within a time series, where the phase component provides fine tuning to precisely find the build-up of apnea events.

There is significant overlap in the physiology between obstructive and central apnea, where CSA has common attributes to OSA whose symptoms include hypoxic high altitude-induced breathing, narcotic-induced central apnea, the effects from obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), and Cheyne-Stokes breathing (CSB) (Lanfranchi et al., 2003; Javaheri eta l, 1998, Eckert et al., 2007). Previous understanding of central sleep apnea events involves periods where the brain stops sending signals to breathe, i.e. a decrease in the activity of the central respiratory network, leading to periods of breathing cessation (Andrade et al., 2018). Neural compensation due to obstructed breathing is emerging as the main idea behind CSA. Neural compensation was thought to be analogous to the diaphragm muscles of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease where other motor units are recruited to control breathing. These motor units in the diaphragm are composed of the phrenic motor neuron which originates in the cervical spinal cord. Their average discharge frequencies are higher because of the increased resistive load to compensate for breathing constriction (De Troyer et al., 2005).

When an apnea threshold is reached from ventilatory instability, oxygen desaturations and/or cortical arousals occur from the sleep state. Compound reduction of arterial CO2 levels perpetuate central apneas (Javaheri et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024). Instances where elevated levels of CO2 in the blood occurs when the body does not sufficiently remove carbon dioxide (hypercapnic respiratory) which forms a CSA event. It is thought to be a manifestation of instability in the ventilatory control pattern, with a compensatory response to arterial CO2 levels causing in increase followed by a decrease of neural oscillations, which has been seen in Cheyne-Stokes breathing (CSB) (Köhnlein et al., 2002).

Physiology of the respiratory control system can be considered as a closed feedback loop. The loop is comprised from the respiratory control center in the pons and medulla, which is comparable to a “controller”, whereas the lungs are the mechanism to drive oxygenation, or the “plant”, and oxygenation within the bloodstream connects the circuit, formally defined as input to the controller for “mixing” (Burgess, 2012). Therefore, central sleep apnea (CSA) is more associated with neurological issues stemming from aberrant brain signaling causing breathing cessation and the brain's response to regulate apnea events by sending neural activations to re-establish sleep states.

Sleep apnea impairs memory consolidation by disrupting essential brain waves through fragmented sleep and intermittent oxygen deficiency. This condition specifically damages slow-wave sleep and spindles—rhythms critical for memory—and increases the abnormal coupling of low-frequency theta with high amplitudes (Kuan et al., 2025). Patients with severe apnea often show intense interaction between low-frequency theta waves and high amplitudes in frontal and occipital regions, which is strongly associated with chronic intermittent hypoxia. Because recent research suggests normal breathing drives these slow oscillations for memory consolidation, the restricted breathing in sleep apnea directly disrupts the coordination of brain rhythms involved in respiratory systems (Zheng et al., 2024).

Apnea states are closely linked to how the neural system functions during sleep. Time intervals that increase without breathing can be detected via cortical activations. As the time interval between breathing cessations increases, a waveform of low frequency, high amplitude neural activity starts to emerge, whereas these apnea states start to cluster as longer breathing cessation states start to increase in time. Waveform detection can enable the eventual detection and prediction of long-term apnea states.

Conclusions

The amplitude component of the EEG signal has shown to be the dominant indicator of apnea events, and the addition of the phase component enables precise tuning and identification of apnea occurrences. Apnea events have been found within the theta frequency bandwidth which correlates with previous studies. The results show a buildup of apnea events, moving into critical state, where a manifestation of instability in the ventilatory control pattern, with a compensatory response to arterial CO2 levels, causes an increase in neural low-frequency oscillations. In individuals with sleep apnea, these normal, state-dependent changes are exaggerated, resulting in repeated airway obstructions, either partial (hypopnea) or complete (apnea) (Eckert et al., 2007). Clusters of apnea events present an increasing waveform of breathing cessation of time, which may aid in the development of a feedback loop to control long term apnea events.
