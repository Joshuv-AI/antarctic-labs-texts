Clonal diversity and trend of methicillin-resistant and methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA/MSSA) clinical isolates from pediatric patients in Myanmar: increased prevalence of clonal complex 1 (CC1) and ST121 MRSA
Authors: Meiji Soe Aung, Thida San, Win Kalayar Kyaw, Lynn Waddy, Nobumichi Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Staphylococcus aureus from pediatric patients in Myanmar was characterized for recent clonal trend.•ST (sequence type) and other genotypes were determined for all 176 S. aureus complex isolates.•Clonal complex (CC)1 methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) became dominant, associated with the absence of ST239 (CC8) MRSA.•ST2885 (CC1) was detected persistently and suggested as an emerging MRSA clone.•S. argenteus (ST2250) was first identified from a pediatric patient in Myanmar.Keywords: MRSA, MSSA, Pediatric patients, Changes in Genotypes, Myanmar

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most common pathogenic bacteria in humans, being responsible for a wide spectrum of infectious diseases [1]. The pathogenicity of this bacterium results from the production of various core and acquired virulence factors, among which certain toxins are associated with specific types of diseases [2,3]. Furthermore, S. aureus often acquires antimicrobial resistance (AMR) conferred by resistance determinants located on mobile genetic elements. Particularly, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has been recognized as a major cause of infections worldwide, in health care settings and in the community [3,4]. A subset of community-acquired (CA)-MRSA is highly virulent due to the production of Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL), a cytolytic pore-forming toxin encoded by chromosomal genes derived from bacteriophage [5]. PVL is involved in skin and soft tissue infections, such as impetigo, which are commonly seen in infants, as well as in invasive diseases, including necrotizing pneumonia. Children, particularly neonates, are vulnerable to S. aureus infections, with a high prevalence of MRSA, because of their developing immune systems, high rates of colonization, and age-specific risk factors associated with frequent exposure to healthcare settings [1,6,7]. Therefore, the dissemination of MRSA among children is a public health concern.

MRSA possesses a staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) incorporated into the chromosome of S. aureus, which contains mecA that encodes penicillin-binding protein-2a, with a low affinity to beta-lactams and defines methicillin resistance [4]. Among the divergent types of SCCmec, types I through V are commonly distributed among hospital-acquired MRSA (HA-MRSA) and CA-MRSA worldwide. The prevalence of MRSA clones varies among different geographical regions. In Asia, some limited HA-MRSA clones are dominant (e.g. ST (sequence type) 5-SCCmec-II and ST239-SCCmec-III), whereas the prevalent CA-MRSA consists of various clones, including ST30-SCCmec-IV, ST59-SCCmec-V, ST88-SCCmec-IV, and ST121-SCCmec-V [4,8]. Associated with the global increase and dissemination of CA-MRSA, a changing prevalence of MRSA clones has been described in long-term epidemiological studies [9,10]. A change in the dominant S. aureus clones implies an alteration of bacterial traits, including virulence and AMR, which are linked to pathogenesis and disease types and also to treatment options. Therefore, monitoring S. aureus clones with their traits is significant for providing basic information for infection control.

In Myanmar, MRSA has been persistently prevalent, showing different rates in S. aureus (14-48%) depending on study settings, for all isolates from tertiary care hospitals (TCHs) [11,12] and multiple healthcare facilities [13]. These studies also revealed a high incidence of PVL genes in MSSA and MRSA. Regarding S. aureus isolates from pediatric patients, only two studies are available, and they described the proportion of MRSA as 20%-22% in Yangon Children’s Hospital (YCH) [14,15]. In one of these studies, we analyzed the genetic types and characteristics of S. aureus in YCH during 2017-2019 and revealed the common clones in children [15]. However, there is no other genotypic information on current S. aureus from children in Myanmar. The present study aimed to clarify the clonal structure and genetic traits of recent MRSA and MSSA from pediatric patients in YCH and to reveal any changing trend of S. aureus clones compared with the previous findings.

Methods

Isolation and identification of S. aureus from clinical specimens

Bacterial isolates were recovered from clinical specimens (except for blood) that were submitted to the Department of Clinical Laboratory in YCH for 19 months consecutively from September 2023 to March 2025. YCH is a 550-bed tertiary care and teaching hospital specializing in children. All the clinical specimens were collected from children with symptoms that might indicate infection. The clinical specimens were inoculated onto blood agar plates, followed by incubation at 35°C for 24 hours aerobically. Bacterial species identification was performed by the Automated Microbiology System, BD Phoenix M50 (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, NV, USA). S. aureus isolates were stored in cryovials (Microbank, Pro Lab Diagnostics, Richmond Hill, Canada) and recovered when they were analyzed.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

To determine antimicrobial susceptibility, minimum inhibitory concentrations of antimicrobials within a limited range were measured by a broth microdilution test using Dry Plate Eiken DP42 (Eiken Chemical, Tokyo, Japan). The antimicrobials tested were oxacillin, cefoxitin, penicillin, ampicillin, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefazolin, cefepime, imipenem, gentamicin, arbekacin, erythromycin, clindamycin, vancomycin, teicoplanin, daptomycin, linezolid, fosfomycin, minocycline, levofloxacin, rifampicin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Inducible resistance to clindamycin, which was included in this test panel, was also examined. Susceptibility/resistance was judged according to Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) break points for broth microdilution (M100-Ed33, 2023) or other break points shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Detection of virulence factors and drug resistance genes

For all the isolates, the presence of the staphylococcal 16S rRNA gene, nuc, mecA, PVL genes (lukS-PV-lukF-PV), and ACME-arcA was tested by multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (initial multiplex PCR assay) according to the experimental conditions described previously [16]. Isolates were genotyped by the schemes of staphylocoagulase genotype (coa type), spa type, PVL phage typing, and SCCmec type for MRSA, as described previously [17]. ST was determined by the multilocus sequence typing (MLST) scheme and assigned to the clonal complex (CC) according to the web-based genotyping tool PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/staphylococcus-aureus). S. argenteus was identified and distinguished from S. aureus according to ST and coagulase genotype [12,18]. The presence of the following selected AMR genes was analyzed by multiplex or uniplex PCR using primers and conditions described previously [16,17]: genetic determinants conferring resistance to penicillin (blaZ), macrolides (ermA/B/C/T, msrA/B), aminoglycosides (aac(6’)-Ie-aph(2”)-Ia, ant(4’)-Ia, ant(6)-Ia, ant(9)-Ia, aph(2”)-Ic/Id, and aph(3’)-IIIa), and linezolid (cfr, optrA, fexA, poxtA) were detected by uniplex or multiplex PCR, as previously described [15,17].

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of MRSA and PVL among different STs and other isolate categories was statistically analyzed using Fisher’s exact test using the js-STAR XR ver.1.1.9 software (https://www.kisnet.or.jp/nappa/software/star/index.htm). A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, a total of 175 S. aureus isolates (one isolate from one patient) were collected, whereas one isolate was genetically identified as S. argenteus. Most isolates were derived from pus (71%), followed by wound swab (11%), tissue (6%), and ear discharge (4%) (Supplementary Table S2). Among S. aureus, 30 isolates were MRSA (17.1% of all S. aureus). PVL genes were detected in 113 isolates (65%) and were more prevalent in methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) (69%) than in MRSA (43%) (Table 1). ACME was not detected in any isolate.

MRSA and MSSA isolates were differentiated into 11 and 18 STs, respectively (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4). Among MRSA isolates, five STs belonged to CC1, which was a dominant clonal group, accounting for 47% of isolates (Table 1). Other common STs of MRSA were ST121 (27%) and ST6 (20%). Among MSSA isolates, ST121 was the most common (48%), followed by CC1 STs (20%) and ST88 (14%). PVL genes in ST121 were detected at high rates in MRSA (100%; eight of eight) and MSSA (86%, 60 of 70). In contrast, in ST772 and ST88, PVL genes were found mostly in MRSA (67%, four of six) and MSSA (85%, 17 of 20), respectively. The PVL phage of all ST772 isolates was typed as ΦSa119, whereas ΦPVL was assigned to all other PVL-positive isolates. MRSA and MRSA/MSSA with CC1 STs (e.g. ST772, ST2885) and ST121 were isolated sporadically during the study period (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4).

The profiles of AMR and selected AMR determinants are summarized in individual categories of genotypes for MRSA (Table 2), PVL-positive and negative MSSA (Table 3, Table 4, respectively). All MRSA/MSSA isolates were susceptible to vancomycin, teicoplanin, daptomycin, linezolid, and arbekacin. MRSA carried either SCCmec types IVa or V, which were found in six and five STs, respectively (Table 2). ST772 and ST2885 were the most common in CC1 MRSA, whereas ST2885 was not found in MSSA. Among MRSA, ST772 (PVL-positive and -negative isolates), ST6 (PVL-negative isolates), and ST2990 (PVL-negative isolates) showed resistance to more antimicrobials than other clones, harboring more of the AMR genes examined.

The incidence rates of CC1 (46.7%) and ST121 (26.7%) in MRSA in the present study were higher than those in the previous study conducted in YCH (2017-2019) [15] (22.9% and 2.1%, respectively) (Supplementary Table S5). In contrast, the previously dominant ST239 (35.4%) was not detected in the present study. Among MSSA isolates in the present study, ST121 showed a higher proportion (48.3%) than that in 2017-2019 (35.7%), whereas the prevalence of CC1 isolates was similar (18.6%-18.9%), and the incidence rate of ST1930 in 2023-2025 (2.8%) was lower than that in the previous study (8.7%) (Supplementary Table S6).

Among PVL-positive MSSA, the dominant clones, ST88 (n = 17) and ST121 (n = 60), were found to comprise various divergent strains with different spa types (14 and 15 types, respectively), although half of the ST121 isolates belonged to spa-t645 (Table 3). Compared with ST121, another dominant clone, ST88, as well as minor clones, ST3206 (CC1) and ST2990 (CC1) MRSA, exhibited resistance to more antimicrobials, including erythromycin, gentamicin, and rifampicim. PVL-negative MSSA had various genetic backgrounds in terms of ST, coa genotype, and spa type, with ST121 (n = 10), ST1 (n = 6), and ST1156 (n = 6) being more commonly detected than other STs (Table 4). Four of the six spa types of ST121 were also found in PVL-positive ST121. In ST88 and ST121 MSSA, the AMR profiles and the prevalence of resistance genes examined were similar between PVL-positive and -negative isolates. In contrast, PVL-negative ST1 and ST361 MSSA showed resistance to more drugs associated with multiple AMR genes examined, compared with PVL(+) ST1 MSSA and PVL(−) ST361 MRSA. In this study, one isolate from pus (from a 1-year-old male patient) was identified as S. argenteus, which belonged to ST2250, was devoid of mecA (methicillin-susceptible), and had only blaZ, showing susceptibility to most antimicrobials except for penicillin and ampicillin.

Discussion

This study revealed the recent trend of MSSA and MRSA clones from pediatric patients in Myanmar by determining the genotypes of all the isolates (except for blood isolates) during the study period. The prevalence of MRSA in this study (17%) was similar to those in the previous studies in YCH (20% in 2019; 22% in 2020) [14,15] and TCHs in Yangon (14%, 2017-2018; 19% in 2020-2021) [11,12]. Therefore, in Myanmar, the prevalence of MRSA in children as well as adults seems to be persistently constant, remaining at a slightly lower rate than that described for the whole of Southeast Asia (ranging from 20% to 30%) [19]. In contrast, the PVL-positive rates in this study (65% in total) and the previous study in YCH (62%) [15] were higher than those in TCHs in Yangon (34%), which have more adult patients [11,12]. The difference in PVL rate was deemed to be attributed to the composition of S. aureus clones in children and adults. The PVL-positive rate in YCH in the present and previous studies was higher in MSSA (68%-69%) than in MRSA (35%-43%), in contrast to the lower PVL rates in MSSA from the TCHs (29%-39%) [11,12]. The reason for the higher PVL rate in pediatric MSSA may be the predominance of ST121 and the higher incidence of ST88 and ST2990, although these three STs were also found at a lower prevalence in general patients in the TCHs. It was noted that ST121 in YCH increased from 30% (71 of 244) in 2017-2019 [15] to 45% in 2023-2025. ST121 S. aureus is known as a globally distributed hypervirulent strain and is mostly MSSA (89%) and PVL-positive (92%) [20], which is in line with the findings in this study. However, the dominant spa type of ST121 in YCH was t645, unlike the globally major type t159. t645 and t159 were detected in the TCHs in Myanmar, but the dominance of t645 was seen in the previous study in YCH [15]. Therefore, an association of spa type t645 with pediatric infections may be suggested. Given that ST121 S. aureus is known to be mainly responsible for neonatal skin blistering disorders and other skin and soft tissue infections [20], the ST121-t645 PVL-positive MSSA might have been successfully adapted to pediatric patients at our study site.

Remarkable changes in MRSA clones were observed in this study compared with those in 2019 in YCH [15]. First, CC1 among MRSA isolates (23%) increased to 47%, associated with a growing number of STs, suggesting genetic diversification. Among CC1 MRSA isolates in our study, ST772 and ST2990 showed multiple drug resistance while being low in frequency. In contrast, CC1/coa-VIIa/SCCmec-IVa (ST1, ST1135, and ST2885) strains were more common and had fewer resistance traits. Second, ST239 (CC8)-MRSA/SCCmec III was not detected, although this clone was the dominant MRSA (35%) in YCH (2017-2019) [15] and was found as one of the common clones in TCH in Yangon (2020-2023) [12]. ST239-MRSA-III is known as an ancient pandemic clone of hospital-associated (HA)-MRSA and is widely disseminated in Asia [3,4,8,19]; it is still reported in some regions of South Asia and Eastern Asia [10,21,22]. ST239 MRSA carries SCCmec III, which is the largest in size among SCCmec types and shows multiple drug resistance [4,12]. However, a decrease in ST239-III associated with a rise of CA-MRSA, such as ST22 and ST30 having SCCmec IV, was described as a recent trend in Southeast Asia [19]. Thus, the absence of ST239-III and the increased proportion of CC1-IV/V in this study are suggested to represent a similar trend, which may be verified by further epidemiologic surveillance.

The clonal characteristics of YCH in the present study are summarized as the increase in CC1 MRSA represented by ST772 and ST2885 and the dominant MSSA with ST121 and ST88, and the persistence of ST6, ST1155, ST1156, and ST1930. Among these STs, although ST2885 was detected in Myanmar previously [12,15], it is a rare lineage first identified in Bangladesh in 2012 [23] and was also recently described in Thailand [24] and Lao PDR [25]. Because ST2885 has been persistently isolated during the present study period, it is considered to be distributed regionally, including in Myanmar. Another common ST772 MRSA is colloquially known as the "Bengal Bay clone", which is described as prevailing in South Asian countries [3,26,27]. Similarly, ST1930 MSSA was detected at a low frequency in Bangladesh [22,28], as in the present and previous studies in Myanmar [12,15]. Although ST6 and ST88, which are detected in both Southeast Asia [19] and South Asia [26,28], are also found in Myanmar, ST5, ST22, ST30, and ST59, which are common in Southeast Asia [19], were rarely detected. These findings suggest that S. aureus clones in Myanmar share epidemiologic features with those reported from South Asia, whereas clones common to both regions are also distributed. In this study, S. argenteus was first identified in a pediatric patient in Myanmar because S. argenteus detected to date were derived from only adult patients [12]. The identified ST2250 is the predominant type worldwide and is associated with the acquisition of virulence and resistance determinants [12,18,29]. Although the prevalence of S. argenteus in this study was low, its significance as an infectious pathogen in children remains to be determined by further surveillance studies.

This study revealed the clonal characteristics of MRSA and MSSA from pediatric patients in Myanmar and the changing trend of some clones. Nevertheless, as limitations of this study, the relatedness of S. aureus STs/lineages and clinical characteristics/disease types could not be analyzed, and the genes for AMR determinants examined were limited to some representative classes. Because the molecular epidemiologic characteristics associated with virulence and AMR may change with antimicrobial therapy, continuous surveillance may be necessary, particularly for pediatric patients who are more vulnerable to S. aureus infections.
