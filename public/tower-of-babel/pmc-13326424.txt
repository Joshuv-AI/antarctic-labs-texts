Anatomical variations of lamina papyracea, internal carotid artery, and optic canal dehiscence and protrusion: a computed tomography retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ozan Tuysuz, Semih Tekin, Ali Dablan, Sude Zeynep Mutlu, Mehmet Karagulle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnatomical variations of the paranasal sinuses, particularly those involving the lamina papyracea (LP), internal carotid artery (ICA), and optic canal (OC), may significantly increase the risk of complications during functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS). High-resolution computed tomography (CT) plays a critical role in identifying these variations preoperatively. In this context, the objective of the present study is to determine the prevalence and radiological characteristics of LP dehiscence, ICA protrusion/dehiscence, and optic canal protrusion/dehiscence in a large CT cohort.MethodsThis retrospective study included 3052 patients who underwent paranasal CT between June 2020 and October 2025. Patients younger than 18 years, those with maxillofacial trauma, or with prior sinonasal surgery were excluded. All CT scans were acquired using a 128-slice multidetector system. Two blinded radiologists evaluated LP, ICA, and OC variations; LP dehiscence was classified into three grades. ICA and optic canal protrusion were graded as < 50% or ≥ 50% based on the extent of canal invagination into the sphenoid sinus cavity. Statistical analyses were performed with a significance level of p < 0.05.ResultsLP dehiscence was identified in 70 patients (2.3%), more common in males (p = 0.046). Most cases were unilateral (95.7%) and located in the anterior ethmoid cells (75.7%). Grade 1 was the most common type (70%). ICA protrusion was detected in 241 patients (7.9%), with 50.6% being bilateral. ICA dehiscence occurred in 24 patients (0.8%), with no significant right–left difference. OC protrusion was present in 544 patients (17.8%), nearly half bilateral (46.3%). OC dehiscence was identified in 101 patients (3.3%). Sex-related differences were observed in the laterality distribution of lamina papyracea dehiscence and optic canal variants, whereas ICA laterality did not differ significantly.ConclusionsLP dehiscence, ICA protrusion/dehiscence, and OC protrusion/dehiscence are clinically relevant variations that must be recognized prior to FESS. Their detection on CT is essential for surgical planning, as failure to identify these high-risk anatomical features may lead to serious complications such as optic nerve injury or ICA hemorrhage. Routine and systematic CT evaluation should therefore be considered mandatory in all sinonasal surgical candidates.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Background

Anatomical variations are among the most frequently observed features in the human body, particularly within the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. A detailed understanding of the complex three-dimensional anatomy of this region and its various morphological variations is essential for surgeons performing endoscopic sinus surgery [1, 2]. Preoperative assessment of anatomical variations such as lamina papyracea dehiscence (LPD), internal carotid artery protrusion (ICA-P), internal carotid artery dehiscence (ICA-D), optic canal protrusion (OCP), and optic canal dehiscence (OCD) is therefore critically important.

The lamina papyracea (LP) is a high-risk anatomical structure during endoscopic sinus surgery. As the thinnest and most fragile portion of the medial orbital wall, the lamina papyracea may develop dehiscence due to congenital, traumatic, iatrogenic, or inflammatory causes. The incidence of LP dehiscence ranges between 0.5% and 10% [1, 2].

Another high-risk region is the sphenoid sinus (SS), which requires particular caution during endoscopic procedures because of its close proximity to vital neurovascular structures. Increased sphenoid sinus pneumatization has been associated with the protrusion of the carotid artery and optic nerve into the lateral wall of the sinus [3]. Studies have reported bone defects over the carotid artery in 5–22% of cases, septal cells in 1–32% of cases, and Onodi cells in 0–18% of cases [3–8]. Bone defects involving the optic nerve have been documented in 1–9% of cases [9, 10].

Identifying these variations preoperatively is crucial for functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS). Furthermore, accurate detection of anatomical anomalies is necessary for accurate diagnosis of sinus pathologies such as infections and tumour infiltration, thereby reducing the risk of intraoperative complications. For these reasons, surgeons must identify relevant variations prior to surgery and tailor their surgical approach accordingly. Computed tomography (CT) is frequently employed for this purpose, as it provides detailed visualization of the anatomy, bony structures, and spatial relationships within the paranasal region.

The aim of the present study is to provide reliable prevalence data of anatomical variations known to vary widely in the literature by analysing several CT scans and to emphasize the critical role of CT imaging in preventing surgical complications.

Methods

The present study was conducted between June 2024 and October 2025 at the Otolaryngology and Radiology Departments of the University of Health Sciences Basaksehir Cam and Sakura City Hospital. This retrospective, observational cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the same institution (Approval No: 2024–117, dated 24.07.2024). This study included an analysis of 5805 paranasal computed tomography (CT) scans obtained at the institution for various indications between June 2020 and October 2025, in which the sinuses and lamina papyracea were fully visualized. Patients whose CT scans were performed because of maxillofacial trauma, who were under 18 years of age, or who had a previous history of endoscopic sinus surgery were excluded from the study.

A total of 3052 patients who met the inclusion criteria were evaluated according to demographic characteristics such as sex. In addition, patients were examined in detail for the presence of lamina papyracea dehiscence (LPD), internal carotid artery protrusion (ICA-P), internal carotid artery dehiscence (ICA-D), optic canal protrusion (OCP), and optic canal dehiscence (OCD).

Patients with LPD were assessed according to the side of the pathology, the grade of dehiscence, and its location within the anterior or posterior ethmoid region. Patients with ICA-P, ICA-D, OCP, or OCD were evaluated on the basis of the laterality of the variation, categorized as right, left, or bilateral.

CT protocol and radiological evaluation

All patients underwent paranasal sinus CT examinations with a 128-slice multidetector CT scanner (Somatom Definition, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). All scans were obtained without the administration of contrast material. Axial images were acquired with a slice thickness of 1 mm, and the coronal and sagittal planes were reconstructed using multiplanar reformation (MPR) techniques. Standard bone and soft tissue reconstruction algorithms were employed to achieve optimal visualization of the sinonasal anatomy.

All the image datasets were transferred to the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Evaluations were conducted by two radiologists, each with five years of experience in head and neck radiology, and all findings required consensus. Reviewers were blinded to all clinical data, including patient history and imaging indications. For patients with multiple CT examinations, the most recent scan without artefacts but optimal visualization of anatomical structures was included in the study.

Lamina papyracea dehiscence grading system

Patients in whom dehiscence was detected were evaluated in terms of sex, laterality (right or left), degree of dehiscence, and anatomical location. Dehiscence was classified into three grades:

Grade 1 dehiscence: involvement of less than one-third of the lamina papyracea wall.

Grade 2 dehiscence: involvement of between one-third and two-thirds of the wall.

Grade 3 dehiscence: involvement of more than two-thirds of the wall [3, 11] (Fig. 1).

Although protrusion of the internal carotid artery and optic canal into the sphenoid sinus has been described using various radiological criteria, a universally accepted and reproducible grading system for the extent of bony dehiscence of these structures has not been established. Across prior studies, definitions and thresholds differ (e.g., qualitative descriptions versus percentage-based criteria), and the distinction between true dehiscence and an extremely thin bony covering may be affected by CT acquisition parameters and partial-volume effects. In light of this heterogeneity, we evaluated carotid canal and optic canal dehiscence primarily as binary findings (present/absent) and focused on clearly definable categories to maximize interobserver reproducibility (Figs. 4 and 5).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables (laterality and anatomical variations) are reported as counts and percentages. The prevalence of lamina papyracea dehiscence, internal carotid artery protrusion/dehiscence, and optic canal protrusion/dehiscence was calculated. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., the presence of each variation) were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Differences in the distribution of laterality categories (right/left/bilateral) and in categorical subgroups (e.g., grades and anatomical locations) were evaluated using chi-square tests. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

The medial orbital wall is composed anterior to posterior of the frontal process of the maxillary bone, the lacrimal bone, and the body of the sphenoid bone. The lamina papyracea (LP), which forms the critical boundary between the orbit and the ethmoid sinus, is recognized as the thinnest and most vulnerable segment of the medial orbital wall, with a typical thickness ranging from 0.2 to 0.4 mm. Lamina papyracea dehiscence (LPD) may arise from a variety of aetiologies, including herniation of orbital fat and/or extraocular muscles, trauma, abscess formation, mass lesions, congenital defects, and iatrogenic injuries.

LPD is considered the most common anatomical variation involving the medial orbital wall; however, the reported incidence rates have shown substantial variability. Early studies described an incidence ranging from 0.5% to 10% in the general population, a discrepancy largely attributed to small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, and inconsistent diagnostic definitions. More recent investigations employing high-resolution paranasal CT imaging revealed a narrower incidence range, typically between 1% and 3% [3, 4].

One of the primary objectives of preoperative paranasal CT imaging in endoscopic sinus surgery is the identification of critical anatomical variations that may increase the risk of intraoperative complications. The acronym “CLOSE”, which represents the cribriform plate, lamina papyracea, Onodi cells, sphenoid sinus pneumatization, and anterior ethmoidal artery, as defined in radiology, underscores the clinical importance of the lamina papyracea in this context [13].

In the present study (n = 3,052), the incidence of lamina papyracea defects was 2.3%. Recent studies have reported varying incidences of lamina papyracea dehiscence: Jing Xu et al. reported 2.58%, Deniz et al. reported 2%, Fadda et al. reported 0.3%, and Dasar et al. reported 0.5% [1, 14–16].

Among the potential causes of orbital complications during endoscopic sinus surgery are LP dehiscence and subsequent herniation of orbital fat and/or extraocular muscles into the ethmoid sinus. Surgical injury to the LP can lead to a variety of orbital complications, including orbital fat herniation, strabismus, diplopia, intraorbital haemorrhage, orbital emphysema, and even blindness.

Both earlier and contemporary series indicate that a substantial proportion of lamina papyracea dehiscences are either not mentioned at all in radiological reports or mistakenly interpreted as inflammatory pathology. This is clinically important, as such misinterpretation may lead to inappropriate management, such as unnecessary antibiotic therapy, biopsy, or surgery, as well as unexpected orbital complications during FESS. It is therefore recommended that the integrity of the lamina papyracea be routinely assessed in radiological reporting, with explicit documentation of any dehiscence, its laterality (unilateral or bilateral), its precise anatomical location (e.g., level of the anterior ethmoidal artery notch or nasolacrimal duct), and the degree of herniation of orbital contents (fat or medial rectus muscle) [13, 14, 17].

In the series by Moulin et al., as well as in more recent case reports, LPD was frequently misinterpreted as an infection or a tumour, resulting in unnecessary invasive procedures [1, 13, 18]. Similarly, Xu et al. reported that in 8 of 23 cases of LPD, the CT findings were misinterpreted, leading to misdiagnosis as ethmoid sinusitis and highlighting the need for improvements in imaging interpretation [1].

LPD is most commonly observed unilaterally, with bilateral cases being relatively rare. In the present study, the rate of unilateral lamina papyracea dehiscence (LPD) was 95.6%, and the dehiscence rates were comparable between the right and left sides. Statistical analysis further demonstrated that the distribution of LPD laterality differed significantly between sexes (Table 2). Xu et al. reported that the incidence of dehiscence on the right and left sides was similar, with no statistically significant difference between them [1]. Similarly, Deniz et al. reported no significant association between the location of dehiscence and its severity [14].

LPD is classified into three grades based on the extent of herniation [3, 11]. In the present study, cases were categorized as grade 1 in 70.0%, grade 2 in 25.7%, and grade 3 in 4.3%. Statistical analysis demonstrated that the distribution of LPD grades differed significantly between sexes (Table 2). In the study by Xu et al., grade 1 dehiscence was reported as the most common (69%), whereas grade 3 was the least frequent (8.70%) [1]. Deniz et al. similarly identified grade 1 as the most prevalent (70%) and grade 3 as the least common (10%) [13]. In the present study, consistent with existing literature, grade 1 LPD was the most frequent (70%), whereas grade 3 was the least common (4.3%).

Internal carotid artery (ICA) dehiscence and protrusion represent some of the most critical anatomical variations in the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, particularly within the sphenoid sinus. Because the lateral wall of the sphenoid sinus is largely formed by the cavernous segment of the ICA, this vessel is at direct risk during both inflammatory processes and endoscopic surgical interventions. Excessive pneumatization of the sphenoid sinus may lead to protrusion of the artery into the sinus cavity. Awareness of this variation is essential for the prevention of potentially fatal complications.

In the literature, the concepts of internal carotid artery (ICA) “bulging/protrusion” into the sphenoid sinus and “dehiscence” (complete absence of the bony cortex separating the vessel from the sinus cavity) have not always been consistently defined. In some studies, protrusion is described as the extension of more than half of the arterial lumen radius into the sinus or as any degree of bony indentation, whereas dehiscence is generally defined as the complete absence of cortical bone or a residual bone thickness of < 0.5 mm. This terminological heterogeneity is among the main reasons for the wide variation in reported prevalence rates [19, 20].

In the present study, the incidence of ICA protrusion was 7.9%, whereas the incidence of ICA dehiscence was 0.8%. ICA protrusion was bilateral in 50.6% of patients, which is consistent with previous reports [9, 21]. With respect to ICA protrusion and dehiscence, the distribution of laterality (right/left/bilateral) did not differ significantly between sexes (p = 0.075 and p = 0.418, respectively). When ICA protrusion was graded according to the < 50% and ≥ 50% thresholds, 54.7% of cases were classified as < 50% protrusion and 45.3% as ≥ 50% protrusion. In large CT series, the reported incidence of ICA protrusion ranges from 2% to 67%, whereas the incidence of ICA dehiscence ranges from 0.4% to 35% [4–6, 8, 15, 19, 22, 23]. In the series by Fadda et al. involving 230 patients, the ICA was normal in 72.2%, protruding in 26.3%, and showing isolated dehiscence in 0.4% [19]. In the study by Asal et al., carotid canal protrusion was identified in 23.9–32.1% of males and 35.5–36.2% of females, whereas carotid canal dehiscence was found in 34% of females and 22% of males [24]. Kazkayası et al. reported an ICA protrusion rate of 5.2% and an ICA dehiscence rate of 1.5%, with 50% of the dehiscences being bilateral [9]. In the study by Mendonça et al., 92.4% of ICA protrusions were classified as < 50%, whereas 7.6% were graded as ≥ 50%. Compared with the findings of the present study, this heterogeneity may be attributable to differences in study populations (including demographic/ethnic characteristics), sample size, and variability in imaging protocols [25].

Variations in the bony thickness overlying the internal carotid artery and the optic canal have been widely documented across different studies. Dehiscence may occur along the course of the optic nerve within the sphenoid sinus. The thickness of the bone separating the carotid artery from the sinus cavity has been reported to be approximately 0.5–1 mm in 66–88% of cases [26, 27]. In certain situations, a thin bony wall over the vessel may be absent, leaving only the mucosa between the internal carotid artery and the sinus cavity. Similarly, in some cases, the optic nerve may lack a bony covering and be separated from the sphenoid sinus solely by the mucosa. These conditions correspond to optic canal and internal carotid artery dehiscence [9].

Optic nerve protrusion and dehiscence, particularly at the level of the sphenoid sinus and posterior ethmoid cells (Onodi cells), are among the most critical neurovascular variations in endoscopic sinus surgery and endoscopic skull base procedures.

In the present study, the prevalence of optic canal protrusion was 17.8%, whereas the prevalence of optic canal dehiscence was 3.3%. Notably, a significant sex-related difference was observed in the laterality distribution (right/left/bilateral) of optic canal protrusion (p = 0.023) (Table 4). Likewise, the laterality distribution of optic canal dehiscence (right, left, and bilateral) differed significantly between sexes (p = 0.015). In large series, the prevalence of optic canal protrusion has been reported to range between 2.3% and 35.6%, while optic canal dehiscence rates range from 0.7% to 30.6% [19, 28–30]. In the CT series by Fadda et al., optic canal protrusion was identified in 13% of cases, and optic canal dehiscence was identified in 1.5%, with broad variability in the literature attributed largely to differing definitions and sample characteristics [19]. Kazkayası et al. reported an optic canal dehiscence rate of 0.7%, whereas Asal et al. reported rates of 11.3% in males and 9.9% in females [9, 24]. In the study by Dahal et al., dehiscence was identified in 14.3% of 105 optic nerves evaluated in 78 individuals. Among these individuals, 41 were female and 37 were male, and no sex-related differences were observed in the incidence of optic nerve dehiscence. The overall prevalence was reported to be 21.3%. Dehiscence was detected in 49 right-sided optic nerves and 56 left-sided optic nerves, with 27 cases showing bilateral involvement; these findings are consistent with those of earlier studies [7]. In the paediatric population, Tursun et al. reported optic canal dehiscence in 20.0% of males and 21.4% of females [31]. Published data on grading optic canal protrusion using the < 50% and ≥ 50% thresholds vary substantially across studies. In the present study, 53.6% of optic canal protrusions were classified as < 50%, whereas 46.3% were graded as ≥ 50%. Similarly, Tarık et al. reported < 50% protrusion in 61.2% and ≥ 50% protrusion in 38.8% of cases, while Itagi et al. reported corresponding rates of 37.5% and 62.5%, respectively. In contrast, Mendonça et al. found a markedly higher proportion of < 50% protrusion (91.6%) and a lower proportion of ≥ 50% protrusion (8.4%). This heterogeneity may be attributable to differences in study populations (including demographic/ethnic characteristics), sample size, imaging protocols, and methodological variability in the definition and assessment of protrusion [25, 32, 33].

Although optic nerve protrusion or dehiscence is not a pathology in itself, it does pose challenges during FESS and endoscopic skull base surgery, as it increases the risk of major complications such as optic neuropathy, visual field defects, and even complete blindness. During endoscopic sphenoidotomy or transsphenoidal pituitary surgery, uncontrolled penetration of the lateral sphenoid wall or Onodi cells may result in optic nerve injury [34]. Moreover, failure to recognize these variations preoperatively may lead to catastrophic outcomes during FESS, such as blindness due to optic nerve trauma or fatal haemorrhage from internal carotid artery injury. Therefore, surgeons must meticulously review radiographic images preoperatively, identify anatomical variations, and avoid potential iatrogenic complications.

The limitations of the study

The present study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-centre design represents a major limitation. Because this study population consisted solely of adult patients over 18 years of age who underwent CT imaging for clinical indications, the findings may not be directly generalizable to asymptomatic individuals in the general population, paediatric groups, or different ethnic/demographic populations. Although all evaluations were performed by two experienced radiologists and the findings required consensus, interobserver and intraobserver agreement coefficients (such as kappa values) were not calculated; therefore, potential hidden observer variability in measurement and classification could not be quantitatively assessed. Furthermore, although variations in the lamina papyracea, internal carotid artery, and optic canal were described in detail, their associations with surgical findings, intraoperative complications, clinical symptoms, and disease severity were not analysed. Another limitation is that only conventional multidetector CT was performed; more advanced modalities, such as cone-beam CT (CBCT) or three-dimensional volumetric imaging, were not employed to validate bony thickness and volumetric relationships. This may represent a technical limitation, particularly in distinguishing dehiscence from protrusion in areas with extremely thin cortical bone. A standardized, universally accepted grading system for the extent of carotid canal and optic canal dehiscence does not exist; therefore, we evaluated these findings primarily as binary variables (present/absent). This may have limited a more granular assessment of dehiscence severity and its clinical implications. However, owing to the retrospective nature of the study, only CT imaging data readily available in system were used. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and systematic evaluation approach constitute major strengths that enhance the contribution of the present study to the literature. Future multicentre, prospective studies incorporating clinical correlations will certainly improve the accuracy and clinical relevance of data in this field.

Conclusion

This study, which is based on a large sample size, clearly demonstrates that variations in the lamina papyracea, internal carotid artery, and optic canal occur at clinically significant rates. Owing to their complex anatomy and close proximity to critical structures, the paranasal sinuses and nasal cavity are considered at high risk of serious injury during surgery. To minimize intraoperative complications and to accurately identify anatomical variations and potential pathologies, paranasal sinus CT, which is considered the gold standard imaging modality, should be routinely performed. The surgeon should perform a comprehensive radiological assessment during the preoperative period. Failure to do so will inevitably increase the risk of severe or even fatal complications. The data obtained from CT imaging should be used to enhance the surgeon’s’understanding of surgical dissection boundaries. A thorough understanding of sinus morphology ultimately reduces the risk of complications.
