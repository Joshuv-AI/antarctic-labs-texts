Comparing Model-agnostic Feature Selection Methods through Relative Efficiency
Chenghui Zheng, Garvesh Raskutti

Abstract
Feature selection and importance estimation in a model-agnostic setting is an ongoing challenge of significant interest. Wrapper methods are commonly used because they are typically model-agnostic, even though they are computationally intensive. In this paper, we focus on feature selection methods related to the Generalized Covariance Measure (GCM) and Leave-One-Covariate-Out (LOCO) estimation, and provide a comparison based on relative efficiency. In particular, we present a theoretical comparison under three model settings: linear models, non-linear additive models, and single index models that mimic a single-layer neural network. We complement this with extensive simulations and real data examples. Our theoretical results, along with empirical findings, demonstrate that GCM-related methods generally outperform LOCO under suitable regularity conditions. Furthermore, we quantify the asymptotic relative efficiency of these approaches. Our simulations and real data analysis include widely used machine learning methods such as neural networks and gradient boosting trees. Keywords: conditional independence; feature selection; generalized covariance measure; leave-one-covariate-out; model-agnostic methods; variable importance.

Introduction

Feature selection is one of the fundamental challenges in statistics and machine learning. While embedded methods such as Lasso and related techniques have been widely used under parametric assumptions, there is a growing trend toward using “black-box” prediction methods such as random forests, neural networks, and others. In such settings, wrapper methods for feature selection which are typically
model-agnostic
have become increasingly popular.

This paper focuses on two classes of wrapper methods: Leave-One-Covariate-Out (LOCO)
[
25
]
and the Generalized Covariance Measure (GCM)
[
35
]
. LOCO may be viewed as a generalization of ANOVA-based methods that measure the change in prediction error when a feature is removed
[
11
,
14
]
. GCM, on the other hand, generalizes traditional correlation-based tests
[
30
,
36
,
39
]
to conditional correlation, and can capture general functional relationships. While there has been prior work analyzing each of these methods and establishing asymptotic normality
[
41
,
35
]
, there is no quantitative comparison of their performance in feature selection that we are aware of.

Concretely, consider a prediction model:

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{p}]=f(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{p})

for some unknown function
f
, where
Y
is the response and
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{p})
are the covariates. The goal of feature selection is to identify which covariates have a significant impact on
Y
. This enhances model interpretability, reduces overfitting, and can improve predictive performance.

In this paper, we compare GCM and LOCO with respect to statistical inference and practical applications under three statistical models: linear models, non-linear additive models and single-index models. We also include other model-agnostic variable importance methods such as:
the
dropout method
, which replaces a feature with its marginal mean to avoid retraining;

Lazy-VI

[
18
,
38
]
, which approximates the reduced model for efficiency while maintaining inferential guarantees.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section

3

4
extends the comparison to dropout and Lazy-VI. Sections
5
and

7

1.1
Related Work

Feature selection techniques fall into three main categories: filter-based, wrapper-based, and embedded methods.
Filter methods
involve calculating a relevance score for each feature independently of any model and rank features accordingly. They are computationally efficient but ignore interactions between features. Examples include mutual information
[
22
]
, information gain, Pearson correlation
[
30
,
43
]
, the Chi-squared test
[
42
]
, and the Fisher score
[
11
]
.
Wrapper methods
involve evaluating subsets of features by training models and measuring performance, often using criteria like AIC, BIC, or Cp. Common algorithms include forward selection, backward elimination
[
24
]
, and recursive feature elimination
[
19
]
. While they account for feature interactions, they are computationally expensive and prone to overfitting due to repeated training.
Embedded methods
perform feature selection during model training. Examples include Lasso
[
26
]
, elastic net
[
46
]
, decision trees, random forests
[
33
]
, and naive Bayes
[
10
]
. Embedded methods are generally more efficient than wrappers but are tied to specific model classes.

Wrapper-style, model-agnostic feature selection has grown in popularity due to its flexibility. Among global model-agnostic approaches, permutation importance
[
6
]
is the most widely used. It disrupts a feature’s relationship with the response by permuting values and measuring the effect on predictive accuracy
[
37
,
13
,
27
,
1
]
. However, this method introduces randomness, which can lead to instability.

The knockoff framework
[
4
,
7
]
uses conditional randomization to control false discovery by generating synthetic “null” copies of features. While powerful, this approach relies on the assumption of feature exchangeability, which is difficult to verify in practice.

Replacing rather than permuting a feature—e.g., the dropout method—offers computational efficiency but may underfit
[
8
]
. LOCO avoids this issue by removing a feature and retraining, and has been generalized for conformal inference
[
25
]
and bootstrapping in high-dimensional settings
[
32
]
.

Conditional Independence Tests

Feature selection directly relates to
conditional independence testing
. In particular, for any feature
X_{j}
, it is informative to
Y
if and only if:

Y\not\perp X_{j}|X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{j-1},X_{j+1},X_{j+2},...,X_{p}

or
Y
is not independent of
X_{j}
given all other variables/features. Thus a feature
X_{j}
is selected based on a conditional independence test. Classical tools like partial correlations rely on strong parametric assumptions. More flexible tests include Conditional randomization
[
7
]
, Holdout randomization
[
40
]
, Kernel-based
[
45
]
and Mutual information-based
[
15
]
. However, many of these methods are computationally expensive and rely on estimating conditional distributions. Generalized Covariance Measure (GCM)
[
35
]
, which tests for vanishing normalized covariance between residuals, is a regression-based test that requires only mild assumptions and works well in high dimensions and nonlinear settings.

This paper focuses on analyzing wrapper-based feature selection using LOCO and GCM. All methods yield population-level variable importance scores and support hypothesis testing. We provide theoretical and empirical comparisons across various settings.

Our contributions

This paper includes the following main contributions:

•

Provides an asymptotic relative efficiency comparison for GCM and LOCO in 3 different model settings: linear models, additive models and single index models. In particular we prove that GCM is asymptotically more efficient compared to LOCO for the linear model case, provide conditions under which GCM is more asymptotically efficient than LOCO for non-linear models, and show that in single-index models it holds under mild conditions.

•

We provide a further comparison of asymptotic variance between LOCO and the more computationally efficient dropout and lazy fine-tuning based estimator
[
18
]
.

•

Finally, we conduct a comparison of these different approaches along with other state-of-the-art approaches over a series of simulated and real data with different algorithms including neural networks and tree-based algorithms. The simulation results support the theory and show that GCM is often more accurate although computationally more intensive than other methods.

2.1
Generalized Covariance Measure

The Generalized Covariance Measure (GCM) is a conditional independence statistic/test used to test whether two univariate variables

2.1.1
GCM limitation

One of the well-known limitations of GCM is that a covariance of
0
does not necessarily imply independence which is especially relevant for non-linear relationship. Consider the simple example

Y=X^{2}+\epsilon

where
X
is a symmetric distribution (e.g. normal) and
\epsilon
is zero-mean independent noise. It follows that
\mbox{Cov}(X^{2},Y)=0
and the GCM statistic satisfies
\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)}=0
when clearly
X
and
Y
are dependent. This extends to any scenario where the functional relationship is an even function and the random variable has a symmetric distribution. This is a clear limitation of GCM that will be observed in both the theoretical and empirical results.

2.2
Leave-one-covariate-out

Variable importance is designed to provide a score for each feature based on how much difference omitting or replacing the feature makes in prediction accuracy. Before outlining how variable importance can be used for feature selection tasks, we first review the framework generalized and extended by Williamson et al.

2.3
Asymptotic efficiency and co-efficient of variation

One of the main focuses of this paper is to provide a comparison of different feature selection estimators. From a theoretical perspective, estimators can be compared through
asymptotic relative efficiency
. In particular, as shown in the previous section, both estimators GCM and LOCO asymptotically follow a normal distribution with characterized asymptotic mean and variance.

When the means of two estimators are the same, efficiency simply equates to the variance. However, since most of the estimators we consider have different means, we use the so-called
co-efficient of variation

\frac{\sigma}{\mu}
, a measure of dispersion defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean.

Definition 2.1
.

Asymptotic relative efficiency
Let
T^{(n)}_{1},T^{(n)}_{2}
be two estimators, then the
asymptotic relative efficiency
(ARE)
e_{T_{1},T_{2}}
based on the co-efficient of variation

e_{T_{1},T_{2}}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{Var(T^{(n)}_{2})/\mathbb{E}^{2}[T^{(n)}_{2}]}{Var(T^{(n)}_{1})/\mathbb{E}^{2}[T^{(n)}_{1}]}.

3
Main results: ARE comparison

In this section, we demonstrate how GCM and LOCO perform variable selection tasks and evaluate their efficiency across different models, such as linear model, single index model and non-linear additive model. Throughout we define

\tilde{X}_{j}:=X_{j}-\mathbb{E}[X_{j}|X_{-j}]

which denotes the residual based on predicting
X_{j}
from
X_{-j}
. Note that if
X_{j}
is independent of
X_{-j}
, then
\tilde{X}_{j}=X_{j}-\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]
, while higher dependence reduces this residual.

3.1
Linear Model

Let’s consider
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}
with joint distribution
P_{0}
satisfying the linear model

Y=X_{-j}^{T}\beta_{-j}+\mathnormal{\beta}_{j}X_{j}+\epsilon

where
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\beta_{-j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
are regression coefficients, and
\epsilon
is a random noise term with zero mean and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
.

To select the most important features in the linear model, we are actually performing individual hypothesis testing
\forall j\in[p]
,
H_{0}:\beta_{j}=0
. This is equivalent to the GCM test: if
X_{j}
is dependent of the target
Y
given the rest features, and LOCO test: if
X_{j}
provide additional model prediction power when the rest features are already included.

Theorem 3
.

For a linear model, where
Y=X_{-j}^{T}\beta_{-j}+\mathnormal{\beta}_{j}X_{j}+\epsilon
and
\epsilon
is random noise with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
.
\forall j\in[p]
, we have

\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}{\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\beta_{j}\sqrt{Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+\frac{1}{\beta_{j}^{2}}\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}}{\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})};\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}{\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\beta_{j}^{2}\sqrt{\mbox{Var}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+\frac{4}{\beta_{j}^{2}}\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}}{\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}

and thus
e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}>1
.

The expressions for the ratio of standard deviation and expectation for each estimator is instructive in that they are equivalent once dividing numerator and denominator by
\beta_{j}
aside from an additional factor of
4
in the standard deviation for the LOCO estimator. This displays a clear increase in efficiency for the GCM estimator. As far as we are aware this is the first direct comparison between the two, even for a linear model. And note that there are no assumptions of dependence/correlation so the result holds regardless of the dependence/correlation structure.

The key quantities that determine how much the efficiency is larger for GCM compared to LOCO is depends on
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\beta_{j}^{2}}
as well as
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})
and
Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})
where
\tilde{X_{j}}=X_{j}-\mathbb{E}[X_{j}|X_{-j}]
. In particular if
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\beta_{j}^{2}}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})
is large relative to
Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})
then the gap in efficiency between GCM and LOCO increases.

Below we provide a simple 2-variable example that illustrates the efficiency ratio.

Example 3.1
.

Suppose
Y=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{1}+\beta_{2}X_{2}+\epsilon
where
X_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),i=1,2
,
Cov(X_{1},X_{2})=\rho
, and
\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
is independent of features. Also, known that
X_{1}|X_{2}\sim N(\rho X_{2},(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2})
, then the asymptotic relative efficiency of LOCO and GCM estimators for the testing of
X_{1}
is

e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,1}}=\frac{4\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}}+2(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}}{\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}}+2(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}}

Specifically, if
\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{\beta_{1}^{2}}\rightarrow 0
, then
e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(loco)}}\rightarrow 1
and if
\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{\beta_{1}^{2}}\rightarrow\infty
then
e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(loco)}}\rightarrow 4
.

3.2
Non-linear Additive Model

While the linear model is instructive, the main focus of this paper is the non-linear setting. In this section, we consider settings where the regression functions are non-linear and additive. Let

Y=\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{0,j}(X_{j})+\epsilon

where
f_{0,j}
’s are non-linear and measurable, and there are no interaction terms among features,
\epsilon
are i.i.d mean zero with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
\epsilon\perp X
.

For each
j\in[p]
, both GCM and LOCO are performing the following hypothesis test:

H_{0}:\psi^{(\cdot)}_{0,j}=0\hskip 14.22636pt;\hskip 14.22636ptH_{a}:\psi^{(\cdot)}_{0,j}\neq 0.

For regressing
X_{j}
and
Y
on
X_{-j}
respectively for GCM, let
g_{0},h_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
for function class
\mathcal{F}
,

X_{j}=h_{0,j}(X_{-j})+\xi_{-j}\hskip 14.22636pt;\hskip 14.22636ptY=g_{0,j}(X_{-j})+\epsilon_{-j}

where
\epsilon_{-j},\xi_{-j}
are i.i.d mean zero with finite variance
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon},\sigma_{\xi}^{2}
respectively,
\epsilon_{-j},\xi_{-j}\perp X
. Let

h_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}),g_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j})
, recall
\tilde{X_{j}}:=X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})
, and
\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}):=f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j})
. We imposing the following assumption on
\mbox{Corr}(\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))
:

Assumption (B)
.

\mbox{Corr}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))>\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{4}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))}

Note that Assumption (B) fails for the example where if
X_{j}
is drawn from a symmetric distribution and
f_{0,j}(.)
is an even function since
\mbox{Corr}(\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))=0
. The assumption therefore ensures that this correlation between
\tilde{X}_{j}
and
\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j})
is sufficiently far away from
0
. Also note that in the case of the linear model where
f_{0,j}(X_{j})=\beta_{j}X_{j}
Assumption (B) is always satisfied.

Theorem 4
.

For a non-linear additive regression model estimated with mean squared error loss,
Y=f_{0}(X)+\epsilon=f_{0,-j}(X_{-j})+f_{0,j}(X_{j})+\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
are i.i.d ransom noise with zero mean and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
.
\forall j\in[p]

\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}{\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{Var(\tilde{X}_{j}\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))};\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}{\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{Var(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))}}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))}

Therefore under condition (B),
e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}>1.

Next, we present two nonlinear additive regression functions as concrete examples to illustrate that the GCM estimator is (approximately) asymptotically more efficient than the LOCO estimator when condition (B) in Theorem

is satisfied, and vice versa. The corresponding simulation study will also be shown in Section

Example 3.2

(3)

3.3
Single Index Model

Next, we will generalize to a semi-parametric extension of the linear model: single index model which has connections to a shallow neural network. It describes the relation between a scalar response variable
Y\in\mathbb{R}
and a predictor vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
via

Y=\eta(X^{T}\beta)+\epsilon

where
\beta=(\beta_{i},...,\beta_{p})^{T}
is unknown parameter vector,
\eta
is an unknown link function, and
\epsilon
are i.i.d errors with zero mean and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
\epsilon\perp X
. Then, for each
j\in[p]
, GCM and LOCO perform the same individual hypothesis testing as discussed in linear model.
One way to achieve the co-efficient of variation of these two estimators in single index model is through Taylor expansion of
\eta(X^{T}\beta)
around
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})
. For notation simplicity, we use
\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))
.

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=\eta(X^{T}\beta)&=\eta(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))+\eta^{\prime}\cdot(X^{T}\beta-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))+\Delta\end{split}

where
\Delta=\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}\eta^{(s)}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))(X^{T}\beta-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))^{s}
.
Let’s introduce the notation for order of approximation used in the following result:

f(n)=o(1)\text{ means if for all }\epsilon>0,\text{there exists }M>0,\text{such that }|f(n)|\leq\epsilon\text{ for all }n>M.

Assumption (A1)
.

\eta
is fixed, monotone differentiable function with bounded derivatives
\eta^{(s)}(X^{T}\beta)

\leq O(a^{-X^{T}\beta})
,
a>1
, for all
s\geq 2
.

Assumption (A2)
.

X
has sub-Gaussian distribution with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
(i.e.:
\forall t\in\mathbb{R},P(|X-\mathbb{E}(X)|>t)\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
.

Assumption (A3)
.

X^{T}\beta
has a density that is bounded from above by
\bar{q}
.

Assumption (A4)
.

For any
\epsilon>0,\delta>0
, there exists a constant C, such that
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})\geq C\max\{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta}},\sqrt{K}\}
, for some constant
K\geq 3
and tail probability
\delta
.

Theorem 5
.

Under the assumptions (A1) to (A4), consider a single index model estimated with mean squared error loss,
Y=\eta(X^{T}\beta)+\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is random noise with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
.
\forall j\in[p],
we have

\begin{split}\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}{\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}&=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\eta^{\prime 2}\beta_{j}^{2}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+o(1)}}{\beta_{j}\eta^{\prime}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+o(1)};\\
\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}{\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}&=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\eta^{\prime 4}\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+4\sigma^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+o(1)}}{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+o(1)}.\end{split}

For any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0,\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}>1-\epsilon^{\prime}.

Remark 1
.

•

The asymptotic efficiency proof technique here is similar to Theorem
3
except that we additionally employ a linear approximation via Taylor series expansion of
\eta(X^{T}\beta)
around
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})
, so assumption (A1) and (A4) ensure that first order approximation of
\eta
is sufficient and higher order derivatives terms are negligible, such that
\Delta,\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})=o(1)
.

•

(A2) and (A3) are relaxed assumptions for the guarantee of the convergence of the least-square estimator of
(\eta,\beta)
in monotone single index models introduced in Theorem 4.1 in
[
2
]
. They will also be used to bound the polynomial terms in Taylor series expansion.

The single index model is useful given its connection to single-layer neural networks if we have an unknown and fixed link function, or a generalized linear model if the link function is unknown. Thus, we illustrate a binary classification example to compare the relative efficiency of GCM and LOCO hypothesis testing where we continue using the same linear predictor set up as in Example
3.1
but with the sigmoid link function.

Example 3.4
.

Suppose
Y=\eta(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{1}+\beta_{2}X_{2})+\epsilon
where
\eta
is sigmoid function (ie:
\eta(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
),
X_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),i=1,2
,
Cov(X_{1},X_{2})=\rho
, and
\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
is independent of matrix
X
.

The sigmoid function
\eta
is an increasing function with recursive derivatives
[
28
]

\eta^{(s)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}(-1)^{k}\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s-1}{k}\right\rangle\eta(x)^{k+1}(1-\eta(x))^{s-k}
,
where
\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s-1}{k}\right\rangle
are Eulerian numbers. Thus,
\eta^{(s)}(x)\leq e^{-|x|}C_{s}=O(e^{-|x|})=o(1)
for
s\geq 2
when
|x|\rightarrow\infty
, so the higher order derivatives in the remainder term of linear Taylor series expansion is negligible. The polynomial term in the remainder decays fast because of normal random variable
X_{j}
. For notation simplicity, let
\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{1}+\beta_{2}X_{2}|X_{2}))=\eta^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\rho X_{2}+\beta_{2}X_{2}))=\eta\left(\beta_{0}+(\beta_{1}\rho+\beta_{2})X_{2}\right)\cdot\left(1-\eta\left(\beta_{0}+(\beta_{1}\rho+\beta_{2})X_{2}\right)\right)

Thus, the relative efficiency of GCM and LOCO test statistics for

e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}_{0,1}^{(loco)}}=\bigg(\frac{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,1}})}{\beta_{1}\eta^{\prime}sd({\hat{\psi}^{(gcm)}_{0,1}})}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{\frac{4}{\beta_{1}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}}\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}+2(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\sigma^{4}}{\frac{1}{\beta_{1}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}}\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}+2(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\sigma^{4}}\bigg(1+o(1)\bigg).

Thus, for any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, there exists a sufficiently large

.

4
Extension to comparison with Dropout and Lazy-VI

The previous section compares the asymptotic relative efficiency for GCM and LOCO. In this section, we extend the comparison to estimators from dropout and Lazy Variable Importance (Lazy-VI)
[
18
]
approaches which are used as computationally faster approaches that attempt to estimate similar parameters to LOCO. Let’s first review the setup of dropout and Lazy-VI.

4.1
Dropout

The dropout test is a counterpart to LOCO, but it does not require re-training a new model when excluding the
j^{th}
feature. Instead, the
j^{th}
feature is replaced by its corresponding marginal mean and then reinserted into the pre-trained full model
f_{0}
. Let’s define the dropout features as
X^{(j)}
(resp.
X_{i}^{(j)}
), where
X^{(j)}=(X_{1},...,X_{j-1},\mu_{j},X_{j+1},...,X_{p})
. The plug-in estimator for the dropout method is

\hat{\psi}^{(dr)}_{0,j}:=V(f_{n},P_{n})-V(f_{n},\tilde{P}_{n,-j})

where
\tilde{P}_{n,-j}
is the empirical distributions of
X^{(j)}
. Let
\epsilon^{(j)}:=Y-f_{0,-j}(X^{(j)})
, and define
\Delta\bm{e}^{2^{(dr)}}_{j}
as the estimator of the mean of the difference in the square of errors causing by replacing the
j^{th}
feature with its mean. Then, with mean squared error as our predictive measure, we have the dropout estimator for the feature
j
:

\widehat{\psi}^{(dr)}_{0,j}:=\Delta\bm{e}^{2^{(dr)}}_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{[Y_{i}-f_{n}(X_{ij})]^{2}-[Y_{i}-f_{n}(X_{i}^{(j)})]^{2}\}.

4.2
Lazy-VI

The prediction function
f_{0}
or
f_{0,-j}
in GCM, LOCO, and dropout tests
can be estimated using user-selected methods such as random forests, gradient boosting, kernel SVMs, and others. The Lazy-VI test
[
18
]
is intended as a more computationally efficient version of LOCO, which enables a faster and more flexible method for estimating the reduced model
f_{0,-s}
, but it is primarily studied on neural network since a key assumption of the algorithm requires gradient computation, and an ensemble-based algorithm like random forest does not apply. This approach only requires training the neural network once using the full dataset. Instead of retraining, a linear system is solved to iteratively update the reduced model parameters for
X_{-j}
. Let’s briefly outline the setup for Lazy-VI:

let a neural network function class
\left\{h_{\theta}(x):\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\right\}
be parameterized by a vector
\theta
, such that

\theta_{f}=\underset{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\arg\min}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[Y_{i}-h_{\theta}\left(X_{i}\right)\right]^{2}.

However, unlike LOCO, which need to retrain the reduced model and need to estimate

\theta_{-j}=\underset{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\arg\min}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[Y_{i}-h_{\theta}(X_{i}^{(j)})\right]^{2},

the Lazy-VI using the linear approximation to estimate the difference between the full model parameters
\theta_{f}
and
\theta_{-j}
to estimate
h_{\theta_{-j}}
. This correction is updated by the ridge regression

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{j}(\lambda,n)=\underset{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\arg\min}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[Y_{i}-h_{\theta_{f}}(X_{i}^{(j)})\right.\right.\left.\left.-\left.\omega^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta}(X_{i}^{(j)})\right|_{\theta=\theta_{f}}\right]^{2}+\lambda\|\omega\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

where
\lambda>0
is the penalty parameter.
Hence, the variable importance score under Lazy-VI is

\psi_{0,j}^{(lazy)}=V\left(h_{\theta_{f}},P_{0}\right)-V\left(h_{\theta_{f}+\Delta\theta_{j}(\lambda,n)},P_{0,-j}\right).

The following assumptions are imposed in
[
18
]
for the estimation of variable importance. For explanations of why these assumptions are necessary we refer readers to
[
18
]
. An extension using the early stopping strategy that applies to any gradient-based method (e.g. neural networks, gradient boosted trees, e.t.c.) is provided in
[
38
]
.

Assumption (C1)
.

For any
j\in[p]
and the regularization parameter
\lambda=O(\sqrt{n})
, define
\mathbf{e}^{(j)}:=f_{0,-j}\left(X^{(j)}\right)-h_{\theta_{f}}\left(X^{(j)}\right)
and
\mathbb{K}_{ik}^{(j)}:=\operatorname{ker}_{\theta_{f}}\left(X_{i}^{(j)},X_{k}^{(j)}\right),i,k\in[n]
, we assume:

(a)
\left\|\left[\mathbb{K}^{(j)}+\lambda I_{n}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{e}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}=O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})
;

(b)
\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{K}^{(j)}\right)=O_{P}(n)
.

Assumption (C2)
.

For the noise term
\epsilon_{-j}
, there exists
\sigma
such that for any
j\in[p]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\epsilon_{-j}}\mid X^{(j)}\right]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2},\quad\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

Assumption (C3)
.

Denote the gradient feature matrix as
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times M}=(\left.\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta}\left(X_{1}\right)\right|_{\theta=\theta_{f}},\ldots
,
\left.\nabla_{\theta}h_{\theta}\left(X_{n}\right)\right|_{\theta=\theta_{f}})^{\top}
. We assume
\left\|\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{e}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\leq O_{P}(1)
.

Theorem 6
.

(Re-formulated from
[
18
]
)
Under assumptions (C1) to (C3), the Lazy-VI estimator under the mean squared error predictive measure

\widehat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(lazy)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\left[Y_{i}-h_{\theta_{f}}\left(X_{i}\right)\right]^{2}-\left[Y_{i}-h_{\theta_{f}+\Delta\theta_{j}(\lambda,n)}(X_{i}^{(j)})\right]^{2}\right\}

has the following result:

\sqrt{n}[\hat{\psi}^{(lazy)}_{0,j}-\psi_{0,j}^{(lazy)}]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,\sigma^{2}_{j})\text{, where $\sigma^{2}_{j}=Var(\epsilon^{2}-\epsilon_{-j}^{2}).$ }

LOCO, dropout and Lazy-VI are asymptotically unbiased estimators used to estimate the ability of
X_{j}
in predicting
Y
in terms of the variance only. Then, we can extend the asymptotic relative efficiency comparison to those methods in the following theorem:

Theorem 7
.

Under assumptions (C1)-(C3), together with respective assumptions in Theorem
3
, Theorem
5
, and Theorem
4
, then we have the corresponding asymptotic variance relationship:

\lim_{n\to\infty}Var(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)}){=}\lim_{n\to\infty}Var(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(lazy)}){\leq}\lim_{n\to\infty}Var(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(dr)}).

7
Conclusion

Variable selection is crucial in diverse real-world applications, including healthcare, marketing, genomics, and social science, where it enhances model performance, interpretability, and efficiency by identifying the most relevant factors. A key result of this paper is that GCM demonstrates greater efficiency in variable selection compared to LOCO under certain conditions in different models. We also compare them with other parallel hypothesis testing methods, such as Lazy-VI and Dropout. An application in a variable selection task on real data sets also confirms that the model chosen by GCM arises smaller mse than that by LOCO. Future work of this study could develop a novel GCM-based test statistic that improves computational efficiency or strikes a compromise with Type II error would be a valuable advancement in hypothesis testing methodologies.

Appendix A
Appendix - Mean and variance for test statistics under different models

In the following sections, we compute all the expectation and variance of test statistics for different models. We will focus on the expression of population parameters since the expectation and variance of the plug-in estimator can directly be derived, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(\cdot)})=\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(\cdot)})
and
Var(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(\cdot)})=\frac{1}{n}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(\cdot)})
.

A.1
Linear Model

Recall linear model setup:
Y=X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\mathnormal{\beta}_{j}X_{j}+\epsilon
where
\epsilon
are i.i.d mean zero with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
\epsilon\perp X
.

A.1.1
GCM Test Statistics

For GCM hypothesis testing of each feature
j
, we have the product of residuals from regression as:

X_{j}=h_{0,j}(X_{-j})+\xi_{-j};\;\;Y=g_{0,j}(X_{-j})+\epsilon_{-j}

where
h_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}),g_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j})=X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})
, and
\tilde{X_{j}}:=X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})
.

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)})&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(Y-\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\beta_{j}X_{j}+\epsilon-X_{-j}\beta_{-j}-\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\big\{[X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})][\beta_{j}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))+\epsilon]\big\}\\
&=\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2});\end{split}

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)})&=\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{{(gcm)}^{2}})-(\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)}))^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\big\{[X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})]^{2}[\beta_{j}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))+\epsilon]^{2}\big\}-\beta_{j}^{2}Var^{2}(X_{j}|X_{-j})\\
&=\beta_{j}^{2}Var(\tilde{X_{j}}^{2})+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}).\end{split}

A.1.2
LOCO Test Statistics

In general,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)})&=\mathbb{E}[Y-g_{0,j}(X_{-j})]^{2}-\mathbb{E}[Y-f_{0}(X)]^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)+\epsilon-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))^{2}-(f_{0}(X)+\epsilon-f_{0}(X))^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))^{2}].\end{split}

Under the setting of multivariate linear regression model, we have

\begin{split}&f_{0}(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\beta_{j}X_{j}\\
&g_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j})=X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})\end{split}

Then,

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)}]=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))^{2}]=\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))^{2}=\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2});

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)})&=Var[(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j})))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j})))]^{2}-\beta_{j}^{4}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\\
&=\beta_{j}^{2}\big\{\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))^{4}]+4\sigma^{2}Var(X_{j}|X_{-j})-\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\big\}\\
&=\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X_{j}}^{2})+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}).\end{split}

A.1.3
Dropout Test Statistics

For Dropout estimator, let’s recall
X^{(j)}
is the
j^{th}
column replaced by its marginal mean
\mu_{j}
. In general,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)})&=V(f_{0},P_{0,-j})-V(f_{0},P_{0})\\
&=\mathbb{E}[Y-f_{0}(X^{(j)})]^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{0}[Y-f_{0}(X)]^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}];\end{split}

Under the setting of multivariate linear regression model, we have

\begin{split}&f_{0}(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\beta_{j}X_{j},\\
&f_{0}(X^{(j)})=X_{-j}\beta_{-j}+\beta_{j}\mu_{j}.\end{split}

Then,

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)}]=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}]=\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}((X_{j}-\mu_{j})^{2})=\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})\text{, where }\hat{X}_{j}:=X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j});

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)})&=Var[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))]^{2}-\beta_{j}^{4}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})\\
&=\beta_{j}^{2}\big\{\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mu_{j})^{4}]+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})-\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})\big\}\\
&=\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\hat{X_{j}}^{2})+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2}).\end{split}

A.2
Non-linear Additive Model

In this section, we compute all the expressions of test statistics under non-linear regression system.
Recall the non-linear additive model
Y=f_{0}(X)+\epsilon=f_{0,-j}(X_{-j})+f_{0,j}(X_{j})+\epsilon
where
\epsilon
are i.i.d mean zero with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
\epsilon\perp X
. Let
h_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}),g_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j})
, and
\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}):=f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j})
.

A.2.1
GCM Test Statistics

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)})&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(Y-\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(f_{0,-j}(X_{-j})+f_{0,j}(X_{j})+\epsilon-f_{0,-j}(X_{-j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\big\{[X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})][f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j})+\epsilon]\big\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}));\end{split}

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)})&=\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{{(gcm)}^{2}})-(\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)}))^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\big\{[X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})]^{2}[f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j})+\epsilon]^{2}\big\}\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt-\bigg\{\mathbb{E}\big\{[X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})][f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j})]\big\}\bigg\}^{2}\\
&=Var(\tilde{X}_{j}\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}).\end{split}

A.2.2
LOCO Test Statistics

Let
f_{0,j}(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=f_{0,-j}(X_{-j})+f_{0,j}(X_{j})
, and
g_{0,j}(X_{-j})=\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j})=f_{0,-j}(X_{-j})+\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j})
. Then,
\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)})=\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})|X_{-j}))^{2}=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))
. By the same computation process in linear system, we have
Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)})=Var[(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-g_{0,j}(X_{-j}))]=Var(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))
.

A.2.3
Dropout Test Statistics

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)})&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))^{2}]\\
&=Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2};\end{split}

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)})&=Var[(Y-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}-(Y-f_{0}(X))^{2}]\\
&=Var[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{4}]+4\sigma^{2}[Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}]\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt-[Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}]^{2}.\end{split}

Expansion of
4^{th}
moment:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{4}]&=\mathbb{E}\{[f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j})]^{4}\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})))^{4}]+6Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt+(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{4}.\end{split}

Thus,

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)})&=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0,j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j})))^{4}]-Var^{2}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))\\
&+4(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}(Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\sigma^{2})\\
&=Var(\hat{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{2}_{0,j}(X_{j}))+4(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}(Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\sigma^{2})\end{split}

where
\hat{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}):=f_{j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}(f_{j}(X_{j}))
.

A.3
Single Index Model

In this section, we will compute all the expressions of test statistics in single index model which is a semi-parametric extension of linear model. Recall the single index model
Y=\eta(X^{T}\beta)+\epsilon
where
\beta
is unknown parameter vector,
\eta
is an unknown link function, and
\epsilon
are i.i.d errors with zero mean and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
\epsilon\perp X
.

Suppose under the conditions in Theorem
5
it is sufficient to approximate
\eta
via linear Taylor series expansion and the remainder term
\Delta
involving higher order terms in the expansion is negligible. That is to say that
\Delta,\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})=o(1)
, and we will prove it later in Appendix
B.3
. Thus, the test statistics derivation procedure is similar to linear model section.
Specifically, let’s recall the Taylor expansion of
\eta(X^{T}\beta)
around
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})
, and notation
\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))
.

\begin{split}f_{0}(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=\eta(X^{T}\beta)&=\eta(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))+\eta^{\prime}\cdot(X^{T}\beta-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))+\Delta\\
&=\eta(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))+\eta^{\prime}\cdot\beta_{j}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))+\Delta\end{split}

where
\Delta=\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}\eta^{(s)}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))(X^{T}\beta-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))^{s}
.

A.3.1
GCM Test Statistics

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)})&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(Y-\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j}))]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(\eta(X^{T}\beta)+\epsilon-\eta(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})))-\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))(\eta^{\prime}\beta_{j}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))+\Delta-\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})]\\
&=\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime}+\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))o(1)]\\
&=\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime}+o(1);\end{split}

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)})&=\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{{(gcm)}^{2}})-(\mathbb{E}(\psi_{0,j}^{(gcm)}))^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}(\eta(X^{T}\beta)+\epsilon-\eta(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})-\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j}]))^{2}]-(\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime}+o(1))^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}(\eta^{\prime}\beta_{j}\tilde{X}_{j}+\epsilon+o(1))^{2}]-\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1)\\
&=\eta^{\prime 2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{4})+[\sigma^{2}+o(1)]\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+2o(1)\eta^{\prime}\beta_{j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{3})-\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1)\\
&=\eta^{\prime 2}\beta_{j}^{2}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+o(1).\end{split}

A.3.2
LOCO Test Statistics

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)}]&=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}(Y|X)-\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j}))^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(\eta^{\prime}\beta_{j}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))+\Delta-\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j}))^{2}]\\
&=\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1);\end{split}

\begin{split}Var[\psi_{0,j}^{(loco)}]&=Var[(\mathbb{E}(Y|X)-\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j}))^{2}+2\epsilon(\mathbb{E}(Y|X)-\mathbb{E}(Y|X_{-j})))]\\
&=\beta_{j}^{4}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{4})\eta^{\prime 4}+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}-\beta_{j}^{4}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 4}+o(1)\\
&=\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 4}+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1).\\
\end{split}

A.3.3
Dropout Test Statistics

Under the single index model, let’s make estimation of
f_{0}(X^{(j)})
also via first order Taylor series expansion around
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})
:

f_{0}(X^{(j)})=\eta(\beta_{j}\mu_{j}+X_{-j}^{T}\beta_{-j})=\eta(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))+\eta^{\prime}\cdot\beta_{j}(\mu_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))+\Delta,

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)}]=\mathbb{E}[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}]=\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\mathbb{E}((X_{j}-\mu_{j})^{2})+o(1)=\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})+o(1);

\begin{split}Var(\psi_{0,j}^{(dr)})&=Var[(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))^{2}+2\epsilon(f_{0}(X)-f_{0}(X^{(j)}))]\\
&=\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\big\{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mu_{j})^{4}]+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})-\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})\big\}+o(1)\\
&=\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\hat{X_{j}}^{2})\eta^{\prime 4}+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1).\end{split}

B.1
Proof of Theorem

Since the estimators of GCM and LOCO have different means: one is the product of residuals between models, and the other is the difference of MSE between models. Thus, we use the so-called
co-efficient of variation

\frac{\sigma}{\mu}
to compare the asymptotic relative efficiency.

\begin{split}e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}&=\bigg(\frac{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}})/\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(gcm)}_{0,j}})/\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}\bigg)^{2}\\
&=\bigg(\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j})}{\beta_{j}sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}\bigg)^{2}\\
&=\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}\big\{\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))^{4}]+4\sigma^{2}Var(X_{j}|X_{-j})-\beta_{j}^{2}Var^{2}(X_{j}|X_{-j})\big\}}{\beta_{j}^{2}\{\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j})]^{4}+\sigma^{2}Var(X_{j}|X_{-j})-\beta_{j}^{2}Var^{2}(X_{j}|X_{-j})\}}\\
&=\frac{\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X_{j}}^{2})+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}{\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X_{j}}^{2})+\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}>1.\\
\end{split}

B.2
Proof of Theorem
4

\begin{split}e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}&=\bigg(\frac{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}})/\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(gcm)}_{0,j}})/\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}\bigg)^{2}\\

&=\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{4})+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})-\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})}{\big\{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}]+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})-\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X_{j}}\tilde{f}_{0,j})\big\}\frac{1}{\rho_{\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{f}_{0,j}}^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X_{j}}^{2})}}\\
&=\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{4})\rho_{\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{f}_{0,j}}^{2}+4\sigma^{2}\rho_{\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{f}_{0,j}}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})-\frac{\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}\tilde{f}_{0,j})}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})}{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}]\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})-\frac{\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{j}\tilde{f}_{0,j})}{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}\mathbb{E}^{2}(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2})}>1\text{, if assumption (B) holds.}\\
\end{split}

B.3
Proof of Theorem
5

In this section, we first prove that the remainder term
\Delta
in Taylor series expansion is negligible, so it is sufficient to apply linear approximation. Then we prove that the GCM estimator is asymptotically more efficient than LOCO.

By assumption (A1) that
\eta^{(s)}\leq O(a^{-X^{T}\beta})
,
a>1
for all
s\geq 2
, assumption (A2) that
X
has sub-Gaussian distribution with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
, and assumption (A3) which makes
\beta_{j}
to be finite, we can bound the higher order derivatives and polynomial terms in
\Delta
. For any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\begin{split}\Delta&=\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}g^{(s)}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))(X^{T}\beta-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))^{s}\\
&\leq\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{O(a^{-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})})\beta_{j}}{s!}(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))^{s}\\
&\leq\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{j}}{C^{\prime}a^{\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})}s!}\sigma^{s}\left(\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right)^{s}\text{, for some constant $C^{\prime}$}\\
&\leq\frac{\beta_{j}}{C^{\prime}a^{\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})}}e^{\sigma\sqrt{2\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}}\\
&=o(1)\end{split}

The last inequality holds due to the exponential series that for any
s\geq 2,\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{t^{s}}{s!}=e^{t}-1-t\leq e^{t}
. Finally, the remainder term vanishes if there exists a constant
C
, such that
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})\geq C\sqrt{\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}
, then
\Delta\leq\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
. Besides, we also have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})&=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}g^{(s)}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))(X^{T}\beta-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))^{s}|X_{-j}]\\
&\leq\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{O(a^{-\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})})\beta_{j}}{s!}\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}(X_{j}|X_{-j}))^{s}|X_{-j}]\\
&\leq\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{j}}{C^{\prime}a^{\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})}s!}(\sigma^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}s\Gamma(s/2)\text{, for some constant $C^{\prime}$}\\
&\leq\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{j}}{C^{\prime}a^{\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})}s!}\sigma^{s}s(s/2)^{s/2}\\
&\leq\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{j}}{C^{\prime}a^{\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})}s!}\sigma^{s}K^{s}\text{, for some constant $K\geq 3$}\\
&\leq\frac{\beta_{j}}{C^{\prime}a^{\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})}}e^{\sigma\sqrt{K}}\\
&=o(1)\end{split}

Similarly,
\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})
is negligible if there exists a constant
C
, such that
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})\geq C\sqrt{K}
, then
\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})<\epsilon
. Thus, as stated in assumption (A4), if there exists a constant C, such that
\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j})\geq C\max\{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta}},\sqrt{K}\}
, where the upper bound
K\geq 3
, then
\Delta,\mathbb{E}(\Delta|X_{-j})=o(1)
.
Next, with the estimator expressions derived in Appendix
A.3
, the same relative efficiency comparison technique, and monotonicity of
\eta
in assumption (A1), we have that

\begin{split}e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}&=\bigg(\frac{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}})/\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)})}{sd({\hat{\psi}^{(gcm)}_{0,j}})/\mathbb{E}(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}\bigg)^{2}\\
&=\bigg(\frac{sd(\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j})}{\beta_{j}\eta^{\prime}sd(\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)})}\bigg)^{2}\text{ , recall notation $\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}(X^{T}\beta|X_{-j}))$}\\
&=\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\big\{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\big\}+o(1)}{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\}+o(1)}\\
&=\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\big\{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\big\}}{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}\{\beta_{j}^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\}}\bigg(1+o(1)\bigg)\\
\end{split}

Thus, if assumption (A1)-(A4) hold, then for any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0,\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
e_{\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(gcm)},\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j}}>1-\epsilon^{\prime}.

Proof of Theorem

Under the neural network assumptions (C1) - (C3), the asymptotic variance of the Lazy-VI estimator
\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(lazy)}
is the same
as that of LOCO estimator
\hat{\psi}_{0,j}^{(loco)}
, which is
\frac{1}{n}Var(\epsilon^{2}-\epsilon^{2}_{-j})

Next, let compare the asymptotic variance relationship of LOCO and dropout estimators. Under assumptions in Theorem
3
, for
j\in[p]

\frac{Var(\hat{\psi}^{(dr)}_{0,j})}{Var(\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j})}=\frac{\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\hat{X_{j}}^{2})+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})}{\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X_{j}}^{2})+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})}\geq 1.

The inequality holds since the unconditional variance of the dropout estimator is greater than or equal to the conditional variance of the LOCO estimator. Under assumptions in Theorem

\frac{Var(\hat{\psi}^{(dr)}_{0,j})}{Var(\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j})}=\frac{Var(\hat{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{2}_{0,j}(X_{j}))+4(\mathbb{E}(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))-f_{0,j}(\mu_{j}))^{2}(Var(f_{0,j}(X_{j}))+\sigma^{2})}{Var(\tilde{f}_{0,j}^{2}(X_{j}))+4\sigma^{2}Var(\tilde{f}_{0,j}(X_{j}))}\geq 1.

The inequality holds due to the extra non-negative term and the replacement of the unconditional moments for the variance of dropout estimator.

Finally, under assumptions in Theorem

B.3

\begin{split}\frac{Var(\hat{\psi}^{(dr)}_{0,j})}{Var(\hat{\psi}^{(loco)}_{0,j})}&=\frac{\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\hat{X_{j}}^{2})\eta^{\prime 4}+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1)}{\beta_{j}^{4}Var(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 4}+4\sigma^{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{j}^{2})\eta^{\prime 2}+o(1)}\\

If assumption (A1)-(A4) in Theorem
