The Effective Dose of Remifentanil Combined with Remimazolam in Inhibiting Tracheal Intubation Responses in Elderly Patients: An Up–Down Dose-Finding Study
Authors: Yue Ma, Jianting Huang, Haipeng Zhou, Jinfeng Zhou, Qiang Zheng, Zhipeng Xu, Wenxi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHemodynamic instability during tracheal intubation in elderly patients poses significant perioperative risks. This study aimed to determine the effective doses (ED50/ED95) of remifentanil combined with remimazolam to suppress intubation-induced stress responses while minimizing hypotension in patients aged ≥60.Patients and MethodsIn this prospective, double-blind trial, 29 elderly patients received remimazolam (0.3 mg/kg) followed by rocuronium and remifentanil (initial dose: 1 µg/kg, adjusted via Dixon’s up-down method). Hemodynamic parameters (blood pressure, heart rate, systemic vascular resistance, cardiac output) and bispectral index (BIS) were recorded. Probit regression analyzed dose–response relationships. A positive hemodynamic response was defined as an increase in systolic blood pressure or heart rate exceeding 20% of the baseline value within 5 minutes after endotracheal intubation. Dixon’s up-and-down method and probit regression analysis to determine the ED50 and ED95 of remifentanil combined with remimazolam to suppress intubation-induced stress responses in elderly patients.ResultsThe ED50 and ED95 of remifentanil were 0.85 µg/kg (95% CI: 0.80–0.90) and 0.97 µg/kg (95% CI: 0.91–1.21), respectively. Positive responders showed elevated blood pressure at 30s–1 min post-intubation (P<0.05), while cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance remained comparable between groups. Hypotension incidence was low (14.3–20%), with transient BIS elevations (60–70) in 51.7% of patients but no intraoperative awareness.ConclusionIn this exploratory dose-finding study, the estimated ED50 and ED95 of remifentanil for suppressing intubation-related hemodynamic responses during remimazolam-based induction were 0.85 and 0.97 μg/kg, respectively.

Introduction

Hemodynamic management during general anesthesia induction presents critical challenges in geriatric anesthesia practice. Global aging trends have increased surgical interventions in elderly patients, currently accounting for approximately 37% of procedures.1 Age-related physiological decline in cardiovascular reserve, autonomic regulation, and pharmacokinetic profiles enhances susceptibility to anesthetic-induced circulatory depression.2 Severe hypotension following general anesthesia induction may precipitate organ hypoperfusion and ischemic complications. This hemodynamic compromise consequently elevates risks of postoperative morbidity, including myocardial injury, ischemic stroke, and acute kidney injury. Furthermore, such hypotensive episodes have been associated with elevated 1-year mortality rates.3 Tracheal intubation triggers sympathetic activation through adrenal medullary stimulation and renin-angiotensin system engagement, markedly elevating catecholamine levels,4 which may precipitate tachycardia, hypertension, arrhythmias, and increased cardiocerebrovascular risks. Consequently, balancing effective attenuation of intubation-induced stress responses with prevention of post-induction hypotension has emerged as a critical challenge in optimizing geriatric anesthesia protocols.

Remifentanil, an ultra-short-acting μ-opioid receptor agonist, offers rapid onset and brief duration ideal for titratable nociceptive blockade.5,6 The recently introduced remimazolam, a benzodiazepine derivative, demonstrates rapid onset/offset kinetics with organ-independent metabolism and minimal accumulation, showing lower hypotensive incidence than propofol during induction.7,8 Their pharmacodynamic synergy enables a “fast-in/fast-out” anesthetic strategy that potentially suppresses intubation stress while minimizing post-induction hypotension through rapid anesthetic depth modulation. However, the effective dose of remifentanil for suppressing tracheal intubation responses during a fixed-dose remimazolam induction regimen (0.3 mg/kg) has not been well established in elderly patients. Because previous dose-finding studies have focused on propofol-based or etomidate-based combinations, leaving the optimal remifentanil dose for remimazolam-based induction undefined. This dose-finding study aims to determine the optimal remifentanil dosage for suppressing intubation stress responses during remimazolam-coadministered anesthesia induction in patients >60 years, guiding safe and effective dosing while minimizing the risk of dose-dependent adverse effects such as hypotension and bradycardia.

Materials and Methods

The Medical Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Qingdao) approved this prospective, double-blind, dose-finding clinical trial (approval number: KYLL-2024066) which was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2500095646). The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients. This clinical trial was conducted between January 2025 and April 2025.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Elderly patients scheduled for elective surgery were enrolled if they accorded with the inclusion criteria: (1) ≥60 years old; (2) body mass index (BMI) 18.5–30 kg/m2; (3) American Society Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA) I–III; (4) patients who intend to undergo single-lumen endotracheal tube surgery under general anesthesia. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with a potentially difficult airway; (2) baseline systolic blood pressure (SBP) >160 mmHg; (3) allergic to the trial medication; (4) using opioid analgesics for chronic pain; (5) baseline heart rate (HR) <55 beats per minute(bpm); (6) severe obstructive or restrictive pulmonary disease or ischemic heart disease; (7) psychiatric disorders; (8) severe liver or kidney disorders.

Study Protocol

The patient’s preoperative fasting protocol strictly complied with ASA guidelines. No premedication was administered to avoid pharmacological interactions. Intravenous catheterization was performed in the pre-anesthesia area with infusion of lactated Ringer’s solution at 4–6 mL/kg. After the patient arrived in the operating room, standard monitoring was implemented, including electrocardiogram (ECG), invasive arterial blood pressure (IBP) monitoring, LiDCO hemodynamic analyzer (Gloryeway Medical Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), and bispectral index (BIS). Following 3 minutes of preoxygenation with 100% oxygen via a tight-fitting facemask, remimazolam (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Jiangsu, China) was administered intravenously at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg over 30seconds. Sedation success was defined as achieving an Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) score of 1 or BIS ≤60. If sedation criteria were not met within 1 minute after initial remimazolam administration, a supplemental bolus of 0.05 mg/kg remimazolam was administered intravenously. Upon successful sedation, rocuronium 0.9 mg/kg was administered as a rapid intravenous bolus. For the first patient, remifentanil (Humanwell Healthcare (Group) Co., Ltd,Hubei,China) 1 μg/kg was infused intravenously within 60 seconds, followed by tracheal intubation performed by an experienced anesthesiologist using video laryngoscopy 60 seconds later. Following successful endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation was initiated with a fresh gas flow rate of 2 L/min and an oxygen concentration (FiO2) of 60%, combined with inhaled sevoflurane at 1.0 vol%. The end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) pressure was maintained within the target range of 30–40 mmHg. At 5 minutes post-intubation, anesthetic agents were titrated according to BIS monitoring and hemodynamic parameters.

The Hemodynamic parameters, BIS and SPO2, were recorded at the following time points: before the administration of remimazolam (T0, T0 was defined as baseline), before the insertion of the video laryngoscope (T1), the moment of glottis visualization (T2), T3, T4, T5, T6 were defined as 30s, 1 min, 2 min, and 5 min after intubation, respectively.

The modified Dixon’s up-and-down method was used to calculate the dose of remifentanil.9,10 Dixon’s up-and-down methodology requires that the starting dose be the dose expected to result in a positive response, which is defined as an increase in the maximum SBP or HR within 5 minutes after endotracheal intubation exceeding 20% of the baseline value. The initial dose of remifentanil was set at 1 μg/kg. If the cardiovascular response during endotracheal intubation was positive, the dose of remifentanil in the next patient would increase by 0.1μg/kg. Conversely, if the intubation response was negative, the dose of remifentanil would decrease by 0.1μg/kg. Patients with a positive hemodynamic response were defined as the positive group, and those without as the negative group. If a patient was excluded prior to study intervention, this patient did not receive any study drug and was therefore not included in the sequential allocation or the final analysis. The subsequent patient received the same remifentanil dose as would have been assigned to the excluded patient, thereby maintaining the integrity of the up-and-down sequence. All completed the study protocol and had complete hemodynamic recordings for all time points.

During the study, the following interventions were implemented based on hemodynamic parameters:

If MAP <65 mmHg and HR >45 bpm persisted for >1 minute, administer phenylephrine 40 μg IV bolus. If MAP <65 mmHg and HR <45 bpm persisted for >1 minute, administer ephedrine 6 mg IV bolus. If MAP ≥65 mmHg but HR <45 bpm persisted for >1 minute, administer atropine 0.5 mg IV bolus. If SBP >180 mmHg persisted for >1 minute, administer urapidil 12.5 mg IV bolus. If HR >110 bpm persisted for >1 minute, administer esmolol 10 mg IV bolus. If vasoactive agents were required prior to tracheal intubation, the patient would be withdrawn from the study, and the subsequent case would repeat the same remifentanil dose trial.

This was a prospective, partially blinded dose-finding trial. The patient and the anesthesiologist performing tracheal intubation and recording hemodynamic data were blinded to the remifentanil dose. An independent anesthesiologist who was not involved in intubation or outcome assessment prepared the study drug (remifentanil diluted to a fixed volume of 10 mL) and adjusted the dose for the next patient based on the previous patient’s response. This unblinded anesthesiologist had no contact with the patient or the intubating anesthesiologist. Therefore, the study maintained blinding of the patient and the outcome assessor, while the dose adjustment was performed by a separate unblinded investigator.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was to determine the ED50 and ED95 of remifentanil in anesthesia induction to inhibit endotracheal intubation reaction. The secondary outcomes included MAP, DBP, HR, SVR, SV, CO and BIS changes during tracheal intubation and adverse reactions, such as bradycardia (HR < 45 bpm), hypotension (MAP<65mmHg or baseline MAP reduction >30%) and the amount of vasoactive drugs during the period from the initiation of anesthesia induction to 5 minutes after tracheal intubation. Intraoperative awareness was assessed by BIS and a structured postoperative interview conducted in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) once the patient reached a modified Aldrete score ≥9. The interview followed a standardized questionnaire (modified Brice protocol), asking patients whether they recalled any events during surgery, heard voices, felt pain, or had dreams.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size of 30 participants was selected in accordance with methodological guidelines indicating that cohorts of 20–40 participants enable robust approximation of the ED50 when employing Dixon’s sequential up-and-down method.9 It is a well-established, efficient sequential design that rapidly converges to the ED50 with a relatively small sample size. Probit regression analysis of the accumulated response data across all patients additionally permits reliable calculation of the ED95. This sample size is sufficient to converge on a stable ED50 estimate. Enrollment continued until a sufficient number of cross-over points were obtained for a stable estimate, with a pre-defined minimum target of 7.

SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA of America) was used for data analysis. A probit analysis was used to calculate the EC50 and ED95. To assess goodness-of-fit, Pearson’s chi-square test was used (P value>0.05 indicates good fit). The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables. If the test indicates a normal distribution, data are described using mean ± standard deviation and analyzed with the independent samples t-test. Variables with non-normal distribution were presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and employed the Mann–Whitney U-test. Comparisons of categorical data utilize either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A P value<0.05 (two-tailed) was defined as statistically significant.

Discussion

This pharmacodynamic study quantifies the remimazolam-remifentanil synergism required for hemodynamic stabilization during endotracheal intubation in geriatric patients. Probit regression analysis determined that coadministration with remimazolam (0.3 mg/kg) achieves effective hemodynamic control at remifentanil doses of 0.85 μg/kg (ED50, 95% CI 0.80–0.90) and 0.97 μg/kg (ED95, 95% CI 0.91–1.21).

Tracheal intubation induces hemodynamic instability characterized by tachycardia, hypertension, and arrhythmias.11 While high-dose induction agents effectively suppress intubation stress responses, they increase post-induction hypotension risks. Remimazolam and remifentanil, both rapid-onset/short-acting agents, are pharmacologically positioned to mitigate intubation responses through synergistic action while minimizing hypotension via rapid anesthetic depth reduction. Remifentanil, a selective μ-opioid receptor agonist, demonstrates optimal pharmacokinetics for geriatric anesthesia with rapid onset (effect-site peak: 1.22–2.26 min) and short duration, facilitating precise induction-maintenance control.12 Existing evidence suggests remifentanil combinations (with propofol or etomidate) may optimize intubation conditions. Arnaud et al demonstrated in elderly rapid sequence induction (0.3 mg/kg etomidate with succinylcholine) that 0.5–1 μg/kg remifentanil boluses within 15s produced differential hemodynamic effects: the low-dose group showed minimal deviation (MAP: +6 mmHg, HR: +4 bpm from baseline), whereas high-dose administration caused paradoxical reductions.13 Park et al observed similar efficacy with thiopental combinations, though 12.5% of patients developed hypotension.14 Habib et al established that 0.5 μg/kg remifentanil bolus followed by 0.1 μg·kg−1·min−1 infusion effectively attenuated pressor responses to laryngoscopy and intubation in elderly patients.11 Intravenous bolus administration of remifentanil may lead to dose-dependent or rate-dependent respiratory depression and skeletal muscle rigidity, consequently resulting in decreased oxygenation.15 Accordingly, drawing on evidence from prior studies, the initial remifentanil dose was set at 1 μg/kg. In our study, the rapid-onset neuromuscular blocking agent rocuronium was administered, and adequate preoxygenation was performed prior to anesthesia; consequently, no chest wall rigidity or hypoxemia was observed during the experimental procedures.

Remifentanil exhibits pharmacodynamic synergy with sedative adjuvants in cardiovascular depression.16 Exceeding the therapeutic window for intubation response suppression may provoke excessive hemodynamic compromise. This pharmacokinetic interaction necessitates rigorous dose-finding studies to establish optimal co-administration regimens with various anesthetic adjuvants. Remimazolam, a novel benzodiazepine derivative, demonstrates rapid onset, short duration, and predictable sedation with lower induction hypotension incidence compared to propofol.17 Propofol induces systemic hypotension by suppressing myocardial contractility and promoting peripheral vasodilation, mediated through its inhibition of excitatory glutamate release and potentiation of central GABA receptor activity. In contrast, remimazolam exhibits favorable pharmacokinetic properties, including high clearance, low volume of distribution, short elimination half-life, and rapid onset and offset of sedation, contributing to its superior controllability. Hemodynamically, remimazolam demonstrates minimal impact, with no clinically significant effects on cardiac repolarization.18 Therefore, remimazolam is widely used in elderly patients.18 To our knowledge, no previous investigations have quantified the effective remifentanil dosage required for intubation response suppression during remimazolam coadministration. Our study design incorporated precise pharmacodynamic alignment,19,20 scheduling intubation at the simultaneous peak effect of both agents. A previous study reported that the ED95 of remimazolam for suppressing tracheal intubation responses in elderly patients was 0.297 mg/kg.21 The calculated ED50 and ED95 for remifentanil in suppressing hemodynamic responses during remimazolam (0.3 mg/kg) coadministration were 0.85 μg/kg and 0.97 μg/kg, respectively.

Invasive arterial monitoring eliminated non-invasive measurement latency, while LIDCO hemodynamic profiling ensured comprehensive cardiovascular assessment. Peak hemodynamic responses (BP/HR) occurred 30–60s post-intubation, consistent with established temporal patterns.5,22 Both responder and non-responder cohorts exhibited comparable BP/HR declines 2–5 minutes post-intubation (p>0.05). At 2-minute post-intubation, hypotension incidence was 14.3% (2/14) in responders vs 20% (3/15) in non-responders, with 4 cases requiring phenylephrine 40μg boluses. Our regimen’s hypotension incidence likely reflects temporal matching between remifentanil’s peak effect and intubation stimulus, coupled with its rapid offset preventing prolonged anesthetic depth - a limitation observed with longer-acting opioids like sufentanil.17

Although statistically significant differences in HR and SBP were observed at T3/T4, the lack of significant differences thereafter suggests that hemodynamic changes 2–5 minutes after intubation may stabilize to a comparable baseline, irrespective of whether the remifentanil dose effectively suppressed the immediate intubation response. Moreover, this underscores the clinical feasibility of using the minimum effective dose to attenuate the stress response without incurring additional hypotensive risks. Nevertheless, the potential divergence in long-term hemodynamic trends (eg, 10–15 minutes post-induction) between remifentanil and long-acting opioids warrants further investigation to elucidate their sustained cardiovascular implications.

Our hemodynamic analysis revealed an intriguing dissociation: despite significantly higher BP in responders versus non-responders at T3 (intubation + 1 minute), we observed no significant intergroup differences in SV, SVR, or CO (p >0.05). This hemodynamic paradox likely reflects BP’s composite nature as a product of SVR and CO interactions.23 Two potential explanations emerge: First, population-level statistics may mask heterogeneous mechanisms - individual BP elevations could stem predominantly from SVR increases in some subjects versus CO changes in others. Second, as a pulse contour analysis device, LiDCO calculates SVR and CO through proprietary algorithms (PulseCO™) based on arterial waveform characteristics. Validation studies demonstrate a mean percentage error of 26–30% compared to thermodilution techniques.24 Although no significant intergroup differences were observed in CO or SVR, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the known limitations of LiDCO. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance does not definitively exclude underlying hemodynamic differences, and further studies using more direct measurements are warranted to clarify the hemodynamic mechanisms underlying the intubation response.

BIS monitoring showed all 29 patients achieved values <60 post-induction. Notably, 15 patients (51.7%) exhibited transient BIS elevations (60–70) within 5 minutes post-intubation, though postoperative interviews revealed no explicit/implicit recall. This observation aligns with previous findings that remimazolam-based total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) maintains BIS 60–80 ranges while preserving adequate sedation, as evidenced by pupillometric parameters.25 Neurophysiological monitoring further confirms BIS 60–80 during remimazolam anesthesia correlates with absent implicit/explicit memory formation,26 suggesting traditional BIS thresholds may require pharmacological context-specific interpretation.

It has several limitations in the current study. First, the study did not investigate how varying remimazolam doses affect remifentanil’s ED50 for suppressing intubation responses in geriatric patients. A systematic investigation is warranted to establish the optimal dosing regimen of remifentanil in combination with varying doses of remimazolam. Second, the paucity of prior research on remimazolam-remifentanil combinations during induction necessitates that our findings be interpreted as exploratory. Third, although our inclusion criteria lacked explicit upper age restrictions, the maximum enrolled age of 78 years precludes direct extrapolation of findings to octogenarian populations. Therefore, dedicated pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies in patients aged over 80 years and frail elderly patients are critically needed. Fourth, while using heart rate (HR) and blood pressure (BP) as primary response indicators, the lack of significant cardiac output (CO) and systemic vascular resistance (SVR) differences between groups suggests multimodal monitoring better quantifies intubation stress. Fifth, while probit regression enabled calculation of the ED95, the Dixon up-and-down method is optimized for ED50 estimation. The ED95 estimate from a cohort of 29 patients may be less stable, and confirmation in larger, independent studies is warranted.

Conclusion

In this exploratory dose-finding study of older adult patients (60–80) receiving remimazolam 0.3 mg/kg for induction, the estimated ED50 and ED95 of remifentanil for suppressing intubation-related hemodynamic responses were 0.85 μg/kg and 0.97 μg/kg, respectively. Further studies with larger samples and comparator groups are needed before this regimen can be recommended as an optimized strategy for geriatric anesthesia. Notably, transient BIS elevations (60–70) were observed in 51.7% of patients within 5 minutes after intubation, but no patient reported intraoperative awareness, suggesting that BIS thresholds may require context-specific interpretation during remimazolam-based anesthesia.
