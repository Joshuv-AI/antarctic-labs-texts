Experimental–AI approach (hybrid) for evaluating the strength characteristics of eco-efficient concrete incorporating coal bottom ash
Authors: C Arvind Kumar, Kunamineni Vijay, V Shiva Chandra, Prathik Kulkarni, K Saibaba, Panga Narasimha Reddy, Bodevenkata Kavyatheja, A Uday Kumar, Abdirashid Omar Alaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThermal power stations generate large amount of coal bottom ash (CBA), which is mostly disposed of in landfills, creating environmental hazards. At the same time, the continual use of natural aggregate in concrete is depleting resources. Present study aims to address both these issues by utilizing coal bottom ash as partial substitute to river sand and quarry sand at values between 0% and 100%. Experimental results indicate that workability decreases with increasing CBA contain. The mixes made using river sand (CBA1) had their compressive and split tensile strengths close to 5% of those of the control mixes for higher curing ages. In contrast, the mixes made using quarry sand (CBA2) had a compressive strength that was 6% to 25% less than that of the control mixes at the 28-day curing period depending on the percentage of CBA used, whereas there was a drop to around ± 0% to 7% after 180 days of curing. Among different models developed, effective prediction approach within the considered experimental dataset Gradient Boosting outperformed the other models, showing the highest accuracy (R² = 0.99) and also with the least number of prediction errors. The outcome of present research suggests that CBA may serve as a durable material and that advanced machine learning techniques might accurately forecast its strength.Subject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

A by-product of coal combustion in thermal power plants named as Coal bottom ash (CBA), has gained increased interest as a viable substitute to natural fine aggregate in concrete due to environmental and resource conservation issues. On the other hand, it also produces fly ash that is already being used a lot in construction industry. Implementing CBA in concrete not only helps with waste management, but it also reduces the dependency natural river sand.

When comparing the particle properties, Coal bottom ash is different from natural sand because its particles are not uniform, as it has rougher surface with lower specific gravity, and has more water absorbing capacity. When it’s added in concrete, the properties of concrete both in fresh and hardened state changes. Many studies have consistently demonstrated that an increase in CBA content decreases workability due to increase internal friction and increased water demand1–3. Study by Andrade et al., found that the grading and surface properties of bottom ash particles have a large effect on how fresh concrete performs, such bleeding and segregation4.

The performance of concrete made with CBA is greatly affected by the fineness modulus and aggregate grading. Fine aggregates that are well-graded make the packing density superior and the void content lower. On the other hand, poorly or coarse graded aggregates make the concrete porosity higher and the strength lowers5,6. Findings in their research by Mangi1 demonstrated that particle size distribution significantly affects bonding between aggregate-cement paste and mechanical performance in concrete containing coal bottom ash. Andrade et al.4 observed similar results in their research, which show how important it is to use a combined aggregate grading when replacing coal bottom ash with natural sand.

Many researchers have examined the mechanical characteristics of concrete containing coal bottom ash. Partial replacement of fine aggregate with coal bottom ash at lower levels yields compressive strength similar to that of conventional concrete3,7. However, with increased quantity of CBA replacement, the early-age strength reduces because of weakened zones of interfacial transition and increased porosity. Study by Arun et al.8 also demonstrate that the primary cause of strength reduction at higher replacement levels is the insufficient bonding of the aggregate with the cement paste.

For coal bottom ash concrete, several studies have observed positive patterns in the long-term strength gain. Cheriaf et al.9 observed that coal bottom ash possesses pozzolanic properties, that facilitate the gradual strength at later ages. Different studies10 found that maintaining material hydration and refining its microstructure during longer curing periods significantly improves its strength. Other studies11 reported similar results, suggesting that long-term curing can partially minimize the early-age strength loss in bottom ash concrete.

In coal bottom ash concrete, the split tensile strength behaviour is often similar to that of compressive strength. Although the tensile strength of bottom ash has less strength initially, research indicates that due to mechanical interlocking and stress transfer the strength improves at later days12,13. Bottom ash particles’ rough surface roughness promotes aggregate-paste bonding.

The durability-related features of coal bottom ash concrete have also been widely researched. Several studies have found that the porosity structure of bottom ash particles causes higher water absorption and sorptivity at early ages14. However, long-term curing has been demonstrated to increase pore refinement and resilience to harsh conditions such chloride intrusion and carbonation15. Studies on internal curing effects have shown that coal bottom ash can improve hydration efficiency and prevent shrinkage-related cracking under certain conditions2,16.

Recent studies have also investigated the use of coal bottom ash in conjunction with various additional materials and other aggregates. According to a study15, fine aggregate blends made from fly ash and bottom ash can increase sustainability while keeping adequate mechanical performance. Thomas in his research17 concluded that the use of industrial by products such as coal bottom ash helps to make green concrete with a lower environmental effect.

Parallel to actual research, machine learning (ML) approaches are increasingly being used to forecast the mechanical characteristics of concrete with extra supplementary materials18–20.

Yeh21 showed that artificial neural networks can accurately predict the concrete compressive strength by identifying nonlinear correlations between mix variables and strength development. Chou and Pham22 observed that ensemble learning models surpass the traditional regression techniques in predicting the accuracy. Recent research has confirmed the effectiveness of machine learning models in predicting the workability and compressive strength of concrete containing different industrial wastes23–25. However, recent research has proven that more advanced ensemble and hybrid artificial intelligence models can accurately model the non-linear interactions underlying the performance of sustainable concrete materials26. Hybrid optimization-assisted learning frameworks and ensemble machine learning models have been observed to enhance prediction performance as opposed to the use of traditional regression modelling, especially where concrete systems have to be predicted based on several interacting parameters like curing time, mix, and material properties27,28. These findings further support the applicability of ML techniques for prediction of strength properties in CBA-based concrete.

Based on the literature findings and preliminary experimental observations, various parameters such as CBA substitution rate, curing time, and fine aggregate type significantly affect the strength gain behaviour of CBA concrete. Since these parameters exhibit complex and non-linear relationships with strength of concrete, traditional methods can be less effective in predicting the strength behaviour. In the current research, machine learning models have been used for accurate predictions of compressive and split tensile strength of CBA concrete.

The existing research from literature review demonstrates that coal bottom ash is a feasible and sustainable alternative for natural fine aggregate in concrete. Although higher replacement levels may initially make the concrete mix less workable and weaker in strength, appropriate mix design, proper aggregate grading, and sufficient curing can improve the long-term mechanical properties and durability of the concrete. However, extensive studies that examine several relevant factors at the same time are limited, highlighting the need for more systematic research.

The properties of coal bottom ash depend greatly on the type of coal, the method of combustion, and the methods of disposing of the CBA. therefore, it is not possible to generalize the behavior of CBA as a replacement for natural fine aggregates. Present study concentrates on assessing the viability of locally sourced CBA as an eco-friendly alternative to fine aggregates in concrete production to mitigate associated environmental problems. Unlike other studies, this study involves the comparative analysis of the performance of CBA in concrete produced from river sand and manufactured sand (quarry sand) across a broad spectrum of 0–100% replacements. In addition, machine learning models have been created for predicting the compressive and split tensile strengths of CBA-based concrete from locally sourced materials based on the CBA percentage, curing age, and concrete mix type.

Experimental details

Materials

The coal bottom ash (CBA) used in this experiment was obtained from Nalgonda, Telangana (India). This CBA was received from Disposed to Ash Pond and covers 400 acres of property. The collected CBA was filtered to remove larger particles before being run through a 4.75 mm sieve to produce fine aggregate, which was then utilized in the production of concrete. The SEM images of CBA used in present study is shown in Fig. 1. Natural river sand and quarry sand utilized in this study were acquired from two distinct places: one from the Godavari river near Karimnagar, Telangana (India), and the other from local quarry sand with fineness modulus of 2.34 and 2.98, respectively. The particle size distribution of CBA, river sand, and quarry dust is depicted in Fig. 2. The course aggregate utilized in this study was sourced from a nearby quarry, with a maximum size of 20 mm, a specific gravity of 2.61, water absorption of 0.35%, and a fineness modulus of 6.32.Ordinary Portland cement of grade 43 was utilized, which has a specific gravity of 3.15. Master GleniumSky 8866 (supplied by BASF) was mixed with 2% weight of cement to make self-compacting concrete.

Results and discussion

Experimental results

Fresh properties

The results of the fresh test, as given in Table 4; Fig. 3, reveal that the flow ability of both concretes reduces as the amount of coal bottom ash used increases. This is because the bottom ash particles are porous and so absorb more water. SEM pictures also show that the CBA particles are rough and angular, causing them to absorb more water and leave less free water for concrete flow. Interestingly, CBA2 with quarry sand as fine aggregate exhibited less flow than CBA1 with river sand as fine aggregate. This is because river sand contains spherical particles, which improves flow qualities as compared to quarry sand. The control mix A0 met the SCC qualities as per EFNARC for both grades, with a flow of 718 mm for CBA1 and 690 mm for CBA2. However, when the CBA dose increased, the flow decreased from 690 mm to 522 mm for A20 and A100 in CBA1 and from 664 mm to 485 mm for A20 and A100 in CBA2.The time required to reach 500 mm dia flow in a flow test likewise increases, lowering flow property of concrete as seen in Table 4; Fig. 3. A similar pattern of decreased workability was seen for the V funnel test as well.

Fresh properties of CBA mixes: (a) Slump flow and (b) V-funnel time of CBA1 and CBA2 mixes.

According to EFNARC standards, the flow diameter should be between 650 and 800 mm, and the time for a 50 cm dia flow should be between 2 and 5 s. Similarly, concrete should take 6–12 s to empty the V funnel.

According to EFNARC guidelines, slump flow values ranging between 550 and 650 mm are classified as SF1, 660–750 mm as SF2, and 760–850 mm as SF3. Based on the obtained results, the SF2 class was mostly met by the control mixes and lower CBA replacement levels (A0–A30), showing high flowability appropriate for typical SCC applications. Because CBA particles are porous and absorb more water, mixes with higher CBA concentrations (A40–A75) typically fell within the SF1 range. According to the V-funnel time classification, the majority of mixes met the VF1–VF2 criteria, however greater replacement levels resulted in longer flow times because of the mix’s increased internal friction and viscosity.

Compressive strength results

Figure 4 shows the compressive strength data for various samples after 28, 90, and 180 days of CBA replacement. The CBA1 mixes with river sand as fine aggregate produced similar outcomes to the control mix at all curing ages (28, 90, and 180 days) during the CBA replacement process. Figure 4a shows that, for CBA1, the 28-day strength declined from 3% to 18% as the CBA dose rose from 20% to 100% compared to the control mix. However, when the curing days progressed to 90 days, the same concrete showed an increase in compressive strength from around 0 to 10% compared to the control mix, with a maximum strength gain of 20–30% CBA. Similarly, after 180 days of curing, the strength improvement improved from 0 to 7%, with the largest benefit occurring at 30% CBA replacement.

(a) Compressive strength for CBA1 (b) Compressive strength for CBA2.

The improved strength observed around 20–30% CBA replacement in CBA1 mixes may be attributed to better particle packing and enhanced micro-filler effects produced by the finer grading of river sand combined with coal bottom ash particles. At moderate replacement levels, the porous CBA particles may also contribute to internal curing and continued hydration, which would enhance in improved bonding and microstructural densification. However, beyond this replacement level, excessive CBA content raise porosity and water demand, leading to reduction in strength.

On the other hand, CBA2, concrete using quarry sand as fine aggregate, exhibited a decrease in strength when the CBA dose climbed above 30%. Figure 4b shows that for 28-day compressive strength, the strength decreases gradually by about 6%, 8%, 10%, 15%, 18%, and 25% for CBA dosages of 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, respectively, compared to the control mix. However, the 90-day strength was not the same; it was shown that strength variance varied from a minor decrease at lower replacements to a 5–12% decline, with the greatest loss occurring at higher CBA levels. In the case of 180 days compressive strength, the strength loss was minimal and even enhanced when compared to early strengths such as 28 and 90 days. When compared to the control mix at all CBA replacement levels, the strength loss was only 0–7%.

Overall, the compressive strength findings demonstrate that CBA1 combines with river sand as fine aggregate, which is finer and more graded in nature, may support a larger amount/dosage of coal bottom ash without significantly reducing compressive strength. Whereas the concrete of CBA2 mixed with quarry sand as fine aggregate requires a longer curing time to attain equal mechanical performance.

Split tensile strength results

Figure 5 shows the split tensile strength findings of CBA1 and CBA2 mixtures at various CBA doses over 28, 90, and 120 days. For CBA1 concrete shown in Fig. 5a, for different mixes at 28 days strength, it was noticed that only 0–4% split tensile strength improved when dosage of CBA changed from 0 to 50%, and then the strength tends to decline from 4 to 10% at 75–100% CBA dosage when compared to the control mix. The 90-day strength appeared to rise by 5–10% for CBA doses of 20–50%, and to stay equivalent to the control mix for higher dosages. For a longer curing time of 180 days, the split tensile strength improved by around 4–9% for all CBA replacement levels, with the greatest improvement occurring at 20–40% CBA. The increased strength during a longer curing period is most likely owing to better interfacial bonding and on-going microstructural densification caused by delayed pozzolanic reactions in the concrete.

(a) Split Tensile strength for CBA1 (b) Split Tensile strength for CBA2.

Figure 5b shows that for CBA2 concrete using quarry sand as FA, the split tensile strength decreased at an early age, comparable to compressive strength. At 28 days, the split tensile strength fell by 5%, 7%, 9%, 11%, 15%, and 18% for the CBA replacement levels of 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, respectively, in comparison to the control mix. The 90-day strength was also found to be reduced, however there was considerable recovery ranging from 3 to 8% lower than control mixes for lower replacement levels to 5–10% for higher replacement levels of CBA. At 180 days, split tensile strength improved with a maximum variation of 5% relative to the control mix at all levels of CBA replacement.

Overall, in terms of split tensile strength, CBA1 with river sand as fine aggregate performed better across all levels of CBA replacement, but CBA2 required a longer curing period to achieve moderate or equivalent results to the control mix.

Relative compressive strength variation with CBA content

Figure 6a,b,c shows the variation in compressive strength with coal bottom ash concentration for CBA1 and CBA2 over 28, 90, and 180 days compared to the control mix. This graphic shows the relative sensitivity of replacing different types of fine aggregate systems with coal bottom ash (CBA)-based concrete. Figure 5a shows that CBA1 had no change in strength until 50% replacement, but CBA2 showed a dramatic decline in strength at lower replacement levels. This demonstrates that the better/finer grading of fine aggregate in CBA1 concrete has superior loss resistance at an early age, but the coarser grading of fine aggregate in CBA2 concrete renders the system more susceptible to CBA-induced porosity.

Relative compressive strength of CBA 1 and CBA2 fro (a) 28 days (b) 90 days and (c) 180 days.

At 90 days, the variation patterns altered considerably, as seen in Fig 6b. CBA1 mixes exhibited positive variation at all replacement levels, indicating a consistent response to delayed hydration and subsequent pozzolanic reactions. In contrast, whereas CBA2 mixes continued to exhibit negative variation, the extent of loss decreased dramatically when compared to 28 days, showing partial recovery due to continuous hydration and internal healing actions associated with porous CBA particles.

At 180 days, a reversal in relative performance was found, as illustrated in Fig. 6c. CBA1 mixtures showed very minor variations, indicating that strength development had mostly stabilized. In contrast, CBA2 mixes exhibited positive variation at modest replacement levels, indicating that the originally porous and loosely packed matrix benefited significantly from long-term pozzolanic activity and microstructural densification.

Overall, the variation analysis reveals that aggregate grading controls the age-dependent response of CBA concrete, with finer systems providing early-age stability and coarser systems exhibiting increased long-term sensitivity and recovery.

Machine learning-based prediction of strength properties

Machine learning (ML) models were developed to forecast compressive strength (CS) and split tensile strength (STS) for coal bottom ash based concrete with input variables of CBA replacement percentage, curing age, and mix type (CBA1/CBA2). For both compressive and split tensile strengths, the machine learning dataset used in the present study was developed from systematically controlled experimental results considering different CBA replacement levels, curing ages, and mix types. To ensure prediction reliability and reduce the possibility of over fitting, the developed models were compared using statistical indicators such as R², RMSE, MAE, parity plots, and residual analysis.

Three regression models were developed compared namely Linear Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), and Gradient Boosting (GB). For these models, Parity plots, feature importance and residual analysis were used for evaluating individual model performance.

The machine learning models developed in the present work were validated using experimentally obtained datasets under controlled laboratory conditions. Since the properties of coal bottom ash vary depending on combustion conditions and source characteristics, availability of comparable external datasets was limited. Therefore, model performance and generalization capability were assessed using statistical indicators, residual analysis and parity plots. Future research may further improve model robustness through validation using larger multi-source datasets.
