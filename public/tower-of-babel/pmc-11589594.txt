A game theoretic complex network model to estimate the epidemic threshold under individual vaccination behaviour and adaptive social connections
Authors: Viney Kumar, Chris T Bauch, Samit Bhattacharyya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In today’s interconnected world, the spread of information is closely linked to infectious disease dynamics. Public awareness plays a crucial role, as individual vaccination decisions significantly impact collective efforts to combat emerging health threats. This study explores disease transmission within a framework integrating social connections, information sharing, and individual vaccination decisions. We introduce a behaviour-prevalence model on an adaptive multiplex network, where the physical layer (Layer-II) captures disease transmission under vaccination. In contrast, the virtual layer (Layer-I) represents adaptive social contacts and the flow of information, shaping vaccination decisions within a socially influenced environment. We derive analytical expressions for the epidemic threshold using the microscopic Markov Chain Method (MMCM). Simulation results highlight that adaptive social contacts lead to a higher epidemic threshold than non-adaptive networks. Additionally, network characteristics, such as the power-law exponent in scale-free networks, significantly impact infection spread within populations. Our results reveal that changes in perceived infection risk and an individual’s sensitivity to non-vaccinated neighbour’s status strongly influence vaccine uptake across populations. These insights can guide public health officials in developing targeted vaccination programs that address the evolving dynamics of social connections, information dissemination, and vaccination choice in the digital era.

Introduction

In the recent digital era, social connections and community-level awareness play a major role in the success of any policy implementation, such as vaccinations or social distancing to control infectious diseases1. In voluntary vaccination programs, while access to vaccines and individual choices are essential for the effectiveness of the program2,3, the information and rumours over social platforms play crucial roles in shaping the dynamics of vaccine uptake4,5. In the face of an outbreak, each community member performs a cognitive evaluation, formulating a unique assessment of the likelihood of contracting the virus and the perceived effectiveness of vaccination depending on information obtained from various social media platforms6. Personal experiences, complex information distribution channels, and societal influences are just a few of the variables that impact this multifaceted decision-making framework7. For instance, a study in the U.S. revealed an approximately 30 % increase in the proportion of individuals willing to vaccinate as the perceived infection risk rises due to information from various social platforms8. On the other hand, social connections can also evolve rapidly, particularly during global or community-level emergencies, such as a disease outbreak like COVID-199,10. While similar levels of risk perception can strengthen virtual bonds among community members by fostering communal understanding and solidarity11, disproportionate risk perceptions can cause individuals to avoid existing connections and actively seek new ones12. Thus, the evolution of virtual connections is a common and natural phenomenon that facilitates the exchange of diverse perspectives and new information on vaccination. This, however, plays a significant role in influencing individual vaccination decisions13.

Several mathematical models have been explored to analyze how individual awareness of vaccines affects their actions during a disease outbreak14–16. d’Onofrio et al.14 established a general framework of behaviour-dependent linear and nonlinear forces of infection (FOI) that accounts for lags and effects due to epidemic memory. Sharmin et al.15 developed a SIR-type generic model that includes media literacy as a moderator to socially isolate sick and vulnerable populations during an outbreak. Buonomo et al.16 statistically examined how human behaviour, seasonality, and latency rate impact the containment and spread of infection. Several mathematical and computational models later focused on population heterogeneity as the key element17–20. Yin et al.18 developed a three-layer coupled network model to study the co-evolution of negative vaccine-related information, vaccination behaviour, and epidemic spread. Juan Wang et al.19 developed a two-layered network strategy for family networks that accounts for both conformity-motivated updates and myopic best response-motivated updates. Xuelong Li et al.21 created a two-layered multiplex network model that mimics human nature while accounting for the SIR epidemic process and vaccination decision-making. Additionally, a handful of mathematical models have been developed to study the impact of adaptive networks in a single-layered network22,23. Their work investigated epidemic dynamics on an adaptive network, where susceptibles could avoid contact with the infected by rewiring their network connections. Keith Paarporn et al.24 developed a framework incorporating game-environment feedback with logit learning, revealing a wide range of dynamics and a bifurcation point where stable limit cycles transition to a stable tragedy of the commons as rationality increases. Recently, Xiong et al.25 developed a UAU-SIS model to simulate the diffusion of awareness and epidemic spreading on temporal multiplex networks. Hota et al.26 developed a dynamic population game model that analyzes individual behaviour in response to an infectious disease, revealing insights into the trade-offs between vaccination, testing, and social activity and the effects of intervention strategies on infection prevalence and vaccination rates. Another unique game theory aspect has been observed by He et al.27, who developed a two-stage trading optimization strategy for shared energy storage in a peer-to-peer market. This strategy incorporates carbon trading mechanisms and a pricing model that balances buyer and seller interests while enhancing demand-side energy storage utilization.

However, the evolving nature of social connections realistically reflects the complexities in dynamics when individual behavioural interactions are taken into account. This perspective is also significant for public health, particularly in promoting disease control through voluntary immunization programs. To date, no studies in the literature have developed models describing such interactions on complex networks that incorporate human choice in vaccination alongside the simultaneous sharing of opinions within adaptive social connections. Furthermore, rarely any modelling studies have examined the emergence of multiple epidemic thresholds and how these are affected by varying vaccination risk perceptions across a community.

In this study, we have developed a framework integrating adaptive multiplex networks and game theory to tackle these research questions. Our adaptive game-theoretic behaviour-prevalence multiplex network model, which combines the intricacies of information dissemination, decision-making, and the dynamics of disease transmission across a diverse population, can shed light on designing intervention strategies or policy-making in controlling disease. We have employed the Microscopic Markov chain Method (MMCM)28 to compute the epidemic threshold by levels of perceived vaccine risk in infected states. We observed that the adaptive nature of social connections disproportionately impacts different epidemic thresholds with different risk perceptions. Our results have shown that the topology of networks, such as an increase in power-law exponents in scale-free networks, increases the cumulative infection. Our findings also show that the vaccine uptake increases with a high number of unvaccinated neighbours with high-risk perceptions, which increases the vaccine uptake in the community. So taken together, this research underscores the significance of the adaptive nature of network dynamics and decision-making processes concerning perceived vaccine and infection costs during a disease outbreak. These findings have potential insights for organizing vaccination campaigns in the community.

The article is framed as follows: Section “Model framework” describes the components of the model and their integration; Section “Results” elucidates the computation of the epidemic thresholds using MMCM, computational simulation algorithm, and numerical simulations of the epidemic thresholds, network metrics, and diverse epidemiological parameters. Finally, in Section “Discussion and conclusion”, we draw the conclusions and limitations.

However, in the case of multiple common categories, any one is chosen randomly.

Computation of epidemic threshold using the microscopic Markov Chain approach

We employed a microscopic Markov chain approach to derive the epidemic threshold in populations with varying risk perceptions. This approach, first introduced by Gómez et al.33, analyzes the spread of contact-based epidemics by considering how the number of contacts evolves over time. In our analysis, we constructed transition trees to illustrate the progression of changes in node status over time (Fig. 3).

Numerical simulations

We implement primarily a scale-free network in both layer-I and layer-II and use different power-law distribution parameters in the scale-free network, which determines the shape of the network’s degree distribution. We generate all of these networks using the igraph-python package34. Barabasi-Albert preferential attachment approach was implemented to generate scale-free networks35. These networks have the same number of nodes: 5000. While the physical network (layer II) has 9997 edges, the virtual network (layer I) has a comparatively higher number of edges: 149535. Mean value of the degree distribution of physical network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document} 4, whereas virtual network has larger mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document} 60. Degree distributions of all of these networks are shown in Figs. S14, S15 in the supplementary information (SI).

In our simulations, we randomly initialize the states of the nodes such that 15% are in the Low (L) state, 70% are in the Medium (M) state and 15% are in the High (H) state. This initialization mirrors observed patterns in public health studies, where most individuals exhibit moderate risk perception, with smaller proportions at either extreme. Each node’s perceived vaccine and infection risks are randomly assigned values between [0,1]32. Additionally, we randomly select 1% of the nodes to be initially infected while the remaining nodes are susceptible32.

We also compute the epidemic threshold (see Fig. 7) for a social network within a selected village in southern India36. A detailed dataset description is in the SI. The supplementary materials provide a visual representation of the degree distribution of the social network (S1, S2, S3), with a total of 1180 nodes, 4626 edges, and an average degree of approximately 8. We used a Monte Carlo approach to capture statistical properties robustly, running 25 simulations per data point for heat maps and 50 simulations per data point for 2D plots, averaging each to reduce random fluctuation effects. This analysis offers insights into epidemic dynamics within social networks, contrasting with scale-free networks to understand structure-specific transmission patterns. All simulations were executed in Python, and results were plotted using MATLAB.

Computational simulation algorithm

Here, we assume that a node in the physical network may be in one of four non-overlapping disease states: susceptible, infected, vaccinated, or recovered and that nodes in the virtual layer can have one of three mutually exclusive perception-states: L, M, or H. The simulation algorithm’s steps are as follows: Generate a multiplex network (with two layers) with 5000 nodes. Layer-I (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_1$$\end{document}) is the information network, and layer-II (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_2$$\end{document}) is the physical network.Initially, at t = 0, in the absence of an information network (layer I), we randomly have some initially infected nodes in the physical network (layer II). Effect of variable risk perceptions and adaptive social connections on epidemic threshold

We investigate how changes in perceived vaccine risk and the evolution of virtual connections influence people’s choices to get a vaccine. Adaptivity in social connections and vaccination risk updates impact model dynamics, particularly vaccine uptake (Fig. 4). A community’s incentive to vaccinate diminishes when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _j = 0$$\end{document}, as non-vaccinators receive no payoffs (Fig. 4a,b). But, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _j = 1$$\end{document}, the decision to vaccinate is mainly driven by the risk of infection. Owing to the high risk of infection, the adaptive nature of social connections does not impact the choice of social infections (Fig. S16). On the contrary, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _j$$\end{document} is defined by above in section 2, there is moderate vaccine uptake (Fig. 4c,d). However, the vaccine uptake is highest when social connections evolve. The risk of infection becomes variable, and in the presence of evolving social contacts, there is an initial increase in non-vaccinator clusters in the population, which, in turn, increases the risk perception of the infection and, hence, the vaccination coverage. This result underscores that public health authorities may conduct more vaccine campaigns for proper risk communication about vaccines and the risk of infection.

The epidemic threshold is crucial, revealing how quickly an outbreak spreads and establishes itself within a community’s population. We conducted an in-depth analytical computation of the epidemic threshold, considering key model parameters, including the number of physical connections, the individual perception of vaccination risk (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta$$\end{document}), and the transmission parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}). Figure 5 illustrates the qualitative changes in the epidemic thresholds under various values of these parameters. Several noteworthy dynamic changes were observed in this simulation.

Firstly, the epidemic threshold decreases as the average number of physical connections increases (Fig. 5a–c), although the rate of decrease varies depending on the perceived risk of vaccination. This variation is evident from equations (4) to (9), which demonstrate that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta ^i, ~~i=L, M, H$$\end{document} are functions not only of physical connections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{ij}$$\end{document} but also of the proportion of neighbours with low (L), medium (M), and high (H) perceived risks of vaccination within the community, which evolve over time. Consequently, the changes in the epidemic threshold differ disproportionately among these three groups. A similar phenomenon is observed when the effective proportionality constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta$$\end{document}) is varied from 0-1 (Fig. 5a–c). A higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta$$\end{document} increases the risk of infection, leading to varying vaccine uptake levels across different perceived risk groups by changing their vaccine payoffs. This, in turn, raises the epidemic threshold. Interestingly, no significant change is observed when the transmission rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) is modified (Fig. 5g–f), as adjusting this parameter does not alter the contact topology of individuals, which is essential in computing the epidemic threshold (see Eqs. 4–9).

Another interesting observation is that adaptive social connections cause the epidemic threshold to be consistently higher for individuals with a medium perceived risk of infection but lower for those with either low or high perceived risk. This pattern emerges because evolving social connections lead to clusters of individuals with similar risk perceptions within the community. For groups with low or high perceived risk, this clustering tends to result in groups of non-vaccinators, whereas for the medium-risk group, it leads to higher vaccination rates. Of course, this observation can vary depending on the initial parameter values and the initial distribution of individuals among the three risk groups. In reality, most people in a community have a medium risk perception, so these findings may have significant implications for public health strategies to control disease transmission in a population.

Discussion and conclusion

Amidst the widespread growth of online social media platforms, the exchange of information and behaviours related to vaccination have a substantial impact on vaccine uptake, which indirectly influences the control of infectious disease outbreaks18,29,46. People now routinely use social media for advice and information on various health-related topics, including vaccination decisions, epidemic awareness, and the ethical implications of antibiotic use47–49. On the other hand, people tend to interact more with others with similar thoughts in the context of disease perceptions, often forming connections through mutual acquaintances. Therefore, as social networks continue to evolve, they create disproportionate risk perceptions among individuals in society, which, in turn, influences their decisions to support the control strategies planned by public health authorities to control the outbreak in the community.

Several key insights from our investigation bear significance in population decision-making in vaccination, network evolution, and disease transmission in the community. We observed a significant impact of adaptive social connections on three epidemic thresholds, influenced by variations in individuals’ perceived vaccine risk. Specifically, the epidemic threshold increased for individuals with moderate perceived vaccine risk. This finding suggests that public health authorities should intensify vaccine campaigns and improve risk communication to encourage individuals with higher perceived risk to shift towards a moderate level of perceived risk. By doing so, the overall epidemic threshold of the population can be raised, thereby reducing the likelihood of an outbreak. The physical connections within the network structure also emerged as a critical factor influencing the epidemic threshold. For example, higher power-law exponents impede vaccine uptake as they accelerate infection spread among individuals, thereby infecting individuals before they have the chance to get vaccinated. Our study also emphasizes that individual sensitivity to vaccinated neighbours enhances payoff differences and promotes increased vaccine uptake. These findings offer valuable insights for public health authorities, emphasizing the need for proper risk communication about vaccines and the severity of infections to increase awareness in the community. Such strategic measures hold the potential for curbing infections in the community.

This research offers valuable insights into the relationships between perceived vaccine risk, vaccine awareness, the structure of individuals’ social networks, and their collective impact on vaccine uptake. However, it is crucial to acknowledge the limitations of our model and the potential for enhancement by including more realistic scenarios. In real-world situations, mainly when a vaccine is newly introduced, its risks are often uncertain, leading to the dissemination of rumours on social media regarding adverse effects such as paralysis, lung congestion, or even death. These negative perceptions can significantly influence the perceived vaccine risk within entire communities32,50,51. Future models could incorporate uncertainty or indirect inferences about others’ vaccination status, providing a more nuanced understanding of privacy considerations. Additionally, One can enhance the model by proposing the parameter representing “inertia” or “commitment” to current strategies. This parameter could reduce the probability of individuals switching strategies, particularly among those strongly favouring one approach52. This consideration is especially relevant for emerging pathogens like COVID-19, as integrating these elements into our model could modify the qualitative aspects of our findings and support the development of more effective infection control policies. Another essential factor to consider is the adaptability of physical networks. During a pandemic, individuals avoid physical contact with sick relatives and friends and may implement social isolation measures. Consequently, physical networks undergo significant changes. Our model could be further refined by accounting for these dynamic shifts, providing an avenue for future research.

Our study provides a potential framework for developing more effective and tailored strategies to control vaccine-preventable diseases by analyzing the interplay between vaccine choices, awareness dissemination, and network structures. In an increasingly interconnected world, enhanced disease control plans and advancing public health programs can be achieved by leveraging social media platforms to convey accurate information about vaccines and infections.
