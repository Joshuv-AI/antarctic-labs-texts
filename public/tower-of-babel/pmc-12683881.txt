Effect of oral sesame oil gelatin capsules on episiotomy pain in primiparous women: a triple-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mahdis Mehrabi Bardar, Maryam Omidi, Golnaz Sadat Ahmadinezhad, Somayeh Arasteh, Roshanak Salari, Fatemeh Zahra Karimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are common treatments to reduce episiotomy pain intensity. Due to the side effects of these drugs, the use of medicinal plants has gained attention. In addition to pain reduction, the fatty acids in sesame oil can increase the pain perception threshold and effectively reduce episiotomy pain intensity. This study aimed to investigate the effect of sesame oil on episiotomy pain intensity in primiparous women.MethodsThis three-blind randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted on 88 primiparous women at Bent Al-Hadi Hospital in Bojnord, Iran. Participants were recruited from December 2022 to February 2024. Women meeting inclusion criteria, 2 h after vaginal delivery, received a sesame oil gelatin capsule or placebo every 6 h for 24 h. Episiotomy pain intensity was assessed before the first intervention and one hour after each dose using a visual pain scale. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 25) with independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney, and chi-square tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsBefore the first intervention, the average episiotomy pain intensity score was 5.88 ± 1.13 in the sesame oil group and 5.80 ± 1.12 in the placebo group (P = 0.74).The average pain intensity score one hour after the first dose (P = 0.92) and the second dose (P = 0.64) showed no statistical significance. However, the average pain intensity score one hour after the third dose (P = 0.038) and fourth dose (P = 0.029) in the sesame oil group was significantly lower than the placebo group.ConclusionAlthough at the start of the study and after the initial two doses, there was no significant difference between the group that received sesame oil and the group that received the placebo. However, with continued use and after the third and fourth doses, the reduction in pain within the group that took the sesame oil capsules was noticeably greater than in the placebo group. This finding y indicates that the analgesic effect of sesame oil may require time to accumulate in the body and become evident with prolonged use.Keywords: Episiotomy, Pain intensity, Pain severity, Sesame, Sesame oil, Primiparous

Background

Episiotomy involves cutting perineal muscles in the second stage of delivery to widen the pelvic outlet and expedite fetal head exit and is the most common surgical incision in obstetrics [1]. This method is prevalent in Asian women due to their short perineum and high risk of perineal rupture [2]. Episiotomy is more frequent in primiparous women due to greater perineal tissue resistance [3]. In developing countries, episiotomy prevalence ranges from 10 to 98% [4] In the Middle East, the prevalence is reported at 67% [5]. Despite recommendations, the rate of episiotomy remains high in some countries and has been reported in 97% of primiparous women in Iran [6].

Episiotomy repair can lead to pain, perineal inflammation, edema, suture opening, dyspareunia, infection, bleeding, repair needs, fistula formation, and negative effects on sexual function, urinary and fecal continence [7, 8]. Pain is a common issue after episiotomy, affecting 94% of women in the first hours after delivery [9]. This pain can hinder daily activities, infant care, breastfeeding, and emotional bonding with the baby [10, 11]. It can also lead to complications such as anxiety, sleep and emotional disorders and depression [12, 13]. Chemical pain relievers are typically used to manage episiotomy pain, but the side effects have prompted interest in alternative methods like medicinal plants [14].

Sesame with the scientific name sesamum inducum is a plant from the pedaliaceae family that has laxative, diuretic, milk-increasing and anti-anemia properties [15]. Sesame oil has palmitic acid, oleic acid, linoleic acid and lecithin. Lecithin is a precursor of acetylcholine and reduces pain by synthesizing acetylcholine and activating nicotinic receptors. The fatty acids in the diet containing sesame oil inhibit prostaglandin synthetase E2 enzymes, which mediate the conversion of cyclooxygenase 1 and 2 enzymes into prostaglandin E2, and thereby increase the threshold of pain perception [16].

In addition, sesame oil contains high amounts of monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids and vitamin E, which moderates immune and inflammatory mediators [17]. According to the mechanism of the analgesic effect, which is related to saturated and unsaturated fatty acids, sesame oil has 39.7 g (per 100 g) of monosaturated fatty acids and 41.7 g (per 100 g) of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Also, the calories in sesame oil are 884 kilocalories, which is higher than sesame seeds and arde (567–586). The results of the study by Askari M, et al. (2016) showed that using sesame oil reduces the pain of the first, second, and third stages of labor [18]. Haidari F et al. (2012) also proved in their study that the use of Arde (containing sesame oil) leads to the reduction of primary dysmenorrhea in students. The reason for the reduction in the intensity of dysmenorrhoea pain is the modulation of eicosanoids biosynthesis due to the effect of sesamin and alpha-tocopherol in sesame [17]. In traditional medicine, sesame oil has been used to relieve pain in people with toothache, joint and muscle pain, premenstrual syndrome, and scratches and cuts [19, 20] M. In the studies by Bigdeli Shamloo et al. (2015, 2019)., Topical application of sesame oil could reduce pain severity and frequency of received NSAIDs in patients with upper or lower extremities trauma and is effective in reducing the pain severity of chemotherapy-induced phlebitis [21, 22]. Also, in Nasiri et al.‘s study (2017) the results showed massage with topical sesame oil was associated with significant reduction in pain severity of patients with limbs trauma [23].Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that coadministration of topical sesame oil with conventional treatments may provide additional benefits in alleviating pain severity [24, 25].The toxic oral dose of sesame in the animal study of Johnson W et al. (1993) has been expressed as more than 5 g per kilogram [26]. According to Ferreira-Valente MA et al.‘s study (2011), a history of sensitivity to sesame was one of the contraindications for using sesame and its products, and no other contraindications were mentioned [27].

Finally, it should be noticed that relieving patients’ pain effectively, could providing physical comfort to the patient, improves the quality of life, in addition to the patient can returns to daily life faster and reduces the length of stay in the hospital [11]. Moreover, since no study has been done to investigate the effect of sesame oil on the episiotomy pain severity and intensity, the present study was conducted with the aim of investigating the effect of sesame oil on episiotomy pain intensity of primiparous women.

Methods

This two-group, three-blind, clinical trial study was conducted on 88 primiparous women who delivered at Bent Al-Hadi Hospital in Bojnord, Iran Participants were recruited from December 2022 to February 2024. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences with the code IR.MUMS.NURSE.REC.1401.007 and at the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Center with the code IRCT20210124050128N2. Informed consent form were completed by participants.

The inclusion criteria consisted of primiparity of the mother, normal vaginal delivery, moderate to severe episiotomy pain intensity (score higher than 4 based on the visual analog index), singleton pregnancy, live, healthy term baby with cephalic presentation, the baby’s weight was between 2500 and 4000 g, no history of allergy to sesame, no medical or mental illness, no need for cesarean, and absence of obstetric complications in the first, second, and third stages of labour.

Attrition criteria included the mother’s unwillingness to continue the study, refusal to take more than one sesame oil capsule or placebo, and the occurrence of allergy symptoms or any complications related to the drug or placebo.

The sample size was calculated based on the results of Haidari et al.‘s study (2012) [17] by using a formula for comparing means with a confidence factor of 99% and a test power of 90%, resulting in 41 people in each group. To account for potential attrition, the sample size was increased by 20% (100 in total). During the study, 7 people from the intervention group and 5 people from the control group were excluded, 88 people were investigated as the final sample (Fig. 1).

Data collection tools included a demographic characteristics questionnaire and a visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain measurement. The demographic characteristics questionnaire included personal information, as well as details regarding obstetrics history details of the participants.

Demographic characteristics questionnaire included personal information, as well as details regarding obstetrics history of the participants.

VAS, a 100 mm line marked from 0 to 10, was used to assess pain intensity. A score of zero indicated no pain, while a score of ten indicated the maximum level of pain.

VAS is a standard tool and its validity has been confirmed in previous studies [27]. Many studies have confirmed its psychometric properties and its utility in clinical trials [28]. In the present study, the reliability of VAS was confirmed through internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding results of 0.81.

In order to collect data, eligible primiparous women who were 2 h past from their vaginal delivery were selected as available method. At first, the amount of episiotomy pain of the mother was measured using a visual analogue tool. In this way, that the mother was asked to rate the level of her episiotomy stitches pain from zero to ten and to mark it on the paper that is provided to her. A 100 mm line is drawn on the paper, with the number zero on the left side meaning no pain and the number ten on the right side meaning the maximum level of pain. If a score of more than 4 was obtained from the visual analog VAS tool, the person was included in the study, and demographic and midwifery information questionnaires were completed.

Sampling was done by available method. Women were allocated by simple randomization with a random sequence generated using the www.randomization.com in two intervention and control groups. The random sequence created on the site was kept in the same order in sealed envelopes until each participant was selected. After each woman entered the study, one of the cans of capsules A or B was delivered to her according to the random sequence mentioned above.

Regarding blinding, Both the Sesame Oil Gelatin Capsules and placebo drugs had identical appearance and were labeled as random numbers. Only the pharmacist was aware about the numbers, while researchers, statistical analyst, participating women were blinded to the group assignments.

Participants received 1.1 mg capsules of sesame oil or placebo 4 times at 6-hour intervals. The mother is asked to take the medicine or placebo with a glass of water. The episiotomy pain intensity of mothers was assessed using a visual analog pain scale one hour after receiving each dose of medicine. In case of any complication related to medicine and placebo, the complication was registered and the person was excluded from the study and the treatment course of the complication is followed by the relevant researcher and related specialist. If the mother felt pain at any time during the study and asked for painkillers, a 250 mg capsule of mefenamic acid was provided to her.

Sesame seeds were obtained from health centers under the faculty of traditional medicine of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. Oiling them was done. Then, the prepared sesame oil was provided to the vice president of Food and Drug of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences to confirm and evaluate the formulation and lipid profile. After the approval, the prepared oil was provided to Pars Tek Rokh Company for the production of gelatin capsules and the 1.1 g of sesame oil capsules were prepared in gelatin form. Also, for the preparation of placebo capsules, similar capsules to the gelatin capsules of sesame oil were used, and they did not have any therapeutic or absorption effect. Since no prior studies have examined the effects of sesame oil on episiotomy pain, the study by Monteiro et al. (2014) and the animal-to-human dose conversion formula [29] were used to determine the appropriate drug dose. The calculated maximum daily human dose was 4,550 mg, which was divided into four administrations every six hours, each containing 1,137 mg. In accordance with international pharmaceutical standards for specifying dosages based on active ingredient content, we formulated gelatin capsules containing 1,100 mg of sesame oil (1.1 g; range: 1.0–1.2 g).

Data analysis was done using descriptive analytical statistics and using SPSS software (version 25). The normality of the quantitative data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov. For normal data analysis of covariance and independent t-test were used. Mann-Whitney, Friedman test were used for abnormal data, and finally variables, exact chi-square, chi-square, and Fisher’s exact test were used for nominal. P value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The majority of women (32 people) 90.7% in the sesame capsule group and 93.3% (42 people) in the placebo group were housewives. There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of demographic and obstetric variables (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

The mean episiotomy pain intensity score was 5.88 ± 1.13 in the sesame oil capsule group and 5.80 ± 1.12 in the placebo group before the first intervention. Mann-Whitney test did not show this difference as significant (P = 0.74).

The mean episiotomy pain intensity score was 4.58 ± 1.17 in the sesame oil capsule group and 4.57 ± 1.13 in the placebo group one hour after receiving the first dose of medicine or placebo. Mann-Whitney test did not show this difference as significant (P = 0.92).

The mean episiotomy pain intensity score was 3.93 ± 1.29 in the sesame oil capsule group and 4.20 ± 1.71 in the placebo group one hour after receiving the second dose of the medicine or placebo. Mann-Whitney test did not show this difference as significant (P = 0.64).

The mean episiotomy pain intensity score was 2.69 ± 1.01 in the sesame oil capsule group and 3.46 ± 1.64 in the placebo group one hour after receiving the third dose of medicine or placebo. Mann-Whitney test showed this difference to be significant (P = 0.038).

The mean episiotomy pain intensity score was 2.16 ± 1.11 in the sesame oil capsule group and 2.86 ± 1.53 in the placebo group one hour after receiving the fourth dose of medicine or placebo. Mann-Whitney test showed this difference to be significant (P = 0.029) (Table 2).

A repeated measures analysis of variance test showed that there was a significant difference in pain intensity between the two groups pre- and post-treatments (P = 0.031). Group (P = 0.047) and time (P = 0.028) had a significant effect on the pain intensity score. However, the group × time interaction on pain intensity was not significant (P = 0.148).

Cohen’s effect size for the fourth dose, which had the largest effect across the stages, was calculated to be 0.52 for between-group values. In the within-group analysis, the effect size before and after the intervention in the intervention group was 3.32 and in the placebo group, the mean was 2.20, indicating an effect size difference of 1.1 between the two groups.

In this study, side effects were not observed.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the effect of sesame oil on episiotomy pain intensity in primiparous women.

The use of complementary medicine methods is considered an important factor in pain control and reduction during delivery and the postpartum period. Nowadays, complementary medicine has gained more attention for pain control in pregnant women and women who have given birth due to fewer maternal and neonatal complications. The present study demonstrates the effect of sesame oil gelatin capsules on reducing episiotomy pain intensity compared to the control group that received a placebo.

The results of the study showed a statistically significant difference in the reduction of episiotomy pain intensity in the intervention group compared to the control group one hour after receiving the third dose of medicine. This may be due to the cumulative effect of the medicine and the duration of the sesame oil gelatin capsule’s effect. Additionally, both the drug and placebo were well tolerated, with no complications observed among the participants.

Jafarzadeh-Kenarsari F et al. (2019) conducted a clinical trial study on the effects of olea ointment on episiotomy pain severity in primiparous women. Their study showed that using olea topical ointment after episiotomy can lead to pain reduction in women. Olea ointment contains honey 33.4%, olive oil 33.3% and sesame oil 33.3%. In Jafarzadeh et al.‘s study, the analgesic effect of olea ointment is more on the fifth and tenth day after the intervention, which indicates the cumulative effect of the medicine and the increase in the use duration of it [29]. Torkashvand S et al.(2021) demonstrated that the use of topical Olea ointment with honey, sesame oil and olive compounds can accelerate episiotomy wound healing [30].

Other studies have also shown the effectiveness of sesame oil in pain reduction. Mohammadian et al. (2017) used sesame seeds to treat dysmenorrhea, which was effective in reducing dysmenorrhea [31] Monteiro et al. (2014) studied the anti-inflammatory effects of sesame oil on rats. Doses of 100, 200, 400 mg/kg of sesame oil were administered orally to rats 60 min before the start of the HOTEPLATE test, which was effective in pain reduction of rats [32].

The results of the clinical trial study of Askari M et al. (2016) found that the use of massage with sesame oil as a safe method without any side effects can reduce labor pain and duration significantly. This study was conducted on 120 primiparous pregnant women, 40 in the sesame oil massage group, 40 in the placebo massage group, and 40 in the control group who received routine care. The intervention was performed in the form of 15 min of regular and circular massage with sesame oil in three dilation stages 1–4, 6–7 and 11 –8 cm during contraction and after contraction and the pain intensity was measured at the end of each stage. In this study, sesame oil reduced the duration and pain intensity of the first, second and third stages of labor significantly, but the amount of pain reduction was higher in the third stage of labor, which is consistent with the present study [18].

Gholami M et al. (2020) conducted a study on the effects of topical sesame oil on pain severity in trauma patients, showing promising results in pain reduction and prevention of skin discoloration.

[33]. Abolfazli M et al. (2021) also found that sesame oil can reduce back pain in nurses with chronic pain [34].

The results of Kafash Mohammadjani, M et al.‘s study (2022) entitled " Effect of Topical Sesame Oil on the Severity of Pain in Patients with Limb Trauma: A Randomized Controlled Trial " which was conducted on 120 patients with non-penetrating limb trauma, that the results do not match with the present study. The difference in the type of injury and its location and the different statistical population of the two studies can be the reason for the discrepancy between the results of these two studies [35].

Our findings indicate statistically significant effects at the third and fourth doses, whereas the first and second doses showed non-significant trends, suggesting a possible dose threshold for detectable effects. These results emphasize the importance of treatment duration in clinical applications and highlight the need for future research to determine optimal dosing schedules and the cumulative effects at higher doses observed in our study suggest that sustained systemic drug levels may be necessary to elicit significant clinical outcomes.

The current study showed that sesame oil capsules as a safe, effective and easy method can be one of the suitable types of complementary medicine. This substance can help reduce the pain intensity of patients with different mechanisms. In addition, this substance can be used in different ways, such as massage and absorption through the skin or orally. However, sesame oil is often preferred in oral formulations owing to its oily texture, patients’ familiarity with it, and its widespread use in daily diets.

Limitations of the study include individual differences in pain threshold and the short follow-up period. Future studies with longer follow-up durations are recommended to better assess sustained effects of the intervention. Despite efforts to reduce bias, placebo effects may have influenced outcomes, but between-group comparisons helped isolate the true efficacy of the intervention.

In order to reduce bias in the study, the sample size was determined to be sufficient based on similar studies, and sample dropouts were considered. To control for the placebo effect, a double-blind design was implemented, and routine care was kept consistent for both groups. However, it’s important to note that these measures help control the placebo effect rather than completely eliminate it. The follow-up period was limited to 24 h due to the standard early discharge policy for mothers after delivery, making longer-term assessments unfeasible. In the study setting, standard non-pharmacological interventions for episiotomy pain (such as the use of cold or hot compresses) were not included in the postpartum care protocol and only routine care was provided. Therefore, all participants in both groups experienced completely similar conditions in this respect, and this variable did not act as a confounding factor in our study.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest a dose threshold for detectable effects of sesame oil capsules on pain intensity. The study emphasizes the importance of treatment duration and optimal dosing schedules for significant clinical outcomes. The use of sesame oil gelatin capsules is a safe and effective method for reducing episiotomy pain in primiparous women after third dose. So considering the importance of maintaining the mother’s health and promoting it, as well as the high incidence and prevalence of perineal pain after delivery and the many complications resulting from it, it is hoped that the results of this research improve the quality of clinical services and provide safe, effective, and non-invasive treatments for episiotomy pain.
