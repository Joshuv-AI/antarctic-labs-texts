Comparing the effects of ischemic compression and kinesiology taping techniques in patients with chronic low back pain
Authors: İlker Demir, Sezgin Zontul, Kezban Yigiter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic low back pain is a common musculoskeletal condition for which various physiotherapy techniques are employed. However, the comparative effectiveness of ischaemic compression and kinesiology taping remains unclear. The aim of this study was to compare the effects of these two techniques on patients with chronic low back pain. Participants with chronic low back pain (defined as pain persisting for at least 12 weeks) were randomly assigned to one of three groups: Conservative treatment (CT), Conservative treatment plus kinesiology taping (CT + KT), Conservative treatment plus ischemic compression (CT + IC). Assessments were performed before treatment commenced and again at the end of the three-week treatment period. The patients’ pain levels during movement, at rest and at night were measured, as were their flexion, extension and right and left lateral flexion values. Significant improvements in pain levels, as well as in flexion and extension scores, were observed in all three groups (p < 0.05). No significant difference was found in the intergroup comparison between kinesiology taping and conservative treatment methods (p > 0.05). However, in the group treated with ischaemic compression, greater improvement was observed in all parameters except lateral flexion angle compared to the other groups (p < 0.05). Adding the ischemic compression technique to conservative treatment for chronic low back pain produced a superior therapeutic effect compared to kinesiology taping or conservative treatment alone.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT07425301 (registered retrospectively on 13 February 2026).Keywords: Chronic low back pain, Kinesiology taping, Conservative treatment, Ischemic compressionSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Pain in the lumbar region that lasts for 12 weeks or more is defined as chronic low back pain (CLBP). The cause of the pain may be related to various structural elements such as trigger points, spinal stenosis, facet joint dysfunction, annulus fibrosus lesions, paravertebral muscles and fascia, spinal nerve roots, disc herniation or congenital anomalies. However, in some cases, the source of the pain may be non-specific. The prevalence of CLBP is reported to be 15–30% in the general population. The main risk factors for the disease are obesity, ageing, working in heavy physical occupations, psychological stress and depression. A wide range of treatments for CLBP exists, from conservative methods to surgical interventions. However, there is no standardised treatment protocol1. Classic treatment methods include medical treatment tailored to symptoms, exercise programmes and physical therapy2. While these methods provide some improvement, alternative approaches have been developed to eliminate pain completely. These approaches include kinesiology taping, ischaemic compression techniques, trigger point therapy and increasing blood flow to reduce muscle and fascia tension, all of which have numerous beneficial effects.

Kinesiology taping is a treatment method developed by Dr Kenzo Kase. Its primary aim is to reduce pressure on the tissue surface to create space and increase blood and lymphatic circulation in the application area3. Depending on how it is applied, kinesiology taping can support muscle contraction, reduce pain and oedema, and alleviate fascia and muscle tension. Thanks to these properties, the method is used to treat a wide variety of clinical conditions, primarily musculoskeletal disorders4.

Trigger points are defined as localised areas of hyper sensitivity within taut bands of skeletal muscle. Patients typically report localised pain, burning sensations and hypersensitivity to palpation in these areas5. Trigger points are classified as either active or latent. Active trigger points cause referred pain without pressure, whereas latent trigger points usually do not cause symptoms6. Active trigger points are an important factor in the aetiology of chronic low back pain and require treatment7.

The ischaemic compression technique, as defined by Travell and Simons, involves applying pressure to a trigger point identified by palpation for a set amount of time. When the pressure is released, reactive hyperaemia develops in the affected area, supporting tissue healing8,9. This method is not only effective in treating chronic low back pain, but also many other conditions, such as neck pain, myofascial pain syndrome, shoulder pain and chronic pelvic pain6,10–12.

Kinesiology taping and ischaemic compression techniques are non-invasive methods that aim to promote healing by increasing blood flow via similar mechanisms. Both methods have a wide range of clinical applications beyond chronic low back pain, yet their comparative effectiveness in this population remains unclear. Although both kinesiology taping and ischemic compression are commonly used in the management of chronic low back pain, the comparative effectiveness of these techniques remains unclear. Previous studies have generally evaluated these methods separately, and there is limited evidence directly comparing their effects. Therefore, there is a need for studies that directly compare these commonly used interventions to guide clinical decision-making. The aim of this study was to compare the effects of ischemic compression and kinesiology taping in individuals with chronic low back pain.

Methods

The study was conducted between November 2024 and November 2025, involving patients who had attended the Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation Outpatient Clinic at the Turgut Özal Medical Centre, and who had experienced back pain for at least 12 weeks. Participants were diagnosed with non-specific chronic low back pain, which is defined as pain persisting for at least 12 weeks, based on clinical evaluation. Patients with specific underlying pathologies (e.g. malignancy, fracture, infection or inflammatory diseases) were excluded in accordance with the predefined exclusion criteria. The same physiotherapist performed assessments using the IC and KT applications. During the study, the recommendations of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) group for conducting parallel-group randomized trials were followed13 (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram.

This clinical trial was retrospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identification number NCT07425301; registered on 13 February 2026). The delay in registration was due to the authors being unaware of the requirement for prospective trial registration when the study was initiated. The study protocol, however, including the objectives, eligibility criteria, interventions and outcome measures, had been predefined prior to participant recruitment and was not modified during or after the study. No changes were made to the study design, outcomes, or analysis after data collection began.

Participants

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power with an alpha level of 0.05 and 90% power, resulting in a sample size of 102 patients. A total of 143 individuals were interviewed. Thirty of these did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 11 patients discontinued the study.

The groups were randomly assigned using the ‘Research Randomizer’ programme. Each group comprised 34 patients: the conservative treatment group (CT) (Group 1); the group receiving conservative treatment with kinesiology taping (CT + KT) (Group 2); and the group receiving conservative treatment with ischaemic compression (CT + IC) (Group 3).

Treatment

All groups received the same conservative treatment, which included hot packs, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and therapeutic ultrasound. Group 1 (CT): Received only conservative treatment. Group 2 (CT + KT): Received conservative treatment plus kinesiology taping. Group 3 (CT + IC): Received conservative treatment plus ischemic compression therapy.

Patients in the KT group (Group 2) received KT plus taping with 5 cm x 5 cm kinesiology tape (Kinesio Tex Gold) every other day, three times a week, for a total of nine sessions. During application, patients were asked to stand upright and bend forward, after which kinesiology tape was applied using the star technique. Four I-shaped kinesiology tapes, each 5 cm wide and 0.5 mm thick, were applied at a 25% stretch in a star pattern to the area of maximum pain14.

Patients in Group 3 underwent nine sessions of IC therapy over three weeks, with three sessions per week, alongside the KT programme. Trigger points in chronic low back pain are localised around the lumbar region, specifically in the iliacus, lumborum, quadratus lumborum and gluteus muscle groups. In the IC method, the physiotherapist applies pressure to the identified trigger point in the stretched muscle until sensory sensitivity is elicited. Once pain was felt, compression was applied to the trigger point for 90 s. Different points were then palpated and the IC procedure was performed on painful trigger points for 20–40 min6,15.

Data collection

Two assessments were conducted: one before the intervention (baseline) and one at the end of the three-week treatment period (post-intervention). Information on patients’ age, gender and marital status was recorded using a personal information form. The patients’ pain intensity was measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), normal joint movement: Trunk flexion, extension and right and left lateral flexion were measured using a universal goniometer16.

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)

Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a widely used and validated tool for measuring subjective pain intensity. The VAS is a 10 cm horizontal line ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the worst imaginable pain). Participants were asked to indicate their level of pain by marking a point on the line, and the distance from the origin was measured to obtain a numerical value. Pain was evaluated under three conditions: at rest, during movement and at night. All assessments were conducted at baseline and at the end of the three-week intervention period17.

Measurements were taken twice, before and after the application, for lumbar flexion, extension, and right and left lateral flexion movements.

Flexion: The lateral trunk projection of the lumbosacral joint was used as the pivot point for the flexion measurement. The fixed arm was held parallel to the lateral midline of the femur and perpendicular to the floor. The movable arm was held so that it followed the lateral trunk midline toward the axilla18.

Extension: For extension measurement, the same pivot, fixed and moving arm was used as for flexion measurement18.

Lateral flexion: The midpoint of the lumbosacral joint was used as the pivot point for lateral flexion measurement. The fixed arm was held perpendicular to the floor at the mid-sacrum level. The movable arm was held so that it followed the spinal processes of the lumbar vertebrae18.

Statistical analysis

The IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) was used to analyse the data. A power analysis was performed to determine the required sample size. The Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to assess the normality of data distribution. For variables that met the assumption of normal distribution, parametric tests were applied; otherwise, non-parametric tests were used. A dependent t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pre- and post-treatment values within groups, depending on data distribution. Intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc tests. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethic statement and consent to participate

Prior to data collection, necessary permits were obtained from İnönü University Turgut Özal Medical Center Hospital and ethical approval was obtained from the Inönü University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024/136, Date: 16.10.2024). All individuals were informed about the study, and those who agreed to participate voluntarily were included. They were also informed that their information would be protected and that they could withdraw from the study if they wished. The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Discussion

This study compared the effectiveness of ischaemic compression and kinesiology taping techniques in individuals diagnosed with chronic low back pain (CLBP). Participants were divided into three groups: one receiving conservative treatment only; one receiving KT + IC; and one receiving CT + KT.

Significant improvement was observed in all groups following treatment when pre- and post-treatment findings were compared. Intergroup comparisons revealed that individuals in the CT + IC group experienced more significant improvements in pain during movement, rest pain, night pain, and flexion and extension angles compared to those in the CT and CT + KT groups. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of right and left lateral flexion angles.

Chronic low back pain is a musculoskeletal condition characterised by pain and restricted mobility. In our study, the mean age of participants was 40.26 ± 8.30 years, 62.8% were female, and most were high school graduates and married. These findings are consistent with those reported in a systematic review by Hoy et al., which included 165 studies from 54 countries. This study found that low back pain is most prevalent in women and in the 40–80 age group19. Previous studies have also emphasised that educational level and marital status can influence the progression of low back pain20. The demographic data obtained in our study are consistent with previous research findings.

Although the aetiology of chronic low back pain remains unclear, it is a complex condition characterised by pain and reduced joint mobility. As there is no definitive treatment protocol, various methods are used alongside conservative treatments, such as exercise programmes, kinesiology taping and dry needling21,22.

Kinesiology taping is a technique that aims to increase blood and lymph circulation through the mechanical tension it creates on the skin, thereby supporting tissue oxygenation and healing23. Different results have been reported in the literature regarding the effects of CT on chronic low back pain. Some studies suggest that CT can reduce pain and functional impairment24–26, while others state that it does not produce a significant difference25–27. When KT was compared with placebo taping in patients with non-specific low back pain, no significant difference was observed between the two groups. This suggests that KT may be more effective when used alongside other treatments rather than on its own21,27.

In our study, the addition of KT to CT resulted in improvements in pain levels and some ranges of motion compared to pre-treatment; however, it did not show a statistically significant advantage over the group that received CT alone. This result is consistent with similar studies in the literature27,28.

A trigger point is a sensitive area within the taut bands of muscle that causes pain29. Studies in individuals with chronic low back pain have reported that trigger points are most commonly located in the quadratus lumborum, iliocostalis lumborum, gluteus minimus, and gluteus medius muscles30.

The ischemic compression technique is one of the most commonly used methods for treating myofascial trigger points. It is based on creating local ischaemia by applying pressure to the trigger point for a certain period of time, thereby supporting tissue healing through increased reactive hyperaemia once the pressure is released. Various studies have demonstrated that IC is an effective treatment for chronic low back pain. For instance, it has been suggested that soft tissue treatment can speed up the healing process in individuals with back pain, and that IC is a safe and effective way to treat sensitive areas31.

In our study, applying the IC technique alongside CT resulted in a more significant reduction in pain levels (during movement, at rest and at night) and a greater increase in flexion-extension and lateral flexion angles than in the CT-only and CT + KT groups. These results suggest that combining IC with CT improves therapeutic efficacy.

The efficacy of IC has been evaluated in various ways in the literature. Alvarez et al.22 compared IC and dry needling techniques in patients with non-specific low back pain and reported that dry needling was more effective in the short term (48 h). However, some studies have also indicated that IC is as effective as dry needling in reducing pain or yields better results in different areas such as neck pain32,33. The findings of our study reveal that the IC technique combined with CT provides a higher effect in pain control and increased range of motion compared to CT and CT + KT treatments.

Our study has certain limitations. It was conducted at a single centre, the sample size was small, and the treatment and follow-up periods were short. Furthermore, the limited socio-cultural diversity of the participants restricts the generalisability of the results. It is thought that future multicentre studies with larger samples and longer follow-up periods will make significant contributions to the literature.

A statistically significant difference in age between the groups was identified at baseline, which could be a potential confounding factor. As no additional covariance analysis was performed, these findings should be interpreted with caution and it cannot definitively be concluded that the observed effects are entirely independent of age differences. Additionally, the short duration of the intervention and follow-up period limits our ability to draw conclusions about the long-term efficacy or sustainability of the treatment effects. Furthermore, the absence of additional patient-reported outcome measures, which are commonly used to assess functional status and quality of life in low back pain research, is another limitation. Finally, the retrospective registration of the trial may raise concerns regarding transparency and potential reporting bias.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that the IC method could be important in controlling pain and increasing range of motion in chronic low back pain. This study was conducted to determine the most effective treatment methods for chronic low back pain. The findings suggest that combining the IC technique with conservative treatment may lead to greater improvements in pain and range of motion than CT or CT + KT alone. These results could inform clinical decision-making in the management of chronic low back pain.
