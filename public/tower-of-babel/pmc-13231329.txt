ASP-COMPLEX: redefining antimicrobial stewardship to improve outcomes in high-risk patients with severe infections
Authors: Juan Pasquau, Francisco Javier Candel, José Luis del Pozo, Ángel Estella, Carmen Hidalgo-Tenorio, Jesús Ruiz-Ramos, Juan González-del-Catillo, Natalia Chueca, Francisco López-Medrano, Esperanza Merino, Francisco Anguita-Santos, Carolina Garcia-Vidal, Miguel Salavert-Lletí
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
At a time of great concern regarding trends in morbidity and mortality from infections, and mindful of the limitations of our current approaches to combating them, this document sets out a structured programme of proposals aimed at improving, in the short and medium term, the survival of the most at-risk patients with severe infections. These are complementary proposals which, on the one hand, reinforce already established initiatives and, on the other, introduce some novel aspects to the approach to this complex problem. They focus on accelerating, standardising, and refining the clinical diagnosis of severe infection and its microbiological diagnosis and, above all, on improving the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatment, based on its immediate initiation with the most effective treatment available, and with an improved capacity to integrate innovation into all stages and processes. This programme is designed to be implemented within the framework of the Antimicrobial Stewardship Programmes (ASPs) (hence the name ASP-COMPLEX), and requires appropriate dissemination and surveillance policies, alongside a collaborative environment among the multiple stakeholders involved in delivering innovation (experts, ASP teams, healthcare administration, academia, the pharmaceutical industry, etc.).

Introduction

The problem

Mortality associated with infections constitutes one of the most significant public health challenges globally. It is estimated that infectious diseases contribute to approximately 20% of global mortality, with particularly high and sustained case-fatality rates in the context of severe infections and sepsis [1]. In recent decades, notable international awareness has developed regarding this problem, accompanied by multiple initiatives aimed at optimising antimicrobial use and curbing the threat of resistance [2–22]. However, despite these efforts, available evidence has not consistently demonstrated a significant reduction in mortality associated with severe infections [23–29]. This apparent mismatch suggests a need to critically review current approaches and explore new conceptual frameworks explicitly oriented towards reducing morbidity and mortality, both at the individual patient level and in terms of global health.

The reasons why we have failed to substantially reduce mortality associated with infections, or to counteract increasingly alarming projections, are complex and multifactorial. Factors certainly include the continuous increase in at-risk patients—who are more prone to infection and to progression to severe disease and death—as well as the complexity of these infections and their clinical management, which diversifies and hinders adherence to standards of care and guidelines. This scenario places increasing pressure on healthcare systems, exposing structural and organisational limitations that directly impact clinical outcomes and threaten the sustainability of the healthcare systems.

Another key factor is the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which is directly linked to antimicrobial exposure and the selective ecological pressure it generates. AMR has substantially increased patient vulnerability, compromised microbiological diagnosis—making diagnostic delay a clear risk factor for mortality—and complicated the therapeutic management of infections due to the limited availability of effective options and higher rates of treatment failure, ultimately leading to a significant increase in morbidity and mortality [23, 25–28, 30].

Our response (The current model)

Status analysis. Although there are many other factors that increase patient vulnerability, complicate clinical management, and compromise outcomes, AMR has emerged as one of the most visible and potentially actionable determinants of this problem. For decades, it has formed the core rationale for antimicrobial control policies and antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs) [2–4, 7–22, 31].

In practice, and predominantly for more than 30 years, the main objective of many of these programmes has been the containment or reduction of antimicrobial consumption, through initiatives promoting the appropriate, rational, or prudent use of antibiotics. This approach explains why the key data used by ASPs, national plans (such as the PRAN in Spain), the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), and the World Health Organization Global Action Plan (WHO GAP) are antimicrobial consumption (measured in DDD, DOT, and expenditure) and the rate or trend of infections caused by resistant bacteria.

Consequently, current objectives—formulated in 2018 and projected through to 2030—prioritise a 20% reduction in global antibiotic consumption (27% in the case of the PRAN in Spain [32]) and promote the use of older, narrow-spectrum antimicrobials in at least two out of three prescriptions, according to the WHO Access, Watch and Reserve (AWaRe) classification [15–18]). These efforts aim to achieve a 3%—15% reduction in the incidence of certain severe infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria by 2030. These are essentially preventive, ecologically driven objectives that seek first to minimise AMR, and subsequently, in the medium to long term, reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with infections.

The overriding concern with the ‘ecological preservation’ of antimicrobial therapies often leads to restrictive usage policies, particularly regarding innovative products. These are frequently viewed as ‘endangered species’ that must be protected to maintain their future efficacy [33], enforced through external controls, validations, and rigorous prescribing criteria [34–36]. As a consequence, the needs of current patients with severe infections and high mortality risk—who require the most effective treatments—may be overlooked. To address this, more real-time attention should be devoted to identifying and correcting insufficient or suboptimal treatments, and to reducing the administrative and scientific barriers that limit access to intensified, personalised antibiotic therapies and innovation.

Results. Although these policies have been in place and have enjoyed broad administrative support for over 30 years, recent data show that, between 2018 and 2023, the targets for reducing overall antibiotic consumption, the consumption rate of antibiotics in the Watch and Reserve group, and AMR-associated infections have not been met. On the contrary, the global incidence of these infections has increased significantly—by approximately 40% worldwide [29], with similar trends observed in Europe [37–39] and Spain [40].

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) predicts that infections caused by bacteria resistant to ‘last-resort antibiotics’ will double (with increases ranging from 50% to 300%) between 2005 and 2035 [41–42]. In Africa, mortality attributable to AMR now exceeds the combined death toll of tuberculosis, malaria, and HIV, despite receiving far less attention and investment [43–44].

Similarly, mortality and case-fatality rates for severe infections associated with AMR have shown no improvement [1, 23–27, 45]. Real-world data from Spain [28] place these figures significantly higher than the international estimates, such as those from the WHO, which identify AMR as the leading cause of death by 2050 [10]. Furthermore, robust predictive models suggest that, without a strategic pivot, mortality (attributable and associated) will enter a phase of continuous increase up to 2050 [27].

This scenario has ultimately prompted a reassessment of the problem on the international political agenda. In September 2024, the political declaration of the second UN General Assembly High-Level Meeting on AMR, and in May 2025 at the 78th World Health Assembly, established a clear global commitment to reduce AMR-associated mortality [46–47]. To this end, the 10-20-30 Agenda was finalised. This agenda aims to achieve, by 2030 relative to 2019, a 30% reduction in the inappropriate use of antibiotics in animals, a 20% reduction in humans, and, for the first time, a specific target of a 10% reduction in mortality [44, 46, 47]. Achieving this seems highly unlikely without fundamental conceptual, structural, and cultural changes to our current strategies [27–28].

Conclusion. Consistently available data indicate that our response to this challenge has not delivered the expected outcomes, and that our current model for combating severe infections and AMR has become disconnected from clinical outcomes.

This realisation demands a strategic review to realign our efforts towards improving clinical outcomes and driving down mortality. We require a reframing and reconfiguration of the existing model to generate, complement, and reinforce concrete, operational initiatives that can impact clinical outcomes and improve the real-world prognosis of these infections in the short to medium term.

Review and reconfiguration of the diagnosis and treatment of severe infections

To bring about real operational changes in how we approach severe infections and their high associated mortality, it is essential to:

Recognise and address the limitations of current antibiotic policy

Conceptual limitations

Current antibiotic policies present at least two significant conceptual issues:

The excess mortality associated with infections is primarily and predominantly due to the problem of AMR. It is vital to acknowledge that many other factors influence mortality besides resistance—including biological vulnerability, as well as social, structural, and care-delivery factors. These can exert an even greater impact than AMR itself and warrant increased attention [27, 41–43]. This involves better, broader, and earlier recognition of severe infections, their specific aetiology, and their complex physiopathology (resistance, virulence, persistence, etc.), alongside the pursuit of more individualised and effective treatments.

As the IDSA succinctly stated: “Here, the resource that must be protected and restored is antibiotic effectiveness” [58].

In practical terms, and to give an example from clinical practice, the focus should be more on the clinical efficacy of antibiotics than on their environmental impact [59]. It entails focusing more on the ‘last-line’ antibiotic that was withheld despite being clinically indicated, than on the antibiotic that was unnecessarily prescribed—provided the latter was effective and did not directly cause clinical harm. This perspective should not be interpreted as a dismissal of ecological stewardship principles, but rather as an attempt to dynamically balance individual patient benefit with long-term ecological responsibility in particularly high-risk scenarios. If our approach over the last 75 years has not been sufficiently successful, we must refine it, pursuing continuous improvement to close the gap between human innovation and the evolutionary advantage of the microbial genome [9, 58–59, 74].

Methodological limitations

In their attempt to reduce variability in clinical practice and control costs, our antibiotic policies harbour methodological problems that may hinder, in the clinical setting and in real time, therapeutic precision, individualisation, and the transfer of the benefits of innovation to the patients who need them most. Examples include:

Inefficiency of academic training. Training clinicians to manage complex infections effectively is challenging [75,76]. Traditional educational programmes are voluntary, highly dependent on teaching quality, often lack clinical context, and tend to oversimplify management. They typically focus on protocolisation, reducing clinical variability, preventing AMR, and achieving cost efficiency [8, 16, 18, 22, 32–33, 38, 48–51, 77]. Consequently, the urgent needs of at-risk patients with severe infections are often omitted from this training framework. Digital solutions, advanced software, and artificial intelligence (AI) tools capable of providing structured, real-time clinical decision support systems (CDSS) offer promising ways to overcome these limitations [78].

Over-reliance on the highest-level scientific evidence when formulating recommendations, protocols and guidelines. While evidence-based medicine is fundamental, insisting on the highest-level evidence (such as large meta-analyses) to guide every prescription can result in suboptimal care for the most at-risk patients [16, 18, 33–34, 79–83]. The methodologies used to generate this evidence are often designed for chronic conditions rather than acute, severe infections. As a result, genuine clinical benefits in critical and complex scenarios may not meet standard evidence thresholds and are excluded from clinical guidelines (e.g., non-inferiority studies, which are accessible but provide little impetus for adopting therapeutic innovations) [79–85]. Optimising the management of these infections requires a more adaptive, less dogmatic approach that incorporates diverse evidence sources, including observational and real-world data, structured clinical experience, and expert consensus.

Outsourcing of governance and control over prescribing. Antimicrobial treatments are often subject to strict control by external departments such as pharmacy, microbiology, preventive medicine, infectious diseases committees, ASPs and health administration, which regulate acquisition and dispensing based on centralised therapeutic positioning reports and adherence to rigid guidelines. While these controls aim to prevent AMR and contain costs, they often favour highly restrictive practices for the most innovative and expensive agents. This can introduce delays and inflexibility in patients with severe infections, where early, individualised therapy directly determines prognosis [4, 84–91]. Properly structured stewardship teams should instead facilitate the positioning and delivery of innovative pharmacological, diagnostic, and clinical resources to these critical patients.

Ethical limitations

Protecting and preserving the long-term effectiveness of antibiotics is a core ethical duty towards future patients. However, in severe infections affecting highly vulnerable individuals, a fundamental ethical tension arises between the immediate benefit to the individual and long-term collective responsibility. This conflict cannot be resolved solely through general policies or isolated individual decisions; it requires an integrated approach that balances the specific clinical context with population-level impacts.

The legitimacy of limiting potential benefits for a current patient to preserve future utility depends on clinical severity, diagnostic uncertainty, and the availability of effective therapeutic alternatives. While the future benefits of antibiotic containment may be contingent and dependent on broader scientific progress, they remain a valid consideration within a framework of intergenerational equity. The ethical challenge is therefore to find a dynamic balance that maximises immediate clinical benefit without disproportionately compromising the future sustainability of antimicrobial therapies [87, 92].

Philosophical limitations

The fact that a particular viewpoint has become the ‘official view’ and remains widely supported for many years does not mean that it is always the most correct or effective solution for complex problems. Self-criticism requires acknowledging that knowledge management is often subject to cognitive biases that entrench intellectually attractive positions, even when they diverge from real-world outcomes [93].

The primary focus on preventing AMR solely through the ‘appropriate use of antibiotics’ is a clear example of anchoring heuristic—a cognitive bias whereby we anchor ourselves to a compelling initial solution and limit our search for better alternatives. This anchoring explains why institutions continue to measure antibiotic consumption and resistance rates—which are familiar and relatively easy to track—rather than monitoring real-world clinical outcomes and mortality, which are more complex to capture but represent the ultimate metric [51, 94].

Recognise the need to refocus and revisit the current antibiotic policy

The strategy should be focused on an ASP aimed at severe infections in the most at-risk patients and centred on therapeutic effectiveness. ASPs have driven major progress in optimising antimicrobial use and raising awareness of AMR. However, given the limitations outlined above, they have not consistently improved clinical outcomes, which should remain their foremost objective. A strategic adjustment is therefore needed. This does not mean replacing current ASP models, many of which already incorporate several of these principles, but rather expanding stewardship towards a more outcome-oriented and effectiveness-driven framework in selected high-risk scenarios.

This reconfiguration is built upon three primary pillars:

Target population: Focussed on high-risk, at-risk patients with severe infections that are ‘difficult to treat’. These are the patients who experience the highest mortality rates and for whom restrictive prescribing policies are often least successful. Although vulnerability to infectious diseases is a very broad, complex and difficult-to-define concept, encompassing genetic, biological, psychological, social, administrative and political factors that are difficult to characterise and apply [27, 95–97], in this document, for operational reasons, we define vulnerability on the basis of clinical and biological criteria that are well-validated in the healthcare setting, with a demonstrated capacity to severely impair the patient’s immune response to infection and/or to hinder or delay access to effective treatment, thereby increasing the risk of disease progression and mortality [98–101] (Figure 1).Thus, the main factors contributing to vulnerability would be: a) clinical or pharmacological immunosuppression (of varying intensity depending on aetiology, duration of illness, treatment, net state of immunosuppression, etc.); b) Immune compromise or immune dysfunction occurring in the critically ill patient (due to immunoparalysis associated with the severity of the condition or the imbalance between immune exhaustion and the hyperinflammatory phases of shock), in patients with severe, multiple or decompensated comorbidities (as these significantly reduce ‘functional reserve’), or in geriatric frailty [100, 102–106]; c) the presence of prosthetic/invasive devices (which, through biofilm, create ‘sanctuaries’ that are very difficult for antibiotics to access, and which impair the defensive capacity of the ‘invaded’ organs); and d) colonisation by AMR-bearing microbes, the risk of which is increased in the conditions described above, but above all in patients with recent (within 6 months) exposure to antimicrobials or to the healthcare system (especially if there are problems with AMR endemicity) [107–112].Although a precise, validated and reproducible definition of vulnerability was critical to our aims, the fact is that, given the need to protect these patients in the short term, we have adopted a pragmatic definition—setting aside the academic rigour required for research—under which we consider a patient to be vulnerable if they meet any of the criteria listed above. We believe that there remain more issues of sensitivity than of specificity in the operational definition of vulnerability commonly used in clinical practice; therefore, we believe that, rather than hindering its application with proposals for rigid, validated scales or ‘scores’, it makes sense to liberalise it, in order to facilitate the attribution of vulnerability.

Primary objective: To maximise the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatment. In the context of realigning antibiotic policy with improved clinical outcomes, priority should be given to achieving therapeutic effectiveness, understood as maximising the likelihood of rapid microbial eradication and early clinical control, which have a direct impact on mortality. This is achieved by taking due account of the probable aetiology, the patient’s characteristics, the clinical syndrome and the site of infection, as well as the pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) of the antibiotic treatment.

These initiatives must be based on the continuous improvement of existing resources and on better and wider access to innovation.

Finally, they must be translated into concrete, measurable and monitorable operational objectives (surveillance), which can be implemented (dissemination), and are capable of demonstrating their impact on clinical outcomes.

Lastly, and very importantly, it is worth highlighting the significance, within this improvement process, of the proper establishment of strategic alliances between all stakeholders involved in the healthcare system (academia, the pharmaceutical industry, government and healthcare professionals).

The available evidence indicates that processes of this type, such as the approach to HIV, tuberculosis, candidemia bundle or some ASPs, can be associated with tangible clinical benefits, which reinforces the viability and necessity of this strategic refocusing.

In summary, we believe that, in this context (at-risk patients with severe infections), it is necessary to take a step further and move from the conceptual framework to the operational level, where decisions are made in real time and their consequences are immediate. This document is therefore conceived as a proposal deliberately geared towards clinical practice, designed to address the complexity inherent in severe infections in these at-risk patients. This is not about adding new layers of regulation, but rather about precisely identifying the critical points where therapeutic effectiveness is gained or lost. From here, the focus shifts to early diagnosis (clinical and microbiological), risk stratification, and the selection of the treatment most likely to have a clinical impact. In the following sections, the reader will find a structured elaboration of these elements, with the aim of facilitating faster decisions, based on sound scientific grounds, and more closely aligned with the actual reduction of morbidity and mortality. This implies accepting that, in this scenario, effectiveness is not an option, but a clinical imperative.

Proposals to improve clinical and microbiological diagnosis of severe infection

In the clinical management of infections, the primary objectives are to assess severity, which stratifies the risk of morbidity and mortality and facilitates rapid access to empirical treatment; to identify the source of infection, which facilitates more targeted empirical treatment and better control of the infectious process; and to determine the microbiological aetiology, which allows for the appropriate antimicrobial treatment. In at-risk patients, delays or inaccuracies in recognising a serious infection, its microbiological aetiology, and the septic focus have a significant impact on prognosis. Therefore, these constitute critical points for optimisation:

Achieving earlier and more widespread diagnosis of severe infection

Recent consensus documents in the field of emergency medicine have emphasised that, despite advances, the early recognition and optimisation of the initial management of severe infection remain significant areas for improvement in clinical practice [120]. The sensitivity of the best-validated current protocols and guidelines for diagnosing severe infection is poor, particularly in the most at-risk patients and those at high risk of harbouring AMR [120–126]. The combined use of clinical scales and biomarkers has proven useful in identifying severe infection, although with limitations in sensitivity and applicability in certain patient subgroups [127]. Identifying and controlling the source of infection more rapidly

Identifying the source of infection facilitates a more targeted empirical treatment using a syndromic approach, and sets a time limit for optimising control of the focus—apart from antibiotic therapy—when it requires additional measures (drainage or instrumental or surgical debridement, replacement or removal of catheters, tubes, prosthetic material, etc.), which, if delayed beyond the first 6 hours after diagnosis, has been shown to worsen the prognosis [142–145].

Proposals for more effective prescribing of antimicrobial therapy

The primary objective of this document is to promote therapeutic effectiveness—that is, prescribing treatments with the highest degree of anticipated effectiveness available for at-risk patients with severe infections. This is because the absence, delay, confusion, or complacency in the intention to design effective treatments is very common and has a significant impact on prognosis. In operational terms, therefore, the goal is to achieve a commitment to effectiveness at all levels that influence and contextualize decision-making regarding antimicrobial treatment: from the prescriber’s perspective to health administration, including expert institutional support (ASPs, guidelines, surveillance, etc) and training and research [177]. Immediate initiation of empirical antibiotic therapy

Delays in starting treatment following the diagnosis of a severe infection are critically important, as they significantly and rapidly worsen the prognosis, and, furthermore, are very common [121, 123, 178–186]. For this reason, the immediate initiation of antibiotic therapy in these patients must be an absolute priority, which must be implemented and maintained at all times.

Although the point from which the time to the start of antibiotic treatment should be measured is the onset of the infection and its symptoms, in practice this must be shifted to the time at which the patient first contacts the health services.

Even though this introduces an obvious bias, the accumulated evidence shows that in severe infections, the speed of treatment initiation, as a continuous variable, leads to significant benefits in terms of survival [121, 123, 178, 180–185]. Thus, in the case of bacterial infections, current evidence suggests that initial or empirical antibiotic therapy should be started: within 1 hour if there is septic shock or definite or probable sepsis; or within 3 hours if sepsis is possible and cannot be ruled out within that time [121, 123, 178, 180, 184].

In special populations, such as oncohaematological patients with febrile neutropenia, a time threshold of as little as 30 minutes has even been established for the initiation of effective antibiotic treatment [187]. In addition to prescribing empirical antibiotic therapy early, it is advisable to monitor and confirm its correct administration, both timely and appropriate, by nursing staff. The same applies to acute and severe viral infections, albeit with different timing. For example, in influenza (and recently in SARS-CoV-2 infection), although it is well established that initiating antiviral treatment in at-risk patients within the first 48 hours of symptom onset reduces morbidity and mortality, this is a very uncommon practice [186].

Selection of an appropriate, active and effective empirical antibiotic therapy

The rate of inappropriate treatment, defined as prescription of a treatment that is inactive or ineffective against the causative agent, in the management of severe infections is very high (15–50%), particularly if the infection is caused by MDR bacteria (>40%) [80, 84–85, 183, 188–205]; and this occurs in the most difficult-to-treat infections, even when guidelines are followed in 90% of cases (87% [84] – 92% [85]). Furthermore, this is a problem that is not improving over time [85, 183, 188, 197, 199, 201, 203–205].

In these infections, ineffective empirical treatment is associated with a significant increase in mortality [80, 84–85, 183, 188–197, 199–205]. Thus, it is imperative to improve the coverage of empirical treatment that we advocate in routine clinical practice, which should be above 90–95% in this patient group. This can only be achieved by providing, in guidelines and expert recommendations, treatments with a better PK/PD profile (i.e. better antibiotics, strategies and combinations), guidance on empirical treatment based on recent surveillance cultures of rectal, oropharyngeal or mucocutaneous swabs, previous colonisations or infection; and AI-based predictive tools such as clinical decision-making and antibiotic selection support systems, which have been shown to achieve greater therapeutic efficacy, a reduction in complications and significant decreases in mortality [85, 188, 200–206].

From efficacy to effectiveness

In patients with severe infection and high vulnerability, the quality of empirical prescribing should not be judged solely on its probability of microbiological coverage, but rather on its ability to elicit an early, robust and effective therapeutic response in a biologically fragile host.

In this context, the effectiveness of antibiotic therapy encompasses at least the following dimensions [207–214]: 1) spectrum or coverage of the likely pathogen; 2) intrinsic bactericidal capacity, derived from its PK/PD profile; 3) optimal timing and speed of initiation; 4) sufficient PK/PD exposure at the site of infection; 5) bactericidal activity in scenarios involving high inoculum, resistance, tolerance, persistence or high virulence, or an altered immune context in the host; 6) complementarity in the use of combinations – which determines the additive and synergistic effects of the combinations; and 7) feasibility of maintaining an effective, safe and sustainable therapeutic strategy during the first 48–72 hours. This perspective allows us to move beyond a binary interpretation of antibiotic therapy as ‘active’ or ‘inactive’ and shifts clinical reasoning towards a scale of probability of therapeutic success and effectiveness (Figure 3).

And furthermore, it would serve to explain, at least in part and aside from critical vulnerability, the mortality that occurs in patients receiving theoretically appropriate antibiotic therapy (active against the causative microbial agent) [215]. This is a form of mortality that is likely to be common, yet relatively unnoticed and insidious, stemming from the difficulty in recognising and acknowledging the existence of this gap between efficacy and effectiveness. There is a substantial body of literature, including high-quality evidence [216–219], that illustrates this problem.

Thus, the aim is to understand that each antibiotic exhibits these characteristics to varying degrees, generating different levels of efficacy that are distributed across a hierarchical, stratified scale. Consequently, among the antibiotics active against the causative agent, some are better or more beneficial than others for curing a specific infection in a specific patient and at a specific stage of the infection, such as hyperinflammatory vs immunoparalysis; microbiologically quiescent vs active rapid-growing, haematogenous vs compartmentalised, etc. [216–219].

Therefore, in this scenario, given the need to move from efficacy towards effectiveness and to ask ourselves how to achieve an effective treatment or the most active one possible from among those available for this type of patient, our proposals to improve effectiveness are as follows:

Selecting the best possible options from among the available antimicrobials

In general, the best possible options for achieving effectiveness in the most difficult and demanding cases are the new antibiotics, as they are the result of innovation and a design focused on continuous improvement. Consequently, compared to their predecessors, they tend to have more favourable PK/PD profiles, which, by enhancing their bactericidal and microbial eradication capabilities whilst minimising their ecological impact and toxicity, confer clinical advantages that have been demonstrated in certain difficult or complex scenarios (Figure 4) [213, 216–217, 220–357].

Theoretical advantages of new vs. classic antibiotics

Although the scientific evidence demonstrating their potential benefits is extensive and robust, access to this evidence is generally not straightforward. They are not prioritised either in research—because they require studies that are difficult to conduct—or in training—because they are applicable only to a select group of patients and are at odds with the predominant ecological and cost-containment objectives. However, the lack of top-quality evidence should not prevent access to a wealth of other scientific knowledge that helps us not only to guide but also to better prioritise the varying degrees of effectiveness of antibiotics across the multiple clinical scenarios encountered in clinical practice.

Under comparable conditions and in specific scenarios, some of these studies suggest that certain groups of antibiotics are relatively more effective than others. Examples of a sufficiently well-established hierarchy, albeit with varying degrees of strength, are shown in Table 2.

The ranking of antimicrobials should always be interpreted in context and not as indicating universal superiority. The relative effectiveness of an antibiotic can vary substantially depending on the infectious syndrome, control of the source of infection, the size of the bacterial inoculum, the degree of specific bacterial resistance present and, most importantly, penetration into the target compartment. Therefore, any proposal for therapeutic prioritisation should explicitly incorporate the site of infection and the anticipated exposure at that site. In other words, in complex infections, it is not enough to choose a microbiologically active antibiotic: it is essential to select the one that offers the best effective exposure at the site where the infection is occurring. [358–360].

It is therefore reasonable, in the case of severe and difficult-to-treat infections in the most at-risk patients, to consider that new antimicrobials may improve the effectiveness of treatment and that their selection may improve the prognosis of these infections (Figure 3).

Design the best possible strategy tailored to each individual case, based on optimising the PK/PD profile

The aim is to tailor treatment by individualising antibiotic dosages and regimens to meet the complex and demanding conditions of these patients, their specific infections, and the underlying pathogens.

Increasing doses, either per dose or by increasing the number of doses, depending on whether the antibiotic is dose- or time-dependent, prolonged or continuous infusions, initial loading doses, and pharmacological and microbiological monitoring of their effects are well-established optimisation strategies for a wide range of antibiotics in many infection models [361–369] (Figure 3).

PK/PD optimisation takes on even greater relevance in subgroups with particularly complex pharmacokinetics, such as critically ill patients with renal hyperfiltration, continuous renal replacement therapy, ECMO, obesity, severe hypoalbuminaemia, extensive burns, or massive expansion of the extracellular space. In these scenarios, standard dosing regimens are particularly vulnerable to under-exposure and, consequently, to treatment failure. Thus, ASPs focused on effectiveness must incorporate specific pathways for advanced pharmacotherapeutic validation, therapeutic monitoring where available, early review of doses (loading and subsequent) and infusion (standard, extended or continuous), and a structural role for the clinical pharmacist in the daily audit of highly complex treatments [370–372] (Figure 5).

The complexity of optimising antibiotic therapy for severe infections

PK/PD optimisation is a pharmacological issue and can therefore benefit significantly from the contribution of the pharmacy department. The ability to audit prescriptions from a PK/PD perspective, to provide real-time guidance on achieving pre-established and validated PK/PD targets, to monitor antimicrobial concentrations), to help minimise drug interactions and toxicities, and to make the necessary pharmacological adjustments in patients with comorbidities that alter antibiotic metabolism, and to rescue beta-lactam agents through a beta-lactam allergies delabelling programme offers significant opportunities for optimisation, just as the correlation of PK/PD parameters with the quantitative evolution of the bacterial load may also improve effectiveness. We must therefore recognise that pharmacy is a key strategic partner in the pursuit of effectiveness.

Consider the improvements in the PK/PD profile that combinations of antimicrobials can offer

Historically, combinations of antimicrobials have proven more effective than monotherapy in treating a range of ‘difficult-to-treat’ infections, includin HIV infection, hepatitis C, tuberculosis, brucellosis, Q fever, endocarditis, malaria, toxoplasmosis, etc. They have also proven effective in the most severe infections caused by multi-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [80, 85, 200, 202, 257, 345, 369, 373–394], multi-resistant Gram-positive bacteria [395–408] and fungi [409–412].

Combination therapy is a strategy of selective intensification in situations with a high risk of early treatment failure, such as septic shock, suspected MDR pathogens with high attributable mortality, persistent bacteraemia, incomplete or delayed control of the source of infection, infection with a high bacterial load, certain forms of profound immunosuppression, the presence of toxins and other virulence factors, etc. Within this framework, combination therapy can lead to improvements in efficacy (Figure 3) that may be critical in the initial treatment of the most complex or difficult-to-treat infections, even in the case of new antibiotics [313, 385–394]. This involves not only broader coverage, but also a faster reduction in the bacterial load, enhanced PK/PD, inhibition of toxin and superantigen synthesis, and prevention of resistant mutant subpopulations [188, 288, 413–420].

The drug interactions involved in combination therapy and their relationship with the mechanisms of microbial resistance are highly complex, have not been sufficiently explored, and give rise to uncertainties that call for caution [421–425]. Furthermore, they can lead to drug antagonism and increase the potential for toxicity and other undesirable drug interactions. However, there is a wealth of literature showing that combinations can hinder the emergence of resistant bacteria during antibiotic treatment [81, 354–355, 420, 425–429]: ‘Perhaps the most important use of combination chemotherapy is in attempts to suppress the emergence of resistance’ [354–355].

However, although we are operating in a context of unacceptably high mortality [80, 215] and assume that combination antibiotic therapy can offer clinical and ecological benefits, its uptake in clinical practice has been slow and insufficient, perhaps unduly hampered by the inertia generated by the paradigm linking antibiotic pressure to the development of AMR [80].

Its adoption could be facilitated by the assumption that every indication for combined antibiotic therapy should be subject to early re-evaluation and an explicit simplification strategy once microbiological and clinical uncertainty has diminished. Furthermore, it is necessary to encourage and maintain close scientific monitoring of the risks and benefits of this strategy.

Consider other pathogenic factors that influence the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatment

Other pathogenic factors, both microbial and host-related, in addition to AMR, can increase the risk of treatment failure, such as virulence, persistence, including in biofilms or ‘biological sanctuaries’, or immune dysfunction generated by the microbial agent and/or the host’s immunoregulatory genotype/phenotype, which may manifest as hyperinflammation, immunoparalysis, etc.

This is a real problem and, although it is an underexplored or insufficiently explored area [430–432], its influence on therapeutic effectiveness would compel us to give it greater consideration and to delve deeper into the selection of patients who might benefit from treatments better adapted to these types of factors.

On the one hand, we must pay greater attention to potential anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory adjuvant therapies, which combat hyperinflammation or immunoparalysis, or interfere with pathogenic processes, such as bismuth, gamma interferon, checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD1, etc. On the other hand, we must deepen our understanding of, and put into practice, how various antimicrobials address these virulence factors, such as the release of polysaccharides and other endotoxins; or better penetrate biofilms or sanctuaries and maintain their activity against persistent bacteria, or how they better adapt to the patient’s inflammatory or immunological profile [430–432].

Minimise the ecological impact as much as possible in every prescription

The emergence of AMR during exposure to antibiotics is their main adverse effect; it is cumulative in nature (it progresses easily and is very difficult to reverse), impacts prognosis in direct proportion to its presence, and is also very difficult to control. It is a highly complex and poorly understood issue, and we have few tools to study it, few targeted studies, little regulatory scrutiny in this regard, and insufficient institutional surveillance. Multiple variables interact in the generation and development of AMR even when their use is appropriate: microbial factors (both individual and ecological), host factors (immune system, microbiome, genotype, habits, comorbidities, etc), and antimicrobial therapy factors (drugs, PK/PD strategies, duration) [59, 343, 354–355, 425, 428–429, 433–447]. It is important to note that the use of antibiotics merely selects for, rather than generates, resistance genes or clones that must already be present, within an altered ecological context that allows for clonal amplification, and under prolonged selective pressure. Moreover, any oversimplification of the problem (for example, ‘more antibiotics = more resistance’ or ‘broad-spectrum antibiotics generate more resistance’) is inaccurate and prevents valid predictions.

Although the ecological impact does not determine initial clinical effectiveness, as it does affect future effectiveness but not current effectiveness, and managing it is complex and difficult, its significance is such that every prescription of antibiotics must include an assessment of its ecological impact and of the ability to prevent the emergence of resistance during treatment [354–355]. Such an assessment should also be given greater weight in scientific research, in the marketing of antibiotics and within the health administration, through ecological impact monitoring systems [354–355].

Therefore, in a scenario such as that outlined in this document, which focuses on the pursuit of effectiveness through the intensification of antibiotic pressure, it is necessary to address the challenge of whether maximising therapeutic efficacy could fuel or drive AMR.

Minimising the ecological impact is a more demanding objective than achieving a cure. Clinical cure is achieved with a certain level of antibiotic pressure (exceeding PK/PD targets centred on the MIC, which, as it is prolonged, selects for and allows the development of more resistant subpopulations (which would require greater antibiotic pressure, with PK/PD targets centred on the Mutation Preventive Concentration (MPC) for their eradication).

However, these two objectives need not be mutually exclusive. The strategy known as ‘front-loading’, which proposes the initial use of the most active and eradicative treatments (with better PK-PD outcomes, which are generally provided by last-line antibiotics, combinations and new strategies – including therapeutic alternatives with adjuvants, inhibitors, potentiators, new formulations, immunomodulators, phages, gene therapies, biomolecules, new products proposed by AI…), administered over very short periods (for this purpose, ideally less than 5 days, probably sufficient up to 7 days), could be a good strategy for reducing the ecological impact, as it would reduce the initial microbial inoculum more rapidly and shorten the duration of selective pressure, whilst also enabling the elimination of more bacterial subpopulations with a higher AMR burden.

Although this strategy lacks sufficient clinical evidence, it is conceptually appealing and biologically plausible, and is supported by considerable experimental evidence [428–429, 441–442, 444–445, 448–455] (Figure 6), which, as long as it does not contradict existing clinical evidence, could help us to make this concept more operational and incorporate it into our recommendations.

First smart: the “front loading” strategy can improve effectiveness and ecological impact. Partial data obtained from Paterson IK et al. [445]

We therefore propose that the restrictive nature of ecological control over prescribing practices should be adjusted, allowing for increased antibiotic pressure during the initial phase of treatment in this patient group. This would reduce the likelihood of prescribing suboptimal treatments which, paradoxically, could lead to greater pressure for the selection and spread of AMR and other ecological costs, since treatment failures lead to greater exposure to antibiotics and a greater progressive deterioration of the microbiota.

And since the selective pressure of antibiotic therapy, regardless of its complexity and difficult predictability, increases with the duration of exposure, we have better opportunities for ecological control than those affecting the choice of empirical treatment, such as the reductions in exposure provided by early adjustment or de-escalation, and the reduction in treatment duration [194, 445, 447, 456–475]. This type of high-intensity, short-course antibiotic regimen—which, as we have already noted, may have a favourable ecological impact—should be subject to a formal review after 24 hours and a second, decisive review after 48–72 hours, taking into account haemodynamic trends, control of the infection focus, microbiological results, biomarkers, toxicity and PK/PD plausibility. This would serve to adjust or de-escalate treatment, and also to detect treatment failure early and activate rescue strategies without delay (which are all the more effective and ecological the earlier they are implemented). Thus, adjustment or de-escalation and rescue represent two complementary expressions of the same principle: rapidly adjusting antimicrobial pressure to the evolving biological reality of the case. Complementarily, the judicious and individualised adjustment of the duration of antibiotic treatment for each patient and episode is another decisive factor in reducing its ecological impact [194, 445, 447, 456–475].

Consider rescue therapy at an earlier stage

It is important to monitor the efficacy of antibiotic treatment in this type of infection and patient and to define treatment failure (and the need for rescue therapy) within the first 48–72 hours of treatment [476]. In this patient group, the best definition of treatment failure is the absence of significant signs of improvement within the first 48–72 hours of treatment [194, 445, 449, 465–467, 476–479].

In patients who fail initial treatment and have multi-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (GNB), a delay (> 4–5 days) in prescribing rescue therapy with the best available antibiotic options is associated with significantly lower survival, 3–5 times lower [216, 285, 290, 302, 305, 308–312, 317].

It is therefore important to rapidly incorporate the best antibiotics and therapeutic strategies into the guidelines and protocols for empirical rescue treatment for at-risk patients with severe infections.

Assessing the importance of convenience in effectiveness

Investing in therapeutic convenience can improve the quality of our interventions and their outcomes. Therapeutic convenience should not be regarded as a secondary or merely logistical attribute, but as a clinical determinant of effectiveness, particularly during phases of care transition. The availability of simplified administration formulations, agents with a prolonged half-life or oral alternatives with high bioavailability can reduce delays, administration errors, loss of adherence, avoidable hospital stays and complications related to vascular access. In selected patients, these strategies allow for the maintenance of adequate therapeutic intensity beyond the initial critical period and facilitate models of shortened hospitalisation, outpatient continuity, and outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) with greater safety and less organisational friction [266–267, 480–484].

These new formulations reflect scientific and technical progress in pharmacological optimisation (representing the pinnacle of PK-PD optimisation); therefore, in addition to advantages in terms of safety, convenience, adherence, cost-effectiveness and quality of life, they have demonstrated benefits in clinical effectiveness [266–267, 485–489].

Give greater and more effective consideration to the benefits of innovation

In the face of the major problem posed by the rise in AMR and the trend in infection-related mortality, the WHO’s programme priorities—based on awareness-raising, surveillance, prevention and optimisation (of existing resources)—are not delivering the expected results [23–29]. There is a need to complement them with a more decisive push towards the incorporation of innovation that is adaptable, sustainable, anticipatory, parameterised, scalable and operational or usable, the benefits of which can be rapidly transferred to patients [490–494] (Figure 7).

Systematic surveillance of improvement initiatives

In order to achieve and consolidate improvements, it is necessary to incorporate systematic surveillance of optimisation initiatives. Monitoring is carried out on the basis of indicators, of which there are three types: 1) Structural, including the existence of the ASP and experts, antibiotic policies, AMR monitoring programmes, guidelines, educational programmes, etc.; 2) Process-related, including recording of antibiotic prescriptions, 48–72-hour review, adherence to ASP recommendations, guidelines or pre-authorisation processes, frequency of de-escalation, oral-to-IV sequencing, time to initiation of antibiotic therapy, culture sampling prior to treatment initiation, etc.; and 3) Outcome-related, including monitoring of antibiotic consumption and expenditure, adverse effects, and, most importantly, clinical outcomes: mortality, cure rates, hospital stays, readmissions) [51, 118–119, 510–511].

Since these are initiatives to improve clinical outcomes, the first requirement is the mortality registry, broken down by Health Areas and Infectious Syndromes, and the analysis of causes of mortality, through ASPs, Mortality committees, etc., and their relationship with antibiotic treatment, including timing, choice of empirical treatment, choice of targeted treatment, whether its efficacy has been assessed in a timely and appropriate manner and whether rescue treatment has been considered, patient vulnerability and the type of infection.

Furthermore, for every optimisation initiative that is successfully implemented, a specific indicator must be added—preferably one focused on clinical outcomes—that correlates with evidence demonstrating benefits in both individual and overall clinical health outcomes, and that enables monitoring, analysis and comparison, both over time within the centre itself, and with other healthcare centres through benchmarking. And this must be done despite the fact that the set of validated indicators for measuring effectiveness is limited [4, 51, 94, 117–119].

Conclusions

At a time of great concern regarding trends in morbidity and mortality from infections, and mindful of the limitations of our current approaches to combating them, this document sets out a structured programme of proposals aimed at improving, in the short and medium term, the survival of the most at-risk patients with severe infections (Figure 9).

Conceptual and operational summary of the ASP- COMPLEX (a complementary proposal aimed at ASPs and designed to improve the treatment and prognosis of serious infections in high-risk patients).

These are complementary proposals which, on the one hand, reinforce already established initiatives and, on the other, introduce some novel aspects to the approach to this complex problem. They focus on accelerating, standardising, and refining the clinical diagnosis of severe infection and its microbiological diagnosis and, above all, on improving the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatment, based on its immediate initiation with the greatest anticipated effectiveness available, and with an improved capacity to integrate innovation into all stages and processes.

This programme is designed to be implemented within the framework of the antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASP) (hence the name ASP-COMPLEX), and requires appropriate dissemination and surveillance policies, alongside a collaborative environment among the multiple stakeholders involved in delivering innovation (experts, stewardship teams, healthcare administration, pharmaceutical industry, etc.). In high-risk patients with severe infections, antimicrobial stewardship should not only aim to reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure, but also to ensure that every patient receives the earliest and most effective treatment possible.
