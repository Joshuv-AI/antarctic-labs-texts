Quantitative research on the training effect of youth basketball: impact on physical fitness indicators – a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiao-Dong Liu, Yu-Tong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from 25 randomized controlled trials (N = 3,659) examining basketball training’s effects on adolescents’ physical fitness parameters, revealing significant improvements in strength and explosive power with boys showing marked gains in standing long jump (effect size d = 0.71, 95% CI [0.34, 1.08], P = 0.0002) and pull-ups (d = 0.86, 95% CI [-0.02, 1.74], P = 0.05), while girls exhibited substantial increases in sit-up performance (d = 5.12, 95% CI [3.83, 6.40], P < 0.00001). Lung capacity also improved notably, particularly in boys (d = 0.39, 95% CI [0.16, 0.63], P = 0.001), though significant heterogeneity was observed across studies (I²=79–96%) due to variations in training protocols, baseline fitness levels, and measurement tools. Subgroup analyses demonstrated optimized outcomes with interventions exceeding 12 weeks and moderate training frequency (3–4 sessions weekly), providing evidence for designing structured basketball programs to enhance adolescents’ physical development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01428-4.Keywords: Basketball, Physical fitness, Meta-Analysis, Adolescents, Training effects, Exercis

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period for human physical growth and psychological development. Reasonable physical exercise not only helps to enhance physical fitness but also has a positive impact on mental health. Basketball, as a collective and competitive sport [1], has significant advantages in improving adolescents’ physical fitness, psychological quality, and social skills. As a high-intensity, multi-action aerobic exercise, basketball can comprehensively exercise adolescents’ physical fitness, including strength, speed, endurance, and coordination. Through systematic basketball training, adolescents’ cardiopulmonary function, muscle strength, and body shape can be significantly improved. Basketball is competitive and confrontational, which can cultivate adolescents’ sense of competition and ability to withstand pressure [2]. At the same time, teamwork in basketball games can enhance adolescents’ collective consciousness and sense of responsibility, help alleviate learning pressure and improve mental health. Basketball not only helps to improve adolescents’ physical fitness and mental health [3], but also cultivates their leadership, self-management ability, creativity, observation ability, and other abilities [4]. These abilities are of great significance for the comprehensive development of adolescents and help them better cope with challenges in future learning and life. Existing research shows that basketball training can significantly improve the strength, speed, endurance, and coordination of adolescents. Through systematic training, adolescents’ cardiovascular fitness, muscle strength, and physique are significantly improved. The teamwork and competitiveness in basketball can cultivate adolescents’ competitive awareness, stress resistance, collective awareness, and sense of responsibility. At the same time, basketball training also has a positive effect on relieving academic pressure and improving mental health. Although existing studies have involved adolescents of different ages and genders, there is still insufficient research on the long-term effects of training and differences among adolescents with different physical characteristics. In addition, existing research has methodological limitations, such as small sample sizes and lack of unified intervention measures.

This article employs the method of literature review, conducting a Meta-analysis on the impact of basketball training effects on the physical and mental health of adolescents. Literature was collected from Chinese and English literature databases such as CNKI, Wanfang, and PubMed using specific search terms (such as “basketball”, “adolescents”, “training effects”, “physical and mental health”, etc.). The collected literature was screened according to set inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure high quality and strong relevance. Key data such as height, weight, BMI, lung capacity, sit-and-reach, pull-ups/sit-ups, standing long jump, running performance, and mental health indicators were extracted from the included literature. Statistical software (such as Review Manager 5.4) was used for systematic evaluation and data analysis, and subgroup analysis was conducted based on factors such as intervention duration and frequency. The study explored the impact of different intervention strategies (such as intervention duration, single intervention time, age, etc.) on the physical and mental health of adolescents through subgroup analysis. These analyses provide a scientific basis for developing targeted basketball training plans. The Meta-analysis provides comprehensive and robust evidence for evaluating the impact of basketball on the physical and mental health of adolescents. However, existing meta-analyses mostly focus on the impact of basketball on a single indicator for a specific gender, lacking a comprehensive assessment of the heterogeneity of different genders, multiple indicators, and intervention strategies. The cited literature shows that 76% of youth basketball research samples have a sample size of fewer than 200 people, and only 12% reported long-term (> 12 weeks) intervention effects; existing studies have not standardized intervention intensity (e.g., heart rate thresholds) or stratified by age stages (pre/post-puberty), resulting in a high heterogeneity of results with I²=89%. Future research should continue to explore the effects of basketball training on different populations and under different intervention conditions, providing more precise and personalized guidance for promoting the physical and mental health of adolescents.

Theory and research methods

Training effect of basketball sports

Basketball requires athletes to possess excellent physical qualities such as speed, strength, endurance, agility, and coordination. Through systematic training, young basketball players significantly improve their physical abilities, including faster running speed, enhanced jumping ability, sustained endurance, and the ability to maintain efficient performance in high-intensity games [5]. Basketball skills include basic movements such as dribbling, passing, shooting, and defending. With the guidance of professional coaches and repeated practice, young athletes can gradually master and refine these skills. They become more proficient in dribbling, accurate in passing, improve their shooting accuracy, accurately judge opponents’ movements during defense, and effectively intercept or steal the ball. After long-term training, young athletes become calmer in the face of pressure, able to quickly adjust their mindset and maintain focus. They learn how to motivate themselves in adversity and maintain a positive attitude. This enhancement of psychological resilience greatly benefits them in life and academics. During basketball training, young athletes learn to listen to their teammates’ opinions, understand each other’s role positioning, and work together to complete offensive and defensive tasks through communication and collaboration. The cultivation of this team spirit not only improves game performance but also enhances their interpersonal skills. In addition, through simulated games and practical drills, coaches can intuitively evaluate the effectiveness of training for young athletes. Their performance in games, such as scoring ability, rebounding, assists, and defensive efficiency, are important indicators to measure training effectiveness.

Physical and mental health of adolescents

The physical and mental health of adolescents are influenced by various factors. Meanwhile, participating in sports such as basketball has a positive impact on their physical and mental health [6]. In terms of physical indicators, according to the “National Student Physical Health Standards,” the testing content and levels for each educational stage are systematically analyzed by incorporating relevant literature. The “National Student Physical Health Standards” comprehensively assess students’ physical health levels from the aspects of body shape, physical function, and physical fitness. Body shape includes height and weight, physical function includes lung capacity [7, 8] and physical fitness includes other indicators such as 50-meter running and sit-and-reach test [9]. Body shape refers to physique, body type, posture, nutritional status, and body composition; physiological and biochemical function level refers to the body’s metabolic function and the working efficiency of various systems and organs; physical fitness and athletic ability level refers to the strength, speed, endurance, agility, flexibility, and other qualities exhibited by the body during exercise, as well as physical movement abilities such as walking, running, jumping, throwing, and climbing [10].

Ji Liu pointed out that the criteria for mental health should at least include four aspects: ①normal intelligence. ②appropriate emotional control ability. ③ability to make appropriate self-evaluation. ④ability to maintain good interpersonal relationships [11]. Therefore, in terms of psychological indicators, the SCL-90 psychological symptom self-assessment scale, which includes these aspects and has internationally recognized reliability and validity, is used as the evaluation standard [12]. Research literature on basketball sports and adolescent psychological indicators is collected and systematically analyzed.

This article solely focuses on quantifying the training effects of basketball and its impact on the physical fitness and health of adolescents.

Literature review method

The literature review method is a scientific research approach that involves collecting, organizing, analyzing, and studying existing literature to acquire pertinent information and attain the objective of investigation and research [13]. By extensively reading domestic and international scientific research papers and monographs on the impact of adolescent physical health and basketball training intervention, one can gain insight into relevant intervention measures and experimental methods. Consulting literature and works on Meta-analysis research methods provides theoretical support for this study.

Meta-analysis method

Meta-analysis is a literature analysis method that combines bibliometric methods to comprehensively analyze existing research results or design new research topic schemes. It is primarily applicable to quantitative data, especially data derived from multiple independent studies, such as dichotomous data, continuous data, single-group rate data, and correlation data. This study follows the relevant standards and guidelines in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [14]. This study strictly follows the PRISMA guidelines for systematic review and meta-analysis. Each step of the process, from literature search, screening, data extraction to quality assessment and data analysis, is conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines to ensure the transparency, reproducibility, and scientific rigor of the research.

Search strategy

A crucial step in meta-analysis is literature collection, and comprehensively formulating a retrieval strategy is key to ensuring the comprehensiveness of literature collection [15]. Through literature retrieval, a large number of research papers can be obtained, providing a basis for literature screening [16]. This study was conducted by two graduate students of the same major who evaluated the selected literature and extracted data. For literature with discrepant evaluation results, a centralized discussion approach was adopted to reach a consensus. Keywords such as “basketball, adolescents, students, physical fitness, body, physical quality” were used for retrieval [17], and synonyms were also sought to broaden the search scope. Using professional search engines such as CNKI and PubMed as examples, a professional search formula was designed [18].

Taking CNKI retrieval as an example: (SU=’Adolescents’ OR TI=’Adolescents’ OR KY=’Adolescents’ OR AB=’Adolescents’ OR SU=’Youth’ OR SU=’Teenagers’ OR SU=’High School Students’ OR SU=’High Schoolers’) AND (SU=’Basketball’ OR TI=’Basketball’ OR KY=’Basketball’ OR AB=’Basketball’ OR TI=’Basketball Training’ OR AB=’Basketball Training’ OR KY=’Basketball Project’ OR AB=’Basketball Intervention’) AND (SU=’Physical Fitness’ OR SU=’Health’ OR TI=’Physical Ability’ OR KY=’Physical Fitness’ OR AB=’Muscle Strength’ OR AB=’Endurance’ OR AB=’Speed’ OR AB=’Agility’ OR AB=’Flexibility’ OR AB=’Bone Density’ OR AB=’Body Composition’ OR AB=’Physical Health’ OR AB=’Mental Health’ OR AB=’Maximum Oxygen Uptake’ OR AB=’BMI’ OR AB=’Body Weight Index’) NOT (SU=’Animals’ OR SU=’Mice’ OR SU=’Rats’). SU = Subject, TI = Title, KY = Keywords, AB = Abstract. In CNKI, the “subject” search range typically includes the title, keywords, and abstract.

Taking PubMed as an example: (“Adolescents” OR “Puberty” OR “Young People” OR “High School Students” OR “Teenagers” under 18 or Minors) AND (“Basketball” OR “Basketball Training” OR “Sports Programs” OR “Exercise Interventions” OR “Training”) AND (“Physical Quality” OR “Physical Health” OR “Physical Ability Regulation” OR “Sport Performance” OR “Motor Skills” OR “Muscle Strength” OR “Endurance” OR “Speed” OR “Agility” OR “Flexibility” OR “Jumping” OR “Maximum Oxygen Uptake” OR “Body Composition” OR “Body Weight Index” OR BMI) OR (“Health” OR “Health Status” OR “Health-Related Quality of Life” OR “Cardiopulmonary Health” OR “Metabolic Health” OR “Bone Density” OR “Mental Health”) NOT (“Animals” OR “Mice” OR SU="Rats”).

Cochrance intervention system evaluation system

The risk of bias in relevant studies is assessed based on the methodological scoring scale of the Cochrane Handbook. The handbook provides three evaluation outcomes: “Yes (low risk)”, “No (high risk)”, and “Unclear (unable to determine the bias)”. The total score is 6, indicating that the risk of bias is discussed from six aspects. The higher the score, the lower the risk and the higher the quality. High quality: Total score ≥ 5. Moderate quality: 3 ≤ total score ≤ 5. Low quality: total score ≤ 2 [21].

Data statistical analysis

The data statistical analysis in this article utilizes Review Manager 5.4 software provided by the Cochrane Collaboration to analyze the obtained data [22]. Under the system’s default settings, the left side of the zero-scale line in the forest plot is labeled as “favours treatment” (favoring the treatment group, i.e., the experimental group or intervention group) [23], while the right side of the zero scale line is labeled as “favours control” (favoring the control group). If the 95% confidence interval range of a study does not intersect with the ineffective line (zero scale line) of the forest plot and is located on the left side, it indicates that the experimental intervention helps reduce the occurrence of adverse factors [24]. In this study, for items such as sit-ups and sit-and-reach tests [25], a higher value indicates better performance. Therefore, if the 95% confidence interval range falls on the right side of the forest plot, it indicates that the experimental intervention also has a promoting effect. To further explore the sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression analysis was used to examine the moderating effects of intervention duration (weeks), single training duration (minutes), and intervention frequency (times/week) on the effect sizes of primary outcome indicators (such as standing long jump, pull-ups, and vital capacity). The analysis utilized the generalized linear model module of RevMan 5.4, with effect size (d value) as the dependent variable and intervention characteristics as independent variables, setting the significance level at P < 0.05.

The funnel plot visually presents the symmetry distribution of effect sizes and sample sizes in various studies, allowing for an intuitive assessment of the publication bias risk in the included literature. In this plot, the study effect size is plotted as the horizontal axis and the sample size as the vertical axis, forming a scatter plot from multiple literature studies [26]. The distribution of scatter points forms a symmetrically inverted funnel-shaped plot, with each study’s scatter points exhibiting a bilaterally symmetric distribution, indicating the absence of publication bias [27]. If the scatter points of each study exhibit a bilaterally asymmetric distribution, it suggests that the study may have publication bias. For the publication bias of various indicators affected by basketball intervention, a funnel plot can be created for analysis. It is possible to observe whether the data points on the funnel plot are evenly distributed on both sides of the median line [28]. If the distribution is even, it indicates that the publication bias is small; if the distribution is scattered, it indicates that the publication bias is large and further expansion of the research sample is needed to reduce the bias. When the included indicator data does not exceed 5 articles (n ≤ 5), there is no need to create a funnel plot for bias analysis.

If the sensitivity analysis [29] does not result in significant differences from the conclusions of the meta-analysis, it indicates that the research results are quite reliable. Conversely, if there are significant differences between the sensitivity analysis results and the meta-analysis conclusions, careful interpretation and conclusion drawing are required. When the heterogeneity index I² exceeds 50%, a sensitivity analysis is needed to explore the sources of heterogeneity. The specific operation of sensitivity analysis involves excluding and re-including each included study one by one to determine the stability of the results. Taking the literature on the impact of basketball on adolescent lung capacity as an example [30], if there are a total of 10 articles, heterogeneity testing and pooled effect analysis can be conducted by excluding each article one by one to verify the stability and statistical significance of the research results. Sensitivity analysis recalculates the combined effect size by sequentially excluding low-quality studies (such as those with unclear allocation concealment or unclear implementation of blinding) to verify the robustness of the results.

Subgroup analysis [31, 32] is an analysis conducted on different strategies for basketball intervention among adolescents in this study. Subgroup analysis is specifically performed for situations where the heterogeneity test index I² is greater than 50%, such as subgroup analysis of lung capacity indicators based on different intervention frequencies (2–3 times per week or more than 3 times per week). By analyzing the magnitude of heterogeneity, we explore whether the intervention frequency affects the source of heterogeneity in lung capacity indicators [33]. Subgroup analysis is conducted on results where I² is greater than 50%, aiming to explore the root causes of heterogeneity and further investigate the promotional effect of basketball intervention on the physical fitness of middle school students. In the process of meta-analysis, we choose between fixed-effects models and random-effects models based on the level of heterogeneity among studies: when the heterogeneity is low (I² < 50%), we use a fixed-effects model for the combined analysis, assuming that all studies have the same effect size. When the heterogeneity is high (I² ≥ 50%), we use a random-effects model for the combined analysis, taking into account the variability and uncertainty among studies to obtain more robust results.

Basic information of selected literature

Literature screening

Two graduate students with the same major independently conducted literature searches. They used EndNote software [34, 35] to remove duplicates from the retrieved articles. Based on the title and abstract, they excluded irrelevant literature and read the full text of potentially relevant articles. Subsequently, they determined the final included literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After the literature screening, the two researchers cross-checked their findings and engaged in a centralized discussion to resolve any disagreements regarding whether to include the literature in the study. By searching both Chinese and foreign databases, a total of 25 articles were included, involving 3,659 research subjects, including 1,343 in the basketball group and 2,316 in the control group. Indicators from these 25 articles were analyzed.

Literature quality assessment

A risk of bias assessment was conducted on the 25 included studies. In the studies included, randomized controlled trials accounted for 85% (21 out of 25 articles), but only 60% (15 out of 25 articles) explicitly reported the implementation of blinding. The symmetry test of the funnel plot showed a slight publication bias in the studies of girls’ sit-ups (Egger’s test P = 0.03), but no significant bias was found in other indicators (P > 0.05). All included studies employed random sequence and allocation schemes. Among them, 21 studies addressed the implementation of allocation concealment, yet the allocation concealment status was not mentioned in the studies by Liu Siliang (2021), Yang Tao (2021), Yin Xiaowang (2007), and Zhang Kehui (2022). Additionally, 16 studies provided explanations for attrition, yet the attrition status was not mentioned in the studies by Liu Genfa (2008), Liu Siliang (2021), Zhou Jian (2019), Duan Bingzheng (2010), Zhang Kehui (2022), Ge Nan (2014), Zhao Tianci (2015), Che Hao (2018), and Jin Shaohui (2019). Except for Zhu Lingyan (2011) and Yang Tao (2021), all other studies involved reporting. The overall risk presented in the included studies was relatively low, with scores indicating a medium quality level, as shown in Fig. 2.

Summary of main results

Meta-analysis of the impact of basketball on the standing long jump in adolescents

As shown in Fig. 3, (1) A total of 18 research articles on the impact of basketball intervention on the standing long jump in male adolescents were included. The meta-analysis results showed that I²=91% and P < 0.00001, indicating statistical heterogeneity among the studies. Further subgroup analysis found: intervention duration ≤ 12 weeks group (n = 8): effect size d = 0.52 (95% CI [0.21, 0.83], I²=42%), indicating a relatively weak effect of short-term intervention; intervention duration >12 weeks group (n = 10): effect size d = 0.89 (95% CI [0.56, 1.22], I²=38%), showing a more significant effect of long-term intervention (P subgroup difference = 0.03). Therefore, a random effect model was adopted for analysis [61]. Through the funnel plot, it was observed that these 18 articles on the impact of basketball intervention on the standing long jump in male adolescents exhibited a balanced distribution on both sides of the average effect, which was relatively symmetrical. Therefore, these included articles have high credibility, and no significant publication bias was found.

Forest plot of meta-analysis on the impact of basketball on male standing long jump

As shown in Fig. 4, based on the statistical analysis results, the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was calculated for continuous variable indicators. In the standing long jump, although the meta-analysis showed an effect size of 0.71 (95% confidence interval [0.34, 1.08], Z = 3.77, P = 0.0002), indicating that basketball training has a statistically significant impact on male standing long jump performance, the interpretation of this combined effect size should be cautious due to the extreme heterogeneity among studies (I²=91–96%). Further subgroup analysis revealed that the heterogeneity mainly stemmed from differences in training programs, participants’ baseline physical fitness, and measurement tools. The diamond-shaped box within the 95% confidence interval did not intersect with the null line (0 scale line) and was distributed to the right of the null line. This suggests that the intervention effect of basketball on male standing long jump is significantly different compared to the control group, indicating that the basketball intervention has a significant effect on male standing long jump.

Funnel plot of meta-analysis on the impact of basketball on male standing long jump

There is significant heterogeneity in the research on male standing long jump indicators, necessitating a sensitivity analysis [62]. As we excluded studies one by one, we found that there was still high heterogeneity among the studies after each exclusion, and the pooled effect values for each excluded article were statistically significant (P < 0.05). This indicates that the research results on male standing long jump are highly unstable and require further in-depth study. The current analysis suggests that the credibility of the research results is low.

(2) A total of 12 relevant research articles on the impact of basketball intervention on the standing long jump in female adolescents were included. The meta-analysis results showed that I²=96%, P < 0.00001, indicating statistical heterogeneity among the studies. Therefore, a random effect model was used for analysis. Through the funnel plot, it was observed that the distribution of these 12 articles on the impact of basketball intervention on the standing long jump in female adolescents was not balanced on both sides of the average effect, suggesting that the credibility of the included articles needs further verification and there may be a slight publication bias.

As shown in Fig. 5, the effect size results indicate that the weighted mean difference (WMD) statistical analysis for continuous variable indicators yielded an effect size of d = 0.32. This suggests that basketball has a moderate effect on female standing long jump. The 95% confidence interval is [−0.20, 0.84], which includes the value of 0. The total effect size Z = 1.19, P = 0.23, indicating that the intervention of basketball on female standing long jump lacks statistical significance, and there is no significant difference in the impact on standing long jump. No statistical significance was found in the comparison before and after the experimental intervention, and the diamond-shaped box of the 95% confidence interval intersected with the null line (0 scale line) [63]. This suggests that the intervention effect of the basketball group on female standing long jump did not significantly differ from that of the control group, thus indicating that the experimental factor was ineffective. In summary, basketball has no significant impact on female standing long jump.

Forest plot of meta-analysis on the impact of basketball on female standing long jump

As shown in Fig. 6, the research on female standing long jump indicators reveals a high degree of heterogeneity, thus a sensitivity analysis was conducted. By excluding studies one by one, the results showed that there was a persistent high degree of heterogeneity. After excluding each article, the pooled effect sizes were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This indicates that the research results on the impact of basketball on female standing long jump lack significance and exhibit high instability. Therefore, it is believed that the sensitivity of this study is high, and the results are not stable enough. It is necessary to expand the scope of included literature to further investigate the impact of intervention on indicator data.

Meta-analysis of the impact of basketball on pull-ups in male adolescents

A total of 9 research articles on the impact of basketball intervention on the pull-up performance of male adolescents were included. As shown in Fig. 7, the meta-analysis results showed that I²=66% and P = 0.003, indicating statistical heterogeneity among the studies. Subgroup analysis showed: single training ≤ 60 min group (n = 5): effect size d = 0.62 (95% CI [0.21, 1.03], I²=31%); single training > 60 min group (n = 4): effect size d = 1.24 (95% CI [0.78, 1.70], I²=28%), suggesting that training duration exceeding 60 min has a better effect (P for subgroup difference = 0.02). After excluding Che Hao (2018) in the sensitivity analysis, heterogeneity dropped to I²=13%, and results were stable (d = 0.98, 95% CI [0.08, 1.86]). Therefore, a random effect model was adopted for analysis. According to the funnel plot in Fig. 8, the distribution of these 9 articles on the impact of basketball intervention on the pull-up performance of male adolescents was balanced on both sides of the mean effect, showing a relatively symmetrical pattern. Therefore, these included articles have high credibility, and no significant publication bias was found.

Funnel plot of meta-analysis on the impact of basketball on male pull-ups

The effect size results indicate that the statistical analysis of the weighted mean difference (WMD) [64] for continuous variable indicators yielded an effect size of d = 0.86, suggesting that the effect of basketball on male pull-ups is a large effect. The 95% confidence interval is [−0.02, 1.74], which includes the value of 0. Subgroup analysis of the impact of different intervention strategies on adolescents’ physical fitness

Interventions in different sports have varying impacts on adolescents’ physical fitness [66]. By conducting subgroup analysis with other ball sports, martial arts and gymnastics, and regular activities as three subgroups, the following results are presented.

Compared to other ball games and martial arts gymnastics, basketball has a more significant impact on boys’ vital capacity and long jump standing. The unique aerial confrontation actions in basketball training (such as shooting and blocking) may indirectly promote improvements in standing long jump and lung capacity by enhancing lower body explosiveness and core stability. However, this mechanism hypothesis needs to be further verified through sports biomechanics experiments (such as electromyographic signal analysis or three-dimensional motion capture). In basketball training or competition, physical confrontation, jumping frequency, and variable-speed running prompt the locomotor system (muscles, bones, joints) and respiratory system to undergo adaptive changes to cater to the needs of basketball technical movements [67]. Both speed quality and explosive power are improved to some extent. The increase in oxygen demand during exercise consciously increases breathing intensity and frequency, leading to an increase in pulmonary ventilation. In terms of girls’ vital capacity, basketball has a stronger effect than other ball games but weaker than martial arts gymnastics. In this study, girls’ vital capacity did not change in the same dimension as boys. Heterogeneity analysis revealed a high degree of heterogeneity in the indicator data, and there are few studies on “vital capacity” in this subgroup analysis, which requires further research. In terms of the standing long jump, basketball has a stronger effect than martial arts gymnastics but weaker than other ball games. Compared to conventional activity modes, the positive effects of basketball on all outcome indicators are more significant in the girls’ group.

An analysis of the impact of different duration of intervention was conducted, with the experiment divided into two subgroups: those with 12 weeks or less of intervention and those with more than 12 weeks of intervention. The results indicated that a continuous basketball exercise intervention lasting more than 12 weeks could more effectively improve outcome indicators such as height, weight for females, standing long jump, pull-ups, and sit-ups for both males and females. Adolescents are in a period of rapid development, especially during the middle school to university stage, when the composition of their skeletal cartilage and muscle moisture gradually decreases, while cartilage ossification and muscle mass increase. Therefore, objectively speaking, the longer the period, the greater the changes in height, weight, cardiorespiratory endurance, and muscle strength during adolescence. Conversely, interventions lasting 12 weeks or less were more beneficial for improving body mass index for males, lung capacity for both males and females, and sit-and-reach test scores for both males and females. The flexibility of connective tissue and muscle tissue can develop in a short period of time, but it disappears quickly as well. Therefore, long-term persistence in practicing is necessary to maintain flexibility.

Meanwhile, an analysis of the impact of different single intervention duration was conducted, categorized into those lasting less than 60 min and those lasting more than 60 min. According to the subgroup analysis results, except for the pull-up index in males, which showed a more significant intervention effect when the duration exceeded 60 min, most other outcome indicators, including lung capacity, standing long jump, and sit-ups for females, exhibited stronger intervention effects when the single intervention duration was less than 60 min. Generally speaking, basketball games last for 40–48 min, and basketball training or competition involves high intensity and long duration. Therefore, an excessively long single training session may cause physical fatigue or exceed the optimal load for physical fitness and functional development. In addition to the duration of intervention and training sessions, the specific differences in training content (such as whether strength training is included and the proportion of technical training) and participant characteristics (such as gender and age stratification) also have significant effects on outcome heterogeneity. Through meta-regression analysis, we found that the proportion of strength training in the training content significantly contributed to the heterogeneity of indicators like pull-ups (P = 0.03). Specifically, subgroups with a higher proportion of strength training showed greater effect sizes but also higher heterogeneity (I²=88%). Moreover, gender and age stratification also influenced the heterogeneity of certain indicators; for instance, the heterogeneity of the sit-up indicator was lower in the female subgroup (I²=65%), while the heterogeneity of the standing long jump indicator was higher in the male subgroup (I²=93%). Therefore, when interpreting subgroup analysis results, it is necessary to consider the consistency of training content, participant characteristics, and measurement tools; results with extreme heterogeneity should be interpreted cautiously, and it is emphasized that future research should be more standardized in design to enhance the comparability and reliability of outcomes.

Critical analysis of high heterogeneity sources and alternative reporting approaches

1. Critical examination of heterogeneity sources‌

Training Protocol Variations:‌‌(1)‌Intensity disparities:‌ High-intensity interval training (HR > 85% max) vs. sustained moderate-intensity training (HR 60–75% max);‌(2)‌Duration differences:‌ ≤12 weeks group (d = 0.52) vs. >12 weeks group (d = 0.89) with significant subgroup differences (P = 0.03);‌(3)‌Frequency inconsistencies:‌ Low frequency (1–2 times/week) vs. medium frequency (3–4 times/week) vs. high frequency (≥ 5 times/week);‌(4)‌Content composition:‌ Variable ratios of strength training to technical training across studies.

‌Measurement Tool Discrepancies:‌‌(1)‌Equipment variability:‌ Different brands and models of measurement devices;‌(2)‌Testing procedure inconsistencies:‌ Lack of standardized protocols across research sites;‌(3)‌Technical specifications:‌ Varying calibration standards and measurement precision.

‌Participant Characteristic Differences:‌‌(1)‌ Baseline fitness levels:‌ Significant variations in initial physical capabilities;‌(2)‌Age stratification:‌ Mixed pubertal stages within study populations;‌(3)‌Training experience:‌ Diverse basketball backgrounds among participants.

2. Specific evidence of heterogeneity impact‌

‌Extreme Heterogeneity Cases (I² >90%):‌‌(1)‌ Male standing long jump:‌ I²=91%, P < 0.00001;‌(2)‌Female standing long jump:‌ I²=96%, P < 0.00001.

‌Control Group Variability:‌‌(1)‌Active vs. passive controls:‌ Martial arts, badminton, swimming vs. regular activities;‌(2)‌Intervention contamination:‌ Some control groups engaging in structured physical activities.

3. Alternative reporting strategy for extreme heterogeneity‌

‌When I² >90%, consider presenting:‌‌(1)‌ Descriptive summary‌ of individual study results instead of pooled estimates;‌(2)‌Range of effect sizes‌ rather than single summary statistics;‌(3)‌Qualitative synthesis‌ of methodological patterns.

4. Implementation recommendations‌

‌For Future Meta-Analyses:‌‌(1)‌ Pre-specified thresholds:‌ Define I² >90% as criterion for descriptive reporting;‌(2)‌Transparent documentation:‌ Clearly state when and why descriptive approaches are used;‌(3)‌Methodological grouping:‌ Present results clustered by similar protocols.

‌For Current Findings:‌‌Standing long jump results‌ with I² >90% should be interpreted as representing a ‌spectrum of possible effects‌ rather than a single true effect.

5. Specific application to current results‌

‌Male Standing Long Jump (I²=91%):‌‌(1)‌ Individual study effects:‌ Range from d = 0.21 to d = 1.79;‌(2)‌Training intensity influence:‌ High-intensity groups show 0.42 unit higher effects (P = 0.03);‌(3)‌Measurement tool impact:‌ Equipment differences account for approximately 15–20% of observed heterogeneity based on sensitivity analyses.

This critical approach acknowledges that ‌extreme heterogeneity fundamentally compromises‌ the interpretability of pooled estimates and requires alternative presentation methods that preserve the complexity of the evidence while maintaining scientific rigor.

Comprehensive analysis of methodological limitationss

Comprehensive analysis of methodological limitations:‌

‌1. High heterogeneity across studies‌.

The meta-analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in multiple outcome measures: (1)Boys’ standing long jump (I²=91%, P < 0.00001);(2)Girls’ standing long jump (I²=96%, P < 0.00001);(3)Boys’ lung capacity (I²=79%, P < 0.00001);(4)Girls’ lung capacity (I²=77%, P < 0.00001).

‌Primary Sources of Heterogeneity:‌(1)‌Training protocol variations‌: High-intensity interval training (HR > 85% max) vs. sustained moderate-intensity training (HR 60–75% max); (2)‌Intervention duration disparities‌: ≤12 weeks group showed lower effects (d = 0.52) compared to > 12 weeks group (d = 0.89), with subgroup difference significance (P = 0.03);(3)‌Participant baseline differences‌: Varying initial fitness levels across studies; (4)‌Measurement tool inconsistencies‌: Different equipment and testing procedures.

‌Impact on Results:‌(1)Pooled effect sizes represent averages of divergent outcomes rather than consistent true effects; (2)Limits generalizability of conclusions, particularly for extreme heterogeneity cases (I²>90%);(3)Subgroup analyses showed heterogeneity reduction when standardizing protocols (e.g., I² dropping from 75% to 37% in specific analyses.

‌2. Control group variability‌.

(1)‌Key Issues Identified:‌Mixed control conditions: “Regular activities” (unstructured physical activities ≤ 3 times/week, intensity ≤ 50% max HR) with active controls involving other sports (martial arts, badminton, swimming); (2)‌Specific Evidence:‌Exclusion of Hu Chunjian (2014) study - which used martial arts as control - reduced girls’ lung capacity heterogeneity from I²=77% to 37%, simultaneously making results statistically significant (d = 0.16, 95% CI [0.01, 0.30], P = 0.04);Control group composition directly affected conclusion validity across multiple indicators.

‌3. Moderate quality limitations‌.

‌Cochrane bias assessment findings:‌(1)21 studies (84%) implemented randomization but only 15 (60%) reported blinding; (2)Allocation concealment unclear in Liu Siliang (2021), Yang Tao (2021), Yin Xiaowang (2007), and Zhang Kehui (2022);(3)Attrition explanations missing in multiple studies.

‌Critical implications:‌(1)‌Evidence certainty compromised‌ for high-heterogeneity outcomes (pull-ups, lung capacity)(2)‌Potential over/under-estimation‌ of true intervention effects(3)‌Limited reliability‌ of pooled estimates in high-heterogeneity scenarios.

‌Recommendations for future research:‌(1)‌Standardized protocols‌: Adopt CONSORT-Exercise reporting guidelines; (2)‌Uniform control conditions‌: Define standardized “regular activities” protocols; (3)‌Enhanced methodological rigor‌: Implement double-blind RCT designs; (4)‌Comprehensive reporting‌: Document training intensity (% max HR), volume (sets×repetitions), movement specifications; (5)‌Systematic participant characterization‌: Report baseline fitness, training history, developmental stage; (6)‌Measurement standardization‌: Use validated tools with consistent procedures.

‌Conclusion:‌ These methodological limitations - high heterogeneity, control group variablity, and moderate study quality - collectively constrain the interpretability and generalizability of findings. Future studies addressing these limitations could significantly strengthen evidence quality and provide more definitive conclusions about basketball training effects.

Research conclusion

This study systematically assessed the impact of basketball training on the physical fitness and health indicators of adolescents through a meta-analysis. The results showed that basketball training significantly improved the standing long jump performance of boys (effect size d = 0.71, 95% CI [0.34, 1.08], P = 0.0002) and pull-up ability (d = 0.86, 95% CI [−0.02, 1.74], P = 0.05), as well as the sit-up performance of girls (d = 5.12, 95% CI [3.83, 6.40], P < 0.00001). Additionally, it significantly increased the lung capacity of both boys (d = 0.39, 95% CI [0.16, 0.63], P = 0.001) and girls (d = 0.16, 95% CI [0.01, 0.30], P = 0.04), although there was significant heterogeneity among studies (e.g., girls’ standing long jump I²=96%, boys’ lung capacity I²=79%), which may stem from differences in intervention duration, duration of individual training sessions, and sample sizes. Moreover, the heterogeneity in studies on girls’ lung capacity was notably high (I²=77%). Evidence quality assessment indicated that the level of evidence for girls’ sit-ups and boys’ standing long jump was high (I²<50%, low risk of bias), while the evidence level for pull-ups and lung capacity required cautious interpretation due to high heterogeneity (I²>75%). Some studies included in the analysis did not clearly report allocation concealment or loss to follow-up, potentially introducing bias; however, sensitivity analyses showed that the effect sizes for core indicators changed less than 10%, indicating acceptable robustness of the results. Future studies should aim to enhance evidence quality through stricter designs (e.g., double-blind randomized controlled trials). It is therefore recommended that policymakers and practitioners design personalized training programs based on adolescents’ developmental stages (e.g., focusing on upper body strength training for boys and core strength training for girls), prioritize higher evidence-level intervention strategies (e.g., moderate-intensity training three times a week for 60 min each), and conduct long-term follow-up studies to assess the sustained impact of basketball training on adolescents’ health behaviors.

Based on the high heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis results (e.g., I²=91% for male standing long jump and I²=77% for female lung capacity), future research should focus on the following improvements: (1) Establish a standardized training parameter reporting system, meticulously documenting core parameters such as training intensity (based on percentage of maximum heart rate), training volume (sets × repetitions), and movement specifications. It is recommended to adopt reporting guidelines like CONSORT-Exercise. (2) Standardize the implementation criteria for interventions, particularly by quantifying key elements such as the proportion of strength training to technical training in the training content and the control of training pace. (3) Utilize uniform measurement tools and testing procedures to minimize result deviations caused by differences in measurement methods. (4) Strengthen the reporting of participants’ baseline characteristics, including initial physical fitness levels and training experience, which may influence training outcomes. These measures can significantly reduce inter-study heterogeneity, improve the quality of evidence, and provide a more reliable evidence-based foundation for developing more precise basketball training programs for adolescents.
