Reconstructing magmatic histories with 3D diffusion modeling of complex crystals
Authors: Adrien J. Mourey, Euan J. F. Mutch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magmatic stopwatches that model 1D diffusion in minerals and melts neglect complex 3D structures common in natural crystals, leading to potentially inaccurate temporal constraints on magmatic processes such as storage, ascent, and volatile transfer. Here we combine X-ray microtomography reconstructions of morphologically diverse olivine crystals with 3D finite element diffusion models to develop an integrated magmatic history for the 1820 Keanakāko’i eruption of Kīlauea (Hawai’i). Fe-Mg diffusion in olivine indicates decadal storage before pre-eruptive mixing of days to weeks. Melt inclusions infer decompression rates of 1 x 10−4 to 6.3 x 10−3 MPa/s and maximum syn-eruptive cooling rates of 7.5 to 15 ∘C/s, as determined by diffusive water loss and Mg diffusion, respectively. Our approach offers a transferable framework to constrain the timescales of volatile degassing and magmatic processes across diverse tectonic settings on Earth and beyond, thereby enhancing our understanding of magma evolution, eruption dynamics, and volcanic hazards.

Introduction

Minerals are natural archives that preserve chemical, physical, and thermal histories in magmatic environments on Earth and other planetary bodies. Dynamic processes in magma reservoirs, such as magma transport, mixing, storage1–10, crystal dissolution11,12, convection13, assimilation3,14, degassing, decompression15–21, and cooling20,22,23 introduce disequilibrium in minerals and produce compositional gradients which diffusively relax over time as the system strives to reach chemical equilibrium. The rates of chemical diffusion and crystal residence time together dictate the extent to which diffusive equilibrium can be reached. In regions of protracted crystal storage, such as crystal mush zones, diffusion may be sufficiently fast for some elements to generate chemically homogeneous crystal cores. For processes in which disequilibrium persist, we can extract timescale information by modeling the thermally activated chemical diffusion, a method known as diffusion chronometry. This approach has become highly popular because it can be applied to volcanic systems irrespective of age, and can track short timescales (hours to years for fast-diffusing elements like hydrogen24 and Fe-Mg in olivine25) otherwise inaccessible to other methods such as short-lived radionuclides1,26. Timescales obtained using diffusion chronometry have been successfully combined with volcano monitoring time series (seismicity, deformation, gas) or observational data (e.g., ash emissions) to unravel subvolcanic architectures and illuminate causative relations that improve eruption forecasts2,9,27–32. Current diffusion chronometry approaches are often limited to simplified 1D models that may fail to capture complex crystal geometries, melt inclusion (MI) networks, and thermal histories associated with natural systems33–37.

As technology advances, there is greater recognition of the importance of considering realistic 3D crystal morphologies in diffusion modeling to estimate the timescales of magmatic processes12,38–40. 3D multiphase finite element models (FEM) have recently been developed to simulate diffusion in both olivine host and MI networks using idealized morphologies21. This has provided important insights into how water is lost from olivine-hosted MIs during magma ascent, and how MIs shield and interact with each other. Micro-computed tomography (X-μCT) has also emerged as an important technology for reconstructing complex mineral and inclusion geometries in 3D38,41,42. We are now approaching the frontier in which X-μCT data can be integrated with 3D diffusion modeling approaches to extract timescales from complex natural crystals. Recent work on garnet diffusion43,44 has highlighted the critical influence of realistic 3D grain geometries and matrix connectivity on diffusion rates and compositional zoning preservation, demonstrating significant compositional re-equilibration in sub-euhedral grains connected to the matrix compared to euhedral grains where zoning is largely preserved. However, this does not account for the large diversity in crystal and MI geometries found in olivine that forms due to variations in cooling rate20, growth rates during undercooling45–47, nucleation rate or number density47, and different magmatic histories following magma storage48, settling41, and compaction49.

Here, we combined X-μCT of natural diverse and complex olivine crystals of the 1820 Keanakāko’i eruption of Kīlauea with a 3D finite element multiphase diffusion model of olivine and olivine-hosted MIs21 (Fig. 1a–c), offering a unified framework to interpret magmatic archives in eruptive products. The model was adapted to estimate magma mixing and storage timescales from Fe-Mg zoning in olivine (five crystals), cooling rates from Mg zoning in MI (three crystals), and to investigate how magma ascent influences water zoning patterns in olivine and MI (three crystals). We assumed that diffusion-based signatures in olivine and MIs record overlapping yet distinct timescales and processes, which, when modeled together in realistic 3D geometries, provide a comprehensive understanding of magmatic dynamics during eruptive events. By applying this integrated modeling approach to these distinct magmatic processes, we have unraveled the complexities of magma evolution at different stages of its journey, from magma storage and mixing to ascent and eruption. Results

Magmatic processes investigated in the 1820 Kīlauea eruption

We investigated magmatic processes occurring at varying depths and timescales, including magma mixing at shallow crustal levels (~ 1 to 5 km depth), magma ascent through the conduit during the final hundreds of meters before eruption, and syn-eruptive cooling. Our case study was the well-characterized 1820 C.E. golden pumice eruption (unit K1) at Kīlauea volcano41,50–54. This eruption, part of the Keanakāko’i Tephra eruptive period (1500–1820 C.E.), was characterized by intense lava fountains55, which deposited primarily rapidly cooled golden pumice with minor lithic fragments50. All crystals and MIs analyzed derive from glassy pyroclasts, weakly olivine-phyric golden pumice (unit K1) from the main fall deposit of the 1820 high-fountaining phase, with bulk composition ~ 11.4 wt.% MgO and glass composition ~ 6.8–9.1 wt.% MgO54. The pyroclasts have a glassy texture and contain multiple populations of olivine crystals from different parts of the magma plumbing system, including olivine in equilibrium with a shallow reservoir at 0–2 km depth with compositions around Fo80 (where Fo is the forsterite component, Fo = Mg/[Mg + Fe] in mol.%), a hybrid reservoir with Fo83−86, and a deeper crustal reservoir with Fo86−90, similar to the present-day South Caldera reservoir depth at 3–5 km below sea level51–53. These olivine populations crystallized in crustal magmatic reservoirs with well-constrained intensive parameters (P, T, fO2), enabling detailed diffusion modeling. Specifically, the 1820 Keanakāko’i eruption has well-constrained melt temperature (1,210 ± 15 ∘C in the shallow Fo83−86 reservoir and 1230 ± 15 ∘C in the deeper one at 3 to 5 km depth with Fo86−90, based on MgO-in-melt thermometry47,53), pressure (40 ± 30 MPa for skeletal olivine with Fo83−86 and 74 ± 10 MPa for euhedral olivine clusters53), and oxygen fugacity (estimated to track the QFM buffer56,57). The olivine cargo of that eruption is also quite well studied with two main olivine populations51–53. Olivine crystals show a variety of textures, such as well-faceted single grains, aggregates, and skeletal growth shapes, reflecting their diverse origins and cooling or crystallization histories41,53. Five morphologically diverse olivines were selected (three skeletal olivines and two polyhedral clusters; with a Fo80−89 composition) for X-μCT analysis. Skeletal olivines have minor Fe-Mg zoning with Fo content variations from core to rim usually < 3 Fo unit (~ Fo83−86). In contrast, polyhedral olivine clusters can have more pronounced Fo zoning with Focore−rim variations up to 8 Fo units51 (core with Fo86−90; Fig. 1d). The polyhedral olivine clusters investigated all appeared normally zoned in Fo (Fo86−89.5). Some of them did not preserve their central plateaus, indicating partial re-equilibration of their Fe-Mg zoning (Fig. 1d). The zoning patterns and partial core re-equilibration signatures are diagnostic of diffusive re-equilibration following magma mixing, rather than growth-related features. This interpretation is supported by comparisons of profile widths along different crystallographic orientations, which show diffusion distances consistent with direction-dependent diffusivities25. Diffusion profiles in olivine are typically wider parallel to the c-axis than along the a- or b-axes (anisotropic pattern of Fe-Mg gradient widths), consistent with diffusion-controlled rather than growth-controlled profiles51.

Combining X-μCT imaging with 3D diffusion modeling to refine magma storage and mixing timescales

We combined X-μCT and 3D FEM to resolve magma mixing timescales from Fe-Mg diffusion. The rate and pattern of Fe-Mg diffusion in olivine are influenced by several factors that are incorporated in the FEM, including temperature, pressure, oxygen fugacity, and compositional zoning25. Crystals were scanned using X-μCT41 and segmented to create accurate surface meshes of olivine and MIs in the MeshLab software58 (Fig. 1a). These meshes were converted to volume representations, refined, and re-oriented in Gmsh59, and incorporated to a 3D FEM with FEniCS60. Models assume an initial uniform Fo89.5 composition (or equivalent Fe/Mg in moles = 0.118) across the entire crystal. The MIs are assumed to be initially in equilibrium with the olivine host whose composition are dictated by the Fe2+-Mg exchange coefficient KD = 0.33547. The partition coefficient is also used to conserve mass and flux between the MI and olivine host. Dirichlet boundary conditions fixed to the measured rim composition from electron probe profiles were applied to the exterior of the crystal. Gradients in Fe-Mg and their associated diffusion time were extracted from sections of the 3D FEM (Fig. 1b, c). Comparison of the resulting 3D diffusion timescales with 1D models highlights substantial differences (Fig. 1c), as realistic mesh-based 3D models indicate that the olivine clusters re-equilibrated their Fe-Mg zoning for approximately 14 years at 1230 ∘C, while the equivalent 1D solution estimates a much longer duration (>30 years; Fig. 1c). A key insight from the 3D FEM is the asymmetry of Fe-Mg gradients across different crystal faces (Fig. 1b, c). While both 1D and 3D best-fit models produce consistent diffusion timescales when the Fe-Mg diffusion profile is shorter than a year, the agreement breaks down at longer durations. The divergence for longer timescales arises due to the influence of crystal morphology in the 3D simulations, which the 1D approach cannot capture. Clear disagreement between 1D and 3D emerges even for short diffusion times (<1 year) in regions where multiple diffusion fronts are present (such as toward point “b” in the “a-b” profile in Fig. 1c). Diffusion is physically enhanced near crystal boundaries and adjacent faces due to steeper concentration gradients and increased effective surface area for mass flux39, leading to observable zonation asymmetries (Fig. 1c). These complex patterns are lost in 1D representations, which average the profile along a straight line and fail to capture geometric effects. It is important to note, however, that while 3D diffusion modeling is necessary for accurately estimating long-term core re-equilibration within irregular crystal geometries, 1D models may remain appropriate for assessing rapid, final mixing events that predominantly affect rim gradients where diffusion distances are limited. The choice between 1D and 3D modeling also depends critically on temperature and diffusivity, which vary according to both the diffusion system (that is, the specific element diffusing) and the host mineral. In our study, the final magma mixing event lasted a few days to weeks (Fig. 1d and supplementary material) and was effectively modeled using a 1D model with Neumann boundary conditions applied to the crystal interior, ensuring no flux across these boundaries. Our X-μCT and 3D FEM methodology enables robust quantification of complex multistage diffusion histories in olivine crystals12,51.

Decompression rates from melt inclusion water contents

We quantify decompression rates from MI water contents for the 1820 Keanakāko’i eruption using inverse modeling of volatile compositions measured in 19 olivine-hosted MIs53 and an analytical solution that models the diffusive equilibration of a spherical MI in an anisotropic host21 (see “Methods”). Diffusion of these MIs was not modeled in 3D because X-μCT scans, required for geometric characterization, were not available for the crystals with measured water contents. Since most uncertainty in decompression rate arises from uncertainty in H+ diffusivity and anisotropy61, we considered two anisotropies of H+ diffusion in olivine (D[100]/D[001] = 3 and 15.621; diffusivity data from ref. 18) and initial melt H2O content of 0.6 wt.%53. We conducted additional sensitivity tests (see the Supplementary Material) examining variations in initial melt H2O content and in alternative H+ diffusivity values18,62. Unlike multi-volatile embayment modeling incorporating slow-diffusing species from bubble exsolution onset to fragmentation17, our H+ modeling reflects final shallow decompression in this dry system (<0.6 wt.% H2O), where significant degassing occurs only in the upper few hundred meters of ascent (~ 14 MPa; see Supplementary Material). Cooling rate reconstruction from Mg zoning in melt inclusions

Building upon the insights gained from Fe-Mg diffusion modeling in olivine, a similar volume mesh-based approach can be employed to estimate syn-eruptive cooling rates using the Mg zoning preserved in three olivine-hosted MIs (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary material). The approach consists of fitting MgO concentration gradients measured in olivine-hosted MIs to a modeled transect derived from the volume mesh to reconstruct a linear cooling history for that MI20 (see “Methods”). Olivine-hosted MIs have Mg zonation with MgO varying from ~ 5.7 to 9.4 wt.% from the rim to the center (Fig. 3d). The estimated best-fits for the cooling rates for the 1820 golden pumice (unit K1) eruption range between 7.5 and 15 ∘C/s, corresponding to cooling durations of roughly 5–10 s (from 1215 to 1138 ∘C). This temperature evolution is constrained by the Fo content of the host olivine, and an Mg partition coefficient of 4.5 that is applied to olivine-melt equilibrium47. The modeled cooling rates represent maximum estimates, as greater diffusive Mg loss or additional post-entrapment crystallization before syn-eruptive cooling would yield slower rates. These 3D finite element-derived cooling rates are significantly higher than those obtained from previous 1D models for other eruptions within the Keanakāko’i Tephra sequence, such as the ~ 1500 basal reticulite (unit B) and ~ 1650 phreatomagmatic (layer 6 in unit E) eruptions17,20, which exhibited cooling rates below 1 ∘C/s (Fig. 4a, b). However, the 1820 eruption cooling rates are comparable to MI cooling rates recorded during the 1959 Kīlauea Iki eruption, which reached up to 11 ∘C/s20. The variability in cooling rates from 1 to 11 ∘C/s is anticorrelated with decompression rates. This is because the adiabatic expansion of volatiles in volatile-rich magmas undergoing rapid decompression may increase the interval over which the magma cools, causing a slower cooling rate20. Slower ascending volatile-poor magmas may therefore rapidly quench during lava fountaining. Alternatively, slower cooling rates may be associated with the insulating properties of subplinian plumes associated with eruptions with higher decompression rates20. Modeling hydrogen diffusion to understand water preservation during ascent

Complementary to cooling rate estimates, modeling of hydrogen diffusion provides additional constraints on how water is preserved within olivine and its hosted MIs during magma ascent. Water diffusion in skeletal olivine crystals and their hosted MIs is modeled with a high-resolution volume mesh (3–7 million vertices) that represents the complex crystal morphology (Fig. 5a). Water diffusion is simulated during magma ascent from 74 MPa to surface pressure at 0 MPa, with temperature set to 1210 ∘C47,53. Under an average decompression rate of 0.07 MPa/s, typical of the 1500 C.E. Kīlauea eruption (estimated from volatile diffusion in olivine-hosted embayments17,63), water concentration distributions indicate water depletion near crystal margins after 1000 s (Fig. 5b). A slower decompression scenario (10−4 MPa/s), representing the lower end-member decompression rate estimated for the 1820 eruption based on diffusive equilibration modeling of H2O in MIs, produces a weaker H2O zoning gradient (~ 0.1 ppm between maximum and minimum values) because slower decompression allows more time for the gradient to re-equilibrate during ascent (Fig. 5c). Water is better preserved close to MIs and at higher decompression rates (e.g., 0.45 MPa/s), particularly when applying the H+ diffusivity values reported by ref. 62 (Fig. 5d).

Profiles measured along the c-axis show sinusoidal shapes with steep gradients near MIs and crystal edge and a plateau in between, consistent with previous modeling of theoretical olivine shapes21. The complexity in the water gradient increases in crystals containing multiple MIs, which exhibit compositional patterns influenced by overlapping diffusion fields and inclusion interactions, affecting preservation and interpretation of volatile records. Olivine water contents range from ~ 4.5 to 5.4 ppm, while MI water concentrations range from 2270 to 5970 ppm. The extent of water re-equilibration correlates with exposed MI surface area, with larger MIs (>5 × 103 μm2) better preserving initial water content (Fig. 5e, f).

However, accurately measuring such complex, low-hydrogen concentrations in olivine via Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy is challenging due to overlapping absorption bands, crystal defects, and calibration uncertainties. Moreover, measuring such low hydrogen levels requires thick wafers, which is particularly difficult for skeletal olivines that are flat along a crystallographic direction and exhibit a high a-/b- axis ratio (e.g., k1820_ol1A olivine grain in Fig. 5a with an a-/b- axis ratio near 14), a morphology indicative of growth under high disequilibrium conditions45. Thus, for reliable interpretation of diffusion profiles, careful defect characterization and calibration protocols are recommended. Analysis of low water concentrations are often complemented by NanoSIMS or SIMS analyses to achieve quantitative spatial resolution17,64.

Discussion

Advances in understanding magmatic processes in the Keanakāko’i tephra sequence

The Keanakāko’i tephra sequence records a complex 300-year period of magmatic evolution at Kīlauea from approximately 1500 to the early 1800s C.E.50 This time period, following summit caldera collapse (in ~ 1500 C.E.), featured diverse eruption styles ranging from phreatomagmatic explosions and high lava fountain effusive events50,51,54,65,66. Thermobarometric analyzes revealed that, during this period, some high-forsterite (up to Fo90) olivines crystallized at deeper levels (equal to or >5 km)53. This indicated episodic inputs of deeper, hotter magma (up to 1330 ∘C53). Mid-crustal magma storage zones appeared to have shallowed over time from depths of 5–8 km during the earlier explosive Keanakāko’i era (unit D ~ 1500–1650 CE eruptions) to ~ 1–5 km during more recent effusive activity (i.e., the 1820 unit K1 eruption)53. This evolution is consistent with changes in the plumbing system architecture over the Keanakāko’i Tephra era51. Concurrently, liquid and vapor inclusion studies also indicate predominantly shallow magma storage at depths of 1–3 km beneath the summit, consistent with a stable, long-lived reservoir system53. Notably, elevated CO2 contents in fluid and MIs from the Keanakāko’i units (up to 2200 ppm for the 1650 unit D eruption) are more frequent than in more recent eruptions53. These CO2 enrichments suggest higher volatile saturation and incomplete degassing despite generally slow to moderate magma decompression rates (0.22–0.31 MPa/s for the 1650 unit E eruption; Table 1 and Fig. 6). Interestingly, the highest CO2 concentrations in MIs correspond to the highest decompression rates among the Keanakāko’i eruptions. Specifically, the 1650 eruption exhibits both the maximum CO2 content (up to 2200 ppm) and the highest decompression rates (0.22–0.31 MPa/s). In contrast, the 1500 eruption, with moderate maximum CO2 levels (659 ppm), displays intermediate decompression rates (0.06–0.08 MPa/s), while the MIs from the 1820 eruption have the lowest maximum CO2 content (304 ppm) and the slowest decompression rates (10−4 to 10−2.2 MPa/s). This correlation highlights the critical role of volatile content in driving magma ascent and decompression dynamics during these eruptive episodes, whereby eruptive episodes with higher decompression rates may have experienced inefficient gas release due to restricted bubble connectivity or bubble coalescence being impeded by higher rates of ascent. To evaluate whether free bubbles could nonetheless segregate from the melt, we calculated bubble rise velocities using Stokes’ law for laminar flow, for viscosities of 10-100 Pa ⋅ s and bubble radii of 10−3–103 mm (see Supplementary Fig. S-29). Hydrogen and Fe-Mg diffusion in olivine from the Keanakāko’i Tephra sequence highlights a multistep evolution of magma storage, transport, and ascent. Fe-Mg diffusion in olivine indicates that olivine clusters were stored in magma reservoirs for at least a decade (Fig. 1d), preserving prolonged chemical zoning that was not previously identified51. Extended storage of olivine crystals in the crust required that crustal reservoirs remained partially molten and physically viable for decades or longer in a high magma-flux system12.

Final magma mixing recorded in skeletal olivine, which potentially triggered the 1820 eruption, occurred within days to weeks (Fig. 1d), indicating rapid rejuvenation and ascent immediately before eruption, similarly to other eruptions from the same eruptive period51. Diffusion modeling of Fe-Mg suggests a dynamic system where magma was not passively cooling but was instead actively recharged and stirred by repeated injections of hotter12, more primitive magma from depth. Such replenishments helped maintain reservoir temperatures above the solidus, limit extensive crystallization, and enhance opportunities for magma mixing, which is supported by the presence of hybrid olivine zoning (normal and reverse zoning51) and diversity of glass compositions in the Keanakāko’i sequence51,54. Multidecadal olivine residence, combined with late-stage mixing only days to weeks before eruption, points to a system that can store and recycle magmatic components for extended periods, but also shift to eruption rapidly when recharge, mixing, and volatile exsolution thresholds are crossed. Therefore, volcanic systems like Kīlauea’s during the Keanakāko’i era are not dominated by simple, rapid ascent of primitive magma from the mantle. Instead, they are defined by complex and dynamic processes of crystal and melt storage, periodic recharge events, and punctuated destabilization leading to eruption. This has broader implications for interpreting pre-eruptive signals in active basaltic systems, as deeper magmatic processes may precede eruption by years to decades, while the final triggers for eruption are closely tied to the most recent events of recharge and mixing.

Re-examining the relationship between decompression and mass eruption rates

Water loss from MIs, embayments, and their olivine hosts has become a popular method for constraining final magma ascent timescales because hydrogen diffusion in olivine is highly sensitive to decompression and records syn-eruptive pressure changes at high temporal resolution18,19. Using MI water contents, we quantified decompression rates for the 1820 Keanakāko’i eruption and evaluated their relationship with mass eruption rate (MER) across a global compilation of basaltic to non-silicic systems (Fig. 6). The 1820 eruption yields decompression rates of 10−4 to 10−2.2 MPa/s, approximately two to three orders of magnitude lower than those estimated for other Kīlauea eruptions using volatile diffusion in olivine-hosted embayments (e.g., 0.06–0.08 MPa/s for the 1500 unit B eruption; 0.22–0.31 MPa/s for the 1650 subplinian unit E eruption; and 0.02–0.04 MPa/s for the 1959 Kīlauea Iki eruption17,63). Despite these lower rates, the 1820 eruption exhibits decompression rates and log10(MER) values (3.1–4.7) that overlap with other basaltic eruptions of a similar magnitude such as the 2001 Etna (log10(MER) = 3.8; dP/dt ~ 10−4 MPa/s68,69) and the 1995 Cerro Negro (log10(MER) = 4.1–4.6; dP/dt = 2 × 10−5 to 2 × 10−2 MPa/s18,70) eruptions.

Previous studies have established that there is a broad positive trend between magma decompression rate and MER18,63; however, our compiled data show greater scatter (Table 1) and uncertainty in this relationship than previously reported (the methods and data used to calculate decompression and MER for all eruptions investigated are provided in the Supplementary Material). This is exemplified by eruptions at Kīlauea that do not show a simple linear correlation between decompression rate and MER. For example, the 1820 eruption exhibits lower decompression rates despite having a higher MER than the 1959 Kīlauea Iki eruption. The greater variability in decompression rates that we calculate for the lower energy 1820 eruption, when compared to the phreatomagmatic 1650 eruption, also supports the idea that fluctuations in flow rate may be more significant in eruptions with low discharge rates19. The variability in decompression rates within a single eruption and between eruptions at Kīlauea may further reflect changing conduit processes over time, such as magma mingling, stalling, and syn-eruptive vapor fluxing, as well as differences in conduit geometry (fissure versus cylindrical pathways) and pre-eruptive volatile budgets, which together modulate decompression efficiency independently of eruptive discharge rate.

We note that part of the variability in decompression rates (approximately one order of magnitude) can be attributed to uncertainties in the selection of diffusivities and anisotropy values within a given modeling approach, highlighting how parameter choices influence decompression rate estimates even when using a consistent method. However, the variability in decompression rates for basaltic eruptions (e.g., up to three orders of magnitude for the 1995 Cerro Negro eruption) remains smaller than that reported for some explosive silicic caldera-forming eruptions. Explosive silicic eruptions exhibit decompression rates spanning up to four orders of magnitude, depending on the rate-meter used (bubble or microlite number densities, hornblende rim zoning, diffusion profiles, experimental constraints, or monitoring data), reflecting variable sensitivities to different stages of the accelerating ascent trajectory71–73.

Linking syn-eruptive cooling rates to ascent dynamics, eruption style, and deposit characteristics

Magma cooling and decompression rates, derived from Fe-Mg and H2O chemical zoning in olivine-hosted MI and embayments, play important roles in controlling the intensity and timing of volcanic activity and eruption dynamics71. The Mg zoning measured in the MIs (Fig. 3d) is believed to be related to subsequent cooling rather than a boundary layer effect upon growth as demonstrated by single-step cooling experiments using a similar H2O-poor tholeiitic natural melt composition that failed to reproduce the Mg zoning between skeletal branches in newly crystallized olivines45,47. The maximum cooling rates we derive from Mg zoning in olivine-hosted MI for the 1820 Kīlauea eruption, ranging from 7.5 to 15 ∘C/s, are indicators of the magmatic conditions during ascent. These rates correspond to rapid quenching timescales on the order of seconds, characteristic of magmas exposed to ambient atmosphere during vigorous, sustained lava fountain activity, which produced abundant golden pumice deposits formed by rapid fragmentation and fast magma cooling50,55. Fountain heights recorded during the 1820 eruption, which likely exceeded several hundred meters55 (Fig. 7), provide the physical context for such rapid cooling rates. These high fountains imply that cooling within the conduit was limited, possibly due to thermal insulation from the conduit walls and the insulating effects of the gas-magma mixture flowing upward, or because adiabatic expansion of gases provided little cooling in this volatile-poor system. Large fountain height promotes extensive magma-air interaction, enhancing heat loss by convective and radiative mechanisms and preserving sharp chemical Mg zoning in MIs. Rapid cooling during lava fountain eruptions, such as the 1820 Kīlauea, 1959 Kīlauea Iki, or 1977 Seguam eruptions, preserved steep Mg zoning profiles in MIs20, reflecting efficient air quenching. The 1820 Kīlauea erupted material is widely distributed across more than 20 km2, with thickness varying spatially but typically reaching from tens of centimeters up to over two meters in proximal areas, and the pumice clasts display high vesicularities (median values often above 90%55), reflecting efficient volatile exsolution and expansion during sustained low decompression rates. The fragmentation that produced pumice significantly increased the surface area for heat exchange and volatile escape, further accelerating cooling and preserving diffusive zoning patterns observed in the MIs. While closed-system degassing helps retain volatiles critical for explosive fragmentation, the efficiency of volatile escape depends on complex factors such as bubble nucleation, growth, connectivity, and magma permeability74. Even during slower decompression, volatiles can be trapped if a permeable network does not develop, preserving explosive potential. This complexity is underscored by the overall negative correlation observed between cooling and decompression rates in the Keanakāko’i eruptions, where slower decompression combined with rapid cooling characterizes lava fountain events, and faster decompression with slower cooling defines subplinian eruptions (Fig. 4)20.

The relationship between rapid cooling rates and eruption style is not unique to the 1820 Kīlauea eruption, but aligns with observations from other lava fountain eruptions, including the 1959 Kīlauea Iki event, which similarly produced fast quenching conditions and steep Mg zoning in MIs consistent with high cooling rates20. The evidence for high syn-eruptive cooling rates during the 1820 Kīlauea eruption is confirmed by the presence of highly vesicular pumice clasts alongside fragile, microvesicular textures and the near absence of thick glassy rinds, indicating that pyroclasts were quickly quenched after being ejected from the lava fountains55. The relatively uniform vesicularity within subunits, limited post-fragmentation, and minimal signs of welding or aggregation further point to rapid syn-eruptive cooling. Additionally, the preservation of fragile features (reticulites) and the consistent relationship between maximum pumice size, deposit thickness, and dispersal distance55 confirm that high cooling rates were an important control on deposit formation, and they validate using these features as reliable proxies for interpreting eruption dynamics from field evidence. This confirms that the thermal histories recorded in olivine-hosted MI chemical zoning are reliable proxies for eruption dynamics and capture the interplay between ascent, fragmentation, cooling, and volatile degassing.

Broader implications and applications of 3D finite element modelling

Incorporating realistic mineral geometries and complex crystal morphologies into diffusion models significantly enhances accuracy in constraining magmatic timescales. This improvement advances geospeedometry techniques used to investigate magma eruption dynamics by accounting for anisotropic diffusion pathways and multiphase boundaries that are often neglected in traditional 1D or simplified models. While we assume that 1D models generally overestimate (for Fe-Mg in olivine) diffusion timescales by neglecting the enhanced diffusion that occurs near crystal edges and faces in 3D39, it is important to recognize that the accuracy of 1D versus 3D modeling depends on several factors: the geometry of the crystal, the extent of diffusion, and the relationship between the diffusion lengthscale and the crystal size, as well as variations in diffusivity. A 1D approximation might still yield accurate estimates if diffusion has advanced only a small portion of the crystal’s total size (so that the diffusion fronts have not reached the crystal center). However, when diffusion has been extensive and the fronts approach or reach the center of the crystal, 3D effects become increasingly significant, and 1D models are more likely to overestimate timescales. Based on these considerations, previously reported diffusion timescales for olivine from other eruptions, calculated using isothermal and isobaric “DIPRA”75, “Autodiff”76, “DiffSim”77, “Diffuser”78 models fitting 1D Fe-Mg transects may be overestimated, particularly for cases with extensive diffusion. Our results imply that when 3D effects are suitably accounted for, the longest diffusion timescales are likely smaller than previously believed51. Thus, when interpreting timescales from diffusion modeling, both the degree of diffusion and crystal geometry need to be considered12,39. It is also important to consider whether crystals occur in clusters, as clustering can cause heterogeneous thermal histories that complicate diffusion profiles and timescale interpretations.

These fundamental principles can be applied to reconstruct thermal histories and constrain crystallization timescales from major and trace element zoning observed in various magmatic minerals (e.g., feldspar, amphibole) in different volcanic systems. For example, Li zoning in feldspar, Sr and Ba zoning in sanidine40, or Sr in amphibole79 could benefit from 3D FEM simulations, which can be quantitatively calibrated using LA-ICP-MS or SIMS transects acquired across the crystal. The increasing availability of high-resolution 3D imaging and chemical mapping techniques80 allows direct application of FEM to spatially resolved major and trace element distributions. Techniques such as X-μCT and synchrotron X-μCT provide detailed 3D crystal morphologies and reveal spatial relationships among host crystals, embayments, MIs, and fractures. Diffraction contrast tomography could also help access crystal orientations, enabling more accurate correction for diffusive anisotropy81. Complementary elemental computed tomography methods, including pink-beam X-ray fluorescence CT, absorption and phase-contrast CT, and infrared CT, deliver volumetric elemental maps that may be used for 3D FEM. These datasets would enable calibration of compositional maps with voxel or mesh geometries, allowing assignment of spatially variable initial concentrations and phase boundaries within finite element meshes. Applications of 3D FEM may be extended to mantle processes as well, where these models can refine interpretations of H isotope fractionation and volatile cycling in mantle olivine16,82, thereby enhancing understanding of volatile transport mechanisms within the mantle and lithosphere. Similarly, applying these methods to planetary materials80,83,84, such as lunar olivine-hosted MIs, may improve constraints on volatile loss and magmatic evolution on the Moon83,84. Further development and application of efficient and versatile 3D diffusion modeling frameworks that integrate realistic mineral geometries and multiphase boundary conditions remains essential for advancing understanding of magmatic processes now and in the future.

Methods

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM 7800F)

Each olivine (single or cluster) was polished and backscattered electron images were acquired using a JEOL JSM7800F Scanning Electron Microscope at the Asian School of the Environment (Nanyang Technological University (NTU), Singapore) using an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a current of 550 pA.

Electron probe micro-analysis (EPMA)

After collecting the X-μCT scan (detailed information on the analytical conditions for the X-μCT scans can be found in ref. 41), five olivines (two polyhedral clusters and three single skeletal grains) and their associated MIs were polished to analyze major elements. Olivine and MIs were analyzed at NTU in Singapore with a JEOL JXA-8530F Hyperprobe. An accelerating voltage of 20 keV, a current of 200 nA and a beam diameter of 1 μm was used for olivine analysis. Relative precision (2σ) based on repeat analysis of the Springwater USNM2566 olivine standard is better than 1% for Si, Mg, and Fe, ~ 3% for Mn. Accuracy is ~ 1% for Si, Mg, Fe, and <2% for Mn. An accelerating voltage of 15 keV, a current of 10 nA and a beam diameter of 3 μm was used for the melt analysis. Relative precision (2σ) based on repeat analysis of the VG-2 standard is better than 1% for Si, Al, Ca, Mg, ~ 1% for Fe, <2% for Na, ~ 3% for Ti, ~ 6% for S, ~ 10% for K, ~ 14% for Mn and P, ~ 23% for Cl. Accuracy is <1% for Si, Al, Ca, Ti, ~ 1% for Cl, Fe, Mn, P, ~ 3% for Mg, ~ 4% for Na, ~ 7% for S, and ~ 40% for K.

Construction of crystal meshes

Finite element meshes were generated in Avizo™ software using the segmented X-μCT olivine data and optimized in Gmsh59 and MeshLab58 softwares (remeshing to simplify and improve the mesh quality, cleaning and repairing of the mesh, including merging of close vertices, removal of duplicated vertices and isolated vertices, repair of non-manifold edges and vertices). The edges of the MI meshes were further refined to increase the code stability. Volume meshes had 3–7 million vertices, depending on the degree of complexity of the grains. We refined the mesh at the boundary between the olivine and MIs, and for more complex skeletal morphologies. The accuracy of the finite element analysis is governed by both mesh resolution and the thoroughness of spatial sampling across the simulated domain. Convergence was evaluated by ref. 21 by systematically running the models at several spatial resolutions21, and in most cases, results agreed within 0.05 wt% of the reference solution for water loss from MIs. After establishing mesh adequacy, individual domains and boundary regions were assigned appropriate labels for subsequent calculations.

Diffusion modeling

This section describes the diffusion modeling methods applied to each of the natural scenarios discussed in the main text, including: Fe-Mg interdiffusion in olivine and MIs during magma mixing and storage, water diffusion in olivine and MIs during magma decompression, and Mg diffusion in olivine-hosted MIs during syn-eruptive cooling upon magma ascent. For each of these scenarios, we combined our X-μCT meshes with the 3D multiphase finite-element model of ref. 21 to model diffusion in natural olivine hosts and their MIs. This approach resolves diffusion in two different domains with different diffusivities and densities, whilst also conserving mass and flux across the domain boundaries. For more details of the constitutive equations, see ref. 21, which have been verified against analytical solutions from ref. 85 (Equations 3-44a and 3-44b). The current approach assumes the diffusion tensor axes are aligned with the principal model axes (off-diagonal terms fixed at zero). Although this is reasonable for the skeletal olivines, it is an approximation for the olivine clusters, where individual grains may exhibit slightly different crystallographic orientations despite their shared early crystallization history, which favors similar orientations for most grains.

The numerical models were implemented using FEniCS legacy60, a free open-source Python package for finite element modeling. A full description of the numerical implementation of FEniCS to diffusion modeling in magmatic systems can be found in ref. 86 and its supplementary material. For all cases, we assumed that the density ratio between the olivine and melt was equal to 1.2. To improve computational efficiency, we opted to use diffusion coefficients that were not spatially dependent (i.e., constant diffusivity), requiring us to use approximations where appropriate (see below for more details). In all numerical models (3D and 1D), we used a Crank–Nicholson (second order) time-stepping scheme, which is unconditionally stable. All 3D models were computed on a Dell Precision 5820 workstation (128 GB RAM), with the FEniCS code parallelized across 18 cores.

1D Fe-Mg diffusion modeling in olivine

One-dimensional comparison models were conducted with the same configuration as the 3D models using the same time step size (20 days) and mesh spatial resolution to ensure consistency. Additional 1D models using both linear and non-linear solvers, in which diffusion coefficients are either compositionally/spatially constant or Fo-dependent, show that within the Fo range of interest (86.5–89.5), both approaches yield similar results over timescales longer than 2 years (Supplementary Fig. S28), supporting the validity of our linear approximation in this case.

We also conducted 1D inversion models to constrain timescales of measured rim profiles using DFENS86 (Supplementary Fig. S5–10, S-12, 13, S-15, and 16), which combines FEM with a nested sampling Bayesian inversion. We ran the inversions using the Fe-Mg interdiffusion coefficient of ref. 25 and the parametrization of ref. 8 for comparison. Timescales estimated using the diffusion coefficient of ref. 25 are generally a factor of 2 longer than those derived using ref. 8.

For profiles that had multiple zones (e.g., k1820_ol4A and k1820_ol5B), we used a non-isothermal modeling approach where the interior and exterior zones are modeled sequentially and at different temperatures, using the DFENS inversion framework (see ref. 88 for more details), and a similar approach to the non-isothermal diffusion incremental step model used for pyroxenes89.

3D water diffusion modeling in olivine and associated melt inclusions

For water loss from MIs via diffusion through the olivine hosts, we used the diffusion modeling code of ref. 21 with modifications to make it more applicable to the Kīlauea volcanic system. Due to uncertainties in olivine H+ diffusion coefficients, we opted to explore two alternative diffusivity values from recent experimental work18,62. The former was calibrated on high-Fo olivines from San Carlos and Kīlauea, whilst the latter were calibrated on Fe-rich olivines (Fo79) from Cerro Negro, an arc volcanic system. Iron content significantly influences H+ diffusivity in olivine, with higher Fe concentrations generally increasing H+ diffusivity18. There is also some uncertainty concerning the anisotropy of H+ diffusion in olivine observed in natural olivines with values ranging from 3 to 15.619,21, whilst experimental values can extend up to 40 times18. Water diffusivity is also controlled by water content, diminishing upon dehydration90. These experiments were calibrated on hydrous basalts from Etna that contained 4–5 wt.% water and have shown that water content has a much smaller influence than Fe-content in the olivine. In our models that use the diffusion coefficient of ref. 18, we explore two different anisotropies (3 and 15.6) where diffusivity along the [100] axis is faster than along the [010] and [001]. The diffusivity of water in the MI was held constant along the decompression path and calculated using the total water diffusion coefficient91 from the measured MI composition, initial water content, starting pressure, and temperature. The olivine-melt partition coefficient for total water is assumed constant at 0.000992, although it may increase with decreasing pressure93. Given that the Kīlauea magmatic system has low starting water contents (~ 0.6 wt %) and pressure (74 MPa), assumptions of constant diffusivity and partition coefficient can be considered reasonable approximations. A fixed Dirichlet boundary condition was maintained at the exterior boundary of the crystal and was modified at each time step according to the equilibrium water content in the melt at the corresponding pressure as calculated by the mixed-volatile solubility model built into Magmasat94.

Degassing paths (defined as a closed system) were used to model water diffusion. The choice of a closed system is motivated by the fact that lava lake activity was less common in the Keanakāko’i period than it has been since the summit was deepened in the modern period ~ 1790 and after95. Therefore, the pathway for volatile degassing from the summit reservoir system had been suppressed by the absence of lava lake activity during the Keanakāko’i period. This period of reduced lava lake activity may have been due to reduced magma flux66, allowing the shallow conduit system to be either more closed or blocked. The MagmaSat solubility model was calibrated using a series of experiments on predominantly sub-alkaline basaltic magmas (typically 1200–1300 ∘C), making it well-suited for the high-temperature tholeiitic basaltic magmas of Kīlauea. Calculations were carried out using the VESIcal tool96. The temperature (1210 ∘C) for degassing path calculations is the average matrix melt temperature of the 1820 golden pumice eruption47,53. The initial H2O content of the model was set at 0.6 wt.% to match the measured H2O contents from MIs53. Volatile solubility experiments show that substantial H2O degassing from H2O-poor tholeiitic magmas only occurs at very low pressures, at depths of less than 1 km97.

3D Mg diffusion modeling in melt inclusions during syn-eruptive cooling

For syn-eruptive cooling rates, we followed the approach of ref. 20. We concurrently modeled diffusion of MgO in both the MI and olivine host, where conservation of mass is governed by an olivine-melt partition coefficient of 4.5, which is the KD of Mg between olivine and melt47. We used identical olivine Fe-Mg interdiffusion coefficients (ref. 25, based on average core composition) and MgO melt diffusion coefficients (ref. 87). Like the modeling frameworks discussed above, we assume the diffusion coefficients are spatially independent (i.e., are constant for a given temperature). The syn-eruptive MgO diffusion modeling applies a fixed Dirichlet boundary condition at the olivine-MI interface to represent rapid olivine growth adjacent to the MI, which effectively fixes the boundary Mg concentration during cooling. The boundary composition was calculated using a rearranged version of a MgO-in-melt thermometer, which was calibrated for Hawaiian tholeiites47.13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{\,{{{\rm{MgO}}}}}^{{{{\rm{melt}}}}}=\frac{T-1017.0}{21.2}$$\end{document}CMgOmelt=T−1017.021.2 To explicitly test for pre-existing post-entrapment crystallization (PEC), we calculated the melt Mg# in equilibrium with each host olivine rim using various KD from 0.27 to 0.35. Several inclusions (e.g., in crystals k1820_ol1A, k1820_ol2B, k1820_ol5B; see supplementary Fig. S3) show measured core MgO values lower than those expected at equilibrium, indicating some PEC occurred prior to syn-eruptive cooling. In these cases, our use of the current maximum MgO as the initial condition underestimates the melt composition before PEC, meaning our modeled cooling rates represent maximum estimates (more diffusive loss or post-entrapment crystallization prior to syn-eruptive cooling would lead to slower cooling rates). Alternatively, the MI initial condition could be higher in Mg, which may then slow down the cooling rates. Where cores are in near equilibrium (e.g., k1820_ol4A), no significant pre-eruptive PEC is required. The starting temperature and MI composition were calculated using Eq. (13). The final temperatures were determined by the edge compositions of the MIs. Initial temperatures ranged from 1193 to 1215 ∘C, whilst final temperatures ranged from 1138 to 1199 ∘C depending on the crystal composition.

Like in ref. 20, we assumed that there was a linear cooling gradient between the initial and final temperatures. At each time step, the temperature was calculated using the cooling rate, and the equilibrium melt MgO composition at the edge of the inclusion was calculated using Eq. (13). Diffusion was then calculated based on the new set of boundary conditions. This would then progress until the final temperature and MI compositions were reached. We modeled different cooling rates from 2.5 to 15 ∘C/s and assessed the misfit with the measured electron probe data to determine the best-fitting cooling rate.

To constrain the decompression rates, we used a Monte Carlo Bayesian inversion in which the MI composition was fitted using the analytical solution described above. The inversion was conducted using pyMultiNest98,99, a nested sampling Bayesian inversion Python package. In each Monte Carlo realization, input parameters would be randomly generated and used to compute the analytical solution, which would then be compared to the measured composition using a likelihood function (see ref. 86 for details). This approach would then explore the parameter space that would converge on a likelihood value (i.e., minimize misfit) between the model and the data. MIs that show limited water loss relative to the initial water content (i.e., the larger MIs) were filtered out as the models had insufficient sensitivity to estimate an accurate decompression rate.

We inverted for a total of 4 parameters, including: decompression rate, temperature (main factor controlling the diffusivity), the olivine-melt partition coefficient for total water, and the MI radius (Supplementary Fig. S26). For each MI, we used a log uniform prior for decompression rate that ranged from 1 × 10−7 MPa/s up to 10 MPa/s, to encapsulate the full range of decompression rates found in natural systems18. We used a Gaussian prior for temperature (1210 ± 13 ∘C) based on a MgO-in-melt thermometer47 (Eq. (13) and its associated uncertainty). A Gaussian prior was applied to the preferred H2O partition coefficient between olivine and melt, along with its associated uncertainty92. Finally, we used a Gaussian prior for the MI radius, in which we assigned a 2σ measurement error of ± 2 μm. Given the uncertainties on initial water content, diffusion mechanism of H+ in olivine, and diffusive anisotropy, we conducted additional sensitivity tests to assess the effect on the inverted decompression rates. We ran models using two alternative diffusion coefficients18,62 and used two initial water contents, including 0.5 and 0.6 wt.% (Supplementary Figs. S24 and 25). For models that used the diffusion coefficient of ref. 18, we tested 2 different anisotropies (3 and 15.6) found in natural crystals21.
