Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics reveal that THBS1 induces radiation resistance in TNBC through the interaction between myCAF and TOPBP1⁺ tumor cells
Authors: Fuzhong Liu, Xing Xu, Wenhao Tong, Yang Xie, Yecheng Ma, Lingling Yang, Xun Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiation resistance remains a clinical challenge in the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and its mechanisms are not yet fully understood.MethodsWe integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and prognostic data to perform analyses such as intercellular communication, pseudo-temporal trajectories, and GSEA and validated the results using experiments including qPCR, single-cell sequencing and flow cytometry.ResultsThis study identified a unique subpopulation of epithelial cells, designated TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cells, which retain normal epithelial transcriptional profiles and are associated with poor patient prognosis. myCAF communicates with this cell cluster via the THBS1 signaling pathway, activating the NOTCH signaling pathway to promote resistance to radiotherapy.ConclusionsTHBS1 plays a pivotal role in mediating radioresistance in triple-negative breast cancer, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08614-z.Keywords: scRNA, TNBC, myCAF, THBS1, NOTCH

Introduction

TNBC is the breast cancer subtype with the poorest prognosis, characterized by the lack of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression [1]. Radiotherapy is an important therapeutic modality for local control in TNBC. However, radioresistance leads to local recurrence, thereby severely compromising patient prognosis [2]. Current research on radioresistance mechanisms has yielded certain results but remains not fully clear, necessitating further in-depth study. Previous studies based on bulk sequencing have made some progress, such as enhanced DNA damage repair capacity [3], as well as dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoint [4] and apoptosis pathways [5]. However, these studies have technical limitations, as bulk-level analysis only captures the transcriptional characteristics of a single sample and fails to provide an in-depth analysis of the tumor microenvironment [6]. Although many immune infiltration algorithms have emerged, such as CIBERSORT, ESTIMATE, and TIMER, their results differ from actual conditions and lose information on stromal cells. Stromal cells comprise fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and pericytes; a growing body of literature indicates that stromal cells, particularly fibroblasts, can promote tumor development through pathways such as remodeling the extracellular matrix and promoting angiogenesis [7]. Current scRNA-seq can compensate for the limitations of bulk sequencing [8]. This technology physically separates cells in a suspension one by one using microfluidic chips, followed by sequencing of individual cells [9]. Subsequent analysis of transcriptional profiles across different cell types allows for the identification of stromal cells [10]. Notably, scRNA-seq yields coordinates in a reduced-dimensional space rather than actual physical locations. Consequently, spatial transcriptomics was integrated into our research framework. By leveraging the true spatial distribution within tissues, this approach enables the visualization of spatial colocalization between stromal and malignant cells. Furthermore, it preserves the transcriptional characteristics of individual ST spots, facilitating in-depth analysis of intercellular communication or other potential interaction modes between these populations [11, 12].

We initially profiled the single-cell landscape of TNBC and identified a distinct tumor subpopulation, termed TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cells. Its prognostic significance was further evaluated by incorporating bulk sequencing data. Subsequent cell-cell communication analysis indicated that myCAFs potentially interact with this subpopulation via THBS1-SDC4 signaling, a finding we validated at the spatial transcriptomics level. Furthermore, THBS1 stimulation of TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cells may activate the NOTCH pathway, ultimately driving radioresistance in TNBC.

Methods

Data collection

We obtained single‑cell RNA sequencing data from 8 TNBC and 13 normal samples in the GSE161529 dataset [13]. Clinical information can be found in Supplementary Table 1. In addition, we acquired RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information from TNBC patients in TCGA‑BRCA [14]. During data filtering, samples lacking survival time and survival status information were excluded to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the analysis. In parallel, spatial transcriptomic (ST) data for four patients were retrieved from the Zenodo repository. We got the transcriptome data of triple-negative breast cancer parent cell lines and their radioresistant strains from GSE120798.

Clustering and dimensionality reduction of single‑cell data

We used the R package “Seurat” to filter single‑cell data with the following criteria: each gene expressed in at least 5 cells, each cell expressing at least 300 genes, and mitochondrial gene proportion < 10% [8]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then used for dimensionality reduction, followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization and cell clustering. Clusters were annotated using marker genes of distinct cell types [15].

Single‑cell copy number variation analysis

Chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs) were estimated for individual cells using the InferCNV R package [16]. Epithelial cell clusters were assessed for CNV levels, with B and T cells serving as the reference population. The resulting “observations” file was subsequently imported and subjected to normalization to derive specific CNV values for each cell.

Trajectory analysis

Pseudo-time trajectory analysis was performed using the Monocle R package to determine the relative distribution of the ET cluster and other tumor cells [17]. We loaded the Seurat object into the Monocle main class and utilized highly variable genes to build a developmental tree, thereby exploring the distribution of various cells across the trajectory. We used VECTOR to perform unsupervised inference of single-cell developmental directions.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Gene expression profiles and cell labels were integrated into the CellChat R package, with the labels designated as the default identities [18]. The ligand-receptor database was integrated into the CellChat object, followed by the identification of overexpressed ligands or receptors within specific cell groups. Gene expression data were then projected onto a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network to infer intercellular communication based on communication probability values. For the spatial transcriptomics data, we explicitly incorporated the spatial coordinates and scaling factors into the CellChat object, applied the triMean algorithm to compute communication probabilities, and ultimately aggregated all cell-cell communication networks at the signaling pathway level.

Pseudo-space analysis

We used the R package CSOmap to investigate the three‑dimensional pseudo‑space of different cell types [19]. First, `runExactTSNE_R` was used to calculate spatial information for each cell, followed by `getDensity3D` to compute density values. Finally, results were visualized using `plot3D`.

High-dimensional weighted correlation network analysis (hdWGCNA)

The R package “hdWGCNA” was used to infer specific gene expression patterns [20]. We first set up the Seurat object using `SetupForWGCNA`, constructed metacells using `MetacellsByGroups`, selected the soft power threshold using `TestSoftPowers`, and finally constructed the co‑expression network using `ConstructNetwork`.

Pathway analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed for differentially expressed genes: functional annotation was conducted using the Gene Ontology (GO) database, and pathway enrichment was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [21]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was also applied to the whole gene expression profile, with a threshold of P < 0.05 [22].

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR) was performed to detect the relative expression levels of target genes and internal reference genes [23]. Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the SPARKeasy kit, and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoOne ultra-micro volume spectrophotometer (Hangzhou Yooning Instruments Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Subsequently, total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using 5X All-In-One RT MasterMix on a LongGene T series Multi-Block Thermal Cycler. RT-PCR was performed using Universal SYBR Green qPCR Supermix on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio series real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument. 18 S was used as the internal reference gene, and the relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to detect the expression of target molecules on cell surfaces or in cells [24]. Cells were collected, washed twice with cold PBS, and incubated with specific fluorescent-labeled antibodies in the dark at 4℃ for 30 min. Unbound antibodies were removed by washing, and cells were resuspended in PBS. Samples were detected by a flow cytometer, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Three technical replicates were set for each sample, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence was used to detect the expression, localization and level of target proteins in samples [25]. After fixation and permeabilization, samples were blocked with blocking buffer to prevent non-specific binding, followed by incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight. After washing to remove unbound primary antibodies, corresponding fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h, and nuclei were stained with DAPI.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic data processing

RNA integrity and total yield were quantified by Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. mRNA was enriched with Oligo dT beads for strand-specific cDNA library construction, which was qualified via Qubit and qPCR for quantification and bioanalyzer for fragment size. Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina platform with SBS strategy. Raw reads were filtered by fastp to obtain clean reads, which were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2; gene expression was quantified as FPKM by featureCounts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. The t‑test and Wilcoxon rank‑sum test were used to evaluate gene expression differences, Pearson correlation coefficients were used to compare relationships between variables, and two‑tailed P values were used to indicate statistical significance, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic atlas of normal and tumor breast tissues

To delineate the cellular landscape of TNBC, we established a high-resolution transcriptomic atlas by profiling a clinical cohort of 21 specimens, comprising 8 tumor tissues and 13 normal breast tissues. We excluded cells with a mitochondrial percentage greater than 10%, leaving 76,059 cells. We then used established cell-specific gene annotations to identify seven cell clusters: epithelial cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, T cells, B cells, iCAFs, and myCAFs. We also used heatmaps to illustrate the expression levels of the top three genes in each cell type (Fig. 1B). The proportion of different cell types differed between the tumor and normal groups; specifically, normal tissue was characterized by a higher proportion of endothelial cells, whereas the tumor group was dominated by T cells (Fig. 1C, D). In addition, we compared differences in cellular composition among patients with different menopausal statuses. Interestingly, while the proportion of myCAFs increases following natural menopause, it decreases significantly in patients who undergo surgery-induced menopause, with a more pronounced decrease observed in patients who undergo oophorectomy (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1). Since CNV values can reflect malignant characteristics, we compared different cell types in the tumor and normal groups. As expected, the CNV values of epithelial cells in the tumor group were significantly higher than those in the normal group; therefore, we plan to conduct an in-depth analysis of the epithelial cells (Fig. 1E).

Identification and prognostic significance of TOPBP1⁺ E.T. epithelial cell clusters

To further analyze the epithelial cells, we isolated them and clustered them into 14 subpopulations (Fig. 2A). Upon closer examination, we found that five of these clusters (C3, C6, C7, C8, and C11) were also present in the normal group. We believe that these tumor cell clusters retain the transcriptional profiles of normal epithelial cells and warrant further investigation 26. We defined these five clusters derived from the tumor samples as “E.T.” (Epithelial, Tumor) cell clusters (Fig. 2B). CNV scores indicate that E.T. cells fall between normal epithelial cells and tumor cells, and that E.T. cell clusters exhibit distinct differentiation trajectories and signature genes compared to tumor cell clusters (Figs. 2C–E).

VECTOR‑based trajectory analysis indicates that the ET6 cluster appears to play an initiating role among the ET clusters (Fig. 2F). To understand the functions of E.T. cells, we performed a functional enrichment analysis, which revealed that they are functionally enriched in hypoxia response and RNA transcription, and that the pathways are enriched in signaling by TNF, IL-17, and others (Fig. 2G–H). It is worth noting that studies have shown that TNF can create a pro-inflammatory microenvironment and promote angiogenesis, thereby driving tumor progression. Among the five ET clusters, we sought to further identify subclusters associated with prognosis. We first selected characteristic genes from each cluster, calculated correlation scores using the ssgsea method, and combined these scores with patient prognosis data to compare their respective impacts on patient outcomes. The results showed that the E.T_6 subcluster was significantly associated with poor patient prognosis (Fig. 2I–J). Finally, to further characterize the E.T_6 subcluster, we performed a screening for marker genes. The results indicated that TOPBP1 exhibits a certain degree of specificity within this cluster; therefore, we subsequently named this cluster the TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cell cluster (Fig. 2K).

The discovery of the THBS1 signaling pathway

To elucidate the patterns of gene co-regulation among different cell populations in the TNBC microenvironment, we employed high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) to construct cell-type-specific co-expression clusters. The analysis revealed that the “red” module representing myCAFs and the “yellow-green” module representing the TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cluster exhibited significant topological proximity in the integrated co-expression network (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this, these two modules are also grouped into the same major branch in the hierarchical clustering tree (Fig. 3B), reflecting strong inter-module connectivity.

This dual, close association at the levels of network architecture and structural hierarchy suggests a potential functional synergy between myCAF and the TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cluster at the level of gene regulation. Intercellular communication is a mechanism by which cells transmit signals within the tumor microenvironment. The TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cluster was identified in our previous work, and we sought to identify the signals acting on this cluster, as well as the key sender of these signals. Using signal strength as a criterion, we identified 13 signals interacting with the TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cluster, among which the THBS1 signal was the strongest (Fig. 3C). Next, we sorted the signal’s transmission contributions and found that myCAF was the strongest contributor to the signal. Notably, the TOPBP1⁺E.T. cluster was also the strongest receiver, suggesting high specificity of THBS1 signaling between these two cell types (Fig. 3D, E).

In the communication network constructed via CellChat, signaling is orchestrated by specific ligand-receptor pairs rather than operating in isolation. Among the pairs mediating THBS1 signaling, THBS1–SDC4 exhibited the highest contribution, indicating its critical role in the interaction between these populations (Fig. 3F). Given that THBS1 is primarily secreted by myCAFs and iCAFs, we compared the communication strength of this pair between these two groups. As expected, the THBS1–SDC4 interaction was significantly stronger in the myCAF–TOPBP1⁺ E.T. group than in the iCAF group (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, as the CSOmap algorithm can reconstruct 3D pseudo-spatial positioning based on ligand-receptor interactions, we sought to delineate the pseudo-spatial landscape of myCAFs and TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cells.

Density and cell distribution maps revealed that the spatial distance between these two cell types is non-uniform; while they are separated in certain regions, they exhibit close proximity in others (Fig. 3H-I). The tumor microenvironment (TME) refers to the “micro-ecosystem” surrounding tumor cells, and cells within the same tumor microenvironment are more likely to interact through signaling molecules, thereby influencing tumor growth, metastasis, and resistance to treatment. We used NMF to partition the complex tumor microenvironment into five distinct microenvironments. The results showed that myCAF and TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cells were consistently enriched in the NMF5 module, suggesting a higher likelihood of cellular interaction between the two (Fig. 3J).

Validation of the spatial transcriptome

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) data can reveal true physical distribution patterns, thereby complementing single-cell findings; therefore, we extracted spatial transcriptomics data from four TNBC patients from Zenodo. It is worth noting that the region where THBS1 is located overlaps with the region of the myCAF marker; furthermore, we observed high expression of SDC4 in the tumor region (Fig. 4A). These results suggest that myCAF actively secretes THBS1 within its microenvironment, while the tumor region actively expresses the receptor SDC4 within its microenvironment. To further validate our findings, we selected Patient P1 for in-depth analysis. We first identified five cell types, mapped the annotation information back into the spatial data, and constructed an intercellular communication network (Fig. 4B). It is worth noting that THBS1 still exhibits strong signaling between myCAF and tumor cells, and THBS1-SDC4 continues to account for a significant proportion of this signaling (Fig. 4C–E). Similar trends were observed in patient P3, where the spatial proximity between myCAFs and tumor cells appeared even more pronounced (Fig. 4F–I).

THBS1 activates NOTCH signaling and promotes radiotherapy resistance

Based on transcriptomic profiling and ssGSEA scoring, SUM159PT and MDA-MB-231 were selected from a panel of TNBC cell lines for further study. These two lines exhibited high transcriptional similarity to TOPBP1⁺ E.T.

cells, serving as robust models for investigating their biological functions (Fig. 5A). PCR results indicate that THBS1 significantly increases TOPBP1 expression levels, with this effect being more pronounced in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 5B–C). We conducted an in-depth analysis using RNA-seq on THBS1-treated cell lines. GSEA revealed that the TOPBP1⁺ E.T.-related signature was enriched in the THBS1-overexpressing group (Fig. 5D). Further exploration of downstream mechanisms identified that the NOTCH signaling pathway was highly enriched following THBS1 treatment (Fig. 5E). In the independent external dataset, we found that THBS1 expression was significantly upregulated in radioresistant MDA-MB-231 cells compared with parental cells (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, qPCR analysis confirmed that THBS1 treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of canonical NOTCH target genes (HES1 and HEY1) in SUM159PT cell lines (Fig. 5G). Previous reports indicate that the NOTCH pathway, beyond maintaining cancer stemness, also mediates radioresistance. Given this, we investigated the impact of THBS1 on the radioresistant phenotype. Our results demonstrated that THBS1 treatment antagonizes radiation-induced apoptosis (Fig. 5H, I).

Discussion

The present study identifies a prognosis-related epithelial subpopulation in TNBC, designated as the TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cell cluster. Upon receiving THBS1 signals secreted by myCAFs, this cluster appears to drive radioresistance via the NOTCH pathway. Drug development targeting THBS1 offers a new approach to overcoming treatment resistance in clinical radiation therapy. It is of some significance to investigate tumor clusters that retain epithelial cell characteristics, as these clusters represent a transitional state between normal tissue and malignant tissue. In terms of their characteristics, in addition to genomic instability, these clusters often exhibit certain homeostatic regulatory functions, thereby demonstrating greater tolerance to DNA damage [27]. This unique state, which lies between physiological homeostasis and pathological dysregulation, has become a mechanism by which tumors evade the cytotoxic effects of radiation therapy [13]. As a highly conserved chaperone protein containing nine BRCT domains, TOPBP1 plays a crucial role in DNA damage repair in tumor cells [28]; it activates the ATR kinase, thereby triggering cell cycle checkpoints and DNA damage response pathways [29]. Furthermore, studies have shown that TOPBP1 can negatively regulate the p53-induced apoptotic signaling pathway by recruiting ATR, thereby affecting the efficacy of radiotherapy [30]. THBS1, a member of the TSP family, is a secreted protein that is widely distributed in the extracellular matrix of mesenchymal cells and can exert its effects in specific regions via the circulatory system [31]. THBS1 has multiple receptors, including CD36, CD47, and SDC4 [32]. The functions mediated by these different receptors vary; for example, CD36 primarily mediates anti-angiogenic effects, whereas CD47 is more closely associated with immune evasion [33]. Our study focuses more on investigating the role of the SDC4 receptor. Previous research has shown that SDC4 plays a role in mediating interactions between tumor cells and stromal cells, which is consistent with our findings [34]. Furthermore, under conditions of oxidative stress, its ability to regulate the MAPK pathway is enhanced, thereby promoting tumor progression.

As one of the ten classic cancer signaling pathways, similar to the JAK pathway, the NOTCH family is divided into different members that perform distinct functions [35]. For example, NOTCH1 primarily maintains tumor stemness [36], while NOTCH2 mediates B-cell maturation [37]. Interestingly, NOTCH2 exhibits both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive effects: it acts as a tumor suppressor in myeloid tumors [38] but as a classic oncogene in breast cancer [39]. This is consistent with our findings, and we have further narrowed our focus to triple-negative breast cancer. Furthermore, studies have shown that the NOTCH signaling pathway does not function independently solely within malignant cells; endothelial cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts also exert a certain influence on its function [40]. Our CNV analysis suggests that E.T. cells might have a moderate level of genomic instability, but we acknowledge that this method comes with technical noise, and future studies using single-cell DNA sequencing or spatial genomics will be needed for further validation.

Conclusion

Overall, this study identified THBS1 signaling associated with radiation resistance by focusing on cellular communication and identified a group of TOPBP1+ E.T. cells associated with prognosis. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying treatment failure in TNBC, but also provide a theoretical basis for targeting this signaling axis to restore radiosensitivity. The features of the TOPBP1⁺ E.T. cluster and its associated THBS1 signaling pathway may serve as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the precision treatment of this aggressive malignancy.
