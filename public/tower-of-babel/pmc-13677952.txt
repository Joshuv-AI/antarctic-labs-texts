Intravascular ultrasound-guided repair of delayed traumatic popliteal artery occlusion using an interwoven nitinol stent
Authors: Jumpei Otsuji, Yuta Imai, Fumihide Sato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traumatic popliteal artery occlusion is a limb-threatening condition traditionally managed with open surgical revascularization. Here, we describe a 66-year-old man with delayed occlusion after high-energy lower-extremity trauma. Initial computed tomography angiography indicated preserved flow despite severe arterial deformation. Progressive heel necrosis developed after fracture fixation and debridement; repeat imaging on day 24 showed complete occlusion of the popliteal artery. Intravascular ultrasound-guided endovascular repair with an interwoven nitinol stent restored antegrade flow. Wound healing was achieved after flap and skin graft procedures, and stent patency was maintained for >2.5 years.

Traumatic popliteal artery injury is a limb-threatening condition with a high risk of major amputation.1 Open surgical revascularization remains the standard treatment; however, endovascular treatment is increasingly used as a less invasive alternative in selected patients.2,3 Stent implantation in the popliteal artery remains controversial because this segment is exposed to repetitive flexion, compression, elongation, and torsion. Here, we describe the case of a delayed traumatic popliteal artery occlusion treated with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided endovascular repair using an interwoven nitinol stent, with complete wound healing and maintained patency for >2.5 years.

Case report

A 66-year-old man was transported to our hospital after a head-on collision between two light trucks. He had open fractures of the right femur and knee region, a proximal tibial fracture, a patellar fracture, metatarsal fractures, and a heel degloving injury. At presentation, the right foot was discolored, and the dorsalis pedis pulse was not palpable, prompting the performance of emergency contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA). Initial CTA revealed preserved distal arterial opacification despite marked deformation and tortuosity of the distal superficial femoral artery (SFA) and popliteal artery adjacent to the fracture site (Fig 1, A-D). Because complete occlusion was not detected and urgent orthopedic stabilization was required, initial management focused on emergency external fixation (Fig 1, E), irrigation, primary closure of the heel wound, and percutaneous pinning of the great toe and metatarsal fractures (Fig 1, F), rather than on urgent revascularization. After orthopedic stabilization, routine bedside observations were performed, but they did not initially raise concern for progressive limb ischemia. However, these assessments were primarily clinical, and detailed physician-documented vascular examinations, bedside Doppler assessments, toe pressure measurements, serial duplex ultrasonography, or early repeat CTA were not systematically performed until progressive wound deterioration prompted further evaluation. Despite repeated debridement and negative pressure wound therapy, progressive heel necrosis developed (Fig 1, G and H). The ankle-brachial index and skin perfusion pressure could not be obtained because external fixation devices and percutaneous pins precluded appropriate cuff placement; toe pressure was not measured. Duplex ultrasonography revealed markedly reduced distal arterial flow, and repeat CTA on day 24 confirmed complete occlusion from the distal SFA to the popliteal artery (Fig 2, A-D; Supplementary Video 1, online only). Subsequently, the vascular team was consulted. Formal vein mapping was not performed before endovascular treatment; if endovascular recanalization failed, open surgical revascularization, including bypass or interposition grafting after conduit assessment, was the backup strategy. Antegrade access was obtained through the ipsilateral common femoral artery. Angiography showed complete occlusion from the distal SFA to the mid-popliteal artery, with collateral distal filling (Fig 3, A). Because marked angulation obscured the true vessel course, crossing was performed under parallel IVUS guidance. The first 0.014-inch guidewire reached the distal true lumen, as confirmed by tip injection, but showed a spiral course in the proximal occlusion (Fig 3, B), and the IVUS catheter could not be advanced despite small balloon dilation. Limited IVUS imaging suggested that the wire was running along the vessel edge; therefore, rewiring was performed under parallel IVUS guidance (Fig 3, C). After rewiring, the IVUS catheter crossed easily over the second guidewire, confirming a more central wire course with only a short subintimal segment (Fig 3, D-F; Supplementary Video 2, online only). Angiography and IVUS indicated a large thrombus burden. Balloon angioplasty with a 2.0-mm balloon restored flow but provided limited luminal gain and left an irregular lumen (Fig 4, A). Because further balloon upsizing before scaffolding was considered to increase the risk of distal embolization, a 6.5 × 80-mm interwoven nitinol stent (Supera Stent; Abbott Vascular) was implanted and postdilated with a 6.0-mm high-pressure balloon (Fig 4, B). Doppler signals, limb warmth, and skin color improved immediately. Additional debridement with free musculocutaneous flap reconstruction was performed on day 30 (Fig 5, A). The skin island failed because of venous congestion, but the muscle component survived (Fig 5, B). Granulation progressed, and full-thickness skin grafting was performed on day 58 (Fig 5, C). Negative pressure wound therapy was discontinued on day 66, and the patient was transferred for rehabilitation on day 99. Discussion

This case highlights three clinically important points. First, traumatic popliteal artery injury can progress to delayed thrombotic occlusion despite initially preserved arterial flow. The popliteal artery is relatively fixed around the knee and is vulnerable to stretch, compression, direct fracture-fragment injury, and hematoma-related compression, all of which may cause intimal injury, dissection, and thrombosis.4,5 In this case, initial CTA showed distal opacification but marked popliteal artery deformation, whereas repeat CTA on day 24 revealed complete occlusion. These findings underscore that severe knee-region trauma with popliteal artery deformation warrants early vascular team involvement and serial objective assessment. If the ankle-brachial index or skin perfusion pressure cannot be obtained, toe pressure, repeated bedside Doppler examination, duplex ultrasonography, repeat CTA, or intraoperative angiography should be considered.

Second, IVUS guidance is useful for crossing a severely deformed traumatic artery. In this case, angiography alone could not identify the true vessel course. Although the first guidewire reached the distal true lumen, its proximal course was unfavorable and prevented passage of the IVUS catheter. Parallel IVUS-guided rewiring enabled the selection of a more central route and confirmed lesion morphology. The large thrombus burden also supported the decision that balloon angioplasty alone would be insufficient.

Third, interwoven nitinol stenting may be useful when durable scaffolding is required for selected traumatic popliteal occlusions. Open revascularization, particularly autologous vein bypass when feasible, remains the conventional treatment, and it has led to excellent limb salvage outcomes.6 Endovascular repair is increasingly used for selected superficial femoral and popliteal artery injuries, but its long-term durability remains a concern. In one traumatic popliteal series, limb salvage approached 89%, whereas primary patency declined to approximately 56% at 4 years.2,3

Stent implantation in the popliteal artery remains controversial because this segment is exposed to repetitive flexion, compression, elongation, and torsion during knee motion. Here, a 2.0-mm balloon restored antegrade flow but provided limited luminal gain in a deformed artery with a large thrombus burden. Therefore, an interwoven nitinol stent was selected to maintain perfusion for wound healing while avoiding further balloon upsizing before scaffolding. Mechanical bench testing has demonstrated favorable deformation characteristics of the Supera Stent under axial compression, bending, radial compression, and torsion.7 Clinical studies of Supera Stent implantation for isolated atherosclerotic popliteal artery lesions have reported acceptable mid-term patency and low fracture rates.8,9 Although the data for traumatic lesions remain limited, complete wound healing and patency for >2.5 years suggest that this strategy may be useful in carefully selected patients.

Conclusions

IVUS-guided endovascular repair using an interwoven nitinol stent restored perfusion and supported wound healing in a patient with delayed traumatic popliteal artery occlusion. This strategy may be a feasible option for carefully selected patients who require durable scaffolding, although open repair remains the standard treatment and long-term surveillance is mandatory.
