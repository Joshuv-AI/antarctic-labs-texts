Differential expression of microRNAs in the prefrontal cortex as indicators of sheep welfare
Authors: Sarah Babington, Luoyang Ding, Alan J Tilbrook, Shane K Maloney, Dominique Blache
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndicators that are used to assess animal welfare tend to be non-specific and provide limited information on how a situation is experienced by an animal. There is growing evidence that microRNAs could be useful biomarkers of brain function and experience in humans, however, there has been limited work evaluating their use for assessing experience, and thus the welfare of animals. In this study, we evaluated microRNAs as indicators of changes in the welfare state of sheep. We induced differences in the experience and coping capacity of 34 female Merino sheep in response to a psychological challenge of isolation at the start and end of a period of three dietary manipulation treatments. The first group was fed to maintain liveweight constant during the three periods (n = 11); the second group was fed to increase liveweight in period 1, keep liveweight constant in period 2, and decrease liveweight in period 3 (n = 12); and the third group was fed to decrease liveweight in period 1, keep liveweight constant in period 2, and increase liveweight in period 3 (n = 11). Next generation sequencing identified seven mature miRNAs that were differently expressed between the sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements and those that were fed at maintenance.ResultsThe sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements had an increase in the expression of oar-miR-329a-3p, oar-miR-329b-3p, and oar-miR-485-5p, and a decrease in the expression of oar-miR-106b, oar-miR-1185-5p, oar-miR-21, and oar-miR-26b compared to the sheep that were fed at maintenance. These differentially expressed microRNA from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex were enriched in pathways that are associated with gene expression, intracellular signalling, and neuronal processes, which could provide insight into the mechanisms of metabolism, and possibly the welfare state of sheep.ConclusionsFurther research is needed to validate the differently expressed miRNA as indicators of welfare given the lack of difference in expression levels between the sheep fed above and below their maintenance requirements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05464-1.Keywords: Sheep, microRNA, NGS, Animal welfare, Stress

Background

The welfare of an animal is largely determined by how they experience a situation which is influenced by internal and external factors [1]. When it comes to the assessment of animal welfare a combination of neurobiological, physiological, and behavioural indicators can be used to provide information on how an animal responses to a situation and infer their welfare state [2]. However, these indicators tend to be non-specific and provide limited information on the experience of the animal during and after exposure to a stimulus [3]. To date, no indicators provide specific insight into the experience of an animal. Indicators that are correlated to brain processes that are responsible for the generation of experience in an animal could provide a new approach to the assessment of animal welfare [3].

Among the candidate indicators of animal experience are non-coding RNAs, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), which are promising because they have links to brain function and mental state in humans [4, 5]. Non-coding RNAs make up the majority of the RNAs that are transcribed in the mammalian genome, with only a minor part of the transcriptome consisting of RNAs that code for functional polypeptides and proteins [6]. MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs (19–23 nucleotides) that can affect gene expression post-transcriptionally by causing degradation or inhibition of the translation of messenger RNA into protein-coding genes [7]. MiRNAs have an important role in the control of biological and cellular functions, as well as being implicated in several disease conditions [8]. The role of miRNAs linked to brain function suggest that they could provide insight into the early molecular mechanisms that are associated with the different welfare states in animals. MiRNAs are also measurable in samples from peripheral biological fluids, making them good candidates as biomarkers because they can be measured using minimally invasive methods [9].

The expression of miRNAs in the brain changes dynamically in response to internal and external stimuli, and they have a role in neurodevelopment, neurogenesis, and neuroprotective mechanisms [10]. Several miRNAs have been identified as promising biomarkers for various conditions that are associated with both positive and negative states in human and other animals, including depression (miR-134, miR-19b, miR-135), stress (miR-9, miR-30e-5p, miR-186-5p), pain (miR-21, miR-23b-3p, miR-134), and reward (miR-124, miR-16, miR-181) [11–13]. More specific to animal welfare, changes in the expression of miRNAs have been associated with negative welfare states like pain and stress in lambs after castration and tail docking [14], as well as transportation [15]. The nutritional and reproductive status of sheep has also been shown to impact the expression of certain miRNAs in the brain [16, 17].

The most informative indicators of the welfare state of animals will likely have links to areas in the brain that are associated with the process responsible for generating experience and emotion. One such area in the brain is the prefrontal cortex which can be divided functionally into the dorsolateral and ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Both of these areas have been implicated in the control and processing of emotions in human [18]. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is associated with cognitive control and modulating emotions [19], and is involved in the activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis in response to stress [20]. Additionally, the expression of miRNAs in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex has been shown to is altered in humans with major depressive disorder [21]. The ventromedial prefrontal cortex has been implicated in emotional processing through value assigning and decision-making functions [19].

In the present study, the expression of miRNAs was measured in samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex that were obtained from sheep during a previous experiment [22]. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex was chosen because this area of the brain is involved in cognitive control, decision making processes, and emotional processing [19]. In the previous experiment, differences in the experience and coping capacity were induced in the sheep by exposing them to two challenge paradigms. First, sheep were exposed to a long-term challenge using three dietary treatments because deviations from energy homeostasis can influence the experience and motivation of sheep [23, 24]. Second, the same sheep were exposed to a short-term psychological challenge of isolation that is known to induce a stress response in sheep [25]. For this present study, we tested the hypothesis that the expression of specific miRNAs would be associated with changes in the welfare state of sheep.

Methods

Animal ethics approval

The experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The University of Western Australia (2020/ET000194) and conducted in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes (8th Edition, 2013). The sheep used in the present study were sourced from a flock kept at The University of Western Australia farm.

Animals and experimental model

The samples used in the present study were collected during a larger experiment that was designed to induce a positive or negative experience in sheep by manipulating their feed over three distinct periods [22]. The experiment was conducted at The University of Western Australia, Shenton Park Field Station (31.9° S, 115.8° E) from the end of October 2021 until the start of March 2022 (during spring and summer). Briefly, 34 female Merino sheep were allocated to one of three treatment groups based on liveweight and temperament [22]. The sheep were between one and two years of age and sourced from a flock that was kept at The University of Western Australia farm (Pingelly, Western Australia, 32.5° S, 117.1° E). The first group was fed to maintain liveweight constant during the three periods (n = 11); the second group was fed to increase liveweight in period 1, keep liveweight constant in period 2, and decrease liveweight in period 3 (n = 12); and the third group was fed to decrease liveweight in period 1, keep liveweight constant in period 2, and increase liveweight in period 3 (n = 11). The coping capacity of each sheep was assessed by exposing them to an isolation box test [26] that acted as a psychological challenge at the start and end of each period of feed manipulation. The isolation box test involved moving each sheep into a wooden box (H: 1.5 m x L: 1.5 m x W: 0.75 m) for one minute, during which they were visually and physically isolated from their conspecifics.

At the time that the samples were collected for the present study, the sheep in the three treatment groups had been fed at maintenance (control, n = 10), fed at a maximum of 20% above their maintenance requirements (above, n = 12), or fed at a maximum of 20% below their maintenance requirements (below, n = 12) for six weeks (Fig. 1). The maintenance requirement of each sheep was calculated using the following equation: Metabolic energy for maintenance (MJ/day) = (0.09 × liveweight) + 1.4 (e.g., 50 kg sheep = 5.9 MJ/day) [27]. Each sheep was housed in an individual pen in a shed with visual contact with conspecifics and provided water ad-libitum. The sheep were fed twice a day with a 70:30 oaten chaff and lupin mix 9.0 MJ/kg, 114 g/kg of crude protein, and 888 g/kg of dry matter) and a mineral powder supplement (Topstock All-Season Minerals, Narrogin WA, Aus).

Overview of the experimental timeline for the present study. The line graph shows the change in bodyweight of the sheep during the last period of feed manipulation from the larger experiment. The sheep were in three treatment groups in which they were either fed at maintenance (control), at a maximum of 20% above their maintenance requirements (increasing), or at a maximum of 20% below their maintenance requirements (decreasing) over six weeks. The coping capacity of the sheep was evaluated at the start and end of the feed manipulation period when the sheep were exposed to a psychological challenge (isolation box test, IBT). At the end of the feed manipulation period, samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex were collected 60 min after the IBT and once each sheep had been sacrificed to measure for microRNAs

Sample collection and processing

At the end of the period of feed manipulation, at 60 min after the IBT, each sheep was sacrificed using an injection of Lethabarb® (Virbac, Aus). Samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex were collected within 15 min of confirmation of death, by cutting a small section of the tissue with a scalpel blade and immediately transferring the sample into a cryotube. Each sample was then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processed.

Expression of microRNA in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex using next generation sequencing

Samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex were used for next generation sequencing of miRNAs.

RNA extraction for sequencing

The total RNA was extracted from the samples of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex from each sheep using a RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Cat.74804, Qiagen, Aus) and stainless-steel beads (Cat.69989, Qiagen, Aus) with the TissueLyser LT (Cat.85600, Qiagen, Aus) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted total RNA samples was measured using a NanoDrop® ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Further quality checks were performed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) at the Australian Genome Research Facility (Perth WA, Aus). The results for the quality assessment of the RNA samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex that were submitted for miRNA sequencing are included in Supporting information material 1.

Library construction and sequencing

Library construction and sequencing was performed by the Monash Genomics and Bioinformatics Platform (Clayton Campus, Melbourne Vic, Aus). The total RNA samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex that had a RIN score > 6.5 were used for library construction. Quantitation of the samples was performed on a Qubit instrument (ThermoFisher Scientific, Aus) with RNA High Sensitivity reagents. RNA sizing and quality measurement was performed on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, USA) with RNA Pico reagents, configured for Eukaryote Total RNA Pico analysis.

Next generation sequencing (NGS) library generation was performed using a NEXTFLEX Small RNA Sequencing Kit V4 (with UDIs) chemistry (manufacturer at the time was Perkin Elmer Genomics, now rebranded as Revvity, Aus), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Each input sample was normalised to 1.5 ug, and 14 cycles of PCR amplification were used. The NGS libraries were pooled in equimolar ratio and sequenced at Azenta on the NovaSeq X (Illumina, USA) instrument, in paired-end, 150 bp configuration.

MicroRNA identification and differential expression analysis

Raw reads from the NGS libraries were processed using the nf-core/smrnaseq pipeline (v2.3.0) [28], using mature and hairpin miRNA sequences for Ovis aries downloaded from miRCarta (v1.1) [29, 30] and the Oar_v4.0 (GCF_000298735.2) reference genome [31]. Briefly, the nf-core/smrnaseq pipeline trims adapters and low quality ends from reads using fastp (v0.23.4) [32] and aligns reads to mature and hairpin (precursor) miRNA sequences for quantification using Bowtie (v1.3.1) [33]. miRDeep2 (v2.0.1) [34] was used for discovery and quantification of unannotated novel miRNAs, and quantification of annotated miRNAs from miRCarta. Read quality was assessed using FastQC (0.12.1) [35] and along with alignment statistics was summarized using MultiQC [36]. Only the R1 reads were used for mapping and quantification because these are sufficient length to capture mature and precursor miRNAs.

Differential expression of the miRNAs was analysed using Degust [37] and R-studio, using voom [38] and limma [39] with sample quality weighting (voomWithQualityWeights) [40], using trimmed-mean of means for effective estimation of the library size. The date that sheep were sacrificed and the date of extraction were included in the linear model to account for batch effects, and all three of the treatment groups were compared. The GenomicRanges package [41] was used to prepare annotations and counts from miRDeep2 for downstream analysis of the differential expression of the miRNAs.

Target gene prediction, enrichment, and pathway analysis

The target genes of the differentially expressed mature miRNAs were predicted using the equivalent human miRNAs in the miRWalk database (version 3) [42]. The sequence of each of the miRNAs that had significant differences in expression levels between the treatment groups were entered into the miRBase database [30] to identify the equivalent human miRNAs based on a perfect sequence match. The target genes from the differentially expressed miRNAs were analysed using DAVID [43, 44] and Enrichr [45–47] for enriched gene ontology (GO) and Reactome pathway [48]. The P-value was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate method [49] and P ≤ 0.05 was taken to indicate that a GO and Reactome pathway term was significantly enriched.

Figures

Figures were created using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

MicroRNA sequencing and library construction from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex

Samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of each sheep were used for the construction of 34 miRNA sequencing libraries. The sequenced miRNA libraries ranged in depths from 5 to 50 million reads however, the majority of the samples had a sufficient depth of > 20 million for mapping based quantification. After the trimming of adapters and low-quality sequences, 8.5 ± 3.3% (mean ± standard deviation) of reads remained with the majority of the sequences being 22 base pairs long. The remaining reads were mapped against the Ovis aries reference genome (GCF_000298735.2_Oar_v4.0) in which ~ 97% of the total reads aligned. Following alignment with the reference genome and using the miRCarta database, ~ 35–40% of the reads mapped to mature miRNAs in sheep and of the remaining unmapped reads ~ 15–20% mapped to hairpin (precursor) miRNAs. The detailed results for the quality control of the sequenced miRNA are included in Supporting information material 2.

Identification of known and ‘novel’ microRNAs in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex

From the 34 sequenced miRNA libraries a total of 144 mature miRNAs and 100 hairpin (precursor) miRNAs were identified and annotated as known miRNAs in sheep. Previously unannotated ‘novel’ miRNAs in sheep were predicted from the miRNA libraries using miRDeep2 compared against RumimiR [50], which represented the best set of annotated and predicted miRNAs in sheep. Only high confidence predicted miRNAs, with 95% or greater probability and a significant randfold P-value ≤ 0.05 were used for the comparison. There were 502 miRNAs that were identified as putative miRNAs that may not have previously been reported. To identify the most likely unannotated ‘novel’ miRNAs the previously unreported miRNAs were filtered where at least one of the sample libraries had > 10 counts, and all 34 sample libraries had non-zero counts. Filtering resulted identified six miRNAs in the samples that were likely ‘novel’ and previously unannotated (Table 1).

Differentially expressed known microRNAs in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex

Analysis for differential expression was performed on the annotated 144 mature and 100 hairpin (precursor) miRNAs from miRCarta v1.1 (includes all miRBase and some predicted miRNAs) with reference genome GCF_000298735.2_Oar_v4.0. The analysis of the mature miRNAs identified that seven miRNAs were differentially expressed between the sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements and those that were fed at maintenance, with corrected P-values that ranged from 0.024 to 0.026 (Table 2). Of the differentially expressed miRNAs, there was a significant increase in the expression of oar-miR-329a-3p, oar-miR-329b-3p, and oar-miR-485-5p, and a significant decrease in the expression of oar-miR-106b, oar-miR-1185-5p, oar-miR-21, and oar-miR-26b in the sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements compared to those that were fed at maintenance (Fig. 2). A detailed list of the known mature and hairpin (precursor) miRNAs that were detected and the differential expression analysis between each treatment group is included in Supporting information material 3.

Differentially expressed ‘novel’ microRNAs in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex

Analysis for differential expression was also performed on unannotated ‘novel’ miRNAs from using miRDeep2. Of the six ‘novel’ miRNAs that were identified, NC_019475.2:19,935,580–19935641(+) on chromosome 18 was significantly decreased in expression by 0.8 times in the sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements than those that were fed at maintenance, with a corrected P-value 0.034. The BLAST search on RumimiR identified that the sequence existed as seven other miRNAs in sheep as Rum-oar-01704, Rum-oar-05468, Rum-oar-00198, Rum-oar-05179, Rum-oar-01703, Rum-oar-00197, Rum-oar-05467. However, these other miRNAs were annotated to different chromosome locations (chromosome 1: ~ 104,852,762- 104,852,822, and chromosome 5: ~ 85,471,083- 85,471,142). Target prediction and enrichment pathway analysis of differentially expressed miRNAs in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex

For the prediction and analysis of the enriched pathway for the seven mature miRNA that showed significant differences in expression, we used the equivalent human miRNAs due to the limited availability of literature on the target genes for sheep miRNA. The miRBase database [30] was used to identify the equivalent human miRNAs based on having a perfect match to the sequence of each of the miRNA that were differentially expressed in the sheep. Then target gene prediction was performed separately for the miRNAs that had significant increases or decreases in expression using miRWalk. Enrichment analysis of the miRNAs that were increased in expression identified biological processes involved in gene expression, developmental processes, cellular signalling, and protein localization mechanisms (Table 3). In the miRNAs that were decreased in expression the enriched biological processes were associated with modulating transcription, cellular processes, and protein localization. The Reactome Pathway analysis showed that pathways involved with signal transduction, intracellular signalling, and neuronal processes were enriched in the miRNAs that were increased in expression (Table 3), whereas the only pathway enriched in the miRNAs that were decreased in expression (Table 4) was associated with gene expression.

Similar to the results of DAVID, enrichment analysis with Enrichr showed that the enriched biological processes in the miRNAs that were increased in expression in the sheep fed below their maintenance requirements were involved in controlling gene expression, developmental processes, and cellular and synaptic processes (Table 3). The only biological process shown to be enriched in the miRNAs that were decreased in expression were involved in protein localization mechanisms (Table 4). The Reactome Pathway analysis using Enrichr showed the enriched pathways of the miRNAs that were increased in expression to be associated with signal transduction, neuronal and synaptic processes, and cellular transport mechanisms (Table 3). The miRNAs that were decreased in expression were enriched in pathways associated with the response of the immune system, gene expression, and neuronal processes (Table 4). Together the results from DAVID and Enrichr indicate the mature miRNAs that had significant differences expression were involved in gene expression, neuronal processes, and the maintenance of cellular functions.

Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the expression of miRNAs as possible indicators of the welfare state of sheep after it was altered by a dietary and psychological challenge. We identified miRNAs that were differentially expressed in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements compared to those that were fed at maintenance, which could be indicative of differences in their metabolic status and welfare state. In contrast, we identified no significant difference in the expression of the identified miRNAs in the sheep that were fed above their maintenance requirements compared to those that were fed below, or at, maintenance. These results should be interpreted while taking into consideration the feeding history of the sheep from the other experiment whereby the feeding treatments were swapped (see Materials and method section – Animals and experimental model [22]).

In the samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of the sheep fed below their maintenance requirements, seven mature miRNAs showed significant differences in expression compared to the sheep that were fed at maintenance. These miRNAs that had a significant difference in the level of expression were linked to pathways associated with modulating genetic expression, various cellular processes, and the neuronal system. A mechanistic link between the differentially expressed miRNAs and the neuronal system could be related to the role of the prefrontal cortex in self-control and motivation as it relates to food consumption and cravings, as seen in studies in human and rodents [51]. In our study however, we did not evaluate self-control or motivation as it related to eating because the feed of each sheep was purposefully manipulated to alter their body condition and hence welfare state. Moderate feed restriction has been demonstrated to have antidepressant effects in studies in human and rodents [52]. We postulated in our study that the sheep that were fed above their maintenance requirements would cope better and have a more positive experience from the psychological challenge than would the sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements, which would cope worse and have a more negative experience. However, the differences in the expression of miRNAs in the dorsolateral cortex of the sheep indicated that the feed restriction may not have affected the experience and coping capacity of the sheep in the negative way that we expected.

We observed an increase in the expression of oar-miR-329a-3p, oar-miR-329b-3p, and oar-miR-485-5p, and a decrease in the expression of oar-miR-106b, oar-miR-1185-5p, oar-miR-21, and oar-miR-26b in the sheep that were fed below their maintenance requirements compared to those that were fed at maintenance. These results suggest that the expression of these miRNAs could be related to the negative metabolic status of the underfed sheep and possibly the experience that was associated with that metabolic status. Few studies have investigated the effect of metabolic status on the expression of miRNAs, especially in ruminants. In hypothalamic tissue from female sheep that were fed a control (normal ratio and ad lib alfalfa hay) or a high-nutrient (supplement fed with high nutrient content) diet, eight mature and 140 novel miRNAs were identified that had significant differences in expression [16, 17]. None of the same miRNAs that were identified in that Yang et al., (2018) study were identified in our present study, and it is difficult to compare when the dietary treatments differed greatly. In the mammary tissue of lactating Holstein cows, 27 miRNAs showed significant differences in expression after six days of feed restriction at 50% of the metabolic needs of the cows [53]. The expression of miR-26b was higher in the cows after the feed restriction [53]. Although this study used mammary tissue and had a shorter period and more severe degree of feed restriction, these results and ours suggest that miR-26b may play a role in modulating the metabolism in ruminants following feed restriction.

The differential expression of miRNAs in the sheep that were fed below maintenance compared to the lack of difference in those that were fed above their maintenance could indicate that feed restriction had a more significant effect on the expression of miRNAs than did feeding at or above their maintenance requirements. In response to feed restriction, sheep, particularly ewes, metabolically adapt through several complex signalling pathways and bodily systems [54]. The mobilization of body reserves and associated physiological mechanisms are a relatively immediate response to a negative energy balance, whereas the response to refeeding after a period of restriction can take several weeks [55]. The differences in the expression of the miRNAs in the sheep fed below their maintenance requirements compared to those fed at maintenance may be reflective of mechanisms associated with maintaining energy homeostasis during feed restriction. In addition, the sheep fed above their maintenance requirements may have been still recovering from their previous period of dietary restriction and hence there was no difference in the expression of the miRNA profile compared to the sheep that were fed at maintenance or those on a restricted diet (see Materials and method section – Animals and experimental model [22]).

Although our results show that miRNAs could be possible indicators of changes in the metabolic status and welfare state of sheep, the design of the study could have impacted the interpretation of our results. The present study was part of a larger experiment in which the feeding history of the sheep in each treatment group was different. Therefore, the sheep that were fed above their maintenance requirements in this study may have still been recovering from their previous period of feed restriction during the first period of feed manipulation in the larger experiment. The changing in metabolic status after a period of restriction could have affected the expression of the miRNAs masking differences that otherwise may have been present if the feed treatments had not been swapped during the larger experiment. For the experimental period the sheep were mostly housed in individual pens with visual access to their co-specifics and provided with periods to exercise in groups at least once a week. It is possible that this housing environment could have had an impact on the welfare state of the sheep and hence impacted the expression of miRNAs. Additionally, in this study the timing of sampling to measure the miRNAs was limited to one-time point for the samples from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex even though the expression of miRNAs is recognised to be able to dynamically change in response to a short-term challenge.

Conclusion

In summary, our study provides evidence that the expression of miRNAs was associated with the metabolic status of sheep in response to a long-term metabolic challenge, and possibly linked to changes in their welfare state from a short-term psychological challenge. While our results are encouraging, it is necessary to further investigate the dynamics of the response of these candidate miRNA indicators and further validate them with other types of challenges that induce stronger positive and negative welfare states.
