Biopsy-based Transcriptomics in Banff 2022 Antibody Mediated Rejection Categories
Authors: Petra Hruba, Eva Girmanova, Dusan Harmacek, Marek Novotny, Vladimir Hanzal, Katerina Jaklova, Kerstin Hübel, Elena Rho, Kai Castrezana Lopez, Florian Westphal, Ariana Gaspert, Brigit Maria Helmchen, Seraina von Moos, Lukas Weidmann, Martin Kment, Ludek Voska, Thomas Schachtner, Ondrej Viklicky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBiopsy-based transcriptomics (BBT) supports antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) diagnostics, but Banff 2022 probable AMR and donor-specific antibodies (DSA)−/C4d−microvascular inflammation (MVI) remain unvalidated by BBT.MethodsWe analyzed 562 kidney allograft biopsies from Prague and Zurich by histology and Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System (MMDx). Biopsies were classified into the following: (i) active AMR-spectrum lesions without transplant glomerulopathy (cg) (active AMR, probable AMR, MVI, DSA-/C4-); (ii) combined active–chronic lesions with cg (chronic-active AMR, probable AMR with cg, MVI, DSA-/C4d- with cg); (iii) chronic AMR, defined by cg without active lesions; and (iv) controls without rejection.ResultsMolecular AMR was detected in 24% of probable AMR and 23% of probable AMR with cg biopsies, 43% of MVI, DSA-/C4d-, 51% of active AMR, 56% of MVI, DSA-/C4d- with cg, and 63% of chronic-active AMR, but was rare in chronic AMR (6%). AMR probability classifier (AMRprob) scores were higher in probable AMR than in controls and similar between chronic AMR and controls. AMRprob scores were comparable among active AMR, chronic-active AMR, and MVI, DSA-/C4d- groups, all of which exhibited elevated T cell-mediated rejection (TCMR) prob scores compared with probable AMR and controls.Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-penalized Cox regression predicting 3-years graft survival trained in the Zurich cohort showed similar performance of molecular (cgprob, ctprob, tprob, acute kidney injury [AKI]score) and histologic model (Banff cg, interstitial inflammation [ci], tubular atrophy [ct], and tubulitis [t]) (concordance 0.84 vs. 0.82), with the external validation in the Prague cohort (0.78 vs. 0.75).ConclusionBBT primarily reflects MVI rather than DSA and is particularly informative in probable AMR and MVI-positive biopsies.

MVI defines most AMR findings in kidney allografts, the leading cause of long-term kidney allograft failure.1 The updated Banff classification introduced novel subcategories, such as probable AMR with subthreshold MVI scores and MVI without both DSA and C4d positivity.2,3 Recently, Sablik and colleagues in a large, multicenter study demonstrated that biopsies with MVI encompasses phenotypes with diverse disease progression and allograft outcomes.4 Therefore, different pathophysiological mechanisms may play a role among those histological categories that need to be further explored. BBT enables more precise molecular phenotyping of AMR5,6 and allows for the detailed study of biological processes in grafted tissue.

Recently, an increasing number of centers have implemented BBT in routine post-transplant care to improve rejection diagnostics and therapy.7, 8, 9 AMR represents a rejection category in which BBT has been frequently indicated by clinicians because of assessment complexity, interobserver variability, and heterogeneity of methods to detect DSA.10 Tissue-based bulk transcriptomics using a validated microarray platform reveals several AMR categories that differ in graft prognostication.5,11 Molecular fully-developed and late antibody-mediated rejections showed impaired graft survival, whereas grafts with molecular early-antibody mediated rejection (EABMR) experienced better outcomes. Before the last Banff update,2 several transcriptomic studies identified molecular rejection in cases that met only 2 AMR criteria and lacked DSA.12,13

BBT might be helpful, especially in subthreshold rejection categories such as probable AMR and newly categorized MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative.12, 13, 14, 15 Similarly, chronic endothelial damage, represented by double contours of the glomerular basement membrane (cg) as an irreversible lesion, may represent a therapeutic dilemma particularly in the context of concomitant rejection-related lesions.16 Therefore, more insights into molecular diagnostics of AMR, especially in late biopsies with already established chronic lesions17,18 or in those from donors with advanced age, are of clinical importance.

In this study from 2 transplant centers, Prague and Zurich, we evaluated kidney transplant biopsies using both histologic and biopsy-based transcriptomic methods to— (i) assess the molecular signatures associated with each Banff 2022 AMR subtype with focus on activity/chronicity and (ii) determine their prognostic implications for graft survival.

Methods

Total Cohort and Biopsy Processing

Between 2018 and 2025, a total of 755 kidney allograft biopsies with both histological and MMDx assessments were available from Zurich (n = 452) and Prague (n = 303). From the final analyzed cohort (n = 562), the majority of biopsies were performed as indication biopsies (82%, Table 119,20). At the Institute for Clinical and Experimental Medicine (IKEM), protocol biopsies were routinely performed at 3 months after transplantation (66/222, 29.7%), with an additional 10/222 surveillance biopsies after treatment. At the Zurich center, protocol biopsies were performed only in ABO-incompatible transplantations (11/340), at 3 and 12 months after transplantation, and further 13 biopsies were performed as follow-up biopsies. All biopsies were evaluated according to the Banff 2022 classification criteria2 by local pathologists, and biopsy-based transcriptomics were assessed by MMDx. In Zurich, MMDx assessments were performed at Kashi Clinical Laboratories (Portland, Oregon, USA) for some biopsies obtained between 2018 and 2021 and for all biopsies obtained after 2021, or at Alberta Transplant Applied Genomics Centre (Edmonton, Canada) for some biopsies obtained between 2018 and 2021. Biopsies from Prague were assessed at the in-house IKEM laboratory since 2022. All laboratories used identical standard operation protocols for RNA isolation and RNA processing for microarray, and the same MMDx algorithm based on a reference set of 120811 biopsies, which enables comparisons. In Zurich, virtually all biopsies with sufficient material were subjected to MMDx analysis. In contrast to Zurich, the Prague center applied MMDx selectively to biopsies based on the following clinical indications: subthreshold rejection categories, TCMR, AMR, and in situations in which histologic findings did not explain graft dysfunction. MMDx was used to refine histology and results were considered along with DSA and clinical findings to make treatment decisions; however, solely MMDx was not used to determine whether or how patients were treated. Selection of the Study Cohort and Group Definition

All biopsies with histological findings of recurrent glomerulonephritis and cases with missing g, cg, and/or ptc lesions were excluded (Figure 121). Peritubular capillaritis ≥ 1 in the absence of glomerulitis was classified as MVI provided that borderline changes, TCMR, pyelonephritis, or BK virus nephropathy were not present. The final study cohort consisted of a total of 562 biopsies, as follows: 177 biopsies with histologically proven AMR, 71 biopsies with MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative, 77 biopsies with probable AMR based on Banff 2022 criteria,2 and 237 biopsies without rejection. AMR cases were characterized when MVI ≥ 2 was present in biopsies with C4d positivity evaluated by immunofluorescence and/or DSA, or when MVI = 1 in C4d ≥ 2 biopsies independent of DSA status. Probable AMR was defined as MVI < 2 in DSA-positive patients, or acute tubular necrosis with C4d ≥ 2 in sensitized patients during the early post-transplant period. The MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative group was defined as MVI ≥ 2 in the absence of both DSA and C4d staining. The biopsies showing features of AMR were further categorized based on the presence of transplant glomerulopathy (cg) and/or g+ptc ≥ 1 into the following (i) active lesions only (probable AMR, active AMR, and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative), (ii) both active and chronic lesions, including probable AMR with cg, chronic-active AMR, and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg, and (iii) chronic lesions only, defined as chronic AMR.

DSA

In both centers, DSA (HLA specific) were detected by local laboratories using a Luminex single antigen bead assay (One Lambda, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with an arbitrary threshold for positivity defined as a mean fluorescence intensity of 500 for the purpose of the study.

Investigations and Molecular Definitions

The primary objective of this study was the correlation between histological Banff 2022 AMR subcategories and molecular sign-outs generated by the MMDx analysis. Eight categories of molecular sign-outs were defined (no-rejection, minor ABMR, minor TCMR, early AMR (EABMR), fully-developed AMR (FABMR), late AMR (LABMR), TCMR, and mixed) based on a reference set of 1208 biopsies11 and generated based on archetype analysis and binary classifiers, following predefined criteria.22

In addition, AMR-related classifiers (AMR probability classifier ABMRprob) and molecular scores for glomerulitis, transplant glomerulopathy, and peritubular capillaritis) and TCMR-related classifiers (TCMRprob and molecular scores for interstitial inflammation and tubulitis) derived from the MMDx platform were compared among histologic diagnoses. Furthermore, AKI scores, inflammation score, and atrophy-fibrosis scores were evaluated.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio (version 4.2.2, Posit PBC, Boston, MA).23 Continuous data were reported as median (interquartile range), whereas categorical data were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). The chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for hypothesis testing where appropriate. Survival analysis was performed by the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences between histological subgroups compared using the log-rank test. For survival analysis, repeated biopsies were excluded (n = 101) and the first findings of subthreshold or definite AMR were used. Additionally, biopsies from patients whose grafts failed because of primary graft nonfunction within the first week were excluded (n = 6). To investigate the prognostic value of histological versus molecular scores in predicting allograft survival, we built 2 separate Cox proportional hazards models— one based exclusively on Banff histological scores and the other on molecular scores. The primary outcome was time to graft failure, with follow-up censored at 3 years. Univariable Cox regression was used to calculate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to measure effect size. When building multivariable models, because of the limited number of events (43 graft failures), we employed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator penalized Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation to reduce model complexity and prevent overfitting using the glmnet package in R Studio.24 We included 12 predefined molecular scores derived from the MMDx, including composite scores (e.g., AMR score, rejection score) and probability scores for specific lesions (e.g., g, ptc, cg). To standardize the interpretation of Banff versus molecular scores, all molecular scores were rescaled such that HRs reflected the effect per 0.25-unit increase. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was selected to retain 4 predictors for each model. In the Banff histological model, we assessed 11 individual Banff histological lesion scores as candidate predictors in a separate least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-Cox model. The Zurich cohort was used as the training dataset (n = 271), and the IKEM cohort (n = 183) served as an independent external validation cohort. The final molecular and Banff models were refitted using standard Cox regression with the selected variables. Model performance in the external IKEM cohort was assessed by calculating the Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and 95% CIs estimated using 1000 bootstrap replicates. In the validation cohort, risk scores were predicted using the linear predictors from the Zurich-trained least absolute shrinkage and selection operator models. A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The final cohort of 562 kidney transplant biopsies consisted of AMR, probable AMR, MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative, and biopsies without evidence of any rejection according to Banff 2022 criteria.2 The biopsies were further stratified according to the presence or absence of transplant glomerulopathy (cg) as a marker of chronicity. The median time from transplant to biopsy was increased significantly with present cg, ranging from 3.3 to 13 months in active AMR, probable AMR, and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative, to 83 to 130 months in chronic-active AMR, MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg and chronic AMR subgroups. The highest activity and chronicity indices19,20 were present in chronic-active AMR and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg, indicating persistent inflammation in chronic-active lesions. Detailed characteristics of each subgroup and Banff histology lesions are presented in Table 1.

In cases with cg = 0, MMDx identified pure molecular AMR (EABMR, FABMR, LABMR, or minor ABMR) in 24% of probable AMR (12/51), 43% of MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative (15/35), and 51% of active AMR (24/47). In cases with cg > 0, molecular AMR was detected in 23% of probable AMR (6/26), 56% of MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative (20/36), and 63% of chronic-active AMR (62/99). In contrast, molecular AMR was rare in chronic AMR (6.5%, 2/31) and comparable to the no-rejection group (14%, 33/237, Figure 2b, d).

Discrepant Findings: Positive Histology with Negative MMDx

To evaluate clinical utility of BBT, an analysis of discrepant cases was performed. There were 60 cases classified by histology as AMR or MVI, DSA-negative C4d-negative, however not as rejection by MMDx (Supplementary Table S1). Interestingly, in 7 cases, MVI was most likely attributable to underlying nonrejection pathology; however 2 grafts failed. In 4 patients, negative MMDx results were observed in surveillance biopsies following AMR treatment, and 1 graft failed early post-transplant. Three patients showed strong C4d positivity (C4d3) with MVI0 or MVI1 in biopsies; none experienced graft failure, and kidney graft function remained stable. In 26 cases, MVI was driven exclusively by glomerulitis without accompanying peritubular capillaritis, of these 24 patients had stable graft function in the follow-up, including 9 cases without rejection treatment; 2 grafts failed. Across the entire cohort, isolated glomerulitis (g1–3, ptc0) was significantly more frequent in discrepant compared with nondiscrepant biopsies (52% vs. 13%, P < 0.001). This association was consistent across diagnostic categories and reached statistical significance in MVI, DSA negative, C4d negative, active and chronic-active AMR, supporting a strong link between g without ptc and histologic–molecular discordance (Supplementary Table S2). In further 18 cases the discrepancy between histology and MMDx remained unclear, and 4 grafts failed.

The median molecular TCMRprob was below the diagnostic threshold across all analyzed biopsies; however, in 33 of 562 biopsies (5.8%) positive TCMR molecular call (defined as TCMR or minor TCMR sign-out) was found. In 10 out of 33 biopsies with molecular TCMR the mixed rejection in histology was found (TCMR or borderline plus AMR or MVI, DSA-negative, C4d-negative). In another 3 cases the probable AMR was accompanied with TCMR or borderline. Molecular scores for glomerulitis (gprob) and peritubular capillaritis (ptcprob) were significantly higher in active and chronic-active AMR and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative groups compared with probable AMR, chronic AMR and no-rejection groups (all p < 0.001).

The molecular transplant glomerulopathy (cgprob) score was significantly higher in both chronic-active AMR (P < 0.05) and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg groups (P < 0.01) compared with active AMR and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative without cg, reflecting transplant glomerular injury. Surprisingly, cgprob was low in chronic AMR, with no difference from the no-rejection group (P = 0.56).

The molecular inflammation score was highest in MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative cases, both with and without cg, and in active AMR. The AKI molecular score peaked in the MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative groups, with no significant difference between the active and chronic active subgroups (P = 0.62).

Surprisingly, chronic AMR exhibited low molecular fibrosis scores, similar to those of no-rejection, active AMR, MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative cases.

Biopsies classified as probable AMR showed significantly higher Rejectionprob, AMRprob, ptcprob, gprob compared with the no-rejection group. In contrast, chronic AMR were similar to no-rejection at the level of individual molecular rejection classifiers.

Death-Censored Graft Survival in Histological AMR Subcategories

Three-year death-censored graft survival post-biopsy differed significantly among AMR histologic subcategories (P < 0.0001, log-rank test) (Supplementary Figure S2, Supplementary Table S5). Compared with the no-rejection group, impaired graft survival was observed particularly in MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg (HR: 9.15, 95% CI: 3.41–24.57, P = 1. 13×10-5), chronic-active AMR (HR: 4.85, 95% CI: 1.94–12.16, P = 7. 57×10-4), chronic AMR (HR: 3.74, 95% CI: 1.09–12.78, P = 0.035) and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative (HR: 4.16, 95% CI: 1.22–14.21, P = 0.023) subgroups. Graft survival in other subcategories (probable AMR, active AMR) did not differ significantly from controls.

We also performed a subgroup analysis comparing 3-year death-censored graft survival post-biopsy according to MMDx findings within each Banff 2022 AMR subcategories (Supplementary Figure S3). Although the small number of events in subgroups and relatively short follow-up limit definitive conclusions, the data suggest trends toward worse graft survival in cases classified as “rejection” by MMDx. In 57 cases in MVI, DSA negative, C4d negative group, 13 grafts failed and in 9 of these cases rejection by MMDx was identified. In other 2 cases subthreshold rejection by MMDx was found. Out of 72 grafts with probable AMR just 3 grafts failed and in 2 of them molecular rejection was identified.

To create models predicting 3-year graft loss, separately for molecular and histological predictors, we performed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-penalized Cox regression using 12 molecular and 11 Banff scores, respectively, with the aim to retain a maximum of 4 predictors. Using the Zurich training cohort (n = 272), the model selected 4 molecular predictors (cgprob, ctprob, tprob, and AKI score) and 4 Banff scores (cg, t, ci, and ct) for inclusion in the final models. Discussion

In this 2-center study, 325 biopsies with histological features of AMR and 237 controls were evaluated by a validated platform of BBT. The key finding is that histologic categories characterized by the presence of both active microvascular injury and transplant glomerulopathy exhibit the highest molecular rejection signals and are associated with the most impaired death-censored graft survival regardless of the DSA status. This result is not surprising, as MMDx was trained on g and ptc lesions and not on DSA positivity.12 Thus, MVI ≥ 2, even in the absence of DSA or C4d staining, is clinically relevant— supporting its inclusion in risk stratification despite its current exclusion from formal Banff AMR diagnosis. Consistent with this, Gupta et al.25 showed that molecular scores for pathogenesis-based transcript sets were similarly elevated for biopsies with MVI ≥ 2 regardless of C4d staining, and were higher than in biopsies with MVI scores of 0 or 1. Similar finding was obtained in another study while using NanoString platform (Bruker Spatial Biology, Seattle, WA).26 The molecular AMR positivity rate in both active and chronic-active AMR was 70% which is in line with Halloran group report from INTERCOMEX cohort.27 The findings in histology-negative but molecular rejection positive biopsies suggest possible early stages of AMR, especially in highly sensitized patients. In contrast, the chronic AMR group, defined by transplant glomerulopathy without active inflammation, showed surprisingly low molecular rejection scores — irrespective of DSA status, as was shown before6,28 — low atrophy-fibrosis scores, and a short-term graft outcome comparable with that of the no-rejection group. As the cgprob score in the MMDx algorithm was trained particularly on a cohort with chronic-active AMR but not chronic AMR, the cgprob alone cannot discriminate AMR calls in the absence of a concomitant activity scores in histology. In chronic AMR, the expression of fibrosis-related genes may decline because of the replacement of active fibrogenic processes with scar tissue. This suggests that chronic injury alone may be insufficient to drive ongoing molecular rejection activity and emphasizes the importance of active lesions predicting poor graft prognosis, which could be reversed by current innovative therapies.4

In active AMR and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative, the highest proportion of molecular EABMR was noticed, whereas in chronic-active AMR and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg FABMR prevailed. Significantly worse graft survival was noticed in chronic-active AMR and MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative (with and without cg). This is consistent with both the results of Sablik et al.'s4 study showing similar risk of graft failure for MVI and AMR and the recent transcriptomics studies.29,30

Interestingly, active AMR and probable AMR were not associated with the increased risk of graft loss. However, as these are diagnosed early post-transplant, the 3-year follow-up may not be sufficient to observe the effect. This observation is in line with others.31

In our study, the performance of histological and molecular models was similar to predict 3-years graft outcome. In both models either histology and molecular scores for atrophy-fibrosis, transplant glomerulopathy, and tubulitis were selected. Interestingly, the molecular TCMR score emerged as a strong predictor of graft loss. Substantial proportion of AMR-spectrum biopsies exhibited borderline changes or TCMR (30/325, 9%) and in 49% (158/325) cases the isolated tubulitis was present, supporting the evidence of subthreshold TCMR features within the AMR spectrum. Previous studies have shown that mixed molecular rejection phenotypes are associated with more severe inflammation and worse graft outcomes, even when histology identifies only 1 type of rejection.22 It is possible that in more advanced stages of chronic-active AMR or MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative, the molecular TCMR activity becomes more prominent. This is supported by recent findings of expression of both natural killer and T cell transcripts in chronic-active AMR and TCMR, but not in active AMR.32 The involvement of natural killer and T cells has been confirmed using flow-cytometry, also in MVI, similarly to AMR.33 Nevertheless, Cristopheri et al.34 found higher activity of T cells when comparing MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative with cg (n = 14) versus chronic-active AMR (n = 22) using gene expression profiling using NanoString platform. This is consistent with our finding of increased TCMR molecular score in the MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative group. These findings highlight the potential of molecular diagnostics to uncover complex and overlapping rejection processes that may be underestimated by histology alone. Similarly, Rosales et al.,35 using the Banff human organ transplant panel, showed that TCMR-associated transcripts negatively impact graft survival at 3 years postbiopsy in cases of AMR, supporting the concept that many biopsies classified as “pure” AMR or “pure” TCMR histologically actually have molecular features of mixed rejection. Molecular TCMR signatures could warrant TCMR-specific treatment decisions (e.g., pulsed steroids / ATG in addition to “conventional” AMR treatments or newer treatments as anti-CD38 or anti-IL6).21,36

There are several limitations to consider. First, although our threshold for DSA positivity (MFI > 500) is sensitive, it may include false-positive cases, potentially misclassifying some patients with probable AMR or active AMR. Secondly, we did not measure non-HLA antibodies, although these did not seem to affect the outcome.37 Thirdly, both histologic and molecular methods share a key limitation— sampling error. MVI lesions are generally less influenced by focality than TCMR; however, sample adequacy, particularly the number of glomeruli (at least 10 according to Banff criteria), remains critical. Importantly, histology and BBT evaluate different and spatially nonoverlapping portions of tissue; histology typically examines a larger and more heterogeneous tissue area than MMDx. Pathologists access another part of the tissue than is analyzed by BBT, which may lead to discrepancies. Thus, negative findings, whether histologic or molecular, should be interpreted cautiously, especially when clinical suspicion is high. In addition, histologic assessment is subject to interobserver variability; therefore, discrepancy analyses assessing agreement between histologic and molecular diagnoses between centers are warranted. Finally, long-term outcomes beyond 3 years remain to be studied, especially in active-only phenotypes.

Finally, BBT is diagnostically most valuable in probable AMR, MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative category, and histologically ambiguous or incomplete biopsies. Not all cases within MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative category reflect rejection; alternative causes may include TCMR, natural killer-cell-mediated injury, innate immune activation, or other glomerular diseases. Nevertheless, MMDx classifier primarily reflects molecular patterns associated with microvascular inflammation and was not designed to distinguish between different underlying etiologies of MVI. In our cohort, molecular AMR was detected only in a subset of probable AMR and MVI-positive biopsies and was not associated with significantly different graft outcomes, although interpretation is limited by short follow-up and low event rates. Molecular changes detected by BBT may precede histological changes in highly sensitized patients early post-transplant. Routine BBT application in all biopsies, particularly chronic AMR without active lesions, is unlikely to provide additional clinical benefit.

In conclusion, our study highlights the clinical significance of molecular diagnostics in evaluating AMR subtypes. Patients with biopsies classified as the MVI, DSA-negative, and C4d-negative category exhibited strong molecular signatures of rejection. Moreover, molecular signals of TCMR, which were not detected by histology, emerged as a significant predictor of graft failure. Finally, whether patients with molecularly active AMR lesions may yield promises from innovative natural killer cells-targeted therapies should be answered by ongoing studies.
