Implementing the HEARTS Initiative in Mendoza, Argentina: A multi-level, staged approach to improving hypertension control in the public health system
Authors: Yanina Mazzaresi Epelman, Carolina Cicero, Javier Ortego, Daniela Ortega, Camila Luna, Rodrigo Rodriguez-Fernandez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective.To describe HEARTS Initiative implementation in Mendoza, Argentina, focusing on governance, adaptations, and lessons from scaling a standardized hypertension care model across the provincial public health system.Methods.This descriptive case study analyzed HEARTS introduction and scale-up in Mendoza from 2022 to 2024. Data sources included ministerial resolutions, technical planning documents, and health system monitoring data. Implementation was examined using a five-stage framework (assess, develop, introduce, expand-early, expand-mature), analyzing actors, facilitators, barriers, and adaptations.Results.In 2022, Mendoza’s Ministry of Health issued a provincial ministerial resolution formally designating HEARTS as the standard hypertension care model for all public health services. This enabled province-wide procurement of validated blood pressure devices, formulary revision prioritizing first-line antihypertensives, and training of nearly 4 000 health workers. HEARTS indicators were incorporated into the provincial digital information system for routine monitoring. By 2024, 36 046 adults were receiving antihypertensive medication through the provincial public system, and 12 617 adults aged 20–64 years met active follow-up criteria (at least one visit and documented medication dispensation in the previous 12 months), representing a 40% increase since 2022. These data suggest approximately 15% of the estimated 243 584 adults with hypertension relying exclusively on public services are now under documented treatment and follow-up.Conclusions.Mendoza demonstrates that binding policy mandates coupled with standardized protocols, centralized procurement, unified information systems, and workforce training can institutionalize HEARTS in decentralized federal health systems. These findings are relevant for other Argentine provinces and subnational health systems in the Americas seeking to strengthen hypertension control through primary care.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of premature death in the Region of the Americas, accounting for one-third of all deaths in the Region. In 2021, CVD caused 2.2 million deaths in the Americas, with an age-standardized cardiovascular mortality rate of 181 per 100 000 persons. Despite representing the lowest age-standardized CVD mortality among World Health Organization (WHO) regions, the absolute burden remains substantial due to population growth and aging, with elevated blood pressure (BP) as the most important modifiable risk factor (1, 2). Despite availability of effective treatment, hypertension control rates in the Region remain low (3). Documented barriers include inconsistent and inaccurate BP measurement, limited access to validated automated BP devices, inconsistent availability of essential antihypertensive medications, and the absence of standardized clinical pathways in routine primary care. In Mendoza province, Argentina, hypertension is defined as BP ≥140/90 mmHg, with treatment targets <140/90 mmHg for most adults (<130/80 mmHg for high-risk groups) (4).

To address these challenges, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) launched HEARTS in the Americas, a regional initiative that aims to improve cardiovascular risk management in primary care and provide countries with a structured technical package that includes standardized clinical protocols, implementation tools, and monitoring instruments (5, 6).

HEARTS is organized around six strategic pillars: (H) Healthy lifestyle counseling, which provides systematic guidance on reducing major cardiovascular risk factors including unhealthy diet, tobacco use, physical inactivity, and harmful alcohol consumption; (E) Evidence-based treatment protocols that standardize the clinical management of hypertension and diabetes using simplified, easy-to-follow algorithms validated for primary care settings; (A) Access to essential medicines and technologies, ensuring continuous availability of first-line antihypertensive medications and validated automatic BP measurement devices; (R) Risk-based cardiovascular disease management, stratifying patients according to their total cardiovascular risk to guide treatment intensity; (T) Team-based care that optimizes task distribution among physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and community health workers; and (S) Systems for monitoring, establishing routine data collection and feedback mechanisms to track coverage, treatment adherence, and BP control at facility, district, and national levels (7–9).

As of 2025, four of Argentina’s 24 provinces (Mendoza, La Pampa, Santiago del Estero, and Salta) have enacted provincial resolutions establishing HEARTS, with all implementing at least four of six pillars. The National Scaling Plan 2021–2025 aims for nationwide coverage by 2025. Implementation maturity varies across provinces, reflecting differences in capacity, prioritization, and resources. Additional provinces have initiated pilots without formal adoption (10, 11).

In Latin America, HEARTS implementation requires standardized protocols, device and medicine procurement changes, staff training, and workflow integration (7). Success depends on political leadership, multisectoral coordination, and alignment with broader health reforms (12, 13).

The Argentine health system comprises three subsectors: public (universal coverage, state-managed facilities), social security (employment-linked schemes including PAMI for formal workers and pensioners), and private (voluntary insurance and out-of-pocket payment). These subsectors operate with separate financing and procurement channels, producing fragmented care and variability in accessibility, quality, and equity.

In Mendoza, 37% of the population (~703 000 people) depend exclusively on public health services, 45% access social security, and 18% use private care. The public sector’s share is slightly lower than in provinces with higher poverty rates. This fragmentation produces variability in hypertension care accessibility and quality, making provincial-level coordination essential for comprehensive coverage.

Mendoza’s public health system is organized across five health regions and comprises 287 primary care centers and 22 public hospitals, serving a population of over 1.9 million. Approximately 37% of the provincial population depends exclusively on the public health system. According to the Fourth National Risk Factor Survey (14), the prevalence of hypertension in Argentina is 46.6%. Based on this estimate and population data from the most recent Mendoza Provincial Census (15), it is projected that approximately 642 700 individuals in the province have hypertension, of whom an estimated 243 584 rely exclusively on the public health system for care.

In Mendoza, HEARTS was adopted as a provincial ministerial priority beginning in 2022 and was established through a formal provincial ministerial resolution as the standard model of care for hypertension across all public health services. The same resolution encouraged alignment with the private sector.

Throughout this article, all ministerial resolutions refer to provincial-level acts issued by Mendoza’s Ministry of Health and Sports unless otherwise noted. These are legally binding for Mendoza’s 287 primary care centers and 22 public hospitals but not for national, social security (e.g., PAMI), or private facilities. The 2022 provincial resolution mandated HEARTS implementation across all public services while encouraging voluntary alignment by other subsectors through technical cooperation and shared training. This reflects Argentina’s federal structure, where provinces control their public health networks but must negotiate collaborative arrangements with other subsectors.

This article provides a staged implementation analysis of HEARTS in Mendoza, including governance structures, operational phases, facilitators, barriers, and adaptations. It contributes to the evidence base of how decentralized health systems can institutionalize cardiovascular prevention through primary care reform and collaborative implementation (7, 13, 16).

METHODS

This descriptive case study documents the implementation process of the HEARTS Initiative in the Province of Mendoza, Argentina, from its formal adoption in 2022 through its expansion in 2024. The study used an implementation science lens to analyze governance structures, operational milestones, and service delivery adaptations that supported the institutionalization of the HEARTS model at the provincial level. The analysis was conducted using a staged implementation framework, identifying five phases: assess, develop, introduce, expand (early phase), and expand (mature phase).

Data sources included official documents issued by the Ministry of Health of Mendoza, including the provincial ministerial resolution establishing HEARTS as the province-wide model of care for hypertension, internal technical planning documents, and health system data drawn from routine service delivery indicators. Epidemiological data were obtained from the provincial Consolidated Information System (SIDICO), Mendoza’s integrated electronic health platform consolidating records from all primary care centers and hospital outpatient departments. During 2022–2024, Mendoza migrated from fragmented systems to a unified electronic record platform. HEARTS indicators (BP measurements, medication dispensing, scheduled follow-up) were embedded into SIDICO from the outset, enabling near-real-time monitoring at facility, regional, and provincial levels. However, the transition created temporary data inconsistencies in Q3–Q4 2023 as health workers adapted to new workflows and the system underwent refinements.

Essential antihypertensive medications are supplied through a dual-channel model: provincial-level centralized procurement (formulary revised in 2022 to prioritize HEARTS-recommended first-line agents: enalapril, losartan, amlodipine, hydrochlorothiazide, atenolol) and the national REMEDIAR program (a federally managed initiative delivering standardized medication kits to primary care facilities). Both formularies include the same first-line agents, creating alignment between supply chains. This dual-channel approach provides redundancy and ensures availability, although requiring coordination to avoid duplication.

These data included the number of health establishments participating in HEARTS, the number of personnel trained, the number and characteristics of validated automatic BP devices procured and deployed, the dispensing of priority antihypertensive medicines, and the number of adults receiving antihypertensive treatment through the provincial public system. Indicators of coverage and continuity of follow-up for adults with documented hypertension were derived from these provincial monitoring datasets. Population denominators and estimates of hypertension burden were derived from the Fourth National Risk Factor Survey and the most recent provincial census. No identifiable personal or patient-level data were used in this analysis.

The HEARTS clinical pathway implemented in Mendoza includes a standardized referral protocol for patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Primary care providers initiate treatment with first-line medications according to the simplified algorithm, which prioritizes monotherapy for patients with grade 1 hypertension (140–159/90–99 mmHg) and combination therapy for those with grade 2 or higher (≥160/100 mmHg) or with established cardiovascular disease or diabetes. If BP targets are not achieved after optimization of two first-line medications at adequate doses with documented adherence, providers are instructed to refer patients to specialist consultation at hospital-based outpatient cardiology or internal medicine clinics. The referral protocol also specifies immediate referral criteria for complicated hypertension, including hypertensive urgency (≥180/110 mmHg with symptoms), suspected secondary hypertension in young patients, or evidence of target organ damage. This structured approach aims to ensure that the majority of uncomplicated hypertension is managed at the primary care level – where more than 95% of documented HEARTS visits occur in Mendoza – while ensuring timely specialist input for complex cases.

The study also reviewed HEARTS-related implementation components, including training activities, clinical protocol standardization, integration of validated devices into procurement processes, and communication strategies deployed to engage health teams and community actors. A narrative synthesis approach was used to organize and describe the implementation across multiple levels of the health system, identifying common facilitators and barriers that emerged throughout the process.

RESULTS

Implementation of the HEARTS Initiative in Mendoza yielded measurable system‑level and service‑level gains primarily through primary health care centers (>95% of visits) between January 2022 and December 2024. Across the five staged phases – assess, develop, introduce, expand (early), and expand (mature) – the province established a province‑wide governance structure, equipped all public facilities with validated automatic BP monitors, and embedded standardized clinical pathways and digital monitoring indicators into routine practice. Continuous capacity‑building reached nearly 4 000 health workers, while the proportion of adults with documented hypertension treatment follow‑up in the public system rose by 40%. These collective achievements demonstrate both the breadth (governance, devices, training, digital integration) and the depth (service coverage, medication use, monitoring) of progress attained during the first two years of implementation and provide the context for the specific findings detailed below.

Main actors and roles

HEARTS implementation in Mendoza was coordinated through a multi-level governance model. At the macro level, the Subsecretariat of Planning and Public Health Policies prioritized HEARTS province-wide, enabling ministerial resolution formalization and resource allocation.

At the meso level, the Cardiovascular Disease Prevention and Treatment Program under the Directorate of Health Prevention and Promotion adapted the HEARTS package and coordinated across five regional health jurisdictions covering 287 primary care centers and 22 hospitals serving 1.9 million residents. Multiple ministry departments (epidemiology, nursing, pharmacy, communications, digital health) contributed to formulary revision, guideline dissemination, training, and digital alignment, minimizing silos.

At the micro level, primary care teams delivered care using HEARTS protocols: measuring BP with validated devices, applying standardized algorithms, and ensuring medication continuity. Community pharmacists, engaged through the provincial pharmaceutical association, aligned BP monitoring with HEARTS standards.

Strategic partnerships strengthened implementation: PAHO provided technical assistance; the National Directorate for Noncommunicable Diseases aligned HEARTS with federal policy and works directly with the Provincial Ministries of Health.

Staged model for implementation

HEARTS implementation followed five sequential stages from 2022 to 2024. The assess stage (Q1 2022) mapped 287 primary care centers and 22 hospitals and quantified baseline gaps: <20% hypertension control; ~70% BP readings ending in zero; <30% facilities with validated devices; ~7 000 active cases.

The develop stage (Q2 2022) adapted HEARTS to local context: standardized protocols, revised provincial formulary, and established centralized BP monitor procurement. Partnerships were formalized.

The introduce stage (Q3–Q4 2022) piloted the initiative in selected facilities, training >500 personnel on BP measurement and algorithms, with device rollout and pharmacist outreach.

The expand (early) phase (Q1–Q2 2023) scaled to all 287 centers and 22 hospitals following the provincial resolution. Capacity-building, protocol integration, HEARTS indicator incorporation into the provincial system, and communication campaigns reinforced adherence.

The expand (mature) phase (Q3 2023–December 2024) consolidated gains: maintaining device supply, expanding professional education, and refining data-driven feedback mechanisms. Table 1 provides detailed milestones.

Process measures and quality improvement indicators

Substantial process improvements in measurement accuracy, medication selection, and workforce capacity preceded clinical outcomes.

Validated BP device adoption. At baseline, fewer than 30% of centers possessed validated automatic devices. Through centralized procurement, all 287 centers and 22 hospitals were equipped by mid-2023. Device usage rose from <30% to >95% by late 2023, evidenced by decline in BP readings ending in zero (from ~70% to <10%), demonstrating consistent use. After battery-operated devices fell into disuse, the province switched to rechargeable models.

Medication protocol adherence. Dispensing data revealed that >90% of antihypertensives dispensed corresponded to five first-line agents (enalapril, losartan, amlodipine, hydrochlorothiazide, atenolol), representing substantial change from pre-HEARTS patterns. Standardization was supported by formulary revision, training, algorithm integration into electronic prescribing, and facility-level feedback.

Capacity-building. The province promoted PAHO’s virtual training platform on accurate BP measurement. QR codes affixed to every device allowed immediate access. Participation expanded from <200 users in Q1 2022 to nearly 4 000 by September 2023, covering substantial workforce proportions. Training extended to nurses, administrative staff, and pharmacists, reflecting team-based care. The virtual format provided flexibility while allowing coordinators to monitor completion rates.

Clinical outcomes

By late 2024, 36 046 people (15% of the estimated 243 584 public-dependent individuals with hypertension) were receiving antihypertensive medications through the public system (Figure 1).

Among adults aged 20–64 years with documented hypertension receiving medications, 12 617 (35% of this age group receiving treatment) met active follow-up criteria: at least one hypertension-specific visit AND documented first-line medication dispensation in the previous 12 months. This dual criterion captures treatment initiation and continuity of pharmaceutical supply and monitoring. This represents a 40% increase from the 2022 baseline (~9 000 adults), reflecting improvements in case detection, treatment initiation, medication supply, and retention following HEARTS implementation. However, substantial gaps remain: 36 046 receiving treatment represents only 15% of the estimated 243 584 adults with hypertension relying on public services, indicating that ~85% may not yet be diagnosed, treated, or retained in systematic follow-up.

Figure 2 presents the number of unique individuals with documented hypertension who had at least one tracer consultation for hypertension management during each four-month reporting period. The graph begins at low numbers in early 2022, not because no individuals with hypertension were receiving care at baseline, but because systematic tracking using standardized HEARTS indicators within the integrated provincial information system (SIDICO) was not yet operational. The steep initial increase reflects both the gradual implementation of digital reporting systems across facilities and genuine expansion of documented follow-up as standardized protocols were adopted. The temporary decline in late 2023 coincided with provincial and national electoral transitions, which often introduce administrative disruptions and staff turnover, affecting program continuity and reporting consistency.

Figure 3 shows the total number of clinical encounters (including both initial and follow-up visits) during which a BP measurement was recorded in Mendoza’s primary care centers. These data include all visits where BP was measured, not exclusively hypertension-specific consultations, reflecting the HEARTS emphasis on opportunistic screening and monitoring. The progressive increase demonstrates growing integration of BP measurement into routine primary care workflows. The high proportion of visits occurring in primary care centers (>95%) rather than hospitals illustrates the successful focus on first-level care as the principal setting for hypertension management.

BP control improved: readings with systolic values <140 mmHg rose from 556 in 2022 to 2 204 in 2023, and 5 153 in 2024 (Figure 4), although these absolute numbers should be interpreted considering the growing treatment denominator. These data represent absolute numbers of controlled BP readings rather than proportions of treated individuals. Because the total number of individuals under treatment increased substantially during this period (Figure 1), the increase in absolute controlled readings may underestimate the true improvement in control rates when considering the expanding denominator. Future analysis should present the percentage of treated individuals with controlled BP to provide more accurate assessment of control rate improvement.

Common facilitators

High-level political commitment enabled resource allocation, ministerial resolution issuance, and alignment with health reforms including digital transformation. Multisectoral collaboration within the Ministry integrated departments (epidemiology, nursing, pharmacy, information systems, communications), creating coordinated implementation and ensuring primary care actions were supported by system-wide procurement and data decisions.

Technical cooperation from PAHO provided evidence-based guidance and training resources. Alignment of national and provincial strategies reinforced coherence. Continuous capacity-building prepared health personnel. Training extended beyond physicians to nurses, pharmacists, and community actors. Integration of BP measurement and clinical algorithms into medical residency programs institutionalized care standards.

Community partnerships with the pharmaceutical association and academic institutions increased reach, acceptability, and sustainability. Procurement of validated monitors and formulary revision ensuring first-line antihypertensive availability addressed structural barriers, creating conditions for accurate diagnosis, standardized treatment, and care continuity.

Main barriers and solutions

Implementation encountered several operational challenges requiring adaptive responses. Transition to new recording systems, including electronic health records, required adjustment to new tools, procedures, and reporting standards. Fluctuations in data consistency, particularly during Q4 2023, coincided with national and provincial electoral processes, which introduced administrative transitions, staff turnover, and temporary program disruptions.

Limited validated BP device availability represented a structural barrier. Most centers relied on outdated or unvalidated devices. Procurement processes were adapted to include validated automatic upper-arm monitors. To prevent discontinuation due to maintenance problems, training addressed proper handling, and subsequent purchases prioritized rechargeable over battery-operated devices.

Medication supply constraints occasionally affected care continuity, particularly initially. Combination formulations were not readily available, and prescribing practices varied. The provincial formulary was revised to include HEARTS-recommended medications, and a procurement plan secured continuous supply. Capacity-building standardized prescribing.

Resistance to change among some providers emerged as a barrier, particularly reluctance to adopt standardized protocols or intensify therapy when targets were not achieved. Regular training, evidence-based guideline integration, and mentorship from the provincial team strengthened adherence. Active involvement of family medicine residents and nursing staff overcame resistance. Nurses contributed through accurate record-keeping, patient follow-up, and modeling protocol compliance.

DISCUSSION

The phased implementation of HEARTS in Mendoza shows how subnational health systems can institutionalize high-quality hypertension care when guided by structured leadership and operational innovations. Similar models have demonstrated positive results for hypertension control in primary care settings in multiple low- and middle-income countries (17).

The ministerial resolution that formalized HEARTS created a unified provincial approach, ensuring adherence to standardized protocols and accountability. This finding mirrors experiences from other jurisdictions where legal mandates improved the consistency of chronic disease programs (18).

The integration of HEARTS with Mendoza’s digital health reform allowed clinical indicators and workflows to be incorporated into new electronic record platforms. Such alignment between digital systems and noncommunicable disease programs has been associated with improved monitoring and long-term continuity of care (19).

A practical innovation was the switch from battery-powered to rechargeable validated BP devices, which resolved a barrier to regular use. This type of adaptation highlights how implementation science can address operational bottlenecks, as shown in other primary care programs across Latin America (18, 20).

The distribution of QR codes linking to standardized online training promoted a rapid expansion of skills among health workers. Evidence from other regions indicates that e-learning approaches significantly increase competency and fidelity in cardiovascular risk management programs (21).

Simplifying clinical management through a limited formulary of recommended medications is another pillar of HEARTS that has proved to improve treatment adherence and supply chain management in decentralized health systems (22). In Mendoza, pharmacological treatment in primary health care centers is supplied through two complementary channels: the provincial-level centralized procurement managed by the Mendoza Ministry of Health pharmacy directorate, and the national REMEDIAR program administered by Argentina’s federal Ministry of Health. The formularies of both provincial and national programs include all medications required for the implementation of the treatment protocol, creating alignment between supply chains and reducing stockout risk.

Facilitators for implementation

The facilitators in Mendoza, including strong leadership, multisectoral collaboration, procurement of validated devices, and continuous training, are similar to those reported in large-scale quality-improvement initiatives targeting hypertension in low-resource settings (23).

Engagement with universities and professional associations provided sustainability to the program and echoes global evidence showing that partnerships with academic institutions strengthen adoption of standardized clinical care models (24).

Programmatic sustainability

The process of institutionalizing HEARTS in Mendoza was supported by embedding key elements of the initiative into existing systems. Training has been incorporated into medical and nursing residency curricula, and procurement processes have been modified to include validated equipment and essential antihypertensive medicines. Data-driven decision-making is facilitated by the integration of HEARTS indicators into provincial health information systems. These factors increase the likelihood of long-term sustainability, although they will require continued political commitment, financing, and periodic review to maintain momentum and ensure continuous quality improvement (25).

Limitations

The findings presented are based on programmatic documentation and routine health system data, which may be limited by incomplete reporting during the transition to integrated digital records. Similar limitations have been described in other multi-country hypertension programs, where initial digital system changes led to incomplete reporting but provided stronger long-term monitoring capacity (26). While the descriptive design provides valuable insights into the processes and contextual factors influencing implementation, it does not allow for causal inferences or quantification of clinical outcomes such as reductions in BP or cardiovascular events. Future evaluations should include longitudinal analyses and patient-level outcomes to fully assess the impact of the initiative.

Conclusion

The implementation of the HEARTS Initiative in Mendoza provides a clear example of how a subnational health system can accelerate improvements in hypertension prevention and control by combining a strong policy mandate with practical, locally adapted solutions. At the center of this process was the ministerial resolution that formally established HEARTS as the model of care for all adults with hypertension in the public health system. This legal instrument anchored the initiative, aligned diverse stakeholders around a common goal, and enabled the allocation of resources necessary for scaling from pilot efforts to full provincial coverage.

Mendoza’s experience also highlights the importance of synchronizing health system reforms. The expansion of HEARTS coincided with the province’s digital health transformation, allowing hypertension care processes and indicators to be integrated into new information platforms from the outset. Although this transition posed temporary challenges for reporting, it created long-term opportunities for more consistent monitoring, continuity of care, and data-driven decision-making.

Equally important were the operational innovations that removed everyday barriers to the use of the HEARTS technical package. The shift from battery-powered to rechargeable automatic BP monitors addressed an unanticipated but critical obstacle to accurate diagnosis and follow-up. The inclusion of a QR code linking directly to the free PAHO online training course with every device ensured that all health personnel, regardless of location, could access standardized, evidence-based guidance on BP measurement. These measures illustrate how small, context-sensitive solutions can have a large impact on implementation.

This experience offers transferable lessons to other Argentine provinces and to subnational health systems across Latin America and the Caribbean: policy commitment must be paired with operational adaptations – including procurement reforms, digital system integration, continuous training, and community engagement – that respond to the realities of health workers and the populations they serve. Future evaluation should include longitudinal patient-level analyses to assess clinical endpoints including cardiovascular events, mortality, and health equity indicators across population subgroups.

The combination of a binding legal framework, intersectoral collaboration, continuous capacity-building, and simple but effective operational innovations has allowed Mendoza to institutionalize HEARTS within its primary care system. This experience offers transferable lessons to other provinces and countries seeking to improve hypertension control: policy commitment must be paired with on-the-ground adaptations that respond to the realities of health workers and communities. Sustained political will, robust supply systems, and digital tools will be essential to maintain the progress achieved and to translate these system-level changes into measurable improvements in cardiovascular health.
