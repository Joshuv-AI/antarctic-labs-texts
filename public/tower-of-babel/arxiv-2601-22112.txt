Distributional Competition
Mark Whitmeyer

Abstract
I study symmetric competitions in which each player chooses an arbitrary distribution over a one-dimensional performance index, subject to a convex cost. I establish existence of a symmetric equilibrium, document various properties it must possess, and provide a characterization via the first-order approach. Manifold applications–to R&D competition, oligopolistic competition with product design, and rank-order contests–follow.



1

Introduction

Many competitive environments are inherently distributional. Agents do not choose a single deterministic output level but instead choose technologies, portfolios, processes, and approaches that shape the probabilities of different outcomes. Agents seek to influence how likely they are to hit a very high quality draw, how quickly they achieve a breakthrough, how often they deliver exceptional performance, or how rarely they crash and burn. Rewards are driven by comparisons to one’s fellows–who is best or second-best; who comes first, second, or third; or who looks strongest.

I study a class of strategic interactions in which each player chooses a probability distribution over a one-dimensional performance index, incurring a convex cost for generating that distribution. Payoffs depend on the realized performance profile, in a way that rewards higher realizations and is symmetric across players. Standard rank-order tournament and contest models fit as special cases, but my framework is broader, subsuming a wide range of competitive problems with flexible production and endogenous risk-taking.

My framework is agnostic vis-à-vis microfoundations. Rather than deriving the feasible set of outcome distributions from, for instance, a particular underlying stochastic process (a diffusion, a Poisson-arrival model, etc.) or a parametrized technology, I allow players to choose distributions directly and fully flexibly, and encode technological constraints through a general cost functional over distributions. This accommodates different underpinnings across applications while keeping the strategic and welfare logic transparent.

This generality also allows me to shelter a grand menagerie of scenarios under the same wide umbrella. In risky R&D, a firm’s choice of project mix and experimentation intensity begets a distribution over discovery times. How does competition affect research intensity and output? In fixed-price markets with quality competition, a firm’s design and quality-control choices determine a distribution of realized quality. How does competition affect quality choices? Suppose these firms choose prices as well; must prices converge to marginal cost in the perfect competition limit? In rank-order contests, how do more inegalitarian prize schedules or more contestants shape output?

I begin my analysis by using results in
39
to prove existence of a symmetric pure-strategy equilibrium, even though discontinuities arise naturally due to ties generating jumps in payoffs. Second, I show that under natural conditions, symmetric equilibria must take a particular atomless form. Third, I characterize both symmetric equilibria and the corresponding symmetric planner’s problem via the first-order approach.

I then visit various particular settings of interest. I tackle the classic question on the relationship between prize inequality and effort in rank-order contests. For a broad class of costs, I derive a stark conclusion: more inegalitarian prizes lead to higher mean but riskier output (formally, a shift in the increasing convex order).

I show that in risky R&D and fixed-price quality competition, equilibrium leads to inefficiently high effort compared to the planner’s problem, clarifying and elucidating classic results. This means that R&D breakthroughs arise inefficiently quickly and firms’ quality choices are inefficiently high. In both cases the equilibrium outcome distributions can be compared with the planner’s solutions according to first-order stochastic dominance (FOSD)–below in the former, above in the latter.

Finally, I study competition between oligopolistic firms who choose both prices and (stochastic) quality. My goal here is to ask–when quality is endogenous–when prices converge to marginal cost as the number of firms grows large. I provide an intuitive necessary condition for this to occur. Notably, for distributional-costs that satisfy a natural steepness property on their derivative, convergence to marginal cost implies no product differentiation. That is, the Bertrand logic is flipped: marginal-cost pricing
implies
perfectly homogeneous products.

2.1

Discussion of Assumptions

Assumption
2.1
places minimal structure on the environment. Boundedness
(P1)
is a simple regularity condition that guarantees expected payoffs are well-defined for any distributional choices. Symmetry
(P2)
is simply our specialization to symmetric environments. The key modeling restriction is
(P3)
: a player’s payoff can jump only when her realization is exactly tied with someone else’s. This fits our leading applications (rank-order contests, product competition, priority races), where discontinuities arise precisely because a tie changes who wins. Monotonicity
(P4)
formalizes the “higher performance is better” interpretation of the index and rules out perverse cases where improving one’s realization could reduce one’s prize. Finally,
(P5)
is another form of regularity, stipulating that the total prize to be allocated depends continuously on the realized performance profile (and not, e.g., on the auxiliary tie-breaking rule).

Assumption
2.2
spells out the production technology. Convexity and continuity
(C1)
capture the idea that pushing probability mass toward desirable outcomes exhibits weakly increasing marginal cost and that small changes in the outcome distribution do not produce large, discontinuous swings in costs. Differentiability
(C2)
provides a clean marginal-cost object
c_{F}
. It is the first-order cost of locally shifting weight, and it is what ultimately facilitates the first-order approach. The last assumption
(C3)
is vaguely an Inada condition, guaranteeing that placing mass at the maximal performance level is too expensive to ever be optimal. This rules out corner solutions at the upper bound and the pernicious potential pathologies that might follow.

A central obstacle in these games is that payoffs jump discontinuously at ties: when two or more players draw exactly the same performance level, “who wins” changes abruptly. This problem shows that, in spite of these discontinuities, a symmetric pure-strategy equilibrium exists. The intuition is that the discontinuities are tightly structured. By construction, they come only from tie events, and those tie events can be made essentially negligible by slightly “smoothing” deviations. Once deviations are smoothed in this way, the deviator’s expected payoff varies continuously with opponents’ strategies in a neighborhood, which is exactly the kind of stability we need for an equilibrium existence argument in this discontinuous game.

The economics of equilibrium and the planner’s optimum are as basic as can be. Namely Propositions
3.7
and
3.8
are intuitive generalizations of the standard first-order-approach logic to a setting where the decision variable is not a single number but a distribution. The key idea is to think about a tiny reallocation of probability mass: take an
\varepsilon
-slice of mass away from one outcome level and move it to another. If such a local reallocation would raise expected payoff, then the original distribution cannot be optimal. Therefore, at an optimum, every outcome level that receives positive probability must deliver the same net marginal return, and any outcome level that is not used must deliver less.

Economically, Proposition
3.7
is exactly the familiar indifference logic of equilibria. The player firm only puts weight on outcomes that maximize private net marginal return, and it must be indifferent (in net marginal terms) across all outcomes it actually uses. The multiplier is the common “benchmark” net marginal return in equilibrium, which arises because probability mass is a scarce resource: adding weight at one
x
necessarily means removing weight elsewhere.

The planner’s problem has the same structure, but the benefit side changes from private to social. When the planner shifts probability mass toward
x
, the relevant marginal benefit is the effect on total surplus (the aggregate prize). The marginal cost of shifting probability mass toward
x
is the same as in the game, and the planner uses exactly the same “equalize net marginal returns on the support” principle, but with social marginal benefits instead of private ones.

3.1
, Equilibrium Existence

We prove Proposition
3.1
through a sequence of lemmas verifying that the assumptions for
39
hold, which guarantees the existence of a symmetric pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. For
F\in\mathcal{F}
, define the diagonal payoff
v(F)\coloneqq u_{1}(F,\dots,F)
.

Lemma A.1
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
,
v\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Under symmetry,
\mathbb E[\pi_{i}]
is the same for all
i
. Summing over
i
and using Assumption
2.1
(P5)
yields

v(F)=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb E\left[\Pi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\right]-C(F)=\frac{1}{n}\intop\nolimits_{X^{n}}\Pi(x)\,d(dF)^{\otimes n}(x)-C(F).

The integrand
\Pi
is bounded and continuous on the compact
X^{n}
.
The map
F\mapsto(dF)^{\otimes n}
is continuous under weak convergence, and
C
is continuous by Assumption
2.2
(C1)
.
∎

Our next lemma establishes diagonal quasiconcavity.

Lemma A.2
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, for any
m\geq 2
, any
F^{1},\dots,F^{m}\in\mathcal{F}
, and any
F\in\co\{F^{1},\dots,F^{m}\}
,
v(F)\geq\min_{1\leq k\leq m}u_{1}(F^{k},F,\dots,F)
.

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Fix
F\in\mathcal{F}
. The mapping
H\mapsto u_{1}(H,F,\dots,F)
is concave in
H
:
the expected prize term is affine in
dH
, and
-C(H)
is concave because
C
is convex.
If
F=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}F^{k}
with
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
and
\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\lambda_{k}=1
, then concavity produces

v(F)=u_{1}(F,F,\dots,F)\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}u_{1}(F^{k},F,\dots,F)\geq\min_{1\leq k\leq m}u_{1}(F^{k},F,\dots,F).

∎

We verify diagonal better-reply security via an atomless smoothing device, generated over several lemmas.

Lemma A.3
.

Fix
\hat{H}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
d\hat{H}(\{x\})=0
for every
x\in X
.
Then the map
F\mapsto u_{1}(\hat{H},F,\dots,F)
is continuous on
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

Let
F_{m}\to F
. Then
(dF_{m}\otimes\lambda)^{\otimes(n-1)}\to(dF\otimes\lambda)^{\otimes(n-1)}
, so

(d\hat{H}\otimes\lambda)\otimes(dF_{m}\otimes\lambda)^{\otimes(n-1)}\to(d\hat{H}\otimes\lambda)\otimes(dF\otimes\lambda)^{\otimes(n-1)}.

Consider the bounded function
\pi_{1}
on
\left(X\times\left[0,1\right]\right)^{n}
.
By Assumption
2.1
(P3)
,

\mathrm{Disc}(\pi_{1})\subseteq\bigcupop\displaylimits_{j=2}^{n}\left\{(x,u):x_{1}=x_{j}\right\}.

Under the limiting product measure
(d\hat{H}\otimes\lambda)\otimes(dF\otimes\lambda)^{\otimes(n-1)}
, for each
j\geq 2
,

\mathbb P(x_{1}=x_{j})=\intop\nolimits_{X}d\hat{H}(\{x\})\,dF(x)=0,

since
d\hat{H}
is atomless on
X
. Thus, the limiting measure assigns zero mass to
\mathrm{Disc}(\pi_{1})
.

By the extended Portmanteau theorem: if
M_{m}\to M
weakly and
f
is bounded with
M(\mathrm{Disc}(f))=0
, then

\intop\nolimits fdM_{m}\to\intop\nolimits fdM
. Applying this to
f=\pi_{1}
yields convergence of the expected prize term.
Since
C(\hat{H})
does not depend on
F
, continuity follows.
∎

Next we argue that a mass at
1
is strictly dominated.

Lemma A.4
.

Posit Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. Fix opponents
(F_{2},\dots,F_{n})\in\mathcal{F}^{n-1}
and a strategy
H\in\mathcal{F}
.
Let
\alpha\coloneqq dH(\{1\})
, and define
\widetilde{H}\in\mathcal{F}
by moving this mass to
0
:
d\widetilde{H}\coloneqq dH+\alpha(\delta_{0}-\delta_{1})
. Then,

u_{1}(\widetilde{H},F_{2},\dots,F_{n})\geq u_{1}(H,F_{2},\dots,F_{n})+\eta_{1}\alpha.

In particular, every best response can be chosen with
dH(\{1\})=0
.

Proof of Lemma
A.4
.

By Assumption
2.1
(P1)
,
0\leq\pi_{1}\leq\bar{\pi}
, hence, moving probability
\alpha
from
1
to
0
can reduce the expected prize term by at most
\bar{\pi}\alpha
.

For the cost, set
H_{s}\coloneqq(1-s)H+s\widetilde{H}
for
s\in\left[0,1\right]
and
\varphi(s)\coloneqq C(H_{s})
.
By convexity of
C
,
\varphi
is convex on
\left[0,1\right]
. By Gâteaux differentiability,

\varphi^{\prime}(s)=\delta C(H_{s};\widetilde{H}-H)=\intop\nolimits_{X}c_{H_{s}}(x)d(\widetilde{H}-H)(x)=\alpha\left(c_{H_{s}}(0)-c_{H_{s}}(1)\right).

From Assumption
2.2
(C3)
,
c_{H_{s}}(0)-c_{H_{s}}(1)\leq-(\bar{\pi}+\eta_{1})
for all
s
, thus,
\varphi^{\prime}(s)\leq-\alpha(\bar{\pi}+\eta_{1})
.
Integrating over
s\in\left[0,1\right]
yields

C(\widetilde{H})-C(H)=\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)\leq-\alpha(\bar{\pi}+\eta_{1}).

Therefore, the net payoff gain is at least
-\bar{\pi}\alpha+\alpha(\bar{\pi}+\eta_{1})=\eta_{1}\alpha
.
∎

Now we smooth profitable deviations.

Lemma A.5
.

Posit Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. Fix
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
If there exist
H^{0}\in\mathcal{F}
and
\eta>0
such that

u_{1}(H^{0},F,\dots,F)\geq v(F)+\eta,

then there exists
\hat{H}\in\mathcal{F}
with
d\hat{H}(\{x\})=0
for every
x\in X
such that

u_{1}(\hat{H},F,\dots,F)\geq v(F)+\frac{\eta}{2}.

Proof of Lemma
A.5
.

First we remove any mass at
1
. Let
\alpha\coloneqq dH^{0}(\{1\})
and let
\widetilde{H}^{0}
be obtained from
H^{0}
by moving
\alpha
to
0
, as in Lemma
A.4
.
We have
u_{1}(\widetilde{H}^{0},F,\dots,F)\geq u_{1}(H^{0},F,\dots,F)\geq v(F)+\eta
. In particular,
d\widetilde{H}^{0}(\{1\})=0
.

Next let
X_{1}\sim d\widetilde{H}^{0}
. For
\delta>0
, define a smoothed
\hat{X}_{1}^{\delta}
by:
if
X_{1}=1
, set
\hat{X}_{1}^{\delta}=1
; and if
X_{1}=x<1
, draw an independent
V\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left[0,1\right]
and set

\hat{X}_{1}^{\delta}\coloneqq x+V\min\left\{\delta,1-x\right\}.

Let
\hat{H}^{\delta}
be the cdf induced by
\hat{X}_{1}^{\delta}
.
Then
d\hat{H}^{\delta}(\{x\})=0
for every
x\in X
, and
\hat{H}^{\delta}\to\widetilde{H}^{0}
weakly as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Fix opponents at
F
. Conditional on opponents’ realizations, increasing
x_{1}
cannot decrease firm
1
’s prize payoff by Assumption
2.1
(P4)
.
Since
\hat{X}_{1}^{\delta}\geq X_{1}
a.s., the expected prize term under
\hat{H}^{\delta}
against
F
is at least that under
\widetilde{H}^{0}
.

By Assumption
2.2
(C1)
and
\hat{H}^{\delta}\to\widetilde{H}^{0}
, we have
C(\hat{H}^{\delta})\to C(\widetilde{H}^{0})
.
Choose
\delta
such that
\left|C(\hat{H}^{\delta})-C(\widetilde{H}^{0})\right|\leq\eta/2
. Then

u_{1}(\hat{H}^{\delta},F,\dots,F)\geq u_{1}(\widetilde{H}^{0},F,\dots,F)-\frac{\eta}{2}\geq v(F)+\frac{\eta}{2}.

Set
\hat{H}\coloneqq\hat{H}^{\delta}
.
∎

Next, we establish
39
’s diagonal better-reply security.

Lemma A.6
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, the game is diagonally better-reply secure.

Proof of Lemma
A.6
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}
be such that
(F,\dots,F)
is not a symmetric equilibrium.
Then there exist
H^{0}\in\mathcal{F}
and
\eta>0
with
u_{1}(H^{0},F,\dots,F)\geq v(F)+\eta
.
By Lemma
A.5
, there exists
\hat{H}
atomless on
X
with

u_{1}(\hat{H},F,\dots,F)\geq v(F)+\eta/2
.
By Lemma
A.3
, the map
G\mapsto u_{1}(\hat{H},G,\dots,G)
is continuous at
F
,
so there exists a neighborhood
U
of
F
such that
u_{1}(\hat{H},G,\dots,G)>v(F)
for all
G\in U
.
Thus, a player can secure strictly above
v(F)
along the diagonal at
F
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

The game is symmetric with compact metric strategy space
\mathcal{F}
.
By Lemma
A.2
it is diagonally quasiconcave, and by Lemma
A.6
it is diagonally better-reply secure.
39
implies the existence of a symmetric equilibrium.
∎

In this appendix, we collect the proofs of Lemmas

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

If
dF(\{1\})>0
, then applying Lemma
A.4
with
H=F
and opponents all equal to
F

yields a profitable deviation, contradicting equilibrium.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
dF(\{x_{0}\})=\alpha>0
for some
x_{0}\in\left(0,1\right)
. Let
a\coloneqq F(x_{0}^{-})\in[0,1)
, so
F(x_{0})=a+\alpha
.

Fix
\delta\in\left(0,\delta(x_{0})\right]
. Let
\nu_{\delta}
be the uniform probability measure on
\left(x_{0},x_{0}+\delta\right]
, and define a deviation
H_{\varepsilon,\delta}\in\mathcal{F}
by

dH_{\varepsilon,\delta}\coloneqq dF+\varepsilon\left(\nu_{\delta}-\delta_{x_{0}}\right)
, which removes an
\varepsilon
-slice of the atom at
x_{0}
and redistributes it continuously over
\left(x_{0},x_{0}+\delta\right]
.

First we observe that the expected prize gain (gross of costs) is first-order positive. Take the event
E\coloneqq\left\{\max_{2\leq j\leq n}X_{j}=x_{0}\right\}
. Since
dF(\{x_{0}\})=\alpha>0
, we have

\mathbb P(E)=F(x_{0})^{n-1}-F(x_{0}^{-})^{n-1}=\left(a+\alpha\right)^{n-1}-a^{n-1}>0.

On
E
, if player
1
realizes
x_{0}
, it is tied for the top value
x_{0}
with at least one opponent. By Assumption
3.3
,
moving its realized value to any
x_{0}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\left(x_{0},x_{0}+\delta\right]
raises its expected prize (averaging over
u_{1}
)
by at least
\Delta(x_{0})
. By Assumption
2.1
(P4)
, increasing player
1
’s realized value never decreases its prize on any realization.
Therefore, the expected prize gain from shifting the
\varepsilon
-slice of mass is at least

\varepsilon\cdot\Delta(x_{0})\cdot\mathbb P(E)>0.

Second, we argue that the cost change can be made (comparatively) small. Fix
\delta>0
. Define
G_{\delta}\in\mathcal{F}
by
dG_{\delta}\coloneqq dF+\alpha(\nu_{\delta}-\delta_{x_{0}})
, so that
H_{\varepsilon,\delta}=(1-s)F+sG_{\delta}
, with
s=\varepsilon/\alpha
. By the Gâteaux differentiability at
F
in direction
G_{\delta}-F
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{C(H_{\varepsilon,\delta})-C(F)}{\varepsilon}=\intop\nolimits_{X}c_{F}(x)d(\nu_{\delta}-\delta_{x_{0}})(x)=\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}c_{F}(x)dx-c_{F}(x_{0}).

Hence, for any
\kappa>0
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon<\bar{\varepsilon}
,

\left|C(H_{\varepsilon,\delta})-C(F)-\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}c_{F}(x)dx-c_{F}(x_{0})\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon\kappa.

Since
c_{F}
is continuous, choose
\delta>0
so small that

\left|\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}c_{F}(x)dx-c_{F}(x_{0})\right|\leq\Delta(x_{0})\mathbb P(E)/4.

Then set
\kappa\coloneqq\Delta(x_{0})\mathbb P(E)/4
and choose
\varepsilon\in(0,\min\{\alpha,\bar{\varepsilon}\})
.
For these choices, the cost increase is at most
\varepsilon\Delta(x_{0})\mathbb P(E)/2
.

Thus, the net increase is at least
\varepsilon\Delta(x_{0})\mathbb P(E)-\varepsilon\Delta(x_{0})\mathbb P(E)/2>0
, a strictly profitable deviation, which is a contradiction.∎

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Let
x_{m}\to x_{0}
. Let
\mu\coloneqq dF\otimes\lambda
on
X\times\left[0,1\right]
.
For
z=\left((x_{2},u_{2}),\ldots,(x_{n},u_{n})\right)\in\left(X\times\left[0,1\right]\right)^{n-1}
, define

g_{m}(z)\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\pi_{1}\left((x_{m},u_{1}),z\right)du_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad g(z)\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\pi_{1}\left((x_{0},u_{1}),z\right)du_{1}.

Accordingly,
a_{F}(x_{m})=\intop\nolimits g_{m}(z)d\mu^{\otimes(n-1)}(z)
and
a_{F}(x_{0})=\intop\nolimits g(z)d\mu^{\otimes(n-1)}(z)
.
By boundedness,
\left|g_{m}(z)\right|\leq\bar{\pi}
for all
m
and
z
.

Let

E\coloneqq\left\{z\colon\ x_{j}\neq x_{0}\text{ for all }j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}\right\}.

Since
dF(\{x_{0}\})=0
, we have
\mu\left(\{x_{0}\}\times\left[0,1\right]\right)=0
, and so
\mu^{\otimes(n-1)}(E)=1
.

Fix
z\in E
. Then for every
u_{1}\in\left[0,1\right]
, the point
\left((x_{0},u_{1}),z\right)
satisfies
x_{0}\neq x_{j}
for all
j\geq 2
,
so
\pi_{1}
is continuous there by Assumption
2.1
(P3)
. Thus,

\pi_{1}\left((x_{m},u_{1}),z\right)\to\pi_{1}\left((x_{0},u_{1}),z\right)\quad\text{for each }u_{1}\in\left[0,1\right].

Dominated convergence in
u_{1}
yields
g_{m}(z)\to g(z)
for all
z\in E
. A second dominated convergence under
\mu^{\otimes(n-1)}
yields
a_{F}(x_{m})\to a_{F}(x_{0})
.∎

Lemma B.1
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
0\leq a<b\leq 1
.
If
\supp(dF)\cap(a,b]=\varnothing
, then
dF((a,b])=0
, i.e.,

is constant on
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Fix

. The set
[a+1/k,b]
is compact and contained in
(a,b]
, hence disjoint from
\supp(dF)
.
Thus
dF([a+1/k,b])=0

0\leq dF((a,b])\leq dF((a,a+1/k])=F(a+1/k)-F(a).

.

. Define

(C1)

Applying Lemma

with

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
X\sim F
and define

.
Since
F
is continuous,
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\left[0,1\right]\right)
.
Recall our definition
g_{v}\left(q\right)\coloneqq\Psi\left(q;\mathbf{v}\right)-\beta\left(q\right)
, so that the equilibrium identity above implies
\gamma\left(X\right)=g_{v}\left(U\right)
a.s. As

is strictly increasing by assumption, it is the quantile function of

. Do analogously for
\mathbf{w}
so that

(where

).

Define

Note that
\Psi\left(q;\mathbf{v}\right)=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}v_{k}p_{k}\left(q\right)
for all

.
Hence,

g_{w}\left(q\right)-g_{v}\left(q\right)=\Psi\left(q;\mathbf{w}\right)-\Psi\left(q;\mathbf{v}\right)=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}\left(w_{k}-v_{k}\right)p_{k}\left(q\right).

Because
\mathbf{w}
majorizes
\mathbf{v}
,

can be obtained from

via a finite sequence of reverse Pigou-Dalton transfers.
Since the map

is linear for each

So fix

and

and suppose

is obtained from
\mathbf{v}
by moving

For

it follows that

for all

, with equality at

By the integrated-quantile characterization of the convex order, this implies

.

Finally, because

is strictly increasing and concave,

exists and is strictly increasing and convex.
Let

be any increasing convex function. Then

is convex, so

Therefore,

∎
