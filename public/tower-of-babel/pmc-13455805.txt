Potential Ferroptosis-Related Genes and Immune Infiltration in Form-Deprivation Myopia: An Integrative Bioinformatics and in vitro Validation Study
Authors: Zhihong Liu, Shudan Ge, Beibei Zheng, Lina Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the molecular mechanisms of form-deprivation myopia (FDM) by identifying potential ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) and characterizing the immune infiltration landscape, with the goal of providing preliminary insights that may inform future research on diagnostic or therapeutic candidates.MethodsTranscriptomic data from GSE223434 (training set) and GSE210238 (validation set) were analyzed to identify differentially expressed FRGs (FRDEGs). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and machine learning algorithms, including LASSO regression and Random Forest, were employed to screen for hub genes. Immune infiltration was assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Finally, the functional roles of hub genes were validated in vitro using TGF-β2-induced human scleral fibroblasts (HSFs) and siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments.ResultsA total of 256 FRDEGs were identified in FDM retinas. Three hub genes PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1, were selected and showed preliminary diagnostic potential value, with AUCs reaching 0.750, 0.725, and 0.762 in the training set. Immune analysis revealed significant differences in monocyte infiltration between myopia and control groups. In vitro assays confirmed that TGF-β2 treatment significantly elevated the expression of these hub genes and reduced GPX4 levels, while the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) reversed these effects. Furthermore, silencing PRKAA1, WWTR1, or TGFB3 promoted ferroptosis and fibrotic markers, which were significantly rescued by Fer-1 intervention.ConclusionThe identified ferroptosis-immune axis, centered on PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1, may play a role in the pathogenesis of myopia. These hub genes represent candidate molecules that warrant further investigation as potential diagnostic markers, and they provide a preliminary biological perspective for understanding myopic scleral remodeling in the context of ferroptosis regulation.Keywords: form-deprivation myopia, ferroptosis, immune infiltration, bioinformatics analysis

Introduction

Myopia has emerged as a significant global public health challenge, with its prevalence increasing at an alarming rate, particularly among children and adolescents.1,2 Among the various types, high myopia is frequently associated with sight-threatening complications such as retinal detachment, macular degeneration, and glaucoma.3,4 While the etiology of myopia remains multifaceted, involving genetic predisposition, and environmental factors, the underlying molecular mechanisms driving axial elongation and scleral remodeling are still not fully understood. Form-deprivation myopia (FDM), induced by altering visual input, serves as a primary experimental model to investigate the biochemical shifts occurring within the ocular tissues during myopic progression.5,6 Recent advancements in high-throughput sequencing and integrated bioinformatics have shifted the research focus toward the intersection of programmed cell death and inflammatory responses. Ferroptosis, a novel form of iron-dependent regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, has been implicated in various degenerative and fibrotic diseases.7–9 The emerging evidence suggests that oxidative stress and metabolic imbalances, it’s the hallmarks of ferroptosis which play a critical role of the scleral extracellular matrix.10,11 By identifying ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) specifically expressed in FDM models which can pinpoint the regulatory nodes that dictate whether a cell maintains its structural integrity or undergoes premature degradation.

Myopia is a multifactorial condition influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and developmental factors. Age of onset and disease progression are closely linked to childhood and adolescent growth, with axial elongation typically stabilizing in early adulthood.12 Environmental exposures, including prolonged near work and reduced time outdoors, have been consistently associated with increased myopia risk, partially through light‑induced retinal dopamine signaling.13 Visual experience, such as form deprivation or hyperopic defocus can trigger axial elongation via retina‑to‑sclera signaling cascades. Genetic susceptibility plays a substantial role, as evidenced by multiple genome‑wide association studies (GWAS) identifying loci involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, retinal development, and refraction.14 More recently, epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non‑coding RNAs, has emerged as a key layer linking environmental cues to long‑term gene expression changes in the sclera and retina.15,16 Recognizing this multifactorial landscape is essential for interpreting bioinformatic findings and experimental models in the context of human myopia.

Several recent studies have directly linked oxidative stress and ferroptosis to scleral remodeling in myopia. Lipid peroxidation products and iron accumulation have been observed in myopic sclera, accompanied by downregulation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a key ferroptosis regulator. In form‑deprivation myopia models, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels correlate with extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and scleral thinning.17,18 Although apoptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis have been implicated in myopia pathogenesis, ferroptosis is particularly relevant to scleral remodeling due to its iron‑dependent, non‑apoptotic nature and its tight link to lipid peroxidation and GPX4 inactivation. The sclera is rich in collagen and elastin, and oxidative stress, a hallmark of ferroptosis is known to directly activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and degrade ECM components. Unlike apoptosis, which typically leads to controlled cell elimination without inflammation, ferroptosis releases damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that can trigger immune cell recruitment, potentially explaining the low‑grade inflammatory infiltrates observed in myopic sclera.19

Neverless, several evidence support immune involvement in scleral remodeling during myopia. First, histological studies have detected increased infiltration of macrophages and monocytes in the sclera of myopic eyes, particularly around areas of active ECM degradation.20 Second, pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‑α, IL‑6, and IL‑1β are elevated in the sclera of form‑deprivation models, and these cytokines can stimulate MMP expression and fibroblast activation.21,22 Third, single‑cell transcriptomic analyses have revealed altered proportions of immune cell subsets in myopic versus control sclera. While the exact mechanisms remain incompletely defined, accumulating evidence suggests that immune cells contribute to scleral thinning and biomechanical weakening through the secretion of proteolytic enzymes and inflammatory mediators.23,24 Beyond cell death pathways, the role of the immune microenvironment in ocular development has gained considerable traction.25 Chronic, low-grade inflammation and the infiltration of specific immune cell subsets are thought to modulate the signaling cascades that lead to scleral weakening.26–28 Understanding the immune infiltration characterization allows for a more holistic view of the myopic eye, moving beyond a purely mechanical perspective to a complex immunological one, and we can quantify the abundance of immune cells and their correlation with ferroptosis-related markers.

Hence, this research aims to construct regulatory networks and performing functional enrichment analyses, we provide new insights into the ferroptosis-immune axis. These findings not only deepen our understanding of myopic pathogenesis but also highlight potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for preventing the progression of this refractive error.

Materials and Methods

GEO Dataset Acquisition and Preprocessing

Two publicly available transcriptomic datasets related to FDM retina were downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and we have acquired the GSE22343429 (GPL33051, Homo sapiens) and GSE210238 (GPL24676, Homo sapiens) datasets. Both GSE223434 and GSE210238 are publicly available datasets deposited in the NCBI GEO database. According to the original publications and the GEO submission records, all samples were obtained with approval from the respective ethics committees, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Besides, both GSE223434 and GSE210238 differ in platform, potentially in the exact definition of tissue source, species, definition of form-deprivation myopia and age. These differences could affect gene expression measurements and the reproducibility of individual gene markers. Raw expression data were normalized using the robust multi-array average (RMA) algorithm for microarray data to ensure the consistency and diagnostic criteria for myopia. Meanwhile, we utilized GSE223434 as the training set, while the GSE210238 served as validation set in the further data analysis, and the summary of GEO datasets used in this study was listed in Table 1. Detailed ethical information can be found in the original GEO accession records and the associated publications.

Ferroptosis-Related Gene (FRG) Collection

A comprehensive list of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) was obtained from the FerrDb V3 database,30 which contains 1371 ferroptosis regulators categorized as drivers. Genes present in both the GSE223434 and FerrDb V3 constituted the analytical universe. A total of 955 overlapping FRGs with the transcriptomic data were used for subsequent analysis.

Identification of Differentially Expressed FRGs (FRDEGs)

Differential expression analysis was performed using the R package “limma”. The significance thresholds were set at |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥ 0.5 and adjusted P-value (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR) < 0.05. Volcano plot was generated using “ggplot2” and “pheatmap” packages in R. The intersection of all DEGs with FRGs yielded differentially expressed FRGs (FRDEGs).

Identification of DEGs and Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis

The R package “WGCNA” was used to construct a co-expression network based on the gene expression data of GSE223434. The soft-thresholding power was determined by the scale-free topology criterion (R2 > 0.85) and the β = 18. Signed co-expression networks were constructed, and modules were identified by dynamic tree-cut with minimum module size of 30 genes and merge cut height of 0.25. Module-trait relationships were evaluated by Spearman correlation between module eigengenes (MEs) and the FDM phenotype. The module most significantly correlated with FDM was designated the hub module. Hub genes within this module, defined as genes with gene significance (GS) > 0.2 and module membership (MM) > 0.8 that also belonged to the FRDEGs were selected as WGCNA-derived key FRGs.

Functional Enrichment Analysis of the WGCNA-FRDEGs

The FRDEGs and genes in the key module from the WGCNA analysis was determined using the “VennDiagram” tools.31 To improve the selection process and reduce the chance of false positives, the Wilcoxon test was used on these candidate genes. This test compared their expression levels between the control and myopia groups. Potential biological roles and pathways associated with the WGCNA-FRDEGs were investigated through gene ontology (GO). The GO analysis focused on identifying enriched biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) were used by DAVID database.32

Machine Learning Algorithms for the Selection of the Hub Genes

To pinpoint hub genes pertinent to myopia diagnosis, machine learning techniques, specifically least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and random forest (RF), were employed through the R packages “glmnet” and “randomforest”, respectively. The LASSO algorithm facilitates both regularization and feature selection, thereby improving predictive accuracy and interpretability. Conversely, the RF algorithm mitigates potential overfitting associated with individual decision trees by synthesizing predictions from numerous trees, thus enhancing predictive dependability. The convergence of findings from both algorithms was then utilized to identify candidate hub genes for diagnostic purposes.

Construction and Validation of the Diagnostic Model

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the hub genes, their expression levels were compared between the control and myopia groups, and their diagnostic values were assessed through the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis by the R package “pROC”. The area under the curve (AUC) and the 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated and represented the diagnostic and predictive value of these genes. Validation of the diagnostic model was subsequently performed using the independent dataset GSE210238.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

To characterize the immune cell profiles, the cell-type identification by “CIBERSORT” algorithm was applied to estimate the immune infiltration based on 22 immune cell markers available from the R package “LM22”.33 CIBERSORT uses gene expression profiles to calculate and infer the composition of immune cells. Therefore, we acknowledge that tissue‑specific references would be more accurate and our immune estimates should be interpreted as relative trends and require histological validation. The relative proportions of immune cells were visualized using bar plots through the R package “ggplot2”, while a heatmap illustrated correlations between different immune cell types were used by LC-Bio Technologies.34 The differences in immune infiltration between the two groups were illustrated via a box plot through the R package “ggplot2”.

Cell Culture and FDM Mimicry

To validate the functional role of hub genes and the efficacy of targeted molecular interventions at the cellular level, Human scleral fibroblasts (HSFs) were used as the primary in vitro model. The HSFs were purchased from Shanghai QiDa Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (CAT: CD5023; China). No animal experiments were conducted in this study. HSFs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; CAT: P6000, Shanghai QiDa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; CAT: SD0200, Shanghai QiDa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (CAT: SD0038, Shanghai QiDa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Although form-deprivation myopia primarily originates from retinal signal changes, TGF‑β2 is a well‑established mediator that transmits myopia‑related signals from the retina/choroid to the sclera, where it induces fibroblast‑to‑myofibroblast transition and extracellular matrix remodeling. Therefore, simulating the form-deprivation environment in vitro, cells were subjected to be treated with TGF-β2 (10 ng/mL; CAT: 91709ES50, Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China) to induce myofibroblast differentiation and metabolic shifts characteristic of myopic scleral remodeling. To validate the role of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs), cells were treated with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin (10 μM; CAT: SC0224, Beyotime Biotech Inc, China) as a positive control. To assess whether the myopic phenotype could be rescued, the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin‑1 (Fer‑1, 1 μM; CAT: Y240805, Beyotime Biotech Inc, China) was co‑applied with TGF‑β2 stimulation. All treatments were compared with vehicle controls (0.1% DMSO, CAT: V87300; Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China) to exclude solvent‑induced effects. Furthermore, ferroptosis was further verified by assessing glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression. All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CAT: C0038; China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5×103 cells/well and treated as described. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Small Interfering RNA Transfection

To investigate the functional roles of the three hub ferroptosis‑related genes (PRKAA1, WWTR1, and TGFB3), HSFs were transfected with specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting each gene. siRNAs were designed and synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific. The target sequences are listed in Table 2. A non‑targeting scrambled siRNA (si‑NC) was used as a negative control. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HSFs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well. For each well, 50 nM of siRNA was diluted in Opti‑MEM medium and mixed with the transfection reagent, then added to the cells. The medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM 6 h post‑transfection. Knockdown efficiency was verified by RT‑qPCR 48 h after transfection. For the knockdown experiments, cells were divided into the following groups, si‑NC, si‑PRKAA1, si‑WWTR1, and si‑TGFB3. After 48 h of transfection, c ells were subjected to be treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β2 conditions for an additional 48 h before subsequent assays.

Rescue Experiment with Ferrostatin‑1

To determine whether the effects of hub gene knockdown are mediated via ferroptosis, a rescue experiment was performed. At 24 h post‑transfection, the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin‑1 was added to the culture medium concurrently with the TGF‑β2. Cells treated with vehicle (0.1% DMSO) served as controls. After 48 h of combined treatment, GPX4, ACSL4, α‑SMA, and COL1A1 were evaluated. The ability of Fer‑1 to reverse the phenotypes induced by hub gene knockdown was assessed by comparing the si‑target with Fer‑1 group and the si‑target group without inhibitor.

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from HSFs using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit. RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR system using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II. The reaction conditions were: 95°C for 30s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5s and 60°C for 30s. The expression levels of the hub ferroptosis genes (PRKAA1, WWTR1, and TGFB3) were quantified using gene-specific primers (listed in Table 2). β-actin was used as the endogenous reference gene. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 and R software (version 4.5.1). For the bioinformatic analyses, normality was assessed using Shapiro‑Wilk test. Parametric tests were applied when normality and homogeneity of variance were met; otherwise, non‑parametric tests were used. Group comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (non-parametric) or Student’s t-test (parametric, after Shapiro–Wilk normality testing). Correlations were assessed by Spearman’s rank correlation, and Correlation networks were visualized using Cytoscape 3.2.1.35 Multiple testing correction was performed with the Benjamini-Hochberg method. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data visualization was conducted using R packages including ggplot2, ggpubr, corrplot, pheatmap, glmnet, randomforest, VennDiagram, pROC, and ComplexHeatmap.

Identification of FDM Differentially Expressed FRGs

After RMA normalization, 16077 genes were detected across the GSE223434 dataset. Similarly, there were 1371 ferroptosis regulators obtained from the FerrDb V3 database. And acquired a total of 955 overlapping FRGs between the two databases (Figure 1A). Then, we have analyzed the 955 overlapping FRGs and differential expression analysis identified 256 DEGs (|log2FC| ≥ 0.5, FDR < 0.05), of which 183 were up-regulated and 73 were down-regulated in FDM retinas compared to controls (Figure 1B). In order to visualize the correlations between up-regulated and down-regulated genes, we utilized the Cytoscape software to map and analyze which genes may exhibit strong potential associations. The up-regulated FRDEGs included key ferroptosis drivers such as BCL2, IL10, MAPK8, KRAS, and MCL1 (Figure 1C), while down-regulated FRDEGs included EZH2, PARP1, PTPRC, DLD, and FAS (Figure 1D), consistent with FDM retina. The function of FRDEGs was investigated by KEGG and GO enrichment analyses afterwards. The most relevant KEGG pathways were Apoptosis, FoxO signaling pathway, Sphingolipid signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and Autophagy (Figure 1E). Meanwhile, the most enriched GO terms included BP terms such as positive regulation of apoptotic process, positive regulation of neuron apoptotic process, intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway in response to DNA, negative regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, and rhythmic process (Figure 1F); CC terms included cytoplasm, cytosol, mitochondrion, protein-containing complex, and nucleoplasm (Figure 1G); MF terms included protein binding, protein kinase activity, transferase activity, cysteine-type endopeptidase activity, and cysteine-type peptidase activity (Figure 1H).

Identifying the Hub Ferroptosis-Associated Genes Using WGCNA and Machine Learning

As depicted in Figure 2A and B, the soft threshold of 18 was chosen based on the scale independence and average connectivity (scale-free R2 = 0.85). Clustered tree diagram demonstrated that sample data belong to the similar original (Figure 2C). Using a module merge threshold of 0.3 and a minimum size of 20, 2 gene co-expression modules were generated and labeled with cyan and grey, the selection of grey contains 22 genes, whereas cyan contains 933 genes (Figure 2D). The LASSO regression and RF machine learning algorithms were applied to select the potential hub genes associated with the diagnosis of myopia. LASSO regression analysis identified 9 crucial genes with the lowest binominal deviance (Figures 2E and F). After ranking the FRDEGs according to the gene importance score, the top 10 potential candidates were detected by the RF algorithm (Figures 2G and H). By integrating the LASSO regression and RF results, 3 hub genes were yielded, namely Protein Kinase AMP-Activated Catalytic Subunit Alpha 1 (PRKAA1), Transforming Growth Factor Beta 3 (TGFB3), and WW Domain Containing Transcription Regulator 1 (WWTR1). To gain a clearer insight into the diagnostic significance of hub genes in FDM, we carried out ROC analyses. As depicted in Figures 2I–K, all 3 hub genes showed preliminary diagnostic potential value in the training dataset. The AUC values of PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1 were 0.750 (P = 0.0217), 0.725 (P = 0.0388), and 0.762 (P = 0.0163), respectively. Furthermore, the GSE210238 dataset served as the test set for validation and the AUC values of PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1 were 0.90 (P = 0.0367), 0.58 (P = 0.676), and 0.52 (P = 0.917), respectively (Figure 2L).

Discussion

The escalating global prevalence of myopia, particularly the rise of high myopia among pediatric populations, has transformed this refractive condition from a simple optical inconvenience into a looming public health crisis associated with irreversible vision loss.36,37 Conventional clinical approaches have largely focused on optical correction via spectacles or contact lenses and peripheral defocus modification; however, the fundamental molecular mechanisms driving pathological axial elongation remain partially obscured.12,38,39 This study bridges this gap by identifying a novel ferroptosis-immune axis that governs scleral remodeling, offering ophthalmologists a potential shift from purely mechanical interventions to targeted molecular therapies. By integrating bioinformatics with rigorous in vitro validation, we have demonstrated that form-deprivation myopia (FDM) is not merely a consequence of physical stretching but is a biologically active process characterized by iron-dependent programmed cell death and shifting immune landscapes. For the clinician, these findings suggest that the metabolic state of the sclera, specifically its vulnerability to lipid peroxidation, may serve as a critical determinant of whether a child’s refractive error stabilizes or progresses toward high myopia and its attendant risks of retinal detachment and macular degeneration.40–42

Myopia progression involves a retina‑to‑sclera signaling cascade, in which TGF‑β2 is a key mediator linking retinal signals to scleral remodeling. Therefore, we first used retinal transcriptomic datasets to screen for differentially expressed ferroptosis‑related genes. To test whether these candidate genes respond to myopia‑relevant signals in the sclera, we then validated their expression in TGF‑β2‑treated human scleral fibroblasts, an established in vitro model for scleral changes in myopia. Although the retina and sclera are distinct tissues, TGF‑β2 mediates retina‑to‑sclera communication during myopia progression, making this two‑step approach biologically plausible. Nevertheless, direct causal links between the retinal expression changes and scleral cellular responses remain to be established in future animal studies. Central to our findings is the identification of three hub genes, PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1, which represent promising candidates for future diagnostic panels and therapeutic targeting. PRKAA1, which encodes the catalytic subunit of AMPK, serves as a metabolic rheostat that, when dysregulated, may leave the sclera unable to withstand oxidative stress, thereby facilitating the transition to a myopic phenotype.43–45 TGFB3 acts as a critical modulator of the extracellular matrix (ECM); its upregulation in our FDM model suggests a specialized role in transforming growth factor signaling that specifically drives the pathological lipid peroxidation and collagen reorganization characteristic of myopic scleral weakening.46–48 Meanwhile, WWTR1 functions as a key downstream effector of the Hippo pathway, potentially translating the mechanical stretching forces of axial elongation into the biochemical signals that trigger ferroptotic cell death and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition.49,50 A seemingly counterintuitive finding of this study is that while TGF‑β2 stimulation increased the expression of PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1, silencing each of these genes paradoxically aggravated ferroptosis and fibrosis. Several non‑mutually exclusive explanations may account for this. First, these hub genes might be part of an adaptive/cytoprotective response. In our experimental model, the siRNA-mediated knockdown of these genes significantly exacerbated the expression of pro-ferroptotic markers like ACSL4 and fibrotic indicators such as α-SMA and COL1A1, while simultaneously suppressing the protective antioxidant enzyme GPX4. From a clinical perspective, the diagnostic performance of these genes, evidenced by AUC values reaching 0.762 for WWTR1 and showing consistent validity in independent test sets, indicates that molecular screening could supplement traditional axial length measurements to identify rapid progressors before significant structural changes occur. Furthermore, the observation that the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) could significantly rescue cell viability and reverse the expression of these remodeling markers provides a robust proof-of-concept for pharmacological intervention. In the form‑deprivation myopia model, their upregulation could represent a compensatory attempt to limit oxidative damage and maintain extracellular matrix homeostasis; knocking them down would then remove this protective brake, unleashing more severe ferroptosis and fibrosis. Second, these genes may be involved in negative feedback loops that restrain the ferroptotic pathway, such as AMPK can enhance antioxidant defenses through NRF2 activation; WWTR1 has been shown to modulate GPX4 expression under certain contexts; and TGFB3 can regulate ECM turnover in a context‑dependent manner. Third, the in vitro model might not fully replicate the chronic, multi‑factorial environment of in vivo myopia, where temporal dynamics and cell‑matrix interactions could alter gene function. This suggests a future where topical or localized administration of ferroptosis modulators could be used alongside low-dose atropine to provide a synergistic inhibitory effect on axial growth by stabilizing the scleral shell at the molecular level.

However, the validation AUC values were inconsistent. PRKAA1 showed an AUC of 0.90, whereas TGFB3 and WWTR1 performed poorly. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the extremely small validation sample size leads to wide confidence intervals and unstable estimates; a single outlier sample can drastically alter the AUC. Second, the training and validation datasets differ in microarray platforms, sample characteristics, and batch effects, which can affect the reproducibility of individual gene markers. Third, PRKAA1 may be a more robust cross‑dataset biomarker, whereas TGFB3 and WWTR1 might be more sensitive to cohort heterogeneity. Given these uncertainties, the validation results should not be interpreted as confirming or refuting the diagnostic value of these genes. Instead, they highlight the urgent need for larger, well‑powered, and standardized independent cohorts to properly assess the reproducibility of our findings.

The role of the immune microenvironment, particularly the significant infiltration of monocytes identified through the CIBERSORT algorithm, adds a critical layer to our understanding of the myopic sclera.51,52 While ophthalmology has long recognized the role of inflammation in uveitis and age-related macular degeneration, its role in myopia has been less clearly defined.53–55 Among the 22 immune cell types examined, only monocytes showed a statistically significant difference between FDM and control groups. Other cell populations did not reach significance, which may reflect the small sample size, the specific retinal tissue context, or a genuinely limited immune involvement in FDM compared to inflammatory eye diseases. In addition, our data suggests that FDM induces a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation where specific immune cell subsets, such as monocytes and neutrophils, may actively participate in the degradation of the scleral extracellular matrix. The negative correlation observed between different immune populations suggests a complex regulatory feedback loop where ferroptotic cell death might act as a damage-associated molecular pattern that recruits immune cells to the site of remodeling.17–19,56 For practitioners, this highlights the potential for using anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory strategies to stabilize the scleral shell, moving beyond the traditional view of the eye as an isolated optical chamber toward seeing it as an active immunological organ.

Despite the promising nature of these results, several limitations must be acknowledged to provide a balanced perspective for clinical translation. First, while our bioinformatic analysis utilized human-derived transcriptomic data, the functional validation was conducted in vitro using human scleral fibroblasts (HSFs). And the use of TGF‑β2‑treated HSFs does not fully recapitulate the complex, multi‑tissue environment of an FDM eye. This model captures acute scleral responses to a key myopia‑related cytokine but cannot model chronic axial elongation or age‑dependent progression, fully replicate the complex architecture and biomechanical tension of a living human eye, although useful for mechanistic study. Second, our study relied on the GSE223434 and GSE210238 datasets which cannot represent specific time points in myopia development; meanwhile, the validation dataset GSE210238 contains only five myopia and five control samples, which limits the statistical power and generalizability of the ROC analyses. The scarcity of human FDM transcriptomic datasets in public repositories is a recognized challenge which our results should be viewed as hypothesis‑generating and require replication in larger independent cohorts. Third, our in vitro experiments were limited to a 48-h stimulation period; longer‑term studies in animal models are needed to determine whether modulating PRKAA1, TGFB3, or WWTR1 can alter axial length and refractive error over time. Fourth, immune infiltration was estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm, which infers cell proportions from bulk transcriptomic data. This method is limited by its reliance on reference gene signatures, batch effects, and the inability to distinguish functionally distinct cell subsets. Direct histological validation would be needed to confirm the observed monocyte enrichment. Fifth, all gene expression data were derived from mRNA levels. We did not perform protein quantification for the hub genes or for key ferroptosis markers in the retinal tissues or scleral fibroblasts because of limited funds. Protein‑level validation is essential to confirm translational regulation and post‑translational modifications, which we acknowledge as a notable gap. Moreover, our study did not evaluate environmental factors or epigenetic mechanisms, which are known to modulate myopia progression in pediatric populations. Therefore, our findings cannot be directly extrapolated to children or to the natural history of progressive human myopia without further longitudinal and population‑based studies.

Despite these limitations, our study provides a novel molecular framework linking ferroptosis to scleral remodeling and offers a rationale for further translational research. Myopia is a longitudinal process that likely involves evolving molecular signatures over years. Furthermore, while we identified monocyte infiltration as a key feature, the precise signaling molecules that mediate the crosstalk between retinal ferroptosis and scleral immune recruitment remain to be fully elucidated in a physiological environment. Hence, future research should focus on optimizing these findings through several key directions to bridge the gap between bench and bedside. Longitudinal studies in larger, more diverse patient cohorts are necessary to validate these hub genes as definitive predictive biomarkers across different ethnicities and age groups. Besides, we acknowledge that, despite the novelty of integrating ferroptosis‑related gene analysis with immune infiltration in FDM, the current evidence is primarily correlative and hypothesis‑generating. Definitive establishment of PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1 as diagnostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets will require large‑scale clinical validation, longitudinal animal studies, and functional rescue experiments in vivo, due to the mammalian FDM models are essential to evaluate the pharmacokinetics and safety of local ferroptosis inhibitors like Fer-1 within the ocular tissues. Additionally, the development of multi-omics approaches, integrating proteomics and metabolomics with our current transcriptomic findings, could provide a more granular map of the ferroptosis-immune axis. Finally, exploring the synergistic potential of combining ferroptosis modulation with existing treatments, such as red-light therapy or specialized lens designs, could pave the way for a personalized, multi-modal approach to myopia control that addresses both the optical and molecular drivers of the disease.

Conclusion

This exploratory study identifies a potential ferroptosis‑immune axis involving PRKAA1, TGFB3, and WWTR1 in form‑deprivation myopia. Our findings provide preliminary evidence that ferroptosis‑related mechanisms may contribute to scleral remodeling, but they do not establish diagnostic or therapeutic applications. The small validation dataset, the limitations of the TGF‑β2 in vitro model, and the lack of environmental/epigenetic assessment preclude direct extrapolation to human myopia progression. Therefore, these candidate genes should be regarded as a molecular foundation for future hypothesis‑driven research, including in vivo animal studies and larger clinical cohort validations. Hence, these findings provide a novel biological basis for myopia pathogenesis and highlight new molecular targets that could been as potentially transformative therapeutic strategies for preventing axial elongation and its vision-threatening complications.
