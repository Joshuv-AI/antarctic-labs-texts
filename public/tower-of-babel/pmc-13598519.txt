Mounier–Kuhn syndrome incidentally diagnosed during the evaluation of atypical chest pain: CT findings and diagnostic challenges
Authors: Chaimae Abourak, Yahya El Harras, Ittimade Nassar, Yassine Hafiani, Mohamed Mahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mounier–Kuhn syndrome, or tracheobronchomegaly, is a rare disorder characterized by abnormal enlargement of the trachea and main bronchi. It may remain clinically silent or present with recurrent respiratory infections and chronic respiratory symptoms. We report the case of a 60-year-old non-smoking man who presented with intermittent atypical chest pain without respiratory symptoms or a history of recurrent pulmonary infections. Electrocardiography, echocardiography, and repeated cardiac troponin measurements were normal, and no specific cause of the chest pain was identified. Contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominopelvic computed tomography revealed marked tracheal enlargement, measuring 34 mm in the transverse diameter and 31 mm in the anteroposterior diameter on axial images, associated with tracheal diverticula and enlargement of the right main bronchus (22 mm), whereas the left main bronchus measured 17 mm. Bilateral centrilobular emphysematous changes and a few calcified lymph nodes were also observed, without bronchiectasis or active pulmonary infection. Pulmonary function testing was reported as normal, although detailed spirometric values were unavailable for retrospective review. These findings supported an incidental diagnosis of Mounier–Kuhn syndrome, with no evidence of a causal relationship between the syndrome and the presenting chest pain. This case highlights the importance of recognizing marked tracheal enlargement and characteristic diverticula on CT, even in patients without respiratory symptoms. In asymptomatic patients with preserved pulmonary function and no significant airway complications, management should be individualized, while the clinical significance of associated emphysematous changes and the optimal strategy for long-term surveillance remain uncertain.

Introduction

Mounier–Kuhn syndrome (MKS), also known as tracheobronchomegaly, is a rare disorder of the central airways characterized by marked enlargement of the trachea and central bronchi, resulting from structural abnormalities of the tracheobronchial wall. These airway abnormalities may impair mucociliary clearance and predispose patients to recurrent respiratory infections and bronchiectasis, although the clinical presentation is highly variable, with some patients remaining asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic [1,2].

Computed tomography (CT) plays a central role in the diagnosis, allowing accurate assessment of tracheal and bronchial dimensions and demonstrating characteristic morphological abnormalities, particularly tracheal diverticula. Classical diagnostic thresholds have been described, although measurement methods and diagnostic criteria vary across studies [1,2].

We report the case of a 60-year-old non-smoking man in whom Mounier–Kuhn syndrome was incidentally identified during the evaluation of atypical chest pain. The patient had no respiratory symptoms or history of recurrent pulmonary infections, and the diagnosis was established on the basis of characteristic CT findings despite preserved pulmonary function. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing tracheobronchomegaly on CT in clinically paucisymptomatic patients while avoiding assumptions regarding its relationship with nonspecific presenting symptoms or associated pulmonary abnormalities.

Case report

A 60-year-old man presented to the emergency department with intermittent atypical chest pain that had persisted for several weeks. The pain was non-exertional, poorly localized, and was not associated with dyspnea, cough, fever, hemoptysis, wheezing, or other respiratory symptoms. He had no history of recurrent respiratory infections, chronic respiratory disease, or asthma. He was a lifelong non-smoker and reported no significant passive smoking exposure during childhood or adulthood. He also reported no relevant occupational, recreational, or other exposure to inhaled dusts, fumes, chemicals, or respiratory toxins. His personal and family medical histories were otherwise unremarkable.

On physical examination, the patient was in good general condition. Hemodynamic parameters were stable, with an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and inflammatory markers, were within normal limits. Cardiac evaluation, including electrocardiography, echocardiography, and repeated measurements of cardiac troponin, was normal. No specific cardiac cause of the chest pain was identified.

Because of the persistent chest pain, contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) was performed. The examination showed marked diffuse enlargement of the trachea along its entire length. On axial images, the maximum tracheal diameter measured 34 mm in the transverse plane and 31 mm in the anteroposterior plane (Fig. 1). These measurements exceeded the commonly reported diagnostic thresholds for adult men. Pulmonary function testing was subsequently performed and was reported as normal in the available medical record. However, the original spirometric report, including the numerical values and Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) z-scores, was not available for retrospective review. No bronchial provocation test was performed. Given the absence of a history of asthma, wheezing, variable respiratory symptoms, or documented airflow obstruction, asthma was considered clinically unlikely, although it could not be formally excluded.

Bronchoscopy was not performed. Although endoscopic assessment may provide complementary information regarding central airway morphology and dynamic airway collapse or tracheobronchomalacia, the characteristic structural abnormalities identified on CT, the absence of respiratory symptoms, and the reported normal pulmonary function supported a conservative approach in this patient.

No specific organic cause of the atypical chest pain was identified during the available clinical and cardiac work-up. Mounier–Kuhn syndrome was therefore considered an incidental finding rather than an established cause of the presenting chest pain. The patient was managed conservatively with symptomatic treatment for the chest pain and pneumological follow-up. He was informed about the nature of the airway abnormality and the potential for future respiratory complications. During follow-up, his clinical course remained favorable, with no development of respiratory symptoms or clinical deterioration.

Discussion

Mounier–Kuhn syndrome (MKS), or tracheobronchomegaly, is a rare disorder of the central airways characterized by marked enlargement of the trachea and main bronchi resulting from structural abnormalities of the tracheobronchial wall [1,2]. Although it classically presents with recurrent respiratory infections, chronic cough, dyspnea, and bronchiectasis, its clinical spectrum is broad, and asymptomatic or paucisymptomatic forms have also been described [1,3]. The present case is noteworthy not because incidental MKS is unprecedented, but because marked tracheal enlargement with characteristic diverticula was identified in a 60-year-old lifelong non-smoker who had no respiratory symptoms, no history of recurrent pulmonary infections, and reportedly normal pulmonary function. The syndrome was discovered during the evaluation of atypical chest pain, without evidence of a causal relationship between the airway abnormality and the presenting symptom.

CT is central to the diagnosis of MKS because it allows accurate assessment of airway dimensions and demonstrates characteristic morphological abnormalities, particularly tracheal diverticula [1,2,4]. Classical diagnostic criteria in men include transverse and anteroposterior tracheal diameters exceeding approximately 25 and 27 mm, respectively, with corresponding thresholds of approximately 21 mm for the right main bronchus and 18 mm for the left main bronchus [1,2]. In our patient, the trachea measured 34 mm transversely and 31 mm anteroposteriorly on axial images, clearly exceeding these thresholds. The right main bronchus measured 22 mm and therefore exceeded the classical threshold, whereas the left main bronchus measured 17 mm and remained below it. Accordingly, the marked tracheomegaly and associated tracheal diverticula were considered the dominant diagnostic findings, with enlargement of the right main bronchus but without unequivocal enlargement of the left main bronchus according to the selected dimensional criteria. This finding highlights the importance of reporting actual airway measurements and applying diagnostic thresholds consistently, particularly because the criteria used for MKS diagnosis vary across studies [1,2].

Tracheal diverticula are characteristic imaging findings of MKS and are related to focal herniation of the membranous tracheal wall between the cartilaginous rings [1,2]. Their demonstration on CT may strengthen the diagnosis in patients with marked tracheal enlargement. In our patient, multiple tracheal diverticula associated with pronounced tracheomegaly supported the diagnosis despite the absence of significant bronchiectasis or respiratory symptoms.

Bronchoscopy may complement CT by directly demonstrating tracheobronchial enlargement and assessing dynamic airway collapse, particularly during expiration and coughing [4]. It was not performed in our patient because he was respiratory asymptomatic and CT demonstrated characteristic structural abnormalities. Consequently, dynamic airway collapse or tracheobronchomalacia could not be assessed endoscopically, representing a limitation of the present case. Similarly, pulmonary function testing was reported as normal, but the original numerical spirometric values and GLI z-scores were unavailable for retrospective review. The patient had no history of asthma, wheezing, or variable respiratory symptoms, and no bronchial provocation test was performed. Asthma was therefore considered clinically unlikely but could not be formally excluded by bronchial challenge testing.

The bilateral centrilobular emphysematous changes observed on CT require cautious interpretation. Emphysema has been described among the pulmonary abnormalities associated with MKS, although the coexistence of emphysematous changes does not establish a causal relationship with tracheobronchomegaly [1,5]. In our patient, there was no history of active or passive smoking and no reported occupational, recreational, or other significant inhalational exposure. Nevertheless, an alternative etiology cannot be completely excluded. We therefore consider the emphysematous changes to be an associated imaging finding rather than a demonstrated consequence of MKS. This distinction is particularly relevant in a non-smoking patient, in whom the presence of emphysema should not automatically be attributed to the underlying airway disorder.

The relationship between the patient's chest pain and MKS also requires careful consideration. The pain was atypical and non-exertional, and the cardiac evaluation, including electrocardiography, echocardiography, and repeated cardiac troponin measurements, was unremarkable. No specific cardiac or other thoracic cause was identified during the available evaluation. However, there is insufficient evidence to attribute the chest pain to MKS. The syndrome was therefore considered an incidental finding identified during the diagnostic work-up rather than an established explanation for the presenting symptom. This distinction is important because recognition of a rare radiological abnormality should not lead to inappropriate attribution of nonspecific symptoms without supporting clinical or pathophysiological evidence.

Management of MKS should be individualized according to symptoms, pulmonary function, airway complications, and disease severity [1,4]. Conservative management is generally appropriate in asymptomatic or paucisymptomatic patients without significant complications, whereas more invasive approaches may be considered in selected patients with severe airway instability or refractory respiratory complications [1,4]. In our patient, the absence of respiratory symptoms or recurrent infections, lack of bronchiectasis, and reportedly preserved pulmonary function supported conservative management. However, the optimal long-term surveillance strategy for asymptomatic patients with preserved respiratory function remains uncertain. Follow-up should therefore be individualized according to clinical evolution and the potential development of respiratory complications rather than assumed to be universally beneficial.

The main educational value of this case lies in demonstrating that marked tracheomegaly with characteristic diverticula may be identified incidentally in a patient without respiratory complaints or functional impairment. It also emphasizes the importance of standardized CT measurements, cautious interpretation of associated emphysematous changes, and avoidance of unwarranted causal attribution of nonspecific presenting symptoms. Recognition of characteristic airway morphology on CT can establish the diagnosis and help guide individualized clinical management.

Conclusion

Mounier-Kuhn syndrome is a rare central airway disorder that may be identified incidentally in patients without respiratory symptoms. In this case, marked tracheal enlargement and characteristic tracheal diverticula on CT supported the diagnosis despite preserved pulmonary function and the absence of bronchiectasis. The associated emphysematous changes should be interpreted cautiously, as their relationship to Mounier-Kuhn syndrome remains uncertain. Likewise, the syndrome was discovered during the evaluation of atypical chest pain, but no causal relationship between the two was established. This case highlights the diagnostic value of careful CT assessment and standardized airway measurements in clinically paucisymptomatic patients, while emphasizing the need for individualized management and follow-up.
