How pairing mechanism dictates topology in valley-polarized superconductors with Berry curvature
Julian May-Mann, Tobias Helbig, Trithep Devakul

Abstract
We investigate how the pairing mechanism influences topological superconductivity in valley-polarized systems with Berry curvature. We demonstrate that short-range attractive interactions, such as those mediated by phonons, favor superconducting states where the Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) Chern number has the same sign as the Berry curvature. In contrast, overscreened repulsive interactions, as in the Kohn-Luttinger mechanism, favor superconducting states where the BdG Chern number has the opposite sign as the Berry curvature. We establish these trends in a fully controlled limit and apply them to a recently reported chiral superconductor in rhombohedral multilayer graphene. Our theory provides a concrete experimental criterion for distinguishing between different pairing mechanisms in valley-polarized topological superconductors.

We reveal that

To illustrate the counterintuitive nature of this result, consider a valley-polarized superconductor emerging from a single circular Fermi surface, as depicted in Fig

In a smooth gauge, the phase winding of the momentum space order parameter

Viewing

This result has profound consequences. Since

Our findings establish that the relative signs of the thermal and anomalous Hall effects, in the superconducting and normal states respectively, can serve as a key experimental test of the nature of the underlying pairing mechanism.

Limit of uniform quantum geometry —
Before moving on to more realistic and complex models, we will first analyze superconductivity in the limit of uniform quantum geometry.
We consider a model with quadratic dispersion,
\epsilon(\bm{k})=|\bm{k}|^{2}/2m
; short-ranged interactions,
V_{\bm{q}}=U_{0}
(const.); and the form factors

F^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{k_{1}},\bm{k}_{2}}=e^{-\tfrac{\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}}{4}|\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}|^{2}-i\tfrac{\mathcal{B}}{2}\left(\bm{k}_{1}\times\bm{k}_{2}\right)}.

(2)

The parameter
\sigma^{2}

We remark that this model is formally equivalent to one with ideal form factors and a Gaussian interaction

Thus,

serves the dual purpose of tuning band geometry or controlling interaction range, depending on the interpretation.

This model is fully described by three dimensionless parameters,
\mathcal{B}k_{F}^{2}

\sigma^{2}k_{F}^{2}

\frac{m}{\pi}U_{0}

These parameters control the Berry curvature, quantum metric, and interaction strength respectively.
For convenience, we set

Attractive interactions —

First, we consider superconductivity driven by an attractive interaction,
U_{0}<0
.
To determine the BdG Chern number associated with the dominant superconducting instability, we project the pairing interaction to the Fermi surface.
This amounts to setting
\bm{k}_{1}=-\bm{k}_{2}=\bm{k}

\bm{q}=\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}
, and
|\bm{k}|=|\bm{k}^{\prime}|=k_{F}

In a rotationally invariant system, if pairing is restricted to the Fermi surface, each superconducting instability is characterized by a unique winding number
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{Z}

The pairing interaction in the

and
\bm{k}^{\prime}

The dominant instability corresponds to the most negative

The transition temperature for this state is
T_{c}\propto\exp(- 1/\rho| V_{\li}| )

For attractive interactions,

\begin{split}&V_{\mathcal{C}}\!=U_{0}e^{-\left[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}\right]}\!\left(\tfrac{2\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{\mathcal{C}/2}\!\!I_{\mathcal{C}}(\sqrt{\!2\mathcal{B}\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{4}})\end{split}

(3)

Repulsive interactions —
Next, we consider superconductors that emerge from the overscreening of a repulsive interaction.
To this end, we take

Using the overscreened interaction, we can then determine the dominant superconducting instability by following the same logic as before. The results are shown in Fig

Strikingly, we find

This can be understood as, in the

By contrast, when

Moreover, for both attractive and repulsive interactions, pairing is strongest when

Microscopic origin —

To understand the physical origin of the trends we have observed so far, let us consider superconductivity from a real-space perspective.
For a given interaction, the electrons that make up a Cooper pair arrange themselves to maximize attraction and minimize repulsion.
Short-range attractive interactions naturally favor Cooper pairs in which electrons are close together.
In contrast, overscreened repulsive interactions can be understood as attractive at long distances, but repulsive at short distances (see Fig.
1
).
As a result, electrons in these Cooper pairs prefer to stay further apart to avoid the short-range repulsion.

Now, consider the Cooper pairs associated with phase winding
\pm|\mathcal{C}|
.
For time-reversal symmetric bands (i.e., those with no Berry curvature), Cooper pairs with
\pm|\mathcal{C}|
have identical spatial profiles.
However, as we shall show, when
\mathcal{B}>0
,
+|\mathcal{C}|
Cooper pairs exhibit a shorter inter-particle distance than
-|\mathcal{C}|
Cooper pairs.
This naturally explains why attractive interactions favor
\mathcal{C}>0
superconductivity, while overscreened repulsion favors
\mathcal{C}<0
.

The real-space distribution of the Cooper pair is given by

|\Delta(\bm{r})|^{2}=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\Delta(\bm{k})\Delta^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime})(F^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}})^{2}e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}},

(4)

where
\bm{r}
is the inter-particle distance.
A key quantity to consider is
|\Delta(\bm{r}=0)|^{2}
, which we refer to as the “local occupancy”.
For bands with trivial form factors (e.g. bands made up of a single microscopic orbital), Pauli exclusion enforces a local occupancy of zero.
However, in bands with non-trivial form factors, the local occupancy can be non-zero.

As an illustration, consider an order parameter
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{k})\sim e^{i\mathcal{C}\theta}\delta(|\bm{k}|-k_{F})
that is nonzero only near the Fermi surface.
In the limit
\sigma^{2}=0
,
|\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{r})|^{2}
takes the form

\begin{split}|\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{r})|^{2}\propto\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\mathcal{B}}\frac{(\mathcal{B})^{n}}{n!}J_{|\mathcal{C}-n|}\left(k_{F}|\bm{r}|\right)^{2}\end{split}

(5)

up to an
\mathcal{C}
-independent prefactor. In Fig
2
(c) we plot
|\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{r})|^{2}
for several choices of
\mathcal{C}
.
In this limit, the local occupancy vanishes for all
-|\mathcal{C}|
states and is nonzero for all
+|\mathcal{C}|
states.
When
\sigma^{2}>0
, the local occupancy is finite for both
\pm|\mathcal{C}|
but, for fixed
\mathcal{C}
, the
-|\mathcal{C}|
local occupancy is always smaller than the
+|\mathcal{C}|
by a factor
(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}})^{\mathcal{C}}<1
.

This directly explains the previously observed trends.
Since
+|\mathcal{C}|
Cooper pairs have a higher local occupancy and are therefore more tightly bound, they are favored by short-range attractive interactions.
Conversely,
-|\mathcal{C}|
Cooper pairs are less tightly bound, so are more favored by the overscreened repulsive interactions.
Thus, the local occupancy provides a direct link between normal state band properties and the preferred superconducting topology under different pairing mechanisms.

In fact, we can prove that the
\mathcal{C}=-1
local occupancy is
always
lower than the
\mathcal{C}=+1
local occupancy for any rotation invariant system with positive Berry curvature, in the dilute limit
k_{F}\rightarrow 0
.
One can expand the form factors in harmonics of
\theta

F_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}=\sum_{n}f_{n}e^{-in\theta}
,
for
|\bm{k}|=|\bm{k}^{\prime}|=k_{F}
,
where
f_{n}
are real, non-negative, and scale as
k_{F}^{|n|}

sup
.
In the dilute limit, we can therefore restrict the sum to
-1\leq n\leq 1

In this case, the
\mathcal{C}=\pm 1
local occupancy is
\propto f_{\pm 1}f_{0}
.
Since the Berry curvature enclosed by the Fermi surface is given by
2\pi(f_{1}-f_{-1})
, we conclude that the
\mathcal{C}=-1
local occupancy is lower whenever the Berry curvature is positive.

Discussion —

We have demonstrated that the relative signs of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{B}
depend on the underlying pairing mechanism.
This relationship holds in explicit examples and is supported by general arguments based on the spatial structure of Cooper pairs.
However, this result is not rigorous, and fine-tuned counterexamples exist
sup
.
Nevertheless, this trend persists across the entire range of realistic scenarios we have explored.

An immediate consequence of our theory is that the relative signs of the anomalous and the thermal Hall effects, in the normal and superconducting states respectively, can serve as a direct indicator of the microscopic pairing mechanism.
While the precise magnitudes of these effects depend on details, their relative signs are robust.
To ensure consistency, these effects must be measured in states with the same valley polarization, which can be stabilized by a small positive magnetic field.
In R
4
G, at least one of the superconductors observed in Ref.
Han et al. 2024
is believed to originate from a single Fermi surface.
If pairing is mediated by an unconventional Kohn-Luttinger mechanism, as suggested by recent theories
Yang and Zhang 2024
;
Shavit and Alicea 2024
;
Jahin and Lin 2024
;
Chou et al. 2024
;
Qin and Wu 2024
;
Geier et al. 2024
;
Parra-Martinez et al. 2025
, our theory predicts that the anomalous and thermal Hall effects should exhibit opposite signs.

Conclusions —
Our results elucidate how both Berry curvature and pairing mechanism play a decisive role in shaping superconducting topology.
In particular, the spatial structure of Cooper pairs provides a simple yet powerful criterion for determining which topological superconducting states are energetically favored under different pairing mechanisms.
These insights provide a tangible experimental criterion for distinguishing between different pairing mechanisms, offering a guiding principle for understanding and characterizing novel topological superconductors.

I
Rotation Invariant Gauge for the Bloch Functions

Here we shall discuss the smooth rotation invariant gauge that we use in the main text. To begin, let us consider a band with Bloch functions
\ket{u(\bm{k})}
in an arbitrary. Our choice of gauge is uniquely determined by the following requirements. First, we require the gauge to be smooth. Second, in order to make the gauge rotation invariant, we require that

\ket{u(R_{\theta}\bm{k})}=e^{-iL^{\prime}\theta}U_{\theta}\ket{u(\bm{k})}

(8)

where the matrix
U_{\theta}
is the rotation operator in the orbital basis for an angle
\theta
, and
R_{\theta}\bm{k}
is the vector
\bm{k}
rotated by
\theta
. The integer
L^{\prime}
is needed to ensure that the gauge is smooth at
\bm{k}=0
. Explicitly,
e^{iL^{\prime}\theta}
is the solution to the eigenvalue equation,

U_{\theta}\ket{u(0)}=e^{iL^{\prime}\theta}\ket{u(0)}.

(9)

Different choices of
L^{\prime}
add vortices at
\bm{k}=0
. Third, we require that
\ket{u(0)}
is be real and positive. Fourth and finally, we require that
\bra{u(0)}\ket{u(\bm{k})}
is real for
\bm{k}=(k_{x},0)
with
k_{x}>0
.

It is useful to point out two important properties of this gauge. First, for
\bm{k}
and
\bm{k}^{\prime}
on the Fermi-surface, the form factors can be written as

F(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})=\bra{u(\bm{k})}\ket{u(\bm{k}^{\prime})}=\sum_{n}|u_{n}(\bm{k})|e^{-i(L_{n}-L^{\prime})\theta}

(10)

where
u_{n}(\bm{k})\equiv\bra{n}\ket{u(\bm{k})}
, is the projection of the Bloch function onto the
n^{\text{th}}
microscopic orbital, and
L_{n}
is the angular momentum of the
n^{\text{th}}
orbital, which satisfies

\bra{n}\ket{u(R_{\theta}\bm{k})}=e^{i(L_{n}-L^{\prime})\theta}\bra{n}\ket{u(\bm{k})}.

(11)

This transformation property is a direct consequence of the fact that we are working in a rotation invariant gauge, and that the microscopic orbitals have well-defined angular momentum.

Second, since the Bloch functions are smooth functions of momentum in this gauge,
\bra{n}\ket{u(R_{\theta}\bm{k})}
, can be expanded in powers of
\bm{k}
. In order to have the transformation property in Eq.
11
, the first non-trivial term in the expansion must be proportional to
(k_{x}\pm ik_{y})^{|(L_{n}-L^{\prime})|}
, where the
\pm
is for
L_{n}-L^{\prime}>0
and
L_{n}-L^{\prime}<0
respectively.

Diagrammatic evaluation

For the diagrammatic analysis, we are using the Hamiltonian

\begin{split}\hat{H}&=\sum_{\bm{q}}\epsilon(\bm{q})\gamma^{\dagger}_{\bm{q}}\gamma^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{q}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}},\\
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}&=\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{q},\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2}}\!\!\!\frac{1}{2A}V^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{q}}\phantom{|}F^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{1}\text{-}\bm{q}}\phantom{|}F^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{k}_{2},\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{q}}\phantom{|}\gamma^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}_{1}}\gamma^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{k}_{1}\text{-}\bm{q}}\gamma^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}_{2}}\gamma^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{q}},\end{split}

(12)

where
\gamma^{\dagger}
are the band projected creation operators,
\epsilon(\bm{k})
is the band dispersion, and
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
is a band-projected density-density interaction.
To calculate the effective overscreened interaction, we use perturbation theory and the random phase approximation (RPA). The corresponding diagrammatic expansions of the effective interaction in the Cooper channel are shown in Fig.
5
(a) and (b) respectively. The effective particle-particle interaction
V_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}
obtained from the diagrammatic approach is projected onto the Fermi surface by setting
\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}=\absolutevalue{\bm{k}^\prime}=k_{F}
. In order to find the superconducting order parameter
\Delta(\bm{k})
, we use a BCS mean field approach with the linearized gap equation (LGE)

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,V_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\,\langle\gamma_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\gamma_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\differential^{2}\bm{k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,V_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\,\frac{\tanh(\frac{E_{\bm{k}^\prime}}{2 k_\text{B} T_c})}{2E_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}\,\Delta(\bm{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\rho_{0}\int_{-\omega}^{\omega}\differential E\ \frac{\tanh(\frac{E}{2 k_\text{B} T_c})}{2E}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}{2\pi}\,V(\theta_{\bm{k}}-\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})\,\Delta(\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\approx-\rho_{0}\log(\frac{\omega\,\ee^{-\psi(\frac{1}{2})}}{ 2 \pi\,k_\text{B} T_c})\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}{2\pi}\,V(\theta_{\bm{k}}-\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})\,\Delta(\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})

(13)

with an energy cutoff
\omega
(
k_{\text{B}}T_{c}\ll\omega\ll E_{f}
) of the quasiparticle energy
E_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{\epsilon(\bm{k})^{2}+\absolutevalue{\Delta(\bm{k})}^{2}}\approx\absolutevalue{\epsilon(\bm{k})}
linearized around the critical temperature
T_{c}
.

\theta_{\bm{k}}
and
\theta_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}
denote the polar angles of
\bm{k}
and
\bm{k}^{\prime}
, respectively, while
\rho_{0}
is the density of states at the Fermi level and
\psi(z)
is the digamma function.
The LGE decouples in terms of the angular momentum channels

with the gap function decomposed in terms of angular momentum components
\Delta_{l}

\displaystyle\Delta(\theta_{\bm{k}})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\Delta_{l}\,e^{-il\theta_{\bm{k}}},

(14)

as

\displaystyle\Delta_{l}=-\rho_{0}\log(\frac{\omega\,\ee^{-\psi(\frac{1}{2})}}{ 2 \pi\,k_\text{B} T_c})\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}{2\pi}\,V(\theta_{\bm{k}}-\theta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})\,\Delta_{l}\,e^{il(\theta_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}-\theta_{\bm{k}})}\equiv-\rho_{0}\log(\frac{\omega\,\ee^{-\psi(\frac{1}{2})}}{ 2 \pi\,k_\text{B} T_c})\,\Delta_{l}\,V_{l}.

(15)

For
V_{l}<0
, where a Fermi instability occurs, the critical temperature

for the
l
-channel is then given by

\displaystyle k_{\text{B}}T_{c}=\frac{e^{-\psi(\frac{1}{2})}}{2\pi}\,\omega\,e^{-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\,\absolutevalue{V_l}}}.

(16)

For spinless fermions, only the odd angular momentum components in (
14

\Delta_{l}=0
for
l

From the Cooper logarithm in (
15
), the largest
T_{c}
is determined by the most negative

, which is identified as the dominant superconducting channel.
In equation (
15

corresponds to the eigenvalue obtained by diagonalization of the interaction on the Fermi surface through a discrete Fourier transformation as

\displaystyle V_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta_{\bm{k}}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}\,e^{il(\theta_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}-\theta_{\bm{k}})}\,V(\theta_{\bm{k}}-\theta_{\bm{k^{\prime}}})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\,e^{-il\theta}\,V(\theta),

Bare interaction

The bare interaction (corresponding to the first diagram on the RHS of Fig.

\displaystyle V_{\bm{k},\bm{k^{\prime}}}=U_{0}\,F(\bm{k},\bm{k^{\prime}})F(-\bm{k},-\bm{k^{\prime}})

The effective interaction in the
l

17

\displaystyle V_{l}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential\theta}{2\pi}\ e^{-il\theta+[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]k_{F}^{2}\,(\cos(\theta)-1)+i\,\mathcal{B}k_{F}^{2}\sin(\theta)}.

(20)

Such an integral of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential\theta}{2\pi}\ e^{il\theta}\,u(\theta)\,w(\theta)

(21)

with periodic functions

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{u\cdot w\}(l)=\left[U\star W\right](l).

(22)

Here,

\displaystyle U_{l}=\mathcal{F}\{u\}(l)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\,e^{il\theta}\,u(\theta).

(23)

with

denotes the discrete Fourier transform of

\displaystyle\left[U\star W\right](l)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}U_{k}W_{l-k}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}W_{l-k}V_{k}.

(24)

We identify the Fourier transformation of
\displaystyle u(\theta)=e^{[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]k_{F}^{2}\,\cos(\theta)}
as the modified Bessel function of the first kind,

\displaystyle I_{l}([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]k_{F}^{2})=i^{-l}\,J_{l}(i[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]k_{F}^{2}),

(25)

and that of
\displaystyle v(\theta)=e^{i\,\mathcal{B}k_{F}^{2}\,\sin(\theta)}
as the Bessel function of the first kind,
\displaystyle J_{-l}(\mathcal{B}k_{F}^{2})
. We then employ the multiplication theorem over a field of characteristic zero,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda^{\nu}}\,J_{\nu}(\lambda x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{(1-\lambda^{2})x}{2}\right)^{n}J_{\nu+n}(x),

(26)

to find an expression for the modified Bessel function

\displaystyle I_{l}\left([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}\right)^{n}}{n!}\,J_{l+n}\left([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}\right)

(27)

in terms of Bessel functions
J_{n}(x)
.
Putting everything together and using

, we obtain the result

\displaystyle V_{l}

\displaystyle=U_{0}\,e^{-[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\left([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}\right)^{n}}{n!}\,J_{k+n}([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2})\,J_{k+l}(\mathcal{B}\,k_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle=U_{0}\,e^{-[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left([\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}\right)^{n}}{n!}\,J_{n-l}(\sigma^{2}k_{F}^{2}),

\displaystyle=U_{0}\,e^{-[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]\,k_{F}^{2}}\,\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{l}\,I_{l}\left(\sqrt{2\mathcal{B}\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{4}}\,k_{F}^{2}\right)

(28)

where we made use of the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{\nu}(x)J_{\nu+n}(y)=J_{n}(y-x)

(29)

to obtain the second line.
For the case
\sigma^{2}=0
, we use the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{\nu}(x)J_{\nu+n}(x)=\delta_{n,0}.

(30)

and obtain

\displaystyle V_{l}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle U_{0}\,e^{-\mathcal{B}k_{F}^{2}}\,\frac{(\mathcal{B}\,k_{F}^{2})^{l}}{l!}&\quad l\geq 0,\\
0&\quad l<0.\\
\end{cases}

(31)

In this case, we observe, that the effective bare interaction in the negative angular momentum channels is zero, while the sign of the interaction in the positive angular momentum channels inherits the sign of
U_{0}

In the large
l

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(z)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\absolutevalue{\nu}}}\,\left(\frac{ez}{2\absolutevalue{\nu}}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\nu}}\sim\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\nu}!}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\nu}}.

This gives rise to the asymptotic large

\displaystyle V_{l}\sim U_{0}\,e^{-[\mathcal{B}+\sigma^{2}]k_{F}^{2}}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{l!}\left((\mathcal{B}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2})\,k_{F}^{2}\right)^{l}&l>0,\\[6.45831pt]

V
Pairing with an annular Fermi-surfaces

Let us consider pairing in a system with two Fermi-surfaces, a smaller one at
k_{f-}
and a larger one at
k_{f+}
. We will assume rotation symmetry throughout. If we assume pairing near the two Fermi-surfaces, we will have two gap functions,
\Delta_{-}
and
\Delta_{+}
. The self-consitency equations for the two gap functions can be written as

\begin{split}\Delta_{+}(\bm{k}_{+})=&\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}\langle\gamma_{\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}\gamma_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}\rangle\\
&+\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}\langle\gamma_{\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}\gamma_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}\rangle,\\
\Delta_{-}(\bm{k}_{-})=&\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{-},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}\langle\gamma_{\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}\gamma_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}\rangle\\
&+\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{-},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}\langle\gamma_{\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}\gamma_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}\rangle,\end{split}

(67)

where
\gamma
are the band-projected annihilation operators. Momentum with a
-
subscript are located near the inner Fermi-surface, and those with a
+
subscript are located near the outer Fermi-surface.
\tilde{V}
is the band-projected interaction in the Cooper (particle-particle) channel. For a bare attractive interaction,

\tilde{V}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}=V_{\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}}F_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}F_{-\bm{k},-\bm{k}^{\prime}}.

(68)

\tilde{V}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}
can also be an effective interaction obtained, for example, by using RPA to resum particle-hole fluctuations.

If pairing is only near the Fermi-surface, then we can take
\Delta_{\pm}
to be independent of
\bm{k}_{\pm}
, and we can diagonalize the self-consitency equation by considering a gap function with phase winding
l
,
\Delta_{l,\pm}
. For small
\Delta_{l,\pm}
, the resulting linearized gap equation is

\begin{split}\Delta_{l,+}=&-V_{l,++}\rho_{+}\log(\frac{W_+}{T})\Delta_{l,+}\\
&-V_{l,+-}\rho_{-}\log(\frac{W_-}{T})\Delta_{l,-},\\
\Delta_{l,-}=&-V_{l,-+}\rho_{+}\log(\frac{W_+}{T})\Delta_{l,+}\\
&-V_{l,--}\rho_{-}\log(\frac{W_-}{T})\Delta_{l,-},\end{split}

(69)

where
W_{\pm}
is a high energy cutoff associated with the inner and outer Fermi-surfaces and
\rho_{\pm}
is the density of states at the inner and outer Fermi-surfaces.
The interactions,
V_{l,\pm\pm}
are

\begin{split}&V_{l,++}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\theta_{++}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}e^{-il\theta_{++}}\\
&V_{l,+-}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\theta_{+-}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}e^{-il\theta_{+-}}\\
&V_{l,-+}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\theta_{-+}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{-},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{+}}e^{-il\theta_{-+}}\\
&V_{l,--}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\theta_{--}\tilde{V}_{\bm{k}_{-},\bm{k}^{\prime}_{-}}e^{-il\theta_{--}}\end{split}

(70)

where
\theta_{\pm\pm}
is the angle between
\bm{k}_{\pm}
and
\bm{k}^{\prime}_{\pm}
with
|\bm{k}_{\pm}|\approx k_{f\pm}
and
|\bm{k}^{\prime}_{\pm}|\approx k_{f\pm}
. Here, the superconducting
T_{c}
will depend in a complicated way on
V_{l,\pm\pm]}
and
W_{\pm}
. However, if
W_{+}=W_{-}\equiv W
, and
\rho_{+}=\rho_{-}\equiv\rho_{0}
and the density of states is the same at both Fermi-surface then
T_{c}=W\exp(-1/V_{l,min} \rho_0)
, where
V_{l,\text{min}}
is the most negative eigenvalue of the matrix,

V_{l}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{l,++}&V_{l,-+}\\
V_{l,+-}&V_{l,--}\end{bmatrix}.

(71)
