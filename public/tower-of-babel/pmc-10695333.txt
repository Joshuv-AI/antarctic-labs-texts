Predicting PD-L1 Status in Solid Tumors Using Transcriptomic Data and Artificial Intelligence Algorithms
Authors: Ahmad Charifa, Alfonso Lam, Hong Zhang, Andrew Ip, Andrew Pecora, Stanley Waintraub, Deena Graham, Donna McNamara, Martin Gutierrez, Andrew Jennis, Ipsa Sharma, Jeffrey Estella, Wanlong Ma, Andre Goy, Maher Albitar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) immunohistochemistry (IHC) is routinely used to predict the clinical response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs); however, multiple assays and antibodies have been used. This study aimed to evaluate the potential of targeted transcriptome and artificial intelligence (AI) to determine PD-L1 RNA expression levels and predict the ICI response compared with traditional IHC. RNA from 396 solid tumors samples was sequenced using next-generation sequencing (NGS) with a targeted 1408-gene panel. RNA expression and PD-L1 IHC were assessed across a broad range of PD-L1 expression levels. AI was used to predict the PD-L1 status. PD-L1 RNA levels assessed by NGS demonstrated robust linearity across high and low expression ranges, and those assessed using NGS and IHC (tumor proportion score and tumor-infiltrating immune cells) had a similar pattern. RNA sequencing provided in-depth information on the tumor microenvironment and immune response, including CD19, CD22, CD8A, CTLA4, and PD-L2 expression status. Subanalyses showed a sustained correlation of mRNA expression with IHC (tumor proportion score and immune cells) across different solid tumor types. Machine learning showed high accuracy in predicting PD-L1 status, with the area under the curve varying between 0.83 and 0.91. Targeted transcriptome sequencing combined with AI is highly useful for predicting PD-L1 status. Measuring PD-L1 mRNA expression by NGS is comparable to measuring PD-L1 expression by IHC for predicting ICI response. RNA expression has the added advantages of being amenable to standardization and avoiding interpretation bias, along with an in-depth evaluation of the tumor microenvironment.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assays for the assessment of CD274 (programmed death ligand-1, PD-L1) expression in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues have been evaluated by multiple trials as a companion test with immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-L1 and its main receptor (programmed cell death 1, PDCD1, or PD-1).1–5 These tests differ according to antibody clones, staining platforms, and scoring systems.6,7 These differences have led to an uncertainty in the general value of PD-L1 protein expression levels as a marker for different tumor types.8–10 Although a good concordance for 3 of the 5 antibody clones used in PD-L1 evaluation has been published,11,12 the interchanging assays and cutoffs could cause the misclassification of PD-L1 status in some cases. In addition, the use of FFPE tissue specimens with different fixation and storage methods may be a source of unexpected results for adequate PD-L1 antigen retrieval, potentially increasing the heterogeneity of the IHC intensity. All of these factors make the prediction of the patients’ clinical response to immune checkpoint inhibitors challenging using PD-L1 IHC.13,14

Recently, RNA profiling has become highly useful for providing information on the tumor microenvironment and immune response.15–23 The use of next-generation sequencing (NGS) to analyze RNA offers a high level of specificity and sensitivity for the simultaneous measurement of several targets to further elucidate the biology of tumors and predict the efficacy of various therapeutic approaches.15–19 Targeted RNA sequencing of various tissue samples has minimal input requirements, has the potential to be much more cost-effective than IHC methods,24 and allows sequencing at a deeper level for better quantification of low-level expressor genes. RNA sequencing and quantification using NGS are more reliable and reproducible than older techniques such as microarrays or PCR-based RNA quantification.17,18,25,26 The purpose of the paper is to demonstrate the reliability of RNAseq data to predict PD-L1 expression and the feasibility of developing a model system to predict specific PD-L1 cutoff points by using RNAseq data in machine learning systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Samples and Patients

A total of 396 (n=396) FFPE cancer samples were obtained for this study. Encompassing lung non–small-cell carcinoma (n=22), lung small cell carcinoma (n=3), breast carcinoma (n=125), melanoma (n=4), endometrial cancer (n=51), colorectal cancer (n=8), esophageal cancer (n=4), gastric cancer (n=7), urothelial cancer (n=3), cervical cancer (n=8), sarcoma (n=8), thyroid cancer (n=5), and prostate cancer (n=3). Samples with an IHC-based PD-L1 status were collected without any selection during routine clinical molecular profiling using NGS of the DNA and RNA. The tumor fractions varied between 30% and 80%, and the samples reflected the real-time occurrence of practice. Diagnoses of the samples are presented in Table 1. Tumor diagnosis was confirmed by morphologic analysis, IHC, and molecular profiling of the DNA and RNA. The Agencourt Forma Pure Total 96-Prep Kit (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) was used to extract both the DNA and RNA from FFPE samples using an automated KingFisher Flex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The Agencourt FormaPure Kit provides a split protocol to extract both the DNA and RNA from the same FFPE lysate. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Western Copernicus Group (New 100 England IRB, Aspire IRB, and Midlands IRB) (Number 1-1476184-1). The requirement for consent was waived due to the incidental collection and lack of risk. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.

RNA Library Construction and Sequencing

The samples were selectively enriched for 1408 cancer-associated genes using reagents provided in the Illumina TruSight RNA pan-cancer panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA). cDNA was generated from the cleaved RNA fragments using random primers during first-strand and second-strand synthesis. The sequencing adapters were ligated into the resulting double-stranded cDNA fragments. The coding regions of expressed genes were captured from this library using sequence-specific probes to create the final library. Sequencing was performed using an Illumina NextSeq 550 system platform (Illumina). Ten million reads per-sample were obtained in a single run, with a read length of 2×150 bp. An expression profile was generated from the sequencing coverage profile of each sample using the Cufflinks software. Expression levels were measured as fragments per kilobase of transcripts per million.

PD-L1 Testing by IHC

PD-L1 expression was assessed using the Dako Autostainer Link 48 instrumentation with the EnVision FLEX visualization system and the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), which is the standard FDA-approved kit.

Using Machine Learning Algorithm for Predicting PD-L1 Status

We first ranked the relevant genes using the Naive Bayes approach (Supplement 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JIT/A790). A Naive Bayesian classifier is used on each gene for distinguishing between 2 classes with k-fold (K=12) cross-validation. The k-fold cross-validation is usually implemented with k=n (ie, leave-one-out). Even though the leave-one-out method is more computationally intensive, the efficiency of the Naive Bayes algorithm would still make the selection process reasonably fast. For a confidence measure for gene selection, we relied on the P-value of a gene in differentiating the classes. Analysis of variance is applied to compute the P-value for a gene to discriminate between groups.

where MSB is the mean sum of squares between the groups, MSW the mean sum of squares within the groups, and F is the analysis of variance coefficient following the F-distribution. The P-value can be obtained from the F -value. This confidence value provides a measure of the stability and robustness of the gene in classifying the groups. It does not give concrete classification accuracy, but it provides overall confidence in the differences between the class means. Both criteria provide quantitative measures of the relevancy of the gene for classification. However, the 2 relevance measures do not always provide the same rankings. Applying both measures would produce effective and stable gene selection methods for machine learning-based classification systems.

After selecting individual genes, we used a random forest classifier to distinguish between classes using multiple selected genes with both confidence and P-values. In each case, 67% of the cases were used for training, and 33% were used for testing. Due to the low number of positive cases in the groups testing high PD-L expression (>30%), we used 60% of cases for training and 40% for testing. Dividing the cases was random, and there was no overlap between the testing and training cases.

Statistical Analyses

The level of significance was set at α=0.05. Standard statistical tests were used to evaluate the correlations between variables.

RESULTS

Similar Pattern Between PD-L1 RNA Levels Assessed by NGS and IHC With In-Depth Details of the Microenvironment

We quantified the expression of PD-L1 RNA using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped. We correlated the PD-L1 expression levels with the PD-L1 IHC results covering the high and low expression ranges [tumor cells <1%, >1%, <10%, and >10%; immune cells (IC) <1%, >1%, <10%, and >10%; and tumor proportion score (TPS) <1%, >1%, <10%, >10%, <30%, and >30%] (Table 1). The linearity of PD-L1 assessment by NGS was determined by comparing the expression levels of PD-L1 relative to PD-L1 IHC and other markers. For instance, PD-L1 RNA expression showed a positive linear correlation with PD-L1 IHC, tumor cells, and CPS (R2 > 0.5). RNA expression also provided in-depth information on the tumor microenvironment, including CD19, CD22, CD8A, and CTLA4. In addition, we demonstrated a high correlation between PD-L1 and PD-L2 (R2 > 0.8) (Table 2). Statistical tests demonstrated a similar pattern between PD-L1 RNA expression and IHC results across the various tumor types tested (Fig. 1). The P-value refers to the difference between various types of tumors. The Kruskal–Wallis test is used to generate the P-value.

High Accuracy in Predicting PD-L1 Status Using Genomic Data and Artificial Intelligence

Initially, we evaluated the ability of machine learning to determine PD-L1 status (Fig. 2). Using a machine learning algorithm, we first selected the proper genes to determine PD-L1 status using the best classifier biomarkers based on the P-value for predicting a specific expression cutoff. As shown in Figure 3, the area under the curve for most cutoff values was greater than 0.8. The artificial intelligence algorithm showed that the higher the PD-L1 cutoff, the fewer genes needed to determine PD-L1 status (Fig. 3). The dot on each curve represents a point at which the sensitivity and the specificity are shown. The first number represents the cutoff in the algorithm to get this specificity and sensitivity. To further clarify the specificity and sensitivity of the random forest approach, we now show the confusion statistics with the F-score. The shown ROC curves show the results of all tested cases based on the model in the testing set. There is no ranking. For testing future samples, we simply run them through the algorithm and obtain the score. DISCUSSION

PD-L1 appears to be a controversial biomarker that can be introduced into clinical practice. Four IHC-based tests are currently approved for guiding treatment decisions in patients with multiple tumor types, despite prior evidence demonstrating that both technological and histologic variability limit clinical utility.26,27

Our data demonstrated that RNA expression is a robust assay for measuring PD-L1 expression across a wide spectrum of expression levels. We used a large cohort of samples from multiple centers (n=396). The specimens included multiple tumor types from academic and community-based practices, reflecting real-world clinical scenarios. The samples were of variable tumor masses ,ranging from resection specimens to needle core biopsies and FNA cell blocks. Furthermore, the tumors and PD-L1 IHC staining were evaluated by different pathologists. RNA expression has the added advantage of being able to quantify RNA transcripts in the entire tumor microenvironment without subjective scoring methods and cell type discrimination. RNA sequencing is convenient as it enables the testing of several patients in a single run, with per-sample costs approaching those of IHC when performed in batches. In addition, measuring multiple transcripts simultaneously makes RNA sequencing well suited to characterize the functional state of ICs in the tumor microenvironment for biomarkers related to PD-1 checkpoint blockade. As demonstrated in our results, we were able to quantify the expression levels of CD19, CD22, CD8A, CTLA4, and PD-L2, which can help in tumor immunogenicity and provide data to explore new biomarker opportunities.27 We observed a positive correlation between PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression. Overexpression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in the same tumor, as well as overexpression of other inhibitory or activating molecules, can reliably indicate whether checkpoint blockade is a significant factor in a specific case.28,29

In addition, RNA expression profiling can be used in machine learning algorithms to provide insights into biological and clinical behavior, along with the therapeutic efficacy of various tumors.30–32 In this study, we also explored the potential of using RNA expression profiles to predict PD-L1 status using a machine learning approach. The machine learning algorithm was able to predict the status of PD-L1 with different clinically relevant cutoffs, with an area under the curve greater than 0.8.

In summary, this study demonstrated the potential of combining artificial intelligence with genomics in the routine practice of oncology and for predicting PD-L1 status. Therefore, clinical decisions can be made based on objective data. This paper demonstrated the reliability of RNA sequencing data to predict PD-L1 expression and the feasibility of developing a model system to predict specific PD-L1 cutoff points by using RNA data in machine learning systems.

Furthermore, this system can be continuously trained by adding additional samples or new biomarkers. The limitation of this study was its retrospective nature. Therefore, the PD-L1 status determined by RNA sequencing and machine learning algorithms needs to be further validated in future prospective clinical trials.
