Introduction

Cosmeticorexia has been proposed as a descriptive clinical label for persistent preoccupation with minor or unobservable skin imperfections, accompanied by excessive cosmetic or skincare behaviors and distress when the routine is interrupted.1,2 Because the construct has not been validated as a diagnosis, the term is used here phenomenologically rather than nosologically. The reported pattern may include influencer-driven comparison, product overuse, skin irritation, financial or social consequences, and persistence despite harm, but none of these features alone establishes psychopathology.

Cosmeticorexia is not included in ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR.3,4 Its closest formal diagnostic reference is BDD, which requires preoccupation with perceived defects that are not observable or appear slight to others, repetitive behaviors or mental acts, and clinically significant distress or impairment. Skin is a common focus in BDD, and camouflage, checking, reassurance seeking, comparison, and treatment seeking are well described.5,6 Recent evidence indicates that BDD commonly begins during adolescence and affects about 2% of adolescents at a given time; specialist youth samples also show substantial comorbidity, poor insight, school disruption, and self-harm risk.7,8 No population prevalence or age-of-onset estimate is currently available for cosmeticorexia.

Cosmeticorexia has gained public visibility through the “Sephora Kids” phenomenon and concern about tweens and adolescents participating in adult-oriented beauty retail and online skincare cultures.9–21 Media and regulatory attention provide sociocultural context but do not establish a disorder.22 Empirical research offers a more cautious picture: appearance-focused social media consciousness has prospectively preceded increases in depressive symptoms within adolescents, and meta-analytic evidence links online social comparison with body image concerns and eating-disorder symptoms, although effects vary and do not prove individual-level causation.23,24 A content analysis of pediatric skincare routines on TikTok documented multistep regimens, infrequent sunscreen use, and exposure to potentially irritating ingredients.25

Appearance concern, grooming experimentation, and interest in skincare can be normative during adolescence. A clinically concerning pattern is suggested not by product use alone but by the convergence of persistent preoccupation, rigidity, loss of control, marked distress, functional impairment, avoidance, and continuation despite physical harm. Problematic cosmetic overuse can also occur without meeting criteria for BDD. The present report therefore uses cosmeticorexia as a provisional descriptor and examines BDD and other psychiatric differentials rather than assuming a distinct disorder.

This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines.26,27 It contributes novel clinical material by integrating direct dermatological findings, patient and family observations, symptom screening, short-term clinical course, and a clearly delimited psychodynamic interpretation. Published cosmeticorexia literature remains largely conceptual and includes work by the present authors; those publications are used to define the provisional label and generate hypotheses, not as independent evidence of diagnostic validity. The sections that follow describe the case, diagnostic reasoning, intervention and outcome, empirical context, psychodynamic formulation, and limitations.

Case Presentation

Patient Information

The patient is a 14-year-old Asian girl in her first year of high school. Identifying details were altered or omitted to preserve confidentiality. She was initially brought to dermatology by her parents for recurrent periocular dermatitis, burning sensation, and intermittent acneiform eruptions (acne medicamentosa) (Figure 1). Her parents initially viewed the problem as purely dermatologic and, to some extent, as an age-related interest in beauty and self-care.

Irritant contact dermatitis and acne medicamentosa associated with cosmetic overuse. The clinical photograph shows malar and frontal papules, scattered open comedones, an erythematous background, and mild periocular dermatitis. No image enhancement or content alteration was performed.

Available information indicated that the patient spent approximately 4–5 hours per day viewing skincare routines on TikTok and comparing her face with influencers and peers. Her routine required approximately 1 hour each morning, with frequent reapplications during the day, and had become rigid and distressing to interrupt. No concurrent dermatoses were identified. Systematic developmental history, family psychiatric history, and broader prior mental health history were not available in the deidentified routine-care material; no inference about these potential vulnerabilities is therefore made. Primary Concerns and Symptoms

The patient reported using 10–12 skincare and makeup products on the face before school, including exfoliating acids, concealer, foundation, and several other products she purchased after watching TikTok routines. She viewed her cosmetic routine as ordinary, necessary, and socially normative. During dermatological consultations conducted by GD, one of the authors, and psychological interviews conducted by a clinical psychologist who was not involved in the authorship of this report, she showed limited insight into the excessive and potentially maladaptive nature of her cosmetic-related behaviors. “I don’t have a problem”, she told the psychologist. “My skin has a problem”. When asked about the time spent applying products, she replied, “Everyone does this. Maybe I do it better because I actually know what I’m doing”.

Her distress became more apparent when, following dermatological advice, her parents restricted cosmetic use because of skin irritation. On the first couple of days of reduced use, she did not attend school, repeatedly asked her mother whether her pores, redness, or “bumps” were visible, and stated, “I can’t go to school with that face”. Later that day, she spent several hours watching skincare videos and comparing her appearance to influencers’ and peers’. Recently, she started to avoid being seen by parents and relatives at home without makeup.

Clinical Findings

Dermatological evaluation identified recurrent periocular dermatitis, a burning sensation due to cosmetic-related photosensitization, eczema (irritant contact dermatitis), and intermittent acneiform eruptions (acne medicamentosa). These symptoms were clinically attributed to excessive and inappropriate use of cosmetic products, including exfoliating acids and multiple makeup products.

Psychological interviews revealed preoccupation with perceived skin flaws and facial appearance, compulsive checking, reassurance seeking, social comparison, distress when unable to use cosmetics, avoidance of school when the skin could not be concealed, and limited insight. The patient described her parents as “dramatic” and said, “They only brought me here because they read some stupid newspaper, and now they think skincare is a disease”.

Diagnostic Assessment

Diagnostic Methods

The assessment included clinical and dermatoscopic dermatological examinations, routine psychological interviews with the patient and her parents, the self-report BDDQ-DV screen, and clinician ratings of belief conviction and BDD-like symptom severity. No structured or semistructured psychiatric diagnostic interview and no standardized measures of depressive, generalized anxiety, social anxiety, or obsessive-compulsive symptoms outside the appearance domain were administered. Accordingly, no formal psychiatric diagnosis was assigned from these data.

Clinical dermatological examination showed malar and frontal papules, scattered open comedones, an erythematous background, and mild periocular dermatitis. Dermatoscopy (Delta 30 dermatoscope; Heine, Germany) confirmed follicular papules and mild erythematous periocular eczema associated with cosmetic overuse. The dermatological formulation was facial acne medicamentosa with cosmetic-associated photosensitization and irritant contact dermatitis of the periocular area due to makeup. The BDDQ-DV,28,29 a brief self-report screen for BDD symptoms in dermatology settings, was positive, with reported severe distress and impairment, interference with school and social functioning, and avoidance (Supplementary Table S1). A positive screen indicates need for diagnostic assessment and is not itself diagnostic of BDD.

Psychological interviews examined the frequency, rigidity, meaning, and consequences of the skincare and makeup routine; insight; checking; reassurance seeking; school avoidance; comparison; and distress when the routine was interrupted. The Brown Assessment of Beliefs Scale (BABS)30,31 and the adolescent BDD-YBOCS (BDD-YBOCS-A)32,33 were rated by the clinician from the available routine interview material rather than administered in a protocolized research assessment. The BABS total was 19/24 (Supplementary Table S2), and the BDD-YBOCS-A total was 38/48 (Supplementary Table S3). These ratings descriptively suggested poor insight and marked BDD-like preoccupation, repetitive behavior, avoidance, and impaired control. Because they were partly reconstructed and derived from a single rater, they are provisional estimates and cannot confirm diagnosis or severity category.

Diagnostic Challenges

The principal diagnostic challenge was to distinguish a culturally normalized adolescent interest in skincare from a clinically impairing pattern. The patient’s behavior crossed that threshold through its time cost, rigidity, school avoidance, intense distress, repeated checking and reassurance seeking, continuation despite dermatological injury, and limited capacity to reduce the routine. A further challenge was circularity: the referral and assessment were already oriented toward cosmetic overuse and BDD-like symptoms, increasing the risk of confirmation bias. Descriptive observations, screening results, formal diagnostic conclusions, and psychodynamic interpretations are therefore kept separate.

Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

The presentation was described provisionally as cosmeticorexia with dermatological complications. BDD was the closest formal differential because the patient showed skin-focused preoccupation, checking, comparison, reassurance seeking, concealment, and functional impairment. The proposed cosmeticorexia label emphasizes a culturally sanctioned and dermatologically active product ritual, distress when it is interrupted, and a self-reinforcing sequence in which product-related irritation becomes further evidence that the skin requires treatment and concealment. These features have not been shown to distinguish a separate disorder and may instead describe a behaviorally specific BDD-like or obsessive-compulsive-related presentation.

The BDDQ-DV result supported further assessment of BDD symptoms but did not establish BDD. The clinician-derived BDD-YBOCS-A and BABS ratings were consistent with marked preoccupation, repetitive cosmetic and checking behaviors, avoidance, and poor insight; however, their interpretation is limited by the absence of protocolized administration, independent scoring, diagnostic interviewing, and repeated measurement. The scores are therefore presented descriptively rather than as evidence validating cosmeticorexia.

Differential considerations included BDD, obsessive-compulsive disorder or related phenomena, social anxiety disorder, depressive and anxiety disorders, and normative or problematic but non-diagnostic cosmetic behavior, alongside irritant contact dermatitis and acne medicamentosa. The available material did not document non-appearance obsessions or compulsions, pervasive social fear across situations, sustained depressive syndrome, or a primary psychotic process. Because these domains were not assessed with a structured interview or disorder-specific measures, they cannot be confidently excluded. The appearance-bound content and partial doubt were more compatible with BDD-spectrum poor insight than with a primary psychotic disorder, but this remains a clinical inference.

Prognostic Considerations

No formal staging system applies to cosmeticorexia. Prognostic concerns included the patient’s low insight, school avoidance, distress when product use was restricted, compulsive checking, social media comparison, and poor tolerance of dermatological simplification. Favorable prognostic factors included parental involvement, dermatological consultation, and referral for psychological assessment. Longer-term prognosis could not be determined from the available follow-up.

Therapeutic Intervention

Dermatological intervention focused on reducing product-related irritation and repairing the skin barrier. The dermatologist advised discontinuing several products and using only a bland cleanser and moisturizer for atopic patients, and using sunscreen with high UVA coverage due to increased photosensitivity from cosmetics in this light spectrum. Exact product names, concentrations, and duration are not reported here because they could not be reliably reconstructed from the deidentified material.

Psychological intervention began with assessment, psychoeducation, and formulation. The initial clinical task was not to persuade the patient to stop the routine but to understand what the routine did for her psychologically. Treatment planning focused on helping the patient and her parents recognize the cycle linking perceived flaws, anxiety, product use, temporary relief, dermatological irritation, and renewed shame. Parent guidance emphasized reducing repeated reassurance and avoiding both collusion with the cosmetic ritual and shaming confrontation.

Because the patient experienced product reduction as humiliating rather than therapeutic, treatment required explicit role induction and shared formulation. The clinician clarified that psychological work would not aim to dismiss her skin concerns or abruptly remove her coping strategies, but to understand why the routine had become necessary and to help her develop additional ways to manage shame, exposure, and peer-related anxiety. This collaborative framing was intended to reduce the risk that treatment would be experienced as another form of scrutiny or control and was consistent with patient-centered psychotherapy principles, which emphasize patient preferences, collaboration, and clear discussion of treatment roles.34,35

Psychodynamically informed work explored, as a clinical hypothesis, whether the routine helped the patient manage exposure, shame, and the anticipated gaze of others. The aim was not to treat this interpretation as causal fact but to test collaboratively whether greater representation of these affects could reduce reliance on concrete skin management.

Follow-Up and Outcomes

Short-term follow-up showed partial adherence and poor tolerability of the dermatological simplification plan. The patient initially agreed to reduce her routine, but the simplified regimen left her feeling exposed. In a psychological session, she said, “They want me to go to school with nothing on my face. That’s not treatment; that’s humiliation”. Within days, she resumed concealer and exfoliating pads because the redness was experienced as intolerable. These products briefly reduced shame by concealing irritation but also perpetuated burning and desquamation.

Adherence was assessed through patient and parent reports. Adverse events included persistent skin irritation, burning, desquamation, and psychological distress when the routine was downsized. At the time of this report, no clinically meaningful improvement in symptom severity or insight had yet been observed. The BDDQ-DV, BABS, and BDD-YBOCS-A were not repeated during follow-up; therefore, change in symptom severity and insight could not be quantified. This limits conclusions regarding treatment response.

Patient Perspective

A separate patient-authored post-treatment perspective was unavailable. Statements recorded during routine interviews nevertheless conveyed that the patient regarded the routine as necessary self-protection rather than pathology: “I don’t have a problem. My skin has a problem”. She experienced product reduction as humiliating: “They want me to go to school with nothing on my face. That’s not treatment; that’s humiliation”. These statements were interpreted as suggesting that public visibility without cosmetic concealment was a central source of distress; alternative meanings cannot be excluded.

Discussion

This case contributes descriptive clinical material rather than evidence for a new diagnostic entity. Its core features—appearance preoccupation, checking, reassurance seeking, concealment, poor insight, and school avoidance—overlap with youth BDD. In a carefully diagnosed cohort of 172 young people with BDD, moderate-to-severe symptoms, psychiatric comorbidity, poor or absent insight, self-harm, and school dropout were common.7 Contemporary guidance therefore emphasizes systematic assessment of BDD, comorbidity, functioning, and risk in young people.8 The absence of a structured diagnostic interview in this case prevents a comparable conclusion.

The clinically informative feature was the convergence of an apparently normative skincare practice, repetitive and inflexible product use, objective dermatological injury, and marked functional impairment. Pediatric TikTok content research confirms that multistep routines can expose young users to potentially irritating ingredients,25 but product complexity or social media use alone should not be pathologized. Normative grooming is generally flexible and compatible with functioning; problematic cosmetic use may cause cost or skin harm without a BDD syndrome; BDD requires the defined pattern of preoccupation, repetitive responses, distress, and impairment. The patient’s presentation lay at the severe end of this continuum, but its formal classification remains unresolved.

The proposed distinction between cosmeticorexia and BDD is phenomenological, not empirically validated. BDD can already involve skin concerns, camouflage, product use, and cosmetic treatment seeking. The cosmeticorexia label draws attention to the centrality of commercially available skincare or makeup rituals, their cultural normalization, and the possibility that the attempted solution produces visible skin damage. These features may define a clinically useful presentation within BDD or the broader obsessive-compulsive and related spectrum; this case cannot determine whether they constitute a subtype or separate disorder.

The psychodynamic account that follows is an interpretive formulation derived from the patient’s statements, observed behavior, and short clinical course. It is not an empirically validated causal model, and it does not supersede descriptive diagnosis or evidence-based assessment. In plan-formulation terms, the working hypothesis was that visible, untreated skin signified a defective and socially unacceptable self.36 The patient’s conscious goal was to correct or conceal the skin; the broader therapeutic goal was to increase her capacity to appear before others without a rigid cosmetic envelope. Her rapid return to concealer and exfoliating pads after dermatological simplification may have reflected both symptom persistence and an attempt to communicate the intensity of exposure. This interpretation was inferred rather than directly tested.

A psychodynamic lens can complement, but not replace, diagnostic reasoning by asking what function a symptom may serve in mental life. The PDM-3 identifies bodily experience and representation as one dimension of mental functioning.37 Applied cautiously here, this perspective directed attention to shame, bodily self-experience, and the difference between being recognized and feeling scrutinized. It does not imply that all excessive skincare has the same meaning or originates in early relational disturbance.

The concepts of a harsh internal observer and a persecutory gaze38 offered one possible account of the patient’s fear that untreated skin would expose her to rejection. Her statement, “I need to know what my classmates will see before they see it”, repeated mirror and selfie checking, zooming in on texture, and transient relief from reassurance provide direct descriptive evidence of anticipatory scrutiny. Interpreting these behaviors as a failure of a benign internal gaze39 is a theoretical inference, not an observed fact.

Skin-ego and second-skin concepts40–42 can likewise be used as metaphors for the apparent containing function of the routine. The fixed sequence of serum, moisturizer, sunscreen, primer, foundation, concealer, and other products may have provided predictability and short-term affect regulation. The patient reported that changing the sequence felt wrong and disrupted concentration. These observations support the narrower conclusion that the ritual had a regulating function; they do not demonstrate a specific developmental origin or psychic structure.

Digital and developmental processes provide a complementary account. Appearance-focused social media consciousness has shown prospective within-person associations with depressive symptoms in early-to-middle adolescence,23 and meta-analytic evidence links online comparison with body image concerns.24 These findings do not establish causality in this patient, but her 4–5 hours of daily skincare content, peer and influencer comparison, and belief that elaborate routines were normative plausibly maintained attention to perceived skin defects and increased the availability of product-based responses.

The most concrete contribution of the case is the observed skin-damage loop. Product overuse was followed by irritation and acneiform eruptions; visible changes increased shame and concealment; concealment and renewed product use then perpetuated the dermatological problem. This temporal sequence is compatible with the authors’ prior conceptual description2 and with evidence that pediatric social media regimens may contain multiple irritants,25 but a single case cannot establish the loop’s frequency, specificity, or psychodynamic cause.

Clinical management should prioritize integrated dermatological and mental health assessment. Dermatological care can reduce irritant exposure and repair the skin barrier, while mental health assessment should examine BDD criteria, depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, suicidality, school functioning, and family responses. If BDD is established, developmentally adapted cognitive behavioral therapy, with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors when indicated, has the strongest current evidence in young people.8 A psychodynamic formulation may be used adjunctively to support engagement and understand individualized meanings, not as a substitute for evidence-based treatment.

In this case, collaborative framing was clinically important because abrupt product restriction was experienced as humiliation. A staged plan may combine medically necessary simplification with validation of distress, reduction of reassurance cycles, monitoring of avoidance and skin injury, and gradual development of alternative ways to tolerate appearance-related anxiety. These principles are consistent with the observed difficulty but were not tested as a standardized intervention, and no treatment efficacy can be inferred from the short follow-up.

For clinicians, the case suggests asking not only which products are used but how much time the routine consumes, how rigid it is, what happens when it is interrupted, whether it causes skin injury, and whether it affects school, relationships, or public visibility. For public health and media literacy, the case supports age-appropriate education about simple skincare, commercial persuasion, image comparison, and warning signs of impairment. It does not support population screening for cosmeticorexia or clinical guidelines based on this label; those steps require validated criteria, prospective studies, diverse samples, and comparative treatment research.

The novelty of this report therefore lies in its case-level integration of dermatological findings, patient statements, daily ritual behavior, and short-term clinical course. The findings refine and constrain earlier conceptual proposals by showing both where the provisional label may be clinically useful and where it risks reifying a diagnosis. Future research should test whether the product-centered skin-damage pattern adds reliable information beyond established BDD and obsessive-compulsive constructs.

Strengths and Limitations of This Case Report

The principal strength is the integration of direct dermatological findings, detailed daily behavior, patient statements, family observations, screening and clinician ratings, and short-term clinical course. This material adds a clinical illustration to earlier conceptual work while explicitly separating observed data from diagnostic and psychodynamic inference. The case also documents a plausible skin-damage loop in which cosmetic overuse, visible irritation, shame, and renewed concealment reinforced one another.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. This single case cannot establish prevalence, causality, diagnostic boundaries, or treatment efficacy. The assessment was formulation-driven and no structured psychiatric interview was conducted; depression, anxiety, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms outside the appearance domain, eating pathology, and suicidality were not systematically measured. BDDQ-DV is a screening tool, and the BABS and BDD-YBOCS-A ratings were partly reconstructed by one clinician from routine interview material; their scores are descriptive and vulnerable to confirmation and common-rater bias. Developmental and family psychiatric history was limited. The psychodynamic formulation is one hypothesis among alternatives and cannot be verified from the available observations. Long-term follow-up, repeated measures, independent diagnostic assessment, and a patient-authored outcome perspective were unavailable. Finally, identifying details were intentionally omitted, reducing clinical granularity.

Conclusion

This case describes a clinically significant adolescent presentation in which skin-focused preoccupation, rigid cosmetic rituals, school avoidance, and product-related dermatological injury converged. The provisional term cosmeticorexia may help draw attention to this pattern, but the case neither establishes a distinct diagnosis nor validates a psychodynamic cause. Clinicians should assess dermatological harm, BDD and other psychiatric differentials, functional impairment, and the individual meaning of the routine, while avoiding both dismissal as vanity and pathologization of normative grooming. Integrated dermatological and evidence-based mental health care is warranted when impairment is present. Diagnostic criteria, clinical guidelines, public health recommendations, and claims of improved outcomes require larger prospective and comparative studies.
