Burdens and patterns of gastrointestinal diseases in Thai children: an analysis based on Thai national health coverage data
Authors: Songpon Getsuwan, Sumitr Sutra, Busara Charoenwat, Kaewjai Thepsuthammarat, Wandee Varavithya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastrointestinal (GI) diseases are significant health problems worldwide. Due to the limited number of national reports among upper-middle-income countries, we aimed to study burdens and patterns of GI diseases in Thai children.MethodsThe study retrospectively analyzed data on inpatients aged <18 years provided by Thailand’s National Health Security Office (NHSO) from 2015 to 2023.ResultsThe primary diagnosis of GI diseases during the study period ranged from 134,000 to 296,040 admissions per year (10.2-16% of all inpatient diagnoses). The most common diagnosis group was intestinal infectious diseases (58.6%-66.3%), of which other gastroenteritis and colitis of infectious and unspecified origin (A09) was prevalent in every age group. Admission mainly was found among children aged 1-5 years (36.5%-48.1%) and in the secondary hospitals (67.8-71.2%). In 2015-2022, the mortality rate of GI diseases was 0.5-0.7 deaths per 1,000 admissions (2.3-3.2% of the total inpatient deaths). In 2023, the mortality rate of GI diseases reduced to 0.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions, describing the highest rates in the groups of malignant neoplasms of digestive organs and diseases of the peritoneum (17.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions, both), followed by diseases of the liver (15.1 deaths per 1,000 admissions). Hepatic failure, not elsewhere classified (K72), had the highest mortality (106.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions). The total paid by the NHSO for GI diseases was 77,370,498 Thai Baht or 2,230,790 US dollars in the fiscal year 2022-2023.ConclusionsPediatric GI diseases burdened the Thai healthcare system, particularly intestinal infectious diseases. Background

Gastrointestinal (GI) diseases are global health problems. Some of the diseases, particularly diarrhea and liver diseases, have been important causes of death in low-income countries [1]. Although the mortality rates are low in high-income countries, a recent report has shown that GI, liver, and pancreatic diseases have significant burdens on the healthcare system [2].

Previous studies have demonstrated that GI diseases, such as intestinal infectious diseases, gastritis, and duodenitis, have been common problems in Thai children and adolescents [3–5]. Burdens and patterns of some pediatric GI diseases have been studied in Thailand. For example, Sutra et al. [6] and Charoenwat et al. [7] have revealed that diarrheal diseases burden the Thai healthcare system. Due to the improvement in hygiene and sanitization, the number of dead children from diarrhea and infected children with hepatitis A virus has been reduced [6, 8]. Nevertheless, a national report on pediatric GI diseases' overall burdens and patterns is limited.

Thailand emerged as an upper-middle-income country in 2011 and successfully established a universal health coverage system in 2012 [9]. Currently, three primary health schemes cover 99.4% of Thai citizens, including the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme, the earliest government-endorsed program, was established to ensure medical benefits for government employees, pensioners, and their dependents. Subsequently Security, the Social Scheme was introduced to extend coverage to employees in the formal sector. Most recently, the Universal Coverage Scheme (UCS) was implemented [10]. The latter scheme covers approximately 75% of the population [9]. In this study, we aimed to identify the burdens and patterns of GI disease, including the diseases in the digestive organs and hepatobiliary system, among pediatric patients under the UCS.

Methods

Study population and data collection

A descriptive-analytic study was performed to determine the burdens of GI diseases regarding admission numbers, inpatient mortality rates, and total payment for primary GI diseases among children under 18 years between the fiscal years of 2015 and 2023. The Thai government’s fiscal year is from October 1 through September 30. The validated National Health Coverage (NHC) data were obtained from Thailand’s National Health Security Office (NHSO), which operates the UCS in the country. The information was extracted from the discharge summary from all government hospitals and some private hospitals under contract with NHSO by using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision, Thai Modification (ICD-10-TM). Inaccurate and incomplete medical records were excluded. The GI diseases were defined according to ICD-10-TM as 1) A00-A09, Intestinal infectious diseases; 2) C15-C26, Malignant neoplasms of digestive organs; 3) K00-K14, Diseases of the oral cavity, salivary glands and jaws; 4) K20-K31, Diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum; 5) K35-K38, Diseases of the appendix; 6) K40-K46, Hernia; 7) K50-K52, Noninfective enteritis and colitis; 8) K55-K64, Other diseases of the intestines; 9) K65-K67, Diseases of the peritoneum; 10) K70-K77, Diseases of the liver; 11) K80-K87, Disorders of the gallbladder, biliary tract and pancreas; 12) K90-K93, Other diseases of the digestive system; and 13) R10-R19, Symptoms and signs involving the digestive system and abdomen. The code data were analyzed for the burdens of GI diseases related to admission, death, and payment using the same code. The denominator for calculating admission numbers was based on the UCS, which covered three-quarters of the Thai population.

The admission numbers were collected as admissions per year, and inpatient mortality rates were calculated as deaths per 1,000 admissions. The hospital expenditure for primary GI diseases was calculated as the cost (Thai Baht, THB) per year. Because the payment system for outpatient visits was per capita, the data might be unreliable and excluded. Demographic data (age and gender), geographic regions, hospital levels, admission details, and mortality rates were collected.

The present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Human Research Ethics Committee, Center for Ethics in Human Research, Khon Kaen University (HE671511) on September 9, 2024, and the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (COA. MURA 2024/647) on September 19, 2024. Informed consent was waived by both committees due to the lack of personally identifiable data. The study was also registered in the Thai Clinical Trials (TCTR20241030014).

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were described as medians (interquartile range), means (standard deviation), and frequencies (%). Inpatient mortality rates were analyzed as deaths per 1,000 admissions of children aged under 18 years. The age groups were divided into 0 to 30 days, 1 to 11 months, 1 to 5 years, 6 to 12 years, and 13 to 17 years. The rates were calculated by using the Poisson regression test. The study applied the Stata program (StataCorp. Version 10.1, College Station, TX) for statistical analysis.

Results

Admission numbers

During the study period from 2015 to 2023, the primary diagnosis of GI diseases varied from 134,000 (in 2021) to 296,040 (in 2018), approximately 10.2–16.2% of all inpatient diagnoses (Fig. 1A). The most common diagnosis group was intestinal infectious diseases (58.6%−66.3%) (Fig. 1B), which was present in all age groups (Fig. 2). The second most common diseases were diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum in young children aged <5 years and diseases of the appendix in older children and adolescents. Figure 3 reveals the top five diagnoses with the highest fiscal year 2022-2023 admissions. Other gastroenteritis and colitis of infectious and unspecified origin (A09) were highly prevalent in almost every age group. Other common diagnoses were gastritis and duodenitis (K29) and viral and other specified intestinal infections (A08), particularly in young children aged <5 years, as well as acute appendicitis (K35) in older children.

From 2015-2022, the most common age groups of children with primary GI diseases were 1–5 years (36.5%−48.1%) and 6–12 years (23.7%−35.3%), respectively (Fig. 4A). This trend was also noted in most diagnostic groups in the fiscal year 2022-2023 (Fig. 5A), including intestinal infectious diseases, malignant neoplasms of digestive organs, diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, hernia, noninfective enteritis and colitis, and other diseases of the intestines. However, some diagnostic groups were more prevalent in adolescents and children aged 6–12 years, such as diseases of the appendix, diseases of the peritoneum, diseases of the liver, and disorders of the gallbladder, biliary tract, and pancreas. Infants generally had low numbers of admissions in all diagnosis groups.

Considering the geographic regions, the overall GI diseases were mainly presented in the northeastern region (34.1%−41.2%), followed by the southern region (16.1%−20.2%) and the central region (17.2–20.3.2.3%), respectively (Fig. 4B). Most diagnosis groups also demonstrated this pattern (Fig. 5B).

According to the levels of care, the secondary hospitals had the highest number of admissions (67.5%−71.2%), followed by the tertiary hospitals (19.3–22.3.3.3%) and the primary hospitals (6.9%−8.6%) (Fig. 4C). A similar pattern was noted in most diagnosis groups (Fig. 5C), except higher admissions in the tertiary hospitals were observed in malignant neoplasms of digestive organs, hernia, diseases of the liver, and disorders of the gallbladder, biliary tract, and pancreas.

Inpatient mortality rates

The overall mortality rate of pediatric GI diseases was low (0.5–0.7.5.7 deaths per 1,000 admissions), with only 2.3–3.2.3.2% of the total inpatient deaths from 2015 to 2012 (Fig. 1C). The mortality rate was even lower in the fiscal year 2022-2023 (0.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions) (supplementary table 1). Among the common diagnostic groups, such as intestinal infectious diseases, diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, and diseases of the appendix, the mortality rates were approximately 0.1–0.4.1.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions. No mortality was noted in diseases of the oral cavity, salivary glands and jaws, hernia, and noninfective enteritis and colitis. Despite having lower numbers of admissions, the inpatient mortality was high in the diagnostic groups of malignant neoplasms of digestive organs and diseases of the peritoneum (17.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions, both), as well as diseases of the liver (15.1 deaths per 1,000 admissions).

According to the primary diagnosis, hepatic failure, not elsewhere classified (K72), had the highest inpatient mortality rate (106.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions), particularly in adolescents (136.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions). The diagnosis with more than ten deaths per 1,000 admissions included malignant neoplasm of colon (C18) (35.7 deaths per 1,000 admissions), malignant neoplasm of liver and intrahepatic bile ducts (C22) (21 deaths per 1,000 admissions), gastric ulcer (K25) (33.3 deaths per 1,000 admissions), vascular disorders of intestine (K55) (24.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions), peritonitis (K65) (19.3 deaths per 1,000 admissions), acute pancreatitis (K85) (16.0 deaths per 1,000 admissions), other diseases of liver (K76) (11.1 deaths per 1,000 admissions), and fibrosis and cirrhosis of liver (K74) (10.4 deaths per 1,000 admissions) (supplementary table 2).

Total paid provided by the NHSO for primary GI diseases

The total paid was mainly for children aged 1–5 years, reported to be approximately 36% (IQR: 31.1–36.4.1.4%) from 2015 to 2022 (Fig. 6A). In the fiscal year 2022-2023, the total paid for primary gastrointestinal diseases among pediatric inpatients was approximately 77,370,498 THB or 2,230,790 US dollars (USD), of which intestinal infectious disease had the most considerable amount (51,730,272 THB, 66.8%), followed by diseases of the appendix (10,316,736 THB, 13.3%) and diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum (6,958,140 THB, 9%) (Fig. 6B). In addition, the paid for intestinal infectious disease is also significant, contributing around 20.5% (IQR: 16.5, 21.2%) of the total paid for all diagnoses among pediatric inpatients (Fig. 1C).

Discussion

Many Thai children with GI conditions, especially those aged less than five years, were admitted to hospitals across the country. Therefore, GI diseases were one of the principal health problems in Thai children. This study revealed that the prevalent GI conditions, including intestinal infectious diseases and diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, as well as the diseases of the appendix, contributed to a substantial amount of admission cost. Despite being less prevalent, certain conditions, such as malignancy, peritonitis, liver, and pancreatic diseases, were reported with mortality rates of >10 deaths per 1,000 admissions.

Intestinal infectious diseases have been a significant problem in Thailand, especially in young children [3, 4, 6, 7]. This study confirmed the mortality of the diseases remained low after improving hygiene and sanitization in the last few decades [11], similar findings from studies concerning diarrheal diseases [6, 7]. Rotavirus has been a principal cause of intestinal infectious disease in Thai children, and the implementation of rotavirus vaccination in 2020 reduces the burden of acute diarrhea in young children, demonstrating the reduction in both outpatient department visits and inpatient admissions, particularly in children aged <1 year [12]. However, there are other etiologies of intestinal infectious disease in Thailand, such as norovirus, Shigella spp., Enteroinvasive Escherichia coli, Campylobacter spp., and Salmonellaspp [7, 13]. As the pattern of diarrheal outbreaks is often seasonal throughout the country [7], a national policy for prevention and control of the outbreak should be periodically implemented to reduce the burdens of the diseases. Moreover, some specific pathogens may have a regional epidemic in children. For example, the southern region occasionally has outbreaks of Typhoid and Amoebiasis, while Cholera could be present in the eastern region [7]. For this reason, infectious control in these specific regions may be additionally promoted.

In this study, other common GI diseases were gastritis and duodenitis (K29) and acute appendicitis (K35), similar to previous reports in 2012.3–5Even though gastritis and duodenitis are less prevalent in patients aged <20 years compared to the older population, the diseases are a significant health problem internationally, reported with increased numbers of patients over the years [14]. H. pyloriinfection is one of the primary causes of gastritis, duodenitis, and peptic ulcers [14], and a study in Chiang Mai, a northern province of Thailand, revealed that 132/440 children (62%) have detectable H. pyloriAg in their stools [15]. Unfortunately, to our knowledge, a large study concerning the overall incidence of H. pylori infection among Thai children is lacking. In addition, acute appendicitis is also a common emergency condition. However, Sukmanee et al. [16] have proposed that the disease might be over-diagnosed and over-treated in Thailand. The study also showed that increasing use of computed tomography may not change the treatment outcome. For this reason, caution should be taken when investigating and treating acute appendicitis, as our study revealed that acute appendicitis requires the second highest pay among GI diseases.

While liver diseases seemed to be less prevalent, several conditions had significant mortality rates in this study. Although pediatric liver transplantation (LT), a life-saving procedure in patients with acute fulminant hepatic failure or end-stage liver disease, could be reimbursed from Thailand’s UCS since 2012 [17], acute hepatic failure is still described with a high mortality rate in Thailand, even in a university hospital [18]. As the disease often progresses rapidly, we proposed that the early referral system for acute hepatic failure should be established since most of the LT centers are in the capital. Moreover, fibrosis and cirrhosis of the liver (K74) were also mentioned, with a mortality rate of >10 deaths per 1,000 admissions. Biliary atresia is a common cause of cirrhosis in Thai children requiring LT [17], However, the mortality rate among LT registrants is high compared to among those in high-income countries according to a recent study by Sihaklang et al., [19] which has postulated that early referral for LT may be needed; this is due to LT registration at a more severe disease may lead to a higher rate of mortality and cost of LT. In addition, biliary atresia should be detected early for a better outcome. Recent applications in digital stool color imaging may improve the detection of BA when compared to stool color cards [20].

Pancreatic diseases also have a burden on Thai children. Although acute pancreatitis is mostly considered mild, some children may experience a severe disease [21]. The mortality rate of this condition in Thai children is slightly higher than that in a high-income country [22]. For these reasons, optimal care in children with acute pancreatitis should be promoted. Moreover, rare diseases with high mortality in this study included malignancy and vascular disorders, which were complicated and required advanced management.

The geographic distribution revealed that many children with GI diseases were not in Bangkok. Hence, healthcare services for GI diseases should be equally distributed across the country, not only in the capital. This strategy might benefit common GI conditions and diseases requiring specialists for therapeutic conditions, such as gastric ulcers. The disease had a high mortality rate in this study, although it could be endoscopically managed. Interestingly, the overall GI conditions were found more in secondary than primary hospitals. Therefore, primary hospitals should be encouraged to increase their capacity, particularly in treating common GI conditions, such as intestinal infectious disease, gastritis, and duodenitis, to reduce the burdens on secondary hospitals.

The empirical findings of this research revealed valuable data on the burdens of GI diseases among children and adolescents in many aspects, exhibiting significant percentages of admission numbers (10.2–16.2%) and hospital expenditure (9.9–23.1.9.1%) compared to all inpatient diagnoses. Our research also demonstrated the distribution of each disease group according to the patient’s age group, geographic location, and levels of hospital access, which may help in designing preventive strategies and health promotion policies at all healthcare levels to reduce the burdens of GI disease, and also preventing the preventable GI diseases.

This study has some limitations. First, this report applied the ICD-10-TM-based diagnosis, which might not be the definite diagnosis in every patient. The retrospective style was also prone to missing data bias. Moreover, the study applied the total paid provided by NHSO, which may underestimate the cost of diagnosis and management in actual practice and could not represent the intangible cost. Lastly, the study did not include data from the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme and the Social Security Scheme, covering another quarter of the Thai population.

Conclusions

Common pediatric GI diseases in Thailand are intestinal infectious diseases, which have a significant burden on the Thai healthcare system. Liver and pancreatic disease were less prevalent but had a higher mortality. To reduce the burden of pediatric GI diseases, several strategies should be implemented at the national level, such as conducting epidemiologic research in pediatric GI diseases, establishing a proper distribution of healthcare services, providing effective referral systems for critical and complicated diseases, and promoting the prevention of outbreaks.
