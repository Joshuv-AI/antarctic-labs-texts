Galaxies in the Epoch of Reionization Are All Bark and No Bite — Plenty of Ionizing Photons, Low Escape Fractions
Casey Papovich, Justin W. Cole, Weida Hu, Steven L. Finkelstein, Lu Shen, Pablo Arrabal Haro, Ricardo O. Amorín, Bren E. Backhaus, Micaela B. Bagley, Rachana Bhatawdekar, Antonello Calabrò, Adam C. Carnall, Nikko J. Cleri, Emanuele Daddi, Mark Dickinson, Norman A. Grogin, Benne W. Holwerda, Anne E. Jaskot, Anton M. Koekemoer, Mario Llerena, Ray A. Lucas, Sara Mascia, Fabio Pacucci, Laura Pentericci, Pablo G. Pérez-González, Nor Pirzkal, Srinivasan Raghunathan, Lise-Marie Seillé, Rachel S. Somerville, L. Y. Aaron Yung

Abstract
Early results from JWST suggest that epoch-of-reionization (EoR) galaxies produce copious ionizing photons, which, if they escape efficiently, could cause reionization to occur too early. We study this problem using JWST imaging and prism spectroscopy for 412 galaxies at 4.5 < z < 9.0 4.5<z<9.0 . We fit these data simultaneously with stellar–population and nebular–emission models that include a parameter for the fraction of ionizing photons that escape the galaxy, f esc f_{\mathrm{esc}} . We find that the ionization production efficiency, ξ ion = Q ⁡ ( H 0 ) / L UV \hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}=Q(\mathrm{H_{0}})/\hbox{$L_{\mathrm{UV}}$} , increases with redshift and decreasing UV luminosity, but shows significant scatter, σ ⁡ ( log ⁡ ξ ion | z , M UV ) ≃ 0.3 \sigma(\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}|z,\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$})\simeq 0.3 dex. The inferred escape fractions averaged over the population are low, ranging from ⟨ f esc ⟩ ≃ 2.6 ± 1.4 \langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle\simeq 2.6\pm 1.4 % at 6 < z < 9 6<z<9 to 6.5 ± 2.2 6.5\pm 2.2 % at 4.5 < z < 6 4.5<z<6 with weak or no indication of evolution with redshift. This implies that in our models most of the ionizing photons need to be absorbed to account for the nebular emission. We compute the impact of our results on reionization, including the distributions for ξ ion \xi_{\mathrm{ion}} and f esc f_{\mathrm{esc}} , and the evolution and uncertainty of the UV luminosity function. Considering galaxies brighter than M UV < − 16 \hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16 mag, we would produce an IGM hydrogen-ionized fraction of x e = 0.5 x_{e}=0.5 at 5.3 < z < 5.8 5.3<z<5.8 , possibly too late compared to constraints from from QSO sightlines. Including fainter galaxies, M UV < − 14 \hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14 mag, we obtain x e = 0.5 x_{e}=0.5 at 6.0 < z < 8.1 6.0<z<8.1 , fully consistent with QSO and CMB data. This implies that EoR galaxies produce plenty of ionizing photons, but these do not efficiently escape. This may be a result of high gas column densities combined with burstier star-formation histories, which limit the time massive stars are able to clear channels through the gas for ionizing photons to escape.



I
Introduction

JWST
has transformed our ability to characterize the nature of galaxies at redshifts as high as
z\sim 14

(
Bunker et al. 2023
;
Arrabal Haro et al. 2023b
;
Carniani et al. 2024
;
Kokorev et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. From the analyses of
JWST
data, galaxies at
z>7
appear to be dominated by younger stellar populations
(
Papovich et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, with stronger nebular emission
(
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Calabrò et al. 2024
;
Mascia et al. 2024
;
Pahl et al. 2025
;
Simmonds et al. 2024a
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
)
, and evidence of more bursty star-formation histories
(
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Cole et al. 2025
;
Trussler et al. 2025
)
, compared to lower redshift galaxies
(
Shen et al. 2024
;
Cole et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

The properties of galaxies at
z>6
are crucially important because this period corresponds to the epoch of reionization (EoR) of the intergalactic medium (IGM). Constraints from the polarization of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) show that the ionized fraction of the IGM rises from nil at the epoch of recombination,
z\approx 1100
, to
x_{e}=0.5
by
z=8.4
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
).

The

Planck Collaboration et al. (2020)
analysis also suggests a non-zero optical depth of
CMB photons scattering off free electrons at
z\approx 15
, which
implies ionization of the IGM had begun by this epoch.
This can be combined with observations of the optical depth of ionizing radiation from QSOs to show that reionization had effectively “ended” by
z\sim 5-6
when the ionized fraction was
x_{e}\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}0.9

(
McGreer et al. 2015
;
Greig et al. 2017
;
Becker et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Mason et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Whitler et al. 2020
;
Nakane et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.

Galaxies during the first billion years appear to be the most likely cause of this reionization
(
Robertson et al. 2022
, see,)
.
Observations from
JWST
have already shown evidence that galaxies at
z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}7
have sufficient ionizing radiation to complete reionization by this time
(
Simmonds et al. 2024a
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
)
. The primary measurement of interest is the so-called ionizing production efficiency,
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, defined as the ratio of production rate of photons with energy
>
13.6 eV,
Q(\mathrm{H_{0}})
, in units of s
-1
, to the luminosity density at 1500 Å,
L_{\mathrm{UV}}
, in units of erg s
-1
Hz
-1

(
Leitherer and Heckman 1995
)
. The analysis of
JWST
data shows that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
spans a wide range, from as low
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\simeq 24.5
to as high as
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\simeq 25-26

(
Atek et al. 2024
;
Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023
;
Pahl et al. 2025
;
Simmonds et al. 2024a
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
;
Begley et al. 2025
;
Hayes et al. 2025
;
Llerena et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, possibly exceeding the canonical value,
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}=25.2
expected for young stellar populations and observed for galaxies at
z\sim 2

(
Robertson et al. 2015
;
Shivaei et al. 2018
)
. That is, EoR galaxies appear to produce sufficient ionizing radiation for reionization to proceed.

However, we lack a good understanding of how much of this radiation escapes into the IGM. This is measured by the escape fraction,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, which is the relative amount of photons with energy greater than the ionization energy of hydrogen (also referred to as Lyman-continuum radiation, LyC, at wavelengths
<
912 Å). Theoretical constraints predict that the escape fraction is low for EoR galaxies as the column density of gas that fuels star formation is very large
(
Ferrara and Loeb 2013
;
Xu et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. The key is how efficiently feedback from star formation can clear channels through the gas through which the LyC can escape
(
Kimm and Cen 2014
)
. Current predictions for EoR galaxies, with masses like those we currently detect with
JWST
, are that they have lower escape fractions, with
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\sim 1-15\%

(
Ma et al. 2015
;
Xu et al. 2016
;
Ferrara et al. 2019
;
Kostyuk et al. 2023
;
Yeh et al. 2023
)
, although some simulations argue for an increase in
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
with increasing redshift
(
Rosdahl et al. 2022
;
Trebitsch et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. As these galaxies are expected to drive reionization
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
, it is of paramount importance that we make constraints on
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
in galaxies at these redshifts.

Measuring the escape fraction is challenging.
At low redshifts, it must be observed from space. Some results for starburst and extreme-emission line galaxies show high values,
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}=6-35
%
(
Izotov et al. 2016
;
Izotov et al. 2021
)
, but that the value for an individual galaxy appears to depend strongly on the geometry
(
Choi et al. 2020
)
. Work from the Low-Redshift Lyman Continuum Survey (LzLCS,
Flury et al. 2022b
) showed that for
z\sim 0.3
galaxies,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
generally increases with decreasing galaxy luminosity, increasing ionization parameter (often parameterized by the
[O
iii
]
/
[O
ii
]
ratio or by the Balmer emission equivalent width [EW]), decreasing UV spectral slope (
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
) and decreasing dust attenuation
(
Flury et al. 2022a
, e.g.,)
. It is not known if these correlations are causal or related to underlying processes.
Jaskot et al. (2024)
showed that
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
has a smaller scatter when using a multivariate analysis, implying a more complicated relation between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
and galaxy properties. Nevertheless, the application of the LzLCS relations to EoR galaxies predicts the latter should have relatively high escape fractions, in excess of 10%
(
Chisholm et al. 2022
;
Cullen et al. 2023
;
Mascia et al. 2024
)
.

At higher redshifts the escape fraction can only be detected directly for galaxies at
z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}4
, as at higher redshifts the density of H I absorbers in the IGM becomes too large for us to detect any escaping LyC photons
(
Inoue et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
. Measurements of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
from galaxies at
1<z<3.5
find low
average
values,
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle\leq 7
%
(
Siana et al. 2010
;
Boutsia et al. 2011
;
Nestor et al. 2013
;
Grazian et al. 2017
;
Matthee et al. 2017
;
Steidel et al. 2018
;
Pahl et al. 2021
;
Pahl et al. 2023
;
Saxena et al. 2022
;
Begley et al. 2022
;
Jung et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
, and these values agree with constraints on global escape fraction measurements of the H I photoionization rate in the IGM from the Lyman
\alpha
forest, which require
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\simeq 1-2
%

(
Haardt and Madau 2012
;
Khaire and Srianand 2015
)
.
However,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
may vary significantly, where individual reports span 20–100%
(
Izotov et al. 2021
;
Saxena et al. 2022
, see, e.g.,)
. Moreover, the average
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
appears to be higher for galaxies with stronger emission lines
(
Steidel et al. 2018
;
Pahl et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, which may be more typical of EoR galaxies. Therefore, it remains unclear what, if anything, we can infer about the nature of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
for EoR galaxies using these results.

Combining all of these results poses a potential problem. Pre-
JWST
studies
(
Robertson et al. 2015
;
Finkelstein et al. 2019
;
Yung et al. 2020a
)
found a wide range of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
(
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\simeq 3-30
%) would be capable of producing reionization.
JWST
observations now show that EoR galaxies produce higher quantities of ionizing photons (see above), which, when combined with high
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
values would cause reionization to occur too early
(
Muñoz et al. 2024
)
. These details depend crucially on the distributions of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
their dependence on the UV luminosity, and on the evolution of the UVLF. For the latter, we have come to a consensus, using deep field studies from
HST
and
JWST
that the UVLF at
7<z<10
extends as a power-law for galaxies as faint as
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-15

(
Finkelstein and Bagley 2022
;
Leung et al. 2023
;
Pérez-González et al. 2023
;
Adams et al. 2024
;
Donnan et al. 2024
)
. Studies from lensed galaxies provide evidence that this extends to at least
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-13

(
Livermore et al. 2017
;
Bhatawdekar et al. 2019
;
Atek et al. 2024
)
, notably fainter than the LMC and SMC
(
Maucherat-Joubert et al. 1980
)
.

Muñoz et al. (2024)
showed that given this evolution of the UVLF, if EoR galaxies have high
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
,
and
high
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, then the global emissivity,
\dot{n}
/ (s
-1
Mpc
-3
), is potentially too high, creating a “photon budget crisis”
(
Ocvirk et al. 2021
, see also,)
. This makes it imperative that we constrain
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
in EoR galaxies.

Here we study this problem using a new analysis of
JWST
imaging and spectroscopy from the Cosmic Evolution Early Release Science (CEERS) survey and
JWST
Advanced Deep Extragalactic survey (JADES) of galaxies at
4.5<z<9.0
that have photometry from
HST
and
JWST
, and spectroscopy from the
JWST
/NIRSpec prism. These data cover the rest-frame region from the rest-frame far-UV (
\sim
1500 Å) through the H
\beta
(+
[O
iii
]
) lines (or to
H
\alpha
, depending on the redshift). We model these spectroscopic and imaging data for each galaxy simultaneously, where the strength of the nebular emission is compared to the shape of the rest-UV/optical continuum. With this we can infer an
indirect
estimate of the escape fraction, as the modeling constrains the number of ionizing photons and the nebular emission constrains the number that do
not
escape.
There is precedence for this type of analysis based on observations of H II regions
Oey and Kennicutt (1997)
and resolved stars in the starburst galaxy NGC 4214
(
Choi et al. 2020
)
. In both cases, these works use the observations of the far-UV and nebular emission to constrain the number of LyC photons, and then compared these to the emission in the nebular component.
At high redshift, similar analyses have attempted to constrain
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, finding that some galaxies have
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
= 50–80%
(
Topping et al. 2022
)
. Here, we test the ability of the SED modeling to constrain
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
using both real and simulated galaxies (see Section
IV.2
and Appendix
B
). Applying this to the real data for the
4.5<z<9.0
galaxies, we find that the nebular emission is sufficiently strong to account for nearly all of the produced ionizing photons: the escape fractions are low, less than a few percent for the majority of galaxies.

II
Data and Sample

For this work, we select galaxies with excellent multiwavelength coverage from imaging from
HST
and
JWST
, and spectroscopy from the
JWST
NIRSpec/PRISM. The prism data are important because they cover the wavelength range 0.8–5.3 µm contiguously, with varying resolution of
R\sim 80
at
\simeq
2.5 µm to
R\sim 300
at
\simeq 5
µm. This provides measurements of the galaxy continua and emission features that can be modeled to constrain properties of the stellar populations and nebular gas. However, the prism spectra face additional challenges including the fact that the amount of flux received depends on the galaxy morphology, location of the galaxy within the MSA slitlet, the wavelength-dependent image quality and spatial resolution. This is a primary reason we combine the prism data with the photometric imaging. Because the imaging provides matched aperture flux densities over a wide range of wavelength, typically 0.6–5 µm, we can model these simultaneously with the prism data to measure any flux-calibration issues from the data itself.

II.1
CEERS Data

CEERS is a
JWST
Early Release Survey that provides
JWST
imaging and spectroscopy of the legacy CANDELS/EGS field
(
Finkelstein et al. 2025
)
. CEERS includes NIRCam and MIRI imaging, as described in
(
Bagley et al. 2023
)
and
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. We use an updated photometric catalog following a similar procedure as that in
Finkelstein et al. (2024)
. This includes matched-aperture flux density measurements from
JWST
/NIRCam with the F115W, F150W, F200W, F277W, F356W, F410M, and F444W filters, combined with
HST
imaging from ACS in the F606W and F814W filters, and from WFC3/IR in the F125W, and F160W, with partial coverage from F105W and F140W, drawn from all the archival
HST
data on these fields, with most of these data coming from CANDELS
(
Grogin et al. 2011
;
Koekemoer et al. 2011
)
. For 22 of the 114 of the CEERS spectroscopic sources (
\approx 20
%), no
JWST
/NIRCam imaging exists. For these we use the
HST
–based catalog of
Finkelstein et al. (2022)
, which includes the
HST
imaging above combined with
Spitzer
/IRAC imaging at 3.6 and 4.5 µm. We include these objects for completeness, but we have tested that removing them does not change the results. The CEERS MIRI data include deep imaging in F560W and F770W in two pointings that overlap with NIRCam
(
Yang et al. 2023
)

II.3
Sample Selection

Our goal is to study the ionization conditions, escape fractions, and stellar population parameters in galaxies at redshifts near and into the EoR. To this end, we select galaxies from JADES and CEERS following conditions. (1) The galaxies have coverage from broad-band imaging using the photometric catalogs above. (2) The galaxies have NIRSpec/PRISM data from the datasets above. (3) The galaxies have a confirmed spectroscopic redshift that places them at
4.5<z<9.0
. For this last step, the lower redshift bound ensures that the NIRSpec/PRISM data cover the rest-frame far-UV (
\sim 1500
Å), while the upper redshift bound ensures the data cover the important rest-frame optical strong emission lines,
H
\beta
+
[O
iii
]

\lambda\lambda
4959, 5008. The former will be important for constraining the rest-frame UV luminosity while the latter will be important for constraining the number of ionizing photons, and therefore the ionizing production efficiency and inferred escape fraction.

We select from the JADES catalog
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
)
all galaxies with
4.5\leq z<9.0
with a spectroscopic quality flag of “A” or “B”. This results in a sample of 129 galaxies in GN and 183 galaxies in GS, after removing duplicates (keeping the spectrum with higher S/N following a visual inspection, see
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
). We then remove candidate AGN, either with X-ray detections
(
Xu et al. 2016
;
Luo et al. 2017
)
or identified as possible AGN from
Juodžbalis et al. (2025)
or
Kocevski et al. (2025)
This reduced the JADES sample to 123 galaxies in GN and 175 galaxies in GS. From CEERS, we select galaxies from the internal team release that includes data from all CEERS NIRSpec observations, with redshifts from an automatic line finder
(
Fernández et al. 2024
)
, and visual inspection by multiple CEERS-team members. We again remove possible AGN using
Kocevski et al. (2025)
and no galaxies are detected in the current X-ray coverage of the CEERS field. We identify 114 unique galaxies in CEERS in this redshift range.

z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}5

(
Becker et al. 2019
)
, through the period when it begins,
z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}8

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. Nevertheless, selection effects will remain important as our sample requires the presence of spectroscopic feature(s) to be included in the sample (i.e., emission lines). For all of the galaxies in our sample, this includes the presence of the
[O
iii
]
4959, 5008 doublet and other lines. Future studies with deeper exposures, primarily from the NIRSpec/PRISM will be needed to determine more complete samples based on redshifts that are not necessarily dependent on the presence of emission lines. This will be possible in the future from surveys such as CAPERS
(
Dickinson et al. 2024
)
. We return to the importance of selection effects below in Section
V
.

Analysis

III.1
Spectral Energy Distribution
Modelling

We fit the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of the galaxies in our sample using stellar population synthesis models. We model each galaxy using
BAGPIPES

(
Carnall et al. 2018
, v1.3.1,)
, which is a Bayesian SED-fitting code that has the capability to fit simultaneously the multiband photometry and spectroscopy with stellar population synthesis models and nebular emission models generated over a wide range of parameters. The code has flexibility in the type of stellar population models, star-formation history, dust attenuation, and nebular emission. The code computes posterior probability densities for model parameters given the data by calculating a likelihood weighted by priors, and samples the posteriors for the parameters using
nautilus

(
Lange 2023
)
. The newest version of
BAGPIPES
also includes updated
Cloudy
nebular models (v25,
Gunasekera et al. 2025
).

We have added several features to
BAGPIPES
for our analysis. First, one of our goals is to determine the constraints on the inferred LyC escape fraction,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
. By default, the current nebular emission models in
BAGPIPES
generated from
Cloudy
assume 100% of the incident radiation is absorbed and used to generate the nebular emission (i.e,.
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
= 0). Following
Morales et al. (2024)
, we have added a parameter (
nebular:fesc
) such that the nebular emission is reduced by a factor of
1-\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}
. This models the LyC escape as a fraction of sightlines that are “clear” (akin to a covering fraction equal to
(1-\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})
). As we show, the majority of galaxies favor very low escape fractions of
<5
%, implying such clear channels are infrequent. In a future work, we plan to explore other types of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, including cases where the nebulae are truncated or “density bounded”
(
Nakajima and Ouchi 2014
;
Plat et al. 2019
)
.

Second, there are several processes that can impact the shape of the UV continuum with respect to the stars. One process is the nebular continuum, produced from free-bound and the two-photon emission from the
2s\rightarrow 1s
transition in hydrogen. These are already included in the
BAGPIPES

Cloudy
models.
Another process is increased H I absorption from the IGM or ISM of the host galaxy, both of which can alter the shape of the UV spectra, particularly around Lyman
\alpha
. Several studies have measured an increase in the strength of the H I damping wing in galaxies at the EOR
(
Heintz et al. 2024
;
Umeda et al. 2024
)
.
We have added to
BAGPIPES
the H I damping wing to allow for this effect following the formalism of
Dijkstra (2014)
. This requires two parameters,
IGM:R
, the comoving distance to the patch of IGM at the edge of the ionized “bubble” around a galaxy, and
IGM:x_HI
, the average gas-neutral fraction of the patch of the IGM (see also
Heintz et al. 2024
). This allows the models not to rely overly on the nebular continuum to reproduce the shape of the rest-UV continuum.

Third, through testing we found that modeling of the JWST/NIRSpec data frequently under-fit the spectral resolution.
BAGPIPES
uses a model for the variable spectral resolution of the NIRSpec/PRISM available from the
Space Telescope Science Institute
(STScI)
2
2

2

https://jwst-docs.stsci.edu/files/97979440/97979447/1/1596073265467/jwst_nirspec_prism_disp.fits
, but this assumes galaxies are unresolved and are centered in the MSA slits. The reality is that the effects of galaxy morphology and location within the slit lead to a type of morphological spectral broadening similar to that seen in slitless data
(
Simons et al. 2023
)
. Indeed, recent updates to the NIRSpec calibration find this effect is wavelength dependent and can be as much as 50%
3
3

3

https://www.stsci.edu/contents/news/jwst/2025/new-pathloss-corrections-for-nirspec-multi-object-and-fixed-slit-spectroscopy-are-available
. To compensate, we added a nuisance parameter that multiplies the spectral resolution by a factor with respect to the spectral file (we refer to this as a line-spread factor (LSF)). This parameter improves the quality of the fits, particularly noticeable in the width of the nebular emission lines. We allow the galaxy spectra to be broadened by an intrinsic velocity dispersion,
\sigma_{v}
, though in practice this is much less than the instrument dispersion. We also allow the noise to be scaled by a nuisance parameter, and we allow for a multiplicative scaling factor (assumed to be a second order polynomial) to match the spectral flux to the broad-band photometry.

Table
1
lists the parameters, limits, and priors we used with
BAGPIPES
for the SED fitting. We use the Binary Population and Spectral Synthesis (BPASS, v2.2.1) models from
Stanway and Eldridge (2018)
formed with a
Chabrier IMF with an upper-stellar mass cutoff of 300
M_{\odot}
, and that include the effects of stellar binaries. This choice is motivated by the fact that high-redshift galaxies appear to require additional ionization from high-mass stars
(
Katz et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, and as we show below these models reasonably reproduce both the spectral continua and emission line intensities
(
Seillé et al. 2024
, consistent with other results, see, e.g.,)
.
For the star-formation history, we employ the parameterization of
Iyer et al. (2019)
, which uses Gaussian Processes to represent the SFR in different time bins, with a boundary condition that it produces the instantaneous SFR and stellar mass at the moment of observation.
We choose three time bins, representing when the star-formation history has formed, 25, 50, and 75% of the stellar mass (
t_{25}
,
t_{50}
, and
t_{75}
). We have also tested the use of star-formation histories with fixed bins in time
(
Leja et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. Using bins that sample the time quasi-logarithmically following
Begley et al. (2025)
, we find that this has no net impact on our results. We nevertheless adopt the star-formation histories parameterized by Gaussian Processes
(
Iyer et al. 2019
)
as they provide flexibility in the star-formation history to account for bursts and quenching events without setting a prior on the width of the star-formation time bins.

We adopt the
Calzetti (2001)
dust attenuation law, which is appropriate for more highly attenuated, blue star-forming galaxies (where for less attenuated galaxies the differences are small between
Calzetti 2001
and laws like the SMC, see
Salmon et al. 2016
). We also assume the stellar continua and nebular emission are attenuated by the same amount
(
Reddy et al. 2015
)
, which is appropriate as our galaxies generally have low dust attenuation, with young ages
(
Lecroq et al. 2024
)
, so systematic shifts in the attenuation will have minimal impact on our results
(
Sanders et al. 2021
;
Shen et al. 2024
)
. We use the dust attenuation derived from the SED fitting to correct emission line fluxes and the UV continuum. We opt not to use the Balmer lines as not all lines are available for all galaxies and the SED fitting uses all available information
(
Vijayan et al. 2025
, see also)
. As we show below (Section
III.4
), the
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values derived from dust-corrected
H
\alpha
and
H
\beta
are consistent, which further justifies these choices. For the escape fraction, we adopted a logarithmic prior, that is, linear in
\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}
. This choice resulted from our experiments that showed most galaxies have low
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, where the logarithmic prior allowed us to better sample the posterior at low values of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
. We have checked that this choice has no impact on our conclusions.

We then use
BAGPIPES
to fit these models to the observed photometry and NIRSpec/PRISM data for each galaxy.
We used the Texas A&M High Performance Research Computing Grace cluster
4
4

4

Named for Grace Hopper,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper
.
to perform the calculations. Grace is a Dell x86 HPC cluster, and has 800 48-core compute nodes, each with 384 Gb RAM. Run times for the fit to converge for each galaxy are typically 1 to 4 hrs of CPU time.

III.2
Measuring
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
,
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
, and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}

For this analysis, we are focused on the evolution of the ionizing production efficiency,
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, and LyC escape fraction,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, as a function of
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
. We will also consider trends between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
and the UV spectral slope,
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
,
f_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}}
, defined using regions devoid of emission features over rest-frame
1268-2580
Å
(
Calzetti et al. 1994
)
. For the UV spectral slope, we measure this from the NIRSpec data after correcting for the slit-loss flux calibration (see above). For
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, we derive their constraints for each galaxy from the SED fits. For each galaxy, we take 400 model fits drawn from the posterior. For each model we measure the 1500 Å rest-frame flux density,
F_{1500}
, using a top-hat filter from 1450–1550 Å, and then calculate the rest-frame UV (1500 Å) absolute magnitude by applying the distance modulus.
In practice we compute
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
twice, once for the dust-corrected spectrum and once without. We use the dust-corrected measurement to estimate
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, but we use the observed
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
with no dust correction when we consider the production of ionizing photons based on the galaxy UVLF (in Section
IV.3
). For each galaxy we take
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
as the median of the draws from the posterior, with an uncertainty from the 16th to 84th percentiles. We also derive constraints on other stellar–population parameters, such as stellar mass, SFR, and dust attenuation. The latter we use to correct derived quantities and emission line fluxes.

III.3
Measuring Emission Line Fluxes

and Equivalent Widths

We measure emission line EWs and line fluxes from the NIRSpec/PRISM spectra. In principal, we could take the emission-line measurements from the
BAGPIPES
SED fits directly, however, these models assume
a priori
knowledge. This includes especially the chemical abundances, where
BAGPIPES
scales the metallicity with a Solar abundance pattern. As this assumption is still under debate, with several studies arguing for
\alpha
-enrichment in these early galaxies
(
Beverage et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, we opt to measure the emission fluxes directly from the data, using the SED fits as guidance. In the Appendix, we compare the emission line fluxes measured directly to those predicted from the SED modeling. We show that these are in good agreement for the strong optical emission lines (
[O
iii
]
,
H
\beta
, and
H
\alpha
), but with higher scatter for
[O
ii
]
.

For each galaxy, we correct for dust attenuation and calibration/slit-loss corrections using the results from the SED modeling (see Section
III.1
). This factor varies from
\sim
0.5 – 2.0 at the wavelength of the redshifted
H
\beta
-line for each galaxy, with a median (inner-68%-tile range) of 0.77 (0.60–0.99) for JADES (with no difference seen between GOODS-N and GOODS-N) and 1.52 (0.83–2.62) for CEERS. Applying this factor ensures the NIRSpec spectra have been adjusted in a robust fashion to match the photometry, and it corrects for any wavelength-dependent calibration issues. We have compared the EWs of the
H
\beta
and
[O
iii
]
lines measured from the raw spectra and compared them to the corrected spectra and find they agree with a difference of
<10^{-4}
dex with a scatter of
\leq 0.04
dex.
We then take use the SED modeling to provide an estimate of the continuum of each spectrum, excluding the contribution of the nebular emission lines. This step is crucial for measuring accurate equivalent widths, and for measuring accurate Balmer emission line fluxes in the case that there is non-negligible stellar absorption present.

We then model the strong nebular lines,
[O
ii
]
,
[Ne
iii
]
,
[O
iii
]
,
H
\beta
, and
H
\alpha
, with Gaussians. Because the
[O
ii
]

\lambda\lambda 3727
, 2729 doublet is unresolved, we fit it with a single Gaussian component and measure the sum of the two lines. For
[O
iii
]
, in some cases the line is unresolved, especially at the lower end of our redshift distribution,
z\sim 4.5
, which places these lines at
\sim 2.8-3.0
µm, where the NIRSpec/PRISM resolution is
R\sim 80-100
. We therefore fit both lines simultaneously, forcing them to have the same dispersion and assuming a line ratio of
[O
iii
]

\lambda 5008
/
[O
iii
]

\lambda 4959
= 2.98. We measure
H
\alpha
with a single Gaussian, but we note that it is blended with
[N
ii
]

\lambda\lambda
6548, 6583 at the resolution of the prism. This is reasonable as we expect the contribution of
[N
ii
]
to be
[N
ii
]
/
H
\alpha
<
10% given the high redshifts and low metallicity of the galaxies in our sample
(
Faisst et al. 2018
)
. Furthermore, we adopt
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
constraints based on
H
\beta
as this line is accessible with the NIRSpec/PRISM data for all galaxies in our sample. Nevertheless, we show below that the
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values measured from
H
\beta
are consistent with those from
H
\alpha
for the cases where both lines are available.

In the analysis that follows, we explore trends with galaxy EW and emission line ratios. For the emission-line ratios, we primarily study the ratio of
[O
iii
]
to
[O
ii
]
, defined here as

\mathrm{O}_{32}\equiv\frac{\hbox{[O\,{\sc iii}]}\lambda 4959+\hbox{[O\,{\sc iii}]}\lambda 5008}{\hbox{[O\,{\sc ii}]}\ \lambda 3726+\hbox{[O\,{\sc ii}]}\lambda 3729}.

(1)

We will compare our results to other studies that explore trends in the ratio of these sums, available primarily from other space-based spectroscopic studies using the
HST
and
JWST
grisms
(
Papovich et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2024
)
.

III.4
Measuring the Ionizing Production Efficiency

Figure 4:
Comparison of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
derived from the
H
\alpha
to that from the
H
\beta
lines. The
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values are derived based on each Balmer emission line.
In both cases we assume the dust attenuation of the stellar continua is equal to that of the nebular emission,
E(B-V)_{\mathrm{nebular}}=E(B-V)_{\mathrm{stars}}
. The solid line shows the unity relation. These results show
H
\beta
is a reasonable surrogate for
H
\alpha
when deriving
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
for our datasets.

As introduced above (Section
I
), the ionizing production efficiency,
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, is defined as

\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\equiv Q(\mathrm{H}_{0})/L_{\mathrm{UV}}
,
where
Q(\mathrm{H}_{0})
is the hydrogen-ionizing production rate in units of s
-1
.
L_{\mathrm{UV}}=F_{1500}\times(4\pi D_{L}^{2})(1+z)^{-1}
, is the rest-frame UV continuum luminosity, using the redshift (
z
) and rest-frame UV flux density (
F_{1500}
) derived in Section
III.1
.
We define the ionizing production rate,
Q
, assuming Case-B recombination
(
Leitherer and Heckman 1995
;
Izotov et al. 2021
;
Hayes et al. 2025
)
,

Q(\mathrm{H}_{0})=2.12\times 10^{12}~\mathrm{s^{-1}}~\left(L(\hbox{H$\beta$})/\mathrm{erg~s^{-1}}\right),

(2)

where
L(\hbox{H$\beta$})
is the dust corrected
H
\beta
luminosity. In cases where we compare to
L(\hbox{H$\alpha$})
, we assume
L(\hbox{H$\alpha$})=2.86\ L(\hbox{H$\beta$})

(
Osterbrock 1989
)
. The definition of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
above implicitly assumes
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}=0
, and we discuss this further in the sections below.

For galaxies in the redshift range
4.5<z<6.8
we can compare
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
derived from
H
\beta
to that derived independently from
H
\alpha
. Figure
4
shows that these values agree in the average. Interestingly, the mean (median) ratio is
\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}(\hbox{H$\beta$})/\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}(\hbox{H$\alpha$})=1.1
(1.0), implying the values from
H
\beta
are 10% higher in the mean compared to those from
H
\alpha
, although this is smaller than the scatter (
\pm 0.4
dex).
There are some objects that show
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
from
H
\alpha
is higher than that from
H
\beta
, and outside the scatter. These may be a result of the dust attenuation law, where we have assumed
E(B-V)_{\mathrm{stars}}=E(B-V)_{\mathrm{nebular}}
for all galaxies, which is appropriate for young star-forming galaxies
(
Lecroq et al. 2024
)
. However, some studies find higher levels of attenuation in the nebular gas compared to the stellar continuum
(
Calzetti 2001
;
Kreckel et al. 2013
;
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Salmon et al. 2016
;
Robertson et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, particularly for dustier galaxies
(
Reddy et al. 2015
)
. In the latter case, this would
increase
the dust-corrected
H
\beta
luminosity and
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
. It is also possible that these galaxies have higher ratios of
[N
ii
]
/
H
\alpha
, inflating
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
(
H
\alpha
). It could also be a result of the assumption of Case B recombination
(
Méndez-Delgado et al. 2024
;
Scarlata et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Nevertheless, the effect of dust attenuation is low because most of our galaxies have low color excess,
E(B-V)\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}0.3
(see Appendix
A
). We therefore adopt the results using
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
from
H
\beta
as this line is available for all of our galaxies, but these results may include a systematic uncertainty of
\sim
10–20%.

\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}
and

may not be linear. At bright magnitudes, e.g.,
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}-19.5

have
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}
below the linear fit, and these may connect to the galaxy populations seen at

(

)
. This is important and may motivate exploring non-linear distributions and incorporating the scatter when describing
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
at a given
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and redshift.

We quantify the average and scatter of the ionizing production efficiency by fitting a Gaussian to the
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}
distribution in bins of
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and redshift, with results shown in Figure
6
and Table
3
. In all bins the scatter is typically
\sigma(\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$})\simeq
0.20-0.30 dex consistent with other recent work
(
Pahl et al. 2025
;
Begley et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. It is notable that a similar amount of scatter is observed in the
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
distributions of
z\sim 2
galaxies
(
Shivaei et al. 2018
)
. This scatter is important as it tells us that galaxies at these redshifts produce different rates of ionizing photons at a fixed UV luminosity. This may be a symptom of increased “burstiness” in the star-formation histories of galaxies as has been suggested from photometric studies
(
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
;
Cole et al. 2025
)
. For the mode (peak) of the
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}
distributions, these increase with decreasing
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and increasing redshift. This says that on average fainter galaxies are more efficient at producing ionizing photons, and this efficiency increases with redshift.

Our sample may be biased by the fact that it requires spectroscopic confirmation. Recent studies using photometric samples demonstrate this bias
(
Simmonds et al. 2024b
;
Begley et al. 2025
)
. Figure
5
shows the
\xi
–
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
relation from
Simmonds et al. (2024b)
, which found a flat or decreasing relation between
\xi
and
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
.
Endsley et al. (2024)
used photometric samples and found that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
declines with UV luminosity, opposite to the trend in our data, which they interpret as evidence that “post burst” galaxies would have lower

IV.3
Constraints on the Cosmic Ionizing Rate Density

Our measurements of the ionizing production efficiency,
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, and the constraints on the LyC escape fraction,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, allow us to compute the cosmic ionization production rate density (also called the emissivity),
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
, which is the rate of ionizing photons produced per cubic (comoving) Mpc. This quantity is derived by integrating the UVLF,
\phi(\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$})
, multiplied by the product of
(\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\times\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\ \times L_{\mathrm{UV}})
, that is

\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}=\int\ \phi(M_{\mathrm{UV}})L_{\mathrm{UV}}\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}f_{\mathrm{esc}}\ dM_{\mathrm{UV}}.

(4)

To compute
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
we use a Monte Carlo simulation, which allows us to sample the range of uncertainties in parameters of the UVLF, and to sample the full distributions that we have measured for
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
as a function of
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and redshift. In each iteration of the Monte Carlo, we first draw a sample of galaxies with
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
selected to reproduce the UVLF of
Finkelstein et al. (2022)
at different redshifts, with
z\in(5,6,7,8)
, where use bins in redshift that match those of the UVLF. We also extend these simulations to higher redshift using the UVLF of
Donnan et al. (2024)
in order to consider the redshift evolution of the cosmic ionized-hydrogen fraction,
x_{e}
(see Section
V.5
). In each instance of the simulation, we vary the parameters of the UVLF within the values and covariances. In this way the total number of galaxies, and the number of galaxies per unit magnitude include the full range of uncertainties in the UVLF.

Second, for each galaxy in the simulated sample we draw a random value for
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
from our measured distribution,
P(\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}|\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$},z)
, using the galaxy’s
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and
z
, using our empirically measured distributions (Table
3
). For
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, we select a value of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
by taking at random a (real) galaxy from our sample at similar
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and
z
as the simulated galaxy, and drawing a value from its
P(\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}|\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$},z)
distribution. In this way we each simulated galaxy receives
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
drawn from the measured galaxy distribution functions.

V
Discussion

V.1
Why are the ionizing production efficiencies of

EoR galaxies high?

In Section
IV.1
we showed that the ionizing production efficiency for our samples increase for fainter galaxies and increase toward higher redshift. This echoes trends observed from other
JWST
studies that find high values,
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}=25-26
, for galaxies at
z\simeq 7-8
. This includes both studies that have used SED fitting of
JWST
imaging data to infer
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}

(
Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023
;
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Simmonds et al. 2024a
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
;
Begley et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, and spectroscopic studies with
JWST
find similar trends of an increase in
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
for EoR galaxies
(
Atek et al. 2024
;
Calabrò et al. 2024
;
Llerena et al. 2025
;
Mascia et al. 2024
;
Pahl et al. 2025
;
Saxena et al. 2024
;
Hayes et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Ours is the first to estimate
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
by combining the photometry and spectroscopic data with a consistent treatment.

The finding that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
is higher at high redshifts seems robust as most studies have employed different methods, assumptions, and samples. For example,
Hayes et al. (2025)
considered the properties of stacked (average) NIRSpec/PRISM spectra for galaxies at
4<z<10
, finding
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\simeq 25.2-25.7
with a strong correlation between
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and many signatures of LyC escape. Using individual galaxy spectra,
Llerena et al. (2025)
measured
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
from Balmer-emission line measurements from JWST/NIRSpec prism and
R\sim 1000
data, combined with photometric modeling to obtain
L_{\mathrm{UV}}
for galaxies at
4<z<10
. Their results yield results similar to those here, at least in the mean (see Figure
5
).

Not all analyses conclude that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
increases with decreasing UV luminosity. This could be related to systematics or to selection effects.
Pahl et al. (2025)
measured the evolution of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
using JWST spectroscopy for galaxies at
1.06<z<6.71
, finding that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
is higher for UV-luminous galaxies and declines with decreasing UV luminosity. This may be related to the sample selection as
Pahl et al. (2025)
required
>3\sigma
detections in both the
H
\beta
and
H
\alpha
lines, or because of differences in the SED modeling and spectroscopic slitloss corrections. Studies using multiband imaging data including medium-band photometry also find that UV-fainter galaxies show lower
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}

(
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
)
. One explanation for this is that some fainter galaxies are “post-burst”, which would have lower
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and fainter UV magnitudes
(
Endsley et al. 2024
)
. These could be missed by spectroscopic surveys, but these galaxies also suffer larger photometric redshift uncertainties. This does highlight the potential for selection effects in spectroscopic samples. Testing these selection effects will require deeper
JWST
/NIRSpec observations

(
Dickinson et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.

An increase in
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
requires a greater ionizing production rate,
Q(\mathrm{H_{0}})
, per unit far-UV continuum luminosity,
L_{\mathrm{UV}}
. This occurs when galaxies either have a higher fraction of O-type stars, or when these stars have hotter effective temperatures, or both.
Shivaei et al. (2018)
measured empirical trends for galaxies at
z\sim 0-2
, showing that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
increases with lower dust content, stellar population age and metallicity. Higher effective temperatures are predicted as a consequence of lower metallicity stars, which lowers the opacity in stellar photospheres, leading to higher LyC production
(
Yung et al. 2020b
;
Mascia et al. 2024
, see, e.g.,)
. Studies using
JWST
observations of the nebular emission find that the gas-phase metallicity is declining with increasing redshift
(
Trump et al. 2022
;
Curti et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Assuming this extends to lower stellar metallicities, this could provide one physical reason for the galaxies’
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
to increase as the metallicity declines with redshift
(
Llerena et al. 2025
)
.

A related consideration is that galaxies in our samples may be dominated by young,
\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}5
Myr, stellar populations, which would increase the relative number of O-type stars. This could be a consequence of “burstier” star-formation histories in these galaxies, which have been reported in some studies using
JWST

(
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Looser et al. 2023
;
Simmonds et al. 2024b
;
Cole et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Indeed,
Yung et al. (2020b)
show that young stellar populations, with ages
\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}
10 Myr, are required to keep
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}>25.5
, without invoking changes to the IMF. In nearby galaxies, the age of H II regions correlates inversely with
H
\alpha
/UV and EW(
H
\alpha
)
(
Scheuermann et al. 2023
)
. If most of our galaxies are experiencing bursts, and if the frequency of bursts increases with redshift
(
Cole et al. 2025
, as argued by, e.g.,)
, then this provides a physical explanation for the trends in our sample. It may be that galaxies experiencing lulls in their star formation have lower
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, which would potentially be underrepresented in spectroscopic samples such as ours
(
Looser et al. 2024
, see, e.g.,)
.
Simmonds et al. (2024b)
considered this by using a mass-complete photometric sample that potentially includes objects with weaker emission lines. They found an increase in the scatter of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, including a tail of objects with lower
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, primarily at
z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}7
, but that
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
remains high,
\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\simeq 25.4
at
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}=-18
.
Begley et al. (2025)
obtain similar results.

\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
at higher redshifts.

As we show above (Section

), changes in

impact the relative strength of these components (see also Appendix

)
Nevertheless, an underlying uncertainty in this analysis is that

requires that the stellar populations and nebular emission resemble reality. We discuss this further below (Section

), but ultimately to test the details of these models will require analyses of a multitude of emission lines for individual galaxies at these redshifts. This is beyond the scope of our present work, but may be possible with forthcoming datasets from

.

We also show in Section

(see Section

. These predictions favor even higher

z>6-7

At low redshift,

V.3
On the evolution of the volume-averaged escape fraction

In Section Section
IV.3
we calculated
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
using a Monte Carlo simulation. This modeling included the fact that for most galaxies the
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
distribution functions,
P(\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})
, are broad. This is apparent by looking at the 16th-to-84th percentile range for individual galaxies in Figure
7
. While the distance from the 16th–50th percentile and 50th-84th percentile is similar in logarithmic spacing, this means the distance between the median to the 84th percentile is substantially larger in
linear
spacing compared to the distance from the median to the 16th percentile. This means that a distribution drawn from
P(\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})
randomly for each galaxy will,
on average
, have an expectation value that will be higher than the median from the
P(\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})
likelihood.

Figure
12
illustrates this effect. The figure shows the distribution of the median
\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}
values for galaxies in our sample at
6.5<z<9.0
(yellow–shaded histogram). The mean of these medians is
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
= 0.015. However, this is
not
to be confused with the expectation value for
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
of the galaxies. For the latter, we can then recompute the distribution if we randomly draw a
\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}
value from the
P(\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})
of each galaxy, which we show as the red-shaded histogram in the figure. Because the tail of possible values skews toward higher
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, the resulting is similarly skewed. The mean of this resampled distribution is
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle=0.03
, a factor of two higher. This highlights the importance of considering the full range of the
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
probability distribution functions,
P(\log\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})
, from the posteriors of the SED fitting when considering the global escape fraction.

Following
Muñoz et al. (2024)
, we calculate the
volume-averaged
escape fraction as the ratio of
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
using the measured distribution of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
to the value assuming all of the ionizing radiation escapes,
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}=1
, that is,

\langle f_{\mathrm{esc}}\rangle=\frac{\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})}{\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}(f_{\mathrm{esc}}=1)}

(5)

This makes
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
the average weighted by the ionizing emissivity. We compute the result from Equation
5
for each iteration of the Monte Carlo simulation, and take the median as the quoted value with the 16th–to–84th percentile as the uncertainty. These values for
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
are listed in Table
6
.

(

;

, see also)
. Interestingly, our results exceed the simulation-based

values from
Finkelstein et al. (2019)
, who used these to match reionization timing constraints using variable
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
M_{lim}
values. However, our results are consistent with predictions from the
Thesan
simulation for galaxies in our stellar-mass range
(
Yeh et al. 2023
)
.

f_{\mathrm{esc}}
values based on correlations from LzLCS at
z\sim 0.3
. Figure

includes values for
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
when we repeat the Monte Carlo simulation using correlations between

and

and the UV spectral slope (
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
) predicted from LzLCS
(
Chisholm et al. 2019
)
.
In this case we would have expected the average
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
to increase with redshift over our redshift range
4.5<z<9
, rising to
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle>0.1
at
z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}7
. This is strongly disfavored by
>5\sigma
from our results.

V.4
Challenges in interpreting the escape fraction of

EoR galaxies

One challenge in interpreting our inferred
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
values is that they depend on the underlying stellar population models used in the SED fitting. The BPASS models used here have been demonstrated to reproduce the properties of young, metal–poor stellar populations dominated by short-lived massive stars under a variety of conditions
(
Eldridge and Stanway 2022
, see, e.g.,)
, but it is conceivable that galaxies during the EoR host stellar populations with different properties
(
Zackrisson et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
. Currently some of the best evidence supporting the use of BPASS models comes from observations that these models reproduce the colors and emission-line properties of high-redshift galaxies
(
Strom et al. 2017
;
Steidel et al. 2018
;
Olivier et al. 2022
;
Larson et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. However, photoionization models using BPASS models fail in some galaxies to produce extreme emission properties, including galaxies with strong He II, C IV,
[O
iii
]
/
[O
ii
]
, and other high ionization emission lines
(
Lecroq et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Several of these studies have invoked stellar populations that include stars with effective temperatures of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim 80-100
kK to explain the high-ionization emission lines and nebular continuum of galaxies
(
Olivier et al. 2022
;
Cameron et al. 2024
;
Katz et al. 2024
)
. However, even if these situations exist in the galaxies in our samples, this would
increase
the ionizing photon rate per unit UV luminosity, increasing
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
. The existence of Pop III or a top-heavy IMF would have a similar impact. If
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
is higher, then to match the emission line strength of the data would require increasing
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
such as to not overproduce the strength of the emission lines of the galaxies in our sample. That is, the product of
\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\times\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}
would increase, leading to an increase in the cosmic ionization rate,
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
leading to an earlier reionization in contrast to the CMB observations and studies of the IGM opacity from QSO studies. Therefore this scenario seems disfavored.

Another challenge is that the inferred escape fractions of the galaxies at
4.5<z<9.0
contrast strongly with expectations from scaling relations observed in low-redshift samples
(
Flury et al. 2022a
, e.g., LzLCS,)
. The
H
\beta
and
[O
iii
]
emission line EWs,
[O
iii
]
/
[O
ii
]
line ratios, and UV spectral slopes of the galaxies in our sample are similar in many ways to those in the LzLCS at
z\sim 0.3
. However, the escape fractions are very different (see Figure
11
). Why this is the case is unclear. It may be that there are spatial disparities in the galaxies such that the regions of strong emission lines are physically distinct from regions of higher
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, or that
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
is not emitted isotropically such that that average
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
is much lower when averaged of
4\pi
steradians
(
Martin et al. 2024
, see, e.g.,)
. This would impact direct measurements of the LyC emission but not values such as ours that are inferred from the reprocessed nebular emission. This possibility may be supported by some studies of the kinematics of emission lines in
z\sim 0.3
galaxies with LyC detections that show they can produce outflows that can clear such channels to promote higher
f_{\mathrm{esc}}

(
Amorín et al. 2024
;
Flury et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Therefore, some of the important missing ingredients in interpreting the escape fractions may result from variations in the spatial distribution of star formation, the gas-covering fractions, or both, all of which may be very different between low-redshift and high-redshift galaxies.

Yet another challenge is that theoretically the escape fraction is difficult to calculate because it depends heavily on the connection between the effects of star formation and stellar feedback on the geometry, density, and ionization structure of the ISM
(
Ferrara and Loeb 2013
;
Xu et al. 2016
)
. The radiation pressure and feedback of massive stars can create channels of ionized gas, through which ionized photons can escape, but in other sight lines, there may be significant clumps of neutral gas that absorb the ionizing radiation
(
Clarke and Oey 2002
)
. The escape fraction of each galaxy will have a temporal, radial, and angular dependence. In addition, many simulations may not have sufficient spatial resolution to resolve the details of the escape fraction on scales of H II regions
(
Mascia et al. 2024
, see, e.g.,)
.

Nevertheless theory provides guidance as many simulations predict low
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
for galaxies with masses like those in our sample. Simulations generally predict that galaxies in the lowest-mass halos have the highest escape fractions, with
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}25
%
(
Wise et al. 2014
;
Paardekooper et al. 2015
;
Kostyuk et al. 2023
;
Yeh et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.
However, these galaxies typically have
\log M_{\ast}/M_{\odot}<7
, and are expected to contribute less than 10% of the total ionizing radiation
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
. Other work argues that there is a “peak mass” at which galaxies produce maximal
f_{\mathrm{esc}}

(
Ma et al. 2020
)
. There may also be changes in the production of dust, as “attenuation-free-models” favor
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
<
10% at
5\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}z\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}9

(
Ferrara et al. 2025
)
.
Many studies predict that higher mass galaxies,
\log M_{\ast}\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$>$}}}8
, like those in our sample, have
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\sim 1-5
%
(
Xu et al. 2016
;
Kostyuk et al. 2023
;
Yeh et al. 2023
)
, though other work finds higher values,
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\sim 5-15
%
(
Xu et al. 2016
)
. This highlights that theory has not yet converged, and that it is important to constrain the escape fraction empirically.

Simulations also predict a large variance in
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
among galaxies.
Kimm and Cen (2014)
showed the galaxies may experience periods of high
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, with a delay following a period of intense star formation.
Rosdahl et al. (2018)
used the
Sphinx
simulation to show that galaxies with high escape fractions,
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}>10
% occur only after a starburst, as feedback from the burst expels gas, temporarily halting star formation and increasing
f_{\mathrm{esc}}

(
Barrow et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2020
;
Rosdahl et al. 2022
;
Kostyuk et al. 2023
;
Katz et al. 2023
, see also,)
.
Therefore, it may be that the periods of highest specific SFR and
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
production occur with low
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, and vice versa, such that the product
(1-\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$})\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}
is roughly constant
(
Kimm and Cen 2014
, e.g.,)
. If so, then at any time only a small fraction of galaxies contribute to the leakage of LyC emission into the IGM
(
Paardekooper et al. 2015
)
. This scenario may also help explain the timing of reionization
(
Finkelstein et al. 2019
, whose low volume-averaged
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
values are illustrated in Figure
13
)
, and the strong
[O
iii
]
/
[O
ii
]
emission in Lyman
\alpha
-selected galaxies
(
Secunda et al. 2020
;
Naidu et al. 2022
)
.

To summarize the challenges from theory, most cosmological simulations predict that
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
is low in most star-forming galaxies in the mass range like those in our sample because they have high gas densities, with few clear channels through which LyC radiation may escape. This is a very different physical situation than observed in starburst galaxies at
z\sim 0.3
with high LyC escape, and may explain the differences between the observed properties and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
values of in our sample (see, e.g., Figure
11
). This may be related to the fact that higher redshift galaxies appear to have higher variance in their star-formation histories
(
Cole et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, where theory predicts that the strong feedback associated with star formation may temporarily expel the gas, pausing star formation and allowing more LyC emission to escape
(
Kostyuk et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.
The net effect is that the low values of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
we infer appear consistent with models, and this has implications for reionization.

V.5
The impact of high ionizing photon production efficiency and low escape fraction on reionization

The evolution of the cosmic ionizing rate density,
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
, from Section
IV.3
and Figure
9
, allows us to estimate the evolution in the hydrogen-ionized fraction of the IGM,
x_{e}
. For each of our Monte Carlo simulations we solve the differential equation for this evolution. Following
Mason et al. (2015, and references therein)
, we define,

\dot{x}_{e}=\frac{\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}}{\langle n_{\mathrm{HI}}\rangle}-\frac{x_{e}}{t_{\mathrm{rec}}}.

(6)

Here,
\langle n_{\mathrm{HI}}\rangle
is the average IGM neutral hydrogen density,
\langle n_{\mathrm{HI}}\rangle=(1-Q)n_{\mathrm{H,0}}
, and
n_{\mathrm{H,0}}=(1-Y_{p})(\Omega_{b,0}/m_{p})\rho_{c,0}
for a primordial helium mass-fraction,
Y_{p}=0.2454
, baryon density parameter,
\Omega_{b,0}=0.049
, and critical density,
\rho_{c,0}
, defined by
H_{0}=67.4
km s
-1
Mpc
-3

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. The recombination time for H II is
t_{\mathrm{rec}}
, where we follow
Robertson et al. (2015, and references therein)
and take

t_{\mathrm{rec}}=[C\alpha_{B}(T)n_{e}(1+z)^{3}]^{-1}
. The hydrogen recombination coefficient,
\alpha_{B}(T)
, depends on the temperature, and we adopt the functional fit of
Hui and Gnedin (1997)
. The electron density is
n_{e}=(1+Y_{p}/4X_{p})\langle n_{H}\rangle
, where
X_{p}
is the primordial hydrogen abundance. We further adopt the evolution of the clumpiness factor,
C
, from the simulations of
Pawlik et al. (2015)
, who find that this evolves from
C=4.8
at
z=6
to
C=1.5
at
z=14

(see also
Madau et al. 1999
;
Finlator et al. 2011
;
Finkelstein et al. 2019
, although see
Davies et al. 2024
, who argue for higher
C
values)
. We then solve the differential equation (Equation
6
) for each iteration of the Monte Carlo (Section
IV.3
) using the
ODEINT
routine in the
SciPy
package.

Figure
14
shows the evolution of the hydrogen-ionized fraction of the IGM,
x_{e}

\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16
(left panel) and
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14
(right panel).
As a comparison, the gray-shaded region in both panels in the figure show the
1\sigma
constraints on reionization from an analysis of the CMB Planck large-scale polarization measurements along small-scale measurements of the kinematic Sunyaev–Zeldovich (kSZ) effect
(
Raghunathan et al. 2024
)
with a prior on
z_{\rm end}=6
using the measurements of the Gunn-Peterson (GP) trough
(
Fan et al. 2006
)
. Excluding kSZ data
(
Raghunathan et al. 2024
)
reduces the constraining power on the high-
z
end by a few percent.
This shows that reionization is expected to begin by
z=8.4
and end shortly after
z=7
(vertical dotted lines,
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
). Considering only galaxies brighter than
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16
, our results find that the hydrogen ionized fraction reached
x_{e}=0.5
at (median)
z_{\mathrm{re}}=6.3
, with a range of
5.3-5.8
(68% confidence). Including fainter galaxies down to
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14
, and again assuming they have
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
and
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
distributions similar to galaxies at
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}=-16
, our results find the hydrogen ionizing fraction of
x_{e}=0.5
is reached earlier, with a median
z_{\mathrm{re}}=7.7
, and with a range of
6.0-8.1
(68% confidence).

Our results provide evidence that galaxies in the EoR have high ionizing photon production efficiencies,
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, and low LyC escape fractions,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
. The combination of these with the current constraints on the UVLF yield a cosmic photon ionization rate,
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
, that causes reionization at times consistent with other observations. This averts the photon crisis
(
Muñoz et al. 2024
)
, but only if our measurements reflect the
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
distributions of the full galaxy population.
While we find low
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, this depends on the balancing between the stellar population synthesis models we have assumed and the strength of the emission lines.
It is possible that
JWST
spectroscopic samples like ours are biased toward galaxies with strong nebular emission, with higher
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and lower
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
. As mentioned above in Section
V.1
, studies using photometric samples of galaxies and some spectroscopic studies
(
Pahl et al. 2025
)
find a weaker relation between
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
and
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
. If our sample is biased against galaxies with lower
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, particularly at faint UV magnitude, then the cosmic ionizing rate density,
\dot{n}
, will be lower, leading to reionization that occurs at later times. Candidate galaxies for this case have been found in stellar-mass-limited photometric studies
(
Simmonds et al. 2024b
)
. Confirming their
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values, and attempting to infer
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, will be paramount to understand these biases. This will require deeper spectroscopy with JWST.

These results depend on some remaining uncertainties. One uncertainty is the clumping factor,
C
, of the IGM, which we have assumed to be
C\simeq 2-5
(see above). If this is higher, as recently argued by
Davies et al. (2024)
, then the universe would require more ionizing photons leaking into the IGM at earlier times.

Another uncertainty is how fainter galaxies,
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}>-16
, impact reionization.
This depends both on the galaxy properties, and on the behavior of the UVLF, specifically the faint-end slope and if there is some minimum UV luminosity a galaxy in the EoR can have. For the faint galaxies, some recent
JWST
studies have measured high
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values for faint galaxies in the EoR
(
Atek et al. 2024
;
Naidu et al. 2024
)
. However, it is unclear if these are a biased subset of galaxies for which it is easiest to detect their emission lines. Regarding the faint end of the UVLF,
Atek et al. (2024)
recently measured constraints from
JWST
, finding the steep slope extends at least to
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}=-13
. If these fainter galaxies are plentiful, if they have high
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
, and especially if have higher
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
, as predicted from models, then they may contribute significantly to reionization, reigniting the photon budget crisis
(
Muñoz et al. 2024
)
. Regardless, our findings are that brighter galaxies
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16
have
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\simeq 2-6
%, which mitigates this crisis, and that fainter galaxies in the EoR with
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}>-16
must contribute for reionization to occur at times consistent with current constraints.

VI
Conclusions

We have used new modeling of
HST
and
JWST
imaging and
JWST
/NIRSpec prism spectroscopic data to study the ionizing production efficiency and LyC escape fractions of galaxies at
4.5<z<9.0
. We use data from the CEERS and JADES surveys which provide imaging and NIRSpec/PRISM data for 412 galaxies. We fit these data for each galaxy simultaneously with stellar population and nebular emission models with
BAGPIPES
, including parameters to correct for slit losses, and we include a parameter to account for the fraction of ionizing photons that escape the galaxy,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
.

We measure the ionization production efficiency,
\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}=Q(\mathrm{H_{0}})/\hbox{$L_{\mathrm{UV}}$}
, using measurements of the Balmer emission line strength taken from the NIRSpec data, combined with measures of the UV continuum luminosity from the SED modeling. (Section
IV.1
).
In this way, the ionizing rate,
Q
, and UV luminosity,
L_{\mathrm{UV}}
, are taken from data that have the same flux calibration applied. We find that the ionizing production efficiency increases with increasing redshift and decreasing UV luminosity. There is a scatter in the ionization production efficiency at fixed UV magnitude and redshift,
\sigma(\log\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$})\simeq 0.3
dex. This means galaxies of fixed UV magnitude produce a range of ionizing photon rate.

We also constrain the escape fractions, which are driven by the strength of the nebular emission lines and the number of ionizing photons predicted from the stellar population models. The inferred escape fractions of the galaxies at
4.5<z<9.0
are typically low, with medians
\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\sim$}}\hss}\hbox{$<$}}}1-3
% (Section
IV.2
). We furthermore see no evidence for evolution in redshift nor in
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
over the range of our samples.

We use a Monte Carlo simulation to compute the cosmic ionizing rate density,
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{ion}}
(Section
IV.3
). This simulation includes uncertainties in the UVLF, and including our empirical distributions of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
. We find that considering galaxies down to our observational limit,
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16
mag, the cosmic ionizing rate density is
\log\dot{n}=50.1\pm 0.1
at
z=8
and rises to
50.5\pm 0.1
at
z=5
. At
z>6.5
, this is too low compared to the the amount needed to reionization the universe as predicted by
Madau et al. (1999)
. Extrapolating to fainter galaxies,
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14
mag, increases the cosmic ionizing rate to
\log\dot{n}=50.5\pm 0.1
at
z=8
and
\log\dot{n}=50.7\pm 0.1
at
z=5
, producing the needed radiation for reionization by
z\sim 6
.

The relations between
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and galaxy properties in our
4.5<z<9
samples are similar to those at lower redshifts,
1<z<3
(Section
V.1
). The high
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values for galaxies in our sample correlate with increased EW(
H
\beta
) and EW(
[O
iii
]
), and
[O
iii
]
/
[O
ii
]
ratio. This implies that the elevated
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
is related to a relative increase in the number of O-type stars, and may be suggestive of burstier star-formation histories.

The relations between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
in our
4.5<z<9
samples differ from predictions from correlations between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
and galaxy properties observed at
z\sim 0.3
(Section
V.2
). We find no correlation between the inferred
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
the UV spectral slope,
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
. We also find no strong correlations between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
and EW(
H
\beta
), nor
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
and
[O
iii
]
/
[O
ii
]
. This may be related to different physical conditions in the EoR galaxies compared to local starburst galaxies.

We use our Monte Carlo simulation of the ionizing rate density to estimate the volume-averaged escape fraction,
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
(Section
V.3
). For galaxies brighter than
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16
, we find
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
ranges from
3\pm 1
% at
z\sim 8
to
7\pm 2
% at
z\sim 5
, with weak or no evolution over
4.5<z<9.0
. There is only a slight change when we include fainter galaxies,
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14
, but this is likely a result of our assumption that galaxies with
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}>-16
have similar properties as those with
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}=-16
. Our measurement of
\langle\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}\rangle
contrasts strongly with pre-
JWST
inferences from
z\sim 0.3
galaxies. However, our results are consistent with predictions from simulations (Section
V.4
), where high gas column densities reduce
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
except in cases where strong feedback from star formation expels the gas, and clears channels through which LyC emission can escape. However, these events typically halt star formation, leading to lower
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
. Therefore, it may mean that galaxies in the EoR conspire to keep the product of
\hbox{$\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}$}\times\hbox{$f_{\mathrm{esc}}$}
roughly the same. This will need to be tested using future data sets.

We estimate the impact of our measurements of
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
on reionization (Section
V.5
), using the Monte Carlo simulations and then solving for the evolution of the hydrogen-ionized fraction,
x_{e}
, as a function of redshift. Including galaxies brighter than
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-16
mag, the universe would not reach a hydrogen-ionized fraction of
x_{e}=0.5
until
5.3<z<5.8
(68% confidence), consistent with the lower-end of reionization based on QSO sight lines. Including an estimate of the contribution from fainter galaxies,
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14
mag, reionization occurs sooner, with
x_{e}=0.5
obtained by
6.0<z<8.1
, broadly consistent with the CMB constraints. However, this assumes that galaxies with
-16<\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}<-14
have ionizing production efficiencies and escape fractions similar to galaxies with
\hbox{$M_{\mathrm{UV}}$}=-16
mag. The implication is that while EoR galaxies produce plenty of ionizing photons, this emission does not efficiently escape. We argue that this is a result of high gas fractions combined bursty star-formation histories which lead to short times in which the galaxies have sufficiently clear channels through the gas through which ionizing photons can escape.

Our results have several caveats. Among these is the assumption that we are able to constrain the
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
in galaxies through the SED fitting. Our tests with simulated galaxy SEDs shows this is reasonable (Appendix
B
), but additional tests will require extending our analysis to galaxies at lower redshifts with similar data, and direct measurements of the LyC escape fractions. We plan to pursue this in a future study. Our results also depend on selection effects, where our samples may be biased toward objects with stronger nebular emission, favoring galaxies in a “burst” stage of star formation. However, even in this case, the implied
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
values of galaxies in post-burst stages would be lower, reducing the number of ionizing photons, unless these galaxies also have higher
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
. To test this will require more complete samples of galaxies in the EoR with
JWST
imaging and prism spectroscopy. This will become possible with forthcoming datasets that include larger samples of fainter galaxies
(
Dickinson et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.

Appendix A
Comparison of Nebular Emission Line Fluxes from Models and Direct Measurements.

In our SED fits,
BAGPIPES
models the nebular emission using the photoionization rate,
Q
, from the stellar populations, and converts it into a nebular spectrum. The nebular spectrum depends on the metallicity of the gas (taken to be equal to the metallicity of the stellar populations) and the ionization parameter,
U=n_{\gamma}/n_{H}
, the ratio of the density of ionizing photons to the gas density. The models assume a gas density,
n_{e}=100
cm
-3
, and that the models are “radiation bounded” such that all of the ionizing photons are absorbed by the gas and converted into nebular emission
(
Carnall et al. 2018
)
. All of these assumptions are reasonable in general, but may have deviations in practice.
