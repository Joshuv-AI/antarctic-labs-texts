The right temperature at the right time: A framework for understanding thermoregulatory pathophysiology in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Esther Blessing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe neuromodulatory subcortical system (NSS) includes thermoregulatory neurons that maintain core body temperature (Tb) and brain temperature within a narrow, diurnally varying range. Cellular pathways in NSS neurons implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD) may be vulnerable to Tb rhythm disturbance because of their sensitivity to temperature and their thermoregulatory function. This review evaluates a framework for pathophysiological interactions between thermoregulation and AD anchored in the functional anatomy of thermoregulatory neurons. Findings in humans show that thermoregulatory neurons exhibit tau pathology in early AD, and abnormal Tb rhythm is seen in advanced AD. In rodents, cellular risk pathways for tau aggregation are both modulated by diurnal range temperature change and involved in thermoregulation. Further research is needed to bridge human and rodent findings, to understand how sex differences in Tb impact AD risk, and to identify modifiable causal links between Tb disturbance and pathophysiology toward improving clinical outcomes in early AD.

INTRODUCTION

Background and rationale

The neuromodulatory subcortical system (NSS) comprises nuclei of the brainstem, hypothalamus, and basal forebrain that release neuromodulators and neuropeptides that maintain arousal and regulate the excitability of large neuronal networks. 1 Multiple NSS nuclei exhibit tau protein associated pathology early in Alzheimer's disease (AD)—before cognitive impairment and prior to entorhinal tau deposition. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 An understanding of the mechanisms driving their selective vulnerability is needed to prevent disease progression. 1 , 5 NSS nuclei serve the vital function of thermoregulation: maintaining core body temperature (Tb) and brain temperature (Tbr) within the physiological range (36‐38°C), and doing so in a diurnal rhythm that is ~1°C higher during waking than during sleep (Figure 1).

Temperature (T) is a thermodynamic variable that influences biochemical processes in the brain without the need for receptor‐mediated interactions. By modulating Tb and Tbr, thermoregulatory neurons in NSS nuclei may therefore influence or entrain diurnal rhythms in neuronal processes and cellular pathways within the brain according to their sensitivity within this T range. This includes cellular pathways implicated in AD and within NSS neurons (Figure 1A). Processes underlying tau proteostasis are known to be T‐dependent within this range, and cellular risk pathways for tau deposition may share this property. Despite this high potential for pathophysiological interactions, the relationship between altered thermoregulation and NSS neuronal vulnerability has received relatively little attention. This omission may reflect the complexity of the relationship: it potentially involves bidirectional interactions with the regulated variable (T) affecting the regulator (thermoregulatory neurons); furthermore, the universality of T effects expands the scope of systems involved to an unknown extent. In the face of this complexity, how does one evaluate “too cold” or “too hot” theories proposed for pathophysiological interactions between thermoregulation and AD—or the proposed health benefits of hypothermia or hyperthermia in preventing AD?

This review proposes a two‐part heuristic framework, grounded in thermoregulatory physiology, for understanding interactions between Tb rhythm disturbance and NSS tau pathology in AD. The framework comprises two questions, indicated by either arm of Figure 1A and expanded upon in Section 4: INTERACTIONS BETWEEN THERMOREGULATION AND ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE PATHOPHYSIOLOGY.

One: Do thermoregulatory NSS neurons exhibit tau pathology, and if so, does this alter Tb and Tbr rhythm? Thermoregulatory NSS neurons are considered to include those implicated in warm and cold defense, as well as those regulating sleep–wake, circadian, inflammatory, metabolic, and proteostatic functional domains and cellular processes (Figure 1A, B). Whether these functional domains are indeed involved in thermoregulation and AD tau pathophysiology is evaluated.

Two: Which cellular pathways and processes belonging to these domains are T dependent and therefore vulnerable to diurnal Tb rhythm disturbance? The overarching hypothesis is that the involvement of the above domains (sleep–wake, circadian, inflammatory, metabolic, and proteostatic) in thermoregulation renders their underlying cellular pathways vulnerable to Tb rhythm disturbance, and furthermore, that ensuing loss of function may in turn worsen Tb disturbance. The central model is that deviations from the normal diurnal Tb rhythm (the right T at the right time of the sleep–wake cycle) are both driven by and drive AD tau pathology.

Given the selective vulnerability of NSS neurons to tau‐driven degeneration, together with evidence that diurnal Tb variation modulates tau protein dynamics, this review takes a tau‐centric perspective. Proposed thermoregulatory domains are considered in terms of their potential to add to tau‐driven Tb disturbance or act as pathways by which Tb may indirectly affect tau. Amyloid beta (Aβ), a core feature of AD pathology, is evaluated within the proteostatic domain. The goals of this review are to evaluate the framework and the central model against existing evidence. Findings are intended to highlight AD risk pathways that may be vulnerable to thermoregulatory/thermodynamic pathophysiological interactions, and to highlight areas in need of further research.

INTRODUCTION TO THERMOREGULATION AND DIURNAL TEMPERATURE DEPENDENCE

This section addresses key aspects of this topic that may be unfamiliar to most readers. These include the physical property of T, diurnal Tb and Tbr regulation in humans, and T‐dependence in the diurnal range.

What is temperature?

Our intuitive conception of T is a subjective experience coupled with physiological responses and motivated behavior aimed at restoring comfort. This review also concerns physical T: that quantity to which we have evolved such exquisite sensitivity and imperative drive to regulate. Temperature is a thermodynamic variable and a fundamental property of all matter. Every particle in the universe undergoes constant random motion referred to as thermal noise or Brownian motion (as observed at the microscale). A system with higher T corresponds to one in which particles move faster and undergo more rapid and forceful collisions—a system with higher kinetic energy. This property is captured by the Kelvin T scale, where absolute zero (0 K) denotes the theoretical absence of all motion. Familiar reference points between Kelvin, Celsius, and Fahrenheit scales include the freezing point of water (273 K, 0°C, 32°F), room T (295 K, 22°C, 72°F), and the boiling point of water (100°C, 212°F, 373 K).

Human core body temperature variation

Core body T (Tb) is best understood as the T of the aortic blood and the T of the thoracic and abdominal organs. In humans and other mammals, Tb and Tbr are maintained higher than peripheral tissues within a narrow range (<4°C in rodents and <2°C in humans) via behavioral strategies and brain‐regulated activity of sympathetic outflow to thermoregulatory end‐organs. Mammals, including human and rodent, regulate Tb about 1–2°C higher during waking compared to sleep. (See Refinetti for a comprehensive review across mammals including humans 9 and Figure 2 for diurnal Tb rhythms in older adults.) Details of the methods used for data collection are in Supplement 1, Body Temperature Data Collection Methods.

The exact values and diurnal range of human Tb and Tbr in naturalistic conditions are not established. For Tb, this is in part because published human Tb data mainly originate from pioneering studies of circadian rhythmicity in which constant routine protocols were used to restrict sleep and other naturalistic sources of Tb diurnal rhythm. In addition, rather than Tb data per se, cosine fit parameters were typically reported, which do not fully describe the diurnal Tb waveform. As shown in Figure 2A, the naturalistic Tb rhythm is closer to a rectangle than a cosine: the waking plateau is longer than the sleeping trough, with abrupt changes at sleep–wake transitions. Few continuous Tbr measurements exist in human. Surgical measurements show that Tbr diurnal rhythm closely corresponds to that in Tb, 11 and Tbr measured with magnetic resonance spectroscopy showed a diurnal rhythm. 12 In animal models, variations in Tbr are tightly correlated with and driven by those in Tb, with Tbr being higher than Tb. (See Supplement 2, Brain Temperature Diurnal Rhythm Regulation for further details.)

Key factors governing inter‐individual variation in Tb are sex and age. In naturally cycling women, Tb is comparable to that in men in the follicular phase, but is ≈0.2–0.4°C higher in the luteal phase. 13 We and other authors 14 found Tb to be higher during waking in postmenopausal women compared to men (see Figure 2B). Sex‐aggregated studies of Tb with aging report a lower mean, decreased amplitude, advanced phase, and reduced stability of Tb with increasing age, associated with attenuated vasoconstriction and decreased metabolic heat production 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ; however, these findings may be in part secondary to age‐associated factors like reduced physical activity. 16 , 17 More sex‐specific data are needed to understand factors governing Tb in aging.

Diurnal temperature dependence in the mammalian brain

T dependence is the degree to which the rate or magnitude of a process changes as a function of T. The fundamental importance of T in biological systems is well recognized, as is the highly T‐dependent nature of neural processes. 20 However, the specific role of diurnal Tbr change as a regulatory parameter is less well understood. Supplement 3, Diurnal Temperature Dependence provides context for why this topic may have remained uninvestigated, and provides the basis for the central model, and background to why the selected domains and AD cellular risk pathways are proposed to be vulnerable to Tb rhythm disturbance. Supplement 3 also includes information relevant to measuring T dependence.

Regarding protocol, seven of eight studies were entrained, and one used a constant routine. Female participants and sex‐specific analyses were almost entirely lacking: five of eight studies were exclusively in male patients and the small sample size in the remaining three studies obscured consequential sex‐specific analyses. Another major limitation is the considerable between study heterogeneity: five of eight studies were of late‐stage male dementia inpatients from the same Veterans Administration (VA) facility, with some patient overlap between studies, as noted previously. 28 The remaining three studies comprised male and female subjects with mild to moderate AD—two from inpatient settings and the third in community dwelling outpatients. In addition to variable setting (institutionalized/outpatient), other limitations affecting between‐study comparability and case‐control comparisons were the lack of control for sleep, medical comorbidities, and medications.

Consistent findings from the male advanced AD VA inpatient studies were elevated mesor and phase delay, whereas amplitude findings were non‐significant or variable (Table 1). By contrast, elevated mesor and phase delay were not seen in the non‐VA studies; higher amplitude was the most consistent finding (although in one study multi‐infarct dementia rather than healthy controls was the comparison group). Only one study in AD patients, Harper et al. (2008), 29 related Tb findings to AD biomarkers or other biological measures as discussed further in Section 4: INTERACTIONS BETWEEN THERMOREGULATION AND ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE PATHOPHYSIOLOGY.

Overall, current studies support the conclusion that males with advanced AD may exhibit Tb rhythms with elevated mesor and phase delay, within the limitations noted. Broad conclusions of “elevated body temperature in AD” 28 (across all demographics) are not supported. Furthermore, because Tb data were not integrated with sleep and only cosine fit parameters were reported, interpreting data according to the current framework is difficult. To illustrate this point, Tb data from an example subject from Harper et al. 10 are replotted using the ward lights on and off times shown (Figure 2A, rightmost plot). The Tb waveform has a loss of sleep–wake transitions and the normal “higher during waking lower during sleep” bimodal distribution over the sleep–wake cycle. This interpretation is speculative given that individual sleep data were not available but nonetheless serves to illustrate the limitations of cosine fits without control for sleep.

Important questions not yet addressed include how AD impacts thermoregulation in women; how major genetic risk factors for AD, for example, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 impact thermoregulation, and how Tb is altered in more moderate, compared to advanced AD. Relatedly, few studies have reported on Tb correlations with preclinical AD diagnosis or biomarker features. The current author's finding that lower waking Tb in cognitively normal older adults correlated with greater tau burden is discussed in Section 4. Finally, whether cold or warm defense is impaired in AD has not yet been established—although notably few cases of altered thermoregulatory behavior or abnormal Tb have been reported in AD.

INTERACTIONS BETWEEN THERMOREGULATION AND ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

This section opens with an introduction to thermoregulatory circuits (4.1) and warm‐ and cold‐defense. In subsection 4.2, warm and cold defense, although not a proposed AD risk domain, is evaluated to test the evidence for “too cold” and “too hot” theories. Subsections 4.3–4.7 each evaluate the evidence for a proposed thermoregulatory domain and its associated AD risk pathway. For each domain, evidence is assessed for: (1) a functional role in thermoregulation; (2) thermal entrainment of cellular or physiological pathways by Tb or Tbr rhythms—or lacking this, T‐dependence within the diurnal range; (3) involvement in tau or tau‐promoting AD pathology; and (4) pathophysiological interactions between AD and thermoregulation. (See Supplement 4 for further detailed methods.)

Thermoregulatory neural pathways

Thermoregulation begins with the detection of temperatures outside of the regulated range by cutaneous thermosensory afferents and preoptic hypothalamic monitoring of core blood temperature. These signals initiate a brain‐integrated physiological and behavioral response that minimizes Tb deviation (Figure 1B). Cold defense involves warm‐seeking behavior, cutaneous vasoconstriction to reduce heat dissipation by the cutaneous circulation, and heat production from non‐shivering and shivering thermogenesis involving brown adipose tissue (BAT) and muscle contraction. 30 , 31 Warm defense involves cool‐seeking behavior, cutaneous vasodilation, inhibition of thermogenesis, and sweating. These response arcs are coordinated by an ascending thermosensory–spinoparabrachial–parabrachial nucleus (PBN)–hypothalamic preoptic area (POA) descending dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH)–rostral medullary raphe (rMR)–spinal thermoeffector feedback loop 6 (Figure 1B). Current models hold that thermoregulation operates through multiple independent effector pathways—vasomotion, sweating, behavior—each with its own threshold defining the T at which corrective responses are initiated; furthermore, these thresholds are not fixed but are rather influenced by sleep–wake, circadian, inflammatory and bioenergetic state change. 6 , 32 , 33 , 34 Circuit entry points for these influences upon the core thermosensory–thermoeffector loop are shown.

Temperature sensation is mediated by first‐order pseudounipolar dorsal root ganglia neurons that express transient receptor potential (TRP) channels on their distal afferent processes. Different TRP channel classes confer cool‐ or warm‐sensing function. 35 Second‐order neurons convey these signals to the thalamus, and via a collateral, to the pontine lateral PBN (LPBN). 36 , 37 In experimental animals, the LPBN is a key thermoregulatory integrative center with major rostral projections to the POA and insular cortex (primary thermoregulatory cortex). 36 , 37 The POA is a group of several nuclei in which neurons (both glutamatergic and γ‐aminobutyric acid [GABA]ergic) integrate afferent LPBN signals with monitoring of local brain T via TRP channels, in addition to multimodal homeostatic functions. POA neurons modulate thermoregulatory output to rMR sympathoexcitatory premotor neurons via projections to the rMR‐projecting DMH. Rostral medullary raphe premotor neurons effect thermogenesis via multiple thermogenic end‐organs (Figure 1B), 7 , 8 , 38 , 39 , 40 and their activity is modulated by local serotonergic neurons. 41 , 42 , 43

Median preoptic area (MnPO) neurons are proposed to function as state‐dependent switches for either promoting or inhibiting Tb rise by modulating DMH‐rMR output. 8 , 44 Current models suggest that MnPO neurons tonically inhibit DMH‐rMR thermogenic output. Although the precise synaptic mechanisms—direct GABAergic inhibition 6 versus glutamatergic drive onto inhibitory interneurons in the DMH 7 , 8 —rremain debated, functional predictions are similar. The extent of MnPO‐mediated DMH disinhibition (and Tb rise) is gated by several factors, including warm and cold defense (below), and sleep–wake, inflammatory, circadian, and metabolic/bioenergetic states, described in subsequent subsections.

Warm and cold defense

Cutaneous cool stimuli are transduced by first‐order afferents expressing the transient receptor potential melastatin 8 (TRPM8) channel at their peripheral terminals. 45 In the LPBN, cool‐sensing third‐order neurons are located in the external lateral subregion (LPBel) and include FOXP2‐ (forkhead box protein P2) and somatostatin‐expressing subpopulations that project to the MnPO. Afferent cool‐sensing by LPBel neurons activates MnPO neurons, some of which express the bombesin receptor subtype 3 (BRS3) receptor, thereby disinhibiting DMH activity and increasing rMR‐mediated Tb rise. 38 , 46 , 47 Some LPBel neurons may also project directly to the DMH, where leptin receptor–expressing neurons have been implicated in cool sensation. 46 , 48 , 49

Warm‐sensing TRP channels in first‐order afferents may include TRPV1, TRPV3, TRPV4, and TRPM2. 30 Warm‐sensing third‐order neurons are located in the dorsal LPBN subregion (LPBd) and include glutamatergic prodynorphin and cholecystokinin neuron subpopulations that project to the POA, 50 predominantly the MnPO. LPBd‐recipient MnPO neurons activated by peripheral warming are intermixed with MnPO cool‐sensing neurons and express the following markers: pituitary adenylate cyclase–activating polypeptide (PACAP), brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), 51 nitric oxide synthase 1 (NOS1), 52 leptin receptor, 53 and estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) 54 , 55 ; see 56 , 57 for reviews. Notably, ERα expression varies across stages of the estrous cycle in parallel with shifts in thermal preference. 55 Warm‐sensitive galanin‐expressing GABAergic neurons, which both reduce Tb and promote non–rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, are also found in the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO) (intermediate nucleus in humans). 58

The POA contains intrinsically warm‐sensing neurons that express TRPM2 channels 59 and TRPC4 channels, 60 which are required for converting local POA Tbr increase into thermo‐effector cooling responses coordinated by these neurons. 60 Although peripheral T and Tbr are accepted thermoregulatory inputs, whether diurnal variation in their signals provides a thermoregulatory feedback cue is not known. 61 Relatedly, few studies have assessed whether the activity of TRP channels on thermoregulatory neurons is modified by diurnal Tbr change. The activation threshold of POA TRPM2 channels was close to the diurnal range (38 ± 1°C) when assessed ex vivo in acute brain slices, as opposed to ≈45°C in dissociated primary culture 59 ; these authors noted that the channel's reported activation T ranges from 33°C to 47°C depending on cellular context. Heat‐sensing thresholds may be modified in vivo by inflammatory mediators, as discussed in Section 4.5.

Tau pathology (hyperphosphorylation and or cytoskeletal changes) occurs in the LPBN and DMH at early Braak stages (0–I) 4 , 62 (Figure 3A), with both nuclei showing extensive tau pathology in later AD. 63 , 64 Few reports of tau pathology in the POA exist: two reports in Senile Dementia of the Alzheimer's Type (SDAT) cases using thioflavin‐S staining (of mature neurofibrillary tangles) suggested relative sparing. 64 , 65 Reports on earlier Braak stages measuring hyperphosphorylated (pre‐tangle) tau are lacking. Galanin‐positive neurons in the POA intermediate nucleus (human analog of the rodent VLPO) showed tau hyperphosphorylation and neuronal loss in advanced AD 66 , 67 ; however, earlier stages were not documented. Only one study (across early to late Braak stages) reported on tau pathology in the rMR (rostral raphe pallidus), finding complete sparing of this nucleus. 68 Studies explicitly relating tau‐ or other AD‐specific pathology in thermoregulatory neurons to impaired warm or cold defense in human are lacking, and abnormal thermoregulatory behavior is not a well‐documented feature of AD.

In animal model tests of cold defense, the 3xTg‐AD mouse model exhibited basal hypothermia and impaired cold defense that correlate with tau hyperphosphorylation and tangle load, and both tau and Aβ pathology were mitigated by raising Tb. 71 , 72 These authors, Vandal et al., proposed a bidirectional, vicious cycle between tau pathology and impaired thermoregulation; however, these studies did not specifically include measurement of tau pathology in thermoregulatory neurons. By contrast, Knight et al. 73 reported that 3xTgAD mice had increased mean Tb at baseline—although they did not measure cold defense—with an absence of hypothalamic pathology noted. Study differences that might explain this discrepancy are sex (Knight et al. was male‐only, whereas Vandal et al. included both males and females), colony metabolic phenotype, and Tb measurement technique (Vandal et al. used a single acute rectal probe at ZT0, whereas Knight et al. used 7‐day continuous abdominal telemetry reporting a 24‐h mean).

In summary, little evidence in humans supports “too hot/cold” theories of AD: human pathological findings implicate thermoregulatory nuclei involved in warm and cold defense, but with no evidence connecting pathology to functional deficits. Mouse findings are heterogeneous, with a need to control sex and strain, with the evidence mostly supporting associations between central tau and hypothermia, with little neuronal specificity.

Sleep–wake promoting systems

Thermoregulation and sleep are functionally and anatomically interdependent. Thermal interventions including skin warming 52 and Tb increase causally alter sleep, 74 , 75 and sleep state strongly correlates with Tbr variation, 76 , 77 , 78 which fluctuates rapidly at sleep–wake transitions. 79 This interdependence stems from thermoregulatory roles of sleep‐ or wake‐promoting neurons (SPn or WPn), detailed below, and from T‐dependent neuronal and network properties (see Table 2).

Both lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) orexin WPns and tuberomammillary nucleus (TMN) histaminergic WPns are known to increase Tb. LHA (and perifornical area) orexin neurons directly excite rMR neurons and indirectly inhibit VLPO SPn. 93 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 TMN neurons increase Tb by exciting glutamatergic MnPO neurons via H1/H2 receptors, and by inhibiting MnPO GABAergic warm‐sensitive neurons via H3 receptors, thereby disinhibiting DMH→rMR thermogenic drive. 98 , 99 , 100 Thermoregulatory roles of other major WPn, the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) and locus coeruleus (LC), are less well studied. Electrical stimulation (in rat DRN) caused robust Tb increase, 101 and LC activity may maintain against Tb drop during NREM sleep. 102 Regarding SPn, GABAergic galanin‐expressing neurons in the VLPO promote both Tb drop and NREM sleep by inhibiting LHA, TMN, LC, and DRN wake‐promoting centers and by inhibiting DMH→rMR thermogenic drive. 58 , 84 , 103 , 104 Nitric‐oxide (NOS1) –expressing glutamatergic neurons in the MnPO drive both NREM sleep and Tb decrease in response to external warmth. 52 Of note, this reciprocal inhibitory coupling between WPn and SPn temporally organizes Tb and Tbr such that peak Tb aligns with waking and minimum Tb with sleep.

As shown in Table 2, naturalistic sleep–wake–associated Tb and or Tbr change strongly correlates with sleep microarchitecture, with causal support from local brain heating and cooling studies. In evaluating how T change may affect neural mechanisms, the likely physiological range of Tbr (as opposed to Tb) should be used. Tbr is uniformly reported as higher than Tb, ranging between 36°C and 39°C under physiological waking conditions; Tbr values up to 40°C were detected by magnetic resonance spectroscopy (see Supplementary Section 2). Therefore, physiological range temperature (PRT) for Tbr is taken here as 36–39°C, with 40°C being suggestive. Within this range, mechanisms associated with oscillatory network activity underlying sleep and arousal states showed higher frequency with increasing T (Table 2): these include hippocampal neuronal activity, 86 , 87 whole electroencephalography (EEG) frequency, 88 , 89 , 105 hippocampal sharp wave ripple frequency, 91 thalamic spindle frequency, 92 hippocampal resting membrane potential, and N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor–mediated calcium influx, both dependent on TRPV4 channel activation. 90 (See also 106 for energy efficiency of Na+/K+ pump within the 37–42°C range.)

Diurnal range Tbr change also modulates SPn activity and sleep‐macroarchitectural features. Szymusiak and McGinty showed that warm‐sensitive SPn POA neurons are stimulated by higher Tbr to drive increased NREM sleep, decreased WPn activity, and Tb decrease, explaining the coupling of these outputs 82 (Table 2). An additional mechanism by which higher Tbr may drive sleep and Tb cooling is T‐dependent prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) production by POA neurons. PGD2 is a lipid hormone produced by prostaglandin‐d synthase with hypothermic 107 somnogenic effects 108 that depend upon DP1 receptor–expressing VMPO neurons 85 ; adenosine (also a hypothermic somnogen) released by local DP1 receptor expressing astrocytes may contribute. 108 , 109 , 110 , 111 These mechanisms effectively link higher pre‐sleep Tbr to the magnitude of subsequent Tb and Tbr drop and NREM sleep; correlations between these outputs are seen in both mouse and human. 112 Peripherally based mechanisms likely contribute to this coupling: Harding et al. reported that peripheral T manipulations acted via POA neurons to couple NREM and Tb cooling outputs, 75 and melatonin also contributes to sleep pressure, peripheral vasodilation, and Tb drop. 52 , 113 Sex differences are notable: women compared to men exhibit greater delta power 114 (in addition to higher waking Tb), and in postmenopausal women but not age‐matched men, a greater Tb drop correlated with greater NREM sleep and delta. 115

Sleep disturbance appears early in AD and may drive disease progression via multiple mechanisms. 116 , 117 Most WPn are affected by tau pathology at early Braak stages; see Lew et al. for a review of their selective vulnerability. 118 These include the LHA, 4 , 119 TMN, 4 LC, 2 DRN, 3 , 5 , 68 and LPBN, 4 , 62 all of which show tau hyperphosphorylation and cytoskeletal pathology at Braak stages 0–I, with progressive tau pathology in late Braak stages 63 , 120 (Figure 3A). LHA orexinergic nuclei are among the first to show neuronal loss, together with neuroinflammatory alterations. 119 Regarding SPn, tau hyperphosphorylation and neuronal loss were reported in galanin‐positive POA intermediate nucleus neurons (VLPOA analog) in advanced AD 66 , 67 ; earlier stages were not examined.

Direct in vivo or post‐mortem evidence in humans correlating NSS WPn or SPn tau pathology with Tb rhythm disturbance is lacking. We reported that in cognitively normal older adults, lower waking Tb was associated with greater cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181) and greater tau tracer uptake in early Braak stage (I–II) brain areas. 121 Although subcortical tau pathology was not measured, it may be assumed present from the level of Braak staging. Notably, these correlations may also arise from the inverse T dependence of tau phosphorylation; (see Section 4.7: Proteostasis). Antemortem sleep features that determine the Tb waveform correlated with neuronal count 66 and postmortem neuropathology within WPn and SPn, 118 providing indirect evidence that WPn tau pathology may impact Tb rhythm.

Few studies to date have simultaneously assessed Tb and sleep disturbance in AD. Increased peripheral vasodilation during waking (typically inversely correlated with Tb) was reported to be associated with increased daytime sleepiness, 122 a known feature of AD. 123 Given this interdependency, other features of sleep disturbance seen in AD including excessive wakefulness at night, 124 reduced slow‐wave activity (SWA), 125 and reduced slow oscillation–sleep spindle coupling 125 may also show coupled disturbances with Tb. Of interest, exposure to thermoneutral ambient T was reported to reduce soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 in mouse hippocampus, in part by enhancing NREM delta power. 126 Tb may also mediate sleep effects upon tau, Aβ, or other AD risk pathways given that sleep–wake Tb range change affects tau phosphorylation and secretion. 127 , 128

In summary, in rodents, wake‐ and sleep‐promoting neurons are themselves thermoeffector nodes whose reciprocal inhibition aligns peak Tb with waking and the Tb nadir with sleep, and the oscillatory and synaptic mechanisms of sleep are T dependent within the 36–39°C physiological range. In humans, both WPn and SPn carry tau pathology; WPn may be more vulnerable. Human evidence linking tau pathology to Tb diurnal rhythm abnormalities is supportive but indirect.

Circadian timing

Circadian timing systems are phylogenetically ancient, far predating mammalian endothermy (detailed in Supplement 3). In mammals, diurnal light cycles entrain circadian rhythms via intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells that project to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) 129 and other hypothalamic nuclei. 130 , 131 The SCN, a small, mostly GABAergic nucleus in the anterior hypothalamus, organizes circadian rhythmicity in all bodily processes, including the Tb diurnal rhythm. 129 , 132 Lesioning the SCN abolishes circadian Tb rhythms in rats, hamsters, and squirrel monkeys 133 , 134 and disrupts circadian rhythms in BAT‐dependent thermogenesis in mice. 135 SCN regulation of Tb rhythm occurs via projections to the dorsal subparaventricular zone (SPZ), which projects to the DMH 136 , 137 (Figure 1B), which in turn coordinates the timing of sleep and thermoregulatory activity via outputs to SPn 138 ; SCN controlled release of melatonin, which contributes to sleep propensity and the Tb nocturnal drop, 139 , 140 adds to this coordination. Neighboring astrocytes 141 , 142 and microglia 143 also maintain SCN rhythms, but few studies have explored their role in Tb rhythm regulation.

Circadian rhythmicity is maintained at the molecular level by a self‐oscillating transcriptional/translational feedback loop (TTFL), present in SCN neurons and almost every cell type. During the circadian day, CLOCK:BMAL1 heterodimers trans‐activate Period (Per1‐3) and Cryptochrome (Cry1‐2) genes and their encoded proteins PER and CRY accumulate and inhibit CLOCK:BMAL1 trans‐activation. Over the circadian night, phosphorylation by casein kinase 1δ/ε, ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of PER and CRY releases CLOCK:BMAL1 inhibition, restarting the cycle. 144 Per2 E’‐box mutant mice have highly fragmented Tb rhythms. 145 In addition to light cues, core clock genes integrate sleep–wake, 79 inflammatory, 146 bioenergetic, 147 , 148 and proteostatic cues via feedback loops ancillary to the core TTFL. SCN connections with other hypothalamic nuclei also mediate this integration. 149 , 150

The integration of T cues by central and peripheral circadian systems involves both T compensation (resistance to T change, typically of period; see Supplement 3 for more detail) and thermal entrainment (detailed subsequently). In the rodent SCN, the period and amplitude of Per2 clock gene expression, and the period but not amplitude of neuronal firing were T compensated in vitro and in vivo, a property requiring interregional SCN neuronal connectivity 151 , 152 , 153 (Table 3). However, recent SCN in vitro work showed that T change slightly outside the physiological range induced Rbm3 transcription and lengthened its period in a Bmal1‐dependent manner. 152 Collectively, findings indicate that SCN T compensation is not absolute and that clock timing and amplitude may be vulnerable to both AD and thermoregulatory disturbance.

Research led by the Takahashi and Schibler labs shows that clock genes throughout the brain and body are thermally entrained by diurnal range T fluctuation 151 , 155 , 162 , 163 , 164 (Table 3, as for all other findings). Key T‐dependent regulators of the TTFL include the warm‐sensitive heat shock factor 1 (HSF1), 151 which peaks at maximal waking Tb and increases Per2 expression, and cold‐sensitive RNA‐binding proteins CIRBP and RNA‐binding motif protein 3; RBM3, which peak at the Tb nadir. 165 , 166 A novel TRPV4‐dependent mammalian cold‐inducible protein, SRSF5, was recently characterized. 158 Hoekstra et al. showed that thermal entrainment modulates TTFL activity in non‐SCN brain areas: sleep‐associated Tbr change in mouse cortex drove Cirbp, Rbm3, Hsf1 and Per2 mRNA levels according to their known T‐dependence, and sleep deprivation with loss of Tbr drop prevented these effects. 79 Diurnal range T‐change also thermally entrains core clock gene expression in peripheral tissues (fibroblasts, liver, lung, pituitary) via HSF1, CIRBP, and RBM3. 154 , 155 , 157 , 158 , 167 Non‐core clock gene mechanisms contribute to thermal entrainment of diurnal rhythms 168 : Heyd and Pruessner et al. showed that T‐sensitive kinases and phosphatases act as molecular thermometers to regulate SR protein phosphorylation, generating diurnal splicing rhythms across hundreds of transcripts. 159 , 169

Disturbances in circadian rest–activity cycles begin early in AD, progress with disease course, and are among its most impactful symptoms, 170 with pathophysiological alterations at multiple levels of the circadian system evident from both human and mouse research. 171 , 172 Evidenced and potential interactions with Tb rhythm disturbance are described below. Preliminary post‐mortem reports show the SCN exhibits glial alterations at Braak stage 0, with small NFTs and tau hyperphosphorylation at Braak stage II. 173 In late AD, the SCN exhibits substantial tau hyperphosphorylation and neuronal loss, including AVP+, VIP+, and neurotensin+ populations, and prominent astrogliosis. 29 , 174 , 175 Harper et al. showed that the degree of global dorsomedial SCN degeneration and neurotensin neuronal loss both strongly correlated with diminished Tb rhythm amplitude. 29 Reports in rodent connecting SCN AD‐related pathology to Tb rhythm disturbance are lacking; however, Warfield et al. reported that APPSwe‐Tau mice exhibited a Tb phase delay coinciding in disease course and correlating in severity with tau pathology in dynorphin‐expressing dLPB neurons that project to the SCN and other circadian structures. 176 Of interest, both Tb disturbance and tau pathology appeared earlier in females (3–5 months) as opposed to males (7–9 months). Knight et al. also reported Tb phase delay in male 3xTg‐AD mice but did not observe hypothalamic pathology. 73

Whether Tb rhythm disturbance contributes to clock gene dysfunction in AD mouse models or patients has not been reported. Hoekstra et al. proposed this relationship in their (wild‐type) mouse study showing physiological changes in Tb affect SCN clock genes via multiple mechanisms. 152 Whether Tb alterations associated with age, sleep disturbance, inflammation, or menopause contribute to clock gene disruption via impaired thermal entrainment remains to be tested in human. Although speculative, tau pathology may impair T compensation by degrading the SCN network interactions Buhr et al. showed to be necessary for it. 151

In summary, in rodent, the SCN and its SPZ→DMH outputs generate the diurnal Tb rhythm; tau in SCN projecting neurons is associated with Tb rhythm alterations, and clock genes are thermally entrained in brain and peripheral tissues. In humans, the SCN carries tau pathology and glial change from Braak stage 0–II, with dorsomedial SCN degeneration correlating with diminished Tb amplitude. Whether thermal entrainment of clock gene activity occurs in humans remains unknown. More studies in rodent and human are needed to establish causality across all framework criteria.

Inflammation

Thermoregulation and inflammation are linked by the evolutionarily ancient febrile response to infection, present in reptiles, fish, and insects 177 , 178 and far predating mammalian endothermy. In mammals, the febrile response is elicited when peripheral immune cells and brain microglia and astrocytes detect pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and or damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), producing pyrogenic mediators and cytokines (interleukin [IL]‐6, IL‐1β, tumor‐necrosis factor α [TNFα], and others). Peripherally produced cytokines reach the brain via circumventricular organs (CVOs), vagal afferents, and blood–brain barrier transport. In brain endothelial cells, cytokines induce prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production by cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2), and microsomal prostaglandin E synthase‐1 (mPGES‐1). Two major pathways involved in both PAMP‐elicited cytokine release and cytokine‐elicited PGE2 release are NF‐κB and JAK‐STAT3 179 , 180 , 181 , 182 ; both show T‐dependent activity detailed further below. In the preoptic area, PGE2 binds to EP3 receptor expressing MnPO neurons, thereby disinhibiting DMH→rMR–mediated Tb increase produced by heat‐seeking behavior, vasoconstriction, and muscle and BAT thermogenesis. 6 , 183 Local astrocytes may contribute to this response. 182 , 184

Recent findings show that PGE2‐MnPO‐EP3 signaling modulates not only the febrile response, but also Tb decreases in warm defense and diurnal Tb rhythm. 8 Ablation of MnPO‐EP3+ neurons in mouse specifically increased Tb during ZT0–ZT12—the usual sleep period. 8 Supporting this, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which inhibit cyclooxygenase‐mediated conversion of arachidonic acid to PGE2, affect the nocturnal T drop in humans. 185 Finally, inflammatory cytokines modulate both WPn and SPn activity 186 , 187 and sleep features, 186 , 188 with linked effects upon Tb and Tbr. 188 , 189 Whether chronic inflammation disrupts the diurnal dynamics of pro‐ versus anti‐inflammatory pathways will be important to determine in order to understand how chronic inflammation associated with Aβ deposition, aging, menopause, APOE ε4, and other AD risk factors may impact thermoregulation, as discussed below.

Microglia, 190 astrocytes, 191 and peripheral immune cells exhibit cell‐autonomous circadian/diurnal rhythms that influence inflammatory pathway output. Previous authors 192 , 193 , 194 proposed that Tb or Tbr may act as endogenous entraining factors, but few studies have tested this. Harper et al. (2018) 194 and Heltberg et al. (2022) 195 showed that in mouse fibroblasts NF‐κB cytoplasmic–nuclear shuttling frequency and inflammatory gene transcription increases linearly between 32°C and 40°C; however, functional consequences remain unclear. For peripheral immune effectors, fever range hyperthermia (FRH), usually defined as 38–41°C, augments multiple response elements during lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–induced inflammation including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNFα production; neutrophil recruitment; immune cell cytotoxicity and phagocytosis; and decreased endothelial barrier integrity. (See 177 , 178 for reviews.)

These effects are linked to T‐dependent pathways including NF‐κB, JAK‐STAT3, 177 , 178 the heat shock pathway, and TRP channel activity 196 (Table 4). However, FRH does not uniformly upregulate the inflammatory response. Cytokine‐ 197 , 198 and tissue‐specific 199 effects are observed. In addition, FRH can both upregulate and repress the febrile response, notably via HSF1–NF‐κB pathway interactions involving feedforward and feedback loops 177 , 178 , 197 , 198 , 200 and time‐dependent effects, that is, thermal history. 201 Few studies were found testing physiological range T (PRT) change (36–38°C in human; 35.0–38°C in mouse) upon peripheral immune cells. T lymphocytes showed increased inflammatory output in vitro at 38.5°C (human) 202 or 39.5°C (mice and human) 203 , 204 as compared to 37°C.

For the present review, PRT modulation of brain inflammatory cell (microglia and astrocytes) responses was taken as 36°C versus 39°C contrasts, with 40°C as suggestive (Table 4). Microglia showed increased motility, cytoskeletal branching, calcium influx, TNFα production, and other measures of activation with increased T within the physiological range, with effects depending on TRPV4 and TRPM2 channels. T increase similarly upregulated inflammatory pathways in astrocytes, although fewer studies were located. Shibasaki showed that astrocyte populations express TRPV4 (activated by physiological Tb change), 212 suggesting that T‐dependent effects in the physiological range are likely.

Systemic and central neuroinflammation are known features of AD that may promote and or be driven by amyloid and tau aggregation. 213 Features include astrocyte activation 214 linked to elevated glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels; microglial activation; centrally and peripherally elevated levels of thermogenic cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNFα 213 , 215 , 216 , 217 ; and raised CSF PGE2. 218 Known AD risk factors age, menopause, 219 , 220 APOE ε4 carriage, 221 , 222 and metabolic syndrome may contribute to systemic and neuroinflammation. 213 Fibrillar Aβ is known to act as a DAMP and induces microglial activation and thermogenic cytokine production in association with NALP3 inflammasomes. 223

Chronic inflammation in AD could raise Tb, as suggested previously. 28 This theory has not yet been tested in humans, and a study in 3xTgAD mice found that NSAID treatment did not reduce Tb. 73 A speculative hypothesis is that chronically elevated cytokines and PGE2 may suppress MnPO‐mediated inhibition of DMH‐rMR thermogenesis, and ensuing blunting of the nocturnal Tb drop would contribute to Tb phase delay and elevated mesor (Section 3). This hypothesis could be tested in AD patients most at risk of inflammation—postmenopausal females and or APOE ε4 carriers. 222 Of interest, human E4 knock‐in mice exhibit higher Tb 224 and the apoE4 variant may be thermolabile, 225 meaning that elevated nocturnal Tb could further impair its already less effective anti‐inflammatory actions relative to other alleles.

Whether AD‐associated Tb or Tbr dysregulation disturbs inflammatory pathway function remains untested in human and mouse. In studying this, interaction and priming effects whereby preexisting inflammation or oxidative stress potentiates Tbr effects should be examined. For example, T‐dependent effects upon microglial motility were increased dramatically following LPS activation, via TRP‐gated mechanisms 205 ; LPS sensitized TRPV1 channels to JAK‐STAT pathway activation in POA astrocytes and CVO endothelial cells 184 ; reactive oxygen species sensitized the TRPM2 channel to activation at physiological T in TRPM2‐expressing HEK293 cells 226 ; and see also for NF‐κB priming interactions. 146 , 196 Of interest, hypoestrogenic states were associated with dysregulated TRP channel expression in mouse POA. 227

Metabolic energy balance

Thermogenesis needed to maintain Tb within 36–38°C comes at great energetic cost and is a major component of resting energy expenditure. 228 , 229 , 230 In rodents, thermogenesis and eating are highly coordinated in the ultradian basic rest activity cycle, 231 and food intake modulates daily torpor. 232 , 233 , 234 AD and its associated risk factors involve metabolic deficits including reduced regional neuronal metabolism, appetite dysregulation, neuroendocrine dysregulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. 235 , 236 Several thermoregulatory NSS nuclei sense nutrients and regulate appetite as well as thermogenesis, and/or are densely interconnected with such neurons. 237 Review of metabolic thermoregulatory interactions in human and mouse showed that although afternoon meal timing has been reported to be associated with an “afternoon (Tb) dip,” how nutrient intake affects Tb rhythms is not well understood. 238 As such, a brief review of notable thermoregulatory or T‐dependent interactions relevant to the metabolic domain and related AD risk pathways is given in Supplement 5.

Proteostasis

Proteostasis (protein homeostasis) is the cell's maintenance of the proper concentration, conformation, and turnover of its proteins to keep the proteome functional and prevent the accumulation of misfolded or aggregated species. 239 , 240 Processes that enable proteostasis, such as balancing synthesis (mTORC1) with degradation (ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy–lysosome systems) and maintaining proper folding (heat shock and other chaperones) exhibit circadian/diurnal variation; further, their master regulators–for example, AMPK, PI3K/AKT, mTORC1, and HSF1 are highly interlinked with the clock gene cycle. 147 , 148 , 241 , 242 , 243 Proteostasis is not currently recognized as a functional domain that influences or is influenced by thermoregulation. However, the evidence summarized below suggests that the proteomic stress‐sensing capacity of HSF1 and potentially other master regulators is functionally interlinked with thermosensation and thermoregulation. In addition, proteostasis is affected by diurnal range T variation, meaning that perturbations of Tb diurnal rhythm disturbance may further perturb proteostasis.

The heat shock pathway, discovered due to a serendipitous mis‐setting of an incubator, 244 is necessary for maintaining protein folding under both baseline and stress conditions, and is critical to both tau and Aβ proteostasis. 245 As a warm‐sensitive modulator of the clock gene TTFL, HSF1 is activated by sleep–wake changes in Tb and Tbr (Section 4.4). We also found that physiological Tb change modulated HSP expression in vitro. 128 HSF1 activation is elicited by febrile hyperthermia and modulates its duration and magnitude (Section 4.5). In mice, pharmacological manipulations of TRPV1, shown to be involved in POA febrile response thermoregulation, 184 lead to alterations in hypothalamic HSF1 expression and loss of Tb drop during inactivity. 246 Ingenwerth et al. showed in mice that Hsf1 deficiency is associated with hyperthermia and increased BAT thermogenesis, and that HSF1 was expressed in the POA where it co‐localized with thermoregulatory neurons. The authors proposed that HSF1 acts as a POA thermosensor restraining heat production. 247 Together, these observations point toward HSF1 as a potential thermosensory and thermoregulatory node via which multimodal proteostatic threats may affect thermoregulation. Aβ and tau, discussed below, are direct chaperone clients of HSP70, HSP90, and other HSF1‐dependent HSPs. 248

Deficits in tau proteostasis are core pathophysiological processes in AD, including intraneuronal aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau, elevated extracellular levels, and extraneuronal spread throughout the brain. Hyperphosphorylation and neurofibrillary tangle formation correlate closely with neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. The role of tau in thermoregulation is not understood. Arendt et al. reported that tau hyperphosphorylation during hibernation correlates with alterations in markers of neuronal gain control and synaptic plasticity, including NMDA receptor inactivation and cytoskeletal remodeling. 249 , 250 This suggests that T‐dependent changes in tau phosphorylation may facilitate neuronal remodeling and or neuroprotection during hibernation. Supporting a potential role of tau‐mediated neuroplasticity, hibernation in a variety of mammals reversibly leads to tau hyperphosphorylation and alternative splicing (increased 3 repeat/4 repeat tau ratio with cooler T). 251 Further studies are needed to elucidate the role of tau in diurnal thermoregulation.

In mice and in vitro, tau phosphorylation, secretion, and other processes governing tau proteostasis are causally Tdependent within the diurnal range (Table 5). Planel and colleagues showed that in mouse, T decrease caused by sleep, 127 experimental cooling, anesthesia, 252 hypoglycemia, 233 diabetes 253 and obesity 254 and aging 255 induce tau hyperphosphorylation that is reversed by maintaining normothermia. 71 , 233 Effects were seen at multiple clinically relevant phosphosites, including AT8/206 used in Braak staging. At T‐sensitive phosphosites, a 1°C T decrease elicited or correlated with an ≈80% increase in phosphorylation. 233 We showed in cognitively normal older adults that lower waking Tb was associated with higher CSF and plasma p‐tau181 levels and greater tau tracer uptake in early Braak stage areas. 121 Notably, although directionally consistent with animal model findings, this finding may also reflect the effects of tau pathology in wake‐promoting/Tb‐raising neurons.

In contrast to tau phosphorylation, tau secretion is upregulated by waking, and downregulated by sleeping T. Using a combination of in vitro and in vivo methods, we showed in mouse (Canet et al. 128 ) that wake‐like T (~38°C) drives an ≈2‐fold greater tau secretion compared to sleep‐like T (~35°C); effects derived from T‐dependent activity in PP2A, caspase 3, and syndecan 3 components in the tau unconventional (UPS‐1) protein secretion pathway (Table 5). Extracellular tau during waking was dephosphorylated, whereas that during sleep was phosphorylated. 128 In older adults, higher waking Tb and a greater difference between waking and sleep Tb correlated with diurnal change in plasma and CSF tau. 128

Aβ accumulation is an early, initiating driver of AD pathology, interacting with pro‐inflammatory effects, proteostatic failure, and other tau aggregation promoting pathways. How Aβ aggregation affects thermoregulation remains unknown, and relatively few studies have examined T‐dependent effects upon the Aβ pathway. Noorani et al. found that raising T in vitro substantially increased Aβ40/42 expression in HEK‐APP and HEK‐C99 cells, with no change in amyloid precursor protein (APP) or Beta site APP cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1); effects were dependent on HSP90 catalyzed γ‐secretase complex formation and activity. 256 (See also 259 for suggestive effects at febrile T, and 260 for T‐dependent effects on protein fibrillation kinetics.) Although no similar effects were found in vivo, the authors attributed this to the low levels of poorly amyloidogenic murine Aβ. Jung et al. 261 reported that sustained 30°C housing in Tg2576 mice raised Tb in both the sleep and wake phases, and that this condition was associated with increased soluble Aβ40/42, insoluble Aβ42 cortical plaque burden, BACE1, and sAPPβ compared to normal housing T; although they did not measure causal effects of T or correlations between T and Aβ outcomes, findings are consistent with but do not confirm T‐dependent effects. Together, these findings indicate that more studies are needed on how diurnal Tb change impacts Aβ levels and underlying production pathways.

Systems involved in protein degradation (the ubiquitin–proteasome system and autophagy–lysosome pathway) and removal from the brain (extracellular glymphatic clearance) may also exhibit diurnal variation; this is less well established for degradation systems, and most evidence supports greater glymphatic system activity during sleep. Whether physiological T variation affects the ubiquitin–proteasome system or autophagic–lysosomal flux in uninjured neuronal models remains unknown; the available evidence derives exclusively from acute injury paradigms, in which mild hypothermia is reported to upregulate autophagic flux. King et al. reported that mild hypothermia increased autophagic and HSP activity in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells in vitro. 262

The glymphatic system comprises astrocytic end‐feet forming perivascular spaces around cerebral vasculature; arterial pulsations create bulk‐flow clearing solute‐containing CSF via perivenous drainage. 263 Glymphatic flux increases during sleep, 264 , 265 as does perivascular localization 266 of the water channel aquaporin 4 (AQP4) in astrocytic end‐feet, 267 which is critical to tau, 268 Aβ, 263 and α‐synuclein 269 clearance. What sleep‐associated factors increase AQP4 perivascular localization remains unknown. Although not explicitly testing T dependence within the diurnal range, two studies 257 , 258 provide compelling evidence that astrocytic AQP4 is cold sensitive and that transcription and expression are markedly increased by relative hypothermia (32°C vs 37°C), involving TRPV4 and calmodulin‐based mechanisms. 257 These findings suggest that sleep‐associated Tb decrease may functionally entrain glymphatic clearance onset. Notably, AQP4 also regulates astrocytic calcium signaling, 270 involved in sleep regulation, creating potential for reciprocal interactions.

SYNTHESIS OF INTERACTIONS BETWEEN THERMOREGULATION AND AD PATHOLOGY

Summary of evidence for the research framework

This structured narrative review used a two‐arm framework anchored within thermoregulatory functional domains to evaluate interactions between NSS neuron tau pathology and thermoregulatory dysfunction in AD. The first arm concerns tau pathology in thermoregulatory neurons affecting diurnal Tb rhythm. Evidence for AD‐type tau pathology in human thermoregulatory neuronal populations has not been systematically assessed previously; this review finds early and progressive involvement of multiple thermoregulatory nuclei. In rodents, the neuronal processes and cellular pathways of the sleep–wake, circadian, inflammatory, metabolic, and proteostatic domains are both functionally integrated with diurnal thermoregulation and implicated in early AD. Evidence that Tb rhythm disturbance is caused by thermoregulatory neuron tau pathology, and by domain alterations that increase risk for that pathology, is preliminary but supportive. It remains mostly indirect: no study has causally related NSS tau pathology to Tb rhythm disturbance.

The second arm concerns the role of the diurnal Tb rhythm in affecting tau and tau‐relevant processes and domains. Rodent findings support T dependence in the diurnal range across the domains assessed, especially for tau‐related processes, with limitations detailed in Section 6. The observed pattern across domains is consistent with a diurnally oscillating thermal niche model (detailed in Supplement 3). That is, the distinct T dependence profiles of sleep–active and wake–active processes enables the diurnal Tb rhythm to orchestrate their sequential ordering over the sleep‐wake cycle (Figure 3B).

Taken together, tau‐pathology–induced thermoregulatory disturbance and T‐dependent pathways implicated in tau pathology suggest the potential for reciprocal interactions that may accelerate disease progression. No studies examining such interactions were found; see Section 6 for suggested future directions. Overall, findings support a high level of functional overlap between AD risk domains and diurnal thermoregulation, and also support the hypothesis that deviations from the right temperature at the right time may lead to pathophysiological interactions. A brief synthesis of findings is provided below, followed by limitations and future directions.

Effects of tau pathology upon thermoregulation

Neurons in several wake‐promoting nuclei that show tau pathology at early Braak stages are thermoregulatory neurons that raise Tb during waking, establishing these neurons as a vulnerable functional population. Other nuclei showing early tau pathology serve thermo‐afferent processing (LPBN), circadian rhythmicity (SCN), and integrative control of rMR outflow (DMH). In all these nuclei, tau pathology progressed with Braak stages. Premotor (rMR) neurons remained uninvolved, even at late AD stages, although evidence remains limited. No studies of the MnPO were found—an important thermoregulatory region.

Advanced dementia in males is associated with phase delay of circadian rhythms. Studies of thermoregulation in AD mouse models show mixed results, with the majority tending toward hypothermia. More research in both humans and rodents is needed to mechanistically connect tau pathology with Tb rhythm disturbance. The few existing studies provide correlative support: in an all‐male sample with advanced AD, NSS (SCN) neuronal pathology correlated with altered Tb waveform suggestive of pathological interactions. 29 In a predominantly female, cognitively normal sample, lower waking Tb correlated with p‐tau burden. 121 In an AD mouse model, dLPB tau burden correlated with Tb rhythm phase delay, with these relationships appearing earlier in female than male mice. 176 Across these studies, correlations may result from thermoregulatory neuron dysfunction and/or disorder in T‐dependent processes leading to tau aggregation.

Effects of AD risk domains upon thermoregulation

Sleep–wake and circadian domains, known risk pathways for AD, were found to be highly integrated with thermoregulation in rodent models, consistent with previous research. For the inflammatory system, implicated in diurnal thermoregulation relatively recently, more basic research is needed to understand how chronic systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation like that implicated in aging and AD affect specific Tb diurnal rhythm features. For metabolic and proteostatic domains, animal model findings warrant further investigation. The role of the heat shock pathway in thermoregulation is particularly important to examine given that it sits at the nexus of proteostatic, circadian, and inflammatory domains and that its thermoregulatory function may be impaired by AD‐associated proteotoxic load.

Diurnal Tb rhythm effects upon tau pathology and AD risk domains

Evidence for the direct effects of diurnal range T change upon tau phosphorylation and secretion in mouse is strong, with supportive correlations in humans. 128 Findings support a model (Figure 3B) in which higher waking Tb supports tau proteostasis by coupling higher extracellular tau levels with dephosphorylation and greater heat shock chaperone support, both of which render that tau less toxic. Lower sleeping Tb means that phosphorylation coincides with lower extracellular tau levels. (See Canet et al. for further details. 128 ) Blunting or phase‐shifting the rhythm would uncouple these processes.

Regarding AD risk pathways, evidence strongly supports a role of Tb diurnal rhythm in modulating sleep (in humans and rodents) and circadian rhythmicity (in vitro and in rodent), within the limitations discussed below. To better understand how diurnal T‐range change affects inflammation, it will be important to accurately measure both Tb and Tbr, given that the latter may extend to the febrile range for Tb, and to test how pro‐inflammatory or pro‐oxidative stress states modify T dependence, given the priming effects between T and inflammation observed in glia and neurons. Although current evidence that lower T drives increased astrocytic AQP4 localization comes from temperatures outside the diurnal Tb range, the involvement of TRPV4 channels in this T dependence suggests that effects will also be seen within this range 212 ; studies are needed to test if the sleep‐associated Tb drop plays a role in enabling glymphatic clearance.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

A major gap in the evidence base is the lack of studies in women with AD and of sex‐specific analyses (further discussed in future directions). In this review, several important thermoregulatory neuronal populations (cholinergic and dopaminergic among others) were omitted, as were important sleep features (REM sleep). These and any other omissions should not be interpreted as negative findings for common links to both thermoregulation and AD. Several neuropathological studies reviewed did not label neuron type within nuclei and so results cannot be definitively connected with thermoregulatory neurons.

Across all domains, few studies were found with tests of true thermal entrainment, i.e., a T rhythm entraining a similar rhythm in a T‐dependent process. In tests of T dependence, most studies used two or three T values, which may miss optimum ranges and do not give a true measure of T dependence. Few studies of T dependence in the brain were found—particularly important for the circadian domain in which tissue differences are likely. Species differences are also important to consider: Beale et al. showed that the cell‐autonomous response to diurnal T cycles is differentiated by temporal thermal niche (night‐active vs day‐active), and that for some pathways, T sensitivity may be reduced in day versus night active species. 271

This review points toward many possible future directions. The following hypotheses concerning wake‐promoting neuron vulnerability illustrate specific testable predictions that emerge from the right T at the right time framework, together with general approaches to testing them. Rationale: In mice, orexin and other WPn populations exhibit marked diurnal rhythms in neuronal activity, energy use, 272 , 273 cytoskeletal remodeling and synaptic plasticity, 274 , 275 which may be required for both wake‐promoting and Tb‐raising functions. Rapid cytoarchitectural remodeling and high metabolic requirements during waking may be accompanied by high oxidative stress and a higher potential for NFκB pathway upregulation with pro‐inflammatory states, necessitating coordinated chaperone upregulation. At the same time, coordinated reciprocal activity of DMH–rMR output needed to silence WPn activity during sleep may depend on precise diurnal dynamics in pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory pathways that determine PGE2 levels. These phase‐dependent interactions between multiple T‐dependent pathways suggest that misalignment of Tbr cues with the sleep–wake cycle will have additive pathological effects. Hypotheses: Diurnal Tb rhythms will functionally entrain activity in key thermosensitive and thermoregulatory nodes and downstream intracellular pathways within interacting WPn, SPn, and supporting astrocytes in the directions predicted by reviewed evidence. Key nodes include p‐tau, HSF1, RBM3, CIRBP, and AQP4. Pathological manipulations will lead to aberrant thermal entrainment, with lower waking Tb increasing tau phosphorylation, reducing HSF1 activation and chaperone expression, and reducing SPn activity upon sleep onset. Higher Tb during sleep will reduce AQP4 localization in local astrocytes and tau glymphatic clearance, and cold‐sensitive RBM3 and CIRBP expression. Pro‐inflammatory states will exacerbate this pattern by raising PGE2 levels, further raising nocturnal Tb, and via inappropriate WPn activity during sleep, increasing metabolic stress and proteotoxic interactions in these neurons. Impairment in local astrocyte function needed for WPn metabolic support 272 , 273 will further impair WPn function. Methodological approaches: In vitro and in vivo experiments could examine whether simulated T rhythms functionally entrain target nodes in WPn, SPn, and neighboring astrocytes. In vivo experiments would employ continuous Tb, Tbr, and EEG recordings during naturalistic sleep–wake activity, and test for thermal entrainment by examining correlations between Tb and target pathway outputs. Causality could be tested with pathological cell‐specific genetic manipulations, together with Tb manipulations (hypo/hyperthermia, stress, sleep deprivation). In vivo human experiments could test for correlations between Tb and target node biomarkers in serially sampled CSF or plasma, together with imaging techniques that capture NSS tau pathology, 276 and test effects of clinical interventions. Clinicopathological approaches in human 277 may relate antemortem Tb patterns to pathological alterations in WPn and functionally associated glia. Hypothesized pathological Tb patterns (loss of amplitude, sleep–wake misalignment, loss of phase coherence across T‐sensitive targets) could be related to pathological outcomes. Bidirectional interactions may be inferred if Tb features predict non‐linear disease progression in longitudinal studies.

How have these types of hypotheses and methods been applied in clinical research to date? Few studies have tested how thermoregulatory interactions relate to clinical outcomes in human; the first studies examining Tb–AD biomarker relationships were published in the last 5 years. 121 , 128 More studies of this type are needed, together with basic research examining how tau or Aβ aggregation in specific NSS neuronal populations affects Tb 176 Future directions with the highest potential to impact clinical outcomes are suggested below.

Female sex is a major risk factor for AD, and altered thermoregulation, a sexually dimorphic function, may contribute to this sex‐specific risk. Testing this hypothesis will require Tb studies in women with AD, which this review shows are lacking. Using sex‐specific analyses, it will be important to determine how sex differences in diurnal Tb rhythm differentially interact with T‐sensitive AD pathways. A speculative but testable hypothesis is that higher waking Tb and greater diurnal Tb variation in females compared to males implies a greater role of thermal entrainment in females, and that thermoregulatory changes following menopause will accelerate risk accordingly. Preoptic neuron circuit interactions (Section 4) suggest that estrogen loss and pro‐inflammatory states may lead to impaired nocturnal Tb drop, and that APOE ε4 carriage may compound this effect, partially explaining the greater risk conferred by this gene in women. 278 , 279 Across both sexes, older adults are disproportionately vulnerable to thermal stress imposed by climate change given that thermoregulatory capacity declines with age. Better understanding whether T regulation in the home reduces AD risk could establish thermal environment as a target for public health intervention.

Clinical trials evaluating whether lifestyle or pharmacological strategies mitigate against NSS thermoregulatory neuron vulnerability may benefit from a right T and the right time model. For example, for sauna, which was associated with lower dementia and AD risk, 280 timing relative to sleep would likely affect its efficacy. Many neuropsychiatric drugs used in AD and aging, including antipsychotics, 281 antidepressants, 282 cannabinoid therapeutics, 283 anesthetics, 252 and sedatives, 284 affect Tb in rodents and/or humans via NSS circuit actions; menopause hormone therapy may also affect Tb given estrogen and progesterone's effects in thermoregulatory circuits in animal models. 54 , 285 The thermoregulatory actions of these drugs may contribute to their therapeutic effects. 282 Knowledge of their hypothermic or hyperthermic effects may be used to optimize dosing time to minimize Tb rhythm disturbance. Finally, in developing accessible biomarkers of early NSS tau pathology, it is essential to understand sources of variability. Diurnal Tb rhythm accounts for variability in the levels of core plasma and CSF AD biomarkers, 286 so measuring Tb may improve their diagnostic utility.

CONCLUSIONS

Thermoregulation remains an underexamined axis of AD pathophysiology, and the evidence reviewed here suggests that this is an omission rather than an absence of signal. Body and brain temperature may be altered by tau pathology and capable of accelerating it, placing it among the few risk‐relevant variables that are measurable and modifiable. Further research should include characterizing sex differences, defining the thermoregulatory roles of AD risk pathways within NSS neurons, reconciling rodent and human findings, and identifying targets for intervention in early disease.
