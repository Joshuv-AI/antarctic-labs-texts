Retinal pigment epithelium-derived extracellular vesicles mediate outer blood retinal barrier disruption in response to AMD-related stress
Authors: Beatriz Martins, Raquel Boia, Diana Correia, Teresa Ribeiro-Rodrigues, José Ramalho, António Francisco Ambrósio, Henrique Girão, Rosa Fernandes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAge-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of vision loss among the elderly, primarily affecting the central vision. This progressive degenerative disease is characterized by the dysregulation and degeneration of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), a crucial cell layer beneath the photoreceptors that maintains outer retinal homeostasis. Emerging evidence suggests that during AMD, stressed RPE cells release extracellular vesicles (EVs) carrying bioactive cargo, which may compromise the outer blood-retinal barrier (oBRB) and accelerate disease progression. This study explores the role of EVs released by RPE cells under pro-inflammatory conditions in disrupting retinal integrity.MethodsHighly polarized primary cultures of porcine RPE (pRPE) and porcine eyecups with the RPE exposed were treated with tumor necrosis factor (TNF), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or EVs derived from inflamed RPE cells. Additionally, Balb/c mice were intravitreally injected with RPE-derived EVs.ResultsWe show that EVs secreted by the apical membrane domain of porcine RPE cells exposed to LPS or TNF impair the RPE monolayer in polarized cultures, disrupt the oBRB in ex vivo porcine eyecups, and induce retinal structural damage detected in vivo in Balb/c mice. Intravitreal injection of LPS-derived EVs triggers photoreceptor and RPE layers thinning, increases reactivity in astrocytes and Müller cells, promotes pro-inflammatory microglial activation and recruitment, particularly into the outer retina, and elevates retinal apoptosis. Mechanistically, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) activity mediates EV-induced RPE monolayer disruption, whereas MMPs activity inhibition mitigates these effects.ConclusionOur findings reveal a novel EV-driven mechanism contributing to retinal degeneration progression, highlighting inflammation-derived apical EVs as key players in diseases involving oBRB dysfunction. Targeting EV-mediated signaling and MMPs activity may offer therapeutic strategies for preserving retinal structure and function in inflammatory retinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02587-0.Keywords: Age-related macular degeneration, Retinal pigment epithelial cells, Extracellular vesicles, Outer blood-retinal barrier, Metalloproteinases, Neuroinflammation, Neurodegeneration

Background

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a progressive degenerative disease that predominantly affects the macula, the central region of the retina responsible for high-acuity vision. AMD is the leading cause of vision impairment and blindness in individuals over 60 years of age in developed countries [1]. The disease begins with an early stage characterized by pigmentary alterations and the accumulation of subretinal deposits called drusen. From this stage, the disease may progress to one of to two advanced forms: (a) the non-exudative type, commonly referred to as dry AMD, which can ultimately lead to geographic atrophy (GA), an advanced subset involving degeneration of retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and photoreceptors; (b) the exudative type, also known as wet AMD, characterized by choroidal neovascularization (CNV) and faster severe vision loss [2]. The course of disease progression varies among individuals, due to genetic and environmental factors, and possibly the crosstalk between them, adding to the complexity of AMD. Despite its clinical significance and many research studies, the molecular mechanisms driving AMD pathogenesis remain incompletely understood.

Accumulating evidence suggests that AMD arises from the complex interplay of pro-inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and dysregulation of autophagy and the immunovascular axis. At the center of these processes lies the RPE, a monolayer of polarized epithelial cells that supports photoreceptor function and maintains retinal homeostasis. The RPE plays essential roles, including photoreceptor outer segments (POS) phagocytosis and visual cycle regulation. Moreover, the RPE is the key component of the outer blood-retinal barrier (oBRB), in which tight junctions (TJ) between adjacent RPE cells have a major role in barrier maintenance [3, 4]. Dysfunction of the RPE compromises these functions, initiating events that contribute to AMD progression.

One emerging mechanism involves extracellular vesicles (EVs), double-membrane vesicles secreted by nearly all cell types, including the RPE [5, 6]. EVs play a central role in intercellular communication by transferring bioactive cargo, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Polarized cells like RPE secrete EVs directionally with distinct compositions [7, 8]. Oxidative stress and inflammation significantly impact on EVs composition and secretion, which can underly pathological processes in AMD. Notably, RPE-derived EVs have been implicated in autophagy dysregulation, drusen formation, and neovascularization, highlighting their dual roles in retinal health and disease [9–11].

Recent studies have provided further evidence for the role of RPE-derived EVs in AMD. Flores-Bellver et al. demonstrated that apically secreted RPE-derived EVs play a crucial role in drusen formation under AMD-relevant stressors [12]. Similarly, Kurzawa-Akanbi et al. showed that EVs secreted to the apical side of RPE cells contribute to AMD-like characteristics in neighboring retinal cells [13].

In addition to EVs, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases, have been implicated in AMD pathology. MMPs modulate extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, tissue homeostasis, and angiogenesis, but their dysregulation can exacerbate retinal damage [14]. MMPs contribute to Bruch’s membrane degradation, a key event in drusen formation [15], facilitate CNV by breakdown the ECM surrounding choroidal vessels, and promote inflammation-driven retinal degeneration [16, 17]. Importantly, MMPs have been identified in RPE-derived EVs, suggesting a mechanistic link between EVs and MMP-driven retinal pathology. By transporting MMPs and other ECM-degrading enzymes into the extracellular space, RPE-derived EVs may enhance ECM remodeling, promote choroidal endothelial cell invasion, and accelerate AMD progression [10, 18, 19].

In this study, we uncover how EVs secreted from RPE cells under inflammatory conditions mediate oBRB disruption and contribute to AMD progression. Using primary porcine RPE cultures and native RPE in porcine eyecups, we isolated and characterized the apical and basolateral EVs populations secreted under the inflammatory stimuli, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS). We further evaluated MMP activity in EVs and the in vitro effects of RPE-derived EVs on monolayer integrity, assessing whether the MMP inhibitor GM6001 could mitigate oBRB disruption. Finally, we investigated whether apical RPE-derived EVs could induce hallmark features in an animal model of dry AMD, including oBRB disruption, neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.

Methods

Polarized porcine retinal pigment epithelial cell cultures

Porcine RPE cells (pRPE) were isolated from the posterior eyecups of freshly harvested porcine eyes obtained from a local slaughterhouse. The eyes were transported to the laboratory in a solution of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with antibiotics and processed within 3 h post-enucleation. Primary RPE cultures were established from 24 eyes. After removing excess surrounding conjunctive tissue, the eyes were decontaminated in a 10% povidone-iodine solution for 30 min. After rinsing with sterile PBS until no visible residue of povidone iodine remained, an incision with a scalpel at the ora serrata was performed and the anterior segment, lens, and vitreous were removed from the eye under sterile conditions, and the resulting eyecups were filled with PBS. The neuroretina was carefully peeled from the underlying pigment epithelium, and PBS was replaced with pre-warmed TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Gibco, USA). The eyecups were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate dissociation. After incubation, RPE cells were gently detached by pipetting and resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium: Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12 GlutaMAX™ supplement; Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 1% sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% nonessential amino acids (Gibco, USA), and 1% Anti-Anti (Gibco, USA) (complete cell culture medium). The cells were then centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 min. Pooled RPE cells from 24 eyes were seeded into six-well plates containing the complete cell culture medium, which was replaced twice weekly until cells reached confluency. Confluent pRPE cells were trypsinized and reseeded onto laminin-coated (BioLamina, Sweden) semi-permeable Transwell® inserts (0.4 μm pore size, Corning) at a density of 250,000 cells/cm2 in DMEM/F-12 GlutaMAX™ with 1% FBS. Cells achieved 100% confluence immediately and were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Porcine RPE tissue in situ

Porcine eyecups were prepared as described above. After the removal of the neuroretina, PBS was replaced with cell culture medium supplemented with 1% FBS. The eyecups were treated according to the experimental protocol and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

pRPE cells and RPE tissue treatments

At the beginning of the experiments, the RPE cell culture medium was switched to 1% EVs-depleted FBS (Exofree) to minimize the influence of exogenous bovine EVs. To study the role of inflammation in the RPE monolayer disruption, primary pRPE cultures were treated with TNF (10 ng/mL; Peprotech, USA) or with LPS (from Escherichia coli; 100 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), applied to both the apical and basolateral compartments of the inserts. Porcine eyecups were similarly exposed to these stimuli for 30 min, 5 h, 12 h, or 24 h, depending on the type of experiments performed.

To assess the effect of RPE-derived EVs on RPE monolayer disruption, isolated EVs from the RPE eyecups or pRPE cultures exposed to TNF (EVs TNF) or LPS (EVs LPS) for 5–24 h, respectively, at a 1:1 ratio, where the number of EV-producing cells matched the number of recipient cells. To determine the role of MMPs-containing EVs to the RPE monolayer disruption, the non-selective MMP inhibitor GM6001 (20 µM; Abcam, UK) was added to the porcine eyecups at the same time as the RPE-derived EVs and incubated at 37 °C for 5 h.

Assessment of metabolic activity by MTT

Cellular metabolic activity was evaluated using the 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]−2,5-diphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. pRPE cells were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to TNF or LPS for 5 h. Following the treatment, 5 mg/mL of MTT (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to allow the reduction of MTT to formazan. The resulting formazan crystals, indicative of metabolic activity, were solubilized, and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy HT; Biotek, USA). Metabolic activity was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control cells.

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) of pRPE monolayers cultured on Transwell inserts was measured using the STX-2 electrode coupled to an EVOM resistance meter (World Precision Instruments, UK). Prior to measurements, the electrodes were sterilized with 70% ethanol, rinsed with sterile deionized water, and equilibrated in cell culture medium. During measurements, the longer electrode tip was immersed into the basolateral compartment (lower chamber) and the shorter tip into the apical compartment (upper chamber). All TEER readings were registered within 5 min of removing the plate from the incubator to minimize temperature-related variability. TEER values were calculated by subtracting the resistance of cell-free laminin-coated Transwell inserts from each experimental measurement. The net resistance values were then normalized by multiplying the surface area of the Transwell insert (Ω x cm2). Monolayers were considered stable when TEER values consistently exceeded 150 Ω x cm2.

Extracellular vesicles isolation

Conditioned media from pRPE cultures or porcine eyecups were collected for EVs isolation using the Total Exosome Isolation Reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, conditioned medium was collected and centrifuged at 2,000 x g for 30 min to remove cells and cell debris. The resulting supernatant was mixed with the isolation reagent at a 2:1 ratio and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 1 h at 4 °C. The EV pellet was resuspended in 50 µL of filtered PBS and used for Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA), zymography, Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and cell treatments. For Western blot analysis, EVs were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer [150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 5 mM ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (EGTA), 1% Triton X100 (Tx-100), 0.5% sodium deoxycholate (DOC), 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 2 mM iodoacetamide (IAD), 30 mM NaF, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, USA)]. The samples were then denatured with non-reducing sample buffer (4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 125 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 0.01% bromophenol blue) before Western Blotting.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

NTA was performed to quantify and characterize the EVs. Samples were resuspended in 1 mL of filtered PBS and analyzed using a NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Panalytical Limited; Malvern, UK), as previously described [20]. Instrument settings were optimized and kept constant across all samples. NTA data, including particle concentration, mean, and mode size, were processed using NTA 2.2 software, and results were presented as a frequency size distribution graph, describing the number of particles per milliliter.

Animal studies

All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the European Community Council Directive (2010/63/EU) and Portuguese legislation on the care and use of experimental animals (DL nº 113/2013 of August 7 and DL nº 1/2019 of January 10). The study was approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of the Coimbra Institute for Clinical and Biomedical Research (iCBR), Faculty of Medicine, University of Coimbra (ORBEA 10/2018), and by the Direção Geral de Alimentação e Veterinária (DGAV) under approval number 0421/000/000/2020. The experiments were also in agreement with the guidelines of the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Male and female Balb/c mice, 2-month-old, were housed in ventilated cages, with controlled temperature/humidity (22 °C/60%) environment on a 12 h light- dark cycle, with free access to water and standard rodent chow.

Intravitreal injection

Intravitreal injection was selected to deliver EVs toward the apical surface of the RPE, reproducing the physiological orientation by which apically released EVs would interact with the RPE in vivo. This administration route also provided a reproducible and minimally invasive alternative to subretinal delivery. For intravitreal injections, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane (IsoFlo; Abbott Laboratories, USA) in 1 L/min O2. Topical anesthesia (oxybuprocaine hydrochloride, 4 mg/ml, Anestocil; Edol, Portugal) was also applied to the ocular surface. Pupils were dilated with tropicamide (10 mg/ml, Tropicil Top; Edol, Portugal). EVs isolated from conditioned medium of porcine eyecups without treatment (EVs CTL) or treated with LPS for 30 min (EVs LPS) were suspended in 100 µL of PBS. In the right eye, 1 µL of the EVs suspension (1 × 1010 particles/mL) or PBS was injected in the vitreous using a NanoFil syringe with a 36G needle. The positioning of the needle in the vitreous was confirmed using a stereo microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Non-injected animals were defined as the control group (CTL). The left eye was untouched and served as a control (EVs CTL contralateral and EVs LPS contralateral). Retinal evaluation was performed 7 days post-injection.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) and eye fundus examination

Eye fundus and retinal structure were assessed by OCT using a Micron IV retinal imaging system (Phoenix Research Labs, USA). Animals were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (75 mg/kg; Nimatek; Dechra, UK) and medetomidine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg; Sedator; Dechra, UK). The pupil was dilated as described above, and topical carmellose sodium (Celluvisc, 1.0%; Allergan, Irland) was instilled in the eye every few min to prevent ocular dehydration during anesthesia. OCT and fundus photography were conducted simultaneously to capture detailed retinal information. Both eyes were imaged, and B-scans centered in the optic nerve head were acquired. For quantitative analysis, 4–7 B-scans were selected per eye. The different retinal layers were segmented as follows: NFL + GCL + IPL = nerve fiber layer + ganglion cell layer + inner plexiform layer; INL = inner nuclear layer (nuclei of bipolar, amacrine, horizontal and Müller cells); ONL = outer nuclear layer (nuclei of photoreceptors); IS/OS + RPE = photoreceptor inner and outer segments + retinal pigment epithelium. The thickness of different retinal layers was obtained after segmentation using the semi-automatic segmentation software InSight (Phoenix Research Labs, USA).

Immunofluorescence analysis

For immunofluorescence analysis in retinal cryosections, animals were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a solution of ketamine and medetomidine hydrochloride, followed by transcardial perfusion with PBS and 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA). Eyes were enucleated and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h. Then, the corneas were carefully removed, and the eyecups were further fixed for 1 h in 4% PFA. After washing with PBS, tissues were cryopreserved in 15% sucrose in PBS for 1 h, followed by 30% sucrose in PBS for another h. Eyecups were then embedded in a 1:1 mixture of optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T., Shandon Cryomatrix; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) compound with 30% sucrose, and stored at − 80 °C. The tissue was sectioned on a cryostat (CryoStar NX50; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) into 14 μm thickness sections, mounted on Superfrost Plus glass slides, and stored at −80 °C until use (Menzel-Gläser; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Before staining, retinal sections were dried at room temperature at least 45 min and fixed in cold acetone for 10 min. Sections were then washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 40 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (Supplementary Table 1). After rinsing with PBS, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies, and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 2.5 µg/mL; Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, USA) for nuclear staining for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were mounted using Glycergel™ mounting medium (Dako, Agilent Technologies, USA) and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Observer.Z1 microscope equipped with an AxioCam HRm and Zen Blue 2012 software (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with a 10 x objective (EC Plan-Neofluar 10x/0.3)).

For arrestin, rhodopsin, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and vimentin, the results are expressed as the mean fluorescence intensity (arbitrary units) and were determined in the entire retinal section normalized to the respective length by applying the following formula: corrected total fluorescence = (integrated intensity) - (area of picture x mean background). The arrestin+ and Iba-1+ cells were counted across the entire retinal section and normalized to the respective section length. The percentage of activated microglia (Iba-1+ and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II)+) per total microglia (Iba-1+) was also normalized to the section length. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.47, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij, provided in the public domain by the National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

For immunofluorescence analysis in pRPE, the monolayers cultured on Transwell inserts were fixed in either pure methanol or 2% PFA for 30 min at 4 °C. Polycarbonate membranes were carefully removed from the insert cups and cut into smaller sections for immunostaining. The pRPE cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 30 min, followed by blocking with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 4% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies, diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA (refer to Supplementary Table 1 for antibody details).

Regarding porcine eyecups, after exposure to the inflammatory stimuli the eyecup was fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min at room temperature. After 3 washes with PBS, the eyecups were radially cut into 6 to 8 pieces under a dissection microscope, and the sclera was carefully removed to isolate the RPE-choroid complex, as previously described [21]. The complex was permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.1% Tween 20 for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C.

After rinsing with PBS, the pRPE cells were incubated with the secondary fluorescent antibody (AlexaFluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody or AlexaFluor568-conjugated anti-mouse antibody) or TRITC-conjugated phalloidin and stained DAPI at room temperature, for 90 min. The RPE-choroid complexes were incubated under the same conditions for 3 h. After rinsed with PBS, pRPE cells on the membrane inserts and RPE-choroid complex were mounted upside down on glass slides and visualized under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM710 laser-scanning confocal (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope body).

For wholemount immunostaining of mice retinas, the animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine and medetomidine hydrochloride and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Eyes were enucleated, and immunostaining was performed on retinal wholemounts and RPE-choroid flat mounts. Retina wholemounts were prepared following a previously described protocol from our group [22], with minor modifications. Retinas were isolated and fixed in 2% PFA for 10 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the tissue was permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (in PBS) for 10 min and blocked in PBS containing 5% BSA for 1 h. Primary antibodies were applied, and the samples were incubated for 2 days at 4 °C (Supplementary Table 1). After PBS washes, retinas were incubated with secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) and DAPI (2.5 µg/mL; Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, USA) for 24 h at 4 °C. Following additional washed with PBS, the retinas were mounted with the vitreous side up for confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM710 laser-scanning confocal (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope body).

The RPE-choroid complex from mice was fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min, washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with a PBS solution containing 1% BSA and 3% goat serum in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) diluted in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. The RPE-choroid complex was then incubated with secondary antibodies and DAPI (2.5 µg/mL; Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the RPE monolayer was mounted faced-up using Glycergel™ mounting medium (Dako, Agilent Technologies, USA) for visualization under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM710 laser-scanning confocal (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope body). The 3D-reconstructed images of EVs-RPE interaction were generated using IMARIS software x64 (version 9.3.1; Bitplane AG) with confocal z-stacks processed for visualization through volume rendering and surface reconstruction. Quantitative assessment of RPE barrier integrity was performed on ZO-1 immunofluorescence images obtained from the RPE-choroid complexes of porcine eyecups and mouse eyes, following the method described previously [23]. Briefly, images were preprocessed and skeletonized using ImageJ (NIH), and the AnalyzeSkeleton plugin was used to quantify: (i) the number of junctions, representing branching points where three or more TJ strands intersect - indicative of overall network connectivity; and (ii) the number of endpoints, corresponding to free strand termini that reflect discontinuities or breaks within the TJ network. The total area of each analyzed image was measured, and both parameters were normalized to the area (junctions/area and endpoints/area) to allow for consistent comparison between samples.

Morphometric analysis of microglia

Morphological alterations in microglia from retina wholemounts were accessed from 3D reconstruction images acquired in a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM710 laser-scanning confocal (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope body). Images were acquired using the following laser lines: diode 405 nm (Diode 405 − 30, W − 405 nm, MP − 30 mW) and argon/2 488 nm (Argon/2, W − 458, 488, 514 nm, MP − 25 mW). Z-stacks were acquired by using an interval of 1.07 nm. Imaging settings were conserved within all conditions. Iba-1+ cells were evaluated from four eyes per group, in total of 23–32 cells analyzed per condition. Microglia reconstruction was performed using Simple Neurite Tracer open-source software from Image J (https://imagej.net/plugins/snt/step-by-step-instructions) as previously described [24]. The morphologic parameters analyzed were total number of processes, total length, maximum intersection radius and Scholl analysis. The 3D-reconstructed images of microglia were generated using IMARIS software x64 (version 9.3.1; Bitplane AG) with confocal z-stacks processed for visualization through volume rendering and surface reconstruction.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP Nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Apoptotic cells in retinal cryosections were detected using the DeadEnd™ Fluorometric TUNEL System following the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, USA). Nuclei in the 10 μm retinal sections were stained with DAPI (2.5 µg/mL; Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, USA) and mounted with Glycergel™ mounting medium (Dako, Agilent Technologies, USA). Images from four sections per eye were acquired in a fluorescent microscope (Zeiss Axio Observer.Z1 microscope equipped with an AxioCam HRm and Zen Blue 2012 software (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with a 10 x objective (EC Plan-Neofluar 10x/0.3)). The total number of TUNEL+ cells was normalized to the length of each section per image.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis of pRPE cells or isolated EVs was performed as previously described [25]. Briefly, both sample types were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 1 h. Following this, lysates were sonicated and pRPE extracts were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA colorimetric assay (Pierce Biotechnology, USA). pRPE protein extracts were denatured with Laemmli buffer. For SDS-PAGE analysis, 60 µg of pRPE protein extracts or total EVs protein extracts were loaded onto polyacrylamide gels. Proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) AmershamTM HybondTM membranes (GE Healthcare, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk in TBS-T (20mM Tris, 150mM NaCl, 0.2% Tween, pH 7.6) and then incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse, anti-rabbit or anti-goat; 1:10,000; Bio-Rad, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate using an imaging system (LAS500, GE Health Life Sciences, USA). Quantitative analysis of band intensity was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.47, http://imagej.nih.gov/ij, provided in the public domain by the National Institute of Health, USA).

Zymography

Gelatin zymography was performed to assess MMP-2 and MMP-9 enzymatic activity in conditioned media and isolated EVs. Samples were denatured in non-reducing SDS-sample buffer at room temperature and then resolved on a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel containing 1 mg/mL gelatin. Equal volumes of sample (30 µL) were loaded into each lane. After electrophoresis, gels were washed twice in washing buffer (2.5% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 μm ZnCl2) for 30 min and then incubated overnight at 37 °C in developing buffer (1% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 μm ZnCl2). Gels were stained with 0.5% Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 for 45 min and then destained. Gelatinolytic activity appeared as clear bands against the blue background. Densitometric analysis was performed using Image J software.

Extracellular vesicles labeling

EVs isolated from porcine eyecups were labeled using the PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Mini Kit for General Cell Membrane Labeling (MINI67-1KT; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 µL of EVs suspension was incubated with 0.4 µL of PKH67 dye, diluted in 50 µL of diluent C, for 5 min. To remove unbound dye, the EVs were purified using Exosome Spin Columns (MW3000, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Porcine eyecups were incubated with PKH67-labeled EVs for 30 min, 1 h, or 2 h before fixation with 4% PFA for 20 min. Immunostaining with TRITC-conjugated phalloidin was performed as previously described. EVs interaction with RPE cells was visualized under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM710 laser-scanning confocal (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope body), capturing Z-stack images. Surface rendering of Z-stack images was performed using IMARIS Software x64 (version 9.3.1; Bitplane, Oxford Instruments, UK).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

EVs samples for TEM analysis were resuspended in PBS, then fixed with 2% PFA, and placed on Formvar-carbon coated grids (TAAB Laboratories Equipment, UK). Following washing with PBS, EVs were further fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde for 5 min. The grids were then rinsed with ddH2O, contrasted with uranyl-oxalate solution (pH 7.0) for 5 min, and transferred to methyl-cellulose-uranyl acetate for 10 min on ice, as previously described [20].

For pRPE cells grown on Transwell inserts, the samples were first washed with PBS and then fixed in a solution of 3% glutaraldehyde and 4% formaldehyde in 0.1 M PIPES (pH 7.2) for 1 h. After two washes with 0.1 M PIPES, the samples were fixed in 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M PIPES for 1 h. After additional washes with 0.1 M PIPES and ddH2O, the samples were contrasted with 2% (w/v) aqueous uranyl acetate for 20 min and dehydrated in a series of ethanol gradients (30% − 100%). After dehydration, the sampled were washed with acetonitrile for 10 min and incubated overnight in a 1:1 mixture of acetonitrile and Spurr resin. On the following day, the inserts were incubated in pure Spurr resin for 6 h before polymerization at 60 °C for 24 h. Ultrathin sections were cut and mounted on copper grids for observation under a TECNAI G2 Spirit BioTWIN electron microscope (FEI Company, USA) at 100 kV.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Porcine eyecups were cut in small pieces and placed on adhesive carbon substrates (12 mm, Agar Scientific, UK). The apical surface of the RPE was visualized using a variable-pressure scanning electron microscope (FlexSEM 1000, Hitachi, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.00, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. For multiple group comparisons (three or more independent variables), normal distribution of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (p < 0.05). For normal distribution, statistical significance between groups was carried out using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as indicated in figure legends, followed by post-hoc analysis (Tukey test). For data with non-Gaussian distribution, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was perfomed, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Differences between groups were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Inflammatory stress triggered by TNF or LPS induces the disruption of RPE monolayer in polarized primary cultures and porcine eyecups

To investigate the inflammatory stress-induced disruption of the RPE monolayer, we established polarized RPE cultures from porcine eyes (pRPE). RPE cells were cultured on transwell inserts until they formed stable and polarized monolayers (Fig. 1a). TEM confirmed the presence of apical microvilli, a hallmark of RPE polarity (Fig. 1b). These cultures also exhibited pigmentation, and immunofluorescence staining revealed well-defined TJ complexes, as indicated by the localization of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-19 at the cell membrane. Furthermore, RPE-specific markers such as RPE65 were also detected (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

The establishment of barrier properties was monitored weekly by TEER measurements that progressively increased over 30 days, stabilizing at values exceeding 300 Ohm/cm2 (Fig. 1c), confirming the formation of an intact and functional RPE monolayer.

To mimic the inflammatory environment characteristic of AMD, we exposed polarized pRPE monolayers and porcine eyecups to TNF (10 ng/mL) or LPS (100 ng/mL) for 5 h. Both stimuli did not compromise cell viability at these concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 1b), but they significantly disrupted barrier function, as indicated by a TEER decrease for both TNF and LPS (Fig. 1d). Notably, TNF-induced barrier loss occurred more rapidly than with LPS.

Immunofluorescence staining revealed that both TNF and LPS disrupted TJ complexes. ZO-1 and occludin exhibited decreased immunoreactivity at the cell membrane after 5 h exposure (Fig. 1e), accompanied by a significant reduction in occludin levels following LPS incubation (Fig. 1g). Not surprisingly, TNF induced an increase of TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) levels, implicating this receptor in TNF-mediated responses (Fig. 1h).

Inflammatory stress also disrupted RPE cell polarity, as evidenced by altered distribution of Na+/K+-ATPase and bestrophin, proteins typically localized at the apical and basolateral membranes of RPE cells, respectively. Immunostaining demonstrated decreased membrane localization of both markers following TNF and LPS incubation (Fig. 1f). LPS treatment significantly reduced bestrophin levels, with TNF showing a similar trend (Fig. 1i).

To complement our data, we utilized an in situ model, porcine eyecups, in which the neuroretina was removed to expose the apical surface of the RPE (Fig. 2a). Similar to the cultured cells, the porcine eyecups demonstrated well-defined TJ complexes and cytosolic localization of RPE65 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The effects observed in primary RPE cultures were mirrored in the porcine eyecup model. Exposure to TNF and LPS led to decreased ZO-1 and occludin immunoreactivity, along with disrupted localization of Na+/K+-ATPase and bestrophin (Fig. 2b). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis further revealed that control RPE cells displayed hexagonal morphology with abundant apical microvilli, whereas TNF and LPS treatment caused a loss of RPE cell border integrity and detachment of cellular fragments (Fig. 2c), consistent with RPE monolayer disruption.

Inflammatory challenges alter the release and composition of EVs from RPE cells

To investigate the effect of inflammatory stress on EVs release, polarized pRPE cells were cultured on permeable supports to maintain appropriate apical or basolateral secretion. EVs isolated from the medium of either compartments were characterized for size, concentration, and specific protein markers.

In control conditions, EVs secreted by primary pRPE cultures displayed a characteristic round-shaped morphology (Fig. 3a), and expressed CD63, a classical marker of small EVs (Fig. 3b). However, following a 5-h exposure to TNF and LPS, there was a significant decrease in EVs secretion from the apical compartment to the medium, accompanied by a reduction in the mean and mode of vesicles size (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, no significant alterations were observed in the basolateral compartment in either EVs concentration or size (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table 3), suggesting that apical EVs release is more sensitive to inflammatory conditions.

We extended these findings using the porcine eyecup model, where only apical EVs secretion can be evaluated. Under control conditions, TEM analysis revealed EVs with spherical morphology (Fig. 3e) and the presence of canonical markers of EVs, namely CD63, CD81 and TSG101 (Fig. 3g). Following 30 min of TNF and LPS incubation, a significant decrease in EVs concentration was observed, although no changes were detected in the mean and modal size of the EVs (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Table 4).

In addition to the reduced secretion, inflammatory stimuli altered the protein composition of EVs from porcine eyecups. Specifically, TNF and LPS exposure led to increased levels of CD63, CD81, and TSG-101, which are proteins involved in EVs biogenesis (Fig. 3g, h). Furthermore, the inflammatory challenge also significantly increased MMP-2 levels in EVs, highlighting changes in vesicle content under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 3g, i).

Previous studies have reported increased EVs secretion under pro-inflammatory conditions [26–28], contrasting with our findings at early time points. To address this discrepancy, we analyzed EVs release dynamics at 5, 12, and 24 h after starting incubation with TNF or LPS, in the porcine eyecups. While EVs secretion initially decreased at 30 min, a time-dependent increase in EVs concentration was observed at later time points, with a small decrease in the average size of EVs, but only 12 h after treatment with LPS (Supplementary Fig. 2a-c; Supplementary Tables 5–7). These results align with prior studies [29] and suggest that inflammatory stress affects EVs release in a biphasic manner. To investigate the role of apical EVs in the RPE monolayer disruption, we focused on the 30-minute time point, where significant changes in both the EVs release profiles and composition were already more evident.

Inflammatory stimuli increase MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity in conditioned medium and RPE-derived EVs

To evaluate the activity of free and EVs-associated MMPs, we performed gelatin zymography on conditioned medium and EVs derived from pRPE primary cultures and porcine eyecups. Both pro and active forms of MMP-2 and MMP-9 were quantified. In primary pRPE cultures, incubation with TNF and LPS for 5 h significantly increased the active forms of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in the conditioned medium of the RPE apical compartment (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, no changes in MMPs activity were observed in the basolateral conditioned medium (Fig. 4a, c). Interestingly, in RPE-derived EVs, TNF and LPS exposure increased the activity of the active form of MMP-2 in both the apical and basolateral compartments and the pro-form of MMP-9 in the basolateral compartment (Fig. 4d-f).

To further validate the effects of inflammatory stimuli, gelatin zymography was performed on conditioned medium and EVs from porcine eyecups after 30 min and 5 h of TNF or LPS exposure. In the conditioned medium, TNF induced a time-dependent increase in MMP-9 activity. After 30 min, an increase in the pro-form of MMP-9 activity was observed (Fig. 4g, h). By 5 h, both the pro and the active forms of MMP-9 increased significantly (Fig. 4g, i). No significant alterations in MMP-2 activity were detected at either time point in the conditioned medium following TNF or LPS incubation (Fig. 4g-i). In contrast, significant changes were observed in EVs-associated MMPs. After 30 min of TNF or LPS exposure, there was a significant increase in the pro-form activity of both MMP-2 and MMP-9 in EVs isolated from porcine eyecups (Fig. 4j, k). The differences in incubation times of the stimuli and the pro vs. active forms of MMPs may be the reason for the differences in MMPs activity profiles between primary pRPE cultures and porcine eyecups.

MMPs-containing EVs derived from the RPE under pro-inflammatory conditions induce RPE monolayer disruption

To determine whether EVs secreted under pro-inflammatory conditions contribute to the oBRB disruption, we exposed pRPE monolayers seeded on transwells to EVs derived from RPE subjected to inflammatory stimuli, TNF and LPS (Fig. 5a). Exposure of pRPE monolayers to apical-derived EVs from TNF- and LPS-treated cells significantly decreased the TEER (Fig. 5b). The increased permeability of the RPE monolayer at 12 and 24 h was accompanied by the disorganization of TJ complexes, evidenced by reduced immunoreactivity and altered localization of ZO-1 and occludin at the plasma membrane at 24 h (Fig. 5c). Interestingly, when pRPE cells were incubated with EVs secreted both apically and basolaterally, no further increase in permeability was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3). This suggests that apical-derived EVs strongly contribute to disrupting the integrity of the RPE monolayer, although a role for basolateral EVs cannot be excluded.

EVs derived from porcine eyecups exposed to inflammatory stimuli were tested using the in situ model (Fig. 5d). To confirm the ability of EVs to act in an autocrine manner, fluorescently labeled EVs derived from porcine eyecups (control or LPS-stimulated) were incubated in fresh eyecups for 30 min, 1 h, and 2 h. A time-dependent association of EVs with the RPE cells was observed, with higher affinity of RPE cells for EVs derived from LPS-stimulated eyecups, confirming their autocrine capability (Fig. 5e).

When porcine eyecups were incubated for 5 h with apical-derived EVs, isolated from the incubation medium 30 min after TNF or LPS exposure, a significant disruption of TJ complexes was detected. Immunostaining revealed a marked reduction in ZO-1 and occludin at the plasma membrane, along with their subcellular redistribution in RPE cells. This was accompanied by a reduction in Na+/K+-ATPase and bestrophin immunoreactivity, indicating a loss of RPE monolayer integrity and polarity (Fig. 5f).

To confirm the involvement of MMPs in RPE monolayer disruption induced by EVs secreted under pro-inflammatory conditions, porcine eyecups were incubated with TNF- and LPS-derived EVs for 5 h in the presence of the broad-spectrum MMP inhibitor GM6001. ZO-1 immunostaining was used to evaluate the effect of the MMP inhibition, as our initial observations identified ZO-1 as showing the most pronounced membrane alterations in adjacent RPE cells following EVs exposure. MMP inhibition with GM6001 effectively preserved RPE monolayer integrity. In contrast to the untreated condition, where TNF- and LPS-derived EVs caused TJ disorganization and induced a decreased in the number of junctions and an increase in the number of endpoints (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), GM6001-treated eyecups exhibited normal ZO-1 localization and immunoreactivity at the plasma membrane of RPE cells (Fig. 5g), accompanied by an increase in the number of junctions (Fig. 5h) and a decrease in the number of endpoints (Fig. 5i), which indicates improved TJ network organization. Overall, these results indicate that MMP activity within the EVs is a critical driver of TJ complex disruption and RPE monolayer breakdown.

Intravitreal injection of LPS-derived EVs induces alterations and cell death in the outer retina of Balb/c mice

To investigate the impact of inflammatory EVs on oBRB integrity in vivo, following our demonstration of EV-induced alterations in cultured RPE cells, we performed intravitreal injections of EVs into Balb/c mice. EVs were isolated from porcine eyecups treated with either vehicle (EVs CTL) or LPS-stimulated (EVs LPS), with only LPS-derived EVs used for in vivo studies, as they induced the most pronounced effects in both in vitro and ex vivo models. This selective approach also minimized animal usage in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Retinal structure was assessed by OCT imaging at 7 days post-injection, followed by immunofluorescence analysis of retinal cross-sections and RPE-choroid flat mounts (Fig. 6a). OCT analysis revealed structural changes in the retinas of animals injected with EVs derived from RPE exposed to LPS. These alterations included retinal layer thinning, particularly affecting the outer nuclear layer (ONL) and the inner segments/outer segments/RPE (IS/OS/RPE) layers. The inner nuclear layer (INL), however, exhibited increased thickness in EVS LPS-injected animals compared to control animals (Fig. 6b, c). Notably, control-derived EVs also caused a mild thinning of the IS/OS/RPE layer compared to non-injected animals, suggesting a baseline effect of EVs injection in the retina (Fig. 6b, c). No alterations in retinal thickness were detected in either the PBS-injected eyes or in the contralateral eyes of animals injected with EVs (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b).

The evaluation of the RPE monolayer integrity using RPE-choroid complex flat mounts revealed a significant disruption of TJ and adherens junctions in animals injected with EVs LPS. Immunofluorescence analysis showed a marked reduction and redistribution of the TJ protein ZO-1, along with a notable decrease in β-catenin, a marker of adherens junctions, in the EVs LPS-treated group (Fig. 6d). Quantitative skeleton analysis of ZO-1 immunofluorescence also demonstrated a marked reduction in the number of junctions accompanied by an increase in the number of endpoints, indicative of fragmentation and loss of continuity within the TJ network (Fig. 6e, f), These alterations suggest a structural disorganization of the RPE monolayer and underscore the role of LPS-derived EVs in compromising RPE junctional integrity.

To evaluate whether LPS-derived EVS could replicate key features of dry AMD, we focused on the changes in cells within the outer retina that are critical to disease progression. To assess the integrity of the photoreceptor layer, we quantified arrestin and rhodopsin immunoreactivity as markers of cone and rod photoreceptors, respectively. The EVs LPS group showed a significant reduction in arrestin immunoreactivity compared to both the control and EVs CTL groups (Fig. 6g; Supplementary Fig. 6a). Additionally, a decrease in the number of arrestin-positive cells was observed in the EVs LPS group (Fig. 6g, h), suggesting cone photoreceptor degeneration. Similarly, rhodopsin immunoreactivity was significantly reduced in animals treated with EVs LPS (Fig. 6g, i), also indicating damage to rod photoreceptors. No changes in arrestin and rhodopsin immunostaining were observed in the PBS-injected or contralateral eyes, confirming that the effects on photoreceptors were exclusively triggered by the EVs LPS (Supplementary Fig. 6b-e).

Additionally, we assessed retinal cell death in response to EVs treatment using TUNEL staining. A significant increase in the number of TUNEL-positive cells was observed in the EVs LPS-treated eyes (Fig. 6g, j), indicating a marked increased apoptosis following exposure to LPS-derived EVs. In contrast, no significant apoptosis was observed in PBS-injected or contralateral eyes (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g).

Intravitreal injection of LPS-derived EVs induces a pro-inflammatory profile in the retina of Balb/c mice

To evaluate the effects of EVs on retinal macroglial cell reactivity, specifically astrocytes and Müller cells, we assessed the immunoreactivity of GFAP and vimentin in retinal sections. EVs LPS treatment resulted in a significant upregulation of both GFAP and vimentin immunoreactivity throughout the retina (Fig. 7a-c), indicating a significant activation of both astrocytes and Müller cells in response to LPS-derived EVs. Conversely, no increased retinal macroglial reactivity was observed in eyes injected with PBS or CTL-derived EVs and no changes were observed in the contralateral eyes as well (Supplementary Fig. 7a-b).

Microglia are the primary resident immune cells in the retina and are typically absent from the outer retina, the main affected region in AMD. However, the migration and infiltration of microglia into the outer retina, particularly under conditions of advanced age and disease, have been implicated in the neuroinflammatory processes underlying AMD [30]. To assess the impact of EVs on microglial recruitment and activation, we immunostained retinal cryosections for Iba1, a marker of microglia, and quantified the distribution of Iba1 positive cells (Iba1+) across retinal layers. Animals injected with EVs derived from control eyecups (EVs CTL) exhibited a significant increase in Iba1+ cell numbers within the ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer and INL (IPL + INL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), and across the total retina compared to non-injected controls (Fig. 8a, b). In contrast, eyes injected with EVs LPS displayed a marked increase in Iba1+ cells within these layers, along with a significant accumulation in the ONL, indicating enhanced migration of microglia into the outer retina (Fig. 8a, b). Both the increased number of Iba1+ cells and the enhanced migration were clearly evident in the transversal view of retinal wholemounts, which were also stained for Iba1 (Fig. 8d; Supplementary Fig. 8 d). Furthermore, EVs LPS treatment led to notable reactivity of Iba1+ cells, as evidenced by the co-localization of Iba1 with MHC-II, a marker of immune activation (Fig. 8a, c). This activation phenotype was specific to the EVs LPS-treated group, suggesting that LPS-derived EVs not only promote microglial recruitment but also induce a pro-inflammatory state. Notably, no significant changes in Iba1+ cell distribution or activation were observed in the contralateral retinas or in the retinas of the PBS-injected group (Supplementary Fig. 8a-c).

Considering the significant accumulation of microglia in the outer retina and the finding that LPS-derived EVs not only promote microglial recruitment but also contribute to a pro-inflammatory state, we next evaluated the impact of EVs on microglial morphology (Fig. 8e). LPS derived-EVs not only induced a decrease in the total number of processes (Fig. 8f), but also induced alterations inthe number of processes according to the branch order. Microglia from EVs LPS-injected animals showed an increase in the number of processes in lower branch orders and a significant decrease for higher branch orders (Fig. 8g). Moreover, the total length of microglial cells and the length of processes in the different branch orders were significantly reduced in EVs LPS-injected animals compared to both control and EVs CTL-injected animals (Fig. 8h, i). These findings indicate that retina microglia in EVs LPS-injected animals are smaller and less ramified.

Additionally, Sholl analysis was performed to assess microglial arborization. Microglial cells from animals injected with EVs LPS presented an increased number of intersections closer to the nucleus, with a marked reduction in complexity as the distance from the nucleus increased (Fig. 8j). The maximum intersection radius, derived from Sholl analysis, also revealed a significant decrease in the last intersection of microglia in the EVs LPS-injected animals, suggesting that these microglial cells occupy a smaller area compared to both EVs CTL- injected and control animals (Fig. 8k). As with the other parameters assessed above, no alterations in microglial morphology were observed in the contralateral eyes of animals injected with either control- or LPS-derived EVs compared to non-injected animals (Supplementary Fig. 9a-g).

Discussion

The present study provides novel insights into the role of MMP-containing EVs derived from RPE cells to the oBRB disruption and their potential involvement in AMD pathogenesis. The RPE forms a stable and tight barrier that plays an instrumental role in maintaining the homeostasis of the outer layers of the retina, but also Bruch’s membrane, and choroid [31]. A key element in this regulation of homeostasis is the specialized cell-cell communication through EVs, which can have dual roles in retinal health and disease [32, 33].

We demonstrate that TNF- and LPS-induced inflammation disturbs the RPE monolayer integrity by altering TJ complexes and EV dynamics. We found a disruption of the RPE monolayer induced by TNF and LPS, together with the associated reduction in TEER, which was independent of changes in cell viability, thus further supporting existing findings on inflammation’s capacity to compromise RPE barrier function, a critical driver of AMD rather than inflammation-induced cell death as the underlying mechanism [34, 35].

Our results show that inflammation disrupted the oBRB integrity by altering apical EVs secretion and modulating EV-associated protein cargo landscape, including the enrichment and activation of MMP-2 and MMP-9, particularly within apical EVs. Importantly, EVs derived from inflamed RPE cells replicated the inflammatory effects of TNF and LPS, causing RPE monolayer disruption in an MMP-dependent manner. Furthermore, in vivo delivery of these EVs into BALB/c mice induced retinal damage, including oBRB disruption, photoreceptor loss, and glial activation, thereby underscoring the pathogenic potential of MMP-containing EVs in AMD-like retinal dysfunction.

The use of porcine RPE cells in culture and eyecups offered a physiologically relevant system for investigating the impact of EVs secretion on oBRB breakdown and retinal impairments. Porcine RPE closely resembles human RPE in structure and function, serving as a suitable model to enhance translational research [36]. Cultured pRPE cells, when maintained on laminin-coated Transwell inserts, exhibit RPE polarity, high TEER values (>300 Ω/cm2) and functional TJs, mimicking the oBRB´s physiological properties [37]. Additionally, porcine eyecups preserved RPE polarity and facilitated the study of EV release in a near-physiological environment. These models revealed a selective reduction in apical EVs secretion upon exposure to inflammatory conditions, consistent with the directional EV secretion observed in human RPE cells. Given the critical role of apical EVs in maintaining retinal homeostasis, this selective suppression aligns with previous reports highlighting the vulnerability of apical EV secretion to AMD-related stressors [12, 13].

Inflammatory stimuli not only reduced EVs secretion but also enriched EVs with biogenesis-related proteins, such as CD63, CD81 and TSG101, suggesting an enhancement of pathways related with EVs formation. Intriguingly, prolonged TNF and LPS exposure led to a biphasic EV secretion response: an initial suppression followed by a later increase, similar to what was previously described [26–29]. This dynamics pattern suggests a protective mechanism during early inflammation. However, the later increased secretion of EVs can potentially exacerbate the progression of retinal disease [6]. These results underscore how inflammation influences the secretion and cargo of RPE-derived EVs. In fact, when cultured pRPE cells and porcine eyecups were exposed to these two inflammatory stimuli for 5 h and 30 min, respectively, we detected a significant decrease in the release of EVs from the apical compartment of the RPE monolayer, without any alterations in the EV secretion profile in the basolateral domain. This result is particularly relevant in the context of the well-established directional secretion of EVs by RPE cells [8], with this directionality being susceptible to modulation in response to AMD-related stimuli. Since the apical EVs primarily interact with the outer retina, playing a pivotal role in retinal homeostasis, this selective decrease in apical EV release under inflammatory conditions aligns with prior reports demonstrating that apical EVs are more susceptible to AMD-related stress. Indeed, two recent studies have shown that EVs released by the RPE from the apical compartment exhibit differential modulation in response to AMD stressors, emphasizing the specialized function of apical EVs in the context of AMD pathogenesis [12, 13]. Such inflammation-induced susceptibility of apical EVs may reflect their critical role in modulating retinal homeostasis and their potential vulnerability under pathological conditions. Notably, EVs secreted under these conditions were enriched with MMPs, key proteolytic enzymes implicated in ECM remodeling and oBRB disruption, that are implicated in both dry and wet forms of the disease [14, 38]. Interestingly, our findings reveal that apical-derived EVs presented enhanced MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity. These findings suggest that MMP-containing EVs may represent a novel mechanism of RPE-mediated inflammatory signaling with pathological consequences for the outer retina.

Since our results show that inflammatory insults can alter not only the secretion profile but also the cargo of EVs, particularly leading to increased levels and activity of MMPs, this strengthens the hypothesis that these EVs can play a pathological role in oBRB disruption. Previous studies with different AMD inducers already demonstrated that EVs derived from stimulated RPE cells could replicate the AMD phenotype in RPE cells [12, 13]. Indeed, in our study we demonstrate that apical RPE-derived EVs secreted after inflammatory stimuli were able to induce RPE disruption in pRPE cells and porcine eyecups, replicating the inflammatory effects induced by TNF and LPS alone. This result demonstrated that these EVs can have an autocrine effect, targeting the RPE cells and inducing TJ complexes disruption, as well as alterations in the expression of RPE polarization-associated proteins. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that basolateral-derived EVs also contribute to RPE disruption, and this represents a limitation of our study. In the experiments performed, we tested the combination of apical and basolateral-derived EVs, and no additive effect on permeability was observed compared to apical EVs alone. While this suggests that apical-derived EVs may play a predominant role in disrupting RPE barrier integrity, the absence of an additive effect does not rule out contributions from basolateral EVs, which may act through distinct mechanisms not evaluated here. The apical localization of TJ, situated at the boundary between the apical and lateral membranes of RPE cells, likely contributes to the pronounced impact of apical EVs on junctional disorganization and barrier disruption [4], whereas basolateral EVs may be more relevant in modulating interactions with the choroid. Consistent with this, it has been recently reported that, under inflammatory and/or oxidative stress conditions, apical EVs secretion is more strongly affected than basolateral secretion, in line with our findings. Moreover, it has been shown that apical-derived EVs induce substantial alterations in RPE cells, while basolateral EVs from the same high-risk RPE cells produce minimal changes in most measured outcomes [13]. Additional work demonstrated that apical-derived EVs are more strongly affected in both concentration and cargo composition compared to basolateral-derived EVs [12]. In contrast, basolateral EVs appear to play specialized roles, such as regulating VEGF secretion, suggesting a functional relevance for modulating the sub-RPE and choroidal microenvironment [39]. Taken together, these data support a model in which apical-derived EVs strongly contribute to RPE barrier disruption, while basolateral EVs may exert complementary, compartment-specific effects in the sub-RPE and choroid. Remarkably, we also found that the disruption of the RPE monolayer induced by apical-derived EVs was attenuated by MMP inhibitors, underscoring the critical involvement of MMPs in EVs-mediated oBRB breakdown.

In vivo, these EVs induced AMD-like retinal changes in mice, including thinning of the outer layers of the retina, photoreceptor loss and oBRB disruption. The intravitreal route was chosen to expose the apical surface of the RPE to EVs, consistent with our hypothesis that apically released EVs influence the outer blood-retinal barrier integrity. Although subretinal injection could replicate basal EV release, the intravitreal approach provides a technically reliable and less invasive method, minimizing retinal damage while maintaining physiological relevance. The impact on the outer retina despite being an intravitreal injection supports the hypothesis that RPE-derived EVs primarily act on RPE and RPE neighboring cells, reflecting their autocrine role. Moreover, these EVs also exert a paracrine effect by inducing robust glial activation, encompassing astrocytes, Müller cells and microglia, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response within the retinal environment. Consistent with prior studies, microglial cells migrated to the outer retina [40, 41] and adopted a pro-inflammatory phenotype [42], highlighting their role in amplifying the retinal inflammatory response in the retina. Also, in accordance with our results, recent research has highlighted the immunomodulatory potential of apical EVs derived from RPE cells. For instance, apical EVs from AMD patients-derived RPE cells induce the expression of pro-inflammatory genes in THP-1-derived macrophages, with a stronger inflammatory response compared to the basolateral EVs [13]. These findings suggest that apical EVs derived from dysfunctional RPE cells play a role in the recruitment of immune cells, potentially contributing to pathological processes underlying AMD pathogenesis. This crosstalk between RPE-derived EVs and microglia suggests a feed-forward mechanism contributing to AMD progression.

Conclusion

These findings complement previous work implicating oxidative stress in AMD pathogenesis by emphasizing inflammation-induced dysregulation of EVs secretion and cargo. This study establishes the directional secretion of EVs, particularly in the apical domain, as a key modulator of retinal health. The enrichment of MMPs in apical EVs highlights a protease-mediated pathway of retinal damage, providing a mechanistic link between inflammation and oBRB disruption.

Overall, this study brings important insights regarding the role of MMP-containing EVs as critical mediator of retinal inflammation and oBRB disruption in AMD. Targeting MMPs or their EV-associated pathways represents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating AMD and other inflammation-driven retinal diseases. Future studies should elucidate the molecular underpinnings of EVs biogenesis and cargo selection under inflammatory conditions, as well as the therapeutic potential of modulating EVs-mediated intercellular communication in AMD and other retinal disorders.
