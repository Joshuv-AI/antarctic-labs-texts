Beyond a diagnostic modality: the pivotal role of ultrasound biomicroscopy in ophthalmic care pathways
Authors: Yuelin Li, Bo Zhang, Jiale Liu, Wenwen Yu, Ting Chen, Jingyi Wu, Yuchan Huang, Pingsheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) is a high-frequency ultrasound imaging system (50–100 MHz) that serves as a typical biomedical device and instrumentation tool, enabling high-resolution, real-time, in vivo imaging of anterior segment structures. It provides visualization of the cornea, iris, lens and zonules, ciliary body, and angle structures, as well as quantitative measurements. This review examines UBM’s technical principles, clinical applications, and recent technological innovations, with a focus on its value in biomedical imaging and image processing. Key applications in glaucoma, corneal diseases, ocular trauma, anterior segment tumors, and lens-related disorders are systematically summarized. Emerging advances, including three-dimensional reconstruction and artificial intelligence-assisted analysis, are also incorporated. UBM holds significant value in anterior segment imaging. Its integration with engineering innovations—such as advanced signal processing and artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms—offers biomedical engineers and clinicians a practical reference for utilizing this technology in precision ophthalmology.Keywords: Ultrasound biomicroscopy, Anterior segment imaging, Glaucoma, Corneal diseases, Ocular trauma, Artificial intelligence

Background

UBM is a noninvasive examination device using high-frequency ultrasound (50–100 MHz) for high-resolution, real-time imaging of anterior segment structures [1, 2]. Unlike conventional diagnostic ophthalmic ultrasound (5–10 MHz), UBM adopts a 50–100 MHz high-frequency probe for superior resolution, clearly displaying anterior segment structures even with anatomical or pathological obstruction [3, 4]. Since its advent in the 1990s, UBM has greatly compensated for the defect of traditional ophthalmic examinations (slit lamp, anterior segment optical coherence tomography, AS-OCT) that cannot observe hidden parts including the area behind the iris, the ciliary body, and chamber angle. As the only tool enabling in vivo observation of anterior segment structures and cross-sectional imaging, it distinctly reveals subtle anatomical relationships among the cornea, anterior chamber, iris, ciliary body, lens zonules, and anterior vitreous [5, 6]. Its imaging principle is to use the time delay of the returned ultrasound signal to assess tissue depth, and it can perform quantitative measurement of intraocular structures (cornea, iris, ciliary body, sclera, and anterior/posterior chamber depth) [7, 8]. During examination, the transducer is placed into an eye cup filled with distilled water to form a water-bath environment, and then axial and longitudinal scanning is performed, similar to conventional diagnostic B-mode ultrasound [1, 6].

With technological advancement, UBM has evolved from initial application in anterior chamber angle (ACA) assessment to applications in corneal diseases, ocular trauma, tumors, congenital abnormalities, and perioperative management of various intraocular surgeries, becoming an indispensable tool for anterior segment diseases—especially when post-iris structures need evaluation.[1, 7] This expansion is due to the clarification of the physical characteristics, imaging features, and inherent limitations of high-frequency ultrasound [8, 9]. Quantitatively, UBM measures important parameters such as corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth (ACD), and angle width [1, 10]. A study used a 35 MHz UBM to conduct biometric measurements of the anterior segment in normal adults, obtaining standard data for ACD, angle width, and the thickness of the iris and ciliary body [11]. Qualitatively, UBM is critical for diagnosing and managing angle-closure glaucoma (ACG), plateau iris configuration, secondary glaucoma, and anterior uveitis with complicated cataracts [1, 12]. Furthermore, UBM can also be widely used in diagnosing scleral diseases, intermediate uveitis, ocular hypotony, ocular surface tumors, and iris cysts, and identifying the location and nature of foreign bodies in the anterior segment via characteristic artifacts [1, 13, 14].

This review is based on a comprehensive literature search of PubMed up to December 2025, focusing on studies related to UBM technology, its clinical applications in major anterior segment diseases, and recent advances in AI-assisted analysis. In recent years, the combination of three-dimensional reconstruction, quantitative analysis, and AI-assisted diagnosis technology for UBM images has greatly improved the clinical application value and diagnostic efficiency of UBM. AI-assisted diagnosis uses deep learning models to automatically and accurately determine lens status (phakic, aphakic, or pseudophakic) with good recall and precision even with limited image acquisition [15, 16]. For example, the ACM-Assessor AI system developed by a research team can diagnose the mechanism of angle closure in UBM images. The diagnostic accuracy of this system is similar to that of glaucoma experts, and it can well help beginners improve their diagnostic level [17]. Additionally, algorithms based on deep learning can automatically identify anterior segment structures and measure parameters of primary angle-closure disease, thus realizing reliable automatic measurement of angle parameters [18]. Therefore, UBM is both a powerful diagnostic tool and an important resource for clinical research and teaching [18]. This paper conducts a detailed analysis of the technical characteristics of UBM, and systematically discusses its diagnostic and therapeutic applications and research progress in various ophthalmic diseases, thus providing a detailed theoretical basis for clinical practice and scientific research.

Basic principles of high-frequency ultrasound imaging and equipment composition

The core structure of UBM is the high-frequency ultrasonic transducer. This transducer emits ultrasonic waves with frequencies between 50 and 100 MHz, much higher than conventional B-mode ultrasound, and achieves an axial resolution of 20 to 50 μm [18, 19]. Because the wavelength of sound is inversely proportional to its frequency, UBM has very high resolution, but this also causes its penetration depth to be only about 4 to 5 mm [20]. This feature makes it very suitable for fine imaging of anterior segment structures [20, 21]. The UBM equipment consists of four main parts [6]: the probe is coupled to the cornea using a water bath or eye cup to eliminate the acoustic impedance difference caused by the air medium and to protect the cornea from mechanical damage; the scanning system uses a mechanical reciprocating drive to achieve real-time imaging; the signal processing unit uses the sub-Nyquist sampling method to reduce computational complexity, for example, 4/3 times the center frequency sampling plus six-fold interpolation, which reduces the hardware burden while ensuring an axial resolution of 63.5 μm [22]; the image display system uses a motion trajectory compensation algorithm to solve the non-uniformity problem of mechanical scanning, and finally outputs real-time B-mode two-dimensional images at 12 frames per second [23]. As shown in Fig. 1, UBM consists of four main components: the probe, scanning system, signal processor, and image display system (Fig. 1). During the examination, the operator selects an appropriate eye cup based on the patient's palpebral fissure size, places it on the ocular surface, and fills it with coupling agent. The probe is then immersed in the coupling agent within the eye cup to perform scanning, thereby obtaining anatomical images of the anterior segment structures (Fig. 2). It is worth noting that some new UBM devices already have three-dimensional scanning capabilities, using multiplanar reconstruction techniques to generate three-dimensional images of anterior segment anatomical structures [24, 25]. This provides a new method for the spatial localization of complex lesions, such as cyclodialysis.

Analysis of UBM’s imaging advantages and limitations

The core strengths of UBM are its penetrability and dynamic observation capability. When optical media are opaque, such as with corneal opacity (edema or scar) and hyphema, UBM can still clearly display structures behind the iris, such as the ciliary body and lens zonules [24]. This characteristic compensates for the defect of OCT which cannot image when media are opaque. In addition, UBM can also observe the opening and closing state of the iridocorneal angle in real time and, using dynamic imaging, observe the mutual influence between iris bombe and anterior displacement of the ciliary body [26], providing a basis for glaucoma classification (for example, the pupillary block mechanism in primary angle-closure glaucoma, PACG). For specific pathologies such as uveal effusion syndrome, UBM can simultaneously show the characteristic changes of both ciliary body detachment and scleral thickening, whereas B-mode ultrasound can only show retinal detachment [27]. A prospective multimodal study found that UBM has a stronger ability to detect these anterior changes than B-mode ultrasound and EDI-OCT, confirming that UBM plays an important role in diagnosing this syndrome [28].

However, UBM also has obvious shortcomings: as a contact examination, it requires surface anesthesia and the use of coupling agents, which can cause a certain degree of discomfort or corneal damage to the patient; and the scanning range is limited by the physical properties of high-frequency ultrasound, resulting in limited penetration depth (only 4–5 mm) and an inability to scan the retina and posterior pole [29]. The operator must have proficient technical skills to accurately identify anatomical landmarks, and image interpretation has a high degree of subjectivity [30]. Recent research attempts to analyze UBM images using deep learning algorithms (YOLOv8) and can automatically measure the diameter of the ciliary sulcus (STS) with an error of only 3.27%, greatly improving measurement efficiency [31], providing a new idea for overcoming operator dependence.

Core applications of UBM in glaucoma diagnosis and treatment

Assessment and mechanism classification of PACG

UBM, which can image the ACA anatomy in vivo at high resolution, has become the gold standard for assessing ACA anatomy [27, 31]. It can accurately perform in vivo measurements of the angle opening distance (AOD), trabecular meshwork–iris angle, iris thickness, and ciliary body position [29, 32]. These quantitative parameters are of great significance for the objective assessment of the degree of angle narrowing or closure, especially when corneal opacity makes gonioscopy impossible; at such times, UBM becomes an indispensable examination method [29]. Using high-resolution imaging, it can clearly display the different pathogenic mechanisms of PACG. It can effectively distinguish pathological conditions such as pupillary block, plateau iris (including plateau iris configuration and plateau iris syndrome), and anterior rotation of the ciliary body [30]. The pupillary block mechanism occurs in patients with a short axial length, shallow anterior chamber, thick lens, and anteriorly positioned lens. When these features reach a certain degree, the contact area between the anterior lens surface and the iris increases, leading to greater resistance to aqueous humor flow from the posterior chamber to the anterior chamber through the pupil. This pushes the iris forward, resulting in a shallower peripheral anterior chamber and a narrow or even closed angle. UBM typically reveals a shallow anterior chamber, anteriorly positioned lens, relatively anterior pupillary margin, peripheral iris bulging, and a narrow or closed angle (Fig. 3).The non-pupillary block mechanism usually refers to peripheral iris thickening with anterior iris root insertion, or anteriorly positioned ciliary body pushing the peripheral iris against the angle, leading to angle narrowing or closure (Fig. 4). When two or more features of both pupillary and non-pupillary block mechanisms coexist, it is classified as the combined mechanism type (Fig. 5). For example, UBM can directly show the extensive contact between the iris and the anterior lens capsule during pupillary block. In plateau iris syndrome, it shows the anterior displacement of the iris root and the special anatomical structure where anterior rotation of the ciliary body supports the iris root, bringing it close to the trabecular meshwork [33]. Accurate typing of the pathogenic mechanism can provide a direct basis for formulating individualized clinical treatment plans. For example, for those with pupillary block, laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) can be performed, while for those with non-pupillary block mechanisms (such as plateau iris), other interventions should be considered [29]. In addition, UBM can also be used to evaluate changes in the ACA after peripheral iridotomy. Using the changes in angle openness, iris morphology, and ciliary body position before and after surgery, it can objectively evaluate whether LPI has successfully relieved pupillary block, providing reliable imaging evidence for evaluating surgical outcomes [30, 34]. Before LPI, UBM shows peripheral iris bulging and a narrow angle; after the procedure, the iris becomes flattened and the angle widens and opens, indicating that pupillary block has been relieved (Fig. 6). This provides reliable imaging evidence for evaluating surgical outcomes.

Etiological diagnosis of secondary glaucoma

Secondary glaucoma is diverse in type and varied in pathogenic mechanism, and UBM can provide evidence support for its etiological diagnosis, mechanism elucidation, and formulation of rational surgical plans. Secondary glaucoma caused by ocular trauma and intraocular surgery is relatively common and complex in clinical practice. For secondary glaucoma following ocular trauma, UBM can visually display subtle structural changes such as iridodialysis, the range and depth of angle recession, ciliary body detachment or tear, abnormal lens position (dislocation or subluxation), and vitreous herniation into the anterior chamber [35, 36]. For secondary glaucoma after cataract surgery, UBM can reveal various etiologies, such as intraocular lens (IOL)-related issues including abnormal IOL position, residual lens cortex like Soemmering’s ring, and anterior and posterior synechiae caused by intraocular inflammation [37]. After retinal vitreous surgery, UBM can reveal pathogenic causes such as silicone oil entering the anterior chamber and silicone oil emulsification obstructing the trabecular meshwork [38]. For the clinically common refractory glaucoma—neovascular glaucoma, UBM examination can clearly display neovascular membranes on the iris and in the angle area, as well as the extent and degree of peripheral anterior synechia (PAS), providing an important basis for assessing the severity of the condition and formulating treatment plans (such as intravitreal injection of anti-VEGF drugs, panretinal photocoagulation, whether to combine with anti-glaucoma surgery, and which anti-glaucoma surgical method to choose) [39, 40]. For the relatively uncommon pigment dispersion syndrome and pseudocapsular exfoliation syndrome, UBM can dynamically observe the contact state between the posterior surface of the iris and the anterior lens capsule, zonular laxity or rupture, and lens tremor. These observations help elucidate the pathogenesis of aqueous humor reflux and pigment dispersion [29, 37]. As shown in Fig. 7, in patients with pigment dispersion syndrome, UBM images reveal reverse pupillary block leading to posterior bowing of the iris, which rubs against the zonules or ciliary processes (Fig. 7). This friction causes loss of iris pigment particles and their deposition in the anterior segment. In rare congenital glaucoma-related diseases such as Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome, UBM can clearly present the morphological classification of ACA dysgenesis. Based on its morphology, it can be classified into different types, including simple high iris insertion, posterior embryotoxon, iris processes, and iridotrabecular synechiae. These morphological classifications are closely related to the age of onset of glaucoma, its severity, and specific gene mutations (such as FOXC1 or PITX2), providing a basis for prognosis judgment and genetic counseling [39, 41]. In summary, with its high-resolution imaging capability for subtle structures in the anterior segment, UBM can systematically reveal anatomical abnormalities and pathogenic mechanisms in secondary glaucoma of different etiologies. It is an indispensable and important examination tool for the etiological diagnosis, condition assessment, plan formulation, and prognosis judgment of secondary glaucoma.

Preoperative planning and postoperative evaluation of anti-glaucoma surgery

UBM can accurately measure scleral thickness, define the specific position and shape of the ciliary body, and evaluate the anatomical structure of the surgical area [42]. This information is important for the selection of surgical incision for glaucoma, the placement of drainage valve (Ahmed valve) and cycloplasty, which can help improve the accuracy and safety of surgery and reduce the occurrence of complications [42, 43]. Preoperative UBM examination can reveal ACA dysplasia. The severity of which is closely related to the success rate of glaucoma surgery, providing an important basis for the selection of surgical methods and prognosis judgment [44, 45]. In addition, UBM is a “window” for observing the morphology and function of filtering blebs after anti-glaucoma surgery. It can clearly show the shape of the bleb (cystic, flat or encapsulated), its internal structure (wall thickness and reflection intensity), and its relationship to the underlying scleral flap, thereby determining the function of the bleb [46]. Therefore, UBM is an indispensable examination tool for preoperative planning and postoperative follow-up evaluation of anti-glaucoma surgery.

Application of UBM in corneal and ocular surface diseases

UBM can penetrate opaque areas of the cornea and clearly display the structure of each layer of the cornea, providing a reference for the diagnosis and treatment of corneal and ocular surface diseases [47]. UBM has an irreplaceable role in the evaluation of deep corneal lesions, preoperative planning for corneal transplantation surgery, and the diagnosis of complications from corneal refractive surgery, especially when optical imaging techniques such as OCT are limited [47].

Assessment of deep corneal lesions and corneal transplant evaluation

UBM has obvious advantages in evaluating deep corneal lesions. For corneal leucoma, deep corneal foreign bodies, corneal stromal effusion, and Descemet's membrane detachment, UBM can clearly display the interlayer structure and accurately measure the size and depth of the lesion [48]. For example, in patients with diffuse corneal edema and Descemet’s membrane folds, UBM images show irregular corneal epithelial echoes, widened stromal echoes, and a wavy posterior corneal surface (Fig. 8). Deep corneal lesions and Descemet’s membrane rupture have been confirmed to be closely related to poor prognosis such as enucleation, so active surgical intervention is required for deep corneal lesions and Descemet’s membrane rupture.[49] For patients who cannot undergo routine examination due to corneal opacity, UBM can show the depth of the lesion and the continuity of the Descemet's membrane, providing a reference for formulating a more accurate surgical plan [48]. For example, in cases of limbal squamous cell carcinoma, corneal stromal abscess, or corneal foreign body, UBM examination can evaluate the depth and extent of lesion invasion, providing a basis for selecting the corneal resection range using a fixed-depth keratome and guiding subsequent β-radiation therapy [49]. UBM examination before corneal transplantation surgery is crucial. It can accurately measure corneal thickness and ACD and detect the presence or absence of anterior synechiae, providing important parameters for surgical design [48]. In conclusion, due to its ability to penetrate opaque media, UBM has become an indispensable examination tool for the diagnosis and treatment of infectious keratitis, complex corneal trauma, and corneal diseases requiring keratoplasty [8, 48]. In addition, for congenital anterior segment dysgenesis (such as congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy, primary congenital glaucoma, and Peters anomaly), UBM can play an important role in differentiating these overlapping phenotypes by measuring parameters such as ACD, iris thickness, and ciliary body thickness [50].

Diagnosis of complications related to corneal refractive surgery

For eyes after laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), UBM can show the position of the corneal flap, measure its thickness accurately, and detect fluid or epithelial ingrowth beneath the flap. All these are important references for managing postoperative complications [47]. Interface fluid syndrome (IFS), a rare but vision-threatening complication after LASIK. UBM can reveal key features—central corneal thickening, peripheral corneal edema—which help differentiate it from diffuse lamellar keratitis (DLK) [51]. Central toxic keratopathy (CTK) is another rare, non-infectious inflammatory complication after refractive surgery. It presents as central corneal opacity, thinning, and hyperopic drift [52]. When combined with AS-OCT, UBM clearly shows structural changes—abnormal reflectivity, tissue disorganization between corneal layers. This both confirms the diagnosis and tracks disease progression over time [53].

Value of UBM in diagnosing ocular trauma and anterior segment tumors

Fine assessment of anterior segment trauma

UBM can clearly show fine structural damage that traditional examination methods cannot show. Specifically, UBM can accurately diagnose complex lesions such as angle recession, ciliary body detachment, and iris root separation after ocular blunt trauma [54]. Angle recession is characterized by an increased distance from the scleral spur to the angle recess and posterior displacement of the ciliary body (Fig. 9). Ciliochoroidal detachment presents as separation between the ciliochoroidal layer and the sclera, with the detached space filled with dense fine granular echoes, indicating suprachoroidal hemorrhage (Fig. 9). UBM can also clearly show the degree and extent of zonular ligament damage in lens subluxation (LS) or complete dislocation. A prospective study of 143 patients with ocular trauma showed that the diagnostic accuracy of UBM for zonular ligament injury was 55.2%, and the incidence of zonular injury in the blunt trauma group (64.28%) was much higher than that in the penetrating injury group (25.74%) [54]. The study also found that zonular ligament injuries at 3–6 o’clock accounted for the majority (70.83% zonule rupture, 96.77% zonule stretching) [54].

In addition, UBM can clearly show minor injuries that are difficult to detect with traditional imaging examinations. UBM also has high clinical value in locating and qualitatively evaluating intraocular foreign bodies. Figure 10 illustrates UBM images clearly revealing foreign bodies in the anterior chamber and the vitreous base (Fig. 10). A retrospective study showed that for vegetal foreign bodies, small-sized foreign bodies (< 1.00 mm), and foreign bodies embedded in the eyeball wall, the detection rate of UBM was significantly higher than that of CT, X-ray, and conventional B-mode ultrasound [55]. UBM can clearly distinguish metal and non-metal foreign bodies and can accurately judge the position of foreign bodies embedded behind the iris or in the ciliary body and the relationship between foreign bodies and surrounding tissues [56]. In clinical practice, direct ciliary body suture guided by UBM has been successfully applied to the treatment of traumatic ciliary body detachment. During the operation, UBM can monitor the repair of ciliary body laceration in real time and is not affected by the transparency of the refractive media [57]. The above research results fully prove that UBM plays an important role in the detailed evaluation of anterior segment trauma and provides an important reference for formulating precise treatment plans in clinical practice.

Localization and nature assessment of anterior segment tumors

UBM yields excellent imaging for anterior segment tumor diagnosis, enabling clear visualization of solid or cystic space-occupying lesions in the iris and ciliary body. UBM distinctly displays tumor boundaries, internal echo characteristics (homogeneous or heterogeneous), and anatomical relationships with key structures such as the lens and iris root, enabling accurate measurement of tumor size [58]. UBM reveals a round hypoechoic lesion at the iridociliary junction, indicating an iris-ciliary body cyst. Iris masses appear as solid elevations on the iris surface with heterogeneous internal echoes and irregular shapes. Ciliary body masses present as diffuse solid elevations on the ciliary body with anterior extension to the iris (Fig. 11). A long-term follow-up study of 89 patients with anterior segment tumors showed that cysts accounted for 41%, solid iris tumors for 37.1%, and ciliary body tumors for 7.9% [58]. UBM uniquely differentiates iris cysts from melanomas by analysis of lesion internal echo and vascular distribution patterns [59]. Pigmented tumors show characteristic low-to-moderate internal echogenicity and acoustic shadowing, whereas non-pigmented tumors typically present as homogeneous, moderately hyperechoic lesions [59]. A large-sample study involving 380 patients systematically summarized the UBM characteristics and growth patterns of various iris tumors, providing key references for clinical differential diagnosis and management [60].

Anterior segment tumors. a, b Iris–ciliary body cyst (red arrow). c, d Iris mass (red arrow): solid elevation on the iris surface with heterogeneous internal echoes and irregular shape. e, f Ciliary body mass (red arrow): diffuse solid elevation on the ciliary body surface with anterior extension to the iris

UBM also plays a crucial role in assessing secondary changes in tumors. It accurately identifies ACA invasion or secondary glaucoma induced by tumors, thereby guiding treatment options (observation, laser, or surgical resection) [59]. A study of 42 iris and ciliary body tumors showed that UBM visualizes the posterior edge of the tumor more clearly than AS-OCT, especially for pigmented tumors thicker than 2 mm [60]. UBM is often the preferred imaging modality for the localization and morphological evaluation of ciliary body tumors due to its excellent penetration and resolution [60]. Additionally, UBM is very valuable in the long-term follow-up of anterior segment tumors; research shows most such tumors exhibit no growth over a 4-year period (with an annual growth rate of only 0.02 mm) [58].

Application of UBM in diseases related to the lens and IOLs

Assessment of lens position abnormalities and zonules

UBM has unique clinical value in observing lens position abnormalities and zonules. As the preferred imaging examination method for diagnosing zonule laxity or rupture in diseases such as LS/total dislocation and Marfan syndrome, UBM can provide high-resolution images of anterior segment structures [61]. UBM images of LS show partial zonular rupture and lens displacement (Fig. 12). UBM examination is particularly important for pediatric patients. Authoritative monographs on pediatric anterior segment diseases also emphasize the significant value of UBM in assessing cases of congenital lens abnormalities and traumatic zonular injuries in children [47, 61]. Studies confirm UBM clearly displays the range and location of zonule rupture and lens tilt angle, facilitating precise formulation of cataract surgery plans [62]. In patients with LS and microspherophakia (MSP), the combined use of UBM and AS-OCT can quantify the degree of lens dislocation. By measuring the coefficient of variation (CV) of the ACA and AOD, the zonular weak areas can be effectively identified [62]. Additionally, UBM also captures dynamic changes in lens position, clearly showing the range and location of dislocation, providing a more comprehensive basis for preoperative assessment [62]. UBM can observe changes in lens position before and after LPI in patients with PACG. It has been found that LPI causes the lens to shift relatively backward and improves its stability, which holds important guiding significance for IOL power calculation [63].

Analysis of IOL position abnormalities and complications

UBM is vital for diagnosing and managing cataract surgery complications, accurately detecting IOL deviation, tilt, iris/ciliary sulcus incarceration, and capsular block syndrome (Fig. 13) [32]. Its high-resolution imaging clarifies the positional relationship between the posterior chamber IOL haptic and the ciliary sulcus, reliably evaluating IOL stability [32]. For abnormal IOL positioning, UBM measures the irido-lens angle (ILA) and irido-lens contact distance (ILCD), whose changes directly reflect IOL stability [63]. With advancing AI in ophthalmology, the combination of UBM and AI algorithms may further improve the prediction accuracy of IOL complications, for example, by applying deep learning to analyze UBM images to predict postoperative complications such as IOL dislocation [64]. This integration offers precise clinical decision support, ultimately enhancing patients’ postoperative visual outcomes.

Intraocular lens-related disease: intraocular lens dislocation. UBM image (a) and schematic diagram (b) demonstrating malposition of the IOL haptic

For patients with phakic posterior chamber Implantable Collamer Lens (ICL) implantation, evaluating contact between the ICL, iris, and crystalline lens is critical. UBM assesses haptic placement in the ciliary sulcus, evaluates angle status, and measures ACD and vault (Fig. 14). Iris–ICL contact may cause pigment dispersion syndrome, with pigment particles depositing in the trabecular meshwork, though this does not necessarily lead to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) or glaucoma [65]. Additionally, EVO ICL implantation improves concave iris morphology, reducing pigment dissemination risk without compromising surgical safety during follow-up [66]. UBM visualizes iris curvature and measures parameters like ILCD, providing a key reference for postoperative management [32]. Consequently, regular postoperative follow-up and detailed imaging are essential to ensure the safety and efficacy of ICL implantation.

Advances and future prospects of UBM technology

Three-dimensional UBM imaging and quantitative analysis

3D UBM reconstructs anterior segment volumetric images by superimposing multiple 2D scans, offering intuitive spatial visualization of lesions for clinical diagnosis. Studies confirm 3D-UBM clearly visualizes critical features, including circumferential angle closure and tumor morphology [67]. Figure 15, reproduced from an established 3D UBM imaging study, illustrates a representative 3D UBM reconstruction of the anterior segment: the left panel shows the acquisition setup, and the right panel demonstrates the volumetric rendering of the ciliary body as seen from multiple orthogonal planes. Compared with conventional 2D UBM, this technology significantly enhances the visualization of complex ocular anatomy [16]. With dedicated software, parameters such as the angle width, anterior chamber volume, and iris area can be automatically measured. For example, it measures single ciliary process volume (0.234 ± 0.093mm3), surface area (3.02 ± 1.07mm2), and total ciliary muscle volume (67.87mm3) [67, 68]. This quantitative analysis method not only improves the objectivity and repeatability of the evaluation but also provides a reliable basis of parameters for disease monitoring and treatment outcome assessment. The newly developed image enhancement technology based on Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) can significantly improve the quality of 3D-UBM images, for instance, by improving the measurement accuracy of important parameters such as the AOD and the angle recess area (ARA) [68]. These advances establish new modalities for the precise diagnosis of anterior segment diseases and the optimization of surgical strategies.

Application of AI in UBM image analysis

AI technology is greatly changing the way UBM images are analyzed. Deep learning-based algorithms have been able to automatically identify and segment important anatomical structures in UBM images, enabling rapid, automated and comprehensive assessment of the ACA [69]. Research shows that the DeepLab-v3 + model trained with Dice loss function has an average F1 score of 0.89 in the ciliary body segmentation task, increasing expert editing efficiency by more than 7 times 16. AI models have been used for automated screening of clinical angle closure, classification of glaucoma mechanisms, and measurement of biomechanical parameters. In particular, the macroscopic area under the curve (AUC) value of the UBM based AI model for differential diagnosis of acute angle closure glaucoma (AACG) with or without zonular ligament lesions reached 0.9046, and the diagnosis and processing time was only 0.03 s/image, which was much better than manual analysis [30]. These advances suggest that AI-assisted diagnostic systems may become important tools in ophthalmology clinical practice, for efficient and accurate disease screening and classification.

Integration and expansion with other imaging technologies

UBM, combined with AS-OCT and confocal microscopy, is forming a new trend in multimodal ophthalmic imaging diagnosis. A systematic comparative study showed that UBM, AS-OCT and conventional B-mode ultrasound have their own advantages for examining anterior segment lesions, confirming the need for multimodal complementarity 70. The fusion of these technologies can achieve comprehensive information acquisition from the cellular level to the macroscopic structure, providing a more comprehensive and complete basis for diagnosing complex ocular diseases 71. In the direction of technology development, developing higher frequency probes to improve resolution or improving algorithms to increase penetration depth on the basis of ensuring resolution is the main direction of future equipment research and development. The fusion of multimodal images is the main direction for building a future “one-stop” anterior segment evaluation system [2]. The future development of UBM technology will develop in two directions: deep intelligence and functional fusion. On the one hand, AI will be more deeply integrated into the whole process from image acquisition, analysis to diagnostic decision-making; on the other hand, the spatial registration and fusion of UBM structural information with functional data such as OCT’s cell-level resolution and hemodynamics may form a new “structure–function” integrated imaging mode, redefining the diagnostic criteria for anterior segment diseases [12]. In terms of hardware technology, the development and use of ultra-high frequency probes above 100 MHz has become a clear research direction 72, aiming to improve the resolution to the microscopic level and open up new applications such as corneal endothelial imaging.

Conclusion

UBM is a major advance in ophthalmic imaging, and its technological development and application value have been widely verified. In the process of glaucoma diagnosis and treatment, the dynamic angle assessment data provided by UBM overcome the subjective limitations of traditional gonioscopy.

Technology integration is promoting UBM to enter a new stage of development. Three-dimensional reconstruction technology converts two-dimensional cross-sectional images into three-dimensional models. The introduction of AI algorithms reduces operator dependence and improves the consistency and efficiency of diagnosis. This technological synergistic effect makes UBM no longer a simple diagnostic tool， but becomes a comprehensive platform integrating screening, diagnosis, and surgical planning, and its clinical value increases exponentially.

In future development, UBM technology faces breakthroughs in miniaturization and intelligence. More promising is the multimodal image fusion technology, which spatially registers the microstructure information of UBM with the cell-level resolution of OCT and the hemodynamic data of ultrasound contrast, forming a “structure–function” integrated imaging mode, which may redefine the diagnostic criteria of anterior segment diseases. In the era of precision medicine, the continuous updating and iteration of UBM technology will promote the development of ophthalmology diagnosis and treatment toward an earlier and more personalized direction. Its application scope may exceed the traditional anterior segment category and open up new application space in fields such as ocular surface diseases and refractive surgery.
