Evaluating physical activity questionnaires for postpartum women
Authors: Karan Pongpanit, Sabrina Belcourt, Marie Laurance Gagnon, Brie-Anna Gerardi, Kim Tran, Mariane Bertagnolli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaintaining adequate physical activity (PA) is essential for recovery and long-term health in postpartum women undergoing physical therapy and rehabilitation. However, no self-report measure has been specifically developed for these women, and selecting appropriate existing tools available for postpartum PA monitoring remains challenging. This study aimed to evaluate the suitability of commonly used PA questionnaires by comparing their outcomes with objective activity data from a wearable tracker.MethodsThis quantitative cross-sectional observational study examined the relationship between self-reported and objectively measured physical activity. Twenty-three postpartum women (average 5 months postpartum) participating in rehabilitation were recruited. Three 7-day recall PA questionnaires were administered: the International Physical Activity Questionnaire - Short Form (IPAQ-SF), the Kaiser Physical Activity Survey (KPAS), and the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ). Objective PA data (daily step count and distance) were concurrently collected over a two-week period using the AmazFit Band 5 Fitness Tracker, with only the second week in the analysis.ResultsThe IPAQ-SF showed moderate-to-strong correlations with both average daily step count (r = 0.781, p < 0.001) and distance (r = 0.783, p < 0.001). In contrast, the PPAQ (r = 0.708, p < 0.001) and KPAS (r = 0.645, p < 0.01) correlated significantly only with their respective sports activity subcomponents.ConclusionsAmong the assessed tools, the IPAQ-SF demonstrated the strongest alignment with objective activity metrics, making it a more suitable option for assessing total PA in postpartum women. However, the PPAQ and KPAS provided valuable insights into specific components of PA, particularly household chores and caregiving tasks, which are highly informative during the postpartum period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40748-026-00263-w.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) is a fundamental component of a healthy lifestyle, encompassing any physical movement that uses skeletal muscles and leads to energy expenditure [1]. PA is widely acknowledged for its numerous benefits, including enhancing overall well-being, preventing noncommunicable diseases, and reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression [2]. For postpartum women, integrating at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic PA per week is recommended for optimal health [2]. However, despite the recommendation and proven benefits, various barriers such as time constraints, childcare responsibilities, and physical issues including pelvic floor dysfunction, diastasis recti, lower back pain, and musculoskeletal discomfort, often hinder postpartum individuals from engaging in physical activity [3, 4].

Monitoring and quantifying PA levels can be done through varying methods, ranging from manual records to sophisticated tracking tools [5]. Among these, self-reported questionnaires have gained prominence for their cost-effectiveness, convenience, and high acceptance rates among study participants [6]. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) is a widely utilized tool in population studies due to its comprehensiveness and ease of administration [6, 7]. However, for specific populations like pregnant and postpartum women, instruments such as the Kaiser Physical Activity Survey (KPAS) and Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ) are used to capture a broader spectrum of PA [8–10]. To our knowledge, however, there is currently no specific questionnaire tailored for postpartum women, and the optimal choice remains undetermined.

While self-reported questionnaires remain prevalent, concerns regarding recall bias and administrative burden prompt consideration of more direct measurement methods [5]. Accelerometers and pedometers, with their automated tracking capabilities, have emerged as favored tools due to their ability to provide objective data without reliance on memory. With advancements in wearable technology, these devices now offer comprehensive insights, measuring not just step count but also heart rate, distance covered, and PA energy expenditure, serving as invaluable tools for health assessment [5, 11].

Given the lack of prior studies on the suitability of subjective measures for assessing PA in women after giving birth, this study aimed to determine the association between postpartum PA levels derived from self-reported questionnaires (IPAQ, KPAS, and PPAQ) and objective data obtained from a wearable activity tracker (daily steps and distance). Our hypothesis posited that the PPAQ, due to its wider usage, reliability, and accuracy among the pregnant population [9], would emerge as the most suitable tool.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a quantitative observational cross-sectional approach to examine the relationships between self-reported and measured PA assessments. The study protocol was approved by the Comité d’éthique de la recherche du Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux du Nord-de-l’Île-de-Montréal (CIUSSS NIM). All participants were given detailed information about study procedures, and written consent was obtained.

Participants

The study recruited participants through convenience sampling, specifically targeting women in any stage of their postpartum period, up to one year post-delivery, aged over 18 years, and able to access and use a computer, mobile phone, and the internet. Participants with any major contraindication to exercise, such as cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction abnormalities, or congenital heart disease, those unwilling to use a wearable activity tracker, and those unable to respond to questionnaires in English or French were excluded.

Protocol

The recruitment of potential participants was conducted over a 4-week period within the Quebec community via online advertisements and brochures. Prospective participants expressing interest in the study were initially contacted by telephone to inform them of the nature of the study. Verbal consent was obtained during the telephone conversation, and electronic consent form requiring a signature was subsequently sent to the participants by email.

Data collection in this study encompassed a comprehensive range of participant information, addressing various aspects of their pregnancy and postpartum health. This involved assessing postnatal depression scores using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [12] and evaluating participants’ knowledge about the benefits and barriers toward PA through the Exercise Benefits and Barriers Score (EBBS) [13]. Demographic details such as age, weight, height, marital status, ethnicity, and occupation were gathered, along with pregnancy-related data including gestational age, birth weight, pregnancy complications, and maternal diseases. Postpartum-related information, including blood pressure, body mass index, and postpartum complications, was also documented. The extensive baseline dataset was collected through an online survey administered via a password-protected REDCap database, with access limited to the chief investigator and required members of the research team.

Upon obtaining informed written consent, a wearable activity tracker was delivered to participants’ homes via Canada Post, accompanied by an instruction guide on installation and proper use. The device continuously recorded objective PA data, including daily steps and distance, over a period of two consecutive weeks: the first week used for adaptation and the second week used for data collection. After completing the tracking period, unique survey links were sent to participants to complete all subjective measures, including IPAQ-SF, KPAS, and PPAQ, directly in REDCap. These questionnaires were selected based on specific criteria, considering their prior use in women-focused or perinatal studies, suitability for electronic completion without supervision, and validity in English or French.

Self-reported PA measures

PPAQ

The PPAQ is a validated and reliable tool specifically designed to assess the duration, frequency, and intensity of total PA during the current trimester in pregnant women [9, 10, 14]. However, for the purposes of the current study, modifications were made to ensure that participants recalled their PA solely during the previous 7 days of the postpartum period. This involved adjusting the recall period from “during this trimester” to “during the postpartum period (last 7 days)”. The PPAQ comprises 35 questions covering various domains of PA, including household/caregiving, occupational, sports/exercise, transportation, and sedentary activities. Participants were asked to select the category that best estimated the amount of time spent in each activity, with responses ranging from 0 h to 3 h or more per day [10]. To derive duration and intensity predictions from the PPAQ data, instructions provided by the questionnaire were followed, which are based on the Compendium of PA [15]. This allows for the determination of metabolic equivalents (METs). The MET values were then multiplied by the number of days in the week to calculate MET-hours/week, providing an estimate of weekly PA intensity [10].

IPAQ-SF

The IPAQ-SF is a valid and reliable self-administered instrument designed to capture the frequency, duration, and intensity of PA over a 7-day recall period [7, 16]. The IPAQ-SF consists of seven items that categorize PA into four generic groups, including light-intensity activities, moderate-intensity activities, vigorous-intensity activities, and sitting (sedentary) [7, 16]. To quantify the total activity score, a combined MET-minutes per week (MET-min/week) was collectively computed. This score was derived by summing the MET-min/week score of each PA category, except for sedentary behavior, as outlined in the formulation published by Craig et al. [17].

KPAS

The KPAS is another validated and reliable questionnaire, similar in structure to the PPAQ, but with the objective of measuring the types of PA performed by women rather than reflecting expenditure in MET or their level of PA [8]. The KPAS assesses a broad range of PA habits through a 75-item survey organized into four activity subscales: household and caregiving, occupation, active living habits, and sports/exercises [8]. For this study, like the modifications made to the PPAQ, adjustments were made to the KPAS to ensure that women recalled PA specifically during the postpartum period. This entailed changing the recall period from “during the past year (12 months back from today)” to “during the postpartum period (last 7 days)”. This adjustment was made to capture recent activity and minimize recall bias. Participants were instructed to answer questions across all indexes using a 5-level categorical response scale, where scores ranged from 1 (never or none) to 5 (always or more than once a week). Activity indices for each activity subscale were calculated based on procedures outlined in research by Schmidt et al. [8]. This involved adding the specific categorical responses for each item within the subscale and dividing their sum by the number of items, resulting in mean values ranging from one to five.

PA tracking

A direct measure of ambulatory PA was conducted using the Amazfit Band 5 (Anhui Huami Information Technology Co., Ltd.). Participants were instructed to wear the tracker wristband on their non-dominant hand for two weeks to track the required PA data, including the number of daily steps and distance, as well as daily minutes of sedentary, light, moderate, and vigorous activities. Upon completion of the tracking period, automatic reports generated by the Zepp application (Zepp North America, Inc.), the tracker device’s application, were collected. To account for potential adaptation effects and minimize reactivity bias, only data from the second week was used for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Demographic data and clinical characteristics of the study population were presented as n/N (%) for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile or full range) for continuous variables. To assess the relationship between the data obtained from the activity tracker and that of the PA questionnaires, Pearson’s correlation analysis was employed. A correlation coefficient of 0.00 to 0.10, 0.10 to 0.39, 0.40 to 0.69, and ≥ 0.70 was considered negligible, weak, moderate and strong, respectively [18].

Results

Participant sample

Of the 29 participants initially expressed interest in the study, 24 were eligible. Among these, 23 provided the PA tracking report and completed all three questionnaires within the specified timeframe. One participant failed to provide any data or complete the questionnaires and was consequently excluded from the analysis. Additionally, three participants completed the questionnaires but did not provide data from a wearable tracker, while one participant provided all necessary information except completing the KPAS.

Descriptive and physical characteristics of the total sample are summarized in Table 1. The average EPDS and EBBS scores indicated no depression symptoms and a good understanding of exercise benefits and barriers, respectively. Pregnancy characteristics (Supplementary material -Table S.1) showed mostly full-term deliveries (96%), vaginal births (83%), and babies born within the normal weight range (87%). 61% of participants delivered a boy, 65% had more than one pregnancy, 39% experienced pregnancy-related complications, and 22% reported baby-related complications. In addition, 56% were medicated during pregnancy, and 61% received medication after giving birth. The average hospital stay was 2 days, with a mean postpartum period of 5 months.

All participants reported access to exercise and leisure facilities (Supplementary material -Table S.2), with 70% using both private and public options. Public parks were most commonly used (83%), often more than 3 days a week (74%). Additionally, 70% of participants had home exercise equipment, with 62% using it 1–2 times weekly. Over half (52%) also participated in fitness group activities, but only 34% exercising at least 3 days a week.

PA levels

The IPAQ-SF reported total activity as 507 min/week or 1386 MET-min/week, with light-intensity activity predominant. The KPAS had a total activity index of 12, with active living index highest among its subscales. Data from activity tracker revealed an average daily step count of 6645 and daily distance of 4414 m, with 3585 m at light intensity.

Table 4 shows correlations between PA questionnaire subcategories and activity tracker parameters. Mean step count and total distance strongly and positively correlated with IPAQ-SF total activity (both p < 0.001). They also showed a moderate positive correlation with KPAS total activity (both p < 0.05). However, no significant association was found between PPAQ total activity and step count or distance (both p > 0.05). The relationship between average daily step count and total activity from each questionnaire is visually depicted in Fig. 1. For other PA components described in Table 4, postpartum daily step and distance strongly and positively correlated with PPAQ sport activity and IPAQ-SF vigorous-intensity activity (both p < 0.001). Moderate positive relationships were observed with PPAQ vigorous-intensity activity, IPAQ-SF moderate-intensity activity, and KPAS sport activity (all p < 0.01). Conversely, moderate negative correlations were found with PPAQ light-intensity and household activity domains (both p < 0.05). In addition, apart from the association of daily total distance, average light- and moderate-intensity distance displayed a moderate-to-strong positive correlation with IPAQ-SF total activity, the sport activity domain of PPAQ and KPAS, and the vigorous-intensity activity domain of both PPAQ and IPAQ-SF (all p < 0.05), while PPAQ light-intensity and domestic activities exhibited a moderate negative correlation (both p < 0.05).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to determine the most suitable questionnaire for assessing PA during the postpartum period. We evaluated the correlations between self-reported (IPAQ, KPAS, and PPAQ) and measured (daily steps and distance) PA assessments. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, which favored the PPAQ due to its common use during pregnancy, our findings indicate that the IPAQ-SF aligned most consistently with objective PA derived from a wearable tracker and may be considered the most suitable questionnaire within this specific sample and study context.

The descriptive characteristics of the sample indicate a cohort of postpartum women with generally positive attributes, as reflected in the low average scores on the EPDS and the high average scores on the EBBS. These findings suggest a favorable mental health profile and a good understanding of the benefits and barriers associated with exercise among participants [12, 13]. Furthermore, the pregnancy characteristics, such as predominantly full-term deliveries, vaginal births, and babies born within the normal weight range, underscore positive outcomes in obstetric health [19]. However, the reported prevalence of pregnancy-related complications and medication use during and after pregnancy highlights the complexity of managing maternal health during the postpartum period, which may potentially impact on postpartum PA levels [20].

The reported access to exercise and leisure facilities, such as public parks and home exercise equipment, suggests environmental support for maintaining PA levels among postpartum women. However, despite access to these resources, a notable proportion of participants did not engage in regular exercise. This disparity between access and utilization underscores the need to explore additional barriers to postpartum PA, such as time constraints, childcare responsibilities, and motivational factors [3]. Understanding these barriers is essential for developing targeted interventions aimed at promoting sustained engagement in exercise among postpartum women and improving overall maternal health outcomes.

In evaluating various measures of PA, the study highlights the importance of considering both self-reported questionnaires and objective measures. While self-reported questionnaires offer valuable data on perceptions and behaviors related to PA, objective measures provide a more accurate assessment of actual activity levels [21]. The significant positive correlation between the IPAQ-SF and measured parameters indicates that this questionnaire aligns well with objective measures of PA.

The correlations between different measures of total activity and specific PA subcategories provide further insights into postpartum activity patterns and preferences. While vigorous-intensity activities were positively associated with daily steps and distance, light- and moderate-intensity activities showed negative correlations. For example, light‑intensity and household tasks typically involve intermittent, low-effort movements that may not meaningfully contribute to overall physical fitness or activity-related adaptations captured by our objective measures. In addition, household activities are often influenced by contextual factors, such as caregiving responsibilities, time availability, or cultural expectations, rather than by habitual PA patterns. These factors may weaken or reverse associations with physiological outcomes, leading to the unexpected negative correlations observed. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating a variety of activities, including both moderate and vigorous-intensity activities, for optimal health outcomes after giving birth, a period when household tasks and related activities may prevail. Moreover, the observed associations between PA subcategories and activity tracking data offer information about the types of activities that contribute most to postpartum PA levels. However, self-reports themselves also have the capacity to over- or underestimate true PA energy expenditure and rates of inactivity. Therefore, recall and response bias, as well as the inability to capture the absolute level of PA, should be taken into account to minimize several limitations in terms of their reliability and validity [22].

Understanding the full scope of PA in postpartum women requires more than measuring structured exercise or recreational movement; it must also account for daily functional activities such as household tasks and caregiving responsibilities, which represent significant energy expenditure during this period. Engaging in low-to-moderate intensity activities, primarily in domestic and caregiving domains, may not be adequately captured by general-purpose PA questionnaires such as the IPAQ-SF. The PPAQ and KPAS are particularly valuable in capturing these domains, offering clinicians insights into the context and type of physical exertion women experience daily. This information is essential for physical therapists and rehabilitation professionals, as it allows for the development of tailored interventions that accommodate the realities of postpartum life and avoid overprescribing structured exercise without considering cumulative daily load. Moreover, identifying high demands in caregiving and household activity may inform risk of musculoskeletal strain or delayed recovery in areas such as the lumbar spine and pelvic floor. Integrating such detailed activity profiles supports more holistic care planning and can improve outcomes by aligning therapy goals with the lived experience and responsibilities of postpartum women.

This study is subject to some limitations. Firstly, due to the recruitment strategy and convenience sampling used, it is important to note that all participants had a high level of education, which may introduce selection bias and limit the diversity of the sample, thereby affecting the generalizability of the results to the broader postpartum population. This study had a modest sample size; however, the observed correlations between self-reported and objective PA measures were generally moderate-to-strong, indicating that the limited statistical power did not prevent detection of meaningful associations in this study. The high average EBBS score indicates a generally positive perception of PA among participants, potentially leading to motivational bias, where individuals may have been more inclined to report favorable attitudes towards PA. This study also relied on step count and distance from a wearable tracker as proxies for ambulatory PA, which may not fully capture other key dimensions, such as intensity, energy expenditure, or activities that do not involve stepping, contributing to measurement bias. Lastly, modifying the recall period of the PPAQ and KPAS to a 7-day window may have affected the original validity and reliability of these questionnaires, as their psychometric properties were initially established for longer recall periods. Future research should examine these findings in larger and more diverse postpartum populations, explore longitudinal changes in activity across the postpartum period, and assess the combined use of subjective and objective PA tools to enhance measurement accuracy and inform tailored rehabilitation interventions.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the IPAQ-SF is a more suitable tool for estimating total PA levels in postpartum women within this specific sample and study context when compared to the PPAQ and KPAS, based on its strong correlation with objective activity data. However, the PPAQ and KPAS remain valuable instruments for capturing functionally relevant activity domains, such as household chores and caregiving, which are highly prevalent and impactful during the postpartum period. These domains provide essential context for physical therapy and rehabilitation planning. The study also underscores the importance of addressing barriers to postpartum PA such as time constraints, childcare responsibilities, and physical limitations, and highlights the need to incorporate a variety of activity types and intensities when assessing postpartum PA. While self-reported questionnaires have inherent limitations, their continued use alongside objective measures can offer a more comprehensive understanding of postpartum women’s activity patterns.
