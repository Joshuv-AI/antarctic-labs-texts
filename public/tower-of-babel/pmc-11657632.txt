Gestational weight gain during pregnancy is the risk factor for Omicron infection: a retrospective case–control study
Authors: Lin Yao, Peizhen Zhang, Jin Zhou, Tiantian He, Zhangmin Tan, Chuo Li, Yuzhu Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt remains unclear whether gestational weight gain (GWG) during pregnancy is associated with Omicron infection in pregnant women.ObjectiveTo investigate whether gestational weight gain during pregnancy is associated with an increased risk of Omicron infection.MethodsThis is a retrospective case–control study of pregnant women from The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from December 1 to 31, 2022. Pregnant women infected with Omicron were compared to those uninfected using chi-square statistics for categorical variables and t-tests or ANOVA for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression, along with subgroup analyses, was used to investigate the association between gestational weight gain during pregnancy and Omicron infection.ResultsOur study included 369 pregnant women who met the eligibility criteria. The proportion of pregnant women in the term, infected and uninfected with Omicron was 113(30.6%) and 256(69.4%). Classified pregnant women according to GWG during pregnancy, GWG during pregnancy above IOM was significantly associated with an increased risk of Omicron infection (P = 0.006). After adjusting for demographic factors and pregnancy complications, this association was particularly notable (OR = 2.55, 95%CI = 1.35–4.85, P = 0.004). Classified pregnant women according to pregestational BMI, in normal-weight pregnant women, GWG during pregnancy above IOM was significantly associated with an increased risk of Omicron infection (P = 0.01). After adjusting for demographic factors and pregnancy complications, this association was particularly notable (OR = 2.56, 95%CI = 1.24–5.31, P = 0.01).ConclusionAmong women with normal weight, gestational weight gain during pregnancy above IOM was an independent risk factor for Omicron infection.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Omicron, Risk factor, Gestational weight gain, IOM

Introduction

The infection of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has increased the risk of adverse maternal and neonatal delivery outcomes [1–7]. Meanwhile, prior research has suggested that obesity is a significant risk factor for COVID-19 infection [8–11] and adverse health outcomes in critically ill COVID-19 patients [12, 13]. However, the association between gestational weight gain (GWG) during pregnancy, particularly above the Institute of Medicine (IOM), and Omicron infection remains uncertain in pregnant women.

The studies indicate that obese individuals exhibit persistent chronic inflammation, attributed to elevated levels of chemokines, adipokines, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. This condition results in a compromised immune response, characterized by delayed and reduced macrophage activation during infection. Furthermore, the immune memory in obese patients, encompassing both humoral and cellular aspects, is diminished, thereby weakening the adaptive immune response to diseases, and reducing the efficacy of immunizations [11, 14–16]. In a study of 383 COVID-19 patients from Shenzhen, being overweight was associated with an 86% increase, and obesity with a 142% increase, in the risk of developing severe pneumonia compared to patients of normal weight. These associations were determined using statistical models that accounted for potential confounding variables [17].

Herein, we hypothesized that GWG above IOM may be associated with Omicron infection in the same body mass index (BMI) categories of pregnant women. Because of the revision of the prevention and control policies, The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University provided care for many pregnant women infected with Omicron in December 2022. We collected the maternal clinical data and conducted multivariate logistic regression and subgroup analyses based on maternal pregestational BMI to explore the correlation between GWG during pregnancy and Omicron infection. The purpose of this study is that in normal-weight pregnant women, GWG during pregnancy above IOM is the risk factor for Omicron infection.

This study is the first to identify a correlation between GWG during pregnancy and Omicron infection, potentially guiding the prenatal management of pregnant women.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective case–control study was conducted at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University in Guangdong Province, China. Data were collected on expectant individuals admitted to the Obstetrics department from December 1 to December 31, 2022, through the hospital's electronic medical record system. All data were meticulously entered and verified by two resident doctors. The study received approval from the Institute Medical Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (reference number: II2023-094–01) and the need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for retrospective case–control studies.

Gene sequencing technology is a vital method for identifying infection strains among individuals. However, research on the dynamics of infection based on the timing of SARS-CoV-2 infection in research subjects is limited by objective constraints. According to reports from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), between December 1, 2022, and March 30, 2023, a total of 28,468 effective genomic sequences of local SARS-CoV-2 cases were submitted nationally, all identified as the Omicron variant. Consequently, during the COVID-19 epidemic period, specifically dominated by the Omicron from December 1, 2022, to December 31, 2022, electronic medical records of COVID-19 patients from a designated hospital were collected and analyzed to identify and include infections caused by the Omicron in the study [18].

Inclusion criteria include: 1. Individuals who are at least 18 years of age; 2. A gestational age of at least 28 weeks; 3. Availability of comprehensive and collected clinical data. Initially, 403 patients with regular antenatal examination records were included. They were excluded if they met the following criteria: 1. Absence of prenatal COVID-19 testing; 2. Fetal or chromosomal abnormalities; 3. Major pregnancy complications or existing severe heart, liver, kidney, or blood disorders; 4. Concurrent other respiratory infections; 5. History of smoking or alcohol use; 6. Under 18 years of age; 7. Multiple pregnancies (Fig. 1). Due to the hospital's unified epidemic prevention policy, before delivery, each pregnant woman underwent a SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid test via real-time fluorescence PCR, conducted using a throat swab. Those testing positive were categorized as ‘SARS-CoV-2 positive’. The ‘Infected Group’ comprised pregnant women diagnosed with Omicron infection, while those who tested negative during this period formed the ‘Uninfected Group’. If the test was positive, the severity of the SARS-CoV-2 disease was determined according to the clinical typing standard of the “Novel Coronavirus Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment Plan (Trial Version 10)” [19].

Flowchart of the patient selection

Additionally, based on the results of this study, we calculated the sample size required to explore “the correlation between gestational weight gain and the incidence of SARS-CoV-2”. According to preliminary studies, the infection rate of SARS-CoV-2 among pregnant women with normal weight gain is 29%, increasing to 49% for those with excessively above weight gain. We set a two-sided test alpha at 0.05, with a test power of 80% (1-β = 0.80), an assumed dropout rate of 10%, and a control group to experimental group ratio of 1:1. These calculations suggest that the required sample size would be approximately 99 participants for each group. The study encompassed all pregnant patients who met the standards within the designated period, and the sample size was determined to be adequate.

Demographic and obstetrical characteristics, including maternal age, maternal gravidity status, GWG during pregnancy, pregestational BMI, comorbidities, and pregnancy-related complications were extracted from the medical record system. GWG during pregnancy was calculated as the difference in maternal weight immediately before delivery compared to pre-pregnancy. For clinical applicability, Body Mass Index (BMI) categories were defined as follows: underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25–29.9), and obese (≥ 30). The recommended GWG ranges, based on BMI categories, are 12.5–18 kg for underweight, 11.5–16 kg for normal weight, 7–11.5 kg for overweight, and 5–9 kg for obese women (Table 1). GWG was classified relative to the Institute of Medicine (IOM) standards as “below IOM”, “within IOM”, or “above IOM”, depending on whether it fell below, within, or above these guidelines, respectively [20]. Diagnosis was conducted based on the standards from the “Novel Coronavirus Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment Plan (Trial Version 10)” issued by the National Health Commission [19]. Before delivery, each pregnant woman underwent a SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid test via real-time fluorescence PCR, conducted using a throat swab. Those testing positive were categorized as “SARS-CoV-2 positive” [19].

Statistics

Data were expressed as mean and standard deviation for normally distributed variables or as the median and interquartile range (IQR) for skewed variables. Differences among groups were analyzed using a t-test or ANOVA. Qualitative data were described as percentages and analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test.

To investigate the risk factors associated with Omicron infection in pregnant women, subgroup analyses were firstly based on the pregnant women’s GWG during pregnancy: GWG below the IOM group, GWG within the IOM group, and GWG above the IOM group. They were then based on the pregnant women’s pregestational BMI: underweight group, normal weight group, overweight group, and obese group. Multiple logistic regressions were performed, adjusting cumulatively for variables such as maternal age, maternal gravidity status (primigravida vs. primipara), pregestational BMI, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and hypertensive disorders in pregnancy (HDP) [21].

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM). Hypotheses were tested using 2-tailed tests with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Maternal baseline demographics

Within the study period at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, a total of 403 pregnant women were identified in the electronic medical record, of which 369 met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 113 pregnant women (30.6%) infected with Omicron, formed the ‘Infected Group’. Conversely, 256 uninfected pregnant women (69.4%), formed the ‘Uninfected Group’ (Fig. 1). According to the clinical classification standards of the “Diagnosis and Treatment Protocol for Novel Coronavirus Infection (Trial Version 10)”, all pregnant women in the ‘Infected Group’ were classified as having either mild cases (105/113, 92.9%) or moderate cases (8/113,7.1%). None of the 113 infected pregnant women received antiviral treatment.

The mean gestational age at delivery was reported as 38.76 ± 1.37 weeks for the 'Infected Group' and 38.68 ± 1.59 weeks for the ‘Uninfected Group’. Among a cohort of 369 pregnant women, there were 34 instances of preterm birth: 10 in the ‘Infected Group’ and 24 in the ‘Uninfected Group’. Importantly, all preterm births were attributed to causes unrelated to the Omicron variant. Specifically, in the ‘Infected Group’, eight cases were classified as idiopathic, and two as iatrogenic (one attributed to placental abruption and another linked to gestational hypertension). In contrast, in the ‘Uninfected Group’, 21 cases were idiopathic and three were iatrogenic (two due to placental abruption and one associated with gestational hypertension).

Maternal demographics and perinatal outcomes were compared among the two groups, there was a significant difference between the two groups in GWG during pregnancy (P < 0.05). The infected group had a higher GWG on initial results than the uninfected group (Table 2).

Association of gestational weight gain during pregnancy with omicron infection

Initially, pregnant women were classified into three categories according to their GWG during pregnancy [13]: GWG below the IOM group (156/42.3%), GWG within the IOM group (150/40.7%), and GWG above the IOM group (63/17.0%). We then examined the potential association between GWG below or above the IOM and Omicron infection. Compared with GWG within the IOM group, those whose GWG was above the IOM had 2.33fold odds of Omicron infection in an unadjusted analysis (OR = 2.33, 95%CI = 1.27–4.28, P = 0.006). They were adjusting for factors: maternal age, maternal gravidity status (primigravida vs. primipara), pregestational BMI, GDM, and HPD. This finding persisted in multivariable analysis: those whose GWG above the IOM had 2.55fold odds of Omicron infection (OR = 2.55, 95%CI = 1.35–4.85, P = 0.004) (Table 3). However, in GWG below the IOM group, there was no significant association between GWG and Omicron infection(P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Subsequently, the study categorized pregnant women into four groups according to their pregestational BMI: underweight group (62/16.8%), normal weight group (285/77.2%), overweight group (22/6.0%), and obese group (0/0%), while excluding the obese group due to its small sample size (n = 0) (Table 3). In the normal weight group, those whose GWG was above the IOM had 2.34fold odds of Omicron infection in an unadjusted analysis (OR = 2.34, 95%CI = 1.18–4.66, P = 0.015)—adjusting for factors such as maternal age, maternal gravidity status (primigravida vs. primipara), GDM, and HPD. This finding persisted in multivariable analysis: those whose GWG above the IOM had 2.56fold odds of Omicron infection (OR = 2.56, 95%CI = 1.24–5.31, P = 0.011) (Table 3). Conversely, there was no association between GWG below IOM and Omicron infection (P > 0.05). Meanwhile, in the underweight and overweight groups, pregnant women’s GWG was not significantly associated with Omicron infection(P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that gaining too much weight during pregnancy increases the risk of getting the Omicron infection, especially for women with a normal weight before pregnancy. So, putting on excess weight during pregnancy is a specific risk factor for Omicron infection in normal-weight pregnant women.

Pregnancy is a unique physiological state characterized by significant immunological adaptations. The primary purpose of these adaptations is to create a favorable environment for the development of what is essentially a semi-allogeneic transplant—the fetus, which shares only half of its genes with the mother and is comparable to a transplant from the same species. These adaptations include enhanced immune tolerance and suppressed inflammatory responses [22, 23]. However, these immunological changes may increase the susceptibility of pregnant women to SARS-CoV-2 infection, potentially leading to more severe outcomes. For instance, immunosuppression during pregnancy may impair viral clearance, thereby enhancing the risk of viral replication and transmission within the body.

SARS-CoV-2 binds to the receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2). This interaction may lead to a reduction in ACE2 levels, which can cause damage to endothelial cells, activate the coagulation system, and trigger the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), contributing to vascular endothelial dysfunction and potential multi-organ damage. This damage may affect the liver, kidneys, and placenta, thus complicating the pregnancy [24, 25].

Additionally, SARS-CoV-2 infection may disrupt the microbiota, leading to imbalances in the angiotensin II/angiotensin-(1–7) ratio. This imbalance can activate an inflammatory response, which might exacerbate the COVID-19 disease course. Potential outcomes include premature birth, low birth weight, pre-eclampsia, cesarean delivery, and admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) requiring mechanical ventilation. Moreover, modulation by the TLR4 pathway during the first or second trimester may lead to reduced interferon-beta (IFN-β) levels, further increasing the risk of premature birth [26].

Previous studies indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection may cause coagulation or microvascular changes, leading to lymphoid tissue cell degeneration, villitis, and chorioamnionitis in the placenta resulting in abnormal maternal–fetal blood flow [27], can trigger systemic diseases involving the heart, liver, pancreas, and kidneys and can induce changes in circulating lymphocytes and the immune system [28–30], and can damage liver cells, leading to increased levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) [31–33]. However, our study does not find these findings. Our findings are partially inconsistent with those from the initial phase of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. This phenomenon may stem from the diminished pathogenicity of the novel Omicron and advancements in research, understanding, and treatment of the virus have led to enhanced monitoring and care for SARS-CoV-2-infected mothers during labor. This progress has decreased the frequency of medically unnecessary preterm births and excessive interventions. Effective treatment has improved outcomes for mothers and infants by preventing the worsening of conditions. In conclusion, identifying risk factors allows for more refined management of pregnant women and strengthened monitoring of high-risk populations, and this is very important.

Based on a review of the research, several major risk factors for severe clinical progression and outcomes in SARS-CoV-2 patients have been identified. These risk factors encompass demographic variables such as age and male gender, racial factors, and clinical presentations including fever, respiratory distress, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Additionally, pre-existing conditions, namely hypertension, diabetes, obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease (ILD), cancer, immunodeficiency, and pregnancy, are significant contributors to adverse outcomes [34].

Prior research suggests that obesity constitutes a significant risk factor for Omicron infection, linked concurrently with its detrimental outcomes. The elasticity of the chest wall diminishes, and respiratory system function deteriorates in response to lung damage [35]. Concurrently, inflammation intensifies, and levels of interleukin (IL)−6 increase, subsequently elevating the risk of thrombosis [36]. Naveed Sattar et al. suggest that Obesity or excess ectopic fat deposition potentially serve as unifying risk factors for severe COVID-19 infection. They diminish protective cardiorespiratory reserves and exacerbate immune dysregulation, which, at least in part, mediates the progression to critical illness and organ failure in some COVID-19 patients [12].

In the past, adipose tissue was perceived merely as a simple energy storage tissue. However, recent insights have demonstrated that it functions as both an active endocrine organ and a bona fide immune organ. Adipose tissue comprises not only fat cells but also includes fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and various immune cells, such as adipose macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, T cells, and B cells. These cells contribute to the stability of the tissue in individuals with low body fat. In cases of obesity, the expansion of fat cells leads to hypoxia, apoptosis, and cell stress in adipocytes, which subsequently triggers the expression of pro-inflammatory factors and the recruitment of inflammatory cells. Adipose tissue can secrete a wide range of molecules, such as adipokines and leptin, which can influence the immune response [37]. Leptin may influence the release of oxygen radicals, such as superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide, by neutrophils and other polymorphonuclear cells, modulate the migration of neutrophils through the activation of p38 MAPK and Src kinases and the increase in the production of TNF-a by monocytes while inhibiting the chemotaxis process by IL-8. The significance of this finding to COVID-19-related lung injury is paramount.

Obesity is recognized as a chronic state of low-grade inflammation. In this condition, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) increases, thereby leading to heightened oxidative stress. Oxidative stress arises when there is an imbalance between the production of ROS and the availability of antioxidants in the body, which results in the accumulation of excessive ROS. In obese individuals, an increase in adipose tissue leads to a corresponding increase in ROS production. These ROS can damage cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA, thereby potentially exacerbating inflammatory responses and tissue damage, Which leads to the body being more susceptible to Omicron. Furthermore, oxidative stress can also influence the differentiation and function of adipocytes, thus exacerbating obesity and related metabolic disorders [38, 39]. The escalation in leptin concentrations contributes to the exacerbation of lung injury, primarily through the amplification of neutrophilic lung inflammation [40]. Furthermore, compared to the general population, pregnant women possess unique physiological and pathological characteristics, making them especially susceptible to infection, particularly during childbirth, where unavoidable excessive ventilation can increase the rate of viral exposure and infection [5, 41].

In 2009, the IOM published guidelines for optimal total and trimester-specific weight gain based on pre-pregnancy BMI [20]. However, globally, women frequently surpass these recommendations, particularly mothers who are overweight or obese before pregnancy [42].

Our data show an increased risk of Omicron infection in normal-weight pregnant women due to gestational weight gain during pregnancy above IOM even when controlling for maternal age, gravidity, parity, GDM, and gestational hypertension, indicating that excessive weight gain is associated with Omicron infection for women of all normal-weight categories. These data underscore the importance of managing weight gain during pregnancy.

This study has limitations as well. First, From the onset of pregnancy (at approximately four weeks' gestation), our hospital adopts a protocol for the unified management of pregnant women. This protocol involves meticulously recording relevant medical history and physical examination data—including height and pre-pregnancy weight—in the hospital's electronic medical records system, ensuring high data accuracy. However, accurately measuring gestational weight gain remains challenging. Second, the observational nature of this study precludes inferring causality, and, like any extensive observational study, it is susceptible to residual confounding. Third, the data, derived from a single hospital, may not be generalizable to other practice settings, and the small sample size could introduce selection bias.

The strength of the study is the first time to indicate that gestational weight gain during pregnancy above IOM is a risk factor for Omicron infection for pregnant women of normal-weight categories.

Conclusion

In conclusion, following adjustments for various variables, excessive weight gain during pregnancy may heighten susceptibility to Omicron. Further investigation is required to elucidate the mechanisms driving this association.
