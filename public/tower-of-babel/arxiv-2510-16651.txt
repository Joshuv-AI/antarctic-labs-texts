Strong-field Driven Sub-cycle Band Structure Modulation Measured with Ultrafast Electric Field Observables
Francis Walz †, Shashank Kumar †, Amirali Sharifi Olounabadi, Yuyan Zhong, Russell Zimmerman, Siddhant Pandey, Eric Liu, Liang Z. Tan, Niranjan Shivaram

Abstract
Over the past decade, ultrafast electron dynamics in the solid state have been extensively studied using various strong light-matter interaction techniques, such as high-harmonic generation. These studies lead to multiple interpretations of light-matter interaction in the strong-field regime, with exact mechanisms not yet fully understood. It is known that strong-field interaction with a crystalline solid leads to significant modification of its band structure and hence its optical properties on ultrafast timescales. In this work, we present measurements with ultrafast electric field observables in magnesium oxide from a non-resonant nonlinear optical interaction. Using field observables, we show that the ultrafast, strong-field light-matter interaction modulates the band structure on sub-cycle time scales, resulting in a modulation of the nonlinear optical response of the material. We perform time-dependent perturbation theory calculations with a field-dependent dispersion relation and non-perturbative semiconductor Bloch equation calculations, which agree with experimental observations. Our work offers a new perspective on strong-field-driven electron dynamics in solids through the lens of electric field observables. The demonstrated attosecond modulation of the nonlinear response could have important implications for quantum light generation using nonlinear optical processes.

I
Introduction

The interaction of strong ultrafast laser pulses with solids can give rise to many intriguing phenomena, which have been extensively studied using multiple ultrafast techniques. Examples include high-harmonic generation (HHG)
Ghimire et al. 2011
;
Ghimire and Reis 2019
, the realization of the Haldane model in a laser-dressed crystal
Mitra et al. 2024
, and transient optical response modulation, also known as the dynamical Franz-Keldysh effect
Jauho and Johnsen 1996
;
Nordstrom et al. 1998
;
Lucchini et al. 2016
;
Reislöhner et al. 2023
. These effects are fundamental to understanding electron dynamics in the strong field regime. Several theoretical models have been developed to study these phenomena, such as the Houston basis formalism
Vampa et al. 2014
;
Wu et al. 2015
, Wannier functions
Osika et al. 2017
;
Silva, Martín, and Ivanov 2019
, and the Semiconductor Bloch equation
Schubert et al. 2014
;
Hohenleutner et al. 2015
;
Huttner, Kira, and Koch 2017
to model HHG in solids. To understand the dynamical Franz-Keldysh effect, more sophisticated time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) techniques have been used
Lucchini et al. 2016
;
Otobe et al. 2016
. Despite differences in approach, these models inherently include non-perturbative strong-field effects on the material to gain insight into ultrafast electron dynamics as measured by different experimental observables.

In the study presented here, we investigate the effects of electron interactions with strong fields in solids using ultrafast nonlinear optical spectroscopy, specifically using electric field observables in four-wave mixing (FWM). This nonlinear spectroscopy technique involves three coherent femtosecond pulses interacting with a crystalline solid to produce a fourth pulse that is proportional to the third-order susceptibility (
\chi^{(3)}
) of the material
Boyd 2008
. Previously, the FWM process has been studied in quantum well and quantum dot systems experimentally
Miller et al. 1983
;
Nickolaus and Henneberger 1998
and theoretically
Dignam and Hawton 2003
;
Flayyih and Al-Khursan 2013
. However, in an optically thick semiconductor, the propagation effect of the FWM output pulse becomes complex
Yu, Lee, and Viswanath 1999
;
Schulze, Knorr, and Koch 1995
;
Schillak and Balslev 1993
. Recently, with the advances in Free-Electron Lasers (FEL), the FWM process with purely extreme ultraviolet (XUV) pulses has also been demonstrated
Bencivenga et al. 2015
;
Foglia et al. 2018
. On the other hand, nonlinear response functions have also been measured using HHG in solids
Han et al. 2019
.

In this article, we report an experiment in which we measure electric field observables for degenerate four-wave mixing (DFWM) in Magnesium Oxide (MgO) to study the strong field modification of the band structure and hence the nonlinear response function in the material. Using the TADPOLE technique
Fittinghoff et al. 1996
, the amplitude and phase of the DFWM output field are measured, providing complete information on the ultrafast nonlinear light-matter interaction. We also develop a theoretical model using time-dependent perturbation theory combined with a field-dependent dispersion relation
Gruzdev and Sergaeva 2018
, which simulates the DFWM process in a large band-gap crystal (such as MgO). We study the modulation of the amplitude and the temporal chirp of the output signal as we vary the time delay between incident pulses with attosecond resolution. It was found that both the amplitude and the chirp oscillate with time delay, and our model, with good agreement, predicts that this oscillation can only be possible due to the ultrafast modulation of the band structure of MgO in the presence of strong electric fields.

II
Results and Discussion

Our experiment employs the spectral interferometry based technique of TADPOLE
Fittinghoff et al. 1996
to completely characterize the degenerate Four-Wave Mixing (DFWM) signal generated using three 60 fs near-infrared (NIR) pulses centered around 800 nm (
\hbar\omega_{0}\approx 1.56eV
). These three linearly polarized pulses interact with the Magnesium Oxide (MgO) crystal with an intensity of
\sim 0.1
TW/cm
2
in a BOXCAR configuration
Shirley, Hall, and Eckbreth 1980
, producing a fourth beam in a new direction that satisfies the phase matching condition
\bm{k_{s}}=\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3}
. Figure
1
(a) illustrates the schematic representation of the highest valence band and the lowest conduction band of MgO, along with the Four-Wave Mixing (FWM) process occurring within the crystal. This FWM process occurs with a simultaneous modulation of the band by the net electric field present during the interaction, as discussed later. Since the bandgap of MgO is about 7.8 eV and the photon energy of each incoming pulse is 1.56 eV, the interaction is highly off-resonant. A reference beam then interferes with the output signal and spectral interference is measured using a spectrometer. Representative interference fringes from the measurement along with the experimental setup are shown in Figure
1
(b). From this interference, we can extract the temporal amplitude and phase of the electric field of the DFWM signal (more details in Section
IV.1
). The electric field is then measured for each time delay (
\tau
) between two phase-locked Gate pulses and the Probe pulse, with a time delay resolution of 200 as. Therefore, the complete electric field of the DFWM signal can be expressed as
E(t,\tau)=|E_{0}(t,\tau)|\exp{\left(-i\varphi(t,\tau)\right)}

|E_{0}(t,\tau)|
and phase
\varphi(t,\tau)
depend on real time
t
and the time delay between the Gate and Probe pulses
\tau
. Both the amplitude and the phase are plotted in Figures

(a) and (b), respectively. For each time delay
\tau

\varphi(t,\tau)=\varphi_{0}+a(\tau)t+b(\tau)t^{2}+c(\tau)t^{3}+d(\tau)t^{4}+e(\tau)t^{5}

(1)

The second-order coefficient
b(\tau)
, which is also called the temporal chirp, is extracted as a function of the time delay
\tau
. We integrate the extracted amplitude over the total pulse duration for each time delay; this is called the time-integrated amplitude and is defined as
\int|E_{0}(t,\tau)|\ dt
. The time-integrated amplitude as a function of time delay is shown in Figure

Chin, Calderón, and Kono 2001
. The time delay between the two mixing pulses leads to a modulation in the MIR sideband spectral intensity. However, sub-cycle modulation in electric field observables, as shown here, has not been observed previously.

The output DFWM signal depends on the third-order polarization of the crystal. The spectrum of third-order polarization can be written in terms of the nonlinear response function, i.e., third-order susceptibility
\chi^{(3)}(\omega;\omega,-\omega,\omega)
, which is a material property. However, for strong fields, one can have higher-order contributions. Most generally, the polarization is
Boyd 2008
,

P_{i}(\omega)=\chi^{(3)}_{ijkl}\mathcal{E}_{j}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{k}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{l}(\omega)+\chi^{(5)}_{ijklmn}\mathcal{E}_{j}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{k}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{l}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{m}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{n}(\omega)+\cdots

(2)

If the polarization vectors of the input and output electric fields are in the same direction, as in our experiment, the indices
i,j,k,\cdots
are the same. For the remainder of this discussion, we omit the polarization subscripts for brevity. Here,
\mathcal{E}(\omega)=(E_{1}(\omega)e^{i\bm{k_{1}}\cdot r}+E_{2}(\omega)e^{i\bm{k_{2}}\cdot r}+E_{3}(\omega)e^{i\bm{k_{3}}\cdot r}+\mathrm{c.c.})
. Based on the phase-matching condition mentioned above, the only terms that contribute to the measured signal are

\displaystyle P_{DFWM}=

\displaystyle\chi^{(3)}_{0}E_{1}(\omega)E_{2}^{*}(\omega)E_{3}(\omega)e^{i(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\cdot\bm{r}}

\displaystyle+\chi^{(5)}_{0}E_{1}(\omega)E_{2}^{*}(\omega)E_{3}(\omega)(|E_{1}|^{2}+|E_{2}|^{2}+|E_{3}|^{2})e^{i(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\cdot\bm{r}}+\cdots

(3)

Here,
E_{1},E_{2}
and
E_{3}
are the two Gate and Probe pulse electric fields in the frequency domain. The introduction of a time delay
\tau
between Gate and Probe pulses will result in a phase term of
\exp{(-i\omega\tau)}
in the electric field of the Gate pulses. However, this phase term does not result in a
\tau
-dependent modulation of the amplitude or phase of the polarization corresponding to the DFWM process when
\chi_{0}^{(3)}
is independent of the input fields (for a detailed calculation, see the supplementary material). In the presence of moderately strong fields, nonlinear response functions are expected to be significantly modified as observed, for example, in studies of the dynamical Franz-Keldysh effect using attosecond transient absorption spectroscopy
Lucchini et al. 2016
;
Chin, Calderón, and Kono 2001
. Thus, we model the attosecond modulation of the nonlinear response function due to the strong field interaction of electrons in the crystal such that
\chi^{(3)}\rightarrow\chi^{(3)}(\mathcal{E})
becomes an instantaneous function of the incoming electric fields.

Under high-intensity laser fields, the excited electron-hole pairs can cross beyond the edge of the Brillouin zone, leading to Landau–Dykhne–type transitions. Such effects have been observed in high-harmonic spectra
Uzan et al. 2020
;
Uzan-Narovlansky et al. 2022
. These transitions provide a powerful means to probe modifications of the band structure induced by intense laser pulses. To directly probe band-structure modifications, time- and angle-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy (Tr-ARPES) can be employed, as it images electron dynamics in materials with simultaneous momentum and energy resolution. Ab initio simulations have been performed to simulate Tr-ARPES in periodic systems driven by strong fields
Neufeld et al. 2022
. Most recently, a Tr-ARPES experiment on bismuth telluride demonstrated the observation of field-dressed bands in the presence of an intense laser field
Wang et al. 2025
. This suggests that the ultrafast modification of the band structure (or adiabatic field-dressed bands) offers a useful framework for understanding off-resonant electron interactions in strong-field regimes. Alternative perspectives for interpreting these field-dressed bands have also been proposed, most notably within the framework of Floquet theory
Tiwari, Gu, and Franco 2023
.

For a rigorous quantitative analysis, we start with time-dependent perturbation theory to simulate the DFWM experiment. However, the time-integrated amplitude and the chirp of the calculated signal electric field do not exhibit any of the oscillatory features observed in the experiment (see supplementary material figure
5
red vs blue curves). This aligns with the expectation that the field-free response function only accounts for perturbative wave mixing processes
Boyd 2008
;
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
and cannot include any field-driven modification of the response function. We thus introduce an ultrafast band modulation model
Gruzdev and Sergaeva 2018
to implicitly account for effects of the strong field on the nonlinear response function. In the presence of an external field, the crystal momentum of the electrons can be written as
\mathbf{p}(t)=\mathbf{p}_{0}+e\mathbf{A}_{ext}(t)

Vampa et al. 2014
;
Keldysh 1958a
;
Keldysh 1958b
;
Ghimire and Reis 2019
;
Gruzdev and Sergaeva 2018
;
Wu et al. 2015
, where
\bm{p}_{0}
is the initial momentum of the electron and
\bm{A}_{ext}(t)
is the vector potential of the external field. This results in the time-dependent modification of the electronic dispersion relation, i.e.
\epsilon(\mathbf{p})\rightarrow\epsilon(\mathbf{p}_{0}+e\mathbf{A}_{ext}(t))
where
\epsilon
is the energy of the electron. The details of the calculations are listed in the supplementary material. A theoretical investigation of the nonlinear optical response under strong light-matter interaction in MgO has previously been carried out using the Floquet–Bloch formalism
Popova-Gorelova and Santra 2024
. Here we demonstrate that incorporating non-perturbative effects (specifically, band-structure modulation) within a perturbative framework yields excellent agreement with the experiment.

Here, it is important to note that the phase matching conditions arising from terms that include the wave vectors

II

III
Conclusion

In summary, we have measured the complete electric field of the degenerate four-wave mixing (DFWM) signal in magnesium oxide using spectral interferometry. By analyzing field observables, such as the time-integrated amplitude and the chirp of the signal, as a function of the sub-cycle time delay between incoming pulses, we identified unique oscillatory features. We attribute these features to the ultrafast modulation of the nonlinear response function of the crystal due to modulation of the band structure. To support this interpretation, we simulated the DFWM experiment using time-dependent perturbation theory and found that oscillatory behavior could only be reproduced when instantaneous ultrafast band structure modulation driven by the strong field is included. Such ultrafast band modulation effects are known to leave signatures in other optical observables, such as high-harmonic spectra
Uzan et al. 2020
;
Uzan-Narovlansky et al. 2022
;
Koll et al. 2025
. Here, we demonstrate that the influence of field-dressed bands is clearly manifested in the field observables of nonlinear optical signals. Our results indicate that field observables can serve as direct probes of strong-field-driven attosecond electron dynamics in solid-state systems.

Furthermore, we have demonstrated that modeling of our experimental observations requires incorporating strong light–matter interactions into the modification of the third-order nonlinear susceptibility. This finding has significant implications for the manipulation and control of observables governed by the nonlinear response function. For instance, it is well established that signals generated through nonlinear optical processes can serve as sources of non-classical light. Processes such as parametric down-conversion (PDC) and degenerate four-wave mixing (DFWM) are known to produce squeezed light
Bondurant et al. 1984
;
Breitenbach, Schiller, and Mlynek 1997
;
Adamyan et al. 2015
;
Onodera et al. 2022
;
Fabre and Treps 2020
. The degree of squeezing in these processes is directly proportional to the nonlinear coupling coefficients of the respective systems, i.e.,
\propto\chi^{(2)}
for PDC and
\propto\chi^{(3)}
for DFWM
Fabre and Treps 2020
. More recently, it has been demonstrated that squeezing in DFWM signals can also be observed from ultrafast light–matter interactions with few-cycle pulses
Riek et al. 2017
;
Sennary et al. 2025
. With the strong sub-cycle modulation of
\chi^{(3)}
demonstrated in this work, we believe that it is possible to control the degree of squeezing on attosecond time scales. This could pave the path to achieve extreme levels of squeezing based on field induced nonlinearities that exceed current squeezing levels achieved using periodically-poled nonlinear materials
Vahlbruch et al. 2016
with numerous applications in quantum metrology and quantum information science.

IV
Methods

IV.1
Experimental Setup

In our setup of the DFWM experiment, as seen in Fig.
1
, three pulses with different propagation vectors interact with the medium in a non-collinear geometry and generate a fourth pulse due to a third-order nonlinear interaction in a BOXCAR configuration. Due to the non-collinear geometry, the signal pulse is spatially separated from the other three beams. The output signal has a momentum of
\bm{k_{s}}=\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3}
. The Probe and Gate beams are centered at 800 nm, with a pulse width of 60 fs and a repetition rate of 1 kHz. The Gate pulse is divided into two phase-locked Gate pulses (Gate 1 and Gate 2) using a mask. The Probe pulse is delayed with respect to the Gate pulses using an optical delay stage, and the Gate polarization can be rotated relative to the Probe polarization. However, in this experiment, the polarizations of all four pulses are fixed along the same direction. The MgO crystal is mounted on a controllable rotation mount to allow for precise control of the crystal angle with respect to the probe polarization. The Probe and the two Gate pulses each have a pulse energy of
\sim
500 nJ and form the input pulses in our DFWM measurement. Before the interaction, a Reference pulse is derived from the Probe pulse. After the interaction, the Signal pulse is isolated by an iris, so the residual Probe and Gate beams are blocked. The Signal pulse is passed through a high-contrast polarizer that extracts the polarization component of the Signal that is along the input polarization direction. The output signal pulse was completely measured using TADPOLE
Fittinghoff et al. 1996
, where the Reference pulse is combined with the Signal pulse in a spectrometer to obtain the measurement of spectral interference. Frequency-Resolved Optical Gating (FROG)
Trebino and Kane 1993
is used to completely characterize the Reference pulse. The measured spectral interference of the DFWM signal and the Reference can be written as:

\begin{split}S(\omega)&=S_{R}(\omega)+S_{S}(\omega)+\sqrt{S_{R}(\omega)}\sqrt{S_{S}(\omega)}\cos(\varphi_{S}(\omega)-\varphi_{R}(\omega)+\omega\,\tau_{R})\end{split}

(6)

S(\omega)
is the spectral intensity,
\omega
is the angular frequency, and
\varphi(\omega)
is the spectral phase. The subscripts
S
and
R
stand for signal and reference, respectively.
\tau_{R}
is the time delay between the Reference and the Signal and the Reference and the background.

IV.2
Simulation

To simulate the complete electric field of the DFWM signal, we employ time-dependent perturbation theory to evaluate the third-order expectation value of the microscopic current operator. The streaking of the electron momentum,
\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}0+e\bm{A}_{ext}(t)
, is explicitly incorporated. The emitted DFWM signal is expressed as
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{DFWM}}(t)\propto\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle\bm{J}(t)\rangle^{(3)}

\displaystyle\langle J^{i}(t)\rangle^{(3)}=

Nonlinear susceptibility modulation

The induced polarization spectrum

\displaystyle P(\omega)=\epsilon_{0}[\chi^{(1)}\mathcal{E}(\omega)+\chi^{(3)}(\omega;\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\mathcal{E}(\omega_{1})\mathcal{E}(\omega_{2})\mathcal{E}(\omega_{3})+\cdots]

\displaystyle E_{1}(t-\tau)

\displaystyle E_{2}(t-\tau)

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow E_{2}(\omega)e^{i(\bm{k}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega\tau)}+c.c

(9b)

\displaystyle E_{3}(t)

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow E_{3}(\omega)e^{i\bm{k}_{3}\cdot\bm{r}}+c.c

(9c)

\displaystyle\chi^{(3)}_{0}E_{1}(\omega)E_{2}^{*}(\omega)E_{3}(\omega)e^{i(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\cdot\bm{r}}

\displaystyle+\chi^{(5)}_{0}(|E_{1}|^{2}+|E_{2}|^{2}+|E_{3}|^{2})E_{1}(\omega)E_{2}^{*}(\omega)E_{3}(\omega)e^{i(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\cdot\bm{r}}+\cdots

By substituting this expression into the wave equation, it can be shown that the DFWM signal is generated when the phase matching condition

\chi^{(3)}\rightarrow\chi^{(3)}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle P^{(3)}_{eff}=\left[\chi^{(3)}_{0}+a\left(E_{1}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}+E_{2}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}+E_{3}(\omega)+c.c.\right)+\cdots\right]E_{1}(\omega)E_{2}^{*}(\omega)E_{3}(\omega)

(11)

\chi^{(3)}_{0}

\displaystyle P^{(3)}_{eff}=f_{0}(\omega)+f_{1}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}+g_{1}(\omega)e^{i\omega\tau}+f_{2}(\omega)e^{-2i\omega\tau}+g_{2}(\omega)e^{2i\omega\tau}+\cdots

(12)

\cos{\omega\tau}
due to the phase

Now similarly we can calculate the spectral phase of the third-order polarization as,

\displaystyle\phi(\omega)

\displaystyle=\arctan{\left(\frac{\Im(P^{(3)})}{\Re(P^{(3)})}\right)}

(13)

\displaystyle=\arctan{\left(\frac{\begin{subarray}{c}\Im(f_{0})+(\Im(f_{1})+\Im(g_{1}))\cos{\omega\tau}-(\Re(f_{1})-\Re(g_{1}))\sin{\omega\tau}\\

(14)

\sin\omega\tau

\displaystyle\phi(\omega)\approx\phi_{0}+\alpha\omega+\beta\omega^{2}+\cdots

(15)

\displaystyle\beta=-\frac{(\Im(f_{1})+\Im(g_{1}))/2+2(\Im(f_{2})+\Im(g_{2})}{\begin{subarray}{c}\Re(f_{0})+(\Re(f_{1})+\Re(g_{1}))\cos{\omega\tau}+(\Im(f_{1})-\Im(g_{1}))\sin{\omega\tau}\\

V.3
Semiconductor Bloch Equation

To fully understand strong field–matter interactions, we employ a non-perturbative microscopic model based on a one-dimensional two-band representation of MgO. When the crystal interacts with a strong laser field
E(t)
, electrons are driven from the valence band to the conduction band, the transition strength being determined by the element of the transition dipole matrix

d^{cv}_{k}=\frac{1}{ia_{0}}\int u^{*}_{c,k}\frac{\partial}{\partial k}u_{v,k}dx,

(21)

where
u_{n,k}
are the cell-periodic parts of the Bloch functions and
a_{0}
is the lattice constant
Wu et al. 2015
.

The transient electron dynamics under a strong, time-dependent electric field are described using the semiconductor Bloch equations (SBEs), which govern the evolution of the electron (hole) populations
n^{e}_{k}
(
n^{h}_{k}
) and the microscopic polarization
p^{h,e}_{k}
between them
Schubert et al. 2014
;
Hohenleutner et al. 2015
;
Huttner, Kira, and Koch 2017
:

\displaystyle\hbar\frac{\partial n^{e}_{k}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-2\hbar\Im\left[d^{cv}kE(t)\right]+|e|E(t)\nabla_{k}n^{e}_{k}-\frac{1}{2T_{1}}(n^{e}_{k}-n^{e}{-k}),

(22a)

\displaystyle\hbar\frac{\partial n^{h}_{k}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-2\hbar\Im\left[d^{cv*}kE(t)\right]+|e|E(t)\nabla_{k}n^{h}_{k}-\frac{1}{2T_{1}}(n^{h}_{k}-n^{h}{-k}),

(22b)

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial p^{h,e}_{k}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\left(\epsilon^{e}_{k}+\epsilon^{h}_{k}-i\frac{\hbar}{T_{2}}\right)p^{h,e}_{k}-d^{cv}_{k}E(t)\left(1-n^{e}_{k}-n^{h}_{k}\right)+i|e|E(t)\nabla_{k}p^{h,e}_{k}.

(22c)

and

denote the electron and hole dispersion relations, respectively, defined as

and
E^{h}_{k}=-E^{v}_{k}
, where

and

and
T_{2}=1.1~\mathrm{fs}

Hohenleutner et al. 2015
;
Huttner, Kira, and Koch 2017
. The input electric field parameters corresponding to the experiment are used in these calculations.
