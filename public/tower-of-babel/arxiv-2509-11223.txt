Glimpsing at Electron’s Form Factor through Quasiparticle Interference in Twisted Bilayer Graphene
D.-H.-Minh Nguyen, Francisco Guinea, Dario Bercioux

Abstract
We show that characteristics of the electron’s form factor in two-dimensional materials are observable in quasiparticle interference (QPI) spectrum. We study QPI in twisted bilayer graphene using real-space tight-binding calculations combined with the kernel polynomial method, which agrees excellently with the form factor norm obtained from the continuum Hamiltonian. The QPI signals, displaying a chiral structure, reveal all distinct interference processes between states near the Dirac points. We propose pseudospin textures of twisted bilayer graphene to explain all the interference mechanisms. Our results provide microscopic insights into electronic eigenstates of twisted bilayer graphene and suggest QPI as a potential method for probing the form factor, which governs the material’s quantum geometry and many-body states.



Introduction.—
Quasiparticle interference (QPI) is a method to study the spectral properties of electrons in quantum materials
Simon et al. 2011
;
Chen et al. 2017
;
Avraham et al. 2018
;
Yin et al. 2021
. It involves analyzing the Friedel oscillations created by quasiparticles scattered at a defect or impurity and then interfering with themselves. These oscillations appear in the electronic local density of states (LDOS) near the defect or impurity
Bena 2015
;
Villain et al. 2015
. By taking the Fourier transform (FT) of the variation in LDOS due to the impurity, we obtain information about the material’s band structure and quasiparticles’ properties. In practice, QPI is performed using scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) measurements, which provide high-resolution imaging of the LDOS at nanoscale. This method has been used to visualize topological surface states of Weyl semimetals
Zheng and Zahid Hasan 2018
;
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Sánchez-Barquilla et al. 2024
;
Hoffmann et al. 2025
, to identify the pairing symmetry
Hanaguri et al. 2009
;
Hänke et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Nag et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Siegl et al. 2025
or the major orbitals contributing to the superconducting states
Huang et al. 2025
in unconventional superconductors, and to reveal the role of electron’s pseudospin in interference
Brihuega et al. 2008
;
Mallet et al. 2012
and Berry phase
Dutreix, C. and González-Herrero, H. and Brihuega,
I. and Katsnelson, M. I. and Chapelier, C. and Renard, V.
T. 2019
in graphene.

Twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) is formed by stacking two graphene layers with a relative twist angle, leading to a moiré superlattice
Andrei and MacDonald 2020
. When the twist angle is close to the so-called magic angle
1.1^{\circ}
, the electronic bands constituting the Dirac cones become relatively flat
Trambly de Laissardière et al. 2010
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
, which is believed to give rise to a range of exotic phenomena observed in TBG
Cao et al. 2018
;
Lu et al. 2019
;
Yankowitz et al. 2019
;
Saito et al. 2020
;
Nuckolls et al. 2020
;
Cao et al. 2020
;
Oh et al. 2021
;
Jaoui et al. 2022
, such as superconductivity, correlated insulating states, and strange metal behavior. A great number of possible origins of the superconducting phase in TBG have been proposed, e.g., phonon-driven superconductivity
Lian et al. 2019
;
Zhu and Devereaux 2025
, plasmon-mediated superconductivity
Liu et al. 2018
;
Cea and Guinea 2021
;
Sharma et al. 2020
;
Peng et al. 2024
, and flat band superconductivity enabled by quantum geometry
Hu et al. 2019
;
Julku et al. 2020
;
Xie et al. 2020
;
Verma et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2023
;
Tanaka et al. 2025
. As the debates become heated, a profound understanding of the fundamental electronic properties of TBG is needed. Studies of QPI provide experimentally verifiable results and access to the wavefunction’s structure, understanding QPI in TBG is, therefore, of great significance
Phong and Mele 2020
;
Rhodes et al. 2025
.

Here, we investigate QPI of states at the Dirac cones of TBG with twist angles
\theta\gtrsim 2^{\circ}
, where many-body effects are negligible. Using a hand-waving argument, we show the connection between the QPI signals, i.e., the FT-LDOS, and the form factor of Bloch states, which determines the quantum geometric tensor
Törmä et al. 2022
;
Yu et al. 2025
, the bands’ topological properties
Song et al. 2021
, and the many-body states
Bernevig et al. 2021a
;
Lian et al. 2021
;
Bernevig et al. 2021b
. A rigorous derivation of this connection is given in the Supplemental Materials (SM). We then present the QPI signals of TBG obtained from atomistic tight-binding calculations, depicting all possible interference processes. The signals are microscopically explained by the form factor of eigenstates obtained from a continuum model. Pseudospin textures of projected eigenstates are proposed to explain the chiral structure of the signals. In the End Matter, we present the QPI signals when the defect location does not respect
C_{3}
symmetry and when the defect is a vacancy, and a comparison between commensurate and incommensurate TBG.

FT-LDOS and form factor.—
In a periodic lattice, the energy eigenstates of electrons are Bloch waves that can be written in terms of plane waves as
\psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{G}}C_{\bm{G}}(\bm{k})e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{G})\cdot\bm{r}}
. Here,
\bm{R}
and
\bm{G}
are direct and reciprocal lattice vectors, respectively, and
C_{\bm{G}}(\bm{k})=C(\bm{k}-\bm{G})
are plane-wave coefficients. In the presence of a defect, each Bloch wave comes from infinity towards the defect, scatters, and interferes with itself. To gain an intuition into the problem, we suppose that the scattered wave is an eigenstate at momentum
\bm{k}+\bm{p}
, the wavefunction of the electron is a superposition of the incident and scattered waves

\Psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})+A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}\psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{p}},

(1)

where
|A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}|\ll 1
since the defect is a small perturbation to the lattice. This interference creates standing waves in the electron’s LDOS

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\left|\Psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\right|^{2}=|\psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})|^{2}+|A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}\psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{p}}|^{2}

\displaystyle+A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}^{*}\psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{p}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})+A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}\psi_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{p}}(\bm{r}).

(2)

Neglecting the second-order term in
A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}
, the LDOS variation due to the defect is given by

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r})\approx\left|\Psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\right|^{2}-|\psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})|^{2}=2\Re\left[A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}^{*}\psi_{\bm{k}+\bm{p}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\psi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\right]

\displaystyle=2\Re\Bigg[A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}^{*}\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{G}^{\prime}}C_{\bm{G}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}+\bm{p})C_{\bm{G}}(\bm{k})e^{i(\bm{G}^{\prime}-\bm{G}-\bm{p})\cdot\bm{r}}\Bigg].

(3)

The Fourier transform of this LDOS variation reads

\displaystyle\Delta\varrho(\bm{q})=\int d\bm{r}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\Delta\rho(\bm{r})=2\Re\left[A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}\mathcal{M}(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k})\right]

(4)

with the form factor, or the overlap matrix
Bernevig et al. 2021c
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Bernevig et al. 2021a
;
Bernevig et al. 2021b
, given by

\mathcal{M}(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k})=\sum_{\bm{G}}C_{\bm{G}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}+\bm{q})C_{\bm{G}}(\bm{k}).

(5)

We see that the FT-LDOS of
\Delta\rho(\bm{r})
is determined by two factors: the scattering amplitude
A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}
denoting the probability of the initial state scattered to the final one, and the form factor responsible for the interference between those states. Since the form factor is generally complex, we express it as
\mathcal{M}(\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q})=|\mathcal{M}(\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q})|e^{i\xi(\bm{k},\bm{q})}
. Consequently, the FT-LDOS can be factorized into

\Delta\varrho(\bm{q})=|\mathcal{M}(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k})|\Re\left[A_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}e^{i\xi(\bm{k},\bm{q})}\right],

(6)

which means that it carries the signatures of the electronic form factor — vanishing
\mathcal{M}(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k})
results in an absent signal in the FT-LDOS.
For instance, in graphene’s QPI patterns, the intervalley signals are disconnected arcs forming circles with two nodes at which the intensity vanishes
Brihuega et al. 2008
;
Mallet et al. 2012
. These two nodes stem from the destructive interference of Bloch states
Mallet et al. 2012
, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k})=0
.

Models.—
In this work, we use an atomistic tight-binding model
Trambly de Laissardière et al. 2010
;
Moon and Koshino 2013
of TBG to compute the QPI and the Bistritzer-MacDonald continuum model
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2007
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
;
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2012
to examine the form factor. For the tight-binding one, within each layer, we only consider the nearest-neighbor hopping terms
t_{0}=-2.7
eV
Neto et al. 2009
. The interlayer hoppings between two atoms separated by
\bm{d}
are modeled through

\displaystyle t(\bm{d})=V_{pp\pi}(d)\!\left[\!1-\left(\frac{\bm{d}}{d}\cdot\bm{e}_{z}\right)^{2}\right]+V_{pp\sigma}(d)\!\left(\frac{\bm{d}}{d}\cdot\bm{e}_{z}\!\!\right)^{2}

(7)

with
V_{pp\pi}(d)=V_{pp\pi}^{0}\exp\left(-\frac{d-a_{\text{cc}}}{r_{0}}\right)
,
V_{pp\sigma}(d)=V_{pp\sigma}^{0}\exp\left(-\frac{d-d_{0}}{r_{0}}\right)
, and
r_{0}=0.184a
;
a=0.246
nm is graphene’s lattice constant.
Here,
V_{pp\pi}=-2.7
eV and
V_{pp\sigma}=0.48
eV are two Slater-Koster parameters

. We impose a cutoff value
d_{\text{c}}\approx 1.934a
, above which the interlayer hopping is negligible. When the two lattices are slightly rotated, their Brillouin zones rotate, resulting in a moiré Brillouin zone [Fig.
1
(a)]. For the continuum model, the Hamiltonian associated with the

\displaystyle H(\bm{k})=\sum_{\bm{K}_{1}}\Big[

\displaystyle\chi^{\dagger}_{\bm{K}_{1}}h_{1}(\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{1})\chi_{\bm{K}_{1}}

\displaystyle+\chi^{\dagger}_{\bm{K}_{1}+\bm{\kappa}_{a}}h_{2}(\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{1}-\bm{\kappa}_{a})\chi_{\bm{K}_{1}+\bm{\kappa}_{a}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mu=a,b,c}\left(\chi^{\dagger}_{\bm{K}_{1}}T_{\mu}\chi_{\bm{K}_{1}+\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}}+h.c.\right)\Big],

(8)

where
\bm{\kappa}_{a}=\frac{4\pi\theta}{3a}(0,1)
,
\bm{\kappa}_{b}=\frac{4\pi\theta}{3a}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right)
, and
\bm{\kappa}_{c}=\frac{4\pi\theta}{3a}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right)
are three reciprocal vectors connecting the

points;
\chi_{\bm{K}_{j}}
are two-component vectors, where each component represents a sublattice in a graphene layer;

h_{1/2}(\bm{k})=\frac{\sqrt{3}t_{0}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&|\bm{k}|e^{-i(\varphi_{\bm{k}}\mp\theta/2)}\\
|\bm{k}|e^{i(\varphi_{\bm{k}}\mp\theta/2)}&0\end{pmatrix}

(9)

describes the Dirac Hamiltonian at each
K
valley, and the coupling matrices between the Dirac cones

T_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}u&we^{i\gamma_{\mu}}\\
we^{-i\gamma_{\mu}}&u\end{pmatrix},\quad\left(\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b},\gamma_{c}\right)=\left(0,-\frac{2\pi}{3},\frac{2\pi}{3}\right).

(10)

Here, the labels
1
and
2
indicate the top and bottom layers, respectively,
\theta
is the twist angle,
\varphi_{\bm{k}}
is the argument of
k_{x}+ik_{y}
, and we choose
u=w=0.105

) — see Fig.

(b). The continuum Hamiltonian associated with the

Quasiparticle interference in TBG.—
We consider a defect modeled by setting the on-site energy of a carbon atom at the center of rotation on layer 1 to
\varepsilon_{0}=0.8|t_{0}|
. The defect strength is selected to induce all key scattering processes while keeping the signals clear
Hong et al. 2021
. We neglect the spontaneous time-reversal symmetry breaking due to the defect
Ulman and Narasimhan 2014
;
Lopez-Bezanilla and Lado 2019
;
Dietrich et al. 2023
;
Chang et al. 2024
. As translation symmetry is broken, we examine the tight-binding model in real space with the TBG sample being a circular disk of radius
100
nm. To get the LDOS variation within a circular area of radius
40

;
Le and Do 2018
;
Do et al. 2019
;
Do et al. 2020
;
Moldovan et al. 2020
;
João et al. 2020

\rho(\bm{r},\tilde{e})=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-\tilde{e}^{2}}}\left[g_{0}\mu_{0\bm{r}}T_{0}(\tilde{e})+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{c}}-1}g_{n}\mu_{n\bm{r}}T_{n}(\tilde{e})\right],

Single-layer interference and pseudospin.—
As we have demonstrated before, the FT-LDOS is proportional to the form factor norm. However, this relation holds only if we obtain the LDOS of all orbitals in the lattice, which is not the case for van der Waals multilayer systems since STS measurements mostly access signals from the top layer. Consequently, for the FT-LDOS of the top layer of TBG, we define the single-layer form factor
\bar{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q})
from the components of eigenstates projected onto that layer, i.e.,
\bar{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q})=\sum_{\bm{K}_{1}}C_{\bm{K}_{1}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}+\bm{q})C_{\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})
. Using the eigenvectors obtained from the continuum model, we find the single-layer form factor norm and show it in Figs.
3
(a) and
3
(b). Here, to compare with FT-LDOS, except for the co-winding intralayer interference, we multiply the others’ form factor norm by
\delta(\bm{K}_{j}-\bm{k},\bm{K}_{l}-\bm{k}-\bm{q})
, simulating back-scattering processes. The result is in excellent agreement with the FT-LDOS: the co-winding interlayer interference has six rings, each of which has a minimum value, forming a chiral structure, while the counter-winding interference is composed of rings with two minima. Hence, we can explain the chiral structure of the FT-LDOS of TBG by examining the eigenvectors of the continuum Hamiltonian. We note that the chiral structure of the form factor norm vanishes at the magic angle — see SM.

We focus on the co-winding interlayer interference, which takes place even for weak or long-range defect
Phinney et al. 2021
. The eigenvectors of the continuum Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle(\bm{k},\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{K}_{1}}C_{\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})e^{-i\bm{K}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}}+\sum_{\bm{K}_{2}}C_{\bm{K}_{2}}(\bm{k})e^{-i\bm{K}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}}

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad C_{\bm{K}_{1/2}}=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{\text{A}\bm{K}_{1/2}}(\bm{k})&\chi_{\text{B}\bm{K}_{1/2}}(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix}^{\text{T}},

(12)

where
C_{\bm{K}_{1/2}}
are plane-wave coefficients, whose two components,
\chi_{\text{A}\bm{K}_{1/2}}(\bm{k})
and
\chi_{\text{B}\bm{K}_{1/2}}(\bm{k})
, correspond to the A and B sublattice of graphene, respectively. The amplitude squared,
\left|C_{\bm{K}_{1/2}}\right|^{2}
, of each plane wave
\bm{K}_{1/2}
is shown in Fig.

\bm{k}

\bm{K}_{1}
point closest to
\bm{k}
and three
\bm{K}_{2}
points surrounding it are significant. Thus, we neglect the contributions from all other plane waves,
\Psi_{K_{1}}(\bm{k},\bm{r})\approx C_{\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})e^{-i\bm{K}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}}+\sum_{\mu=a,b,c}C_{\bm{K}_{1}+\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}}(\bm{k})e^{-i(\bm{K}_{1}+\bm{\kappa}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{r}}
. When
\bm{k}

\bar{\Psi}_{\alpha}(\bm{k}_{\alpha},\bm{r})\approx C_{\alpha\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k}_{\alpha})e^{-i\bm{K}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}}

\Psi_{\beta}(\bm{k}_{\beta},\bm{r})

\bm{K}_{2}-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}

\bar{\Psi}_{\beta}(\bm{k}_{\beta},\bm{r})\approx\sum_{\mu=a,b,c}C_{\beta\bm{K}_{2}-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}}(\bm{k}_{\beta})e^{-i(\bm{K}_{2}-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{r}}

\braket{\bar{\Psi}_{\alpha}(\bm{k}_{\alpha})|\bar{\Psi}_{\beta}(\bm{k}_{\beta})}\approx C_{\alpha\bm{K}_{1}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}_{\alpha})C_{\beta\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k}_{\beta}),

(13)

which varies as the two states traverse their Fermi contours, leading to the presence of a minimum and maximum in each ring in Fig.

associated with other nearest

As the single-layer interference of two states involves only the amplitudes of a single plane wave, each state is effectively represented by a vector
C_{\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})
. Owing to the sublattice symmetry of TBG,
\left|\chi_{\text{A}\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})\right|=\left|\chi_{\text{B}\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})\right|
,
we can thus define the pseudospin vector
\bm{s}(\bm{k})=C_{\bm{K}_{1}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})\bm{\sigma}C_{\bm{K}_{1}}(\bm{k})
with
\bm{\sigma}
being a vector of Pauli matrices. The pseudospin textures corresponding to the co-winding and counter-winding interference are shown in Figs.
3
(e) and
3
(f), respectively. The intralayer interference has pseudospin textures nearly similar to those of monolayer graphene, i.e., the pseudospin vector rotates clockwise or counterclockwise as we go along a Fermi contour without changing length. Meanwhile, the pseudospin vectors associated with interlayer interference almost point in one direction while their lengths vary. The pseudospin textures composed of directions and lengths of the pseudospin vectors explain the chiral structure of the FT-LDOS of TBG.

Conclusion.—
We have demonstrated the connection between the FT-LDOS and form factor of electronic eigenstates. The FT-LDOS of TBG exhibits all possible interference mechanisms, whose characteristics can be explained microscopically by the form factor obtained from the continuum model of TBG. Our results thus unveil insights into electronic wave structure in TBG and propose QPI measurement as a probe for the signatures of electron’s form factor in two-dimensional materials, opening a pathway towards examining materials’ quantum geometry and many-body states.

Analytical description of eigenstate interference.—
As we have seen in Fig.
3
(c), the contributions from the second-nearest neighbor
\bm{K}_{1/2}
points to the eigenstate are small, so we can make an approximation by neglecting them as well as further points. This allows us to use the tripod eight-band model of the continuum Hamiltonian
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
;
Bernevig et al. 2021c
to study the electronic states near a
\bm{K}
point. In particular, the continuum Hamiltonian governing states near a
K_{1}
valley reads

H_{\text{tri}}^{(\bm{K}_{1})}(\bm{k})=\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{1})&T_{a}&T_{b}&T_{c}\\
T_{a}^{\dagger}&h_{2}(\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{1}-\bm{\kappa}_{a})&0&0\\
T_{b}^{\dagger}&0&h_{2}(\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{1}-\bm{\kappa}_{b})&0\\
T_{c}^{\dagger}&0&0&h_{2}(\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{1}-\bm{\kappa}_{c})\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

At
\bm{k}=\bm{K_{1}}
, the two eigenvectors at the Dirac point have the form
\Psi_{\text{tri}}(\bm{K_{1}})=\begin{pmatrix}C_{0}&C_{a}&C_{b}&C_{c}\end{pmatrix}
with
C_{\mu}=-h_{2}^{-1}(-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu})T_{\mu}^{\dagger}C_{0}
and
C_{0}
being eigenvectors of the monolayer Dirac Hamiltonian. When
\bm{k}=\bm{K_{1}}+\bm{p}
(
|\bm{p}|\ll|\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}|
), we use perturbation theory by applying a unitary transformation to the Hamiltonian and neglecting the coupling between the Dirac cone and other remote bands. We obtain an effective Hamiltonian for the Dirac cone as

\displaystyle H_{\text{Dirac}}(\bm{p})=\frac{1-\frac{3w^{2}}{(\hbar v_{\text{D}}|\bm{\kappa}|)^{2}}}{1+\frac{3(u^{2}+w^{2})}{(\hbar v_{\text{D}}|\bm{\kappa}|)^{2}}}h_{1}(\bm{p}),

(15)

from which we obtain the eigenvalues
\mathcal{E}_{0\nu}(\bm{p})
and eigenvectors
C_{0\nu}(\bm{p})
(
\nu=1,2
) of the central Dirac cone. In our work, we assume
u\approx w
since the twist angles of interest are greater than
2^{\circ}
, which allows us to define
\alpha=\frac{w}{\hbar v_{\text{D}}|\bm{\kappa}|}
with
\hbar v_{\text{D}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}|t_{0}|}{2}
. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Dirac cone at valley
K_{1}
read

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{0\nu}(\bm{p})=\pm\frac{1-3\alpha^{2}}{1+6\alpha^{2}}\hbar v_{\text{D}},\quad C_{0\nu}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-e^{i\left(\varphi_{\bm{p}}-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

The pseudospin vectors associated with the intralayer interference form an angle
\varphi_{\bm{p}}+\pi-\theta/2
with the
x
-axis, as can be seen in the central valley in Fig.
3
(e).

To find the two eigenvectors of
H_{\text{tri}}^{(\bm{K}_{1})}(\bm{k})
that are associated with the Dirac cones, we assume that they have the form

\Psi_{\text{tri}}^{(\nu)}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+6\alpha^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}C_{0\nu}^{\text{T}}(\bm{p})&C_{a\nu}^{\text{T}}(\bm{p})&C_{b\nu}^{\text{T}}(\bm{p})&C_{c\nu}^{\text{T}}(\bm{p})\end{pmatrix}^{\text{T}}

(17)

and satisfy the eigen-equation
H_{\text{tri}}^{(\bm{K}_{1})}(\bm{K}_{1}+\bm{p})\Psi_{\text{tri}}^{(\nu)}(\bm{p})=\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\bm{p})\Psi_{\text{tri}}^{(\nu)}(\bm{p})
. In this case, the components are related by
C_{\mu\nu}(\bm{p})=\left[\mathcal{E}_{0\nu}(\bm{p})-h_{2}(\bm{p}-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu})\right]^{-1}T_{\mu}^{\dagger}C_{0\nu}(\bm{p})
. However, as
|\bm{p}|\ll|\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}|
, we can approximate this expression with the one used for
\bm{k}=\bm{K}_{1}
and get

\displaystyle C_{\mu\nu}(\bm{p})\approx-h_{2}^{-1}(-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu})T_{\mu}^{\dagger}C_{0\nu}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\varphi_{-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}}}\left[e^{-i\left(\gamma_{\mu}-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}-e^{i\varphi_{\bm{p}}}\right]\begin{pmatrix}1\\
e^{i\left(\gamma_{\mu}+2\varphi_{-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}}+\theta\right)}\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

At each
K_{2}
valley, the pseudospin vectors point along only one direction that forms an angle
\gamma_{\mu}+2\varphi_{-\bm{\kappa}_{\mu}}+\theta
with the
x
axis, independent of momentum
\bm{p}
. However, their amplitudes vary with respect to
\bm{p}
and vanish when
\varphi_{\bm{p}}=-\gamma_{\mu}+\theta/2
. This analytical description is in agreement with Fig.
3
(e).

Examination of QPI in TBG.—
Here, we present and comment on additional data of FT-LDOS in TBG for some special cases. The twist angle is
2.13^{\circ}
as in the main text. For data of other twist angles and Fermi energies, the reader may refer to the SM.

First, we consider the FT-LDOS in four cases of defect position, as shown in Figs.
4
(a) and
4
(b). At first glance, we see that the signal intensity increases as the defect is placed closer to the AA-stacking region, which is expected since the wave functions of the Dirac cone localize here. When the defect is distant from the center of either AA- or AB-stacking region, the
C_{3}
-rotational symmetry over the moiré length scale is broken, but a
C_{2z}
-rotational symmetry remains intact. The latter results from the reciprocal scattering and interference of eigenstates. The
C_{3}
-rotational symmetry is approximately preserved when the defect is relatively close to the center of rotation, e.g., the orange dot in Fig.
4
(a).

Next, we keep the defect at the center of the AB-stacking region and change it to an atomic vacancy. In this case, the FT-LDOS signals of co-winding interference are merged into a strong background stemming from the effect of the vacancy itself. Despite this, we are still able to differentiate the important signals, including the co-winding interlayer signals that reverse their chirality [Fig.
4
(c)] compared to the on-site energy defect. Meanwhile, the counter-winding signals remain the same [Fig.
4
(d)].

Finally, we examine the commensurability-incommensurability transition of TBG lattice. Throughout the main text of this work, we have considered a commensurate twist angle
\theta_{\text{c}}\approx 2.13^{\circ}
defined by the identity
\cos\theta_{\text{c}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{m^{2}+n^{2}+4mn}{m^{2}+n^{2}+mn}

Trambly de Laissardière et al. 2010
with
(m,n)=(15,16)
. This twist angle allows us to construct a rhombic supercell with side length
6.6
nm that is repeated in space, forming the TBG lattice. However, as our tight-binding calculations are performed in real space, without the reliance on the Bloch theorem, we can investigate twist angles where the two graphene layers are incommensurate. The FT-LDOS for an exact
\theta_{\text{i}}=2.13^{\circ}
is shown in Fig.
4
(e) and
4
(f), whose main features are identical to those of the commensurate lattice [Fig.
2
(b)]. However, if we compute the change in DOS due to the defect,
\Delta\rho(\omega)=\sum_{\bm{R}}\Delta\rho(\bm{R},\omega)
, we see a deviation between the two cases in Figs.
4
(g) and
4
(h). At the charge neutrality point, while
\Delta\rho(\omega)
resembles an odd function in energy for the commensurate lattice, it is similar to an even function in energy for the incommensurate one. The origin of this deviation remains an open question. Nevertheless, as the continuum model is assumed to work for both commensurate and incommensurate TBG
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
, these results validate this statement in calculations related to interference and defect scattering of Bloch waves. Moreover, the existence of the Dirac cone is observable in the QPI signals — see additional data in the SM, confirming that the emergent symmetries protect the Dirac cone when the lattice is incommensurate
Zou et al. 2018
.

Gated TBG.—
Although the single-layer form factor of TBG is captured well by the FT-LDOS, the quantity that determines the quantum geometry of the eigenstates, as well as is manifested in transport phenomena, is the form factor, which involves both layers of TBG. The form factor norm, however, does not exhibit a chiral structure since the chiralities of both layers cancel each other. To make this quantity chiral, we may apply a gate bias to the TBG since the Fermi surface of one chirality shrinks while the other enlarges, resulting in a net nonzero chirality. An example that would have a chiral structure accordingly in this case is the Hilbert-Schmidt quantum distance between eigenstates,
d(\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q})=\sqrt{1-\left|\mathcal{M}(\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q})\right|^{2}}
, whose infinitesimal change when
|\bm{q}|\rightarrow 0
gives the quantum metric of electrons
Bužek and Hillery 1996
;
Rhim et al. 2020
;
Abouelkomsan et al. 2023
.

In the presence of a gate bias, the Dirac cones of the continuum Hamiltonian Eq.
8
are now given by

h_{1/2}(\bm{k})=\frac{\sqrt{3}t_{0}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\pm V_{\text{g}}/2&|\bm{k}|e^{i(\varphi_{\bm{k}}\mp\theta/2)}\\
|\bm{k}|e^{-i(\varphi_{\bm{k}}\mp\theta/2)}&\pm V_{\text{g}}/2\end{pmatrix}

(19)

with
V_{\text{g}}
being the gate bias. The quantum distance is shown in Fig.
4
(i) for
V_{\text{g}}=80
meV. As expected, the quantum distance between eigenstates on different valleys of different layers exhibits a chiral structure. Its minimum value is 0 when
\bm{q}=0
, and its maximum value is 1, occurring between states on the same valley as well as on adjacent ones.

I
Quasiparticle Interference and Overlap Matrix of Bloch States

Here, we present a rigorous derivation of the relation between the overlap matrix of Bloch states and the Fourier transform (FT) of the Friedel oscillations in local density of states (LDOS). We consider a monolayer lattice with a single defect, which is atomically localized and described by a
\delta
-function at the origin:
V(\bm{r})=V_{0}\delta(\bm{r})
.

We write the Dyson equation of the advanced Green’s function in the coordinate representation as follows

\displaystyle G^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\int d\bm{r}_{1}d\bm{r}_{2}G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{1};\omega)\braket{\bm{r}_{1}|V|\bm{r}_{2}}G^{+}(\bm{r}_{2},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

(S1)

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\int d\bm{r}_{1}d\bm{r}_{2}G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{1};\omega)\delta(\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2})V(\bm{r}_{1})G^{+}(\bm{r}_{2},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

(S2)

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},0;\omega)G^{+}(0,\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega),

(S3)

which yields

G^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)=G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},0;\omega)\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}G_{0}^{+}(0,\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega).

(S4)

Inserting the spectral representation of the Green’s function

G^{+}_{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)=\sum_{n}\frac{\psi_{n}(\bm{r})\psi_{n}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})}{\omega-E_{n}+i\varepsilon}

(S5)

into the previous equation, we obtain

\displaystyle G^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}\sum_{n}\frac{\psi_{n}(\bm{r})\psi_{n}^{*}(0)}{\omega-E_{n}+i\varepsilon}\sum_{m}\frac{\psi_{m}(0)\psi_{m}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})}{\omega-E_{m}+i\varepsilon},

(S6)

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}\sum_{n,m}\frac{\psi_{n}^{*}(0)\psi_{m}(0)}{\left(\omega-E_{n}+i\varepsilon\right)\left(\omega-E_{m}+i\varepsilon\right)}\psi_{m}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\psi_{n}(\bm{r}).

(S7)

The change in LDOS due to the disorder is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\rho(\bm{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\left[G^{+}(\bm{r},\bm{r};\omega)-G^{+}_{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r};\omega)\right]

(S8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\left[\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}\sum_{n,m}\frac{\psi_{n}^{*}(0)\psi_{m}(0)}{\left(\omega-E_{n}+i\varepsilon\right)\left(\omega-E_{m}+i\varepsilon\right)}\psi_{m}^{*}(\bm{r})\psi_{n}(\bm{r})\right].

(S9)

For simplicity, let

A_{m}^{n}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}\frac{\psi_{n}^{*}(0)\psi_{m}(0)}{\left(\omega-E_{n}+i\varepsilon\right)\left(\omega-E_{m}+i\varepsilon\right)},

(S10)

and we arrive at
\Delta\rho(\bm{r},\omega)=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{n,m}\left[A_{m}^{n}(\omega)\psi_{m}^{*}(\bm{r})\psi_{n}(\bm{r})-A_{m}^{n*}(\omega)\psi_{m}(\bm{r})\psi_{n}^{*}(\bm{r})\right]
. Notice that each index is a combination of band index and wave vector,
n=(n,\bm{k})
, we write the eigenstates in form of Bloch wave functions as
\psi_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=u_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}=\sum_{\bm{G}}C_{m}(\bm{k}-\bm{G})e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{G})\cdot\bm{r}}
. The FT-LDOS is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\varrho(\bm{q},\omega)=\int d\bm{r}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\Delta\varrho(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{G}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\sum_{n,m,\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\left[A_{m\bm{k}}^{n\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)C_{m}^{*}(\bm{k}-\bm{G})C_{n}\left(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)e^{i\left(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{G}^{\prime}-\bm{k}+\bm{G}\right)\cdot\bm{r}}-c.c.\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{G}^{\prime}}\sum_{n,m,\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\Big\{

\displaystyle A_{m\bm{k}}^{n\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)C_{m}^{*}(\bm{k}-\bm{G})C_{n}\left(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)\delta\left(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{G}^{\prime}-\bm{k}+\bm{G}+\bm{q}\right)

\displaystyle-\left[A_{m\bm{k}}^{n\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)\right]^{*}C_{m}(\bm{k}-\bm{G})C_{n}^{*}\left(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)\delta\left(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{G}^{\prime}-\bm{k}+\bm{G}-\bm{q}\right)\Big\}

(S11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{G}^{\prime}}\sum_{n,m,\bm{k}}\Big\{A_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}+\bm{G}\right)}^{n\bm{k}}(\omega)C_{m}^{*}(\bm{k}

\displaystyle-\bm{G}^{\prime}+\bm{q})C_{n}\left(\bm{k}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)-\left[A_{m\bm{k}}^{n\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}+\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}(\omega)\right]^{*}C_{n}^{*}(\bm{k}-\bm{G}+\bm{q})C_{m}\left(\bm{k}-\bm{G}\right)\Big\}

(S12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{G}^{\prime}}\sum_{n,m,\bm{k}}C_{m}^{*}\left(\bm{k}-\bm{G}+\bm{q}\right)C_{n}

\displaystyle\left(\bm{k}-\bm{G}\right)\left\{A_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}+\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}^{n\bm{k}}(\omega)-\left[A_{n\bm{k}}^{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}+\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}(\omega)\right]^{*}\right\}.

(S13)

As the overlap matrix is defined by
\mathcal{M}_{mn}\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k}\right)=\sum_{\bm{G}}C_{m}^{*}\left(\bm{k}-\bm{G}+\bm{q}\right)C_{n}\left(\bm{k}-\bm{G}\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta\varrho(\bm{q},\omega)=\sum_{n,m,\bm{k}}\mathcal{M}_{mn}\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k}\right)\sum_{\bm{G}^{\prime}}\frac{A_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}^{n\bm{k}}(\omega)-\left[A_{n\bm{k}}^{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}(\omega)\right]^{*}}{2i}

(S14)

with

\displaystyle A_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}^{n\bm{k}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}\frac{\psi_{n\bm{k}}^{*}(0)\psi_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}(0)}{\left(\omega-E_{n\bm{k}}+i\varepsilon\right)\left(\omega-E_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}+i\varepsilon\right)},

(S15)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{V_{0}}{1-V_{0}G_{0}^{+}(0,0;\omega)}\frac{\psi_{n\bm{k}}^{*}(0)\psi_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}\right)}(0)}{\left(\omega-E_{n\bm{k}}+i\varepsilon\right)\left(\omega-E_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}\right)}+i\varepsilon\right)}=A_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}\right)}^{n\bm{k}}(\omega).

(S16)

Here, we have used the relation
u_{n(\bm{k}+\bm{G})}(\bm{r})=e^{i\bm{G}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
, which means that
\psi_{n}(0)
is periodic in
\bm{G}
. The eigenvalues
E_{n\bm{k}}
are periodic in the reciprocal space. Similarly,
\left[A_{n\bm{k}}^{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}-\bm{G}^{\prime}\right)}(\omega)\right]^{*}=\left[A_{n\bm{k}}^{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}\right)}(\omega)\right]^{*}
. Therefore, the FT-LDOS is reduced to

\displaystyle\Delta\varrho(\bm{q},\omega)=\sum_{n,m,\bm{k}}\mathcal{M}_{mn}\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k}\right)\frac{A_{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}\right)}^{n\bm{k}}(\omega)-\left[A_{n\bm{k}}^{m\left(\bm{k}+\bm{q}\right)}(\omega)\right]^{*}}{2i}.

(S17)

The FT-LDOS is a summation of the overlap matrix
\mathcal{M}
over momentum
\bm{k}
and bands, weighted by a factor that depends on the amplitude
A
. This amplitude carries the information of the disorder strength (
V_{0}
) and the Fermi surface.

We should notice that although the derivation above is for Bloch states that are defined in periodic crystals, the relation still holds in TBG with
\theta<10^{\circ}
, which is generally an incommensurate lattice.

II
Band Structure

In this section, we comment on the band structure of TBG for some twist angles. The band structure is considered for only commensurate twist angles, defined by
Trambly de Laissardière et al. 2010

\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}\frac{m^{2}+n^{2}+4mn}{m^{2}+n^{2}+mn}

(S18)

with
m
and
n
being coprime positive integers. These angles ensure that translation symmetry is present and we can identify a moiré supercell and the associative lattice vectors
(\bm{L}_{1},\bm{L}_{2})
. It is worth noticing that another equivalent relation, proposed by
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2012
, is
\cos\theta=\frac{3m^{2}+3mr+r^{2}/2}{3m^{2}+3mr+r^{2}}
, which can be transformed back to Eq.
S18
via the substitution
r\rightarrow n-m
. The band folding mechanisms are different for different values of
r
, as noted by Refs.
Mele 2010
and
Zou et al. 2018
.

Using the tight-binding and continuum models described in the main text, we present the band structure for some different twist angles in Fig.
S1
. The moiré Brillouin zone is assumed to be a hexagon defined by the reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{G}_{1}
and
\bm{G}_{2}
:
|\bm{G}_{1}|=|\bm{G}_{2}|=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{3}|\bm{L}_{1}|}
and
\angle(\bm{G}_{1},\bm{L}_{1})=\frac{\pi}{6}
. We see that the two models agree well with each other, especially for the conduction bands, when
r=1
. The deviation between the models in the valence bands comes from the strong breaking of particle-hole symmetry of the tight-binding band structure. On the other hand, it is not appropriate to compare the two models for
r=2
and
r=3
as the bands fold in different ways. The two cases that are considered most frequently are
\gcd(r,3)=1
and
\gcd(r,3)=3
. In the former,
\bm{K}_{1}
and
\bm{K}_{2}^{\prime}
are folded into the same moiré momentum but they are approximately uncoupled for small twist angles. Therefore, the two valleys
K
and
K^{\prime}
stay uncoupled and can be treated independently. The moiré Brillouin zone is composed of either
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, or
K_{1}^{\prime}
and
K_{2}^{\prime}
. Meanwhile, when
\gcd(r,3)=3
,
\bm{K}_{1}
is folded into
\bm{K}_{2}
and
\bm{K}_{1}^{\prime}
is folded into
\bm{K}_{2}^{\prime}
. For small twist angles, the direct coupling between valleys of the same winding is suppressed
Zou et al. 2018
, leading to a moir’e Brillouin zone composed of two valleys with opposite winding numbers.

Nevertheless, as we will see below (Figs.
S3
and
S4
), the characteristics of the QPI patterns are immune to such commensurate conditions. Those of commensurate angles
3.01^{\circ}
and
4.05^{\circ}
are similar to the QPI patterns of other angles.
