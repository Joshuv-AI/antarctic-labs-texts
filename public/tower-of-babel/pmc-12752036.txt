Effectiveness of social-emotional learning program on psychological distress and dysregulation in students with specific learning disorders
Authors: Shahrooz Nemati, Rahim Badri Gargari, Mahsa Rasi, Elaheh Mahmoudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effectiveness of a social-emotional skills training program on emotional dysregulation and psychological distress among students with Specific Learning Disorders (SLD). The quasi-experimental design involved 32 fourth- to sixth-grade students diagnosed with learning disorders, who were individually randomly assigned to an experimental group receiving the intervention or a control group. The training, implemented in a school setting, consisted of 12 sessions focused on enhancing self-awareness, emotional regulation, and social skills. Pre- and post-test assessments measured outcomes using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) and an emotional dysregulation questionnaire. Results indicated statistically significant improvements in both psychological distress (p < 0.001, η² = 0.661) and emotional dysregulation (p < 0.001, η² = 0.717) in the experimental group. These findings suggest that social-emotional skills training effectively mitigates the psychological challenges faced by students with SLD, enhancing their emotional and social competencies. The study underscores the necessity of integrating social-emotional learning programs in educational settings to support the well-being and academic success of children with learning disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03662-4.

Introduction

Specific Learning Disorder (SLD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that impairs academic skills, including reading, writing, and mathematics [8]. Rooted in biological factors, SLD disrupts the brain’s ability to process verbal and non-verbal information, leading to difficulties in mastering academic tasks [19]. These challenges often result in underperformance at school and increase the risk of long-term consequences, such as psychological problems, school dropout, and unemployment [18]. These academic struggles frequently give rise to significant psychological difficulties, necessitating targeted interventions.

Among these difficulties, psychological distress and emotional dysregulation are prominent in children with SLD. Psychological distress refers to an adverse mental state marked by anxiety, depression, and symptoms like restlessness and irritability [4]. In contrast, emotional dysregulation is the impaired ability to manage cognitive, behavioral, and emotional responses, often tied to executive functioning deficits and neural regulation issues in the prefrontal cortex and limbic system [17]. In students with SLD, these conditions manifest as behavioral issues, procrastination, and academic underachievement [11, 20]. Given these challenges, Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) offers a promising solution.

SEL is a structured intervention that teaches core competencies—self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making—to enhance emotional regulation and social integration [5]. Evidence from recent meta-analyses highlights SEL’s effectiveness in improving emotional regulation, particularly among neurodiverse populations [7, 12]. For students with SLD, SEL provides a framework to develop coping strategies and improve academic engagement [1, 2]. However, despite its potential, the specific effects of SEL on psychological distress and emotional dysregulation in this population remain underexplored.

Moreover, female students with SLD face unique challenges that further complicate their psychological well-being. Research indicates that girls with SLD are more prone to internalizing disorders, such as anxiety and depression, compared to their male counterparts [18]. This disparity is often linked to societal pressures and gender-specific expectations, which can intensify the emotional and psychological struggles associated with SLD. Consequently, tailored interventions like SEL are essential for addressing these gender-specific vulnerabilities and promoting equitable outcomes in educational settings.

Given these considerations, the present study aimed to examine whether a structured SEL intervention can significantly reduce psychological distress and emotional dysregulation in female students with SLD. By addressing this research gap, the study seeks to advance theoretical insights and inform practical strategies in educational and therapeutic contexts.

Methods

This study utilized a quasi-experimental pre-test/post-test design with a control group to evaluate the effectiveness of a social-emotional learning (SEL) program on psychological distress and emotional dysregulation in students with Specific Learning Disorders (SLD). Participants

The statistical population comprised fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-grade students with learning disorders in public schools in Tabriz, East Azerbaijan, Iran. A convenience sampling method was employed to select 32 female students aged 9–12 years, chosen due to heightened emotional regulation challenges in this developmental period and gender-specific patterns in internalizing disorders [18]. All participants had clinically diagnosed SLD (16 with dyslexia, 10 with dyscalculia, 6 with comorbid conditions), confirmed using standardized assessments per DSM-5 criteria. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 16) or the control group (n = 16) using simple randomization to ensure balanced group allocation.

The sample size of 32 was determined based on feasibility and prior studies indicating that small to medium effect sizes can be detected in similar interventions with comparable sample sizes (Cohen, 1988). All participants were from middle-income families, as reported in school records.

Intervention

The experimental group participated in a 12-session SEL program adapted from Beh-Pajooh et al. [3, 10, 15], grounded in the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) framework, which emphasizes five core competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making (CASEL, n.d.). Each session lasted 60 min and included activities such as role-playing (e.g., for anger management), discussions, and skill practice, using standardized materials like workbooks and visual aids. Trained facilitators followed identical scripts, and intervention fidelity was maintained through session checklists and weekly supervisor reviews. The control group continued their regular school activities without receiving the intervention.

To ensure content validity, a two-dimensional table outlining objectives and content was developed prior to implementation. The materials were reviewed by five elementary school teachers and professors, with revisions incorporated based on their feedback. The final package was approved by the supervising professor.

Measures

Psychological distress

Psychological distress was assessed using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), a 21-item self-report questionnaire measuring depression (e.g., low mood, hopelessness), anxiety (e.g., physiological hyperarousal), and stress (e.g., nervous tension, irritability). Originally developed for adults, the DASS-21 was adapted for children through simplified item phrasing, visual aids, breaks during administration, and parental support to ensure comprehension. The Iranian validation study by Pak Nejad et al. (2014) reported high reliability (α = 0.90–0.93) and test-retest reliability (0.84–0.90) for adolescents, with cross-cultural validity supported by Henry and Crawford (2005; α = 0.82–0.93).

Emotional dysregulation

Emotional dysregulation was measured using the Adolescent Dysregulation Inventory (ADI; Mizich et al., 2001), a 30-item self-report scale assessing emotional, behavioral, and cognitive dysregulation. Responses are scored on a four-point Likert scale (0 = never, 3 = always), with higher scores indicating greater dysregulation (maximum score = 90). The ADI was adapted similarly to the DASS-21 for age-appropriateness. It has established validity (α = 0.71–0.92) and significant correlations with emotional and behavioral distress measures [16]; Marci, 2008).

Procedure

The study received ethical approval from the University of Tabriz Ethics Committee. Informed consent was obtained from parents, and verbal assent was secured from all child participants, with assurances that participation was voluntary, confidential, and posed no financial burden. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose and that results would be interpreted collectively, with individual feedback available upon request. Pre-test assessments using the DASS-21, ADI, Psychological Playfulness Questionnaire, and Academic Performance Questionnaire were conducted for both groups. The experimental group then received the 12-session SEL intervention, while the control group continued their regular school activities. Post-test assessments were administered immediately after the intervention. To address ethical considerations, the SEL program was offered to the control group post-study upon their consent and with school approval.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations) were calculated for all variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of regression slopes was verified to meet ANCOVA assumptions. One-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to compare post-test scores between groups for psychological distress and emotional dysregulation, with pre-test scores as covariates. Effect sizes (partial eta squared, η²) were calculated to assess the practical significance of the intervention. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The mean age of participants in the experimental group was 9.45 years (± 0.82), while the mean age for the control group was 9.49 years (± 1.12).

To assess the normality of the psychological dysregulation variable, the Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted, indicating that the distribution was normal (p > 0.05). To evaluate the equality of variances for this variable, Levene’s test was employed. The results of Levene’s test for psychological dysregulation in the pre-test phase yielded F = 1.982 (p = 0.279), while in the post-test phase, the results were F = 1.291 (p = 0.265). These findings demonstrate homogeneity of variance between the groups.

Additionally, the results for the homogeneity of regression slopes were F = 0.214 (p = 0.510), indicating no interaction between the dependent variable (pre-test) and the independent variable (groups), thereby confirming the homogeneity of the regression slopes. Given that all assumptions for one-way ANCOVA were satisfied, this statistical method was employed to examine the hypothesis regarding the effectiveness of the social-emotional skills training program on psychological dysregulation in students with SLD.

Discussion and conclusion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a 12-session social-emotional learning (SEL) program on psychological distress and emotional dysregulation in female students with Specific Learning Disorders (SLD). The results demonstrated significant reductions in both outcomes for the experimental group compared to the control group. Specifically, psychological distress scores decreased from a mean of 50.38 to 44.13 (adjusted mean: 43.62 vs. 51.68 for controls), and emotional dysregulation scores dropped from 73.13 to 61.56 (adjusted mean: 60.91 vs. 72.77 for controls). The large effect sizes (η² = 0.661 for distress, η² = 0.717 for dysregulation) indicate a notable impact of the program, as confirmed by analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) results (F = 56.623, p < 0.001 for distress; F = 73.389, p < 0.001 for dysregulation).

These findings align with a robust body of research supporting SEL’s efficacy in neurodiverse populations. Meta-analyses by Chatterjee Singh and Duraiappah [7, 12] demonstrate that SEL programs enhance emotional regulation and reduce psychological distress in students with diverse needs. Similarly, Badri et al. [1], Nemati, & Bardel [13, 14] reported reduced distress following SEL interventions, while Sousa et al. [21] found that emotional competence training mitigates anxiety in children. Recent studies further confirm SEL’s benefits for students with learning disabilities, noting improvements in self-esteem and emotional resilience [6]. The current study’s focus on SLD-specific outcomes adds to this evidence, particularly for female students, who are at higher risk for internalizing disorders like anxiety [18].

The SEL program’s observed effects likely stem from its alignment with the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) framework, which emphasizes five core competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making (CASEL, n.d.). These skills address the emotional and social challenges faced by students with SLD, who often experience low self-esteem and difficulties in social interactions due to academic struggles [19]. By fostering emotional regulation and social competence, the program enabled participants to manage stress and build healthier relationships, reducing distress and dysregulation. The large effect sizes suggest that SEL may be a clinically meaningful intervention for this population.

Practically, these findings support the integration of SEL programs into special education curricula to support students with SLD. Schools should train teachers to deliver SEL interventions and administer adapted psychological measures, such as the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) used in this study. Combining student self-reports with parent and teacher ratings could enhance assessment accuracy. Additionally, the evidence supports broader adoption of SEL to improve academic engagement and mental health, as noted in a 2023 meta-analysis [9].

Limitations

Despite the promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 16) and female-only, which restricts generalizability to male students with SLD or larger/diverse populations, given potential gender differences in emotional regulation [18]. Second, the quasi-experimental design lacked full randomization, potentially introducing selection bias. Third, reliance on self-report measures (even with parental support) may introduce biases; multi-informant assessments (e.g., teacher ratings) are recommended. Fourth, the short-term pre-post assessment means long-term effects on psychological distress and emotional dysregulation remain unknown. Finally, the study was conducted in an “Iranian educational context”, so findings may not fully generalize to other cultural/socioeconomic settings.

Future research should employ larger mixed-gender samples, randomized controlled trials, multi-informant measures, longitudinal follow-ups, and explore impacts on academic performance/social functioning to confirm and expand these effects.

Conclusion

Social-emotional learning programs offer a promising approach to supporting female students with Specific Learning Disorders. By enhancing emotional regulation and social competencies, SEL was associated with significant reductions in psychological distress and dysregulation, potentially enhancing well-being and academic potential. While this study’s focus on female students highlights their unique needs, the principles of SEL are likely applicable to all students with SLD. Schools are encouraged to prioritize SEL integration and teacher training to maximize these benefits. Further research with diverse samples and long-term follow-up is needed to fully realize SEL’s potential in special education.
