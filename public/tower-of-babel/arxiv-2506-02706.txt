Collective Intelligence Outperforms Individual Talent: A Case Study in League of Legends
Angelo Josey Caldeira, Sajan Maharjan, Srijoni Majumdar, Evangelos Pournaras

Abstract
1 1 footnotetext: Corresponding author: Sajan Maharjan, School of Computing, University of Leeds, Leeds, UK, E-mail: scsmah@leeds.ac.uk Gaming environments are popular testbeds for studying human interactions and behaviors in complex artificial intelligence systems. Particularly, in multiplayer online battle arena (MOBA) games, individuals collaborate in virtual environments of high realism that involves real-time strategic decision-making and trade-offs on resource management, information collection and sharing, team synergy and collective dynamics. This paper explores whether collective intelligence, emerging from cooperative behaviours exhibited by a group of individuals, who are not necessarily skillful but effectively engage in collaborative problem-solving tasks, exceeds individual intelligence observed within skillful individuals. This is shown via a case study in League of Legends , using machine learning algorithms and statistical methods applied to large-scale data collected for the same purpose. By modelling systematically game-specific metrics but also new game-agnostic topological and graph spectra measures of cooperative interactions, we demonstrate compelling insights about the superior performance of collective intelligence.



1
Introduction

The recent advancements in computing technology have evolved the way individuals socialize and interact; online gaming platforms being one of the most prominent mediums for social interaction and cooperation. Zhong reports that in China there are 147 million people playing online games, thus representing new forms of community, social interaction, and collaboration
[
1
]
. These online gaming environments are popular testbeds for studying human interactions and behaviours in complex artificial intelligence systems. The simulation of diverse scenarios ranging from strategic decision-making to adaptive behaviours within online gaming environments allows the testing of complex real-world mechanisms under controlled low-cost conditions. Furthermore, the virtual worlds of such online games are populated with autonomous artificial agents and other human players, thus offering opportunities for interesting interactions that guide players and enhance social dynamics
[
2
]
, resulting in a
collective intelligence
derived from the synergistic cooperation of humans and computing agents. Collective intelligence is the general ability of a particular group to perform well across a wide range of different tasks
[
3
]
. In the field of computer science, it involves the study of how groups of one person and one computer interact (human-computer interaction) as well as how larger groups of people interact with computing technologies to cooperate and make intelligent decisions, for instance, in the development of collaborative software such as Wikipedia
[
4
]
.

Collective intelligence is prominently demonstrated in many online gaming environments among which multiplayer online battle arena (MOBA) games serve as a notable example. MOBA games are a subgenre of real-time strategy games in which two teams, each consisting of 5 individual players, compete against each other with each player controlling a single character (called a
champion
), while strategy revolves around individual character development and cooperative team play in combat
[
5
]
. Riot Games’
League of Legends
is the most popular MOBA game played worldwide with over 150 million monthly active players
[
6
]
.
League of Legends
features 169 uniquely playable champions with their own set of skills, that relies on an individual player’s micro-management ability, paired with a strong understanding of champion
mechanics
along with the collective effort of a team to think strategically and showcase team-work abilities. The game is structured around a map called
Summoner’s Rift
, and the primary objective of the game is to destroy the opponent team’s base, a tower structure called the
Nexus
, while the game progresses with champion development via earning gold, gaining experience, crafting items and scoring kills.

The collaborative dynamics observed among randomly assigned players within a team, each controlling a distinct champion with unique sets of abilities and working towards the shared objective of winning the game, potentially exemplifies the presence of collective intelligence in MOBA games. In fact, exchange of in-games chat
messages
,
signals
and
assists
to secure game objectives elicits a quantitative measure of collective intelligence. Leavitt et al. show that non-verbal communication in the form of in-game
pings
improves team strategy and performance
[
7
]
. On the other hand, individual players can excel in executing their champion
mechanics
and abilities, thus displaying high levels of individual intelligence. Using in-game data to model various metrics of individual and collective intelligence, this paper aims to outline the importance of such individual as well as team-based collective intelligence metrics in influencing the outcome of a game. That is, it tests the hypothesis
H1: collective intelligence outperforms individual intelligence of the players in winning a game
. Additionally, Riot Games maintains a two-tiered in-game ranking system (called
tier
and
division
) that establishes a hierarchy of players based on their recent in-game performances. Using such information, this paper also tests another hypothesis, that is,
H2: Highly-skilled players exhibit higher levels of collective intelligence compared to less-skilled players
. The main contributions of this work are – (i) a framework to evaluate individual and collective intelligence of players in
League of Legends
using in-game metrics and machine learning algorithms, and (ii) the availability of dataset and source code that can be used to reproduce the results.

2
Related Work

Collective intelligence can be observed in human-computer interaction, collective-decision making and collaborative software development; in the decentralized collective learning of self-managed economies
[
8
]
, swarm intelligence in the large-scale coordination of drones
[
9
]
, etc. Woolley et al. first provided the evidence for a collective intelligence factor,
c
in human groups, suggesting that
c
is not correlated with the average or maximum individual intelligence of the group members, but rather it emerges from the way group members interact
[
10
]
. Krause et al. conclude that the potential benefits of human swarm intelligence depend on the type of problem, while advocating for diversity in groups, and that high-performing individuals can be outcompeted by a similar size group of low-performing individuals
[
11
]
. Likewise, Santos et al. argue that social diversity catalyzes cooperative behaviour
[
12
]
. The sports and gaming industries have integrated collective intelligence paradigms to solve problems and generate insights beyond the individual capabilities of a single player.

Duch et al. quantified the contribution of individual players towards the overall team performance during the 2008 European Cup football tournament via network graphs that modeled the flow of passes among players in a team
[
13
]
. Grund confirmed that centrality of interaction between players leads to a decreased team performance by analyzing soccer passes of teams in the English Premier League
[
14
]
. Szabo et al. investigated team behavioral dynamics in
escape room
settings and reported that more effective teams tend to solve problems via coordinated, balanced communication patterns, maintaining a dynamic alternation between different types of tasks
[
15
]
. In the realm of digital gaming,
42 Entertainment
launched
I Love Bees
, an alternate reality game used for the marketing of
Halo 2
in which over 600,000 players collaborated in solving puzzles related to the game prior to its release
[
16
]
. Gonzalez-Pardo et al. conducted an empirical study describing how collective intelligence algorithms like ant-colony optimization and genetic algorithms can be used for problem-solving within video games
[
17
]
. Kokkinakis et al. explore the relationship between fluid intelligence and video game performance in MOBAs under controlled laboratory conditions, and found that fluid intelligence correlates significantly with in-game rank
[
18
]
.

Specifically within
League of Legends
, Kim et al. have shown that teams exhibit group collective intelligence and that such measures for collective intelligence correlate with their in-game performance
[
19
]
. In addition, Kim et al. have shown that collective intelligence can predict the competitive performance of teams controlling for the amount of time that different individuals play together as a team, and that collective intelligence is positively correlated with the presence of a female team member and the average social perceptiveness
[
20
]
. Sapienza et al. study the evolution of individual performance within ad-hoc teams and claim that player performances in successive matches deteriorates over the course of a gaming session
[
21
]
. Kou and Gui report the existence of rich social interactions within temporary teams via semi-structured interviews with experienced players
[
22
]
. Mora-Cantallops and Sicilia have identified 4 different clusters of players associated with team cohesiveness, based on a systematic classification of player-centric networks measured by a set of graph metrics
[
23
]
.

Subsequent research by Mora-Cantallops and Sicilia investigate team efficiency in professional player settings through directed graph networks that indicate the flow of assists between players and argue that team efficiency is positively affected by interactions between players while centralization of resources is detrimental
[
24
]
. Likewise, Kho et al. employ neural networks to reverse engineer games played in three professional league settings and have established an induced logic across different leagues that determine the winning outcome of a game depending on different objectives scored within the game
[
25
]
. Maymin generated high-frequency data on millions of ranked games and calibrated a live, in-game win probability model based on the conditions of the game at each moment
[
26
]
. Also, Ani et al. use ensemble methods to predict the outcome of a match, from a dataset of 1500 games using 97 different
pre-match
and
within-game
features.

The novel contribution of this research emerges from an inter-disciplinary approach that explores collective intelligence among random groups of players in the virtual world of
League of Legends
, by using different machine learning methods to predict the winning outcome of games. That is, the novelty of this work does not lie in the sophisticated design of machine learning models to better predict the winning outcome of a game, but rather quantifies and contrasts the importances of individual and collective intelligence behaviours among players via an empirical analysis of the collected data. The data-driven analysis presented in this work reveals an interesting insight that explains the nature of individual and collective team behaviors i.e.
acquiring
vs
sharing
and
cooperative
vs
non-cooperative
; and necessitates the importance of altruistic behaviors.

3
Methods

To evaluate the aforementioned hypotheses within the
League of Legends
gameplay environment, different statistical methods and machine learning algorithms are implemented. Figure
1
depicts the overall framework of the methods used in this study. Individual in-game performance metrics are extracted from the experimental dataset while team-based collective intelligence metrics are computed from their corresponding directed graph networks that outline the flow of resources between players in a team. Exploratory factor analysis is applied to both individual and team-level data, revealing latent performance factors. Such latent factors are input as features to predict the winning outcome of a game using different classification algorithms in order to understand the nature of such intelligent behaviours. Comparison of the classification results based on the individual and collective performance factors determine whether or not to accept the hypothesis
H1
. Furthermore, k-means clustering is used to profile players and teams into different clusters. Crosstabs analysis of the winning rate of different teams having different player clusters reinforces whether or not to accept the hypothesis
H1
. Finally, statistical hypothesis testing on
H2
is employed to check for the significance in the difference of collective intelligence levels of teams (and players) across different ranks. Details for each step are further outlined as follows–

League of Legends

3.1
Experimental Dataset

League of Legends
game data played on official servers is publicly accessible via a set of RESTful APIs provided by Riot Games
[
27
]
. In order to fetch game data for competitive matches played by a given player, a
summonerName
which uniquely identifies the player, is required. A preliminary list consisting of 10 random
summonerName
s corresponding to the 10 different ranking tiers, is used as initial seed data. For each of these seed
summonerName
s, the API offered by Riot Games is used to retrieve – (a) a list of
N=100
recent matches played by the player, and for each of these matches, (b) detailed match data that include game outcome and individual match performance of the players, (c) a list of game events that occurred at periodic time intervals within the match and, (d) public rankings of the associated players. The API fetches not just the match performance of the seeded
summonerName
player, but also retrieves details for the other 9 players relevant in a given match. An additional set of data i.e., the most recent
N=10
games for all the relevant players identified from the initial fetch, is collected, thus removing any initial selection bias. Using the process described above, a total of 103K matches are fetched. However, due to different modes of gameplay offered in the game, the collected dataset does not exclusively consist of ranked competitive matches only. Furthermore, two different players in this dataset may have played in the same game, or there can be games where a player was disconnected (due to Internet connection issues, or abandoning the game) from the server resulting in less than 10 players, or the game was terminated early via a
remake
or a
surrender
. To remedy such issues, the dataset was filtered to remove duplicate entries, retrieving only those games in which all 10 players were present throughout the game in a competitive ranked match mode that was not terminated early (i.e., game duration is not less than 23 minutes). Thus the experimental dataset consisted a total of 31,055 ranked matches with 164,571 unique players (see
Appendix
for details on data-fetch process). For each game, in-game performance metrics of all players were extracted and directed graph networks were generated to formulate team-based graph metrics.

3.2
In-game Performance Metrics

The dataset collected from the API endpoints, particularly the
match detail
endpoint, consists of fields that reflect the respective in-game performance of all players in a match in terms of metrics such as –
experience gained, gold earned, kills score, assists given, champion deaths, vision score, elite monsters kills, towers destroyed, minions killed
, etc. along with the overall match outcome. Feature scaling was performed to resolve the discrepancies in the range of values for the in-game metrics. Additionally, per-minute metrics for
gold earned, assists given, vision score, experience gained
and
minions killed
were also computed to scale and normalize the range of features in the dataset. Additionally, champions played by individual players feature their own set of unique
skillshots, spells
and
items
used during the gameplay which can impact the outcome of a game. However, incorporating such features only adds complexity without providing high-level insights on the nature of intelligent behaviors. Thus champion
skillshots, spells
and
item builds
are not accounted as in-game performance metrics for simplicity. Details on the gameplay and in-game metrics is further explained in
Appendix
.

3.3
Graph Metrics

Graph metrics characterize the flow and centrality of resources between individuals in a team. Such graph metrics signify how teams and individuals interact, strategize and cooperate throughout the game to secure different in-game objectives and resources. To explore the interaction among players, we utilize the
match timeline
data to fetch game events during which players in a team assisted one another to secure objectives and resources such as opponent kills, enemy tower destruction, elite monster kills as well as unrecorded assists for
positional map-pressure
resulting in a kill.
Positional map-pressure
refers to the intelligent positioning of a player on the map to prevent an opponent player from escaping, forcing its kill without actively participating in it (see
Appendix
).

Based on the flow of these assistance between players in a team, directed graph networks with 5 nodes (signifying the 5 players) with directed, weighted edges corresponding to the flow of assistance are created for both teams in each game. For any such graph network, let
N=5
be the total number of nodes,
A
be the total observed assists between players in the team, and
w_{ij}
be the weight of directed edge (i.e. the number of assists) from node
i
to node
j
. Then, the following graph metrics are computed –

Player In-Degree.
This metrics signifies the weighted sum of assists received by a player from all other players in the team i.e. the in-degree of player
i
is:
C_{ID}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ji}

Player Out-Degree.
This metric signifies the weighted sum of assists given by a player to all other players in the team i.e. the out-degree of player
i
is:
C_{OD}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}

Team In-Degree Centrality.
This metric signifies the extent to which assists are centralized towards any player in a given team i.e.:
C_{I}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(C_{ID}^{max}-C_{ID}(i))}{(N-1)A}

Team Out-Degree Centrality.
This metric signifies the extent to which assists are given out by any player in a given team i.e.:
C_{O}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(C_{OD}^{max}-C_{OD}(i))}{(N-1)A}

Effective Graph Resistance.
This metric measures the robustness of a graph network
[
28
]
. A higher value of this metric signifies the lack of cooperation among nodes in the graph network. This metric is computed by extracting eigenvalues (
\mu
) from the corresponding Laplacian matrix of the graph network for each team i.e.:
EGR=N\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}

A directed graph network signifying the flow of interactions among players in a given team is illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:

Each graph has 5 nodes corresponding to player roles and weighted, directed edges signify the frequency and flow of all forms of assistance from one player to another. 3.4
Individual Metrics vs Collective Metrics

An individual’s performance in a game is accounted by the in-game performance metrics of the individual player such as
gold earned, champion experience, kills
, etc. Correlation analysis and variance inflation factor tests revealed multi-collinearity between in-game metrics which were thus removed from the feature set. Additionally, the graph metrics i.e.
player in-degree
and
player out-degree
which signify the overall receipt and provision of all assists for a single individual player is also accounted towards individual metrics. Thus, the individual metrics include –
vision score per minute, gold earned per minute, champion experience per minute, player in-degree
and
player out-degree
. The collective metrics are composed of average values of in-game performance metrics of the players in a team along with the graph metrics that signify the overall flow of assistance within the team. Thus, the collective metrics consist of
average team gold earned per minute, average team vision score per minute, average team champion experience per minute, team in-degree centrality, team out-degree centrality
and
effective graph resistance
.

3.5
Exploratory Factor Analysis

Prior work by Mora-Cantallops and Sicilia have confirmed that gold earned is one of the most important factors determining the game outcome
[
24
]
. Gold is earned by all players at a steady rate throughout the game in addition to champions and minion kills, tower takedowns and other objectives. However, only using gold earned as a feature doesn’t identify the nature of intelligent behaviors exhibited by individuals and teams. Therefore, exploratory factor analysis is performed to uncover latent factor variables that explain the nature of intelligent interactions between individuals and teams. The optimal number of explanatory factors for individuals and teams are identified via Scree analysis. Factor loading values of the latent factors infer the nature of interactions in both individuals and teams i.e.
acquiring
vs
sharing
factor and
cooperative
vs
non-cooperative
factor.

3.6
Machine Learning Algorithms

Classification:
The winning outcome of a game in most cases is determined either by the exceptional performances of an individual player or by the collective efforts of a team. The explanatory factors that suggest the nature of intelligent behaviours in individuals and teams are then input as features to predict the winning outcome of the game using different classification algorithms i.e., binary logistic regression, decision tree classifier, random forest classifier, and XGBoost classifier. Classification results are evaluated in terms of accuracy, f1-score and area under the curve metrics. To accept or reject
H1
, the evaluation scores by the collective performance factors are compared against individual performance factors.

Clustering:
Further analysis involves profiling individual players and teams based on the explanatory factors into three distinct clusters for each. K-means clustering algorithm is used to categorize individual players and teams into their respective clusters. Based on the average values of the performance factors within each cluster, we manually add labels to these clusters. The frequency of wins (or winning rate) within such distinct clusters of players and teams are then identified. Cross tabulation analysis enables how often a team cluster with a majority of particular type of players win the game. Comparison of the winning rates of such player and teams clusters via cross-tabulation analysis provides further rigour to accept or reject the hypothesis
H1
.

4.3
Highly-skilled players exhibit higher levels of collective intelligence

To test for
H2
, we investigated the association of graph-based collective intelligence metrics with the average rank of a team. An in-game matchmaking algorithm ensures that players having similar skill-levels are often matched together as teammates or opponents. Players who play the game competitively are rewarded with
League Points
which establishes a hierarchy of players based on a two-tiered ranking system of
tiers
and
divisions
(see
Appendix
). Players who excel in the game are signified by a higher positioning in the ranking ladder. We find that highly-skilled players (and thus teams) as signified by the higher rankings within the server, exhibit higher levels of collective intelligence compared to less-skilled teams i.e. teams in ranking tiers such as
Diamond
and above demonstrate higher levels of cooperation and flow of resources within their team. Therefore, higher ranking teams have lower values for effective graph resistance as well as lower in-degree and out-degree centrality compared to lower ranking teams, as shown in Figure
5
. This difference in the levels these graph-based collective intelligence metrics across different rankings is found to be statistically significant by Kruskal-Wallis test (i.e.
p-val
=
1.08\times 10\textsuperscript{-33},6.02\times 10\textsuperscript{-157}
and
1.63\times 10\textsuperscript{-34}
for in-degree centrality, out-degree centrality and effective graph resistance respectively).

Figure 5:
Higher ranking teams (Diamond and above) exhibit higher levels of collective intelligence signified by lower values of effective graph resistance, in-degree centrality and out-degree centrality, compared to lower ranking teams (Silver and below).

5
Discussion

In this paper, we argue and present evidence for the statement –
collective intelligence outperforms individual talent
via a case study in
League of Legends
. By analyzing the frequency of wins, the results show that acting on the collective interest of a group leads to a higher likelihood of winning games rather than relying on individual skills and abilities. Moreover, we demonstrate that cooperative teams with low performing individuals outperform non-cooperative teams with high performing individuals.

Previous work by Mora-Cantallops and Sicilia report that centralization of resources is detrimental to team efficiency and performance
[
24
]
, and our findings align with it. However, unlike this previous work in which the graph metrics –
team in-degree centrality
and
team out-degree centrality
were computed based on
kills
assistance, our work accounts for all forms of assists (in securing game objectives) including a tailored measure of passive assistance in
positional map-pressure
. Likewise, this work is the first study in which effective graph resistance is modeled to study team networks in
League of Legends
. Another work by Kim et. al
[
19
]
analyze group collective intelligence in controlled participants, however this work incorporates a large-scale participation of anonymous, more representative set of players, thereby enhancing the validity and general applicability of the findings. In their work, they show that higher
tier
ed teams exhibit higher levels of collective intelligence which is congruent with our findings where we present statistically significant differences in the levels of collective intelligence across teams of different
tier
rankings. Additional concerns regarding the validity of our work is clarified in
Appendix
.

In our results, we find that non-cooperative teams having a majority of sharing (or low-performing) individuals win 50% of the games (see Table
4
). However, the observed number games for such non-cooperative teams is significantly low (
N=2
). Thus, the winning rate statistic for this particular case can be misleading. To remedy such issues, a larger dataset could be collected and analyzed. As we demonstrate a dominating winning ratio of cooperative teams with low-performing individuals over non-cooperative teams with high-performing individuals in head-to-head matches, it would be interesting to identify the distribution of such winning ratio across different
tier
rankings. Additionally, a compelling study could be carried out whereby random, specific individuals are profiled for matches where they display exceptional performances in non-cooperative teams as well as mediocre performances in cooperative teams, and how often such displays can be attributed to wins, and their significance in climbing up the ranking ladder. As Krause et al.
[
11
]
and Santos et al.
[
12
]
advocate diversity for increased collective performance, the diversity in the types of
champions
picked in game, can be studied to extend this work.

The phenomenon of collective intelligence in multiplayer online gaming environments merits investigation into diverse gaming genres, for instance in First-Person Shooter (FPS) games, Massive Multi-player Online Role Playing Games (MMORPGs). Unlike FPS games (which mostly focus on individual reflex and mechanics) and MMORPGs (in which games can span over a longer time period), the authors chose to study collective intelligence in MOBA games due to their well-defined team structures, player roles, resource and objective trade-offs that bear similarities to collective actions in real-world problems. Specifically, the authors chose to conduct this study in
League of Legends
because – (a) it is the most popular MOBA game played worldwide, (b) features API endpoints that allow access to large-scale structured data, and (c) personally due to the authors’ familiarity and inclination towards the game. The framework that is presented in this work can also be used in other MOBA games like DOTA 2 to quantify the levels of outperformance. However, implementation will require tweaking the source code (data structures and API calls).

On a broader perspective, our results present the contradictory dynamics observed in the behavior of an individual when optimizing for self vs when optimizing for the collective. Such dynamic behaviors can also be observed in the design of sustainable infrastructure products. For instance, individuals can be tempted to use non-sustainable products which are inexpensive (immediate lower negative payoff) compared to eco-friendly products which can be costly (higher negative payoff) to implement but over a long run offers a greater benefit to the entire population
[
29
]
. Insights presented in this study can thus stipulate independent individuals to prioritize collective altruistic behaviors over individual predisposition in complex social systems. Furthermore, collective efforts can be crucial to attain the desired goals not just within online gaming ecosystems, but also in other more fundamentally critical aspects of our online (as well as offline) existence. For instance, collective efforts can help restrict the misinformation that is rampant in online social networks. Policies and regulations set up by the decentralized and collective efforts of the population in socio-economic systems such as consumer markets, governments, etc. prevent monopoly and abuse of authority resulting in products and systems that are favorable for the whole collective.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we explored the presence of collective intelligence among players in the MOBA game,
League of Legends
. Our findings conclude that collective intelligence characterized by sharing of resources, flow of information and cooperative behavior outperforms individual intelligence. Our results reiterate the paradoxical imperative for collaboration and cooperation in highly competitive environments for achieving optimal outcomes. Further work complementing this research can be done by testing for the performance of collective intelligence and individual intelligence in real-world applications beyond gaming environments.

A.3
Resources: Gold, Experience and Vision

Experience is a metric that enables players to increase their Champion’s level, in turn, increasing the Champion’s strength. Experience is primarily gained by farming minions or jungle camps. However, it is not imperative that a player kills the minion to be awarded experience. Gold is used by players to purchase in-game items, making their champion stronger. Gold is one of the most important factors which contributes towards winning the game. Gaining gold and buying items enables a champion to carry their lead forward by helping their team achieve additional objectives. Gold is earned in various ways – passively at a steady rate of time, scoring kills on enemy champions, last-hit on minions and elite-monster kills, destroying enemy tower structures, etc. Vision is a valuable resource measured by ‘vision score’. However, unlike gold or experience, no advantage is given to the player in terms of strength or currency. Players spend their gold to purchase
wards
– these items are placed strategically on the map to reveal dark areas that cannot be seen otherwise. These dark areas may reveal the position of an enemy player, thus, yielding a strategic advantage to the other team. Moreover, players can destroy wards placed by the opposing team – denying them such information. Doing these actions (purchasing, placing and destroying wards) increases their vision score.

A.4
Turrets and Elite-Monsters

Turrets (also known as towers), are positioned in every lane and surround each teams’ base. Turrets will attack all enemy characters within their range, one at a time. Players gain gold by attacking and destroying the opposing teams’ turrets. Destruction of an enemy turret is an example of achieving an objective. Turrets can also be destroyed by minions, in which case players receive no monetary benefit. Elite monsters are considered common objectives as they do not belong to a team, spawning mid-way at the rift. All players in the game look to kill them and reap their benefits. A small amount of gold is rewarded for securing elite monsters objectives. However, their main benefit is improving the base statistics of players (of the team that secure the objective) such as movement speed, healing rate, abilities cool-down, etc, which may be temporary or over the duration of the entire game.

A.5
Kills and Assists

A player is awarded with a kill score if the player uses their abilities or spells such that an enemy’s health points is reduced to zero. A player scoring a kill on enemy champion is rewarded with a significant amount of gold. A player scores an assist when the player helps a team-mate score a kill on an enemy champion. Assists are also awarded for tower destruction and elite-monster kills. Assistance can be in the form of dealing damage to enemy champions or providing utility effects such as healing, shielding, etc. to ally champions that results in the kill. Players receive a small amount of gold when providing assists.

A.6
Positional map-pressure assist

A new tailored metric called the “positional map-pressure" assist, which reflects how a player within a team exhibits intelligent map positioning thereby resulting in an opponent kill, is computed for each match. Figure
8
depicts how positional map-pressure can occur in-game. Opponent kill assists, tower destruction assists or elite monsters kill assists by a given player can be directly retrieved by processing the
match timeline
data. However, positional
map-pressure
assist is not directly accounted within the game. As such, by using the match timeline data, positioning of all team players an opponent kill event were identified, and a player was awarded a positional map-pressure assist if the player’s position was within a specified radius during the kill event.

Another blue team player B obstructs Y from escaping, thus inciting Y to engage in a fight resulting in a kill score for blue. A.7
Ranking System and League Points

The ranking system used in League of Legends employs a two-tiered structured ladder designed to measure and reward player skill through competitive gameplay in ranked matches. The ranking system consists of ten
tiers
:
Iron, Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Emerald, Diamond, Master, Grandmaster,
and
Challenger
. Each tier below
Master
is subdivided into four
divisions
(for example,
Gold IV
to
Gold I
), with IV being the lowest and I the highest within a tier.

League Points
(LP) are the core mechanism that enable progression in the ranking system. Players earn LP by winning matches and lose LP upon defeat. In each sub-division of tiers below
Master
, LP are ranged between 0 and 100, such that upon exceeding 100 LP for a given sub-division within a tier, the player is promoted to the next immediate division (or the next tier if current division is I) and LP is reset to 0. For promotion in tiers above
Master
, there is no fixed rule, but rather depends on pool size of players in the upper tiers as well as higher amount of LP is required. The amount of LP gained or lost per match is influenced by several factors, including a hidden matchmaking Rating (MMR), which reflects a player’s skill level relative to their rank. Higher MMR results in larger LP gains for victories and smaller losses for defeats

A.8
Data Retrieval Process

Data on
League of Legends
ranked matches played on any of the official server maintained by Riot Games is freely accessible via a set of RESTful API endpoints. Figure
9
shows the overall data-retrieval process used in the study. An initial set of 10 (one for each ranking tier) random, hand-picked summoner names are stored in a text file as seed data. A python script reads this seed configuration file and makes the following API calls –

•

summonerName
endpoint: This API endpoint fetches the corresponding unique identifier, called the puuid, for a given summoner name.

•

puuid
/ids endpoint: This API endpoint returns a list of match identifiers for the most recent N-games (configurable) played by a player with a given puuid.

•

matchid
endpoint: This API endpoint fetches the details of a given match id for each player in both teams such as - gold earned, items crafted, experience gained, kills scored, champion roles and levels, etc. Details of each match is saved as a JSON flatfile.

•

matchid
/timeline endpoint: This API endpoint fetches the details of various game event (such as kills, level-ups, items purchase, etc.) as it occurs chronologically over the duration of the game with a given match id. Timeline details of each match id is saved as a JSON flatfile.

•

entries/by-summoner/
summonerId
endpoint: This API endpoint retrieves the rank information data for a given summoner identified by their unique puuid.

The client script thus reads the initial seed summoner names and writes match data and match timeline files in the local directory. Such files are merged and data filters, concerning game-type, duration, disconnection, etc. are applied to remove inconsistent rows.

A.9
Threats to Validity

There may be concerns of initial selection bias when handpicking the seed
summonerNames
used in the data collection process. However, we ensure that the additional fetch retrieves data for players that are not in the initial list. In 50% of the dataset, it is ensured that a single unique player has played no more than 2 games, resulting in a total of 164K unique players. Thus, any selection bias over the initially selected 10 seed summoners is unlikely to impact the results. Additionally, the collected dataset features games played exclusively within the European servers.
League of Legends
is played on different servers across different geographical locations i.e., America, Europe, Asia and Oceania, with notable differences in players’ pool size and skills. However, we expect data from different servers to exhibit similar pattern of results. In-game metrics such as
towers destroyed, elite monster kills, enemy champion kills
and
minion kills
are highly correlated with
gold earned
. Thus, these features are removed. Variance inflation test yields values of
VIF>5
for the feature
gold earned
, which signifies high correlation with other features. However, it was not removed from the feature set as it is the most contributing feature for the acquiring factor. The suitability of factor analysis at the individual and collective levels yield KMO values of
0.57
and
0.61
respectively, which is poor but acceptable. Our results demonstrate that the winning rate for a cooperative team with a majority of resource acquiring players is as high as 94%. However this may be misinterpreted. It does not mean that the same cooperative team with the same set of high-performing individuals will win 94% of their games. This is because the Riot Games’ matchmaking algorithm ensures that as players win games, they are positioned higher in the ranking ladder, as a result they are matched with relatively more-skilled, difficult opponents when winning, and ensuring that most players roughly win 50% of their games. Finally, since the outcome of a match is a zero-sum game (no draws are applicable for a match), a team can lose not because it is bad, but the opponent has a small advantage over the team. On the other hand, a non-cooperative team can also win exploiting a blunder from the opponents. Such extreme cases have not been handled in the dataset.
