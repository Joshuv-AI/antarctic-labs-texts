Zero-dose vaccination status among children in Malawi: patterns, predictors, and geographic hotspots
Authors: Donnie Mategula, Judy Gichuki, Akosua Sika Ayisi, Esther A. Chirwa, Christopher Kaliisa, Boston Zimba, Randy G. Mungwira, Mike Chisema, Jonna Jeurlink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zero-dose children, defined as children who have not received any routine vaccination, are an important indicator of inequity in immunisation systems. Although Malawi reports high national vaccination coverage, little is known about the characteristics and spatial distribution of children who remain unvaccinated. We examined the determinants and geographic patterns of zero-dose status in Malawi. We analysed data from the 2015-16 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey, including 681 children aged 1 to 35 months. Zero-dose status was defined as receiving no routine childhood vaccine in this analysis. Descriptive analyses and mixed-effects logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with zero-dose status, while adjusting for clustering at the enumeration area level. A binomial geostatistical model was applied to map fine-scale spatial variation across the country. Overall, 8.1% of children (n = 55) were zero-dose. Younger children were more likely to be unvaccinated, with one third of zero-dose children aged 1 to 11 months. In adjusted analyses, children aged 12 to 23 months and those aged 24 months or older had significantly lower odds of being zero-dose compared with children aged 1 to 11 months. Home delivery strongly increased the likelihood of being zero-dose (AOR = 4.28; 95% CI: 1.31 to 14.01). More than one antenatal care visit was associated with substantially lower odds of zero-dose status (AOR = 0.06; 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.31). Spatial modelling identified high-prevalence hotspots in Karonga, Nkhatabay, Nkhotakota, Mangochi, and parts of Chikwawa. Zero-dose children in Malawi are concentrated among those with limited maternal health system contact and in specific geographic areas with persistent access barriers. Strengthening antenatal and delivery services, improving early postnatal follow-up, and deploying spatially targeted outreach can help address these inequities and accelerate progress toward national and global immunisation goals.

Introduction

Immunization is one of the most effective public health interventions1. Malawi, like many other countries, aims to ensure that no one is left behind in accessing essential vaccines2. Despite progress in routine immunization, some children remain completely unvaccinated. These are known as zero-dose children, defined as children who have not received a single routine vaccine, usually measured using the first dose of diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine (DTP1)3–6.

Zero-dose children are a key indicator of inequity in the health system. The Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) highlights the importance of using high-quality data to identify and reduce the number of zero-dose children2,4,5,7. Recent global estimates show that about 9% of children in 43 low- and middle-income countries are zero-dose8. The COVID-19 pandemic worsened these gaps by disrupting immunization services in many settings9.

Previous studies show that zero-dose status is linked to socioeconomic disadvantage, low maternal education, young maternal age, and poor access to antenatal and delivery services10,11. Children living in remote rural areas, informal urban settlements, or conflict-affected areas are also more likely to be zero-dose12. Global efforts such as The Big Catch-Up aim to recover and strengthen routine immunization after the pandemic9. Analysis of zero dose children supports targeted interventions that fit the local context and address the causes of missed vaccinations.

Malawi’s Expanded Programme on Immunization has achieved high coverage for several vaccines. For most antigens, the coverage estimates have been above 90% for the last five years13. However, subnational gaps remain. Some children continue to miss all routine vaccinations. The characteristics and spatial distribution of zero-dose children in Malawi are not well described. This limits the ability of policymakers to plan targeted and equitable immunization strategies.

Geospatial methods can help identify where zero-dose children are located and which factors contribute to missed vaccination14,15. Combining survey data with spatial analysis can highlight high-burden areas and guide more efficient allocation of resources.

This study uses data from the 2017 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) (the most recent round with a fully released dataset. although a 2023 DHS was conducted, its microdata has not yet been made publicly available). The objectives of this work are to:

Overall characteristics

Overall, 681 children were included in the analysis. Slightly more than half were male (344, 50.5%), while 337 (49.5%) were female. The largest age group was 24–35 months of age (349, 51.3%), followed by 12–23 months (225, 33%) and 1–11 months (107, 15.7%). Prevalence of zero-dose children

Out of a total of 681 children aged 1–35 months included in the analysis, 8.1% (n = 55) were identified as zero-dose i.e., they had not received any childhood vaccination at the time of the survey.

Descriptive characteristics by zero dose status

Table 1 shows the characteristics of children by zero-dose status. Child age was significantly associated with zero-dose status (p < 0.001). Similarly, zero-dose prevalence was higher among children from the poorest households at 9.8% compared with those from poorer (4.2%) and richest households (4.4%).Differences across education and wealth groups were small and did not reach statistical significance at the 5% level.

Marital status showed a statistically significant association with zero-dose status (p = 0.048), with a higher proportion of zero-dose children among married women (9.2%) compared with 7.1% among women who had never been in a union and 2% among widowed, divorced or separated mothers. Multivariable regression results

Table 2 presents the results of the mixed-effects logistic regression examining factors associated with zero-dose status. After adjusting for sociodemographic and maternal healthcare variables and accounting for clustering at the enumeration area level, several predictors remained significantly associated with zero-dose status.

Child age showed a strong association with zero-dose status. Compared with infants aged 1–11 months, children aged 12–23 months had 69% lower odds of being zero-dose, while those aged 24 months and above had 72% lower odds of being zero dose. Female children had 56% lower odds of being zero dose compared with male children.

Place of delivery and ANC visits were also associated with zero dose status. Children born at home had more than four times higher odds of being zero-dose compared with those delivered in a health facility. Compared with mothers who had no ANC visits, those with more than one visit had 94% lower odds of having a zero-dose child, while a single ANC visit trended toward a protective effect but was not statistically significant (p = 0.054).

The random-effects estimates indicated substantial clustering of zero-dose status at the community level, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.44, suggesting that 44% of the unexplained variance was attributable to differences between clusters. In simple terms zero-dose status of a child depends a lot more on the community they live in.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed assessment of zero-dose children in Malawi, combining nationally representative survey data with geostatistical modelling to examine both individual-level determinants and the spatial distribution of unvaccinated children. Although Malawi maintains relatively high national coverage for routine childhood vaccines, the finding that 8% of children remain completely unvaccinated demonstrates that meaningful gaps persist and are concentrated in subpopulations. These results are consistent with global analyses showing that zero-dose children tend to cluster in settings where health systems face persistent structural and social challenges1,6,8.

Maternal health service utilisation showed the strongest association with zero-dose status. Children born at home were significantly more likely to be unvaccinated than those delivered in health facilities, and limited ANC attendance showed a similarly powerful association. These findings echo evidence from multiple low- and middle-income countries, where weak contact with the health system during the antenatal and perinatal period increases the likelihood of missed opportunities for early intervention6,11,17. ANC visits offer a critical entry point for counselling, risk assessments, and linkage to postnatal services. The protective effect observed among mothers with more than one ANC visit highlights the continued importance of strengthening early maternal-health touchpoints to prevent children from falling outside the immunisation system altogether.

Younger age was another important correlate of zero-dose status. Children aged 12 months and older had substantially lower odds of being zero-dose than those aged 1–11 months. This likely reflects greater opportunity over time for contact with immunisation services, including delayed or catch-up vaccination. This pattern reflects persistent barriers to early postnatal care in Malawi, including postpartum practices, mobility, accessibility, and delays in seeking care. While late vaccination is preferable to none, the loss of timely protection places infants at unnecessary risk and undermines the effectiveness of the national immunisation schedule18. Although female children had lower adjusted odds of zero-dose status in this analysis, this finding was modest and should be interpreted cautiously, as the study was not designed to explain sex-based differences in caregiver behaviour.

Socioeconomic gradients were evident in crude analyses but lessened after adjustment. Although zero-dose children were more common among poorer households and among mothers with no education, household wealth was not independently associated with zero-dose status in the adjusted model. In the Malawi context, where routine childhood vaccination is provided free of charge, this suggests that economic position may not act primarily through direct payment for services. Instead, the observed crude socioeconomic differences may reflect broader structural and access-related inequalities, including geographic accessibility, service contact, and access to health information19,20. However, the cross-sectional nature of the data does not allow us to determine the direction or mechanism of these relationships, and these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with vaccination only indirectly. Previous evidence in similar settings has also found association with other key factors such as media exposure. Specifically, limited radio listening was linked to higher zero-dose status, highlighting that access to information and health communication remains a key determinant of immunization demand15,21. These findings align with the Immunization Agenda 2030, which emphasises the need to strengthen community engagement and communication to build trust and generate informed demand2.

Spatial analysis revealed substantial geographic heterogeneity in zero-dose prevalence. High-burden areas were concentrated in districts such as Karonga, Nkhatabay, Nkhotakota, and parts of Mangochi and Chikwawa. These patterns are consistent with routine reports showing that geographic barriers, population mobility, livelihood-driven migration, and facility distribution shape vaccination access in Malawi and neighbouring countries. The spatial clustering observed in this analysis indicates that zero-dose status is not randomly distributed but instead reflects broader structural determinants, including terrain, distance to health facilities, and local service availability. Although DHS-based geostatistical predictions should not be interpreted as precise microplanning estimates at every 5 × 5 km cell, they remain valuable for identifying broad spatial gradients and relative geographic inequities in zero-dose prevalence, especially in a smaller country context such as Malawi.

This study has several strengths, including the use of nationally representative data, analytical methods that account for multilevel structure, and the integration of geostatistical approaches to produce fine-scale maps of zero-dose prevalence. Together, these enhance the precision and programmatic relevance of the findings. Limitations should also be noted. Vaccination status was partially based on maternal recall when health cards were unavailable, which may lead to misclassification, though this bias would likely underestimate zero-dose prevalence. The cross-sectional nature of the DHS limits causal inference. Additionally, the displacement of survey cluster coordinates for confidentiality introduces spatial uncertainty, particularly at very fine resolutions. Nonetheless, the broad spatial patterns observed are unlikely to be substantially affected.

The implications of these findings for Malawi’s immunisation programme are important. Strengthening maternal health contact points, particularly ANC and facility-based delivery, remains central to reducing the number of children missed by routine immunisation services. The spatial analysis may help identify broader geographic areas where inequities are more pronounced and where additional investigation or intensified outreach may be warranted. However, DHS-based geostatistical maps are not sufficiently granular for direct community-level targeting and should therefore be interpreted alongside other sources of evidence. More precise identification of the communities most in need would likely require triangulation with targeted local surveys, routine program and health facility data, and potentially other small-area modelling approaches based on more spatially detailed covariates. Further research is needed to determine which combinations of data and methods are most appropriate for operational zero-dose targeting in Malawi. Improving communication strategies, especially through channels accessible to households with limited media exposure, may also help stimulate informed demand. Together, these approaches align with IA2030 priorities and support efforts to reduce immunisation inequities and reach remaining zero-dose children.

Conclusion

In conclusion, zero-dose children in Malawi are concentrated among those with limited maternal health contact and in distinct geographic areas with persistent access barriers. Addressing these inequities will require coordinated efforts to reinforce maternal healthcare utilisation, strengthen community engagement, and deploy spatially targeted strategies. The evidence presented here offers actionable guidance for policymakers and programme planners working to ensure that Malawi advances toward the goal of leaving no child behind.

Methods

Setting

Malawi is, bordered by Tanzania, Mozambique, and Zambia, covering an area of approximately 118,000-118,500 km²22,23 Malawi has a population of around 22 million in 2025 and a growth rate of 2.4%24,25. Despite its small size, Malawi’s population density is significantly higher than its neighboring countries, with 58% of its land being arable22.

Data

This secondary analysis draws on data from the 2017 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS), a nationally representative survey designed to monitor key indicators related to health, nutrition, and population dynamics26. Malawi has conducted multiple DHS rounds since its first in 1992, with the 2017 MDHS being the most recent with a complete, publicly released dataset at the time of analysis. The MDHS followed standard Demographic and Health Survey protocols developed under the MEASURE DHS program26,27. The dataset used in this analysis was accessed upon formal request on June 12, 2023. All data were anonymized before release, ensuring that individuals, households, and sampled communities could not be identified. In line with DHS policies, personally identifiable information such as names and addresses was not available to the authors, and geographic data were restricted to broad administrative units.

The 2017 MDHS implemented a two-stage, stratified sampling approach. In the first stage, 850 enumeration areas were randomly selected from the 2008 Malawi Population and Housing Census sampling frame, with stratification by district and urban-rural status across 56 strata. In the second stage, 30 households were systematically selected within each cluster, producing a total sample of 27,516 households. Only the selected households were eligible for interview; no substitutions were allowed. Data collection was conducted using tablets, and geographic coordinates were collected during the listing process to facilitate spatial analysis.

Variables

Outcome variable: zero-dose status

The primary outcome in this analysis was a child’s zero-dose vaccination status. Immunization data in the 2015-16 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS) were obtained through verification of vaccination cards, such as the health passport-and maternal recall when cards were unavailable. Data were collected for children aged 0 to 35 months at the time of the survey. Zero-dose status was defined as a binary variable indicating whether the child had received any routine vaccinations. Programmatically, the absence of the first dose of the pentavalent vaccine (which protects against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b) is often used to identify zero-dose children28. For this analysis, a child was classified as zero-dose if they had received no vaccinations at all by the time of the survey. This definition included all childhood vaccines in the Malawi national routine schedule, including birth doses such as Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG). This broader definition was used to capture children with no contact with routine immunization services across the vaccination cascade. Birth doses serve as an early indicator of contact with child health services, and children missing these doses are more likely to miss subsequent vaccinations. The analysis included all children aged 1–35 months.

Predictor variables

Predictor variables were selected based on prior evidence on determinants of zero-dose or under-immunised children, conceptual relevance to vaccination access, and availability in the DHS dataset. These included maternal, child, household, and healthcare access characteristics. Maternal factors included age, education level, marital status, and number of living children. Child characteristics included age in months and sex. Household socioeconomic status was measured using the DHS wealth index, which reflects household living standards based on asset ownership, housing materials, and access to basic services. Healthcare access variables included place of delivery, number of antenatal care (ANC) visits, and reported travel time to the nearest health facility. For the spatial model, travel time to the nearest health facility was retained as an important geographically referenced accessibility variable. Detailed definitions, coding, and construction of these variables follow the methodology outlined in the Guide to DHS Statistics (DHS-829.

Statistical analysis

We conducted all analyses using R30. We first summarised the characteristics of the study population using frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. We then compared the distribution of explanatory variables by zero-dose status using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where cell counts were small. These analyses were used to describe the sample and to explore unadjusted associations between covariates and zero-dose status.

To identify factors independently associated with zero-dose status, we fitted a multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression model. The outcome variable was zero-dose status, coded as 1 for children who had received no routine childhood vaccine and 0 otherwise. Because children were nested within DHS enumeration areas and observations within the same cluster may be correlated, we included a random intercept for cluster to account for within-cluster correlation and unobserved cluster-level heterogeneity.

Covariate selection for the adjusted model was guided by prior literature, biological plausibility, programmatic relevance, and availability in the DHS dataset, rather than relying solely on statistical significance in bivariate analyses. Variables considered for adjustment included child age, child sex, maternal age, maternal education, marital status, household wealth index, number of living children, antenatal care attendance, and place of delivery. This approach was chosen to improve external validity and reduce the risk of excluding important confounders while also maintaining model parsimony given the relatively small number of zero-dose children. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. We also assessed the contribution of cluster-level variation through the random-effects structure of the model.

To examine spatial variation in zero-dose prevalence, we fitted a binomial geostatistical model using cluster-level data. For each survey cluster, the number of zero-dose children was modelled as a binomial outcome, with the total number of eligible sampled children in that cluster as the denominator. The model included selected spatially referenced covariates and a spatial random effect to account for residual spatial dependence between nearby locations. Travel time to the nearest health facility was included as a geographic accessibility covariate. The Model is described below.

Parameter estimation was done using the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. The fitted model was then used to predict zero-dose prevalence on a 5 × 5 km grid across Malawi, generating continuous maps of predicted prevalence. Model adequacy was assessed using variogram-based validation to evaluate whether the fitted spatial process adequately captured the spatial dependence observed in the data.

Empirical variogram methods were applied to test for spatial correlation, and a simulation of 1,000 empirical variograms around the fitted Model was run to compute 95% confidence intervals at various spatial distances.
