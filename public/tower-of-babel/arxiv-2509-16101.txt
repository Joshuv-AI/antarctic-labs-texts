Personalized Federated Learning with Heat-Kernel Enhanced Tensorized Multi-View Clustering
Kristina P. Sinaga

Abstract
We present a robust personalized federated learning framework that leverages heat-kernel enhanced tensorized multi-view fuzzy c-means clustering with advanced tensor decomposition techniques. Our approach integrates heat-kernel coefficients adapted from quantum field theory with Tucker decomposition and canonical polyadic decomposition (CANDECOMP/PARAFAC) to transform conventional distance metrics and efficiently represent high-dimensional multi-view structures. The framework employs matriculation and vectorization techniques to facilitate the discovery of hidden structures and multilinear relationships via N-way generalized tensors. The proposed method introduces a dual-level optimization scheme: local heat-kernel enhanced fuzzy clustering with tensor decomposition operating on order-N input tensors, and federated aggregation of tensor factors with privacy-preserving personalization mechanisms. The local stage employs tensorized kernel Euclidean distance transformations and Tucker decomposition to discover client-specific patterns in multi-view tensor data, while the global aggregation process coordinates tensor factors (core tensors and factor matrices) across clients through differential privacy-preserving protocols. This tensorized approach enables efficient handling of high-dimensional multi-view data with significant communication savings through low-rank tensor approximations. Keywords: Federated learning, Multi-view clustering, Heat-kernel methods, Tensor decomposition, PARAFAC2, Tucker decomposition, Personalized machine learning, Privacy-preserving clustering.

Introduction

Large-scale data centers become increasingly important as the Internet of Things (IoT) continues to grow, with more devices being massively connected to the internet than ever before. This phenomenal transition of data to distributed environments affects the need for investigating optimal approaches to process heterogeneous information from multiple devices while ensuring security and efficient data exchange. Conventional unsupervised techniques cannot effectively handle these higher-order or higher-dimensional data structures inherent in modern federated systems where distributed clients possess different data modalities, feature dimensions, and local patterns.

The emergence of distributed multi-view data across heterogeneous clients presents unique challenges for federated learning systems. Traditional federated clustering assumes homogeneous data representations, failing to address the complexity of multi-view tensor structures where clients may have different modalities, feature spaces, and local data characteristics.

The massive growing of data is happening as the advance and revolution of technology in many fields becomes so forefront and powerful. Methodology

The integration of federated learning with multi-view clustering and tensor decomposition represents a convergence of several active research domains. This section reviews the foundational work and recent advances in federated learning, multi-view clustering, tensor decomposition methods, and their intersections, positioning our contributions within the broader landscape of distributed machine learning.

Federated Learning and Clustering

Federated learning, pioneered by McMahan et al.
[
4
]
, enables collaborative machine learning across distributed clients without centralizing raw data. The seminal FedAvg algorithm established the foundation for aggregating local model updates through weighted averaging. However, traditional federated learning primarily focuses on supervised tasks, with federated clustering receiving comparatively less attention despite its practical importance in distributed data analytics.

Konečnỳ et al.
[

They proposed modifications to existing algorithms to adapt them to federated optimization settings. Li et al.
[

Recent advances have introduced personalized federated clustering to address client heterogeneity. Caldarola et al.
[
10

2.2
Multi-View Clustering

Multi-view clustering leverages complementary information from multiple data representations to improve clustering performance. Early work by Bickel and Scheffer
[
18

established the theoretical foundations for multi-view learning, demonstrating superior performance when views provide complementary information about the underlying data structure.

Classical multi-view clustering approaches can be categorized into three main paradigms: co-training based methods, subspace learning approaches, and consensus clustering techniques. Co-training methods, such as the work by Kumar et al.
[
19

[
20

and shared subspace clustering
[
21

Graph-based multi-view clustering has gained significant attention due to its ability to capture complex inter-view relationships. Wang et al.
[
22
]
proposed multi-view graph clustering with adaptive neighbor learning, while Nie et al.
[
23
]
developed parameter-free auto-weighted multiple graph learning. These methods construct view-specific graphs and learn optimal view weights for clustering.

Deep learning approaches have revolutionized multi-view clustering through end-to-end representation learning. Zhang et al.
[
24
]
introduced auto-encoder based multi-view clustering, while Zhu et al.
[
25

developed deep multi-view clustering networks with view correlation discovery. However, these methods typically require centralized data access and are not designed for federated environments.

Fuzzy clustering extensions to multi-view scenarios have been explored by Jiang et al.
[
26

and Wang et al.
[
27

, introducing weighted aggregation schemes and view-specific fuzzification parameters. These soft clustering approaches provide uncertainty quantification but lack the scalability and privacy considerations required for federated deployment.

The mathematical foundations of multi-view clustering build upon single-view clustering formulations. In this study, we focus on soft clustering approaches that provide probabilistic cluster assignments, enabling better uncertainty quantification in federated environments. 2.3
Heat Kernel Methods and Quantum-Inspired Learning

Heat kernel methods have emerged as powerful tools for capturing local geometric structure in high-dimensional data spaces. Originally developed in differential geometry and quantum field theory, heat kernels provide a natural way to encode multi-scale geometric information through diffusion processes on manifolds.

The heat kernel, defined as the fundamental solution to the heat equation, has found extensive applications in machine learning through kernel methods and manifold learning. Ham et al.
[
28
]
introduced diffusion maps that leverage heat kernel properties for dimensionality reduction, while Coifman and Lafon
[
29
]
established theoretical foundations for diffusion-based data analysis.

In clustering applications, heat kernel methods have been used to capture local neighborhood structure and improve cluster boundary detection. Zelnik-Manor and Perona
[
30
]
developed self-tuning spectral clustering using local heat kernel scales, while von Luxburg
[
31
]
provided comprehensive analysis of heat kernel properties in graph-based clustering.

Quantum-inspired machine learning has gained attention for its ability to leverage quantum mechanical principles in classical algorithms. Quantum kernels, introduced by Havlíček et al.
[
32
]
, enable the computation of quantum feature maps for enhanced pattern recognition. The connection between quantum field theory and machine learning has been explored through path integral formulations
[
33
]
and quantum neural networks
[
34
]
.

The integration of heat kernel coefficients with clustering algorithms represents a natural extension of kernel methods to soft clustering scenarios. By incorporating heat diffusion properties, clustering algorithms can better capture local data manifold structure and improve robustness to noise and outliers.

2.4
Federated Multi-View Learning and Privacy Preservation

The intersection of federated learning and multi-view clustering presents unique challenges that are not adequately addressed by existing literature. Most federated learning approaches assume single-view data and homogeneous client populations, while multi-view clustering methods typically require centralized data access.

Recent work on federated multi-view learning includes approaches by Chen et al.
[
35
]
for federated multi-view representation learning and Liu et al.
[
36
]
for privacy-preserving multi-view clustering. However, these methods do not leverage tensor decomposition for efficient representation and communication.

Privacy preservation in federated multi-view settings requires careful consideration of information leakage across views and clients. Differential privacy mechanisms must be adapted to handle the multi-view tensor structure while maintaining clustering utility. Secure aggregation protocols need to be extended to handle tensor factor aggregation efficiently.

The communication efficiency challenge is particularly acute in federated multi-view scenarios due to the high-dimensional nature of multi-view tensor data. Existing compression techniques are not optimized for tensor structures and may destroy important multilinear relationships. This motivates the need for tensor-aware compression and aggregation schemes.

2.5
Tensor Decomposition Methods

Tensor decomposition has emerged as a powerful paradigm for analyzing high-dimensional multi-modal data, providing compact representations that capture multilinear relationships inherent in complex datasets. The field has evolved from classical matrix factorization techniques to sophisticated tensor algebra frameworks capable of handling arbitrary N-way data structures.

Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (CPD), also known as CANDECOMP/PARAFAC, represents the foundational tensor decomposition method. Introduced independently by Carroll and Chang
[
37
]
and Harshman
[
38
]
, CPD decomposes a tensor into a sum of rank-one tensors, providing a unique factorization under mild conditions. The uniqueness property of CPD makes it particularly valuable for component analysis and factor identification in multi-way data.

Tucker decomposition, proposed by Tucker
[
39
]
, provides a more flexible alternative through its core tensor and factor matrices structure. Unlike CPD, Tucker decomposition allows different ranks along each mode, making it suitable for data with varying complexity across dimensions. The Higher-Order SVD (HOSVD) algorithm by De Lathauwer et al.
[
40
]
established efficient computational procedures for Tucker decomposition, enabling its application to large-scale problems.

Recent advances in tensor decomposition have focused on addressing practical challenges such as missing data, noise robustness, and computational scalability. Tensor completion methods, including work by Liu et al.
[
41
]
and Yuan and Zhang
[
42
]
, enable handling of incomplete tensors through low-rank assumptions. Robust tensor decomposition techniques
[
43
]
provide resilience against outliers and corrupted entries.

Non-negative tensor factorization (NTF) has gained attention for applications requiring interpretable, parts-based representations. Lee and Seung’s pioneering work
[
44
]
on non-negative matrix factorization was extended to tensors by Shashua and Hazan
[
45
]
, enabling decompositions with non-negativity constraints that often yield more interpretable factors.

The integration of tensor decomposition with machine learning has led to tensorized neural networks
[
46
]
, tensor-based kernel methods
[
47
]
, and multi-task learning frameworks
[
48
]
. These approaches leverage tensor structure to reduce model complexity while maintaining representational power.

In the context of clustering, tensor-based methods have shown particular promise for multi-view and multi-way data analysis. Zhou et al.
[
49
]
developed tensor-based multiway arrays for bid data sets, while Benjamin et al.
[
50
]
proposed tensorized possibilistic c-means for high-dimensional clustering. However, these methods are not designed for federated environments and lack the privacy-preserving mechanisms required for distributed deployment.

2.6
Summary and Research Gaps

The review of existing literature reveals several important research gaps that motivate our proposed approach:

Limited Integration:
While federated learning, multi-view clustering, and tensor decomposition have been extensively studied independently, their integration remains largely unexplored. Existing federated clustering methods do not leverage multi-view structure, and multi-view clustering approaches are not designed for federated environments.

Communication Inefficiency:
Current federated multi-view methods do not exploit tensor structure for communication compression. The high-dimensional nature of multi-view data exacerbates communication overhead in federated settings, requiring novel tensor-aware compression schemes.

Privacy Limitations:
Existing privacy-preserving mechanisms are not optimized for multi-view tensor data, potentially leading to information leakage across views or reduced clustering utility. New differential privacy mechanisms tailored to tensor structures are needed.

Lack of Personalization:
Most federated clustering approaches assume homogeneous data distributions across clients. In reality, clients often have heterogeneous data characteristics requiring personalized clustering solutions that adapt to local data patterns while benefiting from global knowledge.

Theoretical Understanding:
The convergence properties and privacy guarantees of federated multi-view tensor clustering are not well understood. Theoretical analysis is needed to provide convergence guarantees and characterize the privacy-utility trade-offs.

Our work addresses these gaps by proposing a novel heat-kernel enhanced tensorized framework that integrates advanced tensor decomposition with personalized federated learning, providing both theoretical guarantees and practical advantages in communication efficiency and clustering performance.

Federated Kernel Euclidean Distance

Federated Heat-Kernel Coefficients

Part IV: Convergence Analysis and Optimality Conditions

The derived update rules satisfy the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions for the constrained optimization problem. Specifically:

1.

Stationarity:

\nabla J_{E-FKMVC}^{\ell}+\sum_{i=1}^{n(\ell)}\lambda_{1i}\nabla g_{i}+\lambda_{2}\nabla h=0
, where
g_{i}(\mu)=\sum_{k=1}^{c(\ell)}\mu_{[\ell]ik}^{*}-1
and
h(v)=\sum_{h=1}^{s(\ell)}v_{[\ell]h}-1
.

2.

Primal feasibility:

g_{i}(\mu)=0
and
h(v)=0
for all
i
.

3.

Dual feasibility:
All Lagrange multipliers are finite and well-defined.

The alternating optimization scheme converges to a local optimum under the following conditions:

•

Boundedness and Continuity:
The objective function
J_{E-FKMVC}^{\ell}
is continuous and bounded below. This follows from the fact that FKED values are bounded in
[0,1]
by construction (Eq.
4
), ensuring the weighted sum in the objective function remains finite. The exponential decay in the heat kernel guarantees that distances approach finite limits as data points move infinitely far apart.

•

Constraint Compactness:
The constraint set is compact and convex. The membership matrix constraints
\sum_{k=1}^{c(\ell)}\mu_{[\ell]ik}^{*}=1
with
\mu_{[\ell]ik}^{*}\in[0,1]
define probability simplices, which are compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{c(\ell)}
. Similarly, the view weight constraints
\sum_{h=1}^{s(\ell)}v_{[\ell]h}=1
with
v_{[\ell]h}\in[0,1]
form a compact convex simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{s(\ell)}
. The cluster center parameters are unconstrained but remain bounded due to the data distribution.

•

Unique Subproblem Solutions:
Each subproblem (updating
U
,
A
, or
V
while fixing others) has a unique solution. For membership updates, the strictly positive denominators in Eq.
26
ensure uniqueness since FKED values are always positive for distinct data points and cluster centers. For cluster center updates, the weighted exponential terms in Eq.
27
create a strictly convex weighted least-squares problem with a unique minimum. For view weight updates, the strictly decreasing nature of the power function with exponent
-1/(\alpha-1)
in Eq.
28
guarantees uniqueness of the normalized solution.

These conditions are satisfied by construction, as the FKED function is continuous and bounded, the probability simplex constraints define compact convex sets, and the update rules yield unique solutions when the denominators are non-zero (guaranteed by the strict positivity of FKED values and the non-degeneracy assumption that no data points are identical to cluster centers).

4
Proposed Federated Tensorized Clustering

Our proposed personalized federated learning framework integrates heat-kernel enhanced multi-view clustering with tensorized decomposition techniques at the local client level and coordinated global aggregation. The framework consists of three main components: (1) local tensorized heat-kernel enhanced multi-view fuzzy clustering, (2) tensor decomposition for efficient representation, and (3) federated aggregation with personalization mechanisms.

4.1
Tensorized Heat-Kernel Enhanced Local Clustering

Each client
L_{\ell}
possesses multi-view data
X_{[\ell]}=\{X_{[\ell]}^{h}\}_{h=1}^{s(\ell)}
where
X_{[\ell]}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\ell)\times d_{h}^{(\ell)}}
represents the
h
-th view. We tensorize this data structure to capture multilinear relationships across samples, features, and views.

4.1.1
Tensor Data Organization

The multi-view data at client
\ell
is organized into a 3-mode tensor:

\mathcal{X}_{[\ell]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\ell)\times D_{[\ell]}\times s(\ell)}

(59)

where
D_{[\ell]}=\max_{h}d_{h}^{(\ell)}
is the maximum feature dimension (with zero-padding for views with fewer features). This tensorization enables:

•

Unified representation across heterogeneous views

•

Capture of cross-view feature interactions

•

Efficient tensor decomposition operations

4.1.2
Tensorized Kernel Euclidean Distance

We extend the heat-kernel enhanced distance metric to operate on tensor slices. For client
\ell
, the tensorized kernel Euclidean distance between data tensor slice
\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i,:}^{h}
(representing data point
i
) and cluster center tensor
\mathcal{A}_{[\ell],k,:}^{h}
(representing cluster
k
) is:

\text{TKED}_{\ell}(\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i,:}^{h}-\mathcal{A}_{[\ell],k,:}^{h})=1-\exp\left(-\sum_{h=1}^{s(\ell)}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{h}^{(\ell)}}\delta_{[\ell]ij}^{h}\left(\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i,j}^{h}-\mathcal{A}_{[\ell],k,j}^{h}\right)^{2}\right)

(60)

where
\delta_{[\ell]ij}^{h}
are tensorized heat-kernel coefficients held by the
\ell
-th client, computed as:

\delta_{[\ell]ij}^{h}=\frac{\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i,j}^{h}-\min_{i^{\prime}}\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i^{\prime},j}^{h}}{\max_{i^{\prime}}\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i^{\prime},j}^{h}-\min_{i^{\prime}}\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i^{\prime},j}^{h}}

(61)

4.1.3
Tensor Decomposition for Cluster Centers

To reduce computational complexity and enable efficient communication, we decompose the cluster center tensor using Tucker decomposition:

\mathcal{A}_{[\ell]}\approx\mathcal{G}_{[\ell]}\times_{1}\mathbf{P}_{[\ell]}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{[\ell]}\times_{3}\mathbf{R}_{[\ell]}

(62)

where:

•

\mathcal{G}_{[\ell]}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times r_{2}\times r_{3}}
is the core tensor

•

\mathbf{P}_{[\ell]}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times c}
captures cluster relationships

•

\mathbf{Q}_{[\ell]}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{[\ell]}\times r_{2}}
captures feature patterns

•

\mathbf{R}_{[\ell]}\in\mathbb{R}^{s(\ell)\times r_{3}}
captures view relationships

The decomposed representation reduces storage from
\mathcal{O}(c\cdot D_{[\ell]}\cdot s(\ell))
to
\mathcal{O}(c\cdot r_{2}\cdot r_{3}+c^{2}+D_{[\ell]}\cdot r_{2}+s(\ell)\cdot r_{3})
.

4.1.4
Tensorized Local Objective Function

Each client optimizes a tensorized multi-view fuzzy clustering (T-MVFC) objective:

J_{\ell}^{tensor}(\mathcal{U}_{[\ell]},\mathcal{G}_{[\ell]},\mathbf{P}_{[\ell]},\mathbf{Q}_{[\ell]},\mathbf{R}_{[\ell]},\mathbf{v}_{[\ell]})=\sum_{i=1}^{n(\ell)}\sum_{k=1}^{c(\ell)}(\mu_{[\ell]ik}^{*})^{m}\sum_{h=1}^{s(\ell)}(v_{[\ell]h}^{\alpha}\text{ TKED}_{\ell}(\mathcal{X}_{[\ell],i,:}^{h}-\mathcal{A}_{[\ell],k,:}^{h})

(63)

subject to the constraints:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{c(\ell)}\mu_{[\ell]ik}^{*}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\mu_{[\ell]ik}^{*}\in[0,1]

(64)

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{s(\ell)}v_{[\ell]h}

\displaystyle=1,\quad v_{[\ell]h}\in[0,1]

(65)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{[\ell]}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{[\ell]}\times_{1}\mathbf{P}_{[\ell]}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{[\ell]}\times_{3}\mathbf{R}_{[\ell]}

(66)

Theorem 2

(Tensorized Update Rules)
.

The alternating optimization of the tensorized objective function yields:

Membership updates:

\mu_{jk}^{(i)}=\frac{\left(\sum_{h=1}^{s_{i}}(v_{h}^{(i)})^{\alpha}\text{TKED}_{i}(j,k)\right)^{-\frac{1}{m-1}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{c}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{s_{i}}(v_{h}^{(i)})^{\alpha}\text{TKED}_{i}(j,k^{\prime})\right)^{-\frac{1}{m-1}}}

(67)

Core tensor updates:

\mathcal{G}_{i}=\arg\min_{\mathcal{G}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{c}(\mu_{jk}^{(i)})^{m}\sum_{h=1}^{s_{i}}(v_{h}^{(i)})^{\alpha}\|\mathcal{X}_{i,j,:,h}-(\mathcal{G}\times_{1}\mathbf{P}_{i}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{i}\times_{3}\mathbf{R}_{i})_{k,:,h}\|^{2}

(68)

Factor matrix updates:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{i}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{P}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{c}(\mu_{jk}^{(i)})^{m}\sum_{h=1}^{s_{i}}(v_{h}^{(i)})^{\alpha}\|\mathcal{X}_{i,j,:,h}-(\mathcal{G}_{i}\times_{1}\mathbf{P}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{i}\times_{3}\mathbf{R}_{i})_{k,:,h}\|^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{Q}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{c}(\mu_{jk}^{(i)})^{m}\sum_{h=1}^{s_{i}}(v_{h}^{(i)})^{\alpha}\|\mathcal{X}_{i,j,:,h}-(\mathcal{G}_{i}\times_{1}\mathbf{P}_{i}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}\times_{3}\mathbf{R}_{i})_{k,:,h}\|^{2}

(70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{i}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{c}(\mu_{jk}^{(i)})^{m}\sum_{h=1}^{s_{i}}(v_{h}^{(i)})^{\alpha}\|\mathcal{X}_{i,j,:,h}-(\mathcal{G}_{i}\times_{1}\mathbf{P}_{i}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{i}\times_{3}\mathbf{R})_{k,:,h}\|^{2}

(71)

View weight updates:

v_{h}^{(i)}=\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{c}(\mu_{jk}^{(i)})^{m}\text{TKED}_{i}(j,k,h)\right)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}{\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{s_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{c}(\mu_{jk}^{(i)})^{m}\text{TKED}_{i}(j,k,h^{\prime})\right)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}

(72)

Proof.

The proof follows similar principles to the non-tensorized case but operates on tensor slices. The factor matrix updates can be solved using alternating least squares (ALS) with weighted terms based on membership and view weights.

The federated learning framework coordinates local heat-kernel enhanced clustering results while preserving client personalization. The aggregation process operates on cluster centers and view importance weights.

4.2.1
Global Objective with Personalization

The global federated objective balances local clustering quality with personalization constraints:

J_{global}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left[\omega_{i}J_{i}^{local}(U_{i},A_{i},V_{i})+\gamma\mathcal{R}_{person}(A_{i},A_{g})+\eta\mathcal{R}_{view}(V_{i},V_{g})\right]

where:

•

\omega_{i}
represents client-specific weights based on data quality and quantity

•

\mathcal{R}_{person}(A_{i},A_{g})=\sum_{h,k}\|A_{i}^{h}-A_{g}^{h}\|_{F}^{2}

•

\mathcal{R}_{view}(V_{i},V_{g})=\|V_{i}-V_{g}\|_{2}^{2}
maintains view weight consistency

•

\gamma,\eta

4.2.2

To maintain privacy, clients share only aggregated statistics rather than raw cluster assignments:

Definition 1

(Federated Aggregation Protocol)
.

Each client
i

\displaystyle=J_{i}^{local}\text{ and }n_{i}

Conclusion

This paper presents a novel personalized federated learning framework that integrates heat-kernel enhanced multi-view clustering with tensor-based global coordination. The key contributions include:

1.

Heat-kernel Enhanced Federated Clustering
: We adapt heat-kernel coefficients to federated multi-view clustering, enabling more effective capture of complex local patterns while maintaining global coordination.

2.

Personalized Aggregation Mechanism
: Our framework provides adaptive personalization that balances local specialization with global knowledge sharing through learnable personalization parameters.

3.

Privacy-Preserving Protocol
: The aggregation protocol ensures differential privacy while maintaining clustering effectiveness through carefully designed statistical sharing.

4.

Comprehensive Theoretical Analysis
: We provide convergence guarantees, privacy bounds, and complexity analysis that establish the theoretical foundation of the approach.
