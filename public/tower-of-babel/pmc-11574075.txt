A study on the quantification and impact mechanism of regional ecological compensation based on revised carbon accounts: evidence from the Yellow River Basin economic zone in China
Authors: Hang Xu, Ruiyang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
With the progression of regional connectivity, attaining the ‘dual-carbon target’ is no longer the narrative of a single city. To achieve synergistic governance among cities in the basin economic zone, it is essential to establish a multidimensional ecological compensation mechanism. This research amended the conventional carbon accounting model by utilising carbon emissions and economic data from 59 cities in China’s Yellow River Basin, covering the period from 2004 to 2021, after the timeframe of significant alterations in the administrative divisions of cities was removed. It additionally compares and analyses the clustering patterns and trajectories of centre-of-gravity shifts in regional ecocompensation credits while investigating the impact processes through geo-probes. The findings indicate that the amended carbon account has a more decentralised distribution when interregional disparities are considered. Second, ecocompensation credits are characterised mostly by low-high and low-low agglomeration; nevertheless, their regional distribution is uneven. Moreover, the rankings of the top five cities in terms of ecocompensation exhibited minimal variation each year. The degree of green technology is the primary determinant of the spatiotemporal evolution of ecological compensation. The conclusions of this study can serve as a valuable reference for the execution of medium- and long-term ecocompensation policies and aid in attaining the ‘dual-carbon’ aim.

Introduction

Human-induced carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions are the primary contributors to severe climate change, necessitating collaborative efforts from all nations globally to mitigate carbon emissions1. China, the largest carbon emitter globally, established a ‘dual carbon’ objective of achieving ‘carbon peaking’ by 2030 and ‘carbon neutrality’ by 2060 in September 2020 and is actively engaged in global climate governance to mitigate carbon emissions2. The Yellow River Basin, a significant natural region in China, is a primary contributor to carbon dioxide emissions3. The Yellow River Basin serves as a vital ecological barrier and economic zone in China, making the equilibrium between ecological preservation and economic advancement extremely essential4. However, traditional carbon accounting methods have limitations in accurately quantifying regional carbon emissions and sequestration5. These methods inadequately account for the ecological characteristics and economic disparities among regions, thereby posing challenges to the equity and efficacy of ecological compensation mechanisms6. Consequently, developing a revised carbon accounting model that accurately represents regional disparities is crucial for enhancing the precision and equity of the ecological compensation framework7.

This work seeks to quantitatively analyse regional ecological compensation by developing a revised carbon account tailored for the Yellow River Basin and to investigate the influence of this revised carbon account on the ecological compensation mechanism. By utilising the natural geographic attributes, contemporary economic and social development, and ecological environment data of the Yellow River Basin, the conventional carbon accounting model is revised to produce a novel quantitative approach for regional ecological compensation. Through a quantitative assessment of carbon emissions and absorption in cities within the Yellow River Basin, a justifiable ecological compensation standard is established, and the impact of the revised carbon account on the ecological compensation mechanism, along with its influencing factors, is analysed.

Academics have conducted extensive research on quantifying ecocompensation. The main methods used for measurement include model construction8,9, cost measurement10-12, conditional value13-15, and ecological footprint16,17 methods, among others. The modelling approach encompasses models focused on regional pollution sources, models focused on regional ecological value, and so on. The amount of ecological compensation can be calculated more directly and reliably by quantifying the compensation amount through modelling the components of concern. This approach is commonly utilised18,19. The cost method involves quantifying the specific expenses associated with pollution management and evaluating the potential loss of benefits from ecological conservation. The assessment of pollution control costs is more straightforward than the measurement of opportunity costs. It involves relatively low data and computational complexity. On the other hand, the measurement of opportunity costs for ecological preservation is more comprehensive and reasonable20,21. The conditional value method is a quantitative approach that assesses an individual’s willingness to pay and accept22,23. The Ecological Footprint Method (EFM) assesses the ability of a region to support its environmental needs by assessing its capacity to absorb pollution24,25. Scholars have investigated various environmental settings, such as wastewater, exhaust gas, solid waste, grasslands, forests, wetlands, and other ecological environments. These investigations have been conducted across different regions, including transregional areas, provinces, states, and watersheds. The studies mentioned in the text include references from multiple authors and years. A substantial amount of comprehensive research has been carried out on this topic.

The primary focus of research on ecological compensation, specifically related to carbon emissions, is centred on theoretical investigations and quantitative analyses of compensatory value26. Research on carbon ecocompensation has focused primarily on investigating trading mechanisms, practicality, and regional cost disparities27-29. Quantitative research is centred on developing a system and mechanism for carbon ecological compensation and trading. It also involves conducting quantitative research to determine the value of carbon compensation for specific projects, such as protecting marine vegetation, developing reservoirs, and managing forestry and grassland areas30-32. Following the ratification of the Kyoto Protocol, there has been a significant focus on implementing and refining the associated regulations. This includes the oversight and exchange of carbon offset projects, as well as the establishment of a carbon market trading system33. Following the adoption of voluntary emission reduction as a method to regulate greenhouse gases, research on carbon offset has concentrated on assessing the amount individuals are ready to pay to attain carbon neutrality. This evaluation is conducted via the conditional value technique34. Global carbon market trading and carbon offset programs have experienced tremendous growth and serve as crucial mechanisms for carbon emitters to attain carbon neutrality35. Nevertheless, the carbon offset within the carbon market trading system is significantly smaller than the expenses associated with implementing steps to reduce carbon emissions. Additionally, there are variations in carbon offset programs across different regions, which has given rise to numerous inquiries36.

Previous studies have extensively explored quantitative methods for assessing ecocompensation, measuring carbon emissions, and determining theories and mechanisms of carbon ecocompensation from various fields and perspectives. This body of work provides a strong basis for the current study on carbon ecocompensation. However, there is still potential for further expansion in this area. Prior research on ecocompensation based on carbon emissions has focused primarily on limited geographical areas or specialised quantitative analyses; hence, a complete assessment of cross-city regional synergistic governance is lacking. This study focuses on the Yellow River Basin Economic Zone in China to analyse urban ecocompensation throughout the entire basin. The goal is to enhance intercity governance from a basin perspective by promoting synergy among cities. Furthermore, the conventional approach to quantifying carbon ecological compensation fails to consider urban socioeconomic aspects and hence cannot effectively depict the importance of urban ecological compensation. This study integrates indicators of the gross city product, population, and administrative area into the model, enhancing the accuracy and rationality of carbon accounting and ecocompensation measurements. This study can serve as a valuable reference for policy design. Furthermore, previous research has examined the dispersion and clustering patterns of the numerical outcomes of ecological compensation but has not conducted a comprehensive investigation into the factors influencing regional and temporal changes in carbon accounts. This research uses geographic probes to quantify the extent of influence exerted by drivers on the spatiotemporal changes in revised carbon accounts within the Yellow River Basin. Additionally, it examines the interconnections between these factors, aiming to offer insights for policy planning.

This study focuses on the imperative to enhance the holistic control of carbon emissions in watersheds, the necessity of synchronised urban management, and the objectives of achieving “carbon peaks” and “carbon neutrality”. The research specifically examines data from 2004 to 2021, encompassing 59 cities at the prefecture level and above within China’s Yellow River Basin. The conventional carbon accounting model was adapted to examine the alterations in distribution before and after modification. In addition, we conducted measurements of the ecological compensation for the carbon emissions of Chinese cities located in the Yellow River Basin. We also analysed the patterns of agglomeration and migration of the centre of gravity and determined the priority areas for ecological compensation zoning. Ultimately, this analysis examines the factors that drive the changes in the revised carbon account over time and space and how they interact with each other. The goal is to provide decision-makers with valuable insights to support the achievement of the “dual-carbon” objective, foster cooperation among cities in the basin, and promote balanced regional development.

This paper is structured as follows. The first part is the introduction, which introduces the research background of the article and returns to the previous related literature. The second part describes the theoretical mechanism, which introduces the theoretical basis of the study of the ecological compensation mechanism and the impact mechanism in the watershed. The third section presents the research design, which provides an overview of the study area and presents the computational model and methodology for quantifying and analysing ecological compensation. The fourth part presents and analyses the results of the carbon account distribution before and after the modification, the spatial concentration of the amount of ecocompensation, the migration of the centre of gravity, and the ecocompensation priority zoning. The fifth section examines the impact mechanisms of ecological compensation. The sixth section presents the discussion, summarising the findings and comparing them with the existing literature. The seventh section concludes with refined conclusions and policy recommendations derived from the results and discussion, offering targeted policy suggestions.

Theoretical mechanism

Resource and environmental economics posit that a significant factor contributing to the irrational extraction and utilisation of resources, along with environmental contamination and degradation, is an externality37. Externalities, as a formal concept, were initially introduced by Marshall38. A. Pigou differentiated between external economies and external diseconomies, asserting that externalities arise from market failure, necessitating government intervention for resolution39. R. H. Coase posited that externalities do not solely stem from market failure; if property rights are well defined and transaction costs are negligible, resource allocation via market transactions can achieve Pareto optimality40.

Carbon offsetting is a method of ecocompensation that involves internalising external environmental issues as economic problems and utilising economic incentives to compensate for costs or losses incurred by individuals or organisations that promote ecoefficiency or prevent environmental degradation in their production processes41. Carbon compensation can be defined as the actions of carbon emitters providing compensation to carbon sinks or ecological protectors, either economically or noneconomically. The establishment of a carbon compensation mechanism seeks to modify the carbon emissions and management behaviours of these subjects through economic incentives, enabling them to independently alter their emission strategies and foster a balance in regional carbon ecology.

Horizontal spatial carbon compensation is a method for achieving regional ecological compensation that regards the carbon emission space as a limited resource and the carbon sink absorption capacity as a source of revenue42. It leverages the disparities in net carbon sink availability among regions to establish a fair transaction price through efficient exchange mechanisms. The horizontal transfer system of government funding across regions enables the transformation of ecological services from uncompensated to compensated within or between regional industrial processes by utilising carbon as a conduit43. This paper develops a horizontal carbon dioxide ecological compensation mechanism for the Yellow River Basin, focusing on cities as the primary entities, on the basis of the revised carbon account value. The mechanism comprises four components: the delineation and quantitative assessment of the revised carbon account, identification of the compensating and paying cities, establishment of the compensation standard, and specification of the compensation method (refer to Fig. 1). The delineation of the scope of the revised carbon account is the central focus of this research. Major urban centres, often central to industries and services with elevated carbon emissions, may assume the role of carbon offset purchasers, whereas smaller cities may serve as beneficiaries of carbon offsets owing to their comparatively lower emissions or contributions to ecological preservation. When developing a horizontal carbon offset mechanism, it is essential to evaluate the emission reduction potential of large cities alongside the ecological service capability of small cities, thereby reconciling the compensation demands of both. In contrast to the conventional carbon accounting model, which relies on a singular carbon emission indicator and emphasises solely the negative externalities of production activities, the revised carbon accounting model, which incorporates multidimensional indicators of carbon, the economy, and society while highlighting the region’s positive externalities, is essential for developing a more rational and effective ecocompensation mechanism. The examination of the carbon account value distribution, the spatial allocation of ecocompensation credits, and the shift in the centre of gravity before and after the modification enhance the comprehension of the scientific validity and rationality of the amended outcomes. The examination of the influence mechanism centres on identifying the determinants of ecocompensation amounts derived from the updated carbon account, offering insights for urban areas to enhance carbon emission management.

Research design

Overview of the study area

The Yellow River basin serves as a crucial water source and ecological barrier in northern China, significantly contributing to the nation’s food security and biodiversity44. The Yellow River Basin is presently facing several challenges, including soil erosion, climate change, and biodiversity loss45. The establishment of an ecological compensation mechanism for the Yellow River Basin Economic Zone will facilitate the effective internalisation of environmental externalities, promote a fair distribution of responsibilities and benefits among regions, and enhance the sustainability of ecological protection.

The economic zone of the Yellow River basin is located in the northeastern central region of mainland China (Fig. 2), encompassing latitudes ranging from 32° to 42° north and longitudes ranging from 96° to 119° east. This area exhibits considerable climatic variation between the northern and southern areas and includes diverse terrains, such as plateaus and plains, characterised by elevated topography in the northwest and lower elevations in the southeast, with a flatter and more gradual landscape in the east. The Yellow River spans 5,464 km, encompassing a basin area of 795,000 square kilometres, which constitutes approximately 7.8% of China’s total land area. It flows eastwards into the Bohai Sea from Shandong Province and carries a significant sediment load in its lower reaches, primarily within the South Temperate Zone, the Middle Temperate Zone, and the Plateau Climate Zone. In 2023, the combined population of the provinces and regions along the Yellow River Basin was approximately 410 million, representing 28.5% of China’s total population, whereas the gross regional product was estimated at approximately 31 trillion yuan, constituting 25.3% of China’s gross domestic product. The Yellow River Basin holds significant importance in China’s economic and social development, as well as in terms of its ecological security. The middle reaches of the Yellow River feature a loess landscape characterised by significant soil erosion and a delicate regional environment; conversely, coal remains China’s primary energy source, with certain areas in the Yellow River Basin serving as crucial coal mining regions. The waste and carbon dioxide produced from coal mining and consumption pose a substantial threat to the ecological equilibrium of the area. Carbon emissions in the Yellow River Basin are predominantly concentrated in the provinces of Shandong, Henan, Inner Mongolia, and Shanxi, which collectively represent approximately 73% of total emissions. In contrast, emissions from other provinces, such as Sichuan and Shaanxi, are comparatively minimal. Furthermore, the carbon ecological security in the upstream region surpasses that in the middle and lower reaches, highlighting significant disparities in the efficacy of ecological protection across various regions. Since 1997, carbon emissions in the Yellow River Basin have transitioned from rapid to sluggish growth. Although the growth rate of carbon emissions has decelerated since 2012, the region has not yet attained its peak carbon emissions.

Geographical detector

Geoprobe is a statistical technique utilised to identify the spatial heterogeneity of geographic factors and elucidate their driving forces. This method involves partitioning the study area into multiple subregions, assessing the overall variance against the cumulative variance of the subregions to ascertain the presence of spatial correlation among the factors, and examining the statistical relationship between the independent and dependent variables, thereby revealing the driving forces behind the spatial heterogeneity of the dependent variable. Carbon emission governance fundamentally comprises three components—resource input, economic development, and environmental governance—aimed at attaining a synergistic outcome of ‘resource-economy-environment’52. Consequently, the drivers are identified on the basis of these three components. Environmental governance is categorised into social governance and governmental governance, with its efficacy assessed by the level of societal and governmental focus on carbon emission management18. The magnitude of foreign investment and the industrial framework in economic development are significant determinants influencing regional industrial carbon emissions, indicating the extent of regional economic transformation and advancement53. Resource inputs primarily consist of energy inputs and green technology inputs, indicating energy consumption and efficiency54. The annual search index for environmental protection-related terms reflects societal concern (S), the prevalence of such terms in government reports signifies governmental concern (G), actual investment outside the city denotes the magnitude of inwards investment (I), the output value of the tertiary industry as a percentage of GDP illustrates the industrial structure (T), the city’s energy consumption converted to coal usage indicates energy consumption (C), and the quantity of green patents in the city represents innovation in environmental technology. The quantity of authorisations reflects the degree of green technology (E).

Results analysis

Spatial and temporal differentiation of carbon accounts before the revision

The aforementioned model calculation determines the carbon account values before and after the modification, facilitating the analysis of their spatial and temporal evolution. Using ArcGIS 10.2 software, spatial and temporal distribution maps of the carbon account before and after the modification in China’s Yellow River Basin are generated, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Before the revision, the primary annual distribution of the carbon account exhibit a north‒south differentiation trend in 2004, characterised by minimal intraregional disparities. By 2012, an east‒middle‒west differentiation trend begins to emerge, with carbon emission growth rates in certain areas of eastern Shandong and western Shaanxi and Gansu surpassing those of the other regions, leading to an expansion of intraregional differences. This east‒middle‒west differentiation trend intensifies further in 2021, revealing significant disparities between the northern and southern regions. Analysis of the prerevised carbon account values across various periods in the study area reveals distinct regional differentiation within the Yellow River Basin, characterised by a rapid increase in carbon emissions throughout the study duration, with all study areas exhibiting a deficit in carbon accounts. The distribution pattern exhibits high values in the south and low values in the north, with elevated values in the east and west and diminished values in the centre. Furthermore, carbon account values are significantly affected by economic and social determinants, including the level of regional economic development and population density. The carbon account values within the study area clearly differ across provincial administrative regions, specifically Shandong Province in the lower reaches of the Yellow River, Shaanxi Province in the middle reaches, and Gansu Province in the upper reaches, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The trend of carbon account values in the lower reaches is predominantly consistent and primarily situated in high-value areas; the majority of the middle reaches in Shanxi Province fall within the second high-value areas, whereas cities in the upper reaches of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region and the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region are predominantly classified in low-value areas. From an economic analysis, the majority of the high-value areas exhibit a distribution and diffusion pattern centred on Xi’an, Jinan, and other capital cities within the basin. These regions are characterised by a well-developed and densely populated presence of metallurgical, chemical, and other high-energy-consuming industries, resulting in elevated total carbon emissions and minimal carbon absorption. This region has acquired wealth through swift expansion, and its task of reducing carbon emissions is more challenging, necessitating greater investment in ecological conservation. Simultaneously, there remains a category of high-value carbon account areas, such as certain regions in the middle reaches of the Yellow River, where excessive cultivation has resulted in significant soil erosion, and the absence of surface vegetation has diminished regional carbon absorption, thereby exacerbating the carbon account deficit. Nonetheless, the local economy has not progressed to the extent of the previously listed high-value regions, and the inhabitants exhibit limited knowledge of environmental conservation, coupled with insufficient financial resources and technological capabilities for environmental management. Consequently, the carbon accounting methodology, which solely accounts for regional carbon emissions and sequestration, fails to appropriately assess the present ecological compensation priority; thus, it needs modification through the integration of urban economic and social data.

Spatial and temporal variability in the revised carbon accounts

The absence of a prerevised carbon account makes it challenging to adequately represent the social and economic attributes of a city, rendering it unsuitable for quantifying regional ecological compensation. This paper employs the carbon account model, which incorporates economic level, population, and regional area characteristics, to compute the corrected carbon account values. The outcomes are presented in Fig. 4. Figure 4 illustrates a general decline in the revised carbon account over time, with the carbon accounts of most cities transitioning from positive to negative. This shift results from the revised carbon account model Formula (4), which is calibrated on the basis of the mean value of the prerevised carbon account. Consequently, revised carbon accounts falling below this average are deemed negative, indicating a surplus, whereas those exceeding the average are classified as positive, signifying a deficit. In examining the carbon account distribution before the revision, it is noted that in 2004, the overall distribution is elevated in the east and diminished in the west, with certain high-value zones being relatively scattered. By 2012, the disparity between East China and West China increases, with high-value zones becoming concentrated in West China. However, this clustering trend in the west diminishes by 2021, whereas the surplus of the eastern carbon account continues to expand. The updated high-value zones no longer exhibit a distribution pattern centred on the region’s economically developed cities. The high-value zones are primarily concentrated in select cities located in the middle and lower sections of the river, as well as in the southern part of Gansu Province in the upper sections of the river. Cities located in high-value zones, such as Tongchuan City, are characterised by a significant presence of heavy chemical industries. These businesses are known for their high energy consumption and substantial carbon emissions. Simultaneously, cities exhibit minimal focus on reducing carbon emissions and possess inadequate rules for energy conservation and emission reduction. However, their hasty expansion has yielded substantial financial gains, establishing an economic foundation for intercity ecological compensation. Low-value areas encompass not only ecoregions characterised by limited economic development, such as Bayannur in the northern Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, but also numerous regions with substantial economic development and notable reductions in carbon emissions. Furthermore, the revised carbon account values in these areas are significantly diminished. Since 2012, the Chinese government has placed greater emphasis on carbon emissions and other environmental concerns. The low-value area has been rapidly expanding, with the Yellow River Basin transitioning from being low in the north and high in the south to being low in the east and high in the west. The high-value area expands towards the west and reaches its peak in 2012, primarily in Gansu Province. However, the high-value area decreases in 2021.

The revised carbon account can accurately reflect the characteristics of the cities in the Yellow River Basin Economic Zone. The use of this revised carbon account as an ecological compensation measure has dual benefits: it promotes the development of ecological protection areas and creates a sense of urgency for carbon emission reduction in areas with carbon account deficits. It is beneficial to compel cities to prioritise the work of reducing carbon emissions and expedite the region’s achievement of the “double carbon” target.

Ecological compensation amount centre of the gravity migration pattern

ArcGIS10.2 was utilised to create a migration map, as shown in Fig. 6, to examine the annual shift in the centre of gravity of the amount of ecological compensation in the Yellow River Basin. Figure 6 shows that the centre of gravity of the Yellow River Basin is situated in the middle section of the river, between approximately 108°20′-109°20′E and 36°30′-37°15′N. The majority of this centre of gravity is concentrated within the city of Yan’an. The city of Yan’an lacks sufficient ecological compensation, and the shift in the centre of gravity is caused primarily by Tongchuan City, which is located south of Yan’an. This is because Tongchuan City has a highly developed energy and resource industry, and coal mining has historically been the dominant industry in the area, resulting in significant carbon emissions. Owing to the decrease in the local coal mining industry, the city’s economic growth rate has been diminishing, posing challenges in relying on local financial expenditures to manage the environment. Throughout the study period, the centre of gravity predominantly shifted in a northwest‒southeast direction, exhibiting minimal displacement and a gradual migration rate in most years. Before 2012, migration primarily occurs towards the southeast, driven by the significant economic expansion of capital cities in the eastern region, such as Jinan and Zhengzhou, as well as the increase in carbon emissions. After 2012, it relocates to the northwest because of the rapid expansion of heavy industry and mining in areas such as Lanzhou City, Gansu Province, and Wuwei City. The execution of the control measures is delayed, resulting in elevated ecological emissions. Control measures are adopted at a later stage, resulting in a higher level of ecological compensation. As the per capita wealth of the eastern cities steadily increases, stricter carbon emission restrictions are also implemented, resulting in a progressive decrease in their ecocompensation amount over the years. This shift in the amount of ecocompensation results in movement of the centre of gravity.

Mechanisms influencing the ecological compensation level

The geodetector factor detection function is used to calculate the determinant q value and significance level of each driving factor (refer to Table 6). The driving role of these factors in the spatiotemporal evolution of the amount of ecological compensation in the Yellow River Basin is subsequently examined. According to the data presented in Fig. 7a, the impact of the level of green technology is often greater than that of other factors. The significance level of each region is within 1%, indicating that the green technology level is the main factor influencing the spatiotemporal evolution of the amount of ecological compensation. Green digital technology offers technical assistance in managing industrial and agricultural production waste, resulting in a reduction in regional net CO2 emissions. Additionally, it enhances the efficiency of urban technical services and industrial manufacturing, thereby stimulating urban economic development and consequently reducing the value of the ecocompensation quota. Furthermore, when the basin as a whole is considered, the ecological compensation quota is influenced primarily by social concern and inwards investment. Additionally, the industrial structure and energy consumption play significant roles in driving these changes. The degree of effect of elements in each section of the basin often progressively increases as one moves from upstream to downstream. The impact of FDI is particularly significant in the upstream sector, driven by advancements in investment and collaboration in Gansu and other provinces, as well as a heightened global focus on carbon emissions. The midstream region of the basin is a significant coal mining area in China. Owing to its unique geomorphology and lack of reclamation, the region experiences severe soil erosion. As a result, the society and government in this region have prioritised environmental protection and successfully implemented measures to restrict carbon dioxide emissions at both the social and policy levels. The downstream area is most affected by the level of green technology. Importantly, the influence of foreign investment on the spatiotemporal evolution of downstream ecocompensation is less than 0.1 and not statistically significant at the 10% level. This could be because the downstream society and government have higher levels of economic and social development, and the impact of local investment is greater.

Figure 7b illustrates the influence of each driver on the spatiotemporal evolution of the amount of ecological compensation in the Yellow River Basin. Throughout the study period, the variation in green technology levels has a slightly more significant influence on the spatiotemporal changes in the amount of ecological compensation than the other factors do. This influence gradually increases from 2004 to 2019 but rapidly declines in 2020 and 2021, likely due to the disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, which hindered progress in scientific and technological research and development related to this field. The two most influential elements after the degree of green technology are social concern and inwards investment. Social concern experiences a rapid increase during the study period, with an average annual growth rate of 104.2%. On the other hand, the influence of inwards investment remains more stable. The degree of influence of the industrial structure and energy consumption varies significantly between 2007 and 2017, with an inverted U-shaped trend for the industrial structure and a positive U-shaped trend for energy consumption. In particular, the degree of influence of energy consumption reaches a high level after 2014. Throughout the study period, the amount of government attention remains consistently low, with little relevance.

Discussion

This paper calculates the sensitivity of the carbon account by integrating the GDP, population, and land area of the study area. Additionally, a revised carbon account is constructed, and the spatiotemporal distribution pattern of the amount of ecological compensation in the study area is examined via the local Moran’s index and the centre of gravity migration method. The subsequent deliberations are as follows:

(1) The carbon accounts before modification exhibit a conspicuous pattern of distribution and dispersion, with the capital city of the watershed economic zone serving as the central hub of the region with the highest carbon value. After modification, the distribution of carbon accounts becomes more even. The majority of the originally high-value areas in provincial capital cities now have negative values, indicating improved control over regional carbon emissions. These high-value areas are mostly cities with relatively high levels of economic development and high carbon emissions. Compared with positive values, the revised carbon account presents a greater number of negative values, primarily focused on regions with superior ecological environmental protection and environmental governance. This finding aligns with the conclusions drawn by Wang et al.57 concerning the concept of ecological compensation for carbon emissions. The revised carbon account serves multiple purposes. Ecological compensation can support the ecological protection of postdevelopment areas. It also compensates areas with higher pollution control costs. Additionally, it exerts ecological protection pressure on industrially developed and highly polluted areas. Furthermore, it coordinates the environmental governance of the watershed, ensuring a more scientific and reasonable mechanism system.

(2) When the spatial correlation of ecocompensation in the Yellow River Basin Economic Zone is examined via the local Moran index, the distribution of ecocompensation is relatively scattered during the study period. However, there is a noticeable trend of clustering, with some provincial capitals exhibiting a low–high agglomeration pattern. Specifically, the level of ecocompensation in the provincial capitals and a small portion of the surrounding areas is lower than that in nearby cities. This finding indicates that some regions with higher levels of economic development invest more in reducing carbon emissions, whereas some cities with developed industries and subhigh economic levels have lower environmental protection inputs themselves and, at the same time, bear the transfer of high-polluting industries from provincial capital cities, resulting in high ecocompensation expenditures. The results are consistent with Sporkmann et al.‘s58 study of the localised Moran index of carbon emissions but do not draw conclusions similar to those of this study about the special low–high agglomeration in and around the capital city of the province, probably because it is difficult to see a more obvious distribution pattern due to the limitations of the scope of the study area and the type of agglomeration.

(3) This study is the first to examine the centre of gravity transfer law of the amount of ecological compensation in China’s Yellow River Basin economic zone. The findings reveal that the centre of gravity of ecological compensation in the Yellow River Basin is typically located in the central part of the country, with Yan’an city in the middle reaches being the most common location. Before 2012, the centre of gravity of the Yellow River Basin economic zone primarily moves towards economically developed regions, but after 2012, it starts shifting frequently and moves towards the northwestern part of China. The act of migrating from one place to another stems from two factors: first, the Chinese government’s heightened emphasis on carbon emissions in heavily polluted areas since 2012, and second, the rapid economic growth in various regions of the basin. These factors mitigated the polarisation trend in the distribution of ecological credit compensation. The trend of the centre of gravity migration post-2012 aligns with the findings of Song et al.59; however, the migration direction before 2012 is the opposite. This discrepancy arises from the different measurement models of carbon accounting utilised in this study. Before 2012, the disparity in economic and social development among cities in the Yellow River Basin is more pronounced, resulting in divergent migration trends. Conversely, after 2012, this developmental gap diminishes, leading to convergence in the centre of gravity migration trends.

(4) During the study period, the total amount of ecocredit compensation in the Yellow River Basin Economic Zone consistently increases. The total amount of payment and indemnification remains balanced, with the payment area being approximately one-sixth of the compensation area. The top five cities in the Yellow River Basin, ranked by payment and indemnification areas, exhibits a predominantly north‒south distribution. Furthermore, the payment area shows a greater degree of decentralisation in its distribution. The cities at the forefront of the payment area are characterised by the presence of energy-intensive industries such as the chemical industry and metallurgy. The carbon emissions of the regional capital cities are primarily concentrated at the lower end of the spectrum of offsetting as a result of the local carbon emission policy and the relocation of high-pollution sectors. To summarise, the ecological compensation priority order of cities in China’s Yellow River Basin Economic Zone has remained relatively stable from 2004 to 2021. The regional ecological compensation payments and indemnification amounts are well balanced. The findings of this study can serve as a valuable reference for formulating medium- and long-term policies. This paper reaches analogous conclusions to those of Zhai et al.60 regarding the increase in total ecocompensation credits and the highest-ranking regions for ecocompensation surplus; however, these conclusions pertain solely to a limited number of compensated areas and neglect factors such as the expenses associated with urban carbon abatement and the degree of economic development.

(5) The spatial and temporal changes in the amount of ecological compensation in the Yellow River Basin are driven primarily by several factors. Among these factors, the level of green technology, social and environmental concerns, and foreign investment have greater influences than other factors do. Additionally, the interaction between the green technology level and energy consumption consistently has a greater influence. This suggests that the level of green technology plays a crucial role in reducing emissions and mitigating carbon impacts. The downstream factors have a greater degree of influence than the upstream and midstream factors do, suggesting that the green and healthy development of the downstream economy has been successful. The level of influence of green technology is strong during the research period, and the influence of social and environmental concerns and energy consumption increases rapidly. The findings regarding the impact of economic development and technological inputs on carbon emission reduction align with the conclusions of L. Xu et al.61, who asserted that economic advancement and green technological investments are crucial for mitigating carbon emissions, emphasising the need to enhance industrial transformation and green technological innovation.

Nevertheless, there are still certain limitations of this paper: (1) This study may encounter issues related to inadequate or erroneous data, particularly in the quantitative evaluation of carbon emissions and ecological services in specific regions. Future research can enhance data collection, particularly regarding ecological services and carbon emissions at the grassroots level, to increase the model’s accuracy and reliability. (2) This study relies primarily on current ecological and economic data and neglects the effects of long-term environmental changes, which could result in erroneous predictions of future ecocompensation requirements. Future research may establish dynamic ecological compensation models that consider long-term ecological alterations and economic development trajectories to enhance the mechanism’s adaptability. (3) The potential effects of existing policies on the ecological compensation mechanism have not been thoroughly examined, and the intricacies and variability of policy implementation may be neglected. Future studies can enhance the evaluation of the implementation effects of pertinent policies, thereby establishing a foundation for policy optimisation and elucidating the actual influence of policies on the ecological compensation mechanism.

Conclusion and recommendations

Conclusion

Using data on carbon emissions and carbon absorption in cities within China’s Yellow River Basin economic zone from 2004 to 2021, we examined the spatial and temporal trends in carbon accounting and ecological compensation quotas in the region. This was accomplished by constructing a revised carbon accounting model, local Moran index, and centre of gravity relocation model. We also analysed the factors influencing the spatial and temporal evolution of revised carbon accounting. The study yielded the following conclusions:

(1) The agglomeration distribution pattern, which initially focuses on economically developed regions with distinct provincial boundaries, transitions to a decentralised distribution pattern centred on cities’ carbon emission reduction capabilities, resulting in a reduced regional disparity and a more rational carbon account value.

(2) The local spatial correlation of the ecocompensation credit distribution, which deviates from the singular evaluation mode, is minimal, and the local Moran index is insignificant in most regions, except for some low-low and low-high agglomeration cities.

(3) The centre of gravity is situated in the middle portion of the basin, migrating to the southeast due to ongoing regional disparities in ecological compensation.

(4) The total amount of ecocompensation increases annually, with balanced payments and compensation. The priority rankings of ecocompensation within the Yellow River Basin Economic Zone remain relatively stable, reflecting the demand for regional compensation amounts, and the policy is more actionable.

(5) The influences of the level of green technology, social and environmental concerns, and foreign investment are more pronounced, whereas the interplay between the level of green technology and energy consumption plays a greater role in regulating carbon emissions and diminishing ecological compensation requirements.

Policy recommendations

(1) The establishment of a carbon emission ecological compensation mechanism that comprehensively considers regional economic and social aspects should be promoted. When devising ecocompensation policies, it is essential to consider not only the pollution levels and associated expenses of the region but also factors such as the gross local product, population size, area, or other pertinent indicators. These considerations should be factored into the computation of the amount of ecocompensation. It is imperative to assess the financial ability of cities thoroughly while providing specific compensation to cities that excel in environmental pollution control. Additionally, a well-designed and sustainable compensation mechanism should be established on the basis of the relative level of pollution emissions among the cities in the region.

(2) A monitoring method should be implemented to track the transfer of carbon emissions and pollution and facilitate the development of a cohesive ecological management system for the watershed. Provincial capital cities face increased pressure to safeguard the environment, which may lead to the relocation and concentration of highly polluting industries in these areas. However, urban pollution has not been adequately addressed. It is imperative to enhance the exchange and collaboration of carbon emission reductions among cities in the basin. This will lead to the establishment of a unified ecological management system for the basin, the implementation of standardised regulations for the cities in the basin, and the creation of a monitoring mechanism for pollutant emissions across the cities. These measures are necessary to prevent the occurrence of illicit trading of pollutant emissions among cities and to effectively control the formation of pollution hotspots.

(3) Stricter regulations for regions with significant carbon emissions should be implemented. Presently, certain regions within the Yellow River Basin, including Tongchuan City in Shanxi Province, Lanzhou City in Gansu Province, and Wuwei City in the Yellow River Basin, exhibit significant disparities in carbon emissions. These areas have recorded elevated values in the revised carbon account, resulting in an inequitable distribution of regional ecological compensation. It is imperative to prioritise the policy emphasis on ecological compensation for carbon emissions in these locations and employ financial and administrative measures to specifically implement laws that restrict carbon emissions.

(4) A comprehensive system for measuring and monitoring carbon emissions should be established, and policies to assist urban ecological compensation should be implemented. Regional economic, social, and environmental data serve as the foundation for quantifying ecological compensation. It is crucial to measure reliable data in a standardised manner. The oversight of data collection, organisation, and reporting should be enhanced to establish a solid foundation of data for the efficient and logical functioning of the ecological compensation mechanism. However, depending solely on cities to pay for ecological compensation may not fully meet the demands of the areas being compensated. It would be more effective to provide suitable subsidies that offer financial and policy support for regional ecological compensation.

(5) Funding and resources for the advancement of green technology R&D should be enhanced and the fostering of public consciousness regarding environmental conservation should be promoted. The infrastructure for urban technological research and development should be enhanced, financial and resource assistance for green technological research and development should be allocated, public consciousness of environmental preservation should be promoted, environmental knowledge should be disseminated, the initiatives of social environmental protection organisations should be endorsed, and collaboration among societal entities should be fostered.
