Age-related differences in pleural adhesion and perioperative outcomes in uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery segmentectomy
Authors: Seha Ahn, Youngkyu Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvanced age is often associated with increased technical difficulty during lung resection; however, the mechanisms underlying age-related operative burden during segmentectomy remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate age-related differences in pleural adhesion, operative complexity, and perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) segmentectomy for lung cancer.MethodsConsecutive patients who underwent uniportal VATS segmentectomy were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were stratified into two groups according to age (<70 vs. ≥70 years). Baseline characteristics, radiologic and operative findings, pleural adhesion status, and postoperative outcomes were compared between the groups. Pleural adhesion was categorized as absent, partial, or whole. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of pleural adhesion.ResultsA total of 814 patients were included, of whom 683 were aged <70 years and 131 were aged ≥70 years. Pleural adhesion was significantly more frequent in patients aged ≥70 years than in those aged <70 years (26.7% vs. 8.8%, P<0.001), and whole pleural adhesion was also more common in the elderly group (5.3% vs. 1.8%, P=0.02). In multivariable analysis, age remained independently associated with pleural adhesion (adjusted OR 1.11 per year increase, 95% CI: 1.08–1.14, P<0.001). Patients aged ≥70 years demonstrated greater operative burden, with longer operative time (median 91 vs. 89 minutes, P=0.002) and longer postoperative chest tube duration {median [interquartile range]: 1 [1–3] vs. 1 [1–1] days, P<0.001}. The incidence of postoperative complications was significantly higher in the elderly group (12.2% vs. 3.8%, P<0.001), including a higher rate of prolonged air leak. Surgical margin distance was comparable between the two groups.ConclusionsElderly patients undergoing uniportal VATS segmentectomy exhibited a significantly higher incidence of pleural adhesion and were associated with longer operative time and postoperative drainage, as well as higher complication rates. Nevertheless, acceptable postoperative outcomes can be achieved, supporting the feasibility of segmentectomy in carefully selected elderly patients.

Introduction

Randomized phase III trials, including the JCOG0802/WJOG4607L and CALGB 140503 studies, have established anatomical segmentectomy as an oncologically acceptable alternative to lobectomy for patients with peripheral non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) measuring ≤2 cm with pathologically confirmed node-negative disease (1,2).

With advances in minimally invasive techniques, uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become increasingly adopted, offering reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and comparable oncologic outcomes (3,4).

Although segmentectomy was initially considered technically demanding, growing surgical experience has demonstrated its safety and feasibility across various anatomical configurations, including complex segmentectomies that require division of multiple intersegmental planes (5-7). As indications for segmentectomy expand, particularly among elderly patients with limited pulmonary reserve, attention has shifted toward identifying factors that influence operative complexity and perioperative morbidity.

Advanced age is well recognized as a predictor of increased perioperative risk in thoracic surgery (8,9). However, the anatomical contributors underlying this association remain incompletely defined. Pleural adhesion is a clinically relevant intraoperative factor that can increase technical difficulty and postoperative complications in minimally invasive lung resection (10,11).

We hypothesized that elderly patients undergoing uniportal VATS segmentectomy would exhibit a higher prevalence of pleural adhesion, contributing to increased operative burden and postoperative morbidity. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the relationship between age and pleural adhesion and to assess its impact on perioperative outcomes in a large institutional cohort. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0570/rc).

Methods

Patient population and study design

This retrospective observational study included all thoracic surgeries performed by two board-certified thoracic surgeons at Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital between June 2019 and December 2025. Data were extracted from a prospectively maintained institutional database and cross-validated with electronic medical records. All patients who underwent uniportal VATS segmentectomy during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Patients were stratified according to age (<70 vs. ≥70 years) for comparative analysis. Age 70 years was selected as a clinically relevant threshold based on institutional practice, as patients aged ≥70 years undergoing lung cancer surgery under general anesthesia routinely undergo mandatory preoperative cardiac evaluation, including transthoracic echocardiography. This institutional protocol reflects the recognition of increased cardiopulmonary risk in this population and provided a practical and clinically meaningful definition of elderly patients in the present study. A total of 814 patients who underwent anatomical segmentectomy were included in the final analysis. Lobectomies and wedge resections were excluded. Demographic, clinicopathologic, intraoperative, and perioperative data were reviewed, including age, sex, smoking history, tumor size, radiologic characteristics, operative time, anesthesia time, intraoperative blood loss, pleural adhesion status, surgical margin distance, chest tube duration, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, and 30-day mortality.

Pleural adhesion was intraoperatively categorized as absent, partial, or whole based on its functional impact on surgical manipulation. Pleural adhesion status was routinely documented in operative records at the time of surgery. The classification system (absent, partial, whole) was predefined and consistently applied during intraoperative assessment rather than assigned retrospectively for the purpose of this study. All procedures were performed by two board-certified thoracic surgeons who adhered to the same institutional criteria for adhesion classification. Adhesions were classified as “absent” when no adhesion was present or when only focal, band-like adhesions were observed that did not interfere with lung expansion or segmental dissection. “Partial” adhesion was defined as adhesions involving approximately one-third of the pleural surface of the lobe corresponding to the target segment, requiring adhesiolysis during the procedure. “Whole” adhesion referred to extensive adhesions involving the majority of the pleural cavity. This classification is conceptually consistent with previously reported grading systems based on pleural surface involvement. In such systems, adhesions involving less than one-third of the pleural surface without surgical disturbance are categorized as low-grade (grade 0–1), whereas adhesions involving one-third or more typically require adhesiolysis and are associated with increased operative burden (grade ≥2). Accordingly, in the present study, “absent” corresponds to grade 0–1, while “partial” and “whole” correspond to grade 2–3 and grade 4, respectively. Importantly, adhesions located in anatomically limited regions (e.g., hilar or diaphragmatic areas) were categorized as “absent” if they did not compromise surgical exposure or lung mobilization relevant to the segmentectomy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital, College of Medicine, The Catholic University of Korea (No. PC26RASI0056), and informed consent was taken from all the patients.

Institutional protocol for lung cancer resection

Segmentectomy was performed according to institutional oncologic criteria for early-stage NSCLC, which included: (I) peripheral tumor ≤2.0 cm on computed tomography (CT); (II) low metabolic activity [maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) ≤3.0] on fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT); (III) an anticipated surgical margin ≥2.0 cm or exceeding the tumor size; and (IV) absence of N1 metastasis confirmed by intraoperative frozen-section biopsy.

Lymph node dissection was performed either systematically or in a lobe-specific manner depending on tumor characteristics and intraoperative findings. All procedures were conducted via a uniportal VATS approach.

Perioperative management

Postoperative pleural drainage was managed using a digital suction system (Thopaz®, Medela Healthcare, Baar, Switzerland). Continuous negative pressure of −15 cmH2O was applied immediately after surgery and was routinely reduced to −7 cmH2O on postoperative day 1 in the absence of significant air leakage. Chest tubes were removed once air leakage had resolved for at least 12 hours and daily drainage volume was less than 200 mL. Patients were discharged on the day following chest tube removal if they were clinically stable and met institutional discharge criteria.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Pearson’s Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. To identify independent predictors of pleural adhesion, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Age was analyzed as a continuous variable. Clinically relevant covariates including sex, smoking history, tumor size, and type of segmentectomy (simple vs. complex/basal) were entered into the model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Postoperative complications were compared between age groups using univariable analysis. Multivariable modeling for complications was not performed due to the limited number of events, to avoid model overfitting. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study of patients undergoing uniportal VATS segmentectomy, elderly patients (≥70 years) demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of pleural adhesion and experienced greater operative burden and postoperative morbidity compared with younger patients. Postoperative complications in elderly patients are likely multifactorial, reflecting not only intraoperative factors such as pleural adhesion but also age-related physiological decline, comorbidities, and impaired pulmonary reserve. Although the difference in operative time was modest at the cohort level, it should be interpreted with caution. In clinical practice, adhesiolysis can require an additional 30 to 90 minutes depending on the extent and density of adhesions, representing a substantial technical burden. The relatively small observed difference in total operative time may reflect heterogeneity in segmentectomy procedures, where differences in anatomical complexity between segments may offset the additional time required for adhesiolysis. Further studies stratified by segment location and procedural complexity are warranted to better delineate the true operative impact of pleural adhesions. Given that segmentectomy is now an oncologically accepted alternative to lobectomy for selected early-stage NSCLC (1,2), identifying factors that influence operative complexity and perioperative risk is increasingly important.

Pleural adhesion represents a clinically relevant intraoperative factor in minimally invasive thoracic surgery. Prior studies have linked adhesions to prolonged operative time, increased technical difficulty, and higher complication rates in VATS procedures (10,11). While advanced age has consistently been associated with worse perioperative outcomes, the anatomical contributors underlying this association have not been well defined. Our findings suggest that pleural adhesion may partially mediate this relationship. Importantly, age remained an independent predictor of pleural adhesion after multivariable adjustment. However, this association should be interpreted with caution, as age may not represent a direct biological cause but rather a surrogate marker reflecting cumulative pleural insults over time, including prior subclinical infections, smoking-related injury, and environmental exposures. Therefore, the observed relationship between age and pleural adhesion likely reflects the combined effects of long-term pleural remodeling rather than age itself as an isolated causal factor. This perspective may provide a more nuanced explanation for the increased operative complexity observed in elderly patients.

Dense hilar fibrosis and nodal rigidity may also increase operative difficulty (12,13), but these characteristics are subjective and lack standardized intraoperative grading. Their exclusion from quantitative analysis represents a limitation and underscores the need for objective assessment tools in future studies.

Preoperative prediction of pleural adhesion remains challenging. Imaging modalities such as lung ultrasonography, respiratory dynamic CT, and conventional CT findings (e.g., pleural thickening) have shown potential utility (14-18). In clinical practice, patterns observed after CT-guided percutaneous needle biopsy (PCNB), including the presence or absence of pneumothorax, may provide indirect clues regarding pleural mobility. However, this remains a clinical observation rather than validated evidence and warrants further investigation.

Notably, despite increased operative time and postoperative morbidity in elderly patients, surgical margin adequacy was not compromised. These findings suggest that anatomical segmentectomy remains technically feasible in elderly patients when performed with meticulous technique. Therefore, advanced age alone should not contraindicate segmentectomy but should prompt anticipation of adhesion-related complexity. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that surgeons should anticipate a higher likelihood of pleural adhesions in elderly patients and consider tailored intraoperative strategies, including cautious initial port placement, meticulous adhesiolysis, and a lower threshold for conversion when dense adhesions are encountered.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Pleural adhesion grading was based on intraoperative assessment using predefined criteria documented at the time of surgery; however, the possibility of interobserver variability cannot be completely excluded. In addition, our classification, which was based on operative relevance, does not account for adhesion density or detailed anatomical distribution, both of which may further influence surgical difficulty. Pulmonary function parameters and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) status were not included in the analysis, and their potential confounding effects on pleural adhesion and postoperative outcomes cannot be fully excluded. Patients with prior thoracic surgery, pneumothorax, or radiotherapy were not explicitly excluded; however, such cases were rare in our cohort due to referral patterns and clinical practice. In addition, clinically significant new pleural adhesions after prior thoracic surgery were infrequently observed in our experience, possibly related to the use of anti-adhesion measures. Nonetheless, their potential confounding effects cannot be completely excluded. Hilar and nodal characteristics were not objectively quantified. Multivariable analysis was performed for pleural adhesion, where the number of events was sufficient for stable modeling. However, multivariable modeling for postoperative complications was not undertaken due to the relatively low event rate, which may compromise model stability and increase the risk of overfitting. Postoperative complications were comprehensively recorded, including minor events, according to institutional standards. However, a detailed subclassification of complications was not performed, which may limit the interpretation of their specific relationship with pleural adhesion. In addition, the length of hospital stay in our cohort was influenced by institutional practices, including discharge after confirmation of final pathology and patient-related factors such as travel distance. These institutional characteristics have also been described in our previous publications, and therefore length of stay may not directly reflect postoperative recovery. Finally, long-term oncologic outcomes were not evaluated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elderly patients undergoing uniportal VATS segmentectomy exhibited a higher incidence of pleural adhesion, which was associated with increased operative burden and postoperative morbidity. Preoperative anticipation of adhesion risk may facilitate perioperative planning and improve surgical safety in this growing population.
