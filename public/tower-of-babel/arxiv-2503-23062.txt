Shape and Texture Recognition in Large Vision-Language Models
Sagi Eppel, Mor Bismut, Alona Strugatski

Abstract
Figure 1 : Testing vision language models’ (VLM) ability to recognize shapes and textures using the Large Shape and Textures Dataset (LAS&T). The models are asked to identify the same shape in different variations, which include different orientations, textures/colors/materials, and environments. The dataset is composed of both simulated and real-world images. Shapes and textures are the basic building blocks of visual perception. The ability to identify shapes regardless of orientation, texture, or context, and to recognize textures and materials independently of their associated objects, is essential for a general visual understanding of the world. This work introduces the Large Shapes and Textures dataset (LAS&T), a giant collection of highly diverse shapes and textures, created by unsupervised extraction of patterns from natural images. This dataset is used to benchmark how effectively leading Large Vision-Language Models (LVLM/VLM) recognize and represent shapes, textures, and materials in 2D and 3D scenes. For shape recognition, we test the models’ ability to match images of identical shapes that differ in orientation, texture, color, or environment. Our results show that the shape-recognition capabilities of LVLMs remain well below human performance, especially when multiple transformations are applied. LVLMs rely predominantly on high-level and semantic features and struggle with abstract shapes lacking class associations. For texture and material recognition, we evaluated the models’ ability to identify images with identical textures and materials across different objects and environments. Interestingly, leading LVLMs approach human-level performance in recognizing materials in 3D scenes, yet substantially underperform humans when identifying simpler, more abstract 2D textures and shapes. These results are consistent across a wide range of leading LVLMs (GPT/Gemini/LLama/Qwen) and foundation vision models (DINO/CLIP), exposing major deficiencies in the ability of VLMs to extract low-level visual features. In contrast, humans and simple nets trained directly for these tasks achieve high accuracy. The LAS&T dataset, featuring over 700,000 images for 2D/3D shape and textures recognition and retrieval, is freely available at: this URL.

Introduction

Shapes and textures are the fundamental building blocks of visual perception. The ability to identify and match shapes is a trivial task for humans and is one of the most basic cognitive skill related to spatial perception and object recognition

[

3

]
. Therefore, it can be expected that any intelligent system that acquires general visual understanding will master both shape and texture recognition. Large vision language models (LVLM) have emerged as the leading computer vision approaches for achieving a general visual understanding of the world

, but at the same time can fail in seemingly simple tasks
[

,

, which brings to question what they actually “see” in the image.
Most LVLM benchmarks concentrate on higher level tasks like: scene understanding, scientific and logical reasoning, or linking visual inputs to semantic concepts (e.g., scene description, shape classification)
[

,

,

. In contrast, fundamental perceptual skills such as simple shape or texture matching remain largely unexplored in this domain.
The goal of this work is to test whether leading VLMs like GPT, Claude, Gemini, LLAMA, Qwen, and foundation vision models (CLIP, DINO) have a basic understanding of 2D and 3D shapes as well as textures and materials. Mainly, can the models identify the same shape or object in different settings, even when made of different materials and textures or when viewed with different orientations and environments (
Fig.

1
). Similarly, can the model identify the same textures and materials even when they appear on different objects and environments? These tasks are trivial for humans

and can provide valuable insight into how models perceive and represent the world. Cognitive and vision research seeks perception blind spots, where humans can miss the basic aspect of a scene (gorilla in the room
[

[

,

. Similarly, identifying fundamental gaps in models’ performance can help us uncover their internal representation of visual information.

Overview on main results:

The results of these tests show that while all models show some level of shape understanding, they still trail far beyond humans on this task. Transforming aspects of the shape, such as orientation, textures, or background, can lead to a sharp reduction in matching accuracy despite having a minor or no effect on humans. It seems that the models rely on various descriptive features of the shape (ragged, porous, bulky…) and association to semantic classes (cat, house..) and use these features to represent and match shapes. However, in contrast to humans, changing the shape orientation, texture, and even background often changes the descriptive features the model assigns to the same shape. In addition, these descriptive features are often not unique enough to distinguish between different shapes. If the shape corresponds to an object with a clear semantic class (bird, car…), the models perform far better compared to more abstract shapes that have more low-level distinctive features. This suggests that, unlike humans
[
26
]
, VLM visual representation prioritizes the high-level semantic and class-based concepts tied to the specific shape, and has limited access to the raw shape properties. Another interesting result is that the models seem to perform better in 3D physically realistic scenes compared to the much easier but more abstract 2D scenes. Indicating that task complexity alone does not determine performance; familiarity and the level of abstraction also play a significant role. These results seem to be persistent for both shapes and textures and across a wide range of top VLM and foundation vision models, indicating that despite their ability to understand complex scenes, these models fail to learn some of the most basic aspects of the physical world, and have limited capacity to represent low-level visual features. Interestingly, much simpler nets trained, from scratch, directly on these tasks achieved high precision, suggesting that the training methods and data are the main barrier.

3
The Large Shape & Textures Dataset (LAS&T)

We introduce the Large Shape and Texture dataset (LAS&T), a large-scale, highly diverse dataset for evaluating shape and texture recognition and retrieval (
Fig.

1
). The dataset is designed to systematically disentangle key visual factors, including 2D/3D, rotation, environment, semantic/natural shapes, textures, and materials, thereby enabling fine-grained analysis of how each element influences visual perception. Evaluation involves tasks where models are asked to identify the same shape under varying textures, orientations, backgrounds, or the same texture/material across different shapes and environments (
Figs.

1
and
2
). Another goal of the dataset is to substantially increase the diversity and scale of data available for training and evaluation. Existing datasets either rely on manual annotation, restricting their scale and geometric complexity due to human labor constraints
[
29
,
40
]
, or on synthetic data, which, though abundant, are constrained by procedural generation rules and fail to capture real-world complexity
[
6
,
48
,
45
]
. To overcome these limitations, LAS&T employs unsupervised extraction of shapes directly from natural images (
Sec.

4.1
). This yields data constrained only by the variety of available natural images
[
29
,
40
,
31
]
. For 3D shapes, we additionally constructed a general real-world benchmark of 130 photographs, each capturing a 3D shape coated with different materials in different environments and viewpoints (
Secs.

4.2
and
5
). This is the first general real-image benchmark for single-image 3D shape retrieval, testing recognition independently of material, pose, or context. LAS&T comprises four components: 2D Shapes, 3D Shapes, 2D Textures, and 3D Materials, each divided into multiple subsets. As far as we know, each of these sets is the most diverse in its field in terms of different shapes, classes, materials, textures, and environments, often by scale of magnitude (many of the specific tasks in LAS&T are not covered by any dataset we are aware of). The dataset is available under a CC0 license.

4
Dataset preparation

The LAS&T dataset is designed to evaluate visual recognition of shapes and textures by systematically isolating the effects of various factors, including 3D/2D orientation, backgrounds, environments, semantic versus natural shapes, and textures/materials. Each factor is independently or jointly varied, and a Vision-Language Model (VLM) is tasked with identifying the same shape or texture after transformation. This enables precise mapping of how each element influences recognition, yielding insights beyond those available from existing datasets. In the 2D case, shapes are represented as binary masks of objects or natural image contours (
Figs.

3
and
4.1
). Textures are real-world images of uniform textures taken from the VasTextures dataset
[
20
]
. Shapes are positioned in random orientations, and both shape and background regions are either filled with a specific texture or kept uniform (black, white, or solid color) (
Figs.

1
and
2
top). For 3D scenes, we use the Blender 3D engine to simulate realistic light, matter interactions. Objects are sampled from ObjaVerse
[
17
]
, covering roughly 1k different categories and 100k unique 3D models, and rendered with physically based materials (PBRs) from VasTextures (about 100k PBRs). Illumination is provided by 500 distinct High Dynamic Range panoramic Images (HDRI) from the PolyHaven repository
[
47
]
. Each object is rendered with a selected PBR material and a random HDRI environment. The 3D setup presents far greater challenges than the 2D one, as rotations introduce occlusions, and PBR materials capture realistic reflections, refractions, and complex illumination effects.

4.1
Natural Shape Extraction and Pattern Infusion

While 2D shapes can be sourced from segmentation datasets like COCO
[
40
]
or SAM
[
29
]
, which contain millions of human-annotated object masks, these masks tend to have simple, smooth boundaries due to annotation constraints and are semantically tied to real-world objects (
Fig.

3
). To evaluate VLMs’ ability to recognize complex, abstract natural shapes, we propose a new method for sampling shapes directly from images. By thresholding a single image channel (e.g., color or intensity) and extracting connected components above a specified size and thickness (
Fig.

4
), we obtain shapes with intricate, natural boundaries derived from real-world patterns which are not bounded by specific object or semantic classes (
Fig.

3
). This simple method can be seen as a noisy sampling of real-world patterns, producing a far richer and more varied shape distribution than existing datasets (see
Fig.

3
).

4.2
Real-World Natural Images Benchmark For 3D Shape Recognition

The 3D shape retrieval component of the LAS&T dataset simulates physically realistic material surfaces and light interactions. To validate the simulated data, we built a general real-world images benchmark for 3D shapes retrieval, where each object maintains the same 3D shape across images, while materials, orientations, and surroundings vary (
Fig.

5
). Objects, ranging from everyday items and rocks to abstract cast and clay forms, were molded or coated with different materials using adhesives to apply the coating. Coatings include granular materials (e.g., sand, spice, coal, grass), paints or various of ribbons and wrappings (
Fig.

5
). Images were captured in varied indoor and outdoor scenes under different lighting and viewpoints, producing 130 real photographs, each representing a distinct object-material pair. This constitutes the first real-world benchmark for evaluating 3D shape retrieval invariant to material, orientation, parts and environment. Meaning the first general dataset in which the 3D shape is the only element used for recognition.

Shape recognition: Results and discussion

The results of the shape recognition tests (
Fig.

1
) are given in
Tabs.

1
and
2
. It’s clear that all models have understood the task and can recognize both 2D and 3D shapes under various transformations. This can be seen both from the fact that all models performed way above a random guess, and achieved high accuracy for simpler matching tasks (
Tabs.

1
and
2
). In addition, when asked to explain their answers (both correct and incorrect), the models refer to the real features of the shape in each panel (For example, responses see Appendix
Secs.

13
and
14
). However, all models perform well below humans (
Tabs.

1
and
2
), the gap is particularly large for 2D and when multiple transformations are applied. For example, when both texture and orientation of the 2D shape were changed (
Figs.

1
and
1
), the matching accuracy of all models was in the range of 40-80% compared to perfect 100% for humans and 33% for a random guess.

What do the models see?

When asked to explain the answer, the LVLMs usually refers to a set of high-level descriptive features of the shape, like rough/narrow/bulky… or to similarity to a given set of classes (bird, house..). Samples of the explanations given by different models are available in Appendix
Secs.

13
and
14
.
Errors and mismatches often occur because the model assigns different descriptions to the same shape when it appears in different orientations or textures. Although both descriptions may be valid, they are often inconsistent with each other.
In addition, mistakes often arise from different shapes sharing the same high-level features (for example, two shapes being bulky, or curved) or from different shapes with the same perceived class (for example, two different shapes resemble a house). However, it does seem that the top-performing models (GPT, Gemini, Grok) can easily distinguish between the shape, texture, and background and describe each separately.
It should be noted that the verbal explanation the model gives to explain their answer (when asked to explain) strongly correlates with both the shapes in the image and the selected answer, suggesting that the decision process is clearly related to semantic concepts (See Appendix
Sec.

13
). At the same time, it seems that the models do not have access to the full shape information and only rely on a limited set of high-level features.

Semantic vs natural shapes:

The source of the shapes seems to have a significant effect on the accuracy of the models. All LVLMS were able to identify and match 2D shapes that directly correspond to silhouettes of objects related to classes with higher accuracy compared to more abstract 2D shapes, despite the latter having far more distinctive features. (
Fig.

3
,
Tab.

1
semantic vs. natural shapes). This again implies that the way LVLM understands images is by mapping the content into a high-level representation closely related to language concepts; when the shape does not strongly correspond to a unique semantic class, the accuracy drops. Given that LVLMs are usually trained by the image-to-text questions, it is likely that these models are incentivized to map the image into semantic-like concepts. This is in contrast to humans, for whom the vast majority of visual tasks are not related to language and therefore can deal with the shape much more directly
[
52
,
33
]
. This makes abstract visual understanding of VLM very limited compared to humans, who perform almost perfectly on all tests (
Tabs.

1
and
2
Humans).

Is the image verbal description enough?

To further test the hypothesis that the representation of the image in LVLM is strongly related to the semantic/verbal description, we try the following experiment. First, an LVLM was asked to independently describe the shape in each panel without referring to other panels in the image. Then another LVLM received the generated textual description without the image and was asked to determine which of the shapes match based on their description (For more details, see Appendix
Fig.

6
).

Once again, supporting the hypothesis that the internal representation of the images is strongly related to verbal and semantic concepts.

What information do foundation vision models extract?

The most direct way to examine what models ”know” about shapes is by analyzing their internal image representations or embeddings. While access to the embeddings and training data of models like GPT is not available. There are several open-source foundation vision models that give full access to their embedding. OPEN CLIP and DINO V2 are two of the most commonly used open source foundation vision models that generate such embeddings
[
51
,
46
]
. Accessing these embeddings allows us to probe the extent to which shape information is encoded. A basic test involves checking whether embeddings of the same shape (with variations in texture or orientation) are more similar to each other than to embeddings of different shapes. Using cosine similarity, we compared the embeddings for images of similar and different shapes (See Appendix
Fig.

8
). As shown in
Tabs.

1
and
2
(CLIP/DINO descriptors), the matching accuracy is comparable to that of LVLMs, but drops significantly in cases with large visual variations. However, embeddings from these models capture more than just shape. Therefore, embedding similarity does not necessarily reflect shape similarity, especially in complex images. To isolate shape-specific information, we trained an extraction network on the LAS&T dataset. This net takes a CLIP or DINO embedding as input and produces a new embedding that only represents shape similarity, filtering out unrelated visual information. Hence, the extraction net retrieves whatever shape-related information is present in the original embedding and discard the rest (See Appendix
Fig.

8
). The performance of this refined descriptor (
Tab.

1
.DINO/CLIP extracted) is better in most cases compared to the original descriptor. Still, performance remains well below human-level accuracy. This suggests that CLIP and DINO embeddings do not contain the full information about the shape. Nets trained specially for the task of extracting shape descriptors from images (Appendix
Fig.

7
) achieve near-perfect shape-matching accuracy (
Tabs.

1
and
2
.Specialize Net). This implies the limitation lies not in the extraction net or its training, but in the information content of the CLIP/DINO embeddings.

3D vs 2D and Difficulty vs Abstraction:

Another interesting result is that the accuracy of 3D shape matching tests is similar and even higher than that of 2D shape matching tests (
Tabs.

and

). This is surprising since 3D shape matching demands an understanding of occlusion perspective and illumination, making it far more complex than 2D. This result seems to imply that the complexity of the task is not the only factor that affects the model’s performance. It’s likely that the models that were trained on large amounts of real-world 3D scenes
[
51
]
have gained a better understanding of the more complex 3D objects, but have far less exposure to the simpler but also more abstract 2D shapes.

Shape matching difficulty and specially trained net:

The relative difficulty of the shape matching test can be estimated by training specialized nets for this task using the LAS&T dataset. Separate nets were trained to match shapes in 2D and 3D using images as input. These nets were trained to receive images containing shapes with various variations and generate a descriptor vector that represents the shape’s similarity (Appendix

These results confirm that the problem the LVLM has with shape recognition is not a result of the objective difficulty of the task, but rather of the way these models were trained. Training details and code, and weights for the LAS&T trained nets are available in the S.I (Appendix

For material recognition in 3D scenes, the models achieve relatively high accuracy with the highest performing model reaching almost human level, even when both object and environment changed (

Again, these results might be explained by the fact that vision language models are trained mostly on real-world images and, therefore far more familiar with real materials in different 3D environments than with the simpler but more abstract 2D textures.
Humans achieve high accuracy on all tasks and consistently perform better on 2D texture recognition than on 3D material recognition. Nets trained from scratch on this task using the LAS&T dataset achieve almost perfect accuracy and higher accuracy in 2D compared to 3D scene (

What does the model see?

When asked to explain their answer, the models refer to specific textures, colors, and materials features (metallic, stripes, or rock…), which proves that the model understands both the image and the task (Appendix

7
Conclusion

Shape and texture are the fundamental building blocks of visual perception, making their recognition and representation essential for any system seeking to achieve general visual understanding. This work explores how vision-language models (VLMs) recognize and represent these elements using a novel dataset that isolates each visual component’s effect. This separation enables us to gain new insights into how leading VLMs represent these concepts and reveals some of their blindspots and limitations.
Our analysis revealed several interesting findings:

Human-Level Gaps:
LVLMs show some understanding of shapes and textures but perform well below human levels, especially in abstract cases.

2D vs. 3D Performance:
The models performed similarly or even better on 3D shape and texture recognition compared to 2D. This is surprising given the added complexity of 3D tasks, which involve occlusion, perspective, and lighting. These results suggest that model performance is not strictly determined by task complexity, but also by the distribution of training data, which often emphasizes real-world 3D scenes.

Semantic Bias:
VLM representations of shapes rely heavily on semantic descriptions. Models performed substantially better on shapes associated with recognizable object categories than on abstract shapes, despite the latter having far more distinctive visual features. In contrast, human participants showed no such bias, achieving near-perfect accuracy in both conditions. Furthermore, when models were asked to first describe an image verbally and then perform shape matching using only that description, their performance was similar to cases where they directly processed the image. This suggests that VLMs’ internal shape representations closely parallel their verbal descriptions rather than capturing raw visual details.
This indicates that LVLMs tend to rely on high-level semantic features and class associations rather than comprehensive low-level shape representations. These findings suggest that current LVLM training paradigms, while effective in real-world object recognition, may not provide the level of visual abstraction required for human-level perception.
The LAS&T dataset, with its large-scale and highly diverse collection of patterns and images, provides a valuable resource for addressing this limitation, serving as both training data and a benchmarking tool.

9.1
Choosing text query

An important aspect of LVLM testing is how to express the query given to the model. While in theory, any clear description of the task should work, in practice different prompts can lead to up to 8% difference in accuracy. Interestingly detailed and exact prompts perform worse than short general ones. For example the short prompt:

“Which of the panels contains an object with an identical 3D shape to the object in panel A. Your answer must come as a single letter”

Give better accuracy than the long detailed prompt like:

“Carefully analyze the image. In panel A, there is an object with a specific shape. Your task is
to identify which other panel (B, C, or D) contains an object that

1) Has the exact same 3d shape as the object in panel A.

2) Has a different orientation compared to the object in panel A.

3)Has a different texture compared to the object in panel A.

Respond with ONLY the letter of the panel (B, C, or D) that meets all these criteria.”

Figure 8
:
Left: Shape and texture matching using vision foundation model embeddings (CLIP/DINO)
. Using the embedding of pretrained open CLIP/Dino V2 to match shape and textures was done simply by taking the pretrained version of these models and running them on a two different images of the same instance (shape/texture) and using pretrained DINO/CLIP to generate their embedding. One of these images was defined as an anchor and the other as positive. In addition, a few images of different random instances were also taken and processed to generate the embeddings, these were defined as negatives. The cosine similarities between the embeddings of the anchor and the positive descriptor and the anchor and negative descriptors were calculated. If the similarity between the embeddings of images of the same instance (anchor and positive) was the highest of all pairs this was considered correct, else this was considered an error. Note that this is a shape-matching test with 1 positive (Correct) choice and 2 negative (incorrect choices) similar to the 4 panels test given to the LVLMs. No training was used.

Right: Refining CLIP/DINO descriptors:
To obtain task-specific descriptors from the pretrained CLIP or DINO models, we first extract the image embeddings using the frozen backbone. These embeddings are then passed through an additional, trainable multi-layer perceptron (MLP), which outputs refined descriptors tailored to shape or texture recognition. The MLP, typically composed of 4–10 layers (with minimal gains beyond 4 layers), is trained using a contrastive learning approach. Specifically, for each batch, the cosine similarity between the anchor and positive (same instance) descriptors serves as the logit for the positive class, while the similarities between the anchor and negative (different instance) descriptors serve as the logits for the negative classes. These logits are converted to matching probabilities via a Softmax function and used to compute the cross-entropy loss. Importantly, the CLIP/DINO backbone remains frozen throughout training, ensuring that only the MLP adapts to the shape or texture recognition task. Altogether 4 separate nets were trained (for 2d shapes, 3D shapes, 2d textures, and 3D materials). The nets were trained using sets of images with maximum variation between instances, meaning everything including size was changed between instances.
