Characteristics of Cardiovascular Disease Prediction Models Considering Mental Disorders: A Systematic Review
Authors: Sara Siddiqi, Teagan Haggerty, Asia Akther, Simone Rusu, Dawn‐Li Blair, Heidi Eccles, Jessica Yu, Risa Shorr, Arnav Gupta, Karen Bouchard, Douglas Manuel, Jodi Edwards, Ian Colman, Jess G Fiedorowicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrognostic models for cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk have commonly included predictors such as cholesterol levels. Mental disorders are robust predictors of CVD and associated mortality, leading to approximately 2‐fold increases in risk. This systematic review aimed to narratively summarize the key characteristics, strengths, and limitations of all CVD prediction models that consider mental disorders.MethodsA literature search with medical subject headings/key terms related to CVD, mental disorders and prognostic modeling was conducted in Medline and EMBASE. Included studies were: cohort studies of CVD prediction model development, validation, or recalibration that included mental disorders as prognostic factors/covariate(s), or the population of interest. All studies were screened by 2 independent reviewers, followed by data extraction. The Prediction Model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool was used to critically appraise bias. A narrative synthesis was used to summarize mental disorder and sociodemographic factor/intersectionality inclusion.ResultsThere were 31 unique models identified (n=35 records including external validations). Considering these, 77% included mental disorders as a covariate, whereby depression and/or anxiety were the most considered (71%, n=22 studies). Most models were published within the last 5 years, included measures of socioeconomic status; however, many models lacked intersectionality considerations. Only one study was identified with a low risk of bias, while the majority had analytic concerns.ConclusionsDepression and/or anxiety were the most commonly considered mental disorders in modeling, despite larger associations with CVD for other disorders. Further CVD prediction modeling should consider a broader array of mental disorders, and limit methodological biases.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, intersectionality, mental disorders, prediction models, risk calculator, systematic reviewSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology, Mental Health

Future research should focus on developing prognostic models for heart disease that consider mental disorders more broadly as a risk factor.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) refers to any disease impacting the heart, vasculature or brain, including coronary heart disease and cerebrovascular disease. 1 In 2019, there were over 18.6 million CVD‐associated deaths reported worldwide, 2 with 9.4 million being attributable to ischemic heart disease—the most prevalent subtype of CVD in 2021. 3 Given the high global burden of CVD and potential for early intervention to mitigate risk, 4 , 5 , 6 many models have been developed for CVD prediction. These prognostic models have traditionally included CVD predictor variables such as cholesterol levels, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and blood pressure. 7 Importantly, these factors are included in the American Heart Association CVD guideline recommended Pooled Cohort equations, 8 , 9 the newly developed PREVENT calculator, 10 and the Canadian Cardiovascular Society recommended Framingham risk calculator. 11 However, notably absent from these risk algorithms is the risk posed by mental disorders. Recent studies have established a prominent pathophysiological connection between the brain and heart, 12 highlighting the need to consider brain/heart diseases concomitantly in risk prediction. Severe mental illness (SMI) increases the risk of CVD, 13 , 14 to the magnitude of other traditional CVD risk factors like diabetes (approx. 2‐fold increase). 15 A variety of potential factors may be involved in a mental disorder‐CVD link, biological factors such activation of central stress pathways, oxidative stress, and inflammation, 16 , 17 , 18 as well as behavioral factors such as smoking, 18 , 19 sleep quality, 18 , 20 , 21 diet, 22 and lack of exercise. 23 , 24 Mental disorder associated CVD risk is not entirely captured and remains independent of traditional risk factors for heart disease. 25 , 26 Meta‐analyses have shown a 1.95× [95% CI: 1.41–2.70] increased hazard of CVD and 2.45× [95% CI: 1.64–3.65] increased hazard of CVD associated mortality among patients with schizophrenia compared with those without schizophrenia. 27 This is consistent with a 1.41× increased hazard of heart disease with anxiety disorder 28 and 1.74× with substance use disorder. 29 Thus, without consideration of mental disorders, CVD prognostic calculators may not accurately estimate risk among these populations. This is evidenced by the JBS2, 30 a version of the Joint British Societies CVD risk calculator, which underestimates risk for CVD in individuals with schizophrenia, with 42.9% of males and 21.4% of females missed using this score. 30 Notably, pharmacological treatment of mental disorders may lead to side effects in the form of CVD outcomes or CVD risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and/or weight gain, 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 thereby increasing future risk of CVD. Furthermore, there may be sex‐specific effects as females with antipsychotic medication use have a 32% higher hazard rate of acute ischemic heart disease. 35 Therefore, mental disorder pharmacotherapy should be considered in risk estimation.

In parallel to traditional risk factors and mental disorders, the role of sociodemographic factors and intersectionality is a key component of CVD risk. There are established sex differences in CVD, 36 , 37 and gender differences in mental disorders with women showing a higher prevalence of internalizing disorders (including mood and anxiety disorders). 38 Furthermore, socioeconomic status (SES) is an important predictor of CVD, where low educational attainment is associated with increases in risk, 39 and intersecting identities such as higher income/education are associated with decreased CVD risk in women. 40 Lastly, there are ethnic disparities in CVD with impacts on access to care that could impact risk profiles. 41 Disparities in access to care, and SES may be further exacerbated among individuals with mental disorders, 42 , 43 further demonstrating the importance of intersecting sociodemographic factors in risk estimation.

This study addresses an important gap in the literature by providing a comprehensive summary of all CVD prediction models that consider mental disorders, and a summary of the included sociodemographic or intersectional factors. A previous systematic review of all existing cardiometabolic disease prediction models discussed the applicability of certain models for individuals with psychosis; however, it did not systematically search for mental disorders, or comment on models considering other mental disorders (eg, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder). 44 We specifically sought to identify models that considered any mental disorder, and expanded CVD outcomes to vascular impairments, as cerebrovascular disease can lead to vascular dementia and/or vascular cognitive impairment. 45 Therefore, our review compiled prognostic model developments/validation studies for CVD, vascular impairments, and/or CVD mortality, where any mental disorder was included as a prognostic factor, or models specified for psychiatric populations. We aimed to evaluate model characteristics, predictive performance, and critically appraised included studies for potential bias. Secondarily, we aimed to summarize the inclusion of sociodemographic and intersectional factors. This review will be helpful for identifying future directions in CVD prognostic modeling.

METHODS

The review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines, 46 CHARMS (Checklist for Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modeling Studies), 47 and was informed by guidance from Prognosis Research in Healthcare, 48 as well as the Cochrane Prognosis Group. 49 A protocol was posted on Open Science Framework at study initiation and later updated, both versions can be found at: https://osf.io/wndxr/. A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses checklist is available in Table S1. All data supporting the results are included in the manuscript or Supplemental Material.

Eligibility Criteria

Population, Intervention, Outcome

There were no cohort restrictions on sex, gender, age, race, ethnicity, or geographic region. Animal studies were excluded. The review included studies of prognostic model development with internal validation (the internal validation had to be clearly reported as occurring in a subset other than that from which the risk calculator was derived, using cross‐validation, or bootstrapping/other related techniques), model development with only external validation, external validation studies, and model recalibration studies. According to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines, 50 studies classified as model development only (ie, no form of validation) were excluded. The main outcomes of interest were prediction modeling for future CVD, CVD‐associated mortality, or vascular impairments. This included all heart diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, vascular dementia, vascular cognitive impairment, and heart disease/stroke‐related death. Models predicting post‐stroke dementia or post‐stroke cognitive impairments were included as vascular outcomes. Studies that included CVD‐related treatments in outcome definitions, such as coronary artery bypass graft, percutaneous coronary intervention, CVD medication prescriptions, major coronary surgery, and revascularization procedures, were included if a main CVD outcome was present. Models that included outcomes of interest as a composite outcome with all‐cause death were excluded.

Only models developed/validated for use specifically in a population of individuals with mental disorder(s), and/or models with mental disorders/associated pharmacotherapy as a prognostic factor within a final reported/evaluated model, were included. Mental disorders as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders—Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) 51 or the equivalent at the time of study, 51 clinically validated reports, risk assessment using screening tools with a defined threshold, or self‐reports were included. However, major neurocognitive disorder diagnoses (dementias such as: Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, cerebrovascular disease, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, Lewy body disease, traumatic brain injury, HIV infection & prion disease) were not included. Furthermore, alcohol consumption/drinking was considered a mental disorder when characterized as 4+ drinks/day for women, and 5+ drinks/day for men, along with problematic impacts on daily living (in accordance with general guidelines for alcohol use disorders). 52

Study Design

Original peer‐reviewed published research on prognostic model development/validation/recalibration in retrospective or prospective cohort studies were included. All reviews, meta‐analyses, cross‐sectional studies, conference proceedings, editorials, case reports, case studies, study protocols, and gray literature were excluded.

Search Strategy and Information Sources

A literature search was conducted in Medline and Embase in July 2023 and updated on February 19, 2025. The search strategy included keywords and medical subject headings related to prediction modeling, CVD, and mental disorders (Tables S2 and S3). The search strategies were developed with a Research Librarian at The Ottawa Hospital (R.S.), and the Medline search was validated by ensuring 3 previously identified articles were included in the results. There were no time period or language restrictions; Google Translate was used to translate non‐English texts. Reference lists of all included studies were screened to identify any additional articles for potential inclusion. Any identified external validations of the final included studies were added to the review.

Screening and Data Extraction

All search results were imported into the web‐based review manager, Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), for screening. Screening was conducted in duplicate with title, abstract, and full‐text screening. Inclusion/exclusion criteria for each stage were piloted by the reviewers before beginning screening. At the abstract screening stage, conflicts were resolved by a third independent reviewer; for full‐text screening, conflicts were resolved by consensus, and a third independent reviewer, when needed.

A data extraction form was created in Microsoft Excel and piloted by all reviewers to ensure consistency. All extraction was conducted in duplicate, and conflicts were resolved by consensus. Two independent reviewers classified model types in accordance with reporting guidelines. 53 A complete list of data items extracted is shown in Table S4.

Outcomes and Prioritization

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies

Risk of bias assessment was conducted using the Prediction Model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST). 54 This tool is widely used to assess bias in prognostic model development and external validation studies; it involves evaluation of bias on 4 domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis), followed by an overall assessment. 54 The PROBAST tool was piloted to ensure internal consistency with all reviewers. All assessments were conducted in duplicate by 2 independent reviewers, and conflicts were resolved with consensus. In accordance with the tool guidelines, a study was classified as having a “Low” overall bias rating if it received a low rating on each individual domain of bias evaluation. 54 Otherwise, it was rated as “High” or “Unclear”. 54 In addition, the PROBAST tool was used to assess the Applicability of each study for the review, a study was categorized as having “High” applicability if it received a high applicability rating for each individual domain of evaluation. 54

Data Synthesis

A narrative synthesis was completed to describe model characteristics. This included a summary of CVD prognostic models, validations, and inclusion of mental disorders. For studies with available data, model performance and/or prognostic effect measures for mental disorders were descriptively summarized. Measures of model performance summarized include discrimination (c‐statistics, area under the receiver operating curve [AUC]—with values closer to one indicating higher model discriminative utility 55 ), and net reclassification indices (NRI) (quantifying model classification improvement with added factors 55 ), model calibration shown in plots could not be summarized. Since fewer than 5 studies included external validations of the same model, predictive performance, discrimination, and/or calibration data could not be meta‐analyzed. 49 , 56 , 57 , 58 Lastly, to better understand gaps in CVD prediction modeling we narratively summarized the inclusion of sociodemographic factors and intersectional considerations.

RESULTS

The search strategy yielded 16 296 records. After duplicate removal, 10 797 abstracts were screened, followed by 536 full texts, which resulted in 28 studies identified for inclusion (Figure 1). Reference lists of included studies were reviewed, and 2 additional studies were included. 59 , 60 Manual searching of external validations for any included modeling studies led to further identification of 2 studies for inclusion. 61 , 62 The studies by Zheng et al, McCraken et al, and Osborn et al included a unique model development with external validation of a different model, 63 , 64 , 65 and the validations were considered as independent records for the purpose of this review. Therefore, the review included a total of 35 records (32 studies, and 31 unique models—excluding external validation records for which development was included in the review). The review included eight records of model development with external validation, 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 19 records of model development without external validation, 26 , 59 , 60 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 7 validation records, 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 87 , 88 , 89 and one recalibration with external validation was identified. 65 All the studies were published within the last 12 years; 13 were based on retrospective data,* 17 were prospective,† and 2 studies had both retrospective and prospective components. 70 , 71 The top regions of study were United States, and Europe. The main outcomes identified were CVD‐related hospital readmission, CVD (fatal and/or non‐fatal), heart failure (HF), myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, peripartum cardiomyopathy, stroke, atrial fibrillation, peripheral vascular disease, angina pectoris, cerebrovascular accident, transient ischemic attack, vascular dementia, post‐stroke dementia, and post‐stroke cognitive impairment. Only 2 studies assessed post‐stroke cognitive impairment, 68 , 81 3 assessed vascular dementia, 79 , 86 , 89 and one assessed both post‐stroke cognitive impairment/dementia 66 with consideration of mental disorders. Considering analytic techniques, 21 of the 31 included unique model assessments involved using Cox regression or Fine and gray subdistribution (Cox extension for competing risk assessment),‡ 2 with logistic regression, 75 , 76 3 used machine learning, 68 , 81 , 86 3 involved both machine learning and regression, 60 , 66 , 82 while the remaining 2 used accelerated failure time models. 67 , 70 All study characteristics are described in Table 1. 26 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 , 89

Consideration of Mental Disorders

Among the included studies, across all outcomes, most studies included mental disorders or pharmacological treatment of mental disorders as covariates (n=24), compared with as a population of interest (n=5), additionally, only 2 studies that modeled CVD, Fatal or Nonfatal CV Events/Stroke Event, AF, Revascularization, and/or Treatment included mental disorders as both a covariate, and population of interest (Figure 2). There were 5 major categories of mental disorder inclusion identified: (1) Depression, and/or Anxiety, (2) Severe Mental Illness, (3) Schizophrenia, Bipolar Disorder, Psychosis, Mood Disorders, and Other (4) Substance Use, Tobacco Use, and (5) Pharmacological Treatment; importantly, some studies were classified in multiple categories. While assessing unique modeling studies (n=31), most studies included Depression and/or Anxiety (n=22, 71%), whereas Severe Mental Illness was the least considered (n=6, 19%) (Figure 3). Most studies assessed mental disorders based on any reports/records available at baseline assessment, or as a composite of baseline up to a pre‐specified time period (eg, 6 months pre‐baseline measure 70 ). Depression and/or Anxiety

There were 22 unique model studies that considered depression and/or anxiety as the specific covariate or population of interest. Chamberlain et al included depression as a binary covariate (based on inpatient records) where the presence of depression added 2 points on the 30‐day HF readmission scale. 75 In addition, an external validation of this study conducted by Zheng and colleagues also included depression, and identified an AUC of 0.5705. 63 Huque et al included self‐reported depression as a binary covariate that added 7 points for stroke prediction in males, and no points in females. 80 DeBacquer et al included depression and anxiety as individual continuous covariates based on Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale scores to predict fatal/non‐fatal CVD outcomes; Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale scores for depression and anxiety had statistically significant model coefficients (P=0.025, P=0.0021). 67 Polcwiartek et al demonstrated a 61% increased hazard of fatal CVD with severe depression (based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10]) compared with its absence (hazard ratio [HR]=1.61 [95% CI: 1.33–1.96]) 85 ; yet in another study only a 9% increased hazard of CVD was modeled with an ICD‐10 record of a depressive episode. 60 Depressive disorder was shown to be associated with an increasing hazard of CVD in females as age increased. 59 Interestingly, many models for CVD outcomes that included depression and/or anxiety had moderate model performance (c~0.60–0.79). 59 , 60 , 64 , 67 , 78 Rapsomaniki et al demonstrated that addition of depression or anxiety had no statistically significant impacts on NRI when modeling CVD outcomes. 70 Similarly, another study identified that inclusion of depressive symptoms assessed by the 9‐item Patient Health Questionnaire had no statistically significant impact on their model's accuracy. 83 Contrary to these findings, Tillman et al reported a significant categorical NRI in both model development and external validation (+0.07 [P=0.005], +0.14 [P=0.006]) when including psychosocial/behavioral (including current depression) as covariates to model CVD death, 73 similarly a 1.14 NRI was observed by adding a continuous depressive symptom score based on 9‐item Patient Health Questionnaire to predict incident CVD. 84 All 6 studies that modeled outcomes of post‐stroke/vascular: cognitive impairment or dementia considered depression as a covariate (Figure 2). Most of these studies (n=4) had moderate model performance (c~0.70–0.79). 66 , 79 , 81 , 86 Moreover, Tang et al conducted an external validation of a model predicting dementia among individuals that were post‐stroke. 89 Depressive symptoms were included as a binary covariate (defined based on antidepressant use or Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale score) and were associated with a 6‐point increase on the scale, and model performance was moderate (c=0.61 [95% CI: 0.42–0.79]). 89 Hasan et al modeled post‐stroke cognitive impairment and demonstrated a high AUC of 0.93 using a machine learning model that included depression as a feature. 68 Two studies included individuals with depression as their population of interest—Nyugen et al assessed performance of the Pooled Cohort equations in a subsample of individuals with depression 88 ; Liu et al assessed model performance among subsamples of individuals with no, mild, and severe depression according to the 9‐item Patient Health Questionnaire. 82 Both studies indicated moderate model performance (c~0.60–0.80). 82 , 88 Hippisley‐Cox et al was the only study that included postnatal depression as a binary covariate to predict incident CVD. 69

Severe Mental Illness

There were 6 unique model studies that specifically considered SMI as one entity (not further subdivided into distinct mental disorders). Firstly, Hippisley‐Cox et al developed and internally validated QResearch Risk Estimator version 3 (QRISK3) for predicting coronary heart disease, ischaemic stroke, or transient ischaemic attack; this model included a “Yes/No” covariate for SMI. 26 The authors defined SMI as psychosis, schizophrenia, bipolar affective disease or depression. 26 , 90 QRISK3 identified sex‐specific hazard ratios with SMI increasing risk by 13% to 14%; model performance in women and men was good (c=0.85–0.88 range). 26 There were 3 external validations of QRISK3 identified, 2 demonstrated decreasing model performance across males and females with increasing age, 61 , 62 and the third tested model performance on individual CVD outcomes (as opposed to a composite); moderate model performance was observed. 64 Updated QRISK4 was developed and validated in 2024. 69 The sex‐specific hazard ratios for SMI indicated an increasing risk by 18% to 22%, and model performance was good (c~0.8). 69 Next, Cunningham et al conducted a validation study of the PREdicting Cardiovascular Disease Risk (PREDICT) model for CVD prediction in New Zealand; this study included SMI as the population of interest for which the model was evaluated. 87 A 1.37 times higher risk of CVD was observed, compared with the predicted outputs using the calculator, among the sample with SMI. 87 Polcwiartek et al developed and internally validated a model to predict CVD death; this study demonstrated that SMI (outpatient records of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or severe depression) was associated with a 2.17× higher hazard of CVD outcome (HR=2.17 [95% CI: 1.95–2.43]). 85 Furthermore, this group also modeled a subpopulation of individuals with SMI without existing CVD. In this case, model performance was moderate (AUC=0.7439 [95% CI: 0.7044–0.7811]). 85 Lastly, one study used the well‐known Cox‐Framingham BMI and Lipid models, 91 recalibrated and tested in a population of individuals with SMI, model performance was moderate (c~0.77–0.78). 65

Schizophrenia, Bipolar Disorder, Psychosis, Mood Disorders, Other

There were 10 unique model assessments within our review that specifically considered schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, psychosis, mood disorders (as a group without subdivision) or other mental disorders (not captured by other categories). For instance, Zheng et al included psychosis as a covariate on a 1‐year HF readmission scale, where the presence of the disorder was associated with a 1‐point increase in the total risk score. 63 Next, 2 studies by Sun et al and Davis et al included mood disorders as covariates in female‐specific models, to predict HF, and peripartum cardiomyopathy, respectively. 71 , 76 Polcwiartek et al included bipolar disorder and schizophrenia as individual disorders of interest, and both were associated with an increased hazard of fatal CVD. 85 Three studies included groups of disorders as covariates: mental disorders, psychiatric disorders, psychotic disorders; these studies had performance ranging from moderate—very good (c~0.61–0.93). 59 , 68 , 74 There were 2 models identified that included covariates for individual diagnoses: Down syndrome 69 and abnormal weight loss/anorexia. 66 Finally, Osborn et al developed Prediction and Management of Cardiovascular Risk in People With Severe Mental Illnesses (PRIMROSE) models to predict CVD among individuals with SMI, these models included covariates for a record of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, other psychosis, and unknown, listed on the SMI register. 65 Compared with the presence of schizophrenia, only other psychosis indicated a statistically significant increase in risk according to hazard ratios (HR=1.23 [95% CI: 1.10–1.39]). 65 Two models were developed, PRIMROSE lipid model and PRIMROSE BMI model; both had adequate performance (c=0.78–0.80). 65

Substance Use Disorder, Tobacco Use Disorder

The PRIMROSE Lipid and BMI models included a covariate for a history of heavy drinking or problematic alcohol use, identifying a 51% and 36% increased hazard on each model, respectively. 65 The study predicting peripartum cardiomyopathy included substance and tobacco use as individual covariates defined by the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision [ICD‐9]. 76 In addition, the female‐specific model predicting fatal CVD, developed by Sun et al, included a covariate for alcohol use disorder (HR=2.32 [95% CI: 1.44–3.75]). 71 At 9 years of follow‐up this model demonstrated moderate performance in external validation (c=0.78 [95% CI: 0.76–0.79]). 71 Finally, a study, with a population of individuals prescribed pharmacological treatment for attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder included tobacco use disorder (HR=1.84 [95% CI: 1.05–3.21]) and substance use disorder (HR=1.55 [95% CI: 1.25–1.92]) as covariates; overall model performance was moderate (c=0.72 [95% CI: 0.70–0.74]). 77

Pharmacological Treatment

There were 9 studies identified that incorporated pharmacological treatment of mental disorders as covariates. The model developed for individuals currently taking medication for attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder included covariates for: mood stabilizer use, antipsychotic medication use, and substance use disorder medication. 77 Dobrosavljevic et al identified a NRI of 0.16 to predict CVD when pharmacological treatment and Category Four (above) variables were added to a traditional model. 77 There were 2 other studies that included specific classes of relevant medications as individual covariates, such as benzodiazepines, antidepressants and antiseizure drugs. 68 , 72 The QRISK3 models included a covariate for atypical antipsychotic medication use, which was associated with a 14% to 29% increased hazard of CVD outcomes 26 ; the updated QRISK4 included this covariate only in the male‐specific model, demonstrating a~24% increase in CVD hazards. Furthermore, the PRIMROSE models included covariates for: antidepressants, first‐generation antipsychotics and second‐generation antipsychotics use at baseline. 65 Antidepressant use was associated with an increase in risk for CVD outcomes in both PRIMROSE Lipid and BMI models (HR=1.23 [95% CI: 1.12–1.36], HR=1.24 [95% CI: 1.13–1.37]). 65 Second‐generation antipsychotic use was only associated with a significant increase in CVD risk on the PRIMROSE BMI model, similar to first‐generation antipsychotic use. 65 One study in the review included depressive symptoms as a covariate to predict post‐stroke dementia; this covariate definition included antidepressant usage. 89 The remaining 2 studies that considered pharmacology included antipsychotics as a covariate in modeling, without any further classification. 64 , 74

Sociodemographic Factors and Intersectionality

Almost all unique models within the review considered sociodemographic factors (n=30/31) other than age, yet only 32% considered intersectional factors (Figure 4). Several models considered factors of SES (61%, n=19), such as income, 26 , 63 , 70 , 71 , 75 , 78 insurance payer, 63 , 66 , 75 , 76 , 78 deprivation index, 26 , 59 , 64 , 65 , 69 , 86 , 87 education, 60 , 64 , 73 , 80 , 81 , 86 , 89 needing assistance with money, 89 and employment status. 73 All studies that had intersectional factors, included intersections with sex/gender (either as interactions in the model or with a stratified model), where sex‐ethnicity and sex‐deprivation index were the most commonly included. Only 2 models considered sex/gender as part of an interaction term—an age‐sex interaction, 70 and a gender‐high risk region interaction. 73 There were 14 studies that included sex/gender as a covariate (including the 2 with an interaction term).§ Importantly, 42% (n=13) of unique model studies had sex‐specific models,‖ and only 29% (n=9) of unique models considered race/ethnicity as a covariate. 26 , 64 , 69 , 75 , 76 , 82 , 86 , 87 , 88 The other sociodemographic factors (not including age) identified within the review as covariates were: rural residence, 71 and marital status. 73 A complete description of sociodemographic and intersectional factors inclusion is listed in Table S5.

DISCUSSION

Understanding the breadth of prognostic models available in the literature is core to the generalizability and usage of a prediction model. Our review fills an important gap by systematically identifying CVD prognostic models that consider mental disorders and summarizing each model's key characteristics, strengths, and limitations. We identified 31 unique prognostic modeling studies, and 5 external validations published by different authors. Most studies within this review included mental disorders as a covariate in prognostic models, with depression and/or anxiety as the most commonly considered. Furthermore, we highlighted a major strength, that almost all studies in this field consider sociodemographic factors in some form within their models, with SES, followed by sex and/or gender being the most common. However, few studies considered the role of intersectional identities, most studies had methodological concerns, and only Sun et al 71 received an overall low risk of bias rating.

A previous review of all cardiometabolic prediction models by Perry et al identified 3 models that would apply to individuals with psychosis. 44 While our review included 2 of the studies within the previous review, 26 , 65 since our outcome inclusion criteria differed, the third study was not included. We present risk of bias ratings and a comprehensive summary of each model that considered mental disorders, significantly adding to the available summary of the literature. Importantly, our inclusion of vascular outcomes highlighted a significant gap in the literature, as only a few prognostic models for post‐stroke cognitive impairment included mental disorders, yet conditions such as post‐stroke depression have been shown to be linked with cognitive impairment. 92 In addition, modeling for vascular dementia with consideration of mental disorders was limited, despite evidence linking both. 93 This may lead to the overall underestimation of risk for vascular outcomes, similar to the underestimation of CVD observed without consideration of mental disorders. 87 Critically, we identified that many studies that included mental disorders focused on depression and/or anxiety (71%), yet other mental disorders confer significant CVD risk, including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, 27 which were included in less than half as many studies (32%). Future studies should consider a broader range of mental disorders in the prediction of CVD and vascular outcomes.

We identified studies indicating that mental disorders confer independent risk of CVD, and 2 studies showed improvements in model NRI—indicating the importance of including these factors in predictive models. Moreover, one study with depression indicated no changes in reclassification, 70 it is likely that heterogeneity in populations and measures of mental disorder assessment contributed to the variation in results. Given that very few studies have even assessed model NRI, future research should aim to disentangle reclassification observed when more severe mental disorders are added to models. Importantly, the NRI has limitations and can be inflated, thus it should be reported for prediction of CVD events and non‐events; in addition, it should not be the sole measure used to evaluate performance improvements in future research. 94 , 95 Considering model performance with inclusion of mental disorders as covariates, most studies demonstrated moderate performance metrics (c=~0.70). However, there were some notable exceptions. Importantly, studies with poor model performance in development are generally indicative of the need for perhaps simpler models, further categorization, missing data and linearity considerations and/or weighting. 55 , 96 , 97 Whereas, studies with poor performance metrics in external validation may indicate issues with generalizability of the model, potentially because of overfitting, or key population differences. 98 Overall, studies with poor concordance/AUC measures that did not report NRI precluded our ability to interpret the impact of mental disorder covariates on model classification. Most studies received a “High” bias rating on the Analysis‐PROBAST domain, a common concern identified in prediction models. This is similar to a review of all clinical prediction models in psychiatry, which identified that 94.5% of models had a high risk of bias. 99 Many studies in the present review failed to account for competing risks, model optimism, and/or had issues with correctly considering missing data. It is imperative that prediction modeling studies robustly address these analytic considerations as without consideration of competing risks such as death from other causes, risk estimates could be overestimated. Sun et al, the only study to receive a low risk of bias, identified moderate model performance at 9 years of follow‐up (c=0.77), accounted for competing risks, and included mood disorders as well as alcohol use disorder as independent risk factors in a female‐specific model. 71

The association between mental disorders and CVD risk may be influenced by the role of antidepressants, anxiolytics, and antipsychotic medication; this relationship is complex and requires further investigation. Previous work has indicated no evidence to support an association between depressive symptoms and increased HF risk among individuals taking antidepressants. 100 However, antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors could reduce the risk of cardiovascular events by reducing platelet activation, thus, further research is needed. 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 Conversely, pharmacotherapy for mental disorders can be associated with cardiovascular side effects. 32 This review identified 9 studies which included pharmacological variables as independent risk factors in CVD prediction; it is a major strength that these studies considered the role of these nuanced factors during modeling.

Another key strength identified in the present review was the consideration of sociodemographic factors, as most studies considered the role of SES, an important predictor of CVD. Models that fail to include SES factors as covariates could bias prediction results 105 , 106 ; therefore, the present review assessed the inclusion of various factors for SES (education, income, employment, deprivation, etc.). In addition, we identified that many studies considered the moderating role of sex (42% sex‐stratified, 6% sex interaction terms), a key factor in mental disorder‐associated CVD 107 , 108 ; this represents an improvement in comparison to a 2016 review of all CVD prediction models where only 5% of models had sex‐stratified models. 109 However, a limitation identified by our review was that inclusion of intersectional factors in CVD prediction modeling is limited; critically, there were no intersections not involving sex/gender identified. Despite there being evidence to suggest interactions between income, and ethnicity for CVD risk, 110 this has not been studied in prediction modeling that includes mental disorders. Further modeling studies should aim to include sex, income, and ethnicity as moderators to more accurately predict risk of CVD.

There were 16 validations identified during the review, and importantly, 2 of these were not classified as true external validations as per the TRIPOD guidelines. 53 However these were considered within the review to best summarize the existing literature. Nyugen et al used bootstrapped samples from a cohort included in the original Pooled Cohort equations development, 88 and Cunningham et al considered a sub‐sample of individuals with SMI that were included in the original model development. 87 Nonetheless, external validations represent a critical feature in prognostic modeling to estimate model generalizability; further external validation of existing models in the mental disorder and CVD field is warranted.

Our review has many strengths, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of literature, limiting biases, and improving reproducibility. For instance, we focused on prediction modeling studies with clear inclusion criteria; during screening, there were many excluded records of cross‐sectional studies and those that did not involve validation. In addition, our a priori protocol minimized reporting bias, and validation of the literature search with articles identified a priori strengthened our review. Unfortunately, significant heterogeneity in cohorts, analytic techniques, outcomes, and covariates, precluded our ability to conduct a meta‐analysis. There were some studies that initially identified mental disorders as potential covariates but ultimately did not include them in a final reported/evaluated model; these studies were excluded from our review, which could introduce bias. Nonetheless, a systematic search strategy including those studies may not be feasible given lack of reporting, and thus, we restricted our review to the prespecified inclusion criteria.

CONCLUSIONS

The available data from 31 unique prediction models that considered mental disorders support the value of these measures to CVD risk prediction. There was evidence that mental disorders and related variables independently predict risk, improve model performance, and avoid underestimating risk in these vulnerable groups. Studies also considered the role of sociodemographic factors, particularly for the influence of sex and SES. In many studies, inclusion of mental disorders was limited to depression and anxiety, which does not encompass the broader population at risk of mental disorders and excludes some of the highest risk conditions. Most studies were also limited by biases. Our recommendations for future modeling include: incorporating mental disorders and intersectionality broadly (eg, severe mental illness, depression, anxiety, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, among others, and based on the literature of risk factors, with consideration for easily accessible factors in clinical settings). In addition, we recommend improving analytic techniques, together these will enhance generalizability, and scope for clinical usage of prognostic models.
