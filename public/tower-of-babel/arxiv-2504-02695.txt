Mind the Gap? Not for SVP Hardness under ETH!
Divesh Aggarwal, Rishav Gupta, Aditya Morolia, Chuanqi Zhang

Abstract
We prove new hardness results for fundamental lattice problems under the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH). Building on a recent breakthrough by Bitansky et al. [BHIRW24], who gave a polynomial-time reduction from 𝟥 ​ 𝖲 ​ 𝖠 ​ 𝖳 \mathsf{3SAT} to the (gap) 𝖬𝖠𝖷𝖫𝖨𝖭 \mathsf{MAXLIN} problem—a class of CSPs with linear equations over finite fields—we derive ETH hardness for several lattice problems. First, we show that for any p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) , there exists an explicit constant γ > 1 \gamma>1 such that 𝖢𝖵𝖯 p , γ \mathsf{CVP}_{p,\gamma} (the ℓ p \ell_{p} -norm approximate Closest Vector Problem) does not admit a 2 o ⁡ ( n ) 2^{o(n)} -time algorithm unless ETH is false. Our reduction is deterministic and proceeds via a direct reduction from (gap) 𝖬𝖠𝖷𝖫𝖨𝖭 \mathsf{MAXLIN} to 𝖢𝖵𝖯 p , γ \mathsf{CVP}_{p,\gamma} . Our main contribution is a randomized ETH hardness result for 𝖲𝖵𝖯 p , γ \mathsf{SVP}_{p,\gamma} (the ℓ p \ell_{p} -norm approximate Shortest Vector Problem) for all p ∈ ( 2 , ∞ ) p\in(2,\infty) . This result relies on a novel geometric property of the integer lattice ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} in the ℓ p \ell_{p} norm, which says that for any p ∈ ( 2 , ∞ ) p\in(2,\infty) , the number of lattice vectors close to 1 2 ​ 𝟏 n \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n} (in the ℓ p \ell_{p} norm) is exponentially larger than the number of short vectors (namely those close to the origin). We establish this property via a new inequality for the Theta function, which we use to get a randomized reduction from 𝖢𝖵𝖯 p , γ \mathsf{CVP}_{p,\gamma} to 𝖲𝖵𝖯 p , γ ′ \mathsf{SVP}_{p,\gamma^{\prime}} . Finally, we also use our ideas to give some minor improvements over prior reductions from 𝟥 ​ 𝖲 ​ 𝖠 ​ 𝖳 \mathsf{3SAT} to 𝖡𝖣𝖣 p , α \mathsf{BDD}_{p,\alpha} (the Bounded Distance Decoding Problem), yielding better ETH hardness results for 𝖡𝖣𝖣 p , α \mathsf{BDD}_{p,\alpha} for any p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) and α > α p ‡ \alpha>\alpha_{p}^{\ddagger} , where α p ‡ \alpha_{p}^{\ddagger} is an explicit threshold depending on p p .

Introduction

A lattice in
d
dimensions is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Formally, given a set of vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, the lattice generated by these vectors is defined as

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}:a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Several algorithmic problems on lattices are of particular importance. The
Shortest Vector Problem
(
SVP
) asks for the shortest nonzero vector in a given lattice—typically measured in an
\ell_{p}
norm. The
Closest Vector Problem
(
CVP
) asks for the lattice vector closest to a given target vector in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A related variant is the
Bounded Distance Decoding
(
BDD
) problem, which can be viewed as
CVP
under the promise that the target point lies sufficiently close to the lattice.

Algorithms for solving these problems have led to impactful applications across multiple domains. These include polynomial factoring
[
51
]
, integer programming
[
52
,
47
,
33
,
67
]
, and cryptanalysis
[
69
,
62
,
46
,
59
]
. At the same time, the conjectured hardness of lattice problems has enabled the design of powerful cryptographic primitives—particularly in the context of
post-quantum cryptography
. These schemes are not only conjectured to be secure against quantum adversaries but also support advanced functionalities such as fully homomorphic encryption
[
66
,
39
,
30
,
13
]
. Remarkably, many of these constructions rely on the
worst-case hardness
of lattice problems, in contrast to traditional schemes based on the
average-case hardness
of factoring or discrete logarithm
[
12
,
66
]
.

The security of most finalists in the NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization process rests on the assumed difficulty of solving lattice problems
[
61
]
.

Algorithmic Progress on Lattice Problems.

The algorithmic study of
\mathsf{CVP}
and
\mathsf{SVP}
in the Euclidean norm (
\ell_{2}
) began with the LLL algorithm
[
51
]
, which provides a
2^{O(n)}
-approximation, and continued through foundational works of Babai
[
18
]
, Kannan
[
47
]
, Schnorr
[
68
]
, and Ajtai, Kumar, and Sivakumar
[
11
]
. The fastest known algorithms for exact
\mathsf{SVP}
are based on randomized sieving
[
11
]
, extended to all
\ell_{p}
norms
[
29
]
, general norms
[
17
]
, and even asymmetric convex bodies
[
33
]
, yielding
2^{O(n)}\cdot\mathsf{poly}(d)
-time algorithms for
\mathsf{SVP}_{p}
. In the case
p=2
, a long line of work
[
11
,
64
,
57
]
culminated in a
2^{n+o(n)}
-time algorithm
[
5
,
6
,
9
]
for both exact
\mathsf{SVP}
and exact
\mathsf{CVP}
, a
2^{0.835n}
quantum algorithm for
\mathsf{SVP}

[
3
]
, while constant-factor approximation for
\mathsf{SVP}
is achievable in
2^{n/2+o(n)}
time
[
5
]
. Even faster heuristic sieving algorithms are known under plausible assumptions
[
60
,
74
,
50
,
21
]
. For polynomial approximation factors, the best known algorithms for
\mathsf{SVP}_{2}
run in time
2^{Cn}
, where the constant
C
depends critically on the approximation factor
[
38
,
7
,
8
]
, and small improvements in
C
have significant implications for cryptographic security.
Recently, Eisenbrand and Venzin
[

showed that the fastest constant approximation factor
\mathsf{SVP}_{2}
algorithm can be adapted to solve constant approximation factor
\mathsf{SVP}_{p}
and
\mathsf{CVP}_{p}
for any

developed tight, rank- and dimension-preserving reductions between
\mathsf{SVP}_{p}
and
\mathsf{CVP}_{p}
across all norms, handling both constant and polynomial approximation factors, thereby expanding the algorithmic toolkit for lattice problems in general
\ell_{p}
spaces.

Computational Hardness of Lattice Problems.

A series of efforts
[
73
,
14
,
31
,
58
,
48
,
65
,
42
]
have shown that
\mathsf{SVP}_{p}
and
\mathsf{CVP}_{p}
are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate to within any constant factor, and hard to approximate to within
n^{c/\log\log n}
for a constant
c>0
, under reasonable complexity theoretic assumptions. See
[
26
]
for a recent survey on the hardness of
\mathsf{SVP}
. These results however do not rule out the existence of sub-exponential time algorithms for
\mathsf{SVP}
. This question is of immense interest from a theoretical point of view, as well as from a practical (cryptographic) point of view. For instance, to break the minimally secure post-quantum cryptographic schemes currently being standardized (for example,
[
19
]
), one would need to solve
\mathsf{SVP}_{2}
in roughly
400
dimensions. At this stage, a
2^{n/\log n}
time algorithm would be sufficient to break these schemes in practice. We need stronger and more
fine-grained
hardness assumptions to rule out the existence of such algorithms.

Fine Grained Complexity.

The
\mathsf{PCP}
theorem
[
16
,
15
]
was a breakthrough result that states that every language in
\mathsf{NP}
has a polynomial-size proof that can be verified by a probabilistic verifier that reads only a constant number of bits from the proof. This implies
\mathsf{NP}
hardness of approximation for problems such as
\mathsf{MAX3SAT}
and
\mathsf{MAXLIN}

[
41
]
.
This rules out polynomial time algorithms for these problems (unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
), but does not provide any guarantees about the existence of sub-exponential time algorithms, which are of interest especially in cryptography.
The analogous (to
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
) assumption to start from would be the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)
[
44
]
, which states that
\mathsf{3SAT}
on
n
variables cannot be solved in time
2^{o(n)}
.
However, it is not known whether this can be used to rule out the existence of sub-exponential time approximation algorithms for problems such as
\mathsf{MAX3SAT}
or
\mathsf{MAXLIN}
.
More recently,
[
34
,
55
]
formulated this into a yet stronger hardness assumption called the
\mathsf{GapETH}
which states that
approximating

\mathsf{MAX3SAT}
on
n
variables requires
2^{\Omega(n)}
time.

Gap-ETH vs ETH.

The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) and its stronger variant, Gap-ETH, have both played central roles in establishing fine-grained complexity and inapproximability results. Gap-ETH, introduced in
[
34
]
, states that for some constant
0<\eta<1
, no algorithm can distinguish, in
2^{o(n)}
time, between a
3\text{SAT}
instance in which all
m
clauses are satisfiable and one in which no assignment satisfies more than an
\eta
-fraction of the clauses. This stronger assumption has enabled sharp inapproximability results for a wide range of problems, including
2\text{CSP}

[
34
]
, Densest
k
-Subgraph and
k
-Biclique
[
32
]
, parameterized
SVP

[
27
]
, and
TSP

[
49
]
. More recently, however, breakthrough works have shown how to derive similar gap-producing reductions under the weaker ETH assumption, leading to inapproximability results for problems such as
2\text{CSP}
, Gap
k
-Clique
[
40
]
,
gapMAXLIN

[
28
]
, and parameterized
SVP

[
53
]
. These results raise the intriguing possibility that ETH may in fact imply Gap-ETH—a connection that could be established, for instance, via a PCP for
3\text{SAT}
with linear proof blowup, though the existence of such PCPs remains a long-standing open question. In light of this, a promising intermediate goal is to demonstrate that hardness results previously known only under Gap-ETH can in fact be obtained under ETH alone. Our work takes this approach: we use the gap-producing reduction from
[
28
]
to show the ETH hardness for the approximate lattice problems
\text{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
,
\text{CVP}_{p,\gamma}
, and
\text{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
, whose hardness was previously established only under Gap-ETH. Prior ETH-based hardness for these fundamental problems was unknown, though a sequence of works
[
23
,
10
,
4
,
24
,
1
]
established their hardness under the stronger Gap-ETH assumption. Our results thus provide new evidence for the power of ETH and take a step toward bridging the gap between ETH and Gap-ETH in the context of lattice-based inapproximability.

\gamma

\mathsf{NCP}

\mathsf{MDP}

for constants

\mathsf{MDP}

1.2
Our Techniques

From Linear Equations to
\mathsf{CVP}
.

\mathsf{MAXLIN}
is one of the classical
\mathsf{NP}
-hard approximation problems
[
41
]
. An instance of
\mathsf{MAXLIN}
is of the form
(M,\boldsymbol{v})
, where
M\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n},~\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
have entries from a finite field
\mathbb{F}
.
The goal is to find a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
that satisfies as many linear equations
M_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}=v_{i}
as possible.
The gap version
\mathsf{gap}_{c,s}\mathsf{MAXLIN}
is a promise problem where an instance
(M,\boldsymbol{v})
is a
\mathsf{YES}
instance if there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
that satisfies at least
cm
of the linear equations, and a
\mathsf{NO}
instance if every vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
satisfies at most
sm
of the linear equations.

[
28
]
showed that there is a Karp reduction from
\mathsf{3SAT}
to
\mathsf{gap}_{c,s}\mathsf{MAXLIN}
for
c=5/8
and
s=5/8-\varepsilon
for an explicit constant
\varepsilon>0
. This implies that
\mathsf{gap}_{c,s}\mathsf{MAXLIN}
is
\mathsf{ETH}
hard. From now on, we write
\mathsf{MAXLIN}_{\varepsilon}
to denote
\mathsf{gap}_{c,s}\mathsf{MAXLIN}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, for
c=5/8
and
s=5/8-\varepsilon
.

To prove
Theorem

1.1
, we show a deterministic Karp reduction from
\mathsf{MAXLIN}_{\varepsilon}
to
\mathsf{CVP}_{p,\gamma}
for a constant
\gamma:=\left(1+8\varepsilon/3\right)^{1/p}
. Consider the following matrix-vector pair

B:=[M\quad 2I_{m}];\quad\boldsymbol{t}:=\boldsymbol{v},

(1.1)

where
I_{m}
is the identity matrix. Consider the lattice generated by

Appending

are computed modulo 2. Precisely, the presence of

In other words, it suffices to show that for all

, there always exists

\vartheta_{4}(x,q)\coloneqq\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{z}q^{z^{2}}e^{2zix}.

Note that

\vartheta_{4}(0,e^{-\tau/4})=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\tau}}\vartheta_{2}(0,e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\tau}})=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\tau}}\cdot 2\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}e^{-(4\pi^{2}/\tau)\cdot(z+1/2)^{2}}>0,

1

On to

Bounded distance decoding is a lattice problem that has found applications in showing hardness results for important cryptographic primitives such as
Learning With Errors

\mathsf{(LWE)}
.
It can be thought of as
\mathsf{CVP}
under a promise that the closest lattice vector to a target
\boldsymbol{t}
is not too far away from the lattice
\mathcal{L}(B)
, relative to the length of the shortest non-zero vector. Alternatively, it can be thought of as a
decoding
problem over lattices, analogous to decoding noisy code words over finite fields. Quantitatively, a
\mathsf{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
instance promises that there is a closest lattice vector at a distance at most
\alpha\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}(B))
from the lattice. (See
Definition

2.8
for the formal definition.)
Regev
[
66
]
, in a seminal work, gave a reduction from worst case
\mathsf{BDD}
to (an average case problem)
\mathsf{LWE}
, with polynomial (in the dimension of the lattice)
\alpha
.
It is easy to see that there would be a unique solution to the problem if
\alpha<1/2
.
If
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>0
, it is easy to see that
\mathsf{BDD}_{p,\alpha_{2}}
reduces to
\mathsf{BDD}_{p,\alpha_{1}}
. Therefore, when showing hardness results for
\mathsf{BDD}
, a lower
\alpha
corresponds to a stronger result.

\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of
\mathsf{BDD}
for a constant
\alpha
was first shown by
[
54
]
, with
\alpha=\min\{2^{-1/2},2^{-1/p}\}
for

. This reduction however incurs a polynomial blowup in the rank of the lattice.
Recently
[
25
]
studied the quantitative hardness of
\mathsf{BDD}
for the first time and show that for all

, there is no

time algorithm for
\mathsf{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
for all

greater than a certain constant that approaches

as

is false.
In a followup work

2.1
Asymptotic Expansion

We now define the notion of Poincaré Asymptotic Expansion, which we will use in
Section

4.1
. An asymptotic expansion describes the asymptotic behavior of a function in terms of a sequence of (gauge) functions
[
43
, Definition 2.3]
.

Definition 2.1

(Poincaré Asymptotic Expansion)
.

A sequence of functions
\varphi_{n}:\mathbb{R}\setminus 0\to\mathbb{R}
, where
n=0,1,2,\dots
, is a asymptotic sequence as
x\to\infty
if for each
n=0,1,2,\dots
we have

\varphi_{n+1}=o(\varphi_{n})\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.

Moreover, if
\{\varphi_{n}\}
is an asymptotic sequence and
f:\mathbb{R}\setminus 0\to\mathbb{R}
is a function, we write

f(x)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)\quad\text{as }x\to\infty

(2.1)

if for each
N=0,1,2,\dots
we have

f(x)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)=o(\varphi_{N})\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.

We call
Eq.

2.1
the asymptotic expansion of
f
with respect to
\{\varphi_{n}\}
as
x\to\infty
.

For instance, the functions
\varphi_{n}(x)=x^{-n}
form an asymptotic sequence as
x\to\infty
.
We will use the asymptotic expansion of the Fourier transform of super-Gaussian functions as shown in
[
36
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
36
]
, Lemma 10 (i))
.

For fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the asymptotic expansion of

\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-|z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi ixz}dz

as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
is

\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x)\sim 2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m!}\sin\left(\frac{m\pi(2+\varepsilon)}{2}\right)\Gamma(m(2+\varepsilon)+1)(2\pi|x|)^{-m(2+\varepsilon)-1}.

s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)
, i.e.,

\hat{s}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)e^{-2\pi ixz}dz=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi i(x-1/2)z\right)dz.

Recall that the Fourier transform of
e^{-|z|^{2+\varepsilon}}
,
\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-|z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi ixz}dz
, has been well-studied in
[
36
]
. This motivates us to relate
\hat{s}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(x)
to
\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x)
by some translation and scaling as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi i(x-1/2)z\right)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-|C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi i\frac{x-1/2}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}z\right)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-|C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi i\frac{x-1/2}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}z\right)d\left(C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}z\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-|z|^{2+\varepsilon}-2\pi i\frac{x-1/2}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}z\right)dz=\frac{1}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\cdot\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x-1/2}{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\right).

To be eligible for the Poisson summation formula
\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{s}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(x)
, we need to show that for any
\tau>0
and
\varepsilon>0
,
s_{\tau,\varepsilon},\hat{s}_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, which is given by the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
\tau>0
and
\varepsilon>0
,
s_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)|dz=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}+\pi iz}\right|dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2}}dz\right).

Since
e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}
is bounded and continuous on
[0,1]
, and
e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2}}
is a Gaussian function, we can conclude that both integrals are finite, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\tau>0
and
\varepsilon>0
,
\hat{s}_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

By
[
37
]
, for any continuous and piecewise smooth function
f
, if both
f\in L^{2}
and its first derivative
f^{\prime}\in L^{2}
, then its Fourier transform
\hat{f}\in L^{1}
. Since
s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)
is clearly smooth on
z\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)
and continuous at
z=0
as
\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)=s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(0)=1
, it suffices to show that
s_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
s^{\prime}_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

For
s_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we argue in a similar way to the proof of
Lemma

4.2
. Note that,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)|^{2}dz=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}+\pi iz}\right|^{2}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}dz+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2}}dz\right).

Since
e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}
is bounded and continuous on
[0,1]
, and
e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2}}
is a Gaussian function, we can conclude that both integrals are finite, which completes the proof of
s_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Now we calculate the first derivative of
s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)
, i.e.,

s^{\prime}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)=e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}+\pi iz}\cdot\left(-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}(2+\varepsilon)|z|^{1+\varepsilon}\operatorname{sgn}(z)+\pi i\right),

where

\operatorname{sgn}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1,\quad&z>0\\
0,\quad&z=0\\
-1,\quad&z<0\end{aligned}\right.

Note that this formula works for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, one can verify that

\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)-s_{\tau,\varepsilon}(0)}{z}=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}+\pi iz}-1}{z}=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}+\pi iz}{z}=\pi i=s^{\prime}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(0).

Then it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|s^{\prime}_{\tau,\varepsilon}(z)|^{2}dz=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|e^{-C_{\tau,\varepsilon}|z|^{2+\varepsilon}+\pi iz}\right|^{2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{2}(2+\varepsilon)^{2}|z|^{2+2\varepsilon}+\pi^{2}}\right)^{2}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}\cdot\left(C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{2}(2+\varepsilon)^{2}z^{2+2\varepsilon}+\pi^{2}\right)dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}\cdot\left(C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{2}(2+\varepsilon)^{2}z^{2+2\varepsilon}+\pi^{2}\right)dz

\displaystyle+2\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}\cdot\left(C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{2}(2+\varepsilon)^{2}z^{2+2\varepsilon}+\pi^{2}\right)dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}\cdot\left(C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{2}(2+\varepsilon)^{2}z^{2+2\varepsilon}+\pi^{2}\right)dz+\int_{1}^{\infty}C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{\prime}z^{-2}dz\right),

where
C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{\prime}
is a finite constant depending on
\tau
and
\varepsilon
. This is because an exponential function always decays faster than any polynomial function, which implies that for any given
\tau>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, we can find
C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{\prime}>0
such that
C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{\prime}z^{-2}\geq e^{-2C_{\tau,\varepsilon}z^{2+\varepsilon}}\cdot(C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{2}(2+\varepsilon)^{2}z^{2+2\varepsilon}+\pi^{2})
for all
z\geq 1
. Since the integrand of the first integral is bounded and continuous on
[0,1]
, and the second integral
\int_{1}^{\infty}C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{\prime}z^{-2}dz=C_{\tau,\varepsilon}^{\prime}
, we can conclude that both integrals are finite, which completes the proof of
s^{\prime}_{\tau,\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Recall from
Lemma

2.2
that

\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x)\sim 2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m!}\sin\left(\frac{m\pi(2+\varepsilon)}{2}\right)\Gamma(m(2+\varepsilon)+1)(2\pi|x|)^{-m(2+\varepsilon)-1},

and observe that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,2)
, the first term in the asymptotic expansion of
\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x)
is negative as
\sin(\pi(2+\varepsilon)/2)<0
.
By
Definition

2.1
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
|x|>N_{0}
,
\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}(x)<0
.
Therefore, we can always choose
\tau_{0}\in(0,N_{0}^{-(2+\varepsilon)})
, which satisfies that for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,

N_{0}<\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}=\frac{1}{2C_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\leq\left\lvert\frac{x-1/2}{C_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\right\rvert.

Since
C_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}>0
, we have that for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\hat{s}_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{C_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\cdot\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x-1/2}{C_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}}\right)<0.

By
Lemmas

4.2
and
4.3
, we can apply the Poisson summation formula to conclude that

f_{2+\varepsilon}(\tau_{0})=-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}s_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}(z)=-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{s}_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon}(x)>0.

This concludes the proof of
Case 1
.

Case 2:
(\varepsilon\geq 2)
.
In this case, we simply choose
\tau_{0}=1
, which yields that

f_{2+\varepsilon}(1)=f_{p}(1)=-1+2\sum_{z=1}^{\infty}\left(e^{-(z-1/2)^{p}}-e^{-z^{p}}\right).

Since
e^{-(z-1/2)^{p}}-e^{-z^{p}}>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
p\geq 4
, it follows that

f_{p}(1)>-1+2(e^{-1/2^{p}}-e^{-1})\geq-1+2(e^{-1/2^{4}}-e^{-1})>0,

which completes our proof.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

For any
p\in(2,\infty),~\sigma>1
, there exists constants
\delta\in(0,1/2)
,
\phi_{0},\phi_{1}>1
and
C_{r}>0
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, if
r:=C_{r}\cdot{n^{1/p}}
then the following holds for the integer lattice.

\displaystyle{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta)^{1/p}r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)\geq\max\bigg\{

\displaystyle\phi_{0}^{n-o(n)}{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,\boldsymbol{0}\right),

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{n-o(n)}{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta\sqrt{\sigma})^{1/p}r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)\bigg\}.

(4.2)

The constants
\delta,\phi_{0},\phi_{1},C_{r}
only depend upon
p
and can be efficiently computed with required precision, from
p
.

Proof.

Fix any
p
and
\sigma
. Fix
\tau>0
as in
Theorem

4.1
. Then define

\rho:=\frac{\Theta_{p}(\tau,1/2)}{\Theta_{p}(\tau,0)}>1.

Notice that the precision to which we would approximate
\rho
would be independent of the input size, so such a
\rho
is efficiently computable in constant time.
For a
\delta\in(0,1/2)
to be determined in what follows, fix

C_{r}:=\frac{\mu_{p}(\tau,1/2)^{1/p}}{(1-\delta)^{1/p}}.

By
Theorem

2.15
, there exists a constant
C^{*}>0
such that,

{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta)^{1/p}\cdot r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)\geq\exp\left(-C^{*}\sqrt{n}\right)\cdot\exp(\tau n\cdot\mu_{p}(\tau,1/2))\cdot\Theta_{p}(\tau,1/2)^{n}.

(4.3)

By
Lemma

2.14
,

{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,\boldsymbol{0}\right)\leq\exp\left(\tau\cdot\frac{n~\mu_{p}(\tau,1/2)}{1-\delta}\right)\cdot\Theta_{p}(\tau,0)^{n}.

(4.4)

From
Eqs.

4.3
and
4.4
, we get that

\frac{{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta)^{1/p}\cdot r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)}{{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,\boldsymbol{0}\right)}\geq\rho^{n}\cdot\exp\left(-C_{r}^{p}\tau\delta n-o(n)\right).

Because
\rho>1
, we can fix
\delta>0
such that,

\delta<\frac{\log\rho}{\tau\mu_{p}(\tau,1/2)+\log\rho},

which implies that
\rho\cdot\exp(-\tau\delta C_{r}^{p})>1
.
Then set
\phi_{0}:=\rho\cdot\exp(-\tau\delta C_{r}^{p})
. This implies that,

\frac{{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta)^{1/p}\cdot r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)}{{N}_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,\boldsymbol{0}\right)}\geq\phi_{0}^{n-o(n)}.

For the second inequality, again use
Lemma

2.14
to get,

N_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta\sqrt{\sigma})^{1/p}\cdot r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)\leq\Theta_{p}(\tau,1/2)^{n}\cdot\exp\left(\tau\cdot nC_{r}^{p}\cdot(1-\delta\sqrt{\sigma})\right).

(4.5)

Then set
\phi_{1}:=\exp\left(\tau\delta C_{r}^{p}\cdot(\sqrt{\sigma}-1)\right)>1
which implies,

\frac{N_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta)^{1/p}\cdot r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)}{N_{p}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n},(1-\delta\sqrt{\sigma})^{1/p}\cdot r,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)}\geq\exp\left(\tau\delta nC_{r}^{p}\cdot(\sqrt{\sigma}-1)-o(n)\right)=\phi_{1}^{n-o(n)}.

∎

4.2
From
\mathsf{MAXLIN}_{\varepsilon}

\mathsf{SVP}_{p,\gamma}

\mathsf{CVP}_{p,\gamma^{\prime}}

\mathcal{L}

r

\mathcal{L}

For any

(B^{\prime},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},r^{\prime})

\mathsf{CVP}_{p,\gamma^{\prime}}

\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}

\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\gamma^{\prime}>1

,

If
\operatorname{dist}_{p}\left(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}(B^{\prime})\right)\leq r^{\prime}

{N}_{p}\left(\mathcal{L},r_{G},\boldsymbol{0}\right)\geq{N}_{p}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},(r_{G}^{p}-1-r^{\prime p})^{1/p},\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}\right),

(4.6)

\operatorname{dist}_{p}\left(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}(B^{\prime})\right)>\gamma^{\prime}r^{\prime}

\displaystyle\max_{\ell_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\{{N}_{p}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},~(r_{A}^{p}-(\gamma^{\prime}r^{\prime})^{p})^{1/p},\ell_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}\right)\right\}+

(4.8)

Then

\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{B}_{p}\left(\mathcal{L},r_{G},\boldsymbol{0}\right)
