Generalizing the Penrose cut-and-paste method: Null shells with pressure and energy flux
Miguel Manzano, Argam Ohanyan, Roland Steinbauer

Abstract
The cut-and-paste method is a procedure for constructing null thin shells by matching two regions of the same spacetime across a null hypersurface. Originally proposed by Penrose, it has so far allowed to describe purely gravitational and null-dust shells in constant-curvature backgrounds. In this paper, we extend the cut-and-paste method to null shells with arbitrary gravitational/matter content. To that aim, we first derive a locally Lipschitz continuous form of the metric of the spacetime resulting from the most general matching of two constant-curvature spacetimes with totally geodesic null boundaries, and then obtain the coordinate transformation that turns this metric into the cut-and-paste form with a Dirac-delta term. The paper includes an example of a null shell with non-trivial energy density, energy flux and pressure in Minkowski space.

Introduction

In nature, it is common to find neighbouring spacetime regions with different matter content, for instance the interior and exterior of a neutron star. Metric theories of gravity must hence be capable of matching spacetimes, i.e. of constructing one single spacetime by suitably gluing regions with distinct matter-energy properties. In particular, null matchings, i.e. matchings across a null hypersurface, are of special relevance in General Relativity
since they are key to describing gravitational waves, as well as black holes and gravitational collapse. As a result, countless examples of null shells have been studied in various scenarios and contexts, see e.g.
[
3
,
17
,
45
]
and references therein.

There exist two approaches to null matchings: the
standard matching theory
going back to Darmois
[
8
]
in the timelike/spacelike cases and later generalized to the null
[
4
]

and to the arbitrary causal character cases
[
33
,
34
]
, and
Penroses’s so-called
“cut-and-paste” method

[
40
]
.

In the context of the former, particularly relevant to this paper are the recent works
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
. Based on the

formalism of hypersurface data

[
29
,
30
,
31
]
, they provide a
completely general
approach—that is, without
a-priori assumptions on the matching hypersurface or the spacetimes to be matched—which at the same time allows to obtain
very explicit
results, e.g. the
gravitational/matter content of
any
null shell, or the first example of a null shell
with pressure
in Minkowski space. It is worth emphasizing, however, that
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
concentrate primarily on identifying the geometric objects that encode the full information of the matching
and on deriving the expression for the energy-momentum tensor of the null shell, rather than on obtaining an explicit form of the metric of the matched spacetime.

Alternatively Penrose has put forward his vivid cut-and-paste method
[
40
]
. Originally, he used it to construct impulsive
pp
-waves propagating in Minkowski space by cutting it into two “halves” along a null hyperplane
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}=\{\mathcal{U}=0\}
, and reattaching them with a shift along the null generators of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
. This identification of boundary points with a jump along the null direction are called the Penrose junction conditions. The matched spacetime is then described by a metric with a Dirac
\delta
-term supported on
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
,

ds^{2}=-2d\mathcal{U}d\mathcal{V}+d\mathcal{X}^{2}+d\mathcal{Y}^{2}+2\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\delta(\mathcal{U})d\mathcal{U}^{2},

(1.1)

clearly displaying curvature concentrated on the single wave surface
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
. In fact, this metric lies beyond the largest general class of metrics that enables a stable definition of (distributional) curvature
[
11
,
23
,
52
]
, but the high symmetry nevertheless allows one to calculate the curvature explicitly. Penrose also has constructed expanding, spherical impulsive waves in Minkowski space, and later on the method has been extended to all constant-curvature backgrounds, see
[
14
, Ch. 20]
, and also
[
42
]
for a recent pedagogical review.

In his seminal work
[
40
]
, Penrose also has introduced a continuous form of the metric (
1.1
), which was clearly “physically equivalent”, but the precise relation between the two metrics remained obscure. In later works the geometric meaning of the notorious “discontinuous coordinate transformation” relating the two metric forms has been made transparent
[
51
]
, and formulated in a mathematically rigorous way
[
19
]
, recently even with a non-zero cosmological constant
[
41
,
46
]
.

An important limitation of the cut-and-paste method is that, so far, it
has
only
allowed for the construction of purely gravitational or null-dust shells. However, null shells with much more general matter contents have been built in
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
—both in constant curvature backgrounds and in more general settings—, and in
[
24
]
a firm connection to the cut-and-paste method has been established. In this work we generalize the cut-and-paste method for all constant-curvature spacetimes to produce null shells with
completely general
energy-momentum tensors. We also obtain explicit expressions for the corresponding metric forms, which encode the matter content of the shell in a transparent way in the metric functions.

In particular, we first derive a locally Lipschitz continuous form of the metric
g
of the spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
resulting from the
most general
matching of two regions of Minkowski or (anti-)de Sitter space across a totally geodesic null hypersurface (Sections
4
and
6
). In this context, we also revisit the general matching of semi-Riemannian manifolds (Appendix
A
) and prove that under
natural mild conditions the metric of the matched manifold is locally Lipschitz (w.r.t. its natural smooth differentiable structure), thereby straightening an old result of
[
7
]
.

Then we also derive a vivid distributional form of the metric, again displaying a Dirac-
\delta
term supported on the matching hypersurface. To do so, we first introduce a “discontinuous coordinate transformation” together with a distributional ansatz for
g
, and prove that the ansatz and the Lipschitz continuous metric forms are related by the proposed coordinate transformation (Sections
5
and
6
). Here we defer some technical calculations regarding regularizations of distributional products to Appendix
B
. While our treatment here cannot be considered final, we firmly prepare the ground for future studies of the geodesics in these distributional geometries, which we expect in analogy to the classical cut-and-paste case to open the gates to a full nonlinear distributional analysis of the notorious transformation, cf.
[
48
,
47
,
41
,
46
]
.

Our results not only extend the cut-and-paste construction to generic null shells in spacetimes with constant curvature, but also yield the corresponding generalized Penrose junction conditions, which determine the shift along the null generators of the matching hypersurface. Finally, we include an explicit example of a null shell with non-trivial energy density, energy flux and pressure in Minkowski space (Section
7
).

Notation and conventions

All manifolds are smooth and connected. Given a manifold

we use
\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}\right){\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}C^{\infty}\left(\mathcal{M},\mathbb{R}\right)

of sections (i.e. vector fields). We use
\pounds
,

for the Lie derivative and exterior derivative. Given a differentiable
map
\phi
between manifolds, we use the standard notation of
\phi^{\star}
and
\phi_{\star}
for
its pull-back and push-forward respectively. Both tensorial and abstract index notation will be employed. We work in arbitrary dimension

and use the sets of indices

\alpha,\beta,...=0,1,2,...,\mathfrak{n};\qquad a,b,...,i,j,...=1,2,...,\mathfrak{n};\qquad A,B,...,I,J,...=2,...,\mathfrak{n},

(1.2)

irrespectively of whether they are tensorial or identify elements in a set. When index-free notation is used (and only then), we shall distinguish
covariant tensors with boldface. As usual, parenthesis denote symmetrization of indices. Our signature convention for Lorentzian manifolds

is

, and we use

2
Spacetime matching and the hypersurface data formalism

In this section we recall the necessary facts on the null matching of spacetimes using the formalism of hypersurface data, with special emphasis on the results in
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

Originally put forward in
[
29
,
30
]
(see also
[
27
,
28
,
32
,
35
]
), the formalism of hypersurface data relies on the notions of
metric hypersurface data
and
hypersurface data
, which we now introduce specifically in the null case.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a smooth and connected
\mathfrak{n}
-manifold endowed with a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor
\gamma
with one-dimensional radical (i.e. with signature
(0,+,...,+)
), a one-form
\ell
and a scalar function
\ell^{(2)}
. The tuple
\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}\}
defines
null metric hypersurface data
provided that the square
(\mathfrak{n}+1)
-matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\left(\hskip-2.84544pt\begin{array}[]{cc}\gamma_{ab}&\ell_{a}\\
\ell_{b}&\ell^{(2)}\end{array}\hskip-2.84544pt\right)

(2.1)

is non-degenerate everywhere on
\mathcal{N}
.
Null hypersurface data
, on the other hand, is a tuple
\left\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\ell,\ell^{(2)},\textup{{Y}}\right\}
consisting of null metric hypersurface data
\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}\}
equipped with an additional symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor
Y
.

To connect these concepts with the geometry of embedded null hypersurfaces one uses the notion of
embeddedness
. A null metric data
\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}\}
is said to be
\{\phi,\zeta\}
-embedded in an
(\mathfrak{n}+1)
-spacetime
\left(\mathcal{M},g\right)
if there exists an embedding
\phi:\mathcal{N}\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
and
a vector field
\zeta
along
\phi(\mathcal{N})
—called
rigging
—everywhere transversal to
\phi(\mathcal{N})
, that satisfy

\phi^{*}\left(g\right)=\gamma,\qquad\phi^{*}\left(g\left(\zeta,\cdot\right)\right)=\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\qquad\phi^{*}\left(g\left(\zeta,\zeta\right)\right)=\ell^{(2)}.

(2.2)

For hypersurface data
\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)},\textup{{Y}}\}
, embeddedness requires the additional condition

\dfrac{1}{2}\phi^{*}\left(\pounds_{\zeta}g\right)=\textup{{Y}}.

(2.3)

When the data is embedded, it follows from (
2.2
)-(
2.3
) that
\gamma
coincides with the first fundamental form of the hypersurface, while
\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}
codify the transverse components of
g
. Thus,
\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}
capture the full spacetime metric
g
along
\phi(\mathcal{N})
and
Y
codifies first transverse derivatives of
g
at
\phi(\mathcal{N})
.

Now to proceed to the spacetime matching, let us consider two
(\mathfrak{n}+1)
-dimensional spacetimes
(\mathcal{M}^{\pm},g^{\pm})
with null boundaries
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
. For
(\mathcal{M}^{\pm},g^{\pm})
to be matchable across
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
, they must fulfil the so-called

junction conditions
, which have been formulated in various equivalent ways in the literature, see e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
16
]
. In the language of hypersurface data they require
[
29
]
the existence of a null metric data
\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}\}
, two embeddings
\phi^{\pm}:\mathcal{N}\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\pm}
and two riggings
\zeta^{\pm}
(cf. Figure
1
), such that
\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}\}
can be
\{\phi^{\pm},\zeta^{\pm}\}
-embedded in both
(\mathcal{M^{+}},g^{+})
and
(\mathcal{M^{-}},g^{-})

so that

(i)

\phi^{\pm}(\mathcal{N})=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
, and

(ii)

one of the riggings
\zeta^{\pm}
points inwards and the other outwards with respect to
\mathcal{M}^{\pm}
.

The metric data

\{\mathcal{N},\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},\ell^{(2)}\}
and the pairs
\{\phi^{\pm},\zeta^{\pm}\}
must therefore satisfy (cf. (
2.2
))

\displaystyle\gamma=(\phi^{\pm})^{\star}(g^{\pm}),\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$}=(\phi^{\pm})^{\star}(g^{\pm}(\zeta^{\pm},\cdot)),\qquad\ell^{(2)}=(\phi^{\pm})^{\star}(g^{\pm}(\zeta^{\pm},\zeta^{\pm})).

(2.4)

Figure 1:
Matching of two spacetimes

,

As discussed in
[
29

detached
from any ambient spacetime. This is essential when the spacetimes to be matched are yet to be constructed. The purely gravitational and the matter content of the shell are encoded by the jump in the extrinsic geometry of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
, namely by a tensor field
[\textup{{Y}}]
defined by (cf. (

[\textup{{Y}}]{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\textup{{Y}}^{+}-\textup{{Y}}^{-},\quad\textup{where}\quad\textup{{Y}}^{\pm}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\frac{1}{2}(\phi^{\pm})^{\star}\left(\pounds_{\zeta^{\pm}}g^{\pm}\right).

By adapting
\mathcal{N}
to one of the boundaries, one can always choose the corresponding embedding freely and so w.l.o.g. we fix
\phi^{-}
.

Since we are interested in analysing properties of matched spacetimes, it is convenient to write the junction conditions from a spacetime viewpoint as well (see e.g.
[
34
]
). Given two spacetimes
(\mathcal{M}^{\pm},g^{\pm})
with null boundaries

\displaystyle g^{-}(X,W)

\displaystyle=({\Phi}^{\star}g^{+})(X,W),

(2.6)

\displaystyle g^{-}(\zeta^{-},X)

\displaystyle={\Phi}^{\star}\big(g^{+}(\zeta^{+},\cdot)\big)(X),

\displaystyle g^{-}(\zeta^{-},\zeta^{-})

(2.8)

Furthermore, the riggings

(ii)

A key aspect is that,

\Bigl\{\widetilde{\zeta}^{-}=z(\zeta^{-}+V),\textup{ }\widetilde{\zeta}^{+}=\widehat{z}(\zeta^{+}+\widehat{V})\Bigr\},

\zeta^{-}

2.6
) together with a choice of

) provide

[
14
, Ch. 2]
).

We next present the explicit expressions for the jump
[\textup{{Y}}]
(cf. (
2.5
))
and the energy-momentum tensor of the shell. Since the expressions are rather involved in general,
we present them only in the case when the spacetime boundaries
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
are
totally geodesic
.

Proposition 2.4
.

[
24
]

Assume that the matching of
(\mathcal{M}^{\pm},g^{\pm})
across two totally geodesic null boundaries
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
is ruled by
the functions
\{H(v,z^{A}),h^{A}(z^{B})\}
. Let
h_{IJ}
be the induced metric on the leaves
\{v=\textup{const.}\}\subset\mathcal{N}
,
h^{IJ}
its inverse tensor and
\nabla^{h}
its Levi-Civita derivative. Define the vector fields
\{W_{I}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}(\partial_{z^{I}}h^{B})v_{B}^{+}\}
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{+}
, and
the tensor fields

\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{L}_{\pm}\left(X_{\pm},Y_{\pm}\right){\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}g^{\pm}\big(\nabla_{X_{\pm}}L,{Y_{\pm}}\big),\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\pm}_{L}\left(X_{\pm}\right){\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}-g^{\pm}\big(\nabla_{X_{\pm}}k,L\big),\qquad\forall X_{\pm},Y_{\pm}\in\Gamma(TS^{\pm}_{v_{\pm}}).

Then, the components of the tensor
[\textup{{Y}}]{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\textup{{Y}}^{+}-\textup{{Y}}^{-}
are given by

\displaystyle[\textup{Y}_{vv}]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\partial_{v}\partial_{v}H}{\partial_{v}H},

\displaystyle[\textup{Y}_{vz^{J}}]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{L}^{+}(W_{J})-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}_{L}(v^{-}_{J})-\dfrac{\partial_{v}\partial_{z^{J}}H}{\partial_{v}H},

(2.16)

\displaystyle[\textup{Y}_{z^{I}z^{J}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\partial_{v}H}\bigg(2(\nabla_{{\left(I\right.}}^{h}H)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{L}^{+}(W_{\left.J\right)})+\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{L}_{+}(W_{\left(I\right.},W_{\left.J\right)})-(\partial_{v}H)\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{L}_{-}(v_{\left(I\right.}^{-},v_{\left.J\right)}^{-})-\nabla_{I}^{h}\nabla_{J}^{h}H\bigg).

Moreover, the energy-momentum tensor
\tau
of the shell reads

\displaystyle\tau^{vv}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon h^{IJ}[\textup{{Y}}](\partial_{z^{I}},\partial_{z^{J}}),

\displaystyle\tau^{vz^{I}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon h^{IJ}[\textup{{Y}}](\partial_{v},\partial_{z^{J}}),

(2.17)

\displaystyle\tau^{z^{I}z^{J}}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon h^{IJ}[\textup{{Y}}](\partial_{v},\partial_{v}),

where
\epsilon=1
(resp.
\epsilon=-1
) if
\zeta^{-}
points outwards (resp. inwards) with respect to
\mathcal{M^{-}}
.

Remark 2.5
.

If the vector fields
L^{\pm}
are chosen null,
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\pm}^{L}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\pm}_{L}
coincide with the transverse second fundamental form and the torsion one-form of the leaves

.

3
Matching two Minkowski spaces across a null hyperplane

From now on, we focus our analysis on the
matching of two regions of Minkowski space across a null hyperplane. In particular, in this section we shall
prove the feasibility of the matching, provide explicit expressions for
[\textup{{Y}}]
and the
energy-momentum tensor
\tau
, and examine the explicit form of the functions
\{H,h^{A}\}
depending on the type of
shell. We refer to
[
24
,
26
]
for further details.

Consider two
(\mathfrak{n}+1)
-dimensional Minkowski spaces
(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm})
, with metrics

\displaystyle\eta^{\pm}=-2d\mathcal{U}_{\pm}d\mathcal{V}_{\pm}+\delta_{AB}dx_{\pm}^{A}dx_{\pm}^{B},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{+}\geq 0,\quad\mathcal{U}_{-}\leq 0,

(3.1)

and boundaries

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\{\mathcal{U}_{\pm}=0\}
. The hypersurfaces
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\pm}
are null, admit a foliation by spacelike cross-sections and are totally geodesic (because the first fundamental forms of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
are Lie-constant along the null generators). Therefore,
the results of Section
2
, and in particular of Proposition
2.4
, apply. We let
S^{\pm}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\{\mathcal{U}_{\pm}=0,\mathcal{V}_{\pm}=0\}
, and make the following choice for the bases
\{L^{\pm},k^{\pm},v_{I}^{\pm}\}
of
\Gamma(T\mathbb{M}^{\pm})|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}}
:

L^{\pm}=-\partial_{\mathcal{U}_{\pm}},\qquad k^{\pm}=\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{\pm}},\qquad v^{\pm}_{I}=\partial_{x^{I}_{\pm}}.

(3.2)

The foliation functions on
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
are then
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}
, and a direct calculation
[
24
]

yields that the tensor fields
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{L}^{\pm}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{L}_{\pm}
(cf. Proposition
2.4
) vanish everywhere on
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
.

To construct metric hypersurface data that is embedded in both
\mathbb{M}^{+}
and
\mathbb{M}^{-}
,
we consider an embedding
\phi^{-}:\mathcal{N}\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{-}\subset\mathbb{M}^{-}
of the form

\displaystyle\phi^{-}(v,z^{I})=\left(\mathcal{U}_{-}=0,\mathcal{V}_{-}=v,x_{-}^{I}=z^{I}\right).

(3.3)

It then follows that the metric induced on the leaves
\{v=\text{const.}\}\subset\mathcal{N}
is the flat metric, i.e.
h_{AB}=\delta_{AB}
. As mentioned in Section
2
, the viability of the matching relies on the solvability of
(
2.13
), which
in the present case reads

\displaystyle\delta_{IJ}|_{\phi^{-}(p)}=\frac{\partial h^{L}}{\partial z^{I}}\ \frac{\partial h^{K}}{\partial z^{J}}\ \delta_{LK}|_{\phi^{+}(p)}\qquad\forall p\in\mathcal{N},

(3.4)

where the matching embedding
\phi^{+}:\mathcal{N}\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{+}\subset\mathbb{M}^{+}

is given by (
2.14
), i.e.

\displaystyle\phi^{+}(v,z^{I})=\left(\mathcal{U}_{+}=0,\mathcal{V}_{+}=H(v,z^{I}),x_{+}^{I}=h^{I}(z^{J})\right).

(3.5)

Equation (
3.4
)
is an isometry condition between the cross-sections

\phi^{\pm}(\{v=\textup{const.}\})\subset\mathcal{N}^{\pm}
. In particular,
it forces
\phi^{\pm}(\{v=\textup{const.}\})

to be Euclidean planes, whose isometries are translations and rotations. Now,
given a foliation
\phi^{-}(\{v=\textup{const.}\})
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{-}
, the symmetry properties of

(\mathbb{M}^{+},\eta^{+})
allow one to perform the necessary combinations of rotations and translations so that

\{\mathcal{U}_{+},\mathcal{V}_{+},x_{+}^{I}\}

verify
\partial_{z^{I}}x_{+}^{J}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{+}}=\partial_{z^{I}}h^{J}=\delta_{I}^{J}
. Thus, there always exists an isometry of
\mathbb{M}^{+}
which turns (
3.4
) into a trivial equation, which guarantees its solvability. The previous reasoning enables to set
h^{A}=z^{A}
with full generality, and
it is then straightforward to prove that

\Big\{\mathcal{N},\textup{ }\gamma=\delta_{AB}dz^{A}\otimes dz^{B},\textup{ }\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$}=dv,\textup{ }\ell^{(2)}=0\Big\}

defines null metric hypersurface data which
is embedded in
\mathbb{M}^{-}
with embedding
\phi^{-}
(cf. (
3.3
)) and rigging
\zeta^{-}=L^{-}
, as well as embedded in
\mathbb{M}^{+}
with embedding (recall (
3.5
))

\phi^{+}(v,z^{I})=\left(\mathcal{U}_{+}=0,\mathcal{V}_{+}=H(v,z^{I}),x_{+}^{I}=z^{I}\right)

and rigging
(cf. (
2.15
))

\zeta^{+}=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial v}\right)^{-1}\left(\partial_{\mathcal{U}_{+}}+\delta^{AB}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z^{A}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z^{B}}\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}+\partial_{x^{B}_{+}}\right)\right).

(3.6)

Observe that the combination of (
2.11
), (
3.1
), and the choice (
3.2
) of
L^{-}

implies that
\zeta^{-}
points inwards
(because
L^{-}
is past), whereas
\zeta^{+}
points outwards (recall
that
\partial_{v}H>0
). Consequently, the riggings
\zeta^{\pm}

satisfy the orientation condition
(ii)
, and hence the matching of
(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm})
across
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
is feasible. In fact, since
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
are totally geodesic, an infinite number of matchings can be performed (see the discussion in
[
25
,
26
]
).

The jump
[\textup{{Y}}]
and the energy-momentum tensor
\tau
are obtained by particularizing Proposition
2.4

to the present case. Specifically,

\displaystyle[\textup{Y}_{ab}]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\partial_{z^{a}}\partial_{z^{b}}H}{\partial_{v}H},\qquad\tau^{vv}=\rho,\qquad\tau^{vz^{A}}=j^{A},\qquad\tau^{z^{A}z^{B}}=\delta^{AB}p,

(3.7)

where

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=-\delta^{AB}\dfrac{\partial_{z^{A}}\partial_{z^{B}}H}{\partial_{v}H},\qquad j^{A}=\delta^{AB}\dfrac{\partial_{v}\partial_{z^{B}}H}{\partial_{v}H},\qquad p=-\dfrac{\partial_{v}\partial_{v}H}{\partial_{v}H}

(3.8)

are the energy density, the (components of the) energy flux, and the pressure of the shell respectively
[
26
,
45
]
. Observe that in the present case the matching information is entirely codified by the jump function
H
, since

\{h^{A}=z^{A}\}
. This is consistent with the fact that

[\textup{{Y}}]
and
\tau
depend solely on
H
. Notice also that no restrictions have been imposed on
H

apart from
\partial_{v}H>0
. It is hence worth discussing the types of null shells that can arise depending on the specific form of
H

[
24
]
:

1.

No-shell case.
This situation occurs when
[\textup{{Y}}]=0
, and corresponds to
a jump function of the form
H=av+b_{J}z^{J}+c
, where
a,b_{J},c
are real numbers and
a>0
. While
H
is not completely trivial,
there always exists
an isometry of

(\mathbb{M}^{+},\eta^{+})
which transforms it
into
H=v
, which is of course consistent with the absence of a shell.

2.

Purely gravitational or null-dust shell.
Purely gravitational shells are characterized by
[\textup{{Y}}]\neq 0
,
\tau=0
, while null-dust shells satisfy
\rho\neq 0
,
j^{A}=p=0
. In both cases, the jump function is of the form
H=av+\mathcal{H}(z^{A})
with
a\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{N})
is a scalar function on
\mathcal{N}
which is Lie-constant along the generators. The type of shell is determined by
\mathcal{H}
, which dictates whether the energy density vanishes identically or not.

3.

Generic shell.
For a
generic
matching of two regions of Minkowski space across a null hyperplane, the jump function reads

H(v,z^{A})=\beta(z^{A})\int\exp\left(-\int p(v,z^{A})dv\right)dv+\mathcal{H}(z^{A}),

(3.9)

where
p
is the pressure of the shell, and
\beta(z^{A}),\mathcal{H}(z^{A})
are
scalar functions on
\mathcal{N}
, Lie-constant along its generators.
The functions
\beta(z^{A})
and
p(v,z^{A})
must be compatible with the condition
\partial_{v}H>0
.

(\mathcal{M},g)

4
Lipschitz continuous metric form in the Minkowski case

In view of the results of Section
3
, the following natural questions arise
concerning the metric
g
of the spacetime
\mathcal{M}
constructed there from the most general matching of two Minkowski spaces
(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm})
across a null hyperplane:

(1)

What is the regularity of
g
?

(2)

Can we find an explicit form of
g
?

The general expectation concerning (1) is that
g
should be at least continuous (as predicted by a result in
[
7
]
), but we prove that it is in fact locally Lipschitz continuous
2
2

2

Observe that, in the last couple of years, it has become clear that local Lipschitz continuity is the threshold regularity necessary for a proper Lorentzian causality theory, cf.
[
6
,
13
]
.
. Indeed, in Appendix
A
we even show a corresponding result for the matching of general semi-Riemannian manifolds across an arbitrary hypersurface.

In this section we will address (2)
by first constructing a coordinate system in a neighbourhood of the matching hyperplane and then deriving an explicit Lipschitz continuous expression for
g
which also encodes
the physical properties of the shell in a transparent way.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be the spacetime resulting from the matching of
(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm})
across
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
with jump function (
3.9
), and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}
the corresponding matching hypersurface (see Figure
3
). Then
\mathcal{M}\equiv(\mathbb{M}^{+}\cup\mathbb{M}^{-})/\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
(or
\mathcal{M}\equiv\mathbb{M}^{+}\cup_{\Phi}\mathbb{M}^{-}
in the notation of Appendix
A
), where the quotient means that we are identifying the boundaries
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
and this identification gives rise to a single null hypersurface (
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
) where
the null shell is located. In the coordinate systems
\{\mathcal{U}_{\pm},\mathcal{V}_{\pm},x_{\pm}^{A}\}
for
\left(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm}\right)
used above,
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
is given by
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\equiv\{\mathcal{U}_{\pm}=0\}
.

Our aim now is to build a
new
coordinate system
\{u,\hat{v},\hat{z}^{A}\}

in a neighbourhood
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{M}
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
and to explicitly derive metric there. We start by enforcing the following trivial identification between the coordinate systems

\{u,\hat{v},\hat{z}^{A}\}
and
\{\mathcal{U}_{-},\mathcal{V}_{-},x_{-}^{A}\}
on
\mathbb{M}^{-}
:

\left.\Bigl\{\mathcal{U}_{-}=u,\textup{ }\mathcal{V}_{-}=\hat{v},\textup{ }x_{-}^{A}=\hat{z}^{A}\Bigr\}\right|_{\mathbb{M}^{-}}.

(4.1)

Then (
2.11
) together with the choice (
3.2
) force the vector fields
\{\zeta^{-},e_{a}^{-}\}
to be given by

e_{1}^{-}=\partial_{\hat{v}},\qquad e_{I}^{-}=\partial_{\hat{z}^{I}},\qquad\zeta^{-}=-\partial_{u},

(4.2)

in the basis
\{\partial_{u},\partial_{\hat{v}},\partial_{\hat{z}^{A}}\}

of
\Gamma\big(T\mathcal{M}\big)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}
. The bases
\{e_{a}^{\pm},\zeta^{\pm}\}
are identified in the matching process, so the rigging
\zeta^{+}
and the vector fields
\{e^{+}_{a}\}
must verify (recall (
2.12
), (
3.6
) and the choice
h^{A}(z^{B})=z^{A}
)

\displaystyle e_{1}^{+}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{v}H)\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}=\partial_{\hat{v}},\quad\qquad e_{I}^{+}=(\partial_{z^{I}}H)\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}+\partial_{x_{+}^{I}}=\partial_{\hat{z}^{I}},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\zeta^{+}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\partial_{v}H}\left(\partial_{\mathcal{U}_{+}}+\delta^{AB}(\partial_{z^{A}}H)\left(\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{z^{B}}H)\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}+\partial_{x_{+}^{B}}\right)\right)=-\partial_{u}.

(4.4)

Since only tangential derivatives of

H
appear on (
4.3
)-(
4.4
), and

\Bigl\{v=\mathcal{V}_{-}=\hat{v},\textup{ }z^{A}=x_{-}^{A}=\hat{z}^{A}\Bigr\}\text{ on $\mathcal{N}$ (cf.\ \eqref{phi:minus:Mk}),}

from now we drop the hat from the coordinates

\{\hat{v},\hat{z}^{A}\}

and simply write
\{u,v,z^{A}\}
. This, in particular, enables to rewrite
(
4.3
)-(
4.4
) as

\displaystyle e_{1}^{+}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{v}H)\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}=\partial_{v},\quad\qquad e_{I}^{+}=(\partial_{z^{I}}H)\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}+\partial_{x_{+}^{I}}=\partial_{z^{I}},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\zeta^{+}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\partial_{v}H}\left(\partial_{\mathcal{U}_{+}}+\delta^{AB}(\partial_{z^{A}}H)\left(\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{z^{B}}H)\partial_{\mathcal{V}_{+}}+\partial_{x_{+}^{B}}\right)\right)=-\partial_{u}.

(4.6)

From (
4.5
)-(
4.6
), we shall
obtain an explicit relation between the coordinates
\{\mathcal{U}_{+},\mathcal{V}_{+},x_{+}^{A}\}
and
\{u,v,z^{A}\}
as follows. Let
y_{+}
denote any of
\mathcal{U}_{+}
,
\mathcal{V}_{+}
or
x_{+}^{A}
, and
\nabla^{+}
be the Levi-Civita covariant derivative of
\eta^{+}
. First, we notice that
the one-forms
d\mathcal{U}_{+}
,
d\mathcal{V}_{+}
and

are covariantly constant on
\mathbb{M}^{+}\subset\mathcal{M}
, since

\nabla^{+}_{\alpha}(dy_{+})_{\beta}=\nabla^{+}_{\alpha}\nabla^{+}_{\beta}y_{+}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}y_{+}=0.

(4.7)

Secondly, we enforce that the vector field
\xi{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\partial_{u}
is null and affinely geodesic everywhere on
\mathbb{M}^{+}
. This implies

\displaystyle\xi\big(\xi(y_{+})\big)=\xi^{\alpha}\nabla^{+}_{\alpha}\big(\xi^{\beta}\nabla^{+}_{\beta}y_{+}\big)=\big(\xi^{\alpha}\nabla^{+}_{\alpha}\xi^{\beta}\big)\big(\nabla^{+}_{\beta}y_{+}\big)+\xi^{\alpha}\xi^{\beta}\nabla^{+}_{\alpha}\nabla^{+}_{\beta}y_{+}=0

(4.8)

where

\mathbb{M}^{+}
of

\mathcal{U}_{+}=\mathcal{U}_{0}+u\hskip 2.13387pt\mathcal{U}_{1},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{+}=\mathcal{V}_{0}+u\mathcal{V}_{1},\qquad x^{A}_{+}=x^{A}_{0}+ux^{A}_{1},

\mathbb{M}^{+}
that only depend on
\{v,z^{A}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}=u\hskip 2.13387pt\mathcal{U}_{1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{+}=H+u\mathcal{V}_{1},

\displaystyle x^{A}_{+}=z^{A}+ux^{A}_{1},

\displaystyle d\mathcal{U}_{+}=\mathcal{U}_{1}du+ud\mathcal{U}_{1},

\displaystyle d\mathcal{V}_{+}=dH+\mathcal{V}_{1}du+ud\mathcal{V}_{1},

\displaystyle dx^{A}_{+}=dz^{A}+x^{A}_{1}du+udx^{A}_{1}.

The scalar functions
\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
\mathcal{V}_{1}
,

(4.12)

Indeed, inserting (
4.12
) into (

), one obtains (recall that

)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{1}=\dfrac{1}{\partial_{v}H},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{1}=M\mathcal{U}_{1},\qquad x^{A}_{1}=q^{A}\mathcal{U}_{1}

(4.13)

\displaystyle\textup{where}\qquad M{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\dfrac{1}{2}\delta^{AB}\partial_{z^{A}}H\partial_{z^{B}}H,\quad q^{A}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\delta^{AB}\partial_{z^{B}}H.

Observe that the quantities
q^{A}
and
\mathcal{U}_{1}
verify

\displaystyle dq^{A}

\displaystyle=\delta^{AB}\left(\partial_{v}\partial_{z^{B}}Hdv+\partial_{z^{C}}\partial_{z^{B}}Hdz^{C}\right),

(4.14)

\displaystyle d\mathcal{U}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{U}_{1}^{2}\left(\partial_{v}\partial_{v}Hdv+\partial_{z^{B}}\partial_{v}Hdz^{B}\right).

(4.15)

Let us now derive the explicit expressions of the flat metrics
\eta^{\pm}
in the coordinates
\{u,v,z^{A}\}
. The calculation for
\eta^{-}
is immediate because of the trivial relation between
\{u,v,z^{A}\}
and
\{\mathcal{U}_{-},\mathcal{V}_{-},x^{A}_{-}\}
on
\mathbb{M}^{-}
(cf. (

)), which in particular entails

\displaystyle d\mathcal{U}_{-}\mathbin{{\mathop{=}\limits}}du,\quad d\mathcal{V}_{-}\mathbin{{\mathop{=}\limits}}dv,\quad dx_{-}^{A}\mathbin{{\mathop{=}\limits}}dz^{A},

(4.16)

and hence

\displaystyle\eta^{-}=-2dudv+\delta_{AB}dz^{A}dz^{B}.

(4.17)

On the other hand, the results (

)-(
4.11
) and (

) allow us to rewrite
\eta^{+}
as follows:

\displaystyle\eta^{+}=

\displaystyle-2d\mathcal{U}_{+}d\mathcal{V}_{+}+\delta_{AB}dx_{+}^{A}dx_{+}^{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\left(\mathcal{U}_{1}du+ud\mathcal{U}_{1}\right)\left(dH+\mathcal{V}_{1}du+ud\mathcal{V}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle+\delta_{AB}\left(dz^{A}+x^{A}_{1}du+udx^{A}_{1}\right)\left(dz^{B}+x^{B}_{1}du+udx^{B}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{ }du^{2}\left(-2\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{V}_{1}+\delta_{AB}x^{A}_{1}x^{B}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle+2du\Big(u\left(-\mathcal{V}_{1}d\mathcal{U}_{1}-\mathcal{U}_{1}d\mathcal{V}_{1}+\delta_{AB}x_{1}^{B}dx_{1}^{A}\right)-\mathcal{U}_{1}dH+\delta_{AB}x_{1}^{A}dz^{B}\Big)

\displaystyle-2ud\mathcal{U}_{1}dH-2u^{2}d\mathcal{U}_{1}d\mathcal{V}_{1}+\delta_{AB}\left(dz^{A}+udx_{1}^{A}\right)\left(dz^{B}+udx_{1}^{B}\right).

Now, the vector field

-2\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{V}_{1}+\delta_{AB}x^{A}_{1}x^{B}_{1}=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 0=-\mathcal{V}_{1}d\mathcal{U}_{1}-\mathcal{U}_{1}d\mathcal{V}_{1}+\delta_{AB}x^{A}_{1}dx^{B}_{1}.

(4.19)

Besides, since our choice of rigging
\zeta^{-}
is orthogonal to the spacelike sections

of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{-}
,
we know by (

\partial_{u}

5

(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm})
as defined in (

with a warp along the null generators of the matching hypersurface
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}

given by

\bigl\{\mathcal{U}_{+}=\mathcal{U}_{-}=0,\quad\mathcal{V}_{+}=\mathcal{V}_{-}+\mathcal{H}(x_{-}^{A}),\quad x_{+}^{A}=x_{-}^{A}\bigr\}

(5.1)

in the flat coordinates introduced in (
3.1

3.3
)-(
3.5

5.1
) amount to impose a jump function of the form
H=v+\mathcal{H}(z^{A})
which, as discussed in Section
3
, gives rise to purely gravitational or null-dust shells. Observe that this is a first strong connection between the methods employed above an the cut-and-paste approach, see
[
24

4.2

[

(see also
[
1

44

As mentioned before, the cut-and-paste method allows to write
the metric of the resulting spacetime in terms of a Dirac delta distribution
\delta
, cf. (
1.1
), where the coordinates
\{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\}
coincide with
\{\mathcal{U}_{\pm},\mathcal{V}_{\pm},x_{\pm}^{2},x_{\pm}^{3}\}
in the regions
\mathbb{M}^{\pm}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
. The key advantages of the distributional form (
1.1
) are that the spacetime metric of the matched spacetime takes the same, manifestly flat form in the spacetime regions before and after the shell, and that the shell’s information can be entirely encoded in the impulsive term of the metric. This, however, comes at the price that, at face value, the metric (
1.1
) has to be considered as being only formal as it firmly lies beyond the threshold regularity for metrics that can reasonably be assigned a distributional curvature, which is locally
H^{1}\cap L^{\infty}

[
11
,
23
,
52

[
15
, Ch. 3]
, which is especially true for the notorious “discontinuous coordinate transformation” which Penrose used to (formally) demonstrate the equivalence of both his forms of the metric, cf.
[
19
,
46
]

In this section, we will follow this circle of ideas to generalize the Penrose distributional metric and his “discontinuous coordinate transformation” in a formal sense. In particular, the generalization we aim at is the following. In all cut-and-paste constructions so far, the warp (
5.1
) is such that any two points along a null generator experience the
same
jump at the matching hypersurface. This restriction, while still allowing for some generality due to the freedom in the choice of
\mathcal{H}
, significantly limits the class of shells under consideration, and in particular excludes those with nonzero energy flux and pressure (recall (
3.7
)). This raises the quest to extend the cut-and-paste construction by Penrose
et al
. to generic matchings of two regions of Minkowski across a null hyperplane. More specifically, it is worth investigating whether the metric
(
4.24

(\mathbb{M}^{\pm},\eta^{\pm})
across the hyperplanes
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\pm}
can be written in a distributional form in such a way that the full information of the shell is encoded in an impulsive Dirac-delta term. This is indeed feasible, as we shall see next.

\left(\mathcal{M},g\right)

Remark 5.2

Two aspects of the metric form (

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}(\hat{\xi}(\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\nu}))

Appendix A
Abstract matching of semi-Riemannian manifolds

In this Appendix, we describe the matching of general semi-Riemannian manifolds from the abstract point of view of adjunction spaces. This will enable us to prove that the regularity of the metric of the matched spacetime is locally Lipschitz continuous. This can be seen as a straightening and sharpening of
[
7
, Sec. 3, Prop.]
.

We start by recalling a classical result on the gluing of two smooth manifolds along their boundaries. Note that a
regular domain
in a smooth manifold (with or without boundary) is a smooth codimension-
0
submanifold with boundary such the embedding map is a proper map, i.e., preimages of compact sets are compact.

Theorem A.1

(Attaching manifolds along their boundaries, Thm. 9.29 in
[
22
]
)
.

Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be smooth manifolds both of dimension
n
with nonempty boundaries
\Sigma_{i}:=\partial M_{i}
, and let
\phi:\Sigma_{1}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}
be a diffeomorphism. Let
M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
be the adjunction space formed by identifying
x\in\Sigma_{1}
with
\phi(x)\in\Sigma_{2}
7
7

7

That is the quotient space of the disjoint union
M_{1}\dot{\cup}M_{2}
by the equivalence relation
\Sigma_{1}\ni x_{1}\sim\phi(x_{1})\in\Sigma_{2}
.
. Then
M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
is a topological manifold without boundary, and has a smooth structure such that there are regular domains
M_{1}^{\prime},M_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
diffeomorphic to
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, respectively, satisfying
M_{1}^{\prime}\cup M_{2}^{\prime}=M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
,
M_{1}^{\prime}\cap M_{2}^{\prime}=\partial M_{1}^{\prime}=\partial M_{2}^{\prime}
.

Next we turn to the issue of including a metric in the construction, that is the gluing of semi-Riemannian manifolds
(M_{1},g_{1})
and
(M_{2},g_{2})
. As it turns out, upgrading the diffeomorphism
\phi
of the boundaries to an isometry does not quite do the job. More precisely, the existence of an isometry
\phi:\Sigma_{1}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}
is not a sufficient condition to guarantee the existence of a continuous metric
g
on
M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
that coincides with the original metrics on
M_{i}
, i.e.
g|_{M_{i}}=g_{i}

(i=1,2
). Since this fact seems not to have been universally appreciated in the literature, we next give a simple counter example.

Example A.2
.

Consider
M_{1}:=\mathbb{R}\times(-\infty,0]
with metric
g_{1}:=dx^{2}+dy^{2}
and
M_{2}:=\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
with metric
g_{2}:=dx^{2}+2dy^{2}
. Then
\partial M_{1}=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}=\partial M_{2}
. Moreover,
id_{\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}}
is an isometry of the boundaries, and
M_{1}\cup_{id}M_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. But there is no continuous metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
agreeing with the given metrics on either half space.

This example also hints at the solution of the issue. We have to additionally ask for the isometry to be compatible with the action of the metrics in the transverse directions. We formulate this condition in a way most directly related to the formalism used in the body of this text, cf. (
2.6
)–(
2.8
).

Definition A.3

(
\xi
-aligning isometry)
.

Let
(M_{1},g_{1})
and
(M_{2},g_{2})
be smooth semi-Riemannian manifolds with boundary and let
\xi\in C^{\infty}(TM_{1}|_{\partial M_{1}})
be transversal. Let
\phi:\partial M_{1}\rightarrow\partial M_{2}
be a smooth diffeomorphism. Let
M:=M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
denote the corresponding adjunction space.
We say
\phi
is a
\xi
-aligning isometry
if

(i)

\phi:\partial M_{1}\rightarrow\partial M_{2}
is a smooth isometry, i.e.
\phi_{*}(g_{1}|_{\partial M_{1}})=g_{2}|_{\partial M_{2}}
,

(ii)

\phi_{*}(g_{1}(\xi,\cdot)|_{\Sigma})=g_{2}(\xi,\cdot)|_{\Sigma}\ \in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma)
,

(iii)

\phi_{*}(g_{1}(\xi,\xi))=g_{2}(\xi,\xi)\ \in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
.

Note that if we understand
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
(equipped with
g_{1}
resp.
g_{2}
) to be subsets of
M
with common boundary
\Sigma=\partial M_{1}=\partial M_{2}
then the above conditions can be written in the simplified form

(i)
g_{1}|_{\Sigma}=g_{2}|_{\Sigma}
, (ii)
g_{1}(\xi,\cdot)|_{\Sigma}=g_{2}(\xi,\cdot)|_{\Sigma}\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma)
, (iii)
g_{1}(\xi,\xi)=g_{2}(\xi,\xi)\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
.

It is easy to see that if
\phi
is a
\xi
-aligning isometry and if
\tilde{\xi}
is any other transversal vector field to
\partial M_{1}
, then
\phi
is also
\tilde{\xi}
-aligning. Thus, the definition does not depend on the specific choice of
\xi
, but only on the existence of such a vector field (which is a nontrivial condition on
g_{i}
and
\phi
).

Theorem A.4

(Semi-Riemannian matching)
.

Let
(M_{1},g_{1})
and
(M_{2},g_{2})
be smooth semi-Riemannian manifolds both
of dimension
n
and with the same signature. Suppose both have nonempty boundaries
\partial M_{i}
and let
\phi:\partial M_{1}\rightarrow\partial M_{2}
a
\xi
-aligning isometry. Then there exists a unique locally Lipschitz continuous semi-Riemannian metric
g
of the same index as
g_{i}
on the adjunction space
M:=M_{1}\cup_{\phi}M_{2}
agreeing with
g_{i}
on
M_{i}
. In particular,
g
is smooth on
M_{i}\setminus\Sigma
,
i=1,2
, where
\Sigma
is the common boundary of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
in
M
.

Proof.

Given the identification of
M_{i}
with the corresponding subsets of the adjunction space
M
, the property that
\phi
is
\xi
-aligning simply means that it matches
g_{1}
to
g_{2}
: Indeed, to define
g
on
M
, we only need to check that
g_{1}(v,v)=g_{2}(v,v)
for every
v\in T_{p}M
with
p\in\Sigma
. (The fact that
g_{1}(v,w)=g_{2}(v,w)
for all
v,w\in T_{p}M
then follows from polarization.) To see this, let
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
be a basis of
T_{p}\Sigma
. Since
\xi
is transversal,
\xi(p),b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
is then a basis of
T_{p}M
. Writing

v=v^{0}\xi(p)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{i}b_{i},

(A.1)

it follows that

\displaystyle g_{1}(v,v)=(v^{0})^{2}g_{1}(\xi(p),\xi(p))+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{0}v^{i}g_{1}(\xi(p),b_{i})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}v^{i}v^{j}g_{1}(b_{i},b_{j}).

The defining properties of being a
\xi
-aligning isometry precisely say that the terms on the right hand side can be replaced with the corresponding expressions with
g_{2}
. Hence,
g_{1}(v,v)=g_{2}(v,v)
as claimed.

From the above we see that
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
fit at
\Sigma
to give a continuous semi-Riemannian metric
g
of the same signature as
g_{i}
on
M
. Clearly
g
is smooth on
M_{i}\setminus\Sigma
, as it agrees with the smooth metric
g_{i}
on that open set. To see that
g
is locally Lipschitz continuous on all of

, observe that its derivatives exist everywhere on

and the limits of the derivatives from either
M_{1}
or
M_{2}
to
\Sigma
exist as well, hence

is in
W^{1,\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
.
∎

Remark A.5

(On
\xi
-aligning isometries)
.

(i)

In many cases, there is a natural choice of
\xi
: E.g., if
\partial M\subseteq M
is Lorentzian or Riemannian, then one would usually choose
\xi
to be the unit normal vector field.

(ii)

It is in fact not necessary that the boundary have a transversal vector field to perform the gluing: If there is a collection
\xi_{i}
of local transversal vector fields that the isometry
\phi
maps accordingly as in Definition
A.3
, then the proof of Theorem
A.4
goes through unchanged. Local transversal vector fields always exist, but the property that an isometry
\phi
align them appropriately is a nontrivial condition.

(iii)

If
(M_{1},g_{1})
and
(M_{2},g_{2})
in Theorem
A.4
are spacetimes, i.e., of Lorentzian signature and time orientable), then also
(M,g)
can be made time orientable by taking a convex combination of the time orientations of
(M_{i},g_{i})
across a double collar neighbourhood of
\Sigma
. In the embeddings
M_{i}\subseteq M
, one would need to possibly invert the time orientation of one of the spacetimes to make this work. In the setting of matching spacetimes with boundary, one often requires that both boundaries have specified transversal vector fields and that the ”orientation” of these is preserved by the matching, but this is equivalent to the ”matching” of the time orientations as just mentioned.

(iv)

Higher regularity of the “glued metric”
g
can be achieved under additional conditions. If
\phi
preserves the second fundamental forms of
\Sigma
then
g
is in
C^{1,1}
(i.e. its first derivatives are locally Lipschitz) and if
\phi
preserves the curvature operators on
\Sigma
it is even
C^{2,1}
, see
[
50
, Lem. 5.1.1]
for details. However, in the context of matching spacetimes we have seen that a jump in the second fundamental form is associated to the matter content of the shell and a regularity higher than Lipschitz continuity is not possible in such a case.

Appendix B
Regularization products

In this appendix we calculate the regularisation products used in the computations of Section
5
. Our method can be placed in the general classification of intrinsic distributional products of
[
38
, p. 69]
, where it is called by the (hardly celebrity) name “model product (7.4)”. Our main reference for distribution theory is
[
10
]
and we freely use its notation.

To begin with, let
\rho
be a mollifier, i.e. a smooth function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with compact support
\textup{supp}(\rho)
in the unit ball
B_{1}(0)
that satisfies
\int\rho(z)dz=1
. Consider
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and set
\rho_{\varepsilon}(x){\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
. Then
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is called a
model-
\delta
-net
and is used to regularize distributions via convolution. More precisely, we regularize an arbitrary distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
via

u*\rho_{\varepsilon}(x){\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\langle u(x-y),\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)\rangle,

(B.1)

which is a smooth function (in
x
) which weakly converges to
u
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. Here
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
denotes the distributional action (in the variable
y
) and we indeed have
\rho_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\delta
and hence
u*\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\rightarrow\langle u(x-y),\delta(y)\rangle=u(x)
. In particular, the model-
\delta
net
\rho_{\varepsilon}
itself is a very general regularization of the Dirac
\delta
.

The
model product

[u\,v]
of two arbitrary distributions
u
and
v
is then defined as the weak limit

[u\,v]{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}(u*\rho_{\varepsilon})\,(v*\rho_{\varepsilon}),

(B.2)

provided the limit exists for all model-
\delta
nets
\rho_{\varepsilon}
and is independent of the mollifier
\rho
chosen.
We will use this product in the following but generally denote it by
uv
. We remark, however, that it is a routine task to extend our reasoning to the more general class of
strict-
\delta
nets
and the (hence more special) strict product, see
[
38
, Def. 7.1]
.

For reasons explained in Section
5
it is natural in our context to model the Heaviside function
\theta
by the prime function of
\rho_{\varepsilon}
, which is consistent with (
B.1
). Indeed we have

\displaystyle\theta_{\varepsilon}(x){\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\theta*\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{-1}^{x}\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)dz=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-1}^{x}\rho\left(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\right)dz=\int_{-1/\varepsilon}^{x/\varepsilon}\rho\left(y\right)dy=\int_{-1}^{x/\varepsilon}\rho\left(y\right)dy.\

(B.3)

In this case the convergence of
\theta_{\varepsilon}
to

is even stronger as we have

\displaystyle\theta_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{-1}^{x/\varepsilon}\rho\left(y\right)dy=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\textup{if}&x\neq 0,\quad x\leq-\varepsilon,\\
1&\textup{if}&x\neq 0,\quad x\geq\varepsilon,\\
\int_{-1}^{0}\rho\left(y\right)dy&\textup{if}&x=0,\end{array}\right.

and so
\theta_{\varepsilon}
converges to
\theta
pointwise almost everywhere and locally uniformly. Observe that, as a direct consequence of (

\displaystyle(\theta_{\varepsilon})^{2}(x)=\left(\int_{-1}^{x/\varepsilon}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\textup{if}&x\neq 0,\quad x\leq-\varepsilon,\\
1&\textup{if}&x\neq 0,\quad x\geq\varepsilon,\\
\left(\int_{-1}^{0}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)^{2}&\textup{if}&x=0,\end{array}\right.

and so
(\theta_{\varepsilon})^{2}
also converges to
\theta
pointwise almost everywhere and locally uniformly. Since we did not specify

.

Let us now compute the model product
\theta\delta
. For any test function

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\rho_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta_{\varepsilon}(z)\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\varphi(z)dz=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{-1}^{z/\varepsilon}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)\rho\left(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\right)\varphi(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)\rho\left(t\right)\varphi(\varepsilon t)dt=\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)\rho\left(t\right)\varphi(\varepsilon t)dt.

(B.10)

Since

uniformly converges to

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\rho_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle\mathbin{{\mathop{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\limits}}\varphi(0)\,\mathcal{I},\qquad\textup{where}\qquad\mathcal{I}{\mathrel{{\mathop{\,\,=\,\,}\limits}}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)\rho\left(t\right)dt.

(B.11)

We finally calculate

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)\rho\left(t\right)dt=\left[\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\rho\left(y\right)dy\right)^{2}\right]^{1}_{-1}-\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\rho(s)ds\right)\rho\left(t\right)dt
