CH25H-mediated cholesterol metabolism drives chondrogenic differentiation in adipose-derived stem cells and enhances cartilage repair
Authors: Yang Wang, Wang Ye, Yi Chen, Yu Zhuang, Zixiong Qin, Dan Luo, Zhijing Ren, Zhen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCartilage defects are difficult to heal because articular cartilage is both avascular and aneural. Directing adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) toward a chondrogenic fate in vitro is regarded as a promising therapeutic strategy. However, the endogenous pathways that specify chondrogenic fate remain incompletely defined. Herein, we identify cholesterol-25-hydroxylase (CH25H) as a key inducer of early chondrogenic differentiation that upregulates the cartilage marker genes SOX9, ACAN, and COL2A1. In ADSCs, FPR1 knockdown and rescue experiments, together with pharmacological inhibition of FPR1 or AKT, supported a signalling model in which 25-HC engages FPR1 to promote AKT/GSK3β pathway activation and chondrogenic differentiation. Surface plasmon resonance assays show that 25-HC directly binds to FPR1 with a dissociation constant of 1.18 µM. Molecular docking and membrane MD/MM/GBSA analyses provided structural context, suggesting stable accommodation of 25-HC in an FPR1 pocket and nominating candidate contacts (including a F81/V105 hydrophobic clamp) for future mutagenesis. In a rat trochlear cartilage-defect model, intra-defect implantation of ADSC-laden hydrogel combined with weekly intra-articular 25-HC dosing was associated with improved repair features, while co-administration of Cyclosporin H/HCH6-1 reduced these benefits. Together, these findings delineate the CH25H/25-HC–FPR1 axis as an endogenous metabolite–GPCR signalling pathway that couples cholesterol metabolism to chondrogenic fate specification in ADSCs.Subject terms: Stem-cell differentiation, Gene regulatory networks, Molecular modelling, Translational research

Introduction

Articular cartilage defects are both common and notoriously difficult to treat in clinical practice1. Because cartilage lacks blood vessels and nerves and has a very slow matrix turnover, traumatic or degenerative damage seldom heals on its own2, eventually leading to pain, limited mobility and even osteoarthritis3. Surgical procedures such as microfracture, osteochondral autografting, and autologous chondrocyte implantation can offer temporary relief, yet they usually yield fibrocartilage or substitute materials that cannot sustain the mechanical strength and wear resistance of native hyaline cartilage over the long term4. Consequently, regenerative medicine research has shifted toward guided stem cell differentiation, with particular interest in adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) because they are abundant, can be harvested through minimally invasive procedures, and possess robust proliferative capacity. Nevertheless, low induction efficiency and an unstable cartilage phenotype remain major barriers to clinical translation5. Therefore, it is essential to identify new regulatory factors and signalling pathways to improve the quality and durability of tissue-engineered cartilage.

In eukaryotic cells, cholesterol is not only a structural component of membranes and a precursor for steroid biosynthesis, but it is also converted by enzymatic hydroxylation, oxidation and related reactions into various oxysterols6. Cholesterol-25-hydroxylase (CH25H) is a type I transmembrane enzyme in the non-classical cholesterol metabolic pathway7. Within the endoplasmic reticulum, CH25H hydroxylates cholesterol at carbon 25 to generate 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC), which can be further converted by CYP7B1 into 7α,25-dihydroxycholesterol (7α,25-HC)8. Previous studies have shown that oxysterols regulate cell differentiation by binding to nuclear or membrane receptors9–16. However, the precise mechanisms by which oxysterols influence chondrogenic differentiation remain largely unclear.

Formyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) is a member of the G-protein-coupled chemoattractant receptor family. It is functionally versatile and binds a broad spectrum of endogenous ligands with diverse structures, including both proteins and lipids17. FPR1 has been detected on the surface of ADSCs18. Its activation modulates multiple signalling pathways, including PI3K–AKT, ERK, RUNX2 and FoxO1, thereby promoting stem cell migration and differentiation18–21. Nevertheless, the involvement of FPR1 in the chondrogenic differentiation of ADSCs remains largely unknown.

Herein, we demonstrate that 25-HC, the cholesterol metabolite generated by CH25H, is a potential endogenous ligand for the membrane receptor FPR1. Its binding is associated with downstream signalling that promotes ADSC chondrogenesis. Primary human ADSCs lacking CH25H show markedly reduced chondrogenic capacity, whereas ADSCs overexpressing CH25H display enhanced differentiation under chondrogenic induction. Thus, CH25H acts as a metabolic checkpoint for ADSC chondrogenesis via a 25-HC–FPR1–AKT cascade.

Results

ADSC characterization and integrative analysis reveal upregulation of CH25H and its metabolite 25-HC during chondrogenic differentiation

We first validated the identity and chondrogenic competency of the primary human ADSCs. Flow cytometry showed a canonical MSC immunophenotype—CD29⁺/CD44⁺ and CD34⁻/CD45⁻/HLA-DR⁻ with ≥99% positives and ≤0.2% negatives (Fig. 1a). Under chondrogenic induction, cells consistently formed compact cartilage pellets in 15-mL conical tubes (Fig. 1b), and tri-lineage assays confirmed adipogenesis (Oil Red O), osteogenesis (Alizarin Red S) and chondrogenesis (Alcian Blue) relative to undifferentiated controls (Fig. 1c).

With these quality controls established, we queried two independent human MSC RNA-seq resources in GEO (GSE161176 and GSE210984, Supplementary Table 3) using a harmonized early-induction contrast defined a priori as baseline (0 d) versus the earliest available post-induction time point within each dataset. Unsupervised clustering showed that chondrogenic groups segregated cleanly from their negative controls in both datasets (Fig. 1d). DESeq2 yielded 3,317 and 3,128 differentially expressed genes ( | log₂FC | > 1, adjusted P < 0.05), with 848 genes shared between datasets (Fig. 1e–g). GO terms among the shared genes highlighted “chondrocyte differentiation” and “cholesterol metabolic process” (Fig. 1h), while molecular functions included “integrin binding” and “nuclear retinoid-X-receptor binding” (Fig. 1i); KEGG analysis further underscored PI3K/AKT and cholesterol metabolism as central hubs (Fig. 2a). Intersecting the “cholesterol metabolic process” gene list with our earlier ADSC microarray dataset (GSE221879) yielded 14 candidates (Fig. 2b), among which CH25H showed the largest positive log₂FC (Fig. 2c).

To verify its temporal profile in vitro, primary ADSCs were harvested on days 0, 7, 14 and 21 of chondrogenic induction. qPCR showed that CH25H mRNA surged from day 7, reaching ~8-fold over baseline by day 21, whereas CYP7B1 remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 2d). ELISA of cell lysates revealed a parallel stepwise rise in CH25H protein (Fig. 2e). Concurrently, classical chondrogenic markers SOX9, ACAN and COL2A1 exhibited sustained mRNA and protein upregulation, confirming the robustness of the differentiation system (Fig. 2f, h). ELISA of oxidized cholesterol metabolites in the culture supernatant showed a pronounced accumulation of 25-HC from day 12 onward, while 7α,25-HC remained at low, near-constant levels (Fig. 2g), indicating that CH25H upregulation primarily funnels cholesterol toward 25-HC production. Collectively, these data demonstrate a continuous increase in CH25H and its metabolite 25-HC throughout ADSC chondrogenic differentiation.

The CH25H/25-HC axis positively regulates chondrogenic differentiation of ADSCs

To investigate the functional role of the CH25H/25-HC axis in chondrogenic differentiation of ADSCs, we constructed a lentiviral CH25H overexpression cell line (LV-CH25H) and its empty vector control (LV-NC), as well as a CH25H-knockdown line using shRNA (sh-CH25H) with a corresponding negative control (sh-NC) (Fig. 3a). RT-qPCR and Western-blot analyses confirmed that CH25H mRNA and protein levels were significantly upregulated in LV-CH25H cells and effectively silenced in sh-CH25H cells (Fig. 3b, c). Consistently, the accumulation rate of 25-HC in the culture supernatant increased nearly tenfold in the overexpression group, while it decreased by approximately 70% in the knockdown group (Fig. 3d).

After 21 days of chondrogenic induction, CH25H overexpression markedly enhanced the transcription of key chondrogenic markers SOX9, ACAN and COL2A1, whereas CH25H silencing significantly suppressed their expression. Supplementation of 25-HC in the sh-CH25H group largely restored the mRNA levels of these markers to near-control levels (Fig. 3e). Protein expression patterns were consistent with the transcriptional results (Fig. 3f). Histochemical staining further validated these findings: the LV-CH25H group showed the strongest extracellular matrix deposition as evidenced by Alcian Blue and Safranin O staining, while matrix staining was substantially reduced in the sh-CH25H group; 25-HC supplementation reversed this effect (Fig. 3g). Immunofluorescence analysis of COL2A1 exhibited a similar trend (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CH25H expression determines 25-HC production, which in turn positively regulates the chondrogenic phenotype of ADSCs through its downstream metabolic effect.

25-HC, rather than 7α,25-HC, is the active metabolite driving chondrogenic differentiation of ADSCs

To determine whether the pro-chondrogenic effect arises from 25-hydroxycholesterol (Fig. 4b) itself or its downstream metabolite 7α,25-dihydroxycholesterol (Fig. 4c), ADSCs were treated with increasing concentrations (1, 2, 4 and 8 µM) of either 25-HC or 7α,25-HC from day 0 of chondrogenic induction, with DMSO as the vehicle control. RT-qPCR results showed that 25-HC induced a dose-dependent increase in the transcription of SOX9, ACAN and COL2A1, reaching a peak at 4 µM. In contrast, 7α,25-HC had no significant effect on the expression of these genes at any tested concentration (Fig. 4a). Western-blot quantification revealed consistent trends: treatment with 4 µM 25-HC resulted in a 2.5-fold, 5.9-fold, and 3.2-fold increase in SOX9, ACAN and COL2A1 protein levels, respectively, whereas 7α,25-HC showed no significant difference compared to DMSO controls (Fig. 4d–g). Histochemical staining demonstrated that Alcian Blue and Safranin O intensities increased with rising 25-HC concentrations, with the strongest matrix deposition observed at 4 µM. In contrast, all 7α,25-HC-treated groups displayed staining levels similar to the control (Fig. 4h). Immunofluorescence analysis of COL2A1 further confirmed that 25-HC at 4 µM elicited the most robust type II collagen expression, while 7α,25-HC had no appreciable effect (Fig. 4i). These findings indicate that 25-HC, the direct product of CH25H enzymatic activity, is the primary bioactive metabolite responsible for promoting chondrogenic differentiation of ADSCs, with 4 µM identified as the optimal effective concentration.

25-HC activates the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway

We first compiled 312 putative 25-HC targets predicted by SwissTargetPrediction, TargetNet and SuperPred, and intersected them with 428 chondrogenesis-related genes retrieved from GeneCards, OMIM, PharmMapper and SEA, yielding 21 shared candidates (Fig. 5a). GO enrichment showed these genes converge on biological processes such as cell differentiation, with their protein products mainly localised to the plasma membrane and cytoplasm (Fig. 5b). A KEGG chord diagram further highlighted the PI3K–AKT signalling pathway as the top-ranked node (Fig. 5c). To validate this pathway, we performed Western blotting on day 7 of induction in CH25H-knockdown ADSCs (sh-CH25H) and in cells supplemented with 4 µM 25-HC. Total PI3K levels were unchanged, but phosphorylation of AKT (Ser473) and GSK3β (Ser9) rose markedly after 25-HC treatment. Conversely, CH25H knockdown reduced both phosphorylation events, an effect fully rescued by exogenous 25-HC (Fig. 5e). Taken together, these results demonstrate that 25-HC activates the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis by stimulating AKT phosphorylation and subsequent GSK3β inhibition, thereby driving downstream programmes required for chondrogenic differentiation.

FPR1-dependent control of the AKT/GSK3β axis and chondrogenic differentiation

From the 21 targets shared by 25-HC–associated signalling and chondrogenesis, we prioritised five plasma-membrane receptors (TLR4, ANPEP, FGFR1, FPR1 and KIT) and three nuclear receptors (RARB, ESR2 and NR3C1) as candidate mediators (Fig. 5d). Molecular docking across these candidates consistently ranked FPR1 as the most favourable binder, with a predicted binding-free energy of −8.8 kcal mol⁻¹, markedly lower than that of the other receptors (Supplementary Table 1). The top-scoring pose positioned 25-HC deep within the FPR1 lipid pocket, supported by a combined hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic contact network, nominating FPR1 as the leading membrane receptor candidate (Fig. 5f).

To test the functional requirement of FPR1, we generated ADSCs with lentiviral FPR1 overexpression (LV-FPR1) and shRNA-mediated knockdown (sh-FPR1), alongside matched controls (LV-NC, sh-NC), and validated efficient FPR1 modulation at both the mRNA level and protein level (Fig. 6a–c). Following 25-HC stimulation, control cells exhibited increased activation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis and concomitant induction of chondrogenic outputs. In contrast, FPR1 knockdown attenuated these 25-HC responses, whereas re-expression of FPR1 restored both the signalling changes and chondrogenic readouts (Fig. 6d–g). These gain-/loss-of-function data place FPR1 as a key mediator linking 25-HC to AKT/GSK3β activation and chondrogenic differentiation in ADSCs.

To support subsequent pathway pharmacology, we included a canonical FPR1 agonist as a positive control in the same system. fMLF stimulation induced AKT/GSK3β pathway changes together with increased chondrogenic outputs, and co-application of cyclosporin H and HCH6-1 reduced these fMLF-evoked responses, indicating that this dual-inhibitor regimen effectively suppresses FPR1-driven activation under our conditions (Fig. 6h–k).

We next integrated receptor- and pathway-level pharmacology into the 25-HC framework by treating both control (sh-NC) and CH25H-knockdown (sh-CH25H) ADSCs with 4 µM 25-HC, with or without cyclosporin H plus HCH6-1 using a low-dose dual-inhibition strategy to limit off-target liabilities while maintaining pathway coverage (Fig. 7a). 25-HC increased AKT-Ser473 and GSK3β-Ser9 phosphorylation in both genetic backgrounds, and this effect was abolished by FPR1 inhibition, whereas total PI3K levels were not detectably altered. In parallel, substituting FPR1 blockade with AKT pathway inhibition (MK-2206 plus LY294002) prevented 25-HC from restoring chondrogenic outputs in sh-CH25H cells (Fig. 7b–e). These results position AKT downstream of 25-HC signalling and establish its necessity for the chondrogenic phenotype.

Finally, to test whether downstream activation can bypass receptor blockade, wild-type ADSCs were treated with 25-HC in the presence of cyclosporin H plus HCH6-1, followed by co-administration of AKT activators (SC-79 and 740 Y-P). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) confirms the affinity between 25-HC and FPR1

Molecular-docking studies suggested a stable interaction between 25-HC and FPR1. To validate this interaction and quantify its affinity, we performed SPR analysis on a Biacore T200. Purified recombinant human FPR1 carrying a C-terminal His₆ tag was maintained in 0.05% DDM after elution and covalently immobilised on an EDC/NHS-activated CM5 sensor chip via amine coupling ( ≈ 8000 RU). Using HBS-EP⁺ as the running buffer, we injected 25-HC over the chip at concentrations ranging from 0.097 µM to 12.5 µM and recorded real-time sensorgrams, which were fitted with a 1:1 Langmuir steady-state model. The sensorgrams displayed clear dose dependence and fully reversible dissociation (Fig. 9b). Steady-state analysis yielded KD = 1.18 µM with χ² = 0.05 (Fig. 9c), supporting direct micromolar binding between 25-HC and FPR1. As SPR measures ligand–receptor binding rather than receptor activation, these data were interpreted together with the downstream signalling and genetic perturbation experiments.

Stability of the 25-HC–FPR1 complex

A 100-ns all-atom MD simulation in a POPC bilayer was performed to assess complex stability. The backbone RMSD of the 25-HC–FPR1 complex plateaued at ~3 Å after 40 ns, while the ligand itself remained tightly bound, with a ligand RMSD near 1 Å; by comparison, apo-FPR1 displayed a slightly lower RMSD (Fig. 9e), indicating no large-scale structural drift. Radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) likewise stabilised between 40–50 ns (Fig. 9f, g), corroborating the RMSD results. Residue-wise RMSF revealed that flexibility is largely confined to the extracellular loop ECL2 (F190–Y200) and the intracellular loop ICL3 (A230–G240) (Fig. 9h), regions often involved in GPCR conformational rearrangements. MM/GBSA yielded an average binding-free energy of ΔGtotal ≈ –39 kcal mol⁻¹, dominated by a favourable gas-phase contribution (–63 kcal mol⁻¹) partially offset by desolvation (+24 kcal mol⁻¹) (Fig. 9i). Principal-component analysis (PCA) and the resulting free-energy landscape (FEL) revealed two low-energy basins along PC1; the global minimum corresponds to a Gibbs free energy of ~0 kJ mol⁻¹ (Fig. 9k, l). In this state, 25-HC remains securely lodged within the FPR1 binding pocket (Fig. 9m, n). Collectively, these metrics confirm that the 25-HC–FPR1 complex is conformationally stable over the simulated timeframe.

Key residue interactions within the 25-HC–FPR1 binding site

MM/GBSA decomposition revealed that residues F81, V105, F110, R84, Q258 and Y257 each contribute less than −2 kcal mol⁻¹ to the total binding-free energy, with the hydrophobic clamp formed by F81 and V105 providing the strongest stabilising effect (Fig. 9j).

Contact-frequency analysis of the 100-ns trajectory identified the ten residues with the highest occupancy (Fig. 9p). Among them, the NH₁, NE and NH₂ atoms of R84 can donate hydrogens to the O₂ atom of 25-HC; Q258-NE₂ can donate a hydrogen to 25-HC O₁; and the O₁ atom of 25-HC can donate a hydrogen to the carbonyl oxygen of G209. Detailed hydrogen-bond occupancy analysis confirmed the relevance of these contacts: the 25-HC-O₁···G209-O bond persisted from 25 ns to 90 ns (average distance ≈ 3 Å, angle ≈ 150°), while the Q258-NE₂···25-HC-O₂ bond was stable until ~80 ns with comparable geometry (Fig. 9q, r). This polar network, located in the groove between ECL2 and ICL3, works in concert with the β-face and side-chain hydroxyls of 25-HC to lock the ligand in place (Fig. 9o). Notably, break-and-reform events of the R84–O₂ or Q258–O₁ hydrogen bonds correlate negatively with TM6 outward displacement: transient loss of either bond coincides with brief declines in TM6 swing and tilt (Fig. 9s, t), suggesting that this polar cluster may help stabilise the local binding geometry required for 25-HC recognition by FPR1. In summary, the simulations suggest that stable accommodation of 25-HC within the FPR1 pocket is supported by two cooperative features: hydrophobic clamping by F81/V105 and a polar interaction network involving R84, Q258 and G209. These observations provide structural context for the experimentally observed AKT/GSK3β signalling and chondrogenic responses.

CH25H-overexpressing ADSCs drive FPR1-dependent cartilage repair in vivo

25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC) permeates the plasma membrane, engages FPR1, recruits PI3K to generate PIP3, activates PDK1/mTORC2–AKT, relieves GSK3β inhibition, and drives chondrogenic programmes typified by SOX9/ACAN/COL2A1 (Fig. 10b). To test whether this axis can be exploited for tissue-engineered stem cell therapy, we established a full-thickness osteochondral defect in the femoral trochlea of the rat knee and compared five conditions—Control (defect left untreated), Gel (macroporous hydrogel only), Gel+M (hydrogel loaded with ADSCs), Gel+M + 25-HC (ADSCs co-delivered with a mechanism-anchored single dose of 25-HC), and Gel+M + 25-HC + i (same plus FPR1 inhibitor cyclosporin H/HCH6-1)—with a single intraoperative implantation of the hydrogel constructs and, to maintain small-molecule exposure, once-weekly intra-articular injections of 25-HC (Gel+M + 25-HC) or 25-HC plus cyclosporin H/HCH6-1 (Gel+M + 25-HC + i) in a non-gelling vehicle from weeks 1–7, and harvest at week 8 (Fig. 10a). Macroscopic inspection and histology showed Control and Gel healed in a disordered, non-cartilaginous manner with irregular surfaces, fibrovascular, fibrocartilage fill, scant matrix and incomplete closure. Gel+M produced a more continuous cartilage-like fill with visible lacunae and deepened Safranin-O staining indicative of increased proteoglycan, yet the neotissue remained loosely organized and not compact, with surface gaps and protrusions. In contrast, Gel+M + 25-HC yielded robust hyaline-like repair characterized by a continuous, well-integrated articular surface and re-established tidemark with abundant Safranin-O/Fast-Green deposition and strong, widespread COL2A1 staining and improved subchondral integration. High-magnification inspection revealed that the neo–articular surface was not perfectly smooth but displayed subtle, fine serrations (“micro–saw-tooth” contours) consistent with the natural microtopography formed as transplanted ADSCs differentiated into chondrocytes. Gel+M + 25-HC + i presented a pattern distinct from the other groups, not chaotic yet not smooth, resembling aggregates of stem cells interdigitating with the subchondral bone without clear chondrogenic differentiation, with partial Safranin-O and COL2A1 signals but no lacunar architecture and no evidence of chondrocyte maturation (Fig. 10c). Quantitative histopathology ranked Gel+M + 25-HC highest on ICRS and lowest on Wakitani, while Gel+M + 25-HC+i shifted toward Gel+M (Fig. 10d), supporting the interpretation that intra-articular 25-HC, when combined with ADSC-loaded hydrogel, enhances matrix synthesis and structural repair in the trochlear defect, with this benefit being reduced by cyclosporin H/HCH6-1.

Discussion

This study began with a transcriptomic screen and systematically demonstrates that the CH25H–25-HC–FPR1–Gi–PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis is a key pathway promoting ADSC chondrogenic differentiation. CH25H was markedly upregulated early during induction. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments linked CH25H expression to increased SOX9, ACAN and COL2A1 expression, and this effect was largely attributable to 25-HC rather than 7α,25-HC. Network-pharmacology overlap and molecular docking prioritised FPR1 among candidate receptors, and SPR demonstrated micromolar binding between 25-HC and FPR1 (K_D = 1.18 μM), suggesting that FPR1 is a possible membrane receptor for 25-HC. Consistent with this model, FPR1 knockdown and rescue experiments, together with pharmacological inhibition of FPR1 or AKT and CH25H knockdown/rescue, support a signalling cascade compatible with “CH25H → 25-HC → FPR1 → Gi–AKT/GSK3β → chondrogenic differentiation”. MD simulations with MM/GBSA analysis further suggested that 25-HC can be stably accommodated within the FPR1 pocket via a dual hydrogen-bond lock (G209 and Q258), an electrostatic interaction with R84, and hydrophobic packing involving F81, F102, V105, R201, R205, L109 and F110. The observed TM6 displacement is compatible with activation-related conformational rearrangements in Class-A GPCRs, although the simulations alone do not establish receptor activation22–24. In vivo, we used a rat trochlear cartilage-defect model with five treatment arms and found that adding 25-HC to ADSC-laden hydrogel enhanced repair, with the benefit reduced by FPR1 inhibition. Syngeneic Lewis rats and Lewis-derived ADSCs were used to minimize alloimmune confounders. A surface-accessible mechanism is plausible: 25-HC can traverse the plasma membrane and access FPR1, potentially contributing to oxysterol–GPCR signalling linked to Gi-associated downstream responses.

Previous work on oxysterol-regulated stem cell fate has focused mainly on Oxy133 or 20(S)-HC in osteogenesis, 22(R)-HC in neuron-like differentiation, and 7-KC in monocyte maturation. Our data link 25-HC to the cartilage lineage and support signalling through the Gi-coupled GPCR FPR1, rather than relying solely on the canonical nuclear receptor LXR. Notably, 7α,25-HC showed no pro-differentiation activity in our system, suggesting that CYP7B1-mediated downstream metabolism is dispensable for ADSC chondrogenesis. FPR1 is classically a chemotactic receptor for N-formyl peptides and has also been implicated in osteogenesis in some stem cell settings25. Here, network pharmacology, docking and SPR support micromolar 25-HC–FPR1 binding (K_D = 1.18 µM), and genetic and pharmacological perturbations are consistent with the linear pathway “CH25H → 25-HC → FPR1 → Gi–AKT/GSK3β → chondrogenesis.” To provide structural context, membrane MD/MM/GBSA analyses suggested stable association of 25-HC within the FPR1 pocket with an estimated binding-free energy of −39.82 kcal mol⁻¹, within the range reported for small-molecule FPR1 ligands26, and highlighted candidate pocket determinants potentially involved in FPR1 ligand recognition27,28. Overall, these in silico results support the experimental signalling framework and nominate testable residue-level hypotheses for future site-directed mutagenesis and functional validation.

In the rat defect model, local delivery of 25-HC together with ADSC-laden hydrogel was associated with more cartilage-like repair features, including a more continuous articular surface, deeper Safranin-O staining, broader COL2A1 distribution, fewer fissures, a re-emergent tidemark, and improved subchondral integration. Beyond the core FPR1–AKT/GSK3β axis supported by our experiments, prior studies suggest that 25-HC may also influence the repair niche through additional processes, such as oxysterol–GPCR-linked chemotaxis/retention29–31, biasing MSC fate away from adipogenesis and toward chondrogenesis32, limiting aberrant osteoid-like responses33, reducing senescence-associated inflammatory signalling34, modulating macrophage polarisation35,36, and restraining IL-1/IL-6-driven inflammatory outputs in synovial fibroblasts37. These mechanisms remain speculative in our setting and should be tested directly in future work.

25-HC is pleiotropic and can exert context- and dose-dependent effects in immunity and metabolism. In this study, we did not perform a formal toxicology assessment, and routine monitoring during the dosing regimen did not reveal overt adverse events. Dedicated dose-ranging and longitudinal safety profiling with joint- and system-level readouts will therefore be required to strengthen translational inferences. Notably, the in vivo study used a relatively small sample size (n = 6 per group). Larger, independent cohorts will be needed to confirm the robustness and effect size. In addition, longer-term studies with mechanical and functional testing will be important to assess repair durability under joint loading.

This study highlights a signalling principle relevant to cartilage regeneration: a lipid metabolic intermediate can act as a local extracellular cue that engages GPCR signalling and biases stem cell fate. It also guides how lipid cues can be combined with biomaterial delivery and other MSC sources in cartilage-repair models.

Materials and methods

GEO transcriptomic processing and culture and immunophenotypic analysis of hADSCs

Public human MSC RNA-seq datasets were retrieved from GEO and processed for an early-induction contrast (baseline vs the earliest available post-induction time point) within each dataset. The datasets and sample accessions were GSE161176 (baseline: GSM4891037, GSM4891038, GSM4891039; early induction: GSM4891040, GSM4891041, GSM4891042) and GSE210984 (baseline: GSM6443675, GSM6443676, GSM6443677; early induction: GSM6443678, GSM6443679, GSM6443680) (Supplementary Table 3). Differential expression was performed separately within each dataset using DESeq2, and concordant genes were identified by overlap across datasets rather than by merging count matrices; therefore, no between-dataset batch correction was required. Sample structure within each dataset was inspected by unsupervised hierarchical clustering as a basic QC check for potential batch-driven separation.

Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hADSCs) were obtained from de-identified lipoaspirate by-products from elective liposuction procedures at Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital (Guiyang, China)38; Written informed consent was obtained from all donors, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2019064). All tissues were de-identified prior to transfer; samples were coded with study IDs, and the linkage file was retained by the hospital and was not accessible to investigators. Three independent donors were included: female, 35 years; female, 30 years; male, 42 years. Cells were cryopreserved immediately after isolation and stored until use. Cryopreserved second-passage vials were rapidly thawed and expanded in high-glucose DMEM (PM150210, Pricella, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (A5669901, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P9080, Sigma, USA). Once cultures reached ≥90% confluence, cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA and replated at a 1 : 3 split ratio. All downstream assays utilised fourth-passage cells. For surface-marker profiling, harvested hADSCs were chilled on ice and incubated for 1 h with directly conjugated antibodies against CD29 (1 : 100, Cat. 11-0299-42), CD44 (1 : 100, Cat. 11-0441-85), CD34 (1 : 100, Cat. CD34-581-01), CD45 (1 : 100, Cat. 12-9459-42) and HLA-DR (1 : 100, Cat. 12-9956-42) (all Thermo, USA). Fluorescence signals were acquired on a NovoCyte 2060 R flow cytometer (ACEA Biosciences, USA). A main cell population was identified on FSC-H/SSC-H plots, and marker-associated populations were analysed using the same gate (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Directed tri-lineage differentiation (chondrogenic, adipogenic and osteogenic) of hADSCs

ADSC multipotency was assessed with a micromass-pellet protocol. Cells were harvested into 15 mL conical tubes and placed in lineage-specific media as follows: adipogenic medium—high-glucose DMEM (89%), streptomycin/penicillin (1%), FBS (10%), insulin (10 µg mL⁻¹; HY-P73243, MCE, USA), dexamethasone (1 µM; ID01801, Solarbio, China), indomethacin (200 µM; HY-14397, MCE, USA) and IBMX (0.5 mM; HY-12318, MCE, USA); chondrogenic medium—high-glucose DMEM (89%), streptomycin/penicillin (1%), FBS (10%), TGF-β1 (10 ng mL⁻¹; HY-P7118, MCE, USA), vitamin C (50 µg mL⁻¹; A8100, Solarbio, China), dexamethasone (1 µM) and insulin (6.25 µg mL⁻¹); osteogenic medium—high-glucose DMEM (89%), streptomycin/penicillin (1%), FBS (10%), dexamethasone (1 µM), β-glycerophosphate disodium (10 Mm; IG1340, Solarbio, China) and vitamin C (50 µg mL⁻¹)39. Pellets were cultured for 14, 21 or 28 d, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (G1101, Servicebio, China) for 1 h and processed for lineage-specific staining: adipogenic samples were cryosectioned at 10 µm and labelled with Oil Red O (G1015, Servicebio, China) plus haematoxylin (G1077, Servicebio, China), whereas chondrogenic and osteogenic samples were paraffin embedded, sectioned at 5 µm, deparaffinised and stained with Alcian Blue (G1027, Servicebio, China), Sirius Red (G1078, Servicebio, China) or Alizarin Red (G1038, Servicebio, China); images were acquired microscopically and the positively stained area quantified with ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Alcian blue staining

Cartilage pellets were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 6 h, processed into paraffin blocks, sliced at 3–5 µm, and placed on glass slides. Following deparaffinisation, sections were stained for 30 min at room temperature with Alcian Blue solution (pH 2.5), rinsed through a graded ethanol series (three changes, 5 min each), cleared in xylene for 5 min, and coverslipped with neutral resin. Stained sections were then examined and photographed under a light microscope.

Safranin O staining

Cartilage pellets were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 6 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 3–5 µm, and mounted on glass slides. After deparaffinisation, sections were immersed in Safranin O solution (GC307023, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 10–15 s at room temperature, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (three changes, 5 min each), cleared in xylene for 5 min, and mounted with neutral resin. Stained slides were subsequently examined and photographed under a light microscope.

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen, USA), and its quantity and purity were verified by monitoring the A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ ratio on a micro-volume spectrophotometer (Bio-Sun, China). One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT kit (11752050, Invitrogen, USA) to generate first-strand cDNA. Quantitative PCR was performed with the SYBR Premix Ex Taq system (4385618, Invitrogen, USA) in 20 µL reactions composed of 10 µL SYBR Green premix, 2 µL cDNA template, 0.8 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 0.4 µL ROX Dye II, and 6 µL nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s; followed by a melt-curve step at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 90 °C for 15 s. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the reference gene, and relative mRNA levels were calculated with the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Western-blot analysis

Total cellular protein was harvested with RIPA lysis buffer (R0020, Solarbio, China), quantified by a BCA assay (PC0020, Solarbio, China) and 30 µg of each sample was resolved by SDS-PAGE, then electro-transferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Blots were blocked and probed for 2 h at room temperature with the following primary antibodies: SOX9 (1 : 2000, Cat. 67439-1-Ig), COL2A1 (1 : 2000, Cat. 28459-1-AP), GAPDH (1 : 20000, Cat. 60004-1-Ig) (all Proteintech, China), and aggrecan (1 : 1000, YC0042, Immunoway). After three washes, membranes were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies—anti-mouse IgG (1 : 20000, Cat. SA00001-1, Proteintech, China) or anti-rabbit IgG (1 : 20000, Cat. SA00001-2, Proteintech, China)—followed by chemiluminescent detection; band intensities were captured and quantified in ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Immunofluorescence

Cartilage spheres were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded and sectioned. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, permeabilized with Triton X-100, and blocked, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with an anti-COL2A1 primary antibody (1:2000, Cat. 28459-1-AP, Proteintech, China). Negative controls were performed by omitting the primary antibody. After washing, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a fluorescence-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500, Cat. SA00013-4, Proteintech, China), counterstained with DAPI, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Plasmid construction and lentiviral transduction

Recombinant vectors and lentiviral particles were produced by HANBIO (China). For gain-of-function, the human CH25H and FPR1 open-reading frame was ligated into the pLVX-AcGFP1 backbone, packaged into lentivirus, and transduced into hADSCs in the presence of polybrene. For loss-of-function, a short-hairpin construct targeting CH25H was inserted into the pLKO.1-EGFP-puro plasmid, while a scrambled shRNA plasmid served as control. FPR1 knockdown was achieved using shRNA plasmids in the pGPU6/GFP/Neo vector, with the matched non-targeting control plasmid (sh-NC). Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo, USA) was used for all plasmid transfections, and experimental procedures followed established protocols.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Tools40. The protein structure was preprocessed in PyMOL by removing water molecules, ligands and other non-essential components, followed by the addition of polar hydrogen atoms. The receptor was then prepared in AutoDockTools (ADT) by assigning Kollman charges and saving the file in PDBQT format. The ligand structure was similarly processed by adding Gasteiger charges and saved as a PDBQT file. The binding site of the receptor was defined either by the coordinates of known ligands or through structural analysis. Grid box parameters, including the center and dimensions, were configured in AutoDockTools. Docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina, and multiple conformations were generated. The binding poses were ranked by binding affinity, and the one with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal conformation.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

The real-time interaction between human FPR1 and 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC) was analyzed using the Biacore T200 system (Cytiva)41. Purified human FPR1 protein (CSB-CF008854HU, CUSABIO, USA) was covalently immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip via EDC/NHS amine coupling chemistry. The immobilization buffer was 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.0), and the experiments were carried out at 25 °C. After immobilization, unreacted sites were blocked using 1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.5). A reference channel was prepared by activating and blocking the chip surface without protein immobilization to serve as a background control.

For kinetic analysis, PBS-P⁺ running buffer (20 mM phosphate, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.4, containing 1% DMSO) was used. Various concentrations of 25-HC were injected at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 180 s, followed by a 600 s dissociation phase. During intervals between injections, the sensor surface was regenerated with running buffer alone, without the need for additional regeneration solution. Sensorgrams were acquired using the Biacore T200 control software, and representative raw reference-subtracted sensorgrams are shown (Supplementary Fig. 2). Binding kinetics were evaluated using the Steady-State Affinity Model to determine the equilibrium dissociation constant.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation protocol

All-atom MD simulations were carried out in GROMACS 2020.6 on a GPU work-station42. The protein downloaded from the OPM database43 and pre-oriented perpendicular to the bilayer—was embedded with CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder44, which supplied all input files for minimisation, equilibration and production. The membrane patch comprised 90 POPC lipids plus 20% cholesterol, flanked on either side by a 40 Å slab of TIP3P water; 0.15 M NaCl was added for electro-neutrality. Simulations used the CHARMM36 force field (protein topology dated May 2011; lipid topology updated 2016/8). After 5000 steepest-descent minimisation steps, the system passed through an eight-stage protocol: two NVT periods (1 fs step) and four NPT periods (first 1 fs, then 2 fs), during which positional restraints on protein and lipids were gradually released. Temperature (310 K) and pressure (1 bar) were initially controlled by Berendsen coupling and thereafter by a Nose–Hoover thermostat (τ = 1 ps) and semi-isotropic Parrinello–Rahman barostat (τ = 5 ps). Long-range Coulomb interactions were evaluated with PME (12 Å real-space cut-off); LINCS constrained bonds to hydrogen, allowing a 2 fs production time step. A 100 ns trajectory was generated, written every 20 ps and split into ten 10-ns files for analysis. Ligand parameters were derived with CGenFF via the ParamChem web server (CGenFF program v3.0) and merged into the topology45; for 25-HC, the CGenFF parameter and charge penalty scores were 4.900 and 3.627, respectively. Post-run analyses—RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, SASA and protein–ligand hydrogen bonding—were performed to assess complex stability and key contacts.

Free energy calculation and energy decomposition analysis

To evaluate the binding strength between the protein and ligand, the molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) method was used to calculate the binding-free energy of the complex46. Calculations were performed using the gmx_MMPBSA module in GROMACS, analyzing the final 100 frames of the production trajectory. The sign of the free energy reflects whether the interaction is thermodynamically favourable, while the magnitude indicates the strength and stability of binding. Additionally, energy decomposition and entropy contribution analyses were carried out using the same module to identify key residues contributing most significantly to the binding.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and free energy landscape (FEL)

To identify the dominant modes of conformational changes in the protein structure, principal-component analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the MD trajectory data47. This was done by diagonalizing the covariance matrix of atomic coordinates to extract principal motions (PCs), using the gmx_covar and gmx_anaeig tools. Preparation of pluronic F127–ADSC constructs

Pluronic F127 (P2443, Sigma, USA) was prepared under aseptic, low-temperature conditions; pre-chilled powder was slowly added to ice-cold sterile vehicle (PBS or serum-free DMEM) on ice, mixed gently and held at 4 °C until fully dissolved to a small panel of trial concentrations (e.g., 20, 24, 28, 32% w/v); thermosensitivity was assessed by equilibrating 0.5–1.0 mL aliquots from ~10–40 °C and recording the sol–gel transition by vial-tilt/flow, with gelation time measured at 37 °C; the working concentration was defined as freely injectable at 4 °C and forming a cohesive gel within ≤1 min at 37 °C; for construct preparation, all plastics and syringes were pre-cooled, syngeneic Lewis rat ADSCs (P3–P4; Section 14) were harvested, washed with cold PBS, counted by trypan blue, and gently suspended in ice-cold F127 at ~1 × 10⁷ cells/mL; group-specific formulations were: Control—defect left empty (no hydrogel/cells), Gel—F127 alone, Gel+M—F127 with ADSCs only, Gel+M + 25-HC—F127 with ADSCs plus 25-hydroxycholesterol added from sterile DMSO stock (final cosolvent ≤0.1% v/v) to 4 µM in the gel, and Gel+M + 25-HC+i—same as Gel+M + 25-HC with the FPR1 inhibitor cyclosporin H/HCH6-1 co-loaded to 10 µM; mixtures were kept on ice (sol state), gently inverted to avoid bubbles, loaded into pre-chilled syringes, and dispensed at 7 µL per defect to form a level plug upon rapid in situ gelation at body temperature; quality control included a 37 °C hold (30 min) on a small aliquot with Calcein-AM/PI to confirm ≥90% viability, verification of sterility/mycoplasma negativity before mixing, and limiting the interval from cell admixture to implantation to <10 min to prevent premature gelation; note that F127 is a thermoreversible, high-water-content physical gel whose micellar network permits partitioning and diffusion of 25-HC to the embedded cells, providing early local exposure.

Isolation, culture and characterization of rat ADSCs (Lewis)

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee / Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital; male Lewis rats (8 weeks, 250–300 g) served as donors, bilateral inguinal fat was harvested under anesthesia, rinsed in ice-cold PBS with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and minced (~1–2 mm³), digested in 0.1% collagenase I (w/v in PBS with 1% BSA) at 37 °C for 45–60 min with gentle agitation ( ≈ 100 rpm), neutralized with an equal volume of high-glucose DMEM + 10% FBS, passed through 100 µm and 70 µm strainers, and centrifuged (300 g, 5 min) to obtain the stromal vascular fraction followed by RBC lysis (2–3 min, if needed) and PBS wash; primary cultures were established at 1 × 10⁴ cells/cm² in high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, nonadherent cells were removed at 24 h and medium was changed every 2–3 days, cells were passaged at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (2–3 min) at a 1:3 split, and passages P3–P4 were used for implantation in Section 15; where applicable, ADSCs were cryopreserved at 1–2 × 10⁶ cells/mL in 90% FBS + 10% DMSO, cooled at −1 °C/min to −80 °C, transferred to liquid nitrogen, rapidly thawed at 37 °C, and recovered ≥24 h before use; in-house QC confirmed spindle morphology, >90% viability (trypan blue), and mycoplasma negativity; immunophenotyping employed rat-validated antibodies with a CD29⁺/CD44⁺/CD34⁻/CD45⁻/MHC-II⁻ panel following the same staining and acquisition procedures as Methods Sections 1–2 for human ADSCs, and tri-lineage differentiation (adipogenic, osteogenic, chondrogenic) followed the identical induction media, timelines, and staining endpoints described in Methods Sections 1–2; these Lewis-strain ADSCs served as the syngeneic cell payload for implantation into Lewis recipients, minimizing allo-immune confounding while maintaining continuity with the human in-vitro workflow.

Rat femoral trochlear cartilage defect model and implantation

Animals were assigned to treatment groups using computer-generated simple randomization at the time of surgery. Group allocation was recorded by an investigator not involved in outcome scoring. Adult male Lewis rats (8 weeks, 250–300 g) were anesthetized with isoflurane or ketamine/xylazine, the right knee was shaved, disinfected, and draped, a medial parapatellar arthrotomy was performed and the patella gently dislocated laterally with the knee flexed to expose the femoral trochlear groove, and a full-thickness cylindrical osteochondral defect (diameter 2 mm, depth 1.5–2.0 mm) was created with a low-speed microdrill or trephine equipped with a depth stop under continuous saline irrigation to avoid thermal injury and unintended breach of the subchondral plate; the site was rinsed with sterile saline and the 25-HC dosing regimen was selected based on our in vitro dose–response experiments, in which the chosen dose produced robust pro-chondrogenic effects and pathway activation without overt cytotoxicity; specifically, 25-HC was administered at 16 ng per intra-articular injection in 10 µL. The defect was then randomized to one of five prespecified conditions delivered once at surgery (week 0): Control (defect left empty), Gel (hydrogel only), Gel+M (hydrogel loaded with syngeneic Lewis rADSCs, P3–P4, prepared as in Section 14), Gel+M + 25-HC (same cell-laden hydrogel with 25-hydroxycholesterol 11 ng per defect), and Gel+M + 25-HC + i (same formulation with 25-hydroxycholesterol 11 ng per defect plus the FPR1 inhibitor cyclosporin H/HCH6-1 59 ng); the hydrogel or cell–hydrogel mixture was dispensed at 7 µL per defect to fill the cylinder flush with surrounding cartilage and allowed to gel in situ (vehicle: saline with final DMSO ≤ 0.1% for drug-containing arms), the patella was repositioned, and capsule and skin were closed with sutures; postoperative care included analgesia (e.g., buprenorphine 0.05 mg/kg s.c. every 12 h for 48 h) and free cage activity without immobilization; to maintain intra-articular exposure, percutaneous intra-articular injections were performed once weekly on weeks 1–7 via the parapatellar approach at a fixed volume of 10 µL per knee per injection: the Gel+M + 25-HC group received 25-HC 16 ng per injection, the Gel+M + 25-HC + i group received 25-HC 16 ng per injection plus cyclosporin H/HCH6-1 84 ng, and the Control, Gel, and Gel+M groups received vehicle-only 10 µL on the same schedule (all injections with final DMSO ≤ 0.1%); at 8 weeks animals were euthanized and knee joints harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) for 2 weeks at 4 °C with gentle agitation, processed and paraffin-embedded, and sagittal sections (5 µm) through the central defect were stained with Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) and Safranin O/Fast Green (SafO/FG), while COL2A1 immunohistochemistry was performed on adjacent sections; histological repair was graded independently by six blinded observers using ICRS and Wakitani systems. All samples were anonymised with coded identifiers before scoring. Observers scored each animal independently based on the central defect sections, and the mean of the six scores was used as the final ICRS or Wakitani score for that animal (one data point per animal). No animals were excluded from the analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Unless stated otherwise, continuous outcomes (e.g., ELISA, qPCR, Western-blot densitometry) were summarized as mean ± SEM and analyzed at the level of the donor for human cell experiments (n = 3 independent donors; technical replicates averaged within donor) and the knee/animal for in-vivo endpoints (one knee per rat; planned n = 6 per group), while ordinal histological scores (ICRS, Wakitani) were treated as non-parametric data and summarized as median [IQR]. For single-time, multi-group continuous outcomes we used one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests; when variance homogeneity was violated (Levene test), Welch ANOVA with Games–Howell post hoc was applied. For time-course assays (0/7/14/21 d), effects of time or treatment were evaluated with a linear mixed-effects model (fixed effect: time or treatment; random intercept for donor; Satterthwaite degrees of freedom), with planned contrasts obtained from estimated marginal means. ICRS/Wakitani were compared using Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn tests; the observational unit was the knee, with scoring aggregated by averaging section-level scores per observer across three central sagittal sections and then averaging across six blinded observers to yield a single knee-level score. For two-group continuous comparisons with doubtful normality at small n, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Where multiple comparisons occurred, control was applied within each outcome family using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR = 0.05 (for many contrasts) or Holm–Šidák (for a small set of planned contrasts). All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5 and R 4.2.2.
