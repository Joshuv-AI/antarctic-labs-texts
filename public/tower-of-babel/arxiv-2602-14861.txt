Bias analysis of a linear order-statistic inequality index estimator: Unbiasedness under gamma populations
Roberto Vila, Helton Saulo

Abstract
This paper studies a class of rank-based inequality measures built from linear combinations of expected order statistics. The proposed framework unifies several well-known indices, including the classical Gini coefficient, the m m th Gini index, extended m m th Gini index and S S -Gini index, and also connects to spectral inequality measures through an integral representation. We investigate the finite-sample behavior of a natural U-statistic-type estimator that averages weighted order-statistic contrasts over all subsamples of fixed size and normalizes by the sample mean. A general bias decomposition is derived in terms of components that isolate the effect of random normalization on each rank level, yielding analytical expressions that can be evaluated under broad non-negative distributions via Laplace-transform methods. Under mild moment conditions, the estimator is shown to be asymptotically unbiased. Moreover, we prove exact unbiasedness under gamma populations for any sample size, extending earlier unbiasedness results for Gini-type estimators. A Monte Carlo study is performed to numerically check that the theoretical unbiasednes under gamma populations.

Introduction

Quantifying economic inequality from sample data is a central problem in applied economics and statistics. The classical Gini coefficient is arguably the most widely used rank-based inequality measure, but it is well known to exhibit non-negligible finite-sample bias, especially for small and moderate sample sizes and for highly skewed populations; see, e.g.,
Deltas, 2003
;
Baydil et al., 2025
and references therein. This motivates the development and analysis of alternative indices and estimators with improved small-sample properties, while retaining the desirable invariance and rank-based interpretation that make Gini-type measures attractive in practice.

A prominent extension of the Gini coefficient is the class of generalized and extended Gini indices, including the
m
th Gini index,
S
-Gini index and its extensions, which can be expressed as normalized contrasts of expected order statistics
Deltas, 2003
;
Gini, 1936
;
Gavilan-Ruiz et al., 2024
;
Vila and Saulo, 2025
;
Vila and Saulo, 2026
;
Yitzhaki and Schechtman, 2013
. Such measures are particularly appealing because they admit transparent interpretations in terms of dispersion between extreme or intermediate ranks and connect naturally to the broader class of spectral (rank-dependent) inequality measures
Cowell, 2011
;
Chakravarty, 1990
. At the same time, many of these indices are typically estimated through U-statistic-type constructions that average rank contrasts over all subsamples of a given size
m
, often combined with normalization by the sample mean. Despite their conceptual appeal and practical relevance, the finite-sample bias behavior of these estimators has remained largely unexplored beyond specific cases.

The present paper contributes to this literature by developing a unified bias analysis for a broad family of order-statistic-based inequality indices and their canonical estimators. Specifically, for a non-negative random variable
X
with finite, strictly positive mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
, and for
m\geqslant 2
, we consider the linear order-statistic inequality index

I_{m}=\frac{1}{m\mu}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}],\quad\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}=0,

which includes as special cases the classical Gini coefficient (
m=2
), the
m
th Gini index, extended
m
th Gini index, and
S
-Gini index (Table
1
). This class also admits an integral representation

I_{m}=\mu^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}w_{m}(u)\,Q_{X}(u){\rm d}u,

so that it can be viewed as a finite-dimensional approximation to continuous spectral measures of inequality.

Given an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with
n\geqslant m
, we study the estimator
\widehat{I}_{m}
defined in (
6
), which averages the weighted order-statistic contrasts over all
m
-subsamples and normalizes by
\overline{X}
. Our first main result derives a general expression for the finite-sample bias
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},I_{m})
(Corollary
3
). The bias can be decomposed into a linear combination of factors

\Delta_{n,r}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{r:r}]}{\mu},\quad r=1,\ldots,m,

which separates the contribution of each rank level in a manner that is amenable to analytic and numerical investigation. We further provide a characterization of
\Delta_{n,r}
via Laplace-transform methods (Proposition
4
), yielding a route to compute (or approximate) the bias under general non-negative distributions.

Our second main result establishes asymptotic unbiasedness: under mild moment conditions,
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},I_{m})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
(Proposition
5
). While asymptotic unbiasedness is reassuring, it does not address the small-sample bias that often drives empirical discrepancies. The key contribution of the paper is therefore our third main result, which identifies a distributional setting where the estimator is
exactly
unbiased for all sample sizes. Specifically, we prove that if
X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)
, then
\Delta_{n,r}=0
for all
r\leqslant n
, implying (Corollary
3
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},I_{m})=0\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant m,

thereby extending earlier unbiasedness results for the Gini coefficient and its
m
th and extended variants
Deltas, 2003
;
Baydil et al., 2025
;
Vila and Saulo, 2025
;
Vila and Saulo, 2026
. The proof relies on the Dirichlet property of normalized gamma samples and the homogeneity of the maximum functional, which together yield the identity

\mathbb{E}[X_{r:r}]=\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X_{r:r}/\overline{X}]
.

Finally, we complement the theoretical analysis with a Monte Carlo study (Section
7
) under gamma and non-gamma populations. The simulations confirm that the bias is essentially zero for Gamma
(\alpha,1)
distributions, even for
n=10
, while non-gamma heavy-tailed alternatives such as Lognormal
(0,1)
and Lomax
(3,1)
display noticeable negative bias in small samples.

The rest of the paper unfolds as follows. Section
2
defines the index
I_{m}
and establishes key properties and representations. Section
4
derives the general bias formula for
\widehat{I}_{m}
and provides a Laplace-transform characterization. Section
5
proves asymptotic unbiasedness under moment conditions. Section
6
establishes exact unbiasedness under gamma populations. Section
7
reports Monte Carlo evidence, and Section
8
concludes.

2
A linear order-statistic inequality index

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable with finite mean

\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]>0
.
For an i.i.d. random sample of size
m\geqslant 2
, denote the order statistics by

X_{1:m}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant X_{m:m}.

Define the population index

\displaystyle I_{m}\equiv I_{m}(X)={1\over m\mu}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]},

(1)

where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
are fixed real coefficients such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}=0.\end{aligned}

(2)

The index
I_{m}
measures a weighted contrast of expected rank positions relative to the population mean.

If the distribution of
X
is close to equality (i.e.,
X_{k:m}\approx\mu
for all
k
), then the index reflects low inequality (depending on the structure of the weights
a_{k}
). Conversely, large gaps between lower and upper order statistics increase the magnitude of the index, capturing stronger dispersion across ranks.

Therefore,
I_{m}
can be viewed as a finite-dimensional, rank-based summary of inequality determined by the weighting scheme
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
.

3
Basic properties

In this section, we present several properties of the linear order-statistic inequality index defined in (
1
).

•

Ratio-cale invariance.

For any
c>0
,

I_{m}(cX)=I_{m}(X).

•

Lack of translation invariance.

For any
c>0
,

I_{m}(X+c)={\mu\over\mu+c}\,I_{m}(X).

•

Vanishing under equality.

If
X=c
almost surely, then

I_{m}(X)=0.

•

Non-negativity under increasing weights.
Assume that
a_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant a_{m}
. Since

\mathbb{E}[X_{1:m}]\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\mathbb{E}[X_{m:m}],

Chebyshev’s rearrangement inequality yields

\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]}{m}\;\geqslant\;\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}}{m}\,\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]}{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{conditions-an}}}{{=}}0,

because
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]=m\mu
.
Hence, under the condition
a_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant a_{m}
, the index satisfies
I_{m}\geqslant 0
.

•

Upper bound under increasing weights.
Assume that
a_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant a_{m}
. Since

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]}{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{conditions-an}}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{\displaystyle a_{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]}{m}=a_{m}\mu,

we have
I_{m}\leqslant a_{m}
.

•

Integral representation.

Using the representation

\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{X}(u)f_{U_{k:m}}(u){\rm d}u,

where
Q_{X}
is the quantile function of
X
and
U_{k:m}\sim\text{Beta}(k,m-k+1)
is the

k
-th order statistic of a sample of size
m
from
U(0,1)
, we obtain

I_{m}=\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{1}w_{m}(u)\,Q_{X}(u){\rm d}u,

with weight function

w_{m}(u)=(1/m)\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}f_{U_{k:m}}(u).

Thus
I_{m}
belongs to the class of linear (spectral) inequality
measures
Cowell, 2011
;
Chakravarty, 1990
.

•

Covariance representation.

Let
F
denote the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
X
.
Since

\displaystyle\binom{m-1}{k-1}\mathbb{E}[F^{k-1}(X)[1-F(X)]^{m-k}]={1\over m}\quad\text{and}\quad\binom{m-1}{k-1}={k\over m}\,\binom{m}{k},

(3)

we have

{1\over\mu}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,{\rm Cov}\left(X,\binom{m-1}{k-1}F^{k-1}(X)[1-F(X)]^{m-k}\right)\\[5.69046pt]
={1\over m\mu}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{X}(U)\left\{k\binom{m}{k}U^{k-1}(1-U)^{m-k}\right\}\right],

(4)

where
U\sim U(0,1)
and
Q_{X}
is the quantile function of
X
.
By combining the well-known identity (see Item (1) em
Kleiber and Kotz, 2002
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}]=k\binom{m}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{X}(U)U^{k-1}(1-U)^{m-k}\right],

with the definition in (
1
) of
I_{m}
, from (
4
) we have

\displaystyle I_{m}={1\over\mu}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,{\rm Cov}\left(X,\binom{m-1}{k-1}F^{k-1}(X)[1-F(X)]^{m-k}\right).

(5)

•

Lorenz-type inequality representation.
The Lorenz curve
L
for
X
is defined by

L(p)=(1/\mu){\int_{0}^{p}Q_{X}(t){\rm d}t},\text{for any}\ 0\leqslant p\leqslant 1.

Since
L^{\prime}(p)=Q_{X}(p)/\mu
, by (
5
) and identity in (
4
), we have

\displaystyle I_{m}={1\over m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\{L^{\prime}(U)-1\}\left\{k\binom{m}{k}U^{k-1}(1-U)^{m-k}\right\}\right],

where
U\sim U(0,1)
. By integration by parts, the above identity takes the form:

I_{m}={1\over m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\{U-L(U)\}\Bigg\{k\binom{m}{k}U^{k-2}(1-U)^{m-k-1}[k-1-(m-1)U]\Bigg\}\Bigg].

Using the identity (
3
) and the binomial theorem applied to
(1-U)^{m-k-1}
, we derive

I_{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\binom{m-1}{k-1}\sum_{r=0}^{m-k-1}\binom{m-k-1}{r}(-1)^{m-k-1-r}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\left[{k-1\over m-1-r}\,D_{m-2-r}-{m-1\over m-r}\,D_{m-1-r}\right],

where

D_{n}\equiv D_{n}(X)=(n+1)\mathbb{E}[\{U-L(U)\}U^{n-1}],\ U\sim U(0,1),\ n\geqslant 1,

is the Lorenz measure of inequality for
X
introduced in
Aaberge, 2000
.

•

Structural relationship.

\text{Gini (}m=2\text{)}\subset\text{$m$th Gini}\subset\text{Extended $m$th Gini}\subset I_{m}\subset\text{Spectral inequality measures}.

Proposition 1
.

For any
m\geqslant 2
, the linear order-statistic inequality index (
1
) can be computed as

\displaystyle I_{m}={1\over m}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sum_{r=k}^{m}\binom{m}{r}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\dfrac{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{r:r}]}{\displaystyle\mu}.

Proof.

From Proposition 10 of
Salama and Koch, 2020
, it follows that

\displaystyle X_{k:m}=\sum_{r=k}^{m}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\sum_{1\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{r}\leqslant m}\max\{X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{r}}\}.

Taking expectations on both sides of the above identity and using the fact that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are i.i.d. completes the proof.
∎

4
General formula for the bias

In this section, we establish in Corollary
3
a general formula for the bias of the linear order-statistic inequality index estimator
\widehat{I}_{m}
,
m\geqslant 2
, defined by:

\displaystyle\widehat{I}_{m}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\displaystyle\binom{n}{m}^{-1}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}:\\[2.84544pt]
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}X_{k:{\bf i}}}{\displaystyle m\overline{X}},&\text{if}\ \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}>0\ \text{a.s.},\\[17.07182pt]
0,&\text{if}\ \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=0\ \text{a.s.},\end{cases}

(6)

where
\overline{X}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
is the sample mean,
X_{1:\mathbf{i}}=\min\{X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{m}}\}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant X_{m:\mathbf{i}}=\max\{X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{m}}\}
are the order statistics of the i.i.d. sample
X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{m}}
, and
\widehat{I}_{m}
is based on
n\geqslant m
i.i.d. observations.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
be i.i.d. copies of a non-negative, non-degenerate random variable
X
with finite, strictly positive mean
\mu
.
For any
m\geqslant 2
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{m}\right]={1\over m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sum_{r=k}^{m}\binom{m}{r}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right].

Proof.

Making use of the identities provided in

(
Salama and Koch, 2020
, Proposition 10)
:

\displaystyle X_{k:{\bf i}}=\sum_{r=k}^{m}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}:\\[2.84544pt]
1\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{r}\leqslant m\end{subarray}}X_{r:{\bf t}},

we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}:\\[2.84544pt]
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}X_{k:{\bf i}}}{\overline{X}}\right]\\[5.69046pt]
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}:\\[2.84544pt]
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sum_{r=k}^{m}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}:\\[2.84544pt]
1\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{r}\leqslant m\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{X_{m:{\bf t}}}{\overline{X}}\right].

(7)

Using the fact that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are i.i.d., the identity in (
7
) takes the simplified form:

\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}:\\[2.84544pt]
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}X_{k:{\bf i}}}{\overline{X}}\right]=\binom{n}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sum_{r=k}^{m}\binom{m}{r}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right].

Finally, the result follows immediately from definition (
6
) of
\widehat{I}_{m}
and the preceding identity.
∎

By combining Theorem
2
and Proposition
1
, we obtain:

Corollary 3
.

For any
m\geqslant 2
, the bias of
\widehat{I}_{m}
relative to
I_{m}
, denoted by
{\rm Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},I_{m})
, can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},I_{m})={1\over m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sum_{r=k}^{m}\binom{m}{r}(-1)^{r-k}\binom{r-1}{k-1}\Delta_{n,r},

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,r}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right]-\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[X_{r:r}\right]}{\mu}.

(8)

Under suitable integrability conditions, the next result and Corollary
3
yield (not necessarily closed-form) expressions for the bias over general non-negative-support populations. We strongly believe that, within the class of absolutely continuous distributions supported on
[0,\infty)
and having finite and positive mean, the gamma distribution is the only one for which the bias vanishes.

Proposition 4
.

For each
n\geqslant r
the factor
\Delta_{n,r}
in (
8
) can be characterized as

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,r}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[1-F_{z}(t)\right]n\mathscr{L}^{n}(z){\rm d}z\,-\dfrac{\left[1-F^{r}(t)\right]}{\mu}\right\}{\rm d}t,

provided that the Laplace transform
\mathscr{L}(z)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-zX)]
of
X
and the associated improper integrals exist. In the above,
F
is the CDF of
X
and
F_{z}
is the CDF defined as

\displaystyle F_{z}(t)\equiv\dfrac{\mathbb{E}^{r}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{X\leqslant t\}}\exp\left(-Xz\right)\right]}{\mathscr{L}^{r}(z)},\quad t>0,\,z>0.

Proof.

The result is an immediate consequence of the identities

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp{(-wz)}\,\mathrm{d}z={1}/{w},\ w>0,

applied with
w=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, and

X_{r:r}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{r:r}>t\}}\,\mathrm{d}t,

combined with the i.i.d. assumption on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
∎

5
Asymptotic unbiasedness

In this section, we show that the bias of the linear order-statistic inequality index estimator (
6
), as characterized in Corollary
3
, vanishes as the sample size
n
increases.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose that for some
p>1
,
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{r:r}^{p}\right]\in(0,\infty)
,
r=1,\ldots,m
.
For any
m\geqslant 2
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},I_{m})=0.

Proof.

Using the weak law of large numbers,

\displaystyle\overline{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu,\quad\text{as}\ n\to\infty,

(9)

where
``\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}"
denotes convergence in probability.
Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geqslant N
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{X}<\mu-\varepsilon\right)=0.

Taking
\varepsilon=\mu/2
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\right)=0,\quad A_{n}\equiv\left\{\overline{X}<{\mu\over 2}\right\}.

(10)

Next, fix
p>1
. By (
10
), for every
\delta>0
there exists

N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geqslant N_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}\right]<\delta.

(11)

Hence, by using (
11
) we have

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle+\max\left\{\sup_{1\leqslant n<N_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}\right],\sup_{n\geqslant N_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{2^{p}\over\mu^{p}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[X_{r:r}^{p}\right]+\max\left\{\sup_{1\leqslant n<N_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}\right],\delta\right\}.

Since
\mu
and
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{r:r}^{p}\right]
are positive and finite, we obtain that the sequence

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\quad\text{is uniformly integrable}.

(12)

Finally, combining (
9
), the uniform integrability in (
12
), and standard convergence theorems (e.g., convergence in probability plus uniform integrability implies convergence in
L^{1}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right]=\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[X_{r:r}\right]}{\mu}.

Applying Corollary
3
completes the proof.
∎

6
Unbiasedness in gamma populations

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be a random sample from
X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)
.
It is well known
(see
Mosimann, 1979
, Theorem 2.1)
that

\left(\frac{X_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\ldots,\frac{X_{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\right)\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha),

and that this random vector is independent of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.

For
1\leqslant r\leqslant n
, define

g(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=\max\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\}.

Using the homogeneity of
g
and the above independence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{r:r}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[g(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[g\!\left(\frac{X_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\ldots,\frac{X_{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right]\mathbb{E}\!\left[g\!\left(\frac{X_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\ldots,\frac{X_{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=n\mu\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[g\!\left(\frac{X_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\ldots,\frac{X_{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\right)\right]=\mu\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{X_{r:r}}{\overline{X}}\right].

Therefore, the quantity
\Delta_{n,r}
defined in (
8
) satisfies

\Delta_{n,r}=0,\quad n\geqslant r.

An application of Corollary
3
then yields

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{m},\,I_{m})=0,\quad m\geqslant 2.

(13)

Remark 2
.

Observe that (
13
) extends the earlier results of
Deltas, 2003
,
Baydil et al., 2025
,
Vila and Saulo, 2025
, and
Vila and Saulo, 2026
. Furthermore, since the index
I_{m}

includes the S-Gini index as a special case (see Table
1
), Equation (
13
) implies that the S-Gini estimator is unbiased for Gamma populations, a result that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously reported in the literature.

7
Monte Carlo simulation study

This section presents Monte Carlo simulation results assessing the finite-sample performance of the linear order-statistic inequality index estimator
\widehat{I}_{m}
defined in (
6
). The simulations are designed to complement the theoretical results of Sections (
4
)–(
6
) by illustrating the bias and mean squared error behavior of the estimator under gamma and non-gamma populations.

We consider a fixed order
m
and a vector of weights
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}=0
, corresponding to a linear order-statistic inequality index of the form

I_{m}=\frac{1}{m\mu}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k:m}],

where
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
X_{1:m}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant X_{m:m}
denote the order statistics of an i.i.d. sample of size
m
from a non-negative distribution. For each data-generating process, the population value
I_{m}
is approximated by a very large Monte Carlo sample and treated as the true benchmark, denoted by
I_{m}^{\mathrm{true}}
.

The simulations are carried out for sample sizes
n\in\{10,20,30,50\}
. For each distribution and each
n
, the expectation of
\widehat{I}_{m}
is approximated using
R_{\mathrm{MC}}=2{,}000
Monte Carlo replications. Within each replication, the estimator is computed by averaging over
B_{\mathrm{combs}}=8{,}000
randomly selected subsamples of size
m
, providing a computationally efficient approximation to the full U-statistic definition. The population benchmark
I_{m}^{\mathrm{true}}
is approximated using
R_{\mathrm{true}}=250{,}000
Monte Carlo draws.

For each
(\text{distribution},n)
pair, we report:

•

the Monte Carlo mean
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{I}_{m}]
,

•

the bias
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{I}_{m}]-I_{m}^{\mathrm{true}}
,

•

the root mean squared error

\mathrm{RMSE}(\widehat{I}_{m})=\left\{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\widehat{I}_{m}-I_{m}^{\mathrm{true}})^{2}\bigr]\right\}^{1/2}.

The following positive-support distributions are considered:

1.

Gamma
(\alpha,1)
: two shape parameters are examined,
\alpha=2
and
\alpha=5
, with unit rate. In both cases, the mean is
\mu=\alpha
.

2.

Lognormal
(0,1)
: that is,
X=\exp(Z)
with
Z\sim N(0,1)
, yielding
\mu=\exp(1/2)
.

3.

Weibull
(k,\lambda)
: with shape
k=1.6
and scale
\lambda=1
, for which
\mu=\Gamma(1+1/k)
.

4.

Lomax
(\alpha,\lambda)
: with shape
\alpha=3
and scale
\lambda=1
, implying
\mu=\lambda/(\alpha-1)=1/2
.

These distributions were selected to represent light-tailed (Gamma), moderately skewed (Weibull), and heavy-tailed (lognormal and Lomax) cases.

I_{m}^{\mathrm{true}}

5

(5,1)

\widehat{I}_{m}
fluctuates tightly around zero and may change sign across
n
, which is expected due to the variance of the bias estimates. Observe that, as
n
increases, the RMSE declines, reflecting the reduced sampling variability of
\widehat{I}_{m}
. In contrast, the estimator exhibits increased finite-sample bias under non-gamma distributions. For the Lognormal
(0,1)
and Lomax
(3,1)

n
. Although the magnitude of the bias decreases as
n
increases, it remains clearly distinguishable from zero even at
n=50

(1.6,1)

8
Concluding remarks

This paper provides a unified bias analysis for a broad class of rank-based inequality measures defined through linear combinations of expected order statistics. The proposed framework encompasses the classical Gini coefficient, the
m
th Gini index, extended
m
th Gini index, and
S
-Gini index, and connects naturally to spectral inequality measures. We study the finite-sample behavior of the canonical estimator based on averaging weighted order-statistic contrasts over all subsamples and normalizing by the sample mean. A general finite-sample bias decomposition is derived. Under mild moment conditions, the estimator is shown to be asymptotically unbiased. We establish that exact unbiasedness holds under gamma populations for any sample size, extending several existing results for Gini-type estimators. Monte Carlo evidence numerically corroborates the unbiasedness under gamma models and shows non-negligible finite-sample bias under heavy-tailed alternatives.
