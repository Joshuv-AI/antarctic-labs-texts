The Manticore-Local Cluster Catalogue: A Posterior Map of Massive Structures in the Nearby Universe
Stuart McAlpine

Abstract
We present a publicly available catalogue of massive structures in the nearby Universe, constructed from the Manticore-Local posterior ensemble—a Bayesian reconstruction of the local Universe from 2M++ galaxies. We identify massive structures by clustering central haloes at z = 0 z=0 across the 80 posterior realizations, selecting at most one member per realization. These associations serve as probabilistic counterparts to individual massive clusters, each with robust posterior estimates of mass, position, and velocity. The catalogue contains 225 associations with mean masses ⟨ M 200 ⟩ > 10 14 \langle M_{200}\rangle>10^{14} M ⊙ persisting in at least 50% of realizations. We independently validate these systems through stacked Planck thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich measurements, which yield significant detections ( > 3 ​ σ >3\sigma ) with amplitudes following the expected Y Y – M M relation. Many associations exhibit coherent evolutionary histories: even with only z = 0 z=0 constraints, the inference narrows each system’s possible assembly pathway, localizing progenitor configurations to volumes 2–5 times smaller than mass-matched haloes in unconstrained Λ \Lambda CDM simulations. Cross-matches with X-ray catalogues reveal systematic mass-scale differences that align with known observational biases: Manticore-Local masses are typically twice ROSAT-based estimates but agree at unity with weak-lensing-calibrated eROSITA measurements. This demonstrates the catalogue’s capability to diagnose systematics on an object-by-object basis. The resulting catalogue provides an observationally consistent map of massive structures in the local Universe, enabling direct cross-probe comparisons, hybrid analyses combining simulated and observed quantities, and systematic mass-scale studies. All data products will be released via cosmictwin.org as a community resource.

INTRODUCTION

Galaxy clusters are the most massive gravitationally bound structures in the Universe, forming at the intersections of cosmic filaments where matter flows converge
(
Press & Schechter 1974
)
. As dominant nodes of the cosmic web, clusters probe both cosmology and astrophysics: their abundance and clustering trace the growth of structure
(
Allen et al. 2011
, e.g.)
, while their deep, dark-matter–dominated potential wells and dense galaxy populations make them key laboratories for galaxy evolution
(
Kravtsov & Borgani 2012
)
. In the local Universe (within a few hundred Mpc), iconic systems such as Virgo and Coma can be studied in exceptional detail, providing stringent tests of dark matter, galaxy formation, and large-scale dynamics
(
Bland-Hawthorn & Peebles 2006
;
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016a
)
. Mapping and interpreting the nearby cluster population is therefore essential both for understanding environmental effects on galaxies and for exploiting clusters as precision cosmological probes.

Traditional large-scale structure analyses rely on summary statistics—chiefly the two-point correlation function and power spectrum—to constrain cosmology
(
Tegmark et al. 2004
;
Cole et al. 2005
)
. While powerful under nearly Gaussian initial conditions, these statistics discard much of the non-Gaussian information encoded in the evolved morphology of the cosmic web, including higher-order correlations and phase information
(
Aragón-Calvo et al. 2010
;
Obreschkow et al. 2013
;
Bonnaire et al. 2022
)
. Crucially, object-level information needed to connect simulated haloes to observed clusters is not directly accessible in this ensemble-averaged view.

Field-level Bayesian inference reconstructs the three-dimensional matter field by treating the initial conditions as parameters, forward modelling their nonlinear evolution to the present day, and constraining them with galaxy data to obtain a posterior ensemble with quantified uncertainties. The
Bayesian Origin Reconstruction from Galaxies
(
BORG
) algorithm
(
Jasche & Wandelt 2013
)
, and subsequent applications by the Aquila Consortium
1
1

1

https://aquila-consortium.org/
, have reconstructed the local density field and its dynamical history using this technique
(
Jasche & Lavaux 2019
;
Stopyra et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Complementary efforts include the
Constrained Local UniversE Simulations
(CLUES) project
(
Gottloeber et al. 2010
)
, the
Exploring the Local Universe with Reconstructed Initial Density Field
(ELUCID) simulations
(
Wang et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
, and the Local Universe Model
(
Pfeifer et al. 2023
)
, all aiming to reproduce local structures and assess their statistical properties.

Building on this foundation,
Manticore-Local

(
McAlpine et al. 2025
)
represents the most advanced Bayesian reconstruction of the nearby Universe to date. Fitting a physical structure formation model to the
2M++
galaxy catalogue
(
Lavaux & Hudson 2011
;
Carrick et al. 2015
)
, it produces a posterior ensemble of high-resolution constrained simulations—a “digital twin” of our cosmic neighbourhood—that accurately reproduces the observed large-scale structure. The reconstruction identifies high-significance counterparts for fourteen prominent galaxy clusters—including Virgo, Coma, and Perseus—each within one degree of its observed sky position, with inferred masses and redshifts in close agreement with observational estimates. The recovered peculiar velocity field achieves the highest Bayesian evidence across five independent datasets, surpassing state-of-the-art nonlinear models, linear theory, and machine learning approaches. Extensive validation confirms the local supervolume (
R<200
Mpc) is statistically consistent with
\Lambda
CDM expectations, with no evidence for a large-scale underdensity.

Our primary goal for this study is to deliver a publicly available catalogue of cluster-scale objects in the local Universe derived from the
Manticore-Local
posterior. Unlike traditional simulation-based cluster catalogues that represent ensemble averages over
\Lambda
CDM realizations, this catalogue provides observationally anchored counterparts to individual massive clusters in the actual local Universe (
z\lesssim 0.05
), each with robust posterior estimates of mass, position, velocity, and formation history. This enables a fundamentally different type of analysis: direct, object-by-object comparisons between simulated and observed clusters. Rather than relying on average scaling relations derived from idealized populations, researchers can validate cluster properties against the specific systems they observe, identify individual outliers that warrant targeted follow-up, and diagnose systematic biases in observational mass proxies on a case-by-case basis. The catalogue further supports hybrid analyses that combine observed quantities (e.g., X-ray profiles, lensing signals) with simulated properties (e.g., merger histories, dynamical states) for the same physical systems, enabling applications such as mass calibration studies, tests of hydrostatic equilibrium, and environmental quenching investigations. We validate the resulting catalogue through independent observables—including stacked thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich measurements and cross-matches with external X-ray catalogues—demonstrating that our inferred associations correspond to genuine massive structures. The catalogue and ancillary metrics will be released at
cosmictwin.org
.

2
Methods

In this section we describe how we construct and validate the sample of massive structures, referred to as halo associations. We begin from the
Manticore-Local
posterior suite of constrained
N
-body simulations, which provides a statistical ensemble of plausible realizations of the local Universe. From these simulations we identify stable associations—objects that recur across the posterior at consistent locations and masses—prioritizing robustness over completeness. We then introduce the external data products used for validation (
Planck
Compton-
y
and two complementary X-ray catalogues), and finally outline the aperture-stacking methodology used to measure the thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich (tSZ) signal at the association positions.

2.1
The
Manticore-Local
posterior suite

This work leverages the
Manticore-Local
posterior suite, a collection of constrained cosmological simulations of the nearby Universe presented in
McAlpine et al. 2025
.
Manticore-Local
employs field-level Bayesian inference to reconstruct the three-dimensional matter distribution of the local supervolume (
R\lesssim 300
Mpc) from galaxy observations in the
2M++
catalogue. The inference framework builds upon the
Bayesian Origin Reconstruction from Galaxies
(
BORG
) algorithm
(
Jasche & Wandelt 2013
)
and the
Manticore
model, incorporating refined galaxy-bias modelling, physics-informed priors, and a generalized Poisson likelihood, collectively ensuring statistical consistency with
\Lambda
CDM expectations while maintaining a high-fidelity match to the data.

The
Manticore-Local
suite comprises 80 independent posterior resimulations, each representing a statistically plausible realization of the local Universe consistent with both the observed galaxy distribution and the assumed cosmological model. These simulations are generated by sampling initial conditions from the inferred Bayesian posterior and evolving them with the
SWIFT

N
-body code
(
Schaller et al. 2024
)
in a periodic domain of side length
L=1000
Mpc. Only the inner
\sim 300
Mpc are directly constrained by observational data; the outer regions follow unconstrained
\Lambda
CDM priors. Each realization contains
1024^{3}
dark-matter particles with mass
3.7\times 10^{10}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
, such that the lowest-mass systems considered in this study (
M_{200}\sim 5\times 10^{13}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
) are resolved with
\sim 1{,}300
particles, while haloes of
10^{14}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
and above contain several thousand particles. The statistical fidelity of the
Manticore-Local
reconstruction—including consistency with
\Lambda
CDM power spectra, halo mass functions, and velocity fields—has been demonstrated in
McAlpine et al. 2025
.

Dark-matter haloes are identified in each posterior realization using the
HBT-HERONS
subhalo finder
(
Forouhar Moreno et al. 2025
)
, which traces bound particle histories to robustly identify haloes and substructure. Halo properties are computed with the
SOAP
analysis pipeline
(
McGibbon et al. 2025
)
, which measures spherical-overdensity masses
M_{200,\mathrm{crit}}
and
M_{500,\mathrm{crit}}
; in what follows we drop the “crit” subscript for brevity. We restrict the analysis to central haloes at
z=0
, defined as the most massive subhalo within each halo. This avoids double-counting substructure and ensures an unambiguous mass definition for association finding. All halo positions and velocities are expressed in an observer-centric equatorial system, with the fiducial observer placed at
(500,500,500)
Mpc within the box, enabling direct comparisons to observed local clusters (e.g. Virgo, Coma, Perseus).

2.2
Identification and robustness of halo associations

We seek objects that recur across the
Manticore-Local
posterior in consistent locations and masses. An
association
is defined as a set of
z=0
central haloes from different posterior realizations that correspond to the same underlying physical object. Associations are identified by clustering haloes in Eulerian space, with each posterior realization contributing at most one
member halo
to any given association (this term does not imply a satellite or subhalo; it simply denotes the single representative halo from that realization). For example, the Coma cluster association comprises the specific Coma-like halo identified in each of the 80 posterior realizations—though their precise positions and masses vary slightly due to posterior uncertainty, they cluster together in space and are recognized as counterparts to the same observed system.

The input sample comprises all
z=0
central haloes within
R<400
Mpc of the observer and with
M_{200}\geq 5\times 10^{13}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
from each of the 80 realizations, yielding
5.6\times 10^{5}
entries in total. Including haloes down to
5\times 10^{13}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
ensures robust grouping near our scientific threshold of
\langle M_{200}\rangle\geq 10^{14}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
.

Associations are identified with the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN;
Ester et al. 1996
) applied in three-dimensional comoving Cartesian coordinates. Fiducial parameters, chosen empirically to balance over–merging against fragmentation, are
\epsilon=1.75
Mpc and
N_{\mathrm{min}}=9
, broadly consistent with the spatial extents of
\sim 10^{14}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
haloes and effective in both isolated and crowded regions. Each realization may contribute at most one member to a given association; if DBSCAN initially links multiple haloes from the same realization, only the halo closest to the current association centroid is retained. We additionally remove mass outliers more than
0.3
dex from the association mean; this has negligible impact in practice.

Standard DBSCAN supplies core/border/noise labels but no membership probabilities, so we quantify robustness solely with the persistence

f_{\rm mem}\equiv\frac{N_{\rm members}}{N_{\rm real}},\qquad N_{\rm real}=80,

which measures the share of realizations contributing a member. Unless stated otherwise, analyses are reported for associations with
N_{\rm members}\geq 40
(
f_{\rm mem}\geq 0.5
), ensuring well–sampled posteriors for their properties.

The catalogue is robust to chance alignments and to modest variations in hyperparameters. Applying the identical pipeline to mock halo catalogues drawn from random regions of ten independent
\Lambda
CDM simulations (the same as those in
McAlpine et al. 2025
) yields no associations passing our cuts across 100 random trials with the fiducial parameters, indicating that spurious detections are almost impossible. Varying the parameters within reasonable ranges (e.g.
\epsilon=1.5
–
2.5
Mpc,
N_{\rm min}=7
–
11
) changes the total number of associations by
\sim 15
–
25\%
but leaves the key validation results qualitatively unchanged, while substantially looser thresholds (
\epsilon\gtrsim 3.75
Mpc,
N_{\rm min}\lesssim 5
) begin to spuriously group associations together.

For each association we compute posterior summaries of mass, positional scatter, and shape. As shown in
Fig.

1
, typical values for the positional scatter within associations,
\sigma_{R}
, are
\sim 2
–
3
Mpc, smaller than the 3.9 Mpc inference grid scale, and consistent with
McAlpine et al. 2025
for the fourteen prominent clusters investigated in that study. Associations with more members, for a given mean mass, are generally more compact, while the most massive systems exhibit larger
\sigma_{R}
due to lower asymmetry (
b/a
) ratios, reflecting elongation along filaments or redshift–space distortions. Examples in
Fig.

2
illustrate that the chosen DBSCAN parameters separate neighbouring associations cleanly while preserving coherence across realizations. Systems with fewer than 80 members reflect either larger positional scatter or occasional non–formation in the posterior, capturing genuine uncertainties encoded by the inference.

2.3
External data products

We use both Sunyaev–Zel’dovich and X-ray datasets as independent observational benchmarks. For the thermal SZ signal we adopt the
Planck
PR4 Compton-
y
map produced with the needlet internal linear combination (NILC) method
(
Chandran et al. 2023
)
. The PR4 processing offers improved noise and foreground mitigation relative to earlier releases. The map is delivered at
N_{\rm side}=2048
(
\sim 10^{\prime}
effective beam); we apply the official PR4 masks (Galactic, point source, and quality) to exclude contaminated regions. This dataset is used in stacking analyses (see
Sections

2.4
and
3.2
) to test that our associations correspond to real SZ signals on the sky.

For X-ray clusters we rely on two complementary catalogues. The MCXC-II meta-catalogue
(
Sadibekova et al. 2024
)
provides a homogenized compilation of ROSAT-detected clusters with masses standardized to
M_{500}
. The eROSITA eRASS1 catalogue
(
Bulbul et al. 2024
)
contains modern detections with masses calibrated against weak lensing
(
Ghirardini et al. 2024
)
, defining an updated X-ray mass scale. These catalogues serve two roles: (i) to confirm that our stacking pipeline recovers SZ signals at the sky locations of known clusters, and (ii) to provide an external reference for comparing our halo mass estimates (see
Section

3.4
). These products are used only for validation; they were not inputs to the
Manticore-Local
inference.

2.4
Compton-
y
aperture-stacking procedure

To test whether posterior associations correspond to genuine massive structures, we perform a tSZ stacking analysis using the
Planck
PR4 NILC Compton-
y
map. Each association is treated as a single halo centred on its posterior mean sky position. We measure the tSZ signal using compensated aperture photometry with an inner aperture of radius
1.2\,R_{500}
and a background annulus from
1.2\,R_{500}
to
6.0\,R_{500}
. The aperture contrast

\Delta y\equiv\langle y_{\rm inner}\rangle-\langle y_{\rm outer}\rangle

(1)

is our primary observable, where averages are taken over unmasked pixels. This compensated design suppresses large-scale offsets and foregrounds while isolating the local cluster signal. Associations with less than
80\%
unmasked coverage in either region are discarded. Unless stated, associations are equally weighted in stacks; weighting by
f_{\rm mem}
yields consistent results.

We apply only the official PR4 Galactic, point-source, and quality masks, and otherwise use the NILC map at its native
\sim 10^{\prime}
resolution, without additional filtering. Noisy large-scale modes are further mitigated by subtracting the median signal in a
5^{\circ}
–
7^{\circ}
annulus around each association prior to photometry.

Uncertainties are estimated via bootstrap resampling of associations with
N_{\rm boot}=500
realizations, which captures sample variance and propagates uncertainties to the stacked signal. As a null test, we repeat the entire pipeline on random sky positions matched in mask coverage, obtaining results consistent with zero. In addition to integrated aperture measurements, we compute stacked radial profiles by averaging the
y
signal in concentric annuli after background subtraction; these profiles provide a qualitative check that the signal is centrally concentrated as expected. Detection significance in our figures is quoted as

{\rm S/N}\equiv\frac{\langle\Delta y\rangle}{\sigma_{\rm boot}},

where
\langle\Delta y\rangle
is the mean aperture contrast of the stacked sample and
\sigma_{\rm boot}
is the bootstrap standard error.

3
Results

We now turn to the main scientific outcomes from the
Manticore-Local
posterior cluster catalogue.
We begin with a census of robust halo associations and a check for independent signatures in
Planck
tSZ maps.
We then examine how present-day constraints shape the inferred evolutionary pathways of these systems, and conclude with a comparison to external X-ray mass estimates.
Together, these analyses demonstrate both the reliability of the catalogue and the new types of cluster-level science enabled by field-level inference.

3.2
Independent validation through tSZ detection

In
McAlpine et al. 2025
, the accuracy of the
Manticore-Local
reconstruction was confirmed, in part, through detailed object-by-object comparisons of prominent local clusters. Here we perform a complementary, population-level validation by testing whether the ensemble of posterior halo associations coincides with hot gas structures in the real Universe. We use stacking measurements of the thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich (tSZ) signal in the
Planck
Compton-
y
map (see
Section

2.4
for methodological details). The tSZ signal provides an entirely independent check: it traces hot baryonic gas in the intracluster medium rather than the galaxy distribution used to constrain
Manticore-Local
. A coherent decrement in the stacked map thus constitutes strong evidence that our inferred halo associations correspond to genuine massive structures.

We compare against two established X-ray cluster catalogs as methodological benchmarks: the MCXC-II meta-catalogue
(
Sadibekova et al. 2024
)
and the eROSITA eRASS1 catalogue
(
Bulbul et al. 2024
)
. Both demonstrate that our stacking pipeline recovers expected tSZ signals from well-established samples. For the external catalogs and for
Manticore-Local
, we restrict to the mass range
1\times 10^{14}\leq M_{500}/M_{\odot}<4\times 10^{14}
and redshifts
z\leq 0.06
, matching the depth of the
Manticore-Local
sample. We require
\geq 80\%
aperture coverage in the
Planck

y
-map and restrict to Galactic latitudes
|b|\in[20^{\circ},80^{\circ}]
, excluding the Galactic plane (
|b|<20^{\circ}
) and the polar caps (
|b|>80^{\circ}
) to mitigate contamination and projection distortions. This cut prioritizes robustness over completeness, reducing sample sizes but ensuring reliable photometry. After these selections, 67 of the 225 posterior halo associations remain.

Having validated the
Manticore-Local
catalogue against independent hot-gas measurements (
Section

3.2
), we now examine what the inference reveals about the
histories
of the posterior associations. The central question is whether observational constraints on the galaxy distribution at
z=0
alone—without direct velocity measurements or high-redshift data—suffice to restrict the range of possible formation pathways that led to these present-day structures.

Figure

6
provides a qualitative view for nine of the richest associations. Member haloes are traced along their main progenitor branches back to
z=5
. The resulting tracks show that systems typically originate from well-defined regions of Lagrangian space, with coherent infall along preferred directions. Some evolve from compact, near-isotropic patches, others from elongated filaments, and others from more dispersed initial configurations. In some cases haloes migrate only a few Mpc before assembling, while in others they travel more than
10\,\mathrm{Mpc}
. This variety is consistent with the diversity of cluster formation pathways predicted in
\Lambda
CDM
(
Bond et al. 1996
;
Forero-Romero et al. 2014
;
Libeskind et al. 2018
, e.g.)
. Crucially, however, the posterior solutions do not explore this diversity randomly: they occupy restricted, anisotropic regions of initial configuration space. This localization indicates that the inference has genuinely constrained their formation histories, even though the input data used in the
Manticore-Local
inference were collected only at
z=0
.

To quantify this effect and isolate the impact of observational constraints, we compare each posterior association to "control" haloes drawn from unconstrained
\Lambda
CDM simulations with identical resolution and cosmology. For each constrained association, we select control haloes matched in two key properties: (i) final mass
M_{200}(z=0)
within
\pm 0.1
dex, and (ii) comoving travel distance between
z=5
and
z=0
within 20%. These criteria ensure that constrained and control haloes have comparable bulk properties—they are equally massive and have traversed similar distances during their evolution—while leaving the spatial distribution and morphology of their progenitors entirely unconstrained. To enable direct spatial comparison, we translate each control halo so that its
z=0
position coincides with the centroid of the corresponding constrained association. This construction isolates the effect of observational constraints: any differences in progenitor localization arise solely from the
z=0
galaxy data informing the
Manticore-Local
inference.

z=0
positions coincide, but the evolutionary tracks differ markedly. Constrained haloes exhibit coherent infall along preferred directions, with progenitors clustered in compact regions of Lagrangian space. In contrast, controls are scattered more isotropically across a much larger volume, despite having identical masses and travel distances. This difference directly demonstrates that present-day galaxy data restrict the allowed assembly pathways.

We quantify this localization using three complementary metrics applied to the spatial distribution of progenitors at
z=5
:

•

Convex hull volume ratio
(
\mathcal{R}_{V}
): the ratio of the volumes enclosed by the convex hulls of control and constrained progenitors. Larger values indicate that controls occupy a proportionally larger initial volume.

•

Dimensionless scatter ratio
(
\mathcal{R}_{s}
): the ratio of root-mean-square dispersions (normalized by the mean distance from centroid) between control and constrained progenitors. Values above unity indicate greater spatial dispersion in controls.

•

Information gain
(
I
): derived from the ratio of covariance determinants, this metric quantifies the reduction in configuration-space volume in units of bits. An information gain of
I
bits corresponds to a factor of
2^{I}
reduction in effective volume.

All three metrics are constructed such that larger values indicate stronger localization of constrained progenitors relative to their matched controls.

3.4
Comparison to external cluster mass estimates

We conclude the results section by demonstrating a key capability enabled by constrained catalogues: direct, object-by-object comparison of posterior masses to external measurements. Unlike traditional simulation-based studies that must rely on ensemble-averaged scaling relations to connect observations to theory, a digital twin of the local Universe allows us to compare individual observed clusters to their specific simulated counterparts. This enables systematic trends and outliers to be identified directly, without requiring statistical corrections for ensemble completeness or sample selection—each discrepancy can be traced to a specific physical system.

Here we focus on two independent X-ray–derived datasets: the MCXC-II compilation
(
Sadibekova et al. 2024
)
, an updated version of the
Piffaretti et al. 2011
meta-catalogue, and the first eROSITA all-sky cluster catalogue
(
Bulbul et al. 2024
)
. Cross-matching is performed with deliberately strict criteria—an angular separation
\leq 0.5^{\circ}
and redshift difference
\Delta z\leq 0.005
—retaining only the closest angular match when multiple candidates qualify. This prioritizes secure, high-quality identifications at the cost of excluding ambiguous matches.

These systematic differences directly reflect known biases in X-ray mass estimation methods, though the magnitude of the offset requires careful interpretation. The larger offsets with MCXC-II—with a median ratio of

(

. However, several compounding factors contribute to this discrepancy. First, MCXC-II masses are not direct hydrostatic measurements but are derived from

(
Piffaretti et al. 2011
;
Sadibekova et al. 2024

. This introduces additional systematic uncertainties beyond the hydrostatic bias itself, as the scaling relations may not accurately represent the full cluster population, particularly dynamically disturbed systems. Second, mock X-ray analyses of modern cosmological simulations demonstrate that the
combined
effect of hydrostatic assumptions, spectroscopic systematics, and scaling relation scatter can produce biases exceeding 30% for massive clusters
(
Barnes et al. 2021
;
Kay et al. 2024
)
, with the most disturbed systems showing even larger offsets. Third, cosmological analyses attempting to reconcile X-ray cluster counts with CMB and SZ measurements have inferred effective biases as large as

—corresponding to a factor of
\sim 1.7

(

;
Medezinski et al. 2018

. The broad, positively skewed distribution of mass ratios we observe (
Fig.

9
) suggests that multiple systematic effects contribute at varying levels across the cluster population, with the largest discrepancies occurring for the most massive and potentially most disturbed systems where all biases are amplified.

By contrast, eROSITA explicitly calibrates its mass proxy using weak-lensing measurements from DES, KiDS, and HSC
(
Ghirardini et al. 2024
)
, anchoring the X-ray mass scale directly to gravitational lensing. This brings eROSITA masses closer to the true gravitational mass—the same quantity that
Manticore-Local
measures through forward-modelled gravitational dynamics in the N-body simulations. Both methods therefore trace the total gravitational mass, whereas MCXC-II retains the hydrostatic bias. This explains the near-unity median ratio between
Manticore-Local
and eROSITA.

Importantly, a subset of systems remain strong outliers in both comparisons, particularly at the highest masses. In traditional ensemble analyses, such outliers might be dismissed as statistical scatter or attributed to sample variance. However, the object-by-object nature of this comparison changes the interpretation: each discrepancy now points to a specific physical system that requires explanation. These outliers may signal extreme dynamical states (e.g., ongoing major mergers), unresolved observational systematics affecting individual clusters, or fundamental limitations in the mass proxies for certain environments. Constrained catalogues thus provide a natural framework to systematically flag such systems for targeted follow-up, where detailed multi-wavelength observations can diagnose the origin of discrepancies. This capacity to identify and investigate problematic individual objects—rather than marginalizing over them in population-level fits—is a key advantage of posterior-based reconstructions for precision cluster cosmology.

4
Conclusions

We have presented a posterior catalogue of cluster-scale halo associations constructed from the
Manticore-Local
inference of the nearby Universe. This catalogue builds directly on the validation framework of
McAlpine et al. 2025
, extending it from statistical characterizations of the reconstructed density field to a catalogue of individually identified systems. It links present-day observational constraints to the inferred positions, masses, and evolutionary histories of massive structures, providing the community with a validated set of digital analogues to real clusters in the local Universe. The full catalogue, including posterior distributions of key properties for all identified systems, will be made publicly available at
cosmictwin.org
.

The principal outcomes of this study are:

•

Identification of posterior associations:

We have defined and catalogued “halo associations” as coherent structures that remain clustered across the posterior ensemble, representing real physical systems inferred from present-day galaxy observations. A total of 225 associations with mean
\langle M_{200}\rangle>10^{14}\,M_{\odot}
are identified, each with posterior distributions of position, mass, and velocity. This establishes a bridge between field-level inference and traditional cluster catalogues, delivering a physically grounded, uncertainty-aware sample of massive structures.

•

Validation via independent observables:

Stacked Planck PR4 NILC
y
-map measurements yield statistically significant thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich detections at the
Manticore-Local
association positions. The radial profiles follow the expected
y
–
M
scaling relation, with a factor-of-two suppression that can be attributed to
\sim
Mpc-level positional scatter. This confirms that the associations correspond to real massive clusters in the Universe, demonstrating that our digital reconstructions accurately represent physical structures rather than statistical artifacts.

•

Constraints on evolutionary pathways:

By tracing halo merger trees back to
z=5
, we find that association progenitors occupy compact and anisotropic regions of Lagrangian space, exhibiting coherent infall along preferred directions. Comparisons with mass- and displacement-matched control haloes from unconstrained simulations show that the inference reduces allowed configuration-space volumes by typical factors of
2
–
5
, demonstrating that present-day galaxy data alone—even without direct velocity measurements or high-redshift observations—constrain not only final cluster properties but also aspects of their formation histories.

These findings naturally connect to the recent analysis of
Stiskalek et al. 2024
on the Lagrangian overlap of constrained simulations, which quantified—at the population level—how observational constraints restrict the accessible regions of initial condition space. While that work established the effect in terms of global overlap statistics, the present study demonstrates the same principle at the level of individual objects, showing directly that the evolutionary pathways of specific cluster associations are localized and non-random. Together, these complementary approaches reinforce the conclusion that field-level inference encodes genuine information about cosmic history: constrained reconstructions do not merely reproduce present-day structures, but systematically narrow the range of possible past configurations that could have led to them.

•

External mass scale comparisons:

One-to-one cross-matches with X-ray catalogues recover known systematic offsets. Relative to MCXC-II,
Manticore-Local
masses are typically higher by factors of
\sim 2
, consistent with the compounding effects of hydrostatic bias and scaling relation systematics in X-ray mass proxies. By contrast, agreement with the weak-lensing-calibrated eROSITA mass scale is close to unity. This demonstrates the ability of constrained catalogues to reveal systematics in existing mass proxies on an object-by-object basis, identifying individual outliers that warrant targeted follow-up rather than being marginalized over in population-level analyses.

The
Manticore-Local
cluster catalogue thus provides a robust and observationally validated representation of massive structures in the nearby Universe. The catalogue’s key strength lies in providing digital counterparts to real observed clusters, each with validated positions, masses, and formation histories. Because it is rooted in the posterior ensemble, it encodes observational uncertainties in a transparent way, allowing the community to propagate them rigorously into downstream analyses.

An important capability enabled by this framework is direct, object-by-object comparisons between simulated and observed clusters. Unlike traditional population-level simulation studies that must rely on ensemble-averaged scaling relations, constrained catalogues allow mismatches to be localized to individual systems, where astrophysical or methodological origins can be probed in detail. This shifts the paradigm from identifying general trends to diagnosing specific discrepancies: outliers can no longer be dismissed as statistical scatter, but must be explained through targeted investigation of the physical systems themselves. With the addition of hydrodynamical follow-up simulations on the posterior initial conditions, this capability will extend to direct comparisons of X-ray emission, SZ profiles, and galaxy properties, further enhancing the diagnostic power of constrained reconstructions.

The catalogue also enables hybrid analyses that combine observed and simulated quantities in complementary ways. As demonstrated in our tSZ validation, one can use observed cluster positions for stacking analyses while drawing masses, velocities, and formation histories from the simulation. This approach can be extended to probes such as the kinetic SZ and moving-lens effects, which require accurate velocity information that is inaccessible through direct observation but naturally available in constrained simulations.

The present catalogue forms part of a growing
Manticore
product suite. Alongside the local void catalogue introduced in
Malandrino et al. 2025
, it delivers complementary perspectives on the most underdense and overdense environments of the nearby Universe. Forthcoming expansions, particularly
Manticore-Deep
, will extend the framework to constrained volumes approximately two orders of magnitude larger than
Manticore-Local
, providing statistically powerful cluster samples while maintaining high resolution. This will enable population-level studies of cluster properties, environmental dependencies, and rare massive systems that are underrepresented in the local volume.

Looking ahead, this programme represents a step toward comprehensive digital twins of the local Universe, where galaxies, clusters, and voids are reconstructed within a physically self-consistent posterior framework. Such digital twins will enable the community to test models of galaxy and cluster formation with unprecedented realism, perform joint analyses that fully integrate observational and simulated data, and develop new diagnostic tools that exploit the unique combination of observational anchoring and forward-modeled physics. The
Manticore-Local
cluster catalogue, validated through independent observables and released as a community resource, provides a foundation for this next generation of cosmological analysis.
