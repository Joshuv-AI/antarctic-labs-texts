SurvBench: A Standardised Preprocessing Pipeline for Multi-Modal Electronic Health Record Survival Analysis
Munib Mesinovic, Tingting Zhu

Abstract
Electronic health record (EHR) data present tremendous opportunities for advancing survival analysis through deep learning, yet reproducibility remains severely constrained by inconsistent preprocessing methodologies. We present SurvBench, a comprehensive, open-source preprocessing pipeline that transforms raw PhysioNet datasets into standardised, model-ready tensors for multi-modal survival analysis. SurvBench provides data loaders for three major critical care databases, MIMIC-IV, eICU, and MC-MED, supporting diverse modalities including time-series vitals, static demographics, ICD diagnosis codes, and radiology reports. The pipeline implements rigorous data quality controls, patient-level splitting to prevent data leakage, explicit missingness tracking, and standardised temporal aggregation. SurvBench handles both single-risk (e.g., in-hospital mortality) and competing-risks scenarios (e.g., multiple discharge outcomes). The outputs are compatible with pycox library packages and implementations of standard statistical and deep learning models. By providing reproducible, configuration-driven preprocessing with comprehensive documentation, SurvBench addresses the “preprocessing gap” that has hindered fair comparison of deep learning survival models, enabling researchers to focus on methodological innovation rather than data engineering.



1
Background & Summary

Survival analysis, the statistical framework for modelling time-to-event data with censoring, has become increasingly important in healthcare applications ranging from mortality prediction to treatment response modelling
(
Cox 1972
;
Kaplan and Meier 1958
)
. The advent of deep learning has introduced powerful non-linear methods capable of learning complex temporal patterns from electronic health records
(
Kvamme et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2018
;
Katzman et al. 2018
;
Mesinovic et al. 2024
;
Mesinovic et al. 2025
)
. However, progress in this field has been substantially impeded by a critical “preprocessing gap”: the absence of standardised, reproducible pipelines for transforming raw EHR data into formats suitable for deep learning models in survival modelling.

Existing benchmarks for clinical prediction tasks, such as those proposed by
Harutyunyan et al. 2019
for MIMIC-III, have demonstrated the value of standardised preprocessing in enabling fair model comparison. However, these efforts have focused primarily on classification and regression tasks, with limited support for survival analysis. Furthermore, many preprocessing pipelines rely on intermediate CSV files that are not publicly available or require manual data transformation steps that introduce variability across implementations. This lack of standardisation makes it difficult to determine whether performance differences between methods stem from genuine algorithmic innovations or from subtle variations in data preprocessing decisions.

1.1
The Preprocessing Challenge in EHR Survival Analysis

Electronic health records present unique challenges for survival analysis. These complexities require numerous preprocessing decisions that profoundly impact the performance of the downstream model
(
Rajkomar et al. 2018
;
Shickel et al. 2018
)
. Critical choices include temporal aggregation strategies such as hourly binning versus fixed-width windows, each offering distinct trade-offs between temporal resolution and computational tractability
(
Che et al. 2018
;
Lipton et al. 2016
)
. Missingness handling approaches range from simple imputation to explicit encoding through binary masks that preserve information about data availability
(
Wells et al. 2013
;
Luo et al. 2016
)
. Feature selection criteria must balance clinical relevance against statistical considerations such as prevalence thresholds that determine which sparsely measured variables warrant inclusion
(
García et al. 2016
;
Johnson et al. 2016a
)
. Normalisation and scaling methods require careful consideration to ensure that features span comparable ranges while preserving meaningful variation
(
Johnson et al. 2016b
;
Harutyunyan et al. 2019
)
. The outcome definition and the censoring rules must align with clinical endpoints while accommodating administrative censoring and competing risks
(
Putter et al. 2007
;
Andersen and Keiding 2012
)
. Finally, train-validation-test splitting protocols must enforce patient-level separation to prevent data leakage from repeated hospital encounters by the same individuals
(
Wiens et al. 2016
;
Nestor et al. 2019
)
. Without standardised implementations of these decisions, researchers are forced to either re-implement preprocessing pipelines from scratch or rely on undocumented code, both of which introduce substantial variability and hinder reproducibility.

The rapid proliferation of deep learning methods for survival analysis has created an urgent need for a standardised preprocessing and benchmarking infrastructure. As illustrated in Figure

(
Ren et al. 2019

, competing risk frameworks handling multiple mutually exclusive outcomes (DeepCompete)
(
Aastha et al. 2021

, and conditional variational autoencoders for dynamic risk prediction (DySurv)

. The peak of 17 publications in 2022 reflects the rapid maturation of the field and the growing recognition of survival analysis as a critical application domain for deep learning in healthcare
(
Wiegrebe et al. 2024
)
.

However, this methodological diversity has paradoxically hindered rather than facilitated progress. Each publication typically introduces custom preprocessing pipelines tailored to specific datasets and prediction tasks, with limited documentation of critical implementation details, such as temporal aggregation strategies, feature selection criteria, and censoring protocols
(
Harutyunyan et al. 2019

Nestor et al. 2019

. Consequently, the performance differences reported between methods may reflect preprocessing variations rather than genuine algorithmic improvements, making it difficult to identify which innovations truly advance the state-of-the-art

(

Furthermore, the absence of standardised benchmarks specific to survival analysis with electronic health records means that many studies evaluate methods on small-scale datasets or inappropriate tasks originally designed for classification rather than time-to-event modelling. This hinders fair comparison across methods and limits the ability of the community to assess generalisability across different clinical settings, patient populations, and outcome definitions
(
Rajkomar et al. 2018
;
Sendak et al. 2020
)
. The emergence of diverse methodological approaches thus underscores the critical need for a transparent, reproducible preprocessing framework that enables rigorous evaluation while accommodating the unique challenges of survival analysis with censored, multi-modal EHR data.

)
.

1.4
SurvBench: Standardised Preprocessing for Multi-Modal Survival Analysis

We present SurvBench, a Python-based preprocessing pipeline specifically designed to bridge the gap between raw EHR data and the structured tensors required by deep learning survival models. SurvBench makes several key contributions:

(1)
Direct Raw Data Processing
– Unlike prior benchmarks that rely on pre-processed intermediate files, SurvBench operates directly on raw CSV files from PhysioNet, ensuring complete transparency and reproducibility. This “raw-to-tensor” approach eliminates dependencies on unavailable intermediate data products.

(2)
Multi-Dataset Support
– SurvBench provides data loaders for three major critical care databases: MIMIC-IV v3.2
(
Johnson et al. 2023
)
, eICU v2.0
(
Pollard et al. 2018
)
, and MC-MED (a private emergency department dataset). This multi-dataset capability enables cross-database validation studies and generalisability assessments.

(3)
Multi-Modal Integration
– The pipeline seamlessly integrates heterogeneous data modalities, including high-frequency vital signs, laboratory measurements, static demographics, ICD diagnosis codes, and radiology report embeddings. Each modality is processed according to domain-appropriate strategies while maintaining temporal alignment.

(4)
Survival-Specific Features
– SurvBench natively supports both single-risk survival analysis (e.g., time to in-hospital mortality) and competing-risk frameworks (e.g., discharge to home vs. ICU admission vs. death in the emergency department). The pipeline implements standard survival preprocessing operations, including time discretisation, censoring management, and horizon truncation.

(5)
Explicit Missingness Tracking
– Rather than solely imputing missing values, SurvBench generates explicit missingness masks that record which values were observed versus imputed. This enables models to learn to distinguish between true zeros and missing data that were imputed to zero, which is particularly important for laboratory results and vital signs.

(6)
Configuration-Driven Design
– All preprocessing decisions, from temporal windows to feature selection thresholds, are controlled via human-readable YAML configuration files. This design enables researchers to systematically explore the impact of different preprocessing strategies while maintaining reproducibility.

(7)
Patient-Level Splitting
– The pipeline enforces patient-level data splitting, ensuring that all stays in the ICU or admissions of the same patient are assigned to the same split (training, validation, or test). This prevents data leakage and ensures a realistic evaluation of the generalisability of the model.

2
Methods

We developed
SurvBench
, an end-to-end and configurable preprocessing pipeline designed to build standardised, reproducible benchmarks for deep learning-based survival analysis from raw electronic health records. The complete workflow, illustrated in Figure
2
, forms the core of our methodology. The pipeline ingests data from diverse, large-scale public databases (eICU,
MIMIC-IV, and MC-MED), processes multiple data modalities (static, time-series, ICD codes, and radiology), and constructs model-ready data tensors with corresponding missingness masks. Crucially, it enforces strict patient-level data splitting to prevent data leakage. The resulting outputs are designed to directly support the training of deep learning models for both single-risk and competing-risks survival analysis. The following subsections detail each component of this pipeline.

Figure 2
:
Overview of the
SurvBench
preprocessing and modelling pipeline.
Multimodal source data, including static features (e.g., demographics, comorbidities via ICD codes), time-series (e.g., vital signs, lab results), and radiology (e.g., free-text reports), are extracted from the eICU, MIMIC-IV, and MC-MED databases. The pipeline generates labels for both single-risk (e.g., mortality) and competing-risks (e.g., ED disposition) survival tasks. Processed data for each patient is structured into aligned tensors with corresponding binary missingness masks. These tensors serve as inputs to deep learning models, such as a multi-head architecture designed to estimate cause-specific cumulative incidence functions for competing risks.

2.1
Data Sources

SurvBench supports three major electronic health record databases from critical care and emergency medicine:

2.1.1
MIMIC-IV v3.1

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) is a comprehensive, deidentified database containing data from patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Centre between 2008 and 2019
(
Johnson et al. 2023
)
. MIMIC-IV adopts a modular architecture that separates hospital-level data (
hosp
) from ICU-level data (
icu
), representing a substantial improvement over its predecessor MIMIC-III in terms of data quality, temporal coverage, and organisational structure
(
Johnson et al. 2016b
)
. SurvBench utilises the following core files from MIMIC-IV’s modular architecture. The
icu/icustays.csv
file provides ICU admission records that link patients to specific ICU stays, serving as the temporal anchor for all time-series data. Patient demographics, including age and gender, are extracted from
hosp/patients.csv
, while
hosp/admissions.csv
provides hospital admission details and discharge outcomes essential for survival label generation
(
Purushotham et al. 2018
)
. Time-series vital signs and physiological measurements are captured in the high-volume
icu/chartevents.csv
file, complemented by laboratory test results from
hosp/labevents.csv

(
Johnson et al. 2016a
)
. For multi-modal analyses, ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes are available in
hosp/diagnoses_icd.csv
, enabling incorporation of diagnostic histories and comorbidity patterns
(
Rasmy et al. 2021
;
Choi et al. 2016
)
. Radiology report text can optionally be incorporated from
hosp/radiology.csv
for natural language processing applications
(
Huang et al. 2019
;
Alsentzer et al. 2019
)
. Access to MIMIC-IV requires completion of human subjects research training and acceptance of a data use agreement through PhysioNet
(
Goldberger et al. 2000
)
.

2.1.2
eICU Collaborative Research Database v2.0

The eICU Collaborative Research Database contains data from over 200,000 admissions to the ICU across 335 hospitals in the United States, collected between 2014 and 2015 through the Philips eICU telehealth programme
(
Pollard et al. 2018
)
. Unlike MIMIC-IV, which represents a single academic medical centre, eICU provides multi-centre data that allow the evaluation of model generalisability across different clinical settings, hospital types, and geographic regions
(
Sheikhalishahi et al. 2020
;
Futoma et al. 2017
)
. The structure of the eICU database differs from MIMIC-IV by providing pre-integrated patient records in the central
patient.csv
file, which contains core cohort information including demographics, admission details, and outcomes. Time-series data are distributed across specialised files:
vitalPeriodic.csv
captures high-frequency vital sign measurements recorded at regular intervals (typically every 5 minutes),
vitalAperiodic.csv
contains lower-frequency vital sign measurements taken at irregular intervals based on clinical need, and
lab.csv
provides laboratory test results spanning hundreds of distinct assays. In particular, SurvBench deliberately excludes the
nurseCharting.csv
file despite its clinical richness. This exclusion reflects pragmatic considerations regarding the substantial size of the file (exceeding 100 GB uncompressed) and the predominantly unstructured format, which exceed the scope of structured time-series preprocessing while significantly increasing memory requirements during processing
(
Pollard et al. 2018
)
.

2.1.3
MC-MED v1.0.1

MC-MED is a publicly available, multi-modal, and de-identified dataset from the Stanford Health Care Emergency Department (ED)
(
Kansal et al. 2025a
)
. The dataset, detailed in Nature Scientific Data and available on PhysioNet, includes 118,385 adult ED visits from 2020 to 2022. It is designed to support research on emergency care, patient trajectories, and physiological monitoring
(
Kansal et al. 2025b
)
. The dataset provides comprehensive, high-resolution data through several core files. The
visits.csv
file serves as the master record, containing visit identifiers, patient demographics, triage information (including chief complaint and acuity), and ED disposition outcomes (e.g., Discharge, Inpatient, Observation, ICU). Time-series data include continuously monitored vital signs at minute-level resolution in
numerics.csv
and laboratory results in
labs.csv
. In addition, the dataset contains high-frequency physiological waveforms (ECG, PPG, and respiration) stored in WFDB format. The historical context is provided by
pmh.csv
(past medical history with ICD codes) and
meds.csv
(home medications). Free-text radiology reports are also available in
rads.csv

(
Kansal et al. 2025a
)
.

2.2
Cohort Definition and Filtering

For each dataset, SurvBench applies configurable cohort selection criteria to ensure data quality and clinical relevance. These filters are specified in dataset configuration files and can be adjusted by researchers to accommodate alternative inclusion criteria or disease-specific populations.

The MIMIC-IV cohort is defined through linkage of ICU stays to hospital admissions and patient demographics, following established critical care cohort definition practices. Age filtering excludes patients younger than 18 years to focus on adult critical care, with ages recorded as “greater than 89” converted to 90 for HIPAA compliance reasons related to deidentification requirements for patients of advanced age
(
Johnson et al. 2023
;
Malin and El Emam 2013
)
. A minimum duration of stay threshold (default: 24 hours) filters very short stays in the ICU that may represent transfers or administrative artefacts rather than substantive critical care episodes requiring mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, or other intensive interventions
(
Verburg et al. 2014
;
Rosenberg et al. 2001
)
. Stays lacking valid admission timestamps or discharge outcomes are removed to ensure label completeness and prevent training instability from undefined targets. The pipeline uses
subject_id
as the unique patient identifier to enforce patient-level data splitting, while
stay_id
serves as the primary index for data tensors, maintaining consistency with the MIMIC-IV database conventions
(
Johnson et al. 2023
)
.

For eICU, the cohort definition proceeds directly from the
patient.csv
file, which aggregates patient-level information across the multi-centre database
(
Pollard et al. 2018
)
. Age filtering removes patients with non-numeric age values or age below 18 years, including entries recorded as “greater than 89” in accordance with eICU’s deidentification protocols. The minimum stay duration filter applies to
unitdischargeoffset
, excluding unit stays shorter than the configured threshold (default: 24 hours) to ensure adequate observation periods for a meaningful survival prediction
(
Goldhill and Sumner 1999
;
Desautels et al. 2016
)
. Stays without valid
hospitaldischargestatus
values are excluded to ensure outcome availability, as discharge status represents the primary survival endpoint in critical care research
(
Kramer and Zimmerman 2006
;
Fuchs et al. 2012
)
. The pipeline uses
patienthealthsystemstayid
for patient-level splitting while
patientunitstayid
serves as the primary data index, reflecting the hierarchical data structure of the eICU where individual patients may have multiple ICU stays across different hospitals within the health system
(
Pollard et al. 2018
)
.

MC-MED cohort selection focuses on emergency department presentations that meet quality criteria for competing-risk survival analysis
(
Wolbers et al. 2009
;
Varadhan et al. 2010
)
. Age filtering excludes paediatric patients (younger than 18 years), as paediatric emergency medicine involves distinct pathophysiology, treatment protocols, and outcome patterns compared to adult emergency care
(
Cushing et al. 2013
;
Martin et al. 2015
)
. Minimum duration of stay thresholds remove extremely brief visits (e.g., less than 30 minutes) that may represent only triage encounters, patient departures without medical screening, or administrative registrations without clinical evaluation
(
Welch et al. 2011
;
Rowe et al. 2011
)
. The completeness of the outcome requires valid disposition codes from the set of competing risks that encompass discharge to home, admission to the ward, admission to the ICU, and death. This ensures that all included visits have well-defined endpoint information suitable for competing-risks survival analysis, where each patient must experience exactly one of the mutually exclusive outcomes or be censored
(
Putter et al. 2007
;
Pintilie 2006
)
.

2.3
Data Processing Pipeline

The preprocessing pipeline consists of nine sequential stages, each implemented as a modular component. This architecture enables researchers to modify individual stages while maintaining compatibility with the rest of the pipeline.

2.3.1
Stage 1: Cohort Loading

The pipeline first loads and filters raw data to create the base cohort according to the criteria defined in Section 2.2. For eICU, we implemented a memory-efficient pre-aggregation strategy to handle the large
lab.csv
,
vitalPeriodic.csv
, and
vitalAperiodic.csv
files. Rather than loading entire files into memory and then filtering, the pipeline:

1.

Identifies cohort patient identifiers from
patient.csv

2.

Sequentially loads each large file in chunks

3.

Filters to cohort patients and relevant time windows

4.

Aggregates to hourly bins using groupby operations

5.

Releases memory before loading the next file

6.

Merges the pre-aggregated hourly data

This approach prevents out-of-memory errors (exit code 137) that occurred in naive implementations that attempted to load and merge gigabyte-scale files simultaneously.

2.3.2
Stage 2: Patient-Level Data Splitting

Data splitting occurs at the patient level rather than the stay/visit level to prevent data leakage. If multiple stays in the ICU or hospital admissions belong to the same patient, all associated records are assigned to the same split. The default split ratios are 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test, but these can be configured.

The splitting procedure:

1.

Extracts unique patient identifiers (
subject_id
)

2.

Randomly partitions patients into three sets using scikit-learn’s
train_test_split
with a fixed random seed for reproducibility

3.

Maps all stays/visits for each patient to their assigned split

4.

Logs the number of unique patients and total stays in each split

This patient-level approach is critical for survival analysis, as it ensures that the test set represents truly unseen patients rather than new stays from previously encountered individuals.

2.3.3
Stage 3: Survival Label Processing

SurvBench implements comprehensive survival label processing that supports both single-risk and competing-risk scenarios
(
Cox 1972
;
Fine and Gray 1999
)
. The
SurvivalLabelsProcessor
class handles time discretisation, censoring, and label transformation using established survival analysis methodologies
(
Mesinovic et al. 2024
)
.

Label Extraction

For each cohort, survival labels consist of two essential components: duration (
T
) representing the time from admission to the event or censoring measured in hours, and event (
\delta
) indicating the status of the result. For single-risk scenarios, the event indicator is binary with
\delta\in\{0,1\}
where 1 denotes the event occurrence and 0 indicates censoring, following the standard survival analysis framework
(
Cox 1972
;
Kaplan and Meier 1958
)
. For competing-risk frameworks, the event variable adopts integer encoding
\delta\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,K\}
where 0 indicates censorship and
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
denotes the occurrence of the
k
-th competing event, enabling cause-specific hazard modelling
(
Mesinovic et al. 2024
)
.

In
SurvBench
, censoring (
\delta=0
) is applied in two cases: (1) if a patient’s observation period ends without any recorded terminal event (i.e., standard right-censoring, such as being discharged alive); or (2) if a known event occurs
after
a pre-defined maximum prediction horizon,
T_{horizon}
, specified in the pipeline configuration (e.g., 240 hours for MIMIC-IV). In this second case, a form of administrative censoring, the event is set to
\delta=0
and the duration is capped at
T=T_{horizon}
(
preprocessing/labels.py
,
data/eicu_loader.py
). This ensures a standardised prediction window across all individuals, a pragmatic requirement for developing and evaluating dynamic machine learning models.

Dataset-specific label extraction follows standardised procedures adapted to each database’s structure and outcome definitions. For MIMIC-IV, the duration equals the length of stay in the ICU measured in hours, while the event indicator derives from the hospital discharge status with “expired” mapped to 1 and alternative outcomes (home, skilled nursing facility, rehabilitation) to 0
(
Johnson et al. 2023
;
Vincent et al. 1996
)
. The eICU database provides duration through
unitdischargeoffset
(converted from minutes to hours by division by 60), with event status determined from
hospitaldischargestatus
where “Expired” equals 1 and “Alive” equals 0
(
Pollard et al. 2018
;
Zimmerman et al. 2006
)
. MC-MED implements competing risks that are encoded directly from the outcome variable, representing discharge home (1), admission to the ward (2), admission to the ICU (3), and death (4), plus censoring (0), allowing the evaluation of models in the competing risk paradigm where multiple types of events preclude one another.

Horizon Truncation and Censoring

A maximum prediction horizon
H
(e.g., 240 hours = 10 days) is imposed to focus on short-to-medium-term outcomes. Events occurring after this horizon are censored:

T^{\prime}=\min(T,H),\quad\delta^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\delta&\text{if }T\leq H\\
0&\text{if }T>H\end{cases}

(1)

This truncation serves two purposes: (1) it removes extremely long-stay outliers that may be clinically distinct, and (2) it ensures that predictions focus on actionable time horizons relevant to clinical decision-making.

Time Discretisation

For discrete-time survival models, continuous event times are discretised into
B
bins using quantile-based binning. The
pycox.preprocessing.label_transforms.LabTransDiscreteTime
class
(
Kvamme et al. 2019
)
implements this discretisation:

1.

Compute quantiles of observed event times in the training set to define bin boundaries:
\{b_{0}=0,b_{1},\ldots,b_{B}\}
where
b_{B}=H
.

2.

Assign each event time to its corresponding bin:
t_{i}\in[b_{j-1},b_{j})\rightarrow
bin
j
.

3.

Transform labels into a suitable format for discrete-time hazard models.

The bin boundaries (
cuts
) are saved and applied consistently to the validation and test sets.

Label Statistics

For each data split, the pipeline computes and logs comprehensive label statistics, such as overall event and censoring rates, mean, and median durations (both overall and stratified by event occurrence), and, for competing risks, event-specific prevalence and mean times. These statistics enable researchers to assess the class imbalance and outcome distributions.

2.3.4
Stage 4: Static Feature Processing

Static (time-invariant) features capture patient demographics and admission characteristics that remain constant throughout each hospital encounter. These features are extracted once per patient stay and subsequently broadcast across all temporal windows to enable their incorporation into recurrent neural network architectures and temporal convolutional models
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
;
Bai et al. 2018
)
. Feature extraction typically includes age as a continuous variable along with categorical variables such as gender, ethnicity, and admission type or unit stay type, all subjected to one-hot encoding that expands each categorical variable into binary indicator columns
(
Potdar et al. 2017
)
. For example, an ethnicity variable with four categories (Caucasian, African American, Hispanic, Asian) generates four binary features in the processed dataset, enabling models to learn ethnicity-specific patterns without imposing ordinal relationships
(
Obermeyer et al. 2019
;
Rajkomar et al. 2018
)
.

Feature filtering addresses rare categorical values that appear infrequently across the cohort and may lead to overfitting or numerical instability
(
Batista et al. 2004
;
Japkowicz and Stephen 2002
)
. Categories present in fewer than a configurable percentage of training patients (default: 1%) may optionally be merged into an “Other” category, preventing sparse feature representations that could impair model training through high-dimensional but uninformative feature spaces
(
Guyon and Elisseeff 2003
;
Saeys et al. 2007
)
. This threshold applies only to training data to prevent information leakage, with the resulting category definitions applied consistently to validation and test sets, maintaining the separation between training-time feature selection and test-time evaluation
(
Ambroise and McLachlan 2002
;
Reunanen 2003
)
.

2.3.5
Stage 5: Time-Series Processing and Aggregation

Time-series features, including vital signs and laboratory results, require careful temporal processing. SurvBench implements a windowed aggregation strategy to convert irregularly sampled measurements into fixed-width temporal windows.

Temporal Extraction Window

The pipeline extracts time-series data for the first
T_{\text{max}}
hours of each stay (default: 24 hours). This extraction window is divided into
W
non-overlapping windows of size
w
hours each, such that
W\times w=T_{\text{max}}
. For example, with
T_{\text{max}}=24
and
w=4
, we obtain
W=6
windows of 4 hours each. Within each window, all measurements are aggregated using the mean. This aggregation serves multiple purposes:

1.

Reduces dimensionality from potentially thousands of irregularly sampled points to a manageable fixed-length sequence

2.

Smooths measurement noise

3.

Creates a consistent temporal structure across patients despite varying sampling frequencies

Memory-Efficient Loading for eICU

As described in Stage 1, eICU’s large time-series files (
vitalPeriodic
,
vitalAperiodic
,
lab
) require special handling. The pipeline:

1.

Loads
vitalPeriodic.csv
, filters to cohort and time range, bins measurements to hourly intervals using
observationoffset // 60
, and aggregates each hour using
groupby().mean()

2.

Repeat this process for
vitalAperiodic.csv

3.

For
lab.csv
, the lab names are pivoted to columns (
pivot_table
), then the bins and aggregates by hour

4.

Merges the three hourly dataframes using outer joins

5.

Finally, re-aggregates from hourly to 4-hour windows (or other configured window size)

This two-stage aggregation (raw
\to
hourly
\to
windows) keeps memory usage manageable while preserving temporal patterns.

Feature Filtering by Missingness

Time-series features with excessive missingness are excluded. A feature is retained only if it is present (non-NaN) in at least a configurable percentage (default: 1%) of training set time windows. This threshold is computed on the training data only to prevent data leakage, then applied to all splits. For example, if a laboratory test is measured in fewer than 1% of the training windows, it is deemed too sparse to be informative and removed from all datasets.

Temporal Alignment and Tensor Construction

After aggregation and filtering, time-series data are structured with an index composed of the
patient_id
and
time_window
. The columns represent the dynamic feature names (e.g., heart_rate”, glucose”), and the values contain the aggregated measurements, or NaN if no data was present in that window.

2.3.6
Stage 6: Multi-Modal Integration

After processing static and time-series modalities separately, they are concatenated along the feature dimension to form unified tensors.

Let
\mathbf{X}^{\text{static}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F_{s}}
denote the static features for
N
patients with
F_{s}
static features, and
\mathbf{X}^{\text{dynamic}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times W\times F_{d}}
denote the time-series features with
W
windows and
F_{d}
dynamic features. Static features are broadcast across all time windows:

\mathbf{X}^{\text{static}}_{\text{broadcast}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times W\times F_{s}}

(2)

where each time window receives an identical copy of the static feature vector. The final multi-modal tensor is:

\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{X}^{\text{dynamic}}\;|\;\mathbf{X}^{\text{static}}_{\text{broadcast}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times W\times(F_{d}+F_{s})}

(3)

The pipeline stores metadata (
feature_names.pkl
) mapping feature indices to names and modalities, enabling models to identify dynamic versus static feature boundaries.

Optional Modalities: ICD Codes and Radiology

For MIMIC-IV and MC-MED, additional modalities can be integrated. Diagnosis histories, represented as ICD diagnosis codes, are vectorised using multi-hot encoding (binary indicators for presence/absence of each code). Given their high dimensionality, these are stored as separate tensors (
x_train_icd.npy
) rather than being concatenated with time-series features. Similarly, free-text radiology reports are processed using pre-trained clinical language models, such as BioClinicalBERT, to generate fixed-length embeddings. These are also stored separately (
x_train_rad.npy
) to accommodate their distinct dimensionality and processing requirements.

2.3.7
Stage 7: Missingness Mask Creation

Before imputing missing values, SurvBench creates explicit missingness masks recording which values were observed versus missing. The mask
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times W\times F}
has the same shape as the data tensor, with:

M_{ijf}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }X_{ijf}\text{ was observed (not NaN)}\\
0&\text{if }X_{ijf}\text{ was missing (NaN)}\end{cases}

(4)

This mask enables models to distinguish between true zeros (e.g., a heart rate of 0, which would be clinically alarming) and imputed zeros. Many deep learning survival models can incorporate these masks as additional input to improve performance
(
Che et al. 2018
)
.

2.3.8
Stage 8: Scaling and Normalisation

Feature scaling is applied to ensure that all features have comparable magnitudes, which aids neural network training. SurvBench uses scikit-learn’s
StandardScaler
, which applies
z
-score normalisation:

X^{\prime}_{ijf}=\frac{X_{ijf}-\mu_{f}}{\sigma_{f}}

(5)

where
\mu_{f}
and
\sigma_{f}
are the mean and standard deviation of feature
f
computed over all observed values in the training set. Critical implementation details include:

1.

The scaler is fit only on training data to prevent data leakage.

2.

Scaling is computed over observed values only (using the missingness mask to exclude NaNs).

3.

The same scaler is applied to the validation and test sets.

4.

The fitted scaler is saved (
scaler.pkl
) to enable consistent preprocessing of new data.

2.3.9
Stage 9: Imputation

After scaling, the remaining NaN values are imputed. The imputation strategy differs between static and dynamic features. For static features, mean imputation using the means of the training set. Since static features are typically demographics with few missing values, mean imputation is reasonable. And for dynamic time-series features, zero imputation after scaling. This approach treats missing measurements as “without information” relative to the population mean. Crucially, the missingness mask preserves information about the values that were imputed, enabling models to learn the appropriate handling strategies. Alternative imputation strategies (such as forward-fill and median) can be configured via the YAML files.

3.4
Reproducibility

All preprocessing decisions are controlled via YAML configuration files, enabling exact reproduction of processed datasets through specification of key parameters
(
Sandve et al. 2013
;
Wilson et al. 2017
)
. Essential path parameters include
dataset.base_dir
specifying the raw data location and
output.dir
determining the processed data destination. The temporal window configuration encompasses
max_hours
defining the observation period (e.g., 24 hours to capture the acute phase of critical illness),
num_windows
specifying the number of temporal bins (e.g., 6 windows that provide a resolution of 4 hours), and
window_size_hours
determining the width of the bin
(
Che et al. 2018
;
Harutyunyan et al. 2019
)
. Survival analysis parameters include
max_horizon_hours
for outcome truncation (e.g., 240 hours focusing on short-to-medium-term outcomes),
n_time_bins
for discrete-time model support (typically 10 bins balancing temporal resolution against statistical power), and
discretisation_method
for bin boundary computation (quantile-based or uniform spacing)
(
Kvamme et al. 2019
;
Gensheimer and Narasimhan 2019
)
. Feature filtering operates through
missingness_threshold
, which determines the minimum prevalence required for feature retention (default: 0.01, meaning that features observed in less than 1% of training windows are excluded). Imputation strategies are configured separately for
static_method
and
dynamic_method
to accommodate modality-specific requirements, with options that include mean imputation, forward-fill, zero imputation, and missingness masking. Normalisation proceeds according to
scaling_method
specification, usually employing z-score standardisation computed over observed training values
(
LeCun et al. 2012
)
. Modality selection occurs through boolean flags (
timeseries
,
static
,
icd
,
radiology
), enabling ablation studies investigating the contribution of each data modality to predictive performance. The data splitting configuration specifies
train
,
val
, and
test
proportions (default: 0.70, 0.15, 0.15) along with a random seed for reproducibility, ensuring consistent splits across different runs and research groups.

By specifying these parameters explicitly, researchers can reproduce the exact datasets used in published research, systematically evaluate the impact of preprocessing choices on model performance through controlled experimentation, and create custom preprocessing configurations tailored to novel research questions. This configuration-driven approach eliminates ambiguity with respect to data preparation procedures while maintaining the flexibility required for diverse research applications, from mortality prediction to treatment effect estimation under competing risks.

4.3
Data Quality Checks

Several automated checks validate data integrity throughout the preprocessing pipeline, implementing defensive programming practices to detect errors early
(
Meyer 1992
)
. Shape consistency verification ensures that feature tensors maintain the expected
(N\times W\times F)
dimensionality across all data splits, with missingness masks exhibiting identical shapes to their corresponding feature tensors. Label arrays must have length
N
matching the patient count, while feature name lists must have length
F
corresponding to the feature dimension. Any dimensional mismatches trigger immediate error conditions, preventing downstream processing with malformed data, protecting users from subtle bugs that could compromise model training
(
Sculley et al. 2015
;
Paleyes et al. 2020
)
.

Validation of the value range confirms that the processed data meet the expected constraints derived from domain knowledge and statistical requirements
(
Kahn et al. 2012
)
. Missingness masks must contain exclusively binary values in the set
\{0,1\}
, without intermediate or invalid entries that would indicate processing errors in mask generation. Event indicators must fall within the correct range, specifically
\{0,1\}
for single-risk scenarios and the appropriate integer set for competing-risks frameworks such as
\{0,1,2,3,4\}
for MC-MED’s four competing outcomes (including censoring). Duration values must be non-negative and not exceed the maximum horizon
H
, as violations would indicate incorrect censoring application or data corruption
(
Cox 1972
;
Klein and Moeschberger 2003
)
. Scaled features should exhibit approximately standard normal distributions in the training set, with mean near zero and standard deviation near one, although validation and test sets may exhibit some distributional shift reflecting temporal or population differences
(
Quiñonero-Candela et al. 2009
;
Moreno-Torres et al. 2012
)
.

Missing data analysis provides comprehensive missingness statistics for each feature, including the percentage of windows containing observed values before imputation, identification of features excluded due to excessive missingness relative to the configured threshold, and comparison of missingness rates across training, validation, and test splits
(
Sterne et al. 2009
;
van Buuren 2018
)
. Large discrepancies in missingness rates between splits may indicate temporal drift in data collection practices (e.g., changes in laboratory ordering protocols), systematic changes in clinical protocols (e.g., adoption of new monitoring technologies), or population shifts (e.g., evolving patient demographics), which warrants careful investigation by researchers to assess potential impacts on model generalisability and deployment safety
(
Finlayson et al. 2021
;
Subbaswamy and Saria 2019
)
.

4.4
Comparison to Existing Benchmarks

Although there are several influential benchmarks for clinical time-series processing, SurvBench is specifically designed to address a critical gap: the lack of a standardised, reproducible pipeline for survival analysis in critical care datasets.

The most widely used benchmark, by Harutyunyan et al.
(
Harutyunyan et al. 2019
)
, established a crucial standard for multitask classification and in-hospital mortality prediction using MIMIC-III. However, this foundational work is not directly comparable for several key reasons. First, it was designed for classification and regression tasks, not for the time-to-event and censoring labels required by survival models. Second, SurvBench is built on the modern MIMIC-IV and eICU datasets, which have different data schemas, patient populations, and de-identification protocols than the legacy MIMIC-III dataset. Finally, our pipeline’s temporal aggregation and outcome definitions are explicitly tailored to handle the complexities of survival analysis, such as competing risks, which were outside the scope of prior classification-focused benchmarks.

To our knowledge, no widely adopted, public benchmark for time-to-event survival analysis (including competing risks) exists for MIMIC-IV or eICU. SurvBench was therefore developed to fill this methodological gap. It provides the first reproducible and configurable pipeline for creating standardised survival cohorts, allowing robust and fair comparisons of future survival models in critical care.

5.5
Common Pitfalls and Troubleshooting

Memory limitations represent the most common technical challenge when preprocessing large datasets such as eICU, which contains over 200,000 ICU admissions with dense time-series measurements
(
Pollard et al. 2018
)
. Insufficient RAM often manifests as out-of-memory errors terminating the preprocessing pipeline, particularly during operations involving large dataframe merges or pivot operations on laboratory results spanning hundreds of distinct assays
(
tang2006personal
)
. Several mitigation strategies can address these constraints. Using compressed CSV files (.
csv.gz
format) reduces memory footprint during file reading operations by enabling streaming decompression, though at a modest computational cost
(
Deutsch 1996
)
. Reducing
max_hours
to limit the temporal extraction window (e.g., from 48 to 24 hours) decreases the volume of data retained in memory simultaneously, accepting the reduced temporal context in exchange for feasibility on memory-constrained systems. Increasing system swap space provides overflow capacity, though at substantial performance cost due to disk I/O latency orders of magnitude slower than RAM access
(
Tanenbaum and Bos 2014
)
. For institutional users, processing datasets on high-memory compute nodes (e.g., 128–256 GB RAM) offers the most robust solution, particularly for multi-dataset processing workflows or when incorporating additional modalities such as free-text clinical notes requiring embedding generation
(
Huang et al. 2019
)
.

Patient-level split failures typically arise from missing or incorrectly named subject identifier columns, a critical requirement to prevent data leakage in longitudinal clinical datasets
(
Wiens et al. 2016
;
Nestor et al. 2019
)
. The pipeline requires a
subject_id
column for enforcing patient-level data separation, ensuring that all stays in the ICU or hospital admissions for a given patient are assigned to the same split. Data loaders must correctly rename dataset-specific patient identifier columns (such as
patienthealthsystemstayid
in eICU or
subject_id
in MIMIC-IV) to the standardised
subject_id
field during cohort loading. Failure to perform this renaming triggers errors during split generation, as the splitting logic cannot locate the required identifier column. This stringent requirement, while potentially requiring additional preprocessing steps for new datasets, ensures methodological rigour by preventing subtle data leakage that could artificially inflate model performance estimates
(
Kaufman et al. 2012
;
Kapoor and Narayanan 2023
)
.

Feature filtering aggressiveness requires careful tuning to balance dimensionality reduction against preservation of clinically important measurements
(
Guyon and Elisseeff 2003
;
Saeys et al. 2007
)
. Setting
missingness_threshold
excessively high (e.g., 0.10 or 10%) may remove features that, despite sparse measurement, carry significant clinical information when observed. Laboratory tests measured primarily in specific clinical contexts exemplify this trade-off: while appearing infrequently across the general cohort, their measurements often carry substantial prognostic information
(
Churpek et al. 2016
;
Rothman et al. 2013
)
. For example, troponin levels are typically measured only when acute coronary syndrome is suspected, yet their presence and magnitude strongly predict mortality
(
Thygesen et al. 2018
)
. We recommend initiating experiments with the default threshold of 0.01 (1%), which increases only if the resulting feature dimensionality proves computationally prohibitive (e.g., exceeding 500 features) or if preliminary experiments suggest that sparse features contribute primarily noise rather than signal to predictive models
(
García et al. 2016
)
.

Temporal alignment issues arise from misconfiguration of window parameters, which require strict mathematical relationships between temporal settings. The pipeline enforces the constraint that
num_windows

\times

window_size_hours
must equal
max_hours
exactly. Violating this constraint results in immediate error conditions, as partial windows cannot be properly formed without introducing boundary effects or undefined behaviour
(
Bai et al. 2018
)
. Users should verify this relationship when customising temporal parameters, adjusting values to maintain this equality while achieving the desired temporal resolution. For example, 48-hour observation windows could be divided into 8 windows of 6 hours each, 12 windows of 4 hours each, or 24 windows of 2 hours each, but not 7 windows or 13 windows without remainder
(
Che et al. 2018
)
.

5.6
Performance Considerations

Preprocessing time varies by dataset and hardware:

1.

MIMIC-IV: 1–2 hours on a workstation with 64 GB RAM

2.

eICU: 2–4 hours due to larger file sizes

3.

MC-MED: 1–2 hours

Preprocessing is a one-time cost. Once processed, datasets can be reused indefinitely for model development.

5.7
Ethical Considerations

Although all processed datasets are derived from deidentified EHR data that have undergone rigorous privacy protection procedures, researchers using SurvBench must:

1.

Maintain compliance with PhysioNet data use agreements

2.

Ensure that processed data are stored securely and not shared publicly

3.

Avoid attempting to re-identify patients

4.

Recognise potential biases in historical clinical data (e.g., racial or socioeconomic disparities)

5.

Consider the implications for fairness and equity when deploying survival prediction models
