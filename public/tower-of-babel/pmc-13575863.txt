Concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted synthesis of tubular g-C3N4 for photoactivating peroxymonosulfate and the synergistic degradation mechanism of rhodamine B
Authors: Xiaocan Liu, Ping Liang, Dandan Wang, Weiye Yang, Mei Ge, Lijian Meng, Shihua Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has attracted considerable attention in photocatalytic peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation for organic pollutant degradation owing to its metal-free nature, structural stability, and visible-light response. However, conventional bulk g-C3N4 still suffers from a low specific surface area, limited active sites, and severe recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, which restrict its photocatalytic activity and PMS activation efficiency. In this work, a series of tubular g-C3N4 photocatalysts (TCN) were prepared using melamine as the precursor via a concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted hydrothermal treatment followed by thermal polymerization. The results showed that an appropriate amount of concentrated sulfuric acid could regulate the precursor condensation process, promote the formation of a tubular porous structure, and improve the local chemical environment and photoelectrochemical properties of the obtained materials. Among the prepared samples, TCN-3 exhibited the best photocatalytic PMS activation performance, achieving nearly complete degradation of RhB within 15 min under simulated sunlight irradiation, with an apparent reaction rate constant of 0.42477 min−1, approximately 15.3 times higher than that of pristine MCN. Structural and photoelectrochemical characterizations revealed that TCN-3 possessed a larger specific surface area, a narrower band gap, suppressed recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, and enhanced interfacial charge transfer ability. Reactive species trapping experiments and ESR analysis demonstrated that 1O2 and ˙O2− were the dominant reactive species responsible for RhB degradation in the TCN-3/light/PMS system, while h+, ˙SO4−, and ˙OH also participated in the reaction process. In addition, the system maintained good degradation performance over a wide pH range (3–9), exhibited satisfactory tolerance toward most coexisting anions, and showed good cycling stability. This work provides a useful strategy for constructing efficient metal-free g-C3N4-based photocatalysts and developing photocatalytic PMS activation systems for advanced oxidation processes.

Introduction

With the rapid development of industrialization and urbanization, large amounts of refractory organic pollutants have been discharged into aquatic environments. These pollutants pose serious threats to ecosystems and human health. Among them, dye wastewater has become an important concern in water pollution control because of its complex composition, high chromaticity, poor biodegradability, and potential toxicity.1,2 Rhodamine B (RhB), as a typical organic dye, has been widely used in textile, printing, dyeing, and paper-making industries. However, RhB is chemically stable, difficult to biodegrade, and highly persistent in aquatic environments. Therefore, it is regarded as a typical refractory organic pollutant. Previous studies have shown that RhB may pose potential risks to aquatic ecosystems. Long-term exposure to RhB may also cause adverse effects on human health, including cytotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and even potential carcinogenicity.3,4 Therefore, developing efficient, green, and sustainable technologies for organic pollutant removal is of great theoretical significance and practical value.

In recent years, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have attracted extensive attention because they can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) with strong oxidation ability.5,6 Among various AOPs, peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-based oxidation systems have developed rapidly. Compared with conventional oxidants, PMS has several advantages, such as strong oxidation ability, convenient storage and transportation, wide pH adaptability, and environmental friendliness. After activation, PMS can generate various reactive species, including sulfate radicals (˙SO4−), hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), superoxide radicals (˙O2−), and singlet oxygen (1O2). These species can efficiently degrade refractory organic pollutants. Several PMS activation methods have been developed, such as thermal activation, electrochemical activation, transition-metal activation, and photocatalytic activation.7–9 However, some of these methods still suffer from high energy consumption and high operating costs. In contrast, photocatalytic PMS activation can utilize solar energy to drive the reaction. It can also promote the effective utilization of photogenerated charge carriers. Therefore, this strategy is considered a promising technology for environmental remediation.10,11

As a typical metal-free polymeric semiconductor, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has shown great potential in photocatalytic pollutant degradation and photocatalytic PMS activation. This is mainly due to its suitable band gap of approximately 2.7 eV, excellent chemical stability, visible-light response, and low preparation cost.12,13 However, bulk g-C3N4 prepared by conventional thermal polymerization usually suffers from several intrinsic limitations. These include a small specific surface area, limited active sites, severe interlayer stacking, and rapid recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs.14,15 These drawbacks greatly restrict its photocatalytic activity and PMS activation efficiency.

Therefore, improving the structural features and charge transfer properties of g-C3N4 has become an important research direction. To overcome these limitations, various modification strategies have been proposed, including elemental doping, defect engineering, heterostructure construction, and morphology regulation.16–20 Among them, hollow tubular structures have attracted particular attention because of their unique structural advantages. Their large specific surface area can expose more active sites and enhance the contact between reactants and catalyst surfaces. In addition, the tubular framework can provide continuous electron transport pathways and promote interfacial transfer of photogenerated charge carriers. This is beneficial for improving the utilization efficiency of photogenerated charges.21 Meanwhile, the internal cavity of tubular structures can enhance light reflection and scattering within the material, thereby improving light harvesting efficiency.22 Therefore, constructing tubular g-C3N4 is considered an effective strategy for enhancing its photocatalytic performance.

Previous studies have shown that acidic environments can significantly influence the intermolecular interactions and polymerization behavior of melamine precursors. This provides an effective route to regulate the microstructural evolution of g-C3N4. For example, Ge et al. used HNO3 and HIO3 to assist the polymerization of melamine for the formation of tubular g-C3N4. In this process, the mixed acids affected the hydrolysis and self-assembly behavior of melamine, leading to controlled morphology regulation.23 Zhang et al. prepared porous tubular g-C3N4 by iodic acid/nitric acid-assisted hydrothermal treatment of a melamine–cyanuric acid supramolecular precursor followed by thermal polymerization. Compared with conventional tubular g-C3N4, the obtained material exhibited a 3.64-fold enhancement in photocatalytic hydrogen evolution activity.24

Acid treatment can not only induce the formation of special g-C3N4 morphologies, but also regulate the local electronic structure and surface chemical environment of the material. During acid treatment, the reassembly and polymerization behavior of the precursor may affect the pore structure, defect distribution, and electron delocalization of the final product. These changes can further influence the separation and migration of photogenerated charge carriers.25,26 However, studies on the construction of tubular g-C3N4 through concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted treatment and its application in photocatalytic PMS activation for organic pollutant degradation remain limited. In particular, the synergistic effects of sulfuric acid-induced structural regulation on surface properties, charge carrier behavior, and PMS activation performance have not been systematically investigated.

Based on these considerations, a series of tubular g-C3N4 materials (TCN) were prepared in this work through concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted hydrothermal treatment followed by thermal polymerization. The effects of different sulfuric acid dosages on the structural characteristics and photocatalytic PMS activation performance of the samples were compared. RhB was selected as the target pollutant to evaluate the degradation performance of TCN in the photocatalytic PMS activation system. The effects of catalyst dosage, PMS concentration, initial pollutant concentration, solution pH, and coexisting anions were also investigated. Furthermore, XRD, FTIR, XPS, PL, photoelectrochemical measurements, reactive species trapping experiments, and electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis were carried out to explore the possible mechanism by which sulfuric acid-assisted tubular g-C3N4 promotes PMS activation and RhB degradation. This work provides a new strategy for constructing high-performance metal-free photocatalysts and developing efficient photocatalytic PMS activation systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Melamine (C3H6N6, AR), potassium peroxymonosulfate (PMS, AR), rhodamine B (RhB, AR), tert-butanol (TBA, C4H10O AR), l-histidine (C6H9N3O2, AR), and p-benzoquinone (p-BQ, C6H4O2, AR) were purchased from Macklin. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, AR), hydrochloric acid (HCl, AR), sodium chloride (NaCl, AR), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, AR), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, AR), sodium phosphate (Na3PO4, AR), disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate dihydrate (EDTA-2Na, C10H14N2Na2O8·2H2O, AR), and methanol (MeOH, AR) were obtained from Xihua. Concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and peroxydisulfate (PDS) were also used in this study. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of CN materials

Modified carbon nitride samples were prepared using melamine as the precursor through a concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted hydrothermal treatment followed by thermal polymerization. Typically, 3 g of melamine was added into a beaker containing 50 mL of deionized water and magnetically stirred in a water bath at 80 °C. Then, different volumes of concentrated sulfuric acid (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 mL) were slowly added to the suspension. The mixture was further stirred for 1 h to ensure sufficient mixing. After that, the obtained mixture was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and treated hydrothermally at 180 °C for 8 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the products were washed three times with deionized water and absolute ethanol to remove residual impurities. The obtained samples were then dried at 60 °C and ground into white precursor powders. Finally, the precursor powders were placed in covered corundum crucibles and thermally polymerized in a tube furnace under a nitrogen atmosphere. The temperature was increased to 550 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 and maintained for 2 h. After cooling, the final samples were obtained. According to the volume of concentrated sulfuric acid added, the samples were denoted as TCN-1, TCN-2, TCN-3, TCN-4, and TCN-5.

For comparison, 2 g of melamine was directly placed in a covered corundum crucible and calcined under the same conditions (550 °C for 2 h) in a nitrogen atmosphere. After cooling and grinding, pristine carbon nitride was obtained and denoted as MCN.

Characterization

The crystal structure and phase composition of the samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using the KBr pellet method, with a resolution of 4 cm−1. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV). The base pressure in the analysis chamber was maintained below 2 × 10−8 mbar. Survey spectra and high-resolution spectra were collected at pass energies of 100 and 50 eV, respectively. The binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV.

The surface morphology and microstructure of the samples were characterized by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi SU-8010). Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 surface area analyzer. Before analysis, all samples were degassed under vacuum at 373 K for 12 h. The specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. The total pore volume was estimated from the adsorption amount at P/P0 = 0.988. The pore size distribution was obtained from the adsorption branch using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded using the fluorescence module of a HORIBA micro-Raman spectrometer with an excitation wavelength of 375 nm. UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) were collected on a Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus spectrophotometer over the wavelength range of 300–800 nm, using BaSO4 as the reflectance standard. The reflectance data were converted using the Kubelka–Munk function F(R), and the optical band gap (Eg) was estimated from the corresponding Tauc plots. Electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements were carried out on a Bruker EMXplus A300 spectrometer.

Photocatalytic activity test

The photocatalytic performance of the samples was evaluated by the degradation of rhodamine B (RhB) through simulated sunlight-driven PMS activation. In a typical experiment, 20 mg of catalyst was added to 100 mL of RhB solution with an initial concentration of 20 mg L−1. The suspension was ultrasonically dispersed for 10 min and then magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, a predetermined amount of PMS was added to the suspension. A 300 W xenon lamp placed approximately 10 cm above the liquid surface was used as the simulated sunlight source. During the reaction, aliquots were withdrawn at given time intervals and immediately quenched with an appropriate amount of methanol. The collected samples were centrifuged and filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter. The residual RhB concentration was determined by measuring the absorbance at 554 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. Results and discussion

Morphology and structure analysis

The morphologies of MCN and TCN-3 were characterized by SEM, as shown in Fig. 1. Fig. 1(a) shows the SEM image of MCN. It can be observed that MCN mainly exhibits an irregular bulk-like aggregated structure with obvious agglomeration, which is a typical morphology of g-C3N4 prepared by conventional thermal polymerization.27,28 In contrast, the morphology of TCN-3 changed significantly. As shown in Fig. 1(b–e), a large number of regular prismatic tubular structures were formed in TCN-3, and some regions showed obvious hollow features. Compared with MCN, TCN-3 displayed a looser structure and a reduced degree of interlayer stacking. The formation of the tubular morphology may be related to the acid-assisted assembly and condensation behavior of melamine-derived intermediates during hydrothermal treatment. Previous studies have shown that one-dimensional or tubular g-C3N4 structures can be obtained from melamine–cyanuric acid supramolecular intermediates through subsequent thermal polymerization.29,30 In these systems, hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions between melamine-derived species play an important role in guiding the ordered assembly of precursors. In addition, the in situ conversion of part of melamine into cyanuric-acid-related species under hydrothermal conditions has been reported to promote molecular self-assembly and the formation of g-C3N4 nanotubes.31 Therefore, in the present work, an appropriate amount of concentrated sulfuric acid may regulate the precursor assembly and condensation environment, while gas release during calcination may further promote structural rearrangement and the formation of hollow tubular TCN-3.

SEM images of (a) MCN and (b–e) TCN-3; (f–i) EDS elemental mapping images of TCN-3 showing the distribution of C, N, and S elements.

Such a hollow tubular structure can effectively reduce material aggregation, facilitate reactant diffusion, and promote the migration of photogenerated charge carriers, thereby improving the photocatalytic activity. Fig. 1(f–i) shows the EDS elemental mapping images of TCN-3. The results show that C and N elements are uniformly distributed throughout the tubular structure, indicating that the sample is mainly composed of C and N elements. In contrast, the S signal is weak and scattered, which may be related to trace sulfur-containing residues after acid treatment.

Fig. 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of MCN and the TCN samples. All samples exhibited two characteristic diffraction peaks of g-C3N4 at approximately 2θ = 12.8° and 27.6°, which were assigned to the (100) and (002) planes, respectively. The (100) reflection originates from the in-plane periodic arrangement of triazine/heptazine units, whereas the (002) reflection corresponds to the interlayer stacking of conjugated aromatic layers.32 These characteristic peaks indicate that the basic framework of g-C3N4 was retained after the acid-assisted hydrothermal treatment. Compared with MCN, the TCN samples showed decreased intensities of both characteristic peaks. The most pronounced decrease was observed for TCN-3, particularly for the (002) reflection. This result suggests a lower degree of interlayer stacking and reduced in-plane structural order after the acid treatment. Combined with the SEM observations, the acidic environment may have altered the polymerization and condensation behavior of melamine, thereby promoting the formation of a more loosely assembled tubular structure during subsequent calcination. TCN-3 exhibited the lowest intensity of the (002) diffraction peak, indicating that an appropriate amount of concentrated sulfuric acid was more effective in reducing the interlayer stacking of g-C3N4. Further increasing the acid dosage did not cause an additional decrease in peak intensity, suggesting that an appropriate dosage range was required for effective structural regulation.

Fig. 2(b) shows the FTIR spectra of MCN and the TCN samples. All samples displayed the characteristic absorption bands of g-C3N4. The band at 810 cm−1 was assigned to the breathing vibration of the heptazine ring structure.33 The multiple bands in the range of 1200–1700 cm−1 originated from the stretching vibrations of C–N and C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> N bonds in the heterocyclic framework.34 The broad band at 3000–3400 ccm−1 was related to the stretching vibrations of residual –NHx groups resulting from incomplete condensation and O–H groups from adsorbed water molecules.35 Compared with MCN, no obvious new absorption bands were observed for the TCN samples. This indicates that the acid treatment did not destroy the main chemical framework of g-C3N4, which is consistent with the XRD results. However, the absorption band of the TCN samples in the range of 3000–3400 cm−1 was slightly enhanced. This suggests that more incompletely condensed amino groups or surface defect sites may have been retained after the acid treatment. These surface sites may facilitate the subsequent adsorption and activation of PMS.

To further investigate the pore structure of the samples, N2 adsorption–desorption measurements were performed for MCN and TCN-3, as shown in Fig. 2(c). Both samples exhibited typical type IV adsorption–desorption isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of abundant mesoporous structures and larger pores.36 Compared with MCN, TCN-3 showed a markedly higher N2 adsorption capacity, suggesting a larger specific surface area and a more developed porous structure. According to the BET calculation, the specific surface area of TCN-3 was 39.25 m2 g−1, approximately 3.38 times that of MCN (11.60 m2 g−1). The pore size distribution curves in the inset further show that the pores of TCN-3 were mainly distributed in the range of 30–70 nm, with a higher pore volume in this region than that of MCN. The tubular porous structure endowed TCN-3 with a larger specific surface area and a more developed pore network. These features could facilitate reactant diffusion and expose more accessible active sites, thereby promoting PMS activation and photocatalytic reactions.

XPS analysis was performed to investigate the surface elemental composition and chemical states of MCN and TCN-3. Fig. 2(d) shows the XPS survey spectra of MCN and TCN-3. Both samples mainly exhibited C 1s and N 1s signals, indicating that their surfaces were primarily composed of C and N elements. Meanwhile, no obvious S 2p signal was detected in TCN-3, implying that sulfur-related species, if present, were below the detection limit of XPS. To further examine the possible presence of sulfur species, the high-resolution S 2p XPS spectrum of TCN-3 was analyzed, as shown in Fig. S1. No discernible S 2p peak was observed, indicating that there is no direct XPS evidence supporting effective sulfur doping in TCN-3. Although trace sulfur species below the detection limit cannot be completely excluded, they are unlikely to be the dominant factor responsible for the enhanced photocatalytic PMS activation performance. Fig. 2(e) shows the high-resolution C 1s spectra of MCN and TCN-3. Both samples can be deconvoluted into two characteristic peaks. The peak at around 284.8 eV is assigned to surface amorphous carbon or C–C bonds, while the peak at around 288.1 eV corresponds to sp2-hybridized N–CN bonds in the g-C3N4 framework.37 Compared with MCN, the relative area of the N–CN peak in TCN-3 increased from 79.90% to 90.93%. In contrast, the relative area of the C–C peak decreased from 20.10% to 9.07%. These results indicate that the proportion of N–CN units associated with the conjugated framework increased after acid-assisted treatment, while the amount of disordered carbon species decreased. This suggests that the local conjugated structure of TCN-3 was partially reconstructed. Previous studies have reported that N–CN units are important components of the conjugated g-C3N4 framework. An increased proportion of these units may facilitate electron delocalization within the conjugated system, thereby promoting the separation and transfer of photogenerated charge carriers.38,39Fig. 2(f) presents the high-resolution N 1s spectra of MCN and TCN-3. The peaks at around 398.6, 399.8, 401.1, and 404.4 eV can be assigned to sp2-hybridized C–NC, bridging nitrogen N–(C)3, surface amino groups C–NHx, and π–π* electronic excitation, respectively.40–42 Compared with MCN, the relative area of the C–NC peak in TCN-3 decreased from 77.22% to 69.11%, whereas that of N–(C)3 increased from 7.87% to 13.67%. In addition, the π–π* excitation peak increased from 3.92% to 5.96%. These changes indicate that acid treatment altered the local coordination environment of nitrogen atoms and modified the π-conjugated electronic structure of TCN-3, which may be favorable for electron delocalization. Moreover, the slightly increased C–NHx peak area suggests that more uncondensed amino groups or edge active sites may exist on the surface of TCN-3. These sites could contribute to PMS adsorption and activation.

Overall, acid-assisted treatment did not destroy the main framework of g-C3N4, but it significantly affected the local surface chemical environment of the material. The absence of discernible S 2p signals in both the survey and high-resolution XPS spectra indicates that there is no direct evidence for effective sulfur doping in TCN-3. Therefore, the enhanced photocatalytic PMS activation performance is more reasonably attributed to the acid-assisted structural regulation and electronic modulation rather than detectable sulfur doping. On the one hand, the proportion of conjugated framework-related units increased in TCN-3, while disordered carbon species decreased. On the other hand, the increased contents of bridging nitrogen and π–π* excitation indicate changes in the π-conjugated electronic structure, which may facilitate electron delocalization. These structural and electronic changes are beneficial for the migration and separation of photogenerated charge carriers,43 and can further promote PMS activation, thereby enhancing the photocatalytic performance.

Photocatalytic performance assessment

To evaluate the photocatalytic PMS activation performance of the prepared samples, RhB was selected as the target pollutant, and its degradation was investigated under simulated sunlight irradiation using different catalysts. Fig. 3(a) shows the degradation performance of RhB over different carbon nitride photocatalysts in the Light/PMS system. As shown in Fig. 3(a), MCN exhibited relatively low degradation activity toward RhB in the Light/PMS system, with only about 43% of RhB degraded within 15 min. In contrast, all TCN samples prepared by concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted treatment showed enhanced catalytic activity. Among them, TCN-3 exhibited the best performance, achieving 97.88% degradation of 20 mg L−1 RhB within 12 min, which was much higher than those of the other samples.

Photocatalytic degradation of RhB over different catalysts: (a) degradation curves, (b) pseudo-first-order kinetic fitting curves, and (c) apparent rate constants. Control experiments under different reaction systems: (d) degradation curves, (e) pseudo-first-order kinetic fitting curves, and (f) apparent rate constants.

To compare the catalytic reaction rates of different samples, the RhB degradation process was fitted using a pseudo-first-order kinetic model, as shown in Fig. 3(b). All samples showed good linear relationships, indicating that the degradation process followed pseudo-first-order reaction kinetics. The corresponding apparent rate constants (K) are shown in Fig. 3(c). The K value of TCN-3 reached 0.42477 min−1, which was approximately 15.3, 3.4, and 2.9 times higher than those of MCN (0.0277 min−1), TCN-1 (0.12516 min−1), and TCN-2 (0.14747 min−1), respectively. This result indicates that TCN-3 possessed the fastest RhB degradation rate and the strongest PMS activation ability. However, when the amount of concentrated sulfuric acid was further increased, the catalytic performance decreased. The degradation performance decreased when the amount of concentrated sulfuric acid was further increased. This result suggests that an appropriate acid dosage is more favorable for structural regulation and the formation of accessible active sites, whereas an excessively acidic environment may adversely affect the development of these beneficial structural features, thereby reducing the photocatalytic activity.

To further investigate the synergistic effect among light irradiation, PMS, and the catalyst, control experiments were carried out under different reaction systems. The results are shown in Fig. 3(d–f). As shown in Fig. 3(d), RhB removal was negligible under single Light, single PMS, or single TCN-3 conditions. This indicates that light irradiation, PMS, or the catalyst alone could not effectively degrade RhB. In the TCN-3/Light system, the RhB degradation efficiency increased obviously, indicating that TCN-3 possessed certain photocatalytic activity. In contrast, the TCN-3/PMS system showed relatively weak degradation performance, suggesting that PMS was difficult to activate effectively in the absence of light. However, rapid RhB degradation was achieved in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. This system exhibited much higher catalytic activity than the other systems, confirming the significant synergistic effect between photocatalysis and PMS activation. The corresponding pseudo-first-order kinetic fitting results and apparent rate constants are shown in Fig. 3(e and f). The K value of the TCN-3/Light/PMS system reached 0.42477 min−1, which was much higher than those of the TCN-3/Light system (0.04352 min−1) and the TCN-3/PMS system (0.02058 min−1). These values were approximately 9.8 and 20.6 times higher, respectively. Moreover, the reaction rate was also greatly improved compared with those of the single PMS and single Light systems. These results demonstrate that light irradiation and PMS activation have a clear synergistic effect. Under simulated sunlight irradiation, TCN-3 can more effectively promote PMS activation, thereby significantly enhancing RhB degradation efficiency.

To further evaluate the mineralization degree of RhB, TOC analysis was performed for the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. The initial TOC concentration of RhB solution was 9.90 mg L−1, which decreased to 4.07 mg L−1 after 15 min of reaction, corresponding to a TOC removal efficiency of 58.9%. This result indicates that RhB was partially mineralized during the reaction. Although nearly complete RhB decolorization was achieved within 15 min, the lower TOC removal efficiency suggests that some organic intermediates still remained in the solution. The relatively high TOC removal may be attributed to photocatalytic PMS activation over TCN-3, which generated multiple reactive species and promoted the further oxidation of RhB intermediates.

To further optimize the reaction conditions and evaluate the applicability of the system, the effects of catalyst dosage, PMS concentration, initial RhB concentration, initial pH, and coexisting anions on RhB degradation in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system were investigated.

Fig. 4(a) shows the degradation performance of RhB in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system with different catalyst dosages. As the TCN-3 dosage increased from 50 to 200 mg L−1, the RhB degradation efficiency increased significantly. The corresponding apparent rate constant (K) increased from 0.14003 to 0.42477 min−1. This improvement can be attributed to the increased number of active sites provided by the higher catalyst dosage, which promoted PMS activation and the generation of more reactive species. However, when the catalyst dosage was further increased to 300 mg L−1, the degradation rate slightly decreased, with a K value of 0.36138 min−1. This may be because excessive catalyst increased the turbidity of the suspension and caused a light-shielding effect, thereby reducing the utilization of incident light and decreasing the photocatalytic efficiency.44 Therefore, 200 mg L−1 was considered the suitable catalyst dosage for this system.

Effects of (a) catalyst dosage, (b) PMS concentration, (c) initial RhB concentration, (d) initial pH, (e) coexisting inorganic anions, and (f) different oxidants on RhB degradation in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system.

Fig. 4(b) shows the RhB degradation performance of the TCN-3/Light/PMS system under different initial PMS concentrations. When the PMS concentration increased from 0.25 to 0.5 mM, the RhB degradation efficiency was markedly enhanced. The k value increased from 0.14322 to 0.42477 min−1. This indicates that an appropriate increase in PMS concentration can promote the generation of reactive oxygen species, thereby accelerating RhB degradation. However, when the PMS concentration was further increased to 0.75 and 1.0 mM, both the degradation efficiency and reaction rate slightly decreased. This was mainly because excessive PMS may enhance side reactions and self-quenching among reactive species, thus reducing the effective utilization of reactive oxygen species.45 Therefore, an optimal PMS dosage existed in this system, and the best degradation performance was obtained at 0.5 mM PMS.

Fig. 4(c) shows the degradation performance of RhB at different initial concentrations in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. The highest apparent rate constant was obtained at an initial RhB concentration of 20 mg L−1. When the RhB concentration was further increased to 40 mg L−1, both the degradation efficiency and reaction rate decreased markedly. At higher pollutant concentrations, more RhB molecules compete for the available active sites and reactive species in the system. In addition, the increased solution absorbance limits light penetration, thereby reducing the effective utilization of incident light and ultimately decreasing the RhB degradation rate.

Fig. 4(d) shows the RhB degradation performance under different initial pH conditions. The TCN-3/Light/PMS system maintained degradation activity over a wide pH range. The best performance was obtained under neutral conditions (pH = 7). Under acidic conditions (pH = 3 and 5), the system still showed relatively high degradation activity. However, under alkaline conditions, especially at pH = 11, the RhB degradation efficiency decreased significantly. This may be because a strongly alkaline environment affects the stability of PMS and causes partial quenching of reactive species, thereby inhibiting the degradation reaction.46

Inorganic anions are widely present in natural water bodies and industrial wastewater. It has been reported that they may affect the degradation of organic pollutants. To evaluate the influence of common inorganic anions on the photocatalytic performance of the TCN-3/Light/PMS system, Cl−, PO43−, CO32−, SO42−, and NO3− were added into the reaction solution at a concentration of 5 mM. The results are shown in Fig. 4(e). Compared with the control group without added anions, Cl−, SO42−, NO3−, and CO32− had only a slight effect on RhB degradation. In all these systems, about 99% of RhB could still be degraded within 15 min. In contrast, PO43− showed the strongest inhibitory effect. This may be mainly attributed to the tetrahedral structure of PO43−, which can easily compete with pollutants for adsorption on the active sites of the catalyst surface. As a result, the adsorption and degradation of RhB were inhibited. In addition, the hydrolysis of PO43− can alter the solution pH and generate OH−, which may further affect the formation of reactive species and the degradation efficiency of the system.47,48

To compare the activation effects of different oxidants in the TCN-3 photocatalytic system, H2O2, PDS, and PMS were used as oxidants for RhB degradation. The results are shown in Fig. 4(f). Under the same experimental conditions, all oxidants promoted RhB degradation, but their reaction activities were clearly different. Among them, the PMS system exhibited the highest degradation efficiency and achieved nearly complete removal of RhB within 15 min. In comparison, the PDS and H2O2 systems showed much lower degradation rates. The superior catalytic performance of the PMS system can be mainly attributed to its asymmetric O–O bond structure, which makes PMS easier to activate under light irradiation and enables the generation of multiple reactive oxygen species. Meanwhile, the high redox potential of HSO5− also contributes to the enhanced oxidation ability.49 In contrast, the peroxide bond in PDS is more stable and more difficult to activate, while H2O2 mainly relies on the ˙OH generation pathway. Therefore, their overall degradation efficiencies were relatively lower.

To evaluate the practical applicability of the TCN-3/Light/PMS system, RhB degradation experiments were further conducted in different water matrices, including deionized water, humic acid-containing water, river water, and seawater. As shown in Fig. S2, RhB could still be efficiently degraded in all tested matrices. The apparent rate constant decreased from 0.42477 min−1 in deionized water to 0.31343, 0.28565, and 0.27247 min−1 in humic acid-containing water, river water, and seawater, respectively. The slightly decreased degradation rates may be attributed to the presence of natural organic matter and background inorganic ions, which could compete for reactive species, occupy active sites, or affect light penetration. Nevertheless, nearly complete RhB removal was still achieved within 15 min, indicating that the TCN-3/Light/PMS system maintained good degradation performance in complex water matrices and showed potential for practical wastewater treatment.

Optical and photoelectrochemical properties

The light absorption capability, band structure, and separation efficiency of photogenerated charge carriers play important roles in determining the photocatalytic performance of a photocatalyst. The inset of Fig. 5(a) shows the UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra of MCN and TCN-3, while the corresponding Kubelka–Munk-transformed plots are presented in the main panel.50,51 Both MCN and TCN-3 exhibited strong light absorption in the range of 200–450 nm, with an obvious absorption edge at approximately 450 nm. The absorption in this region mainly originates from the π→π* electronic transitions within the conjugated framework of g-C3N4.52 Compared with MCN, TCN-3 showed stronger absorption in the visible-light region, indicating that the acid-assisted treatment enhanced its light-harvesting capability. The plots of (αhv)1/2versus photon energy (hv), obtained using the Tauc equation, are shown in the main panel of Fig. 5(a). The optical band gaps (Eg) of MCN and TCN-3 were estimated to be 2.63 and 2.55 eV, respectively. The narrower band gap of TCN-3 enables it to respond to light over a broader wavelength range, which is beneficial for improving light absorption and utilization.

(a) UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra and corresponding Kubelka–Munk plots, (b) Mott–Schottky plots, (c) band structure diagram, (d) photoluminescence (PL) spectra, (e) transient photocurrent response curves, and (f) electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) Nyquist plots of MCN and TCN-3.

Mott–Schottky (M–S) measurements were performed to investigate the semiconductor properties and band structures of the samples. Fig. 5(b) shows the M–S plots of MCN and TCN-3. Both samples exhibited positive slopes, indicating that they are n-type semiconductors. The flat-band potentials were obtained by extrapolating the linear regions of the M–S plots to the x-axis. The flat-band potentials of MCN and TCN-3 were approximately −1.27 and −0.90 V versus Ag/AgCl, respectively. For n-type semiconductors, the flat-band potential is generally close to the conduction band potential (ECB). After conversion to the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) scale, the ECB values of MCN and TCN-3 were estimated to be −1.07 and −0.70 V, respectively.53,54 Combined with the corresponding optical band gaps, the valence band potentials (EVB) of MCN and TCN-3 were calculated to be approximately 1.56 and 1.85 V versus NHE, respectively. The resulting band structures are illustrated in Fig. 5(c). The conduction band potential of TCN-3 remains more negative than the standard redox potential of O2/˙O2− (−0.33 V versus NHE). Therefore, its photogenerated electrons are thermodynamically capable of reducing dissolved O2 to ˙O2−. Meanwhile, the more positive valence band potential of TCN-3 indicates a stronger oxidation capability, providing favorable thermodynamic conditions for the generation of reactive species during photocatalytic PMS activation.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was used to evaluate the separation behavior of photogenerated charge carriers in the samples. Generally, a higher PL emission intensity indicates more severe recombination of photogenerated charge carriers.55,56Fig. 5(d) shows the PL spectra of the samples recorded at an excitation wavelength of 375 nm. All samples exhibited a characteristic emission peak at approximately 460 nm. Among them, TCN-3 showed the lowest PL intensity, indicating that the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs was effectively suppressed. This result suggests that the tubular structure formed after the acid-assisted treatment favored the separation of photogenerated charge carriers.

To further investigate the photoelectrochemical properties of the samples, transient photocurrent response (i–t) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed for MCN and TCN-3. Fig. 5(e) shows the transient photocurrent response curves. Under periodic light on/off irradiation, TCN-3 exhibited a markedly higher photocurrent density than MCN, indicating more efficient separation and migration of photogenerated charge carriers.57Fig. 5(f) presents the corresponding EIS Nyquist plots. In general, a smaller semicircle radius indicates a lower interfacial charge-transfer resistance and faster electron transport. TCN-3 displayed a substantially smaller arc radius than MCN, demonstrating its enhanced interfacial charge-transfer capability.58

Active species quenching experiments and ESR analysis

To identify the reactive species involved in RhB degradation and evaluate their relative contributions in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system, reactive species quenching experiments and electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements were systematically conducted. Methanol (MeOH), tert-butanol (TBA), disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA-2Na), p-benzoquinone (p-BQ), and l-histidine were employed as scavengers. Specifically, MeOH was used to quench both ˙SO4− and ˙OH, while TBA was used as a selective scavenger for ˙OH. EDTA-2Na, p-BQ, and l-histidine were used to quench photogenerated holes (h+), superoxide radicals (˙O2−), and singlet oxygen (1O2), respectively.59

Fig. 6(a) shows the RhB degradation performance and corresponding apparent rate constants of the TCN-3/Light/PMS system in the presence of different scavengers. The addition of MeOH or TBA decreased the RhB degradation rate to different extents, although their inhibitory effects were much weaker than those of p-BQ and l-histidine. The addition of EDTA-2Na also caused a moderate suppression of RhB degradation. In contrast, the degradation efficiency decreased markedly in the presence of p-BQ or l-histidine. l-histidine exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect, with only approximately 15% of RhB degraded after 15 min and the apparent rate constant decreasing to 0.01796 min−1. The addition of p-BQ also substantially inhibited RhB degradation, resulting in an apparent rate constant of only 0.05137 min−1. These results indicate that 1O2 and ˙O2− were the dominant reactive species responsible for RhB degradation in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. Photogenerated holes also contributed to the reaction, while ˙OH and ˙SO4− played auxiliary roles.

(a) Radical quenching experiments for RhB degradation in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. ESR spectra of (b) 1O2, (c) ˙O2−, and (d) ˙OH/˙SO4−.

The formation of reactive oxygen species in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system was further examined by ESR spectroscopy.60 As shown in Fig. 6(b–d), almost no characteristic signals were detected under dark conditions, indicating that the TCN-3/PMS system could not spontaneously generate substantial amounts of reactive species. After 6 min of light irradiation, clear characteristic signals assigned to 1O2 and ˙O2−, as well as signals related to ˙OH/˙SO4−, were observed. This result demonstrates that light irradiation effectively promoted PMS activation and the generation of reactive species. Compared with those under dark conditions, the characteristic signals were markedly enhanced under irradiation, confirming the essential role of light in reactive species formation. Combined with the quenching results, the ESR analysis demonstrates that 1O2, ˙O2−, ˙OH, and ˙SO4− were simultaneously generated in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. Among them,1O2 and ˙O2− made the major contributions to RhB degradation, while ˙OH, ˙SO4−, and h+ also participated in the reaction. These findings further support the synergistic interaction among TCN-3, light irradiation, and PMS, which promoted reactive species generation and consequently enabled efficient RhB degradation.

Proposed photocatalytic mechanism

Based on the reactive species trapping experiments, ESR measurements, and band structure analysis, a possible mechanism for RhB degradation in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system was proposed, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Under simulated sunlight irradiation, TCN-3 was photoexcited to generate electrons (e−) and holes (h+) (eqn (2)). According to the Mott–Schottky results, the conduction band potential of TCN-3 was approximately −0.70 V versus NHE, which is more negative than the O2/˙O2− redox potential (−0.33 V versus NHE). Therefore, the photogenerated electrons could reduce dissolved O2 to ˙O2− (eqn (3)). The generated ˙O2− could subsequently produce 1O2 through disproportionation reactions or reactions involving photogenerated holes (eqn (4)–(6)). Meanwhile, PMS could participate in redox reactions with the photogenerated charge carriers. Photogenerated holes could oxidize HSO5− to SO5− (eqn (7)), which could be further converted into 1O2 (eqn (8) and (9)). In addition, some photogenerated electrons could activate PMS to generate ˙SO4− (eqn (10)), followed by the formation of ˙OH (eqn (11)). Therefore, the photocatalytic process and PMS activation were coupled in the TCN-3/Light/PMS system. Stability and reusability

The cycling stability of TCN-3 was evaluated through five consecutive RhB degradation experiments, as shown in Fig. 8(a). TCN-3 maintained rapid RhB degradation within 15 min during all five cycles, with no obvious loss of catalytic activity. To further examine the stability of the catalyst after repeated photocatalytic PMS activation, FTIR and XRD analyses of fresh and used TCN-3 were performed. As shown in Fig. 8(b), the characteristic FTIR absorption bands of g-C3N4 were retained after the reaction, and no obvious shifts in the main peak positions were observed, although slight changes in peak intensity occurred. In addition, the XRD patterns of fresh and used TCN-3 are shown in Fig. 8(c). The used TCN-3 retained the characteristic diffraction peaks of g-C3N4, especially the (002) peak at around 27.6°, and no obvious new diffraction peaks were detected after five cycling runs. Although the peak intensity decreased slightly after cycling, the main framework structure of TCN-3 was largely preserved. These results demonstrate that TCN-3 maintained high catalytic activity and good structural stability after repeated use, indicating its satisfactory cycling stability and reusability.

Conclusion

In summary, tubular graphitic carbon nitride photocatalysts (TCN) were successfully prepared through concentrated sulfuric acid-assisted hydrothermal treatment followed by high-temperature calcination. The sulfuric acid-assisted treatment regulated the condensation process of melamine, promoted the formation of a tubular porous structure, and improved the local electronic environment and photoelectrochemical properties of the material. Compared with pristine MCN, TCN-3 exhibited a larger specific surface area, stronger visible-light absorption, more efficient separation of photogenerated charge carriers, and enhanced interfacial charge transfer. Under simulated sunlight irradiation, TCN-3 showed excellent performance for photocatalytic PMS activation toward RhB degradation. Nearly complete degradation of RhB was achieved within 15 min, with an apparent rate constant of 0.42477 min−1, approximately 15.3 times that of MCN. Reactive species trapping experiments and ESR analysis confirmed the simultaneous generation of multiple reactive species. Among them, 1O2 and ˙O2− were the dominant species, while h+, ˙SO4−, and ˙OH also contributed to RhB degradation. The proposed mechanism suggests that the coupling of photocatalysis with PMS activation promoted the continuous generation of reactive oxygen species, thereby enhancing the overall oxidative degradation efficiency. In addition, the TCN-3/Light/PMS system maintained high degradation activity over a wide pH range, showed good tolerance toward most common coexisting anions, and exhibited excellent cycling stability. This work provides a simple and effective strategy for constructing high-performance tubular g-C3N4 photocatalysts and offers experimental guidance for developing efficient metal-free photocatalytic PMS activation systems for organic wastewater treatment.
