Screening for intrinsic capacity and frailty in older adults with multimorbidity in the primary care setting: application of the ICOPE tool and two frailty instruments
Authors: Sai Zhen Sim, Xinyao Ng, Poay Sian Sabrina Lee, Hui Li Koh, Shu Yun Tan, Teck Yong Gabriel Ding, Eng Sing Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntrinsic capacity (IC) co-exists with frailty and multimorbidity in primary care and guidelines recommend screening for IC and frailty to improve health outcomes. To inform effective implementation of such screening, this study aimed to understand the association between IC and frailty, and the discriminative ability of IC for frailty in older adults with multimorbidity.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted across three primary care clinics from August to October 2022. The participants were multimorbid older adults who had the triad of diabetes mellitus, hypertension and hyperlipidemia and who could walk independently. Data collected included sociodemographic factors, Integrated Care Older People Screening Tool (ICOPE), social vulnerability, chronic conditions, functional impairment, modified Frailty Phenotype (mFP), and Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). Multinomial logistic regression was used to assess the association between IC and frailty while area under the curve of the receiver operating characteristic (AUC-ROC), sensitivity, specificity, and false positive result were used to assess the discriminative ability of IC for frailty.Results411 participants were included in the study and the mean age was 69.9 (± 6.2) years. 11.9% of the participants were mFP frail while 7.5% were CFS frail, and almost all the participants (98.0%) had reduced IC. Higher composite IC score was associated with reduced odds of frailty (OR 0.39 (mFP) and OR 0.45 (CFS), p ≤ 0.05). The IC domains associated with frailty depended on the frailty measure used and socio-cultural factors. An IC cut-off score of 2 had AUC-ROC, sensitivities, specificities, and false positive results of 0.72, 59.2%, 77.9% and 22.1% respectively for mFP; and 0.74, 61.3%, 76.3% and 23.7% respectively for CFS.ConclusionsIn a primary care population with multimorbidity, IC screening inadequately discriminates for frailty and functional impairment with insufficient sensitivity and high false positive results. In view of the very high prevalence of IC losses, IC screening followed by frailty screening of selected patients with IC losses may not be feasible and other approaches should be considered to optimise screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06569-2.Keywords: Intrinsic capacity, Frailty, Multimorbidity, Screening, Older adults

Introduction

In older adults, intrinsic capacity (IC) losses commonly co-exist with frailty [1] and both are associated with adverse health outcomes such as functional disability, mortality, poorer well-being, and higher healthcare utilisation [2, 3]. Various guidelines have recommended screening and intervening for IC losses and frailty to improve health outcomes [4, 5]. However, IC and frailty are distinct from each other and in view of limited healthcare resources, it is unclear how IC and frailty screening should be implemented.

Frailty is commonly defined as a physiological state of increased vulnerability to stressors while IC encompasses the sum of an individual’s physical and mental capacities [1]. Notably, there is significant overlap between IC and frailty with considerable proportions of robust and pre-frail adults already having IC impairments [6, 7]. Both IC and frailty are complementary, and IC is thought to be a determinant of frailty in which a clinically relevant reduction in IC and functional reserve leads to the onset of frailty [1, 8]. The risk of developing frailty is increased with two or more declines in any IC domains or a decline in the locomotion domain [6, 7, 9]. In this context, the ICOPE guideline recommends screening for frailty in those with impaired locomotion [5]. Thus, IC measurement and monitoring may support both concurrent and subsequent evaluation of frailty.

However, most studies on IC utilise the frailty phenotype (FP), which is characterised by sarcopenia, to measure frailty. Given the close relationship between the IC and FP domains, it is unsurprising that there are significant associations between the two. While the FP measure is well validated and widely used in research, it is not commonly used in clinical practice due to the need for objective measurements that are not part of routine care [10]. It is unclear how IC correlates with more practical assessments of frailty such as the CFS which is recommended by the Ministry of Health National Frailty Strategy Policy Report of Singapore for rapid large-scale frailty screening in community and primary care settings [11]. Additionally, most IC studies have primarily focused on the general population. This raises questions about the applicability of their findings to a primary care population characterized by high levels of multimorbidity [12] that is also at increased risk of developing frailty and other adverse outcomes[13–15].

For resource allocation and intervention planning in primary care, it is important to understand the specific needs and conditions of this demographic. Many older adults in the community already have IC losses on screening and the prevalence of IC losses is likely to be higher within primary care settings. Given the challenges associated with screening, assessing and optimising IC for every individual in primary care, it may be more practical to target specific high-risk groups such as older adults with multimorbidity. Thus, this study aimed to understand the extent of IC and frailty within this important population subgroup, examining the association between these two concepts and their potential overlap. Furthermore, we explored if IC could concurrently discriminate for frailty and functional impairment. If IC demonstrates strong discriminative ability for frailty, it could lead to a more streamlined screening process.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted from August to October 2022 across three primary care clinics which are part of a bigger network of clinics in the central region of Singapore under the National Healthcare Group Polyclinics (NHGP). Participants were recruited through convenience sampling where healthcare staff approached potential candidates and referred them to the study with their consent. Enrolment criteria included (i) individuals aged 60 years to 100 years, (ii) those with multimorbidity, specifically the triad of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidaemia which represents the most prevalent three-disease combination in Singapore and (iii) participants who were able to walk independently with or without walking aids. The questionnaire was administered by trained interviewers to ensure consistency and accuracy. Participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the ethical review board of National Healthcare Group (NHG DSRB Ref: 2022/00379).

Sociodemographic data

Sociodemographic information and lifestyle risk factors including age, gender, ethnicity, education level, housing type, smoking, and alcohol consumption were collected using a self-reported questionnaire.

Intrinsic capacity

Intrinsic capacity was measured using the WHO ICOPE tool [5] and followed the recommended ICOPE cut-offs for all domains except locomotion.

Cognitive domain was evaluated by two questions testing immediate recall and orientation to time and space. The domain was scored 0 (impaired) if one or both questions were answered incorrectly, or 1 (intact) if both questions were answered correctly.

Locomotion was evaluated by the five times sit-to-stand test. The domain was scored 0 (impaired) if the time taken to complete the test was ≥ 12 s, or 1 (intact) if it was < 12 s using the Asia Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWSG) 2019 guideline cut-off [16]. This cut-off has been used in previous local studies to assess the locomotion domain [6, 17].

The composite IC score was summed up by addition of the scores from all five domains, to give a range of 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating better IC.

Social vulnerability factors

Social vulnerability factors included social network, loneliness and social participation. Social network was measured using the 6-item Lubben Social Network Scale [19], which comprises of the family subscale and friend subscale. Each subscale has a set of three questions evaluating social connectedness, and each question has six response options ranging from 0 to 5 which correspond to the number of people that participants feel they are socially connected to. The score for each subscale is derived by adding up the responses to all the questions, giving a score range of 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating better social engagement.

Loneliness was assessed using the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale [20] with questions about feeling a lack of companionship, feeling left out and feeling isolated from others. Each question has three options to reflect the frequency of feelings of loneliness: 1 (hardly ever) to 3 (Often). The values for each question were summed up to give a score ranging from 3 to 9. Participants were categorised as “not lonely” if they scored 3, “somewhat lonely” if they scored 4 to 5, and “lonely” if they scored 6 to 9.

Social participation was measured using the social role domain of the Late-Life Function and Disability Instrument (Late-Life FDI) [21] which consist of nine items reflecting the frequency of performing various social and community activities, including keeping in touch with others, visiting friends and family in their homes, providing care or assistance to others, volunteering, participating in active recreation, travelling out of town, inviting people into your home, going out with others to public places, and participating in organised social activities. Each item has five response options from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). The scores from each question were summed up and transformed to a score from 0 to 100 based on a Rasch model, with lower scores indicating worse social participation.

Multimorbidity

The level of multimorbidity was measured using a simple count of self-reported conditions from a pre-determined list of 23 chronic conditions that are prevalent or of high impact to patients in primary care: hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, arthritis, obesity, cardiovascular disease, asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic hepatitis, stomach problems, thyroid disorders, stroke, heart failure, kidney disease, depression or anxiety, chronic urinary problem, physical disability, cancer, osteoporosis, dementia and colon problems [22]. To better identify patients with increased healthcare needs who may be at higher risk of poorer outcomes, participants with three or more conditions were considered as having multimorbidity [22–24].

Functional impairment

Functional impairment was assessed by the need for assistance or complete dependence on others for basic activities of daily living (bADLs) and instrumental ADLs (iADLs). The bADL and iADL are measured by the Barthel Index [25] and Lawton and Brody scale [26], respectively and participants were assessed to have impairment if they did not have full scores.

Frailty was operationalised using the mFP criteria [17]:

Frailty was defined as the presence of at least three of the components, pre-frailty as the presence of one or two components and robustness as the absence of any of the components.

Clinical frailty scale (CFS)

The CFS has nine stages representing the full spectrum of robustness (CFS 1, very fit) to severe frailty and terminal illness (CFS 9, terminally ill with life expectancy < six months) [29]. Our assessment was guided by the National Health System CFS app which evaluates bADLs and iADLs, symptoms limiting activities, physical activity level, and active disease symptoms [30]. The assessment started from the most advanced stage (CFS 9) and moved sequentially down to the stage applicable to the participant. The CFS scores were based on the CFS version 2.0[29], and they were subsequently categorised into robust (CFS 1 and 2), pre-frail (CFS 3), and frail (CFS ≥ 4). The CFS was administered by research staff who had received prior CFS training and standardisation exercise.

Sample size

A study exploring the levels of intrinsic capacity and frailty among community-dwelling older adults found that 24% had reduced intrinsic capacity and were classified as pre-frail, while 10% exhibited reduced intrinsic capacity and frailty [31]. In our estimation, within a primary care population of older adults with multimorbidity, the prevalence of pre-frailty and frailty is likely to be significantly higher, nearly doubling the proportions observed in the general community and resulting in a narrower difference in proportion between the two groups. Consequently, we projected the rates of reduced intrinsic capacity among those classified as pre-frail and frail to be approximately 45% and 35%, respectively, with a difference of 10% between the two groups. Based on the data presented in Table 6B.1 from Browner et al.[32] and assuming a beta (ß) of 0.2 and a two-sided alpha (α) of 0.05, the calculated minimum sample size required for this study is 395.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented as means (± SD) for quantitative variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. The IC scores and IC domains across different frailty levels (FP and CFS) were analysed using Fisher exact test and Chi-square test respectively. The AUC-ROC, sensitivity and specificity were used to assess the discriminative ability of IC in relation to frailty and functional impairment. Lastly, the associations between IC (composite score and individual domains) and the different frailty measures were examined using multinomial logistic regression with adjustment for sociodemographic variables, social vulnerability factors and level of multimorbidity. In the regression analyses, robust participants were taken as the reference group. Odds ratio (ORs) with 95% confidence interval were calculated and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R studio (Ver. 2023.09.1 + 494, MacOS 13.4.1) using R-Base (4.2.2, MacOS 13.4.1).

Results

A total of 666 polyclinic patients were approached, of which 414 (62.2%) participants were recruited. Two participants were subsequently excluded as they did not fulfil the inclusion criteria and one participant was excluded due to incomplete data. A total of 411 participants were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the participants was 69.9 (± 6.2) years old and there were slightly more males (54.5%) than females. The majority were Chinese (81.3%), married (74.5%), and owned their own homes (85.9%). Table 1 describes the sociodemographic characteristics of the study sample in greater detail.

With regards to the level of multimorbidity, the mean number of chronic conditions was 4.0 (± 1.2). The mean HbA1c was 7.1 (± 1.1), mean blood pressure 137/72 (± 17/9) mmHg, and mean LDL-calculated 2.1 (± 0.7) mmol/L. 76.6% of participants were mFP pre-frail and 11.9% were mFP frail. The proportions of CFS pre-frail and CFS frail participants were almost half of that for mFP i.e., 38.7% and 7.5% respectively. 14.8% of participants had bADL impairment while 10.7% had iADL impairment. The mean IC score was 3.0 (± 0.9) and the IC domain with the most deficits was the sensory domain, with 83.9% of participants having hearing impairments, 46.2% with visual impairments, and 90.0% having impairments in one or both subdomains. Other significantly affected domains were locomotion (52.8%) and cognition (31.6%). The least affected domains were the psychological (10.9%) and vitality (10.7%) domains.

There was significant overlap between IC losses and frailty (refer to Table 2). Reduced IC score was present in almost all participants (97.6%) and those with poorer composite IC scores were more likely to be frail. However, the affected IC domains differed slightly between the two different frailty measures. Impaired vitality, locomotion, cognition and psychological domains were associated with mFP (p-value < 0.05) while impaired sensory, locomotion and cognition were associated with CFS (p-value < 0.05).

In the adjusted multinomial logistic regression models investigating the association of IC and frailty status (robust, pre-frail and frail) with robust as the reference category, IC score was still significantly associated with both mFP and CFS. As detailed in Table 3, higher IC scores were associated with lower odds of being mFP frail (OR 0.39, 95% C.I. 0.22 to 0.69, p = 0.001) and CFS frail (OR 0.45, 95% C.I. 0.25 to 0.79, p = 0.006) when compared to the robust group. The domains of vitality and locomotion remained significantly associated with mFP frail compared to mFP robust (OR 6.35, 95% C.I. 2.13 to 18.97, p < 0.001 and OR 4.25, 95% C.I. 1.02 to 17.70, p = 0.047, respectively). However, the cognition and psychological domains did not show significant associations (p-value > 0.05). In contrast, for the CFS, the sensory and locomotion domains maintained their association while the cognition domain did not. Specifically, individuals with impaired locomotion had higher odds of being CFS frail (OR 4.09, 95% C.I. 1.25 to 13.39, p = 0.020), while those with an impaired sensory domain had higher odds of being CFS pre-frail (OR 7.01, 95% C.I. 2.12 to 22.37, p < 0.001), relative to the robust group.

Additionally, several socio-cultural factors identified in the multinomial logistic regression models significantly influenced the relationship between the composite IC score and frailty (see Supplementary Table S1) and that between the IC domains and frailty (see Supplementary Table S2). These factors also varied according to the frailty measure used. Across both models, social participation, social network, loneliness, ethnicity and level of multimorbidity (number of chronic conditions) were associated with CFS but not mFP. Based on Table S1, individuals with higher social participation had lower odds of CFS pre-frailty and CFS frailty compared to the robust participants (OR 0.93, 95% C.I 0.90 to 0.96, p-value < 0.001 and OR 0.90, 95% C.I 0.85 to 0.95, p-value < 0.001, respectively). A similar trend was observed for those of Chinese ethnicity, with OR 0.38, 95% C.I 0.21 to 0.69, p-value = 0.002 for CFS pre-frailty and OR 0.18, 95% C.I 0.06 to 0.53, p-value = 0.004 for CFS frailty. Conversely, those with more multimorbidity had higher odds of CFS pre-frailty and CFS frailty (OR 1.43, 95% C.I 1.15 to 1.78, p = 0.002 and OR 1.95, 95% C.I 1.41 to 2.68, p-value < 0.001, respectively) and those with better social network were at higher odds of being CFS pre-frail (OR 1.08, 95% C.I 1.03–1.12, p-value < 0.001), relative to the robust group. The ORs and p-values for Table S1 were similar for Table S2. Additionally, Table S2 demonstrated that being lonely increased the odds of being CFS pre-frail compared to CFS robust, with OR 1.04, 95% C.I 1.04 to 3.32, p-value 0.036).

In comparison, only age and social network were associated with mFP. According to Table S1, older age was associated with higher odds of being mFP pre-frail (OR 1.13, 95% C.I 1.05 to 1.21, p-value < 0.001) and mFP frail (OR 1.15, 95% C.I 1.05 to 1.25, p-value = 0.002) while better social network was associated with lower odds of being mFP frail (OR 0.91, 95% C.I 0.84 to 0.99, p-value 0.045), compared to the robust group.

In the evaluation of the discriminative ability of IC in relation to frailty and functional impairment, the AUC-ROC values were as follows: 0.72 (95% C.I 0.65 to 0.78) for mFP, 0.74 (95% C.I 0.67 to 0.81) for CFS, 0.68 (95% C.I 0.61 to 0.75) for iADL, and 0.68 (95% C.I 0.63 to 0.71) for bADL. We also explored various IC cut-offs from 0 to 4 to identify participants who were frail and those with functional impairment. A cut-off score of 2 was established to identify frail individuals, specifically, those with scores of 0, 1 and 2 were classified as frail and those with scores of 4 and 5 were non-frail. This classification yielded a Youden Index of 0.37 for mFP, with sensitivity, specificity and false positive values of 59.2%, 77.9%, 22.1% respectively. In comparison, the CFS had a Youden Index of 0.38, with sensitivity, specificity and false positive values of 61.3%, 76.3% and 23.7% respectively (refer to Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

This study explored the level of IC and frailty in a high-risk subgroup of the primary care population, focusing on older adults with multimorbidity who were capable of walking independently. It also investigated how IC was associated with two commonly used frailty measurements representing different aspects of frailty. The results revealed that almost all the participants had reduced IC regardless of their frailty status and the most affected IC domains were the sensory domain followed by locomotion and cognition. While the composite IC score consistently correlated with frailty across different frailty measures and socio-cultural factors, the specific domains of IC that are linked to frailty can differ depending on these influencing factors. We also found that an IC cut-off score of 2 was able to discriminate moderately against the frailty and functional impairment, but with inadequate sensitivity and significant false positive results. Understanding these findings is crucial for tailoring screening strategies in resource-limited settings.

Given that a significant proportion of those rated as robust in the general population already had IC losses on screening [6, 7], it is unsurprising that almost all the primary care participants with multimorbidity had reduced IC. The most affected IC domain was the sensory domain and this is similar to the findings from the INSPIRE-ICOPE care programme [33] in which 68.1% and 50.6% of participants had losses in the vision and hearing domains respectively. However, our prevalence of hearing and visual impairments was much higher and this was likely due to our population of patients with the multimorbidity triad of diabetes, hypertension and hyperlipidemia [34, 35]. Additionally, our study found that the cognition and mobility domains were also commonly affected and this was similar to other studies, highlighting the importance of reduced mobility and cognitive abilities in older adults [36]. Our findings will help us plan and expand the resources required for implementation of IC screening and optimisation especially for these domains which will involve specialist referrals for further assessment.

Similar to other studies in the community dwelling population, we found that locomotion, vitality, and lower IC were associated with mFP frailty, which focuses on physical measures [6, 7, 13]. The mFP assessment includes handgrip strength and BMI, both of which are considered components of vitality [13, 37]. The assessment also includes gait speed which is part of functional mobility and locomotion. Given such overlap between the IC and FP domains, the significant association between the two is expected. In comparison, the sensory domain, locomotion and lower IC were associated with CFS frailty which serves as a global, function-oriented assessment tool [29]. Our findings emphasise the potential impact of impaired locomotion and sensory loss in the development of physical “slowing” and the decline in activities of daily living linked to frailty.

Due to the differences in the two frailty measures, it is unsurprising that the socio-cultural factors influencing the relationship between IC and frailty also varied depending on the frailty measure. Compared to the mFP, the CFS is a multidimensional tool based on the deficit-accumulation approach that directly incorporates social factors into its scoring, thus making it is more sensitive to psychosocial influences especially social vulnerability factors. Hanlon et al. demonstrated that individuals with CFS frailty had declining social participation and increasing loneliness over time [38]. In contrast the mFP focuses on physical deficits, it is strongly associated with age which is closely linked to physical decline [39]. Therefore, understanding the socio-cultural factors influencing these measures is essential for refining frailty screening methods that utilise IC.

Despite the significant associations between IC and frailty, our study showed only moderate discriminative abilities of IC and locomotion domain in differentiating frailty. This was characterised by significant false positive results and sensitivities that are insufficient for clinical application. Our findings differed from Ma et al., [40] who showed better results in hospitalised participants, despite both studies having similar prevalences of frailty. This suggests that various screening approaches are required for different patient populations. Although the ICOPE guideline suggests screening for frailty in those with impaired locomotion, the false positive rate in our primary care population with multimorbidity was more than 50%, indicating low yield of such screening.

Clinical implications

Patients with multimorbidity make up more than half of the primary care population with chronic diseases [12] and in our study, almost all of them had losses in IC. This raises concerns about the resources required to support IC screening, secondary assessment, management, and monitoring in primary care, especially if primary care were to support such screening in its own setting as well as in the community. To date, studies on the adoption of ICOPE screening [41] are still ongoing and it remains to be seen if the suggested IC workflow can be sustainably scaled up.

Instead of screening for frailty after screening for IC in the primary care setting, healthcare providers can consider reversing this order by screening for frailty first. This approach allows for subsequent identification of IC losses in individuals who are pre-frail and frail [11, 42], and may be feasible in the primary care setting for several reasons. Firstly, it leverages on existing frailty screening programmes that may already be in place at many primary care clinics [43, 44], facilitating a smoother integration into current practices. Secondly, early interventions aimed at reversing pre-frailty or preventing frailty in older adults who are pre-frail are more likely to be yield positive outcomes [42]. Thirdly, this approach may reduce the need for comprehensive geriatric assessments that are more suited for individuals who are already frail [42]. However, one disadvantage of this approach is the neglect of those assessed as robust. Although the risk of frailty progression in robust older adults is lower than those with pre-frailty, it remains significant, estimated at 10–20% over 1 to 2 years [6, 45] and may even be higher in those with multimorbidity. Despite this, there is limited evidence on the interventions that can effectively prevent frailty progression in robust older adults [46]. Given the constraints of healthcare resources and the challenges posed by an ageing population, healthcare providers and policy makers might consider a balanced approach that prioritises higher risk multimorbid adults with pre-frailty and frailty. Further studies are required to prove the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of this strategy.

An alternative approach could involve the incorporation of an individual’s interactions with their environment, focusing on easily quantifiable and measureable social factors, into the existing IC scores, thus providing a global assessment of the individual. This enhancement may improve sensitivity and specificity in identifying CFS frail patients. Additionally, it would maintain the comprehensive assessment and management of the ICOPE framework and facilitate care integration aimed at preventing declines in individual capacity (IC) and frailty. To achieve this, further research is essential to develop and validate the inclusion of social factors into the established ICOPE tool.

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this study is the use of different measurements of frailty, including the CFS which is easy to use in the busy primary care setting. Additionally, we used valid instruments e.g., JAMAR dynamometer to measure frailty and intrinsic capacity. However, this study also has a few limitations. Firstly, we sampled participants only from public polyclinics and did not include those from private primary care clinics. Polyclinics provide a wide range of highly subsidised services in-situ including medical, nursing, allied-health, laboratory and basic radiological services while private clinics, being individually owned or group-owned, have varying services and limited subsidies for patients. Compared to private clinic attendees, polyclinic attendees are older and more likely to have chronic diseases. This is supported by the 2014 Primary care survey which showed that 29% of polyclinic attendees were elderly compared to 11% in private clinics, and 52% of polyclinic consults were for chronic disease management, compared to 20% in private clinics [47]. Thus the representativeness of our primary care sample may be limited.

Secondly, our study exclusively included older participants who could communicate in English or Mandarin and this could have contributed to the under-representation of Malay and Tamil participants compared to the national population. Notably, Malay and Tamil older adults have higher rates of frailty compared to the Chinese [48] and the under sampling of non-Chinese ethnic groups may limit the applicability of our findings. Additionally, we employed convenience sampling to recruit our participants and that could also have affected the generalizability of our findings. To address potential selection bias, we recruited participants from three different primary care clinics, ensuring that the sociodemographic characteristics of the study population were similar to that of the townships served by the clinics. Lastly, as the study was cross-sectional in nature, causal relationships could not be examined.

Conclusions

Our findings highlighted the very high prevalence of IC losses regardless of frailty status and complex relationship between IC and frailty that is influenced by socio-cultural factors. Additionally, we observed the inadequate sensitivity and significant false positive results from screening for frailty using IC score or assessing locomotion. This suggests that the use of IC to concurrently screen for frailty may not be applicable in an at-risk primary care population. It is essential to investigate various integrated screening methods to optimise and streamline the process of identifying individuals with reduce intrinsic capacity and frailty.
