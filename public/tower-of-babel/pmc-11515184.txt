Treatment with oclacitinib, a Janus kinase inhibitor, down-regulates and up-regulates CD25 and Foxp3 expression, respectively, in peripheral blood T cells of dogs with atopic dermatitis
Authors: Agnieszka Jasiecka-Mikołajczyk, Piotr Socha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOclacitinib (OCL), a Janus kinase inhibitor, is a novel immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive agent which is an approved as the first-line treatment for atopic dermatitis (AD) in dogs. The aim of the study was to investigate the effects of OCL on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and their selected subsets under clinical conditions, i.e. in dogs suffering from AD, in terms of both safety and immune mechanisms underlying its therapeutic actions. Eight dogs were treated for 28 days with OCL at the recommended dose. Blood samples were taken at day 0, 7, 14 and 28.ResultsThe study showed that the mean percentage and absolute count of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells on the 14th and 28th day of the treatment with OCL did not differ from the corresponding baseline values, i.e. those before the treatment. On the 7th day of the treatment, the mean absolute count of CD4+ T cells and the mean percentage and absolute count of CD8+ T cells were significantly increased. The research found that on the 14th day of the treatment, the mean percentage and absolute count of CD25+CD4+ and CD25+CD8+ T cells were significantly decreased; the reduction in the percentage of CD25+CD4+ T cells persisted on 28th day of the treatment. A two-week treatment with OCL resulted in an increase in the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells, and this effect was sustained at the last time point. The treatment with OCL decreased the eosinophil level but does not affect the absolute counts of basophils, monocytes and neutrophils.ConclusionsThe findings of the study strongly suggest that: (a) in terms of the impact of OCL on the number of PB CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, monthly treatment with the drug should be considered as a relatively safe; (b) the eosinophil-reducing effect and the down-regulation of the AD up-regulated CD25 expression on CD4+ Teff cells may constitute significant elements of the mechanism of action underlying the therapeutic effects of the drug in the treatment of canine AD; (c) the generation of inducible Foxp3-expressing CD4+ regulatory T cells - resulting in the shift of the CD4+ Treg cell (i.e. Foxp3+CD4+)/activated Teff (i.e. CD25+CD4+) cell balance toward an increased proportion of Treg cells - may be considered as additional mechanism involved in producing the immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive properties of OCL.Keywords: Oclacitinib, CD4+ cells, CD8+ cells, Treg cells, Atopic dermatitis, Dogs

Background

Oclacitinib (OCL) is a drug that modulates inflammatory and pruritogenic cytokines through the inhibition of the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of the transcription (JAK/STAT) signalling pathway [1]. OCL is an approved first-line treatment drug for atopic dermatitis (AD) and pruritus associated with allergic dermatitis in adult dogs [2]. OCL is classified as both an immunomodulator [3] and immunosuppressant [4]. Typically, immunosuppression induced by immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive agents can be a double-edged sword, i.e. on the one hand, it interrupts the pathogenesis of allergic diseases or eliminates/relieves symptoms of these diseases; on the other hand, it can increase the patient’s susceptibility to severity of microbial infections. The producer of the only registered preparation containing OCL (Apoquel, Zoetis; Belgium) does not mention in the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) [5] that the administration of this drug is associated with the elevated susceptibility to such infections. However, safety studies performed on dogs received high OCL doses provided clinical evidence of immunosuppression, including the manifestation of adverse effects of bacterial pneumonia and generalized demodex mange infections [4]. What is more, pyoderma, a bacterial infection of the skin [6, 7], is a very common adverse effect of applying OCL [5]. The above facts suggest that the administration of OCL can lead to immunosuppression and consequently to increased susceptibility to microbial and parasitic infections. Our previous research indicates that one of the mechanisms underlying the immunosuppressive properties of OCL (understood as an undesirable effect of the drug) can arise from the depletive effect of the drug on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [8]. Based on these results, a hypothesis was put forward, suggesting that this drug can cause depletion of these cells as well as B cells in lymphoid tissues and peripheral blood (PB). This hypothesis was verified in an experimental study, which revealed that OCL induced depletion of T cells in the lymphoid tissues [9]. The results of both studies strongly suggested that the treatment of dogs with OCL might result in depletion of T cells. Therefore, we decided to verify this assumption under clinical conditions, i.e. in dogs with AD treated with OCL. To sum up, the first aim of this study was to broaden the knowledge and understanding of the effects of OCL on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in terms of safety (i.e. a possible undesired effect on T cell-mediated immunity), which in turn can improve the benefit-risk assessment of using this agent in dogs, especially those with immunosuppression and microbial infections.

JAK2 (and to a lesser degree JAK3) is a target for OCL [1]. It is important to note that the inhibition of JAK enzymes is only an initial event in the mechanism of action of OCL, which triggers a cascade of different events underlying the therapeutic properties of OCL [8, 10]. The results of these studies [8] strongly suggest that the impairment of CD4+ T cell activation - via down-regulation of the expression of α-chain (CD25) of the receptor complex for interleukin (IL) 2 on these cells - may constitute one of such mechanisms. Therefore, the second aim of the present study was to verify under clinical conditions the effect of OCL on CD25 expression on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.

Immune tolerance is an important mechanism counteracting the development of allergies and autoimmune diseases. Foxp3 (Forkhead Box P3 protein)-expressing CD4+ T regulatory cells (hereinafter referred to as CD4+ Treg cells) play a critical role in establishing and maintaining self-tolerance and immune homeostasis. There is a growing body of evidence indicating that Foxp3-expressing CD8+ Treg cells (hereinafter referred to as CD8+ Treg cells), also play a role in maintaining immune tolerance [11, 12]. Treg cells can be divided into two principal subsets: naturally occurring Tregs (nTregs) and induced (iTreg) Treg cells [13–15]. It should be pointed out that Foxp3 not only is a marker of Treg cells, but it also confers a suppressive capacity to conventional T cells [16].

Some studies suggest that CD4+ and CD8+ Treg cells that CD4+ and CD8+ Treg cells may represent a promising therapeutic approach to the treatment of allergic disorders, including AD [17, 18]. Therefore, the induction of the generation of iTreg cells may constitute a potential treatment in patients with such diseases. In general, the JAK/STAT signalling pathway is involved in the initiation and maintenance of Foxp3 expression in CD4+ Treg cells [19]. However, our preliminary in vitro study demonstrated that OCL paradoxically up-regulated Foxp3 expression in these cells (Fig. 1). Hence, the third aim of this study was to verify under clinical conditions the hypothesis that the generation of CD4+ and/or CD8+ iTreg cells might constitute an additional mechanism, i.e. acting independently of the inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway, responsible for the immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive properties of OCL.

Results

OCL increases the percentage Foxp3+CD4+ and Foxp3+CD8+ T cells under activation conditions

The preliminary research demonstrated that exposure of canine PBMCs under activation conditions to OCL in both concentrations caused a significant increase in the percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1A). Similarly, the exposure of cells to the higher concentration of OCL resulted in an increased percentage of Foxp3+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1C); moreover, a strong trend (p = 0.06) toward increasing this parameter was found at the lower concentration of the drug (Fig. 1C). Although the treatment with OCL did not affect significantly the absolute count of Foxp3+CD4+ and Foxp3+CD8+ T cells, it is worth noting that the mean values of these parameters were by 40% (10− 6 M), 27% (10− 7 M), 25% (10− 6 M) and 30% (10− 6 M) greater, respectively, than the corresponding control values (Fig. 1B and D).

Treatment with OCL does not deplete CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in PB

The hypothesis that treatment of dogs with OCL may result in the depletion of T cells in PB was disproved. The study showed that the mean percentage and absolute count of CD4+ (Fig. 2A, A’, B and B’) and CD8+ (Fig. 2C, C’, D and D’) T cells on the 14th and 28th day of the treatment with OCL did not differ considerably from the corresponding baseline values (day 0). Interestingly, on the 7th day of the treatment, the mean absolute count of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 2B’) and the mean percentage and absolute count of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2C’ and D’) were significantly higher compared to the baseline values. Thus, it can be concluded that the treatment with OCL resulted only in a rapid but short-lasting and relatively small increase in the absolute number of PB CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. It is worth noting, however, that a trend (p = 0.072) toward increasing the absolute count of CD4+ T cells was observed on the 28th day of the treatment (Fig. 2B’).

Treatment with OCL results in the reduction of the percentage and absolute counts of CD25-expressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, but this effect does not occur immediately

Another hypothesis that OCL may down-regulate CD25 expression on T cells in dogs suffering from AD was confirmed. The research demonstrated that on the 14th day of the treatment with OCL, the mean percentage of CD25+CD4+ and CD25+CD8+ T cells was significantly decreased (Fig. 3A, A’, C and C’) compared to the baseline values What is more, the mean absolute count of CD25+CD4+ and CD25+CD8+ T cells was also considerably reduced at this time point (Fig. 3B, B’, D and D’). Furthermore, the reduction in the percentage of CD25+CD4+ T cells persisted on 28th day of the treatment (Fig. 3B’). It should be noted that although the mean absolute count of CD25+CD4+ T cells at this time point was not affected in a statistically significant manner, the value of this parameter was about 22% lower than the baseline value and a certain trend (p = 0.20) toward decreasing this parameter could be found (Fig. 3B’). Similarly, although the mean percentage of CD25+CD8+ T cells on 28th day of the treatment was not altered statistically significantly, the value of this parameter was about 30% lower than the baseline value and also a certain trend (p = 0.164) toward decreasing this parameter could be observed (Fig. 3C’). Interestingly, on the 7th day of the treatment, the mean absolute count of CD25+CD4+ T cells was significantly elevated compared to the baseline value (Fig. 3B); however, this effect was not accompanied by an increase in the percentage of these cells (Fig. 3A’). The 7-days treatment had no effect on either the percentage or the absolute count of CD25+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3C, C’, D and D’).

Administration of OCL induces an increase in the percentage and absolute count of Foxp3+CD4+ cells, but this effect appears with some delay

The last hypothesis tested in the study was that OCL may generate Foxp3-expressing CD4+ and CD8+ iTreg cells. The results indicate that this hypothesis was partially positively verified. A two-week treatment with OCL resulted in a significant increase in the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells compared to the baseline value, and this effect was sustained after the termination of the treatment (Fig. 4A and A’). However, the magnitude of this effect on the 14th day was weaker than on the 28th day of the treatment, as the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T was increased only by about 32% relative to the baseline value. It should also be noted that the increase in the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T was not accompanied by an increase in the mean absolute count of these cells (Fig. 4B and B’) although a certain trend (p = 0.09) toward increasing the value of this parameter was observed. The increase in the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells was expressed more strongly on the 28th day of the treatment, as the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells increased by about 80% compared to the baseline value. Moreover, this effect was accompanied by a significant increase in the mean absolute count of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4B’); the mean value of this parameter was increased by about 104% compared to the baseline value. Interestingly, the increase in the mean percentage of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4A and A’) was preceded by an increase in the mean absolute count of these cells (Fig. 4B and B’) as well as Foxp3+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4D and D’) because the values of these parameters were considerably higher than the corresponding baseline values on the 7th day of the treatment. The increases in the mean absolute count of Foxp3+CD4+ and Foxp3+CD8+ T cells were not accompanied by increases in the mean percentages of these cells (Fig. 4A, A’, C and C’).

A 4-week treatment with OCL decreases the eosinophil level but does not affect the absolute counts of basophils, monocytes and neutrophils

The administration of OCL resulted in a significant increase in the mean percentage of lymphocytes at all time points (Fig. 5A and A’), and a decline in the mean percentage of monocytes (Fig. 5C and C’) and neutrophils (Fig. 5E and E’) on the 7th and 28th day of the treatment. However, the mean and individual (with a few exceptions) values of these parameters were still within the normal range. Moreover, the OCL-induced changes in the mean percentage of lymphocytes, monocytes and neutrophils were not accompanied by significant changes in the mean absolute count of these cells, with one exception (Fig. 5B, B’, D, D’, F and F’). Namely, the mean absolute count of lymphocytes on the 7th of the treatment was higher than the baseline value, but it was still within the normal range (Fig. 5B’).

Before the treatment, the mean absolute count of eosinophils almost reached the upper limit of normal range and the administration of OCL significantly decreased the value of this parameter at all time points (by 32%, 45% and 41%, respectively; Fig. 5H and H’). Discussion

In the present study, it was hypothesized that the treatment of dogs with OCL may result in the depletion of T cells. However, this hypothesis was disproved as the treatment with OCL did not reduce the absolute count of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in PB. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report on the effect of OCL on the absolute count of these cells in dogs. De Caro Martins et al. [20] investigated the effect of OCL on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in PB of dogs suffering from AD, but only with respect to their percentage. In the cited study, it was found that a 60-days treatment with the OCL did not affect the percentage of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which is generally consistent with the results of the present study obtained after a 28-days treatment with the drug. There should be no doubt that the absolute count represents a more reliable parameter than the percentage for the assessment whether some agent/factor exerts a depletive effect on particular lymphocyte subset/s, because such effect/s will always be reflected by this parameter. It is not the same when using percentage data. If some agent induces the depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and B cells of the same/similar magnitude, then their proportions are not affected and hence such changes may not be captured by a percentage analysis.

The obtained results strongly suggest that in terms of the impact of OCL on the number of PB CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, monthly treatment with the drug should be considered as a relatively safe. However, it is important to underline that this conclusion cannot be extrapolated to a potential effect of OCL on T cells in lymphoid and non-lymphoid organs. In our previous study [9], it was found that a 2-week treatment of mice with OCL induced a loss of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in lymphoid organs, while - similarly to the present results - it did not affect the absolute count of these cells in PB. Therefore, on the basis of the cited results, it can be concluded that a lack of influence of OCL on the number of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in PB of dogs does not necessarily preclude such influence in other immune compartments of the body.

Interestingly, a transient and small in magnitude increase in the absolute number of PB CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was observed during the treatment with OCL. There is no logical and scientific basis to assume that this effect was due to the stimulation of proliferation [10]. The only possible explanation is that this effect was caused by the redistribution of these cells from lymph nodes and/or other lymphoid tissues. However, such an action would have to be caused by the loss/downregulation of homing receptors, which are responsible for lymphocyte homing to lymph nodes, Peyer’s patches (i.e. L-selectin or α4β7 integrin molecules, respectively) or other lymphoid tissues. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are no reports in the available literature connecting the inhibition of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway with the down-regulation of the aforementioned homing receptors.

The treatment with OCL did not affect the absolute counts of basophils, lymphocytes monocytes and neutrophils; the only exception was an increase in the absolute count of lymphocytes at one time point. Therefore, it can be concluded that the changes in the percentages of monocytes, neutrophils and lymphocytes induced by OCL were just relative, i.e. did not result from actual changes in the number of these cells, but were a consequence of some alterations in the mutual proportions of these cell subsets. All these changes were only slightly expressed, and the mean and individual (with a few exceptions) values of particular parameters were still within the normal range. Therefore, it can be concluded that a 4-week treatment with OCL did not affect the number of basophils, lymphocytes monocytes and neutrophils in a clinically relevant manner. These results are relatively consistent with the information included in the SmPC of Apoquel [5]. The manufacturer states that although treatment with OCL induces a decrease in the absolute count of eosinophils, neutrophils and monocytes, all the values of these parameters remain within the laboratory reference range and changes are not clinically significant.

The study demonstrated that the treatment with OCL significantly decreased the percentage and absolute count of eosinophils. It should be noted that before the treatment the mean absolute count of eosinophils almost reached the upper limit of normal range and these cells play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of skin lesions and pruritis in patients with AD [21, 22]. In the light of these facts, the eosinophil-lowering effect of OCL should not be considered as negative, but rather as the effect which may contribute to the beneficial influence of the drug in the treatment of AD.

CD25, i.e. IL-2Rα, expression on T cells can be divided into constitutive (i.e. stable) and inducible expression. In mice, constitutive CD25 expression on T cells is correlated with Foxp3 co-expression and therefore with the Treg phenotype. In dogs, same as previously demonstrated in cattle [23], a certain pool of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells also expresses CD25 constitutively, although it is not correlated with Foxp3 co-expression; a role of constitutive CD25 expression on Foxp3-negative T cells is not elucidated. In turn, the inducible CD25 expression takes place during the activation of effector T (Teff) cells but this effect occurs transiently; it enables the effective binding of IL-2 to Teff cells, increasing their responsiveness to IL-2 [24]. Therefore, CD25 is considered to be an early T cell activation marker.

Undoubtedly, the number of activated CD4+ T cells is increased in the PB of humans suffering from AD [25]; an increased number of CD4+ T cells with increased expression of CD25 was noted in the PB of these patients [26]. A recent study also demonstrated an increased number of CD25-expressing CD4+ T cells in the PB of dogs with AD [27]. Moreover, an increased percentage of CD8+ T cells in the PB of dogs with AD was found, which suggests the contribution of these cells to the development of the disease [28]. The present study demonstrated that administration of OCL led to a reduction in the percentage and absolute counts of CD25-expressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Thus, these results indicate that the treatment with OCL down-regulated CD25 expression on Teff cells. This finding opens the door to new questions. Firstly, did OCL down-regulate constitutive or activation-induced CD25 expression? Unfortunately, we have no tools to explore that issue. Nevertheless, our previous in vitro studies demonstrated that OCL did not affect constitutive CD25 expression on canine T cells, but reduced the activation-induced CD25 expression on CD4+ T cells [8]. In the light of these results, it may be assumed that the treatment with OCL decreased the disease up-regulated CD25 expression on T cells. Another question is whether OCL directly down-regulated CD25 (i.e. down-regulated CD25 gene expression) or whether this effect was indirectly induced, i.e. it was as a consequence of decreased T cell activation due to the inhibition of the JAK/STAT signalling pathway by the drug. Answering this question requires further investigations. Notwithstanding this, the results obtained so far strongly suggest that the down-regulation of the disease up-regulated CD25 expression on CD4+ Teff cells (and possibly on CD8+ Teff cells) may constitute a significant element of the mechanism of action underlying the therapeutic effects of the drug in the treatment of canine AD.

The research revealed that the treatment with OCL increased the percentage and absolute count of Foxp3+CD4+ T cells, although, objectively speaking, the scale of this effect was relatively small. Nevertheless, this finding suggests that OCL may induce Foxp3 expression in Foxp3-negative CD4+ T cells, and thereby induce/promote conversion of non-regulatory CD4+ T cells into iTreg. In addition, taking into consideration that the percentages of CD25+CD4+ and Foxp3+CD4+ T cells at the same time points were decreased and increased, respectively, it can be stated that treatment with OCL may shift the CD4+ Treg cell/activated Teff cell balance toward an increased proportion of CD4+ Treg cells. The increase in the mean absolute count of Foxp3+CD8+ T cells at the second time point was not accompanied by an increase in the percentage of these cells, but was associated with an increase in the absolute count of total lymphocyte population. Therefore, this effect shall not be interpreted as a result of the OCL-induced increase in Foxp3 expression in Foxp3-negative CD8+ T cells, but rather as a result of the redistribution of Foxp3+CD8+ T cells from lymphoid tissues into the PB. To conclude, the results of the study suggest that the generation of inducible Foxp3-expressing CD4+ regulatory T cells may constitute additional mechanism, i.e. acting independently of the inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway, underlying the therapeutic efficacy of OCL. However, this effect potentially may have a negative side, as Foxp3+CD4+ Treg cells are known to inhibit the anti-cancer immunity [29].

Conclusion

In summary, the results of the present study indicate that a 4-week treatment with OCL did not affect the number of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells or basophils, monocytes and neutrophils in a clinically relevant manner. These findings strongly suggest that: (a) in terms of the impact of OCL on the number of PB CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, monthly treatment with the drug should be considered as a relatively safe; (b) the eosinophil-reducing effect and the down-regulation of the AD up-regulated CD25 expression on CD4+ Teff cells may constitute significant elements of the mechanism of action underlying the therapeutic effects of the drug in the treatment of canine AD; (c) the generation of inducible Foxp3-expressing CD4+ regulatory T cells - resulting in the shift of the CD4+ Treg cell (i.e. Foxp3+CD4+)/activated Teff (i.e. CD25+CD4+) cell balance toward an increased proportion of Treg cells - may be considered as additional mechanism involved in producing the immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive properties of OCL.

Limitation of the study

The primary limitation of this study is that the sample size is relatively small (n = 8), which might limit the robustness and generalizability of the findings. The second limitation is that patient the participants were from only one clinic. The third limitation of the research is the short length of the study period. In order to obtain more reliable results, the study should be replicated with larger number of patients treated with OCL for longer period of time.

Materials and methods

Animals and OCL administration - in vivo study

The research presented in this article was neither experimental study nor clinical trial. All participating dogs were patients treated with OCL for AD. The diagnosis of AD was based on patient history, clinical signs using the criteria described by Favrot et al. [30], and exclusion of other possible skin dermatoses that can present as AD. Characteristics of dogs and the number assigned to each dog are shown in Table 1. All dogs were orally treated with OCL maleate (Apoquel) at a dose of 0.4 mg/kg body weight twice a day for the first 14 days, and then once a day at the same dose for another 14 days. The dosage regimen was based on the recommendations of the manufacturer [5]. PB was collected before OCL administration (day 0) and 7, 14 and 28 days after the first dose of the drug. PB samples were collected for regular monitoring of complete blood count (CBC) on request by the attending veterinary doctor. CBC was performed using a hematology analyzer Advia 2120i; the reference values were provided by the manufacturer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Erlangen, Germany).

Animals, isolation of lymphocytes and culture conditions - in vitro study

PB was obtained from fifteen clinically healthy dogs [Australian Shepherds (n = 6, age 2 to 12 years); Big Swiss Shepherd (n = 4, age 2 to 5 years) and German Shepherd (n = 5, age 1 to 5 years); overall n = 15]. PBMCs were separated by Histopaque 1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, Schnelldorf, Germany) density gradient centrifugation as previously described [31]. In each experiment, PBMCs were exposed to OCL (Cayman Chemical, Michigan, USA) in concentrations reflecting its plasma levels achieved in vivo at a typical dose (10− 6 M) [32] and in a ten-fold lower concentration (10− 7 M). OCL was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich), therefore the same amount of DMSO was added to control wells. PBMCs were diluted to a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL in complete medium [31], seeded in 24-well plates and cultured in the absence (control) or presence of OCL. In order to stimulate T cell activation, PBMCs were stimulated with phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin as previously described [33]. Each experiment was repeated three times independently. The experiments included 6 (1st experiment), 4 (2nd experiment) and 5 (3rd experiment) wells of PBMCs (for individual dogs) for each condition tested.

Flow cytometry

Extracellular staining

100 µl of whole blood was transferred into flow cytometry tubes and the following fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were added for surface antigen staining: anti-canine CD4 FITC (1:40; clone YKIX302.9), anti-canine CD8a APC (1:40; clone YCATE55.9) and anti-canine CD25 PE (1:20; clone P4A10; all from eBioscience, San Diego, USA). Erythrocytes were removed using BD Pharm Lyse™ Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In turn, culture samples were harvested as previously described [33] and stained with the above listed fluorochrome-conjugated mAbs. After 45 min incubation (on ice and in the dark), the cells were washed in staining buffer.

Intracellular staining

Following surface staining, the cells were labelled for Foxp3 expression using a Foxp3 Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBiosciences), as previously described by other investigators [34]. Briefly, the cells were fixed with Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer for 12 h at 4 ⁰C in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were washed and permeabilized with Permeabilization Buffer for 30 min at RT. Then, the cells were washed with staining buffer and stained with anti-mouse/rat Foxp3 PerCP-Cyanine5.5 (1:20; clone FJK-16 S; eBiosciences) for 60 min at RT in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Although Foxp3-expressing Treg cells are typically identified as cells representing the phenotype Foxp3+CD25+CD4+, it should to be pointed out that this phenotype concerns murine Treg cells. Foxp3 confers a suppressive function to these cells [35], while CD25 is not an absolute requirement for suppressive ability [36]. CD25 is expressed constitutively on T cells, but also is extensively up-regulated on these cells upon activation [23]. Therefore, this molecule represents an early T cell activation marker.

In their fundamental work, Biller et al. [34] reported that Foxp3 expression was used as the only marker to identify Treg cells in dogs. Moreover, our analysis of the distribution of Foxp3+CD25+, Foxp3+CD25− and Foxp3−CD25+ cells within CD4+ T cells demonstrated that these subsets constituted 4.3%, 0.64% and 2.8%, respectively (Fig. 6). Thus, Foxp3+CD25− T cells represented a 6.68-fold more numerous subset than Foxp3+CD25+ T cells. In light of this finding, it can be stated that CD25 expression is not correlated with Foxp3 expression in canine CD4+ T cells. Taking above into consideration, we decided in the past study [33] and in the present one to identify canine Treg cells only as Foxp3+CD4+ and Foxp3+CD8+ T cells.

FACS acquisition and analysis

Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a FACSCelesta cytometer (BD Biosciences) and Fig. 7 presents the flow cytometric gating strategy. The data were acquired by FACSDiva version 9.0 software (BD Biosciences) and analyzed by FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc., Stanford, USA). Absolute cell counts of lymphocyte subsets (i.e. number of cells from a particular subpopulation per µL of sample) were calculated using the dual platform method, i.e. the absolute cell count was determined by calculating the data obtained from CBC (PB) or a cell counting chamber (in vitro study) by the percentage of particular cell subsets (the data from flow cytometric immunophenotyping).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented for individual dogs and as a mean ± SD. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test (when data were not normally distributed) or paired t-test (normally distributed data) were used for comparison of values obtained from the in vivo study. Student’s unpaired t-test was used in the in vitro study for comparison of control vs. OCL group. Differences were deemed significant when the p values were < 0.05. The data were graphed with Sigmaplot software (version 12, Systat Software, Inc, Chicago, USA).
