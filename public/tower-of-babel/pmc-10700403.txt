A combination of all-trans retinoic acid derivative and COX-2 inhibitor has anticancer effects in human pharyngeal carcinoma cells
Authors: Le Zhu, Lei Xiong, Jianshang Huang, Chonggui Jiang, Wentao Xu, Jing Zhang, Chaojie Hu, Ying Zhong, Zijian Dong, Feihu Chen, Huaqing Zhu, Wei-Ting Kuo, Feng Cao, Li Zuo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Backgrounds and aimsCarcinogenesis is characterized by an unlimited growth of cells exacerbated by Cox-2 overexpression. Cox-2 inhibitors have been proven effective in preventing and treating tumors. In our previous studies, we found that 4-Amino-2-Trifluoromethylphenyl Retinate (ATPR) induces cell apoptosis and inhibits cell proliferation to exhibit anti-cancer properties. The use of ATRA as well as Cox-2 inhibitors in clinical settings can cause adverse reactions. It is unknown what the effects and mechanisms of co-administration of ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors are.ResultsA combination of ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors, Celecoxib, inhibited pharyngeal cancer cell proliferation in vitro and induced apoptosis. The cell cycle was arrested at G0/G1 by activating P53 and CDNA1. By activating MAPK/JNK pathways, ATPR and Celecoxib led to intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis in pharyngeal cancer cells. ATPR/Celecoxib combined treatment suppressed tumor growth in the pharyngeal cancer cell-derived xenograft mouse model by increasing the number of apoptotic cells. The expression of the RARA and PTGS2 genes was significantly increased in tumor tissue compared to non-tumor tissue in the clinical analysis of the head and neck squamous cell carcinoma dataset. An association was found between this and the level of intrinsic apoptotic signals. Furthermore, a survival analysis conducted over a period of five years indicated that higher levels of RARA expression were associated with a better clinical outcome.ConclusionATPR and celecoxib inhibit the proliferation of cancer cells as well as induce apoptosis. Co-administration of ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors has the potential to be a novel treatment plan for cancer.

Introduction

Pharyngeal carcinoma is a rare disease affecting a small number of people with head and neck cancer [1]. As a result of the growing consumption of tobacco and alcohol over the past few decades, the incidence of this disease has increased [2]. It is thought that pharyngeal cancer behaves more aggressively and is more difficult to detect early than laryngeal cancer, leading to a remarkably poor prognosis [3]. A poor prognosis is attributed to advanced primary disease, a high rate of locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, and second primary malignancy [4,5]. The development of new treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [6], has taken place in recent years. There has, however, been no noticeable improvement in the survival rates of patients with pharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HSCC) [7].

Studies have shown that cyclooxygenase-2 (Cox-2) is overexpressed in human pharyngeal carcinoma [8,9], and that specific inhibitors of Cox-2 reduce the growth of pharyngeal carcinoma cells [10,11]. Cox-2 inhibitors have been identified as new therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of cancer [12,13]. Inhibitors such as celecoxib (CXB) have been shown to reduce the risk of breast cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and liver cancer [14,15]. There are several adverse effects associated with these inhibitors, including gastrointestinal disorders, headaches, and dizziness [16]. In the prevention and treatment of cancer, Cox-2 inhibitors may reduce their adverse effects by combining them with other anti-cancer medications.

A novel derivative of all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), 4-amino-2-trifluoromethyl-phenylretinate (ATPR), has been demonstrated to exert anticancer properties at low doses by inducing cell cycle arrest and p53/ASPP1 mediated apoptosis [17,18]. Besides promoting the differentiation of various solid tumor cells, ATRA and its derivatives also inhibit the proliferation of lung, breast, liver, and gastric cancer cells, which may related to CRABP2 and FABP5 [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Arsenic trioxide has been successfully treated with ATRA in the clinic with high efficacy and reduced hematological toxicity [[23], [24], [25]]. Clinical trials have recently examined the use of retinoic acid in combination with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies to treat advanced melanoma [26]. The results suggest that ATPR may be able to have a therapeutic effect when combined with other drugs, such as Cox-2 inhibitors.

The purpose of this study is to determine whether ATPR enhances the anti-cancer effects of Cox-2 inhibitors, such as Celecoxib. We evaluated the effects and mechanisms of co-administration of Celecoxib and ATPR on proliferation and apoptosis in hypopharyngeal FaDu and nasopharyngeal CNE-2 cancer cells in this study. In addition to inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, both drugs induce apoptosis in cancer cells. These drugs induced intrinsic apoptosis via increasing expression of BAX, cleaved caspase-8, and cleaved caspase-3, which are associated with MAPK/JNK signaling. A xenograft of hypopharyngeal FaDu cells was performed on nude mice to determine the in vivo effects of both drugs on the suppression of tumor growth. With ATPR and Celecoxib treatment, the tumor size was significantly reduced, accompanied by an increase in cleaved caspase-3 expression. Additionally, higher levels of ATRA receptors in the clinic were associated with a higher five-year survival rate. This study suggests that ATPR and Celecoxib may induce cancer cells to undergo apoptosis, which may be applied to cancer treatment in the future.

Materials and method

Mice and ARRIVE guidelines

All studies were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Anhui Medical University. SCSK (Jing) 2016-0006 was the license number. Nude mice were obtained from Beijing Weitong Lihua Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Peking, China). Live mice aged six to ten weeks were used in the study. For all experiments, mice of both sexes were used. The figure legends provide information regarding the number of mice used in each study. A minimum of three experiments using mice were conducted with similar results.

Cell culture

FaDu (HTB-43) cells were established in 1968 after a punch biopsy of a hypopharyngeal tumor was taken from a 56-year-old, White, male patient with squamous cell carcinoma. CNE-2 (XY-XB-1691) cells were isolated from a 68-year-old male patient with a nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Cancer cell lines with epithelial morphology are provided by the ATCC. A humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 was used to grow both cells in MEM culture media (Gibco, Cat#11095080) containing 10 % FBS (Gibco, Cat#10099).

4-amino-2-trifluoromethylphenyl retinoate (ATPR) (Anhui Medical University) (25 μM) and/or Celecoxib (25 and 50 μM) (Peking, China, Solarbio Co., Ltd., Cat#169590-42-5) was added 48 h for respective analysis. The specific JNK/MAPK inhibitor SP600125 (Cat#129-56-6) and the protein kinase C (PKC) activator phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Cat# HY-18739) were purchased from MedChemExpress.

Cell proliferation assay

In 96 well plates, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml. During the second day of culture, cells were treated with ATPR (25 μM), Celecoxib (25 μM, 50 μM) for 48 h; after this, cells were incubated in 20 μL of 5 mg/ml MTT (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#M2128) for 4 h. After discarding the media, DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#67-68-5) was added to dissolve the MTT crystals. A spectrophotometer was then used to measure the absorbance at 570 nm.

TUNEL assay

In accordance with manufacturer's instructions, dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays (Roche, Cat#11684795910) were used to detect double-strand DNA breaks in drugs-induced cells. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1800×g for 10 min, washed in PBS, and then fixed in in 4 % (w/v) paraformaldehyde (Merck, Cat#200505-35-7) with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (Merck, Cat#6131-99-3), pH 7.4, for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were permeabilized for 5 min with 0.1 % Triton X-100 with 0.1 % sodium citrate. Incubation was conducted at 37 °C 1 h in the dark with a TUNEL reaction mix containing fluorescein-conjugated dUTP and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase. TUNEL-positive cells were determined by image analysis using a Zeiss microscope.

Flow cytometric analysis

A flow cytometric analysis was carried out on cells dual stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) (Invitrogen, Cat#BMS500FI-20) to identify apoptotic cells. ATPR and Celecoxib were administered for 48 h, followed by cell harvesting and three washes with ice-cold PBS. After dilution to 1 × 106 cells/ml with 1X Annexin binding buffer, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. A PI stain was applied to the cells following incubation. In the end, data were collected by using a flow cytometer (BD). In order to determine cell proliferation, PI was applied to the cells, and the phase of the cell cycle was determined using a BD flow cytometer (BestBio). An analysis of these data was performed using Flowjo 7.61.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

An RT-qPCR analysis was performed in order to determine the mRNA levels of TP53, CCND1, RB. Total RNA was extracted from FaDu cells, which were treated with either ATPR or Celecoxib for 48 h at 37 °C using RNAiso Plus (Takara Biotechnology, Cat#9109). A total of 500 g of RNA was reverse transcribed using the 5X PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Biotechnology, Cat#RR036A). The cDNAs were amplified using Takara Biotechnology's TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (Tli RNaseH Plus, Cat#RR82LR). As shown in Table 1, all primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co. The thermocycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. During the melting process: 95 °C was applied for 5 s followed by 60 °C for 1 min. Western blot analysis

FaDu cells were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer for 30 min. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were mixed with SDS sample buffer, boiled for 7 min, and then separated on 10, 12, or 15 % SDS-PAGE gels. After blotting, membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies (Cell Signaling) against JNK (Cat#4672), p-JNK (Cat#58328), p-p38 (Cat#4511), p38 (Cat#8690), ERK (Cat#4695), p-ERK (Cat#4370), P53 (Cat#48818), CDN1A (p21) (Cat#2947), cleaved caspase-3 (Cat#9661), cleaved caspase-8 (Cat#8592), cleaved caspase-9 (Cat#20350), BCL2 (Cat#15071), BAX (Cat#41162) and β-actin (ACTB) (Cat#4970) (dilution of all primary antibodies 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation with the secondary antibody (Millipore), the target proteins were visualized using ECL (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Cat#32106). Densitometry was measured using Quantity One.

Xenograft mouse model

An injection of 1 × 107 cells/100 μl was made into each BALB/c nude mouse at 4 weeks of age. The mice were divided into six groups after one week, including a sham group (saline), ATPR (25 mg/kg), Celecoxib (25 mg/kg), ATPR (12.5 mg/kg) plus Celecoxib (12.5 mg/kg). Each group of mice received 200 μl of drugs per day for four weeks following the development of the tumor. A vernier caliper was used to measure the tumor size on a weekly basis. Additionally, body weights were recorded every three days. Based on formula V = 1/2 ab2 (a: major diameter; b: minor axis), the tumor volume (V) was calculated.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated in ethanol, and incubated in 3 % H2O2 solution for 10 min. To perform the antigen retrieval, the FFPE slides were immersed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and placed in a pressure cooker for 15 min. An overnight incubation at 4 °C was performed with a primary antibody against cleaved caspase-3. Thereafter, the biotinylated secondary antibody and horseradish streptavidin were sequentially added for 1 h and 30 min, respectively. Images were taken using a Zeiss microscope. A semi-quantitative scale of 0–3 was applied to the staining intensity of all immunostains by two independent observers. In the case of a score of 0–1, it is considered to be cleaved caspase-3 negative. In the case of a score of 2, the score is considered borderline. Those who have a score of 3 are considered to have cleaved caspase-3 positive.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using the Graphpad Prism software. Analyzing the experimental data with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student-Newman-Keuls methods enabled comparisons to be made between mean and standard deviation (SD). Significant results were defined as those with a P value less than 0.05.

Results

Human pharyngeal cells are inhibited in viability and proliferation by ATPR in combination with Celecoxib, a cox-2 inhibitor.

At the bright-field level, there is shrinkage of cells and rounding of the cell surface in FaDu cells treated with ATPR and Celecoxib, especially when ATPR and Celecoxib are administered together, which also results in apoptotic-like membrane protrusions (Fig. 1A, arrow). We administered 25 μM ATPR and 50 μM Celecoxib, respectively, simultaneously to FaDu cells to assess the effects of ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors on the viability of the cells. The proliferation of the cells was determined using the MTT assay. The results of the study indicate that 25 m ATPR does not affect cell proliferation. By contrast, 50 μM Celecoxib inhibited cell proliferation, which was augmented by ATPR (Fig. 1B). For the purpose of determining the cell cycle phase, flow cytometric analysis was used. When ATPR and Celecoxib are treated simultaneously, FaDu cells are arrested at the G0/G1 phase, while in the vehicle and single-drug treatments, the G0/G1 phase cell population is lower. The results indicate that ATPR and Celecoxib inhibit cell proliferation more effectively when used together than when used individually (Fig. 1C). ATPR-induced apoptosis in human pharyngeal cells is promoted by celecoxib

Based on the shrinkage of cells following ATPR and Celecoxib treatment, we hypothesized that the synergic effect of these two drugs may also induce apoptosis. By using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL), the cell death of FaDu cells was assessed as DNA fragmentation. Combining ATPR and Celecoxib resulted in a greater proportion of apoptotic TUNEL-positive cells (30–40 %) than either ATPR alone or Celecoxib alone (Fig. 2A). As well, cell death was detected using the Annexin V-PI staining method. As a result of the ATPR and Celecoxib combined, more apoptotic FaDu cells were observed, including the late phase (Q1) and early phase (Q4) cells. Comparatively, the cell death percentage was only 20–30 % (Q1 and Q4) in the ATPR and Celecoxib groups, respectively (Fig. 2B). Apoptosis was also increased in human nasopharyngeal CNE-2 cells after the coadministration of ATPR and Celecoxib (Supplementary Figs. 2A–B). The ATPR contributes to the inhibition of proliferation of FaDu cells by celecoxib, which is mediated by the P53 signal

It has been shown in previous studies that inhibition of COX-2 increases nuclear localization of active P53, which influences cellular proliferation [27]. Furthermore, ATPR inhibited the proliferation of leukemia cells [28]. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that ATPR and Celecoxib inhibited the proliferation of FaDu cells at the G0/G1 phase, suggesting that these cells are hypoproliferating. We assessed the P53 signals as the molecular mechanism involved in cell proliferation in order to delineate the mechanism responsible for the cell cycle arrest caused by ATPR and Celecoxib. ATPR and Celecoxib significantly inhibited the mRNA transcript of CCND1, whereas tumor suppressor genes, TP53 [29] and RB [30], were upregulated (Fig. 3A–C). Additionally, ATPR and Celecoxib treatment resulted in an increase in the expression of P53 and CDN1A proteins (p21, the primary target of P53 [31,32]) (Fig. 3D). A combination of ATPR and celecoxib activated caspase-3 in FaDu cancer cells by activating the JNK/MAPK pathway

It has been shown that there are two main apoptotic pathways: the extrinsic (death receptor) pathway and the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway that induce cell death [33]. Following treatment with ATPR and Celecoxib, FaDu cells were harvested and probed for caspases in order to determine which apoptotic pathway was induced. It was noted that pro-caspase-8 was degraded while caspase-3 and caspase-8 cleavages were present, as well as cleaved caspase-9 (Fig. 4A), suggesting that ATPR and Celecoxib induced apoptosis through intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms. In addition, we examined the BCL2 family of proteins, which are involved in the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis. There was an increase in the expression of the apoptotic activator BAX. As a contrast, ATPR and Celecoxib treatment decreased the anti-apoptotic level of BCL2 (Fig. 4B). Studies have demonstrated that MAPKs play a crucial role in the conversion of extracellular stimuli into a variety of cellular responses, including apoptosis. Physical, chemical, and biological stress stimuli activated JNK and p38 MAPK, whereas ERK1/2 cascades were induced by cell growth factor-stimulated signaling [34,35]. In order to determine whether MAPKs were involved in ATPR-induced and Celecoxib-induced apoptosis, phosphorylated and total JNK, p38, and ERK signals were examined by Western blotting. Neither p38 nor ERK signals were activated by ATPR or Cox-2 inhibitors in FaDu cells. On the other hand, the phosphorylated JNK signal was inhibited (Fig. 4C). A JNK inhibitor (SP600125) and MAPK agonist (Phorbol myristate acetate, PMA) were used to determine whether the JNK pathway was involved in the regulation of apoptosis. Using SP600125, the JNK signal was dephosphorylated, which was then activated by PMA. In the meantime, cleaved caspase-3 was increased, followed by an inhibitory effect on JNK signaling. In contrast, PMA-induced JNK activation resulted in a reduction in the activity (Fig. 4D). A joint ATPR/celecoxib treatment suppressed tumor formation in a mouse model of FaDu cell xenograft

Xenografting FaDu cells subcutaneously into BALB/c nude mice was conducted to assess the anti-cancer effects of ATPR/Celecoxib integrated treatment in vivo. A weekly measurement of the tumor size and body weight was conducted for a period of eight weeks. There was a significant reduction in tumor volumes in mice treated with ATPR in combination with Celecoxib compared with mice treated with vehicle, ATPR, and Celecoxib alone (Fig. 5A and B). In the nude mice treated with ATPR and Celecoxib, there was no apparent loss of body weight, indicating that there were no systemic defects induced (Fig. 5C). In addition, immunostaining for caspase-3 cleavage on tumor tissue revealed a significant increase in apoptosis in the joint ATPR/Celecoxib treatment group compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 5D and E). A bioinformatic analysis of RARA and PTGS2 in squamous cell carcinomas of the head and neck

To examine the clinical significance of ATPR/Celecoxib combined treatment, we analyzed 530 samples of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), which included pharyngeal carcinoma, derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) by cBioPortal. RARA (ATRA receptor gene) and PTGS2 (Cox-2 gene) transcripts were significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 6A and B), suggesting that ATPR/Cox-2 signals are associated with tumor progression in humans. We determined the pro-apoptotic genes in the TCGA database by analyzing tumor and non-tumor tissue in order to investigate the relationship between RARA and PTGS2. It was found that apoptotic signals such as CASP3, CASP8, and BAX were universally higher in tumors than in nontumor parts (Fig. 6C), which was positively correlated with RARA and PTGS2. In the final step, we grouped the HNSC samples according to the level of RARA expression and conducted a three-year survival analysis. In terms of clinical prognosis, the high RARA group had a better outcome (Fig. 6D). According to the results, there is no significant difference in survival rates between patients with high and low levels of CASP3, CASP8, BAX, PTGS2, and TP53 genes (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, expression levels of RARA may not be the sole mechanism by which apoptotic and COX-2 signals inhibit tumor growth in vivo. Discussion

Patients with pharyngeal cancer have a poor prognosis due to the difficulty of early diagnosis. These individuals suffer from severe nutritional deficiencies and poor general health. Radiotherapy, cytotoxic agents, and immunotherapy are currently used to treat pharyngeal cancer. Drugs and surgery can be used in clinical settings to increase life expectancy for a short period [1,3]. Cox-2 inhibitors have been used to treat cancer. However, they can also cause non-specific cytotoxicity. ATRA was used together with arsenic trioxide in the treatment of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) [23]. Co-administration of ATPR with Cox-2 inhibitors enhanced apoptosis of hypopharyngeal carcinoma FaDu cells, which may reduce or eliminate adverse effects in vivo.

P53 regulates several signaling pathways, such as DNA damage [36], stem cell maintenance [37], tumor hypoxia [38], and angiogenesis [39,40]. Human tumors are thought to be caused by P53 functional inactivation [41]. Despite the fact that the molecular mechanism through which P53 inhibits tumors is unknown, it is generally believed that P53 arrests the cell cycle [42,43]. The RB-E2F complex is created when CDN1A (p21) binds to the cyclin-E/CDK [44] complex, suppressing cell cycle gene expression, including many genes required for G1/S transition [45]. Combined ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors arrest FaDu cells during G0/G1 in this study. Co-treatment with ATPR and Celecoxib decreased cyclin-D1, but increased P53, CDN1A, and RB. ATPR and Celecoxib-induced cell cycle arrest may be mediated by P53-CDN1A-RB signaling.

According to our flow cytometric results, ATPR combined with Cox-2 inhibitor induced early and late apoptosis of FaDu cells in a dose-dependent manner. According to research, P53 has three domains that are responsible for regulating pro-apoptotic genes [46], including activation of p21 [47], Bax [48], Puma [49], Noxa [50], as well as inhibition of BCL2, survivin [51]. Our findings support this, as ATPR and Celecoxib together increased the expression of the apoptosis-inhibiting protein BCL2 while down-regulating the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX. The Bcl-2 family promotes Ca2+ efflux from the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to the release of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm and activating apoptosis [52].

Extrinsic apoptosis is caused by caspase-8 [53,54]. Induction of this effect was via CD95 receptors (Fas and APO-1) and tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1). By cleaving caspase-8, p18 and p10 activate downstream effector molecules such as caspase-1, -3, -6, and -7 [55]. In addition, caspase-9 participates in intrinsic apoptosis, which is also known as mitochondrial apoptosis. The release of cytochrome c from mitochondria is associated with procaspase-9/Apaf-1. Apaf-1 activation of caspase-9 was accompanied by intrinsic proteolytic processing, resulting in an increase in apoptosis. In addition to caspase-9, caspase-3 and caspase-7 were also processed by the caspase cascade. Endogenous and exogenous apoptosis pathways act on caspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3 initiates cascade reactions [53,54,56], resulting in DNA fragmentation and shrinkage, morphological markers of apoptosis. In this study, ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors increased caspase-8 and caspase-9, which eventually activated caspase-3, suggesting both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways are involved. Studies are currently being conducted on the mediators by which ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors regulate apoptosis.

Eukaryotic cells mediate a series of stress responses by expressing C-Jun terminal kinase (JNKs), which belongs to the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family [57,58]. There has been a strong association between MAPK and apoptosis in colorectal cancer in previous studies [59]. The JNK protein kinase may function as an anti-apoptotic protein kinase in certain tumors, promoting malignant transformation and tumorigenesis [57]; however, the opposite effect has also been demonstrated [58]. Several studies have demonstrated that the JNK pathway has anti-tumor effects in some tissues when large-scale tumor genome sequencing is undertaken [60]. As a result of the combination of ATPR and Celecoxib, JNK signals were downregulated and its phosphorylation was inhibited in this study. Consequently, ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors alone or in combination induced apoptosis through the JNK signaling pathway.

Ultimately, we demonstrated that ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors synergistically induced cell cycle arrest in FaDu cells derived from hypopharyngeal carcinomas via P53. ATPR combined with Cox-2 inhibitors induced cell apoptosis, which was dependent on the activation of caspases involved in both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. Under treatment with ATPR and a Cox-2 inhibitor, the JNK/MAPK signaling pathway was suppressed, contributing to the apoptosis of FaDu cells. The rare incidence of hypopharyngeal cancer makes it impossible to study any other hypopharyngeal cell line. As for the nasopharyngeal CNE-2 cell, which is also known as a head and neck tumor, it also showed the same results following treatment with ATPR and Celecoxib. As a result, it is possible that co-administration of ATPR and Cox-2 inhibitors might have a therapeutic effect on cancer treatment.
