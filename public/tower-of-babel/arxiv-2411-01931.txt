Differentially private and decentralized randomized power method
Julien Nicolas, César Sabater, Mohamed Maouche, Sonia Ben Mokhtar, Mark Coates

Abstract
The randomized power method has gained significant interest due to its simplicity and efficient handling of large-scale spectral analysis and recommendation tasks. However, its application to large datasets containing personal information (e.g. web interactions, search history, personal tastes) raises critical privacy concerns. This paper addresses these issues by proposing enhanced privacy-preserving variants of the method, which reduce the variance of the noise introduced to achieve Differential Privacy (DP). We also adapt the method to a decentralized framework with a low computational and communication overhead, while preserving the accuracy. We leverage Secure Aggregation to run the algorithm on data distributed among multiple users or devices, while using a noise scale in the decentralized setting that is similar to the one in the centralized setting yet without revealing individual data. We improve upon existing convergence bounds for both the centralized and decentralized versions.



1
Introduction

The randomized power method is a simple and computationally efficient tool for solving large-scale linear algebra problems, particularly in spectral analysis and recommendation systems. It approximates the principal singular vectors of a matrix with low computational complexity (nearly linear), making it particularly suitable for analyzing large modern datasets.

In large-scale machine learning systems, protecting user privacy is paramount. Datasets often contain sensitive personal or organizational information, so privacy has become a critical requirement. The standard randomized power method does not inherently provide privacy guarantees. While its output might seem less sensitive than the input data, there is no formal guarantee that private information embedded in the data cannot be inferred.

To address and quantify privacy leakages, Differential Privacy (DP) has emerged as a powerful mathematical framework that provides strong formal guarantees and mitigate potential privacy leaks of an algorithm. DP ensures that the output of an algorithm reveals little about any individual record in the input. Several works have attempted to apply DP to the randomized power method. For instance,
Hardt & Price 2014
and
Balcan et al. 2016
introduced differentially private versions of the method, and
Guo et al. 2021
;
Wang & Xu 2020
explored federated approaches to private power iteration. Other close works explore centralized differentially private versions of PCA
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
and federated
(
Wang & Xu 2020
;
Briguglio et al. 2023
)
and give optimal convergence bounds given certain assumptions.

Despite these advancements, existing approaches suffer from several limitations. First, their performance heavily depends on the number of singular vectors being computed
(
Hardt & Price 2014
;
Balcan et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Wang & Xu 2020
)
, which impacts both utility and privacy guarantees. Second, they are primarily designed for centralized settings
(
Hardt & Price 2014
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, where a trusted curator is assumed to hold the data. Moreover, some methods
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
make strong assumptions about the underlying data distribution (i.e. that the data is sub-Gaussian) which makes it harder to use these methods in practice. Some federated versions
(
Briguglio et al. 2023
)
claim to guarantee privacy by use of the federated setting, although it has been shown that decentralization does not offer privacy by design
(
Geiping et al. 2020
)
. Finally, no fully decentralized versions exist to our knowledge. The previously proposed federated versions
(
Balcan et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2021
;
Wang & Xu 2020
)
use public channel communication and ignore advances in Multi-Party-Computation (MPC). This makes them unsuitable for decentralized environments, such as distributed recommender systems and social networks, where data is partitioned across users and/or devices and communications are restricted to a predefined communication graph.

1.1
Contributions

The core focus of this article is to introduce improved privacy-preserving randomized power methods and to extend them to a decentralized setting, while maintaining computational efficiency and performance. We propose new convergence bounds for the (decentralized) privacy-preserving power method; these show that our proposed algorithm has a weaker dependence on the number of singular vectors that are computed.

We propose an improved differentially private version of the randomized power method, with tighter convergence bounds, reducing the noise required to achieve privacy and therefore improving the accuracy of the method. In the process of deriving these bounds, we present a new, more straightforward proof of a privacy result for the differentially private randomized power method.

This makes the proposed method a promising candidate for decentralized and privacy-conscious applications.

2.2
Differential privacy:

2.2.1
Approximate differential privacy:

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}
provides (
\epsilon,\delta
)-differential privacy if for all datasets
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
differing on at most one element (adjacent datasets), and for all measurable subsets
S\subseteq\text{Range}(\mathcal{M})
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D_{1})\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D_{2})\in S]+\delta,

(2)

where
\epsilon
is a small positive parameter representing the privacy loss (smaller values indicate stronger privacy guarantees), and
\delta
is a small probability representing the chance that the privacy guarantee may fail, allowing for a small probability of privacy breach.

2.2.2
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity:

The
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity of a function
f

\Delta_{2}(f)
or
\Delta_{2}
for brevity, measures the maximum change in the output of
f
when its input changes by at most one element (for neighboring datasets
D_{1}\sim D_{2}
). It is defined as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}=\max_{D_{1}\sim D_{2}}\|f(D_{1})-f(D_{2})\|_{2},

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
represents the
\ell_{2}
-norm.

2.2.3
Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy (zCDP):

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}
is said to satisfy
\rho
-zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy (zCDP) if for all neighboring datasets
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, and for all
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
, the following holds:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(D_{1})\|\mathcal{M}(D_{2}))\leq\rho\alpha\,,

(4)

where
D_{\alpha}
is the Rényi divergence of order
\alpha
and
\rho
is a positive parameter controlling the trade-off between privacy and accuracy (smaller values of
\rho
imply stronger privacy guarantees).

The following lemma, introduced by
Bun & Steinke 2016
, specifies how the addition of Gaussian noise can be used to construct a randomized algorithm that satisfies zCDP.

Lemma 2.1

(Gaussian Mechanism (Proposition 1.6
(
Bun & Steinke 2016
)
))
.

Let
f:X^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a sensitivity-
\Delta
function. Consider the mechanism (randomized algorithm)
M:X^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as
M(x)=f(x)+Z_{x}
where for each input
x
,
Z_{x}
is independently drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Then
M
satisfies
\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)
-zCDP.

The next lemma, which is a generalized version of a result presented by
Bun & Steinke 2016
, explains how a randomized algorithm, constructed by recursively composing a sequence of zCDP-satisying randomized algorithms, also satisfies zCDP.

Lemma 2.2

(Adaptive composition (Generalization from Lemma 2.3
(
Bun & Steinke 2016
)
))
.

Let
M_{1}:X^{n}\to Y_{1}
,
M_{2}:X^{n}\times Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
, …,
M_{L}:X^{n}\times Y_{1}\times\dots\times Y_{L-1}\to Y_{L}
be randomized algorithms. Suppose
M_{i}
satisfies
\rho_{i}
-zCDP as a function of its first argument for each
i=1,2,\dots,L
. Let
M^{\prime\prime}:X^{n}\to Y_{L}
, constructed recursively by:

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(x)=M_{L}(x,M_{L-1}(x,\dots,M_{2}(x,M_{1}(x))\dots)).

(5)

Then
M^{\prime\prime}
satisfies
(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\rho_{i})
-zCDP.

3.1
Proposed runtime-dependent bounds

3.1.1
Adjacency notion:

In prior works
(
Hardt & Price 2014
;
Balcan et al. 2016
)
, two matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
are considered adjacent (denoted
\bm{A}\sim\bm{A}^{\prime}
) if they differ by a change in a single element, with a Frobenius norm of at most 1:

\bm{A}^{\prime}=\bm{A}+c\cdot\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top},

(11)

where
\bm{e}_{i},\bm{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are canonical basis vectors of dimension
d
,
c\leq 1
represents the magnitude of the change, and
0\leq i,j<n
. This adjacency notion models a sensitive change as a modification to a single element in
\bm{A}
to protect individual element-wise updates under Differential Privacy.
However, since
\bm{A}
is symmetric positive semi-definite and changes must preserve this property, this necessitates updates on the diagonal elements. We propose a new, more general, notion of adjacency to allow for other types of updates:

\bm{A}^{\prime}=\bm{A}+\bm{C},

(12)

where
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric matrix representing the update, subject to the condition:

\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{C}_{i,:}\|_{1}^{2}}\leq 1.

(13)

The proposed adjacency notion is strictly more general than Equation (
11
). For example, setting
\bm{C}=c\cdot\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}
recovers the original definition. Additionally,
\bm{C}
can have non-zero entries on the diagonal, anti-diagonal, or any symmetric pattern, allowing for a variety of updates while maintaining symmetry.

In the context of recommender systems where
\bm{A}=\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}
and
\bm{R}
is a user-item interaction matrix, this adjacency notion allows for element-wise changes in
\bm{R}
(i.e. protect user-item interactions). By adopting this more general adjacency definition, we make our privacy guarantees applicable to a wider range of real-world scenarios.

3.1.2
Improved sensitivity bound:

The previous sensitivity bound
\Delta_{l}^{\text{prior}}

(
Hardt & Price 2014
;
Balcan et al. 2016
)
for

was estimated by extrapolating from the case where
\bm{X}^{l-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
to the general case
\bm{X}^{l-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. This leads to an overestimation of the sensitivity and to unnecessarily large noise addition. We directly analyze the change in
\bm{X}^{l-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and derive a tighter bound on the sensitivity
\Delta_{l}
. By using our proposed adjacency notion from Equation (
12

Theorem 3.1

(Improved Sensitivity Bound)
.

Let
\bm{A}^{\prime}
be defined as in Equation (
12
), and consider the sensitivity
\Delta_{l}=\|\bm{A}^{\prime}\bm{X}^{l}-\bm{A}\bm{X}^{l}\|_{F}
. Then,

\Delta_{l}\leq\max_{i}\|\bm{X}_{i,:}^{l}\|_{2}\triangleq\hat{\Delta}_{l}.

(14)

This improved bound

i
holds a private matrix
\bm{A}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, such that the global matrix is the sum of these local matrices:
\bm{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\bm{A}^{(i)}.

The goal is for the clients to collaboratively compute an orthonormal basis for the range of
\bm{A}
, similar to the centralized randomized power method, but without revealing their individual private matrices
\bm{A}^{(i)}
to the server or to other clients.

The randomized power method involves linear operations, making it well-suited for parallelization and distributed computation.
Balcan et al. 2016
proposed a private and federated power method using communication over public channels between clients and a server. However, this approach relies on Differential Privacy, since the data exchanged can be observed by everyone, which requires high levels of noise to ensure privacy.

To enhance privacy while retaining the benefits of distributed computation, we use Secure Aggregation, a lightweight Multi-Party Computation protocol. Secure Aggregation allows clients to collaboratively compute sums without revealing individual data, enabling the use of distributed DP. This approach offers privacy guarantees similar to central DP and eliminates the need for a trusted curator. Distributed DP has been extensively studied in the literature
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Kairouz et al. 2021
;
Goryczka et al. 2013
;
Wei et al. 2024
;
Ghazi et al. 2019
)
.

In distributed DP, each client adds carefully calibrated noise to their local contributions before participating in the Secure Aggregation protocol. The noise is designed such that the sum across clients has a variance comparable to that used in central DP, thereby achieving similar Differential Privacy guarantees without requiring a trusted aggregator, and allowing clients to keep the data locally.

We operate under the “honest-but-curious“ threat model, where clients follow the protocol correctly but may attempt to learn additional information from received data. We also assume there are no corrupted or dropout users during the computation. For simplicity, this paper focuses on a basic implementation of distributed DP, neglecting the effects of data quantization or errors introduced by modular arithmetic. We refer to
Kairouz et al. 2021
for an implementation of Distributed DP that takes these effects into account.

Finally, it is possible to use a fully decentralized and differentially private protocol to perform averaging without relying on a central server for Secure Aggregation, as demonstrated by
Sabater et al. 2022
. Adopting such approaches can further enhance decentralization while maintaining similar utility and communication costs.

7
Conclusion and limitations

We presented a differentially private and decentralized randomized power method that addresses privacy concerns in large-scale spectral analysis and recommendation systems. We introduced new convergence bounds, improving the accuracy of the method while ensuring privacy guarantees. By employing Secure Aggregation in a decentralized setting, we were able to reduce the noise introduced for privacy, maintaining the efficiency and privacy of the centralized version but adapting it for distributed environments.

However, our privacy guarantees rely on the honest-but-curious threat model and assume no collusion or malicious behavior from participants, which may not always be realistic. In the future, it would also be interesting to analyze a private and decentralized version of the accelerated version of the randomized power method, which could potentially have a faster convergence.

Impact Statement (required by ICML)

Our contributions directly address the need for privacy-preserving algorithms in the context of large-scale machine learning systems, where sensitive personal or organizational data is often at stake. The decentralized implementation ensures that the method can be applied in some real-world environments where data is distributed across multiple users or devices, making it particularly suitable for recommender systems and other distributed applications. This work is expected to have implications for privacy-conscious machine learning in real-world systems, to perform large-scale data analysis while maintaining robust privacy protections.

\sigma^{2}=\frac{2n^{2}(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}(n-4)}
. Therefore
\bm{Q}_{ij}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\frac{\mu}{n},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}})
and:

\displaystyle k\bm{Q}_{ij}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\frac{k\mu}{n},\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\bm{Q}_{ij}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\frac{k\mu}{n},\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}).

(45)

We can approximate the expectancy of the maximum (noted as m) of
d
Gaussian variables distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2})
by
m=\sigma_{2}\sqrt{2\cdot\log(d)}+\mu_{2}
by Lemma 2.3 from
Massart 1896
. We therefore get approximations of
\mathbb{E}[\max_{ij}k\bm{Q}_{ij}^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\max_{i}\|\bm{Q}_{i:}\|^{2}]
. We assume that the variances of
\Delta_{l}^{prior}
and
\hat{\Delta}_{l}
(as
n
and
k
are large) are small and therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{l}^{prior}]^{2}\approx\mathbb{E}[(\Delta_{l}^{prior})^{2}]=\sqrt{\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}}\sqrt{2\cdot\log(kn)}+\frac{k\mu}{n}

(46)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\Delta}_{l}]^{2}\approx\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\Delta}_{l})^{2}]=\sqrt{\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}}\sqrt{2\cdot\log(n)}+\frac{k\mu}{n}.

(47)

∎

:

Proof.

First bound:
We know by Section 2.4 from

of a matrix with orthonormal columns

has squared norm at most 1. Therefore
\max_{i}\|\bm{X}_{i:}\|_{2}\leq 1

Second bound:
Without loss of generality and to simplify notation, we note
\bm{X}
to denote a matrix
\bm{X}_{l}

As proposed in
Hardt & Price 2014
, we can write any column

of
\bm{X}
(

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{c}^{i}u_{i}
where
u_{i}
denotes the
i
-th eigenvector of
\bm{A}
, and
\alpha_{c}^{i}
is a scalar. Therefore we have:

\displaystyle<x_{c},x_{o}>

\displaystyle=<\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{c}^{i}u_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{o}^{i}u_{j}>

(48)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{c}^{i}\alpha_{o}^{i}<u_{i},u_{j}>

(By bilinearity)

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{c}^{i}\alpha_{o}^{i}.

(Orth. columns of
\bm{U}
)

(50)

\bm{X}
has orthonormal columns, so if
c=o
, then
<x_{c},x_{o}>=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{(\alpha_{c}^{i})}^{2}=1
. Otherwise, we have
<x_{c},x_{o}>=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{c}^{i}\alpha_{o}^{i}=0
. We can therefore define a matrix
\bm{B}

\bm{X}=\bm{U}\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}_{jc}=\alpha_{c}^{j}

i

\displaystyle\|\bm{X}_{i:}\|_{2}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\bm{U}_{ij}|\|\bm{B}_{j:}\|_{2}

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\bm{U}_{ij}|.

(Orth. columns of

(54)

A.7
Application to recommender systems:

Let
s
be the number of users in our system and
n
the number of items.
Let
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
be the user-item interaction matrix, such that
\bm{R}_{ui}=1
only if user
u
has interacted with item
i
, and
\bm{R}_{ui}=0
else.
Let
\bm{U}=\text{Diag}(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{1}_{|\mathcal{I}|})
be the user degrees matrix, and
\bm{I}=\text{Diag}(\bm{1}_{|\mathcal{I}|}^{\top}\cdot\bm{R})
the item degrees matrix.
In
Shen et al. 2021
;
Choi et al. 2023
, the normalized interaction matrix is defined as:

\displaystyle\bm{R}^{\prime}=\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}\bm{I}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

and the item-item normalized adjacency matrix as:

\begin{split}\bm{P}^{\prime}&=\tilde{\bm{R}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{R}}\\
&=(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})^{\top}(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})\\
&=(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}\bm{I}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\top}(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}\bm{I}^{-\frac{1}{2}}).\\
\end{split}

(58)

To simplify our analysis, we consider
\bm{I}
public and do not use item-wise normalization in the computation of ideal low pass filter, leaving it for future work. We therefore define
\tilde{\bm{R}}=\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}
and
\tilde{\bm{P}}=(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})^{\top}(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})
.

Lemma A.1
.

We can use Algorithm
2
with
\Delta_{l}=\sqrt{2}\max_{i}\|\bm{X}_{i:}^{l}\|_{2}
to compute the top-
p
eigenvectors of
\tilde{\bm{P}}
in a decentralized setting with under a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-Differential Privacy guarantee.

Proof.

We note
\tilde{\bm{P}}_{ij}
as the element of
\tilde{\bm{P}}
at line
i
and column
j
and
d_{user}(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}r_{ui}
. We can write
\tilde{\bm{P}}_{ij}
as such:

\begin{split}\tilde{\bm{P}}_{ij}&=((\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})^{\top}(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}))_{ij}\\
&=(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})^{T})_{i,*}(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})_{*,j}\\
&=((\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})_{*,i})^{\top}(\bm{U}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R})_{*,j}\\
&=\sum_{u=0}^{s-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{user}(u)}}r_{ui}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{user}(u)}}r_{uj}\\
&=\sum_{u=0}^{s-1}\frac{1}{d_{user}(u)}\cdot r_{ui}\cdot r_{uj}.\\
\end{split}

(59)

By noticing that

). Therefore we have:

\bm{A}_{ij}=\sum_{u=0}^{s-1}\frac{1}{d_{user}(u)}\cdot r_{ui}\cdot r_{uj},

(60)

and
