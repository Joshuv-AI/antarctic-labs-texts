Brief Report: Are Customs and Border Protection (CBP) Apprehensions a Reliable Proxy for Undocumented Migration? New Evidence on Migration from Guatemala
Authors: Matthew Blanton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearchers have long relied on United States Customs and Border Protection (CBP) apprehension data as a proxy for undocumented migration, yet the last time their validity was assessed was in 1995. Significant changes in the composition of migrants and border crossings since then raise questions about their continued validity. In this study, I provide an updated analysis using two independent data sources: the 2018 Guatemalan Census and annual estimates of undocumented Guatemalan arrivals to the United States from the Center for Migration Studies (CMS). Descriptive and regression analyses reveal a strong alignment between apprehensions, household migrations reported in the census, and estimated undocumented entries, suggesting that CBP apprehension data remained a reliable indicator of undocumented migration trends at both national and subnational levels during this period. These findings underscore the continued relevance of apprehensions and the need for regular empirical updates as migration dynamics evolve.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11113-026-10012-8.Keywords: Border enforcement, Undocumented migration, Apprehension data, Guatemala, Data validation

Introduction and Background

Of all population processes of interest to demographers, international migration suffers from the weakest and least-reliable data sources (Massey & Capoferro, 2004). This is particularly true for undocumented migration because hostile immigration enforcement in receiving contexts creates incentives for people trying to cross borders without authorization to stay off the radar (Massey et al., 2016). Nevertheless, accurate data are vital for understanding the determinants of migration, projecting population change, and assessing migration’s social and economic effects in both sending and receiving regions (Massey & Capoferro, 2004).

In the absence of comprehensive records, academics, nonprofit organizations, and journalists in the U.S. have long relied on Customs and Border Protection (CBP) apprehensions1 as a proxy for underlying migration flows. For example, CBP data have been used to examine the effects of border enforcement on migration patterns (Hanson & Spilimbergo, 1999) and to draw conclusions about migration push factors, such as violence, climate change, and poverty (Amuedo-Dorantes & Puttitanun, 2016; Clemens, 2021; Linke et al., 2023).

However, interpreting apprehension data is more complex than it might initially appear. Apprehensions represent the “sum of failed attempts to enter,” which often include multiple counts of the same individuals (Warren, 2024, p. 5).2 Complicating matters further, the volume of encounters, often characterized as indicative of some underlying reality about migration flows, has always been shaped by the nature and scale of enforcement efforts (Briggs, 1984; Espenshade & Acevedo, 1995; Kelly, 1977; Massey et al., 2016). These efforts, in turn, are driven by cultural and political forces (Massey & Pren, 2012; Massey et al., 2016).

Still, early work by demographer Thomas Espenshade showed that apprehension data could serve as a useful proxy for undocumented flows (1990; 1995). His repeat trials model (RTM) of illegal entry used the recidivist ratio—the share of apprehended individuals who had been previously caught—to estimate the probability of apprehension and, in turn, the total number of unauthorized crossings (Espenshade, 1990). Espenshade evinced a strong linear correlation (r = .89) between the number of border apprehensions and the gross number of unlawful entries estimated by the RTM (1995). He concluded that apprehension data could reliably track trends in unauthorized migration, even if not the precise volume (Espenshade, 1995). The legacy of Espenshade’s work is significant; the RTM framework underpins Department of Homeland Security’s (DHS) most recently available model-based estimates of apprehension rates and undetected entries (2023).

Although both demographers and federal agencies continually rely on CBP data as a proxy for undocumented migration, no study has reassessed their validity since 1995. In the more than three decades since then, migration patterns have changed considerably. First, the regional composition of undocumented migrants to the United States has shifted. Although Mexican nationals historically made up nearly all undocumented migrants apprehended at the U.S.–Mexico border, migrants from northern Central America (Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador) have become a substantial share of the total in recent years. They first surpassed Mexican nationals in total apprehensions in 2014 and again by a wide margin in 2019 (Singer & Kandel, 2019). Even though Mexicans have since regained the majority (since 2022), Central Americans continue to account for a large share of total apprehensions (Office of Homeland Security Statistics, 2025). Second, the underlying motivation for migration has changed as well, with threat evasion—migration to escape violence, persecution, and instability—increasingly prompting massive migration flows (Donato & Massey, 2016).

Third, and relatedly, there has been a sharp rise in apprehensions of family unit individuals and unaccompanied minors—patterns largely driven by the shifting national composition of migrants. Whereas undocumented Mexicans have mainly been single adults who do not seek asylum, threat evaders from northern Central America are often compelled to migrate as a family unit or to send children alone in search of refuge (Massey, 2020). Reflecting this shift, although fewer than 10% of apprehended migrants in 2012 were unaccompanied minors or part of a family unit, that figure surged to 65% by 2019 (Singer & Kandel, 2019). Although the proportion has waned in subsequent years, family units and unaccompanied minors continue to represent a substantial share of all apprehensions at the U.S. border (Office of Homeland Security Statistics, 2025).

Changes in who is migrating and why are relevant to apprehension data. Unlike single adults, who might try to avoid detection, many families and unaccompanied minors actively seek out Border Patrol agents to request asylum (Singer & Kandel, 2019). This shift fundamentally alters the meaning of apprehension data: it now captures not just failed entry attempts, but a strategic effort to gain entry through legal channels. As such, DHS categorizes unaccompanied children and migrants in family units as “non-impactable by traditional DHS enforcement activities,” assuming that 100% of these border crossers intend to present themselves to border agents (2023).

As the demographic composition of migrants changes, it is increasingly important to reassess the validity of apprehension data in capturing broader migration trends. In addition to reassessing apprehension data at the national level, this study extends prior validation efforts by examining whether apprehensions capture subnational variation in migration within a sending country. If apprehensions track undocumented migration only in aggregate, their usefulness for identifying sending regions or assessing localized drivers of migration would be limited. By contrast, close alignment between apprehensions and independent migration measures at the state level would provide stronger evidence that apprehension data capture underlying migration dynamics rather than coincidental national trends. Moreover, apprehension data are increasingly used—implicitly or explicitly—to infer subnational migration patterns within sending countries (Linke et al., 2023; Morales Rodas, 2023; Selee et al., 2022), despite little empirical validation of this practice. Assessing the reliability of apprehensions at the state level can therefore both strengthen confidence in their use as a proxy and clarify the conditions under which they can be meaningfully applied.

Thus, in this Brief Report, I revisit the extent to which CBP data serves as a reliable proxy, focusing on migration from Guatemala—the fastest-growing source of undocumented migrants in the United States (Warren, 2024). I analyze trends from 2002 to 2018, the period for which independent data sources are available, and assess alignment at both the national and subnational (state) levels. Although this time frame predates the record-high apprehensions in 2023 and subsequent declines under the second Trump administration (Putzel-Kavanaugh, 2025), it nevertheless allows for a critical reevaluation of long-standing assumptions using two external datasets. I do not claim to definitively resolve debates over the use of apprehensions as a proxy for undocumented migration. Rather, these findings provide the most up—to-date empirical assessment of the practice since Espenshade’s original work, laying the groundwork for future validation efforts as new data and changing migration patterns continue to reshape immigration and border enforcement.

Data and Methods

This study compares CBP apprehensions to two external datasets: reported household migrations in the 2018 Guatemalan Census and annual estimates of undocumented Guatemalan arrivals to the U.S., from the Center for Migration Studies (CMS) (Warren, 2020). Unlike Espenshade (1995), who estimated gross undocumented migration using apprehensions and then correlated those estimates with the original apprehension data itself, I leverage independent data sources to assess the association directly. I conduct analyses at both the national and state levels, using data that spans from 2002 to 2018.

Apprehensions, from United States Customs and Border Protection

CBP publishes aggregated apprehension statistics through its public data portals and annual reports,3 which support national-level analyses beginning in 2005. However, subnational information on migrants’ places of origin within sending countries is not publicly available in any year. To conduct both national- and state-level analyses beginning in 2002, I obtained a dataset from CBP through a Freedom of Information Act request.4 These data include all apprehensions of Guatemalan nationals at the U.S.–Mexico border from 2002 to 2017, along with reported state of origin within Guatemala for each apprehended individual. National-level analyses therefore span 2002–2018, whereas state-level analyses cover 2002–2017.

Using these data, I construct annual counts of apprehended Guatemalan migrants at the national level and, for state-level analyses, aggregate individual apprehensions by state of origin and year. To facilitate comparison with census-reported migration, state-level apprehension counts are normalized per 100,000 residents using population estimates from the 2018 census.

Reported Migrations, from the Guatemalan Census

In 2018, the Guatemalan National Population Census included a new module on migration that collected data on migrants who left Guatemala during the last census period. Specifically, household heads were asked, “Since the year 2002 has someone who belonged to this household gone to live in another country and not yet returned?”5 When interviewees responded affirmatively, they were asked the year of departure, age at departure, and sex for each household migrant. From the provided information, I summed the total number of migrants reported leaving each year across all surveyed households (N = 3,275,935 households). This national measure of reported migrations formed the basis of comparison with CBP figures of total Guatemalan apprehensions in a given year, from 2002 to 2017.6 I also normalized these migrant counts per 100,000 residents for every year in each of Guatemala’s 22 states.

Undocumented Guatemalan Arrivals, from the Center for Migration Studies

The Center for Migration Studies (CMS) publishes annual estimates of the undocumented population in the U.S. by country of origin and U.S. state of residence (Warren, 2021). These estimates are based on American Community Survey data, which are collected by the U.S. Census Bureau. Starting with an estimate of all noncitizens, a series of “logical edits” identify and remove as many legal residents as possible. Population controls are then estimated for individual countries of origin and figures are adjusted for undercount (For a more detailed explanation, see Warren, 2021). The undocumented population is then further decomposed to estimate new arrivals specifically, meaning all individuals who join the undocumented population in a given year, whether by crossing the border irregularly or overstaying a legal visa (Warren, 2020). New arrivals estimates include newly arrived “quasi-legal” residents, such as asylees, parolees, immigrants with temporary protected status, or those who have unresolved asylum claims (Warren, 2021). I analyzed estimated undocumented arrivals of Guatemalans for the period that they are available, from 2011 to 2018 (Warren, 2020).

Analytic Approach

The analysis proceeded in three parts. First, focusing on Guatemalan migration at a national level, I compared overall trends in the total number of Guatemalan apprehensions reported by the CBP, total reported migration in the Guatemalan census, and total estimated undocumented arrivals as calculated by the CMS.7 To more formally determine the strength and direction of associations across data sources over time, I also assessed cross-measure correlations. Second, examining state-level migration trends from Guatemala, I explored the extent to which the number of apprehensions originating from each Guatemalan state in a given year (reported by CBP) predict the number of reported migrations from that state in that same year (reported in the Guatemalan census). To do so, I created a panel dataset in which state-years are the unit of observation and fit an OLS model with state fixed effects. This approach rigorously accounts for all immutable characteristics of states, such as their topography and prior sending history. I normalized reported migrations by state population to facilitate comparisons across states. Third, to further compare state-level migration trends as reported in CBP apprehension data and the Guatemalan census, I ranked states by their number of apprehensions and number of reported migrations, separately, in each year and calculated Spearman rank correlation coefficients to measure the consistency of these rankings across years.

Results

Although general trends are similar across data sources, their magnitudes differed considerably. This reflects differences in what is captured by each data source: apprehensions are derived from people apprehended at the border, including multiple counts of those who tried to cross and were removed repeatedly. Reported migrations depict successful migrations in which household members left and never returned. Meanwhile, estimated undocumented arrivals convey individuals who newly joined the undocumented population in the U.S. It thus excludes those who were apprehended and removed, seasonal workers, or undocumented residents who were returning from trips abroad (Warren, 2020).

Apprehensions and reported migrations were highly correlated (r = .90, p < 0.001), closely replicating the strength of association (r = 0.89) identified by Espenshade (1995). Apprehensions and estimated undocumented arrivals showed a similarly strong correlation, with a coefficient of .88 (p < 0.01). Reported migrations and estimated undocumented arrivals are also highly correlated (r = 0.96; p < 0.001). Thus, all three data sources appear to capture similar underlying migration trends. Figures S1-S3 in the Online Supplement plot the correlations between each data source for each year.

State-Level

Beyond national patterns, examining trends at the state level allows us to assess whether apprehension data can reliably capture subnational variation in migration—offering greater confidence in their utility and highlighting their potential for identifying regional migration origins. As with national patterns in Fig. 1, the volume of apprehensions consistently exceeds that of reported migrations across all states. In panel data, traditional correlation matrices are not the most suitable method for analyzing associations between variables, especially when there are multiple observations of the same entity (e.g., state) over time. Instead, I used state and year fixed-effects OLS regression, which controls for all time-invariant state characteristics and common temporal shocks. The results convey how variation in reported migrations from a given state varies with apprehensions from that same state, holding constant both state-specific factors and year-specific trends. As shown in Table 1, for each additional apprehension per 100,000 residents, there is an associated 0.32 increase in reported migrations per 100,000 residents (p < 0.001). Moreover, the model explains 84% of the variance in reported migrations within states over time. Thus, on average, increases in apprehensions are closely aligned with corresponding increases in reported migrations—and vice versa.

A further question remains, however: can apprehension data also help identify which states are the most significant sources of migration? To answer this question, I ranked all Guatemalan states from highest to lowest based on their respective counts in each data source and then assess the correlation between these rankings using Spearman rank correlation. For the overall period, the correlation coefficient between state rankings for apprehensions and reported migrations was .78 (p < 0.001). Thus, on average, sending states with higher volumes of apprehensions also tend to have higher volumes of reported migrations. Discussion and Conclusion

Recognizing that international migration dynamics are rapidly changing and demographers need to capture these dynamics as accurately as possible, this study provides the first systematic evaluation in nearly three decades of the reliability of CBP apprehension figures as a proxy for undocumented migration trends. Apprehensions align closely with both reported migrations in the Guatemalan Census and CMS estimates of undocumented Guatemalan arrivals. Importantly, this alignment holds not only at the national level but also across sending states within Guatemala, indicating that apprehension data capture subnational variation in migration in addition to aggregate trends.

This continued reliability is particularly valuable in a context of limited data availability on undocumented migration. It means that researchers can confidently employ apprehensions to investigate a broad range of research questions, including the effects of changing economic conditions, violence, or policy shifts on migration flows, as well as to identify regional origins of migration within sending countries. Moreover, because apprehensions reflect real-time enforcement encounters, they can serve as a nowcasting tool for changes in migration trends that are not yet captured in population surveys or other metrics.

This study also highlights best practices for interpreting and using these data in future research. Although apprehensions are a strong proxy for migration flows, they do not reliably capture volume (Espenshade, 1995). This limitation reflects fundamental differences in what available data sources measure: apprehensions count enforcement encounters, including repeated attempts; reported migrations capture successful departures that leave at least one household member behind; and estimates of undocumented arrivals approximate net additions to the undocumented population through multiple pathways. The strong alignment across these imperfect measures reinforces the utility of apprehensions for identifying migration trends and underscores the difficulty of estimating volume with precision.

In practice, translating apprehensions into migration volume requires reliable estimates of detection probabilities and undetected entries—inputs that even federal agencies struggle to measure consistently. Recent shifts in migrant composition and border-crossing behavior have led the Department of Homeland Security to move away from the repeat trials model (RTM) originally developed by Espenshade and toward more complex operational simulations that integrate enforcement resources, terrain, and surveillance capabilities (2025).8 Even though such approaches remain beyond the reach of most academic researchers, they highlight the level of information required to estimate migration volume accurately. For most empirical applications, apprehension data are therefore best suited for analyzing trends and patterns, with careful attention to how evolving government methodologies may affect their interpretation.

Caution is also needed when comparing apprehension data over long periods without accounting for shifts in migration dynamics or enforcement practices. Simply examining overall apprehensions without decomposition by factors like national origin or demographic category can lead to misleading conclusions. For instance, claims of “record high apprehensions” may reflect changes in enforcement or the rise in asylum seekers rather than actual increases in migration attempts. Researchers should take care to disaggregate apprehension data whenever possible and provide context on how migration flows and enforcement practices have evolved over time.

Finally, the data used in this analysis have some limitations. The census migration module does not account for households in which all members have migrated, those who attempted migration unsuccessfully, or migrants who returned within the survey period. Despite these omissions, however, the census provides a robust representation of the country’s migration patterns.

As noted, the utility of apprehension data may also shift over time as migration patterns and U.S. policy responses continue to evolve. Although the findings presented here suggest that apprehensions remained a valuable tool for understanding undocumented migration trends during the period of major demographic and enforcement shifts since Espenshade’s 1995 analysis, questions remain about how well such data reflect migration flows in the wake of more recent surges and sharp declines in 2023 and beyond. As migration becomes increasingly shaped by volatile conditions in sending countries and a broader global turn toward immigration restrictionism, continued evaluation of apprehension data is essential. Future research should prioritize more regular and systematic validation efforts to ensure that this widely used metric continues to reflect the changing dynamics of undocumented migration to the United States.
