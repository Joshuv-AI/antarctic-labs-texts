Blind-spots of Randomized Benchmarking Under Temporal Correlations
Varun Srivastava 1, Abhinash Kumar Roy 1, Soumik Mahanti 1,2, Jasleen Kaur 1, Salini Karuvade 3, Alexei Gilchrist 1

Abstract
Randomized benchmarking (RB) is a widely adopted protocol for estimating the average gate fidelity in quantum hardware. However, its standard formulation relies on the assumption of temporally uncorrelated noise, an assumption often violated in current devices. In this work, we derive analytic expressions for the average sequence fidelity (ASF) in the presence of temporally correlated (non-Markovian) noise with classical memory, including cases where such correlations originate from interactions with a quantum environment. We show how the ASF can be interpreted to extract meaningful benchmarking parameters under such noise and identify classes of interaction Hamiltonians that render temporal correlations completely invisible to RB. We further provide operational criteria for witnessing temporal correlations due to quantum memory through RB experiments. Importantly, while classical correlations may remain undetectable in the ASF data, they can nonetheless significantly affect worst-case errors quantified by the diamond norm, a metric central to fault tolerant quantum computing. In particular, we demonstrate that temporal correlations may suppress worst-case errors highlighting that temporal correlations may not always have detrimental effects on gate performance.



I
Introduction

Quantum characterization, verification, and validation (QCVV) is essential for developing reliable quantum technologies
Eisert et al. 2020
;
Blume-Kohout et al. 2025
. Among QCVV tools, randomized benchmarking (RB) has become one of the most widely implemented methods for characterizing the average performance of a gate set via a single decay parameter
Emerson et al. 2005
;
Knill et al. 2008
;
Magesan et al. 2011
;
Magesan et al. 2012a
.
RB offers two significant practical advantages for characterizing gates, namely, scalability and insensitivity to state-preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors. In contrast, process tomography suffers on both counts
Chuang and Nielsen 1997
.
This practicality has driven broad adoption of RB for gate characterization across platforms including trapped-ion
Knill et al. 2008
, superconducting
Chow et al. 2009
;
Córcoles et al. 2013
, and neutral-atom processors
Olmschenk et al. 2010
;
Xia et al. 2015
.

RB assumes temporally uncorrelated (i.e. Markovian) and gate-independent noise in its standard formulation. Under these assumptions, the average sequence fidelity (ASF), which is the quantity the RB protocol estimates, follows a simple exponential decay model
Silva and Greplova 2025
.
Since its inception, numerous variants have been proposed to adapt the protocol to more general scenarios, namely simultaneous RB to probe multi-qubit crosstalk
Gambetta et al. 2012
, interleaved RB to estimate the average error of a targeted gate
Magesan et al. 2012b
, extensions beyond the Clifford group to more general gate sets
França and Hashagen 2018
;
Helsen et al. 2019
;
Eisert et al. 2020
, relaxations to weak gate-dependent noise
Magesan et al. 2011
;
Wallman 2018
, direct RB for multi-qubit native gates
Proctor et al. 2019
and logical-level RB on encoded qubits
Combes et al. 2017
. These variants largely inherit the assumption of Markovianity of the noise; however, most of the present quantum hardwares routinely exhibit temporally correlated noise
McEwen et al. 2021
;
Morris et al. 2022
. Only recently, the RB framework has been extended to accommodate non-Markovian dynamics
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2021
;
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2022
. Despite this progress, a systematic analysis of RB under diverse memory structures in the environment remains unexplored. These memory structures can be broadly categorized into classical or quantum memory depending on the type of correlations mediated by the environment
Giarmatzi and Costa 2021
;
Taranto et al. 2024
.

Prior studies have examined RB in the presence of classically correlated noise arising from coupling to stochastic classical fields, revealing departures from the Markovian ASF model
Ball et al. 2016
;
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2021
;
Fogarty et al. 2015
;
Qi and Ng 2021
;
Veldhorst et al. 2014
. For instance, Ref.
Fogarty et al. 2015
shows that slowly varying 1/f noise can produce decay curves better captured by multi-exponential fits. In Ref.
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2021
, two specific models, the controlled dephasing noise model and the shallow pocket model, were presented as examples of environment with classical memory, where the ASF curve showed monotonic decay.
Crucially, the effective memory in the environment can be classical even when it originates from interactions with nearby quantum environment for certain classes of interaction Hamiltonians
Goswami et al. 2024
, which prominently includes couplings naturally present in the present hardware, such as ZZ interactions in superconducting devices
Mundada et al. 2019
;
Ni et al. 2022
. These observations motivate a systematic study of RB in the most general classical-memory scenarios and what RB can (and cannot) reveal about both average gate errors and worst-case errors across gate sequences. Importantly, the process matrix formalism offers a unified and operational way to model and compare memory structures and assess their impact on RB
Pollock et al. 2018a
;
Giarmatzi and Costa 2021
;
Taranto et al. 2024
.

In this work, we present a general investigation of the RB protocol in the presence of classical memory, including memory effects that originate from interactions with a quantum environment. We derive analytical expressions for the average sequence fidelity (ASF), which reveal the emergence of multiple decay parameters, and we outline how these parameters can be reliably extracted and interpreted from RB data. A central message of our study is that classical memory effects often remain hidden in ASF curves. In particular, we show that the ASF remains monotonically decreasing with sequence length under such memory. Hence, any observed non-monotonicity provides a strong diagnostic for genuinely quantum memory effects. We further identify parameter regimes in which the ASF of a classical memory process is indistinguishable from that of a Markovian model, rendering RB effectively blind to these correlations, and we specify a class of interaction Hamiltonians that lead to such RB-blindness. Finally, we demonstrate that even when RB fails to reveal memory effects, these correlations can substantially influence the worst-case performance of gates, a metric that is critical for assessing fault-tolerance thresholds
Sanders et al. 2015
;
Kueng et al. 2016
.

The paper is structured as follows: In Sec.
II
, we provide a background for the RB protocol. We also present a brief introduction to the process matrix formalism and various memory structures. In Sec.
III
, we cast the RB procedure in the process matrix formalism and derive the resulting ASF curves for time-dependent Markovian and classical memory scenarios. In Sec.
IV
we study the various blindness criteria and class of RB-blind Hamiltonians. Sec.
V
includes discussion on worst-case error performance of gates under RB blind scenario and relevance of memory effects. We conclude and discuss future prospects in Sec.
VI
.

II
Preliminaries

In this section we summarize the randomized benchmarking (RB) protocol based on the Clifford group under the assumption of gate-independent noise. We then introduce the process-matrix formalism for multi-time quantum processes and illustrate how its structure captures different types of non-Markovian memory effects. This framework naturally supports modeling non-Markovian errors within the RB protocol, which we explore in next section. All the mathematical notations we use will also be introduced in this section.

II.1
Randomized Benchmarking Protocol

In this work our analysis includes the standard randomized benchmarking protocol, where the gate set is sampled from the
n
-qubit Clifford group
\mathcal{CG}_{n}

Ryan et al. 2009
;
Magesan et al. 2011
;
Emerson et al. 2005
;
Magesan et al. 2012a
;
Epstein et al. 2014
. Although numerous variants of RB have been developed in the literature (see
Helsen et al. 2022
;
Silva and Greplova 2025
and references therein), the Clifford-based protocol is a widely used approach and its results can be generalized to other variants. The standard RB protocol proceeds through the following steps:

1.

An initial state
\rho
is prepared. Ideally, this is taken to be a pure state
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
, though in practice it may be subject to state-preparation errors.

2.

The prepared state
\rho
is subject to a sequence of
m+1
Clifford gates
S_{i}\equiv\mathcal{C}_{m+1}^{(i)}\circ\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(i)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(i)}\circ\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(i)}
where the first
m
gates are sampled uniformly at random from
\mathcal{CG}_{n}
and the final gate is a fixed
motion reversal
gate chosen to invert the preceding sequence,
\mathcal{C}_{m+1}^{(i)}=\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}^{(i)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(i)}\circ\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(i)}\right)^{\dagger}
. Under ideal gate implementation
S_{i}\equiv\mathcal{I}
, i.e. the overall action is an identity map. More generally, each gate is noisy and modeled as
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}=\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(i)}\circ\mathcal{N}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{i}
is the ideal gate implementation at time-step
t
and
\mathcal{N}_{t}
is a completely-positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map representing the noise present in the implementation of
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{i}
. Here, we adopt the convention that noise acts
before
the ideal gate; equivalently, one can represent the noisy gate as
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t}\circ\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(i)}
with appropriate modification to the analysis.

3.

The final state is measured using a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\{E,E_{\perp}\}
satisfying
E+E_{\perp}=\mathbb{I}
where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity operator and
E,E_{\perp}
are positive semi-definite. Ideally,
E=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
, corresponding to perfect projective measurement onto the prepared state, but in practice we allow for measurement errors.

4.

For each sequence
S_{i}
, the experiment is repeated multiple times to gather sufficient statistics. The outcomes from these runs are averaged to obtain the sequence fidelity
F_{i}^{(m)}:=\operatorname{Tr}\left\{ES_{i}(\rho)\right\}\in[0,1]
. In this work, we do not explicitly account for statistical fluctuations due to finite number of shots. This analysis can be dealt with separately as pointed out in
Wallman and Flammia 2014
;
Granade et al. 2015
.

5.

It is infeasible to sample the entire space of all the possible sequences. Even for a qubit the sample space comprises
24^{m}
random sequences for sequence length
m
. For practical implementation, small
k\ll|\mathcal{CG}_{n}|
subset of random sequences are chosen uniformly. The corresponding sequence fidelity values
\{F_{i}^{(m)}\}_{i=1}^{k}
can be treated as instances of a random variable whose mean gives us an estimate of
average sequence fidelity
(ASF). We assume that this mean,
F^{(m)}:=1/k\sum_{i=1}^{k}F_{i}^{(m)}
, is close to the true ASF denoted as
\bar{F}^{(m)}
. An in-depth analysis for the optimal number of sequences
k
can be found in
Wallman and Flammia 2014
.

6.

Finally, the procedure is repeated for multiple values of the sequence length
m
. The dependence of the ASF on
m
is then fitted to an exponential decay model, from which one extracts the average error rate of the implemented Clifford operations.

Under the assumption of time and gate independent
Markovian
noise, the ASF takes the exponential form.

\bar{F}^{(m)}=Aq^{m}+B

(1)

where
q\in[0,1]
captures the average error rate of each gate. The coefficients
A
and
B
capture the state preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors, respectively.
Fitting the experimentally obtained ASF data to Eq. (
1
) yields an estimate of the decay parameter
q
, which is related to the average gate error by the relation
Emerson et al. 2005
,

r=(1-q)\frac{d-1}{d}

(2)

where
d
is the dimension of the system we are dealing with. A key feature of Eq. (
1
) is that it separates SPAM errors from gate errors, enabling a robust estimation of
q
from experimental data. This separation is, in fact, a general feature of Markovian noise. For time-dependent Markovian noise, it has been shown in
Wallman and Flammia 2014
that the ASF takes the form

\bar{F}^{(m)}=A\prod_{t=1}^{m}q_{t}+B

(3)

where
q_{i}\in[0,1]
quantifies the average error rate at time step
t
.

In contrast for non-Markovian noise models, as noted in
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2021
;
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2022
, the SPAM parameters do not separate from the gate errors, making it nontrivial to define an average error rate per gate and only allows the estimation of an average error over entire gate sequence of length
m
.

II.2
Process Matrix Formalism

A multi-time quantum process describes a scenario in which a quantum system is subjected to interventions at multiple time steps, where the interventions can be depicted as ‘labs’ performing a valid quantum operation, i.e. completely positive and trace non-increasing maps. Between these interventions, the system interacts with the environment through joint system-environment unitary operations. The process matrix framework provides a natural language for the operational description of multi-time processes. Process matrices, as proposed originally in
Oreshkov et al. 2012
characterize the most general correlation compatible with the local validity of quantum mechanics in the labs (See Fig.
1
). However, when a definite causal order exists between the labs, as is the case in the multi-time scenario, the process matrix satisfies causality constraints
Costa and Shrapnel 2016
and is equivalent to the quantum comb/process tensor
Pollock et al. 2018b
and has been widely used to model non-Markovian quantum dynamics and provides an operational definition of quantum stochastic processes
Pollock et al. 2018b
;
Pollock et al. 2018a
.

We consider a system
S
interacting with an environment
E
. The joint initial state is
\lambda^{S_{I}E}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{1})
, where
\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}^{E}_{1}
are the initial system and environment Hilbert spaces, respectively, and
\mathcal{L}(.)
represents the space of linear operators. At each time step
t
, a quantum instrument acts locally on the system. Each instrument is represented by a set of CP trace non-increasing maps
\{\mathcal{T}_{m_{t}|x_{t}}\}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t})
, where
\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t}
and
\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t}
are the input and output Hilbert spaces of the system at time-step
t
, respectively. The label
x_{t}
denotes the instrument setting and
m_{t}
indexes the measurement outcome. The map
\mathcal{T}_{x_{t}}=\sum_{m_{t}}\mathcal{T}_{m_{t}|x_{t}}
is a CPTP map corresponding to the case where outcome information is ignored. Between successive instrument applications, the system interacts with the environment through a joint unitary operation, thereby generating system-environment correlations that can lead to non-Markovian effects (see Fig.
2
).

For calculational purposes, we represent all operations as linear operators on the relevant Hilbert spaces using the Choi–Jamiołkowski (CJ) isomorphism
Choi 1975
;
Jamiołkowski 1972
. For a linear map
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{B})
the CJ form is defined as
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{M}\big]\!\big]=\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{M}|\mathbb{I}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathbb{I}|\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{B})
where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity map on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{A})
and
|\mathbb{I}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{j}\ket{j}\otimes\ket{j}
is the unnormalised maximally entangled state and
\{\ket{j}\}
is an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}^{A}
.

In this formalism, all system-environment correlations across time are captured by a single process matrix
W
which is a positive semi-definite operator acting on the tensor product of all the input-output Hilbert spaces. For an
n
-step process
W\in\mathcal{L}(\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t})
, the joint probability of observing a sequence of outcomes
\vec{m}_{n}\equiv\{m_{1},m_{2},\cdots m_{n}\}
given instrument settings
\vec{x}_{n}\equiv\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots x_{n}\}
is determined by the generalized Born rule
Oreshkov et al. 2012

p(\vec{m}_{n}|\vec{x}_{n})=\operatorname{Tr}\left(W^{T}.\left(\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n}\big[\!\big[\mathcal{T}_{m_{t}|x_{t}}\big]\!\big]\right)\right)

(4)

where
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{T}_{m_{t}|x_{t}}\big]\!\big]
is the CJ form of the instrument at time-step
t
. The transpose
W^{T}
is taken with respect to the basis in which the CJ isomorphism is defined.

The process matrix can be viewed as a quantum state (up to a normalization factor) on the space
\mathcal{L}(\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t})
but it is constrained to lie within a proper subset of this space by a set of linear constraints that ensure valid probability distributions for all admissible instruments
Araújo et al. 2015
. This “state-like” encoding of multi-time correlations is powerful, as it allows one to infer memory effects of the environment directly from the structure of
W
. Given a particular system-environment evolution (like a sequence of joint unitaries as in Fig.
2
), the process matrix can be explicitly constructed using the link product which provides a way of composing linear maps in their CJ representation. Given two linear operators
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{M})
,
\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
, the link product
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{M}\big]\!\big]\star\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}\big]\!\big]=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{M}\cap\mathcal{H}_{N}}\left[\left(\mathbb{I}^{\mathcal{H}_{N}\backslash\mathcal{H}_{M}}\otimes\big[\!\big[\mathcal{M}\big]\!\big]^{T_{\mathcal{H}_{M}\cap\mathcal{H}_{N}}}\right)\left(\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}\big]\!\big]\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\mathcal{H}_{M}\backslash\mathcal{H}_{N}}\right)\right]
where
T_{\mathcal{H}_{M}\cap\mathcal{H}_{N}}
is the partial transpose over the shared Hilbert space. In Appendix
A
we detail the properties of the link product and how it can be used to evaluate time-ordered process matrices and Eq. (
4
).

II.2.1
Markovian processes

In a Markovian process, environment does not mediate temporal correlations across time steps. In terms of the structure of process matrix, a Markovian process admits a tensor product factorization in terms of initial state and the channel connecting the labs as following
Costa and Shrapnel 2016
;
Giarmatzi and Costa 2021
;
Pollock et al. 2018a

W_{M}=\rho^{S_{I}}\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n-1}\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{t}\big]\!\big]^{S_{O}S_{I}},

(5)

where
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{t}\big]\!\big]\in\mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t+1}\right)
is the CJ representation of a CPTP map connecting time
t
to
t{+}1
, and
\rho^{S_{I}}
is the initial system state. As evident from the structure, a Markovian process can be simulated as a sequence of system–environment interactions in which the system couples to a fresh, uncorrelated environment at each time step.

II.2.2
Non-Markovian processes

A process which does not admit factorization in the form of Eq. (
5
) is called a non-Markovian process. Physically, a non-Markovian process can result from interaction with a common environment at various time steps, allowing the environment to retain memory and mediate temporal correlations between labs. Depending on the type of correlation mediated across time-steps, non-Markovian processes can be categorized into two broad subclasses, namely
classical memory
and
quantum memory
processes
Giarmatzi and Costa 2021
;
Roy et al. 2024
;
Taranto et al. 2024
.

The process-matrix formalism provides a structured way to characterize such memory effects. In this work, we focus specifically on RB protocols where the noise originates from a classical memory.

For a non-Markovian process with classical memory, each system-environment interaction can be simulated as the environment effectively measuring the system and storing a classical record of the outcome. Between each lab at times
t
and
t+1
, the environment acquires classical information (an outcome
a_{t}
) conditioned on both the chosen setting
x_{t}
and all previous outcomes and settings denoted by
\vec{a}_{t-1}\equiv(a_{0},a_{1}\cdots a_{t-1})
and
\vec{x}_{t-1}\equiv(x_{0},x_{1}\cdots x_{t-1})
. This stored information is then fed-forward and determines the future system-environment interactions. This picture is captured mathematically by a sequence of conditional quantum instruments
\{\mathcal{N}_{a_{t}|x_{t}}\}_{a_{t}}
where each instrument depends on the entire classical history and is modeled with a probability distribution
p(x_{t}|\vec{a}_{t-1},\vec{x}_{t-1})
. The overall process is then described by a process matrix that averages over all possible settings and measurement outcomes (see
Giarmatzi and Costa 2021
for detailed derivation):

W_{CFF}=\sum_{\vec{x}_{n},\vec{a}_{n}}p(x_{0})\rho_{a_{0}|x_{0}}\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n-1}p(x_{t}|\vec{a}_{t-1},\vec{x}_{t-1})\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{a_{t}|x_{t}}\big]\!\big]

(6)

where
\{\rho_{a_{0}|x_{0}}\}_{a_{0}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{S_{I}})
is a set of sub-normalized states satisfying
\operatorname{Tr}\sum_{a_{0}}\rho_{a_{0}|x_{0}}=1
and is the output of quantum instrument
\{\mathcal{N}_{a_{0}|x_{0}}\}_{a_{0}}
at the “zeroth” time-step. The choice for this form will become clear in Sec.
III.3
. We refer to this mechanism as the
classical feed-forward (CFF) mechanism
.

A special case arises when the environmental instruments are deterministic (CPTP maps) and depend only on a common classical label
x
. The process matrix reduces to

W_{CCC}=\sum_{x}p(x)\rho_{x}\otimes\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{x}\big]\!\big]\otimes\underbrace{\cdots}_{n-1~\text{times}}\otimes\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{x}\big]\!\big]

(7)

where
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{x}\big]\!\big]
is a CPTP map conditioned on
x
. We refer to such processes as
classical common-cause (CCC)
. This form shows that CCC processes are convex mixtures of Markovian processes, Eq. (
5
)
Goswami et al. 2024
. Each CPTP map can also have time-dependence in which case we will have a convex mixture of time-dependent Markovian processes.

We emphasize that such classical correlations may arise either from coupling to classical stochastic fields (e.g. fluctuating electromagnetic backgrounds) or from genuine interactions with a quantum environment, as demonstrated in
Goswami et al. 2024
;
White et al. 2025
. Importantly, even when the memory is mediated by a quantum environment, classical-memory processes can always be
simulated
by classical stochastic processes. In Appendix
B
, we summarize the classes of system–environment interaction Hamiltonians known to generate such memory effects. Any process matrix not of the form of Markovian or classical memory, constitutes a quantum memory process.

III
Modeling non-Markovian noise in Randomized Benchmarking

The process matrix formalism offers a natural framework for analyzing non-Markovian effects in multi-time quantum processes. In this section, we demonstrate how this formalism can be applied to model non-Markovian noise within the randomized benchmarking protocol.

We start with initial system-environment state
\rho\otimes\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0})
and
the action of the noisy gate is given by
\mathcal{C}_{t}(\cdot)=\mathcal{U}^{SE}_{t-1}\circ\left(\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)(\cdot)
where
\mathcal{U}^{SE}_{t-1}
is the joint system-environment unitary that can generate non-Markovian correlations and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
are the instruments in the lab as defined in the section
II.2
and are sampled from the
n
-qubit Clifford group. In the final time-step we apply the motion-reversal gate and measure with POVM element
E
. All components except for the control operations
\{\mathcal{G}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{m+1}
and
E
can be collected into the process matrix, which acts as a higher-order quantum operation mapping the chosen sequence of gates to corresponding sequence fidelity (see Fig.
3
)
Taranto et al. 2025
. More generally, if instead we started with an initially correlated state
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0})
then we can apply a trace-and-replace operation on the system to prepare a product state. In practice, this means that we require very weak coupling between the system and the environment at the beginning of the protocol as strong initial correlations would prevent the separation of SPAM errors from gate errors
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2021
.
In the following subsections, we first formulate the ASF calculation within the process matrix framework, which allows us to model Markovian as well as non-Markovian gate errors. We then evaluate the ASF for time-dependent Markovian processes and for non-Markovian processes with classical memory. Finally, we argue that the ASF provides meaningful characterization of average gate errors even in the presence of classical memory effects.

III.1
ASF calculation using process matrix formalism

The instruments under consideration are unitary gates sampled from the
n
-qubit Clifford group,
\mathcal{CF}_{n}
.
For a given sequence of Clifford gates
S_{\alpha}
, the instrument at time step
t
has the Choi–Jamiołkowski (CJ) representation

\big[\!\big[\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(\alpha)}\big]\!\big]=\sum_{i,j}\ket{i}\bra{j}\otimes G_{t}^{(\alpha)}\ket{i}\bra{j}G_{t}^{(\alpha)\dagger},

(8)

where
\{\ket{i}\}
denotes an orthonormal basis, and
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(\alpha)}\big]\!\big]\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t})
is the CJ representation of the unitary gate
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(\alpha)}(.)=G_{t}^{(\alpha)}(.)G_{t}^{(\alpha)\dagger}
.

The final instrument consists of the action of
\mathcal{G}_{m+1}^{(\alpha)}
followed by a POVM measurement
E
.
Letting
\sigma=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{E}}(\lambda)
, the multi-time process matrix can be written using the link product (see Appendix
A
) as

W=(\rho\otimes\sigma)\star\big[\!\big[\mathcal{U}^{SE}_{0}\big]\!\big]\star\cdots\star\big[\!\big[\mathcal{U}^{SE}_{m}\big]\!\big]\star\mathbb{I}^{\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m+1}},

(9)

where
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{U}^{SE}_{0}\big]\!\big]\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{1})
and, for
t>0
,

\big[\!\big[\mathcal{U}^{SE}_{t}\big]\!\big]\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t+1}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{E}_{t+1}).

Here
\mathcal{H}^{S_{I}}_{t}
and
\mathcal{H}^{S_{O}}_{t}
denote the input and output Hilbert spaces of the system at time
t
, while
\mathcal{H}^{E}_{t}
corresponds to the environment.

Since all instruments, except for the final POVM measurement, are CJ representation of Clifford unitaries, Eq. (
4
) provides the fidelity of the sequence
S_{\alpha}
. Subsequently, we can express the sequence fidelity as a link product between the process matrix
W

III.2.1
Randomisation of initial state and final measurement

III.3
Non-Markovian processes with classical memory

We now derive the ASF for non-Markovian noise models whose multi-time correlations exhibit classical memory as described in Sec.
II.2
. In the following section, we analyze both cases for CCC and CFF type memory models and derive the modified ASF expression from RB protocol.

III.3.1
ASF for classical common cause (CCC)

The process matrix for a CCC-type process is of the form given in Eq. (
7
), which is a
convex mixture of Markovian processes
. The underlying cause of this correlation can be either classical or quantum, and in
Goswami et al. 2024
a class of system–environment interaction Hamiltonians was identified that gives rise to such memory effects (see Appendix
B
). Importantly, regardless of the physical mechanism generating the correlations, the resulting process matrix has the same mathematical structure. This allows us to study all such classically correlated noise models within a unified framework.

We can write Eq. (
7
) as
W_{CCC}=\sum_{x}p_{x}W^{(x)}_{M}
where each
W^{(x)}_{M}
corresponds to a Markovian process matrix with associated noise map
\mathcal{N}_{x}
and weight factor
p_{x}
. Here we are considering the scenario, where each Markovian process matrix
W^{(x)}_{M}
correspond to time-independent process. It is straightforward to extend the analysis to convex mixture of time-dependent Markovian processes.
From Eq. (
14
) we have
F_{\alpha}^{(m)}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\left[\left(\tilde{W}^{(x)}_{M,\alpha}\otimes E\right){\Gamma}\right]
, for some sequence
S_{\alpha}
. Using Eq. (
III.1
) we obtain the ASF

\bar{F}^{(m)}=\sum_{x}p_{x}A_{x}q_{x}^{m}+B,

(24)

where
A_{x}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(E\left(\mathcal{N}_{x}(\rho)-\mathbb{I}/d\right)\right)
, and
B=\operatorname{Tr}(E)/d
.
Although Eq. (
24
) appears similar to the ASF of a purely Markovian noise model, we see that the SPAM contributions are not separate from gate errors. Moreover, the ASF is now expressed as a
sum of exponential decays
, rather than a single exponential, making parameter estimation more challenging. This is a well-studied problem in signal processing (see
Helsen et al. 2022
;
Roy et al. 1986
;
Li and Liao 2022
and references therein and Sec.
IV.2
).

In order to decouple the SPAM error term
A_{x}
from the gate errors, we employ the randomization of initial state and the final measurement as discussed in Sec.
III.2
. This approximates the ASF to be of the form

\bar{F}^{(m)}\approx A\sum_{x}p_{x}q_{x}^{m+1}+B-\mathcal{O}(r_{\mathcal{M}}),

(25)

where
A=\operatorname{Tr}\left(E\left[\mathcal{P}(\rho)-\mathbb{I}/d\right]\right)
,
B=\operatorname{Tr}(E)/d
and correction term
\mathcal{O}(r_{\mathcal{M}})=\sum_{x}p_{x}q_{x}dr_{\mathcal{M}}/(d-1)
now includes local measurement errors. It is important to note that this procedure would require more resources in terms of the number of runs of the RB experiment, but this is expected as we are trying to benchmark correlated noise.

Assuming that Eq. (
25
) can in principle be fitted efficiently to extract the parameters
\{p_{x},q_{x}\}
, the interpretation of the ASF differs significantly from the Markovian case. Although the error maps
\mathcal{N}_{x}
are CPTP maps acting only on the system, they are correlated through a common environmental degree of freedom. For Markovian errors, the independence of error maps allows one to assign an average error rate to each gate individually. In contrast, for errors modeled by CCC, it is not meaningful to define a per-gate error rate. Rather we can provide the average error rate of a
sequence
of gates of length
m
. For a given gate sequence
S_{i}
of length
m
, the entire process is described by the CPTP map
\mathcal{R}_{S_{i}}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{S_{i}}(\rho)=\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{G}_{m+1}\circ\mathcal{N}_{x}\cdots\circ\mathcal{G}_{1}\circ\mathcal{N}_{x}(\rho)
. With ideal state preparation and measurement (
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{I}
), the average fidelity of
\mathcal{R}_{S_{i}}
is
\hat{F}^{(m)}_{(\mathcal{R}_{S_{i}})}
and its average error rate is
\hat{r}_{{(\mathcal{R}_{S_{i}})}}=1-\hat{F}^{(m)}_{(\mathcal{R}_{S_{i}})}
. Averaging over all sequence
S_{i}
gives the average error rate of the
m
length process

r(m)=\frac{d-1}{d}\left(1-\sum_{x}p_{x}q_{x}^{m+1}\right).

(26)

Thus, the parameters
\{p_{x},q_{x}\}
provide a quantitative measure of how far, on average, the system state deviates from the ideal target state after
m
noisy gates. Even though per-gate error rates are ill-defined, the framework still yields a meaningful characterization of overall computational accuracy for sequences of arbitrary length.

IV
non-Markovian ASF and conditions for Blindness to RB protocol

Randomized benchmarking provides a scalable method to estimate the average error rates of a gate set without incurring the resource costs of full process tomography. However, this averaging procedure can also mask important non-Markovian effects. As shown in Sec.
III.3
, classical-memory error models can produce ASFs with multiple decay parameters, which can in principle reveal signatures of non-Markovian behavior. Nevertheless, there exist scenarios where the resulting ASF is indistinguishable from that of a purely Markovian noise model.

In this section, we establish criteria under which classical-memory errors are effectively
blind
to the RB protocol, rendering them undetectable through standard ASF analysis.

IV.1
Monotonicity of ASF

A deviation in the ASF curve from a single-parameter exponential decay is often interpreted as a signature of non-Markovian dynamics
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2021
;
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2022
;
Epstein et al. 2014
;
Ball et al. 2016
;
Fogarty et al. 2015
;
Qi and Ng 2021
;
Ceasura et al. 2022
;
Gandhari and Gullans 2025
;
Mavadia et al. 2018
;
Veldhorst et al. 2014
. A definitive indicator is an increase in the ASF with sequence length, since under the assumption of time-independent Markovian noise, the decay parameter
q
in Eq. (
1
) is expected to satisfy
q\in[0,1]
. More generally, for Markovian noise described by completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps the admissible range is
-1/\left(d^{2}-1\right)\leq q\leq 1
, where
d
is the Hilbert space dimension of the system. This is evident from the fact that given any CPTP noise map
\mathcal{N}
, the corresponding decay parameter is
q=\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left(\big[\!\big[\mathbb{I}\big]\!\big]\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}\big]\!\big]\right)-1\right]/\left(d^{2}-1\right)
, which can take the minimum value of
-1/\left(d^{2}-1\right)
(when the map’s Kraus operators are all traceless). In practice, however, we typically assume
q
to be positive and close to unity, reflecting the expectation that gates are implemented with high fidelity.

For CCC-type errors, we model the noise as a convex combination of time-independent Markovian error channels represented by CPTP maps
\mathcal{N}_{x}
, each of which we refer to as a Markovian
branch
. Note that we can also consider time-dependent Markovian branches where each error channel also has a time-dependence.
A priori
, there is no requirement that the decay parameter
q_{x}
associated with a given branch be strictly positive—consider the action of a single Clifford gate
\mathcal{C}
applied to a pure state
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
:

\mathcal{C}\!\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)=\mathcal{G}\circ\Bigg(\sum_{x}p_{x}\,\mathcal{N}_{x}\!\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)\Bigg),

(29)

where
\mathcal{G}
denotes the ideal (noiseless) gate implementation,
p_{x}
are the branch probabilities, and
\mathcal{N}_{x}
the associated error channels. The average fidelity of the error channel is given by
\sum_{x}p_{x}\int\bra{\psi}\mathcal{N}_{x}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)\ket{\psi}d\psi=\sum_{x}p_{x}\left(q_{x}+(1-q_{x})/d\right)
. Since physical gates are expected to have high average fidelity, we impose the condition
\sum_{x}p_{x}\left(q_{x}+(1-q_{x})/d\right)=1-\epsilon
, for
0<\epsilon\ll 1
, and therefore
\sum_{x}p_{x}q_{x}=(1-d\epsilon)/(d-1)
. Thus, even though the overall gate fidelity may be close to unity, the individual
q_{x}
may in principle take negative values for some
x
. Such unfavorable branches will have correlations along the entire sequence—albeit with small probability—and may lead to oscillatory behavior in the ASF.

A toy example for such a case could be a convex combination of two Markovian branches
\mathcal{N}_{1}=\mathcal{I}
with
q_{1}=1
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}=\mathcal{X}
with
q_{2}=-1/3
where
\mathcal{X}
is a bit-flip operation. If we target the average fidelity of single gate application to be
0.9
, the mixture weight on the bit-flip branch is
0.15
. The ASF for an
m
length sequence is
A\left(0.85+0.15(-1/3)^{m+1}\right)+B
which exhibits exponentially damped even–odd oscillations. Although this is a contrived example, it illustrates that the ASF for CCC noise models need not be monotonic. In practice however, such oscillatory deviations will be strongly suppressed by the exponential damping factor and may be further obscured by statistical fluctuations arising from finite sampling and the use of a limited Clifford gate subset.

Moreover, realistic noise models may not exhibit negative decay parameters and still be non-Markovian as was shown in
Fogarty et al. 2015
, where the non-exponential decays observed in silicon quantum-dot qubit
Veldhorst et al. 2014
were explained by low-frequency drift of qubit resonance frequency which fits the CCC-type error model.

For CFF-type errors, the relevant decay parameters are
\beta_{a_{i}|x_{i}}
as defined in Eq. (
28
). These parameters can take values in the range
\left[-\operatorname{Tr}\left(\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{a_{i}|x_{i}}\big]\!\big]\right)/d(d^{2}-1),1\right]
with the constraint
\sum_{a_{i}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\big[\!\big[\mathcal{N}_{a_{i}|x_{i}}\big]\!\big]\right)=d
. Consequently, the ASF can, in principle, also exhibit non-monotonic behavior under CFF noise. However, as in the CCC case, such deviations are likely to be difficult to observe in experiments. On the other hand if all the decay parameters are positive then the ASF will exhibit monotonic decaying behaviour.

As long as the decay parameters lie in the range
(0,1)
, it follows that
\bar{F}^{(m)}>\bar{F}^{(m+1)}\;\;\forall m
. For CCC-type errors it is evident from

\bar{F}^{(m)}-\bar{F}^{(m+1)}=\tilde{A}\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}q_{x}^{m+1}\left[1-q_{x}\right]\right)

(30)

where parameters
\{p_{x}\}\in[0,1]
are branch weights and
\tilde{A}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(E\left[\mathcal{P}(\rho)-\mathbb{I}/d\right]\right)
which is typically positive. For CFF-type errors, the analogous difference is

\displaystyle\bar{F}^{(m)}-\bar{F}^{(m+1)}=A\left(\sum_{\vec{a},\vec{x}}\prod_{i=0}^{m}\gamma_{a_{i},x_{i}\mid\vec{a}_{i-1},\vec{x}_{i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(1-\sum_{a_{m+1},x_{m+1}}\gamma_{a_{m+1},x_{m+1}\mid\vec{a}_{m},\vec{x}_{m}}\right).

(31)

which is also non-negative provided
\gamma_{a_{i},x_{i}\mid\vec{a}_{i-1},\vec{x}_{i-1}}\in(0,1)
. Thus, under reasonable assumption, the ASF curves for noise models with classical memory are guaranteed to decrease monotonically with sequence length, implying that randomized benchmarking is insensitive to such non-Markovian noise when using non-monotonicity as the diagnostic criterion. Furthermore, as noted in
Wallman and Flammia 2014
, any parameters exceeding 1 can serve as a marker of non-Markovian noise. Our analysis extends this point by showing that for non-Markovian noise with purely classical memory, all the relevant parameters are necessarily upper bounded by 1.
Hence, observing any parameter value greater than 1 constitutes strong evidence of non-Markovian noise with genuinely quantum memory effects.

IV.2
Complete blindness to RB

In general, the ASF for classical memory noise models deviates from a single exponential decay, making it crucial to resolve and distinguish the different decay parameters present in the RB data. In
Helsen et al. 2022
, the authors showed that the ASF for various variants of RB under Markovian noise can present as a sum of matrix exponents and studied the problem of fitting and extracting multiple decay parameters from the RB data. In particular the authors employ high-resolution spectral estimation techniques, specifically ESPRIT
Roy et al. 1986
and MUSIC
Schmidt 1986
, and identify parameter regimes in which reliable estimation of the decay parameters is feasible. As we have shown, the ASF data for CCC type process follows sum of exponentials, and therefore these techniques can be implemented to figure out the decay parameters. Here, we first demonstrate resolving multiple exponents for a CCC noise model using the ESPRIT algorithm. We will then show that RB data can also be completely blind to some non-Markovian interactions and present a class of interaction Hamiltonians that exhibit this feature.

Consider a qubit process where the system-environment interaction unitary at every time step is the controlled unitary of the form
U^{ES}=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|^{E}\otimes U_{1}+|1\rangle\!\langle 1|^{E}\otimes U_{2}

Even in cases where distinct decay parameters can be resolved (with methods like ESPRIT), non-Markovian noise may still mislead optimization strategies. Consider, for instance, a CCC-type model that is a convex combination of two Markovian branches with parameters
\{p,q_{1}\}
and
\{1-p,q_{2}\}
. Suppose
q_{1}>q_{2}
and one attempts to tune experimental parameters to bias the system toward the higher-fidelity branch (i.e.,
p\approx 1)
. While this tuning increases the average sequence fidelity, the physical nature of the corresponding error channels is essential, for instance, consider a scenario where
q_{1}
corresponds to a coherent unitary rotation, whereas
q_{2}
corresponds to a stochastic noise process. It is well known that coherent errors accumulate more adversely than stochastic errors with respect to worst-case error metrics
Sanders et al. 2015
. As a result, simply maximizing average fidelity may not yield the most robust error performance.

On the other hand consider the case where the decay parameters associated with the two Markovian branches are nearly identical,
q_{1}\approx q_{2}=q
. In this situation, the ASF reduces to a form that appears indistinguishable from a purely Markovian process with a single decay parameter
q
. Nevertheless, the underlying noise remains non-Markovian and can lead to non-trivial effects, which we discuss in Sec.
V
. This indicates that for non-Markovian errors with classical memory, if all decay parameters are equal, the ASF is
completely blind
to the underlying non-Markovian correlations.

As noted earlier, classical memory effects can arise from system-environment interactions where the environment itself is quantum mechanical. A natural question, therefore, is whether there exist class of system-environment interactions that generate classical memory noise, which is completely blind to the RB protocol.

A simple set of sufficient conditions for RB blind Hamiltonians can be determined.
Consider the environment system Hamiltonian of the form
H^{ES}=\sum_{i}H_{i}^{E}\otimes H^{S}_{i}
. The constraint that each of the environment terms
H^{E}_{i}

[H^{E}_{i},H^{E}_{j}]=0
) ensures the resulting process is a convex sum of Markovian processes, i.e., a CCC process (see Appendix
B
). Consider that the eigenspectrum of
H_{i}^{E}
is
\{\lambda_{i},|\lambda\rangle\}
, where
\{|\lambda\rangle\}
is the common eigenbasis of the environment Hamiltonian terms. A straightforward rearrangement leads to
H^{ES}=\sum_{\lambda}|\lambda\rangle\!\langle\lambda|\otimes H^{S}_{\lambda}
, where
H^{S}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}H_{i}^{S}
. Consider
\{e_{\lambda,m}\}
is the spectrum of
H_{\lambda}^{S}

q_{\lambda}=1/(d^{2}-1)\left(|\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{exp}(-itH^{S}_{\lambda}))|^{2}-1\right)
is equivalent to
C_{\lambda}=\sum_{m>n}\cos(t(e_{\lambda,m}-e_{\lambda,n}))
being invariant for all
\lambda

, where

Clearly, such Hamiltonians lead to non-Markovian noise which is invisible in RB protocol. As we will show in the following section, the memory effects in such processes still have a non-trivial impact on the worst-case error, an important parameter to assess fault tolerance.

V
Worst case errors

RB protocols are designed to estimate the average error rates of gates, and the protocols are experimentally accessible. However, fault-tolerance thresholds are typically determined by worst-case error metrics, such as the diamond norm, which are not directly measurable in standard experiments

;
Magesan et al. 2012a
;
Wallman and Flammia 2014

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we developed a general framework to study randomized benchmarking (RB) under non-Markovian noise models that introduce classical temporal correlations, either through stochastic classical fields or interactions with a quantum environment. We derived analytical expressions for the average sequence fidelity (ASF) in such settings and demonstrated that, unlike the standard Markovian case, the ASF can be expressed as a sum of multiple exponential decays. We showed how the additional decay parameters can be interpreted to yield useful information for estimating average errors, and how established signal-processing techniques such as ESPRIT and MUSIC can be employed to extract these parameters from RB data.

Our analysis revealed several important features. First, while ASF curves with classical correlations can in principle exhibit non-monotonic behavior, such deviations are exponentially suppressed, hence difficult to observe in practice. When all decay parameters remain positive, the ASF is guaranteed to decrease monotonically, making non-Markovian effects invisible to monotonicity-based diagnostics. Second, we established that the decay parameters are necessarily bounded above by unity; thus, any observed violation of this bound provides a witness for genuinely quantum memory effects. Moreover, we identified classes of interaction Hamiltonians that generate classical memory processes completely blind to RB, yielding ASF curves indistinguishable from Markovian noise despite the presence of temporal correlations. Finally, we investigated the implications for worst-case error metrics. While RB provides estimates of average gate errors, fault-tolerance thresholds are governed by worst-case performance quantified by the diamond norm
Kueng et al. 2016
;
Sanders et al. 2015
;
Epstein et al. 2014
. We argued that classical memory processes can alter worst-case error behavior, and in some scenarios non-Markovian effects may even reduce worst-case errors. These findings underline the importance of going beyond average metrics when assessing the reliability of quantum devices especially in the presence of correlated noise.

Overall, our results clarify both the capabilities and blind spots of randomized benchmarking in the presence of temporal correlations. They also motivate the development of complementary protocols capable of jointly estimating average and worst-case error metrics under realistic noise models
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Sanders et al. 2015
;
Wallman and Flammia 2014
. Moreover, it is important to come up with theoretical bounds on worst-case errors in case of non-Markovian noise, similar to the bounds presented in
Wallman and Flammia 2014
for Markovian noise models. It would also be interesting to explore whether the RB protocol can be modified to reliably provide an operational metric on the amount of non-Markovian noise present during gate implementations. Finally, we note that the present analysis has been limited to Clifford operations under the assumption of gate-independent errors. We anticipate that our framework can be readily extended to more general gate sets, and that established techniques
Figueroa-Romero et al. 2022
;
Wallman 2018
can be naturally integrated to treat these broader scenarios.

Here, we provide a brief summary of the link product and the use of it to construct multi-time processes.

The link product (
\star

A\in\mathcal{L}\left(\bigotimes_{p_{j}\in p}\mathcal{H}_{p_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle A\star B=\operatorname{Tr}_{p\cap q}\left[\left(\mathbb{I}_{q\setminus p}\otimes A^{T_{p\cap q}}\right)\left(B\otimes\mathbb{I}_{p\setminus q}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{H}_{q\setminus p}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{p\setminus q}\right),

(39)

where the operators
(.)^{T}_{p\cap q}
and

represent the partial transpose and partial trace, respectively, over the common space
p\cap q
. In particular, when the operators do not share common space, link product reduces to the usual tensor product, i.e.,
A\star B=A\otimes B
when
p\cap q=\phi
. The link product has the following useful properties. It is (i) Hermiticity preserving, i.e.,
A,B
Hermitian
\implies A\star B
is Hermitian, (ii) positivity preserving, i.e.,
A,B\geq 0\implies A\star B\geq 0
, (iii) commutative
A\star B=B\star A
and (iv) associative, i.e.,
A\star(B\star C)=(A\star B)\star C)
, where operators
A,B,
and
C
do not have any joint overlap.

Note that the action of a map
\mathcal{M}:S_{I}\rightarrow S_{O}
on state
\rho^{S_{I}}
in terms of the link product is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\rho^{S_{I}})

\displaystyle=Tr_{S_{I}}(\big[\!\big[\mathcal{M}\big]\!\big]^{S_{I}S_{O}}(\rho^{T_{S_{I}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{S_{O}})

(40)

\displaystyle=\big[\!\big[\mathcal{M}\big]\!\big]^{S_{I}S_{O}}\star\rho^{S_{I}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{S_{I}}\star\big[\!\big[\mathcal{M}\big]\!\big]^{S_{I}S_{O}}

\displaystyle\big[\mathcal{M}_{a_{N}}(\mathcal{U}_{N-1}\cdots

(41)

\displaystyle\cdots(\mathcal{U}_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{a_{2}}(\mathcal{U}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{a_{1}}(\rho))))].

This case however, is not as straightforward as the CCC case, where the interaction was with a common environment at every time step. For instance, again consider a three intervention scenario, with the interaction during the first time step of the form
H_{1}=\sum_{m}H^{E_{0}}_{m}\otimes H^{E_{1}S}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{E_{2}}
and the interaction during the second time step of the form
H_{2}=\sum_{m}H_{m}^{E_{0}}\otimes H_{m}^{E_{1}}\otimes H_{m}^{E_{2}S}
. If the following conditions are satisfied,

1.

[H^{E_{0}}_{m},H^{E_{0}}_{n}]=0
for all
m,n
and for both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}

2.

[H^{E_{1}}_{m},H^{E_{1}}_{n}]=0
for all
m,n
for
H_{2}

3.

Initial system-environment state is uncorrelated in spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}

then it is straightforward to show that the resulting process matrix is
of the form,

\displaystyle W=\sum_{xy}p_{x}\rho_{x}^{A_{I}}\otimes\big[\!\big[\mathcal{E}_{y|x}\big]\!\big]^{A_{O}B_{I}}\otimes\big[\!\big[\mathcal{T}_{xy}\big]\!\big]^{B_{O}C_{I}}.

(51)

Here,
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{E}_{y|x}\big]\!\big]
is CJ map corresponding to the CP map
\mathcal{E}_{y|x}
with
\sum_{y}\mathcal{E}_{y|x}
being a CPTP map for all
x
, and
\big[\!\big[\mathcal{T}_{xy}\big]\!\big]
are CJ maps corresponding to CPTP maps, and
p(x)\rho_{x}^{A_{I}}
has its origin in the reduced states of the initial
ES
state in some basis
|x\rangle
of Hilbert space of
E_{0}
. Evidently, the process is not a classical common cause process but a more general classical memory process with feed-forward.

Appendix C
Data fitting for Randomised Benchmarking

Here, we present our data fitting algorithm and examples of extracting the decay parameters from RB data for different CCC processes, where the origin of the RB data is a model comprising of two exponential decay terms and additional noise:

y_{i}=A_{1}q_{1}^{i+1}+A_{2}q_{2}^{i+1}+B+n_{i},

(52)

where
A_{1}=Ap_{1}
and
A_{2}=Ap_{2}
. The constant terms
A
and
B
capture the information of SPAM errors, while
p_{1}
and
p_{2}

denote the probabilities corresponding to the two different Markovian branches of the CCC process; and
n_{i}
is a noise term representing the deviation from the true ASF due to finite sampling or other possible noise. The fitting program aims to extract the probabilities
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
, the constants
A,B
, and the exponents
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
from the provided data {
y_{i}
}. For data fitting, we employ ESPRIT technique followed by solving a linear equation to extract the required parameters.
The complete workflow comprises of the following steps:

1.

The ASF data for a sequence length
m

[y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{m}]
can be expressed in the following matrix equation:

\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\\
\vdots\\
y_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}^{2}&q_{2}^{2}&1\\
q_{1}^{3}&q_{2}^{3}&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
q_{1}^{m+1}&q_{2}^{m+1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\\
A_{2}\\
B\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}\\
n_{2}\\
\vdots\\
n_{m}\end{pmatrix}

2.

Splitting and writing the data in the form of a Hankel matrix of dimension
l\times(m-l+1)
:

\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&\cdots&y_{m-l+1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
y_{l}&\cdots&y_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}^{2}&q_{2}^{2}&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
q_{1}^{l+1}&q^{l+1}_{2}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{1}q_{1}^{m-l}\\
A_{2}&\cdots&A_{2}q_{2}^{m-l}\\
B&\cdots&B\end{pmatrix}\\
+\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&\cdots&n_{m-l+1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
n_{l}&\cdots&n_{m}\end{pmatrix},

(53)

we obtain its singular values. Eq. (
53
) can be rewritten in shorthand as
Y=VA+N
, where
Y
is the Hankel matrix,
V
is called the Vandermonde matrix and
N
is the noise matrix. We choose
l=\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function.
The dominant singular values of the Hankel matrix denotes the number of different exponents (or decay parameters) present in the data (see Fig. (
8
)).

3.

The number of dominant singular values (supplied as the “model order”) as well as the data serve as input for the ESPRIT algorithm, which outputs the different exponents.

4.

Using the exponents, we determine
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and constant
B
by solving the linear equation Eq. (
53
).

5.

Once we obtain the parameter values for
A_{1}\text{ and }A_{2}
, we can find
p_{1}\text{ and }p_{2}
from the following relations:
p_{1}=\frac{A_{1}}{A_{1}+A_{2}}
and
p_{2}=\frac{A_{2}}{A_{1}+A_{2}}
.

Figure 8:
The plot shows singular values of the Hankel matrix from the simulated data presented in Sec.
IV.2
. The singular values are plotted in logarithmic scale in the Y-axis, and the two dominant singular values are clearly distinguished from the rest which correspond to two different expopnents present in the data.

ESPRIT algorithm is based on a rotational invariance property of the Vandermonde matrix. This relation takes the form

J_{2}V=J_{1}VH

(54)

where
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are the last and first row deletion matrix respectively, and
H
is the diagonal matrix comprising the exponents.

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=\scriptsize{\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0&0\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\end{pmatrix}_{l-1\times l}}=[\mathbf{I}_{l-1}~~\mathbf{0}]

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=\scriptsize{\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}_{l-1\times l}}=[\mathbf{0}~~\mathbf{I}_{l-1}]

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\scriptsize{\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}&0&0\\
0&q_{2}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}}

The singular value decomposition of the Hankel matrix in Eq. (
53
) can be broken into two parts and selecting the dominant singular values as the signal and the rest as noise, we obtain
Y=U_{s}\Sigma_{s}W_{s}^{\dagger}+U_{n}\Sigma_{n}W_{n}^{\dagger}
. Here,
\Sigma_{s}
contain the dominant singular values that are identified as signal and
U_{s},W_{s}
are the corresponding part of the isometries
U,W
which constitute the singular value decomposition. Similarly,
U_{n},W_{n}
correspond to the noise part. Comparing it to
Y=VA+N
, one can write the signal part as
VA=U_{s}\Sigma_{s}W_{s}^{\dagger}
or
V=U_{s}\Sigma_{s}W_{s}^{\dagger}A^{-1}
. Replacing this relation in Eq. (
54
), it follows that

J_{2}U_{s}=J_{1}U_{s}\Phi

(55)

where the eigenvalues of
\Phi
corresponds to the exponents of the data. The ESPRIT algorithm uses Eq. (
55
) and upon solving the linear equation for an unknown
\Phi
, gives out the eigenvalues (exponents
q_{1},q_{2}
). For the ideal case with zero noise and sequence length 200, it can perfectly resolve different exponents as with difference as close as
10^{-4}
. In presence of noise the performance depends both on the noise strength as well as the separation between the exponents (see
Helsen et al. 2022
for details).
