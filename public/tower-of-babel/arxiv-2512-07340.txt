Lyapunov maximizing measures for balanced pairs of matrices
Rui Gao

Abstract
. We show that every balanced pair (see Definition 1.1 ) of real 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrices admits a unique Lyapunov maximizing measure, and the measure is always Sturmian.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
denote the general linear group of
2\times 2
matrices over
\mathbb{R}
. We shall mainly consider matrices in
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
with positive determinant, so let us denote

\mathbf{G}:=\{X\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R}):\det X>0\}.

For each
X\in\mathbf{G}
, let
\rho(X)
denote its spectral radius. Given
\mathbb{A}=(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
, let

\hat{\rho}(\mathbb{A}):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\max\{\rho(X_{1}\cdots X_{n}):X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\in\{A,B\}\}\right)^{1/n}\in(0,+\infty),

called the
joint spectral radius
of
\mathbb{A}
. If the limit superior above can be attained by a finite product
X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
, then
X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
is called a
spectrum maximizing product
of
\mathbb{A}
. The
finiteness conjecture
of Lagarias and Wang
[
15
]
asserts that a spectrum maximizing product always exists for any
\mathbb{A}
. Actually the concept of joint spectral radius and the finiteness conjecture can be stated under more general settings (see, for example,
[
15
,
11
]
), but in this note we are only concerned about the special version described above.

Let us restate the finiteness conjecture in the language of ergodic theory. Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\cdots\}
and let
\sigma:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the one-sided full shift. Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the collection of
\sigma
-invariant Borel probability measures on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, endowed with the weak-* topology. Given
\mathbb{A}=(A_{0},A_{1})\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
and a matrix norm
\|\cdot\|
, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, by the sub-additive ergodic theorem, the limit

\chi(\mathbb{A},\mu):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}\log\|A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{2}}A_{i_{1}}\|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{n}\cdots)

(1)

exists (independent of the choice of
\|\cdot\|
), and it is called the (top)
Lyapunov exponent
of
\mathbb{A}
with respect to
\mu
. It is well-known that

\sup\{\chi(\mathbb{A},\mu):\mu\in\mathcal{M}\}=\log\hat{\rho}(\mathbb{A}).

If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
attains the supremum above, then it is called a (
Lyapunov
)
maximizing measure
of
\mathbb{A}
. Then the finiteness conjecture can be restated as: there always exists a maximizing measure supported on a periodic orbit of
\sigma
. The maximizing measures of
\mathbb{A}
introduced above can also be defined under more general settings; see, for example,
[
19
,
6
]
.

The finiteness conjecture was disproved by Bousch and Mairesse
[
7
]
, who constructed the first examples of
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
which admits no spectrum maximizing product; such
\mathbb{A}
with be called a
finiteness counter-example
. The basic idea of their construction is roughly as follows: choose a pair
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
carefully and show that there exist (uncountably many) finiteness counter-examples in the one-parameter family
(A,tB),t>0
. To the best of our knowledge, all the known finiteness counter-examples in the literature were constructed in this way, including
[
7
,
2
,
13
,
9
,
18
,
10
,
20
]
among others (we shall provide a different approach in Proposition
5.5
). Moreover, it turns out that every pair
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
in all the constructions cited above is a balanced pair defined below; see Proposition
5.3
for more details.

Let
I\in\mathbf{G}
denote the identity matrix and let
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})=\{X\in\mathbf{G}:\det X=1\}
. Define

\mathbf{A}:=\{A\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}):\tr A\geq 2\}\setminus\{I\}=\mathbf{H}\cup\mathbf{P},

where
\mathbf{H}=\{A\in\mathbf{A}:\tr A>2\}
and
\mathbf{P}=\{A\in\mathbf{A}:\tr A=2\}
. Define

H_{s,u}^{\lambda}:=\begin{pmatrix}s&u\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}s&u\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\in\mathbf{H}\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda>1,u<0<s,

(2)

and

P_{x}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf{P}\qquad\text{for}\quad x>0.

(3)

Let
A^{t}
denote the transpose of a matrix
A
.

Definition 1.1
.

\{A,B\}\subset\mathbf{A}
is called a
balanced pair
, if there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
such that for
\mathbb{A}=\{T^{-1}AT,T^{-1}BT\}
(called a
normal form
of
\{A,B\}
), one of the following mutually exclusive conditions holds:

(h)

\mathbb{A}=\{H_{s_{1},u_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}},H_{s_{2},u_{2}}^{\lambda_{2}}\}
with
u_{2}<u_{1}<0<s_{1}<s_{2}
;

(m)

\mathbb{A}=\{H_{s,u}^{\lambda},P_{x}\}
;

(p)

\mathbb{A}=\{P_{x},P_{y}^{t}\}
.

In the case (h),
\{A,B\}
is called a
co-parallel pair
. For
\{A,B\}\subset\mathbf{G}
, if
\{aA,bB\}\subset\mathbf{A}
is a balanced (resp. co-parallel) pair for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\{A,B\}
is also called a
balanced
(resp.
co-parallel
)
pair
. A balanced pair
\{A,B\}\subset\mathbf{G}
will also be denoted as an ordered pair
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
.

Remark
.

It is easy to see that our definition of co-parallel pairs coincides with that given at the beginning of
[
12
]
, or that in
[
16
, Definition 3.3]
. Taking the finiteness counter-examples given by
[
2
,
9
]
into consideration, we generalize this definition to balanced pairs, which is close to the concept “well oriented” in
[
21
, Definition 2.1]
.

To provide more details of the argument originated from
[
7
]
and to introduce our main result, let us recall the concept of Sturmian measure first. For each
\alpha\in[0,1]
, let
s_{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be defined by

s_{\alpha}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\cdots,\qquad i_{n}=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor-\lfloor(n-1)\alpha\rfloor,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4)

Here for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the maximal integer not exceeding
x
. Let
S_{\alpha}
denote the
\omega
-limit set of
s_{\alpha}
. It is well-known that the sub-shift
S_{\alpha}
supports a unique measure in
\mathcal{M}
, called the
Sturmian measure of slope
\alpha
, and denoted by
\mu_{\alpha}
. Note that
\mu_{\alpha}
is supported on a periodic orbit iff its slope
\alpha
is rational. The statements above can be found in, for example,
[
18
, Theorem 2.1]
.
For certain (balanced) pair
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
and an associated interval
I\subset(0,+\infty)
, in
[
7
,
13
,
9
,
18
,
10
]
among others, the authors studied the one-parameter family
(A,tB),t\in I
and proved the following:

•

for every
t\in I
,
(A,tB)
admits a unique maximizing measure, which is a Sturmian measure of slope
\alpha(t)
;

•

the map
t\mapsto\alpha(t),t\in I
is continuous and non-constant (even of range
[0,1]
).

As a result, for every
t\in I
with
\alpha(t)\notin\mathbb{Q}
,
(A,tB)
serves as a finiteness counter-example.

A result of Jørgensen and Smith
[
12
, Theorem 5-4]
(see also Theorem
3.2
for a slightly generalized version) is closely related to the way of searching finiteness counter-examples in co-parallel pairs described above. It seems that this fact did not receive much attention until the recent works
[
21
,
16
]
. In particular, based on
[
12
, Theorem 5-4]
, recently Laskawiec
[
16
, Theorem 9.8]
proved that for any co-parallel pair
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
,
\mathbb{A}
admits exactly one Sturmian maximizing measure. Using a variation of Jørgensen and Smith’s theorem (see Proposition
3.1
) as a key ingredient, we can prove that the maximizing measure of such
\mathbb{A}
is always unique, confirming a suspicion of Laskawiec
[
16
, Note 9.9]
. Our main result in this note reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
be a balanced pair. Then
\mathbb{A}
admits a unique Lyapunov maximizing measure; moreover, this measure is Sturmian.

The rest of this note is organized as follows. In §
2.1
we recall the notion of balanced words, which is closely related to Sturmian measures. In §

we discuss basic properties of balanced pairs. In §

we prove Proposition
3.1
, following the original argument in
[

]
. In §

we deduce Theorem
1.1
from Proposition

. Some further discussions related to Theorem

will be given in §
5
. Finally, in §

.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Balanced words

Given an integer
n\geq 0
, an element in
\{0,1\}^{n}
is called a
finite word
of
length

n
. By definition,
\{0,1\}^{0}
consists of a unique element, called the
empty word
, denoted by
\epsilon
. A finite word of length
n\geq 1
is denoted by
i_{1}\cdots i_{n}
for
i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}\in\{0,1\}
. Let
\{0,1\}^{*}=\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}\{0,1\}^{n}
denote the collection of all finite words. Recall that
\{0,1\}^{*}
can be seen as a free monoid with generator
0,1
and identity
\epsilon
, by defining the product of
u=i_{1}\cdots i_{m}
and
v=j_{1}\cdots j_{n}
as
uv=i_{1}\cdots i_{m}j_{1}\cdots j_{n}
.
For each
n\geq 0
, let
u^{n}
denote the
n
-th power of a finite word
u
; in particular,
u^{0}=\epsilon
. An element in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called an
infinite word
, which is denoted by
i_{1}i_{2}\cdots
for
i_{1},i_{2},\cdots\in\{0,1\}
.
The product
uv
of a finite word
u
and an infinite word
v
can be defined in a similar way.

From now on, by saying a
word
, we mean either a finite or an infinite word. Given a word
x
and a finite word
v
,
v
is called a
factor
of
x
, if there exists a finite word
u
and a word
w
such that
x=uvw
. For a finite word
w
, let
|w|
denote its length. When
w=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}
for some
n\geq 1
, define
|w|_{1}=i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}
. Define
|\epsilon|_{1}=0
. A word
w
is called
balanced
, if for any two factors
u
and
v
of
w
,
|u|=|v|
implies that
||u|_{1}-|v|_{1}|\leq 1
.

Sturmian measures mentioned in the introduction are closely related to balanced words. In the three lemmas below we collect some well-known facts about balanced words that will be used later. For more information on balanced words and their relation to Sturmian measures, we refer to
[
7
,
17
,
18
]
and references therein. Recall that for each
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
S_{\alpha}
denotes the
\omega
-limit set of
s_{\alpha}
defined by (
4
). An infinite word
s
is called
recurrent
, if any factor of
s
occur infinitely often in
s
; or equivalently,
s
is contained in the
\omega
-limit set of itself.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
s\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
s
is recurrent and balanced iff there exists
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that
s\in S_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

See, for example,
[
18
, Theorem 2.1]
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Given
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the following hold.

(i)

The sub-shift
S_{\alpha}
is uniquely ergodic.

(ii)

|\sum_{m\leq k<m+n}i_{k}-n\alpha|<1
holds for any
i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in S_{\alpha}
and any
m,n\geq 1
.

Proof.

For (i), see, for example,
[
18
, Theorem 2.1]
. For (ii), see, for example,
[
7
, Corollary 1.4]
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
x
be a word which is not balanced. Then there exists a word
w
such that both
0w0
and
1w1
are factors of
x
.

Proof.

See, for example,
[
17
, Proposition 2.1.3]
.
∎

2.2.
Balanced pairs of matrices

In this subsection we summarize properties of balanced pairs of matrices that will be used later, listed in Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.5
below. Most of them can be founded in
[
12
,
21
,
16
]
.

Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf{G}
. If
a,b,c,d\geq 0
, then
A
is called
non-negative
; if
a,b,c,d>0
, then
A
is called
positive
. We shall identify the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, via the projective map
(x,y)^{t}\mapsto x/y,\,(x,y)^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)^{t}\}
. Then
A
naturally induces a real Möbius transformation

\underline{A}:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1},\quad x\mapsto\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}.

When
A\in\mathbf{A}
, the following facts are evident.

•

A\in\mathbf{H}
iff
\underline{A}:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
has exactly two (hyperbolic) fixed points, one attracting and the other repelling, denoted by
s_{A}
and
u_{A}
respectively. In this case, by Perron-Frobenius theorem, the following three conditions are equivalent:

–

A
is positive;

–

s_{A},u_{A}\in\mathbb{R}
and
u_{A}<0<s_{A}
;

–

A=H_{s,u}^{\lambda}
for some
\lambda>1
and
u<0<s
.

Moreover, if
A=H_{s,u}^{\lambda}
, then
s=s_{A}
and
u=u_{A}
.

•

A\in\mathbf{P}
iff
\underline{A}:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
has a unique (parabolic) fixed point, denoted by
p_{A}
. In this case,
A
is non-negative iff one of the following holds:

–

either
p_{A}=0
and
A=P_{x}
for some
x>0
;

–

or
p_{A}=\infty
and
A=P_{x}^{t}
for some
x>0
.

Given
\mathbb{A}=(A_{0},A_{1})\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
, define
[\epsilon]_{\mathbb{A}}=I
, and define
[w]_{\mathbb{A}}=A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{n}}
for each
w=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{*}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
. Then
[\cdot]_{\mathbb{A}}
defines a homomorphism from
\{0,1\}^{*}
to
\mathbf{G}
. For an open subset
U
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, let
\overline{U}
denote its closure in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The following facts are evident.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{A}=(A_{0},A_{1})\in\mathbf{A}^{2}
be one of the norm forms given in Definition
1.1
. Define

I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}(s_{1},s_{2})&,&\text{case (h)}\\
(0,s)&,&\text{case (m)}\\
(0,+\infty)&,&\text{case (p)}\end{array}\right.,\quad I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}(u_{2},u_{1})&,&\text{case (h)}\\
(u,0)&,&\text{case (m)}\\
(-\infty,0)&,&\text{case (p)}\end{array}\right..

(5)

Given a non-empty finite word
x
, denote
X=[x]_{\mathbb{A}}
. Then the following hold.

(i)

X\in\mathbf{A}
,
\underline{X}(I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+})\subset I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}
and
\underline{X}^{-1}(I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-})\subset I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-}
.

(ii)

X\in\mathbf{P}
iff there exists
i\in\{0,1\}
such that
x=i^{|x|}
and
A_{i}\in\mathbf{P}
.

(iii)

If
X\in\mathbf{H}
, then
s_{X}\in\overline{I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}}
and
u_{X}\in\overline{I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-}}
.

(iv)

If
x\neq 0^{|x|},1^{|x|}
, then
\underline{X}(\overline{I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}})\subset I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}
and
\underline{X}^{-1}(\overline{I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-}})\subset I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-}
.

Lemma
2.5
(iv) below is a special case of
[
12
, Lemma 5-3]
when
\mathbb{A}
is a co-parallel pair. To prove that this statement holds for all balanced pairs, let us introduce the notion of crossing pairs for preparation. For
\{A,B\}\subset\mathbf{H}
, if there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
such that
\{T^{-1}AT,T^{-1}BT\}=\{H_{s_{1},u_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}},H_{s_{2},u_{2}}^{\lambda_{2}}\}
with
u_{1}<u_{2}<0<s_{1}<s_{2}
, then
\{A,B\}
is called a
crossing pair
. It is easy to see that this definition coincides with
[
16
, Definition 3.1]
for pairs of matrices in
\mathbf{H}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathbb{A}=(A,B)\in\mathbf{A}^{2}
be a balanced pair.

(i)

[w]_{\mathbb{A}}\in\mathbf{A}
holds for any non-empty finite word
w
; if
w\neq 0^{|w|},1^{|w|}
additionally, then
[w]_{\mathbb{A}}\in\mathbf{H}
.

(ii)

For each
X\in\{A,B\}
and each
Y\in\{AB,BA\}
,
\{X,Y\}
is a balanced pair.

(iii)

\{AB,BA\}
is a crossing pair.

(iv)

\tr((AB)^{2})>\tr(A^{2}B^{2})
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathbb{A}
is in a normal form. Then assertions (i)(ii) follows from Lemma
2.4
. To prove (iii)(iv), denote
C=AB
and
D=BA
below. We may further assume that the right end point of
I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}
is a fixed point of
\underline{B}
, so
\underline{B}(x)>x
holds for any
x\in I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}\cup I_{\mathbb{A}}^{-}
.

(iii). By Lemma
2.4
,
C,D\in\mathbf{H}
,
s_{C},s_{D}\in I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}
and

\underline{D}(\underline{B}(s_{C}))=\underline{B}(\underline{C}(s_{C}))=\underline{B}(s_{C})\in I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+},

so
s_{D}=\underline{B}(s_{C})>s_{C}
. A similar argument shows that
u_{D}=\underline{B}(u_{C})>u_{C}
. Therefore
\{C,D\}
is a crossing pair.

(iv). Since
\{C,D\}
is a crossing pair, it is known that
\rho(C)\rho(D)>\rho(CD)
. See, for example,
[
21
, Theorem 2.5]
, or the displayed line in the last paragraph of
[
16
, Proof of Corollary 5.4]
. Then it follows that

\rho((AB)^{2})=\rho(C)\rho(D)>\rho(CD)=\rho(A^{2}B^{2}),

which is equivalent to
\tr((AB)^{2})>\tr(A^{2}B^{2})
.
∎

(6)

For

Let

4.
Proof of the main theorem

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We shall fix a (sub-multiplicative) matrix norm
\|\cdot\|
in this section. In view of Lemma
4.2
, we choose to use the

; namely, for a

real matrix

c&d\end{pmatrix}
,

is defined by

\|X\|^{2}=\tr(X^{t}X)=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}.

Note that

holds for any

. We shall use this fact occasionally without further explanation.

To deduce Theorem
1.1
from Proposition
3.1
, the starting point of our argument consists of some well-known facts about joint spectral radius summarized in Lemma
4.1
. The proofs of these facts are usually based on the existence of an

, which is a fundamental tool for the study of joint spectral radius. We refer to
[
11
,
3
]
and references therein for more information on extremal norms. In the following let
\supp\mu
denote the support of a measure
\mu
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
be a balanced pair. Then there exists

such that the following hold.

(i)

\|[w]_{\mathbb{A}}\|\leq C_{\mathbb{A}}\cdot\hat{\rho}(\mathbb{A})^{|w|}

.

(ii)

Let
\mu
be a maximizing measure of
\mathbb{A}
. Then for any

and any factor

of

,

. As a result, any invariant measure supported in
\supp\mu
is also a maximizing measure of
\mathbb{A}
.

Proof.

A balanced pair is
irreducible
in the sense of
[
11
, Page 16]
, and hence (i) follows from
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
. (ii) follows from (i) and
[
19
, Theorem 2.3 (iv)]
; see also
[
3
, Proposition 6.2]
.
∎

\Sigma_{N}

\mathcal{M}_{N}^{\prime}
be the collection shift-invariant Borel probability measures for the sub-shift
\Sigma_{N}^{\prime}
. The Lyapunov exponent
\chi(\mathbb{A},\nu)
for
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{N}^{\prime}
can be similarly defined, and it suffices to prove that
\nu\mapsto\chi(\mathbb{A},\nu)
is continuous on
\mathcal{M}_{N}^{\prime}
.

Note that
\Sigma_{N}^{\prime}
is a sub-shift of finite type. Also note that by Lemma
2.4
(iv), if
w
is a word of length
N

w\neq 0^{N},1^{N}
, then for
W=[w]_{\mathbb{A}}
,
\underline{W}(\overline{I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}})\subset I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}
. Then by
[
1
, Theorem 2.3]
, it can be seen that
\mathbb{A}
is uniformly hyperbolic with respect to
\Sigma_{N}^{\prime}
. To proceed, we shall follow the proof of Equation (1.7) in
[
5
, § 2.1]
. Fixing a norm
|\cdot|
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, thanks to the uniform hyperbolicity, it is not difficult to show that there exists a continuous map

e^{+}:\Sigma_{N}^{\prime}\to\{(x,y)^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y>0,x/y\in I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+}\}

such that for every
\underline{i}=(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\Sigma_{N}^{\prime}
, we have (denote
\mathbb{A}=(A_{0},A_{1})
below):

•

\frac{A_{i_{0}}e^{+}(\underline{i})}{|A_{i_{0}}e^{+}(\underline{i})|}=\frac{e^{+}((i_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}})}{|e^{+}((i_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}})|}
;

•

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log|A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}A_{i_{0}}e^{+}(\underline{i})|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}A_{i_{0}}\|
, provided that the limit on the right hand side exists.

Indeed,
e^{+}(\underline{i})
can be taken as
(x(\underline{i}),1)^{t}
, where
x(\underline{i})
denotes the unique point in
\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\underline{A_{-i_{1}}\cdots A_{-i_{n}}}(I_{\mathbb{A}}^{+})
. Then it follows that
\chi(\mathbb{A},\nu)=\int_{\Sigma_{N}^{\prime}}\log|A_{i_{0}}e^{+}(\underline{i})|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(\underline{i})
, which implies that
\chi(\mathbb{A},\nu)
is continuous in
\nu
.
∎

Since
N
can be arbitrarily large, we have proved that
f
is strictly concave on
(0,1)
. On the other hand, it is easy to see that as

,
f(\frac{1}{n+1})=\frac{1}{n+1}\log\rho(A^{n}B)\to\log\rho(A)=f(0)
and
f(\frac{n}{n+1})=\frac{1}{n+1}\log\rho(AB^{n})\to\log\rho(B)=f(1)
. Combining this with
f
being concave on
(0,1)
, we conclude that
f
is continuous at
0,1
, which completes the proof.
∎

5.
Further discussions

In this section we shall discuss some relation between our result and previous results in the literature, and also give some applications of Theorem
1.1
. The main results in this section are Proposition
5.3
and Proposition
5.5
. For preparation let us begin with Proposition
5.1
.

From now on denote

\mathbf{B}:=\{\mathbb{A}\in\mathbf{G}^{2}:\mathbb{A}\text{ is a balanced pair}\}.

For every
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbf{B}
, by Theorem
1.1
, there exists a unique
\tau(\mathbb{A})\in[0,1]
such that
\mu_{\tau(\mathbb{A})}
is the unique maximizing measure of
\mathbb{A}
. This defines a function
\tau:\mathbf{B}\to[0,1]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Identifying
\mathbf{B}
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{8}
, then the function
\tau:\mathbf{B}\to[0,1]
is continuous.

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
4.6
, we can apply Lemma
5.2
below to the function
f(\mathbb{A},\alpha)=\chi(\mathbb{A},\mu_{\alpha}),(\mathbb{A},\alpha)\in\mathbf{B}\times[0,1]
by taking
D=[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
. Then the conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
be a topological space and suppose that
f:X\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following properties.

•

For every
x\in X
,
\alpha\mapsto f(x,\alpha)
is continuous and strictly concave on
[0,1]
, so this function attains its maximum at a unique point
\tau(x)\in[0,1]
.

•

There exists a dense subset
D
of
[0,1]
such that for every
\alpha\in D
,
x\mapsto f(x,\alpha)
is continuous on
X
.

Then the function
\tau:X\to[0,1]
is continuous.

Proof.

Given
x_{0}\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, it suffices to show that there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
such that
|\tau(x)-\tau(x_{0})|<\varepsilon
for any
x\in U
. Let us only prove the statement under the additional assumption
\tau(x_{0})\in(0,1)
; the special cases
\tau(x_{0})=0,1
are similar and omitted.

Since
\tau(x_{0})\in(0,1)
and
D
is dense in
[0,1]
, there exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in D
with
\tau(x_{0})-\varepsilon<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\tau(x_{0})<\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\tau(x_{0})+\varepsilon
. Since the function
\alpha\mapsto f(x_{0},\alpha)
is strictly concave and since it attains its maximum at
\tau(x_{0})
, we have
f(x_{0},\alpha_{1})<f(x_{0},\alpha_{2})
and
f(x_{0},\beta_{1})>f(x_{0},\beta_{2})
. Then by continuity, there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
such that both
f(x,\alpha_{1})<f(x,\alpha_{2})
and
f(x,\beta_{1})>f(x,\beta_{2})
hold for any
x\in U
. For such
x
, by the concavity of
\alpha\mapsto f(x,\alpha)
, we have
\alpha_{1}<\tau(x)<\beta_{2}
.
∎

All the finiteness counter-examples in
[
7
,
2
,
13
,
9
,
18
,
10
,
20
]
are constructed in the following way: for certain pair of non-negative matrices
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
, prove that there exist finiteness counter-examples in the one-parameter family
(A,tB),t>0
. The proposition below shows that such
(A,B)
must be a balanced pair.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(A,B)\in\mathbf{G}^{2}
and suppose that there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
such that both
T^{-1}AT
and
T^{-1}BT
are non-negative. Then
(A,B)
is a balanced pair iff there exists
t>0
such that
(A,tB)
is a finiteness counter-example.

Proof.

Let us prove the “only if” part first. For every
t>0
, since
(A,tB)
is a balanced pair, by Theorem
1.1
, there exists a unique
\alpha(t)\in[0,1]
such that
\mu_{\alpha(t)}
is the unique maximizing measure of
(A,tB)
. By Proposition
5.1
, the function
t\mapsto\alpha(t)
is continuous on
(0,+\infty)
. By the definition of
\alpha(t)
, it is easy to see that
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\alpha(t)=0
and
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\alpha(t)=1
. Then there exists (uncountably many)
t>0
with
\alpha(t)\notin\mathbb{Q}
, and
(A,tB)
is a finiteness counter-example for such
t
.

For the “if” part, we may assume that
A,B\in\mathbf{G}
are both non-negative. Since for
r,s,t>0
,
(A,tB)
is a balanced pair iff
(rA,sB)
is a balanced pair, it suffices to prove the following statement.

Claim
.

Let
A,B\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\setminus\{I\}
be non-negative matrices. If
\mathbb{A}=(A,B)
does not admit a spectrum maximizing product, then
\mathbb{A}
is a balanced pair.

It remains to prove the claim. To begin with, note that if
C\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\setminus\{I\}
is non-negative, then

C\in\mathbf{A}
, and hence either
C
is in the form of (
2
), or
C\in\{P_{x},P_{x}^{t}\}
for some
x>0
. If
A
and
B
share a common eigenvector, it is well-known (and easy to see) that either
A
or
B
is a spectrum maximizing product of
\mathbb{A}
. Then we may assume that
A
and
B
share no common eigenvector, so there are three cases as follows.

•

\{A,B\}=\{H_{s_{1},u_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}},H_{s_{2},u_{2}}^{\lambda_{2}}\}
with
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
and
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
.

•

\{A,B\}=\{H_{s,u}^{\lambda},P_{x}\}
or
\{H_{s,u}^{\lambda},P_{x}^{t}\}
.

•

\{A,B\}=\{P_{x},P_{y}^{t}\}
.

Therefore,
\mathbb{A}
is either a balanced pair or a crossing pair (recall its definition ahead of Lemma
2.5
). According to
[
21
, Theorem 3.4]
or
[
16
, Corollary 5.4]
, for a crossing pair
(A,B)
, either
A
or
B
is a spectrum maximizing product. The proof is done.
∎

Remark
.

From Proposition
5.3
we know that a result of Morris and Sidorov
[
18
, Theorem 2.3]
can be applied to any balanced pair. In particular, given
(A,B)\in\mathbf{B}
, for every
t>0
, letting
\mu_{\alpha(t)}
be the unique maximizing measure of
(A,tB)
, then we have: the set
\{t>0:\alpha(t)\notin\mathbb{Q}\}
is of Hausdorff dimension zero. As an immediate corollary of this fact and Fubini’s theorem, identifying
\mathbf{B}
as a Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}^{8}
(the interior of
\mathbf{B}
consists of co-parallel pairs), we have: for Lebesgue almost every
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbb{A}
admits a unique maximizing measure and this measure is supported on a periodic orbit.

To prove Proposition
5.5
, we need a result of Panti and Sclosa
[
21
, Theorem 3.6]
as follows. Let us give an alternative proof of it as an application of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(A,B)\in\mathbf{A}^{2}
be a balanced pair with
\tr A=\tr B
. Then its unique maximizing measure is
\mu_{1/2}
.

Proof.

For
A,B
given in the lemma, it is not difficult to prove the following statement directly: there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
such that
T^{-1}AT=B
and
T^{-1}BT=A
. Alternatively, this statement follows easily from a more general result of Friedland
[
8
, Theorem 2.1, Theorem 2.9]
; see also
[
14
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Now suppose that
\mu_{\alpha}
is the unique maximizing measure of
(A,B)
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then
\mu_{1-\alpha}
is the unique maximizing measure of
(B,A)
. However, from
(T^{-1}AT,T^{-1}BT)=(B,A)
we know that
\mu_{\alpha}
is also a maximizing measure of
(B,A)
, so
\alpha=1/2
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Fix
u^{\prime}<u<0<s<s^{\prime}
and
\lambda^{\prime}>1
. Then there exist uncountably many
\lambda\in[\lambda^{\prime},+\infty)
such that the co-parallel pair
\mathbb{A}_{\lambda}=(H_{s,u}^{\lambda},H_{s^{\prime},u^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}})
is a finiteness counter-example.

Proof.

For each
\lambda\in[\lambda^{\prime},+\infty)
, let
\mu_{f(\lambda)}
be the unique maximizing measure of
\mathbb{A}_{\lambda}
. By Proposition
5.1
, this defines a continuous function
f:[\lambda^{\prime},+\infty)\to[0,1]
. By Lemma
5.4
,
f(\lambda^{\prime})=1/2
. On the other hand,
\chi(\mathbb{A}_{\lambda},\mu_{0})=\log\lambda
, and it is easy to see that there exists
C>0
such that

\chi(\mathbb{A}_{\lambda},\mu_{\alpha})\leq(1-\alpha)\log\lambda+C,\quad\forall\alpha\in(0,1],\forall\lambda\in[\lambda^{\prime},+\infty).

Then
\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}f(\lambda)=0
, and the conclusion follows.
∎
