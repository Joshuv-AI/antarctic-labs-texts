Microbiome of the Eustachian tube in patients with chronic obstructive Eustachian tube dysfunction and healthy individuals
Authors: S Rettschlag, I Barrantes, A Hahn, J Bull, U Raschke, R Mlynski, B Kreikemeyer, P Warnke, R Skusa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Eustachian tube (ET) is essential for middle ear ventilation. Chronic obstructive ET dysfunction may lead to chronic otitis media and cholesteatoma, but its pathophysiology remains unclear. Commensal bacterial colonization of the ET has not previously been studied in healthy individuals or affected patients.Study Design and PatientsProspective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary academic referral center including 13 patients with chronic obstructive ET dysfunction and 37 controls. Between 2017 and 2020, intraoperative tissue samples and swabs were collected using a contamination-minimized Yankauer suction technique. Samples underwent conventional culture diagnostics, microbiome analysis via 16S rDNA sequencing, and histopathological examination.ResultsCulture identified 552 bacterial strains, with greater diversity in controls (34 genera) than in patients (19 genera). Microbiome analysis confirmed a larger core microbiome in controls. Rothia was more abundant in patients, whereas Veillonella predominated in controls. Genera such as Rothia and Gemella may be linked to microbial alterations in chronic ET dysfunction.ConclusionThis first combined culture-based and molecular analysis using a novel sampling-technique demonstrated reduced bacterial diversity in chronic ET dysfunction patients, resembling patterns seen in other chronic respiratory morbidities. Future studies should incorporate complementary sampling approaches and cutting-edge sequencing technologies, additionally assessing viral etiologies.Keywords: Eustachian tube, microbiome, chronic obstructive ET dysfunction

Introduction

The Eustachian tube (ET) is essentially a connection between the nasopharynx and the tympanic cavity, playing a crucial role in the ventilation and function of the middle ear. The ET, measuring approximately 31 to 44 mm in length, is lined with ciliated epithelial cells [1]. It comprises a bony, always-open part that forms the lateral one-third of the canal, starting in the middle ear, and a cartilaginous, normally closed part making up the medial two-thirds, ending in the epipharynx [2]. Starting at the pharyngeal orifice, the ET ascends at a 45-degree angle to the tympanic cavity [3], with this angle being lower in children [4]. The incidence of ET dysfunction in adults is about 1% [5], and it can lead to chronic otitis media with effusion, adhesive processes, or cholesteatoma. The introduction of balloon Eustachian tuboplasty (BET) has increased scientific interest in pathophysiology and causes of ET dysfunction [6]. However, the exact mechanism of BET and the underlying pathophysiology of ET dysfunction are still not well understood. Some patients experience ET dysfunction since childhood, while others develop symptoms later in life, often following upper respiratory tract infections. Anatomically identifiable causes of obstructive ET dysfunction are rare but may include space-occupying lesions, scarring at the torus tubarius, conditions following radiation therapy, temporal bone fractures, craniofacial dysmorphisms, or cleft palate. One theory for post-infectious obstructive ET dysfunction involves the formation of bacterial biofilms with increased surface tension and mucosal adhesion within the cartilaginous tube. This study aims to gather information on the bacterial colonization of the ET mucosa, addressing a gap in the current research [7].

Pathogenic colonization to epithelial cells of the upper respiratory tract is typically prevented by the host's microbiota. However, a disrupted microbiota can lead to an unstable community that predisposes the host to infection or inflammation. Microbiome studies are crucial for investigating the etiology of acute and chronic upper respiratory tract infections, revealing differences in host microbiota, including species prevalent in common ear, nose, and throat disorders. For instance, dominant otopathogens in acute otitis media in children include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis [8]. In contrast, children with chronic otitis media exhibit higher relative abundances of Fusobacterium, Haemophilus, Neisseria and Porphyromonas [9]. In paranasal disorders, studies have shown that Staphylococcus aureus is commonly detected in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis compared to those without [10, 11]. Interestingly, research by Ramakrishnan et al. [12] suggests that the relative abundance of microbiota may have a greater impact on the pathogenesis of sinus disorders than the mere presence or absence of particular species. However, to the best of our knowledge, no microbiome data are yet available for ET specimens nor of patients with ET dysfunction.

Material and methods

Study design

From 2017 to 2020, we conducted a study to characterize the microbiome of the ET in patients with chronic obstructive ET dysfunction and in clinically ET healthy individuals. For this purpose, two tissue samples measuring 1–2 mm in size and a swab from the ET were collected during surgery under general anesthesia, and consecutively processed microbiologically. The study group consisted of patients with confirmed chronic obstructive ET dysfunction who were already scheduled for BET, tympanoplasty (TPL), or myringotomy with tube insertion (PC/PD).

The control group consisted of patients without evidence of chronic obstructive ET dysfunction who were undergoing indicated panendoscopy, tonsillectomy, or septoplasty. The presence or absence of chronic obstructive ET dysfunction was determined using the ET Dysfunction Questionnaire (EDTQ-7) [13, 14] and the ET Score (ETS-5/7) [15]. Exclusion criteria included oncological patients, autoimmune diseases, history of head or neck radiation therapy, age under 18 years, alcohol, drug, and nicotine abuse, as well as antibiotic use within the last two months.

Patient sample acquisition

Sample collection was performed as part of a planned surgery under general anesthesia. The primary challenge was to develop a contamination-free technique for sample extraction. Various techniques, including the use of brushes and introducers, were tested on cadavers. The most effective technique was then utilized in our study. A McIvor mouth gag (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) was used with additional velotraction by a medical suction hose (Primed, Halberstadt, Germany), adjusting the epipharynx via a 70° Hopkins optic (4 mm, 18 cm, Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) to focus on the torus tubarius. A slightly curved Yankauer suction tip (Primed, Halberstadt, Germany) was inserted orally into the torus tubarius, avoiding contact with the pharynx, and used as a guide and protection for the swab. An eSwab orange (Copan Diagnostic, Murrieta, CA) was inserted into the ET without resistance and rotated six times. The swab was then completely withdrawn into the Yankauer suction tip and removed from the patient within the device. It was subsequently pulled backward out of the Yankauer suction tip and placed into a transport medium. Figure 1 illustrates the swabbing procedure. Following the swabbing, two small biopsies measuring 1–2 mm in size were taken from the same tubal entrance using a Weil nasal forceps (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany). One biopsy was transferred to sterile falcon tube, 15 mL (Sarstedt, Numbrecht, Germany), filled with 4% formalin solution (Grimm MED logistic, Torgelow, Germany) and sent to histopathological, while the other was placed in a sterile container, covered with sodium chloride, and sent for microbiological diagnostics.

Sample processing

The patient samples were transported to the laboratory within a maximum of 2 h after collection and processed immediately upon arrival. Biopsies were homogenized using a Precellys 24 high-throughput homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Rockville, Washington D.C., USA). The biopsies were transferred into 2 mL Precellys homogenization tubes (CKMix 1.4 mm & 2.8 mm ceramic beads; Bertin Technologies), pre-filled with ultrapure, sterile filtered deionized water (PCR grade water; biotech rabbit, Berlin, Germany). Homogenization was performed at 5,500 rpm for 20 s, following the protocol of Redanz et al. [16].

Conventional microbiological diagnostic

For conventional culture, 100 µL of each sample was inoculated onto both aerobic and anaerobic solid media (Columbia agar with 5% sheep blood, Chocolate agar [GC II Agar with BD IsoVitaleX], Schaedler agar with Vitamin K1 and 5% sheep blood) and into liquid media (brain-heart infusion broth, thioglycollate broth, Becton Dickinson GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). The cultures were incubated at 37 °C under CO2-enriched aerobic conditions (5% CO2/20% O2) or anaerobic conditions (0% O2/10% CO2/10% H2/80% N2). For CO2-enriched aerobic conditions, the media were incubated in a KB 115 incubator (Binder GmbH, Tuttlingen, Germany). Anaerobic conditions were achieved using the Anoxomat III (Advanced Instruments, Norwood, MA, USA) with appropriate jars.

Culture assessment and bacterial species identification

Primary assessment of the agar plates was conducted after 18–24 h in a semiquantitative manner with subsequent daily inspections for a total incubation period of 5 days. Pathogen identification was carried out using MALDI-TOF-MS (Vitek MS) (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In cases were results were negative, 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene PCR was performed using primers 27F (AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG) and 519R (GWATTACCGCGGCKGCTG) [17, 18].

16S rDNA amplicon based microbiome sequencing

For microbiome analysis, standard extraction was performed with 200 µL aliquots of patient samples (swab and homogenized tissue specimens) using the NucliSens easyMAG instrument with the Generic protocol as stated by the manufacturer (bioMérieux, Nürtingen, Germany). The elution volume of extracted sample was 50 µL, from which subsequent analysis was carried out. To control for potential contamination, blank negative controls were processed alongside patient samples through the entire workflow, including DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing. Later, these negative controls yielded zero amplicons for all taxa upon 16S amplicon sequencing and bioinformatics analysis, confirming the absence of contamination artifacts. For microbiome analysis/16S rDNA amplicon sequencing of bacterial communities, sequencing libraries were prepared from the isolated chromosomal DNA of the collected biopsies and brush swabs. Total DNA was isolated from the materials using the QIAamp Kit. Subsequently, PCR was performed on these DNA samples to amplify the bacterial 16S rDNA. Oligonucleotides serving as primers were chosen to allow amplification of the variable V3 and V4 regions of the 16S rDNA. Bioanalyzer chips (DNA 1000 Chips) and Qbit kits were used for qualitative and quantitative assessment of the amplicon libraries. It was planned to incorporate a maximum of 50 individual libraries from patient materials into an individual sequencing run.

Culture data analysis and statistic evaluation

Calculations for culture results were performed with Stata BE 18. The interrater agreement was calculated using Fleiss' Kappa. The following classification was used for interpreting interrater agreement: <0 Poor agreement, 0.01–0.20 Slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 Fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 Moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 Substantial agreement and 0.81–1.00 Almost perfect agreement [19]. The average number of detected bacteria strains was analyzed using a paired T-test, preceded by a test for equality of variance, which in no case led to the rejection of the tested hypothesis of equality of variance. The test for proportion was used to compare the germination groups. A nominal significance level of 0.05 was defined for all statistical tests performed. Adjustment for multiple testing was not performed due to the exploratory nature of the study.

Microbiome data analysis and statistic evaluation

The microbiome data analysis was carried out as following: 16S Illumina sequencing amplicons were denoised using DADA (version 1.18.0; [20]), and operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were assigned to the denoised fragments using the sklearn classifier (version 0.23.1; [21]) against rRNAs clustered at 99% identity from the SILVA database version 138 [22]. Both denoising and OTU assignment were performed using the QIIME2 pipeline version 2020.11 [23]. The OTU table was then processed using the bioconductor package phyloseq (version 1.44.0; [24]) within R version 4.3.1 [25]. First, contaminant OTUs, such as those classified as chloroplast or mitochondrial, were removed from the data. Then, statistical significance of microbial communities at the phylum and genus level was assessed using the Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis tests when comparing pairs or multiple groups, respectively, as implemented natively in R. Afterwards, differentially abundant genera were obtained using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.40.2; [26]), via a Wald test and Benjamin-Hochberg correction for P-values (false discovery rate <0.01). Core microbiomes were calculated and plotted following the protocol developed in the microbiome R package (version 1.22.0; [27]). To assess the statistical power of our study design, we conducted a power analysis using the vegan [28] and pwr R packages [29]. For this, we performed a PERMANOVA on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix to obtain the observed effect size (R-squared) and calculated power across a range of effect sizes, which allowed us to determine the minimum effect size detectable with 80% power [30]. Associations between the control and study conditions and relevant taxa (obtained from the core microbiome and differential abundance analyses), were tested using generalized linear models, following a protocol described in the documentation of the microbiome R package. Finally, the Illumina sequencing data was deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) database under the study accession number PRJEB83810.

Ethical approval

Informed consent forms were obtained from all participants, and the study received positive ethical approval (Registration number A2016-0151).

Results

Patients characteristics

The average age in the study group was 37.5 years, with a nearly equal gender distribution: 6 males and 7 females. In the control group, the average patient age was 36.6 years, with 25 males and 13 females. In the study group, patients with chronic obstructive ET dysfunction, had a mean ETS-5 score of 2.85, a mean ETS-7 of 3.75, and a mean ETDQ-7 of 27.83, indicating significantly pathological tube scores. In contrast, all patients in the control group had normal tubal scores.

Pathological findings in the study group revealed chronic inflammation in 38% of cases, hyperplastic lymphoid tissue in 16%, and normal tissue in 48%. In the control group, chronic inflammation was found in 52% of cases, hyperplastic lymphoid tissue in 11%, and normal tissue in 32%. Histologically, there were no significant differences between the study and control group.

Culture based bacterial composition of patient samples

Microbiological analysis was conducted on biopsies and swabs from the ET of 13 patients with confirmed chronic obstructive ET dysfunction and a control group of 37 individuals with healthy ET.

A total of 552 bacteria strains were identified. In the control group, the mean number of bacteria isolates was 6.8 (± SD 3.0) detected in swab specimens and 4.6 (± SD 2.8) detected in biopsies (P < 0.001). In the study group, a mean number of 4.9 (± SD 2.8) bacterial strains was detected in swabs and 5.3 (± SD 2.4) in biopsies (P = 0.708).

In the study group, 19 different bacterial genera were detected. The most commonly detected genus was Staphylococcus in all patients, followed by Streptococcus in 12 patients (92.3%) and Neisseria in 7 patients (53.8%). In the control group, 34 different bacterial genera were detected. The dominant genus was Streptococcus in 34 patients (91.9%), followed by Staphylococcus in 30 patients (81.1%), Veillonella and Actinomyces in 23 patients each (62.2%). Bacterial genera detected in more than one patient were further analyzed statistically for differences between the study and control group. No significant differences were found in the detection rates of Capnocytophaga (P = 0.962), Rothia (P = 0.153), Moraxella (P = 0.747), Prevotella (P = 0.780), Corynebacterium (P = 0.646), Haemophilus (P = 0.856), Cutibacterium (P = 0.526), Neisseria (P = 0.747), Schaalia (P = 0.256), or Streptococcus (P = 0.962) between the study and control group. However, a higher detection rate in the control group was observed for Actinomyces (P = 0.051) and Veillonella (P = 0.004), whereas Staphylococcus was detected more frequently in the study group (P = 0.091). Sequencing based microbiome analysis

From the 16S amplicon sequencing analysis we observed that the sequencing depth varied across samples, with a minimum of 284 reads and a maximum of 24,989 reads, averaging 7,434 reads per sample. Regarding the microbiome of the ET (control samples from this cohort), it is characterized by a diverse community, from which we identified a total of 312 unique genera and 140 unique bacterial species. The relative abundance of the most prevalent taxon was 0.0361, suggesting a balanced community structure. Proteobacteria was the most abundant phylum (50.23%), followed by Firmicutes (26.7%) and Bacteroidota (13.8%; Table 2). At the genus level, Sphingomonas was the most prevalent (18.64%), followed by Streptococcus (8.89%) and Ralstonia (8.52%; Table 3).

In addition, the microbial composition at the phylum level showed distinct profiles when comparing the control and study groups. The five most abundant phyla were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Fusobacteriota. Proteobacteria remained the most dominant phylum in both groups (50.23% in control vs. 48.44% in study; Table 2), while Firmicutes increased in the study group (32.2% vs. 26.7% in control). Bacteroidota showed a decrease in the study group (9.2%) compared to control (13.8%). At the genus level, notable shifts were observed. Sphingomonas was the most abundant genus in both groups, representing 18.64% and 16.17% in the control and study groups respectively (Table 3). Streptococcus increased in the study group (12.3%) compared to the control (8.9%), while Prevotella declined (7.3% in control vs. 4.8% in study). Kruskal-Wallis tests revealed that no differences were encountered between the two groups, neither at the phylum, nor at the sub-phylum level. Core microbiome composition

For the core microbiome analysis, we identified genera present in at least 25% of individuals per group, at a minimum detection threshold of 0.1% relative abundance. According to this, we found that 20 genera were part of the core microbiome in the control group (out of a total of 399 genera), while the study group exhibited a slightly reduced core of 18 genera. Seventeen genera were found in common across both groups: Rothia, Porphyromonas, Alloprevotella, Prevotella, Granulicatella, Streptococcus, Gemella, Staphylococcus, Veillonella, Fusobacterium, Caulobacteraceae, Sphingomonas, Ralstonia, Neisseria and two unidentified genera pertaining to the families Comamonadaceae and Pasteurellaceae. In addition, the control group entailed three unique core genera, i.e. Corynebacterium, Leptotrichia, and a genus pertaining to the Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum group; while the study group uniquely possessed Campylobacter. Despite these differences, a large portion of the core microbiome remained consistent between the two groups, suggesting a degree of microbial stability. Still, the unique taxa in each group may provide insights into potential biomarkers or microbial interactions relevant to the observed condition.

Statistical significance of microbiome differences

Later, a power analysis was conducted to assess the statistical robustness of the study design of the microbiome analysis. Our approach involved calculating the observed effect size from our current data, determining the effect size at which we achieve 80% power (a conventional threshold for adequate power), and examining how power changes across a range of effect sizes. Our power calculations indicated that the current design achieved 80% power for detecting effect sizes of 0.10 or greater. For the 90 samples employed in this investigation (66 control, 24 study), we found that the observed effect size (R-squared) from PERMANOVA was 0.01, which falls below the effect size 0.1 threshold (80% power). This suggests that the study design may be underpowered to detect small effect sizes reliably. However, the observed effect size (R2 = 0.010) falls within the range typically reported for clinical microbiome studies, where small but biologically meaningful effects are common due to high inter-individual variability [31]. Therefore while the current study provides valuable insights, the power limitations should be considered when interpreting these results.

Finally, a differential abundance analysis was carried out, to identify those genera that were significantly more abundant on either group. In this case, we identified seven genera with statistically significant differences between the control and study groups (FDR <0.01). One genus, Gemella, showed increased abundance in the study group, while six taxa were significantly reduced, including two OTUs pertaining to the Porphyromonas and Prevotella genera, as well as Alloprevotella, and Veillonella (Table 4). At the phylum level, differentially abundant genera were primarily from Bacteroidota and Firmicutes. The largest decrease was observed in Porphyromonas (−24.10 log2 fold change), whereas Gemella exhibited the highest increase (+24.53 log2 fold change). These findings suggest specific microbial taxa as potential biomarkers of the studied condition. Further functional analysis may reveal the biological implications of these shifts. The differential abundance patterns indicate potential disruptions in microbial interactions, which secures further investigation into their roles in this context.

Discussion

The causes of chronic obstructive ET dysfunction are multifactorial. Anatomical and physiological factors, such as altered force vectors of the tubal musculature, particularly the tensor veli palatini muscle, can underlie dysfunction, especially if it has persisted since childhood [1]. However, many patients develop symptoms in adulthood, with some reporting exacerbation after air travel, diving, or following viral or bacterial upper respiratory tract infections [1].

Histological findings in ET dysfunction

Studies on the etiology of ET dysfunction are limited. Poe et al. published a study in 2015 on biopsies from the tubal mucosa before and after BET. Preoperative samples showed inflammatory changes in the epithelium and subepithelial tissue, which could indicate a chronic inflammatory reaction or, in the absence of a healthy control group, represent the normal condition of the ET mucosa. Immediately after tubal dilatation, there was a shearing of the epithelium and compression of the submucosa and lymphatic tissue. Repeated biopsies at least five weeks after BET in three patients showed a healthy epithelial layer, with the lymphatic infiltrate no longer detectable and only a thin layer of connective tissue remaining [32]. In our histological examinations, we did not observe any significant differences between patients with chronic obstructive ET dysfunction and healthy individuals before intervention. However, a follow-up biopsy after a certain period was not performed in our study setting.

Novel insights into the ET microbiome

In the literature, there is only one prior study on the bacterial colonization of the ET [7]. This study examined the tips of balloon catheters removed postoperatively from 35 patients undergoing BET. Additionally, comparative swabs were obtained from nasal and throat mucosa. In 46% of cases, the balloon catheter tip were sterile. In 23% of cases, normal commensal microorganisms of the nasopharynx were detected, while the remaining 31% showed specific bacterial presence. These included Corynebacterium, Staphylococcus hominis, Proteus mirabilis, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus pyogenes and Klebsiella oxytoca [7]. However, the classification as a specific bacterial presence in distinction to the pharyngeal flora, also taking into account the quantity, remains questionable in this study. To the best of our knowledge, our study represents the first microbiome analysis of ET and, consequently, the first description based on molecular biological data.

Shared microbial profiles of upper respiratory tract and ET microbiome in healthy individuals

The healthy microbiome of the upper respiratory tract is variable over time and depends on environmental factors. Microbiota shifts also occur with increasing age [33]. Jervis-Bardy et al. describe Haemophilus, Streptococcus, and Granulicatella as dominant genera in the nasopharynx of patients with healthy middle ears [34]. A review published by Flynn and Dooley in 2021 discusses the colonization of the nasopharynx throughout life and different influencing factors. They mention colonization across different age groups in childhood with Streptococcus, Moraxella, Corynebacteria, and Dolosigranulum, but note the lack of larger longitudinal studies within the adult population [35]. Stępińska et al. demonstrated the intracellular coexistence of Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Streptococcus species in monocytes and macrophages of the nasopharynx in healthy individuals [36]. Published studies concentrate on aerobic bacteria with easier culture conditions, while slow-growing, fastidious, and anaerobic organisms are hardly described. The entire previously described aerobic spectrum of bacteria shows similarities with those detected in the ET in our study, both in healthy individuals and those with ET dysfunction. Both our molecular biological and culture-based findings reveal that bacterial genera previously described in the literature - such as Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Haemophilus, and Moraxella - are also among the most frequently detected in our study.

Potential protective and pathogenic roles of the ET microbiome

A key focus has often been the identification of defined causative agents and pathomechanisms of designated diseases. For example, N-acetylgalactosamine (GaINAc) promotes the adhesion of Streptococcus pneumoniae to the epithelium of the ET in patients with otitis media [37]. Simultaneously, the colonization with the same bacteria in both healthy and symptomatic patients makes it difficult to assign a causative agent. As a result, the focus has shifted to the composition of the microbiome. In our study, Veillonella was detected significantly more frequently by culture in the control group, and the microbiome analysis likewise revealed a significantly higher abundance in the control group in the differential abundance analysis. Anaerobes such as Veillonella are considered normal commensals of the nasopharynx and indicative of respiratory health [38]. The role of Actinomyces, which also showed higher detection in the culture-based analysis, is better studied in the context of the oral microbiome and its influence on oral infections and dental plaque [39]. A protective role in etiology of nasopharyngeal diseases has not yet been described. Infections of actinomycosis of the nasopharynx are rare and can occur as a result of nasal trauma, surgical manipulation or contact with foreign material [40]. In contrast, Staphylococcus was identified with higher frequency in the study group culture-analysis, with the most frequently identified species being Staphylococcus epidermidis, a typical nasopharynx commensal. A protective role of nasal S. epidermidis in defending against viral infection has been described [41]. The main defined virulence factor of S. epidermidis is its ability to produce biofilms, making it an ideal partner for multispecies biofilm-like communities that can be responsible for persistent middle ear infections [42].

The identification of pathogens associated with chronic ET dysfunction appears more challenging. Notably, in the microbiome analysis, Rothia was found to be significantly more abundant in the study group. These findings suggest that Rothia may be linked to the microbial alterations observed in chronic obstructive ET dysfunction. A similar increase in detection rates was observed in the culture-based analysis for the study group, although this was not statistically significant. No studies have conclusively identified a pathogenetic role [43]. On one hand, Rothia has been shown to have a high abundance in adenoid samples from children with middle ear effusion [44], and may enhance the pathogenicity of other otitis media pathogens [45]. On the other hand, some reports a protective role and suggest that Rothia may restrict M. catarrhalis colonization in the human respiratory tract [46].

Reduced microbial diversity as a feature of respiratory diseases

A comparison of the whole spectrum of bacteria detected shows a greater diversity in the control group (34 bacterial genera) than in the study group (19 bacterial genera). Furthermore, the microbiome analysis revealed a slightly larger core microbiome in the control group compared to the study group, with the majority of genera (17 in total) being shared between both groups. Although the genus-level diversity appeared greater in the culture-based analysis than in the molecular analysis, it is important to note that culture-based methods do not exclusively reflect the core microbiome. Moreover, culture techniques detect only viable, replicating, and culturable bacteria, whereas molecular methods also capture fastidious or non-culturable organisms, as well as remnants of microbial DNA. The observed reduction in diversity of colonized microorganisms aligns with literature data on microbiome studies in patients with respiratory diseases. For example, studies on chronic rhinosinusitis have shown that the complex polymicrobial communities of sinus cavities are often characterized by reduced diversity [47]. Prospective cohort studies have demonstrated a lower diversity of nasopharyngeal microbiome in children with pneumonia compared to healthy children [48]. However, it must be noted that the smaller size of the study group is a limiting factor in this comparison.

Sampling strategies for ET microbiome analyses

In the present study, a negative culture result was observed in only one swab and two biopsies. This suggests that biopsies and swabs are more sensitive for detecting bacteria compared to simply submitting the balloon catheter tip to microbiologic diagnostics, as performed by Schröder et al. [7]. The relative value of swab culture versus tissue biopsy is well-studied for skin and soft tissue infections, but there is ongoing debate regarding the preferred material - wound swab [49–51] or deep intraoperatively obtained soft-tissue specimens [52]. For the upper respiratory tract, Sarkar et al. showed that fine-needle aspiration core sampling is a reliable material for culture diagnostics compared to surface swab specimens in patients with chronic or recurrent tonsillitis [53]. Torretta and colleagues reached a similar conclusion, showing that biopsy should be preferred for diagnosing biofilm-producing bacteria in children with adenoiditis compared to nasopharyngeal swabs [54]. Biofilm-producing bacteria, such as those dominating in our study, are particularly significant in the context of ET dysfunction. A biofilm is a complex and well-structured aggregation of microorganisms of one or more species. Biofilms confer several advantages, including multifactorial antibiotic resistance mechanisms that complicate therapy [11]. The significance of biofilms and bacterial colonization in ET dysfunction has been discussed in recent years as a possible causes of tubal dysfunction [55, 56]. It is conceivable that bacterial colonization or biofilms may lead to chronic inflammation of the tubal mucosa, promoting mucosal hyperplasia and contributing to the formation of thick, viscous mucus often extracted during BET. Biopsies, while more invasive, offer advantages in penetrating deeper tissue for microbiome analysis and identifying biofilm-producing microorganisms. However, they are limited to accessing only the entrance of the ET. Swabs, on the other hand, can sample a larger surface area of the cartilaginous ET without causing tissue damage. The main challenge with swabs is avoiding contamination with pharyngeal flora during specimen collection. The interrater agreement for comparing detection outcomes between swab and biopsy could not be calculated for the majority of the bacterial genera in the study group due to the low detection rate. Specifically, 16 bacterial genera in the control group and 28 in the study group were detected in at most one patient, totaling 39 bacterial genera. This rarity inhibited comparative calculations. Consequently, interrater agreement was primarily calculated for the dominating bacterial genera. The confidence intervals often showed wider ranges and sometimes overlapped 0, indicating instability. For more reliable conclusions regarding the most suitable material for microbiological diagnostics, a combination of swab and biopsy should be used. Future studies with more patients, especially in the study group, are recommended to increase statistical power. In conclusion, while biopsies and swabs both have their advantages, the choice of method may depend on the specific diagnostic goals and the need to balance invasiveness with the comprehensiveness of the sample. To facilitate such optimized sampling, practical and contamination-minimized collection techniques are essential, particularly in the low-biomass environment of the ET. We were unable to find commercially available brushes that fit through the Yankauer suction tip. However, the technique of sample collection via the oral route using the Yankauer suction tip as a protective cover proved easy to implement and effective. The successful implementation of this contamination- minimized Yankauer suction technique is further validated by the complete absence of amplicons in blank negative controls, which is particularly critical given that the ET represents a low-biomass environment where even minimal contamination could significantly confound microbiome analysis results. This method can be recommended for further investigations.

Limitations and future perspectives in ET microbiome research

This study had limitations that should be considered, especially since there are no other publications on the spectrum of bacteria in healthy and obstructive ET. Our results can thus be seen as a foundation for further research. The inclusion of study participants proved challenging due to widespread reluctance toward additional sampling and the frequent presence of the exclusion criterion of smoking. This led to a limited number of study sample materials and consequently restricting the statistical power of the analysis. However, despite the limited statistical power, our study successfully detected statistically significant differences in microbiome compositions between groups, demonstrating that meaningful biological differences can be identified even with modest effect sizes. One key limitation is the exclusion of viral diagnostics. Viruses, such as influenza A virus, respiratory syncytial virus, human rhinovirus, and adenovirus play a significant role in otolaryngological infections, including otitis media. These viruses can influence ET function by increasing bacterial adherence and colonization [57]. Viral infections can precede ET dysfunction and might not be detectable at the time of symptom presentation. Immunological detection of antibodies may not accurately indicate the timing of the infection. Although next-generation sequencing approaches from middle ear samples have presented challenges [34], future NGS approaches will be very valuable for studying bacterial communities in health and disease, providing information on the relative abundance of potential pathogens or protective bacteria in patients with chronic otolaryngologic diseases. Additionally, the gut microbiome has been discussed in connection with recurrent acute otitis media [58], auditory disorders [59], and chronic rhinosinusitis [60]. Comparing microbiome analysis of the ET and gut using NGS techniques, applicable even in very-low-biomass contexts, would be desirable.

Conclusion

This study successfully established an effective method for collecting ET swabs using a Yankauer suction tip. The microbiome of the ET has been analyzed and described for the first time using both culture-based and molecular techniques. It highlighted a greater bacterial diversity in the control group compared to the study group representing patients with chronic ET dysfunction, aligning with existing literature on microbiome studies from other respiratory diseases. The results underscore the need for combining swab and biopsy methods in future studies and the importance of considering viral factors and further advanced sequencing technologies for a deeper understanding of ET dysfunction.
