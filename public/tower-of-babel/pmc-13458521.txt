Freezing depth prediction of surrounding rock in seasonally frozen tunnels based on bayes-optimized XGBoost
Authors: Jinglong Li, Jianqing Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring freeze-thaw cycles, frost heave forces induced by pore water phase transition in the surrounding rock of seasonally frozen tunnels act continuously on the lining structure, impairing its stability and service performance. The evolution of freezing depth is governed by the coupling effects of temperature, moisture and surrounding rock properties, and directly correlates with the distribution of frost heave forces. Thus, accurate prediction of freezing depth is critical for frost heave disaster control and structural design of cold-region tunnels. In this study, moisture migration and phase transition during surrounding rock freezing were considered to establish a hydrothermal coupling model and identify key influencing factors. On this basis, a dataset of tunnel freezing depth in seasonally frozen regions was constructed by combining Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) with numerical simulation methods. Subsequently, a Bayes-optimized XGBoost (BO-XGBoost) prediction model was constructed, where Bayesian optimization (BO) was employed for hyperparameter tuning. Distinguished from traditional manual or grid search methods, this algorithm realizes automatic and precise parameter optimization with higher efficiency, and effectively avoids the model from falling into local optimality during the tuning process. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was used to quantify feature contributions, and the Stefan solution verified prediction reliability. Results show that BO-XGBoost outperforms Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), with high sensitivity to temperature, moisture and thermal conductivity properties but low sensitivity to specific heat properties. Increased thermal conductivity of surrounding rock and lining deepens the predicted freezing depth, while higher specific heat capacity, initial moisture content, initial rock temperature and average temperature of the coldest month reduce it. The maximum relative error between model predictions and field monitoring data is only 7.9%, significantly lower than the 22.4% of the Stefan solution, providing a more accurate method for predicting the freezing depth of seasonally frozen tunnels.Keywords: Seasonally frozen tunnels, Freezing depth, Hydro-thermal coupling, Machine learning, Bayesian optimization, SHAP analysisSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

China’s transportation infrastructure is rapidly expanding into high-latitude and high-altitude regions, with cold-region tunnels increasingly dominating key transportation hubs. Under coupled climatic and hydrological effects, surrounding rock freezing depth exposes tunnel linings to high-intensity frost heave forces, severely endangering long-term tunnel stability and operational safety1,2. Thus, accurate determination of surrounding rock freezing depth is fundamental for optimizing frost-resistant schemes and disaster mitigation in cold-region tunnels.​

Theoretical calculation is a primary method for determining freezing depth. Zhang et al.3 proposed an approximate analytical method for maximum freezing depth based on equivalent negative accumulated temperature and quasi-steady state theory, which outperformed the Stefan solution in case verifications. Xia et al.4 derived an analytical formula considering lining, insulation layer, and unfrozen water content via quasi-steady state assumption and integral method. Zhao et al.5 measured surrounding rock temperature of the Greater Khingan Mountains Tunnel, established a freezing depth equation using the Stefan method, and evaluated factor impacts. Liang et al.6 developed a modified prediction formula incorporating unfrozen water content with verified accuracy. Wu et al.7 derived a phase transition-included temperature field analytical formula using separation of variables and Fourier integral transform, validated by field monitoring data. However, these theories primarily rely on quasi-steady state assumptions and struggle to accurately capture temperature field redistribution under coupled moisture migration and phase transition, limiting their calculation accuracy.​

Freezing depth prediction in cold-region tunnels is a typical multi-field coupling problem, influenced by air temperature boundary conditions, surrounding rock thermophysical parameters, and initial stratum water content. Traditional analytical methods are unable to characterize the strong nonlinear interactions among these factors. In contrast, machine learning (ML) methods possess robust pattern recognition and function approximation capabilities. They can thus effectively capture the nonlinear coupling between characteristic factors and dependent variables, which facilitates the construction of high-precision surrogate models. Consequently, ML has been gradually applied to freezing depth prediction. Ma et al.8 proposed a temperature field-based hybrid prediction model, verified via engineering applications. Sun et al.9 constructed a BP neural network for efficient prediction of freezing depth and frost damage location. Yan et al.10 adopted RF and other ML methods to predict freezing depth under 7 working conditions. However, existing predictions focus solely on the temperature field, neglecting transient moisture migration and latent heat release during phase transition. Additionally, lack of systematic hyperparameter optimization and interpretability analysis results in low prediction accuracy and limited generalization ability.​

To address these issues, this study considers nonlinear temperature-moisture coupling in seasonally frozen tunnel surrounding rock during freezing periods and the impact of water-ice phase transition on freezing depth. Taking temperature, moisture content, surrounding rock properties, and lining characteristics as input features, a seasonally frozen tunnel freezing depth dataset was established via finite element numerical simulation combined with LHS. The BO algorithm was employed to automatically search for the optimal hyperparameter combination of the XGBoost prediction model. Stability and accuracy of the BO-XGBoost algorithm were verified via comparative analysis with RF, SVR, and LightGBM models. Furthermore, SHAP analysis was integrated to interpret feature contribution degrees and dependence relationships between each feature and freezing depth. Research results provide valuable references for cold-region tunnel structural design.

Finite element model calculation

Model establishment and boundary condition setting

A certain seasonally frozen tunnel is located in the moderate-deep seasonal frozen soil region in the eastern Qinghai-Tibet plateau, with a total length of 3355 m and an annual average temperature of 5.9 °C. The surrounding rock is classified as grade V14. In this study, the partial differential equation (PDE) module of the COMSOL finite element software was adopted for numerical simulation. The boundary conditions of the model are set as follows15: the average temperature of the inner boundary of the lining is 5.9 °C with a temperature amplitude of 15 °C; the initial ground temperature of the surrounding rock is 2.9 °C; boundaries AB, CD and BD of the model are defined as adiabatic interfaces; the initial water content of the surrounding rock is 28%. It is assumed that there is no moisture migration between the lining and the surrounding rock, and the seepage energy at the contact interface is zero16. Meanwhile, the seepage flux across boundaries AB, CD and BD is set to zero. The finite element calculation model is illustrated in Fig. 1, and the calculation parameters are listed in Table 115,17.

Simulation results and analysis

The contour plots of temperature, unfrozen water content, and volumetric ice content of the tunnel at a specific moment during the freezing period, derived from the numerical simulation, are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Temperature and moisture cloud maps of tunnels during the freezing period. (a) Temperature field (Unit: ℃), (b) Unfrozen water content (Unit: %), (c) Volumetric ice content (Unit: %).

As can be seen from the Fig. 2, in the tunnel freezing stage, the external cold energy conducts from the tunnel lining surface to the deep part of the surrounding rock, forming a significant radial temperature gradient. When the temperature of the surrounding rock zone adjacent to the lining drops below the freezing temperature, pore water migrates and freezes. This results in a reduction in the unfrozen water content and an increase in the volumetric ice content, thus forming a frozen ring of the surrounding rock.

To further investigate the variation law of surrounding rock freezing depth under the coupling effect of temperature and moisture migration, we extracted the surrounding rock freezing depth at different moments during the freezing period. The corresponding results are presented in Fig. 3. It can be observed that the maximum freezing depth of the tunnel during the freezing period is approximately 1.25 m, which is basically consistent with the maximum freezing depth of 1.22 m obtained from field monitoring14. In the first 36 days under negative temperature exposure, the cold energy only conducts from the lining to the surface layer of the surrounding rock, and no freezing front is formed. The temperature of the surrounding rock remains basically above the freezing temperature, and the surrounding rock is entirely in a thawed state. With the continuous application of negative temperature, the cold energy continuously transmits to the surrounding rock, leading to a rapid growth trend of the freezing depth, and the evolution of the surrounding rock temperature field is significantly dominated by cold energy conduction18. After 160 days of negative temperature exposure, the ambient temperature turns positive, and the thermal resistance effect of the deep geotemperature of the surrounding rock becomes increasingly pronounced. This results in a slowdown in the advancement of the freezing front, a decrease in the growth rate, and the gradual emergence of a thawed layer.

Variation of surrounding rock freezing depth with time under negative temperature exposure.

Establishment of a dataset for surrounding rock freezing depth

Based on numerical simulation results and relevant research findings19–21, starting from the intrinsic mechanism of hydrothermal coupling during surrounding rock freezing: temperature directly affects cold energy input and serves as the energy basis for freezing front advancement, moisture conditions determine the total latent heat released during freezing, and thermophysical parameters of surrounding rock and lining govern heat conduction efficiency between the external environment and interior. To cover the entire physical process of freezing front propagation, the core influencing factors of surrounding rock freezing depth are identified as: average temperature of the coldest month, initial rock temperature, initial moisture content, and thermal conductivity/specific heat of surrounding rock and lining. To establish a scientifically controllable parameter variation gradient and fully capture factor fluctuation impacts, each characteristic factor was adjusted by ± 20%22 to quantify the response trend of freezing depth under different factor values. The value ranges of each factor are as follows: surrounding rock thermal conductivity (1.104, 1.656), surrounding rock specific heat (0.712, 1.068), lining thermal conductivity (1.48, 2.22), lining specific heat (0.8368, 1.2552), initial surrounding rock temperature (2.32, 3.48), initial moisture content (22.4, 33.6), and average temperature of the coldest month (−12.1, −6.1).

Given the characteristic variable ranges, a hybrid method combining LHS23 and numerical simulation was adopted for sample generation. The 7-dimensional sample data affecting freezing depth were uniformly stratified in the parameter space, yielding 100 representative characteristic variable combinations. This ensures the reliability and stability of the subsequent freezing depth prediction model.

Table 2 presents the statistical results of LHS sampling, showing that the mean value of each characteristic variable is close to the interval midpoint with a small standard deviation, achieving uniform variable coverage and enhancing model training stability. Pearson correlation analysis of LHS results (Fig. 4) indicates uniform and independent sampling of characteristic variables, with absolute correlation coefficients ≤ 0.16 (weak correlation). This confirms that the influence of each variable on freezing depth is dominated by nonlinear coupling effects, avoiding multicollinearity from strong variable correlations and effectively mitigating model overfitting risks.

Establishment process of the BO-XGBoost prediction model

The XGBoost algorithm belongs to the Boosting ensemble learning family, built on the core principle of Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT). It forms the final predictive model via ensemble optimization of multiple decision tree models24. BO is a hyperparameter tuning method incorporating a prior process25. In this study, surrounding rock freezing depth was designated as the model’s dependent variable, with other variables defined as characteristic variables. The dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set at an 8:2 ratio. To eliminate feature dimensionality effects, Z-Score standardization was performed on the dataset, and the calculation formula is as follows:

where: Z is the standardized value; X is the original feature value; µ is the mean value of the original feature dataset; σ is the standard deviation of the original feature dataset.

During model training, preset hyperparameter ranges were adopted as prior information, with the optimization objective of minimizing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of freezing depth prediction. BO was then employed for global hyperparameter optimization of the XGBoost algorithm. To avoid model overfitting and ensure its generalization performance, this study referred to the universal hyperparameter ranges widely adopted in geotechnical engineering prediction studies for parameter setting. The hyperparameter ranges were set as follows: max_depth (3 ~ 10), learning_rate (0.01 ~ 0.3), n_estimators (50 ~ 200), gamma (0 ~ 1), min_child_weight (1 ~ 10), subsample (0.6 ~ 1.0), colsample_bytree (0.6 ~ 1.0), and reg_lambda (0 ~ 1). Model convergence was determined when the loss curves of both the training and validation sets stabilized (Fig. 5).​

Loss function curve of BO-XGBoost model training process.

To ensure model generalization, 5-fold cross-validation (CV) was applied to the training set. Specifically, the 80 training samples were equally divided into 5 subsets: 4 subsets for training and 1 for validation, with the process repeated 5 times. Optimal model parameters were determined based on the average prediction accuracy of 5-fold CV, yielding a model with higher stability and stronger generalization. Prediction results and analysis

To verify the accuracy of the BO-XGBoost algorithm in predicting the freezing depth of surrounding rock in seasonally frozen tunnels, RF, SVR, and LightGBM were employed for comparative prediction. The actual and predicted values of the test set for each model are illustrated in Fig. 7. It can be observed that the prediction errors of all four models are within ± 0.12 m, which confirms the feasibility of applying machine learning algorithms for surrounding rock freezing depth prediction in frozen tunnels.

Figure 8 presents the prediction performance plots of the test sets for the four models. The results demonstrate that all four machine learning prediction models can satisfactorily predict the freezing depth. However, the BO-XGBoost model exhibits a higher fitting degree between the measured and predicted value curves, along with a more uniform residual distribution. The residuals of the surrounding rock freezing depth obtained by BO-XGBoost, RF, SVR, and LightGBM are within the ranges of ± 0.1 m, ± 0.12 m, ± 0.12 m, and ± 0.11 m, respectively. It can be concluded that the BO-XGBoost model outperforms the other three models in terms of both prediction accuracy and error control.

SHAP interpretability analysis and validation of surrounding rock freezing depth prediction model

SHAP interpretability analysis

Figure 10 shows the schematic diagram of the SHAP interpretability model. When conditioned on a specific feature, the variation in the model’s expected prediction result is attributed to that feature. The baseline value refers to the mean output of all samples. Red represents a positive promotion effect (e.g., Features 1, 2, and 3), while blue denotes a negative inhibitory effect (e.g., Feature 4). A single prediction result is derived from the superposition of multiple features with either positive or negative contributions27.

SHAP interpretability schematic diagram.

The feature importance ranking based on SHAP analysis is illustrated in Fig. 11(a). Characteristic variables including temperature, initial moisture content, and thermal conductivity exhibit relatively high impacts on prediction results, while specific heat shows a lower influence. Figure 11(b) presents the SHAP summary plot. It can be observed that when the average temperature of the coldest month, initial moisture content, initial surrounding rock temperature, and surrounding rock skeleton specific heat decrease, the corresponding SHAP values are positive, and the predicted surrounding rock freezing depth tends to increase. Conversely, as the surrounding rock skeleton thermal conductivity and lining thermal conductivity increase, the SHAP values of these features show a positive growth trend, with the predicted freezing depth increasing synchronously. During the evolution of the surrounding rock freezing front, changes in ambient temperature, moisture migration, and thermal conductivity properties exert significant impacts on freezing depth, as reflected by the higher absolute magnitudes of their SHAP values. In contrast, during the water-ice phase transition, the regulatory effect of specific heat on freezing depth is relatively limited, resulting in significantly lower absolute magnitudes of SHAP values compared to other features and a lower ranking in feature importance.

SHAP analysis. (a) Feature contribution, (b) Summary plot.

To better understand the influence of each characteristic variable on freezing depth, single-factor SHAP value plots were generated as illustrated in Fig. 12. The key observations are as follows: (1) With the increase in the average temperature of the coldest month, the cold energy input from the external environment to the surrounding rock decreases, leading to a reduction in SHAP values and a corresponding decrease in the predicted freezing depth; (2) As the initial surrounding rock temperature rises, the internal heat of the surrounding rock during the early freezing stage increases, resulting in a downward trend of SHAP values—indicating that a lower initial surrounding rock temperature corresponds to a greater predicted freezing depth; (3) An increase in initial moisture content leads to more latent heat release from phase transition within the surrounding rock, which inhibits the advancement of the freezing front28. Consequently, the SHAP values of this feature show a decreasing trend, meaning a lower initial moisture content results in a greater freezing depth; (4) Higher thermal conductivity of the surrounding rock and lining enhances the heat transfer capacity between the external environment and the surrounding rock, suggesting that increased thermal conductivity contributes to a larger predicted freezing depth; (5) With the increase in soil specific heat, more heat is released during the freezing and cooling process of the surrounding rock, consuming additional cold energy and leading to a decreasing trend of freezing depth. The influence of lining specific heat is relatively weak, with SHAP values fluctuating around 0, indicating it is a weakly influential feature.

Conclusions

In this study, a freezing depth dataset of seasonally frozen tunnels was constructed via LHS combined with numerical simulation. BO-XGBoost model was employed to predict the surrounding rock freezing depth, with comparative analysis conducted against predictions from RF, SVR, and LightGBM models. Critical factors influencing surrounding rock freezing depth were identified using SHAP analysis. Furthermore, field monitoring results from three engineering cases, BO-XGBoost predictions, and Stefan solution calculations were compared to verify the applicability of the BO-XGBoost model for freezing depth prediction in seasonally frozen tunnels. The main research conclusions are as follows:​

(1) A hydrothermal coupling finite element model of seasonally frozen tunnels was established considering moisture migration and water-ice phase transition, and the time-dependent evolution law of the freezing front during the freezing process of tunnel surrounding rock was investigated. The simulated freezing depth was approximately 1.25 m, which is consistent with the field monitoring value of 1.22 m, with a relative error of only 2.46%, thus verifying the accuracy of the established model.​

(2) The original dataset was determined using LHS combined with numerical simulation. The BO algorithm was adopted to automatically search and optimize the hyperparameters of the XGBoost prediction model, which significantly improved the model performance. Comparative analysis with the prediction results of RF, SVR, and LightGBM models indicated that the BO-XGBoost model exhibits the optimal performance in both error control and prediction accuracy, providing a novel method for freezing depth prediction of seasonally frozen tunnels.​

(3) Feature importance ranking was performed using the SHAP interpretable model. The results indicated that temperature, moisture content, and thermal conductivity exert significant impacts on freezing depth, while the influence of specific heat is relatively weak. This conclusion is highly consistent with the basic theory of hydrothermal transfer in frozen soil, which verifies the physical rationality of the model prediction results.​

(4) SHAP dependence plots explained the influence of individual characteristic factors on freezing depth prediction. The findings demonstrated that: with the increase in the average temperature of the coldest month, initial moisture content, initial rock temperature, and surrounding rock specific heat, the SHAP values of the model gradually decrease to negative values, indicating an inhibitory effect on freezing depth advancement (negative correlation); the increase in thermal conductivity of surrounding rock and lining raises the SHAP values to positive values, promoting freezing depth advancement (positive correlation); the contribution of specific heat to freezing depth prediction is weak.​

(5) Comparative analysis of monitoring data and theoretical calculation results from typical tunnel projects in seasonally frozen regions reveals that the BO-XGBoost model can effectively predict freezing depth with an error of approximately 0.1 m. Additionally, the BO-XGBoost model exhibits high prediction accuracy within the characteristic variable range, while the error becomes relatively larger when extreme working conditions are considered.
