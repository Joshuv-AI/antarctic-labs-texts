Comparing the outcome of pediatric femoral neck fracture between Ti-Robot surgery and traditional surgery
Authors: Yuan Ma, Hao Yang, Yunfeng Xu, Fangyu liu, Wenyu Liao, Zhen Bian, Lin Huang, Xieyuan Jiang, Zheng Yang, Xuemin Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the efficacy of Ti-Robot system (Beijing TINAVI Medical Technology Company, China) compared with conventional surgery.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 56 pediatric patients with femoral neck fractures. Patients ranged in age from 3.06 to 16.06 years. Twenty-six patients underwent Ti-Robot-assisted surgery, while thirty patients underwent freehand surgery. Baseline and intraoperative data were collected. During a follow-up period of at least 1 year, fracture healing and functional outcomes were assessed using radiographic evaluation and questionnaires.ResultsAll patients were successfully fixed with three screws. Baseline characteristics were similar between the two groups. Screw parallelism and femoral neck width coverage were better in the Ti-Robot group. Intraoperative fluoroscopy frequency, radiation dose, drilling frequency, and total screw implantation time were lower in the Ti-Robot group than in the conventional group. There were no significant differences in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, healing time, complication rate, Harris Hip Score, Non-Arthritic Hip Score, or Hip and Groin Outcome Score between the two groups.ConclusionThe Ti-Robot system is a feasible adjunctive approach for the treatment of pediatric femoral neck fractures. It improves screw placement accuracy and reduces radiation exposure, although its effect on long-term clinical outcomes requires further investigation.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.Keywords: pediatric femoral neck fractures, Ti-Robot-assisted fixation, parallel screws, closed reduction, internal fixation, radiation dose

Introduction

Femoral neck fractures are a rare subset of pediatric hip fractures. 1 They are usually caused by high-energy trauma, such as traffic accidents and falls. Common complications include avascular necrosis (AVN), nonunion, and deformity, with AVN being one of the most severe, occurring in up to 22% of patients.2–4 According to the Delbet classification, these fractures are categorized as type I (transepiphyseal), type II (transcervical), type III (cervicotrochanteric), and type IV (intertrochanteric). 5 The Garden classification is commonly used to evaluate the degree of displacement. Closed reduction and percutaneous fixation using three screws is the most common surgical treatment. 6

Although positive outcomes of robotic surgery compared to freehand techniques have been demonstrated in adults, research on its application in pediatric patients across all types of Delbet fractures remains limited.7–9

Conventional surgery is the gold standard for pediatric femoral neck fractures, also offering distinct advantages in resource-limited settings. Its well-established procedural protocols and broad applicability have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy for numerous patients. Experienced surgeons can achieve satisfactory outcomes by leveraging refined technical skills and tactile feedback during fracture reduction and fixation.

Robotic systems have been shown to facilitate precise screw placement through preoperative planning and robotic arm execution. 10 The high accuracy of robotic screw placement may reduce intraoperative injury and radiation exposure. We conducted an analysis of patients who underwent surgery between 2020 and 2023 to compare robot-assisted with conventional techniques. We hypothesized that robot-assisted surgery would improve technical and procedural outcomes, while some follow-up clinical outcomes might remain comparable between groups.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and inclusion criteria

A total of 56 patients who underwent closed reduction and percutaneous internal fixation for pediatric femoral neck fractures at our hospital from January 2020 to December 2023 were included. The inclusion criteria were as follows 1 : age younger than 18 years 2 ; availability of preoperative anteroposterior hip radiographs and CT scans of the affected hip 3 ; no pathological fracture 4 ; no previous fracture or surgery on the affected side 5 ; no serious underlying disease; and 6 successful closed reduction on the traction table before screw fixation.

To maintain a consistent treatment framework and minimize additional confounding factors, patients in whom satisfactory closed reduction could not be achieved and who therefore required primary open reduction were treated surgically but were not included in the present analysis. Although non-operative treatment may be considered for very young children with stable, minimally displaced femoral neck fractures, patients in this cohort were treated with closed reduction and percutaneous internal fixation to maintain reduction stability, prevent secondary displacement, and allow close radiographic follow-up.

Assignment to the robot-assisted or conventional group was not randomized. In routine clinical practice, the choice of procedure was mainly influenced by the availability of the robotic system, insurance coverage, family preference, and routine clinical decision-making, rather than by a prespecified study protocol or deliberate selection based on fracture complexity or body habitus.

All patients were followed up for at least one year after surgery. Written informed consent for surgery was obtained from the patients’ guardians, and the study was approved by the institutional ethics review board. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Surgical technique

After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position on an orthopedic traction table. Closed reduction was performed by gentle abduction and internal rotation with axial traction (Figure 1(A)). Repeated abduction was avoided to minimize soft-tissue damage. A C-arm was used to confirm satisfactory reduction quality. The surgical field was then prepared and draped in a sterile manner.

Steps in the robot-assisted surgical procedure: (A) Patient positioned supine (B) The patient tracking device was fixed on the ipsilateral distal femur (C) Small skin incisions (∼1 cm) made at entry points (D) Guide sleeves inserted (E) Guide pins placed through sleeves (F–H) Precise alignment and parallelism of guide pins achieved. (I) After confirming satisfactory screw positions with fluoroscopy (Figure 3D), guide pins removed; incisions closed with sutures.

All procedures in both groups were performed by the same trauma team, consisting of three senior pediatric orthopedic surgeons (at the associate chief level or above) with extensive experience in robot-assisted procedures, including slipped capital femoral epiphysis fixation and deformity correction for achondroplasia. In the robot-assisted group, two K-wires were used to fix the patient tracker to the distal femur on the affected side (Figure 1(B)). The Ti-Robot system, as shown in Figure 2, was used for surgical navigation. First, a C-arm fluoroscope was used to obtain frontal and lateral images of the femoral head and neck on the affected side. This ensured that all ten robotic localization points were clearly visible. Fluoroscopic images acquired with the C-arm were transferred to the robotic workstation. The robot’s image recognition module identified these localization points and performed registration and matching within the workstation. The system subsequently updated the registration data continuously to enable real-time tracking of the femur. The screw trajectories were planned and calculated virtually on the workstation (Figure 3B). The paths of the three screws were parallel to the femoral neck and separated from each other. This inverted triangular configuration, with no screws penetrating the hip joint, was designed to ensure stable fixation.

The Ti-Robot system integrates a robotic workstation, an optical tracking unit, and a robotic arm.

Preoperative planning and intraoperative verification. (A) Confirmation of fracture alignment via fluoroscopy. (B) Virtual planning of screw trajectories on robotic workstation. (C) Fluoroscopic verification of guide pin alignment with planned paths. (D) Confirmation of satisfactory position and depth of screws with fluoroscopy.

Following this, the robotic arm moved to approach the skin according to the planned trajectories. The surgeons made skin incisions approximately 1 cm in length at each designated entry point (Figure 1(C)). Guide sleeves were attached to the robot arm (Figure 1(D)). Guide pins were placed through the sleeves (Figure 1(E)). Intraoperative fluoroscopy was used to confirm the direction of each guide pin (Figure 3C). The three screws were then placed with satisfactory parallelism (Figure. 1(F)–(H)). Subsequently, cannulated compression screws of appropriate lengths were chosen and inserted over the guide pins to stabilize the fracture. Once the satisfactory position and depth of the screws were confirmed with fluoroscopy (Figure 3D), the guide pins were removed, and the incisions were closed with sutures (Figure 1(i)).

Follow-up and evaluation

After surgery, the number of guide pin placement, total screw placement time, anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopy, radiation dose, operative time, and estimated blood loss were collected. All patients received the same postoperative management, such as maintaining the affected limb in an abducted neutral position, pain control, and early mobilization. If there was no significant discomfort, discharge was permitted, and patients were instructed to engage in home rehabilitation exercises and gradually increase weight-bearing. In addition, standard anteroposterior and lateral X-rays were collected to compare radiological differences between the two groups. As described by Liebergall, the alignment of screws was measured to determine screw parallelism.11,12 The percentage of the femoral neck width covered by the screws at the fracture level was calculated to evaluate screw distribution.

According to the Haidukewych criteria 13 : 1) excellent reduction: displacement less than 2 mm and angulation less than 5 degrees; 2) good reduction: displacement between 2-5 mm and angulation between 5-10 degrees; 3) poor reduction: displacement greater than 10 mm and angulation greater than 20 degrees, we used postoperative hip radiographs to assess the quality of fracture reduction.

All patients were followed up for at least 12 months after surgery, with scheduled clinical and radiographic evaluations at approximately 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively, and thereafter as needed. At the final follow-up, questionnaire assessments were administered. Fracture healing time was defined as the interval from surgery to the first follow-up radiograph on which the fracture line was no longer clearly visible and continuous cortical bridging was observed, and was determined by reviewing all available inpatient and outpatient radiographs. All treatment quality assessments were independently completed by two senior orthopedic surgeons, each with over 10 years of clinical experience in pediatric orthopedics, and neither surgeon participated in the surgical procedures. The Harris Hip Score (HHS), Non-Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS), and Hip and Groin Outcome Score (HAGOS) were used to evaluate functional outcomes. Complications, including avascular necrosis, femoral neck nonunion, and premature physeal closure, were documented.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized all variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test checked the normality of continuous data. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (‾x ± s) and compared using Student's t-test. Non-normal data were reported as median (P25–P75) and analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were tested using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 56 patients were included in this comparison, including 36 males and 20 females. The mean age of the children was 10.36 ± 3.0 years (range, 3.06–16.06 years). In this series, robot-assisted fixation was used only in school-aged children and adolescents (≥ 8 years). Twenty-six patients underwent robot surgery, and 30 underwent conventional surgery. There were no significant differences between the two groups in age, gender, BMI, laterality, fracture classification or the time from injury to surgery (P > 0.05). All types of Delbet fractures were included, with type II being the most common, ensuring comparability between the two groups.

Intraoperative and surgical performance outcomes

As shown in Table 2, although the robot-assisted group had a longer operative time and greater blood loss than the traditional group, the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The robot-assisted group showed better screw parallelism on both anteroposterior and lateral radiographs (P < 0.01). Femoral neck width coverage was also greater in the robot-assisted group than in the conventional group (P < 0.01). In robot-assisted group, nearly all the screws were placed at the intended position on the first attempt, thereby reducing drilling frequency. The median drilling frequency was 4 in the robot-assisted group compared to 8 in the conventional group (P < 0.01). The total screw placement time was 2.4 minutes in Ti-Robot team, compared with 5.38 minutes in the conventional group (P < 0.01). Consequently, the robot-assisted group required fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy images and had a lower radiation dose (P < 0.001). Regarding fracture reduction quality, 50% of patients in the robot-assisted group achieved anatomic reduction compared to 43.3% in the conventional group (P = 0.223).

Postoperative outcomes and complications

In Table 3, there was no significant difference in healing time between the robot-assisted group and the traditional group (P = 0.774). The median Harris Hip Score in the robot-assisted group was 95 [89, 95.5], while in the freehand group it was 94.5 [86.75, 95] (P > 0.05), showing no statistically significant difference (P = 0.965). The Harris Hip Score, which is closely related to hip joint pain, showed similar results between the groups. Additionally, hip joint function was further assessed using the Non-Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS) and the Hip and Groin Outcome Score (HAGOS). The median NAHS was 100 [88.5, 100] in the robot-assisted group and 100 [79.75, 100] in the freehand group (P = 0.774). The HAGOS included six domains: pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sports/recreation, quality of life, and participation. None of these domains showed statistically significant differences (P > 0.05 for each domain), indicating similar health conditions after surgery. Complications occurred in 5 cases (19.2%) within the robot-assisted group, all involving varying degrees of avascular necrosis (AVN). In the conventional surgery group, there were 5 cases (16.6%) of complications, including 4 cases of AVN alone and 1 case of AVN with limb shortening.

Discussion

In pediatric femoral neck fractures, the vascular anatomy of the proximal femoral epiphysis is distinct. A fracture can easily disrupt the blood supply to the femoral head.14,15 The risk of postoperative AVN is high because the femoral head is highly dependent on this blood supply. The incidence of AVN has been reported to be as high as 22%.4,16,17 Therefore, stable internal fixation and precise reduction have been shown to be key to reducing complications and improving the quality of fracture healing.7,18,19 Ideally, the screws should be placed parallel to each other and perpendicular to the fracture plane. An inverted triangular configuration near the femoral neck cortex can provide stable fixation and additional support, thereby contributing to better clinical and functional outcomes in children. 20

In the conventional surgery group, surgeons had to manually identify the entry points based on their experience. These puncture sites, located near the inferior border of the greater trochanter, relied on C-arm fluoroscopic guidance. This may require repeated adjustments of the guidewire position to determine the optimal implantation site. 21 During these iterative maneuvers, the presence of pre-existing tunnels can cause the guidewire to slip back along the original path. This increases radiation exposure and may damage bone tissue, compromising skeletal integrity and healing outcomes.22,23

Our study compared robot-assisted surgery with conventional surgical techniques in the treatment of pediatric femoral neck fractures. Robot-assisted surgery showed better screw parallelism and femoral neck width coverage (P < 0.001). In addition, the robotic team required fewer intraoperative fluoroscopic images and had a significantly lower radiation dose (P < 0.001). To further assess the postoperative hip function, we used three questionnaires. In addition to the traditional HHS, we also included the NAHS and HAGOS to further assess activity level and quality of life beyond pain alone. Both conventional and robot-assisted surgery achieved generally satisfactory postoperative functional outcomes. This suggests that the conventional method remains the gold standard in the treatment of pediatric femoral neck fractures. Although robot-assisted fixation improved screw placement accuracy, reduced drilling attempts, and decreased fluoroscopy frequency and radiation exposure, these findings should be interpreted primarily as technical and procedural benefits. Fewer drilling attempts and less repeated guidewire manipulation may reduce iatrogenic bone injury around the femoral neck. In addition, reduced fluoroscopy frequency and radiation exposure represent safety-related benefits for pediatric patients and the surgical team. However, these advantages did not translate into a statistically significant reduction in avascular necrosis or overall complication rate, nor into improved functional outcomes in the present cohort. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superior long-term clinical outcomes.

From a practical standpoint, for senior surgeons who are already proficient in conventional closed reduction and percutaneous screw fixation, the learning curve for adopting the robotic workflow appears relatively short, as the closed reduction steps on the traction table remain unchanged.

Our findings in pediatric femoral neck fractures are consistent with evidence from adult populations, in which robot-assisted surgery has been widely used and shown to be effective.24–26 Particularly in minimally displaced fractures, this technique enables efficient and reliable fixation with satisfactory functional outcomes. 1

We do not suggest that all pediatric femoral neck fractures undergo robot-assisted surgery. The high cost of the robotic system and the need for dedicated infrastructure may limit its widespread applicability. In our institution, the maximum additional robot-related charge is approximately 20,000 Renminbi (RMB) per case before reimbursement; however, the actual out-of-pocket cost for families is usually lower and varies according to local insurance coverage, reimbursement policy, and the disposable instruments used.

Potential drawbacks include longer operative duration and a possible increase in the risks of infection and intraoperative blood loss. In this study, no iatrogenic fractures related to the placement of K-wires for tracking markers were observed. However, our broader institutional experience suggests that this risk should not be ignored. During robot-assisted treatment of slipped capital femoral epiphysis, we encountered an isolated case of distal femoral fracture at the marker insertion site. A plausible explanation for this discrepancy is the difference in postoperative management: children with femoral neck fractures in this cohort were routinely immobilized with a brace and restricted from early weight bearing, whereas patients with slipped capital femoral epiphysis were generally allowed earlier mobilization, potentially increasing mechanical stress at the marker site.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, limited follow-up duration, and a non-randomized design. Second, treatment allocation was not randomized and was influenced by the availability of the robotic system, insurance coverage, family preference, and routine clinical decision-making in daily practice. Although patients were not deliberately assigned to either group based on fracture complexity, body habitus, or other specific patient characteristics, residual selection bias cannot be excluded. Third, patients requiring primary open reduction after failed closed reduction were not included in the present analysis; therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to more complex fractures. Fourth, most patients in this cohort were older children or adolescents, and the applicability of robot-assisted fixation in younger children requires further validation, especially with regard to protecting the femoral head blood supply during reduction. Fifth, the lack of routine capsular decompression may have influenced the incidence of postoperative avascular necrosis. Finally, the functional outcome measures used (HHS, NAHS, and HAGOS), although widely applied in adult hip preservation and femoral neck fracture literature, are not specifically validated for younger children and showed evident ceiling effects, potentially masking subtle differences between groups.

Conclusions

Overall, robot-assisted fixation showed technical and procedural advantages in selected pediatric femoral neck fractures. Further multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to determine whether these advantages translate into meaningful long-term clinical benefits.
