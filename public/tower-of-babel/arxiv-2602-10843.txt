Christian Bertram and Mads Vestergaard Jensen

Abstract
Given an undirected graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) , the Personalized PageRank (PPR) of t ∈ V t\in V with respect to s ∈ V s\in V , denoted π ⁡ ( s , t ) \pi(s,t) , is the probability that an α \alpha -discounted random walk starting at s s terminates at t t . We study the time complexity of estimating π ⁡ ( s , t ) \pi(s,t) with constant relative error and constant failure probability, whenever π ⁡ ( s , t ) \pi(s,t) is above a given threshold parameter δ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \delta\in(0,1) . We consider common graph-access models and furthermore study the single source, single target, and single node (PageRank centrality) variants of the problem. We provide a complete characterization of PPR estimation in undirected graphs by giving tight bounds (up to logarithmic factors) for all problems and model variants in both the worst-case and average-case setting. This includes both new upper and lower bounds. Tight bounds were recently obtained by Bertram, Jensen, Thorup, Wang, and Yan [ 4 ] for directed graphs. However, their lower bound constructions rely on asymmetry and therefore do not carry over to undirected graphs. At the same time, undirected graphs exhibit additional structure that can be exploited algorithmically. Our results resolve the undirected case by developing new techniques that capture both aspects, yielding tight bounds.

Introduction

Estimation of
Personalized PageRank (PPR)
is a standard tool for ranking vertices in a graph.
Given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices, the PPR
\pi(s,t)
of a target
t\in V
with respect to a source
s\in V
is defined as the probability that an
\alpha
-discounted random walk starting at
s
terminates at
t
.
An
\alpha
-discounted random walk is a random walk which in each step terminates with probability
\alpha
, and otherwise moves to a uniformly random neighbor.
Following prior work, we assume
\alpha
to be a constant
[
13
,
15
]
.
If we choose the source
s
uniformly at random, the probability of terminating at
t
is
\pi(t)=\sum_{s}\pi(s,t)/n
, which is referred to as the
PageRank centrality
of
t
.
This global ranking notion is the quantity studied in the seminal work of Page, Brin, Motwani, and Winograd
[
12
]
in the context of ranking web pages.
These PageRank problems have been extensively studied for undirected graphs, with some of the most important results in
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
14
]
.
PageRank also has numerous applications in information retrieval
[
8
,
12
]
, recommendation systems
[
7
,
9
]
, machine learning
[
2
,
3
]
, and graph partitioning
[
1
,
5
]
.

In this work, we study the computational complexity of estimating
\pi(s,t)
.
Concretely, for an approximation threshold
\delta\in(0,1]
, we require an estimate of
\pi(s,t)
that is within a constant relative error whenever
\pi(s,t)>\delta
, with constant failure probability.
If instead
\pi(s,t)\leq\delta
, we only require an additive error of
O(\delta)
.
We formalize this estimation requirement later in the introduction.
We further consider the following query problems from the litterature:

•

Single pair:
Given a pair
(s,t)\in V^{2}
, estimate
\pi(s,t)
,

•

Single source:
Given a source
s\in V
, estimate
\pi(s,t)
for each target
t\in V
,

•

Single target:
Given a target
t\in V
, estimate
\pi(s,t)
for each source
s\in V
, and

•

Single node:
Given a vertex
t\in V
, estimate
\pi(t)
.

We measure the computational complexity in the standard
adjecency-list model

[
4
,
15
]
, where the algorithm can access the input graph only through an oracle supporting the query
\textnormal{{DEG}}(v)
to obtain the degree of
v
and
\textnormal{{NEIGH}}(v,i)
to obtain the
i
-th neighbor of
v
, each in constant time.
These queries can only be applied to vertices given in the input or returned by previous queries.
Following the literature, we also consider extensions of this model in which the algorithm additionally has access to any subset of the following queries:

\textnormal{{JUMP}}()
which returns a uniformly random vertex
[
13
,
15
]
,

\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}}(v,i)
which returns the
i
-th neighbor of
v
when neighbors are ordered by degree in increasing order
[
4
,
14
]
, and
\textnormal{{ADJ}}(u,v)
which tests whether
uv\in E

[
4
]
.
In the literature, the adjacency-list model with
JUMP
is referred to as the
graph access model

[
15
]
.

We also consider
average-case
complexity, in the following sense.
For the single pair problem, this measure the expected running time when
(s,t)
is drawn uniformly at random from
V^{2}
.
For the single source, single target, and single node problems, this measures the expected running time under a uniformly random choice of the designated source or target vertex, respectively.
Note that the graph is still “worst-case” and that the algorithm must still be correct (with constant probability) for every source/target.

Recently, all of the above problems and model variants were studied for directed graphs, where tight bounds were established in
[
4
]
.
A main goal of this work is to understand how these bounds change in the undirected setting.
In
[
4
]
, they provide lower bounds that exploit asymmetry, and these do not directly carry over to undirected graphs.
In contrast, undirected graphs satisfy the
reversibility property

[
10
]
, which relates
\pi(s,t)
and
\pi(t,s)
, by the identity

\displaystyle d(s)\pi(s,t)=d(t)\pi(t,s),

(1)

where
d(s)
and
d(t)
are the degrees of
s
and
t
, respectively.
This gives additional structure in the undirected case and can be exploited algorithmically.
For instance, it excludes a situation where
\pi(s,t)=\Omega(1)
and
\pi(t,s)=0
, which is possible in directed graphs.

Our result: A complete characterization.

We give tight bounds, up to polylogarithmic factors, for all problems (single pair, single source, single target, and single node) on undirected graphs, in the adjacency-list model with every combination of additional queries from
\{\textnormal{{JUMP}},\allowbreak~\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}},~\textnormal{{ADJ}}\}
.
An overview of all bounds can be found in
Table

1
.
The bounds are stated as functions of the number of vertices
n=\absolutevalue{V}
, number of edges
m=\absolutevalue{E}
, approximation threshold
\delta
, and average degree
d=m/n
.
It should be noted, that some of the known upper bounds have been parameterized in
d(t)
in prior work, but in this work, we do not include this parameter when constructing the lower bounds.
Overall,
Table

1
, gives a complete picture of the computational landscape of (Personalized) PageRank estimation in undirected graphs, and shows how the different query types affect the computational complexity.
This could be important when deciding which queries to support when designing an application programming interface (API) for large graphs.

Lower bounds.

We construct tight lower bounds for all problems and model variants.
Before this work, only basic folklore lower bounds existed
[
4
]
, together with a tight lower bound for the single node problem
[
16
]
in the bare adjacency-list model.
Our lower bounds are inspired by the directed lower bound constructions of
[
4
]
, which in turn are inspired by the directed single node construction of
[
15
]
.
As their constructions are asymmetric, they do not directly carry over to undirected graphs.
For each Personalized PageRank problem, we give an undirected hard instance with tunable parameters that can be set to match the different model variants.
This construction is more complex than in
[
4
]
, since the undirected setting requires additional parameters.

Upper bounds.

We provide four main new upper bounds.
Let us briefly present the novel ideas behind each of them.
Some of them build on existing algorithms, which we modify or combine to get tight bounds.

Single node algorithm [
Theorem

3.1.1
].

The previous best single node algorithm is known as
backwards monte carlo

[
16
]
and runs in
O(m^{1/2})
time. It works by generating
\alpha
-discounted random walks from
t
and then using the reversibility property
1
to get an estimate for
\pi(t)
.
This is tight in all models except when we have access to all queries
JUMP
,
NEIGH-SORTED
, and
ADJ
, in which case, we improve the running time to
O(n^{1/2})
.
This is the first application of this query combination in undirected graphs.
The main observation is that we can treat high-degree and low-degree neighbors of
t
(separated efficiently using
NEIGH-SORTED
) differently.
For low-degree neighbors we can efficiently compute their contribution to
\pi(t)
using backwards monte carlo.
For high-degree neighbors, backwards sampling becomes too costly, and instead we estimate their contribution to
\pi(t)
by moving to a random vertex using
JUMP
and walking from there.
The intuition is that high-degree vertices have a higher probability of being hit by a random walk.
To check if we actually hit a high-degree neighbor, we use the
ADJ
query.

For the average-case single target problem, we provide an algorithm with running time

The novel idea behind the

[

]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\hat{\pi}(s,t)-\pi(s,t)|\geq c\max\{\pi(s,t),\delta\}\right]\leq p_{f}

where

are small constants.

We say that an algorithm solves the

if, for every

, and every

satisfying

for every target

.

.

2
Preliminaries

We denote the underlying (simple) undirected graph by
G=(V,E)
, where
n=|V|
and
m=|E|
.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we let
d(v)
denote its degree,
\mathcal{N}(v)
denote its set of neighbors, and
\mathcal{N}[v]=\mathcal{N}(v)\cup\{u\}
denote the closed neighborhood.
We write
d=m/n
for the average degree.
We use
\delta\in(0,1]
for the approximation threshold of our estimation requirements, and we use
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
notation to hide polylogarithmic factors in
n
and
\delta
.
For any integer
k
, we denote by
[k]
the set of integers
\{1,\dots,k\}
.

2.1
Pagerank and PPR

An
\alpha
-discounted walk starts at a vertex and, at each step, terminates with probability
\alpha
and otherwise moves to a uniformly random neighbor.
The PageRank score of a vertex
v
in an undirected graph
G
, denoted
\pi_{G}(v)
, is the probability that an
\alpha
-discounted random walk starting at a uniform random vertex ends in
v
.
The personalized PageRank (PPR) score of a vertex
v
with respect to
u
in
G
, denoted
\pi_{G}(u,v)
, is the probability that an
\alpha
-discounted random walk starting at
u
ends in
v
.
When
G
is clear from context, we drop the subscript and write
\pi(u,v)
(or
\pi(v)
) instead of
\pi_{G}(u,v)
(or
\pi_{G}(v)
).
Observe that
\pi(v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in V}\pi(u,v)
.
By definition the following equations holds for each
v\in V
:

\displaystyle\pi(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(u)}{d(u)}+\frac{\alpha}{n},

(3)

\displaystyle\pi(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(u,w)}{d(w)}+\alpha\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=v\right\}.

(4)

Since we are considering an undirected graph
G
, the following reversibility property holds
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

For any vertices
u,v\in V
, we have
\pi(u,v)d(u)=\pi(v,u)d(v)
.

Proof.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{u,v}
the set of all paths in
G
from
u
to
v
.
For a path
p=v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k}
we write
\bar{p}=v_{k},v_{k-1},\dots,v_{1}
for the reverse path.
We let
\pi(p)
denote the probability that an
\alpha
-discounted random walk starting at
v_{1}
follows the path
p
and terminates at
v_{k}
.
Then
\pi(p)=\frac{1}{d(v_{1})},\dots\frac{1}{d(v_{k-1})}(1-\alpha)^{k-1}\alpha
, and we have
\pi(p)d(v_{1})=\pi(\bar{p})d(v_{k})
.
From this, it follows that

\displaystyle\pi(u,t)d(u)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{u,v}}\pi(p)d(u)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{u,v}}\pi(\bar{p})d(v)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{v,u}}\pi(p)d(v)=\pi(v,u)d(v).

∎

2.2
Monte Carlo Sampling

Arguably, the simplest way of solving the single source problem is by generating multiple independent
\alpha
-discounted random walks starting at the source vertex, and estimate
\pi(s,u)
for each
u\in V
by the fraction of walks that end at
u
.
This is known as Monte Carlo sampling
[
6
]
.
A small relative error is required only when
\pi(s,u)>\delta
, so standard Chernoff bounds imply that
O(1/\delta)
walks are sufficient to achieve a constant relative error with constant success probability.
Since the expected length of an
\alpha
-discounted walk is
1/\alpha=O(1)
, this method solves the single source problem in time
O(1/\delta)
.
In
Section

A.1
, we give an algorithm for sampling an
\alpha
-discounted walk from a vertex
u
,
\textnormal{{RandomWalk}}(G,u,\alpha)
, in expected time
O(1/\alpha)
.

In a similar way, we can also solve the single target problem by generating multiple independent
\alpha
-discounted random walks starting at the target vertex (known as
backwards Monte Carlo sampling
)
[
16
]
.
This gives estimates
\hat{\pi}(t,u)
of
\pi(t,u)
for every
u\in V
. Using the
reversability property
stated in
Lemma

2.1.1
, this also gives estimates
\hat{\pi}(u,t)=\hat{\pi}(t,u)d(u)/d(t)
of
\pi(u,t)
.
A small relative error is required only when
\pi(u,t)>\delta
, or equivalently when
\pi(t,u)>\delta d(u)/d(t)=\delta^{\prime}
.
Standard Chernoff bounds imply that
O(1/\delta^{\prime})=O(d(t)/\delta)
walks are sufficient to achieve a constant relative error with constant success probability.
The worst-case complexity is
O(d(t)/\delta)=O(n/\delta)
, and averaged over all targets
t
, the complexity is
O(d/\delta)
.

2.3
Local Push

Another paradigm for computing PageRank starts with all probability mass at the target and then uses
Equation

4
to
push
, or distribute, probability mass to neighboring vertices.
This algorithm is known as
BackwardsPush

[
11
]
, and the pseudocode is given in
Algorithm

1
.

BacwardsPush
maintains two values,
p(u)
and
r(u)
, for each vertex
u
.
The
reserve

p(u)
serves as an underestimate of
\pi(u,t)
, and will approach
\pi(u,t)
as we keep pushing probability mass around in the graph.
The
residual

r(u)
is the remaining probability that currently sits at
u
and still needs to be propagated according to
Equation

4
.
When pushing at a vertex
v
, we increment the residual
r(u)
of each neighbor
u
by
(1-\alpha)r(v)/d(u)
, increment the reserve
p(v)
by
\alpha r(v)
and set
r(v)=0
.
This procedure maintains, for each
u\in V
, the invariant

\displaystyle\pi(u,t)=p(u)+\sum_{w\in V}r(w)\pi(u,w).

(5)

graph

reserves

2.4
Power Method

The local pushing algorithm
BacwardsPush

[
11
]
, introduced above, works by pushing only from vertices
v
whose residual exceeds the threshold
r_{\max}
.
If we instead run
BacwardsPush
in a global manner, where we push at every vertex synchronously over
L
rounds, we get the standard
power method
for PageRank
[
12
]
.
Each round takes
O(m)
time, since we push across every edge twice.

Let
r_{i}(v)
be the residual at vertex

after
i
rounds.
In one round, the residual value is scaled by a factor of
(1-\alpha)

r_{i}(u)\leq(1-\alpha)^{i}
for every
u\in V
.
Hence, after
L=\log_{1-\alpha}c\delta=O(\log 1/\delta)
rounds, we have
r_{L}(u)\leq c\delta
for every
u\in V
.
By the same argument as in

, this suffices to solve the single target problem.
The total running time is
O(mL)=\tilde{O}(m)
.

Similarly, one can run a forward variant that starts with all probability mass at the source and applies the same synchronous updates, thereby solving the single source problem in time
\tilde{O}(m)
.

3.1
Single node

In this section, we present an algorithm solving the single node problem that achieves the following upper bound in the adjacency-list model with queries
\textnormal{{JUMP}},~\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}}
, and

.
Note that the

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Consider the adjacency-list model with
JUMP
,
NEIGH-SORTED
, and
ADJ
.
There exists an algorithm solving the single node problem with target
t
in expected time
O(\min\{d(t),n^{1/2}\}=O(n^{1/2})
and, when averaging over all targets
t

O(\min\{d,n^{1/2}\})

Overview.

By
Equation

3

\pi(t)
by estimating
\pi(u)
for each neighbor
u
.
The algorithm treats low- and high-degree neighbors of
t
differently.
Specifically, we fix a threshold
1\leq\tau\leq n
and partition the neighbors into two sets
X_{L}
containing all neighbors with degree at most
\tau
and
X_{H}
containing the remaining neighbors (degree larger than
\tau

The algorithm estimates the contribution of
X_{L}
to
\pi(t)
denoted
\pi_{L}(t)=\sum_{u\in X_{L}}{(1-\alpha)\pi(u)}/{d(u)}
, the contribution of
X_{H}

\pi(t)

\pi_{H}(t)=\sum_{u\in X_{H}}{(1-\alpha)\pi(u)}/{d(u)}
, and then combines this into an estimate
\hat{\pi}(t)=\pi_{L}(t)+\pi_{H}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{n}
of
\pi(t)

To estimate
\pi_{L}(t)

NEIGH-SORTED
to identify the set
X_{L}
without scanning all
d(t)
neighbors.
Because neighbors are sorted by degree, we can find
X_{L}
in time
O(\log d(t))
.
We then generate
\alpha
-discounted random walks, each starting from a random vertex in
X_{L}

\pi_{L}(t)
using the reversibility property of
Equation

1

Using the fact that the degree of each vertex in
X_{L}
is at most
\tau

O(\min\{d(t),\tau\})
random walks to estimate
\pi_{L}(t)
within a constant relative error.
Without any bound on the degree, we would get the same time complexity as in
[
16
]
of
O(\min\{d(t),m^{1/2}\})
.

To estimate
\pi_{H}(t)
it becomes expensive to generate enough random walks from

Specifically, the algorithm repeatedly uses

to pick a uniform source vertex, generates an
\alpha
-discounted random walk, and takes its endpoint
x
.
We then check whether

by using the

d(x)>\tau
.
If
x\in X_{H}
, the sample is included in the estimate of
\pi_{H}(t)
.
Since all vertices in
X_{H}
have degree larger than
\tau
, we can show that
O(n/\tau)
samples suffice to obtain a constant relative error of
\pi_{H}(t)
.

The total running time is
O(\min\{d(t),\tau\}+\log d(t)+n/\tau)
.
Then, if
d(t)\leq n^{1/2}
, we set
\tau=n
, giving a running time of
O(d(t))=O(\min\{d(t),n^{1/2}\})
and if
d(t)>n^{1/2}
, we set
\tau=n^{1/2}
, giving a running time of
O(n^{1/2})=O(\min\{d(t),n^{1/2}\})
.

We will now show this formally.

Analysis.

This section presents the proof of
Theorem

3.1.1
.

Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{w_{L}}
and

be the result of
RandomWalk
on line
5
and line
8
respectively of
Algorithm

3
.
Define the indicator variable
\chi_{i,u}=\mathbbm{1}\left\{u_{i}=u\right\}
for each
u\in V
and
i\in[w_{L}]
, and the indicator variable
\phi_{i,x}=\mathbbm{1}\left\{x_{i}=x\right\}
for each
x\in X_{H}
and
i\in[w_{H}]
.
It is easy to see that we can express
\hat{\pi}(t)
as a sum of these indicator variables

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(t)=\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot|X_{L}|}{n\cdot w_{L}}\sum_{i\in[w_{L}]}\sum_{u\in V}\frac{\chi_{i,u}}{d(u)}+\frac{1-\alpha}{w_{H}}\sum_{i\in[w_{H}]}\sum_{x\in X_{H}}\frac{\phi_{i,x}}{d(x)}+\frac{\alpha}{n}.

We will start by showing that
\hat{\pi}(t)
is an unbiased estimator.

Lemma 3.1.2
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]=\pi(t)
.

Proof.

In each of the
w_{L}
samples, the algorithm first picks a start vertex
x
uniformly at random from
X_{L}
, and then samples an
\alpha
-discounted walk from
x
.
Therefore, for any
u\in V

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{i,u}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[u_{i}=u\right]=\frac{1}{|X_{L}|}\sum_{x\in X_{L}}\pi(x,u)=\frac{1}{|X_{L}|}\sum_{x\in X_{L}}\pi(u,x)\frac{d(u)}{d(x)}.

The last equality follows from
Lemma

2.1.1
.
In each of the
w_{H}
samples, the algorithm generates a random walk from a uniformly random start vertex.
For any
x\in X_{H}
, this sampled random walk terminates at
x
with probability
\pi(x)
, and hence
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{i,x}\right]=\pi(x)
.

Using the above, it follows by linearity of expectation that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\sum_{u\in V}\sum_{x\in X_{L}}\frac{\pi(u,x)}{d(x)}+(1-\alpha)\sum_{x\in X_{H}}\frac{\pi(x)}{d(x)}+\frac{\alpha}{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}(y)}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(x)}{d(x)}+\frac{\alpha}{n}

Then using
Equation

3
it follows that
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]=\pi(t)
.
∎

Next, the following lemma upper bounds the variance of the estimator
\hat{\pi}(t)
returned by the algorithm.

Lemma 3.1.3
.

\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]\leq\left(\frac{|X_{L}|}{nw_{L}}+\frac{1}{w_{H}\tau}\right)(1-\alpha)\pi(t)

Proof.

Since the two groups of random walks are generated independently, we have that the
\chi
’s and
\phi
’s are independent of each other. This implies that

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)|X_{L}|}{nw_{L}}\right)^{2}\text{Var}\left[\sum_{i\in[w_{L}]}\sum_{u\in V}\frac{\chi_{i,u}}{d(u)}\right]+\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{w_{H}}\right)^{2}\text{Var}\left[\sum_{i\in[w_{H}]}\sum_{x\in X_{H}}\frac{\phi_{i,x}}{d(x)}\right].

Considering a pair of two different indicator variables
(\chi_{i,u},\chi_{i^{\prime},u^{\prime}})
, if
i\neq i^{\prime}
they are independent, and otherwise they are negatively correlated, hence
\text{Cov}\left(\chi_{i,u},\chi_{i^{\prime},u^{\prime}}\right)\leq 0
.
This also holds for the
\phi

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]\leq\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)|X_{L}|}{nw_{L}}\right)^{2}\sum_{i\in[w_{L}]}\sum_{u\in V}\frac{\text{Var}\left[\chi_{i,u}\right]}{d(u)^{2}}+\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{w_{H}}\right)^{2}\sum_{i\in[w_{H}]}\sum_{x\in X_{H}}\frac{\text{Var}\left[\phi_{i,x}\right]}{d(x)^{2}}.

Each of the
\chi
’s and
\phi
’s are Bernoulli variables and therefore
\text{Var}\left[\chi_{i,u}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{i,u}\right]
and
\text{Var}\left[\phi_{i,x}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\phi_{i,x}}\right]
.
Using the expression of
\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{i,u}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{i,x}\right]
found in the proof of
Lemma

3.1.2
, we further get

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}|X_{L}|}{n^{2}w_{L}}\sum_{u\in V}\sum_{x\in X_{L}}\frac{\pi(u,x)}{d(x)d(u)}+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{w_{H}}\sum_{x\in X_{H}}\frac{\pi(x)}{d(x)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\alpha)|X_{L}|}{nw_{L}}\sum_{x\in X_{L}}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(x)}{d(x)}+\frac{(1-\alpha)}{w_{H}}\sum_{x\in X_{H}}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(x)}{d(x)^{2}}

Finally, recall that
d(x)>\tau
for each
x\in X_{H}
, so using
Equation

3

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{|X_{L}|}{nw_{L}}+\frac{1}{w_{H}\tau}\right)(1-\alpha)\pi(t),

which finishes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove the theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1.1
.

We will set
w_{L},w_{H}
and
\tau
so that
\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(t)\right]\leq(c\pi(t))^{2}p_{f}
. Then, since by
Lemma

3.1.2
the estimator is unbiased, we can use Chebyshev’s inequality, and we get that
\mathbb{P}\left[|\hat{\pi}(t)-\pi(t)|\geq c\pi(t)\right]\leq p_{f}
, hence solving the single node problem.

By
Lemma

3.1.3
, it suffices to set
w_{L}\geq\frac{2(1-\alpha)|X_{L}|}{nc^{2}\pi(t)p_{f}}
and
w_{H}\geq\frac{2(1-\alpha)}{\tau c^{2}\pi(t)p_{f}}
.
However, we do not know the value of
\pi(t)
at the beginning of the algorithm.
To alleviate this, we will derive a lower bound of
\pi(t)
, so we need not know it.
We have

\displaystyle\pi(t)\geq\sum_{x\in X_{L}}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(x)}{d(x)}+\frac{\alpha}{n}\geq|X_{L}|\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha}{\tau n}+\frac{\alpha}{n}\geq\max\left\{|X_{L}|\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha}{\tau n},\frac{\alpha}{n}\right\},

where the first inequality comes from
Equation

3
.
Using this lower bound and that
|X_{L}|\leq d(t)
, we set

\displaystyle w_{L}=\min\left\{\frac{2\tau}{c^{2}\alpha p_{f}},\frac{2(1-\alpha)d(t)}{c^{2}\alpha p_{f}}\right\}=O\left(\min\left\{\tau,d(t)\right\}\right),

and

\displaystyle w_{H}=\frac{2(1-\alpha)n}{\tau c^{2}\alpha p_{f}}=O\left(\frac{n}{\tau}\right),

and note that these satisfy the wished inequalities.

Now we will argue how to set
\tau
to get the claimed running time.
First of all, to find
|X_{L}|
by using the
NEIGH-SORTED

|X_{L}|
in
O(\log d(t))
using binary search.
Then, combining this with the sampling process, the total running time is

\displaystyle O(\log d(t)+w_{H}+w_{L})=O\left(\log d(t)+\min\{\tau,d(t)\}+\frac{n}{\tau}\right).

Clearly, setting
\tau=\Theta(n^{1/2})
suffices to achieve a worst-case running time of

However, we can sometimes get a better running time by doing the following.
If

then we set
\tau=n
, giving a running time of
O(d(t))=O(\min\{d(t),n^{1/2}\})
.
Otherwise, we set
\tau=n^{1/2}
as before, giving a running time of
O(\sqrt{n})=O(\min\{d(t),n^{1/2}\})
.
Finally, averaging over all targets, this running time is
O(\min\{d,n^{1/2}\})
.

3.2
Single target worst-case

In this section, we will present an algorithm solving the single target problem, achieving the following upper bound in the model with
NEIGH-SORTED
.

Theorem 3.2.1
.

Consider the adjacency-list model with
NEIGH-SORTED
.
There exists an algorithm solving the single target problem with target
t
in expected time
O((d(t)n/\delta)^{1/2})=O(n/\delta^{1/2})
and, when averaging over all targets
t
, expected time
O((m/\delta)^{1/2})
.

Overview.

The algorithm will use a modified version of
RandPush

[
14
]
described in
Section

2.5
.
In each push of
RandPush
at a vertex
u
, we increase the estimate
\hat{p}(u)
by at least
\theta
, where
\theta
is the push threshold.
Since
\hat{p}(u)
is always an underestimate of
\pi(u,t)
in expectation, the expected number of pushes is
O\left(\sum_{u}\frac{\pi(u,t)}{\theta}\right)=O(n\pi(t)/\theta)
, which also bounds the expected running time due to the randomized pushing.
Setting
\theta=O(\delta)
solves the single target problem, as mentioned in
Section

2.5
.
As we can have
n\pi(t)=\Omega(d(t))
, this gives an expected running time of
O(d(t)/\delta)
, which is not the running time that we opt for.
Moreover, we cannot set
\theta=o(\delta)
, as this would cause the additive error to be larger than
\delta
.

Instead, we begin with a phase where we push deterministically as long as there exists a vertex
u
with a large residual
r(u)
, that is, when
r(u)>\tau
, where
\tau\in(0,1)
is a predefined threshold.
This will accelerate the running time, as we will quickly distribute large chunks of probability mass, instead of performing many small pushes.
Once this phase terminates, we will perform the remaining pushes using the standard randomized pushing with push threshold
\delta
.

Considering the running time, the deterministic phase costs at most
O(d(t)/\tau)
, which is the worst-case running time of
BacwardsPush

[
11
]
.
After this, we know that for each
u
the current estimate satisfies
\pi(u,t)-\hat{p}(u)\leq\tau
.
It then follows that, in expectation,
RandPush
will perform at most
\tau/\delta
pushes at each vertex, resulting in a running time of
O(n\tau/\delta)
.
Balancing both phases, by setting
\tau=(d(t)\delta/n)^{1/2}
, results in an expected running time of
O((d(t)n/\delta)^{1/2})
.

Algorithm.

The pseudocode is given in
Algorithm

4
.
We start with
BacwardsPush
with
r_{\max}
, which returns all the reserve and residual values.
We then run
RandPush
with a push threshold of
\theta
, and it will run for
L
rounds.
These parameters will be set in later proofs.

Algorithm 4

Input:
Undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, target vertex
t\in V
, stopping probability
\alpha
, residual threshold
r_{\max}
, number of pushing rounds
L
, push threshold
\theta
.

Output:
Dictionary for estimates
\hat{p}()
.

1:

p(),r()\leftarrow\texttt{BacwardsPush}(t,r_{\max})

2:

\hat{p}()\leftarrow\textnormal{{RandPush}}(t,L,\theta)
initialized with
\hat{p}_{0}(),\hat{r}_{0}()\leftarrow p(),r()

3:

return

\hat{p}()

Analysis.

We let
p_{i}(u)
and
r_{i}(u)
be the deterministic version of
\hat{p}_{i}(u)
and
\hat{r}_{i}(u)
of the
RandPush
algorithm, that is, the reserve and residual we get if we push
\Delta_{i+1}(u,v)
to every neighbor
u
.
To show that our estimate satisfies the requirement of
Equation

2
, we want to show that the actual reserve and residual values, i.e.
\hat{p}_{i}(u)
and
\hat{r}_{i}
, do not deviate too far from the “ideal” deterministic version.
This basically follows from the below lemma, which is shown in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

The following statements hold for the
RandPush
algorithm,

(1)

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{p}_{i}(u)\right]=p_{i}(u)
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,L\}
,

(2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{r}_{i}(u)\right]=r_{i}(u)
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,L\}
, and

(3)

\text{Var}\left[\hat{p}(u)\right]\leq L\theta p(u)
.

Furthermore, in the normal
RandPush
all
r_{0}(u)
and
p_{0}(u)
is set to
0
except
r_{0}(t)
, so it is no longer clear what
p_{i}(u)
represents.
Normally,
p_{i}(u)=\pi^{i}(u,t)
where
\pi^{i}(u,t)
is the probability than an
\alpha
-discounted walk starting at
u
terminates at
t
after exactly
i
steps.
However, we know that the deterministic reserves and residuals correspond to
BacwardsPush
, and using the invariant given in
Equation

5
, we get

\displaystyle\pi(u,t)

\displaystyle=p(u)+\sum_{v\in V}\pi(u,v)r_{L}(v)

Using this, we start by showing that setting
L=\log_{1-\alpha}(s)
rounds only introduces a
s
additive error.

Lemma 3.2.3
.

For any
s>0
, if
L=\log_{1-\alpha}(s)
then
\pi(u,t)-s\leq p(u)\leq\pi(u,t)

Proof.

We start by showing that
r_{i}(v)\leq(1-\alpha)^{i}
for all
v
using induction over
i
.
For
i=0
,
r_{i}(v)\leq 1
is clearly true.
Consider any vertex
v
.
It receives residual mass from each neighbor in the
(i-1)
’th round, and we have

\displaystyle r_{i}(v)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\frac{(1-\alpha)r_{i-1}(w)}{d(v)}\leq(1-\alpha)^{i}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\frac{1}{d(v)}=(1-\alpha)^{i}.

After
L
steps we have
r_{L}(v)\leq(1-\alpha)^{L}=s
, implying that

\displaystyle\pi(u,t)

\displaystyle=p(u)+\sum_{v\in V}\pi(u,v)r_{L}(v)\leq p(u)+s.

Thus,
\pi(u,t)-s\leq p(u)\leq\pi(u,t)
.
∎

We are now ready to show
Theorem

3.1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.2.1
.

We will show that setting

\displaystyle\theta=c^{2}\delta p_{f}/(4L),~\text{and}~L=\log_{1-\alpha}(c\delta/2)

suffices for the algorithm to solve the single target problem.
We will set
r_{\max}
later to achieve the stated running time.
By
Lemma

3.2.3
, we have
\pi(u,t)-c\delta/2\leq p(u)\leq\pi(u,t)
for each
u\in V
.
Then by the triangle inequality
|\hat{p}(u)-\pi(u,t)|\leq|\hat{p}(u)-p(u)|+c\delta/2
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle p_{u}=\mathbb{P}\left[|\hat{p}(u)-\pi(u,t)|\geq c\max\{\delta,\pi(u,t)\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[|\hat{p}(u)-p(u)|+c\delta/2\geq c\max\{\delta,\pi(u,t)\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[|\hat{p}(u)-p(u)|\geq(c/2)\max\{\delta,\pi(u,t)\}\right].

Recall that from
Lemma

3.2.2
we have that
\text{Var}\left[\hat{p}(u)\right]\leq\theta Lp(u)
.
Then Chevyshev’s inequality,
Lemma

3.2.2
and
p(u)\leq\pi(u,t)
implies that

\displaystyle p_{u}\leq\frac{4\theta Lp(u)}{c^{2}\max\{\delta,\pi(u,t)\}^{2}}\leq\frac{\delta p(u)p_{f}}{\max\{\delta,\pi(u,t)\}^{2}}\leq p_{f}.

This finishes the first part of the proof.

Note that the
BacwardsPush
phase runs in time
O(d(t)/r_{\max})
.
For the remaining part of the proof, we will try to upper bound the running time of the randomized pushing phase.
As noted in
[
14
]
, conditioned on
\{\hat{r}_{i}\}
the push operation at
v
in round
i
can be implemented in expected time
O\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\Delta_{i+1}(u,v)/\theta+1\right)
.
Thus, we can upper bound the expected time complexity of the randomized pushing phase as follows, where
N
is the expected number of times we have a non-zero residual,

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{i+1}(v,u)\right]+N\right).

(6)

Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{r}_{i+1}(v)\mid\{\hat{r}_{i}\}\right]=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\Delta_{i+1}(v,u)
, as
v
receives an expected residual increment from each neighbor
u
of
\Delta_{i+1}(v,u)
, implying together with
Lemma

3.2.2
that
r_{i+1}(v)=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{r}_{i+1}(v)\right]=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{i+1}(v,u)\right]
.
Then for the first term of
Equation

6
we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{i+1}(v,u)\right]=\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{v\in V}r_{i+1}(v)=\frac{1}{\theta\alpha}\sum_{v\in V}(p(v)-p_{0}(v)).

(7)

It follows from the invariant given in
Equation

5
maintained by
BacwardsPush
, that for each
u\in V
we have
\pi(u,t)\geq p_{0}(u)\geq\pi(u,t)-r_{\max}
, and hence

\displaystyle p(v)-p_{0}(v)\leq p(v)-\pi(u,t)+r_{\max}\leq r_{\max}.

Applying this to
Equation

7
shows that the first term of
Equation

6
is upper bounded by
O(nr_{\max}/(\theta\alpha))=\tilde{O}(nr_{\max}/\delta)
.
Now we are only missing to bound
N
.
For
i>0
we only push at
v
when
\hat{r}_{i}(v)\geq\theta
.
When
i=0
, there are at most
O(d(t)/r_{\max})
non-zero residuals, as the running time of
BacwardsPush
upper bounds the number of pushes and hence number of non-zero residuals.
Thus,

\displaystyle N\leq O\left(\frac{d(t)}{r_{\max}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{v\in V}\frac{\hat{r}_{i+1}(v)}{\theta}\right]=O\left(\frac{d(t)}{r_{\max}}\right)+\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{v\in V}r_{i+1}(v)=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{d(t)}{r_{\max}}+\frac{nr_{\max}}{\delta}\right).

The second-to-last equality follows from
Lemma

3.2.2
and the last equality follows from above (note that the sum-term appears in
Equation

7
).
Thus, the final expected time complexity is
O\left(\frac{d(t)}{r_{\max}}+\frac{nr_{\max}}{\delta}\right)
, and setting
r_{\max}=(d(t)\delta/n)^{1/2}
, yields a time complexity of
O((nd(t)/\delta)^{1/2})
, finishing the proof.
∎

3.3
Single target average-case

In this section, we will describe an algorithm
BackwardsPushAvg
with the following properties

Theorem 3.3.1
.

Consider the adjacency-list model.
There exists an algorithm solving the single target problem with target
t
in expected time
O(d(t)/\delta)
and, when averaging over all targets
t
, expected time
O(\min\{d/\delta,(1/\delta)^{2}+d\})
.

Considering instead the adjacency-list model with
NEIGH-SORTED
, there exists an algorithm solving the single target problem in expected time
O(\min\{d/\delta,\allowbreak(1/\delta)^{2}\})
, averaged over all targets.

Overview.

The algorithm is a modified version of the standard
BacwardsPush

[
11
]
.
Instead of starting with all probability mass at the target
t
, we will distribute the probability mass among the neighbors of
t
, but ignoring all neighbors with degree larger than
O(1/\delta)
.
In that way, on average, we avoid a lot of pushes on these expensive high-degree vertices, reducing the asymptotic time complexity.
We show that this only introduces an additive error of at most
O(\delta)
.
Specifically, we show that the worst-case running time is
O(h_{t}/\delta+d(t))
where
h_{t}
is the number of neighbors of
t
with degree at most
O(1/\delta)
.
The
d(t)
comes from the initial scan of neighbors to distribute the probability mass.
Importantly,
\sum_{t}h_{t}\leq O(n/\delta)
since each vertex can contribute at most
O(1/\delta)
to this sum, giving an average running time of
O((1/\delta)^{2}+d)
.
Note that the worst-case time complexity is the same as
BacwardsPush
, as a target
t
can have
h_{t}=d(t)
, resulting in a time complexity of
O(d(t)/\delta)
.
Furthermore, if the
NEIGH-SORTED
is available, then we can distribute the initial probability mass in time
h_{t}
, and we can remove the
d
term from the average time complexity.

Algorithm.

The pseudocode of
BackwardsPushAvg
is given in
Algorithm

5
.
The input is a target vertex
t\in V
and a residual threshold
r_{\max}
, which will be set in later proofs.
Let
Y\subseteq\mathcal{N}(t)
be the subset of neighbors of
t
with degree at most
1/r_{\max}
.
Furthermore, let
X=\mathcal{N}(t)\setminus Y
.
Then for each
x\in X
we have
d(x)>1/r_{\max}
.
We will now run the
BacwardsPush
algorithm, but with the initialization slightly changed.
Instead of setting
r(t)=1
and all other values to zero, we set
p(t)=\alpha
and
r(y)=(1-\alpha)/d(y)
for each
y\in Y
and all other values is set to zero.
We then run
BacwardsPush
with push threshold
r_{\max}
.

Analysis.

Below, we let
p(u)
and
r(u)
denote the final reserve and residual value.
We will start by showing that the following invariant is maintained, where the proof is given in
Section

A.2
.

Lemma 3.3.2
.

For the target vertex
t
, the push operation in
BackwardsPushAvg
algorithm maintain the following invariant for each
u\in t
:

\displaystyle\pi(u,t)=p(u)+\sum_{v\in V}\left(r(v)+\frac{1-\alpha}{d(v)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\in X\right\}\right)\pi(u,v).

We have by the following Lemma that the final estimate
p(u)
is within a
O(r_{\max})
additive error of
\pi(u,t)
.

Lemma 3.3.3
.

\pi(u,t)-2r_{\max}\leq p(u)\leq\pi(u,t)
.

Proof.

For each vertex
v\in V
if
v\in X
then
d(x)>1/r_{\max}
and also
r(v)\leq r_{\max}
implying that

r(v)+\frac{1-\alpha}{d(v)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\in X\right\}\leq r_{\max}+(1-\alpha)r_{\max}\leq 2r_{\max}
.
Using this, we have that

\displaystyle p(u)

\displaystyle=\pi(u,t)-\sum_{v\in V}\left(r(v)+\frac{1-\alpha}{d(v)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\in X\right\}\right)\pi(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\pi(u,t)-2r_{\max}\sum_{v\in V}\pi(u,v)

\displaystyle=\pi(u,t)-2r_{\max}.

∎

We are now ready to prove the theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3.1
.

By
Lemma

3.3.3
, setting
r_{\max}=c\delta/2
, given an additive error of at most
c\delta
therby solving the single target problem.
For the remaining part of this proof, we will argue about the running time.

First of all, we can upper bound
p(u)
as follows:

\displaystyle p(u)

\displaystyle=\pi(u,t)-\sum_{v\in V}\left(r(v)+\frac{1-\alpha}{d(v)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\in X\right\}\right)\pi(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\pi(u,t)-\sum_{x\in X}\frac{1-\alpha}{d(x)}\pi(u,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{(1-\alpha)\pi(u,y)}{d(y)}+\alpha\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=t\right\}

\displaystyle(\text{By \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:rec-target}})

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\alpha)}{d(u)}\sum_{y\in Y}\pi(y,u)+\alpha\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=t\right\}.

\displaystyle(\text{By \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:reverse}})

For a vertex
u
, we denote by
R(u)=p(u)/\alpha-\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=t\right\}
the total amount of residual pushed from
u
to its neighbors.
Whenever we push at a vertex
u
, we have
r(u)>r_{\max}
, and after pushing, we set
r(u)=0
.
Therefore the number of pushes at
u
is upper bounded by
R(u)/r_{\max}
.
We can then upper bound the running time as follows,

\displaystyle O\left(\sum_{u\in V}\frac{R(u)}{r_{\max}}d(u)\right)=O\left(\sum_{u\in V}\frac{p(u)-\alpha\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=t\right\}}{\alpha r_{\max}}d(u)\right)

Using the above upper bound of
p(u)
, we further get

\displaystyle O\left(\sum_{u\in V}\frac{p(u)-\alpha\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=t\right\}}{\alpha r_{\max}}d(u)\right)=O\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)}{\alpha r_{\max}}\sum_{y\in Y}\sum_{u\in V}\pi(y,u)\right)=O\left(\frac{|Y|}{r_{\max}}\right).

Note that
O(|Y|/r_{\max})=O(d(t)/r_{\max})
.

For the average-case running time (when averaging over all targets), we combine two bounds.
First, the running time is at most
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\in V}O(d(t)/\delta)=O(d/\delta)
.
Secondly, we bound the running time by applying a charging argument.
For the
\Theta(|Y|/\delta)
random walks sampled at
t
, each vertex
y\in Y
is charged a cost of
\Theta(1/\delta)
. A vertex
u
is charged at most
d(u)
times, and only when
d(u)\leq\Theta(1/\delta)
, so its total charge is at most
O((1/\delta)^{2})
.
Thus, the average-case running time is
O(d+(1/\delta)^{2})
, by also adding the average-cost of the neighboring search.
Combining the two bounds give
O(\min\{d/\delta,(1/\delta)^{2}+d\})
.
Note that if the
NEIGH-SORTED
operation is available, then we can do the neighboring search in time
|Y|
, and then the average-case running time is simply
O(\min\{d/\delta,(1/\delta)^{2})\}
.
∎

3.3.1
Extending to single pair average case.

In this section, we will describe an algorithm
BiPPRAvg
with the following running time.

Theorem 3.3.4
.

Consider the adjacency-list model.
There exists an algorithm solving the single pair problem in expected time
O((1/\delta)^{2/3}+d)
, averaged over all targets.

Considering instead the adjacency-list model with
NEIGH-SORTED
and
ADJ
, there exists an algorithm solving the single pair problem in expected time
O((1/\delta)^{2/3})
, averaged over all targets.

We note that in
[
4
]
, they also give an
O((1/\delta)^{2/3})
algorithm in the model with
NEIGH-SORTED
and
ADJ
.
However, our algorithm achieves the corresponding bound with a substantially simpler design and analysis.
Moreover, we show that for undirected graphs, the same running time can be obtained even without these additional queries, up to an additive
d
term

Overview.

The bidirectional estimator given in
[
10
]
combines the backwards-push algorithm with Monte Carlo sampling.
Using the same idea for our new
BackwardsPushAvg
algorithm gives the stated running times.

Algorithm.

BiPPRAvg
first runs
BackwardsPushAvg
with target
t
and push threshold
r_{\max}
computing reserves
p()
and residuals
r()
.

BiPPRAvg
then modifies all residuals for each
x\in X
by updating
r(x)\leftarrow r(x)+(1-\alpha)/d(v)
.
Then it follows from
Lemma

3.3.2
that for each
u\in V
:

\displaystyle\pi(u,t)=p(u)+\sum_{v\in V}r(v)\pi(u,v).

(8)

From
Equation

8
, we use Monte Carlo sampling to construct an estimator
\hat{\pi}(s,t)
of
\pi(s,t)
.
Specifically, we will generate
n_{r}

\alpha
-discounted random walks starting at
s
using the
RandomWalk
subroutine, and let
u_{i}
be the end vertex of the
i
random walk for each
i\in[n_{r}]
.
We then construct the bidirectional estimator as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(s,t)=p(s)+\frac{1}{n_{r}}\sum_{i\in[n_{r}]}\sum_{v\in V}r(v)\mathbbm{1}\left\{v=u_{i}\right\}.

(9)

The pseudocode is given in
Section

A.1
.

Analysis.

We start by bounding the residual values as follows.

Lemma 3.3.5
.

r(v)<2\cdot r_{\max}
for each
v\in V
.

Proof.

After running
BacwardsPush
we have that
r(v)\leq r_{\max}
for each
v\in V
.
Then for each
x\in X
, we add
(1-\alpha)/d(x)<(1-\alpha)r_{\max}\leq r_{\max}
, and hence the residual value is less than
2\cdot r_{\max}
at each vertex.
∎

This is an unbiased estimator with variance bounded as follows.

Lemma 3.3.6
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\pi}(s,t)\right]=\pi(s,t)
and
\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(s,t)\right]\leq 2r_{\max}\pi(s,t)/n_{r}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\left\{v=u_{i}\right\}\right]=\pi(s,v)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\pi}(s,t)\right]=p(s)+\sum_{v\in V}r(v)\pi(u,v)=\pi(s,t),

where the last equality follows from
Equation

8
.
The variance can be bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{r}^{2}}\sum_{i\in[n_{r}]}\sum_{v\in V}r(v)^{2}\cdot\text{Var}\left[\mathbbm{1}\left\{v=u_{i}\right\}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n_{r}}\sum_{v\in V}r(v)^{2}\pi(s,v).

Using
Lemma

3.3.5
, we further get

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2r_{\max}}{n_{r}}\sum_{v\in V}r(v)\pi(s,v)\leq\frac{2r_{\max}}{n_{r}}\pi(s,t).

∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.3.4
.

From
Lemma

3.3.6
, setting
n_{r}=2r_{\max}/(c^{2}\delta p_{f})=O(r_{\max}/\delta)
, results in a variance upper bounded by
\text{Var}\left[\hat{\pi}(s,t)\right]\leq c^{2}\delta\pi(s,t)p_{f}
.
Then, by Chebyshev’s inequality, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\hat{\pi}(s,t)-\pi(s,t)|\geq c\max\{\pi(s,t),\delta\}\right]\leq\frac{c^{2}\delta\pi(s,t)p_{f}}{c^{2}\max\{\pi(s,t),\delta\}^{2}}\leq p_{f},

thereby solving the single pair problem.

By
Theorem

3.3.1
, the average-case running time of
BackwardsPushAvg
is
O((1/r_{\max})^{2}+d)
.
Correcting the residuals can be done in
O(d(t))
time or
O(d)
averaged over all targets, and the Monte Carlo part runs in
O(r_{\max}/\delta)
time in expectation.
We can balance the two terms
(1/r_{\max})^{2}
and
r_{\max}/\delta
by setting
r_{\max}=\delta^{1/3}
, resulting in a average running time of
O((1/\delta)^{2/3}+d)
.

Lastly, we will describe how to get rid of this
d
term in the running time if the
NEIGH-SORTED
and
ADJ
queries are available.
By
Theorem

3.3.1
, the
NEIGH-SORTED
query removes the
d
term from the running of the
BackwardsPushAvg
algorithm.
To remove the
d
term incurred by correcting the residuals so that
Equation

8
is satisfied, we observe that we do not need to update the residuals for each
x\in X
before the Monte Carlo phase.
Instead, if we end up at vertex
u_{i}
, we can check whether
u_{i}\in X
by using the
ADJ
query to check if
u_{i}\in\mathcal{N}(t)
and by checking that

.
This gives the exact same estimate
\hat{\pi}(s,t)
for each
u
, but the average expected running time is improved to
O((1/\delta)^{2/3})
.
∎

Theorem 3.4.1
.

]

[

]
.

4
Lower bounds

In this section, we present our lower bounds.
See
Table

1
for an overview.
The lower bounds are inspired by
[
4
]
, and are similarly based on a reduction of a decision problem to PageRank estimation.
We construct two graphs that only differ by a few edges, but on which
\pi(s,t)
differs significantly.
Any algorithm that can estimate
\pi(s,t)
within the required accuracy must therefore be able to distinguish between the two graphs.
The following lemma describes this reduction.

Lemma 4.0.1
.

Let
G
be a graph containing vertices
s
and
t
.
Let
\hat{\pi}(s,t)\in[0,1]
be an estimate of
\pi(s,t)
satisfying
\absolutevalue{\hat\pi(s,t)-\pi(s,t)}\leq c\max\{\pi(s,t),\delta\}
.
Then,

•

\hat{\pi}(s,t)\leq c\delta
if
\pi(s,t)=0
, and

•

\hat{\pi}(s,t)>c\delta
if
\pi(s,t)>2c\delta
.

Proof.

We assume
c<\frac{1}{2}
.
If
\pi(s,t)=0
, the claimed inequality follows by substituting
\pi(s,t)
by zero in the assumed inequality.
If
\pi(s,t)>2c\delta
, we have
\pi(s,t)-\hat{\pi}(s,t)\leq\absolutevalue{\hat\pi(s,t)-\pi(s,t)}\leq c\max\{\pi(s,t),\delta\}<\frac{1}{2}\pi(s,t)
, so
\hat{\pi}(s,t)>\frac{1}{2}\pi(s,t)>c\delta
.
∎

To obtain our lower bounds from the above lemma, it suffices to construct graph classes that are hard to distinguish, but on which
\pi(s,t)
differs significantly.
We will do this by hiding an important edge in a graph using a
swap
, which only modifies the graph locally, but significantly alters the PPR.
Let us first define this swap.

Definition 4.0.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with a fixed order on the neighbors of each vertex.
For each
q=(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})\in V^{4}
, write
E^{-}_{q}=\{\{q_{1},q_{2}\},\{q_{3},q_{4}\}\}
,
E^{+}_{q}=\{\{q_{1},q_{3}\},\{q_{2},q_{4}\}\}
, and
E_{q}^{\pm}=E_{q}^{-}\cup E_{q}^{+}
.
A quadruple
q\in V^{4}
is
swappable
if
E^{-}_{q}\subseteq E
and
E^{+}_{q}\subseteq\binom{V}{2}\setminus E
.
For a swappable quadruple
q\in V^{4}
, write
G_{q}
for the graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
(E\setminus E^{-}_{q})\cup E^{+}_{q}
, inheriting the orders of vertex neighbors from
G
, placing edges of
E^{+}_{q}
at indices where edges of
E^{-}_{q}
were.
For a set
Q\subseteq V^{4}
of swappable quadruples, define
\mathcal{G}(G,Q)=\{G\}\cup\{G_{q}\mid q\in Q\}
.

See
Figure

1
for an illustration of a swap.
We now lower bound the number of queries required to detect whether a swap has occurred.
In the following lemma, the input is either
G
or
G
with a single quadruple in
Q
swapped, and the algorithm must decide which is the case.

Lemma 4.0.3
.

Consider the adjacency-list model augmented with
U\subseteq\{\textnormal{{JUMP}},\allowbreak\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}},\allowbreak\textnormal{{ADJ}}\}
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
Q\subseteq V^{4}
a set of swappable quadruples.
If
\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}}\in U
, assume that
d(q_{1})=d(q_{4})
and
d(q_{2})=d(q_{3})
for all
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})\in Q
.

Fix an ordered list
W
of at least one vertex in
V
.
If
\textnormal{{JUMP}}\in U
, let
V_{W}=V
, and otherwise, let
V_{W}\subseteq V
be the union of components in
G
intersecting
W
.
If
\textnormal{{ADJ}}\in U
, let
E_{W}=\binom{V_{W}}{2}
, and otherwise, let
E_{W}=\binom{V_{W}}{2}\cap E
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm which, when given a graph
H\in\mathcal{G}(G,Q)
and the list
W
of some of its vertices, produces an output
\mathcal{A}(H,W)
.
Assume that
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}(G,W)=\mathcal{A}(G_{q},W)\right]\leq 2p_{f}
for all
q\in Q
.
Then, the expected query complexity of
\mathcal{A}
on
(G,W)
is
\Omega(\absolutevalue{Q}/K)
, where
K=\max_{e\in E_{W}}\absolutevalue{\{q \in Q \mid e \in E^\pm_q\}}
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
be the set of edges and other size-2 vertex sets queried by
\mathcal{A}
when given
(G,W)
.
Note that if
S\cap E_{q}^{\pm}=\emptyset
for some
q\in Q
, then the query/answer sequence of
\mathcal{A}
is identical on
(G,W)
and
(G_{q},W)
(for the same random bits), hence
\mathcal{A}(G,W)=\mathcal{A}(G_{q},W)
.
To see that
NEIGH-SORTED
behaves the same in both graphs, note that the degree of no vertex changes when going from
G
to
G_{q}
, and that when e.g.
q_{1}
has its neighbor
q_{2}
replaced by
q_{3}
, this new neighbor
q_{3}
takes the place of
q_{2}
in the degree-sorted adjacency list of
q_{1}
, assuming appropriate manual tiebreaking, since we assume
d(q_{2})=d(q_{3})
when
\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}}\in U
.
Taking probabilities,
\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E_{q}^{\pm}=\emptyset\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}(G,W)=\mathcal{A}(G_{q},W)\right]\leq 2p_{f}
for all
q\in Q
.

Note that
S\subseteq E_{W}
, since the algorithm can only visit nodes in
V_{W}
and hence only query pairs in
E_{W}
.
We have
\absolutevalue{\{q \in Q \mid S \cap E^\pm_q \neq\emptyset\}}\leq\sum_{e\in S}\absolutevalue{\{q \in Q \mid e \in E^\pm_q\}}\leq K\absolutevalue{S}
, where the last inequality uses
S\subseteq E_{W}
.
Taking expectations yields
\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\{q \in Q \mid S \cap E^\pm_q \neq\emptyset\}}\right]\leq K\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{S}\right]
.
On the other hand, since
\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E_{q}^{\pm}=\emptyset\right]\leq 2p_{f}
for all
q\in Q
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\{q \in Q \mid S \cap E^\pm_q \neq\emptyset\}}\right]=\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E^{\pm}_{q}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq(1-2p_{f})\absolutevalue{Q}
.
Combining these bounds gives
\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{S}\right]\geq(1-2p_{f})\absolutevalue{Q}/K=\Omega(\absolutevalue{Q}/K)
.
∎

To apply

together with a large set of swappable quadruples

In our model it is not even necessary to randomly permute the labels of

in

.
This maximum is non-zero only for edges

between

and

and between

and

.
Since

, we get a lower bound of
\Omega(x)
.
We finish the proof for this model by choosing

, and noting that
x\leq d
and
x\delta\leq 1
as required.

Let us now consider the adjacency-list model with
JUMP
and
ADJ
.
We add two independent vertices to

and for each

, we subdivide the edges

and
\{q_{B},q_{C}\}
in

using these two new vertices.
We still have

and
\pi_{G_{q}}(s,t)\geq\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\alpha}{2x}\geq 2c\delta
for all
q\in Q
, if we require
x\delta\leq 1
when choosing
x
.
The proof of
Lemma

4.0.3
and thus
Lemma

4.1.1
carries through analogously with these subdivisions.
The algorithm can find one of the two new vertices using

, but this requires
\Omega(n)
queries in expectation, which is more than the lower bound we wish to prove.
So if we assume that the algorithm runs in time

on

When determining

in

4.0.3
, we can thus remove the two new vertices from

When doing so, the halves of the subdivided edges do not appear in

We finish as above by setting

.
∎

We first combine
Lemma

4.0.1
and
Lemma

4.0.3
to obtain a general lemma for proving lower bounds for the single source problem.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm solving the single source problem with approximation threshold
\delta
in the adjacency-list model augmented with
U\subseteq\{\textnormal{{JUMP}},\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}},\textnormal{{ADJ}}\}
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
Q\subseteq V^{4}
a set of swappable quadruples.
If
\textnormal{{NEIGH-SORTED}}\in U
, assume that
d(q_{1})=d(q_{4})
and
d(q_{2})=d(q_{3})
for all
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})\in Q

Let
s\in V
be a vertex such that for all
q\in Q

\pi_{G}(s,t)=0
and
\pi_{G_{q}}(s,t)>2c\delta
.

If
\textnormal{{JUMP}}\in U

V_{W}=V
, and otherwise, let
V_{W}\subseteq V
be the union of components in
G
containing
s

If
\textnormal{{ADJ}}\in U

E_{W}=\binom{V_{W}}{2}
, and otherwise, let
E_{W}=\binom{V_{W}}{2}\cap E
.

.
Then, the expected query complexity of
\mathcal{A}
on
G
with source

is
\Omega(\absolutevalue{Q}/K)
.

Proof.

The lower bound follows from
Lemma

4.0.3
with
W=(s)
if we show
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}(G,W)=\mathcal{A}(G_{q},W)\right]\allowbreak\leq 2p_{f}
for all
q\in Q
.
For each
q\in Q
, let
t_{q}\in V
be a vertex such that
\pi_{G}(s,t_{q})=0
and
\pi_{G_{q}}(s,t_{q})>2c\delta
, which exists by assumption.
For each
H\in\mathcal{G}(G,Q)
and

, let
\hat{\pi}_{H}(s,t_{q})
be the estimate of
\pi_{H}(s,t_{q})
produced by
\mathcal{A}
on
(H,W)
.
Then for each
q\in Q
, the event
\mathcal{A}(G,W)=\mathcal{A}(G_{q},W)
implies that
\hat{\pi}_{G}(s,t_{q})=\hat{\pi}_{G_{q}}(s,t_{q})
, so either
\hat{\pi}_{G}(s,t_{q})>c\delta
or
\hat{\pi}_{G_{q}}(s,t_{q})\leq c\delta
.
Both of the latter two events happen with probability at most
p_{f}
by
Lemma

4.0.1
, since
\mathcal{A}
solves the single source problem.
By a union bound,
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}(G,W)=\mathcal{A}(G_{q},W)\right]\leq 2p_{f}
.
∎

We now prove the lower bound for the single source problem in the adjacency-list model with all query types available by applying

4.2.1
to the hard instance used in the proof of

4.1.3

This gives the following average-case lower bound, which is tight even for worst-case analysis.

Theorem 4.2.2

and

We assume

Lemma 4.4.1
.

Let
