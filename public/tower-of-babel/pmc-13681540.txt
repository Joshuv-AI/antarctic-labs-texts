The ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module bifurcates into two pathways to coordinately regulate leaf angle and thermotolerance in maize
Authors: Jing Wang, Yurong Xie, Xueli An, Yingyue Wen, Ziwei Chu, Yilin Jiang, Liping Su, Panqi Qiu, Zejian Li, Xu Tang, Yan Long, Yingwei Qu, Shuangshuang Liu, Dexin Kong, Yong Zhang, Xiangyuan Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global warming increasingly threatens agricultural productivity, necessitating the development of new crop varieties that combine high yield potential with climate resilience. Optimizing plant architecture, particularly by reducing leaf angle (LA), enables dense planting and enhances light capture, yet the mechanisms that coordinate architectural improvement with heat tolerance remain largely unknown. Here, we identify the microRNA ZmMIR319A as a key regulator of both LA and thermotolerance in maize. ZmMIR319A increases LA by post-transcriptionally repressing the Teosinte branched1/Cycloidea/PCF (TCP) transcription factors ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44, thereby relieving their suppression of the auxin transporter genes ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 and stimulating parenchyma cell proliferation in the ligular region. Under heat stress, rapid downregulation of ZmMIR319A leads to the accumulation of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 proteins, which repress the heat shock chaperone genes ZmHSP70-4 and ZmHSP90-5 in leaves to modulate thermotolerance while continuing to restrain ZmPIN1/8-mediated growth. Thus, the maize ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module bifurcates into two regulatory pathways that coordinately govern LA through auxin transport and thermotolerance through heat shock protein signaling. Notably, ZmMIR319A-knockout mutants exhibit increased grain yield under high-density planting conditions despite reduced thermotolerance, highlighting a trade-off between architectural optimization and stress adaptation. Collectively, our findings reveal that a single regulatory module simultaneously controls plant architecture and stress adaptation, thereby providing a dual-target strategy for the molecular breeding of maize varieties with ideal plant architecture and climate resilience.

Introduction

The global population is projected to reach 9–11 billion by 2050, necessitating a 50%–70% increase in food, feed, and biofuel production using limited resources (Gerland et al., 2014). This challenge is compounded by climate change, with global average temperatures predicted to rise by 1.4°C–5.8°C by the end of the century (Williams et al., 2007), exacerbating heat stress (HS) during plant reproductive stages and causing substantial yield losses. A 1°C increase in temperature is estimated to reduce the yields of wheat, rice, maize, and soybean by 6%, 3.2%, 7.4%, and 3.1%, respectively (Zhao et al., 2017), and extreme heat events such as the 2003 European heatwave and the 2012 US drought have already caused maize yield losses of up to 36% in Italy and 13% in the US (Ciais et al., 2005; Chung et al., 2014). Maize, the world’s most widely cultivated crop, is highly sensitive to HS, which causes protein denaturation and disrupts membrane integrity (Yang et al., 2023). Therefore, identifying key regulators of thermotolerance is essential for the development of climate-resilient maize varieties. Plants have evolved sophisticated physiological and molecular responses to high-temperature stress, including the induction of heat shock proteins (HSPs), activation of stress-responsive transcription factors, remodeling of membrane lipids, and modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and phytohormone signaling (Jacob et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020, 2024; Kan et al., 2025; Zuo et al., 2025). Epigenetic regulation and microRNAs (miRNAs) have also emerged as key components of HS responses (Ohama et al., 2017). However, the regulatory circuits governing thermotolerance in maize remain poorly understood, constraining the development of climate-resilient varieties. Moreover, how plant architecture and thermotolerance are coordinated at the molecular level remains largely unclear.

Increasing the planting density of crops is an effective strategy for boosting yield per unit area, with maize planting density in the US increasing from ∼30,000 to ∼70,000 plants ha−1 between the 1930s and 2010, contributing significantly to a sevenfold increase in yield (Mansfield and Mumm, 2014; Haarhoff and Swanepoel, 2018). Similar practices are used in rice, wheat, and soybean (Qu et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2026). A key determinant of adaptability to high-density planting is plant architecture, particularly leaf angle (LA), the angle between the leaf blade and stem. Upright leaves with smaller LAs can reduce shading, improve light penetration, and enhance photosynthetic efficiency, as demonstrated by the maize liguleless2 (lg2) mutation, which confers up to 40% higher yield compared with wild-type (WT) plants (Pendleton et al., 1968), and by the rice OsDWARF4 and maize lac1 mutants, which show similar advantages under dense planting (Sakamoto et al., 2006; Tian et al., 2024). LA is determined by the ligular region (LR) at the blade–sheath boundary, where mechanical support provided by the sclerenchyma promotes leaf erectness and asymmetric parenchyma expansion increases LA (Kong et al., 2017; Cao et al., 2022). LR development is regulated by phytohormones, including auxin, brassinosteroids, abscisic acid, and cytokinins (Tsiantis et al., 1999; Kir et al., 2015; Cao et al., 2022), but the upstream regulators integrating these signals remain incompletely characterized. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms that coordinate architectural optimization with thermotolerance is crucial for meeting the dual challenges of climate change and food security.

Among the many miRNA regulatory modules implicated in plant development and stress responses, the miR319–Teosinte branched1/Cycloidea/PCF (TCP) module stands out as a potential regulator of both processes. The miR319 family is an ancient and highly conserved miRNA lineage, with members identified in nearly all land plants, from mosses to angiosperms (Palatnik et al., 2003). Its primary targets are TCP transcription factors, which function as key regulators of plant development and stress responses (Martin-Trillo and Cubas, 2010). The miR319–TCP module has been extensively characterized for its pleiotropic roles. In Arabidopsis, miR319-mediated repression of TCPs promotes leaf curling and alters lamina morphology (Palatnik et al., 2003; Koyama et al., 2010); in rice, it regulates tillering and panicle branching (Wang et al., 2021); and in wheat, miR319 has been implicated in the regulation of plant architecture and grain yield (Jian et al., 2022; Hao et al., 2024). In addition to TCPs, miR319 also targets other genes, including members of the GAMYB, bHLH, and WOX families, thereby influencing cell wall formation, secondary metabolism, and thermotolerance in rice and other cereals (Hou et al., 2020; Jian et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2026). Notably, miR319 expression is responsive to temperature cues in multiple species (Yang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2018; Lan et al., 2023), and its involvement in oxidative and salt stress tolerance has also been documented (Shi et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2024). However, whether this module simultaneously governs LA, a trait critical for adaptation to high-density planting, and HS tolerance in maize remains unknown.

We therefore selected miR319 for functional investigation in maize, reasoning that its documented roles in development and stress responses across species may reflect a conserved role in growth–stress coordination. We found that the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module coordinates plant architecture and thermotolerance through two distinct downstream pathways. Under normal conditions, this module governs LA through a ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44–ZmPIN1/8 regulatory axis that promotes parenchyma cell division in the LR. Under HS, downregulation of ZmMIR319A relieves the repression of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 by ZmMIR319A, leading to enhanced ZmTCP5/44-mediated repression of ZmHSP70-4 and ZmHSP90-5, thereby establishing a parallel regulatory pathway for thermotolerance. Our findings reveal how a single regulatory module integrates temperature signals to balance plant growth and stress adaptation, offering a dual-target strategy for breeding climate-resilient maize varieties suited for high-density planting.

Results

ZmMIR319A promotes maize leaf angle and functions redundantly with ZmMIR319C and ZmMIR319D

The maize genome contains four members of the ZmMIR319 family (ZmMIR319A–D), which are clustered into two distinct subclades, with ZmMIR319B and ZmMIR319D forming one subclade and ZmMIR319A and ZmMIR319C forming the other (Supplemental Figure 1). These four genes exhibited distinct expression patterns across maize tissues. ZmMIR319A and ZmMIR319C were highly expressed in leaves and LRs, whereas ZmMIR319D showed the highest transcript abundance in ligule tissue (Supplemental Figure 2A). Notably, the expression levels of all four genes were higher in younger, light-shielded LR tissues than in older LR tissues. This trend was observed for both primary transcripts and mature miR319 (Supplemental Figures 2B–2D).

To investigate the role of ZmMIR319A in regulating maize LA, we first generated knockout mutants of ZmMIR319A (ZmMIR319A-KO) using CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing and obtained seven homozygous mutant lines after sequence verification. Among them, ZmMIR319A-KO1 and ZmMIR319A-KO2, harboring 9- and 3-bp deletions, respectively, in the primary ZmMIR319A region and 8- and 3-bp truncations, respectively, in the mature miR319 sequence, were selected for further investigation (Supplemental Figures 3A–3C). Concurrently, we generated seven independent ZmMIR319A overexpression lines (ZmMIR319A-OE1 to -OE7). Among them, ZmMIR319A-OE1 and -OE2, which showed 27.4- and 12.8-fold increases in precursor transcript levels, respectively, compared with the WT (Supplemental Figures 3D and 3E), were selected for further investigation. Phenotypic analysis revealed that seedlings from the two ZmMIR319A-KO lines exhibited substantially smaller LAs than the WT, whereas seedlings from the two ZmMIR319A-OE lines exhibited substantially larger LAs (Supplemental Figure 4). The trends observed in maize seedlings were consistent with those in adult plants under field conditions. At the tasseling stage, the LAs of the three leaves directly above the primary ear were significantly reduced in the two ZmMIR319A-KO lines but increased in the two ZmMIR319A-OE lines compared with the WT (Figures 1A and 1B; Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). Analysis of transverse LR sections revealed that LR thickness was significantly reduced in the two ZmMIR319A-KO lines but markedly increased in the two ZmMIR319A-OE lines compared with the WT (Figure 1C). Similar changes were observed in the proportion of parenchyma tissue and number of parenchyma cells in the LR (Supplemental Figure 5C), whereas no significant difference in the size of individual parenchyma cells was observed between the WT, ZmMIR319A-KO, and ZmMIR319A-OE lines (Supplemental Figure 5D). Analysis of longitudinal sections of the LR from the second leaves of V3-stage plants showed no significant differences in parenchyma cell length among the three genotypes (Supplemental Figures 5E and 5F). Together, these results indicate that ZmMIR319A primarily increases LA by promoting cell division and increasing the proportion of parenchyma tissue in the LR, thereby increasing overall LR thickness.

To assess functional redundancy among ZmMIR319 family members, we generated individual knockout mutants of ZmMIR319B, ZmMIR319C, and ZmMIR319D (Supplemental Figures 6 and 7). Field observations revealed that the ZmMIR319B-KO lines exhibited no significant change in LA compared with the WT, whereas both the ZmMIR319C-KO and ZmMIR319D-KO single mutants exhibited reduced LA (Figure 1D and 1E; Supplemental Figures 7D and 7E). To further investigate genetic interactions among ZmMIR319 family members, we generated higher-order mutants through double (A×B, A×C, and A×D) and triple (A×C×D) crosses. The ZmMIR319A/C/D triple mutant displayed nearly upright leaves. The LAs of the ZmMIR319A/C and ZmMIR319A/D double mutants were intermediate between those of the triple mutant and the ZmMIR319A/B double mutant (Figures 1D and 1E; Supplemental Figures 7D and 7E). Collectively, these results indicate that ZmMIR319A plays a predominant role in regulating LA among its family members, whereas ZmMIR319C and ZmMIR319D function partially redundantly with ZmMIR319A.

ZmMIR319A knockout increases grain yield under high-density planting conditions

To investigate the role of ZmMIR319A in grain yield, we cultivated the ZmMIR319A-KO and ZmMIR319A-OE lines in the field under different planting densities, including 45 000, 90 000, and 135 000 plants per hectare (plants ha−1). Under low planting density (45 000 plants ha−1), ZmMIR319A-KO lines exhibited reduced ear and kernel width and length, lower hundred-kernel weight, fewer kernels per plant, and decreased grain yield per plant and per hectare compared with WT plants. In contrast, ZmMIR319A-OE lines displayed slightly longer ears, increased kernel width and length, higher hundred-kernel weight, more kernels per plant, and increased grain yield per plant and per hectare relative to WT plants, although most of these differences were not statistically significant (Figure 2; Supplemental Figure 8). However, under high planting density (particularly at 135 000 plants ha−1), ZmMIR319A-KO lines showed larger ears, increased kernel width and length, more kernels per plant, higher hundred-kernel weight, and higher grain yield per plant and per hectare compared with both WT and ZmMIR319A-OE plants (Figure 2; Supplemental Figure 8). ZmMIR319A targets TCP family members in maize

miR319 regulates plant growth and development by repressing multiple transcription factors, including AtTCP2, which functions in Arabidopsis leaf morphogenesis (Palatnik et al., 2003); TaPCF8, which regulates plant height in wheat (Jian et al., 2022); OsGAMYB, which regulates tillering in rice (Wang et al., 2021); and PtoTCP20, which regulates secondary vascular development in poplar (Hou et al., 2020). To identify candidate targets of ZmMIR319A involved in the regulation of maize LA, we performed computational prediction using psRNATarget (www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/), which identified at least 12 candidate genes, including multiple ZmTCPs and ZmMYBs, along with ZmPIN8 and ZmCDK8. Among these, ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, and ZmTCP44 contain target sites that are perfectly complementary to the mature miR319 sequence (Supplemental Figure 9). We further compared their transcript levels in the LR of ZmMIR319A knockout and overexpression lines. ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, and ZmTCP44 were significantly upregulated in ZmMIR319A-KO1 but downregulated in ZmMIR319A-OE1 compared with the WT (Figure 3A), which indicates that they are putative targets of ZmMIR319A. To verify these regulatory relationships, we constructed GFP-based reporter vectors for ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, and ZmTCP44 (Figure 3B) and co-transfected maize protoplasts with each reporter and either a ZmMIR319A overexpression construct or an empty vector. Co-expression of ZmMIR319A with GFP-tagged ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, or ZmTCP44 resulted in a significant reduction in fluorescence at 24 h and an almost complete loss of fluorescence at 48 h, whereas the corresponding controls retained strong GFP signals (Figure 3C). To investigate the biological functions of ZmMIR319A target genes that regulate LA, we generated knockout mutants of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 using CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing, as well as overexpression lines expressing GFP-tagged versions of each gene (Supplemental Figures 10A–10E and 11A–11E). Field observations revealed that the two ZmTCP5-KO lines exhibited significant increases in LA1–LA3 compared with the WT, resembling the phenotype of ZmMIR319A-OE plants (Figures 3D and 3E; Supplemental Figure 10F). In contrast, the two ZmTCP5-OE lines showed markedly reduced LAs, mirroring the phenotype of ZmMIR319A-KO plants (Figures 3D and 3E; Supplemental Figure 10F). Similar results were also observed in the ZmTCP44-KO and ZmTCP44-OE lines (Figures 3D and 3E; Supplemental Figure 11F). These reciprocal phenotypic relationships indicate that ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 function downstream of ZmMIR319A and have important roles in regulating maize leaf architecture. In addition, both ZmTCP5-KO lines showed significantly reduced plant and ear height, whereas these effects were not observed in ZmTCP44-KO lines (Figure 3D), which suggests that ZmTCP5 has a broader role in regulating plant architecture beyond LA.

ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 suppress the expression of ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 in maize

Emerging evidence indicates that diverse phytohormones participate in regulating LA formation (Cao et al., 2022). To determine whether ZmMIR319A is involved in phytohormone networks that control LA, we performed transcriptome profiling using light-shielded and developing LR tissues in WT, ZmMIR319A-KO1, and ZmMIR319A-OE1 plants at the V9 stage (Supplemental Figure 12A). Pairwise comparisons identified 6080 differentially vexpressed genes (DEGs) (p < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1; Supplemental Data 1) among WT, ZmMIR319A-KO1, and ZmMIR319A-OE1 (Supplemental Figure 12B). In the three pairwise comparisons (WT versus ZmMIR319A-KO1, WT versus ZmMIR319A-OE1, and ZmMIR319A-KO1 versus ZmMIR319A-OE1), 944, 1138, and 1058 DEGs were upregulated, whereas 868, 696, and 1079 DEGs were downregulated, respectively (Supplemental Figure 12C). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis identified terms related to the plasma membrane, protein kinase activity, carbohydrate metabolism, secondary cell wall biogenesis, and auxin signaling, among others (Supplemental Figure 12D).

To further explore the transcriptomic landscape, we examined the expression profiles of ZmTCP family members and known LA regulators. Among these, multiple predicted ZmMIR319A targets, including ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, and ZmTCP44, were significantly upregulated in ZmMIR319A-KO1 and downregulated in ZmMIR319A-OE1 relative to the WT (Supplemental Figure 13A), consistent with their post-transcriptional repression by ZmMIR319A (Figure 3C). Notably, other TCP family members displayed varied expression patterns, which suggests that only a subset of TCPs is responsive to ZmMIR319A-mediated regulation. Importantly, ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, ZmPIN1, and ZmPIN8 were co-expressed in LR tissues (Supplemental Figures 13A and 13B), consistent with their functional interaction in LA regulation. Beyond TCPs, several genes implicated in ligule and auricle development (LG1, LG2, and RS2), mechanical tissue formation (lignin and cellulose biosynthesis genes), and gravitropic responses (ZmLAZY1) also exhibited significant expression changes among the three genotypes (Supplemental Figure 13A), which indicates that ZmMIR319A may modulate LA through coordinated transcriptional reprogramming of multiple developmental and hormonal pathways.

Among the DEGs, auxin-related components were prominently represented. At least 190 auxin-associated genes, including the auxin transporter genes ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 and auxin response factor-encoding genes, showed altered transcript levels (Supplemental Figures 13B–13E). Quantitative real-time PCR (real-time qPCR) confirmed that both ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 were downregulated in ZmMIR319A-KO1 but upregulated in ZmMIR319A-OE1, exhibiting expression patterns opposite to those of ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, and ZmTCP44 (Figure 3A; Supplemental Figure 13F). Given that auxin is a well-established driver of cell proliferation in the LR, we hypothesized that ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 may serve as key downstream effectors of the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP module in controlling LA. To test whether ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 are direct targets of ZmMIR319A, we performed co-transfection assays in maize protoplasts. However, unlike ZmTCPs, ZmPIN8 did not show reduced GFP fluorescence upon ZmMIR319A co-expression (Supplemental Figure 14), which indicates that ZmPIN8 is not a direct cleavage target.

We therefore investigated whether ZmMIR319A regulates ZmPIN genes through its direct targets, such as ZmTCPs. Promoter analysis revealed conserved TCP-binding elements (5′-G/TGGNCCA/C-3′) in the promoters of ZmPIN1–4 and ZmPIN8 (Supplemental Figure 15A). To test whether ZmTCPs bind to these five promoters, we cloned ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 into the pJG4-5 vector and the promoters of the PIN genes into the pLacZi2μ vector for yeast one-hybrid assays. ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 bound to the promoters of ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 (Figure 4A; Supplemental Figure 15B). Field observations revealed that the two ZmPIN8-KO lines exhibited significantly reduced LAs, whereas the two ZmPIN8-OE lines displayed markedly increased LAs compared with WT plants (Figures 4F and 4G; Supplemental Figure 16F), phenotypes opposite to those observed in the ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 knockout and overexpression lines. Collectively, these data establish that ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44, which act downstream of ZmMIR319A, directly repress the expression of ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8, thereby linking the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP module to auxin transport-mediated regulation of maize LA.

ZmMIR319A and its targets ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 have opposite regulatory effects on thermotolerance

Given that LA is a key determinant of plant architecture and light capture, we investigated whether its developmental regulators also contribute to environmental adaptation, particularly under HS, which frequently accompanies high-density cultivation in warming climates. To investigate the potential role of ZmMIR319A in thermotolerance, we first analyzed its expression under high-temperature conditions. In maize seedlings exposed to HS, the primary transcript levels of all ZmMIR319 members and the level of mature miR319 clearly declined, with ZmMIR319A, ZmMIR319C, and mature miR319 showing the greatest reductions, followed by gradual recovery (Supplemental Figures 17A and 17B). In contrast, the transcript levels of several previously predicted target genes of ZmMIR319, especially ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, and ZmTCP44, were significantly increased after HS treatment (Supplemental Figures 17C–17H). These rapid and opposite responses to HS establish ZmMIR319A as an early heat-responsive regulator. Its downregulation serves as a switch that reprograms downstream TCP activity, shifting TCP function from developmental regulation to the stress response.

To test this hypothesis, we compared the thermotolerance of V2 seedlings from ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 knockout and overexpression lines after HS treatment. Discussion

Global food security is challenged by population growth and climate change. Optimization of crop architecture is a key strategy for enhancing yield through high-density planting, particularly in the context of global warming. Although the independent regulation of plant architecture and environmental resilience has been increasingly studied, the coordinated mechanisms linking these traits remain largely unknown. Our study reveals that the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module does not simply govern two parallel processes; rather, it coordinates two distinct physiological outputs in response to temperature signals. This coordination originates from the temperature-sensitive expression of ZmMIR319A, which serves as the primary regulatory switch. Under optimal conditions, high ZmMIR319A levels maintain low levels of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 proteins, thereby permitting auxin-mediated LA enlargement and limiting ZmTCP5/44-mediated repression of HSP genes. Under HS, rapid downregulation of ZmMIR319A leads to accumulation of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 proteins. The regulatory activity of these transcription factors then bifurcates: in the LR, they continue to suppress ZmPIN1 and ZmPIN8 expression to restrain leaf expansion; concurrently, in leaf tissues, they directly repress the heat-inducible chaperone genes ZmHSP70-4 and ZmHSP90-5, thereby attenuating activation of the heat shock response (Figure 7).Figure 7Working model of the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module in the coordinated regulation of leaf angle and thermotolerance in maize.Under normal conditions, high ZmMIR319A expression results in low levels of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 proteins. The trade-off between architectural optimization and thermotolerance is apparent when examining the reciprocal phenotypic relationships across different genetic backgrounds. ZmMIR319A-OE plants exhibit enhanced thermotolerance but looser leaf architecture (i.e., larger LAs), whereas ZmMIR319A-KO plants display compact leaf architecture (i.e., smaller LAs) but compromised thermotolerance. This reciprocal relationship demonstrates that optimizing one trait comes at the expense of the other. At the molecular level, ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 function as shared repressors of both downstream pathways. Under HS, their accumulation simultaneously suppresses ZmPIN1/8 (thereby limiting growth) and ZmHSP70/90 (thereby attenuating the heat shock response). Therefore, the same repressors that restrain growth during stress also dampen full defense activation, revealing an inherent physiological trade-off. This mechanism establishes a direct link between plant architecture and thermotolerance: the same thermal signal, acting through ZmMIR319A and the transcriptional repressors ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44, coordinately adjusts both canopy architecture and cellular resilience. This “stimulus-coupled bifurcation” represents an efficient strategy for balancing growth and stress adaptation to optimize plant fitness in fluctuating environments. Consequently, manipulating the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 regulatory module through molecular design, such as by combining ZmPIN knockout with ZmHSP overexpression, offers a promising route for accelerating the development of maize varieties suited to dense planting and with enhanced heat tolerance.

ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44: An integrated regulatory module for growth–stress coordination

TCP transcription factors are known to participate in the shade avoidance response, thermotolerance, and leaf development. In this study, we reveal how these pre-existing functions are orchestrated by an upstream environmental regulator, ZmMIR319A, to achieve a balanced output. Unlike static pathways, the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module operates as a conditionally bifurcating regulator: under favorable temperatures, it promotes growth via auxin signaling, whereas HS redirects the same TCP repressors to restrain growth and modulate the heat shock response. This stimulus-dependent reprogramming of a common set of transcription factors is a resource-efficient strategy for balancing adaptive trade-offs and represents a mechanistic model for trait integration through centralized regulatory nodes.

Our ChIP–qPCR data demonstrate enhanced occupancy of ZmTCP44 at the promoters of ZmPIN1/8 and ZmHSP70-4/90-5 under HS (Figures 4C and 6C). Notably, ZmTCP44 occupancy at the promoters of ZmHSP70-4 and ZmHSP90-5 was enhanced under HS (Figure 6C). This enhanced binding is likely attributable to at least two factors. First, HS downregulates ZmMIR319A transcripts and upregulates ZmTCP44 transcripts (Supplemental Figures 17A and 17H), leading to increased ZmTCP44 protein accumulation and consequently greater availability for promoter binding. Second, HS may also modulate the intrinsic DNA-binding affinity of ZmTCP44, as has been observed for other transcriptional regulators in plants (Ohama et al., 2017; Kan et al., 2025). However, our EMSAs did not detect a substantial increase in the DNA-binding activity of MBP-ΔTCP44 following temperature pretreatment (Supplemental Figure 21D), which suggests that increased protein abundance, rather than a change in intrinsic affinity, is the primary driver of the increased occupancy observed in vivo. Nevertheless, given that EMSA provides only semi-quantitative information on binding, a more precise investigation of the relative contributions of protein abundance and binding affinity would require complementary biophysical approaches, such as surface plasmon resonance or thermal shift assays. Overall, our data unequivocally demonstrate that HS enhances the in vivo occupancy of ZmTCP44 at its target promoters, providing direct molecular evidence for the temperature-sensitive regulatory dynamics of this module.

Although definitive genetic evidence from double mutants or epistasis analyses is currently lacking, multiple lines of evidence support a hierarchical regulatory relationship. First, the expression patterns of ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, and their targets (ZmPIN1/8 and ZmHSP70-4/90-5) show reciprocal changes in the knockout and overexpression backgrounds (Figures 4E and 6E). Second, the phenotypic concordance between the ZmMIR319A-OE and ZmTCP5/44-KO lines (both exhibiting larger LAs and enhanced thermotolerance) and between the ZmMIR319A-KO and ZmTCP5/44-OE lines (both exhibiting smaller LAs and reduced thermotolerance) further supports their genetic interaction (Figures 1, 3, and 5; Supplemental Figure 19). Third, biochemical data from Y1H, EMSA, ChIP–qPCR, and dual-luciferase assays establish that ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 directly regulate ZmPIN1/8 and ZmHSP70-4/90-5 (Figures 4A–4D and 6A–6E). We are currently generating a ZmTCP5/ZmTCP44 double mutant and will analyze its phenotypes in future studies to investigate the potential functional redundancy between these two duplicated homologs.

Our model of the ZmMIR319A–ZmTCP5/44 module is currently based on a set of validated downstream targets (ZmPIN1/8 and ZmHSP70-4/90-5). Although the combined biochemical, molecular, and genetic evidence provides a coherent mechanistic framework, we acknowledge that the integrative role of this module is inferred from these characterized targets. Future genome-wide analyses, such as ChIP sequencing or DAP-seq, will be valuable for defining the full regulatory networks of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 and further characterizing their roles in coordinating growth and stress responses.

An emerging regulatory network for maize architecture and stress tolerance

Beyond miR319–TCP, other miRNA families also regulate leaf architecture or HS responses in maize and rice, collectively forming a complex regulatory network that integrates developmental and environmental signals. For leaf architecture, miR156, miR166, and miR390 are key regulators. In rice, miR156–IPA1 modulates plant architecture via auxin-related pathways (Jiao et al., 2010). In maize, miR166 defines the expression domain of the HD-ZIPIII family member rld1, whereas miR390 guides AGO7 to trigger TAS3 ta-siRNA biogenesis and restricts miR166 to demarcate leaf abaxial identity (Juarez et al., 2004; Douglas et al., 2010). These miRNAs primarily function in developmental programming rather than rapid environmental responses. In HS responses, multiple miRNAs have been implicated in modulating thermotolerance through distinct regulatory modules. Transcriptomic analyses in various plant species, including maize, have identified heat-responsive miRNAs such as miR169, miR159, miR156, and miR160, which are generally downregulated under HS. Functional modules involving these miRNAs, including miR169–SBP, miR159–MYB, miR156–SPL, miR164–NAC, miR166–HD-ZIP, and miR396–GRF, have been reported to participate in HS regulation (Stief et al., 2014; Rao et al., 2022). For instance, miR156 targets SPL genes and has been shown to regulate HS memory in Arabidopsis (Stief et al., 2014), whereas miR169 targets NF-YA and is consistently downregulated under HS across cereals (Rao et al., 2022). Knockdown of Fa-miR159 and Fa-miR160 reprograms development and enhances heat tolerance in tall fescue, revealing their coordinated roles in balancing growth and thermotolerance (Lou et al., 2026). miR319 itself is also heat-responsive and has been shown to regulate thermotolerance in creeping bentgrass through the miR319/bHLH094 module by modulating auxin biosynthesis and signaling (Dong et al., 2026).

Compared with these pathways, the miR319–TCP module is distinctive within this emerging network because it concurrently regulates LA under normal conditions and modulates heat tolerance under stress through a shared switch (miR319) and common targets (ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44). Unlike miR166 and miR390, which specialize in developmental patterning, miR319–TCP responds dynamically to thermal cues. Unlike miR159, miR160, and miR169, which predominantly function in stress-specific pathways, miR319–TCP integrates leaf architecture with stress adaptation. This dual functionality, achieved through a single module that bridges growth and stress responses, highlights the module’s role in linking developmental and stress-related signals within the broader regulatory network that governs plant architecture and environmental resilience.

Our findings also provide new insights into TCP-mediated regulation of maize plant architecture. A recent study identified ZmTCP23, a transcription factor not targeted by miR319, as a regulator of leaf and tassel branch angles through modulation of the ABA signaling pathway (Yang et al., 2025). This finding identifies a phytohormonal pathway that responds to developmental cues. In contrast, our study elucidates an miRNA-guided post-transcriptional circuit centered on auxin transport and HSP chaperones that directly responds to leaf development and HS cues. These findings indicate that maize employs parallel, non-redundant regulatory pathways, including the ZmTCP23–ABA and miR319–ZmTCP5/44–auxin–ZmHSP pathways, to fine-tune canopy structure and stress responses, underscoring the agronomic importance of robust architectural control and providing an integrated strategy for achieving high and stable maize yields under variable environments.

Beyond its role in leaf development, the ZmPIN8 homolog in Arabidopsis has been shown to function in male gametophyte development, with mutant studies revealing a role for auxin transport in regulating pollen development and function (Dal Bosco et al., 2012; Ding et al., 2012). In contrast, our study demonstrates that maize ZmPIN8 is expressed in the LR and functions in LA regulation. This species-specific functional divergence highlights the functional diversity of PIN family members across different developmental and physiological contexts.

ZmMIR319 is a pleiotropic regulator with translational potential

The regulatory repertoire of ZmMIR319 extends well beyond abiotic stress and development. A recent study identified the ZmMIR319–ZmMYB74 module as a positive regulator of maize resistance to Fusarium stalk rot (Cao et al., 2026). Upon pathogen infection, ZmMIR319 expression suppresses ZmMYB74, a transcriptional repressor, thereby relieving repression of the lignin biosynthesis gene ZmCAD and enhancing cell wall fortification (Cao et al., 2026). ZmMYB74 also participates in endosperm cell division and proliferation and regulates endosperm development and grain size in maize (Wang et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings establish ZmMIR319 as a key pleiotropic regulator that modulates multiple agronomic traits in maize by targeting distinct transcription factors and activating phytohormonal signaling pathways. This multifunctionality reflects an evolutionarily conserved paradigm. The miR319a–TCP4 module interacts with WRKYs to regulate defense against fungal disease in Brassica rapa (Dong et al., 2021), and it also regulates plant architecture in rapeseed (Lu et al., 2023). This recurring recruitment of miR319 into diverse stress-response networks across species highlights its fundamental role as a versatile signaling component, consistent with the evolutionary conservation of miRNA regulatory programs in plant development (Luo et al., 2013).

As more evidence emerges, the unified working model of ZmMIR319 will need to be updated. We propose that ZmMIR319 acts as an important regulatory node that processes diverse endogenous and environmental signals. Its expression is tuned by upstream factors sensitive to developmental stage, temperature, and pathogen elicitors. In response to these signals, ZmMIR319 selectively represses a repertoire of target transcription factors (such as ZmTCPs, which function in development and heat responses, and ZmMYB74, which functions in disease resistance); these transcription factors act as gatekeepers of specific downstream pathways. This working model enables coordinated and resource-efficient restructuring of plant architecture under biotic and abiotic stresses. From an evolutionary standpoint, co-opting a central, fast-acting post-transcriptional regulator such as miR319 to manage multiple traits demonstrates an adaptive advantage during plant evolution. It enables rapid and synchronized adaptation to concurrent stresses, such as combinations of heat and pathogen pressure commonly encountered in field environments, by coordinately adjusting plant architecture, protein homeostasis, physiological processes, and physical barriers. From a translational perspective, the ZmMIR319-mediated regulatory module provides a promising target for multi-trait genetic engineering. Molecular design-driven manipulation of this module, such as by fine-tuning ZmMIR319A expression or generating functional allelic variants of its direct or indirect target genes through gene editing, holds promise for pyramiding multiple desirable traits, including dense-planting architecture, thermotolerance, and disease resistance. This strategy offers a potential avenue to circumvent the yield–resilience trade-offs that constrain conventional crop breeding.

To further validate the in vivo functionality of the ZmTCP44-GFP fusion protein, we have crossed the ZmTCP44-KO mutant with the ZmTCP44-OE line to generate complemented lines in the knockout background. These lines will provide further evidence for the biological activity of the fusion protein in future studies. Nevertheless, it is important to contextualize the field trial results presented in this study. The field trials were conducted in Sanya (18.2°N, 109.1°E) during the winter season, with average growing temperatures of approximately 18°C–25°C, conditions that are relatively cool compared with the summer HS conditions typical of major maize-producing regions. Although this represents a limitation of the current study, the mechanistic basis of the growth–thermotolerance trade-off was rigorously established through controlled HS assays and transcriptomic analyses. To further validate yield performance under warmer conditions, we are conducting additional field trials in Beijing during the summer season, when temperatures frequently exceed 35°C, and at other warm-season locations during upcoming growing seasons.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The X249 inbred line was used as the genetic background for all maize (Zea mays) materials in this study. We generated overexpression lines driven by the maize Ubiquitin promoter for ZmMIR319A, ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, ZmPIN8, ZmHSP70-4, and ZmHSP90-5 and CRISPR–Cas9-mediated knockout mutants for ZmMIR319A–D, ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, and ZmPIN8 following the methods of An et al. (2020). All transgenic and genome-edited lines were validated by sequencing. For gene cloning and expression pattern analysis, X249 plants were grown in a growth chamber under a 16-h light (28°C)/8-h dark (22°C) photoperiod, with a light intensity of 300 μmol m−2 s−1 and approximately 60% relative humidity. For phenotypic analyses, X249 plants were grown either in the growth chamber or under field conditions at experimental stations in Beijing (China; 40°30′ N, 116°37′ E) and Sanya (Hainan Province, China; 18°37′ N, 109°21′ E) under natural conditions.

Field trials under different planting densities

Field trials were conducted in Sanya, China (18.2°N, 109.1°E) in 2025. The experiment followed a randomized complete block design with three replicates. Each replicate consisted of a four-row plot, and the plots were established under different planting densities. The experimental design was based on Tian et al. (2019). For the transgenic materials, three planting densities were used: low (45 000 plants ha−1), medium (90 000 plants ha−1), and high (135 000 plants ha−1). At each planting density, the following genotypes were compared: WT, ZmMIR319A-KO1, ZmMIR319A-KO2, ZmMIR319A-OE1, and ZmMIR319A-OE2. Phenotypic measurements were collected at maturity. Before phenotypic analysis, to minimize border effects, the first and last two plants were excluded from the low-density plots, the first and last four plants were excluded from the medium-density plots, and the first and last six plants were excluded from the high-density plots. Harvested ears were dried to a uniform moisture content and then evaluated for ear and grain traits. Data from each subplot were calculated on a per-plant basis, and grain yield was subsequently converted to a per-hectare basis.

Plasmid construction and transformation

For the yeast one-hybrid assay, the full-length coding sequences of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 were amplified from X249 cDNA and cloned into the pJG4-5 vector (Clontech, USA), yielding the AD-ZmTCP5 and AD-ZmTCP44 constructs, respectively. Approximately 3-kb promoter fragments of ZmPIN and ZmHSP family genes were amplified from X249 genomic DNA and inserted into the pLacZi2μ vector to generate the corresponding 2μ-proZmPIN or 2μ-proZmHSP reporter constructs.

For the transient expression assay, the full-length coding sequences of ZmTCP2, ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, and ZmPIN8 were amplified from X249 cDNA and cloned into the pCUB-Ubi::eGFP vector, yielding the reporter constructs ZmTCP2-GFP, ZmTCP5-GFP, ZmTCP44-GFP, and ZmPIN8-GFP, respectively. The precursor sequence of ZmMIR319A was amplified from X249 genomic DNA and inserted into the pCAMBIA3300-like vector to generate the corresponding Ubi-ZmMIR319A construct. The GFP fusion constructs and the ZmMIR319A construct were introduced into maize protoplasts using PEG-mediated transformation.

For the dual-luciferase transient expression assay, 3-kb promoter fragments from ZmPIN and ZmHSP family genes were amplified from X249 genomic DNA and cloned into the pEASY-LUC vector to generate the corresponding proZmPIN/HSP::LUC reporter constructs. The Renilla luciferase gene driven by the 35S promoter in the same vector served as an internal control. For effector constructs, the full-length coding sequences of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 were individually cloned into the pRTBD vector under the control of the 35S promoter to generate the corresponding 35S::ZmTCP constructs. The empty pRTBD vector was used as a negative control.

To generate overexpression lines, the full-length coding sequences of ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, ZmPIN8, ZmHSP70-4, and ZmHSP90-5, excluding the stop codons, were cloned into the binary vector pCUB-GFP (Biovector) under the control of the Ubi promoter. For ZmMIR319A overexpression, a 181-bp genomic fragment containing the stem–loop structure of the ZmMIR319A precursor was amplified and cloned into the pCPB vector downstream of the Ubi promoter. These constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV4404.

The ZmMIR319A–D knockout constructs were generated using a CRISPR–Cas9 system as described by Tian et al. (2026). In brief, the binary vector pGEL757, containing expression cassettes for Cas9 and multiplex sgRNAs, was used. A Golden Gate assembly strategy was used to clone a designed tRNA–sgRNA array targeting all four ZmMIR319 members into the vector, replacing the lacZ placeholder. The final construct was introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV4404 for maize transformation.

Constructs for ZmTCP5, ZmTCP44, and ZmPIN8 knockout mutants were generated as described in Zhang et al. (2024). In brief, two target sites were selected within the first exon of each gene using SnapGene Viewer 3.2 based on the criterion 5′-G-(N)19-NGG-3′. Then, individual CRISPR–Cas9 plasmids were constructed by introducing gRNAs targeting the selected sites into the pBUE411 vector.

All primer sequences used for vector construction are listed in Supplemental Table 1. All constructs were verified by sequencing prior to use.

Leaf angle measurement

A commercial inclinometer (Victor, China) was used to measure LA. LA was defined as the angle between the vertical axis and the midrib at the base of the leaf, with 0° representing a fully vertical leaf and 90° representing a fully horizontal leaf. For seedlings grown in the growth chamber, the angle of the first leaf was measured at the V2 stage (approximately 14 days after sowing). For field-grown adult plants at the silking stage, the angles of the three leaves directly above the primary ear were designated LA1 to LA3 (from top to base).

Heat stress treatment

For heat stress (HS) treatment, 2-week-old seedlings grown under normal conditions were transferred to a pre-heated growth chamber at 45°C for a defined duration, then returned to the original control chamber (28°C/22°C, day/night) for recovery before subsequent analysis. The light intensity and relative humidity in the chambers were 300 μmol m−2 s−1 and approximately 60%, respectively.

Histological analysis

For histological analysis, LR samples were collected at the silking stage from the first leaf above the ear in ZmMIR319A mutants, overexpression lines, and WT plants. The dissected samples were fixed overnight at 4°C with gentle agitation in ice-cold 3.7% formaldehyde–acetic acid–alcohol solution (50% ethanol, 5% acetic acid, and 3.7% formaldehyde). The samples were then dehydrated using a 50%–100% ethanol gradient, embedded in paraffin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a Leica rotary microtome. The sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated, and stained with FASGA solution (0.05% safranin O, 0.2% Alcian blue, 1.5% acetic acid, and 46% glycerol) for 1 h to distinguish between lignified (red) and non-lignified (light blue) tissues. Images were acquired using a Leica DFC450 light microscope.

NBT staining and quantification

For NBT staining, 2-week-old seedlings grown under normal conditions were transferred to a pre-heated chamber at 45°C for 1 h, after which the second leaves were detached and immediately immersed in 1 mg ml−1 NBT solution for 12 h. Subsequently, the leaves were decolorized by washing them three times in 75% ethanol. The resulting intracellular formazan precipitates were photographed and quantified using ImageJ (v.1.38).

Measurement of SOD, POD, and CAT activities

To assess the oxidative stress response under HS, the second leaves from V2-stage seedlings, either untreated or subjected to a 4-h heat treatment at 45°C, were harvested for enzyme activity assays. The activities of SOD, POD, and CAT were measured using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The catalog numbers for the SOD, POD, and CAT assay kits were A001-1-1, A084-3-1, and A007-1-1, respectively.

ZmMIR319A-mediated repression assay

To assess ZmMIR319A-mediated post-transcriptional repression of its candidate targets, the ZmMIR319A-OE construct was mixed at different ratios with ZmTCP- or ZmPIN8-eGFP reporter constructs and co-transfected into maize protoplasts via PEG-mediated transformation. After incubation in the dark for 24 h, fluorescence signals were detected using a Leica fluorescence microscope and further quantified using ImageJ (v.1.38).

RNA-seq analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed using fresh LR tissues from the unexpanded, light-shielded tenth leaves of maize plants at the V9 stage. Three biological replicates were analyzed for WT, ZmMIR319A-KO1, and ZmMIR319A-OE1 plants. Each replicate consisted of LR tissues pooled from 10 individual plants. Total RNA was extracted and sequenced by Novogene using an Illumina HiSeq platform. Clean reads were aligned to the maize reference genome Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p ≤ 0.05.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

For the tissue-specific expression assay, different tissues from X249 plants grown under field conditions were harvested at the V9 stage and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. To analyze ZmMIR319 expression during leaf development, LR tissues (excluding the ligule and auricle) were collected from the fifth through tenth leaves (numbered from base to top) of X249 plants at the V9 stage. To analyze the expression of ZmMIR319 and ZmTCP family members under HS, the second leaves from the base of V3-stage X249 seedlings were harvested. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the FastQuant RT Kit with gDNA Eraser (Tiangen Biotech, China). To measure the levels of mature miR319, total RNA was isolated using the miRcute miRNA Isolation Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China). First-strand cDNA was subsequently synthesized from the purified RNA using the miRcute miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China). qPCR was performed with an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System using TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mature miR319 levels were then quantified by real-time qPCR using the miRcute miRNA qPCR Detection Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ZmTubulin5 (Zm00001eb107490) was used as an internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct method.

Yeast one-hybrid assay

The AD-ZmTCP5/44 plasmids and their respective 2μ-proZmPIN and 2μ-proZmHSP reporter constructs were co-transformed into the yeast strain EGY48 using PEG-mediated transformation. Positive transformants were selected on SD/-Trp/-Ura plates (Clontech, USA) and then transferred to selective medium containing raffinose, galactose, and 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-galactopyranoside and incubated at 30°C for blue color development. To quantify β-galactosidase activity, positive yeast clones were cultured overnight in liquid SD/-Trp/-Ura medium. Cells were collected and resuspended in Z buffer (40 mM NaH2PO4, 60 mM Na2HPO4, 1 mM MgSO4, and 10 mM KCl [pH 7.0]) and lysed with chloroform and sodium dodecyl sulfate. After addition of o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside as the substrate, the reaction mixtures were incubated at 30°C for 5 min and terminated with 1 M Na2CO3, after which the absorbance of the resulting supernatant was measured.

Recombinant protein production

For recombinant protein expression, DNA fragments encoding the DNA-binding domains of ZmTCP5 and ZmTCP44 were cloned into the pMCSG7 vector to generate the MBP-ΔZmTCP5 and MBP-ΔZmTCP44 fusion constructs. These plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli strain Transetta (DE3) (TransGen Biotech, China). Positive clones were inoculated into Luria–Bertani medium and grown overnight at 37°C. The overnight cultures were diluted in fresh Luria–Bertani medium and incubated at 37°C, after which protein expression was induced by the addition of 0.4 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside. Cells were harvested and resuspended in Buffer A (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, and 5 mM imidazole [pH 8.0]) with 1 mg/ml lysozyme. After sonication on ice, the supernatants were loaded onto pre-charged Ni2+-affinity resin. The resin was washed with Buffer B (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, and 20 mM imidazole [pH 8.0]), and the fusion proteins were eluted with Buffer C (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, and 250 mM imidazole [pH 8.0]). Purified proteins were desalted using PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM NaH2PO4, and 1.76 mM KH2PO4 [pH 7.4]), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C before use.

EMSA

Complementary 60-bp oligonucleotides corresponding to putative binding sites in the target promoters were synthesized and labeled with biotin at their 5′ ends. Protein–DNA binding reactions were performed using an EMSA kit (GS002, Beyotime, China). In brief, biotin-labeled probes were incubated with the purified MBP-ΔZmTCP5 or MBP-ΔZmTCP44 fusion proteins, with MBP alone used as a negative control, in binding buffer at room temperature for 20 min. For competition assays, a 10- or 50-fold molar excess of unlabeled probe was included in the reaction mixture. Reaction products were then resolved on 5% native polyacrylamide gels and detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China). For the temperature-pretreatment assay, the purified MBP-ΔZmTCP44 fusion protein was pretreated at 0°C, 28°C, or 45°C for 20 min before incubation with the biotin-labeled ZmHSP70-4 promoter probe under the standard EMSA conditions described above.

ChIP–qPCR assay

LR tissues that remained enclosed and light-shielded within leaves were harvested from ZmTCP5-GFP and ZmTCP44-GFP plants at the V9 stage, cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, and then ground into a fine powder. Chromatin extraction and immunoprecipitation were performed according to the method of Xie et al. (2017). In brief, solubilized chromatin was pre-cleared with 40 μl of Protein A agarose beads (SM015001, Smart-Lifesciences, China) at 4°C for 1 h. The pre-cleared supernatant was incubated overnight at 4°C with 5 μl of anti-GFP monoclonal antibody (SM038001, Smart-Lifesciences, China). Subsequently, 40 μl of Protein A agarose beads were added, followed by an additional incubation of 2 h at 4°C. After extensive washing, the immunoprecipitated chromatin complexes were eluted from the beads. The recovered DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR using specific primer pairs. Enrichment was calculated relative to input DNA, with the ZmACTIN1 promoter region serving as an internal control. ChIP–qPCR assays were performed with three biological replicates, and similar results were obtained in two independent experiments.

Transient expression assay in maize protoplasts

Maize protoplasts were isolated from etiolated leaves of 12-day-old X249 seedlings. The proZmPIN/HSP::LUC and 35S::ZmTCP constructs were co-transfected into protoplasts via PEG-mediated transformation. Luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative firefly luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase to Renilla luciferase luminescence.

Statistical analysis

Student’s t-tests were used to evaluate the significance of differences between two independent groups. When comparing more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics 20, Armonk, USA). Graphs showing individual data points and summary bars were generated from the raw data using GraphPad Prism (v.7.04).
