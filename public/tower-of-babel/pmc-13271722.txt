Oropharyngeal Tumor in the Newborn: Our Experiences with Short-term Follow-up
Authors: Halder Sudeshna, Shibsankar Barman, Sarkar Ruchirendu, Biswas Sumitra Kumar, Chatterjee Uttara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims and Objective:The aim of this study is to highlight the rarity of this disorder and it’s clinicopathological features, associated anomalies and our objective is to review our experiences in the management and outcomes of the babies with short term follow up.Materials and Methods:This was a retrospective study carried out in a tertiary care center from June 2013 to May 2020 with a follow-up period ranged from 5 years to 12 years.Results and Analysis:Twenty newborn babies were included in our study out of which nine babies having mature teratoma (45%), five having congenital epulis (25%), and two having multiple hamartoma (10%). Rest four were epignathus, rhabdomyosarcoma, dermoid, and hairy polyp. Except rhabdomyosarcoma, all babies were doing well in follow-up.Conclusion:The overall prognosis for newborn oropharyngeal tumor (OPT) is good. Early diagnosis, the establishment of good airway, complete excision of tumor and timely follow-up are the key factors for the survival of newborn. For congenital teratomas, oropharyngeal cavity is one of the rarest sites (2% of all teratomas).

INTRODUCTION

Oropharyngeal tumor in newborn is extremely rare, representing only 2% of childhood tumors. The reported incidence is 1 in 12,500–27,500 live births.[1] Most of them are teratoma. These tumors are usually benign in histology. However, morbidity and possible mortality may be seen due to their size and location and airway obstruction. Ninety percent of head-and-neck teratomas present during the neonatal and infantile period, predominantly involving the neck and nasopharynx and occur in 1 in every 4000 live births.[2] Of the head-and-neck teratomas, pure oral presentation is especially rare. These tumors can be diagnosed at birth, presenting as an oral tumors with or without life-threatening airway obstruction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective study performed over 7 years at a tertiary care hospital. Twenty newborns with oropharyngeal tumor were studied from June 2013 to May 2020. All newborns presented at emergency from a rural background with a protruding or nonprotruding tumor from mouth. The age at the presentation ranged from 4 h to 42 h and the mean age at the presentation was 9 h.

After stabilization and adequate resuscitation, babies were shifted to OT for surgical removal of the tumors. Usually, most of the tumors having a narrow stalk hanging from the palate, gum, cheek, tongue, etc. Majority of them out protruded outside of the mouth with varying size and shape. Few were confined in the oral cavity such as under the surface of tongue; floor of the mouth, soft palate, etc. Simple bipolar cauterization and excision to complex surgical enucleation were done to achieve the complete removal of the tumor and also to establish airway. Feeding was started 4–6 h after the operation [Table 1].

RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

Twenty newborn were included in our study, out of which nine babies had mature teratoma (45%), five had congenital epulis (CE) (25%), and two had multiple hamartoma (10%). Rest four were epignathus, rhabdomyosarcoma, dermoid, and hairy polyp. Except rhabdomyosarcoma, all babies were doing well in the follow-up.

Mature teratoma [Figure 1b-e] was more common in female babies in comparison to male newborn with the ratio of 8:1. This group of patients was the most common variety among all with a very good prognosis. The next most common group was CE [Figure 2d]. Lower alveolar margin was the most common site of origin, although upper alveolar margin was also involved (4:1). Rest of the patients had extremely rare anomalies. Two of them had multiple globular masses arising from the different wall of the mouth cavity with bilobed tongue, high arched palate, cleft palate, and tongue [Figure 3a and b]. However, surprisingly, syndromic association was not found in any one of them.

All babies had undergone complete surgical excision, and at follow-up, all of them were doing well. The entire patient population had adequate stalk for simple excision by bipolar cautery. Highly vascular oral mucosa healed very easily. Most of the babies could be discharged on the 2nd postoperative day with full oral intake.

All babies were followed up at the outpatient department after 1 month, then 3 monthly for 1st year, and then at 6-month interval for 3 years. None of them had local recurrence. All babies had gained adequate weight without any feeding or respiration problem. Primary dentition was also healthy in all of them.

DISCUSSION

Congenital oral tumors in the newborn are rare and even rarer when they protruded outside the oral cavity producing a demonic appearance of the baby. Intra-oral tumors are often of varied etiology and nomenclature creating confusion among the treating physicians. They are also referred as congenital granular cell tumor, congenital granular epulis, congenital granular cell myoblastoma, and congenital granular cell fibroblastoma.[3] Hence, to minimize the confusion, we hereby try to depict the complete scenario as newborn “congenital oropharyngeal tumors” (COT). After thorough searching in the available literature database, we could find only few case reports and very few case series. To the best of our knowledge, it is going to be the largest series of COT.

COT represents only 2% of childhood tumors. Reported incidence is 1 out of 4000 live births.[45] Most of them are teratoma followed by CE. Very few percentages of babies had epignathus, rhabdomyosarcoma, hamartoma, hairy polyp, and dermoid cyst. All these entities are usually benign in histology. However, high degree of morbidity and possible mortality can be caused by virtue of their size and location, causing airway obstruction and respiratory distress.

The term “Teratoma,” derived from the Greek word “Teraton” (meaning monster), was coined by Virchow in 1863. These tumors have been considered to be foreign to the site with independent growth and gradually distorting the site by increasing in size.[6] Usually, they are benign tumor, although malignancy has not been reported in oral teratoma, there are reports of malignant transformation of residual elements.[57]

In late 19th century, Arnold’s system was introduced to classify teratomas which is still accepted and widely used. Histopathologically, they have been classified into four types: (1) dermoid cyst (epithelial lined with skin elements, composed of ectodermal and mesodermal cells) [Figure 1a]; (2) teratoid cyst (all 3 germ layers but poorly differentiated); (3) true teratoma (3 germ layers differentiated into specific tissues or organs); and (4) Epignathi (oral tumors with developmental fetal organs and limbs due to incomplete twinning very rare with a high mortality rate). Fetuses with head-and-neck teratomas, especially large cervical and nasopharyngeal teratomas may often associated with polyhydramnios.[4]

Etiopathogenesis of teratoma is still unclear, however several theories exist to explain their etiology, including: (1) Acquired traumatic implantation of ectodermal or mesodermal structures within deeper tissues; (2) Congenital inclusion of germ layers into deeper tissues at the fusion line during embryogenesis; and (3) Implantation of pluripotential cells, which subsequently grow in a disorganized manner. However, all these theories failed to justify other coexistent anomalies within the same location adequately.[6]

Coexistent anomalies are reported as 6% of all cases of teratoma, among which cleft palate is the most common. Other associations include branchial cleft cyst, bifid tongues, bifid nose, cardiac abnormalities, and microcephaly, respectively.[6]

Clinical differential diagnosis of neonatal oral mass includes embryonic congenital rhabdomyosarcoma, retinoblastoma, nasal glioma, heterotopic thyroid, cystic lymphangioma, and sphenoid meningoencephalocele.[6]

There is still controversy regarding the exact cell of origin. Vandenhaute et al. in 2000 stated that most teratomas were originated from the nasopharyngeal region with a pedicle at the base of the cranium in proximity to the Rathke pouch, whereas many published literature said that they have an attachment to the base of the tongue. Lopes et al. in 2005 reported the first case of a benign teratoma in the oral mucosa of a 9-year-old girl.[8]

It is usually attached to the maxillary region, sphenoid, the lateral wall of the pharynx, and the palate[5] which were similar to our study.

Of the head-and-neck teratomas, pure oral presentation is especially rare. Head-and-neck occurrence is generally localized to the neck and nasopharynx and comprises 1%–10% of cases.[9] Other extracranial presentations of teratomas are very rare and only 13 cases of pure oral teratomas (seven from the tongue and six from the hard palate) have been reported in the literature [Table 1].[4]

All the babies came from a rural background with limited ante-natal scans, henceforth all were diagnosed postnatally. Early resection is advised, especially for airway obstructing teratomas.[4] Surgical excision is not usually a problem for tumors having a narrow base, but in those arising from the base of the skull with a wide base, a complete excision may not be possible and should be on close follow-up. Although malignancy has not been reported in epignathi, there are reports of malignant transformation of residual elements. Alpha feto protein (AFP) has been shown to be a reliable indicator of disease activity, and some authors advocate investigating teratoma recurrence by doing serial serum AFP levels. April et al. suggested that these measurements should be continued for patients with extensive lesions or those without a capsule.[4] The long-term prognosis is excellent.[7]

COT is considered to be mature teratomas in 60%–80% of cases, i.e. composed of well-differentiated tissues. Due to the heterogeneous tissue structure, the benign nature of these tumors cannot be confirmed on simple biopsy.[10] Most of the childhood teratomas are congenital in which majorities are mature varieties. Although mature teratomas are considered benign on the basis of histological findings, they may cause death if the vital structures are involved. Unique clinical presentations are associated with large, space occupying lobulated mass protruding into the oral cavity from the pharynx.[11] Immature teratomas are different to mature teratoma, which contain the elements of all three germ layers with a prominent neuroectodermal component.

There is no established guideline regarding the management of neonatal malignant teratomas till now. The recommended mainstay of treatment is surgery, comprising resection of the tumor and its metastases when they are accessible (lymph node dissection, lobectomy, and partial hepatectomy). Adjuvant radiotherapy is recommended when resection is incomplete. However, irradiation must be limited due to its long-term sequelae: Neurological deficit, mental retardation, radiation osteonecrosis, sarcoma, and anomaly of facial development. Chemotherapy is administered as the first-line treatment when surgery is impossible. It is therefore reserved for disseminated immature teratomas or recurrent or unresectable teratomas. In the case of recurrence, elevation of AFP levels is sufficient to define the indication for chemotherapy without the need for biopsy. Chemotherapy protocols vary as a function of AFP levels and teratoma grade. Following incomplete initial surgical resection, adjuvant chemotherapy (4–6 cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy)[12] must be continued until AFP becomes negative.[10]

The second most common entity is CE. Epulis [Figure 2d] is a tumor-like growth that appears on the gums or alveolar mucosa. The word comes from the Greek word epouλίς, which means “growth on the gingiva.”

CE of the newborn is a rare benign tumor of the soft tissues, mainly arising from alveolar ridge. Maxillary alveolar ridge is more commonly affected. The exact pathogenesis of CE is still uncertain. It is normally diagnosed at birth, but prenatal 3rd trimester documentation is also reported. The recommended treatment for CE is prompt surgical excision due to interference with feeding, respiration, or adequate closure of the mouth. CE has been reported with an 8:1 female and 3:1 maxillary alveolar site predilection. CE normally arises from the anterior part of the maxillary alveolar ridge of the newborn and frequently occurs lateral to the midline in the area of the developing primary lateral incisor and canine. Pellicano et al. have reported that the tumor may obstruct the fetal mouth and cause polyhydramnios, a medical condition describing an excess of amniotic fluid in the amniotic sac.[13]

Classically, there is a single firm tumor with a quite regular surface, sometimes multilobed, sessile or pedunculated, pink or red in color, and nonpainful on palpation. CE may regress spontaneously but requires surgical removal due to problems in feeding and respiration.[3]

Initially described in 1871 by Neumann, this condition is rare, with only 186 published cases found in a literature review by Yavuzer in 2001.[14]

There are other rarer varieties exist which include hairy polyp, epignathus, tongue mass, etc. These tumors are extremely rare and even sparsely reported in the literature. The site of origin is also very much diversified. Majority of the lesions are arising from the base of the mouth but hard palate, soft palate, cheek mucosa, gum, alveolar margins may also contribute as the originating site.

Hairy polyps [Figure 2c] are usually presented as a firm or elastic pedunculated mass. They have smooth external surface containing hair. They may also have tooth element, mature adipose tissue, mucin-secreting glands, tooth structure, skin adnexes, neural tissue, skeletal muscle, and bone. Usually, they are well-differentiated teratoid lesion without any immature component. Hairy polyp of the pharynx may be associated with an ipsilateral branchial sinus.[15]

Another pathologic variation of epignathi [Figure 2b] is fetus-in-fetus, which may be considered to be incomplete twinning of monozygotic twins at a primitive stage when axial development begins. Intraoral teratomas and epignathus are thought to arise in embryonic life from pluripotent cells of the Rathke sac. This rare, benign, congenital tumor with incidence of 1/35,000–1/200,000, grows in the mouth or upper jaw of a fetus. It may fill the oral cavity or protrude out causing other developing tissues to shift by exerted pressure on. They have footing in the base of the cranium and are associated with facial anomalies, especially with cleft palate. Macroscopically, they have solid areas with cystic and patchy calcifications. The epignathus consist of 2% of all COT. It can cause life-threatening airway obstruction resulting in asphyxia at birth. In the literature, multiple localized epignathus has not been reported so far.[16]

Congenital lingual masses [Figure 2a] associated with cleft palate and bifid tongue are rare; only ten cases were reported. Due to rarity, they have broad differential diagnoses, including hamartoma; teratoma; hemangioma; rhabdomyosarcoma; lingual cyst; ectopic salivary, glial, or thyroid tissue; and fibrolipoma.[17]

CONCLUSION

OPT are rare. Among all teratomas, oropharyngeal cavity is one of the rarest sites (2%). The presentation may vary from simple intra-oral mass to a large protruding tumor with life-threatening airway obstruction which may demand preoperative as well as postoperative ventilation support. Early diagnosis, establishment of good airway, complete excision of tumor, and timely follow-up are the key factors for survival. Surgery is the main stay of treatment. Overall prognosis is very good.
