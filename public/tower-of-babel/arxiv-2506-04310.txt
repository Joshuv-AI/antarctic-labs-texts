Fast Rotators at Cosmic Noon: Stellar Kinematics for 15 Quiescent Galaxies from JWST-SUSPENSE
Martje Slob, Mariska Kriek, Anna de Graaff, Chloe M. Cheng, Aliza G. Beverage, Rachel Bezanson, Natascha M. Förster Schreiber, Brian Lorenz, Pavel E. Mancera Piña, Danilo Marchesini, Adam Muzzin, Andrew B. Newman, Sedona H. Price, Katherine A. Suess, Jesse van de Sande, Pieter van Dokkum, Daniel R. Weisz

Abstract
We present spatially-resolved stellar kinematics of fifteen massive ( M ∗ = 10 10.5 − 11.5 ​ M ⊙ M_{*}=10^{10.5-11.5}M_{\odot} ) quiescent galaxies at z ≈ 1.2 − 2.3 z\approx 1.2-2.3 from the JWST-SUSPENSE program. This is the largest sample of spatially-resolved kinematic measurements of quiescent galaxies at cosmic noon to date. Our measurements are derived from ultra-deep NIRSpec/MSA stellar absorption line spectra, using a forward modelling approach that accounts for optics, source morphology, positioning, and data reduction effects. Ten out of fifteen galaxies are orientated such that we can measure rotational support. Remarkably, all ten galaxies show significant rotation ( V r e = 117 − 345 V_{r_{e}}=117-345 km s -1 , σ 0 = 180 − 387 \sigma_{0}=180-387 km s), and are classified as ”fast rotators” from their spin parameter. The remaining galaxies are too misaligned with respect to the slitlet to constrain their rotational velocities. The widespread rotational support in our sample indicates that the process responsible for quenching star formation in early massive galaxies did not destroy rotating disc structures. When combined with other quiescent galaxy samples at z ≈ 0.5 − 2.5 z\approx 0.5-2.5 , we find a trend between rotational support and age, with younger quiescent galaxies being more rotationally supported. This age trend has also been found at z ∼ 0 z\sim 0 , and likely explains why our high-redshift galaxies show more rotational support compared to massive early-type galaxies at z ∼ 0 z\sim 0 , which are, on average, older. Our kinematic modelling also enables us to calculate dynamical masses. These dynamical masses greatly exceed the stellar masses for our sample (median M dyn / M ∗ = 2.73 M_{\text{dyn}}/M_{*}=2.73 ); they even allow for the bottom-heavy IMF found in the cores of low- z z massive ellipticals. Altogether, our results support a scenario in which distant quiescent galaxies evolve into nearby massive early-type galaxies, gradually building up their outskirts and simultaneously losing rotation, due to a series of (mostly minor) mergers.



1
Introduction

The most massive and oldest quiescent galaxies that exist in the universe today are predominantly slow-rotating ellipticals
(
van de Sande et al. 2017
;
Veale et al. 2017
;
Emsellem et al. 2011
)
.
To explain their slow rotation and morphologies a picture emerged in which the mechanism responsible for the quenching of star formation may also be responsible for significant structural changes, as suggested by the classic major merger scenario
(
Barnes 1988
;
Hernquist 1993
;
Struck 1999
;
Hopkins et al. 2008
, e.g.)
.
This classic scenario of massive galaxy evolution thus posits that the rotating disc structure of massive galaxies may be destroyed during the initial quenching event, and that the structures of distant, quiescent massive galaxies resemble those of nearby massive early-type galaxies.

However, over the past few decades this classic picture has been challenged by detailed studies of distant quiescent galaxies. First, distant quiescent galaxies are more compact than low-
z
early-type galaxies, indicating that significant size growth occurs after quenching
(
Daddi et al. 2005
;
van Dokkum et al. 2008
;
van der Wel et al. 2014
;
Suess et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. This picture is supported by colour-gradient studies, which show that the outskirts of old quiescent galaxies are bluer than their cores, and thus may have been built-up by the accretion of low-mass, low-metallicity galaxies
(
Greene et al. 2015
;
Martín-Navarro et al. 2018
;
Suess et al. 2019
;
Cheng et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Moreover, photometric studies showed that a significant fraction of distant massive quiescent galaxies are disk-like, with lower Sérsic indices and flatter shapes than nearby early-type galaxies
(
McGrath et al. 2008
;
van Dokkum et al. 2008
;
van der Wel et al. 2011
;
McLure et al. 2013
;
Bruce et al. 2012
;
Bruce et al. 2014
;
Buitrago et al. 2013
;
Chang et al. 2012
;
Chang et al. 2013
)
. Detailed studies of nearby early-type galaxies also challenge this classic picture. While the most massive, oldest low-
z
early-type galaxies show little rotational support, younger low-
z
quenched galaxies still have significant rotation
(
van de Sande et al. 2017
;
van de Sande et al. 2018
;
Croom et al. 2024
, e.g.)
.

To confirm whether quiescent galaxies beyond the low-
z
universe are rotationally supported discs, direct measurements of their dynamical properties from resolved stellar kinematic studies are needed. Indeed, results from the LEGA-C survey reveal that a significant fraction of massive quiescent galaxies at
z\sim 0.7
are rotationally supported
(
Bezanson et al. 2018
;
van Houdt et al. 2021
)
.
Beyond
z>1
, until recently such studies were only possible for lensed galaxies using large ground-based telescopes, because these measurements rely on spatially resolving faint absorption lines. In particular, this has been done for four strongly lensed quiescent galaxies at
z\sim 2
, all of which show significant rotation
(
Newman et al. 2015
;
Newman et al. 2018
;
Toft et al. 2017
)
.

These results, if true for the general population of distant quiescent galaxies, have important implications for massive galaxy evolution theory; they imply that rotating discs are not destroyed during the quenching of star formation. Instead, other physical processes, including the accretion of low-mass satellites after the initial quenching event, may be responsible for perturbing the galaxies’ structures and forming the low-
z
population of slow-rotating ellipticals. This picture is also supported by simulations, which show that dry mergers can effectively destroy ordered rotation in galaxies from cosmic noon to
z\sim 0

(
Bournaud et al. 2007
;
Naab et al. 2009
;
Schulze et al. 2018
;
Dubois et al. 2016
;
Lagos et al. 2018
;
Lagos et al. 2018
;
Lagos et al. 2022
, e.g.)
.

With the launch of JWST, the high sensitivity and spatial resolution of JWST/NIRSpec
(
Ferruit et al. 2022
)
now make it possible to study detailed kinematic properties of statistically significant samples of distant quiescent galaxies. Indeed, NIRSpec/IFU studies have identified quiescent galaxies at
z>3
with strong rotational support
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
;
Pascalau et al. 2025
)
. Moreover,
Newman et al. 2025
used the NIRSpec/IFU to confirm the rotational support of one of the three lensed galaxies presented in
Newman et al. 2018
. Although these results confirm that NIRSpec/IFU is a powerful tool for measuring accurate resolved stellar kinematics of individual galaxies, its field of view and sensitivity limit the sample size of distant quiescent galaxies for which kinematic parameters can be measured.

Instead, the micro-shutter array (MSA) on NIRSpec offers a powerful and more efficient strategy for measuring stellar kinematics for large samples of galaxies at
z>1
. NIRSpec/MSA can observe
\sim
100 objects simultaneously, making it highly efficient for studying large samples of high-redshift galaxies. By leveraging spatial information from high-resolution imaging, we can mitigate the fact that the slit-based observations from NIRSpec/MSA only cover one spatial dimension. Combined with detailed models of the instrument and detector optics we can thus measure the spatial and kinematic properties from NIRSpec/MSA spectra with a forward modelling approach (
de Graaff et al. 2024
, see also
Price et al. 2016
;
Price et al. 2020
;
van Houdt et al. 2021
;
Straatman et al. 2022
).

In this paper, we present the kinematic properties of 15 massive (
\log M_{*}/M_{\odot}\sim 10.2-11.5
) quiescent galaxies at
z=1-2.5
from the JWST-SUSPENSE programme
(
Slob et al. 2024
)
. These galaxies represent the first sample of kinematically modelled quiescent galaxies at
z>1
. The galaxies are all observed in a single NIRSpec/MSA pointing and have deep JWST/NIRCam or HST imaging, providing both spatially resolved spectroscopic and imaging data. From these data we derive the intrinsic stellar kinematics, using a forward modelling technique based on
msafit

(
de Graaff et al. 2024
)
, and accordingly constrain the kinematic properties of these galaxies. We use these measurements to assess the prevalence of quiescent rotating discs in our sample and discuss the implications for galaxy evolution models.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we present the data. Our measurements and forward modelling technique are outlined in Section
3
. In Section
4
we present the results of our modelling, the inferred measures of rotational support for our sample, and the dynamical masses. We discuss caveats and the implications for galaxy evolution, the initial mass function (IMF), and virial mass estimates in Section
5
, and finally summarise our findings in Section
6
. Throughout this work we assume a
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
\Omega_{\rm m}=0.3
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
, and
H_{0}=70\rm\,kms^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}
.

2
Data

2.1
NIRSpec MSA spectroscopy

For this work, we use NIRSpec/MSA observations from the JWST-SUSPENSE programme (Programme ID 2110,
Slob et al. 2024
), which observed 20 massive (
\log M_{*}/M_{\odot}\sim 10.2-11.5
) quiescent galaxies at
z=1-3
. Below, we provide a summary of the observing strategy and reduction of the data. For a full description we refer to
Slob et al. 2024
.

We used the NIRSpec/MSA with the medium resolution (
R\sim 1000
) G140M-F100LP disperser/filter combination, which covers rest-frame optical wavelengths for our sample. The galaxies were observed using a custom 2-point nodding pattern with a nod size of 2 shutters, which ensures that self-subtraction for the extended galaxies in the sample is minimised. The data reduction was done using a modified version of the JWST Science Calibration Pipeline
(
Bushouse et al. 2023
)
, with a custom outlier detection algorithm described in
Slob et al. 2024
. With a total on-source integration time of 16.4 hr, the resulting 2D spectra have median signal-to-noise ratios between rest-frame
4600-4800
Å of
S/N=10-58
Å
-1
, and extend over multiple effective radii for the majority of sources in the sample.

To study the resolved kinematics for the galaxies in our sample we extract spectra for each individual row along the spatial direction of the rectified 2D spectra. The data were taken at two dither positions, and we combine the extracted 1D spectra row-by-row for each dither separately. Due to the undersampling of the NIRSpec point spread function (PSF) along the spatial axis, these row-by-row spectra are affected by sinusoidal patterns (‘wiggles’) in their fluxes (see
Perna et al. 2023
for an example with NIRSpec/IFU data). We correct for these wiggles by fitting a sinusoidal profile to each spectral row, and dividing our data by this fit, as described in C. Cheng et al. (in prep.).

Throughout this work we adopt the stellar masses derived from full spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting, including the NIRSpec spectrometry, reported by
Slob et al. 2024
. These masses were derived using a
Chabrier 2003
IMF, with a non-parametric star-formation history.

2.2
Imaging

Fifteen out of twenty galaxies in our sample fall within the imaging footprint from the COSMOS-Web programme
(
Casey et al. 2023
, Programme ID: 1727)
. These galaxies were observed with four different NIRCam filters (F115W, F150W, F277W, and F444W). For our analysis we use the images that are available in MAST
1
1

1

https://mast.stsci.edu/
, which are reduced using the JWST Calibration Pipeline v11.17.14 with the CRDS pipeline mapping (pmap) context 1230. For the five galaxies in the JWST-SUSPENSE sample that are not covered by COSMOS-Web, we use HST/ACS imaging in the F814W filter
(
Scoville et al. 2007
)
. Although this filter is bluer compared to COSMOS-Web imaging, the galaxies for which we use F814W are at
z\sim 1.2
. At these redshifts, the F814W filter still covers rest-frame optical wavelengths.

3
Kinematic modelling

Obtaining intrinsic stellar kinematics from NIRSpec/MSA data is challenging because these slit-based spectra cover only one, randomly aligned, spatial dimension. In Figure
1
we demonstrate the significant change in the observed line-of-sight velocity profile when a source is misaligned and/or positioned off-centre within an MSA shutter. However, from detailed imaging and measurements of structural parameters of the galaxies in our sample we know the position, photometric orientation, and inclination of the galaxies with respect to the MSA shutters. If we assume the kinematic and photometric axes are aligned, and that the galaxies are axisymmetric, we can use the imaging and structural parameters to measure the intrinsic kinematic properties of our galaxies, using a forward-modelling approach based on
msafit

(
de Graaff et al. 2024
)
. This software package takes into account the source position and morphology, as well as the complicated instrumental effects of NIRSpec/MSA.

The left panels show the models for a galaxy with an intrinsic velocity field with a maximum rotational velocity of 300 km s
-1
. The right panels show the LOS velocity profiles for a galaxy with no intrinsic rotational velocity, representing the effects from optics, the PSF, and data reduction steps. These effects can lead to observed LOS velocities of up to
\sim 50
km s
-1
. The modelled galaxies have
r_{e}=0.13
″(1.1 kpc at
z\sim 1.5
), a Sérsic index of 1.5, and an axis ratio
q
of
0.3
. The grey bands indicate areas of the velocity profile that are affected by bar shadows from the MSA.

To obtain stellar kinematics from absorption line spectra, several adaptions to
msafit
were made.
msafit
is designed to model emission lines in unrectified spectra. However, stellar kinematics of distant quiescent galaxies are based on many low equivalent width absorption lines, which are most readily measured in a combined, rectified spectrum. Our approach thus requires comparing the model and the data in reduced space, specifically by directly matching their 1D velocity profiles. We therefore use
msafit
to generate 2D model spectra, and then apply our observing strategy and reduction procedure. The resulting 2D model spectra can be directly compared to our reduced observations. Below, we describe our full procedure, starting with the velocity and structural measurements in Section
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively. In Sections
3.3
and
3.4
we describe the forward model and our sampling method.

3.1
Velocity measurements

We measure velocity and dispersion profiles for each galaxy in our sample by determining the velocity shift (
V_{i,{\rm obs}}
) and velocity dispersion (
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
) for each row
i
in the 2D spectrum using
ppxf

(
Cappellari & Emsellem 2004
;
Cappellari 2017
;
Cappellari 2023
)
. To obtain reliable
V_{i,{\rm obs}}
and
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
measurements, even in galaxy outskirts where the
S/N
is low (
(S/N)_{i}\sim 3-5
Å
-1
, rest-frame), we restrict the stellar population templates used in
ppxf
fitting
(
van de Sande et al. 2017
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
)
. We select the template for each galaxy by first fitting the integrated spectrum with
ppxf
. We also include a 12th-order polynomial in the fit to correct for flux-calibration issues. We use the resulting stellar population template for each row. Thus, we assume that the stellar populations do not vary significantly across the spatial profile of the galaxy. While spatial gradients in stellar population parameters have been observed in distant quiescent galaxies
(
Jafariyazani et al. 2020
;
Cheng et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, they are generally mild and thus are unlikely to affect the measured kinematic information.

We use the full wavelength range of our spectra in the fitting, but to ensure that our results are not biased by nebular emission, we mask out any emission lines. Furthermore, we mask the Na
i
absorption doublet, as this line is a tracer of neutral gas in the interstellar medium (ISM) as well as AGN outflows, and can have a significant kinematic offset from stellar absorption features
(
Belli et al. 2024
;
Davies et al. 2024
, e.g.)
.

To measure
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
with
ppxf
, we must take into account the line spread function (LSF) of NIRSpec/MSA. For the G140M grating, the LSF of NIRSpec/MSA changes by a wavelength-independent factor depending on source position and morphology
(
de Graaff et al. 2024
;
Slob et al. 2024
)
, and is thus different for each galaxy in our sample. Since we will determine the exact slit position with our forward modelling method, initially we do not have the exact LSF for each source. For the first iteration we thus assume an LSF that is a factor 10 (rather than a typical factor
\sim 1.3
) narrower than the NIRSpec/MSA LSF reported on the JDox user documentation
2
2

2

https://jwst-docs.stsci.edu/jwst-near-infrared-spectrograph/nirspec-instrumentation/nirspec-dispersers-and-filters
for both the model and the data. This high factor ensures that we underestimate the true LSF.

After the initial model fit we have the exact position of the source in the slit, and we model the true LSF for each galaxy following the method in
Slob et al. 2024
. To obtain the LSF we use our best-fit galaxy position in the slit, and generate 50 emission lines with no intrinsic velocity or dispersion, apply all relevant data reduction steps, and fit a Gaussian profile to each emission line. We then use the width of the Gaussians over the wavelength range of the detector to find the rescaling factor for the JDox NIRSpec/MSA LSF for each galaxy. We refit the dispersion and velocity of each row in the data and the model after convolving the stellar templates with the rescaled LSF of each galaxy.

For each galaxy in our sample we fit all rows with a
(S/N)_{i}>4
pixel
-1
. To ensure the
V_{i,{\rm obs}}
and
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
measurements are of good quality, we remove rows from our analysis for which the error on
V_{i,{\rm obs}}
and
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
from
ppxf
is larger than 70 km/s. Visual inspection shows that for larger errors the fits become unreliable. We set the systemic velocity of the galaxy to the best-fit velocity of the integrated spectrum.

Our resolved fitting procedure results in
V_{i,{\rm obs}}
and
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
measurements for up to 11 rows for the galaxies in our sample. In Figure
2
we illustrate how the velocity and velocity dispersion profiles from
ppxf
compare to example absorption lines from our observed spectra.
In order to constrain the kinematic profiles, we require
V_{i,{\rm obs}}
and
\sigma_{v,i,{\rm obs}}
measurements in at least 4 rows, and exclude the three galaxies from our sample that do not meet this criterion. We thus have resolved velocity curves for 17 galaxies.

3.2
Structural parameters

We use
GALFIT

(
Peng et al. 2002
;
Peng et al. 2010
)
to measure the morphological parameters of our sample using NIRCam imaging in the F115W, F150W, F277W, and F444W filters from the COSMOS-Web programme (Programme ID: 1727,
Casey et al. 2023
). See Appendix
A
for more details on our fitting procedure. The morphological parameters are required to account for the source morphology in our kinematic modelling.

The structural parameters measured using the different NIRCam filters are in good agreement, with no systematic offsets between different filters. For our modelling we use the median structural parameters of the NIRCam filters which cover rest-frame optical wavelengths for our galaxies. In practice, this means we exclude the F115W filter for galaxies with
z>1.3
, and for galaxies with
z>2
we also exclude the F150W filter.
We note that our kinematic modelling results do not change significantly if we use the structural parameters of one specific filter, rather than the median values. For sources that are not covered by the COSMOS-Web footprint, we use the morphological parameters from HST/ACS F814W observations
(
Griffith et al. 2012
)
. We find that the morphological parameters from COSMOS-Web and HST/ACS F814W agree within 1
\sigma
for galaxies with
z<1.3
, and thus we expect no significant bias in the structural parameters of the galaxies for which we use the F814W parameters.
Two out of seventeen galaxies for which we measure resolved velocity curves are excluded from our sample as they are not covered by COSMOS-Web and are too faint to be included in the
Griffith et al. 2012
morphological catalogue. Our final sample thus consists of fifteen galaxies, for which we report the measured morphological parameters in Table
1
.

3.3
Forward modelling

We use a forward modelling procedure to derive the intrinsic kinematics from the observed velocity and velocity dispersion profiles for each galaxy. The first step in this procedure is to generate model line profiles for a set of morphological and kinematic parameters using
msafit

(
de Graaff et al. 2024
)
. We refer to
de Graaff et al. 2024
for a detailed description of the modelling set-up, but provide a summary of the main steps, and key differences between our methods and
msafit
below.

Our kinematic model depends on 9 parameters, which describe the surface brightness profile, location and orientation with respect to the microshutters, and intrinsic stellar kinematics of the source. For the surface brightness profile, we use a Sérsic model
(
Sersic 1968
)
, parametrised by the half-light radius
r_{e}
, minor-to-major axis ratio
q
, and Sérsic index
n
. The position in the central shutter (
\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y
), and the position angle (PA) with respect to the shutter, together with the surface brightness profile define which part of the galaxy falls within the MSA shutters. As we are only interested in
v
and
\sigma_{v}
, not the intensity, of the modelled line profiles, we set the total flux
F
of the Sérsic profiles to a fixed number for each generated model.

We use a thin disc model for the velocity field, which is defined using an arctangent rotation curve
(
Courteau 1997
)
:

v(r)=\frac{2}{\pi}v_{a}\arctan\left(\frac{r}{r_{t}}\right),

(1)

where
v_{a}
is the maximum velocity with respect to the systemic velocity of the galaxy, and
r_{t}
is the turnover radius. To allow for a dynamically warm disc, we include a constant velocity dispersion profile
\sigma(r)=\sigma_{0}
. We note that a thin disc model is likely too simplistic of a description of the velocity profile of most galaxies in our sample, and discuss the effects of this assumption in Section
5
.

The model is initialised using the nine parameters described above, by generating flux and velocity profiles to obtain a model flux cube (
I(x,y,\lambda)
) in the MSA plane coordinates (
x,y
). To evaluate the steep Sérsic profile in the center, we oversample the innermost region of the profile (
<0.05r_{e}
) by a factor of 500, and the outer region (
>0.2r_{e}
) by a factor 10. The profile is then integrated onto a coarser grid, with a sampling equal the NIRSpec pixel size (0.01″) or to the Nyquist frequency of the PSF if that is smaller.

To mitigate PSF undersampling effects and ensure that we take spectral information into account across the full wavelength range of the spectrum, we generate three emission lines for each model galaxy, spaced evenly in wavelength. This number is a compromise between model accuracy and computation time. Though including more lines would slightly decrease the uncertainties, this would not affect our overall results.

To model the NIRSpec/MSA PSF we use a custom version of the PSF model described in
de Graaff et al. 2024
, with a factor 2 greater oversampling. This higher oversampling is necessary to ensure the PSF is oversampled compared to Nyquist sampling.
Using
msafit
, the model flux cube is convolved with the PSF following
de Graaff et al. 2024
, and this PSF-convolved model is then projected onto the two
2048\times 2048
pixel NIRSpec detectors, following the trace for the shutter (
s_{ij}
) in which the source was observed. The resulting model spectrum, showing three emission lines, encapsulates slit losses, bar shadows, interpixel capacitance, and spatial undersampling. This model is equivalent to a noiseless, unrectified, single frame NIRSpec/MSA observation.

To convert these single-frame model spectra into combined, rectified model spectra, we generate a model for each nod (i.e. shutter position
s_{ij}
) using the above method. We then apply all reduction steps that were done on the observed data (see Section
2
) to reduce and combine the individual frames. This step results in a single, rectified, combined 2D model spectrum containing the three modelled emission lines. The line profiles of these lines now account for optics and detector effects (from
msafit
), as well as resampling and combination effects (from the data reduction steps).

Finally, we obtain row-by-row line-of-sight velocity (
V_{l,i,\text{mod}}
) and dispersion (
\sigma_{v,l,i,\text{mod}}
) measurements of each line
l
by fitting a Gaussian profile to each row
i
. By taking the median value of
V_{i,\text{mod}}
and
\sigma_{v,i,\text{mod}}
of the three lines, we obtain line-of-sight velocity and dispersion profiles for each model. In Figure
1
we illustrate the line-of-sight velocity profiles for galaxy models with different velocity profiles and slit allignments.

3.4
Parameter inference

msafit
was originally developed to perform
MCMC
sampling. However, the adaptions to the code described in the previous section make it significantly more expensive in both RAM and CPU usage, and thus it is currently unfeasible to use MCMC sampling for our method. We therefore perform the fitting using
scipy.optimize
, evaluated using the
\chi^{2}
metric, which requires fewer models to be generated.

We fit for five parameters in our modelling ([
\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y,v_{a},r_{t},\sigma_{0}
]). The remaining four parameters,
n,q,r_{e},
and PA, are fixed to the
GALFIT
measurements, as the uncertainty in these parameters are small, and the fitting times are significantly reduced with fewer free parameters. We initialise the slit position
\mathrm{d}x
and
\mathrm{d}y
as the astrometric solution in the slit, and let the values vary by 0.75 pixels in either direction, to allow for pointing and astrometric inaccuracies. For each galaxy we estimate the initial guess for
v_{a}
by eye, and set the initial guess for
\sigma_{0}
as the integrated velocity dispersion measured from
ppxf
. We allow
v_{a}
and
\sigma_{0}
to vary 200 km/s around this initial guess in the fit. The choice of initial
v_{a}
and
\sigma_{0}
does not affect the results of our fitting, but a reasonable initial guess speeds up the sampling process. We initialise
r_{t}
as 0.75
r_{e}
, and allow it to vary from 0.05
r_{e}
to 2
r_{e}
.

We perform the fitting using two iterations of
scipy.optimize
. In the first iteration, we use the Powell method, with a tolerance of
10^{-2}
. This sampling method incorporates random jumps within the bounds of the parameter space, to ensure that the sampler explores the full grid, and does not get stuck in a local minimum. For the second iteration the model is initialised at the best-fit parameters of the first iteration, and we then run
scipy.optimize
with the Nelder-Mead method and a tolerance of
10^{-4}
to explore a finer grid around these best-fit values.

We obtain errors on the best-fit model and inferred velocity parameters by creating 2000 simulations of the data, randomly perturbed around the uncertainties of the data points and GALFIT parameters. For each perturbed sample we find the best-fit model and parameters from the models that were generated in the fitting routine. The uncertainties on the model and best-fit parameters are calculated from the standard deviation of all perturbed samples.

4.2
Dynamical masses

From the best-fit kinematic and structural parameters we can derive the total dynamical mass of the galaxies in our sample, as defined in
Price et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{dyn,tot}}=k_{\mathrm{tot}}\frac{v^{2}_{\mathrm{circ}}(r_{e})r_{e}}{G}.

(3)

Here
v_{\mathrm{circ}}^{2}=v^{2}(r_{e})+3.35\ \sigma_{0}^{2}

(
Burkert et al. 2010
)
, where the factor 3.35 accounts for an “asymmetric drift” correction from the velocity dispersion. We discuss this further in Section
5.4
. Since we assume a thin disc model in our dynamic modelling, we use
k_{\mathrm{tot}}=1.8
, which is the virial coefficient for an oblate potential with an intrinsic axis ratio
q_{0}=0.2
and
n\sim 1-4

(
Price et al. 2022
)
. We note that the thin disc model is likely a too simple description for many galaxies in our sample, and the true value of
k_{\mathrm{tot}}
may be higher. In Section
5.4
we further discuss how the thin disc assumption influences our dynamical mass estimates.

The resulting dynamical masses are reported in Table
1
, and shown against stellar masses measured from SPS modeling
(
Slob et al. 2024
)
in Figure
6
. For all galaxies in this study the dynamical masses are higher than the stellar masses, assuming a
Chabrier 2003
IMF, as is expected from the fact that the dynamical masses includes the stellar, gas, and dark matter components of a galaxy. If we assume that the gas mass of the galaxies in our sample is low
(
Williams et al. 2021
;
Belli et al. 2021
, e.g.)
, we find that our sample has a median dark matter fraction of 63% within 1
r_{e}
, with individual galaxies having fractions up to 83%.

The dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios of our sample are
\sim 0.2
dex higher than those of quiescent galaxies with similar masses and redshifts, for the same IMF assumption
(
van de Sande et al. 2013
;
Belli et al. 2017
;
Forrest et al. 2022
;
Kriek et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. In Section
5.2
we discuss this offset further, and also investigate
the implications of our dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios for the IMF.

5
Discussion

5.1
Build-up and quenching of massive galaxies

Figure 7:
V_{r_{e}}/\sigma_{0}
as a function of stellar age for the distant quiescent galaxies in our sample (squares). We also include samples from literature using the same symbols as in Figure
4
. The black dashed line represents a ratio of 1, corresponding to the definition of rotational support. The symbols are coloured by stellar mass. Grey squares indicate galaxies for which we can only obtain lower limits to

.

In the previous section, we showed that the majority of the distant quiescent galaxies in our sample are rotationally supported. Interestingly, the four distant quiescent galaxies at
z>2
for which kinematic properties were previously measured all have higher spin parameters. Moreover, our galaxies are, on average, more rotationally supported than massive nearby galaxies (see Figure
5
). Taken at face value, these results imply that rotational support of quiescent galaxies decreases over cosmic time.

To investigate whether the difference in rotational support between nearby early-type galaxies and distant quiescent galaxies is a genuine redshift effect, we also plot
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
as a function of age in Figure
7
. For our sample we use the ages derived using
prospector

(
Johnson et al. 2021
)
from
Slob et al. 2024
. For the LEGA-C galaxies we use ages from spectral fitting with
alf

(
Conroy & van Dokkum 2012a
;
Conroy et al. 2018
)
presented by
Cheng et al. 2025
.
Beverage et al. 2025
show that the
alf
ages are consistent with the
prospector
ages, and thus, no systematic biases are expected.

Figure
7
shows a trend between
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
and age, with older galaxies being less rotationally supported than young quiescent galaxies. We calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient for the combined LEGA-C, SUSPENSE and
Newman et al. 2018
samples to assess the degree of correlation between
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
and age, and find a negative correlation (
r=-0.40
) with a
p
-value
<0.01
. We quantify this relation between age and rotational support by fitting a linear function, resulting in a best-fit relation of the form

(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}=-0.95\pm 0.13\times\log(\text{Age})+1.4\pm 0.05.

(4)

Although the trend between rotational support and age had already been identified for low-
z
galaxies
(
van de Sande et al. 2018
;
Croom et al. 2024
)
, our results imply that it was already in place at higher redshifts. This trend could also explain why our galaxies are less rotationally supported compared to other quiescent galaxies at
z>2
; they are all relatively old (ages
>1
Gyr), while three out of four quiescent rotating discs from
Newman et al. 2018
and
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
are post-starburst galaxies with ages of
\sim 0.5-0.8
Gyr.

We find no strong trend between stellar mass and
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
for distant quiescent galaxies, with a Pearson-
r
coefficient of
-0.19
. From Figure
5
we also do not see a trend between stellar mass and spin parameter for our sample. These results are in contrast with findings for the spin parameter in the nearby universe (see Figure
5
), where the most massive nearby galaxies are slow rotators, while less massive galaxies are fast rotators. However, we note that the
z>0
samples for which stellar kinematics have been obtained to date have few galaxies with
\log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})<10.5
, so that the dynamic range probed by these studies is small.

The trend between rotational support and age we observe at
z>1
implies that the difference between rotational properties of nearby and distant massive quiescent galaxies is driven by the fact that more distant galaxies are, on average, younger and thus have higher
V/\sigma
ratios. This trend also implies that significant structural changes must take place after a galaxy has stopped forming stars, instead of a complete destruction of the stellar disc during the quenching event. These structural changes likely happen gradually as galaxies evolve, since the rotational support in
z\sim 0.7
LEGA-C galaxies is not significantly lower than the oldest SUSPENSE galaxies.

This gradual decline of rotational support in the quiescent population is consistent with a scenario where dry mergers “spin down” galaxies after quenching. Cosmological simulations also show that multiple consecutive dry mergers are the main mechanism responsible for generating the low-
z
population of massive slow rotators
(
Penoyre et al. 2017
;
Schulze et al. 2018
;
Dubois et al. 2016
;
Rodriguez-Gomez et al. 2017
;
Lagos et al. 2018
;
Lagos et al. 2018
, e.g.)
. While both major and minor mergers can be responsible for a decrease in stellar angular momentum, the cumulative effect of a series of dry minor mergers can gradually and more effectively decrease angular momentum compared to a single major merger
(
Naab et al. 2014
;
Lagos et al. 2018
)
. These mergers do not only slow down rotation, but can also explain the size growth
(
van Dokkum et al. 2010
;
Bezanson et al. 2009
;
Patel et al. 2013
, e.g.)
and the build-up of the blue and metal-poor outskirts of massive galaxies
(
Suess et al. 2020
;
Martín-Navarro et al. 2018
;
Cheng et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. The finding that distant quiescent galaxies have many small companions
(
Newman et al. 2012
;
Suess et al. 2023
)
also directly supports the minor merger scenario.

If we assume that the galaxies in our sample are the direct progenitors of massive galaxies at
z\sim 0
and are representative of the general population at cosmic noon, we can estimate how their rotational properties will evolve up to
z\sim 0
. The galaxies for which we measure rotational support have a median stellar mass of
\log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})\sim 11
, and a median
\lambda_{r_{e}}=0.28
. Based on number density arguments, we expect these galaxies to grow
\sim 0.3
dex in stellar mass since cosmic noon
(
van Dokkum et al. 2010
;
Muzzin et al. 2013
;
Hill et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. We take all galaxies from the SAMI, ATLAS3D, and MASSIVE surveys with masses
\log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})=11.15-11.45
, which are the likely descendents of the SUSPENSE galaxies, and find that the median spin parameter of this population is
\lambda_{r_{e}}=0.14
. While the above calculation is only a rough estimate, and the evolution of individual galaxies in our sample will be varied, we can conclude that, on average, the spin parameter of massive quiescent galaxies should decrease by a factor
\sim 2
between cosmic noon and
z\sim 0
. Thus, the majority of our population of fast-rotating quiescent galaxies at cosmic noon will likely gradually evolve into the (cores of) old, slow-rotating early-type galaxies. However, we also note that some massive quiescent fast-rotators still exist by
z\sim 0
, such that some of our galaxies may still be fast-rotators by
z\sim 0
.

Our results show that significant structural changes occur after quenching. However, the relatively low
V/\sigma
ratios (
V/\sigma\sim 1
) of our sample compared to cold and ionised gas kinematics of star-forming disc galaxies of similar masses at
z>2

(
Förster Schreiber et al. 2009
;
Epinat et al. 2009
;
Wisnioski et al. 2015
;
Wisnioski et al. 2019
;
Neeleman et al. 2020
;
Rizzo et al. 2021
;
Fraternali et al. 2021
;
Parlanti et al. 2023
;
Danhaive et al. 2025
,
V/\sigma\gtrsim 3
, e.g.)
suggest that some loss of rotational support may occur during star-formation quenching. Due to the fact that the galaxies in our sample are relatively old (ages
>1
Gyr), it is not straightforward to discern if this decrease in
V/\sigma
occurred during quenching, or in the period between quenching and the time of observation. It is also unclear how the stellar kinematics compare to the gas kinematics in distant star-forming galaxies, although at
z\sim 1
stellar kinematics lead to significantly lower
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
ratios compared to gas kinematics
(
Übler et al. 2024
)
.

Nonetheless, even the younger quiescent rotating discs from
Newman et al. 2018
have
V/\sigma
ratios that are low compared to high-redshift star-forming galaxies.
Newman et al. 2018
argue that while quenching through major mergers could explain this observed decrease in rotational support, the flattened shapes and lack of significant bulges in their galaxies imply that this scenario is likely too simplistic. Furthermore, we note that from their rotational velocities, our galaxies fall on the
z\sim 1
stellar-mass Tully-Fisher relation for star-forming galaxies
(
Conselice et al. 2005
)
. This finding implies that the difference in
V/\sigma
between star-forming and quiescent galaxies is driven by an increase in the velocity dispersion, rather than a decrease of the rotational velocity.
In a future study, we plan to test this hypothesis by measuring ionised gas and stellar kinematics of star-forming galaxies beyond
z>1
using the method described in this work.

5.2
Implications for the IMF

In the previous section we showed that our distant quiescent galaxies likely grow inside-out into low-
z
early-type galaxies through (mostly) minor mergers. Our results thus imply that the galaxies in our sample are expected to become the cores of massive galaxies today. These cores of the most massive (dispersion-dominated) galaxies are found to have a more bottom heavy IMF compared to the Milky Way
(
Treu et al. 2010
;
Conroy & van Dokkum 2012b
;
Cappellari et al. 2012
;
Tortora et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. To assess whether our dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios would allow for a bottom-heavy
\sigma_{v}
-dependent IMF, we calculate
\log(M_{*})
assuming the relation from
Treu et al. 2010
. For this relation, the IMF is more bottom heavy than the Salpeter IMF for galaxies with
\sigma_{v}>250
km/s. We show the resulting stellar masses against the dynamical masses as the blue circles in Figure
6
. The
\sigma_{v}
-dependent IMF assumption results in a median dynamical-to-stellar mass ratio of 2.5 for our sample, implying a dark matter fraction of 59% within one
r_{e}
. Furthermore, while for this IMF assumption two galaxies in our sample have stellar masses that exceed the dynamical mass, all galaxies have stellar masses that are consistent with the dynamical mass within 1
\sigma
.

Previous ground-based studies of distant quiescent galaxies showed that stellar masses calculated using the
\sigma_{v}
-dependent IMF from
Treu et al. 2010
result in median dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios
<1

(
Mendel et al. 2020
;
Kriek et al. 2024
;
Forrest et al. 2022
)
. Thus, these studies claimed that the bottom-heavy IMF observed today could not be in place yet in distant quiescent galaxies. However, in this work we find dynamical masses that allow for a
\sigma_{v}
-dependent IMF, which implies distant quiescent galaxies can become the cores of nearby elliptical galaxies.

The relatively high dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios we derive compared to previous ground-based studies are likely the result of a combination of factors. First, NIRCam provides longer wavelengths, higher spatial resolution, and deeper imaging compared to HST, enabling more robust structural measurements and resulting dynamical masses. Secondly, previous studies relied on spectra with lower
S/N
, potentially biasing their velocity dispersions and resulting dynamical masses. Our high
S/N
spectra also allow for more accurate
M/L
ratio calibrations compared to ground-based studies, from which we can measure more reliable stellar masses. Moreover, our detailed kinematic modelling, including rotation, likely results in more reliable dynamical masses compared to those inferred from integrated velocity dispersions. Altogether, a range of observational challenges, affecting both structural and spectral measurements, may be responsible for the lower dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios found in earlier work.

5.3
Using virial relations for NIRSpec/MSA spectra?

When no resolved kinematic measurements are available, dynamical masses can be calculated from the observed velocity dispersion of the integrated spectrum,
\sigma_{v,\text{int spec}}
, using virial relations. Here we assess how these dynamical masses compare to the dynamical masses from our kinematic models. We use the integrated velocity dispersions from
Beverage et al. 2025
for our sample to calculate the dynamical masses as

M_{\text{dyn, int spec}}=\frac{K(n)\ \sigma^{2}_{v,\text{ int spec}}\ r_{e}}{G}.

(5)

Here
K(n)
is the virial coefficient defined in
Cappellari et al. 2006
. In Figure
8
we show these dynamical masses (
\log(M_{\text{dyn, int spec}})
) against the dynamical masses we calculate from our kinematic models (
\log(M_{\text{dyn, kin model}})
) in Section
4.2
. We find that, on average, the dynamical masses from our kinematic models are
0.11_{-0.14}^{+0.18}
dex higher than the dynamical masses from the integrated spectra.

Figure 8:
Dynamical masses calculated from the integrated velocity dispersion (
\log(M_{\text{dyn, int spec}})
) against dynamical masses calculated from our kinematic models (
\log(M_{\text{dyn, kin model}})
). The dashed line indicates a one-to-one ratio. Pink squares indicate galaxies for which only lower limits to the rotational velocity could be obtained.

The large offset between the two dynamical mass estimates can be attributed to the fact that the integrated velocity dispersions have not been corrected for the aperture losses of the NIRSpec/MSA slits. An aperture correction has been defined for slit-based observations for both rotation-dominated
(
Price et al. 2014
)
and dispersion-dominated sources
(
van de Sande et al. 2013
)
, which can increase the integrated velocity dispersion by a factor up to
\sim 1.4
in rotation dominated systems. However, as most sources that are observed with the NIRSpec/MSA are not centred and aligned within the MSA shutter, defining an aperture correction for NIRSpec/MSA observations is non-trivial. Thus, dynamical masses derived from the observed integrated velocity dispersion of NIRSpec/MSA spectra are likely underestimated. This is especially true for the extended galaxies in our sample, with effective radii that span across multiple shutters for many sources in our sample.
Using our kinematic models we assess how big the offset is between the velocity dispersion of the integrated spectrum (
\sigma_{v,\text{int spec}}
) and the intrinsic line-of-sight integrated velocity dispersion within 1 effective radius (
\sigma_{v,r_{e}}
).

To compute
\sigma_{v,r_{e}}
, we assume that the observed integrated velocity dispersion is equal to
V^{2}_{\text{rms}}(r)=\sigma_{0}^{2}+V_{\text{los}}^{2}(r)
, following
Cappellari 2008
. Here
\sigma_{0}
is the best-fit velocity dispersion, and
V_{\text{los}}(r)
is the best-fit line of sight rotational velocity from our modelling (see the bottom right panels in Figure
3
). From this line-of-sight
V_{\text{rms}}(r)
we calculate
\sigma_{v,r_{e}}
by integrating over the elliptical aperture corresponding to 1
r_{e}
, and weighting by the Sérsic profile of the galaxy, following
van de Sande et al. 2013
.

In Figure
9
we show the velocity dispersion of the integrated spectrum (
\sigma_{v,\text{int spec}}
), taken from
Beverage et al. 2025
, against the inferred integrated line-of-sight velocity dispersion within 1
r_{e}
as derived from our kinematic model (
\sigma_{v,r_{e}}
) for the galaxies in our sample. As expected, the velocity dispersion within 1
r_{e}
is systematically higher than the velocity dispersion of the integrated spectrum, with a median offset of
33_{-34}^{+47}
km s
-1
. The errors on the median are calculated using Monte Carlo simulations of the data. In Appendix
C
we show that the inferred dynamical masses from the aperture corrected velocity dispersions are consistent with the dynamical masses from the kinematic models.

Our results show that caution is needed when deriving dynamical masses using the integrated velocity dispersion of NIRSpec/MSA spectra without applying an aperture correction, as significant offsets between
\sigma_{v,\text{int spec}}
and
\sigma_{v,r_{e}}
are expected if a source is large compared to the size of the micro-shutter.
We note that this bias from the observed integrated velocity dispersion is also expected for measurements of emission line kinematics of star-forming galaxies observed using NIRSpec/MSA, such that dynamical masses calculated from emission lines are also sensitive to this effect.

5.4
Caveats

In this work we present the kinematic properties of fifteen distant quiescent galaxies from their resolved MSA spectra using a forward modelling approach that takes into account source position and alignment with respect to the MSA shutters. For all ten galaxies for which we can constrain the rotational velocity, we measure strong rotation (
V_{r_{e}}>114
km s
-1
). To assess the significance of the rotational support in these galaxies, we compute the intrinsic velocity as a function of radius from Equation (
1
), for 2000 simulations of
v_{a}
,
r_{e}
, and
r_{t}/r_{e}
, randomly perturbed around their uncertainties. We show these simulated
v(r)
curves for the ten galaxies in Appendix
D
, with the 1
\sigma
and 2
\sigma
levels indicated in pink and red, respectively. This test illustrates that the large errors we find for
V_{r_{e}}
in part of our sample are not only driven by uncertainties in our kinematic modelling, but is also strongly dependent on the uncertainty of the measurement of
r_{e}
. Nonetheless, from the 2
\sigma
confidence intervals we can conclude that all ten galaxies are significantly rotating.

Interestingly, we find that the galaxies that are most aligned along the major axis with respect to the MSA shutter have the highest
V/\sigma
measurements. This finding suggests that the degree of rotational support we find for galaxies that are slightly misaligned within their shutter may be underestimated. Detailed IFU observations or duplicate slit-based observations under multiple telescope angles are thus needed to confirm if our forward modelling approach systematically underestimates the rotational velocities of misaligned galaxies. However, our overall conclusions would not change qualitatively if the rotational support of the galaxies in our sample is systematically higher.

Finally, the thin disc assumption that we made in our modelling may bias the dynamical mass and
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
calculations. For a thin disc model, the virial coefficient in Equation (
3
) is
k_{\text{tot}}=1.8
. However, based on the high velocity dispersions (
\sigma_{0}>250
km/s) of some of the galaxies in our sample, it is more likely that these galaxies are thick disks, with more complicated dynamical profiles. For thick discs, the virial coefficient
k_{\text{tot}}
is higher
(
Price et al. 2022
)
, which would result in larger inferred dynamical masses. However, our kinematic modelling results and
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
likely also change if we allow for intrinsically thick discs, as a thin disc assumption maximises
V(r)
. Another limitation to the thin disc parametrisation is the assumption that
\sigma_{v}
is radially invariant, while in reality the velocity dispersion may be lower in the outskirts of these galaxies. To fully assess how our thin disc model parametrisation affects the inferred dynamical masses and
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
we plan to model our sample using JAM methods
(
Cappellari 2008
)
in a future study. JAM methods assume a variable intrinsic thickness in the models, and will allow us to further constrain the dynamical properties. We also note that JAM methods will allow us to calibrate the “asymmetric drift” parameter for stars used in our dynamical mass calculations, further decreasing the uncertainties on the dynamical masses.

6
Conclusions

We present the kinematic properties of fifteen distant (
z=1.2-2.3
) quiescent galaxies from the JWST-SUSPENSE program, the largest sample of kinematic measurements of quiescent galaxies at
z>1.2
to date. The galaxies were observed for 16 hours in a single NIRSpec/MSA configuration, and the resulting ultra-deep, medium-resolution spectra resolve numerous stellar absorption lines in both the spatial and dispersion direction. From these absorption line spectra we measured the stellar velocity and velocity dispersion profiles.

As the MSA shutters are misaligned and mis-centred with respect to the galaxies, the observed kinematics cannot be directly interpreted. Instead, we derive the intrinsic kinematics using a forward modelling approach, making use of the high-resolution images from JWST/NIRCam COSMOS-Web
(
Casey et al. 2023
)
and HST/F814W
(
Scoville et al. 2007
)
. In our forward modelling approach we take into account source morphologies and positions with respect to the shutters, the NIRSpec PSF, and optical as well as resampling effects from the data reduction, and assume a thin disc model. From the inferred velocity and velocity dispersion profiles we quantify the degree of rotational support in our sample using
V_{r_{e}}
and
(V/\sigma)_{r_{e}}
. For ten galaxies we can constrain the rotational velocities, and we find that all have significant rotation. The remaining five galaxies are too misaligned with respect to the MSA shutter to constrain their rotational velocity. Moreover, all ten galaxies with measured stellar rotation are classified as fast rotators from their position in the
\lambda_{r_{e}}-\epsilon
plane.

Compared to massive early-type galaxies in the nearby universe, we find that distant quiescent galaxies are, on average, more rotationally supported. We find a trend between rotational support and age in the combined LEGA-C, SUSPENSE, and
Newman et al. 2018
samples at
z\approx 0.5-2.5
, with young galaxies having more rotational support. This age trend is thus a likely explanation for the increased rotational support we find at high-
z
, where galaxies are, on average, younger than in the nearby universe. Surprisingly, we find no trend between stellar mass and rotational support at
z>0.5
.

Our findings imply that distant quiescent galaxies are still rotating disks just after they stopped forming stars. Thus, the physical process responsible for quenching star formation in massive galaxies likely did not disrupt rotational support. Instead, to explain the population of slow-rotating massive quiescent galaxies at low
z
, significant structural changes must occur after the galaxies have already become quiescent. Our results support a scenario in which galaxies experience a series of (mostly minor) dry mergers after quenching. These mergers lead to growth in both mass and size, and gradually disturb the ordered rotation of galaxies. We cannot rule out though that the galaxies in our sample may have lost part of their specific angular momentum during quenching, as their
V/\sigma
values are lower than the
V/\sigma
values (inferred from gas kinematics) of massive star-forming galaxies at
z>2
. On the other hand, the rotational velocities are similar to those of star-forming galaxies of similar masses, implying that the low
V/\sigma
ratios are mostly driven by an increase in the velocity dispersion.

From the kinematic and structural properties we also derive dynamical masses. The resulting dynamical masses imply dark matter fractions of
\sim 63\%
within 1
r_{e}
for our galaxies, assuming a
Chabrier 2003
IMF. The dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios of our sample are higher compared to previous studies, and in contrast to these studies, our dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios do allow for a bottom-heavy (
\sigma_{v}
-dependent) IMF. This finding implies that distant quiescent galaxies can evolve into the cores of nearby massive early-type galaxies, which are found to have more bottom-heavy IMFs. Furthermore, using our kinematic models we find that the observed integrated velocity dispersions from our NIRSpec/MSA spectra are
\sim 10
% lower than the inferred integrated line-of-sight velocity dispersions from our models. These results show that extreme caution is needed when using integrated velocity dispersions from NIRSpec/MSA spectra to calculate dynamical masses, as significant biases can be introduced by not applying the relevant aperture corrections.

Our study demonstrates the power of the NIRSpec/MSA to obtain stellar kinematic properties of large samples of massive quiescent (and possibly also star-forming) galaxies at and beyond cosmic noon. This work is enabled by ultra-deep integration times, our custom observing strategy (large nods and long slits), and the identification of a field of many bright quiescent targets. To further improve the constraints on stellar kinematics beyond
z=1
, larger samples are needed. Although archival data could be used to this end, the shorter integration times and smaller spatial coverage along the MSA shutters of most MSA programs observed to date make this challenging. Thus, future targeted larger and deeper surveys, with a similar observing strategy as the JWST-SUSPENSE program, may be required to obtain stellar kinematics for larger samples of distant galaxies. Moreover, to assess possible biases in our forward modelling method, especially for galaxies that are misaligned with respect to the MSA shutter, detailed IFU observations are needed. Finally, the method presented in this paper offers a promising avenue to combine stellar kinematic measurements with direct measurements of the IMF using the NIRSpec/MSA, paving the way for robust probes of dark matter halos of quiescent galaxies beyond the low-
z
universe (see approved JWST program 5629).
