Abstract
Best-of- n n (BoN) sampling is a practical approach for aligning language model outputs with human preferences without expensive fine-tuning. BoN sampling is performed by generating n n responses to a prompt and then selecting the sample that maximizes a reward function. BoN yields high reward values in practice at a distortion cost, as measured by the KL-divergence between the sampled and original distribution. This distortion is coarsely controlled by varying the number of samples: larger n n yields a higher reward at a higher distortion cost. We introduce Soft Best-of- n n sampling, a generalization of BoN that allows for smooth interpolation between the original distribution and reward-maximizing distribution through a temperature parameter λ \lambda . We establish theoretical guarantees showing that Soft Best-of- n n sampling converges sharply to the optimal tilted distribution at a rate of O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) in KL and the expected (relative) reward. For sequences of discrete outputs, we analyze an additive reward model that reveals the fundamental limitations of blockwise sampling.

I
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are a type of generative machine learning model that, given a string of input tokens, output a distribution
P
that approximates the likelihood of the next tokens in a sequence.
By learning from large corpora of text data, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding, reasoning, and generation across a range of domains. However, training models for accurate next token prediction does not explicitly optimize for
alignment
between the model’s outputs and human preferences
[
1
,
2
]
. As a result, the behavior of LLMs may not always conform to the intentions or expectations of human users
[
3
,
4
]
.

Given a reference distribution
P
over a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
and a reward function
r:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a popular approach to address the
alignment problem
is via an optimization formulation that is essentially equivalent to the well-known information projection optimization
[
5
,
6
]
. The goal is to find a new distribution
P^{*}
that is similar to
P
while maximizing the expected reward:

\displaystyle\max_{P^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}}~\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}}\left[r(X)\right]\ \mbox{subject to }D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*}\|P)\leq\epsilon.

(1)

In LLMs,
P
is the distribution of whole responses given the prompt (often referred to as “policy” in the alignment literature) and
r
is a proxy model for the human preferences that we seek to align
P
with. The function
r
itself is usually another language model trained to score or rate responses
[
7
]
. In theory, the solution to this problem is a tilted distribution
[
6
]
, which adjusts
P
to place more weight on high-reward responses. The optimal distribution is given by:

P^{*}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{P(x)e^{r(x)/\lambda}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r(X)/\lambda}]}.

(2)

where
\lambda>0
controls the trade-off between reward maximization and the divergence from the reference distribution.
Variations of information projection and tilted distributions are common in information theory
[
8
,
9
,
5
,
10
]
and have appeared across various fields, including Monte Carlo simulation, large deviations theory, and econometrics
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
; see also
[
18
]
.

In generative models, we do not have an analytical expression for
P
nor
r(X)
, and thus cannot compute
P^{*}
directly; instead, we can only sample from
P
and evaluate
r(X)
for individual samples. When limited to only samples from
P
, there are generally two strategies for alignment: model fine-tuning using reinforcement learning to maximize reward with a KL penalty
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
, and inference-time methods such as Best-of-
n
(BoN) which samples
n
generations from
P
and then picks the sequence with the highest reward. While fine-tuning methods require human-annotated preference data and additional GPU-intensive training, BoN has emerged as a simple yet effective approach to alignment.

Empirically, BoN has been shown to be competitive with fine-tuning approaches in terms of KL-reward tradeoffs
[
22
,
23
]
, and theoretically, it has been shown to be equivalent to the fine-tuned solution asymptotically
[
24
]
while
[
25
]
bounds the sub-optimality of BoN in the non-asymptotic regime. However, BoN always selects the reward-maximizing generation, limiting our ability to navigate and control the KL-reward tradeoff.

We introduce
Soft Best-of-
n
sampling, a generalization of BoN sampling that provides non-asymptotic guarantees for approximating the optimal tilted distribution
P_{\lambda}^{*}
with a finite number of samples
n
. While BoN can be viewed as a special case of Soft Best-of-
n
, the latter offers a more flexible approach by incorporating a temperature parameter
\lambda
. This allows for a smooth interpolation between the reference distribution
P
and the reward-maximizing distribution
P_{\lambda^{*}}
, enabling finer control over the KL-reward tradeoff.

In this paper, we focus on deriving theoretical guarantees on the performance of Soft Best-of-
n
sampling relative to the optimal distribution
P_{\lambda}^{*}
. Our main contributions are:

•

We establish sharp bounds on the convergence rate of Soft Best-of-
n
sampling to the optimal tilted distribution
P_{\lambda}^{*}
, showing that the KL-divergence between the two and the relative difference in reward both decrease at a rate of
O(1/n)
(Theorems
1
and
3
) with explicit constants depending on the temperature parameter. We also provide a converse result showing that this rate cannot be improved in general (Theorem
2
).

•

We analyze the trade-off between reward maximization and approximating
P_{\lambda}^{*}
and demonstrate that, unlike BoN, Soft Best-of-
n
sampling can provably span the optimal KL-reward Pareto frontier. We show that, as long as
\lambda
is not too small (at least
O(1/\log(n\epsilon)
), the Soft Best-of-
n
sampling is
\epsilon
-close in KL-divergence to
P_{\lambda}^{*}
(Corollary
1
).

•

We study an additive reward model that provides insights into the limitations of using Best-of-
n
with blockwise sampling and motivates efficient sampling alternatives.

II
Preliminaries

We use uppercase letters (e.g.,
X
) to denote random variables and lowercase letters (e.g.,
x
) for their realizations. We use superscripts to denote sequence length (e.g.,
x^{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
) and subscripts to denote sequence elements (e.g.,
x_{i}
is the
i
-the element of sequence
x^{m}
). For a finite set
\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
the probability simplex over
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{P:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]\;\middle|\;\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)=1\right\}
. We define the
coefficient of variation
of a random variable
X
with finite mean and variance as
\mathsf{CV}(X)\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{Var}(X)}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}}
.

Let
P
be a distribution over a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
and
r:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
P
-measurable reward function. Recall that the goal of alignment is to find a new distribution
P^{*}
that is similar to
P
but also maximizes the expected reward (
1
), while being limited to only samples from
P
.

A popular approach to this problem is
Best-of-
n
(BoN) sampling

[
20
]
, which draws
n
samples from
P
and returns the one with the highest reward.

Definition 1
.

For a fixed integer
n\geq 1
, the
Best-of-
n
sampling strategy consists of:

1.

Draw
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
i.i.d. from
P
;

2.

Compute
R_{1}=r(X_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}=r(X_{n})
;

3.

Return
Y=X_{Z}
, where
Z=\arg\max_{j=1,\ldots,n}R_{j}
.

While effective, BoN sampling can be viewed as an extreme case of a more general framework. We propose
Soft Best-of-
n
sampling
, which offers a smoother approach to trading off between reward maximization and distribution similarity.

Definition 2
.

For a fixed integer
n\geq 1
and
\lambda>0
, the
Soft Best-of-
n
sampling
strategy consists of:

1.

Draw
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
i.i.d. from
P
;

2.

Draw
Z
from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with distribution

\Pr(Z=i)=\frac{e^{r(X_{i})/\lambda}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{r(X_{j})/\lambda}};

(3)

3.

Return
Y=X_{Z}
.

The distribution of
Y
produced by the Soft Best-of-
n
sampling for a fixed
\lambda>0
is denoted by
P_{n,\lambda}
.

Here,
\lambda>0
is a temperature parameter that controls the trade-off between reward maximization and similarity to the original distribution
P
. As
\lambda\to 0
, Soft Best-of-
n
sampling converges to BoN sampling, making the latter a special case of our more general framework. This relationship can also be understood through the lens of the “softmax” nature of the sampling probabilities: as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, the softmax becomes increasingly sharp, converging to the argmax function that characterizes BoN sampling. Conversely, as
\lambda\to\infty
, the sampling becomes uniform among the
n
candidates, effectively recovering the original distribution
P
. Also, observe that Soft Best-of-
n
sampling only requires i.i.d. samples from
P
and computation of
r(X_{i})
for the selected samples.
The distribution induced by Soft Best-of-
n
sampling approximates the optimal solution (
2
) to problem (
1
), i.e., for any fixed
\lambda
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, it converges to
P^{*}_{\lambda}
.

III
Guarantees for Soft Best-of-
n
Sampling

This section aims to characterize the convergence rate (in KL-divergence) of
P_{n,\lambda}\to P^{*}_{\lambda}
with
n
. Our main result shows that the KL divergence between these distributions decreases at a rate of
O(1/n)
. Naturally, convergence will depend on the value of
\lambda
: if
\lambda\ll 1
, then
n
has to be large for
P_{n,\lambda}\approx P^{*}_{\lambda}
. To see why, as
\lambda\to 0
,
P_{\lambda}^{*}
converges to the distribution where the output
x^{*}
corresponding to the highest reward
r(x^{*})
has probability 1. If we aim to approximate this optimal distribution via
P_{n,\lambda}
, then
n
has to be sufficiently large such that the output
x^{*}
will be sampled with high probability, so
n>1/P(x^{*})
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
\lambda>0
,
r_{\lambda}(x)=r(x)/\lambda
, and
X_{1},...,X_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}P
. For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the distribution
P_{n,\lambda}
of the sample resulting from Soft Best-of-
n
sampling satisfies

\displaystyle P_{n,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=P(x)e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}\times\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}\left(e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}e^{r_{\lambda}(X_{i})}\right)}\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\geq P(x)e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}\times\left(\frac{1}{n}e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}+\frac{n-1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}\right]\right)^{-1}.

(5)

Proof sketch of Lemma
1
.

The proof exploits symmetry in the definition of
P_{n,\lambda}(x)
. This symmetry allows to reduce the analysis to the case where
Z=1
as the chosen sample in Defn.
2
(up to a scaling factor of
n
). The proof then follows by computing
\Pr(Z=1|X_{1}=x)\times P_{\lambda}^{*}(x),
yielding (
4
), then applying Jensen’s inequality, resulting in the lower bound (
5
).
∎

Theorem 1
.

For
\lambda>0
and integer
n\geq 1
,

D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P_{\lambda}^{*}\|P_{n,\lambda}\right)\leq\log\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{CV}\left(e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}\right)^{2}\right).

(6)

Consequently,
D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P_{\lambda}^{*}\|P_{n,\lambda}\right)=O(1/n)
. Moreover, if
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then

D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sinh\left(\frac{1}{2\lambda}\right)^{2}.

(7)

Proof sketch of Theorem
1
.

The key insight is to leverage the explicit form of
P_{n,\lambda}
derived in Lemma
1
. We can thus bound the KL divergence

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda}\right)=\sum_{x}P^{*}_{\lambda}(x)\log\left(\frac{P^{*}_{\lambda}(x)}{P_{n,\lambda}(x)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\frac{P(x)e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}\log\left(\frac{1/\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}{1/\left(\frac{1}{n}e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}+\frac{n-1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}\right]\right)}\right)

Using Jensen’s inequality, we obtain:

D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})\leq\log\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\text{CV}\left(e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}\right)^{2}\right)

where
CV
denotes the coefficient of variation. When
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
, we can further bound the coefficient of variation using properties of exponential functions and the Bhatia-Davis inequality
[
36
]
to obtain the bound
\mathsf{CV}\left(e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{(e^{1/\lambda}-1)^{2}}{4e^{1/\lambda}}
, leading to (
7
).
∎

The bounds in Theorem
1
can be inverted to derive a sufficient condition
\lambda
to guarantee that
P_{n,\lambda}
is “close” (in KL-divergence) to the optimal tilted distribution
P_{\lambda}^{*}
.

Corollary 1
.

For
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
and fixed
n\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
, if
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{\log(1+4n\epsilon)},
then
D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda}\right)\leq\epsilon
.

sampling converges to BoN sampling as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, and it is precisely in this regime where our results do not guarantee near-optimality. For a fixed

).

D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P_{\lambda}^{*}\|P_{n,\lambda}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\frac{p(1-p)(e^{1/\lambda}-1)^{2}}{(pe^{1/\lambda}+(1-p))^{2}}

Next, we show that the
O(1/n)
decay rate in KL-divergence is tight. To show this result, we focus on a lower bound for
\mathsf{TV}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})
. The next theorem establishes a lower bound of
O(1/\sqrt{n})
on the convergence rate of
\mathsf{TV}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})
which, via Pinsker’s inequality, gives an
O(1/n)
lower bound on
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda}),
though the TV convergence rate is of independent interest.

Theorem 2
.

For
\lambda>0
and integer
n\geq 1
, the following bounds hold on the TV distance:

\mathsf{TV}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})\geq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n-1}},

(8)

where
C^{\prime}
is a constant depending on
\lambda
, the reward function
r
, and the second and third moments of
e^{r(X)/\lambda}
.

Proof sketch of Theorem
2
.

We bound the total variation distance between
P^{*}_{\lambda}
and
P_{n,\lambda}
by considering large enough
n
and applying the Berry-Esseen theorem
[
37
,
38
]
. Let
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r(X)}].
By carefully examining the selection probability in step 2 of Definition 2, we can show that for any
\delta<1-\frac{1}{\mu}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\frac{P(x)e^{r(x)}}{\mu}|1-L_{n}(x)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta}{4}-\frac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n-1}}-O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\right)

where
L_{n}(x)
captures the sampling dynamics and
C^{\prime}
is a constant depending on the reward properties. The result follows by optimizing the choice of
\delta
. By applying Pinsker’s inequality
[
39
]
, we derive the bound
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})\geq\Omega(1/n)
.
∎

Next, we derive a bound on the convergence of the expected reward under
P_{n,\lambda}
relative to
P_{\lambda}^{*}
, assuming that
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
. One strategy is to leverage the result of Theorem
1
and directly apply (yet again) Pinsker’s inequality, which would yield an upper-bound on total variation and, consequently, on the expectation of any bounded reward function
r(X)
. However, this route renders a convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{n})
in reward. The next bound proves a
relative
convergence rate of
O(1/n)
in expected reward, albeit at the cost of additional constants. We focus on bounding only one side of the reward, i.e., how much greater the optimal reward under
P_{\lambda}^{*}
is relative to
P_{n,\lambda}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
and
M(\lambda)\triangleq(e^{1/\lambda}/\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}])-1.
Then

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\lambda}^{*}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n,\lambda}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\lambda}^{*}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]}\leq\frac{M(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)+n}.

(9)

In particular, the reward achieved by Soft Best-of-
n
sampling converges to that of the tilted distribution with relative error
O(1/n).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

For
P_{\lambda}^{*}
given in (
2
),
P_{n,\lambda}
given in (
3
),
r(x)\geq 0
, and applying (
5
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{\lambda}^{*}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n,\lambda}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\frac{P(x)e^{r_{\lambda}}(x)r_{\lambda}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}\left(1-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}{\frac{1}{n}e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}+\frac{n-1}{n}E_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}\right)

Since
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
, we have
e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}\leq e^{1/\lambda}
for all
x
and:

\frac{e^{r_{\lambda}(x)}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}\leq\frac{e^{1/\lambda}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)}]}=M(\lambda)+1

(10)

The relative difference in expected rewards can be bounded:

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}_{\lambda}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n,\lambda}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]}{\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}_{\lambda}}[r_{\lambda}(X)]}\leq\frac{M(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)+n}

As
\lambda
increases, both the numerator
e^{1/\lambda}
and
\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r(X)/\lambda}]
in
M(\lambda)
decrease and approach 1, causing
M(\lambda)\rightarrow 0
. This leads to faster convergence in the relative error bound, albeit at the cost of reduced reward discrimination between sequences.
∎

This improved convergence comes at a cost: larger values of
\lambda
also reduce the effective reward difference between sequences (since we are dividing rewards by
\lambda
), making the sampling distribution closer to the original distribution
P
. This creates a fundamental trade-off: smaller
\lambda
values allow for more aggressive optimization of the reward but require more samples for convergence (as
M(\lambda)
is large), while larger
\lambda
values provide faster convergence but result in more conservative optimization (as
M(\lambda)
is small).
Our results highlight that there is a delicate balance between
\lambda
and
n
in order to ensure that
P_{n,\lambda}
approximates
P_{\lambda}^{*}
. Such a balance is not feasible in general when restricted to BoN sampling.

IV
The Additive Reward Model

We analyze next a simplified setting in which the reward of a sequence of outcomes is additive (or “separable” in rate-distortion parlance). Consider an i.i.d. source
P_{X}^{\otimes{m}}
over
\mathcal{X}^{m}
and a reward function
r:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
. The reward of a sequence
x^{m}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
is given by
r(x^{m})\triangleq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}r(x_{i})
.
Of course, for an LLM, the source will absolutely not be i.i.d., and the reward will almost certainly not be additive. Nevertheless, this model will help us to elucidate the limitations of best-of-
n
sampling and motivate controlled decoding strategies. We consider two different sampling strategies to maximize reward:
symbolwise
, where each symbol is sampled
n
times and selected based on their respective reward, and
blockwise
, where instead each block of
m
symbols is sampled
n
times.

A. Tilting Symbols vs. Tilting Blocks.
When both
P
and
r
are known, the same KL-divergence vs. reward trade-off curve is spanned by either solving (
1
) at the individual symbol level or for a sequence of
m
symbols. To see why this is the case, consider blockwise counterpart of (
1
), given by the optimization

\displaystyle\max_{P^{*m}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}^{m}}}~\mathbb{E}_{P^{*m}}\left[r(X)\right]\ \mbox{subject to }D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*m}\|P^{\otimes m})\leq\epsilon.

(11)

Naturally, the optimal solution
P^{*m}
is of the form

\displaystyle P^{*m}_{\lambda}(x^{m})=\frac{P^{\otimes m}(x^{m})e^{r(x^{m})/\lambda}}{\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes m}}[e^{r(X^{m})/\lambda}]}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{P(x_{i})e^{r(x_{i})/m\lambda}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r(X)/m\lambda}]}

(12)

and
P^{*m}_{\lambda}=P^{*\otimes m}_{m\lambda}
.

The simple derivation above shows that to maximize reward subject to a KL constraint for the additive reward model, we can either (i) sample
m
symbols i.i.d. from
P_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}
or (ii) sample a block of size
m
from
P^{*m}_{\lambda}
. As long as
\lambda^{\prime}=m\lambda
, both solutions render exactly the same reward and KL-divergence relative to
P^{\otimes m}
. However, as we show in the next subsections, this is not the case when we approximate this trade-off curve via BoN or Soft Best-of-
n
sampling! A significant performance gap exists between applying Best-of-
n
at a symbol or block level. Next, we analyze why block-level BoN sampling becomes inefficient as the block length increases.

B. Blockwise Best-of-
n
sampling.
Let
\mathbb{E}_{P}[r(X)]=\mu
be the average reward under the base distribution. For a sequence of length
m
, we analyze the probability of finding rewards significantly above this mean through blockwise sampling.

Proposition 1
.

Let
Y^{m}
be a sequence produced via best-of-
n
sampling of
n
sequences drawn from
P_{X}^{\otimes{m}}
. Then, for
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(r(Y^{m})\geq\mu+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log n}{2m}}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\left(1-n^{-\alpha}\right)^{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }0<\alpha<1,\\
1-1/e&\mbox{if }\alpha=1,\\
0&\mbox{if }\alpha>1.\end{cases}

(14)

Proof.

Let
X_{i,j}
be drawn i.i.d. from
P_{X}
for
i\in[m]
and
j\in[n]
, and
Z_{j}\triangleq r(X_{1,j},\dots,X_{m,j})
be the reward of the
j
-th sequence. Then for
t>0

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\max_{j\in[n]}Z_{j}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=1-[\Pr(Z_{1}<t)]^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\left[1-\exp\left(-2m(t-\mu)^{2}\right)\right]^{n},

where the inequality follows from Hoeffding’s inequality. The result follows from choosing
t=\mu+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log n}{2m}}
.
∎

This result reveals a fundamental limitation of BoN: to achieve a gain of
\epsilon
over the average reward
\mu
using best-of-n sampling, we need
n=e^{O(m\epsilon^{2})}
samples. Intuitively, this exponential dependence on sequence length arises because long sequences tend to concentrate around their mean reward (by the law of large numbers), requiring an exponentially large number of samples
n
in a Best-of-
n
sampling strategy to achieve a meaningful gain in reward.

In contrast, consider the symbolwise BoN sampling strategy in Definition
1
applied to a sequence of
m
symbols: we sample each of the
m
symbols
n
times, keeping the ones with the highest reward value. For simplicity, assume that
r:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
. In this case, using the notation in Definition
1
, the expected reward of the
i
-th symbol is

\mathbb{E}[r(Y_{i})]=\Pr\left(r(Y_{i}\right)=1)=1-(1-\mu)^{n}\triangleq p_{n}.

(15)

Thus, using a Chernoff bound
[
40
, Thm. 4]
for
\delta>0
,

\Pr\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}r(Y_{i})\leq p_{n}-\delta/m\right)\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}mp_{n}}.

(16)

Note that
\mu\leq p_{n}\leq 1
, so to achieve a reward of
p_{n}-\delta/m
with probability
\tau
only requires
m\geq-2\log(1-\tau)/\delta^{2}\mu
which is independent of
n
. For any
\delta
, we can pick an
m
sufficiently large such that we achieve a reward with probability
\tau
and that
\delta/m
is arbitrarily close to 0. Then, to achieve an
\epsilon
gain over the average reward
\mu
requires
n\geq\log(1-\mu-\epsilon)/\log(1-\mu)
. Intuitively, if at each symbol we can pick the reward-maximizing option out of a set of samples, we will very quickly achieve or beat
\mu
over
m
symbols. However, the amount by which we beat
\mu
depends on the number of samples
n
, and
\mu
. In this simple reward setting, symbolwise sampling decouples
n
and
m
, providing a dramatic improvement over the exponential dependence of
n
on
m
found in blockwise sampling.

C. Blockwise Soft Best-of-
n
.
We now analyze the performance of Soft Best-of-
n
in the blockwise additive reward model.

Definition 3
.

For a fixed integer
k\geq 1
and
\lambda>0
, the
blockwise Soft Best-of-
n
sampling
strategy consists of:

1.

Draw
X^{m}_{1},\ldots,X^{m}_{n}
i.i.d. from
P^{\otimes m}_{X}
;

2.

Draw
Z
from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with distribution

\Pr(Z=i)=\frac{e^{r(X^{m}_{i})/\lambda}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{r(X^{m}_{j})/\lambda}};

(17)

3.

Return
Y=X_{Z}
.

Theorem 4
.

For
\lambda>0
and integers
n,m\geq 1
, let
P_{X}^{\otimes m}
be an i.i.d. source over
\mathcal{X}^{m}
and
r:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be a reward function. Then, for the blockwise Soft Best-of-
n
sampling strategy:

D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*m}_{\lambda}\|P^{m}_{n,\lambda})\leq\log\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}^{\otimes m}}[e^{2r_{\lambda}(X)/m}]}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}^{\otimes m}}[e^{r_{\lambda}(X)/m}]^{2}}-1\right)^{m}\right)

Moreover, if
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*}_{\lambda}\|P_{n,\lambda})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sinh\left(\frac{1}{m\lambda}\right)^{2m}
.

Proof sketch of Theorem
4
.

The proof follow the same steps of the proof of Theorem
1
but adapted for sequences of length
m
drawn i.i.d. from
P_{X}^{\otimes m}
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

For
\epsilon>0
, if
0\leq r(x)\leq 1
and
\lambda\geq\frac{2}{\log(1+4(n\epsilon)^{1/m})}
, then
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P^{*m}_{\lambda}\|P^{m}_{n,\lambda})\leq\epsilon
.

We analyze how many samples are needed to achieve a target KL divergence when using different block lengths for Soft Best-of-
n
. This analysis reveals a fundamental tradeoff between the symbolwise (
m=1
) and blockwise (
m>1
) approaches. Consider the simplified bound in (
7
). To achieve
D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P^{*}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\|P^{m}_{n,\lambda}\right)^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
, we need the previous bound to be at most
\epsilon
. For the symbolwise sampling, we fix a target
\lambda^{\prime}
and recall that the relationship between
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\lambda
for the blockwise sampling must satisfy
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda m
to ensure equivalent optimal distributions
P^{*}_{\lambda^{\prime}}=P^{*m}_{\lambda}
. From Corollary
2
, if

\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda m\geq\frac{2}{\log(1+4(n\epsilon)^{1/m})}\iff n\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{e^{\frac{2}{\lambda^{\prime}}}-1}{4}\right)^{m},

(18)

then
D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(P^{*}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\|P^{m}_{n,\lambda}\right)^{\prime}\leq\epsilon.

Consequently,
n=O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}e^{cm/\lambda^{\prime}}\right),
and this condition cannot be improved in general due to our lower bound. This shows that to achieve the same “operating point” of KL-reward tradeoff (determined by
\lambda^{\prime}
), tilting a block of size
m
requires exponentially more samples
n
to approach the optimal Soft Best-of-
n
distribution
P^{*}_{\lambda^{\prime}}
.

This reveals a fundamental tension in sampling-based alignment strategies. While symbolwise sampling requires exponentially fewer samples to achieve a given point on the reward-KL curve, it necessitates evaluating the reward model at every symbol generation step. In practical applications with expensive reward models, this per-symbol evaluation can become prohibitively costly. On the other hand, blockwise sampling requires exponentially more samples to approach the optimal KL-reward distribution.

Future Work

Future directions for this work include the implementation of Soft Best-of-
n
sampling in real-world generative models, with analysis of resource consumption trade-offs for
n,m
and
\lambda

[

Finally, this framework can be expanded to more sophisticated and realistic reward models, such as those exhibiting temporal dependencies or causal structures.

Alignment.
LLM alignment is generally done via Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
[
19
]
, which uses reinforcement learning to steer an LLM’s sampling distribution to maximize a reward signal, oftentimes with a KL penalty to enforce similarity to the pre-trained LLM’s behavior. This can be done with a separate reward model such as in Proximal Policy Optimization
[
26
]
or directly via Direct Policy Optimization
[
21
]
.

Best-of-
n
.
Alternatively, Best-of-
n
sampling is a simple inference-time approach for alignment
[
20
,
33
,
34
]
and has even been used in the development of industry models such as Llama 2
[
41
]

[

propose training techniques to fine-tune a language model to sample from the BoN distribution while preserving model performance.

Prior Results.
Early works proposed an analytical formula for the KL divergence between BoN sampling and the reference model
[
20
,
34
]
, but recent work demonstrated that this formula is in fact an upper bound and overly-conservative characterization of KL
[
35
,
25
]
, indicating that BoN sampling might perform better than expected in terms of KL-reward tradeoffs. Furthermore,
[
24
]
demonstrated the asymptotic equivalence between BoN and KL-constrained reinforcement learning under certain conditions, providing theoretical justification for BoN’s strong empirical performance. Empirically,
[
22
]
and
[
34
]
found that the KL-reward tradeoff for both RL and BoN methods scaled as

between the optimized and base distribution, and
[
25
]
demonstrated that this relationship is an information-theoretic upper bound.

Process Reward Modeling in Reasoning.
There has been a recent surge of interest in properly developing models with reasoning capabilities by encouraging long generations where models can work through complex problems step-by-step
[
43
]
. To encourage proper reasoning, process reward models (PRMs) have emerged as a way to provide reward at each reasoning step of a model, and have been shown to improve reasoning capabilities over rewarding outcomes only
[
44
,
45
]
. PRMs benefit from exhibiting similar properties to the additive reward model mentioned above; intuitively, by rewarding on a finer-grained scale (during each reasoning step instead of rewarding the final output), we can sample “symbolwise” (where a symbol is a reasoning step) to more efficiently achieve a gain in reward. The work most similar to ours by Puri et al.
[
46
]
uses a sampling approach similar to Soft Best-of-
n
for PRMs. However, where our method would sample
once
from the soft distribution over outputs for each generation, and then generate
n

n

[
46

instead maintains
n
particles throughout generation and at each step samples with replacement
n
times from the soft distribution to continue generation from these
n
particles.

\displaystyle P_{n,\lambda}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pr(Y=y,Z=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pr(X_{i}=y)\Pr(Z=i|X_{i}=y)

Now, to compute

\displaystyle\Pr(Z=1|X_{1}=y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}}\cdots\sum_{x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}}\Pr(Z=1|X_{1}=y,X_{2}=x_{2},\ldots,X_{n}=x_{n})

\displaystyle\times\Pr(X_{2}=x_{2},\ldots,X_{n}=x_{n})

where the last line follows from the fact that
X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. samples from

\displaystyle P_{n,\lambda}(y)

\displaystyle=nP(y)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{r(y)/\lambda}}{e^{r(y)/\lambda}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}e^{r(X_{i})/\lambda}}\right]

\displaystyle=P(y)e^{r(y)/\lambda}\times\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}(e^{r(y)/\lambda}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}e^{r(X_{i})/\lambda})}\right],

expectation is with respect to the joint distribution of
(X_{2},\dots,X_{n})

\mathbb{E}[1/X]\geq 1/\mathbb{E}[X]
.
∎

.

Lemma 2
.

For a sequence
y^{m}\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
,
n\geq 1

\displaystyle P_{n,\lambda}(y^{m})

\displaystyle=n\cdot P_{X}^{\otimes m}(y^{m})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}}{e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}e^{r(X_{j}^{m})/\lambda}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq P_{X}^{\otimes m}(y^{m})e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}+\frac{n-1}{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}^{\otimes m}}[e^{r(X^{m})/\lambda}]\right)}

\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}^{\otimes m}}[e^{r(X^{m})/\lambda}]=\left(\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{r(X)/m\lambda}]\right)^{m}

Proof.

Following Definition 2 for sequences, we have:

\displaystyle P_{n,\lambda}(y^{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Pr}(Y^{m}=y^{m},Z=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Pr}(X_{i}^{m}=y^{m},Z=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Pr}(X_{i}^{m}=y^{m})\cdot\text{Pr}(Z=i|X_{i}^{m}=y^{m})

By symmetry, we can focus on
i=1
and multiply by
n
:

\displaystyle P_{n,\lambda}(y^{m})

\displaystyle=n\cdot P_{X}^{\otimes m}(y^{m})\cdot\text{Pr}(Z=1|X_{1}^{m}=y^{m})

\displaystyle=n\cdot P_{X}^{\otimes m}(y^{m})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}}{e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}e^{r(X_{j}^{m})/\lambda}}\right]

Since
f(x)=1/x
is convex for

, we can apply Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}}{e^{r(y^{m})/\lambda}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}e^{r(X_{j}^{m})/\lambda}}\right]
