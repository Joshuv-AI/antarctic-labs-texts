Spatial fluctuation for stochastic heat equation with Hölder coefficients
Carl Mueller and Fei Pu

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish associativity, spatial ergodicity and a central limit theorem for certain nonnegative solutions to the stochastic heat equation ∂ t u = 1 2 ​ ∂ x 2 u + u γ ​ ξ \partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u+u^{\gamma}\xi with γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \gamma\in(0,1) . When γ = 1 2 \gamma=\frac{1}{2} , we derive a limit for the moment generating function of the spatial integral and provide a lower bound on the spatial growth of the solution.



1.
Introduction

We study nonnegative solutions to the following stochastic
heat equation.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)+u(t,x)^{\gamma}\xi(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\\
u(0)\equiv 1,\end{cases}

(1.1)

with
\gamma\in(0,1)
, where
\xi=\xi(t,x)
denotes space-time
white noise. As usual, we regard (
1.1
) as shorthand for the
following mild equation.

\displaystyle u(t,x)=1+\int_{[0,t]\times{\mathbb{R}}}p_{t-s}(x-y)u(s,y)^{\gamma}\xi(\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}y),

(1.2)

where
p_{t}(x)=(2\pi t)^{-1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/(2t)}
is the heat kernel and the stochastic integral is in sense of Walsh
[
31
]
. The
existence of nonnegative weak solutions to (
1.1
) was proved in

[
19
,
30
]
by tightness arguments. When
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
,
uniqueness in law among nonnegative solutions is established by the well-known exponential duality
between
u(t,x)
and nonnegative solutions
v(t,x)
of the PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}v-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}.

Indeed, the solution to (
1.1
) with
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
corresponds
to the density of one-dimensional super Brownian motion (see Section III.4
of
[
27
]
). For diffusion coefficient
|u(t,x)|^{\gamma}
with
\frac{3}{4}<\gamma<1
, pathwise uniqueness among
all solutions was proved in
[
21
]
.

When
\frac{1}{2}<\gamma<1
and for
|u(0,\cdot)|
rapidly decreasing,
Mytnik
[
20
]
extended exponential duality and hence proved
uniqueness in law among nonnegative solutions. Since our initial data is
not rapidly decreasing, we cannot assume uniqueness in law for

\frac{1}{2}<\gamma\leq\frac{3}{4}
.

To cover all of these possibilities, we define
\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
to be
the set of nonnegative weak solutions to (
1.1
).

We are interested in the spatial asymptotic behavior of the solution to (
1.1
). For stochastic heat equation with diffusion coefficient

\sigma(u)
, where
\sigma
is a Lipchitz continuous function on
{\mathbb{R}}
, Huang, Nualart and Viitasaari
[
15
]
initiated the study of
central limit theorem for the spatial average of the solution using Malliavin-Stein’s method. Fix
T>0
. According to
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{N}[u(t,x)-1]\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}\xrightarrow{C[0,T]}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\mathrm{E}[\sigma(u(s,0))^{2}]}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm B}_{s}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}

where
{\rm B}
denotes Brownian motion and “
\xrightarrow{C[0,T]}
” denotes the convergence in law in the space of continuous functions
C[0,T]
. Since the work of Huang, Nualart and Viitasaari
[
15
]
, there has been a rapidly growing research on CLT for SPDEs with Lipschitz continuous diffusion coefficient; see, for instance,
[
2
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
23
]
and references therein.
Meanwhile, spatial ergodicity for stochastic heat equation Lipschitz continuous diffusion coefficient has been established by Chen et al.
[
4
]
using Poincaré inequality. See also
[
24
]
for spatial ergodicity of stochastic wave equations in dimensions
1,2
and
3
.

Li and Pu
[
17
]
consider the square root diffusion coefficient. Using the Laplace functional of super Brownian motion, they show that
as a process in time,
the normalization of the spatial average of the solution to (
1.1
) converges in law to the standard Brownian motion in the space of continuous functions; see
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
. However, the spatial ergodicity for the density of super Brownian motion is unknown.

We are aiming to establish the spatial ergodicity and CLT for the solution to (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
or
\gamma\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
, and
let
u\in\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
. Then the following assertions hold. If

\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\cup(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4}]
, then there exists

u\in\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
such that these assertions hold.

(1)

For each
t>0
, we have that
u(t,\cdot)
is spatially ergodic.

(2)

Fix
T>0
. As
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{N}[u(t,x)-1]\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}\xrightarrow{C[0,T]}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\mathrm{E}[u(s,0)^{2\gamma}]}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm B}_{s}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}.

In
[
15
,
4
]
, Malliavin calculus plays a crucial role in the
study of spatial ergodicity and CLT for stochastic heat equation with
Lipschitz continuous coefficient. For Hölder continuous coefficients, we
cannot apply Malliavin calculus to study spatial limit theorems of the
solution, at least not in a direct way. In fact, when

\frac{1}{2}\leq\gamma\leq\frac{3}{4}
, we do not know if the solution is a
functional of the underlying space-time white noise. Even when

\frac{3}{4}<\gamma<1
, there exists a unique strong solution (see

[
21
, Theorem 1.3]
), it is not clear to us if the solution is
Malliavin differentiable. By the exponential duality, the Laplace
functional of the solution to (
1.1
) is available. In the case

\frac{1}{2}<\gamma<1
, the dual process is a solution to an SPDE driven by a
one-sided stable process (see
[
20
]
). However, we will not apply
this approach to prove the CLT in Theorem
1.1
.

The main tool to prove Theorem
1.1
is associativity. We recall from Esary et al.
[
13
]
that
a random vector
X:=(X_{1}\,,\ldots,X_{m})
is said to be
associated
if

\Cov[h_{1}(X)\,,h_{2}(X)]\geq 0,

(1.3)

for every pair of functions
h_{1},h_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that are nondecreasing in
every coordinate and satisfy
h_{1}(X),h_{2}(X)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. A random
field
\Phi=\{\Phi(x)\}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
is
associated
if

(\Phi(x_{1})\,,\ldots,\Phi(x_{m}))
is associated for every

x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We remark that an associated random vector is
also said to satisfy the FKG inequalities; see Newman
[
25
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
or
\gamma\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
, and
let
u\in\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
. Then the following conclusion holds. If

\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\cup(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4}]
, then there exists

u\in\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
such that the conclusion holds.

Conclusion:

u
is associated.

Chen et al.
[
5
, Theorem A.4]
prove associativity for a class of
stochastic heat equations with Lipschitz continuous diffusion
coefficients. Using the comparison principle for the stochastic heat
equation, they show that under certain assumptions on the initial data and
diffusion coefficient, the Malliavin derivative of the solution is
nonnegative. Then an appeal to Clark-Ocone formula shows that
(
1.3
) holds and hence proves associativity. See the proof of

[
5
, Theorem A.4]
for details. As we mentioned before, it does
not make sense to show that Malliavin derivative of the solution to
(
1.1
) is nonnegative. However, as in the proof of existence
of weak solution to (
1.1
) (see
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
and

[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
), we will approximate the Hölder coefficient
by Lipschitz continuous functions and show that each approximating process
is associated by
[
5
, Theorem A.4]
. Thus, as a weak limit of
these approximating processes, a solution to (
1.1
) is
associated. The detailed proof of Theorem
1.2
will be
given in Section
2
. Then we will apply Theorem
1.2
to
prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
.

According to Theorem
1.1
, the spatial integral of the solution to (
1.1
) satisfies law of large numbers and CLT.
It is natural to ask if it satisfies large deviation principle. In Section
4
, we will consider the moment generating
function of the spatial integral of the solution to (
1.1
) and derive a precise limit in the case
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
.
We can also apply associativity to study the spatial growth. In Section
5
, we consider
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}

and give a lower bound on the asymptotic maximum of the solution to (
1.1
) (based on an assumption on the distribution of
u(t,0)
).

We conclude this section with a brief overview of the notation used throughout the paper.
For every
Y\in L^{k}(\Omega)
with
k\in[1,\infty)
, we write
\|Y\|_{k}=(\mathrm{E}[|Y|^{k}])^{1/k}
. Throughout we write “
g_{1}(x)\lesssim g_{2}(x)
for all
x\in X
” when
there exists a real number
L
such that
g_{1}(x)\leq Lg_{2}(x)
for all
x\in X
.
Define

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\lambda}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}|f(x)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|x|}

and set

C_{tem}({\mathbb{R}})=\{f\in C({\mathbb{R}}):\|f\|_{\lambda}<\infty\,\,\text{for any $\lambda>0$}\}
.
Finally, symbols such as
C,C_{T},c,c_{n}
, without any explicit dependence on

{\mathbb{R}}
or other spaces, will denote constants which may change from
line to line.

2.
Associativity

We prove associativity in this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We will modify the arguments in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
to approximate the solution to (
1.1
).
Choose an even function
\psi_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
so that

0\leq\psi_{n}\leq 1
,
\|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,
\psi_{n}(x)=1
if

|x|\leq n
and
\psi_{n}(x)=0
if
|x|\geq n+2
. For
n\geq 1
, define the
function

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(x)=\psi_{n}(x)\left(|x|^{\gamma}1_{\{|x|>1/n\}}+n^{1-\gamma}|x|1_{\{|x|\leq 1/n\}}\right),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then for each
n\geq 1
, the function
\sigma_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\sigma_{n}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot(1+|x|),\quad\text{for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}

\displaystyle|\sigma_{n}(x)-\sigma_{n}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq c_{n}|x-y|,\quad\text{for all $x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}$}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\text{for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=0.

(2.3)

In addition,

\displaystyle\left|\sigma_{n}(x)-|x|^{\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|1-\psi_{n}(x)|\left(|x|^{\gamma}1_{\{|x|>1/n\}}+n^{1-\gamma}|x|1_{\{|x|\leq 1/n\}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+1_{\{|x|\leq 1/n\}}\left||x|^{\gamma}-n^{1-\gamma}|x|\right|

\displaystyle\leq|1-\psi_{n}(x)|(2+|x|)+n^{-\gamma}\left(\gamma^{\gamma/(1-\gamma)}-\gamma^{1/(1-\gamma)}\right).

Thus,
\sigma_{n}
converges to
|x|^{\gamma}
uniformly on compact sets as
n\to\infty
.

Condition (
2.1
) ensures that
for each
n\geq 1
, there exists a unique solution (denoted by
u_{n}
) to the following equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{n}(t,x)+\sigma_{n}(u_{n}(t,x))\xi(t,x),\\
u_{n}(0)\equiv 1.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Because of (
2.3
), the solution
u_{n}
is nonnegative (see
[
18
,
30
]
).
Moreover, by (
2.2
) and
[
5
, Theorem A.4]
,
u_{n}
is associated.
Furthermore, from the proof of

[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
, there exists a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that as
k\to\infty
,
u_{n_{k}}
converges a.s. to
u
in
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+},C_{tem}({\mathbb{R}}))
on some probability space, where
u
is a weak solution to the following stochastic heat equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)+|u(t,x)|^{\gamma}\xi(t,x),\\
u(0)\equiv 1.\end{cases}

Clearly,
u
is nonnegative, which implies that
u
is a weak solution to (
1.1
).
Moreover, the almost sure convergence of
u_{n_{k}}
to
u
in

C({\mathbb{R}}_{+},C_{tem}({\mathbb{R}}))
implies that the finite-dimensional
distributions of
u_{n_{k}}
converge to those of
u
as
k\to\infty
.
Since convergence of finite-dimensional distributions preserves
associativity (see
[
13
, (P
5
)]
), it follows that the process
u

is associated. If
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
or
\gamma\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
, then (
1.1
) has a unique solution in
law and it follows that any
u\in\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
is associated. If
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\cup(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4}]
, we conclude that
there exists an associated
u
in
\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}
.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
is complete.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

(1) From the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we see that
associativity holds for the solution to (
1.1
) with some general
nonnegative initial condition. As a consequence of associativity, we have
for
(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,(t_{m},x_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
and for Lipschitz
continuous functions
f,g:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\left|\Cov(f(u(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,u(t_{m},x_{m}))\,,g(u(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,u(t_{m},x_{m})))\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\text{\rm Lip}_{j}(f)\text{\rm Lip}_{\ell}(g)\Cov(u(t_{j},x_{j})\,,u(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})),

(2.5)

where
\text{\rm Lip}_{j}(f)
denotes the Lipschitz constant with respect to the
j
-th component; see Bulinski and Shabanovich
[
3
]
and
[
28
, Theorem 6.2.6]
.

(2) Fix
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. According to
[
8
, Lemma 18]
, for each
n\geq 1
, the process
\{u_{n}(t_{1},x),\ldots,u_{n}(t_{k},x)\}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}
is stationary, where
u_{n}
is the solution to (
2.4
). As a consequence, the process

\{u(t_{1},x),\ldots,u(t_{k},x)\}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}
is stationary, where
u
is the weak limit of
u_{n_{k}}
and is a weak solution to (
1.1
).

3.
Spatial ergodicity and CLT

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
using Theorem
1.2
. We start by giving an estimate on the spatial covariance of the solution to (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix
T>0
. There exists a constant
C_{T}>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,0))\leq C_{T}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4T}}.

Proof.

By the moment estimates in
[
6
, Theorem 1.6]
, for
k\geq 2
, there exists a constant
c_{k}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)\|_{k}^{2}\leq c_{k}t^{\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)}}

(3.1)

all for
t>0
. By stationarity and Ito’s isometry, we have

\displaystyle\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,0))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t-s}(x-y)p_{t-s}(y)\mathrm{E}\left[u(s,y)^{2\gamma}\right]\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{E}\left[u(s,0)^{2\gamma}\right]p_{2(t-s)}(x)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq c_{T}\int_{0}^{T}p_{2s}(x)\mathrm{d}s\leq c_{T}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4T}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
: spatial ergodicity.

Chen et al.
[
4
, Lemma 7.2]
provides a general criterion on the ergodicity of a stationary process; see also
[
1
, Lemma 4.2]
. Indeed, we can apply Lemma
3.1
and the inequality (
2.1
) and follow along
the same lines as in the proof of
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
to obtain that for each
t>0
,
\{u(t,x):x\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
is ergodic.
∎

In order to prove the CLT in Theorem
1.1
, we first establish the tightness. For
t>0
and
N\geq 1
, denote

\displaystyle S_{N,t}=\int_{0}^{N}u(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.2)

Proposition 3.2
.

For
T>0
and
k\geq 2
, there exists a constant
C_{T,k}>0
such that for all
s,t\in[0,T]
and
N\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[|S_{N,t}-S_{N,s}|^{k}\right]\leq C_{T,k}N^{k/2}|t-s|^{k/2}.

(3.3)

Moreover, for
t>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{E}[(S_{N,t}-N)^{2}]}{N}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{E}\left[u(r,0)^{2\gamma}\right]\mathrm{d}r.

(3.4)

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
[
15
, Proposition 4.1]
. We write

\displaystyle S_{N,t}-S_{N,s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{N}[u(t,x)-u(s,x)]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{N}\left[1_{\{r<t\}}p_{t-r}(x-z)-1_{\{r<s\}}p_{s-r}(x-z)\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\times u(r,z)^{\gamma}\xi(\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}z).

By Burkholder’s inequality and Minkowski’s inequatlity,

\displaystyle\|S_{N,t}-S_{N,s}\|_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\int_{0}^{N}\left[1_{\{r<t\}}p_{t-r}(x-z)-1_{\{r<s\}}p_{s-r}(x-z)\right]\mathrm{d}x\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\times\|u(r,0)^{\gamma}\|_{k}^{2}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\int_{0}^{N}\left[1_{\{r<t\}}p_{t-r}(x-z)-1_{\{r<s\}}p_{s-r}(x-z)\right]\mathrm{d}x\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}r,

where the second inequality holds by (
3.1
). Then, we apply
[
15
, (4.1)]
to derive
(
3.3
).

Finally, we can perform the same calculation as in the proof of

[
15
, Proposition 3.1]
to obtain (
3.4
).
This finishes the proof of Proposition
3.2

∎

We are now ready to prove the CLT in Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
: CLT.

Recall the spatial integral
S_{N,t}
defined in (
3.2
).
In light of (
3.3
), we need show that the finite-dimensional distributions of

\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(S_{N,t}-N)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
converge to those of a centered Gaussian process, denoted by
\{\mathcal{G}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}

with covariance

\displaystyle\Cov(\mathcal{G}_{t_{1}}\,,\mathcal{G}_{t_{2}})=\int_{0}^{t_{1}\wedge t_{2}}\mathrm{E}\left[u(s,0)^{2\gamma}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

Fix
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in[0,T]
. By Cramér-Wold theorem, we need prove that for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle a_{1}\frac{S_{N,t_{1}}-N}{\sqrt{N}}+\ldots+a_{k}\frac{{S_{N,t_{k}}-N}}{\sqrt{N}}\xrightarrow{\rm d}a_{1}\mathcal{G}_{t_{1}}+\ldots+a_{k}\mathcal{G}_{t_{k}},

(3.5)

where the symbol
\xrightarrow{\rm d}
refers to convergence in distribution.
Without loss of generality, we assume
N
is an integer.

We first show that (
3.5
) holds for all positive
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
. Fix
a_{1}
,
\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
We write

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\ell}(S_{N,t_{\ell}}-N)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j},

where

\displaystyle X_{j}=\int_{j-1}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(a_{\ell}u(t_{\ell},x)-1)\mathrm{d}x,\quad j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

According to Remark
2.1
(2), we see that

\{X_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is a stationary sequence.
Moreover, we can approximate the Riemann integral that defines
X_{j}
by Riemann sum and use
Theorem
1.2
and the fact that

a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
are nonnegative to conclude that
the sequence
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is associated. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Cov(X_{0}\,,X_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{-1}^{0}\int_{j-1}^{j}\Cov(\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\ell}u(t_{\ell},x)\,,\sum_{m=1}^{k}a_{m}u(t_{m},y))\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}a_{\ell}a_{m}\int_{-1}^{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Cov(u(t_{\ell},x)\,,u(t_{m},y))\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}a_{\ell}a_{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Cov(u(t_{\ell},x)\,,u(t_{m},0))\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}a_{\ell}a_{m}\Cov(\mathcal{G}_{t_{\ell}}\,,\mathcal{G}_{t_{m}})<\infty.

Therefore, by
[
25
, Theorem 2]
, we have as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{X_{1}+\ldots+X_{k}}{\sqrt{N}}\xrightarrow{\rm d}{\rm N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

which implies that (
3.5
) holds for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

We proceed to prove that (
3.5
) holds for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Fix
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By (
3.4
), the sequence of random vectors

(\frac{S_{N,t_{1}}-N}{\sqrt{N}}
,
\ldots,\frac{{S_{N,t_{k}}-N}}{\sqrt{N}})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is tight.
Thus, for any sequence
\{N_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
N_{j}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
, there exists a subsequence
\{N_{j}^{\prime}\}
such that
as
j\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{S_{N^{\prime}_{j},t_{1}}-N_{j}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{N^{\prime}_{j}}},\ldots,\frac{S_{N^{\prime}_{j},t_{k}}-N_{j}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{N^{\prime}_{j}}}\right)\xrightarrow{\rm d}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})

for some random vector
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})
.
Since (
3.5
) holds for all
a_{1}
,
\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we see that for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the linear
combination
a_{1}G_{1}+\ldots+a_{k}G_{k}
is a centered Gaussian random variable. Hence, by Lemma
A.1

below, it follows that
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})
is a centered Gaussian vector. Clearly,
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})
has the same
covariance matrix as
(\mathcal{G}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{t_{k}})
, which is independent of the choice of the sequence

\{N_{k}\}
. Therefore, we have as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{S_{N,t_{1}}-N}{\sqrt{N}},\ldots,\frac{S_{N,t_{k}}-N}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\xrightarrow{\rm d}(\mathcal{G}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{t_{k}}).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.
∎

4.
Large deviations

Recall
S_{N,t}
in (
3.2
). Fix
t>0
. If
N
is an integer, we can write

\displaystyle S_{N,t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j,t},\quad\text{with}\quad X_{j,t}=\int_{j-1}^{j}u(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

By Theorem
1.1
,
S_{N,t}
satisfies law of large numbers and CLT. A further question is that if
S_{N,t}
satisfies large deviation principle. We refer to
[
12
]
for large deviations for spatial average of stochastic heat and wave equations with bounded and Lipschitz continuous diffusion coefficients. In principle, large deviation for a sum of random variables
S_{N,t}
is related to the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\theta S_{N,t}}\right],\quad\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.1)

Since the sequence
\{X_{j,t}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is stationary and associated, the limit in (
4.1
) exists for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
(see the proof of
[
26
, Theorem 3.16]
). When
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
, we are able to compute the precise limit in (
4.1
)
for negative
\theta
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
. Fix
t>0
. For
\lambda>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{N,t}}\right]=-\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda t+2}.

(4.2)

Proof.

When
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
, the solution to (
1.1
) corresponds to the density of super Brownian motion. By
the exponential duality, we write for
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{N,t}}\right]=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\langle u(t,\cdot)\,,\bm{1}_{[0,N]}\rangle}\right]=\mathrm{e}^{-\langle 1,v(t,\cdot)\rangle},

where
v
solves the heat equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}v-\frac{1}{2}v^{2},\\
v(0,x)=\lambda\bm{1}_{[0,N]}(x).\end{cases}

(4.3)

Thus, it remains to verify

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{N}=\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda t+2}.

(4.4)

The solution to (
4.3
) is nonnegative and satisfies the following integral equation

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda\bm{1}_{[0,N]}(y)p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t-s}(x-y)v(s,y)^{2}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s

and hence we have

\displaystyle v(t,x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda\bm{1}_{[0,N]}(y)p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y,\quad t>0,x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.5)

Let
w
be the solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}w-\frac{1}{2}w^{2},\\
w(0,x)=\lambda.\end{cases}

(4.6)

Note that
w
depends only on
t
, so will write
w(t)
. Thus,
w
solves an ODE with an explicit formula given by

\displaystyle w(t)=\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda t+2},\quad t\geq 0.

Let

\displaystyle h(t,x)=w(t,x)-v(t,x).

Because the initial value of
w
is larger than that of
v
, by the comparison principle,
h
is nonnegative (and bounded above by
\lambda
). In addition, we see that
h
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}w-\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}w-\frac{1}{2}w^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}v+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}h-\frac{1}{2}(w+v)h\leq\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}h.

A supersolution for
h
is
\bar{h}
, where

\displaystyle h\leq\bar{h},

(4.7)

and
\bar{h}
solves

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\bar{h}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\bar{h},\\
\bar{h}(0,x)=\lambda\bm{1}_{[0,N]^{c}}(x).\end{cases}

(4.8)

The solution to (
4.8
) is given by

\displaystyle\bar{h}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda\bm{1}_{[0,N]^{c}}(y)p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y.

In order to prove (
4.4
),
we use (
4.5
) and (
4.7
) to see that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t,x)\mathrm{d}x-w(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{N}\left[\int_{[0,N]^{c}}v(t,x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{N}\bar{h}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\lambda}{N}\Big[\int_{[0,N]^{c}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bm{1}_{[0,N]}(y)p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bm{1}_{[0,N]^{c}}(y)p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2\lambda}{N}\int_{[0,N]^{c}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bm{1}_{[0,N]}(y)p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x.

We only need to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x=0

(4.9)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\int^{\infty}_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x=0

(4.10)

For the integral in (
4.9
), we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{N}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-y}p_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}z\right)\mathrm{d}y.

By L’Hôpital’s rule,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{N}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-y}p_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}z\right)\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{-N}p_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0.

For the integral in (
4.10
), we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int^{\infty}_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{N}\int^{\infty}_{2N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{N}\int^{2N}_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x.

It is clear that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{N}\int^{\infty}_{2N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x&\leq\frac{1}{N}\int^{\infty}_{2N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x/2)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{2N}^{\infty}p_{t}(x/2)\mathrm{d}x\to 0,\quad\text{as $N\to\infty$.}\end{split}

To justify the first line, we briefly explain why
|x-y|\geq x/2
for
x,y
in
the domain of integration.
Indeed, for such
x,y
we have
x\geq 2N
and
y\leq N
. Therefore
y\leq x/2

and so
|x-y|=x-y\geq x-(x/2)=x/2
.
Moreover, by a change of variable,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int^{2N}_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}y\frac{N}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{N^{2}(x-y)^{2}}{2t}}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{1}{x-y}\frac{N(x-y)}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{N^{2}(x-y)^{2}}{2t}}.

Since

\displaystyle\sup_{N\geq 1,x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left[N|x-y|\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{N^{2}|x-y|^{2}}{2t}}\right]<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{1}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{1}{x-y}<\infty,

by the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\int^{2N}_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{N}p_{t}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x=0.

Therefore, (
4.10
) holds and it proves (
4.4
).

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is complete.
∎

Based on Theorem
4.1
, we can derive a upper bound on the lower tail probability of
\frac{S_{N,t}}{N}
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Assume
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
. Fix
t>0
. Then for all
a\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\frac{S_{N,t}}{N}<a\right\}\leq-\frac{2(1-\sqrt{a})^{2}}{t}.

(4.11)

Proof.

For
\lambda>0
, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\frac{S_{N,t}}{N}<a\right\}

\displaystyle=\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{N,t}}>\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Na}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda Na+\log\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{N,t}}\right].

Hence, by Theorem
4.1
, for all
\lambda>0
and
a\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\frac{S_{N,t}}{N}<a\right\}\leq\lambda a-\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda t+2}.

We minimize the right-hand side of the above line over
\lambda>0
to obtain (
4.11
).
∎

5.
Discussion on spatial growth

Throughout this section, we assume
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
and fix
t>0
.
Note that Hu et al.
[
14
, Proposition 1.4]
have obtained the asymptotic of the upper tail of the solution to (
1.1
), namely, there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
(independent of
t
) and
z_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}t^{-1/2}z}\leq\mathrm{P}\{u(t,0)>z\}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c_{2}t^{-1/2}z}\quad\text{for all $z\geq z_{0}$}.

(5.1)

The above estimate on tail probability together with Kolmogorov continuity theorem implies that there exists a deterministic constant
c>0
such that a.s.

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\max_{0\leq x\leq N}u(t,x)}{\log N}\leq c\,t^{1/2},

(see
[
6
, Theorem 1.11]
).

We would like to get a lower bound on the asymptotic maximum of the solution. We refer to Conus et al.
[
7
]

for the asymptotic growth of the solution to stochastic heat equation with Lipschitz continuous coefficient, where a localization method was introduced to study the lower bound of the asymptotic maximum of the solution. The localization in
[
7
]
is based on Picard iteration of the solution and does not apply to our case. We will make use of associativity to approach the lower bound. We impose the following condition on the distribution of
u(t,0)
.

Assumption 1.
There exist
\alpha_{0},K_{0},C_{0}>0
such that for all
z\geq K_{0}
and
\delta>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,0)\in[z,z+\delta]\right\}\leq C_{0}\delta^{\alpha_{0}}.

(5.2)

Under the above assumption, we have the following estimate on the joint probability of the solution to (
1.1
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
K_{0}
be the constant in Assumption 1.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\sup_{a,b\geq K_{0}}\left[\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x)\leq a;u(t,y)\leq b\right\}-\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x)\leq a\right\}\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,y)\leq b\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq C\cdot\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,y))^{\alpha_{0}/(2+\alpha_{0})}.

(5.3)

Proof.

The proof is a modification of that of
[
28
, Theorem 6.2.15]
.
For
a,\delta>0
, we define the function

\displaystyle h_{a,\delta}(z)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $z\leq a$}\\
\frac{a+\delta-z}{\delta},&\text{if $z\in[a,a+\delta]$}\\
0,&\text{if $z>a+\delta$}.\end{cases}

For
a,b\geq K_{0}
, we write

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x)\leq a;u(t,y)\leq b\right\}-\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x)\leq a\right\}\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,y)\leq b\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\Cov(\bm{1}_{\{u(t,x)\leq a\}}\,,\bm{1}_{\{u(t,y)\leq b\}})

\displaystyle\qquad=\Cov(h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))\,,h_{b,\delta}(u(t,y)))

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\Cov(\bm{1}_{\{u(t,x)\leq a\}}-h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))\,,\bm{1}_{\{u(t,y)\leq b\}})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\Cov(h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))\,,\bm{1}_{\{u(t,y)\leq b\}}-h_{b,\delta}(u(t,y))).

Because
u
is associated, by the inequality (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\Cov(h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))\,,h_{b,\delta}(u(t,y)))

\displaystyle\leq\text{\rm Lip}((h_{a,\delta})\text{\rm Lip}(h_{b,\delta})\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,y))

\displaystyle=\delta^{-2}\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,y)).

Since
|\bm{1}_{\{u(t,x)\leq a\}}-h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))|\leq\bm{1}_{\{a\leq u(t,x)\leq a+\delta\}}
, we have

\displaystyle|\Cov(\bm{1}_{\{u(t,x)\leq a\}}-h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))\,,\bm{1}_{\{u(t,y)\leq b\}})|\leq 2\mathrm{P}\{u(t,0)\in[a,a+\delta]\}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle|\Cov(h_{a,\delta}(u(t,x))\,,\bm{1}_{\{u(t,y)\leq b\}}-h_{b,\delta}(u(t,y)))|\leq 2\mathrm{P}\{u(t,0)\in[b,b+\delta]\}.

The preceding yields for all
\delta>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x)\leq a;u(t,y)\leq b\right\}-\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x)\leq a\right\}\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,y)\leq b\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\delta^{-2}\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,y))+2\mathrm{P}\{u(t,0)\in[a,a+\delta]\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+2\mathrm{P}\{u(t,0)\in[b,b+\delta]\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\delta^{-2}\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,y))+4C_{0}\delta^{\alpha_{0}},

where the last inequality is due to (
5.2
). We choose

\displaystyle\delta=\Cov(u(t,x)\,,u(t,y))^{1/(2+\alpha_{0})}

to obtain (
5.1
).
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Almost surely,

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\max_{0\leq x\leq N}u(t,x)}{\log N}\geq c_{1}t^{1/2},

(5.4)

where
c_{1}>0
is the constant in (
5.1
).

Proof.

As in the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
, we will use Borel-Cantelli lemma to prove (
5.4
).
Choose
\tilde{c}\in(0,t^{1/2}/c_{1})
. Let
a\in(0,1)
such that

a>\tilde{c}c_{1}t^{-1/2}
. Define
x_{j}=jN/\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor
for
j=1,\ldots,\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor
.
Assume that
N
is sufficiently large so that
\tilde{c}\log N
is larger than
K_{0}
, where
K_{0}
is the constant in
Assumption 1.
We write

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\max_{0\leq x\leq N}u(t,x)\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}\leq\mathrm{P}\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}u(t,x_{j})\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathrm{P}\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}u(t,x_{j})\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}-\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x_{j})\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(1-\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,0)>\tilde{c}\log N\right\}\right)^{\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}

\displaystyle\quad:=\mathcal{P}_{N,1}+\mathcal{P}_{N,2}.

Using (
5.1
) and the inequality
1-z\leq\mathrm{e}^{-z}
for all
z\geq 0
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N,2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}c_{1}t^{-1/2}\log N}\right)^{\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}=\left(1-N^{-\tilde{c}c_{1}t^{-1/2}}\right)^{\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor N^{-\tilde{c}c_{1}t^{-1/2}}\right).

Since
\tilde{c}c_{1}t^{-1/2}<a
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{N,2}<\infty.

We proceed to estimate
\mathcal{P}_{N,1}
. Since
u
is
associated, by
[
29
, (11)]
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N,1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}\big(\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x_{j})\leq\tilde{c}\log N;u(t,x_{k})\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x_{j})\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}\mathrm{P}\left\{u(t,x_{k})\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}\Cov(u(t,x_{j})\,,u(t,x_{k}))^{\alpha_{0}/(2+\alpha_{0})},

where the second inequality holds by (
5.1
). Because
|x_{j}-x_{k}|\geq N^{1-a}
for
j<k
, by Lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle\Cov(u(t,x_{j})\,,u(t,x_{k}))\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{N^{2(1-a)}}{4T}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N,1}\lesssim\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq\lfloor N^{a}\rfloor}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2+\alpha_{0}}\frac{N^{2(1-a)}}{4T}}\leq N^{2a}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2+\alpha_{0}}\frac{N^{2(1-a)}}{4T}}

and hence we have

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{N,1}<\infty.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{P}\left\{\max_{0\leq x\leq N}u(t,x)\leq\tilde{c}\log N\right\}<\infty.

By Borel-Cantelli lemma, it follows that almost surely

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\max_{0\leq x\leq N}u(t,x)}{\log N}\geq\tilde{c}.

(5.5)

The limit in (
5.5
) is taken along integer
N
. Because the quantity
\max_{0\leq x\leq N}u(t,x)
is
increasing in
N
, the limit in (
5.5
) also holds along real numbers
N
.
Finally, we let
\tilde{c}
increase to
c_{1}t^{1/2}
to obtain (
5.4
).
∎

Appendix A

The following lemma might be available somewhere in the literature. We include the proof for reader’s convenience.

Lemma A.1
.

If for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the linear
combination
a_{1}X_{1}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k}
is a centered Gaussian random variable, then
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
is a centered
Gaussian random
vector.

Proof.

The condition ensures that
X_{j}
is a centered Gaussian random variable for
j=1,\ldots,k
.
We need show that for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the linear
combination
a_{1}X_{1}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k}
is a Gaussian random variable.
Fix
a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Define

\displaystyle F_{1}(z)=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(zX_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}\right],\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

For
z=x+\char 16\relax y
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, since
X_{1}
is a centered Gaussian random variable and

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(zX_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}\right|=\mathrm{e}^{-\theta yX_{1}}

we see that
F_{1}(z)
is well defined for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Define

\displaystyle G_{1}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}\text{\rm Var}(zX_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

It is clear that
G_{1}
is analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}
and the condition ensures that for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle F_{1}(a)=G_{1}(a),\quad\text{for all $a\geq 0$}.

(A.1)

We proceed to show that for each fixed
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
F_{1}
is
analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}
. Fix
z_{0}=x_{0}+{\rm i}y_{0}
.
Then for
0<|z-z_{0}|<1
,

\displaystyle\frac{F_{1}(z)-F_{1}(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}-\mathrm{E}\left[{\rm i}\theta X_{1}\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(z_{0}X_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{z-z_{0}}\mathrm{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(zX_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}-\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(z_{0}X_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-(z-z_{0}){\rm i}\theta X_{1}\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(z_{0}X_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(z_{0}X_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}+\ldots+a_{k}X_{k})}\left(\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(z-z_{0})X_{1}}-1-(z-z_{0}){\rm i}\theta X_{1}\right)\right]}{z-z_{0}}.

Using the Taylor series, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\theta(z-z_{0})X_{1}}-1-(z-z_{0}){\rm i}\theta X_{1}}{z-z_{0}}=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{({\rm i}\theta X_{1})^{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-1}}{n!}.

Therefore,
