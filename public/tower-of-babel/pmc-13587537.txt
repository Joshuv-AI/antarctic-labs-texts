A rare collision tumour of basal cell carcinoma and malignant melanoma of the lower abdomen with distant metastases: a case report
Authors: Shota Murakami, Masato Tsuchiya, Mieko Uno, Takaya Ichimura, Ryuichi Azuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCollision tumours are uncommon lesions in which two histologically distinct neoplasms coexist at one anatomical site, creating diagnostic difficulty when one component predominates clinically or histologically. A 70-year-old man presented with a large exophytic pigmented tumour on the lower abdomen that had been present for approximately 20 years and had enlarged rapidly during the preceding 5 years. He was brought to the emergency department because of tumour bleeding, progressive anaemia, poor nutritional status, and difficulty with mobilisation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography during emergency systemic assessment revealed multiple pulmonary, hepatic, and bilateral lymph node lesions suspicious for distant metastases. Because urgent local control was required, no preoperative incisional biopsy was performed, and complete excision with immediate reconstruction was undertaken as a therapeutic excisional biopsy. Initial histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation showed basal cell carcinoma with BerEP4 positivity. However, the metastatic pattern was inconsistent with basal cell carcinoma, prompting biopsy of a pulmonary nodule, which demonstrated malignant melanoma. Because primary pulmonary melanoma is extremely rare, the abdominal lesion was re-evaluated. Additional sampling from previously unsampled areas identified a MelanA-positive atypical melanocytic component adjacent to and partially intermingled with the basal cell carcinoma, confirming a cutaneous collision tumour. The distant lesions were interpreted as metastases from the melanoma component. BRAF V600 mutation analysis was negative, and nivolumab plus ipilimumab was initiated. However, the disease progressed rapidly, further systemic therapy was discontinued, and the patient died shortly thereafter from cancer-related complications. This case highlights how sampling bias and clinicopathological discordance can obscure aggressive malignant components in cutaneous collision tumours. In large, heterogeneous, or clinically aggressive cutaneous tumours, systematic multi-site sampling and prompt clinicopathological reassessment are essential when the initial pathological diagnosis does not fully explain the clinical behaviour.Keywords: Basal cell carcinoma, malignant melanoma, collision tumour, cutaneous neoplasm, distant metastasis

Introduction

Most cutaneous neoplasms originate from single-cell lineages and display uniform histopathological characteristics. In contrast, collision tumours, defined as the coexistence of two histologically distinct neoplasms within a single anatomically contiguous lesion, are uncommon but clinically important entities [1–3]. As their components may differ markedly in their biological behaviour, collision tumours can present substantial diagnostic challenges. When one tumour component predominates clinically or histologically, the other component may remain unrecognised, particularly if tissue sampling is limited or if the lesion exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity. Such situations may lead to clinicopathological discordance, misdiagnosis, and delays due to inappropriate therapy [4].

Here, we describe a rare cutaneous collision tumour in which this diagnostic pitfall plays a central role. This case illustrates how the reliance on a seemingly straightforward initial pathological diagnosis without full consideration of the clinical context or lesion heterogeneity can obscure a more aggressive underlying process. This highlights the need for careful sampling, thorough histologic evaluation, and readiness to reconsider the diagnosis when the clinical behaviour does not align with the initial pathological findings.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man presented with an exophytic pigmented cutaneous tumour measuring approximately 20 cm in diameter on the lower abdomen. The lesion had been present for approximately 20 years, but had grown rapidly during the preceding five years. The patient had not sought medical attention because the lesion had remained largely asymptomatic for a long period. He was eventually brought to the emergency department because of persistent tumour bleeding, progressive anaemia, poor nutritional status, and difficulty with mobilisation due to the large tumour burden. (A) Clinical photograph showing a large exophytic, pigmented cutaneous tumour on the lower abdomen at initial presentation. (B) Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography demonstrating a pulmonary nodule (arrowhead).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) had already been performed in the emergency department as part of the systemic assessment to investigate the causes of anaemia and poor nutritional status, to evaluate the tumour, and to assess the extent of disease. CT revealed multiple nodular lesions in the lungs, liver, and bilateral lymph nodes, raising concern for distant metastases (Figure 1). Before histopathological assessment, the leading clinical diagnosis was advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma with distant metastases. Malignant melanoma, aggressive cutaneous adnexal malignancy, and pigmented basal cell carcinoma were also considered in the differential diagnosis. Representative chest CT findings are shown in Figure 1(B).

No incisional biopsy was performed before excision of the abdominal tumour. Although metastatic disease from an aggressive cutaneous malignancy was suspected, immediate local control was considered necessary because of ongoing tumour bleeding, anaemia, poor nutritional status, and impaired mobility. Therefore, complete excision of the abdominal tumour was performed with a 2-cm peripheral margin down to the rectus abdominis muscle. The defect was immediately reconstructed using a split-thickness skin graft. The procedure was performed as a therapeutic excisional biopsy to obtain a definitive histological diagnosis, control bleeding, prevent further deterioration of anaemia and nutritional status, and improve the patient’s ability to mobilise.

At the initial pathological evaluation, a single central cut surface of the resected tumour was selected for sampling. From this cut surface, eleven tissue blocks were prepared, yielding eleven haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides. Immunohistochemical staining included BerEP4, EMA, AE1/AE3, S-100, MelanA, and HMB45. Within the initially sampled area, the basal cell carcinoma (BCC) component predominated. Initial histopathological examination of the resected abdominal tumour revealed nests and cords of small, uniform basaloid cells with peripheral palisading, consistent with BCC (Figure 2). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated positive staining for BerEP4, which supported the diagnosis of BCC. Histological examination confirmed that the surgical margins were free of tumour involvement. Melanocytic markers did not identify a definite melanoma-dominant component within the initially sampled area. Because the initial pathological assessment required external pathological consultation, the diagnosis of BCC was finalised approximately four weeks after excision of the abdominal tumour. The patient remained continuously hospitalised throughout the pathological assessment and subsequent diagnostic work-up. The gross appearance, H&E-stained section, and BerEP4 immunostaining are shown in Figure 2(A–C), respectively.

(C) Immunohistochemical staining showing positive expression of BerEP4 in the tumour cells. Scale bar = 100 μm.

However, given that distant metastasis from BCC is exceedingly rare [1], the presence and distribution of multiple pulmonary and hepatic lesions were considered discordant with a diagnosis of BCC alone. To clarify the nature of these distant lesions, a biopsy of a pulmonary nodule was performed using video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery.

Histopathological examination of the pulmonary nodule revealed sheets of markedly atypical cells with nuclear pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, and a high mitotic rate consistent with MM (Figure 3). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for S-100, further supporting this diagnosis. Given the rarity of primary pulmonary MM [2,3], the abdominal skin tumour was reconsidered as the primary lesion and the original specimen was re-evaluated. The H&E-stained section and S-100 immunostaining are shown in Figure 3(A,B), respectively.

Histopathological findings of the lung nodule.

(A) Hematoxylin and eosin–stained section of the pulmonary nodule showing sheets of markedly atypical cells with nuclear pleomorphism and prominent nucleoli, consistent with malignant melanoma. Scale bar = 100 μm. (B) Immunohistochemical staining showing positive expression of S-100 in the tumour cells. Scale bar = 100 μm.

During re-evaluation, an additional twelve tissue blocks and twelve H&E-stained slides were prepared from a nearby cut surface approximately 1.5 cm away from the initially sampled plane. The same immunohistochemical panel, including BerEP4, EMA, AE1/AE3, S-100, MelanA, and HMB45, was performed. These sections revealed a malignant component composed of highly atypical cells with marked nuclear pleomorphism and prominent nucleoli on haematoxylin and eosin staining. This component was identified in close proximity to the BCC component and was partially intermingled with it. Immunohistochemical analysis using MelanA in these regions demonstrated melanocytic tumour cells in these areas, helping to delineate the MM component from the BCC component (Figure 4). The pulmonary lesion was considered pathologically compatible with metastasis from the melanoma component. External pathological consultation was also obtained during this re-evaluation. The definitive diagnosis of a cutaneous collision tumour composed of BCC and MM was established approximately five weeks after the initial diagnosis of BCC and approximately ten weeks after excision of the abdominal tumour. The gross appearance at re-evaluation, H&E-stained section, and MelanA immunostaining are shown in Figure 4(A–C), respectively.

(A) Gross appearance of the resected abdominal tumour at re-evaluation. Yellow lines indicate representative sites sampled for additional histopathological examination. (B) Hematoxylin and eosin–stained section obtained during re-evaluation, in which a clear histological boundary between the basal cell carcinoma and malignant melanoma components is not evident. Scale bar = 100 μm. (C) MelanA immunostaining of the same area as in (B), showing the malignant melanoma component adjacent to and partially intermingled with the basal cell carcinoma component. Scale bar = 500 μm.

Based on these histological and immunohistochemical findings, the abdominal lesion was diagnosed as a cutaneous collision tumour composed of BCC and MM, and the pulmonary and hepatic lesions were interpreted as metastases from the melanoma component rather than from the BCC. At that time, the patient was diagnosed with stage IV (T4bN2bM1c) MM according to the AJCC 8th edition. Additional staging subsequently revealed brain metastases, and the disease was restaged as M1d. BRAF V600 mutation analysis was performed and showed no mutation. PD-L1 expression was detected in 1-10% of tumour cells. Because no actionable BRAF V600 mutation was identified, BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy was not indicated. Combination immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab was initiated. However, after the initial administration, the disease progressed rapidly and the patient’s general condition deteriorated. Further systemic therapy was discontinued, and best supportive care was initiated. Although whole-brain radiotherapy was considered because of brain metastases, it could not be performed owing to the rapid deterioration of the patient’s general condition. The patient eventually died of cancer-related complications.

Discussion

This case involved a 70-year-old man with a long-standing abdominal tumour that had slowly enlarged over two decades before exhibiting rapid growth and haemorrhagic discharge in recent years. The patient did not seek medical attention while the lesion was largely asymptomatic and presented only after tumour bleeding led to progressive anaemia, poor nutritional status, and difficulty with mobilisation. From a clinical standpoint, the presence of distant lesions was not unexpected, given the extremely large size of the tumour, its rapid growth in recent years, and its haemorrhagic appearance. Rather, the key diagnostic problem was that the initial histopathological diagnosis of BCC alone was discordant with the aggressive clinical presentation and the extent and distribution of the distant lesions.

The initial pathological assessment was not limited by a lack of clinical information. The pathology requisition included the large tumour size, long-standing history with recent rapid growth, pigmented and haemorrhagic/exudative clinical appearance, and the presence of distant lesions suspicious for metastases on CT. In addition, the initial clinical differential diagnosis included MM, squamous cell carcinoma, giant pigmented BCC, and adnexal carcinoma. The initial histopathological evaluation was based on tissue sections obtained from a single area of the resected tumour, which demonstrated a predominant BCC component. At the initial pathological evaluation, one central cut surface of the tumour was sampled, yielding eleven tissue blocks and eleven H&E-stained slides. Immunohistochemical analysis included BerEP4, EMA, AE1/AE3, S-100, MelanA, and HMB45. However, the initially sampled area was dominated by the BCC component.

After the pulmonary lesion was diagnosed as MM, the abdominal tumour was re-evaluated. Additional sampling was performed from a nearby cut surface approximately 1.5 cm from the initially sampled plane, yielding twelve additional tissue blocks and twelve additional H&E-stained slides. The same immunohistochemical panel was performed, and the melanoma component was identified in close proximity to the BCC component. The pulmonary lesion was considered pathologically compatible with metastasis from the melanoma component. This difference between the initially sampled BCC-dominant central plane and the additionally sampled nearby plane led to the initial failure to identify the melanoma component. Previous reports have suggested that diagnostic pitfalls in collision tumours often arise from inadequate or unrepresentative sampling, marked spatial heterogeneity of tumour components, and focal intermingling between distinct neoplasms [4–7]. When one component occupies a larger proportion of the lesion, routine sectioning may easily capture only that area and fail to include a second component located in a smaller or more limited region. Consistent with these mechanisms, the initial sampling in our case captured only a region dominated by indolent BCC, highlighting that systematic multi-site sampling is crucial for the accurate diagnosis of collision tumours.

These findings indicate that the initial diagnostic difficulty was primarily attributable to sampling bias caused by marked spatial heterogeneity within an extremely large tumour, rather than to insufficient clinical information. Although melanoma can occasionally show basaloid morphology and mimic BCC, the main issue in this case was that the initial sections captured a BCC-dominant area and did not include the MM-dominant component. In our case, the initial histopathological examination identified the tumour as BCC. However, the presence of multiple pulmonary and hepatic lesions raised concerns regarding a diagnosis that could not be fully explained by BCC alone. This clinicopathological discrepancy prompted reassessment of the resected specimen and ultimately led to the identification of the melanoma component, confirming a collision tumour. This case therefore underscores the importance of systematic multi-site sampling from macroscopically distinct areas in large, pigmented, haemorrhagic, ulcerated, or rapidly growing cutaneous tumours. When the initial pathological diagnosis does not adequately explain the clinical behaviour or metastatic pattern, prompt clinicopathological reassessment with additional sampling and appropriate immunohistochemical analysis is essential. Approximately five weeks elapsed between the initial diagnosis of BCC and the definitive diagnosis of the collision tumour following additional sampling of the abdominal specimen. The patient remained continuously hospitalised during this period, and the diagnostic work-up was performed sequentially without an interval of discharge or outpatient convalescence. The diagnostic interval reflected the need for external pathological consultation during both the initial assessment and the subsequent re-evaluation of the abdominal tumour, as well as the sequential investigation of the clinicopathological discordance between the initial diagnosis of BCC and the extensive metastatic disease. Specifically, the atypical metastatic pattern prompted biopsy of a pulmonary lesion, and the diagnosis of MM in the pulmonary specimen subsequently led to additional sampling and external pathological review of the abdominal tumour.

We acknowledge that delayed recognition of the melanoma component may have postponed the initiation of melanoma-directed systemic therapy and could have adversely affected the patient’s prognosis. However, the extent of this effect cannot be determined. The patient already had extensive pulmonary, hepatic, and lymph node metastases at initial presentation, and brain metastases were subsequently identified. Furthermore, the disease progressed rapidly despite the initiation of nivolumab plus ipilimumab. Therefore, although earlier recognition of the melanoma component would have been preferable, it remains uncertain whether a shorter diagnostic interval would have substantially altered the clinical outcome.

In this case, the usual stepwise process of preoperative biopsy followed by definitive excision was not performed because urgent local control was required for persistent tumour bleeding, anaemia, and systemic deterioration. This resulted in the loss of an opportunity for staged sampling. Furthermore, because the resected specimen was extremely large, the initial pathological evaluation focused on a limited intratumoural area together with margin assessment. In retrospect, this approach was insufficient to capture the spatial heterogeneity of the lesion. We therefore recommend that, even when urgent excision is required for local control, large and clinically aggressive cutaneous tumours should be sampled systematically from multiple regions, particularly those that are pigmented, haemorrhagic, ulcerated, nodular, or rapidly growing.

The pathogenesis of BCC-MM collision tumours remains uncertain. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including the independent development of two adjacent tumours, colonization of one tumour by another, differentiation from a common progenitor cell, or transformation of one tumour type into another [4–7]. In the present case, direct transformation of BCC into MM is not supported by the available pathological findings, because the two components showed distinct immunophenotypic profiles. The pathological findings suggest that a melanoma component may have arisen in close proximity to a pre-existing BCC component and subsequently enlarged rapidly while colonizing or incorporating the BCC area. However, because molecular analysis was not performed, we cannot definitively determine whether the two components arose independently or whether one component colonized the other. We therefore interpret this lesion as a collision tumour composed of biologically distinct BCC and MM components with focal intermingling.

Collision tumours composed of BCC and MM are uncommon, and published cases have almost exclusively involved sun-exposed sites such as the head and neck [6–10]. Only exceptional cases have been described at other anatomical locations. Therefore, the occurrence of this tumour combination at a non-sun-exposed site, as seen in our patient, is highly unusual and may have contributed to diagnostic difficulty and delayed recognition of the melanoma component.

This case highlights how sampling bias and clinicopathological discordance can obscure malignant components in collision tumours. Clinicians should be vigilant of the coexistence of multiple malignancies within a single lesion, particularly when faced with atypical clinical presentations or metastatic patterns that are not adequately explained by the initial pathological diagnosis. Comprehensive histological evaluation, including repeat examinations when indicated, coupled with appropriate immunohistochemical analysis, is essential for accurate diagnosis. Awareness of collision tumours can prevent diagnostic errors, inform appropriate biopsy strategies, and guide optimal therapeutic decision making when pathological diagnoses and clinical behaviour are incongruent. In such situations, immediate clinicopathological reassessment and additional multi-site sampling should be performed.
