Gypsum and press mud application alleviate the deleterious effects of sodic water irrigation in Guava (Psidium Guajava L. cv. Taiwan Pink)
Authors: Akshay Machra, Raj Kumar, Anshuman Singh, D. S. Mishra, Priyanshu J. Patel, Nirmal Singh, Antim Kundu, Ashwani Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Guava is a major fruit crop in sodic soil regions of north-western India. However, the responses of guava to sodic irrigation in terms of growth, physiology, and fruit quality remain elusive. This study evaluated the effects of sodic irrigation with varying residual sodium carbonate (RSC; 2.5–7.5 meq L⁻¹), with or without amendments (gypsum and press mud), on the growth, physiological traits, mineral nutrition, and fruit quality of guava cv. Taiwan Pink over two consecutive seasons. Sodic irrigation markedly suppressed plant growth, including height, canopy spread, stem girth, leaf area, and branching, with greater reductions at higher RSC levels (5.0–7.5 meq L⁻¹). Photosynthetic rate, leaf chlorophyll content, relative water content, osmotic potential, and membrane stability also declined under sodicity stress, whereas proline accumulation and antioxidant enzyme (ascorbate peroxidase, catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase) activities increased, reflecting adaptive responses to sodicity-induced osmotic and oxidative stresses. Sodic conditions reduced leaf K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺ contents and increased Na⁺ accumulation. Despite these physiological and biochemical disruptions, fruit quality attributes remained largely stable across treatments. Principal component analysis indicated strong interrelationships among growth and physiological traits, whereas fruit quality traits formed a distinct cluster, and Na⁺ accumulation and stress indicators were negatively associated with plant vigor. Gypsum was more effective than press mud in alleviating sodicity stress, enhancing the plant growth, increasing photosynthetic rate (~ 10%) and chlorophyll content (4–6%), and reducing leaf Na⁺ (9.8–38.2%) across RSC levels, compared to comparatively smaller improvements achieved with press mud. Overall, the findings indicate that Taiwan Pink guava can tolerate moderate sodicity without appreciable declines in fruit quality, and that soil amendments- especially gypsum- effectively improved plant growth and physiological performance under sodic irrigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07780-4.

Introduction

The unprecedented growth of the human population is driving a sharp escalation in global food demand, while the availability of natural resources, including productive land and freshwater, is steadily declining [1, 2]. This situation is exacerbated by various biotic and abiotic stresses, including insect-pests, salt stress, and climate change [3], all of which pose significant threats to agricultural sustainability. The intensifying global water crisis, driven by industrialization, urbanization, agricultural expansion, climate change, and declining freshwater reserves, makes water reuse inevitable, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions characterized by high temperatures and low precipitation [4]. Amid escalating freshwater scarcity, harnessing non-conventional, marginal-quality waters through sustainable management practices has become imperative to meet agricultural water demands [5].

Salt-induced land degradation is a major abiotic stress affecting over one billion hectares in more than 100 countries [6]. Excessive accumulation of soluble salts and precipitated electrolytes- the key drivers of saline and sodic soil formation- profoundly alters soil physical, chemical, and biological properties [7], impairing soil structure, impeding water infiltration, limiting nutrient availability, and creating ionic imbalances that ultimately degrade soil quality and hamper crop production [8]. In India, salt-affected soils currently span 6.74 million hectares and are projected to expand to 16.2 million hectares by 2050, posing a serious threat to food security. The problem is particularly severe in north-western India, where about 2.7 million hectares of degraded sodic soils are underlain by alkali groundwaters [9]. In Haryana, groundwaters with alkali hazard have been reported from several districts, accounting for approximately 20% of the state’s total groundwater resources [10]. While irrigation with alkali waters may accelerate sodicity build-up, strategies such as conjunctive use, application of amendments, cultivation of salt-tolerant cultivars, and improved nutrient management offer feasible options for their sustained use in crop production [11]. Guava (Psidium guajava L.; family Myrtaceae) is widely grown in tropical and subtropical regions, with India being the largest producer, followed by Indonesia, China, and Mexico [12]. Guava fruits are rich in vitamins, minerals, dietary fiber, and bioactive compounds such as phenols and flavonoids [13–15], earning it recognition as a ‘super fruit’. In Haryana, guava occupies 15,000 hectares with an annual production of 261,000 metric tonnes [12], predominantly in Fatehabad, Hisar, and Sirsa districts [16], where guava productivity is often constrained by freshwater scarcity and salt stress [17], and limited adoption of improved production technologies [16]. Similar constraints affect guava cultivation in other semi-arid regions, where freshwater shortages and reliance on marginal-quality waters limit its commercial potential [18]. Alleviating salt stress through exogenous chemicals and soil amendments could be a key strategy to sustain guava production under such conditions [18, 19].

Excess salts adversely affect guava growth and physiology, inhibiting vegetative development by delaying leaf emergence, reducing branch number and leaf area, suppressing stem elongation, and restricting canopy development [20–22]. These effects are largely due to impaired water relations, increased electrolyte leakage, reduced chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, decreased quantum efficiency of photosystem II, altered endogenous phytohormone levels, oxidative stress, and impaired cellular metabolism [6, 19, 23]. Elevated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ concentrations in salt-affected soils disrupt nutrient uptake and assimilation, further suppressing growth [24]. Depending on experimental conditions, guava has been categorized as either moderately tolerant or sensitive to salt stress. It exhibits stage-specific responses to salt stress, with seed germination and early seedling stages being more vulnerable than later developmental phases. Studies suggest that guava’s response to excess salt is strongly influenced by plant growth stage and experimental conditions, highlighting the need for cultivar-specific evaluations under different stress environments [25].

Despite its economic importance, the effects of sodicity stress on guava growth and development remain poorly studied. Previous studies offer limited insights: Dagar et al. [26] identified guava as a promising crop for sodic soils but focused primarily on planting methods rather than comparative sodicity tolerance. Similarly, Santhi et al. [27] assessed 31 guava genotypes in a sodic soil and observed contrasting variation in growth and yield. However, the absence of a control treatment restricted conclusive insights into genotypic performance under varying degrees of sodicity stress. Extensive use of alkali groundwater for irrigation in north-western India can further degrade soil health without proper soil–water–crop management [28]. The ‘Taiwan Pink’ guava, valued for its high yield, attractive pink pulp, and good taste [29], represents an ideal candidate to study the effects of sodic irrigation. Evaluating its growth, leaf physiology, and fruit quality under such conditions can provide insights into its adaptability in sodicity-affected regions.

The present study was carried out with the primary aim of assessing the effects of sodic water irrigation on plant growth, leaf physiological attributes, and fruit physico-biochemical characteristics of guava cultivar ‘Taiwan Pink’. Furthermore, the research aimed to examine the role of soil amendments, namely gypsum and press mud, in mitigating the detrimental effects of sodic water irrigation with the ultimate objective of developing a sustainable soil and water management strategy for guava cultivation in sodicity-affected regions.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted at the ICAR-Central Soil Salinity Research Institute, Karnal, India (29°42′ N latitude, 76°57′ E longitude; 250 m above mean sea level). The study area experiences a semi-arid subtropical with hot summers and cool winters. Most of the annual precipitation is concentrated during the southwest monsoon season (July–September). The summers are extremely hot, with mean maximum temperatures often reaching 40–45 °C in May and June, whereas the winter months (December–January) are markedly cold, with minimum temperatures occasionally approaching 0˚C. The trends in key weather parameters- air temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall during the course of present investigation are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Experiment conditions and plant material

The experiment was conducted in lysimeters (100 kg capacity; 53 cm × 40 cm) having drainage holes at the bottom. The potting mixture (85 kg) was prepared by mixing garden soil, organic compost, and river sand in a 3:1:1 (v/v) ratio. The homogenized mixture was filled into each lysimeter to a depth of 45 cm. The initial properties of the potting mixture are presented in Supplementary Table 2. It was sandy loam in texture with pH of 7.21, electrical conductivity of saturated soil extract (ECe) of 1.40 dS m⁻¹, and bulk density of 1.50 g cm⁻³. One-year-old uniform and healthy plants of guava (Psidium guajava L.) cv. ‘Taiwan Pink’ were procured from an accredited nursery (Maurya Nursery, Loni, Uttar Pradesh, India). During the rainy season of 2021, healthy and disease-free planting material was carefully transplanted into lysimeters. Different treatments were imposed based on the calculated bulk density and porosity of the potting mixture. The recommended management practices were followed to ensure healthy plant growth.

Experiment details

The experiment was laid out in a randomized complete block design with 10 treatments; each replicated five times and each replication comprising a single lysimeter. The treatment details are as follows: T1: control (Best Available Water; BAW), T2: residual sodium carbonate (RSC) of ~ 2.5 meq L⁻¹, T3: RSC ~ 2.5 meq L⁻¹ + press mud, T4: RSC ~ 2.5 meq L⁻¹ + gypsum, T5: RSC ~ 5 meq L⁻¹, T6: RSC ~ 5 meq L⁻¹ + press mud, T7: RSC ~ 5 meq L⁻¹ + gypsum, T8: RSC ~ 7.5 meq L⁻¹, T9: RSC ~ 7.5 meq L⁻¹ + press mud, and T10: RSC ~ 7.5 meq L⁻¹ + gypsum.

Naturally sodic groundwater, with an RSCiw of approximately 10 meq L⁻¹, was collected from a sodic aquifer in Mundri village, Kaithal district, Haryana, India (29°46’41.7"N 76°30’17.1"E). It was stored in a plastic tank of 2000 L capacity, and diluted with normal tap water to obtain treatment waters having RSCiw levels of ~ 2.5, 5.0, and 7.5 meq L⁻¹. The BAW (control) was non-sodic, with a pH of 6.2, EC of 0.2 dS m⁻¹, and no RSC, providing a baseline for comparison with sodic water treatments. Both gypsum and press mud were applied at the onset of each season. Gypsum was dissolved in water prior to application, whereas press mud was directly incorporated into the soil. The quantities of gypsum applied were 1.08 g, 3.78 g, and 6.48 g for irrigation water with RSC levels of 2.5, 5.0, and 7.5 meq L⁻¹, respectively, mixed in 9.04 L of water. The corresponding amounts of press mud applied were 193.2 g, 386.4 g, and 579.6 g, respectively. These rates were adjusted according to the RSC level of the irrigation water to achieve proportional amelioration of sodicity stress [30]. Maintaining a constant rate for all treatments would not have accounted for differences in sodicity levels and could have led to inconsistent responses.

Irrigation frequency and amount

The irrigation requirement was estimated based on the lysimeter volume and the field capacity of the potting mixture. Each lysimeter, with a radius of 20 cm, was filled with potting mixture to a depth of 45 cm, resulting in a total volume of 0.056 m³. Based on the lysimeter volume, the theoretical water requirement for complete saturation of the potting mixture was approximately 56.5 L. However, the actual amount required was lower because the potting mixture contained roughly equal proportions of micropores and macropores (about 50% each). Considering a field capacity of 0.32, the volume of water needed to bring each lysimeter to field capacity was calculated as 9.0 L. Irrigation was applied at 10–12-day intervals in accordance with the recommendations for the study area.

Observations recorded

Plant growth attributes, including plant height, canopy spread, stem girth, and canopy volume were recorded at three growth stages: prior to the initiation of the experiment (second week of January 2022), during the rainy fruiting season (first week of August 2022), and during the winter fruiting season (second week of January 2023). Physiological and biochemical attributes were assessed twice, once during the summer season (last week of July 2022) and once during the winter season (second week of January 2023).

Plant growth parameters

Plant growth attributes, including plant height, plant spread [north–south (N-S) and east–west (E-W) directions], stem girth, and canopy volume, were recorded from each replication across all treatments. Plant height and spread were recorded using a measuring scale, while stem girth was measured with a digital Vernier caliper (Mitutoyo, Model VC-11, Japan). Canopy volume (CV) was estimated using the formula: CV = H × SNS​× SEW​ × 0.5238; where, CV = canopy volume (m³), H = plant height (m), SNS = Plant spread in the north–south direction (m), and SEW = plant spread in the east–west direction (m). The total number of branches per plant was counted manually for each replication. The length of newly emerged shoots was measured with a measuring scale and expressed in centimeters (cm).

Leaf area

Leaf area was determined on five fully expanded leaves from the 3rd and 4th nodes below the shoot apex of each plant. The leaves were carefully detached, and their lamina surface area (cm²) was measured with a leaf area meter (CI-201 Laser Leaf Area Meter, Spectra Agritech, USA).

Photosynthetic rate

The photosynthetic rate of guava leaves was measured in terms of net CO₂ assimilation (µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹) using a portable Infrared Gas Analyzer (IRGA, LI-6800, LICOR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA). Measurements were taken on fully expanded, healthy, and sun-exposed leaves between 09:00 AM and 11:00 AM to minimize diurnal variability. Standard instrument settings were maintained for reference CO₂ concentration, leaf temperature, relative humidity, and photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) to ensure consistency across treatments.

The total chlorophyll content of the top fully expanded leaves was estimated using the 80% acetone extraction method described in Arnon [31]. Leaf osmotic potential

Leaf osmotic potential (Ψₛ) was determined using a vapor pressure osmometer (Model 5600, ELITech Group, Belgium). Fully expanded leaves were sampled from each plant, and leaf sap was extracted by gently macerating the tissue. A small quantity of the expressed sap was placed in the osmometer, and the osmotic potential was recorded following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Membrane stability

The membrane stability of guava leaves was assessed following the procedure described in Dionisio-Sese and Tobita [33]. For this purpose, 8–10 leaf discs of 1 cm diameter were excised from fully expanded leaves and placed in 20 ml vials containing 10 ml of deionized water, maintained at 25 °C. After an incubation period of five hours, the electrical conductivity (EC) of the bathing solution was measured using an electrical conductivity meter (Eutech Instruments, India), and the values were recorded as C1 (initial leakage). Subsequently, the samples were transferred to a boiling water bath and heated for 50 min to ensure complete killing of the tissue. After cooling the samples to room temperature, the electrical conductivity of the solution was again measured and designated as C2 (total leakage). Where C1 is the electrical conductivity (ion leakage) measured from the stressed sample, and C2 is the electrical conductivity (ion leakage) measured after complete tissue damage, representing total leakage.

Proline

Leaf proline concentration was estimated following the ninhydrin-based spectrophotometric assay described in Bates et al. [34]. The absorbance of the reaction mixture was recorded at 520 nm using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer (GENESYS 1XX, USA). For extraction, 200 mg of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 10 ml of 3% sulphosalicylic acid. From the resulting homogenate, 2 ml of the extract was transferred into a test tube, to which 2 ml each of acid-ninhydrin reagent and glacial acetic acid were added. The reaction mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 1 h to allow the development of a red chromophore. After incubation, the tubes were cooled to room temperature, and the chromophore was extracted with 4 ml of toluene. The mixture was vigorously stirred for 15–20 s using a test tube stirrer to facilitate complete extraction. The absorbance of the toluene phase was then measured at 520 nm against toluene as a blank. The concentration of proline in the leaf tissues was calculated by referring to a standard curve prepared with known concentrations of L-proline, and the results were expressed as µg g⁻¹ fresh weight.

Antioxidant enzymes

The activities of antioxidant enzymes viz., superoxide dismutase (SOD, EC 1.15.1.11), catalase (CAT, EC 1.11.1.6), ascorbate peroxidase (APX, EC 1.11.1.11), and peroxidase (POX, EC 1.11.1.7) were determined following standard protocols. The fresh leaf samples (300 mg) were homogenized in the phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.5) containing polyvinyl polypyrrolidone (5% w/v), EDTA (1 mM), and b-mercapto-ethanol (10 mM) to assay the activities of APX and SOD. APX activity was measured according to Nakano and Asada [35], based on the oxidation of ascorbic acid. The reaction mixture (2.7 ml) consisted of 2.25 ml of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.2 ml of 0.5 mM ascorbate, 0.2 ml of 0.1 mM H₂O₂, and 0.05 ml of enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance at 290 nm was monitored using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer. Enzyme activity was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of ascorbic acid (2.8 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹). One unit of APX activity was defined as the amount of enzyme oxidizing 1 µmol of ascorbic acid per minute. SOD activity was estimated by the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich [36], based on its ability to inhibit the photochemical reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT). The reaction mixture (3.0 ml) contained 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.8), 14 mM L-methionine, 10 µM NBT, 3 µM riboflavin, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. The tubes were placed 30 cm below three 20 W fluorescent light bulbs (Philips, India) and illuminated for 40 min. A non-irradiated mixture served as a control. The intensity of blue color due to NBT reduction decreased with enzyme addition. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit the photoreduction of 1 mmol of NBT. CAT activity was assayed following Aebi [37]. The reaction mixture (1.0 ml) consisted of 0.5 ml of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.4 ml of 0.2 M H₂O₂, and 0.1 ml of enzyme extract. After incubation at 37 °C for 3 min, the reaction was terminated with 3 ml of a glacial acetic acid–potassium dichromate mixture (1:3, v/v; 5% w/v potassium dichromate), followed by heating in a boiling water bath for 10 min. After cooling, the absorbance was read at 570 nm. One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the decomposition of 1.0 µmol of H₂O₂ per minute. POX activity was determined according to Rao et al. [38], based on guaiacol oxidation. The reaction mixture (2.9 ml) consisted of 2.5 ml of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), 0.1 ml of 0.5% H₂O₂, 0.1 ml of 0.2% o-dianisidine, and 0.1 ml of enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by adding H₂O₂, and the increase in absorbance at 470 nm was recorded for 3 min. One unit of POX activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing an increase of one absorbance unit per minute at 470 nm. All spectrophotometric readings were recorded using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (GENESYS 1XX, USA).

Mineral ions

The ionic contents of guava leaves were analyzed to assess the influence of treatments on mineral nutrient balance. The concentrations of sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) were determined following the standard flame photometric method described by Jackson [39], whereas calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺) contents were estimated using the versenate (EDTA) titration method as outlined by Hanlon [40]. For this purpose, oven-dried and finely ground plant tissue samples were first digested using a suitable acid digestion procedure, and the clear aliquots obtained were subjected to ionic analysis. The results were expressed in terms of % dry weight (DW).

Fruit physico-biochemical parameters

Total soluble solids and titrable acidity

The total soluble solids (TSS) were estimated using a digital hand-held refractometer (range: 0–53 °Brix). For this purpose, the pulp was homogenized, and the juice was extracted by filtering through a double-layered muslin cloth to remove coarse particles. A drop of the clear juice was then placed on the prism surface of the refractometer, and the reading was recorded at room temperature. The results were expressed in degree Brix (°B). The titratable acidity of fruits was determined following the method described in Baur and Ensminger [41]. A known weight of fruit pulp was homogenized, and juice was extracted by filtration through muslin cloth. A measured aliquot of the juice sample was titrated against 0.1 N NaOH solution using phenolphthalein as an indicator, until the appearance of a faint pink endpoint that persisted for at least 30 s. The acidity was calculated and expressed as a percentage of citric acid equivalents on a fresh weight basis.

Hydrogen peroxide

The hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content of guava fruits was estimated following the procedure of Sinha [42]. For extraction, 3 g of fresh fruit tissue was macerated in 5 ml of ice-cold 0.01 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min, and the resulting supernatant was used for assay. For the estimation, 50 µL of the supernatant was added to 1.95 ml of 0.01 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), followed by the addition of 2 ml of a reagent mixture containing 5% potassium dichromate and glacial acetic acid (1:3, v/v). The reaction mixture was incubated, and absorbance was recorded at 570 nm against a reagent blank prepared under identical conditions but without the tissue extract. The concentration of H₂O₂ in the samples was determined from a standard curve prepared with graded concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (20–100 µmol), and the results were expressed on a fresh weight basis (µmol g⁻¹ FW).

Total phenols

The total phenol content of fruits was estimated following the colorimetric method of Swain and Hills [43]. For the assay, 1 ml of fruit extract was transferred into a test tube and diluted with 7.5 ml of distilled water. To this mixture, 0.5 ml of diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent was added and the solution was vortexed thoroughly. After exactly 3 min, 1 ml of saturated sodium carbonate solution and 0.5 ml of distilled water were added. The volume was then made up to 10 ml with distilled water, mixed well, and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to allow complete color development. The absorbance of the reaction mixture was recorded at 725 nm using distilled water as a blank. A standard calibration curve was prepared using tannic acid solutions of known concentrations (µg ml⁻¹). The phenolic content of the samples was calculated from the standard curve and expressed on a fresh weight basis (mg g⁻¹ FW).

Total flavonoids

The total flavonoid content of fruits was determined following the method of Delcour and Devarebeke [44], using catechin as the standard reference compound. For the assay, the extract was reacted with the chromogen reagent under controlled conditions, and the absorbance of the colored complex was recorded at 640 nm using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (GENESYS 1XX, USA). A standard calibration curve was prepared with graded concentrations of catechin, and the flavonoid content of the samples was calculated accordingly. The results were expressed as mg catechin equivalents (CE) per 100 g fresh weight (FW) of tissue (mg CE g⁻¹ FW).

Carotenoids

The carotenoid content was determined according to the method of Lichtenthaler and Buschmann [45]. The procedure is based on the quantitative determination of chlorophyll a (Chl a), chlorophyll b (Chl b), and total carotenoids from pigment extracts, which largely depends on the solvent system employed. In this study, 1 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized and extracted with 80% acetone until the pellets were completely colorless. The resulting supernatants were pooled, and the absorbance of the extract was measured at 452.5, 646.8, and 663.2 nm using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (GENESYS 1XX, USA). The pigment concentrations were calculated using the equations proposed for 80% acetone as solvent.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed following the Randomized Block Design as outlined by Panse and Sukhatme [46]. All values were expressed as the mean of replications.The critical difference (CD) at the 5% level of significance was calculated to assess treatment effects. The means were compared by Tukey test. The statistical computations were carried out using the software OPSTAT [47] and GRAPES [48]. In addition, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using the Facto-extra package in R software (Version 4.3.3) to explore patterns of variation and trait associations among treatments.

Results

Plant growth parameters

Irrigation with sodic water adversely affected the growth of ‘Taiwan Pink’ plants, including plant height, canopy spread (E–W and N–S), stem girth, canopy volume, number of branches, length of newly emerged shoots, and leaf area (Supplementary Fig. 1). The reductions increased with rising RSC levels. For instance, plant height declined by 9.68–26.89%, stem girth by 14.96–45.98%, and canopy volume by 11.58–54.11% across RSC levels of 2.5–7.5 meq L⁻¹ during summer and winter seasons. Similarly, the number of branches per plant, shoot length, and leaf area decreased progressively with increasing sodicity. The maximum growth was recorded under non-sodic control water (T₁), with varying extents of increase in plant height (117.21%), canopy spread (E–W: 81.63%, N–S: 77.65%), stem girth (57.94%), and canopy volume (607.0%) during summer. These values were statistically comparable to low sodicity treatments (RSC 2.5 meq L⁻¹), and treatments involving press mud or gypsum application. Conversely, irrigation with 7.5 meq L⁻¹ RSC water without amendments (T₈) resulted in the lowest growth increments and reduced branching, shoot length, and leaf area. Among the amendments, gypsum application with RSC ~ 2.5 meq L⁻¹ water proved most effective in mitigating the adverse effects of sodic irrigation, sustaining plant growth, canopy development, and leaf area compared to other treatments (Table 1).

Leaf physiological attributes

Irrigation with sodic water reduced photosynthetic rate and total chlorophyll content, with the greatest declines observed at RSC ~ 7.5 meq L⁻¹ (photosynthesis: 40–44%; chlorophyll: 23–24%) compared to the control. Incorporation of press mud or gypsum improved both parameters, with gypsum being more effective. For instance, gypsum increased the photosynthetic rate by ~ 10% and total chlorophyll content by 4–6% across RSC levels (Table 2), while press mud application resulted in smaller improvements (1.6–4.4%). The results revealed that the maximum relative water content (RWC, %), osmotic potential (MPa), and membrane stability (%) of leaves were recorded under irrigation with best available water (T₁), with values of 89.38%, − 0.51 MPa, and 77.25%, respectively, during the summer season, and 76.18%, − 0.45 MPa, and 73.45%, respectively, during the winter season. In contrast, the minimum RWC, osmotic potential, and membrane stability were observed under irrigation with RSC ~ 7.5 meq L⁻¹ without any amendment (T₈), with corresponding values of 65.02%, − 1.52 MPa, and 36.53% during summer, and 56.72%, − 1.41 MPa, and 33.23% during winter (Table 2).

Physico-biochemical parameters of fruits

The data presented in Table 5 reveal that varying levels of RSC water, with or without amendments, did not exert a statistically significant effect on the physico-biochemical attributes of fruits, including total soluble solids (TSS), total sugars, titratable acidity, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content, total phenols, total flavonoids, and carotenoids, in either summer or winter season. This suggests that the fruit quality parameters measured were relatively resilient to changes in sodicity levels and amendments within the tested range. However, it was consistently observed that the values of these physico-biochemical parameters were higher in fruits harvested during the winter season compared to those harvested in summer across all treatments. For instance, pulp TSS was 19.92–24.84% higher in winter than in summer fruits. Similarly, total phenols were 5.78–9.51% higher, and total flavonoids 10.10–13.93% higher in winter compared to summer (Table 5). This seasonal difference may be attributed to more favorable environmental conditions in winter, such as lower temperatures and reduced stress, enhancing the accumulation of sugars, phenolic compounds, and pigments in fruits.

Principal component analysis

The results of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) are shown in Fig. 1 (A-C). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), with eigenvalues ≥ 1, together accounted for 87.2% of the total variation, indicating that most of the variability in the dataset can be captured by these two components. This suggests that the measured traits are highly interrelated and that PC1 and PC2 effectively summarize the major patterns of variation in data. The PC1 exhibited strong positive correlations with key growth and physiological traits, including stem girth, canopy spread (E–W and N–S), leaf area, relative water content, membrane stability, proline content, photosynthetic rate, total chlorophyll, and leaf K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺ contents. This suggests that PC1 effectively reflects overall plant vigor and performance under different treatments. PC2 was primarily associated with the number of branches per plant and leaf Na⁺ content, indicating that this component reflects variation related to plant branching and sodium accumulation. PC3, explaining an additional 5.2% of the total variation, was primarily influenced by total sugars (TS) and carotenoids, indicating that this component captures variation related to fruit quality attributes (Fig. 1 C). Discussion

Sodic soils are marked by high pH, low organic carbon, poor fertility, restricted infiltration, and hardened CaCO₃ layers, which severely restrict crop production [28, 49]. Such constraints often compel the farmers to rely on sodicity tolerant but low-value crops. Cultivation of high-value crops like guava, in combination with soil amendments, offers a promising alternative for sustaining productivity under sodic conditions [50]. While guava’s adaptability to saline [21, 51] and sodic [27, 52] soils is known, its growth, physiological, and yield responses to sodic water irrigation remain elusive. Likewise, although the benefits of amendments have been demonstrated under saline [53, 54] and sodic [55] conditions, evidence is scarce regarding their role under sodic irrigation. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the efficacy of gypsum and press mud as ameliorants for enhancing growth, yield, and fruit quality of guava (cv. Taiwan Pink) under sodic water irrigation.

The results clearly indicated the repressive effects of sodic irrigation on guava plant growth, with the most pronounced reductions observed at the highest RSCiw level (7.5 meq L⁻¹). Reductions in plant growth under increasing soil or water sodicity have been reported in several fruit crops [56–59]. This decline is largely attributed to residual alkalinity, which promotes Ca²⁺ precipitation as CaCO₃, leading to compact, poorly aerated soils [10, 60]. Elevated carbonate and bicarbonate levels further impair soil structure, causing poor drainage, restricted nutrient and water availability, and oxygen deficiency [49, 61]. Sodic irrigation also induces osmotic stress and disrupts ion homeostasis, often resulting in excessive salt accumulation in plant tissues [50, 62]. Nutrient deficiencies and imbalances compound these effects, particularly under irrigation with high residual sodium carbonate (RSC >2.5 meq L⁻¹), which depletes soil Ca²⁺ and accelerates sodicity build-up [63]. The consequent rise in soil pH, exchangeable Na⁺, and salt content further impairs the transformation and availability of both native and applied nutrients [11, 64]. Collectively, these constraints disrupt essential physiological functions, hampering plant growth and development.

The best plant growth was recorded under non-sodic irrigation, whereas increasing RSCiw levels progressively suppressed the growth. However, the incorporation of amendments (press mud and gypsum) at each RSCiw level consistently improved the growth relative to the unamended controls. This response can be attributed to a decrease in soil pH, displacement of exchangeable Na⁺, and an increase in Ca²⁺ concentration in the soil solution, which together improved nutrient availability and created more favorable soil conditions following gypsum application [50]. These changes likely improved plant physiological functions, helping to regulate cytosolic Na⁺ and maintain a lower Na⁺/K⁺ ratio [65, 66]. Press mud alleviates sodicity stress in a similar manner by lowering the soil pH and exchangeable Na⁺, enhancing the availability of nutrients such as K⁺, Ca²⁺, and S, and improving the soil conditions. These changes improve plant water relations and osmotic adjustment, reduce oxidative stress, enhance photosynthetic activity, and help maintain lower Na⁺/K⁺ ratios in plant tissues, leading to better growth [9, 67, 68].

Irrigation with sodic water markedly reduced the photosynthetic rate and total chlorophyll content in Taiwan Pink guava plants, with the most pronounced declines observed at higher RSC levels. Available evidence suggests that sodicity stress induces excessive production of ROS, leading to chlorophyll degradation and impaired photosynthesis. Additionally, sodicity stress disrupts plasma membrane stability, perturbs ion homeostasis, and restricts CO₂ diffusion, collectively causing significant reductions in photosynthesis [69]. Ca, Mg and K are essential nutrients for structural development, photosynthetic activity, and abiotic stress tolerance in plants. Sodic conditions often induce deficiencies of these nutrients, disrupting physiological functions. Severe Ca deficiency impairs cell wall structure, causing leaf necrosis and reduced growth, which in turn can lower leaf chlorophyll content and photosynthesis. Likewise, Mg deficiency impairs chlorophyll synthesis and other metabolic processes, further diminishing chlorophyll levels and photosynthetic efficiency [70].

Physiological traits such as relative water content (RWC), osmotic potential, membrane stability, and proline content were negatively affected by sodic water irrigation. The maximum RWC, osmotic potential, and membrane stability were observed under control treatment, with progressive declines as RSCiw increased. In contrast, proline content showed an opposite trend, being lowest under non-sodic (control) treatment and highest at the maximum RSCiw level, indicating its role in osmotic adjustment under sodicity stress. The reductions in RWC, osmotic potential, and membrane stability can be attributed to the adverse soil conditions created by excessive Na+, which increase the osmotic stress, hinder root water uptake, and reduce the relative water content in plant tissues [57, 59]. Osmotic adjustment through increased proline accumulation is a key adaptive mechanism in plants under sodicity stress. Elevated proline maintains cell turgor by balancing osmotic potential, stabilizes proteins and membranes, and lessens the oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9, 58]. The increased activity of antioxidant enzymes under elevated RSCiw levels can be ascribed to enhanced oxidative stress; excess ROS generated under sodic conditions stimulate the plant’s defense system, leading to higher synthesis of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants including ascorbate peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and catalase which play a crucial role in protecting plants from ROS-induced oxidative stress. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) serves as the first line of defense by converting superoxide anions (O₂⁻) into molecular oxygen (O₂) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). Catalase (CAT) subsequently decomposes H₂O₂ into water (H₂O) and O₂, thereby mitigating oxidative damage and maintaining cellular homeostasis [55]. Application of press mud and gypsum with RSCiw irrigation reduced the activities of antioxidant enzymes with gypsum being more effective. This reduction was likely due to the ability of these amendments to improve soil conditions and plant physiological status, thereby alleviating ROS-induced oxidative stress, which in turn decreased the need for increased anti-oxidant enzyme activity [71]. The increased accumulation of salts consequent to irrigation with higher RSC (~ 7.5 meq L⁻¹) compared to lower RSC (~ 5.0 and ~ 2.5 meq L⁻¹) waters explain the greater reductions in chlorophyll content, net photosynthesis, membrane stability and RWC. Elevated salt concentrations can disperse clay particles, leading to poor soil aeration and reduced permeability, which further limits root growth, and uptake of water and nutrients [49, 50].

Our findings showed that varying levels of RSC water, with or without amendments, did not significantly affect the quality attributes of Taiwan Pink guava fruits. This indicates that the measured fruit quality traits remained largely stable despite variations in sodicity and amendment application. Interestingly, our findings are in contradiction to the previous reports in different crops including guava which indicate that fruit quality attributes including TSS, total sugars, ascorbic acid, total phenols and flavonoids either decreased or increased due to the application of saline or sodic water [68, 72, 73]. By implication, our results suggest that the effects of excess salt on fruit quality are quite variable and seem to be genotype-dependent.

The present study has certain limitations, including the evaluation of a single guava cultivar under relatively controlled conditions. This may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other guava genotypes differing in sodicity tolerance, and provides only limited insights into the long-term effects of sodic irrigation on tree physiology and yield stability. Furthermore, only two amendments (gypsum and press mud) were tested, potentially overlooking the efficacy of other individual or combined ameliorants in alleviating sodicity stress in guava plants. Long-term studies incorporating diverse guava genotypes and amendments are needed to enhance the robustness of the findings and develop efficient practices for sustainable guava cultivation under sodic conditions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that irrigation with sodic water, particularly at elevated residual sodium carbonate levels (5.0–7.5 meq L⁻¹), causes marked reductions in plant growth, leaf area, chlorophyll content and photosynthetic rate in Taiwan Pink guava. These adverse effects were closely associated with impaired membrane stability, excessive Na⁺ accumulation, and depletion of K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺. Elevated proline levels and antioxidant enzyme activities reflected an adaptive response to sodicity-induced osmotic and oxidative stresses. Importantly, physico-biochemical fruit quality attributes remained largely unaffected across RSC levels and amendment treatments, indicating a degree of resilience in Taiwan Pink guava. Application of soil amendments significantly alleviated these effects, with gypsum proving more effective than press mud across various levels of sodicity. Collectively, these findings underscore that while high-RSC irrigation markedly hampers guava plant growth and physiology, the application of soil amendments- particularly gypsum- can sustain guava productivity in sodicity-prone regions.
