Optical response in six-level 85Rb and 87Rb atomic media
Authors: Le Quang Trung, Hoang Thi Bich Ngoc, Nguyen Thu An, Ho Nguyen An, Dang Khanh Huyen, Nguyen Huy Bang, Hoang Dinh Hai, Le Van Doai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we analytically investigate the optical response including absorption, dispersion, and group index of a six-level atomic system under electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT). A key advantage of this configuration is its ability to simultaneously generate four transparency windows at distinct frequency regions using only two controlling laser fields. The analytical model is applied to both 87Rb and 85Rb atoms in cold and hot conditions, enabling exploration under various experimental environments. The results provide a comprehensive picture of the fundamental optical properties of 87Rb and 85Rb atomic systems with the six-level configuration, offering useful insights for experimental predictions and potential applications.Subject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Understanding and controlling the optical response of atomic media-how atoms absorb, refract, and delay light-has been a longstanding goal in both classical and quantum optics. These fundamental properties not only reveal the underlying energy structure and coherence dynamics of atomic systems, but also underpin a wide range of photonic applications, from slow-light buffers to quantum memories and nonlinear optical devices. Among the most striking phenomena related to optical response is electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT)1, where quantum interference enables a resonant atomic medium to become nearly transparent to a weak probe beam. Along with transparency, EIT introduces sharp dispersion features, leading to highly tunable group indices and slow or fast light propagation2. These dramatic modifications of the optical response, suppressed absorption, enhanced dispersion, and large group delay have made EIT a key effect in the design of next-generation light-based technologies.

Since its discovery in 19911, EIT has become a cornerstone of research in coherent light-matter interactions, finding applications in slow light3, quantum memory4, all-optical switching5, high-resolution hyperfine spectroscopy6, and low-power nonlinear optics7 and so on. Early studies focused primarily on three-level atomic systems8–10, which offer a single transparency window and are relatively simple to analyze both theoretically and experimentally. However, advances in laser technology and atomic control have enabled exploration of more complex multi-level schemes, allowing for the generation of multiple EIT windows and richer optical responses.

Multi-window EIT configurations are particularly attractive for multi-frequency slow-light applications, optical signal multiplexing, and multi-channel quantum control. These can be realized through various architectures, including N-type, ladder-type, M-type, K-type schemes, often requiring several control fields to simultaneously address different transition pathways11–14. Recent studies have demonstrated that even with a limited number of control fields, multi-window EIT can emerge in atomic systems possessing closely spaced hyperfine or Zeeman sublevels15–18. Such configurations are not only experimentally feasible but also offer improved spectral flexibility and enhanced control over optical dispersion.

In parallel, the role of temperature has received increasing attention. While cold atom systems offer high spectral resolution and reduced Doppler broadening, hot vapor cells are easier to operate and integrate into compact devices. Understanding how EIT manifests under both cold and thermal conditions is crucial for real-world applications, particularly in atomic systems such as rubidium, where both isotopes (87Rb and 85Rb) exhibit rich hyperfine structures19–21.

Despite considerable experimental progress, most multi-level EIT studies rely on numerical simulations. Analytical approaches are scarce but essential, as they provide direct physical insight and scalability to broader parameter spaces. Recent analytical developments have paved the way for compact modeling of complex atomic systems, enabling accurate prediction of absorption, dispersion, and group index behavior under varying conditions16,19,22–24.

This work presents an analytical study of the optical response in a six-level atomic system driven by two coherent control fields. We focus on key quantities of interest in EIT media, namely, the absorption spectrum, dispersion profile, and group refractive index, and explore their behavior in both cold and hot ensembles of 87Rb and 85Rb atoms. A key characteristic of our model is the simultaneous generation of four distinct EIT windows, achieved without the need for additional control lasers. This multi-window structure offers enhanced flexibility for manipulating light propagation at multiple frequencies, a feature relevant for quantum information processing and broadband optical buffering. By systematically comparing the EIT spectra under varying isotopes and thermal conditions, we aim to identify optimal regimes for achieving large, tunable group indices and high-resolution transparency features. Our findings advance the theoretical understanding of EIT in complex atomic systems and support the development of practical schemes for light control in both laboratory settings and integrated photonic devices.

Applying to 87Rb and 85Rb atoms

In general, an analytical model can be applied to any atomic or molecular systems that have an energy-level diagram similar to that presented in Fig. 1. In this paper, we apply the analytical model for the Doppler broadened medium for the 5S1/2 – 5P3/2 – 5D5/2 transition of 85Rb and 87Rb atoms. The natural linewidths of the 5P3/2 and 5D5/2 states are 6.1 MHz and 0.97 MHz, respectively. For 85Rb atom, the probe laser drives the transition 5S1/2, F = 2→5P3/2, F′ = 3, while the coupling laser couples three sets of transitions 5P3/2, F′ = 3→5D5/2, F″ = 4, F″ = 3 and F″ = 2, and the signal laser drives the transition 5S1/2, F = 3→5P3/2, F′ = 3. For 87Rb atom, the probe laser drives the transition 5S1/2, F = 1→5P3/2, F′ = 2, and the coupling laser drives three transitions 5P3/2, F′ = 2→5D5/2, F″ = 3, F″ = 2 and F″ = 1, and the signal laser drives the transition 5S1/2, F = 2→5P3/2, F′ = 2. The frequency separations between the hyperfine levels of 87Rb atom are δ1 = 23.0 MHz and δ2 = 15.9 MHz while for the 85Rb atom, the corresponding values are as δ1 = 9.0 MHz and δ2 = 7.6 MHz. The relative transition strengths are a32 : a42 : a52 = 1 : 1.46 : 0.615.

Within the scope of this study, we investigate the optical responses of the atomic medium, including absorption, dispersion, and group refractive index. For each case applied to 87Rb and 85Rb atoms, we consider both cold atoms (cooled and trapped in MOT) and hot atoms at room temperature (T = 300 K). The coupling laser frequency is chosen to be resonant with the atomic transition, while the signal laser frequency is detuned from atomic resonance in order to generate four distinct EIT windows. In principle, this six-level configuration allows for a maximum of four EIT windows, corresponding to the four terms A, B, C, and D in Eq. (25). The positions of these EIT windows are determined by the two-photon resonance conditions: Δp + Δc = 0, Δp + Δc + δ1 = 0, Δp + Δc − δ2 = 0 and Δp − Δs = 0. Accordingly, if we set Δc = 0, the positions of the four EIT windows become: Δp = 0, Δp = −δ1, Δp = δ2 and Δp = Δs. It should be noted that if the signal laser is also tuned to atomic resonance (Δs = 0), two EIT windows will overlap at Δp = 0, resulting in only three observable EIT windows.

First, we apply the theoretical model to cold 87Rb atoms with hyperfine level separations of δ1 = 23 MHz and δ2 = 15.9 MHz. The absorption spectra (upper curves) and dispersion spectra (lower curves) of the cold 87Rb atoms are shown in Fig. 2. Here, the Rabi frequencies of the controlling laser fields are chosen as Ωc = Ωs = 10 MHz, Δc = 0, and Δs = −10 MHz (a) and Δs = 10 MHz (b). In the case of Fig. 2(a), with Δs = −10 MHz, four EIT windows are clearly observed at the probe detunings Δp = 0 (induced by the coupling laser on the |2〉−|3〉 transition), Δp = −δ1 = −23 MHz (induced by the coupling laser on the |2〉−|4〉 transition), Δp = δ2 = 15.9 MHz (induced by the coupling laser on the |2〉−|5〉 transition), and Δp = Δs = −10 MHz (induced by the signal laser on the |6〉−|2〉 transition). Similarly, in Fig. 2b, where Δs = 10 MHz, the EIT windows appear at Δp = 0, Δp = −δ1 = −23 MHz, Δp = δ2 = 15.9 MHz, and Δp = Δs = 10 MHz. Thus, the coupling laser can induce three EIT windows due to its ability to simultaneously couple three hyperfine transitions, while the signal laser contributes an additional EIT window in the probe absorption spectrum.

From this analysis, we can conclude that for room-temperature applications involving Rb atomic media, the EIT spectra of 87Rb atoms offer better resolution than those of 85Rb atoms.

One of the hallmark features of EIT spectra is the emergence of extremely steep dispersion curves with large amplitudes, significantly greater than those found in conventional materials. This steep dispersion leads to a dramatically enhanced group index, enabling the slowing down or even complete stopping of light within the EIT transparency window.

The presence of alternating slow-light (positive group index) and fast-light (negative group index) regions reflects the rapid variation of the dispersion slope near the transparency windows. In particular, the negative group index corresponds to anomalous dispersion, which is known to be associated with pulse advancement and fast-light propagation. Although fast light does not imply superluminal information transfer, such behavior can be exploited for applications such as temporal cloaking, optical delay compensation, and enhanced phase sensitivity in interferometry. In contrast, the positive group index regions correspond to normal dispersion and light slowing, which are essential for optical buffering, quantum memory, and nonlinear enhancement. The ability to tailor the group index through laser detuning and atomic configurations thus provides a versatile tool for controlling light propagation in atomic media. The results here demonstrate that cold 87Rb atoms under EIT conditions can support both slow and fast light over multiple spectral regions, offering rich possibilities for tunable light–matter interaction.

Figure 7 presents the group index curves for hot 87Rb atoms at room temperature (T = 300 K), corresponding to the absorption and dispersion profiles shown in Fig. 3. In this case, the Rabi frequencies of the controlling laser fields are set to Ωc = Ωs = 30 MHz, while the detuning parameters are kept the same as in Fig. 6. As in the cold atom case, both positive (slow-light) and negative (fast-light) group index regions emerge at similar spectral positions. However, the peak values of the positive group index are significantly enhanced compared to the negative ones. Moreover, the overall amplitude of the group index in the hot atom case is reduced by several orders of magnitude relative to that of the cold atoms, due to Doppler broadening. In addition, the spectral features are broadened significantly; resulting in reduced resolution and less sharply defined EIT-induced group delay structures.

These observations highlight the substantial influence of Doppler broadening on the group index behavior in hot atomic vapors. While the EIT features and associated slow- and fast-light regions persist, the reduction in peak group index and spectral resolution limits their effectiveness for applications requiring strong dispersion or long optical delays. Nevertheless, the enhanced stability, simplicity, and practicality of room-temperature atomic systems make them attractive for compact and robust implementations of EIT-based technologies. In particular, hot 87Rb vapor still supports tunable slow-light propagation, with sufficiently high group indices for applications such as optical delay lines, magneto-optical sensing, and phase-sensitive measurements. The results suggest that careful optimization of the laser parameters, especially the Rabi frequencies and detunings can partially compensate for Doppler-induced broadening. Thus, room-temperature Rb vapor cells remain viable platforms for EIT-based functional devices, especially where cryogenic or laser-cooling setups are impractical.

Similarly, Figs. 8 and 9 present the group index curves for cold and hot 85Rb atoms, respectively, corresponding to the absorption and dispersion conditions shown in Figs. 4 and 5. For 85Rb atoms, positive group index regions appear at spectral positions associated with EIT windows: Δp = 0, Δp = −9 MHz, Δp = 7.6 MHz and Δp = −15 MHz (a) or Δp = 15 MHz (b), interleaved with regions of negative group index. In the cold atom case (Fig. 8), prominent peaks of negative group index are observed, whereas in the hot atom case (Fig. 9), the positive group index peaks become significantly enhanced. Due to the relatively small hyperfine level separations in 85Rb, the corresponding group index features are more closely spaced, making individual EIT contributions more difficult to resolve.

The close spacing of group index peaks in 85Rb atom is a direct consequence of its smaller hyperfine separations compared to 87Rb atom, which leads to spectral crowding and reduced resolution in both the absorption and group index spectra. This spectral overlap becomes particularly problematic in the presence of Doppler broadening, where individual EIT-induced features merge, thus limiting the ability to isolate narrow slow-light windows. In contrast to 87Rb atom, where the group index structure is well separated and symmetric, 85Rb exhibits more complex and asymmetric group index profiles. Notably, in the cold atom regime, the fast-light (negative group index) regions dominate in amplitude, while in the hot atom case, slow-light (positive group index) peaks become more pronounced. This inversion of dominance between cold and hot conditions is indicative of different sensitivity of the EIT features to temperature and Doppler effects in the two isotopes. Overall, while 85Rb atoms can still exhibit EIT and associated dispersion control, the more favorable spectral separation in 87Rb makes it a superior choice for applications requiring high group index contrast, sharper resolution, and better tunability, especially in room-temperature environments.

Conclusion

In this study, we have analytically investigated the optical response of a six-level atomic system under electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT), focusing on absorption, dispersion, and group index. This configuration enables the generation of four distinct transparency windows using only two control fields, offering flexible multi-channel light manipulation within a single atomic ensemble. Our model has been systematically applied to both 87Rb and 85Rb isotopes under cold and hot conditions. The results highlight several key findings. In cold 87Rb atoms, the EIT windows are spectrally well-resolved, producing sharply varying dispersion profiles and giant group indices on the order of 104 − 106, suitable for slow-light and optical buffering applications. The ability to control the positions and strength of these transparency windows by adjusting laser detunings provides a high degree of spectral tunability and selectivity. In contrast, hot atomic vapors at room temperature suffer from Doppler broadening, which reduces the amplitude and sharpness of the EIT and group index features. Nevertheless, observable transparency windows and meaningful dispersion effects are retained when the Rabi frequencies of the control fields are increased appropriately. This demonstrates the practicality of implementing EIT-based slow- and fast-light phenomena even in thermal vapor cells, which are more accessible than cold-atom setups. For 85Rb, the narrower hyperfine level separations lead to denser spectral features and more significant overlap between adjacent EIT windows, particularly in the hot atom case. This limits the achievable spectral resolution but still allows for light-matter interaction modulation in regimes where 87Rb may be less favorable, such as in compact or isotopically mixed systems. The significance of this research lies in its comprehensive characterization of the fundamental optical properties of a complex atomic system under realistic conditions. The ability to predict and control EIT behavior across multiple channels and temperature regimes provides a strong foundation for the development of advanced quantum and classical optical technologies. Looking forward, this six-level EIT scheme can be extended to include nonlinear effects, interactions with structured light, or integration with nanophotonic structures such as hollow-core fibers and metasurfaces. Experimental validation of the predicted multi-window EIT spectra and group index dynamics, especially under room-temperature conditions, will be the next step toward practical implementations in optical memory, magnetometry, delay lines, and all-optical switching.
