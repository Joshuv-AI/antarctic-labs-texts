Poset-enriched pretoposes and compact ordered spaces
Jérémie Marquès, Luca Reggio

Abstract
. We provide a characterisation of the category 𝖪𝖮𝗋𝖽 \KOrd of Nachbin’s compact ordered spaces as a poset-enriched category. Up to equivalence, 𝖪𝖮𝗋𝖽 \KOrd is the only non-degenerate poset-enriched pretopos whose terminal object is a (discrete) generator and in which every object is covered by an order-filtral object. Order-filtral objects satisfy an appropriate form of compactness and separation. Throughout, we make extensive use of the internal language of poset-enriched pretoposes.



1.
Introduction

Compact ordered spaces, introduced by Nachbin in
[
Nac65
]
, are compact topological spaces equipped with a partial order that is closed in the product topology. The latter condition ensures compatibility between the topology and the order, and implies that every compact ordered space is Hausdorff. The natural morphisms between compact ordered spaces are the continuous order-preserving maps. We denote by
\KOrd
the resulting category.
Every compact Hausdorff space can be viewed as a compact ordered space with the trivial order, which gives rise to a fully faithful functor
\KH\hookrightarrow\KOrd
, from the category
\KH
of compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps, into
\KOrd
.
In logic, compact ordered spaces naturally arise as spaces of (model-theoretic) types of theories in coherent first-order logic, and extensions thereof; see e.g.
[
GM24
]
.

In
[
MR20
]
, the category
\KH
was characterised, up to equivalence, as the unique non-degenerate pretopos
\C
such that its terminal object is a generator (equivalently,
\C
is well-pointed and admits copowers of its terminal object), and each of its objects is covered by a
filtral
object. An object of a coherent category is filtral if its lattice of subobjects is canonically isomorphic to the filter completion of its Boolean center
[
MR20
, Definition 4.1]
. In
\KH
, filtral objects coincide with Stone spaces, i.e. totally disconnected compact Hausdorff spaces. The property of having enough filtral objects distinguishes
\KH
from the category
\Set
of sets and functions. The latter is also a non-degenerate pretopos whose terminal object is a generator; however, the filtral objects in
\Set
are the finite sets, therefore no infinite set is covered by a filtral object.

The main result of this paper is a characterisation, up to equivalence, of the category
\KOrd
of compact ordered spaces. Our characterisation takes place within the class of
poset-enriched categories
, i.e., categories enriched in the category
\Pos
of posets and order-preserving maps. Any ordinary category can be regarded as a poset-enriched category, albeit not necessarily in a unique way. This means that we are considering a broader setting. However, the advantage is that we can leverage a richer language. In fact, given a poset-enriched category with finite limits, its ordinary internal language is naturally enriched with a partial order
\leq_{X}
for each object
X
, and morphisms respect these orders. This viewpoint will be developed in detail in Section
3
.

Our main result can be stated as follows (for all undefined terms, see the additional details and references below):

Theorem 1.1
.

A poset-enriched category
\C
is equivalent to
\KOrd
if, and only if, it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

\C
is a non-degenerate poset-enriched pretopos;

(ii)

The terminal object of
\C
is a discrete generator;

(iii)

Every object of
\C
is covered by an order-filtral object.

While the latter characterisation of
\KOrd
is formally very similar to that of
\KH
obtained in
[
MR20
]
, the key difference lies in the poset-enrichment. Indeed, while
\KOrd
is not a pretopos in the ordinary sense,
\KH
is not a poset-enriched pretopos when considered as a poset-enriched category in the obvious way.

2

[

. A

The notion of a generator in Theorem

, is weaker than the more standard notion of generator in poset-enriched categories (as used in, e.g.,
[

). The main difference lies in the use of copowers rather than arbitrary tensors. However, the two notions coincide for poset-enriched exact categories and, a fortiori, for poset-enriched pretoposes. Generators and projective covers are discussed in Section

In particular, if such a category is exact, it can be recovered from any of its projective covers. As an aside, we derive a sufficient criterion for a poset-enriched exact category to be monadic over

In Section
5

Finally, in Section

2.
Preliminaries on poset-enriched categories

A
poset-enriched category
is a category enriched in the category
\Pos
of posets and order-preserving maps. In other words, it is a category
\mathsf{A}
whose hom-sets are equipped with partial orders in such a way that composition is order-preserving. For any two objects
x,y\in\mathsf{A}
, the poset of arrows
x\to y
is denoted by
\mathsf{A}(x,y)
.
Many of the basic concepts of ordinary category theory extend to enriched categories, and in particular to poset-enriched categories. We refer the reader to
[
Kel05
]
for a thorough treatment.

A
poset-enriched functor
is a functor
F\colon\mathsf{A}\to\mathsf{B}
between poset-enriched categories such that the maps
\mathsf{A}(x,y)\to\mathsf{B}(F(x),F(y))
are order-preserving. We say that
F
is
faithful
if these maps are order-embeddings, and
fully faithful
if they are order-isomorphisms. Given two poset-enriched categories
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, the category of poset-enriched functors
[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}]
is also poset-enriched (although it is not locally small unless
\mathsf{A}
is small).

By a
category
or a
functor
, we will always mean a poset-enriched category or a poset-enriched functor. An
ordinary
category or functor is one that is not enriched. We may occasionally still speak of a
poset-enriched
category or functor to emphasise that it is not ordinary. The canonical way to regard a poset-enriched category as an ordinary category is to discard the orders. Conversely, the canonical way to view an ordinary category as a poset-enriched category is to equip its hom-sets with discrete orders.

Given a category
\mathsf{A}
, the category
\mathsf{A}^{\mathrm{op}}
is obtained by reversing the direction of the arrows, but
not
their order. A functor
\mathsf{A}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\Pos
is called a (poset-enriched)
presheaf
. The hom-functor
\mathsf{A}^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathsf{A}\to\Pos
is poset-enriched, and by currying, we obtain the enriched
Yoneda embedding

\mathsf{A}\to[\mathsf{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\Pos]
. As in the ordinary case, the Yoneda embedding is fully faithful; see, for example,
[
Kel05
, §2.4]
.

The notions of limit and colimit for poset-enriched categories are direct extensions of the corresponding notions for ordinary categories, yet they do not reduce to them. For example, a product of objects
X
and
Y
in a poset-enriched category
\mathsf{C}
is an object
X\times Y
such that, for every object
Z
, the natural maps

\C(Z,X\times Y)\to\C(Z,X)\times\C(Z,Y)

are order-isomorphisms. Thus, while
\mathsf{C}
may have binary products as an ordinary category, it may not have them as a poset-enriched category because the natural bijections may fail to reflect the order. In the poset-enriched setting, limits and colimits are defined by requiring the associated natural bijections to be order-isomorphisms.

In a poset-enriched category
\mathsf{C}
, the usual limits, built from equalisers and products, are called
conical
and are a special case of
weighted
limits. Dually, conical colimits are a special case of weighted colimits. We recall some examples of non-conical limits and colimits that will be relevant later on, by describing the presheaves that they represent:

•

The

General weighted limits are obtained as follows. We start with a diagram

3.
Poset-enriched pretoposes and their internal logic

In this section, we recall the notion of poset-enriched pretopos. As mentioned in the Introduction, this is a simpler version of the concept of
2
-pretopos, i.e. an exact
2
-category with universal, disjoint finite coproducts. These
2
-categorical exactness conditions were introduced in
[
Str82
]
; for the poset-enriched case, see
[
KV17
,
AS22
]
.

Rather than recalling the general
2
-categorical definition, we will introduce poset-enriched pretoposes and their internal language in an elementary way. This approach is guided by the analogy with ordinary pretoposes, which are syntactic categories of first-order coherent theories. First, we will review the internal language of an ordinary category with finite limits. For a poset-enriched category with finite limits, we will see that its (ordinary) internal language is further equipped with a distinguished partial order for each object and that morphisms preserve these orders. By progressively adding more structure, we arrive at the notion of a poset-enriched pretopos. At each step, we will explain how the internal language is enriched or modified.

3.1.
Finite limits

We briefly recall the internal logic of categories with finite limits, that is,
finite-limit
or
cartesian
logic. For more details, the reader can consult
[
Joh02
, Chapter D1]
. Formulas of cartesian logic for a given first-order signature are formed in the usual way by using the logical operations of conjunction
\varphi\land\psi
and the equality of variables
x=y
, in addition to the truth constant
\top
.
4
4

4

For simplicity, we leave aside provably unique existential quantification, which is needed to obtain an exact correspondence between formulas and subobjects when constructing the syntactic category of a cartesian theory. However, this is not necessary here.

Let
\C
be an ordinary category with finite limits. The
internal language
of
\C
consists of a multi-sorted first-order signature, together with a canonical interpretation in
\C
of cartesian formulas on that signature. The signature is defined as follows:

•

the sorts are the objects of
\C
;

•

the predicates of type
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
are the subobjects of
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
in
\C
, i.e., the equivalence classes of monomorphisms with this codomain;

•

the operations of type
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}\to Y
are the morphisms in
\C
with matching domain and codomain.

Each term
t
in this signature comes equipped with a
context
, which is a finite family of typed variables
(x_{i}:X_{i})_{i}
that contains all the variables appearing in
t
. Similarly, each cartesian formula
\varphi
comes with a context containing all the variables that occur freely in
\varphi
. However, not every variable in the respective context needs to occur in
t
, or freely in
\varphi
. Given a term
t
in context
(x_{i}:X_{i})_{i}
, its interpretation
\llbracket t\rrbracket\colon\prod_{i}X_{i}\to Y
is defined inductively:
\llbracket x_{j}\rrbracket\colon\prod_{i}X_{i}\to X_{j}
is the canonical projection, and
\llbracket f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\rrbracket
is the composite of
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket\times\dots\times\llbracket t_{n}\rrbracket
and
\llbracket f\rrbracket
. Given a formula
\varphi
in context
(x_{i}:X_{i})_{i}
, its interpretation
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
is the subobject of
\prod_{i}X_{i}
defined inductively as follows:

•

when
R
is a predicate,
\llbracket R(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\rrbracket
is the pullback of
R
by
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket\times\dots\times\llbracket t_{m}\rrbracket
, where
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
is a list of terms in context
(x_{i}:X_{i})_{i}
;

•

conjunctions are interpreted as pullbacks (i.e., as intersections of subobjects);

•

equality is interpreted as the diagonal
\langle\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}\rangle\colon X\hookrightarrow X\times X
, written
\Delta_{X}
.

Given two formulas
\varphi
and
\psi
in the same context, we write

\varphi\vdash\psi

provided that
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\psi\rrbracket
as subobjects. We sometimes write
\vdash\varphi
instead of
\top\vdash\varphi
.

An important feature of the internal language is the following
principle of compositionality
. Let
\varphi
be a formula in context
x_{1}:X_{1},\dots,x_{n}:X_{n}
. Let
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
be terms with common domain, and respective codomains
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. We denote by
\varphi[t_{1}/x_{1},\dots,t_{n}/x_{n}]
the formula obtained by simultaneously substituting
x_{i}
by
t_{i}
in
\varphi
.
The interpretation
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
of
\varphi
is a subobject of
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
, so it can be seen as a predicate of this type, from which we can build the formula
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
. Then

\llbracket\varphi[t_{1}/x_{1},\dots,t_{n}/x_{n}]\rrbracket=\llbracket\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\rrbracket.

In other words, substitutions are realised as pullbacks. This can also be regarded as a principle of substitution of equals for equals: if two formulas are equivalent, they will remain so after a substitution.

Example 3.1
.

We can define the concept of an (internal) equivalence relation in the internal language: it is a predicate
x\sim y
of type
X\times X
such that
x\sim y\vdash y\sim x
,
\vdash x\sim x
, and
(x\sim y)\land(y\sim z)\vdash x\sim z
. A preorder relation
\leq_{X}
on
X
is defined similarly, but without the symmetry condition. A morphism
f\colon X\to Y
is said to be
order-preserving
with respect to two preorders
\leq_{X}
and
\leq_{Y}
if
a\leq_{X}b\vdash f(a)\leq_{Y}f(b)
.

The internal language provides a concise notation for manipulating subobjects. In practice, we will describe the proofs in plain English where more convenient, manipulating the variables as if they were elements of sets. However, the reader should bear in mind that these proofs can be unpacked. We illustrate this “unpacking” with an example.

Example 3.2
.

Using the internal language, let us show that, in a category
\mathsf{C}
with finite limits, the composite of two order-preserving arrows
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to Z
is order-preserving. The formal proof is simply

a\leq_{X}b\vdash f(a)\leq_{Y}f(b)\vdash g(f(a))\leq_{Z}g(f(b)).

The first entailment, which is an inclusion of subobjects in
X\times X
, holds because
f
is order-preserving. The second entailment is obtained from
u\leq_{Y}v\vdash g(u)\leq_{Z}g(v)
by substituting
u
with
f(a)
, and
v
with
f(b)
. To interpret this in
\mathsf{C}
, we start with the inclusion of
[\leq_{Y}]
in the pullback of
[\leq_{Z}]
along
g\times g
. The substitution corresponds to pulling back once more along
f\times f
. As pullback squares compose, the right-hand side of the inclusion is the pullback of
[\leq_{Z}]
along
(fg)\times(fg)
. In terms of the internal logic, the fact that these pullbacks compose is the aforementioned principle of compositionality. Finally, we obtain
a\leq_{X}b\vdash g(f(a))\leq_{Z}g(f(b))
by composing the inclusions.

We will often use the set comprehension notation to make the context of a formula explicit. For instance,

\{(x,y)\in X\times Y\,|\,\mathopen{}\varphi(x,y)\}

stands for
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
, where
\varphi
is understood as a formula in context
x:X,y:Y
, even though
x
and
y
do not necessarily occur freely in
\varphi
.

Remark 3.3
.

All concepts expressible in cartesian logic admit an equivalent formulation using generalised elements (recall that a
generalised element
of
X
is an arrow
T\to X
). For instance, an internal equivalence relation is often defined as a subobject
R
of
X\times X
such that
\C(T,R)\subseteq\C(T,X)\times\C(T,X)
is an equivalence relation, in the usual set-theoretic sense, for every object
T
. This correspondence between internal and external descriptions holds because the Yoneda embedding preserves (finite) limits and is conservative. For any formula
\varphi(x)
, the subobject
\{p\in X\,|\,\mathopen{}\varphi(p)\}
of
X
represents the sub-presheaf of
\C(-,X)
that takes
T
to the set of arrows
p\colon T\to X
such that
\varphi(p)
is true when interpreted as an “external” statement: conjunctions and equalities are interpreted externally, and
f(p)
is interpreted as the composite
pf
.

3.2.
Finite weighted limits

Firstly, let us review the interplay, in an ordinary category
\mathsf{C}
, between the external equality of arrows and the diagonals
\Delta_{X}\colon X\hookrightarrow X\times X
. The object
X\times X
represents the pairs of parallel morphisms to
X
, and the diagonal represents the sub-presheaf of those pairs of morphisms that are
equal

Definition 3.4
.

A category
\C
with finite limits has
epi-diagonals
if, for every
X\in\C
, the presheaf

F_{X}\colon\C^{\mathrm{op}}\to\Pos,\ \ Y\mapsto\{f,g\colon Y\rightrightarrows X\,|\,\mathopen{}f\leq g\}

is representable. In this case, the object representing
F_{X}
is denoted by
[\leq_{X}]\hookrightarrow X\times X
.

Note that, if it exists, the epi-diagonal
[\leq_{X}]\hookrightarrow X\times X
is a partial order by Remark
3.3
.

We will now assume that
\mathsf{C}
has finite limits and epi-diagonals. In particular, it has finite limits as an ordinary category. Furthermore, its internal language as an ordinary category is enriched with a partial order
\leq_{X}
for each object
X
. These orders are “uniform” in the sense that every morphism
f\colon X\to Y
is order-preserving. Indeed, we have
x\leq_{X}x^{\prime}\vdash f(x)\leq_{Y}f(x^{\prime})
, as can be seen by reasoning as in Remark
3.3
, replacing
x
and
x^{\prime}
with two parallel arrows in
\mathsf{C}
. We will simply write
\leq
instead of
\leq_{X}
.

Definition 3.5
.

A morphism
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathsf{C}
is an
embedding
if
f(x)\leq f(y)\vdash x\leq y
, and an
injection
if
f(x)=f(y)\vdash x=y
. Embeddings are denoted by
X\hookrightarrow Y
.

By Remark
3.3
,
f
is an embedding if and only if the induced map
\C(T,X)\to\C(T,Y)
is an order-embedding for all
T
; that is, in
2
-categorical terminology,
f
is an
ff
-morphism
, where
ff
stands for
(representably) fully faithful
. Similarly,
f
is an injection precisely when the induced maps
\C(T,X)\to\C(T,Y)
are injective, i.e., when
f
is a monomorphism.

Example 3.6
.

Any split monomorphism

is an embedding. Just observe that if

The nerve functor
N_{P}\colon\mathsf{A}\to[{P}^{\mathrm{op}},\Pos]
preserves finite weighted limits for any full subcategory
{P}\subseteq\mathsf{A}
, and it preserves surjections if and only if each object of
{P}
is projective (just recall that the surjections in
[{P}^{\mathrm{op}},\Pos]
are the component-wise surjections). Hence,
N_{{P}}
is a regular functor.
Now, let
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
in
\mathsf{A}
satisfy
N_{P}(f)\leq N_{P}(g)
, and let
q\colon P\twoheadrightarrow X
be a surjection with
P\in{P}
. Since
qf\leq qg
and
q
is a surjection, we get
f\leq g
by Lemma
3.12
. This shows that
N_{P}
is faithful.

To prove that
N_{P}
is full, let
F\colon N_{P}(X)\to N_{P}(Y)
be a natural transformation. In the spirit of the Yoneda lemma, consider
F(q)\in\mathsf{A}(P,Y)
. We will show that there exists a morphism
f\colon X\to Y
making the following diagram commute.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(q)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

Since
{P}
is a projective cover, there exists
Q\in{P}
that covers the lax kernel
\lker(q)
.
Let
a,b\colon Q\rightrightarrows P
be the two induced morphisms, which satisfy
aq\leq bq
. By naturality of
F
, we get
aF(q)=F(aq)\leq F(bq)=bF(q)
. This shows that
\lker(q)\subseteq\lker(F(q))
. Hence, there is
f\colon X\to Y
as desired. We claim that
F=N_{P}(f)
. Let
g\colon P^{\prime}\to X
with
P^{\prime}\in{P}
. By projectivity, there is
g^{\prime}\colon P^{\prime}\to P
such that
g^{\prime}q=g
. Thus,
F(g)=F(g^{\prime}q)=g^{\prime}F(q)=g^{\prime}qf=gf
.
This concludes the proof that
N_{P}
is regular and fully faithful.

Now, assume
\mathsf{A}
is exact. As we have just seen, each object
X\in\mathsf{A}
has a “presentation”

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

with
P,Q\in{P}
. The image of
Q\rightrightarrows P
(more precisely, of the induced arrow
{Q\to P^{2}}
) is the congruence
\lker(q)
, therefore
X
is the quotient of
P
by this image. Since regular functors preserve congruences,
N_{P}(X)
is the quotient of the image of
N_{P}(Q)\rightrightarrows N_{P}(P)
.
Conversely, given arrows
N_{P}(Q)\rightrightarrows N_{P}(P)
whose image is a congruence, the image of
Q\rightrightarrows P
is also a congruence, because
N_{P}
is fully faithful and thus conservative. Since
\mathsf{A}
is exact, the quotient of
Q\rightrightarrows P
exists and is sent to the quotient of
N_{P}(Q)\rightrightarrows N_{P}(P)
, which is therefore in the image of
N_{P}
. This proves the second part of the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The proof that
N_{P}\colon\mathsf{A}\to[{P}^{\mathrm{op}},\Pos]
is a fully faithful regular functor can be phrased using the
(1,2)
-categorical notion of a Grothendieck topology. For further information on
2
-categorical sheaf theory, we refer the interested reader to
[
Str82
]
.

Let
J_{\mathrm{reg}}
be the topology on
\mathsf{A}
generated by the surjections. We have the following chain of embeddings and equivalences:

\mathsf{A}\subseteq\Sh(\mathsf{A},J_{\mathrm{reg}})\simeq\Sh({P},J_{\mathrm{reg}})=[{P}^{\mathrm{op}},\Pos].

The first embedding
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\Sh(\mathsf{A},J_{\mathrm{reg}})
is fully faithful and regular. Since every object of
\mathsf{A}
is covered by an object of
{P}
, we have
\Sh(\mathsf{A},J_{\mathrm{reg}})\simeq\Sh({P},J_{\mathrm{reg}})
, where the second topology is the restriction of the first one. Finally, in
({P},J_{\mathrm{reg}})
every cover is a split epimorphism, hence the topology is trivial and
\Sh({P},J_{\mathrm{reg}})=[{P}^{\mathrm{op}},\Pos]
.

Remark 4.4
.

Theorem
4.2
can be seen as a variant of Barr’s embedding theorem that applies to (poset-enriched) regular categories with enough projectives. Barr’s embedding theorem states that if
\mathsf{A}
is an ordinary regular category, then the regular functor
\mathsf{A}\to[\cMod(\mathsf{A})^{\mathrm{op}},\Set]
is fully faithful, where
\cMod(\mathsf{A})
is the category of
models
of
\mathsf{A}
, i.e., the regular functors
\mathsf{A}\to\Set
. An object
P
of
\mathsf{A}
is projective exactly when
\mathsf{A}(P,-)\colon\mathsf{A}\to\Set
is a regular functor. Hence, the projective objects can be considered a particularly simple type of model, sitting at the intersection of syntax and semantics. When there are enough projectives, we can replace the category of models
\cMod(\mathsf{A})
in Barr’s embedding theorem with its full subcategory determined by the projective objects.

Theorem
4.2
has the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}
be exact categories, and let
{P}_{\mathsf{A}}\subseteq\mathsf{A}
and
{P}_{\mathsf{B}}\subseteq\mathsf{B}
be projective covers. If
{P}_{\mathsf{A}}\simeq{P}_{\mathsf{B}}
then
\mathsf{A}\simeq\mathsf{B}
.

In
\Pos
, every object is covered by a set, and every set is projective. Thus,
\Set\hookrightarrow\Pos
is a projective cover (in fact, every projective object in
\Pos
is order-discrete). The following is a generalisation of the observation that sets are projective in
\Pos
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X
be a projective object in a regular category
\mathsf{A}
. For any set
S
, if the copower
S\cdot X
exists, then it is projective.

Proof.

The functors
\mathsf{A}(X,-)
and
\Pos(S,-)
preserve surjections because
X
and
S
are projective in
\mathsf{A}
and
\Pos
, respectively. Therefore, their composite
\Pos(S,\mathsf{A}(X,-))\cong\mathsf{A}(S\cdot X,-)
preserves surjections. That is,
S\cdot X
is projective.
∎

4.2.
Generators and discrete generators

We start by recalling the customary definition of a generator, cf. e.g.
[
KV17
]
.

Definition 4.7
.

An object
G
of a regular category
\mathsf{A}
is a
generator
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

for every poset
P
, the tensor
P\cdot G
exists in
\A
;

(ii)

for every
X\in\mathsf{A}
, the canonical arrow
\A(G,X)\cdot G\to X
is a surjection.

Given a
projective generator

G
in
\mathsf{A}
, i.e., a generator that is also a projective object, we want to construct a projective cover of
\mathsf{A}
. To this end, consider the full subcategory of
\mathsf{A}
spanned by the objects of the form
P\cdot G
, with
P
a poset. Since
G
is a generator, every object of
\mathsf{A}
is covered by an object of this form. But in general, objects of the form
P\cdot G
need not be projective, unless
P
is order-discrete (Lemma
4.6
). This naturally leads us to consider copowers of
G
, and the following variant of the notion of generator.

Definition 4.8
.

An object
G
of a regular category
\mathsf{A}
is a
discrete generator
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

for every set
S
, the copower
S\cdot G
exists in
\A
;

(ii)

for every
X\in\mathsf{A}
, the canonical arrow
\lvert\A(G,X)\rvert\cdot G\to X
is a surjection.

Remark 4.9
.

Item
(ii)
in Definition
4.7
is equivalent to saying that for every
X\in\mathsf{A}
there exist a poset
P
and a surjection
P\cdot G\twoheadrightarrow X
. Similarly, item
(ii)
in Definition
4.8
is equivalent to saying that for every
X\in\mathsf{A}
there exist a set
S
and a surjection
S\cdot G\twoheadrightarrow X
.

The discussion above yields the following fact.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a regular category and
G
a projective discrete generator in
\mathsf{A}
. The full subcategory of
\mathsf{A}
spanned by the copowers
S\cdot G
, with
S
a set, is a projective cover.

The next proposition shows that, for regular categories, the concept of a discrete generator is a weakening of that of a generator. However, the two notions coincide for exact categories.

Proposition 4.11
.

The following statements hold in any regular category
\mathsf{A}
:

(a)

Every generator is a discrete generator.

(b)

If
\mathsf{A}
is exact, then every discrete generator is a generator.

Moreover, if
\mathsf{A}
is exact and admits a generator, then it is well-powered and cocomplete.

Proof.

(a)
Suppose
G
is a generator. Since all tensors
P\cdot G
exist, there is an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pos}
\top
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\cdot G}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A(G,-)}

The right adjoint preserves (finite) weighted limits, and in particular embeddings, and so the left adjoint
-\cdot G
preserves surjections (see, e.g.,
[
Cis19
, Proposition 2.1.12]
). Therefore, the identity map
\lvert\A(G,X)\rvert\twoheadrightarrow\A(G,X)
in
\Pos
induces a surjection

\lvert\A(G,X)\rvert\cdot G\twoheadrightarrow\A(G,X)\cdot G.

If
X
is any object of
\mathsf{A}
, the canonical map
\A(G,X)\cdot G\to X
is a surjection, and so the composite yields a surjection
\lvert\A(G,X)\rvert\cdot G\twoheadrightarrow X
. Hence,
G
is a discrete generator.

(b)
Assume that
\mathsf{A}
is exact, and let
G
be a discrete generator. To show that
G
is a generator, it is enough to prove that the tensor
P\cdot G
exists for every poset
P
. This, in turn, follows if we prove the second part of the statement, namely that
\mathsf{A}
is well-powered and cocomplete.
Fix an arbitrary
X\in\mathsf{A}
, and write
\mathcal{P}(T)
for the power-set of a set
T
. We claim that there is a Galois connection

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sub(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(|\mathsf{A}(G,X)|),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}

where
R
sends
Y\subseteq X
to the set of arrows
G\to X
that factor through
Y
, and
L
sends
S\subseteq|\mathsf{A}(G,X)|
to the image of
S\cdot G\to X
. Since every
Y\subseteq X
is covered by
|\mathsf{A}(G,Y)|\cdot G
, we have
L(R(Y))=Y
. Furthermore, every morphism in
S\subseteq|\mathsf{A}(G,X)|
factors through the image of
S\cdot G\to X
, that is
S\subseteq R(L(S))
. Hence,
\Sub(X)
is a reflective sub-poset of
\mathcal{P}(|\mathsf{A}(G,X)|)
. This entails that
\Sub(X)
is small and complete.

Next, we prove that
\mathsf{A}
admits coinserters and small coproducts, hence it is cocomplete; cf.
[

, Proposition 5.2]
.
Consider arrows

in
\mathsf{A}
. For any relation
R\subseteq X^{2}
, define the functor

C_{R}\colon\mathsf{A}\to\Pos,\ \ C_{R}(Y)\coloneqq\{f\colon X\to Y\,|\,\mathopen{}R\subseteq\lker(f)\}.

The universal property of the coinserter of
u
and
v
is to represent the functor
C_{\im(u,v)}
, where the relation
\im(u,v)
is the image of
u,v\colon Y\rightrightarrows X
. We claim that
C_{R}
is always representable. In particular, it will follow that the coinserter of
u
and
v
exists.

If
R
is a congruence, then
C_{R}
is representable by Lemma
3.18
, because
\mathsf{A}
is exact. In general, note that
R
is a congruence exactly when it is a fixpoint of

\Sub(X^{2})\to\Sub(X^{2}),\ \ R\mapsto[\leq_{X}]\lor R\lor(R\circ R).

This map is expansive and order-preserving. As the poset
\Sub(X^{2})
is complete, for every
R\subseteq X^{2}
there is a smallest fixpoint
R^{*}
above
R
, by the Knaster–Tarski fixpoint theorem. Moreover, since
\lker(f)
is a congruence,
C_{R}=C_{R^{*}}
. Thus, since
C_{R^{*}}
is representable, so is
C_{R}
. We shall call the object representing
C_{R}
the
quotient
of
X
by
R
.

It remains to show that
\mathsf{A}
admits small coproducts. Let
(A_{i})_{i}
be a set of objects of
\mathsf{A}
. For each
i
, let
T_{i}\cdot G\rightrightarrows S_{i}\cdot G\twoheadrightarrow A_{i}
be a presentation of
A_{i}
as in the proof of Theorem

, with
S_{i}
and
T_{i}
sets. The coproduct of the

is then the quotient of
(\coprod_{i}S_{i})\cdot G
by the union of the images of the arrows
Y_{j}\cdot G\rightrightarrows(\coprod_{i}S_{i})\cdot G
.
∎

Although this fact will not be needed later on, we will prove that any exact category

that has a projective generator

is monadic over

. When
\mathsf{A}
has coinserters and

is presentable, this follows from
[

, Theorem 5.9]
.
We offer a proof that does not rely on the aforementioned theorem (which uses a result by Bloom and Wright
[

]
), but rather on Theorem

.
The analogue of this result for ordinary categories was proved in
[

, Proposition 3.2]
, as part of a characterisation of categories monadic over
\Set
.

Theorem 4.12
.

If
\A
is an exact category admitting a projective (discrete) generator

Write

is exact, it is equivalent to the full subcategory

consisting of those presheaves that are obtained as the quotient of a representable presheaf by a congruence that is covered by another representable presheaf. The proof of Theorem

shows that even in the absence of exactness, the essential image of

is contained in

.

Since the composite

∎

Remark 4.13
.

From Theorem

, we can recover the result of Flagg stating that the underlying-poset functor

5.
Well-pointed cocomplete pretoposes

In this section, we study pretoposes whose terminal object
\mathbb{1}
is a generator.
By Lemma
5.1
below, this implies that
\mathbb{1}
is a projective generator, and so the pretopos is “controlled” by its projective cover
\{S\cdot\mathbb{1}\,|\,\mathopen{}S\in\Set\}
by Corollary
4.5
. Further, we shall see that these pretoposes are precisely the well-pointed cocomplete pretoposes.
For any non-degenerate pretopos in this class, we will construct a functor into
\KOrd
that sends
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
to the Stone–Čech compactification
\beta(S)
. While the functor itself is not an equivalence, it will allow us to characterise
\KOrd
as a pretopos in Section
6
.

Throughout this section, we fix an arbitrary pretopos
\mathsf{C}
. We say that
\mathsf{C}
is
degenerate
if
\vdash\bot
, i.e.,
\mathsf{C}
is equivalent to the category with only one object and one morphism. Given
X\in\mathsf{C}
, recall from Section
3.5
that a point, or constant, of
X
is an arrow
\mathbb{1}\to X
; each such point is an embedding.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mathbb{1}
is a generator of
\mathsf{C}
, then it is projective.

Proof.

Let
f\colon X\twoheadrightarrow Y
be a surjection and let
p\colon\mathbb{1}\to Y
be a point. The unique arrow
f^{-1}(p)\to\mathbb{1}
is surjective because surjections are stable under pullbacks. If
f^{-1}(p)
has no points, then it is empty, since
\mathbb{1}
is a generator. In this case,
\mathbb{1}
is also empty and the pretopos is degenerate, so
\mathbb{1}
is trivially projective. Otherwise,
f^{-1}(p)
has a point
\mathbb{1}\to f^{-1}(p)\subseteq X
, which yields a point
q\colon\mathbb{1}\to X
satisfying
qf=p
.
∎

Definition 5.2
.

\mathsf{C}
is
well-pointed
if for every subobject
\varphi\subseteq X
, we have
\vdash\varphi(x)
if and only if, for every point
c\colon\mathbb{1}\to X
,
\vdash\varphi(c)
.

Proposition 5.3
.

\mathsf{C}
is well-pointed and cocomplete if, and only if,
\mathbb{1}
is a generator.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{1}
is a generator. By Proposition
4.11
,
\mathsf{C}
is cocomplete. Moreover, if
X\in\mathsf{C}
, and
Y
is a subobject of
X
that contains all the points
\mathbb{1}\to X
, then it contains the image of
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},X)\cdot\mathbb{1}\twoheadrightarrow X
. That is,
Y=X
. This shows that
\mathsf{C}
is well-pointed.

For the other direction, suppose that
\mathsf{C}
is well-pointed and cocomplete. In particular, the tensor
P\cdot\mathbb{1}
exists for every poset
P
. For each
X\in\mathsf{C}
, the image of
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},X)\cdot\mathbb{1}\to X
contains all the points of
X
, hence it covers
X
because
\mathsf{C}
is well-pointed.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Direct inspection shows that Lemma
5.1
and Proposition
5.3
hold not only for pretoposes, but more generally for exact categories.

To define a functor
\mathsf{C}\to\KOrd
, whenever
\mathsf{C}
is non-degenerate and its terminal object is a generator, we consider upward complemented subobjects in
\mathsf{C}
. These can be defined in any regular category as follows.

Definition 5.5
.

In a regular category, each subobject
U\subseteq X
has an
upward closure

{\uparrow}U\subseteq X
defined as
\{x\in X\,|\,\mathopen{}\exists u\in U.\ u\leq x\}
. If
U={\uparrow}U
, we say that
U
is
upward closed
, or that it is an
upward subobject
of
X
. Moreover, a subobject
U\subseteq X
is
complemented
if it has a complement in
\Sub(X)
. We denote by
\CU(X)
the sub-poset of
\Sub(X)
consisting of the upward complemented subobjects of
X
.

It is not difficult to see, using the internal logic, that for any object
X\in\mathsf{C}
,
\CU(X)
is a sublattice of
\Sub(X)
. Furthermore, if
f\colon X\to Y
, then
f^{-1}
restricts to a lattice homomorphism
\CU(Y)\to\CU(X)
. Unlike
\Sub\colon\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\DL
, the presheaf

\CU\colon\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\DL

is poset-enriched. Just observe that, if
f,g\in\C(X,Y)
satisfy
f\leq g
, then
f(x)\leq g(x)
and so
f(x)\in U\vdash g(x)\in U
for any
U\in\CU(Y)
. That is,
f^{-1}\leq g^{-1}
.

For the next lemma, recall from Example
3.26
that
\mathbf{2}
is the two-element poset
\{0<1\}
, and the tensor
\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}
can be characterised in the internal logic as the only object with two constants
0
and
1
such that
\vdash(x=0)\lor(x=1)
,
\vdash 0\leq 1
and
1\leq 0\vdash\bot
.

Lemma 5.6
.

The presheaf
\CU\colon\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\DL
is represented by
\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}
.

Proof.

The universal element, witnessing the representability of
\CU
, is
\{1\}\in\CU(\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})
. It is indeed an upward complemented subobject: its complement is
\{0\}
, and if
1\leq x
then either
x=0
or
x=1
, but the former is impossible since
1\leq 0\vdash\bot
.
We must show that the map sending
f\in\mathsf{C}(X,\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})
to
f^{-1}(1)\in\CU(X)
is an order-isomorphism.

To see that it is an order-embedding, consider
f,g\colon X\to\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}
. We claim that if
f(x)=1\vdash g(x)=1
, then
f\leq g
. There are four possible cases, depending on whether
f(x)=0
or
f(x)=1
, and whether
g(x)=0
or
g(x)=1
. The only case in which
f(x)\leq g(x)
does not hold is when
f(x)=1
and
g(x)=0
. However, the latter cannot occur because
f(x)=1\vdash g(x)=1
.

For surjectivity, let
U\in\CU(X)
and let
D
be its complement in
\Sub(X)
. We define a relation
R\subseteq X\times\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}
by

R(x,y)\iff(U(x)\land y=1)\lor(D(x)\land y=0).

We have
\vdash\exists y.\ R(x,y)
, since either
U(x)
and we take
y=1
, or
D(x)
and we take
y=0
. To show that
R
is also order-preserving, suppose that
x\leq x^{\prime}
,
R(x,y)
and
R(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. We need to deduce that
y\leq y^{\prime}
. By case analysis on
(y,y^{\prime})
, it is enough to note that
(y=1)\land(y^{\prime}=0)
is impossible. In fact, it would mean that
x\leq x^{\prime}\land R(x,1)\land R(x^{\prime},0)
. But
R(x,1)
implies
U(x)
, and
R(x^{\prime},0)
implies
D(x^{\prime})
, contradicting the fact that
U
is upward closed. By Proposition
3.15
, there is a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}
whose graph is
R
. Since
R(x,1)
if and only if
U(x)
,
f
satisfies
f^{-1}(1)=U
.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

If
\mathbb{1}
is a generator of
\mathsf{C}
, then its only subobjects are
\bot
and
\top
.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{1}
is a generator of
\mathsf{C}
, then every object with no points is the empty coproduct, and so it is empty. Thus, any non-empty subobject
A\subseteq\mathbb{1}
has a point. The composite
\mathbb{1}\to A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{1}
is surjective because it is the identity, and therefore
A=\mathbb{1}
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

If
\mathsf{C}

\mathbb{1}

Proof.

By Lemma

, it suffices to show that
\mathsf{C}(P\cdot\mathbb{1},\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})\cong\Pos(P,\mathbf{2})
. Suppose for a moment that the statement holds when

\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})\cong\mathbf{2}
. Then, for every poset

\mathsf{C}(P\cdot\mathbb{1},\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})\cong\Pos(P,\C(\mathbb{1},\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}))\cong\Pos(P,\mathbf{2}).

It remains to prove that
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})\cong\mathbf{2}

0,1\colon\mathbb{1}\to\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}
are distinct and for any

or

\mathsf{C}

and
(x=0)\land(x=1)\vdash\bot

\Sub(\mathbb{1})

or

∎

6.
Order-filtrality, and a characterisation of
\KOrd

In this section, we provide a proof of the characterisation of the category

For any set

\xi_{S}\colon\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},S\cdot\mathbb{1})\to\beta(S)

\xi

\beta(S)
with its set of points.
Explicitly,
\xi_{S}
sends a point

to the ultrafilter of all the

(recall from Lemmas
5.6
and

\mathsf{C}(S\cdot\mathbb{1},\mathbf{2}\cdot\mathbb{1})\cong\mathcal{P}(S)

This inverse image, denoted by
\overline{T}

S\cdot\mathbb{1}
. It is possible to show that
\overline{T}
is isomorphic to
T\cdot\mathbb{1}
with the canonical arrow to
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
, but we will not use this fact.

Remark 6.1
.

In fact,
\xi_{(-)}
is the
unique
natural transformation
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},-\cdot\mathbb{1})\to\beta

\beta
is terminal among the endofunctors on

that preserve finite coproducts. This result is due to Börger
[

and was recently extended by Garner in
[

. Börger’s theorem was used by Richter in
[

]
to characterise

as the unique monad

on
\Set
that preserves finite coproducts and satisfies separation and compactness properties. These properties correspond, respectively, to the injectivity and surjectivity of
T\to\beta
. We will adapt this idea to the context of compact ordered spaces.

Lemma 6.2
.

\xi

To identify a sufficient condition ensuring that the maps
\xi_{S}
are bijective, we introduce the notion of order-filtrality, extending the concept of filtrality from
[
MR20
]
to the poset-enriched setting. Given an object
X
of a coherent category, let
\Up(X)
be the sub-poset of
\Sub(X)
consisting of the upward subobjects. It is not difficult to see that
\Up(X)
is a sublattice of
\Sub(X)
. If
L
is a distributive lattice, we denote by
\Filt(L)
the set of non-empty filters of
L
, partially ordered by reverse inclusion.

Definition 6.3
.

An object
X
of a coherent category is
order-filtral
if the order-preserving map

\Up(X)\to\Filt(\CU(X)),\ \ U\mapsto\{V\in\CU(X)\mid U\subseteq V\}

is an order-isomorphism.

Example 6.4
.

Order-filtral objects in
\Pos
are finite posets. In
\KOrd
, they coincide with Priestley spaces, i.e.,
totally order-disconnected
compact ordered spaces
[
Pri70
]
.

We show that, in a well-pointed cocomplete pretopos, order-filtral objects are compact and separated in an appropriate sense defined below.
Note that in a cocomplete pretopos, each poset of subobjects
\Sub(X)
is complete. This implies that
\Up(X)
is also complete, since it is a reflective sub-poset of
\Sub(X)
; the reflector sends
Y\subseteq X
to
{\uparrow}{Y}
.

Definition 6.5
.

An object
X
of a cocomplete pretopos
\C
is
compact
if, for every codirected set
\Gamma\subseteq\CU(X)
of upward complemented subobjects of
X
,

\bigwedge\Gamma=0\ \text{ in $\Up(X)$ }\Longrightarrow\ 0\in\Gamma.

Moreover,
X
is
separated
if for any two distinct points
p,q\colon\mathbb{1}\to X
, there exists an upward complemented subobject
U\in\CU(X)
containing exactly one of
p
or
q
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
X
be an object of a well-pointed cocomplete pretopos. If
X
is order-filtral, then it is compact and separated.

Proof.

Since compactness is a property of
\Up(X)
, it is enough to observe that for any distributive lattice
L
, and every codirected subset
\Gamma\subseteq\Filt(L)
, if
\bigwedge\Gamma=0
then
0\in\Gamma
.

To prove that
X
is separated, pick distinct points
p,q\colon\mathbb{1}\to X
. The subobjects
{\uparrow}p
and
{\uparrow}q
are different, for otherwise
p\leq q
and
q\leq p
, which implies
p=q
. Hence, there is an upward complemented subobject of
X
containing one of
{\uparrow}p
and
{\uparrow}q
, but not both. This shows that
X
is separated.
∎

Compact objects, and separated ones, have the following useful closure properties.

Lemma 6.7
.

The following statements hold for every arrow
f\colon X\to Y
in a well-pointed cocomplete pretopos:

(a)

If
f
is a surjection and
X
is compact, then
Y
is compact;

(b)

If
f
is an injection and
Y
is separated, then
X
is separated.

Proof.

(a)
Suppose that
X
is compact and
f
is a surjection. Then
f[f^{-1}(A)]=A
so that
f^{-1}\colon\Sub(Y)\to\Sub(X)
is injective. Moreover,
f^{-1}
preserves upward complemented subobjects as well as arbitrary infima, since it has a left adjoint
f[-]
. Hence, if
\Gamma\subseteq\CU(Y)
is codirected and
\bigwedge\Gamma=0
in
\Up(Y)
, then

\bigwedge\{f^{-1}(\gamma)\,|\,\mathopen{}\gamma\in\Gamma\}=f^{-1}\big(\bigwedge\Gamma\big)=0

in
\Up(X)
.
Since
X
is compact, there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
f^{-1}(\gamma)=0
, and so
\gamma=0
because
f^{-1}
is injective. This shows that
Y
is compact.

(b)
Assume that
Y
is separated and
f
is an injection. If
p,q\colon\mathbb{1}\to X
are distinct points, then
f(p)\neq f(q)
because
f
is injective. Since
Y
is separated, there is
U\in\CU(Y)
containing exactly one of
f(p)
or
f(q)
. It follows that
f^{-1}(U)\in\CU(X)
contains exactly one of
p
or
q
. Therefore,
X
is separated.
∎

Finally, we prove that compactness and separation force the maps
\xi_{S}
to be bijections.

Lemma 6.8
.

For any set
S
, if the copower
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
is compact and separated, then
\xi_{S}\colon\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{1},S\cdot\mathbb{1})\to\beta(S)
is a bijection.

Proof.

We claim that if
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
is compact, then
\xi_{S}
is surjective. Let
\mu\in\beta(S)
be an ultrafilter. For any
T\in\mu
, the upward complemented subobject
\overline{T}\subseteq S\cdot\mathbb{1}
is non-empty since it contains at least a point coming from
T
. By compactness of
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
, the intersection of all the
\overline{T}
for
T\in\mu
is non-empty, so it has a point, whose image under
\xi_{S}
is
\mu
.

Next, we claim that if
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
is separated, then
\xi_{S}
is injective. Given distinct points
p,q\colon\mathbb{1}\to S\cdot\mathbb{1}
, there is an upward complemented subobject
U\subseteq S\cdot\mathbb{1}
containing, without loss of generality,
p
and not
q
. By Lemma
5.8
,
U
is of the form
\overline{T}
for some
T\subseteq S
. Such a
T
is in
\xi_{S}(p)
but not in
\xi_{S}(q)
, therefore
\xi_{S}(p)\neq\xi_{S}(q)
.
∎

We are now in a position to characterise
\KOrd
among the poset-enriched categories. As previously mentioned,
\KOrd
is a (non-degenerate) pretopos. Moreover, the
S
-indexed copower of its terminal object is
\beta(S)
, which is order-filtral, and each compact ordered space is covered by the Stone–Čech compactification of a set. Thus,
\KOrd
satisfies the properties in Theorem
1.1
. We show that any category that satisfies these properties is equivalent to
\KOrd
, thus concluding the proof of the latter theorem.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\C
be a non-degenerate pretopos satisfying the following properties:

(i)

The terminal object
\mathbb{1}
of
\C
is a discrete generator;

(ii)

Every object of
\C
is covered by an order-filtral object.

Then
\C
is equivalent to
\KOrd
.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
4.11
that, in an exact category, an object is a generator if and only if it is a discrete generator. By Lemmas
4.10
and
5.1
,
\{S\cdot\mathbb{1}\,|\,\mathopen{}S\in\Set\}
is a projective cover of
\mathsf{C}
, and
\{\beta(S)\,|\,\mathopen{}S\in\Set\}
is a projective cover of
\KOrd
. In view of Corollary
4.5
, it is thus sufficient to show that the functor

\xi\colon\{S\cdot\mathbb{1}\,|\,\mathopen{}S\in\Set\}\to\{\beta(S)\,|\,\mathopen{}S\in\Set\}

from eq. (
6
) is an equivalence. By Lemmas
6.2
and
6.8
, this holds if the copower
S\cdot\mathbb{1}
is compact and separated for each set
S
.

Let
f\colon X\twoheadrightarrow S\cdot\mathbb{1}
be a surjection whose domain is order-filtral. The pretopos
\mathsf{C}
is well-pointed and cocomplete by Proposition

, hence

is compact and separated by Proposition

. It follows from Lemma

(a)
that

is compact. Moreover, since

such that

. Such a split mono

(b)
.
∎
