Synthesis and characterization of Borassus flabellifer flower waste-generated cellulose fillers reinforced PMC composites for lightweight applications
Authors: Krishnasamy Karthik, Ramesh Velumayil, Sunesh Narayana Perumal, Elumalai Perumal Venkatesan, D. Siva Krishna Reddy, Vivek Anand Annakodi, Mamdooh Alwetaishi, S. Prabhakar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The development of eco-friendly materials is a challenging one in the research field. Natural fibers are more accessible, biodegradable, inexpensive, and less dense. They offer fewer health risks and are eco-friendly compared to synthetic fibers. Natural fiber-reinforced polymer composites are new, eco-friendly materials with excellent mechanical and practical applications. Adding biofillers to composites improves strength and will replace synthetic materials. Utilizing cellulose as a filler for a natural starch matrix is an effective way to reduce the environmental impact of non-biodegradable materials. This study covers the influence of natural Borassus flabellifer flower microcrystalline cellulose (BFF MCC) fillers on banana fiber-reinforced polymer matrix composites. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopic peaks at 2357, 1730, and 1245 cm-1 were absent. This indicates that the amorphous proportion of banana fiber mat/BFF MCC-reinforced hybrid composites decreased. At 1% BFF MCC, thermogravimetric examination revealed an increase in the peak temperature of maximum degradation (389.6 °C). The hybrid banana composite’s tensile strength (31.36 ± 4.39 to 39.83 ± 3.07 MPa) and flexural strength (71.05 ± 2.66 to 82.4 ± 1.66 MPa) were also improved after adding 3% BFF MCC as filler material. The primary objective is to evaluate the suitability of fiber-reinforced hybrid polymers with natural fillers for future engineering applications such as automotive parts, construction materials, 3D printing, etc. In addition, this study investigated how the reinforcement of microcrystalline cellulose can result in a material with enhanced performance as well as its mechanical characteristics (XRD, FTIR, TGA, and SEM characterization).

Introduction

Bio-based fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) composites have recently caught the interest of researchers because of their eco-friendly nature. Natural fibers are preferred in fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) composites. They have better tensile strength, light weight, minimal investment, thermal recyclability, a longer lifespan, and higher performance1. These fibers are used in the production of a variety of complex structures in industries, including the construction sector, aerospace, marine, and automotive industries2. Micro-nanocellulose from coconut shells, groundnut shells, rice husks, coir, banana, sisal, hemp, jute, date palm, and sugarcane bagasse is easy to find in India. When combined in the right ratio with polymer materials (both thermoplastic and thermosetting), these fibers can be used to successfully improve a variety of mechanical and thermal properties. The choice of the right fiber material and resin is critical for composite materials. The combination of fiber and resin in a suitable ratio can only create good-quality polymer composites. Because they are created through engineering methods and primarily useful to reduce weight and hence increase efficiency, they have a substantial advantage3,4. Two or more materials in a separate phase make up a composite material. The matrix is the continuous phase, while the reinforcement is the discontinuous phase, which is typically harder and stronger than the matrix. The characteristic features of polymer matrices are improved by adding fillers like graphite, minerals, and cellulose5,6. For example, the addition of graphite enhances the lubricating property of the matrix. Some fibers require surface modification and moisture removal before processing. Therefore, fiber orientation, surface treatments, fiber size, and production techniques, which affect fiber composite qualities, are also addressed7. Many research investigators heavily utilize filler materials to enhance the qualities of FRP composites. Filler materials are inert substances that can reduce costs and enhance performance. They also improve the mechanical properties of composites8. Fillers are often used with the matrix to make a smooth surface. Without them, the structure would be rough, and the composite’s mechanical performance would be minimal. The overall performance of hybrid composites can be improved by using natural fillers9,10. For this, microcrystalline cellulose from Borassus flabellifer flower (BFF) was utilized. The size reduction of Borassus flabellifer flower microcrystalline cellulose (BFF MCC) from macrometer to micrometer levels reached by energy ball milling was examined11. BFF MCC is an agro-based filler material that is inexpensive, lightweight, and widely accessible as a waste filler material. Because of its low extraction cost and higher strength, it is a popular choice as a matrix biofiller. For the composite materials, banana mat was utilized in this study, which had higher strength and an eco-friendly nature12,13. Numerous studies have explored the use of banana fiber mat composite materials in combination with resins. To attain a smooth surface and good mechanical properties, hybrid composites were developed. The crystallinity and other physiochemical properties of BFF MCC gave the polymer composites good workability. The raw material for BFF MCC is readily available, and processing costs are lower14,15. Thus, the reproducibility of BFF MCC is not affected for as long as possible. Polyester resins have good-to-use mechanical properties. The chemical resistivity, low cost, and good mechanical properties of polyester resin attract attention. For most of the FRP composites, polyester resin is used. In this study for hybrid composites, polyester resin was used. Hybrid polymer composites outperform FRP in terms of mechanical strength, durability, and chemical resistance16–18. Additionally, the effect of the BFF MCC weight fraction on the natural fiber-matrix interface was investigated using SEM. According to the survey, the effects on polymer composites have not received as much attention as the natural cellulosic filler from BFF MCC. So, the goal of this work is to determine how natural microcrystalline cellulose fillers made from the BFF affect hybrid polymer composites with reinforced banana fibers. The made hybrid composites were looked at with a scanning electron microscope (SEM) to make sure that the fabric banana and BFF MCC particles were evenly distributed inside the matrix resin. Mechanical parameters like tensile strength, flexural stiffness, impact resistance, hardness, water absorption, XRD, FTIR, and TGA were evaluated when varying amounts of BFF MCC (0, 1, 3, 5, and 7%) were added to the composites.

Extraction of microcrystalline cellulose from BFF

The extraction of BFF MCC and fabrication of composites are shown in Fig. 1. The Asian palmyra palm, commonly known as the Borassus flabellifer, and this agricultural waste material were used in this study to extract the MCC. The leftover dried blossoms of the tree were gathered and pulverized into macro-sized particles. Initially, the BFF powder weighs 500 g. To eliminate the low-density amorphous areas, the raw BFF powder was alkaline-treated with 40 wt% NaOH. In a non-metallic pan, the treatment is carried out for 12 h at room temperature. After multiple alkali treatments, the lignin present in the raw material is removed in this process. After soaking for 12 h at 32 degrees Celsius, the resulting solid mass was acid hydrolyzed with a mixture of 20% KCl and 20% HCl in 1000 milliliters of distilled water. To facilitate acid hydrolysis and eliminate surplus lignin and hemicellulose, KCl is added at this stage. The leftover bulk was rinsed with water until the pH level reached neutral after the acid hydrolysis procedure. Sodium hypochlorite was used to bleach the cellulose particles that were produced25–27. To get rid of the acids and chlorides that were still present in the final residual filler material, it was washed multiple times. Hydrogen peroxide was used in the purification procedure to get rid of the undesirable missing residuals. The purifying agent hydrogen peroxide also improved the MCC’s crystalline properties. The remaining BFF MCC is collected and dried in an oven at 40 °C for six hours. Eventually, a ball milling machine was used to grind up the remaining filler material into micro-sized cellulosic material14,28.

Fabrication of hybrid composites

The polyester resin reinforced with banana fiber and BFF MCC fillings was manufactured by hand layup compression molding. The fabrication die’s interior length, width, and thickness are 150 mm * 120 mm * 3 mm. Applying wax to the mold before laminating helps the composite laminate release from the mold easily after fabrication. The wax also prevents the composite material from bonding to the mold, making the removal of the finished product easier. By weight, the percentages of polyester resin, hardener, and accelerator used to manufacture composites were 100:1.5:1.5. The BFF MCC filler is carefully mixed with polyester resin before being poured into the mold29,30. Initially, a base composite plate is created without the inclusion of fillers. After preparing a cellulosic-filled matrix in a beaker, the interior surface of the mold is coated with the first layer. Then, the first banana mat is laid on the resin-coated surface. Following the application of the resin coating, four banana mats are set within the mold, and excess resin-containing filler is poured over them. After a thorough packing of the male die, the mold was placed in a compression testing machine and subjected to a constant 5KN force for 24 h. The applied load eliminates any air pockets in the composite material. Eventually, the material was extracted from the mold using mechanical equipment, and specimens for testing and analysis were created in accordance with the ASTM standard31. Tensile analysis

Composite laminates are used to create samples that are 160 mm × 12.5 mm × 3 mm in size. The samples’ holdability is enhanced by the addition of aluminium strips. To evaluate the tensile and flexural strength of biocomposites with different attachments, a 20KN-Electronic tensometer, Kudale Instruments, METM 2000 ER-1 model, India, is employed at a crosshead.

speed of 2 mm/min. Instead of testing using a machine, samples are prepared according to ASTM D-638 standards and subjected to a progressive cell load of 10 N32.

Flexural analysis

The flexural strength of biocomposites is typically evaluated using the three-point bend test. There is also the four-point bend test. Nevertheless, the three-point bend test was chosen due to its modest material requirements and straightforward testing procedure. Midsections of samples are loaded in a perpendicular direction. According to ASTM D790 specifications, the sample measurements should be 100 mm × 25 mm × 3 mm33.

Impact examination

Standard ASTM D 256 M practice says that samples should be 63.5 mm long, 12.7 mm deep, and 10 mm wide, and that a 45° V-notch should be cut with a file at the 90° saw cut to the sample’s axis. The V-shaped cut serves as a crack starter. The Impact Tester, which is made by Indian International Equipment, is used to measure how tough Izod is in terms of impact34.

Hardness examination

In line with ASTM Standard D 2583, the hardness of composite samples was measured with a Rockwell hardness tester. The hardness of the material was measured with an indenter that was a truncated steel cone with a 26° angle cut into each of its two opposite faces. The mean hardness value was found by putting a force of 25 N on four points along the x-axis35.

Moisture absorption test

Important is the degree to which a material repels water. We were able to determine the relative humidity with ASTM Standard D 570 − 98. The moisture absorption of composites reinforced with BFF MCC at their maximal mechanical characteristics was studied. From the composite laminated sheets, square samples measuring 30 mm were extracted. All of the samples were soaked in water at room temperature for approximately twenty-four hours in order to measure how much water the composites could absorb. After determining that removing the water from the surface would result in a more precise weight, the samples were reweighed. A calculation was performed to establish the percentage of growth in immersion weight. In order to prevent errors caused by evaporation, the weighing procedure was completed in thirty seconds36,37. Surface imaging with a high resolution is used for SEM evaluation. In both high and low vacuum modes, the cellulose SEM testing apparatus has a magnification range of 5x to 300,000x. FTIR analysis

Thermo Nicolet’s Avatar 370 KBr beam splitter in the United States is used to separate out different types of molecules. It has a spectral resolution of 4 cm-1 from 4000 to 400 cm-1. Each sample undergoes 32 scans throughout the treatment process, and the DTGS detector and HATR assembly are added to the mix to facilitate quantitative analysis at each stage40.

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is employed to investigate heat deterioration behavior. The Perkinn Elmer Damond TG/DTA model from the USA is used to heat the sample from 40 °C to 730 °C per minute in a nitrogen atmosphere with a gas flow rate of 20 ml/min to study the degradation behavior41.

Results and discussions

Tensile test

The tensile property of banana fiber/BFF MCC-reinforced hybrid composites was improved by adding microcrystalline cellulose at concentrations of 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 wt%. Tensile properties and elongation at break are measured on the hybrid composite constructed from banana fibers and BFF MCC. Figure 5 shows that the tensile strength of composites increased with up to 3 wt% MCC and subsequently decreased with higher MCC content. The tensile strength of pure unsaturated polyester resin is estimated to be 20 MPa by authentic sources. Without any filler, the tensile strength of matrix polyester reinforced with banana is 31.36 ± 4.39 MPa. At 3% MCC, the greatest tensile strength recorded is 39.83 ± 3.07 MPa. The tensile strength of 3% MCC is 99.575% greater than that of matrix material (Fig. 2a). Similarly, incorporating 3% BFF MCC into the composite material resulted in the highest tensile strength of 39.83 MPa. Furthermore, there was a 21.27% increase in tensile strength compared to the composite without MCC. Due to the excellent crystallinity index of BFF MCC and the strong link between reinforcement and matrix, the tensile strength is enhanced42. The majority of the improvement in tensile strength can be attributed to the uniform dispersion of cellulose and the stress transfer from the resin to the structurally robust MCC. Due to inadequate adhesion and aggregation of BFF MCC, the tensile strength decreased at the highest MCC weight% since aggregation increased the local stress concentration areas43.

Fig. 2Effect of filler addition on the tensile performance of hybrid composites (a) tensile strength of the hybrid composite; (b) tensile modulus of the hybrid composite; and (c) elongation percentage.

Effect of filler addition on the tensile performance of hybrid composites (a) tensile strength of the hybrid composite; (b) tensile modulus of the hybrid composite; and (c) elongation percentage.

The tensile strength modulus followed the same pattern as the tensile strength. Studies reveal that unsaturated polyester has a tensile modulus of 236 MPa. The tensile modulus is now 82.4 ± 1.66 MPa, due to the incorporation of 3% MCC shown in Fig. 2b. Microcrystalline cellulose frequently increases the brittleness of hybrid composites. However, the tensile modulus of biocomposites increases when a small amount of MCC is introduced. This impact can be attributed to the higher rigidity of composites. Due to stress concentration zones, tensile modulus values are drastically reduced as the percentage of MCC in a material increases beyond 3%44. As the MCC content of an unsaturated polyester matrix increased, the elongation at break decreased, as shown in Fig. 2c. This effect may be caused by the strong link between the reinforcement and matrix, which prevents the displacement of polymer chains under stress. The pure matrix has an elongation break rate of 10.2%. For C2, C3, C4, and C5, this declined at a rate of 8.1%, 5.01%, 4.02%, and 3.15%, respectively.

Flexural test

The flexural strength and flexural modulus of hybrid composites reinforced with banana fiber/BFF MCC are shown in Fig. 3a,b. The weight% of MCC varies between 0%, 1%, 3%, 5%, and 7%, whereas the weight% of banana mat remains constant with four layers. The flexural load applied to the midspan of the composite matrix increases the deflection at each point of loading45. Incorporating MCC with a greater crystallinity percentage and crystallinity size into the composite improves its flexural performance. When 3% BFF MCC is added to the banana-reinforced composite, the flexural strength increases to 82.4 ± 1.66 MPa. The flexural strength of hybrid composite C3 exceeds that of composite C1 by 14%. As a result of the filler material aggregating at a higher concentration, the flexural values of composites C4 and C5 were reduced.

Impact strength

Impact testing is performed to measure the toughness of hybrid composites reinforced with banana fiber or BFF MCC. Figure 4 displays the impact strength of C3 with 3% BFF MCC, which is 62.93 ± 3.36 KJ/m2. As a result, the improved MCC increased the impact strength by making the fiber-matrix interface more durable. The filler added to the polymer composite increases its durability and toughness46. This study is comparable to another one that looked into hybrid polymer composites reinforced with hemp fiber and cellulose. It was determined that the impact strength was 58.62 KJ/m2. Adding cellulose to a polymer composite causes it to have a higher impact strength47. Because of its durability, banana fiber/BFF MCC-reinforced matrix has a variety of uses.

Hardness test

The inclusion of microcrystalline cellulose enhances the hardness value, as shown in Fig. 5. The C5 composite is hardened to 85.1 ± 4.2, which is 7.8% greater than the C1 composite. This increased hardness value makes composite materials more brittle. At a larger filler addition, MCC particles are present on the surface of the matrix, which increases its hardness48. Substituting a smaller amount of microfiller reduces the composite’s hardness properties. The impact strength of composites comprised of polyester and jute fiber is only 30–40 kJ/m2, according to the literature. Impact strength ranges from 40 to 60 kJ/m2 for polyester with kenaf fiber49. Therefore, in comparison to previous research, the impact strength increases when BFF MCC is included.

Water absorption test

The water absorption capacity of banana fiber mat/BFF MCC-reinforced hybrid composites was analyzed after their fabrication. The banana fiber mat is hydrophilic, whereas the BFF MCC is hydrophobic. The results of a water absorption test are depicted in Fig. 6, which shows how the addition of filler affected a hybrid composite. The matrix filler minimizes the microvoids present in the composite, resulting in a decrease in the hybrid polymer composite’s water absorption capacity. The BFF MCC contains adequate hydroxyl OH groups. However, its hydrophobic nature does not allow water molecules to enter the composite49,50. The matrix-bonded banana fiber/BFF MCC-reinforced composite yielded outstanding results with reduced moisture absorption capability. The microfillers permeated the composite material’s micro-gaps, producing a superior result. As a result, the use of BFF MCC filler reduces the hydrophilicity of composites reinforced with banana fiber51. Thermal stability analysis

The thermal properties of composites depend on the effectiveness of the filler particles, which retain their qualities when the temperature rises. To calculate the effect of BFF MCC filler loading on the composite material, TG analysis was performed and shown in Fig. 7. The acquired results were compared to the banana fiber/polyester composite without filler. The composite material degraded in two distinct stages. In the first step, moisture and volatile substances are extracted from the material, resulting in a loss of mass52. The initial weight loss of unfilled composite is between 40 °C and 170 °C, whereas the initial weight loss of BFF MCC-filled composite is between 40 °C and 182 °C. Increasing the BFF MCC filler improves thermal stability compared to an unfilled composite. Strong interfacial contacts are produced by the presence of BFF MCC in the matrix, which inhibits the segmental motion of the polyester matrix53.

Fig. 7Hybrid polymer composite thermal deterioration with filler addition.

Hybrid polymer composite thermal deterioration with filler addition.

During the second phase of degradation, the temperature rises to 451 °C. During this phase, banana fiber mat, cured polyester matrix, and BFF MCC are degraded. The composite with 1% BFF MCC filler reaches a maximum temperature of 389.6 °C, which is 7.5 °C higher than the composite without filler54. Furthermore, increasing the number of BFF MCC fillers in a polyester matrix reduces the composite’s residual mass percentage. At 370 °C, a polyester resin composite with hybrid basalt jute fiber showed the highest thermal degradation. Furthermore, in another study, the thermal decomposition of hemp fiber reinforced with polyester was found to be 360 oC. Thus, the addition of BFF MCC to the polymer matrix enhances the thermal stability compared to other studies55. Therefore, the filler-added hybrid composites find application in the construction and packaging industries.

XRD analysis

The crystalline characteristics of various MCC filler percentages in the hybrid composite are determined. Figure 8 depicts the crystalline and amorphous regions of the composites that were manufactured. Peak values of 22.10, 16.60, and 33.80 indicate the existence of cellulose I. The corresponding lattice planes for the respective peaks are 1 1 0, 2 0 0, and 0 0 4. Segal’s equation was used to get the composite material’s crystallinity index. The crystallinity index of various percentages of BFF MCC is 64.5%, 65.8%, 69.6%, 72.8%, and 73.9%, respectively. According to scientific literature, chemical treatment gets rid of lignin and hemicellulose, which makes the isolated BFF MCC more crystallized (698.11%). BFF MCC’s crystallinity index is enhanced by the alkaline treatment, acid hydrolysis, and bleaching processes20,56. The filler with a higher crystallinity index can fill pores and voids in the composite in an orderly manner. The higher crystallinity of BFF MCC increases the crystallinity index of composite materials. In previous studies, the polymer composite was found to have a lower crystallinity index compared to this one. Some of the polymer composites blend with fibers such as sisal fiber (25%), flax fiber (40%), coir fiber (35%), and hybrid flax pine apple leaves (55%). Different percentages of cellulose-filled composites have crystalline sizes of 23.17, 28.26, 32.48, 38.41, and 41.5 nm, respectively. The larger crystalline size increases hydrophobicity, which in turn enhances matrix and reinforcement adhesion57.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectroscopy used to determine the functional groups in the hybrid composite sample is depicted in Fig. 9. The O-H stretching vibration and free hydroxyl group are absent between the peaks 3300 cm-1 and 3400 cm-1 due to the chemical treatment (alkali and acid hydrolysis). The C-H stretching vibrations, which correspond to the large peak between 298 and 2827.31 cm-1, are present in all of the samples58. One further peak between 1777.02 and 1669.42 cm-1 indicates the existence of O-H and C-H stretching vibrations. At 1252.36 cm-1, a peak shared by all materials indicates C-O stretching and deformation. Peaks 948.45 cm-1 and 734.28 cm-1, which correspond to the C-H rock group and the C-Cl stretching vibration group, are shared by the entire composite59. The amorphous proportion of banana fiber/BFF MCC-reinforced hybrid composites decreased when the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopic peaks at 2357, 1730, and 1245 cm-1 were removed.

Conclusion

The utility of agrowaste, the synthesis of a hybrid biobased polymer matrix, and the effectiveness of the composite were well studied. Chemical processes are used to successfully isolate microcrystalline cellulose from the Borassus flabellifer flower. In turn, the microcrystalline cellulose that was isolated displayed greater crystallinity and thermal stability. Microcrystalline cellulose reinforcement in the polymer matrix increased the tensile strength of microcomposites containing up to 3% microcrystalline cellulose (39.83 ± 3.07 MPa). Similarly, the flexural (82.4 ± 1.66 MPa) and impact strengths (62.93 ± 3.33 MPa) of the composite containing 3% filler were equally strong. With SEM micrographs of fracture surfaces, the impact of microcrystalline on mechanical behavior can also be better comprehended. Additionally, the percentage of elongation, hardness, and water absorption of banana fiber/BFF MCC hybrid composites improved dramatically. The composite material using 1% MCC filler reaches a maximum temperature of 389.60 °C. Therefore, these cellulose-based hybrid microcomposites with notable qualities can be applied in the field of engineering applications such as automotive and aerospace parts, packaging materials, and construction.
