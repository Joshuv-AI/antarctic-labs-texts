The efficacy of metronidazole rinses in reducing surgical site infections and improving wound healing in patients with purulent appendicitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ping Han, Sheng Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical efficacy of metronidazole topical solution in preventing surgical site infections and promoting wound healing.MethodsA comprehensive literature review was conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Database, and China National Knowledge Base to evaluate the impact of metronidazole topical (MS) and physiological saline rinses (SS) on infection rates and wound healing in patients with septic appendicitis after treatment. Review Manager 5.0 was utilized to evaluate the outcomes of the selected studies. The impact of the selected articles was assessed through Forest plot analysis, sensitivity analysis, and bias analysis conducted on the gathered data.ResultsEight studies met the inclusion criteria. The results showed a significantly lower surgical site infection rate in the metronidazole (MS) group compared to the saline (SS) group (RR = 0.28, 100% CI [0.15, 0.51], P = 0.05, I ²= 59%). However, no significant difference was observed in grade I healing rates between the two groups (RR = 1.02, 100% CI [0.91, 1.15], P = 0.41, I ² = 0%). Similarly, there was a significant difference in grade II healing rates ((RR = 0.7, 100% CI [0.32, 1.55], P = 0.30, I² =5%). A significant heterogeneity was observed in grade III healing rates (RR = 0.56, 100% CI [0.17, 1.89], P = 0.21, I² =36%). Sensitivity analysis and funnel plots showed that the study was credible with limited publication bias.ConclusionMetronidazole topical rinse may be more effective and safer than saline rinse in treating purulent appendicitis. Local application of metronidazole to purulent appendicitis incisions is a promising approach.LimitationsThe review was not registered prospectively and some included studies had moderate bias.Future directionsLarger multicenter trials are needed to validate the efficacy of metronidazole irrigation across diverse patient populations.

Introduction

Appendicitis is a common abdominal emergency requiring immediate surgery. If left untreated, the appendix can become inflamed and rupture, leading to serious infection. Approximately 25.8% of appendicitis cases result in perforation. Surgical site infections (SSI) are a significant concern, especially in cases of perforated appendicitis [1], with rates reaching up to 53% in complicated cases [2].

Appendicitis remains a common surgical emergency. While appendectomy is the standard treatment for perforated appendicitis, the optimal management strategies, including the use of antimicrobial agents, continue to be debated [1, 3].Appendectomy has a relatively high rate of postoperative infections, often exceeding 10%. Therefore, it serves as an excellent model for studying surgical site infections (SSIs) in abdominal surgeries [4, 5].In the United States, approximately 350,000 appendectomies are performed each year [6].

Bacterial contamination of the surgical site during wound closure significantly increases the risk of wound infection. Nitroimidazoles, such as metronidazole, are effective against anaerobic bacteria commonly associated with appendicitis. Additionally, E. coli and Klebsiella species can be treated with antibiotics like ampicillin, cephalosporins, quinolones, and aminoglycosides, which are used both preventively and therapeutically [6].

These antibiotics are administered either orally or intravenously in combination to cover both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, helping to prevent wound infections after appendectomy. While systemic antibiotics have been a cornerstone of preventing post-appendectomy infections, their limitations have prompted exploration of alternative approaches. Topical application of antimicrobial agents has emerged as a promising strategy to target bacteria at the surgical site. Laboratory studies have demonstrated that metronidazole can achieve significant bacterial kill rates within a short period [4].

Clinical studies, such as the one conducted by Mubinul Islam et al. (2020), have shown that topical metronidazole can significantly reduce the incidence of post-appendectomy wound infections [4]. The researchers also concluded that intraperitoneal irrigation with metronidazole significantly reduces postoperative infections compared to saline solution. Therefore, the use of metronidazole in surgical irrigation is recommended to lower the incidence of postoperative infections [4].

Various topical antimicrobial agents have been explored for intraoperative use, including povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, gentamicin, and doxycycline. However, metronidazole’s specific action against anaerobic bacteria makes it a targeted and potentially more effective solution in purulent abdominal infections [7, 8].

Metronidazole is an effective antibiotic against anaerobic bacteria, making it an ideal choice for managing infected wounds. Topical irrigation with metronidazole delivers a high concentration of the drug directly to the infection site, enhancing its antimicrobial effect. When combined with systemic antibiotics, metronidazole topical can significantly reduce postoperative infection rates, accelerates granulation tissue growth, and shortens recovery time. Studies have shown that metronidazole outperforms standard saline irrigation in managing wounds with abscesses or necrotic tissue [1, 9].

For decades, various antimicrobial agents, including ampicillin, metronidazole, doxycycline, cefazolin, and gentamicin, have been used for peritoneal irrigation. While these agents have shown promise in reducing post-operative infection rates, their optimal use and effectiveness remain subject to ongoing debate [1]. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the effect of metronidazole topical irrigation on wound infection and overall recovery in patients with purulent appendicitis.

Methods

Search strategy

The PRISMA 2020 approach for Systematic Review (PRISMA-SR) was used to conduct this study [10]. The search included three databases: Cocoa Library, EMBASE, and the China National Knowledge Base. A combination of keywords was used to identify relevant studies, including septic appendicitis disease, metronidazole, and local flushing. The search focused on articles published between January 1999 and June 2023. It specifically targeted studies comparing metronidazole topical irrigation with saline irrigation in treating septic appendicitis. To enhance sensitivity, manual searches of reference lists from retrieved articles was performed. The following Boolean operators and keywords were used: (“appendicitis” OR “acute appendicitis” OR “suppurative appendicitis” OR “septic appendicitis” OR “gangrenous appendicitis” OR “complicated appendicitis” OR “perforated appendix” OR “infected appendix”) AND (“metronidazole” OR “topical metronidazole” OR “metronidazole solution” OR “metronidazole irrigation” OR “metronidazole lavage” OR “metronidazole rinse” OR “intraoperative metronidazole” OR “local metronidazole administration”) AND(“irrigation” OR “rinsing” OR “lavage” OR “washing” OR “flushing” OR “wound irrigation” OR “intraoperative lavage” OR “surgical irrigation” OR “topical application” OR “local instillation” OR “peritoneal lavage”) AND (“infection” OR “infections” OR “infected wound” OR “infected incision” OR “surgical site infection” OR “surgical site infections” OR “SSI” OR “SSIs” OR “wound infection” OR “wound infections” OR “incision infection” OR “incisional infection” OR “postoperative infection” OR “surgical wound infection” OR “surgical wound complications” OR “surgical infection” OR “surgical contamination” OR “bacterial contamination” OR “contaminated wound” OR “abscess”). Language restrictions were not applied to capture a broader range of relevant research.

Study selection

This systematic review was not registered with PROSPERO due to the retrospective nature of its planning and resource limitations at the time. However, the review strictly followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and rigor. To ensure the rigor of the study, specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were established. Inclusion criteria included randomized controlled trials comparing metronidazole topical and saline rinses for septic appendicitis incisions, patients diagnosed with septic appendicitis, and full-text articles with accessible data.

Excluded studies were non-randomized studies, case reports, case series, or reviews, patients with conditions other than septic appendicitis, and studies with no accessible data or comparable outcome measures.

Data extraction

To ensure the reliability and validity of the included studies, a rigorous quality assessment process was implemented. Two researchers independently reviewed the full-text articles to assess their eligibility and quality, including the publication year, the name of the first author, sample size, age, and physical condition of each group, and detailed information on the interventions and outcomes of each study.

Study risk of bias assessment assessment

We utilized the CASP Randomized Controlled Trial Standard Checklist to evaluate the study design, methodology, reporting of results, and the local implications of the findings [11]. Additionally, we assessed the risk of bias for each study using the Revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias (RoB 2). This tool identifies potential sources of bias across five domains, including bias due to randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result [12].

Statistical analysis

Review Manager (Version 5.0, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2011) was used to conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis of the included studies. For continuous variables, the mean difference was calculated to assess the effect size. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic (low heterogeneity: I2 between 25% and 50%, moderate heterogeneity: I2 between 50% and 75%, and high heterogeneity: I2 > 75%). When I2 exceeded 50%, potential sources of heterogeneity were explored through sensitivity analysis. This involved iteratively excluding studies to assess their impact on the overall results. Additionally, a fixed-effect model was used when there was heterogeneity, while a random-effects model was employed when there was no heterogeneity. Funnel plots were employed to investigate potential publication bias. To assess the presence of publication bias, funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were performed. The funnel plot exhibits a symmetrical inverted funnel shape which indicated the absence of publication bias. We visually examined the funnel plots to identify any signs of asymmetry and used the Egger’s Egger’s test to quantitatively evaluate the presence of publication bias. A p-value below 0.10 was considered as significant publication bias. According to the funnel plots and Egger’s test (p = 0.318), there was no evidence of publication bias.

Results of meta-analysis

Patients with purulent appendicitis who received metronidazole exhibited lower incision infection than those treated with saline-flush incisions.

Five articles compared the incidence of incision infections between metronidazole topical and saline rinses (Fig. 4). A meta-analysis of these studies revealed a significant reduction in infection rates in the metronidazole group compared to the saline group (RR = 0.28, 100% CI [0.15, 0.51], P < 0.0001, I ²= 59%).

Forest plot comparison of the incidence of incision infections between Ms and Bs

Figure 5 illustrates the grade I healing rates for incisions with metronidazole (MS) and saline (SS) rinses. A fixed-effect model was used to analyze the data, and no significant heterogeneity was detected across the studies. The results indicate a significant difference in grade I healing rates between the MS and SS groups (RR = 1.02, 100% CI [0.91, 1.15], p = 0.69, I²=0%).

Figure 6 presents the grade II healing rates for incisions treated with metronidazole (MS) and saline (SS) rinses. A comparison of these rates between the two groups revealed no significant heterogeneity. The results show no significant difference in grade II healing rates between the MS and SS groups (RR = 0.7, 100% CI [0.32, 1.55], p = 0.38, I²=5%).

Figure 7 displays the grade III healing rates for incisions treated with metronidazole (MS) and saline (SS) rinses. A heterogeneity analysis of the two included studies revealed no significant differences in grade III healing rates between the MS and SS groups. The results suggest no significant difference in the grade III healing rate between the groups (RR = 0.56, 100% CI [0.17, 1.89], p = 0.35, I²=36%).

Discussion

Eight studies met the inclusion criteria to assess the effectiveness and safety metronidazole (MS) versus saline (BS) for topical rinsing of purulent appendicitis incisions. A meta-analysis of these studies showed significant differences between the two treatments [13, 14]. The MS group exhibited a lower incision infection rate than the BS group. Additionally, the MS group demonstrated a significantly higher rate of grade I wound healing. No significant differences were observed in the grade II or III healing rates between the two groups.

Topical rinsing of septic appendicitis incision with metronidazole (MS) is an adjunctive method for the treatment of septic appendicitis. During appendectomy, surgeons use a metronidazole solution to irrigate the abdominal cavity, removing pus and bacteria to reduce the risk of infection spread [15]. Studies have shown that direct application of metronidazole to the incision site achieves high drug concentrations at the site of infection. This targeted approach offers more effective bacterial killing and reduces the systemic side effects associated with oral or intravenous medication, forming the basis for the topical rinsing technique. A comparative analysis of the differences between MS and BS groups was reported by Aslam, H (2023) [4, 16]. This study compared the outcomes of patients with septic appendicitis treated with metronidazole to those receiving standard care.

Surgical site infections, a common complication following appendectomy, significantly impact patient recovery and healthcare costs. These infections, particularly in cases of purulent appendicitis, can lead to delayed healing, prolonged hospital stays, and increased risk of systemic infection​ [4, 17]. Given the high prevalence of appendectomy, minimizing infection risks is critical. In the United States alone, approximately 350,000 appendectomies are performed annually, with infection rates post-surgery ranging from 10% to 30%​ [4, 18].

Metronidazole, a well-known antibiotic, offers a promising solution. It effectively targets anaerobic bacteria, such as Bacteroides fragilis and Clostridium difficile, which are frequently implicated in post-appendectomy infections​ [17, 18].

Topical application of metronidazole offers a targeted approach to combatting infection at the surgical site. By delivering high concentrations of the drug directly to the wound, this method maximizes efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. In comparison to other topical antimicrobial agents such as povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, gentamicin, and doxycycline, metronidazole demonstrates superior specificity against anaerobic pathogens commonly found in purulent abdominal infections. This specificity supports its preferential use in surgical site management following appendectomy, especially in cases complicated by infection. Several studies have suggested that while other agents provide broad-spectrum coverage, metronidazole’s focused anaerobic action is particularly effective in contaminated surgical fields [7, 8]. Research has demonstrated the advantages of metronidazole rinsing, including reduced infection rates, accelerated tissue healing, and shortened recovery times [4, 16]. For example, one study found that only 5% of patients who received metronidazole irrigation developed postoperative infections compared to 22% in the control group​ [4].

A comprehensive meta-analysis found that incision infection rates were significantly lower in patients treated with metronidazole rinsing (RR = 0.28, 95% CI [0.15–0.51], P < 0.0001) compared to those treated with saline. This finding underscore metronidazole’s superior efficacy in preventing SSIs, particularly in purulent cases. Moreover, while metronidazole showed a slight improvement in grade I healing rates (RR = 1.02, 95% CI [0.91–1.15]), the difference was not statistically significant​. No substantial differences were observed in grade II or III healing rates between metronidazole and saline.

Reducing infection rates after appendicitis surgery is a critical concern. Bacterial contamination plays a significant role in postoperative infections, particularly in complicated cases. The narrow lumen of the appendix, prone to trapping fecal matter and bacteria, further exacerbates this risk. Factors such as the presence of foreign objects (like sutures) can significantly increase infection likelihood [19].

Metronidazole enters bacterial cells and undergoes activation under anaerobic conditions, producing toxic metabolites that disrupt DNA replication and transcription. This mechanism is particularly effective against pathogens, such as Bacteroides fragilis and Clostridium difficile, which are often involved in purulent appendicitis [20, 21]. Beyond its antibacterial effects, metronidazole promotes microcirculation around the incision site, enhancing tissue repair and regeneration. This dual action of both controlling infection and facilitating wound healing makes it a valuable tool in managing post-appendectomy infections [20]. Future research should focus on providing a stronger scientific foundation for the use of metronidazole topical irrigation in the later stages of purulent appendicitis treatment.

In the present meta-analysis, the relative risk (RR) for Grade III healing rates was reported as 0.56 with a 95% confidence interval [0.17, 1.89], which was not statistically significant. However, this finding does not necessarily imply the absence of a clinical effect. A study by Gewandter et al. (2017) [22] demonstrated that in many clinical trials with non-significant results, the confidence intervals still include clinically meaningful effects, although this is often overlooked in the interpretation of findings. In fact, statistical non-significance may result from insufficient statistical power or small sample sizes, rather than the true absence of a treatment effect. Therefore, careful interpretation of confidence intervals and consideration of potential clinical effects, even in the absence of statistical significance, is essential to avoid prematurely discarding potentially beneficial treatments.

While some findings, such as Grade III healing rate differences, did not reach statistical significance, the observed effect directions still hold potential clinical relevance. In high-risk patients, even small improvements in healing rates can translate into significant clinical outcomes, such as reduced length of hospital stay or antibiotic use. These findings support the incorporation of topical metronidazole irrigation as a complementary approach within existing surgical protocols for managing purulent appendicitis. While current SSI prevention guidelines emphasize systemic antibiotic prophylaxis, our data suggest that localized antimicrobial strategies may enhance outcomes, particularly in high-risk wound classifications.

Our study has some limitations. While metronidazole is a valuable tool against anaerobic pathogens, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. It is ineffective against aerobic bacteria and fungi, meaning infections caused by these organisms may require additional or alternative treatments. Additionally, prolonged use of metronidazole can lead to the development of drug-resistant bacteria. Patients may also experience adverse reactions, ranging from mild skin irritation to more serious responses like rashes or respiratory distress​ [20, 23, 24]. Another important limitation is the lack of consistent reporting on the baseline health status of patients across studies. Factors such as comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, obesity) and nutritional status may influence wound healing outcomes and could act as potential confounders. Future studies should stratify outcomes based on these clinical variables and also due to the limited number of included studies, subgroup analyses and meta-regression to explore sources of heterogeneity could not be performed.

Conclusion

The meta-analysis highlighted the potential of metronidazole topical rinsing as an effective method in reducing incision infection rates in purulent appendicitis. By directly targeting the infection site, metronidazole offers several advantages over saline rinsing, including enhanced efficacy against anaerobic pathogens. However, its limitations, particularly against non-anaerobic organisms and the potential for drug resistance, underscore the need for careful application and further research. By expanding our knowledge of metronidazole’s role in preventing SSI, we can significantly improve patient outcomes and reduce post-appendectomy complications.
