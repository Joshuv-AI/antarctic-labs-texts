Fabrication and thermal performance of ternary fatty acid/expanded graphite form–stable composite phase change materials
Authors: Dongyi Zhou, Jiawei Yuan, Fanchen Zhou, Yicai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryForm-stable composite phase change material (CA–LA–MA/EG) was prepared by vacuum impregnation, using ternary fatty acid eutectic as the core and expanded graphite as the porous matrix. Its composition, morphology, and thermal properties were characterized by FT–IR, SEM, and DSC, with thermal conductivity measured by the heat flux method. CA–LA–MA was uniformly dispersed in EG with an optimal loading of 91.4%, showing suitable phase-change temperature and latent heat for cold chain use. EG greatly enhanced thermal conductivity and heat storage/release rates. The composite maintained excellent thermal and chemical stability after 1500 thermal cycles. With low supercooling, high conductivity at only 8.6 wt % EG loading, and good cycling stability for 10°C–20°C cold chain logistics, it is a promising candidate for low-temperature thermal management.

Introduction

Since the 21st century, the global energy system has faced unprecedented pressure, with the energy crisis emerging as a critical bottleneck for the sustainable development of the economy and society. Beyond its direct impact on the economy, it has also triggered severe environmental issues. Thus, developing efficient energy storage and utilization technologies to reduce reliance on traditional fossil energy has become a global consensus. Among these technologies, phase change thermal energy storage (PCTES)—a highly promising energy storage method—has gradually emerged as a research hotspot.1 PCTES technology leverages the ability of phase change materials (PCMs) to absorb or release large amounts of latent heat during phase transitions, thereby enabling energy storage and release.2 When the ambient temperature exceeds a PCM’s phase change temperature, the PCM absorbs heat, undergoes a phase transition, and stores energy in the form of latent heat. When the temperature drops below this threshold, it releases latent heat and reverts to its initial phase, achieving on-demand energy release.3 In solar energy utilization, PCTES addresses the intermittency of solar power. For example, in integrated photovoltaic-thermal (PV-T) systems, collectors integrated with PCMs can store excess heat generated during the daytime and release it at night—boosting solar energy utilization efficiency by over 30%.4 In building energy efficiency, incorporating PCMs into wall materials helps reduce energy consumption: In summer, they absorb indoor heat to lower air-conditioning loads; in winter, they release stored heat to reduce heating energy demand. Buildings adopting this technology typically see a 15–25% reduction in energy consumption.5 Additionally, PCTES is widely applied in industrial waste heat recovery, electronic device heat dissipation, and cold chain logistics. For instance, massive amounts of medium-to-low temperature waste heat (100°C–300°C) generated in industrial production are often underutilized. PCTES systems can recover this heat for applications such as heating and power generation, enabling cascaded energy utilization.6,7,8

CPCM-based passive thermal management is superior to liquid cooling for cold chain logistics: liquid cooling, the mainstream for high-power scenarios such as battery modules, is limited by complex pipelines, high costs, leakage risks, and poor adaptability to lightweight cold chain packaging, while CPCM systems realize on-demand temperature regulation via latent heat storage/release, matching cold chain cyclic temperature fluctuations well. Recent cutting-edge advances in high-performance CPCMs for battery thermal management have achieved multi-dimensional optimization of thermal and structural performance via diverse design strategies: Triphenyl phosphate-modified composite CPCMs realize high flame retardancy for power battery module thermal safety,9 intrinsic flame-retardant solid-solid CPCMs with melamine crosslink enhance battery pack thermal stability,10 flexible in situ prepared solid-solid CPCMs eliminate leakage issues for battery thermal management,11 Zn2+ metal-organic framework and metal organic gel-based composites achieve high thermal conductivity for efficient battery heat dissipation,12,13 macro-coating/micro-impregnation strategy optimizes hydrated salt composite stability for battery thermal safety,14 epoxy reinforcement frameworks further mitigate battery thermal runaway risks,15 anti-leakage and fire-resistant composite CPCMs improve lithium battery safety,16 and eutectic hydrated salt CPCMs realize two-stage thermal storage to match battery thermal safety demands.17 All these high-performance CPCMs are tailored for high-temperature (>30 °C) battery systems, and their core optimization directions (flame retardancy, anti-leakage, high thermal conductivity) provide valuable design references for low-temperature CPCM development. Yet their phase change temperature ranges are completely incompatible with the core 10°C–20°C cold chain window, thus developing low-temperature CPCMs with high thermal conductivity, leakage resistance, and good cycling stability is critical for efficient cold chain thermal management.

Fatty acids are a key choice for medium-to-low temperature PCMs, thanks to their wide availability and excellent phase change performance.18 Primarily derived from animal and plant oils, they cost only 60–70% of as much as paraffin wax and have a biodegradation rate of over 90%, meeting the needs of green development.19 They also exhibit good chemical stability: After 1,000 phase change cycles, their enthalpy decay rate is only 3–5%—far lower than paraffin’s 10–12%.20,21 Most fatty acids have a phase change enthalpy of 150–250 J g−1 (e.g., 210 J g−1 for stearic acid (SA) and 200 J g−1 for palmitic acid). Blending different fatty acids yields CPCMs with phase change temperatures ranging from 0°C to 100 °C, making them suitable for construction, HVAC systems, refrigeration, and waste heat recovery.22,23,24

However, fatty acids have limitations for practical applications. First, their thermal conductivity is low (0.1–0.2 W m−1 K−1 for most), much lower than that of metals. This slows heat absorption and release during phase change, prolongs system response time, and reduces efficiency.25 Second, during the solid-liquid phase transition of fatty acids, the liquid form is prone to leakage—an issue that worsens significantly in high-temperature environments. This not only causes the loss of thermal storage materials but also poses a risk of corroding other components of the thermal storage system, thereby shortening the system’s service life.26 To address these issues, fatty acids can be compounded with high thermal conductivity materials (e.g., metal powders, carbon materials, and nanoparticles) to form heat-conducting networks.27 Expanded graphite (EG) is an ideal option: It has a 3D porous structure (porosity >90%, specific surface area 100–300 m2/g) that immobilizes liquid fatty acids via capillary action and adsorption.28 EG also has high thermal conductivity (10–30 W m−1 K−1), forming continuous heat transfer paths in the composite.29 With 15% EG content, the compo-site’s thermal conductivity is 6–12 times that of pure fatty acids.30 Additionally, EG’s flake structure enhances mechanical properties (with compressive strength 30–40% higher) and delays thermal decomposition (increasing the initial de-composition temperature from 200 °C to 230 °C).31 Recent advances have further optimized EG’s thermal conductivity enhancement effect via structural regulation: Dai et al. prepared SA/EG/graphene composite films with high directional thermal conductivity, confirming that EG’s lamellar stacking density is a key factor in improving heat transfer efficiency.1,31 Meanwhile, Zhou et al. reported that fatty acid/EG composites with 8–10 wt % EG content achieve the optimal balance between latent heat retention and thermal conductivity, providing a reference for the EG dosage selection in this study.1,32

The preparation methods for ternary fatty acid/EG CPCMs include direct mixing, impregnation, and melt blending.33,34,35 Yuan et al.36 prepared a capric acid-palmitic acid-myristic acid/EG CPCM at a 13:1 ratio, which exhibited a phase change temperature of 18.61°C, a melting enthalpy of 128.2 J g−1, and a thermal conductivity of 3.67 W m−1 K−1. Via thermo-gravimetric analysis (TGA), Zhao et al.37 found that the palmitic acid (PA)-SA/bentonite/EG CPCM starts to decompose at approximately 180 °C and completes decomposition at around 320 °C. After 50 thermal cycles, the melting latent heat and solidification latent heat of the composite with 5% EG decrease by 14.6% and 13.7%, respectively. Anand et al.21 conducted 300 cyclic tests on six ternary fatty acid eutectics, namely CA-LA-MA, CA-LA-PA, CA-LA–SA, CA–MA–PA, CA–MA–SA, and CA–PA–SA. The results showed that the variation in melting enthalpy remained within 10%. Yang et al.38 reported that after 1000 heating/cooling cycles, the LA/EG composite PCM’s phase change temperature varied by < 0.46 °C with no obvious leakage, meeting long–term use requirements. Sett et al.39 prepared SA/EG (SA/EG) composites with EG mass fractions of 10%, 15%, and 20%, and measured their thermal conductivity using a heat transfer experimental set-up. The results showed that the addition of 10% EG increased the thermal conductivity of SA from 0.28 W m−1 K−1 to 0.64 W m−1 K−1, representing a relative increase of 129%. Karaipekli et al.40 prepared SA/EG composites with different EG contents (2%, 6%, and 10%) via the melt impregnation method. They tested the thermal properties of the composites, including thermal conductivity, phase change temperature, and latent heat. The results showed that the thermal conductivity of the composite with 6% EG was 9.6 times that of pure SA, and the heat transfer uniformity was significantly improved. Li et al.41 found that in a solar water heater, the thermal conductivity of the SA/EG composite material with 6% EG content was 9.6 times that of pure SA. Additionally, its heat storage time was reduced by 63.3%, and it exhibited the optimal heat release rate.

In summary, most existing studies focus on single fatty acids or multi–component fatty acid/EG CPCMs, but these studies are limited to the 30°C–80°C temperature range—primarily for applications in building energy efficiency and solar energy systems.42,43 Recent reviews confirm that low-temperature fatty acid/EG composites (10°C–20°C) for cold chain logistics are still underdeveloped, with only a few studies reporting ternary fatty acid systems.1,5 Chalivendula et al. emphasized that organic PCMs with phase change temperatures around 15 °C are in high demand for cold chain thermal management but lack effective form-stable composite solutions.1 Most existing low-temperature fatty acid/EG composites focus on the 20°C–30°C range for building energy efficiency or low-grade waste heat recovery,27,32,44,45 and their phase change temperatures deviate from the core 15 °C window required for cold chain logistics; meanwhile, the limited reported 10°C–20°C ternary systems suffer from low thermal conductivity or high supercooling degree,27,45,46 failing to meet the practical application requirements of rapid thermal response and stable phase change. However, research on fatty acid–based phase change energy storage materials tailored for refrigeration equipment (which requires a phase change temperature of 15 °C) remains rarely reported. To address this research gap, this study prepared a capric acid–lauric acid myristic acid/expanded graphite (CA–LA–MA/EG) CPCM) with a relatively low phase change temperature. Its structure and thermal properties were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT–IR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and thermal cycle tests. This CPCM is expected to be applied in low-temperature energy storage scenarios such as cold chain logistics.

Analysis of the optimal mass ratio between CA–LA–MA and EG

It is necessary to determine the optimal mass content of fatty acids in CA–LA–MA/EG. Previous studies have shown that the maximum mass fraction of fatty acids that can be loaded by EG (loading capacity of EG) in fatty acid-based PCMs exceeds 90%.50 The optimal mass ratio between CA–LA–MA and EG was determined using a visual approaching method, a method systematically validated in our prior study for balancing thermal performance and leakage resistance in fatty acid/EG composites.51 In this study, six groups of CA–LA–MA/EG samples (5 g each, with fatty acid contents ranging from 90% to 95%) were tested via the visual approximation method, which was also used to evaluate the optimal mass content range. As shown in Figure 2A, after heat treatment, the materials remained evenly distributed in the test area, with no penetration observed outside the area. As shown in Figure 2B, after brushing off the samples from each filter paper, the following results were obtained: Samples with 90% fatty acid content showed no penetration into the filter paper. This indicates an insufficient fatty acid proportion, leading to low latent heat per unit mass that fails to meet the requirements of PCMs. Samples with 91%, 92%, 93%, 94%, and 95% fatty acid contents exhibited varying degrees of leakage. Among these, samples with 93%, 94%, and 95% content had severe leakage; the 91% sample showed no obvious leakage; and the 92% sample had a small amount of leakage on the filter paper. Thus, the optimal mass ratio was preliminarily determined to be between 91% and 92%. Four additional sample groups (with fatty acid contents of 91.2%, 91.4%, 91.6%, and 91.8%) were then prepared for further testing, and the results are presented in Figures 2C and 2D. Figure 2C shows that the samples inside and outside the test area remained intact without leakage. After brushing off the samples, observations from Figure 2D (via visual inspection) revealed the following: Samples with 91.2% and 91.4% fatty acid content had minimal leakage, indicating good stability. Samples with 91.6% and 91.8% fatty acid content had more obvious leakage on the filter paper compared to the former two groups, so these formulations are recommended to be discarded. FT–IR analysis of the CA–LA–MA/EG

Figure 3 presents the infrared spectra of CA, LA, MA, the prepared CA–LA–MA, and CA–LA–MA/EG. An absorption peak at 2916.80 cm−1 corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the –CH2 functional group, while the peak at 2849.31 cm−1 is attributed to the symmetric stretching vibration of –CH2. A strong stretching vibration peak at 1694.15 cm−1 is attributed to the C=O functional group in the CA–LA–MA/EG composite. A characteristic absorption peak at 1470.93 cm−1 is due to the bending vibration of –CH2. The peak at 934.34 cm−1 results from the non–planar rocking vibration of O–H dimers. The FT-IR spectra of the ternary fatty acid eutectic (CA–LA–MA) show that all characteristic absorption peaks remain at their original positions, with no new peaks emerging and no existing peaks disappearing among the five spectral curves, indicating the absence of chemical reactions between the components. For the CA-LA-MA/EG composite, although the peaks corresponding to C=O (∼1694 cm−1), CH2 (∼2916 cm−1), and O–H (∼934 cm−1) exhibit slight shifts compared to the pure eutectic—suggesting possible hydrogen bonding or van der Waals interactions—no new characteristic peaks associated with chemical bonds are formed. Supported by published EG spectral data and its inherent thermodynamic stability (graphite lamellar structure is chemically inert under low-temperature, catalyst-free conditions),52 the composite’s FT-IR spectral features (no new peaks/shifts indicative of chemical reactions) and consistent physical properties with physical mixing rules collectively confirm that no chemical reaction occurs between CA-LA-MA and EG, and their interaction is purely physical in nature.Figure 3FT–IR spectra of CA, LA, MA, CA–LA–MA, and CA–LA–MA/EG

Thermal properties of CA–LA–MA/EG

The thermal performance of the PCMs was measured via DSC. Figure 5 shows the DSC curves of CA–LA–MA and CA–LA–MA/EG. As shown in Figure 5, the peak positions of CA–LA–MA and CA–LA–MA/EG exhibit almost no shift, indicating that both undergo a single phase change with similar phase change temperatures. The melting and solidification temperatures of CA–LA–MA are 15.6 °C and 15.2 °C, respectively, with corresponding melting and solidification latent heats of 139.8 J g−1 and 124.1 J g−1. For CA–LA–MA/EG, the melting and solidification temperatures are 15.9 °C and 15.6 °C, respectively, while the melting and solidification latent heats are 135.6 J g−1 and 123.8 J g−1. The PCM exhibited a supercooling behavior, wherein crystallization was delayed until 15.6 °C, 0.3 °C lower than the melting temperature (15.9 °C), resulting in a supercooling degree of 0.3 °C. This hysteresis reflects kinetic limitations in nucleation and crystal growth, which is a common phenomenon in PCMs. This behavior highlights the need for effective nucleation mechanisms to enhance the responsiveness of the CPCM. For comparison, the pure CA–LA–MA eutectic was determined to have a supercooling degree of 0.4 °C, indicating that the incorporation of EG moderately but effectively reduces the supercooling degree of the fatty acid eutectic. EG acts as heterogeneous nucleation sites for CA–LA–MA molecules during crystallization, lowering the nucleation energy barrier and promoting crystal nucleus formation at a temperature closer to the equilibrium freezing point. Meanwhile, the enhanced thermal conductivity of the composite by EG accelerates uniform heat dissipation during cooling, avoiding local overcooling caused by slow heat transfer in pure CA–LA–MA, which jointly contributes to the reduction of supercooling degree. Thermal conductivity and heat storage/release behavior of CA–LA–MA/EG

This experiment investigated the relationship between the thermal conductivity (λ, W·m−1·K−1) of CA–LA–MA/EG CPCMs and packing density. Five samples with varying bulk densities were prepared and denoted as PCM 1 to PCM 5 (Figure 6). The parameters and thermal conductivity of each sample are listed in Table 2. Thermal conductivity was measured using a DRE–III thermal conductivity meter. Three parallel tests were performed for each sample to ensure data reliability, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The average thermal conductivity of pure CA–LA–MA eutectic was 0.185 ± 0.0015 W m−1 K−1. The in-plane thermal conductivity of EG is generally reported to be 200–800 W m−1 K−1, with 300–500 W m−1 K−1 being the most widely accepted range for well-prepared samples.53,54,55 In contrast, CA–LA–MA/EG at a bulk density of 559.96 kg m−3 exhibited a thermal conductivity of 3.438 ± 0.0092 W m−1 K−1, representing an approximately 19-fold enhancement. EG introduces an efficient thermal conduction network and enhances intermolecular interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding with fatty acid carboxyl groups), overcoming the low thermal conductivity limitation of pure fatty acid PCMs for cold-chain applications requiring rapid thermal response. The test results indicate a linear relationship between thermal conductivity and bulk density. Thermal conductivity increases with increasing packing density, as shown in Figure 7 and Equation 4, with the linear fitting derived from the mean values of three parallel tests and satisfactory R2 goodness of fit. As bulk density increases from 559.96 to 660.12 kg m−3, EG lamellae stack more compactly, reducing porosity and enhancing thermal conductivity via two key mechanisms: The compacted EG forms a more continuous 3D thermal conduction network, shortening solid-phase heat transfer paths and lowering interfacial thermal resistance between EG sheets; Reduced porosity minimizes gaseous thermal resistance and suppresses air convection in pores, which is a major heat transfer limitation in low-density porous materials. Notably, this linear correlation has an upper limit: A bulk density exceeding 700 kg m−3 may fracture EG lamellae, disconnect the 3D thermal network, and reduce thermal conductivity. Thus, the density range of 559.96–660.12 kg m−3 optimally balances structural integrity and thermal conductivity enhancement. The comparison of properties the fatty acid/EG with those reported in the Literature

Compared with reported fatty acid/EG composites, CA–LA–MA/EG exhibits clear advantages for low-temperature thermal management. Even with a low EG loading of only 8.6 wt %, the composite achieves a high thermal conductivity of 3.438–4.775 W m−1 K−1, superior to most literature values. In contrast, CA–MA–PW/EG shows a much higher supercooling degree of 3.37 °C that may destabilize phase transition,56 while LA-PA-PS/EG has an unsuitable phase change temperature of 33.81 °C for cold chain storage.57 These results verify that CA–LA–MA/EG satisfies cold chain requirements with better low-temperature adaptability, higher thermal conductivity enhancement efficiency, and lower supercooling degree. The remarkable thermal conductivity enhancement and excellent thermal cycling reliability achieved by low EG loading in this work are consistent with the general conclusion that EG can effectively construct heat-conducting networks and improve the thermal stability of eutectic-based composite PCMs.58 Moreover, recent comprehensive reviews on form-stable PCMs for cold chain logistics have explicitly emphasized that high thermal conductivity, ultra-low supercooling degree, and precise matching with the 10°C–20°C core temperature window are the three key performance criteria for practical cold chain PCM applications,59 all of which are well satisfied by the prepared CA–LA–MA/EG composite.

Although thermal conductivity increases linearly with stacking density, excessively high density raises volume and weight, conflicting with the lightweight demand of cold chain packaging. Thus, multi-objective optimization among thermal conductivity, latent heat, volume/weight, and cost is necessary for practical applications. Suitable stacking density should be selected based on real-world conditions to balance thermal performance and usability. In industrial production, density can be flexibly tuned by controlling molding pressure or optimizing EG particle size matching, enabling the composite to meet diverse cold chain design needs and improving its engineering applicability.

The addition of EG enhances the thermal conductivity of the composite material, which can also be verified by comparing the heat storage and release characteristics of CA–LA–MA and CA–LA–MA/EG. The experimental setup is shown in Figure 8.45 Temperature probes were inserted into the center of test tubes, which contained equal volumes of CA–LA–MA and CA–LA–MA/EG, respectively. 50.0 ± 0.1 g of each sample (CA–LA–MA and CA–LA–MA/EG) was then placed in the same constant temperature environment, and the test was initiated once their temperatures stabilized at the set value. The temperature for the exothermic process was set to 0 °C, and that for the endothermic process to 50 °C. The thermal cycling reliability of PCMs refers to their ability to resist degradation in heat storage performance after repeated heat storage and release cycles; this is a crucial parameter for evaluating their service life.60 For CPCMs to be widely applied, they must exhibit excellent thermal stability after undergoing multiple heat storage and release cycles. The CA–LA–MA/EG CPCM was subjected to 500, 1000, and 1500 heat storage–release cycles. Its DSC curves and phase change thermal property parameters during thermal cycling are presented in Figure 11 and Table 4, respectively. The enthalpy retention rate was calculated using Equation 5 and the calculation results are shown in Table 4. As shown in the Figure 11, the DSC curve of the CA–LA–MA/EG CPCM remains nearly identical to its pre–cycle curve even after multiple cycles. Table 4 indicates that there is no significant change in the melting and solidification temperatures of the CA–LA–MA/EG CPCM before and after thermal cycling. After 1500 cycles, the melting enthalpy of the material slightly decreases from 135.6 J g−1 to 133.3 J g−1, corresponding to an enthalpy retention of approximately 98.3%, while the solidification enthalpy marginally declines from 123.8 J g−1 to 122.6 J g−1. The minimal enthalpy loss after 1500 thermal cycles stems from minor physical microstructural changes instead of chemical degradation, consistent with the confirmed pure physical combination between CA–LA–MA and EG (only capillary force and surface tension interactions, no chemical bond formation). Specifically, a small portion of CA–LA–MA molecules slightly desorb from the micropores and interlamellar gaps of EG’s 3D porous network during repeated melting-solidification transitions, causing a tiny reduction in the effective phase change core content. In addition, the EG lamellar structure undergoes negligible stacking compaction under cyclic thermal stress, which slightly weakens the capillary adsorption force but does not damage EG’s overall porous support structure. The unobvious changes in phase change temperatures (Table 5) and nearly overlapping DSC curves (Figure 11) after cycling further verify the absence of chemical degradation or phase change behavior deterioration, indicating the slight enthalpy decay is merely a physical effect that does not affect the overall thermal reliability of the CPCM. Notably, the CA–LA–MA/EG exhibits superior thermal cycling stability compared with the latest fatty acid/EG composite materials reported in 2024–2025 Ref [45,56,57]. After 1500 consecutive thermal cycles, the composite retains a high enthalpy retention rate of 98.3%, which not only far surpasses the general thermal cycling requirement (≥500 cycles) for cold chain PCMs in practical applications, but also outperforms the recently published fatty acid/EG systems in terms of both cycle durability and enthalpy retention efficiency. Discussion

In summary, form-stable CA–LA–MA/EG CPCM for cold chain logistics was prepared.1The composite achieves excellent shape stability with only 8.6 wt % EG loading and maintains a high phase change enthalpy.2It shows an ultra-low supercooling degree of only 0.3 °C, effectively avoiding phase transition hysteresis.3The thermal conductivity is significantly enhanced from less than 0.2 W/(m·K) for the pure PCM to above 3.4 W/(m·K), greatly improving heat transfer efficiency.

The synergistic advantages of low EG content, near-zero supercooling, and high thermal conductivity make the composite highly competitive in cold chain thermal management.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted at the laboratory scale, so the scalability of the vacuum impregnation process for industrial production needs verification. Additionally, the long-term performance of CA–LA–MA/EG under actual cold chain conditions (humidity, mechanical vibration, temperature fluctuations) remains unevaluated. Future work will optimize the pilot-scale preparation process for higher efficiency and lower costs, conduct field application tests, and explore EG modification to enhance the composite’s thermal and mechanical properties for wider low-temperature thermal management applications.

Method details

Materials

Capric acid (CA, purity ≥98.5%), lauric acid (LA, purity ≥98.5%), and myristic acid (MA, purity ≥98.5%) were supplied by Shanghai Zhanyun Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Expandable graphite was purchased from Qingdao Hengrunda Graphite Products Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China), with the following specifications: 350 meshes, an expansion rate of 100 mL/g, a carbon content of 99%, and a density of 1.1 g/cm3.

Preparation of CA-LA-MA/EG CPCM

A specific quantity of CA, LA, and MA was mixed in a beaker. The beaker was then placed in a 60 °C constant–temperature water bath to melt the samples, with magnetic stirring at 300 r/min for 30 minutes to ensure thorough homogenization of the three components. After cooling the beaker to room temperature, the CA–LA–MA ternary eutectic mixture was obtained. Separately, a defined amount of expandable graphite was dried to remove moisture. The dried expandable graphite was transferred to a ceramic cup and heated in a muffle furnace at 900 °C for approximately 50 seconds, yielding expanded graphite.

The vacuum impregnation experiment was conducted in a digital display vacuum drying oven (DZF-6020B, Hangzhou Lipei Instrument Tech. Co., Ltd.). A beaker containing a defined amount of CA–LA–MA was placed in the oven and heated to a constant 40 ± 1 °C (liquid state). The vacuum system was activated to pump the vacuum degree of -0.095 MPa (gauge pressure) and maintained for 2 h to remove trapped air in EG pores. Pre-weighed EG (CA–LA–MA:EG = 91.4:8.6, mass ratio) was added under vacuum, with the same vacuum degree maintained for another 4 h for sufficient impregnation. The vacuum was then slowly relieved to atmospheric pressure at 0.01 MPa/min to avoid air re-entry. The mixture was further heated at 40 ± 1 °C for 48 h, with gentle stirring for 1.5 h at the 24th hour to ensure uniform adsorption. Finally, the product was naturally cooled to room temperature to obtain the CA–LA–MA/EG CPCM.

Penetration testing

The optimal adsorption capacity of expanded graphite (EG) for the composite of CA–LA–MA was determined via the visual approximation method. Specifically, 5 g of CA–LA–MA/EG CPCM was weighed and placed on a filter paper with a diameter of 30 mm, followed by transfer to a vacuum drying oven at 60 °C for heat treatment. After 30 minutes, the sample was taken out and cooled to room temperature. Through this process, the optimal mass content range of phase change materials in CPCMs was preliminarily determined.

Thermal conductivities test

The thermal conductivity of CA–LA–MA/EG composite phase change energy storage materials was determined via a thermal conductivity meter (Model DRE–III, Xiangtan Xiangyi Instrument Co., Ltd., Xiangtan, China). Five groups of CA–LA–MA/EG samples with distinct mass ratios were weighed, sealed in glass beakers, and thermally conditioned in a constant-temperature oven at 40 °C until the completion of phase transition. The treated samples were then pressed, ground, and demolded into smooth disc-shaped specimens with a fixed diameter of 30 mm and varying bulk densities. Prior to testing, thermal conductive silica gel was uniformly coated on both surfaces of each disc, which was subsequently mounted coaxially onto the pressure rod of the thermal conductivity meter. A consistent pressure load was applied to all samples throughout the measurement, with the test temperature set at 70 °C. Thermal conductivity values were recorded automatically once the temperature of the hot electrode stabilized at 70 °C. Key technical parameters of the instrument were as follows: thermal conductivity measurement range of 0.1–300 W·m-1·K-1, thermal resistance stabilization time of 300 s, thermal impedance stabilization deviation of ±5%, operating temperature range from ambient temperature to 70 °C, temperature-controlled heating rate of 1 °C /min, and constant temperature deviation of ±0.1%. The relative error and repeatability error of thermal conductivity measurements were both ≤3%. To ensure the reliability and repeatability of the thermal conductivity measurements, three parallel tests (n=3) were performed under identical conditions for each sample. The final thermal conductivity value was expressed as the mean value of the three parallel tests, and the standard deviation (SD) was calculated to evaluate the experimental repeatability and data reliability. The small standard deviations confirm the good repeatability of the measurements. Characterization

The thermal properties of the samples were characterized via a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, NETSZCH 214 Polyma, Selb, Germany). Prior to testing, the instrument’s temperature and heat flow axes were calibrated with indium standard following the vendor-recommended protocols, ensuring deviations in phase transition temperature and latent heat were ±0.1% and ±4%, respectively. Measurements were performed under a 0.5 bar nitrogen atmosphere (≥99.99% purity). The sample chamber was purged at 50 mL·min-1 for 30 min to remove residual air, with pressure stabilized via a calibrated pressure-regulating valve and the instrument’s automatic control module. The temperature program ranged from –10 °C to 60 °C at a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C·min-1. Blank baseline experiments (empty crucible under identical thermal conditions) were conducted alongside sample tests; sample curves were baseline-corrected via software to eliminate instrument drift and background heat capacity effects. All phase change temperatures (melting temperature Tm and freezing temperature Tf were defined by the onset temperature of DSC curves to guarantee data objectivity and comparability. FT-IR spectra were recorded on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 spectrometer with an attenuated total reflectance accessory. Spectra were acquired over the range of 4000–400 cm-1 at a resolution of 4 cm-1 (32 scans per spectrum). A background scan was performed prior to sample testing to eliminate interference from ambient air and instrument noise, and absorbance values were derived via spectral processing with OMNIC software.

The morphological evolution of phase change energy storage materials before and after composite preparation was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S–4800, Hitachi, Japan). Samples were affixed to conductive adhesive tape and loaded into the SEM chamber; micrographs were captured at magnifications of 1000×–5500×.

Thermal resistance and stability of CA–LA–MA/EG were evaluated via thermos gravimetric analysis (TGA, TA TGA5000IR, New Castle, DE, USA). The instrument was calibrated for temperature using indium oxide (In2O3), tin (Sn) and zinc (Zn) reference materials (10 °C/min heating rate, nitrogen atmosphere), with temperature deviation controlled within ±2 °C. Balance sensitivity was calibrated with standard weights, limiting mass measurement errors to ±0.05%. Blank baseline experiments (empty Al2O3 crucibles) were conducted under identical conditions for all tests, and sample curves were baseline-corrected using instrument software to eliminate background interference.

Long-term isothermal thermogravimetric analysis was performed to evaluate the long-term thermal stability under the extreme cold chain service temperature. The sample was heated from room temperature to 40 °C at 10 °C/min under N2 atmosphere, and then held isothermally for 100 h to simulate the oxygen-free enclosed environment inside the heat exchanger. The mass loss and thermal stability of the composite were continuously recorded during the isothermal process.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with three parallel replicates (n=3). No significant statistical difference was observed among the parallel tests, indicating good data reliability.
