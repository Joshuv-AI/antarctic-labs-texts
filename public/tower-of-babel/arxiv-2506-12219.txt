Abstract
We study the problem of communicating a sample from a probability distribution P P given shared access to a sequence distributed according to another probability distribution Q Q . Li and El Gamal [ 1 ] used the Poisson functional representation to show that the minimum expected message length to communicate a sample from P P can be upper bounded by D ( P | | Q ) + log ( D ( P | | Q ) + 1 ) + 4 D(P||Q)+\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+4 , where D ( ⋅ | | ⋅ ) D(\,\cdot\,||\,\cdot\,) is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. We generalize this and related results to a cost which is exponential in the message length, specifically L ⁡ ( t ) L(t) , Campbell’s average codeword length of order t t [ 2 ] , and to Rényi’s entropy. We lower bound the Campbell cost and Rényi entropy of communicating a sample under any (possibly noncausal) sampling protocol, showing that it grows approximately as D 1 α ( P | | Q ) D_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(P||Q) , where D β ( ⋅ | | ⋅ ) D_{\beta}(\,\cdot\,||\,\cdot\,) is the Rényi divergence of order β \beta . Using the Poisson functional representation, we prove an upper bound on L ⁡ ( t ) L(t) and H α ​ ( K ) H_{\alpha}(K) which has a leading Rényi divergence term with order within ϵ \epsilon of the lower bound. Our results reduce to the bounds of Harsha et al. [ 3 ] as α → 1 \alpha\to 1 . We also provide numerical examples comparing the bounds in the cases of normal and Laplacian distributions, demonstrating that the upper and lower bounds are typically within 5-10 bits of each other.



I
Introduction

We consider the problem of communicating a sample from a probability distribution
P
, given that the sender and receiver have access to a shared source of randomness
\quantity{U_i}_{i\geq 1}
drawn independently according to another probability distribution
Q
. This problem, called exact sampling, is visualized in Fig.
1
.

Fig. 1
:
Block diagram of the exact sampling problem.

As studied in
[
3
,
4
,
1
]
, among others, the key question is how to design a communication protocol such that the expected message length needed to communicate the sample from
P
is minimized. Naively communicating a sample from
P
using lossless coding is often impossible, as in principle, the sample can be have a continuous distribution. Instead, protocols aim to transmit an index
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that the
K
th sample in the shared randomness has exact distribution
P
, i.e.,
U_{K}\sim P
.

The goal is then to minimize the expected message length of
K
, or (almost) equivalently, to minimize
H(K)
, where
H(\cdot)
denotes Shannon entropy. Formally, with
\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\quantity{0,1}^{*}
denoting a uniquely decodable binary variable-length code and
l(\mathcal{C}(k))
denoting the length of the codeword of
k
, the goal is to design a sampling algorithm and code
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]
is minimized. This is intimately related to the problem of channel simulation (visualized in Fig.
2
), where
X
and
Y
are general random variables with joint distribution
\text{P}_{XY}
and we let
P=\text{P}_{Y|X}(\,\cdot\,\mid x)
and
Q=\text{P}_{Y}
; i.e., on input
x\sim\text{P}_{X}
we wish to sample from the conditional distribution
P_{Y\mid X}(\,\cdot\,\mid x)
given shared access to the marginal distribution
P_{Y}
.

Fig. 2
:
Block diagram of the channel simulation problem.

Channel simulation, also called reverse channel coding or relative entropy coding, has recently gained significant theoretical and practical attention due to its promise as an alternative for quantization in deep learning-based compression systems
[
5
,
6
]
. There has been a corresponding research effort to understand the theoretical limits of channel simulation and its related problems
[
7
,
8
]
.

Generalizing, one may also be concerned about costs other than the expected message length. Motivated by Campbell’s lossless source coding theorem
[
2
]
, we consider one-shot exact sampling under an exponential storage cost. Such a cost is appropriate for applications where buffer overflow can occur
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
, and therefore, the cost of long codewords is especially significant
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Here for a given
t>0
, we aim to minimize the average codeword length of order
t
, also called the normalized cumulant generating function of codeword lengths
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, given by

L(t)=\frac{1}{t}\log(\mathbb{E}[2^{tl(\mathcal{C}(K))}]).

(1)

As
t\rightarrow 0
in (
1
), by L’Hôpital’s rule, we recover the average codeword length,
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]
. In this work, we provide Rényi generalizations of prior results to the Campbell cost
L(t)
. In particular, we lower bound
L(t)
for any (possibly noncausal) sampling algorithm and use the Poisson functional representation to prove an upper bound on
L(t)
whose leading Rényi divergence term has order within
\epsilon
of the lower bound. We also describe an operational procedure for encoding
L(t)
with a universal code and prove an upper bound on
L(t)
that reduces to the bound of Harsha et al.
[
3
]
as
t\to 0
. Qualitatively, the lower and upper bounds match in shape and are within 5-10 bits of each other for most
0<\alpha<1
, even for distributions where
L(t)
is very large. Unlike the natural bound
L(t)<H_{\alpha}(K)+1
(see Proposition
1
), the right side of which is computable for special cases of
P
and
Q
when
K
is generated using the Poisson functional representation, our bounds can be computed instantaneously for all
P,Q
and are comparably tight. As illustrated in Section
V
, as
L(t)
grows,
H_{\alpha}(K)
becomes exceedingly heavy-tailed and intractable to compute, while our bounds remain tight (within 5-10 bits) and easy to calculate.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
II
contains some preliminary definitions and a review of the relevant literature. Section
III
lower bounds
L(t)
in the exact sampling problem, and Section
IV
describes two communication protocols using the Poisson functional representation which closely approximate this lower bound. Section

gives numerical comparisons of the lower and upper bounds in the cases of normal and Laplacian distributions and a discussion of the difficulty of computing

Preliminaries

II-A

Rényi Information Measures

Let
X
be a discrete random variable with alphabet
\mathcal{X}
and distribution
\text{P}_{X}
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, we define the Rényi entropy of order
\alpha
of
X
as

H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log(\sum_{x \in\mathcal{X}} \text{P}_X(x)^\alpha).

(2)

H_{\alpha}(X)
is nonincreasing in
\alpha
, and if
H_{\alpha}(X)<\infty
for some
0<\alpha<1
, then
\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}H_{\alpha}(X)=H(X)
, recovering the Shannon entropy. In
[
2
]
, Campbell provided an operational meaning to the Rényi entropy by connecting it with the average code length of order
t
,
L(t)
.

Proposition 1

(​
[
2
]
)
.

Suppose the discrete random variable
X
is encoded using a uniquely decodable binary code
\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\quantity{0, 1}^{*}
such that each codeword has length
l(C(x))=n_{x}
, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then the average code length of order
t>0
given by

L(t)=\frac{1}{t}\log(\sum_{x \in\mathcal{X}} \textup{P}_X(x) 2^{tn_x}),

(3)

satisfies

H_{\alpha}(X)\leq L(t),

(4)

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{t+1}
. Moreover, there exists a uniquely decodable binary code
\mathcal{C}
such that

L(t)<H_{\alpha}(X)+1.

(5)

We note that while Campbell’s original proof was for
X
with finite alphabet, it can easily be extended to the case of a countably infinite alphabet. For simplicity, we will often refer to
L(t)
as just the Campbell cost.
L(t)
is a nondecreasing function of
t
, and is strictly increasing in
t
unless all the lengths
n_{x}
are equal. As a result, if
X
takes countably infinite values and not all the lengths
n_{x}
are equal, then
L(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. If
X
is finite-valued, then
L(t)\to\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}n_{x}
.

Rényi also defined the divergence of order
\alpha
between probability distributions
P
and
Q
,
D_{\alpha}(P||Q)
, as

D_{\alpha}(P||Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(\mathbb{E}_{X \sim Q} \left[\left(\frac{\dd P}{\dd Q}(X) \right)^\alpha\right]),

where
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
and it is assumed that
P\ll Q
. Note that
\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}D_{\alpha}(P||Q)=D(P||Q)
, i.e., the Kullback-Leibler divergence is recovered when
\alpha\nearrow 1

[
16
]
.

II-B

Prior Work

The problem of channel simulation was first studied by Wyner
[
17
]
, who considered the asymptotic version (communicating
n
samples at a time) without common randomness but allowing error between the simulated and target distributions. Winter
[
18
]
showed that, by allowing a shared source of unlimited randomness, it is possible to simulate the channel with small error at an asymptotic cost equal to the mutual information. Bennett et al.
[
19
]
showed that the same result holds even for exact channel synthesis. More recently, Cuff
[
20
]
, Bennett et al.
[
21
]
, and Yu and Tan
[
22
]
studied the tradeoff between communication rate and amount of common randomness in the asymptotic regime. Sriramu and Wagner
[
8
]
and Flamich et al.
[
23
]
examined the optimal rate of exact channel synthesis in the asymptotic case. There has also been recent work, motivated by applications in deep learning-based compression, on developing algorithms which are computationally efficient, e.g.
[
6
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. In our problem of one-shot exact sampling, it is not hard to show that a lower bound on the cost is
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\geq D(P||Q)

[
3
]
. The first achievability bound is due to Harsha et al.
[
3
]
, who described a communication protocol for discrete
P,Q
for which

\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\leq D(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+c_{\epsilon},

with
c_{\epsilon}
a constant depending on
\epsilon
. Their proof effectively amounts to showing that
\mathbb{E}[\log K]\leq D(P||Q)+2\log e
and encoding
K
using a universal code. As noted in
[
1
]
, by using instead a power law code one can bound
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\leq D(P||Q)+\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+7.78
for discrete
P,Q
. The Poisson functional representation, first proposed by Li and El Gamal in
[
1
]
, has since been used to prove a tighter upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]

[
29
]
.

Definition 1

(Poisson functional representation
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a Polish space with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra and
P,Q
two probability distributions on
\mathcal{U}
with
P\ll Q
. Let
\{T_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a rate-one Poisson process and let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be an i.i.d. sequence distributed according to
Q
and independent of
\quantity{T_i}_{i\geq 1}
. Define

K\coloneqq\underset{i\geq 1}{\arg\min}\frac{T_{i}}{\frac{\differential P}{\differential Q}(U_{i})}.

(6)

It is shown in
[
30

that under this definition,
U_{K}\sim P
. For
P
and
Q

generated according to Definition
1
,
[

\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\leq D(P||Q)+\log(D(P||Q) + 2)+3.

Moreover,
[

showed that there exist distributions for which
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\geq D(P||Q)+\log(D(P||Q) + 1)-1
, i.e., the upper bound cannot be tightened in general.

II-C

Contributions

In this work, we study the Campbell cost,
L(t)
, of one-shot exact channel simulation algorithms. Although channel simulation and its associated minimum communication cost are well-studied problems, to the best of our knowledge, this natural extension (from linear cost to an exponential cost in message lengths) has not been investigated in the literature. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We lower bound the
L(t)
cost of encoding
K
for any (possibly noncausal) sampling algorithm, showing that it grows approximately as
D_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(P||Q)
, where
\alpha=\frac{1}{1+t}
. We also prove the lower bound
L(t)\geq D_{2-\alpha}(P||Q)+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log(\frac{1}{2-\alpha})
, which is a tighter bound when
\alpha
is close to
1
or
D_{\alpha}(P||Q)
is small.

•

We use the Poisson functional representation to prove the upper bound
L(t)\leq(1+\epsilon)D_{\frac{1+\epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+c_{1}(\alpha,\epsilon)
, for any
0<\alpha<1
and
\epsilon>0
, where
c_{1}(\alpha,\epsilon)
is a constant depending on
\alpha
and
\epsilon
. Using numerical examples, we demonstrate that this upper bound is within 5-10 bits of the lower bound, even for distributions where
L(t)
is large. As a corollary, we give an upper bound on
L(t)
for the channel simulation problem used to motivate this paper.

•

We describe an operational procedure for encoding
K
using a universal code that gives
L(t)\leq D_{\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+c_{2}(\epsilon)
, for any
2/3<\alpha<1
and
0<\epsilon\leq\frac{3\alpha-2}{2-2\alpha}
, where
c_{2}(\epsilon)
is a constant. We show that this upper bound reduces to the bound of Harsha et al.
[
3
]
as
\alpha\to 1
.

We present our main results as bounds on the Campbell cost
L(t)

(7)

and
\text{LB}_{2}^{\alpha}\coloneqq D_{2-\alpha}(P||Q)+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log(\frac{1}{2-\alpha})

IV
Upper Bounds using the Poisson Functional Representation

Theorem
1
tells us that the minimum Campbell cost of any sampling algorithm grows approximately as
D_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(P||Q)
. The natural question is whether any one-shot sampling algorithms exist that achieve this lower bound. Suppose that we could bound
L(t)\leq D_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+C
for some constant
C
and all distributions
P,Q
. Then, taking
\alpha\to 1
(equivalently
t\to 0
) would yield
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\leq D(P||Q)+C
, a bound shown to be invalid by an explicit counter-example
[
1
]
. Therefore, it is reasonable to conjecture that a tight and general upper bound, valid for all
P
and
Q
, does not exist for the Campbell cost, either. Instead, we have the following upper bound on
L(t)
using the Poisson functional representation.

Theorem 2
.

Let
P,Q
be probability distributions on a Polish space
\mathcal{U}
with
P\ll Q
and let
K

\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log( \Gamma\left( \frac{1 + \epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha} \right) )+4+3\epsilon-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}+\log(1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon}),&0<\alpha<1/2\text{ or }\epsilon\geq\frac{2\alpha-1}{1-\alpha}.\end{cases}

Lemma 2

Let

Lemma 3
.

Let

be a geometric random variable with parameter

Proof.

The proof can be found in Appendix

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We will prove Theorem

in two cases. In both cases, we will relate the Campbell cost

to the expected moments of

, with

. For

(equivalently

), we will bound the moments of

) in Lemma

, while in the case

(equivalently

) we will use the bound on the moments of a geometric random variable in Lemma

. In particular, we will show that for any
1/2<\alpha<1
and
0<\epsilon<\frac{2\alpha-1}{1-\alpha}
, with

,

(16)

Then, for any
0<\alpha<1
, for
t=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
and
\epsilon\geq\frac{2\alpha-1}{1-\alpha}
(so that

) we will show that

L(t)\leq(1+\epsilon)D_{\frac{1+\epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log( \Gamma\left( \frac{1 + \epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha} \right) )+4+3\epsilon-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}+\log(1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon}).

(17)

Taking (
16
) and (
17
) together yields the statement of Theorem

.

Fix
1/2<\alpha<1
and
0<\epsilon<\frac{2\alpha-1}{1-\alpha}
. Then, by the Kraft inequality there exists a uniquely decodable encoding of
K
with codeword lengths

; this is shown in Appendix

. Let

. Then,

, so we can apply the upper bound on

in (

) to get

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\log(\sum_{k=1}^\infty p(k) 2^{tn_k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\log(\sum_{k=1}^\infty p(k) 2^{t (1 + \epsilon) \log k + t + t\log( 1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon})})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\log(\mathbb{E}[K^{(1+\epsilon)t}])+1+\log(1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\log(2^{(1 + \epsilon)tD_{(1 + \epsilon)t + 1}(P||Q)} + (1 + \epsilon)t)+1+\log(1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon})

(18)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)D_{(1+\epsilon)t+1}(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log e+1+\log(1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon}).

(19)

Here, (

Suppose now that

\epsilon\geq\frac{2\alpha-1}{1-\alpha}

,
(1+\epsilon)t\geq 1
. From

(after substituting

K\mid\quantity{U_K = u}\sim\text{Geo}(\beta(u)),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[K^{r}\mid U_{K}=u]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{r-1}\left(\frac{\Gamma(r+1)}{\beta(u)^{r}}+1\right)

\displaystyle=2^{r-1}\Gamma(r+1)\mathbb{E}_{U\sim Q}\left[\max\quantity{\frac{\dd P}{\dd Q}(u), \frac{\dd P}{\dd Q}(U)}\right]^{r}+2^{r-1}

\displaystyle\leq\Gamma(r+1)2^{2r-2}\left(\left(\frac{\differential P}{\differential Q}(u)\right)^{r}+1\right)+2^{r-1},

\mathbb{E}[K^{r}]\leq\Gamma(r+1)2^{2r-2}\left(2^{rD_{r+1}(P||Q)}+1\right)+2^{r-1}.

Again encoding

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\log(\mathbb{E}[K^r])+c(\epsilon)

Here, (

) and (

for all

. Substituting

The Rényi divergence term in

has order
\frac{1+\epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}

goes to

as

is not tight. Numerical examples in Section

is typically within 5-10 bits of
\text{LB}_{1}^{\alpha}

for all

. The bounds of

and

have the operational advantage of encoding

using a power law code and universal code, respectively, therefore requiring no knowledge of the distribution of

using a universal code gives the following upper bound, which is strictly worse than

Theorem 3
.

Let
P,Q
be probability distributions on a Polish space
\mathcal{U}
with
P\ll Q
and let
K
be the output of the Poisson functional representation. Then, for any
2/3<\alpha<1
and
0<\epsilon\leq\frac{3\alpha-2}{2-2\alpha}
, encoding
K
using a universal code with codeword lengths
n_{k}=\log k+\log(\log k + 1)+O(1)
gives

L(t)\leq D_{\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+c_{2}(\epsilon),

(26)

with
t=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
and
c_{2}(\epsilon)=3+\epsilon+\log(\frac{\ln(2)}{\epsilon} + \frac{3}{2})
.

Proof.

Let
2/3<\alpha<1
and
0<\epsilon\leq\frac{3\alpha-2}{2-2\alpha}
. We will bound
L(t)
using a universal coding of the natural numbers. In particular, using the prefix-free encoding described in
[
34
, Ex. 1.11.16]
(see also
[
3
]
) we can set
n_{k}\leq\log k+(1+\epsilon)\log\log(k + 1)+1+\log(\frac{\ln(2)}{\epsilon} + \frac{3}{2})
for any
\epsilon>0
; this is proved in Appendix
-C
. Since
t=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
,
0<t<1/2
. Then,

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\log(\mathbb{E}\left[ 2^{tn_K} \right])

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\log( \mathbb{E} \left[ K^t \log(K+1)^{(1+\epsilon)t} \right])+1+\log(\frac{\ln(2)}{\epsilon} + \frac{3}{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\log(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[K^{2t}]\mathbb{E}[\log(K+1)^{2(1+\epsilon)t}]})+1+\log(\frac{\ln(2)}{\epsilon} + \frac{3}{2})

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2t}\left(\log(\mathbb{E}[K^{2t}])+\log(\mathbb{E}[\log(K+1)^{2(1+\epsilon)t}])\right)+1+\log(\frac{\ln(2)}{\epsilon} + \frac{3}{2}).

(28)

Here, (
27
) follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. We will bound each of the two terms in (
28
) separately, beginning with
\mathbb{E}[\log(K+1)^{2(1+\epsilon)t}]

0<t<\frac{1}{2}

gives that

, and so Jensen’s inequality can be applied as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2t}\log\left(\mathbb{E}[\log(K + 1)^{2(1+\epsilon)t}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2t}\log\left(\mathbb{E}[\log(K + 1)]^{2(1+\epsilon)t}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)\log\big( \mathbb{E}[\log(K + 1)] \big)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\log\big( \mathbb{E}[\log K] + 1 \big)

(29)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1 + 1)

(30)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+1+\epsilon.

(31)

Here, (
29
) and (
31
) both follow from the inequality
\log(x+1)\leq\log(x)+1
for all
x\geq 1
and (
30
) follows from the upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\log K\right]
in Lemma
2
, (
14
). In the first term of (
28
), because
0<t<1/2
by assumption, we can apply the upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[K^{\alpha}\right]
in Lemma
2
, (
15
) to get that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2t}\log(\mathbb{E}[K^{2t}])

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2t}\log( 2^{2tD_{2t+1}(P||Q)} + 2t)

\displaystyle\leq D_{2t+1}(P||Q)+1,

(32)

where (
32
) holds again by
\log(x + a)\leq\log(x)+a
. Combining (
31
) and (
32
), and noting
2t+1=\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}
, we obtain

\displaystyle L(t)\leq D_{\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+c_{2}(\epsilon),

with
c_{2}(\epsilon)=2+\epsilon+\log(\frac{\ln 2}{\epsilon} + \frac{3}{2})
.
∎

We will refer to the upper bound in Theorem
3
as
\text{UB}_{2}^{\alpha}\coloneqq D_{\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}}(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+c_{2}(\epsilon)
. As
\alpha\to 1
,
\text{UB}_{1}^{\alpha}
reduces to
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\leq D(P||Q)+(1+\epsilon)\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+c_{2}(\epsilon)
for any

, the same upper bound as in
[
3
]
. Note that
\text{UB}_{2}^{\alpha}
is valid only for
2/3<\alpha<1
, whereas
\text{UB}_{1}^{\alpha}
is valid for all
0<\alpha<1
. Moreover, for all
2/3<\alpha<1
,
\text{UB}_{1}^{\alpha}<\text{UB}_{2}^{\alpha}
. To see why, observe that for
\epsilon<1
,
\frac{1+\epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}<\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}
, i.e., the order of the leading Rényi divergence term is strictly less in

. Since

is nondecreasing in

and is strictly increasing unless

for some event

(see, e.g.,
[

) one has

for general

and

. For such distributions,

for

small enough. As

for all

,

is better even for small

. Numerical results, shown in Section

, illustrate this conclusion.

are bounds on the Campbell cost, we can use Proposition

Corollary 1
.

Let
P,Q
be probability distributions on a Polish space
\mathcal{U}
with
P\sim Q
. Suppose that both sender and receiver have access to a shared sequence
\quantity{U_i}_{i\geq 1}
i.i.d. according to
Q
. Let
K
be the output of any exact sampling algorithm, i.e.,
U_{K}\sim P
. For any
0<\alpha<1
,
H_{\alpha}(K)>\max\quantity{\text{LB}_1^\alpha, \text{LB}_2^\alpha}-1
, with
\text{LB}_{1}^{\alpha}
and
\text{LB}_{2}^{\alpha}
given in Theorem
1
. Moreover, for
K
the output of the Poisson functional representation, for any
0<\alpha<1
and
\epsilon>0
we have that
H_{\alpha}(K)\leq\text{UB}_{1}^{\alpha}
, with
\text{UB}_{1}^{\alpha}
given in Theorem
2
.

Proof.

The upper bound is immediate after substitution in (
4
) in Proposition
1
. The lower bound follows from (
5
) in Proposition

, specifically that for any sampling algorithm outputting

, there exists an encoding of

such that

. Then, the lower bound

.
∎

In the problem of one-shot exact channel simulation used to motivate this paper, we have the following corollary to Theorem
2
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
X
and
Y
by arbitrary random variables with alphabets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively. Suppose that
X
and
Y
have joint distribution
\text{P}_{XY}
, marginal distributions
\text{P}_{X}
,
\text{P}_{Y}
, and conditional distribution
\text{P}_{Y\mid X}(\,\cdot\,|X)
such that
P_{Y\mid X}(\,\cdot\,|x)\ll P_{Y}
for
P_{X}
-almost all
x
. The sender is provided with
x\in\mathcal{X}
generated according to
P_{X}
and wishes to transmit to the receiver so that they can generate
y\in\mathcal{Y}
with exact distribution
P_{Y\mid X}(\,\cdot\,|x)
. We assume that both sender and receiver have access to a shared sequence
\quantity{U_i}_{i\geq 1}
i.i.d. according to
\text{P}_{Y}
and let
K
be the output of the Poisson functional representation. Then, for any
0<\alpha<1
and
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[H_{\alpha}(K)\right]\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[D_{\frac{1+\epsilon(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}}\big(P_{Y\mid X}(Y|X)||P_{Y}\big)\right]+c_{1}(\alpha,\epsilon),

(33)

with
c_{1}(\alpha,\epsilon)
as in Theorem
2
.

As
\alpha\rightarrow 1
in (
33
), the leading term becomes
\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[D(P_{Y\mid X}(Y|X)||P_{Y})\right]=I(X;Y)
, Shannon’s mutual information.
\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[D_{\alpha}(P_{Y\mid X}(Y|X)||P_{Y})\right]
is not a commonly defined
\alpha
-mutual information
[
35
]
, although it does satisfy the key requirements of nonnegativity (by the nonnegativity of the Rényi divergence
[
16
]
) and recovers the Shannon mutual information as

. Corollary
2
gives a functional characterization for
\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[D_{\alpha}(P_{Y\mid X}(Y|X)||P_{Y})\right]
that, to the best of our knowledge, other
\alpha
-mutual informations lack.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have generalized bounds on the expected communication cost of one-shot exact sampling to the Campbell cost and Rényi’s entropy. Such bounds on the exponential cost are useful in situations where one wishes to disproportionally penalize long codewords, such as applications with buffer overflow. We have shown that any sampling algorithm will have Campbell cost that grows approximately as
D_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(P||Q)
and used the Poisson functional representation to prove upper bounds that are numerically within 5-10 bits. One of the upper bounds, called
\text{UB}_{2}^{\alpha}
, recovers the bound on the average codelength in
[
3
]
as
\alpha\rightarrow 1
. There are several interesting directions of future work stemming from these results. Most obviously, it is an open question if
\text{UB}_{1}^{\alpha}
can be tightened to be in the spirit of
[
1
]
, specifically so that it reduces to
\mathbb{E}[l(\mathcal{C}(K))]\leq D(P||Q)+\log(D(P||Q) + 1)+O(1)
as
\alpha\to 1
.
More generally, the strong functional representation lemma has been applied to several problems outside of one-shot exact channel simulation, most notably one-shot variable-length lossy source coding and multiple description coding
[
1
]
. Extending the strong functional representation lemma to the Campbell cost (or Rényi entropy) could have similarly broad applications.
