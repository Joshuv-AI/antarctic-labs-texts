Effect of regular treadmill exercise on astrocyte proliferation-related proteins in the motor cortex and hippocampus after traumatic brain injury
Authors: Woo-Hyeok Yang, Dong-Eun Kim, Tae-Beom Seo, Young-Pyo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study was to investigate whether regular treadmill exercise after traumatic brain injury (TBI) positively affects the regenerative environment in the motor cortex and hippocampus of the brain. Subjects were randomly divided into three groups: the normal control (Norm, n=8), the TBI+sedentary group (TBI+SED, n=8), and the TBI+ treadmill exercise group (TBI+Ex, n=8). Following a 2-week post-TBI recovery period, treadmill exercise was performed regularly for 4 weeks. The results showed that regular walking exercise after TBI increased the expression of growth associated protein-43 and tropomyosin receptor kinase-B, which are associated with neuronal survival, in both the motor cortex and hippocampus. The TBI+SED exhibited elevated glial fibrillary acidic protein and phosphorylated mammalian target of rapamycin signaling in both the motor cortex and hippocampus of the brain. In contrast, phosphorylated phosphatase and tensin homolog (p-PTEN) expression showed region-specific patterns: in the motor cortex, p-PTEN levels were higher in the TBI+SED than the Norm group and further increased following treadmill exercise, whereas in the hippocampus, p-PTEN expression decreased after TBI but was significantly elevated in the TBI+Ex group. These findings indicated that regular exercise after TBI might reduce glial scar formation and promote neural regeneration by elevating PTEN expression and inhibiting the mammalian target of rapamycin signaling pathway.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI), resulting from external impacts such as traffic accidents, falls, or sports activities, is a major cause of neurological damage worldwide (Hyder et al., 2007). In the acute phase, neuronal death, vascular injury, and blood-brain barrier disruption occur, followed by chronic neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and scar formation that contribute to long-term cognitive and motor deficits (Chodobski et al., 2011; Simon et al., 2017). The motor cortex and hippocampus are particularly vulnerable to these pathological changes (Kempermann, 2022).

In the motor cortex and hippocampus of the injured brain, it has been known that growth associated protein-43 (GAP-43) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), tropomyosin receptor kinase-B (TrkB) signaling pathway are related to neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity, and that proliferation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive astrocytes increases reactive astrogliosis around the injury site (Aungst et al., 2013; Cieri and Ramos, 2025). Moreover, TBI has been reported to induce activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway in the motor cortex and hippocampus of injured brain; however, excessive mTOR activation paradoxically increases GFAP expression and reactive astrogliosis, ultimately suppressing neurogenesis in the hippocampus (Fan et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2022; Movahedpour et al., 2022). In contrast, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) has been shown to reduce mTOR signaling in the nervous system, thereby modulating neuronal survival and plasticity in the motor cortex and hippocampus of the brain (Shi et al., 2025). Thus, these alterations into the brain after TBI can ultimately lead to cognitive and motor dysfunction (Agrawal et al., 2015; Gao and Chen, 2009; Kim et al., 2024).

Current treatments for TBI mainly involve pharmacological therapy and surgical intervention; however, these approaches have been reported to impose a significant economic burden due to long-term treatment costs (van Dijck et al., 2020). Consequently, the therapeutic role of exercise has recently attracted attention as an economical and sustainable non-pharmacological intervention.

Regular physical activity has been shown to enhance cerebral blood flow, reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, and support neuronal survival in the brain (Sutkowy et al., 2021). Among various exercise types, treadmill-based aerobic training has been reported to facilitate hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity after TBI, accompanied by increased expression of neuroplasticity-related proteins such as BDNF and TrkB (Kim et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). In addition, exercise appears to regulate astrocyte reactivity in the injured central nervous system, thereby improving the neural microenvironment for regeneration (Kim et al., 2022). Furthermore, regulation of key molecules including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), 3-phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1), protein kinase B (Akt), mTOR, PTEN by exercise may contribute to reduced glial scar formation and enhanced neural recovery after brain injury (Chen et al., 2016).

However, most previous studies have primarily investigated the effects of exercise on hippocampal neurogenesis in healthy brains or have been limited to examining only the expression of specific nerve growth factors. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to confirm whether regular treadmill exercise after TBI would have a positive effect on regenerative environment of the motor cortex and hippocampus of the brain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

Sprague-Dawley male rats (4 weeks old) were housed under controlled conditions (23°C±1°C, 60% humidity) with a 12/12-hr light/dark cycle. Food (Commercial chow, Samyang Co., Korea) and water were provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jeju National University (protocol number: 2021-0053). Total sample size was calculated in G*Power software version 3.1.7 and considering an effect size=0.7, power-0.8 and α=0.05. Animals were randomly assigned to three groups using a computer-generated random number sequence: the normal control (Norm, n=8), the TBI+sedentary group (TBI+SED, n=8), and the TBI+treadmill exercise group (TBI+Ex, n=8). Throughout the experimental period, all animals were monitored daily for general health and behavior. No animals died or were excluded from the study, and therefore no missing data handling procedures were required. All animals were sacrificed 24 hr after the completion of exercise by CO2 inhalation, and brain tissues were extracted, which were stored at −80°C until further use.

Traumatic brain injury

For TBI, all animals were anesthetized with isoflurane using an inhalation anesthesia apparatus (Jeundo Bio and Plant, Korea), and the concentration of isoflurane was maintained at 1.5%–2.5%. When the animals showed no response (absence of corneal and paw reflexes), their heads were shaved and fixed in a digital stereotaxic frame (Benchmark Deluxe, MyNeurolab, USA). A circular craniotomy performed using a Dremel motor tool equipped with a specially designed drill bit to avoid damage to the meninges and cortex (2.4 mm lateral to the midline, 4.2 mm posterior to the coronal suture). Cortical contusion was induced with an electromagnetic impactor device (Impact One, Stereotaxic Impactor, MyNeurolab) using a sterilized stainless-steel impactor tip operated at a velocity of 5.00 m/sec. The impactor tip was positioned on the cortical surface and displaced the cortex to a depth of approximately 2.5 mm. Following contusion, bleeding controlled with a sterile sponge soaked in cold saline, and the incision site was sutured with nylon thread.

Exercise program

All rats in the TBI+Ex group took a rest for 2 weeks after TBI surgery, and treadmill walking exercise was administered 5 times per week for 4 weeks. Following the protocol of Jung et al. (2016), exercise was performed on a treadmill (CAST-10R, CASSCALE, Korea) set at 0° inclination and a speed of 6 m/min. The regimen consisted of four repetitions of 3-min walking during the first week and six repetitions of 5-min walking from the second to the fourth week. The rest interval between each session was set at 5 min.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

The injured brain tissues were embedded in frozen section compound (Leica Biosystems Richmond, Inc., USA) and sectioned at 30-μm thickness using a cryostat (Leica CM1860, Leica, Germany). The coronal sections were mounted onto slides and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) staining. The slides were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 30 sec, rinsed with running water to clear the background, and counterstained with eosin for 30 sec, followed by a second rinse. Dehydration was performed by sequential immersion in 95% ethanol, 100% ethanol, and xylene, each twice. Finally, the sections were coverslipped and observed under a light microscope (E-600, Nikon, Japan).

Immunohistochemical analysis

The sections were mounted onto positively charged slides (Fisher Scientific, USA). For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were fixed at room temperature for 40 min in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 4% paraformaldehyde and 4% sucrose. The sections were then permeabilized with 0.5% Nonidet P-40 and blocked in 2.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 hr. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst dye 33258 (bis- benzimide, Sigma-Aldrich, 2.5 μg/mL) for 10 min prior to the final wash. After incubation at room temperature for 1 hr, the sections were coverslipped with gelatin mounting medium. Stained samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon E-600, Nikon, Japan), and images were captured with a digital camera and analyzed using Adobe Photoshop software (version CS6; Adobe, USA).

Western blot analysis

The extracted motor cortex and hippocampus were rinsed with PBS and homogenized in Triton lysis buffer. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes for 2 hr. The membranes were blocked at room temperature for 1 hr in 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) in tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C for 24 hr. Primary antibodies included anti-GAP-43 (mouse monoclonal, 1:1,000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-TrkB (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1,000; Cell Signaling Biotechnology, USA), anti-GFAP (mouse monoclonal, 1:1,000; Cell Signaling), anti-phospho-mTOR (p-mTOR, mouse monoclonal, 1:1,000; Santa Cruz), anti-phospho-PDK1 (p-PDK1, rabbit polyclonal, 1:1,000; Cell Signaling), anti-PI3K (mouse monoclonal, 1:200; Santa Cruz), anti-phospho-PTEN (p-PTEN, rabbit polyclonal, 1:1,000; Cell Signaling) and β-actin (mouse monoclonal, 1:2,000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG, 1:5,000; GeneTex, USA) at room temperature for 1 hr. Protein bands were detected using Westar ECL reagent (Cyanagen, Italy) and imaged with a Chemidoc system (Bio-Rad, USA) for subsequent analysis. Protein band intensity was quantified. Background subtraction was applied, and each protein signal was normalized to β-actin. All densitometric analysis was performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as the mean±standard error of the mean. Differences among groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance, followed by Duncan multiple range test. Statistical significance was accepted at P<0.05. Data handling and figure generation were performed using Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, USA).

RESULTS

Histological changes in motor cortex and hippocampus after TBI

As shown in Fig. 1, the motor cortex and hippocampus were examined by H&E staining at 2 weeks after TBI. In the motor cortex, neuronal damage was observed in both the TBI+SED and TBI+Ex groups, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 1A), indicating that the extent of cortical injury was comparable prior to the exercise intervention. No structural damage to the hippocampus was observed in either group before the start of exercise following TBI (Fig. 1B).

Changes of GAP-43 and TrkB in the motor cortex and hippocampus after TBI

Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate GAP-43 and TrkB protein expression in the motor cortex and hippocampus following TBI (Fig. 2). In the motor cortex (Fig. 2A), GAP-43 expression was significantly decreased after TBI (P<0.001), but the TBI+Ex group showed a significant increase of GAP-43 induction level compared to the TBI+SED (P<0.05). Expression levels of TrkB in the motor cortex were significantly elevated after TBI+SED and TBI+Ex (respectively, P<0.01) group compared with Norm group. In the hippocampus, GAP-43 showed a similar trend observed in the motor cortex, and TrkB was significantly increased in the TBI+Ex group (P<0.05) compared to the TBI+ SED (Fig. 2B).

Changes in reactive astrocyte-related proteins in the motor cortex and hippocampus after TBI

In the motor cortex (Fig. 3), expression levels of GFAP (P<0.01) and p-mTOR (P<0.001) were significantly elevated following TBI, whereas treadmill exercise significantly reduced levels of GFAP (P<0.01) and p-mTOR (P<0.05) compared to the TBI+SED. Similarly, the expression of p-PDK1 was significantly decreased in the TBI+Ex group than those in the TBI+SED (P<0.05). In contrast, p-PTEN expression was increased after TBI+SED and exhibited an additional elevation following exercise. In the hippocampus (Fig. 4), GFAP expression was significantly elevated after TBI+SED (P<0.001) but significantly decreased in the TBI+Ex group (P< 0.01). p-mTOR expression was also markedly increased after TBI+ SED (P<0.001) and decreased following exercise (P<0.001). PI3K expression increased significantly after TBI+SED (P<0.01) and was significantly reduced after exercise intervention (P<0.05). While p-PTEN expression did not change after TBI+SED, it was significantly upregulated in response to exercise (P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to investigate the effects of regular treadmill exercise on neuroregeneration-and reactive astrocytes-related protein signaling pathway in the motor cortex and hippocampus after TBI. To this end, treadmill exercise was performed for 4 weeks following TBI, and cell lysate from the extracted tissues were analyzed through biochemical analyses.

To assess the extent of damage in the motor cortex and hippocampus following TBI, H&E and immunostaining were performed. The results showed that both the TBI+SED and TBI+Ex groups exhibited similar cortical cavities in the brain of animals with TBI. Additionally, Hoechst staining confirmed that no direct damage occurred in the hippocampus, suggesting that TBI did not cause substantial hippocampal injury and that subsequent assessments of hippocampal variables could be conducted under equivalent conditions.

GAP-43 serves as a marker of neural regeneration and can be upregulated by BDNF activation. TrkB, the principal receptor of BDNF, regulates synaptic plasticity, neuronal excitability, and cognitive function in the central nervous system (Lee et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024; Nagappan and Lu, 2005). In the present study, GAP-43 expression was decreased while TrkB expression was increased in both the motor cortex and hippocampus following TBI. Notably, no significant change in TrkB expression was observed in the motor cortex of the TRE group, which may be attributed to the limited neurogenesis in this region of the adult brain (Kornack and Rakic, 2001). Although it remains unclear whether the GAP-43 observed in the TBI+Ex group was directly induced by exercise, the elevated levels of GAP-43 and TrkB in the motor cortex can be interpreted as positive indicators of potential neurogenesis signaling activation.

In contrast, TrkB expression in the hippocampus further increased in the TBI+Ex group compared to the TBI+SED, suggesting a possible association between exercise and neural regeneration in this region. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, which observed that exercise did not alter TrkB expression in the motor cortex (Chen et al., 2017), but enhanced TrkB expression and neuroregeneration markers in the hippocampus (Liu et al., 2008). Consistent with these findings, the present study indicates that regular exercise may be potential as an effective intervention strategy to promote neuroregenerative signaling and environment after TBI.

GFAP is a specific marker of reactive astrocytes in the injured central nervous system, and its upregulation is associated with the induction of glial scar formation (Middeldorp and Hol, 2011). PI3K activates PDK1, which in turn phosphorylates Akt, leading to activation of the mTOR signaling pathway (Bamodu et al., 2020; King et al., 2015). This pathway has been reported to activate protein synthesis in the skeletal muscle, but to induce the inhibition of neurogenesis through glial scar formation in the injured brain (Codeluppi et al., 2009). Moreover, p-PTEN suppresses Akt activation, thereby attenuating mTOR signaling and reducing glial scar formation in the brain of animals with TBI (Nikolaeva et al., 2016). In this study, the TBI+SED increased the activation of the mTOR signaling pathway in both the motor cortex and hippocampus after TBI, but the TBI+Ex group showed a significant downregulation of this pathway and upregulation of p-PTEN expression in the same region. These results suggest that regular exercise may be associated with reduced hyperactivation of the mTOR pathway and glial scar formation following TBI, which may in turn promote neural regenerative processes. Previous studies on central nerve system (CNS) reported that p-PTEN could inhibit Akt signaling in specific regions of the CNS (Chen et al., 2016; Nikolaeva et al., 2016) and regular exercise could suppress activation of this signaling, thereby attenuating mTOR signaling (Chen et al., 2020). Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that exercise might reduce GFAP expression and mTOR pathway activation in the motor cortex and hippocampus after TBI through activation of p-PTEN, leading to the alleviation of glial scar formation (Ding and Chen, 2022; Middeldorp and Hol, 2011).

Taken together, our findings suggest that regular exercise may be associated with attenuation of glial scar formation and promotion of neural regeneration in the brain of animals with TBI. However, several limitations should be considered. First, this study was conducted in a limited sample of animal models, which may affect generalizability. Second, while biochemical analyses provided insight into exercise-associated signaling changes, additional mechanistic studies are required to further clarify causal relationships. Third, exercise intensity, duration, and timing were specific to this study and may not directly translate to clinical rehabilitation. Future studies should systematically explore exercise protocols to better understand the mechanistic link between exercise and neuroregeneration after TBI.
