The diagnostic contribution of bronchoalveolar lavage to lung cryo-transbronchial biopsy in interstitial lung diseases
Authors: Umut İlhan, Baris Demirkol, Demet Turan, Efsun Ugur Chousein, Ramazan Eren, Saniye Koç Ada, Aytül Hande Yardımcı, Mustafa Çörtük, Erdogan Cetinkaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), achieving a definitive diagnosis based on clinical and radiological findings alone can be challenging, often necessitating lung biopsy. Considering the high risk of complications associated with surgical biopsy, transbronchial cryobiopsy (c-TBB) has emerged as a less invasive alternative. However, due to its more limited histopathological yield compared to surgical biopsy, additional diagnostic methods may be required. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic contribution of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) performed simultaneously with c-TBB.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary training and research hospital between November 2023 and June 2024. A total of 127 patients with a preliminary diagnosis of ILD who underwent simultaneous diagnostic BAL and c-TBB were included. Patients’ clinical and radiological features, BAL and c-TBB findings, and any complications were recorded. Final diagnoses for all cases were established through multidisciplinary discussion (MDD).ResultsOf the included patients, 51.2% (n = 65) were male, with a mean age of 60 years (range: 19–79). Histopathological diagnosis was achieved with c-TBB in 76.4% of cases (n = 97), with the most common diagnoses being fibrotic NSIP (31.5%, n = 40) and non-fibrotic NSIP (12.6%, n = 16). Among 30 patients without a definitive diagnosis by c-TBB, 22 received a diagnosis through MDD. The contribution rate of BAL in patients with a histopathological diagnosis was 30.9% (n = 30), and 46.6% (n = 14) in those without a histopathological diagnosis. Overall, BAL contributed to the multidisciplinary diagnosis in 34.6% of all patients (n = 44), with a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.04); the highest contribution was observed in the non-fibrotic HP group (p = 0.01). In cases without a histopathological diagnosis, BAL provided the highest contribution in the fibrotic HP group. No adverse events related to BAL were observed, while complications occurred in 42.5% of patients undergoing c-TBB. The most common complications were hemorrhage (mostly mild to moderate), pneumothorax (17.3%, with tube thoracostomy performed in 11.8% of all cases), and mild respiratory failure (6.3%), with no life-threatening complications recorded.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that the combined use of BAL and c-TBB provides significant added value to the multidisciplinary diagnostic process in ILDs. Performing simultaneous BAL in patients undergoing c-TBB can be considered a safe and effective approach that enhances diagnostic yield in the multidisciplinary evaluation of ILDs.

Introduction

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), also known as diffuse parenchymal lung diseases (DPLDs), are a heterogeneous group comprising over 200 disorders characterized by inflammatory and fibrotic processes affecting the lung interstitium. While ILDs primarily involve the interstitial space, they can progressively affect the peripheral airways and vascular structures as well [1]. These rare diseases have been reported with an incidence of 74.3 per 100,000 people in the United States, 76 in Europe, and 25.8 in Turkey [2].

ILDs are classified into four main categories: interstitial lung diseases with known causes, idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs), granulomatous interstitial lung diseases, and other interstitial lung diseases [3]. Within these subgroups, interstitial lung diseases with known causes include drug-induced lung diseases and pulmonary involvement of rheumatologic diseases; among IIPs, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) are prominent; among granulomatous interstitial lung diseases, sarcoidosis is significant; and within other interstitial lung diseases, pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH) occupies an important place [3, 4].

The presence of diverse etiologies and numerous subtypes in ILDs leads to variability in treatment options and prognosis across different disease groups. Therefore, establishing a reliable diagnosis in ILD is critical for effective disease management. It is recommended that a specific diagnosis be reached through a multidisciplinary discussion integrating clinical features, radiologic findings, and histopathologic data. However, it has been reported that a definitive diagnosis often requires a surgical lung biopsy, which remains the gold standard in the diagnostic process. Nevertheless, surgical lung biopsy carries risks such as infection, prolonged air leaks, extended hospitalization, and increased mortality. Considering these risks, transbronchial cryobiopsy (c-TBB) has emerged as a novel, minimally invasive method for obtaining lung tissue in ILD diagnosis. c-TBB has been demonstrated to be a safe sampling technique, with diagnostic yields varying between 50% and 90% when evaluated within a multidisciplinary framework. In patients with multiple comorbidities and high perioperative risk, cryobiopsy has been suggested as the preferred diagnostic option given the potential complications of surgical lung biopsy in this group [5].

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is a minimally invasive procedure performed via flexible bronchoscopy to obtain alveolar cell samples. Through cell counts, cytological analysis, and cultures of the obtained material, BAL provides valuable insights into immunologic, inflammatory, neoplastic, and infectious processes at the alveolar level. While BAL does not serve as a direct diagnostic tool for ILD, it plays a guiding role in differential diagnosis and, in some cases, can contribute directly to the definitive diagnosis of specific ILDs [6].

Given that c-TBB alone may not achieve the same level of histopathologic diagnostic accuracy as surgical biopsy, and that BAL alone provides limited diagnostic precision, it is hypothesized that the combined or complementary use of these two methods could enhance diagnostic accuracy and further reduce the need for surgical biopsy.

Despite the increasing use of c-TBB in the diagnostic workup of ILDs, there is a lack of comprehensive studies evaluating the added diagnostic value of combining BAL with c-TBB. Most existing research focuses on these methods individually, and few studies have systematically explored whether performing BAL concurrently with c-TBB improves diagnostic accuracy or alters clinical decision-making in ILD patients. Furthermore, current guidelines do not provide clear recommendations regarding the routine use of BAL in conjunction with c-TBB, leading to significant variability in clinical practice. Addressing this gap is crucial because a combined approach could potentially optimize diagnostic yield, minimize the need for invasive surgical biopsies, and enable earlier initiation of appropriate therapy, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic contribution of performing BAL simultaneously with c-TBB in patients with a preliminary diagnosis of ILD, and to assess whether this combined approach enhances diagnostic accuracy and reduces the need for surgical lung biopsy.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2023 and June 2024 at the University of Health Sciences Yedikule Chest Diseases and Thoracic Surgery Training and Research Hospital. Our center is one of Turkey’s leading referral hospitals for interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) and interventional pulmonology cases. Ethical approval was obtained from our hospital’s Ethics Committee, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1989) (Approval Date: 09.11.2023, Decision No: 2023 − 411).

Study population

A total of 141 patients who underwent diagnostic BAL and simultaneous c-TBB with a preliminary diagnosis of ILD between November 2014 and September 2023, and who received a final diagnosis of ILD following multidisciplinary discussion, were retrospectively reviewed. Patients diagnosed with malignancy (n = 4) and those with inadequate BAL samples (n = 10) were excluded. Ultimately, 127 eligible patients were included in the study.

Data collection

Collected data included demographic characteristics, smoking history, antigen exposure, pulmonary function test results, carbon monoxide diffusing capacity (DLCO), radiological findings, c-TBB complications, BAL findings, and histopathological results.

Definitions

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)

The BAL protocol, including pre-procedural preparations and the lavage procedure itself, was performed according to the American Thoracic Society (ATS) clinical practice guidelines [7]. Radiologic involvement was a key factor in determining the site for BAL: in cases of diffuse radiological involvement, BAL was performed in the middle lobe or lingula, while in localized disease, it was performed in the affected segment. During the procedure, the entrance to the target segment was sealed using the bronchoscope, and room-temperature 0.9% NaCl solution was instilled in 20 mL aliquots, with slow aspiration to prevent airway collapse. For optimal sampling, at least 100 mL (up to 300 mL) of saline was instilled sequentially, with a recommended minimum recovery of 30%. The cellular analysis of BAL fluid was conducted per ATS guidelines, with normal cell distributions defined as alveolar macrophages > 85%, lymphocytes 10–15%, neutrophils ≤ 3%, eosinophils ≤ 1%, and squamous/ciliated columnar epithelial cells ≤ 1% [8].

Transbronchial cryobiopsy (c-TBB)

All c-TBB procedures were performed under general anesthesia, guided by rigid bronchoscopy. The target biopsy site was selected as the area with the densest involvement on chest CT. After evaluating the trachea and main bronchi with the rigid bronchoscope, a flexible bronchoscope was advanced through the rigid scope to the target lung segment. A 1.9 mm, 90 cm cryoprobe was used with nitrous oxide (N₂O, − 89 °C) as the cryogen. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the cryoprobe was advanced to the distal part of the target segment, retracted by at least 1 cm from the pleura, and positioned (ERBE, Germany). Freezing was applied for 3–5 s, and the frozen lung tissue was extracted by rapidly withdrawing the flexible bronchoscope with the attached cryoprobe. The specimen was immediately placed in formalin without damage. Biopsies were taken either from a single segment or two different segments within the same lobe. After each biopsy, a Fogarty balloon was inflated to control bleeding and then slowly deflated. Once hemostasis was confirmed, the procedure was completed. Two hours post-procedure, a chest X-ray was performed to check for pneumothorax. For post-procedural pneumothorax, asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients with an apical pleural-to-chest wall distance < 2 cm on chest X-ray were monitored with supplemental nasal oxygen. Tube thoracostomy was performed if the distance exceeded 2 cm, if symptoms worsened despite a distance < 2 cm, or if severe symptoms occurred regardless of distance [10, 11].

Patients who were breathing room air pre-procedure but developed oxygen requirements post-procedure were classified as having minimal oxygen requirement if they exhibited non-hypercapnic hypoxemic respiratory failure (pO₂ <55 mmHg) and required 1–6 L/min of nasal oxygen. Patients needing more than 6 L/min of oxygen were considered to require advanced respiratory support [12]. Specimen adequacy was defined as obtaining at least two tissue fragments ≥ 5 mm in diameter with well-preserved alveolated parenchyma and minimal crush artifact.

Multidisciplinary diagnosis

Patients were evaluated by a multidisciplinary team including specialists in pulmonology, radiology, pathology, rheumatology, and occupational/environmental medicine, with final diagnoses established based on the team’s consensus. The MDD meetings were conducted weekly and included two pulmonologists, one thoracic radiologist, and one pulmonary pathologist, who reviewed each case collaboratively.

The diagnostic contribution of BAL within the multidisciplinary discussion (MDD) was defined as any change in the working diagnosis, modification in diagnostic confidence, or confirmation of the pre-test impression, as independently evaluated by two ILD pulmonologists, one thoracic radiologist, and one pulmonary pathologist using a structured two-step approach (pre-BAL clinical–radiologic assessment followed by reassessment after incorporation of BAL results).

According to the 2020 ATS/JRS/ALAT guideline on hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), diagnostic criteria include identifying antigen exposure, typical high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) findings, lymphocytosis in BAL, and histopathologic confirmation. The guideline classifies HRCT findings as typical, compatible, or indeterminate. In cases with typical or compatible HRCT and/or histopathologic findings of HP, a definitive diagnosis can be made regardless of antigen exposure or BAL lymphocytosis. However, for cases with possible or indeterminate HRCT, antigen exposure and BAL lymphocytosis contribute significantly to the final diagnosis. The guideline also recommends BAL in all ILD patients with suspected fibrotic or non-fibrotic HP. A BAL lymphocyte threshold of ≥ 30% is considered diagnostically meaningful [13]. In our study, patients with histopathologically confirmed HP were classified as definite HP; patients with NSIP histopathology, antigen exposure, and BAL lymphocytosis ≥ 30% were diagnosed as HP after multidisciplinary review; and patients without histopathology but with typical HRCT findings, antigen exposure, and BAL lymphocytosis ≥ 30% were also diagnosed with HP by the multidisciplinary team.

For sarcoidosis, the ATS BAL clinical practice guideline suggests that BAL lymphocytosis ≥ 25% should prompt consideration of sarcoidosis in the differential diagnosis, and a CD4+/CD8 + ratio > 4 is highly specific for sarcoidosis [8]. The updated 2020 ATS sarcoidosis guideline also emphasizes the diagnostic utility of BAL lymphocytosis and/or elevated CD4/CD8 ratio [14]. In our study, patients with histopathologic reports of non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, BAL lymphocytosis ≥ 25%, and CD4/CD8 ratio > 4 were diagnosed with sarcoidosis following multidisciplinary evaluation.

In pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH), the presence of > 5% CD1a-positive dendritic cells in BAL fluid, along with compatible clinical features, strongly supports the diagnosis [15]. In our study, PLCH was diagnosed in patients without histopathologic confirmation but with > 5% CD1a-positive cells in BAL and appropriate clinical findings.

According to the 2022 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guideline for IPF/PPF, in the absence of typical HRCT or histopathologic features, specific criteria should be used for IPF diagnosis, including moderate-to-severe traction bronchiectasis/bronchiolectasis in men > 50 or women > 60 years, widespread reticulation (> 30%) on HRCT in patients > 70 years, and absence of BAL neutrophilia or lymphocytosis [13]. Our study applied these criteria in multidisciplinary discussions to establish IPF diagnoses.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM® SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used: quantitative variables were characterized by mean, maximum, and minimum values, and qualitative variables were presented as percentages. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test assessed normality of distributions. Normally distributed variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) and compared between groups using the Student’s t-test when applicable. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were recorded as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test if expected cell counts were ≤ 5. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 127 patients included in our study, 51.2% (n = 65) were male, with a mean age of 60 years (range: 19–79 years). A smoking history was present in 51.2% (n = 65) of patients. Comorbidities were identified in 50.3% (n = 64) of cases, with hypertension (HT) being the most common comorbidity (n = 26, 20.5%), followed by diabetes mellitus (DM) (n = 22, 17.3%). A significant antigen exposure relevant to hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) was observed in 34.6% (n = 44) of patients.

Regarding pulmonary function tests, the mean forced vital capacity (FVC) was 75.6% of the predicted value (range: 38–105%), while the mean forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV₁) was 79.3% of the predicted value (range: 30–133%). The mean DLCO was 59.0% of the predicted value (range: 20–108%).

Histopathological diagnosis was achieved by c-TBB in 76.4% of patients (97/127; 95% CI 68.3–82.9) through c-TBB. Analysis of histopathological diagnoses revealed that the most common findings were fibrotic NSIP (n = 40, 31.5%) and non-fibrotic NSIP (n = 16, 12.6%). Additionally, non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation was identified in 13 patients (10.2%), fibrotic HP in 9 patients (7.1%), organizing interstitial pneumonia (OIP) in 4 patients (3.1%), non-fibrotic HP in 4 patients (3.1%), organizing pneumonia in 3 patients (2.4%), and pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH) in 2 patients (1.6%). Other less frequent diagnoses included respiratory bronchiolitis (n = 1, 0.8%), lung involvement of connective tissue disease (n = 1, 0.8%), unclassifiable interstitial lung disease (n = 1, 0.8%), acute lung injury (n = 1, 0.8%), pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis (n = 1, 0.8%), and respiratory bronchiolitis-associated ILD (RB-ILD) (n = 1, 0.8%).

In 30 patients who did not achieve a definitive histopathological diagnosis by c-TBB, a final diagnosis was established through multidisciplinary discussion (MDD). Among these, 22 patients were diagnosed with fibrotic HP, 4 with IPF, and one patient each with non-fibrotic HP, iNSIP, PLCH, and organizing pneumonia. The main reasons for nondiagnostic c-TBB included sampling error, airway-predominant tissue, fibrosis-only parenchyma, and substantial crush artifact.

Among the 97 patients with a histopathological diagnosis established by c-TBB, a final diagnosis was reached in 30 cases through MDD supported by BAL findings. Specifically, of the patients histologically diagnosed with fibrotic NSIP, 12 were reclassified as fibrotic HP following MDD with BAL contribution. Among those with a histopathological diagnosis of non-fibrotic NSIP, 4 patients were ultimately diagnosed with non-fibrotic HP based on BAL findings and MDD. In patients with histologically identified non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, 5 were diagnosed with sarcoidosis after incorporating BAL findings into MDD. Additionally, in patients with histopathological findings suggestive of non-fibrotic HP (indeterminate/probable), 4 were confirmed to have non-fibrotic HP through BAL-assisted MDD, and among those with fibrotic HP (indeterminate/probable), 4 patients were definitively diagnosed with fibrotic HP. One patient initially diagnosed histologically with OIP was ultimately classified as fibrotic HP after BAL and MDD evaluation.

Among the 30 patients who did not receive a definitive histopathological diagnosis from c-TBB, a final diagnosis was reached in 22 cases (22/30) through multidisciplinary discussion. Of these, the most common diagnoses were fibrotic HP (n = 10), followed by iNSIP (n = 4), non-fibrotic HP (n = 3), PLCH (n = 3), and IPF (n = 2). Among patients with a histopathological diagnosis from c-TBB, the contribution of BAL to the final diagnosis was 30.9% (30/97; 95% CI 22.6–40.7), whereas among those without a histopathological diagnosis, the contribution rate was 46.6% (14/30; 95% CI 30.2–63.9). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.113; odds ratio 0.512, 95% CI 0.222–1.181). When stratified by diagnostic category, the diagnostic contribution of BAL appeared more prominent in non-fibrotic HP, whereas in non-HP interstitial lung diseases it mainly played a supportive role by reinforcing the pre-test clinical impression.

Overall, BAL contributed to the final multidisciplinary diagnosis in 34.6% (n = 44) of all patients (n = 127). Among the patients in whom BAL contributed diagnostically, HP was the most common final diagnosis. Specifically, of the HP cases, 27 (38.5%) were fibrotic HP, and 9 (64.2%) were non-fibrotic HP. Additionally, BAL findings contributed to the diagnosis of sarcoidosis in 5 patients, and to the diagnosis of iNSIP, PLCH, and IPF in one patient each. The highest diagnostic contribution rates of BAL were observed in non-fibrotic HP, fibrotic HP, and sarcoidosis (64.2%, 38.5%, and 38.5%, respectively). Among diagnoses with more than five cases, BAL had the lowest diagnostic contribution rates in iNSIP and IPF (9.1% and 16.7%, respectively). Discussion

Our study found that transbronchial c-TBB achieved a histopathological diagnosis rate of 76.4%. BAL contributed to the final multidisciplinary diagnosis in 34.6% of cases overall, including 46.6% of cases where c-TBB alone was inconclusive. BAL provided its highest diagnostic value in non-fibrotic HP, while in cases where histopathological diagnosis could not be reached with c-TBB, BAL findings significantly contributed to diagnosing fibrotic HP. Overall, BAL played a pivotal role in reaching the final diagnosis, especially in HP, NSIP, sarcoidosis, PLCH, and IPF. Regarding complications, c-TBB was associated with at least one complication in 42.5% of patients, with hemorrhage (33.8%) and pneumothorax 17.3% of patients (22/127; 95% CI 11.7–24.8).being the most frequent, though no life-threatening events were observed. No complications related to BAL were reported.

Our study’s c-TBB diagnostic yield of 76.4% is consistent with literature reporting diagnostic yields between 60% and 82% [16], confirming c-TBB as a valuable tool in the histopathological evaluation of ILD. However, we also demonstrated that c-TBB alone was insufficient for a definitive diagnosis in a subset of patients, highlighting the need for additional diagnostic methods such as BAL.

The significant contribution of BAL to the multidisciplinary diagnosis of HP in our cohort mirrors findings from the ATS/JRS/ALAT 2020 Clinical Practice Guideline, which recommends lymphocyte cellular analysis in BAL fluid for suspected HP cases, regardless of whether transbronchial biopsy is performed. This guideline also notes that diagnosis is more readily achieved in patients with typical radiologic findings, antigen exposure history, and BAL lymphocytosis [13]. Supporting this, Patolia et al. analyzed data from 49 studies comparing BAL lymphocyte percentages in HP and IPF, revealing a mean lymphocyte percentage difference of 30.38% in favor of HP, with subgroup differences of 34.21% (non-fibrotic HP vs. IPF) and 20.81% (fibrotic HP vs. IPF) [8]. Sobiecka et al. similarly showed that fibrotic HP patients had significantly higher BAL lymphocytosis rates than IPF patients, with ≥ 20% lymphocytosis seen in 75% of fibrotic HP cases compared to only 10% of IPF cases, and ≥ 30% lymphocytosis in 60% of fibrotic HP patients but none of the IPF cases [17]. Our observation that BAL’s highest diagnostic contribution occurred in non-fibrotic HP, with statistically significant lymphocytosis in this subgroup (p = 0.01), aligns directly with these prior findings. Furthermore, our use of a ≥ 30% BAL lymphocyte threshold in HP diagnosis is consistent with ATS/JRS/ALAT guidelines, which recommend this cutoff as a reasonable and balanced diagnostic criterion [13].

For NSIP, literature indicates BAL lymphocyte percentages typically range from 20% to 40% in idiopathic NSIP (iNSIP) [19], though these levels may overlap with other diseases causing NSIP patterns, including connective tissue diseases and exposure-related conditions. Therefore, as our findings underscore, BAL results should always be interpreted alongside clinical, radiological, and exposure history data to establish an accurate diagnosis. In our study, BAL contributed to reclassifying 28.5% of patients with NSIP pattern on histology to a final multidisciplinary diagnosis of HP, highlighting its diagnostic utility in differentiating NSIP mimics.

In sarcoidosis, our observation of increased BAL lymphocyte percentages (≥ 25%) and elevated CD4/CD8 ratios (> 4) contributing to diagnosis in 38.4% of patients with histologically detected non-caseating granulomas corresponds well with the findings of Wessendorf et al., who reported CD4/CD8 ratios ≥ 3.5 yielding sensitivities of 53–59% and specificities of 93–96% [19], and with BAL guidelines suggesting CD4/CD8 ratios > 4 for improved diagnostic accuracy [20]. Additionally, Winterbauer et al. emphasized that a CD4/CD8 ratio > 4 combined with lymphocytosis ≥ 16% and non-caseating granulomas offers the highest diagnostic specificity for sarcoidosis [21], directly supporting our approach and results.

For pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH), our finding of CD1a positivity > 5% in BAL fluid of an undiagnosed patient aligns with studies indicating that 25–50% of PLCH patients have 5–10% CD1a + cells in BAL, with CD1a > 5% considered a highly specific indicator [22]. This underscores BAL’s value as a non-invasive diagnostic adjunct when histopathology is inconclusive.

In IPF, although guidelines do not recommend routine BAL for diagnosis, they suggest using BAL to help distinguish IPF from HP and other fibrotic interstitial lung diseases when radiologic and clinical findings are equivocal [24]. Previous studies reported BAL neutrophil percentages between 5% and 20% in IPF, with higher neutrophil counts correlating with increased likelihood of IPF [23]. Our case of an IPF patient with inconclusive c-TBB but BAL showing 25% neutrophils and 8% lymphocytes, leading to a final diagnosis in multidisciplinary discussion (MDD), exemplifies BAL’s adjunctive role in selected scenarios.

Comparing our overall BAL contribution rate of 34.6% to prior studies, we observed higher diagnostic contribution than the 22% reported by Serra et al., who analyzed 60 patients and noted their cohort predominantly consisted of connective tissue disease-associated ILD (17/60) [25]. In contrast, our cohort’s predominant diagnosis was HP (84/127), suggesting that differences in patient populations may account for our higher BAL contribution rate. Similarly, Hostettler et al. reported a 51.1% contribution of BAL in 100 patients when c-TBB alone was inconclusive [26], and our finding of 46.6% contribution among 30 patients without definitive histopathology aligns closely with their data.

In cases where BAL did not contribute to the final diagnosis, several factors may account for its limited diagnostic utility. Predominantly fibrotic parenchyma can markedly reduce cellular retrieval, thereby diminishing the interpretability of BAL cytology, particularly with respect to lymphocytic patterns relevant for HP and NSIP. Low lymphocytic response may also reflect advanced disease, sampling limitations, or subacute presentations, which may weaken BAL’s contribution to MDD. Incomplete exclusion of coexisting infection may confound BAL cellularity and limit diagnostic confidence. Finally, clinical–radiological discordance in some cases may reduce the added value of BAL, as its findings may not align with the dominant phenotypic features considered in MDD. These factors collectively highlight the contexts in which BAL provides limited diagnostic information despite concurrent c-TBB assessment.

Regarding complications, our observed rates of hemorrhage (33.8%) and pneumothorax (17.3%) are comparable with a meta-analysis reporting pneumothorax frequency from 0% to 25.9% and variable hemorrhage rates, with moderate-to-severe bleeding averaging 26.6% and up to 39% in some studies [27]. Harari et al. reported major bleeding in 1.4% and pneumothorax in 10.9% of cases [28], Linhas et al. reported a 22.4% pneumothorax rate requiring chest tube in 81.8% of those cases [29], and Davidsen et al. observed 16.3% moderate bleeding and 14.9% pneumothorax, with only 9.9% requiring drainage [30]. Serra et al. described ≥ 1 complication in 47% of cases with hemorrahge rates up to 35% but no serious events [25]. Our findings are in line with these reports and further confirm the acceptable safety profile of c-TBB.

Our findings of no life-threatening complications reinforce c-TBB’s safety profile. Our results highlight BAL’s complementary role in ILD diagnostics when combined with c-TBB, particularly for HP where BAL can bridge diagnostic gaps when histopathology is inconclusive. The data affirm that BAL is especially valuable in non-fibrotic HP but also contributes meaningfully in fibrotic HP and other ILDs like sarcoidosis and PLCH. Our findings support BAL’s integration into MDD, consistent with guidelines emphasizing its use alongside imaging and histopathology. The observed complication rates, comparable to prior studies, confirm the acceptable safety profile of c-TBB, strengthening its role as a minimally invasive diagnostic alternative to surgical lung biopsy, especially in patients at high surgical risk.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of our study is its relatively large patient cohort compared to similar studies, allowing detailed subgroup analyses across various ILDs. Our comprehensive assessment of both diagnostic yield and complications of combined BAL and c-TBB contributes meaningful data to a relatively underexplored area.

However, the retrospective and single-center design limits the generalizability of our results. Potential selection biases, variations in procedural techniques, and institutional expertise may affect the diagnostic performance and complication rates reported. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm and extend these findings.

A key strength of our study is its comprehensive, real-world evaluation of both BAL and c-TBB in a relatively large cohort of ILD patients (n = 127) treated at a high-volume tertiary referral center specializing in interstitial lung diseases. By including simultaneous BAL and c-TBB in all cases and using standardized protocols in line with ATS guidelines, we ensured consistent sampling quality and minimized procedural variability. The integration of MDD involving experienced pulmonologists, radiologists, pathologists, and rheumatologists further enhanced the reliability and clinical relevance of the final diagnoses, which were aligned with current international guidelines for HP, sarcoidosis, and other ILDs. Additionally, the study’s detailed analysis of BAL lymphocytosis across specific diagnostic subgroups (e.g., non-fibrotic HP, fibrotic HP, NSIP, sarcoidosis) and assessment of BAL’s contribution to the final diagnosis provide valuable, disease-specific insights rarely examined in prior studies. Reporting complications with clear severity classification offers an important real-world perspective on the safety profile of c-TBB. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and single-center design introduces inherent selection and information biases, limiting generalizability to other centers with different expertise, patient populations, or procedural volumes. Second, our analysis did not include long-term follow-up data on treatment response or prognosis, which would be valuable for understanding the clinical implications of BAL findings and final diagnoses.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that BAL significantly enhances the diagnostic process for interstitial lung diseases within a multidisciplinary framework, particularly in non-fibrotic HP. When c-TBB alone is inconclusive, BAL findings—especially lymphocyte percentages—provide crucial diagnostic clues, as exemplified by the high contribution in fibrotic HP cases. The combined use of c-TBB and BAL represents a safe, effective, and complementary approach that increases diagnostic yield in ILD, supporting their integration into routine multidisciplinary evaluations [18].
