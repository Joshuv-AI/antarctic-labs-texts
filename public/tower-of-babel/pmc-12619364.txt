Heart failure in Kenya: epidemiology, management patterns, and outcomes from a national multicenter registry
Authors: Mohamed Hasham Varwani, Nadeem Kassam, Jasmit Shah, Anders Barasa, Mzee Ngunga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHeart failure (HF) poses a significant and growing public health challenge in Kenya, largely driven by the increasing prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and obesity. The condition disproportionately affects younger adults and is primarily linked to non-ischemic causes. Despite the existence of evidence-based treatment options, major barriers remain—particularly in timely diagnosis, access to care, and long-term management—leading to high rates of hospital readmission and mortality. Additionally, the absence of robust national data limits the development of effective policies and optimized HF care strategies. This study aimed to describe the clinical and demographic characteristics, underlying etiologies, management practices, and short-term outcomes—including in-hospital and six-month mortality—of HF patients in Kenya, utilizing data from the National Research Fund (NRF) Registry on Cardiovascular Diseases.MethodsThis was a prospective observational study, conducted at three tertiary hospitals in Kenya—Kenyatta National Hospital, Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital, and Aga Khan University Hospital—representing public and private sectors from December 2021 to December 2022. Adults (≥ 18 years) with clinically and objectively confirmed heart failure were enrolled from both inpatient and outpatient settings. Data were managed via REDCap and analyzed using Stata 17.0. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the population. Logistic regression was used to assess survival and predictors of mortality. Statistical significance was set a P value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 795 HF patients were enrolled across three tertiary hospitals in Kenya. The mean age was 52.3 years (SD 18.4), 51.7% were female. The majority were of African descent (n = 763, [97.2%]) who presented with new-onset HF (n = 704, [88.6%]). Hypertension was the most common comorbidity (39%). On ECG, 6.3% had left bundle branch block (LBBB). Echocardiographic findings showed that majority (n = 598, [76%]) had a LVEF of < 40%. Most patients (n = 534, [83.3%]) were symptomatic, classified as NYHA class II or higher. In-hospital mortality was 6.6%, and the median length of stay was 8 days. Independent predictors of in-hospital mortality included age > 50 years [OR 2.65 (1.25–5.61)] and NYHA class III/IV symptoms [OR 7.74 (1.02–58.5)].ConclusionHF patients in Kenya are predominantly middle-aged and commonly present with new-onset symptoms and reduced LVEF. In-hospital mortality was 6.6% and was independently associated with age over 50 years and advanced symptoms (NYHA class III/IV). These findings highlight the need for earlier detection and more aggressive initial management in this population. Introduction

HF is an escalating global public health concern, currently affecting over 64 million individuals worldwide [1]. This burden is especially severe in developing regions and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where healthcare systems often lack the capacity and infrastructure to effectively manage chronic cardiovascular conditions [1]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of HF continues to rise, largely due to an ongoing epidemiological shift from communicable to non-communicable diseases [2]. Contributing to this trend is the increasing incidence of key risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and obesity, which has led to a growing number of HF cases—often presenting at younger ages than typically observed in high-income countries [1, 2].

In (SSA), the clinical characteristics of HF patients differ markedly from those observed in high-income countries. Data from major registries such as THESUS-HF [1] and INTER-CHF [2] have shed light on these differences, indicating that non-ischemic causes—particularly hypertensive heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, and various cardiomyopathies—are the primary drivers of HF in the region [3]. Conversely, in high-income settings, ischemic heart disease remains the predominant cause, especially among older adults [4]. This contrast underscores the necessity for region-specific approaches and interventions that address the distinct etiological landscape of HF in SSA [5].

The mortality rates associated with HF in sub-Saharan Africa are alarmingly high [1], compounded by limited access to evidence-based therapies and healthcare resources. While medications such as beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), and newer agents like SGLT2 inhibitors have been shown to reduce mortality and improve quality of life in HF patients, their availability and utilization remain limited in many SSA countries [2]. In Kenya, the situation mirrors the broader trends observed in sub-Saharan Africa, with HF presenting a major public health concern [3]. Local data on the clinical outcomes, etiologies, and treatment practices for HF patients in Kenya are scarce, yet essential for informing healthcare policy and improving patient care [4]. The rising burden of HF, coupled with the challenges in diagnosis, treatment, and adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), called for a comprehensive investigation into the clinical and demographic profiles of HF patients in Kenya [5].

This study used data from the NRF Registry on Cardiovascular Diseases to explore the clinical and demographic profile of HF patients in Kenya. Additionally, it evaluated the clinical outcomes, including in hospital mortality, readmission rates and 6- month mortality in three tertiary level hospitals. The research also investigated the predominant etiologies of HF in the Kenyan population, assessed local treatment practices, and compared these practices with international guidelines for HF management.

Methods

The NRF Registry on Cardiovascular Diseases in Kenya is a national, multicenter initiative aimed at providing a comprehensive insight and knowledge of the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, management patterns, and outcomes of cardiovascular diseases in Kenya. Funded by the National Research Fund, the registry seeks to bridge data gaps in cardiovascular care by establishing a reliable and prospective dataset to inform health policies, improve clinical practice, and foster research capacity.

Study population

The registry included all adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with HF, recruited from both inpatient and outpatient settings across three tertiary hospitals. The diagnosis of HF was based on clinical assessment supported by objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction. This included symptoms and signs consistent with HF—such as dyspnea, fatigue, peripheral edema [6], or elevated jugular venous pressure—alongside diagnostic confirmation [7]. Patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF < 40%) were included, as were those with LVEF ≥ 40% provided they had supporting echocardiographic evidence of structural heart disease (e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, left atrial enlargement, or elevated right ventricular systolic pressure > 35 mmHg) or elevated cardiac biomarkers (NT-proBNP > 200 pg/mL or BNP > 100 pg/mL, or BNP > 600 pg/mL in patients with atrial fibrillation). These criteria aligned with the 2021 universal definition of HF [8].

Patients were excluded if they presented with symptoms suggestive of heart failure but lacked objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction. Specifically, patients with preserved EF (≥ 50%) and no demonstrable structural or functional cardiac abnormalities were excluded. Additionally, patients diagnosed with HF mimics—such as chronic lung disease with isolated right heart failure, severe anemia, or other systemic conditions that could explain their symptoms independently—were not included. This ensured the accuracy of HF diagnosis and improved the reproducibility of the findings.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out using standardized, paper-based case report forms (CRFs), which were completed by trained research nurses. The CRFs captured comprehensive patient data including demographics, medical history, clinical presentation, diagnostic test results, treatment regimens, and hospital outcomes. Each month, completed forms were independently reviewed for accuracy and completeness by the quality assurance team. After verification, data were transcribed into REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based platform hosted by Aga Khan University. REDCap provided real-time data entry with audit trails and ensured confidentiality through role-based access. At sites where, diagnostic tests such as electrocardiograms (ECGs) or troponin assays were not routinely available, the study supplemented these to ensure data completeness and consistency across sites.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with continuous variables reported as means and standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons—such as those based on gender or hospital type—were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for skewed data, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess factors associated with in-hospital mortality, with results presented as odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

We identified several factors associated with inpatient mortality. Among these, age over 50 years [OR 2.65 (1.25–5.61)] and the presence of NYHA class III or IV symptoms [OR 7.74 (1.02–58.5)] were statistically significant predictors (P- value < 0.05) of increased in-hospital mortality

Of the initial cohort of 795 patients, after excluding the 37 inpatient deaths, follow-up was planned for 758 individuals. However, follow-up data on patient status was only available for 717 patients, and complete information on medication use at follow-up was available for an even smaller subset. At follow-up, the most commonly prescribed medications were diuretics (74.2%) and beta blockers (62.8%). ACE inhibitors were used in 55.7% and MRAs in 45.2% of patients, reflecting suboptimal uptake of core heart failure therapies. Significant variation was noted across centers: ACE inhibitor use was particularly low at AKUHN (16.8%), and diuretic use ranged from 55.5% at AKUHN to 94.6% at MTRH. Digoxin (18.8%) and DOACs (13.8%) were used infrequently, though DOAC use was higher at AKUHN (22.5%). These findings highlight significant variability in prescribing patterns across centers. Discussion

HF is a growing public health challenge in Kenya [9], driven by both communicable and non-communicable diseases. Our study, leveraging data from the National Research Fund Registry on Cardiovascular Diseases, provides valuable insights into the demographic characteristics, etiological patterns, management practices and outcomes of HF patients across three major Kenyan hospitals, representing both public and private healthcare settings.

The HF population in our study was relatively young, with a mean age of 52.3 years. This mirrors findings from other Sub-Saharan African HF registries, such as THESUS-HF [10] and INTER-CHF [11], which also reported mean ages in the early 50 s. In contrast, cohorts from high-income countries tend to be significantly older, with the ADHERE [12] and OPTIMIZE-HF [13] registries in the United States reporting median ages of 70 and 65 years, respectively, while the ESC Heart Failure Long-Term Registry in Europe cites [14] a mean age of 67 years. This disparity reflects the unique epidemiological landscape in Sub-Saharan Africa, where early-onset HF is driven by hypertensive heart disease, rheumatic heart disease (RHD), and HIV-associated cardiomyopathy. Limited preventive strategies, delayed diagnosis, and barriers to healthcare access further exacerbate the early onset of disease.

Hypertensive heart disease was the predominant cause of HF in our cohort, followed by ischemic heart disease and dilated cardiomyopathy. This trend is consistent with African HF registries like THESUS-HF [10] and INTER-CHF [11], where hypertension remains the leading cause. In contrast, ischemic heart disease is the predominant etiology in high-income regions in Europe, North America [15] and Latin America [16, 17]. The comparatively lower burden of ischemic HF in Kenya may reflect differences in cardiovascular risk profiles. However, poor recognition of ischemic heart disease by primary care facilities and limited access to diagnostic tools such as coronary angiography also potentially lead to underdiagnoses. Unfortunately, community-based data in most African countries is non-existent.

A peculiar finding in our study is the 5% prevalence of HIV-associated cardiomyopathy, a condition rarely reported in Western HF cohorts [18]. This is characteristic of the dual burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases in Sub-Saharan Africa. As HIV treatment advances, improved survival rates are likely to increase the prevalence of HIV-related cardiac complications, underscoring the need for integrated care models that address both infectious and chronic diseases in HF management.

A considerable proportion of patients in our study presented with advanced HF (NYHA class III or IV) at the time of diagnosis. This aligns with findings from the THESUS-HF registry [10] and studies in neighboring Tanzania [19] and Uganda [20], where late-stage presentation is common. In contrast, high-income countries report earlier-stage diagnoses [21] due to routine screening, better healthcare access, and systematic risk factor management. This discrepancy highlights gaps in early detection and outpatient management of HF in Sub-Saharan Africa, driven by limited healthcare infrastructure, low community awareness, and delayed presentation. Enhanced screening programs and decentralized HF clinics could mitigate this disparity.

The overall in-hospital mortality rate in our cohort was 6.6%, with the highest observed in the Public Health system. The median length of stay was 8 days, with shorter durations in the private setting, suggesting and advantage of better-resourced facilities. These findings are consistent with regional studies [22, 23]. Expanding access to specialized HF care and implementing early intervention strategies may improve these outcomes. Advanced age (> 50 years) and severe HF (NYHA class III/IV) emerged as significant predictors of in-hospital mortality. This aligns with data from the THESUS-HF [10] and INTER-CHF [11] registries, as well as European studies such as the ESC-HF [24] Long-Term Registry and the Euro Heart Failure Survey. However, the wide confidence interval observed for NYHA class III/IV suggests that the precise magnitude of its impact on mortality may be over- or underestimated, and this should be interpreted with caution. Older patients often present with multiple comorbidities, diminished physiological reserve, and poorer responses to therapy.

At six months, the mortality rate in our cohort was 24.6%, with a readmission rate of 10.6%. Compared to high-income settings [25], where six-month mortality is typically 10–15% and readmission rates exceed 20%, the disparity is evident. The lower readmission rate may reflect underreporting due to loss to follow-up or healthcare access barriers rather than superior clinical outcomes. Enhanced follow-up programs and community-based care models could improve post-discharge outcomes in this population.

Finally, Despite guideline recommendations, the uptake of key evidence-based medications at six-month follow-up in our cohort was suboptimal and varied significantly across centers. While diuretics and beta blockers were the most commonly used medications, foundational therapies that improve long-term outcomes in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)—such as ACE inhibitors, MRAs, and beta blockers—were inconsistently prescribed. ACE inhibitor use was especially low at AKUHN (16.8%), and MRAs were underutilized at the same center (15.1%), raising concerns about missed opportunities to reduce morbidity and mortality. Notably absent from our dataset is the use of angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), which have demonstrated superior mortality and hospitalization benefits compared to ACE inhibitors in trials such as PARADIGM-HF [26]. AKUHN, being a private tertiary center serving a largely insured population, may preferentially prescribe ARNIs over ACE inhibitors. In clinical practice at AKUHN, ARNIs are frequently used as first-line therapy in eligible patients with HFrEF, which likely explains the low recorded use of ACE inhibitors in this setting. Unfortunately, due to limitations in data capture during the registry period, ARNI use was not documented, which is an important consideration when interpreting these findings. The absence of ARNI data potentially underrepresents the true extent of guideline-directed medical therapy at this site.

Similarly, no data were captured on sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, which have emerged as a cornerstone in HF treatment following landmark trials such as DAPA-HF [27] and EMPEROR-Reduced [28]. These agents significantly reduce cardiovascular death and HF hospitalizations, regardless of diabetic status. Their omission from the dataset limits comprehensive assessment of optimal Goal Directed Medical therapy (GDMT)for HF and underscores the need for improved data collection on novel agents in HF management. Compared to the African Heart Failure Registry (AHFR) [29], where ACE inhibitor use was reported at 69% and beta blocker use at 63%, our population shows relatively similar beta blocker use but falls short on ACE inhibitors, especially in AKUHN. In contrast, ESC-HF Long-Term Registry [24], CHAMP-HF [30] and the AMERICASS registry [17] report markedly higher adherence to GDMT, with ACEi/ARB or ARNI use exceeding 80% and MRA use near 60%, reflecting more robust systems for chronic HF management. These discrepancies underscore systemic gaps in prescribing practices, affordability, and possibly physician training across African centers. Enhancing the availability and prescription of GDMT, particularly with the inclusion of ARNI, is essential to improving survival and reducing rehospitalization in our setting.

Clinical utility of findings

The insights from this study have direct clinical and public health relevance. Understanding the younger age profile and predominant etiologies such as hypertensive cardiomyopathy can help clinicians tailor screening and management strategies specific to the Kenyan context. Recognizing the high prevalence of advanced HF at presentation underscores the urgent need for earlier detection and intervention in outpatient settings. The documented disparities in access to diagnostics and guideline-directed therapies between public and private sectors highlight critical targets for resource allocation and capacity building. Additionally, the suboptimal uptake of evidence-based medications calls for clinician education, improved prescribing practices, and health system reforms to increase availability and affordability of these therapies. Integrating these findings into national HF guidelines and training programs could improve risk stratification, patient outcomes, and healthcare efficiency across diverse Kenyan healthcare settings.

Strengths

This first multicenter, registry-based study in Kenya provides unique insights into the regional epidemiology and clinical features of HF, including younger patient age, predominance of hypertensive cardiomyopathy, and advanced symptomatology at presentation. It identifies significant disparities between public and private healthcare sectors in access to diagnostics, adherence to guideline-directed therapies, and patient outcomes, reflecting broader systemic challenges in healthcare delivery and resource allocation. Importantly, the study offers clear, context-specific recommendations for strengthening primary care and early detection, expanding affordable diagnostic access, integrating heart failure care into existing chronic disease programs, and leveraging digital health innovations like telemedicine. These strengths make the study a pragmatic roadmap for health system strengthening, aiming to improve heart failure outcomes in Kenya while informing policy, clinical practice, and future research tailored to the country’s unique healthcare realities.

Limitations

Despite offering valuable insights into heart failure in Kenya, this study has several limitations. As a hospital-based registry from three tertiary centers, the findings may not reflect the broader population, especially those in rural or primary care settings. The observational design introduces potential selection and information bias, with possible underreporting or misclassification of comorbidities, etiologies, and outcomes. Missing data, particularly at follow-up, may have affected six-month mortality and readmission estimates. One key limitation of our study was the absence of data on ARNIs and SGLT-2 inhibitors. This oversight stemmed from the initial design of the data collection tool, which focused on medications commonly prescribed in both public and private healthcare settings, but did not specifically capture these emerging therapies. As a result, we were unable to assess the use, adherence, and potential optimization of these important components of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). Furthermore, the lack of detailed follow-up data on medication use limited our ability to evaluate longitudinal treatment adherence, which could have provided valuable insights into the real-world application and impact of these therapies on patient outcomes. This gap in data underscores the need for more comprehensive follow-up in future studies to assess the full spectrum of GDMT, including newer pharmacologic agents like ARNIs and SGLT-2 inhibitors. Additionally, we were unable to construct Kaplan–Meier survival curves due to the absence of time-to-event data for major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and mortality—an analysis that could have significantly strengthened our findings. Although aggregate 6-month mortality and readmission rates were captured, the lack of precise timing data for these outcomes precluded formal time-to-event analysis. This limitation reflects a gap in our data collection process, which has been acknowledged and will inform the design of future registry-based studies. Incorporating structured follow-up with accurate time stamps will be essential to enable survival analyses and better characterize outcome trajectories. Differences in resources across hospitals may have influenced diagnostic and therapeutic practices, limiting comparability. Lastly, the short follow-up period, relatively small sample size, and absence of qualitative data on adherence, access, and systemic barriers constrain generalizability. Future studies should include larger, more representative cohorts, longer follow-up durations, and mixed-method approaches to capture a fuller picture of heart failure care in the region.

Conclusion

This first multicenter HF registry-based study provides valuable insight into the epidemiology, clinical patterns, management practices, and outcomes of HF in Kenya. It highlights distinctive features of HF in this setting, including younger patient age, a predominance of hypertensive cardiomyopathy, and advanced symptomatology at presentation—patterns that differ markedly from those observed in high-income countries. Importantly, the study reveals substantial disparities between public and private healthcare settings in terms of access to diagnostic tools, adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy, and patient outcomes. These disparities reflect broader systemic gaps in healthcare delivery, infrastructure, and resource allocation.

Addressing these challenges requires targeted investment in primary care, diagnostic capacity, and expertise in HF management programs. Ensuring equitable access to evidence-based therapies across both public and private sectors is essential to reducing mortality and improving quality of life for HF patients in Kenya. Our findings also underscore the importance of ongoing, long-term data collection systems. These are essential for monitoring trends, evaluating practice, and informing local health policy and guidelines.

Future directions

Reducing the burden of HF in Kenya demands a comprehensive, system-wide response aligned with the country’s unique healthcare realities. Strengthening primary care remains foundational, with efforts focused on early detection and aggressive management of modifiable risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and rheumatic fever. This can be achieved through widespread screening, public awareness campaigns, and upskilling frontline health workers in cardiovascular care. Expanding access to affordable diagnostic tools—like echocardiography, natriuretic peptide testing, and coronary angiography—is equally essential for timely and accurate diagnosis. Furthermore, integrating HF care into existing chronic disease and HIV programs can optimize resource use and promote continuity of care. Importantly, expanding digital health initiatives and telemedicine could bridge access gaps, especially in rural areas, where specialist services are limited. Mobile health platforms can support follow-up, medication adherence, and patient education at scale. Policy changes focused on early detection and community-based HF management could also enhance outcomes across the region, reducing late-stage presentations. Sustainable investment in public health infrastructure, equitable access to essential medications, and implementation of national treatment guidelines are vital for ensuring high-quality, evidence-based care across both public and private sectors. A unified national HF strategy—anchored in data, policy, and cross-sector collaboration—will be essential for reversing current trends and improving long-term outcomes for Kenyan patients.
