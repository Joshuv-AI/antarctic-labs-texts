Recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide for the recovery stage of septic shock: an interventional study protocol
Authors: Jing-Chao Luo, Tao Mou, Ma Li, Wen-Tao Feng, Rui-Ming Yue, Chun Pan, Xiao-bo Huang, Eduardo Kattan, Yi Zhang, Ling-Ai Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the de-resuscitation phase of septic shock, resolving vasoplegia and fluid mobilisation can increase venous congestion in patients with sepsis-related myocardial dysfunction. This study will characterise the haemodynamic effects and safety of recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide (rh-BNP) in this population.Methods and analysisThis single-centre, prospective, single-arm study will enrol 30 adults recovering from septic shock with improving infection/vasopressor trends, sinus rhythm, ongoing pulse-index continuous cardiac output (PiCCO) monitoring and measurable arm-equilibrium pressure (Parm). Eligibility will require cardiac dysfunction (left-ventricular ejection fraction ≤50% and/or ≥10% absolute decline when available) and volume overload (global end-diastolic volume index >800 mL/m² and extravascular lung water index >10 mL/kg). Participants will receive rh-BNP (2 µg/kg intravenous bolus over 15 min, then a 0.01 µg/kg/min infusion for up to 72 hours). Measurements will be obtained at baseline, acute response (30–60 min), 24, 48 and 72 hours. The primary outcome will be within-patient change in venous return pressure gradient (ΔPVR, Parm−central venous pressure) from baseline to acute response. Secondary outcomes will include indices of preload, cardiac function, tissue perfusion and venous congestion. Haemodynamic instability will be the safety endpoint. Paired tests and repeated-measures analyses will estimate within-patient changes over time.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital, Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences (No. 2024-653-1). Written informed consent will be obtained. Findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and conferences.Trial registration numberNCT06745206.

Introduction

Septic shock, a severe form of distributive circulatory failure, represents the critical progression of sepsis.1 While early fluid resuscitation and haemodynamic optimisation are crucial for survival, the recovery phase presents unique challenges.2 As infection control improves and circulation stabilises, treatment de-escalation begins, accompanied by fluid redistribution from interstitial spaces to the vasculature, increasing cardiac volume load.3 Notably, septic shock often involves multi-organ dysfunction, with over half of patients experiencing cardiac impairment and reduced volume tolerance.4 Thus, efficient excess fluid removal and cardiac preload reduction during this phase may significantly accelerate patient recovery.5

Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), an endogenous neuroendocrine hormone secreted in response to increased ventricular wall tension, induces vasodilation, particularly in the venous system, alleviates cardiac congestion and enhances natriuresis and diuresis.6 Synthetic recombinant human BNP (rh-BNP) has been employed clinically for over two decades to treat congestive heart failure.7 Given its established physiological effects and clinical efficacy in heart failure management, we hypothesise that rh-BNP administration during the recovery phase of septic shock, particularly in patients exhibiting signs of cardiac dysfunction, could rapidly reduce venous return and enhance fluid removal while maintaining adequate tissue perfusion, thus offering a novel therapeutic approach for optimising recovery.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study will test the hypothesis that, in adults recovering from septic shock with objective cardiac dysfunction and fluid accumulation, rh-BNP will generate an early, quantifiable venous-side decongestive signal, which is captured as a reduction in the venous return pressure gradient (ΔPVR = arm-equilibrium pressure (Parm) − central venous pressure (CVP)), and will facilitate fluid removal while preserving blood pressure and tissue perfusion. To capture this rapid physiology under tightly standardised conditions and to minimise time-varying confounding during the acute-response window, we will conduct a single-centre, prospective, single-arm interventional physiological study using within-patient pre–post comparisons.

Participants

The target population for this study comprises patients in the recovery phase of septic shock with concurrent cardiac dysfunction. All consecutive septic-shock patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences and Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital will be screened daily by research personnel. Screening logs will capture reasons for non-inclusion. Based on the 2025 admission rate (100 eligible cases/year), we anticipate completing enrolment within 6 months, with recruitment planned to start on 1 June 2026 and end by December 2026. Intervention (rh-BNP)

rh-BNP for injection (generic name: lyophilised recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide for injection; brand name: Xinhuosu) will be used in this trial. The manufacturer is Chengdu Nuodikang Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd., located in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, China. rh-BNP is reconstituted to a concentration of 10 µg/mL and administered as an initial intravenous bolus of 2 µg/kg over 15 min, followed by a continuous infusion at a rate of 0.01 µg/kg/min. Patients will receive at least the first 500 µg dose infusion, with a recommended duration of 72 hours. The specific timing of discontinuation will be determined by the treating clinician. Infusion pumps with automatic log files will be used. Rates will be cross-checked every 2 hours by nursing staff and documented in the electronic case-report form. Concomitant care

All participants will receive standard intensive care management for septic shock recovery according to institutional practice. This protocol does not mandate other therapeutic decisions. However, because the primary endpoint is an acute physiological change assessed at 30 min after rh-BNP initiation, we will minimise time-varying co-interventions during the controlled early observation period to reduce confounding.

Permitted throughout the trial

Concomitant treatments required for routine clinical management are permitted at the treating clinician’s discretion, including antimicrobial therapy, mechanical ventilation, analgesia, sedation, nutrition, prophylaxis measures and haemodynamic support with vasoactive agents as clinically indicated. All concomitant medications and organ-support therapies will be recorded.

Rescue therapy and exceptions

No clinically necessary intervention will be withheld. If haemodynamic instability occurs or patient safety requires urgent treatment (eg, vasopressor escalation, initiation of additional vasopressors, fluid bolus, initiation/escalation of diuretics or CRRT), these actions are permitted and will be implemented immediately according to standard care. Such co-interventions will be recorded with exact timestamps and doses, consistent with the protocol’s safety monitoring and data-collection procedures.

Outcomes

The primary outcome

The primary outcome is the within-patient change in ΔPVR from baseline (immediately before rh-BNP initiation) to 30 min after starting rh-BNP. Parm is obtained by transient upper-arm occlusion and is used as a bedside surrogate of the mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf),15 which reflects venous tone and the ‘stressed’ blood volume in the systemic venous reservoir. CVP approximates right atrial pressure and represents the downstream venous return. Therefore, Parm − CVP (ΔPVR) provides an interpretable estimate of the driving pressure for systemic venous return and a quantitative marker of venous capacitance/stressed-volume changes and venous congestion.

Importantly, rapid haemodynamic effects of BNP occur within minutes.16 17 In our prior physiological study in post-cardiotomy congestive heart failure,18 a standard rh-BNP regimen (bolus followed by infusion) produced measurable changes within 30 min. Pmsf decreased from 32±7 to 28±7 mm Hg, CVP from 12±3 to 11±3 mm Hg and ΔPVR from 19±7 to 17±6 mm Hg, accompanied by a reduction in venous return resistance and preserved cardiac output.

Therefore, we selected the 30 min time point to capture the immediate pharmacodynamic ‘signature’ of rh-BNP on venous capacitance and stressed volume before substantial confounding from evolving fluid removal strategies (diuretics/ultrafiltration), vasopressor titration and the natural recovery trajectory in septic shock. This is aligned with our protocol emphasis on a tightly controlled early observation window to minimise co-interventions.

The secondary outcomes

Physiological secondary outcomes, assessed at 30 min, will include absolute and relative changes from baseline in: (1) perfusion pressure (MAP − CVP); (2) CVP; (3) preload indices (GEDI and left-ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV)); (4) renal resistive index and (5) lactate clearance ([baseline lactate – follow-up lactate]/baseline lactate × 100%).

Clinical secondary outcomes will include: (1) duration of invasive mechanical ventilation; (2) ICU length of stay; (3) new initiation of CRRT after enrolment; (4) performance of tracheostomy; (5) mortality and (6) ventilator-free days to day 28 (VFD-28). VFD-28 will be defined as the number of calendar days within the first 28 days after enrolment during which the patient is alive and free from invasive mechanical ventilation. Patients who die before day 28 will be assigned 0 VFD-28. For patients who are successfully weaned, VFD-28 will be calculated as 28 minus the total number of days receiving invasive mechanical ventilation. For those remaining invasively ventilated at day 28, VFD-28 will be 0.

The safety outcome is haemodynamic instability, defined as an increase in norepinephrine of ≥0.1 µg/kg/min sustained for ≥15 min to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg or initiation of any additional vasopressor agent, or administration of a fluid bolus. Haemodynamic instability meeting this definition will trigger discontinuation (temporary interruption, dose reduction, or permanent cessation) of rh-BNP at the treating clinician’s discretion.

Discontinuation of rh-BNP due to haemodynamic instability will not, by itself, constitute withdrawal from the study. Participants will remain in follow-up whenever possible to ensure complete safety reporting.

Measurements

Overview and timing

Within 12 hours after a patient fulfils the recovery-phase septic-shock eligibility criteria, analgesia and sedation will be titrated to a Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score between 0 and −4. Patients will be positioned supine. Venous and arterial pressure transducers will be secured at the fourth intercostal space on the mid-axillary line and zeroed to atmospheric pressure. A comprehensive baseline dataset will be obtained immediately before rh-BNP administration, followed by a prespecified acute-response assessment and repeated follow-up assessments. Measurements are scheduled at: (1) baseline (−30 to 0 min), (2) acute response (30–60 min after infusion initiation), and (3) 24, 48, and 72 hours after drug administration. These repeated measures are designed to support within-patient paired comparisons at each time point and assessment of overall temporal patterns across follow-up.

If advanced haemodynamic monitoring (PiCCO) is discontinued before 72 hours, follow-up assessments will be limited to parameters that remain obtainable, and the reason for missingness will be documented.

Primary outcome measurement (ΔPVR = Parm − CVP)

Parm will be obtained using a transient stop-flow upper-arm arterial–venous equilibrium pressure technique on the arm containing the arterial line.19 A narrow cuff will be placed on the upper arm and instantly pressurised (in less than one cardiac cycle) to 70 mm Hg above systolic pressure, with flow arrest confirmed on the arterial trace. After occlusion, 30 s will be allowed for arterial and venous pressures to equilibrate, and Parm will be recorded. The cuff will then be deflated to resume circulation. This procedure will be repeated twice more with 3 min rest intervals. The mean of three measurements will be used as Parm for the time point.

CVP will be measured via a central venous catheter connected to a calibrated pressure transducer. Values will be recorded in the supine position at end-expiration, and ΔPVR will be calculated using simultaneously recorded Parm and CVP.

Safety monitoring

Haemodynamic instability will be monitored continuously during rh-BNP administration and follow-up, and rescue interventions (vasopressor escalation, initiation of additional vasopressors or fluid bolus) will be recorded with timestamps and doses.

Adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) will be collected and classified in accordance with institutional policy and International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) (E6 [R2]) definitions. Drug-relatedness (causality) will be adjudicated by the principal investigator with oversight by the Clinical Research Office. Only serious drug-related adverse events will count towards the prespecified recruitment stopping rule (halt recruitment if ≥3 participants experience serious drug-related adverse events).

Sample size

Our anticipated effect size was informed by our prior physiological study in post-cardiotomy congestive heart failure.18 In the heart failure with reduced ejection fraction subgroup, rh-BNP produced a rapid reduction in the venous return driving pressure gradient (Pmsf − CVP) from 21±9 to 16±6 mm Hg within 30 min (mean difference ≈5 mmHg). Accordingly, for sample size planning we expected Parm − CVP to decrease from approximately 20±8 to 15±8 mm Hg, corresponding to a mean difference of 5 mm Hg and an SD of the paired differences of 8 mm Hg.

We considered a 5 mm Hg reduction physiologically meaningful because it represents a 25% decrease from a baseline gradient of 20 mm Hg, a magnitude that is unlikely to be explained by measurement variability alone. For the stop-flow arm arterial–venous equilibrium pressure technique, the least significant change is 14% for a single determination and improves to 7–10% when averaging repeated measurements.21 Sample-size requirements are calculated for a paired two-sided t-test (α=0.05, power 90%). Under these assumptions, 27 evaluable patients are necessary. We will enrol 30 patients to allow for potential protocol violations or missing measurements.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will follow a prespecified statistical analysis plan finalised before database lock. Continuous variables will be summarised as mean±SD for approximately normally distributed data or median (IQR, IQR) for skewed distributions (assessed by histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test). Categorical variables will be reported as counts and percentages.

The primary outcome and secondary physiological outcomes

For the primary outcome and secondary physiological outcomes, we will compare baseline vs 30 min ΔPVR using a paired t-test if the distribution of paired differences is approximately normal; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test will be used. To evaluate whether each continuous variable shows an overall change over time (30 min, 24 hours, 48 hours and 72 hours), we will use repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) for approximately normally distributed outcomes in participants with complete data across all follow-up time points. The Mauchly test will be used to assess sphericity; if violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction will be applied. When the overall RM-ANOVA is significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons vs baseline will be reported with multiplicity adjustment.

For outcomes that are markedly non-normal or where repeated measurements are incomplete due to the removal of PiCCO monitoring, RM-ANOVA assumptions may not hold. In such cases, the time-trend analysis will be supplemented by (1) descriptive plots of trajectories and (2) a sensitivity analysis using an appropriate repeated-measures approach on available data (eg, non-parametric longitudinal methods or mixed-effects modelling).

Secondary clinical outcomes

Haemodynamic instability will be analysed as a binary safety endpoint. The incidence proportion will be reported overall and by timing (acute response window vs later follow-up).

Given the single-arm design and limited sample size, clinical secondary outcomes (duration of invasive mechanical ventilation, ICU length of stay, tracheostomy, new CRRT initiation after enrolment, day-28 mortality and VFD-28) will be analysed descriptively and considered exploratory. Continuous clinical outcomes will be summarised as median (IQR) and mean (SD) as appropriate. Binary outcomes will be reported as proportions. For VFD-28, the prespecified definition will be applied (death before day 28 assigned 0 VFD-28), and its distribution will be summarised accordingly.

Exploratory associations between the acute physiological response (eg, change in Parm–CVP at 30 min) and selected clinical outcomes (eg, VFD-28 or ICU length of stay) may be assessed using correlation analyses or simple regression models, recognising the risk of overfitting and the hypothesis-generating nature of these analyses.

Missing data

If ≥5% of the primary endpoint values (baseline to 30 min ΔPVR) are missing, multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing-at-random assumption will be performed (20 imputations). Parm and CVP at baseline and 30 min will be imputed, and ΔPVR will be derived by passive calculation. Results will be pooled using Rubin’s rules. The imputation model will include baseline demographic variables (age, sex and body mass index), baseline haemodynamic, perfusion and congestion-related variables (Parm, CVP, MAP, HR, norepinephrine dose, cardiac index, stroke volume index, GEDI, EVLWI, systemic vascular resistance index, lactate, ScvO2, CO2 gap, capillary refill time, cumulative fluid balance and urine output prior to enrolment) and their corresponding acute-response measurements. Ultrasound indices (LVEF, renal resistive index, portal vein pulsatility index and VExUS grade), collected at baseline and in the acute-response window, will also be included as predictors. A complete-case analysis will be presented as a sensitivity analysis. Missingness for secondary outcomes will be reported transparently.

No interim efficacy analysis is planned. All tests will be two-sided with p<0.05. All analyses will be performed using Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol (V.2.0, 4 November 2024) was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital, Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences (No. 2024-653-1) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06745206). Any protocol amendment will be documented in a version-control table and submitted to the ethics committee, trial registry and all investigators within 14 days of approval. The study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and follow the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines. Written informed consent will be obtained from patients or legally authorised representatives prior to enrolment. A template of the participant consent form is available in online supplemental appendix S2. Study-related injury is covered by a no-fault clinical-trial insurance policy. Results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal and presented at international conferences.

Participants are identified by a coded number. All data are entered into a purpose-built electronic case report form with automated range and plausibility checks. Servers are housed in a secure data centre with encryption, nightly back-ups and disaster-recovery mirrors. The final, locked dataset will be accessible only to the principal investigator, trial statistician and an independent data-verifier appointed by the principal investigator. Given the short intervention period (≤ 72 h), minimal risk profile of low-dose rh-BNP, and single-centre nature of this study, an independent Data Monitoring Committee is not convened. Safety is overseen by the principal investigator and the Clinical Research Office of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. Cumulative safety data will be reviewed after every 10 participants.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public are not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this single-centre physiological study. The target population comprises adults in the recovery phase of septic shock who require intensive care and advanced haemodynamic monitoring. Therefore, direct involvement in protocol development is not feasible.

Discussion

This protocol tests a focused, clinically common problem: once septic shock stabilises, many patients remain congested and cannot tolerate further volume loading because cardiac function has deteriorated. We designed the study to determine whether a standard rh-BNP regimen can produce a rapid, measurable decongestive haemodynamic signal during this de-resuscitation window without sacrificing perfusion. Our primary endpoint, the change in venous return driving pressure (Parm–CVP) at 30 min, targets the earliest pharmacodynamic ‘fingerprint’ of rh-BNP, before diuretics, ultrafiltration, evolving vasopressor titration or spontaneous recovery confound interpretation.

The conventional label of septic shock as ‘hypovolemic’ makes rh-BNP appear counterintuitive, and the drug’s prescribing cautions in low filling pressures should not be ignored. The physiology shifts after initial resuscitation. Large volumes of fluid often accumulate in the venous reservoir and interstitium and mobilised interstitial fluid can further expand the intravascular compartment as inflammation abates. At the same time, sepsis-associated myocardial dysfunction is frequent, resulting in reduced volume tolerance.22 23 AKI and diuretic resistance are also common during this phase, so conventional fluid removal strategies may be slow, inconsistent or hemodynamically costly.24 Our eligibility criteria deliberately operationalise a ‘therapeutic window’. We enrol patients who are improving from an infection and vasopressor standpoint yet show objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction and volume overload, thereby reducing the risk of exposing genuinely preload-dependent patients to a venodilator.

Two design choices make the mechanistic question answerable. First, we anchor the primary analysis to Guytonian venous return physiology. rh-BNP should reduce venous tone and the stressed venous volume, lowering mean systemic filling pressure. In our protocol, Parm serves as a bedside surrogate for that upstream pressure. A fall in Parm–CVP would, therefore, be consistent with venodilation-mediated decongestion. That signal alone is not enough. If venous return resistance rises or cardiac performance cannot compensate, systemic flow may fall. We therefore interpret changes in Parm–CVP alongside cardiac output indices and perfusion pressure (MAP–CVP), and we pair these with tissue perfusion markers (including lactate dynamics and oxygen-derived indices) to detect any haemodynamic ‘trade-off’ early. Second, we add congestion phenotyping beyond pressure numbers. Ultrasound-based venous congestion markers (including VExUS-related assessments and renal Doppler indices) are well suited to detect clinically relevant venous hypertension and its downstream organ effects. Tracking these measures over 24–72 hours allows us to link early venous return changes to the evolving decongestion trajectory, while acknowledging that natriuresis/diuresis is unlikely to be fully expressed in the first 30 min.

The existing clinical evidence for natriuretic peptide therapy in sepsis is limited and heterogeneous, which partly reflects variation in timing, dosing and endpoints. A randomised trial in older patients with septic cardiac dysfunction using a small-dose rh-BNP infusion without a bolus did not demonstrate a mortality reduction, yet it reported improvements in selected cardiopulmonary organ dysfunction components and shorter lengths of stay.25 Those findings leave a practical question unanswered: is there a reproducible, targetable haemodynamic effect that could justify a larger, outcome-driven trial in a carefully selected phenotype and at the appropriate phase of shock? Our protocol addresses that gap by shifting the intervention to the de-resuscitation stage, standardising co-interventions within a tightly controlled early observation window, and prioritising physiological endpoints that sit directly on the proposed mechanism of action. Experimental work also suggests natriuretic peptides may modulate endothelial activation and inflammatory signalling, and animal studies have reported organ-protective effects in sepsis-relevant models.26 27 However, whether these pleiotropic pathways translate into clinically meaningful benefit in septic shock remains uncertain. This is precisely why an initial human physiological study, designed to separate signal from noise, is a rational step before escalating to larger trials.

Several limitations are inherent to this design and should shape interpretation. The single-arm, pre–post structure cannot establish causal efficacy and remains vulnerable to time-varying confounding, even with protocolised restriction of co-interventions early on. The sample size is appropriate for mechanistic estimation but underpowered for patient-centred outcomes. Any clinical endpoints will be exploratory and hypothesis-generating. External validity will be constrained by strict eligibility and reliance on advanced monitoring (PiCCO and detailed ultrasound), which may limit applicability in settings without such resources. Parm is a practical surrogate for mean systemic filling pressure but is still an indirect measurement, and its precision and operator dependence require disciplined technique and reporting. Finally, rh-BNP carries a foreseeable risk—hypotension or worsened perfusion in susceptible patients. Our protocol embeds explicit haemodynamic instability criteria and close monitoring, but larger controlled studies would be needed to quantify risk more precisely. Even with these constraints, if rh-BNP yields a consistent early decongestive signature with preserved perfusion in this selected recovery-phase phenotype, the study will provide effect-size and variability estimates that are essential for designing a definitive, controlled trial with credible endpoints and safety boundaries.
