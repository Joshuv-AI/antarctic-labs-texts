Defining a Cataract Surgery Protocol in Diabetic Patients: A Pilot Study
Authors: Christolyn Raj, Christopher Hodge, David Levitz, Uday Kumar Bhatt, Raj Pathmaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCataract surgery in patients with Type II diabetes mellitus (T2DM) can lead to complications. A well-defined surgical protocol that optimizes the procedure and applies strict selection criteria may reduce the risk of adverse outcomes. We evaluate safety and efficacy outcomes in diabetic patients undergoing cataract surgery by employing a specific protocol for preoperative evaluation and surgery selection.MethodsA prospective, non-randomized pilot study was performed on patients with T2DM and visually significant cataract. Patients adhered to a strict preoperative selection protocol including classification as per Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale (DRSS), stable ocular coherence tomography, and anti-VEGF treatment-naive for a minimum 3 months before surgery. Patients underwent phacoemulsification surgery with monofocal intraocular lens implantation (IOL) without intra-operative corticosteroid or anti-VEGF injection and undertook the same post-operative regimen. Final patient evaluation was performed at 3 months to evaluate vision, refractive and safety outcomes.ResultsAt 3 months (n=25 eyes), the visual acuity efficacy index was 1.32. The incidence of clinical CME was 16%. An increase from baseline CMT of 1 micron to 25 microns was seen in 53% of eyes with an increase between 26 microns to 50 microns seen in 20% of eyes. A recurrence or new onset of DME was diagnosed in 8.0% of eyes. Factors found to increase the recurrence of DME included higher DRSS at baseline (p=0.032) and previous treatment with anti-VEGF (p<0.001).ConclusionPatient selection with adherence to a robust preoperative protocol can result in excellent visual outcomes and manageable complication rates.Keywords: diabetes mellitus, cataract surgery, macular oedema, protocol

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) has been estimated to impact almost half a billion people worldwide with a projected increase of 25% by 2030.1 It has been reported to accelerate the progression of cataract causing significant visual debilitation.2 Patients with DM who undergo cataract surgery can have variable outcomes due to postoperative sequalae. Of these, the most common reported in the literature include pseudophakic macula oedema (CME) and diabetic macula oedema (DME).3 The reported incidence of clinically diagnosed CME in diabetic patients following routine cataract surgery remains variable ranging within the literature from 1.7% to 41.7%.4,5 Absent from these studies is a clear definition of the clinical definition of CME or a classification of the stage of diabetic retinopathy at the time of surgery. Whilst studies have looked at the incidence of new onset DME or recurrence of DME in patients with DM following cataract surgery, with reported incidence estimated at 6.6%,6,7 these are mostly retrospective and various pre-operative protocols have been described.

Much of the literature is focused on prophylactic management strategies to manage CME and DME in patients undergoing cataract surgery. This has included advocating for the use of topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAIDs) eye drops pre and/or post operatively, peri-ocular or intravitreal corticosteroids and/or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) intraoperatively.8,9 To date this remains a challenging area mainly due to the lack of standardized triaging protocols.

Providing strict guidelines can assist the decision to proceed to surgery. With increasing presentations of patients with visually significant cataract and DM, surgeons need to be well-informed to optimize patient selection and pre-operative preparation. To investigate this, we conducted a non-randomized prospective pilot study where patients adhered to a strict pre- and post-operative surgical protocol. After meeting the study inclusion criteria, patients were staged using the Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale (DRSS), assessed for ocular coherence tomography (OCT) stability and ceased anti-VEGF treatment for at least 3 months prior to surgery. Within this context, we aim to provide guidelines for cataract surgeons to effectively triage DM patients proceeding to surgery warranting the best possible visual outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

This multicenter, prospective, non-randomized interventional exploratory study was performed in a private clinic setting.

Human Ethics Research Committee Board Approval

This study was assessed and approved by an external Human Research Ethics, Low and Negligible Risk Committee, (University of Melbourne HREC No. 2023-25806-41513-8). All patient information remained deidentified and the study complied with the Tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Standardised Study Protocol

Inclusion criteria were adults aged 55 years or older; confirmed history of Type 2 DM, graded Diabetic Retinopathy (none, mild or moderate using DRSS), stable OCT appearance at time of surgery, 3 months anti-VEGF treatment-naïve status.

Exclusion criteria were proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), previous ocular surgery, retinal laser, refractive laser correction, pre-existing ocular conditions such as primary or secondary glaucoma, ocular inflammation, age-related maculopathy, other retinal conditions or an only seeing eye.

All patients who met the inclusion criteria were then assessed with a standardized protocol prior to surgery. This included grading each patient’s diabetic retinopathy using the DRSS grading system which is a clinically relevant measure that correlates functional anatomical outcomes. The DRSS uses an Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (EDTRS) scale that indicates the presence of non, mild, moderate or severe retinopathy (Figure 1).10

Modified DRSS.

Following this, each patient’s OCT was analysed to ensure a stable OCT prior to surgery. An OCT was deemed stable if there were no changes in retinal structure including fluid accumulation, intra-retinal or epi-retinal membrane thickening. Any patients who required treatment for centre involving macula oedema following the initial baseline assessment were treated monthly for 6 months as per standard protocol (anti-VEGF intravitreal injections). Patients were then reviewed monthly for 3 months prior to the surgical date to ensure a stable OCT.

All patients underwent routine phacoemulsification surgery with femtosecond-laser assistance or manual with a monofocal intraocular lens implant (Clareon monofocal, Alcon Ft. Worth TX USA).

Post operatively (day 1 to week 4) all patients were administered topical eyedrops including NSAIDs (Acular, Ketorolac tromethamine 0.5% AbbVie Inc, Chicago IL, USA), antibiotic (Chlorsig, Chloramphenicol 0.5% or Ocuflox, Ofloxacin AbbVie) and corticosteroid (Prednefrin Forte: Prednisolone Acetate, AbbVie or Maxidex, Dexamethosone Alcon Ft. Measurements

At baseline, a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination was performed including uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA, Snellen converted to Decimal), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), subjective refraction, biometry, dilated fundus examination, retinal colour fundus photography (Optos, Inc Marlborough, MA USA). Spectral domain OCT and OCT angiography (OCT-A) were performed (Cirrus HD-OCT Model 4000; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany). Central macular thickness (CMT) was manually measured as between the inner limiting membrane and Bruch’s membrane using a default OCT measurement function. All investigations except for biometry were repeated at 3 month follow‑up visit.

Definition

In this study CME and DME were defined using both clinical and OCT characteristics. A clinical diagnosis of CME relates to the presence of intraretinal fluid or thickening at the central macula. A clinical diagnosis of DME refers to the presence of central or para-central macula thickening in presence of microaneurysms and/or hard exudates.

OCT findings of CME were demonstrated by high central macular thickness to retinal volume ratio and intact hyperreflective outer retinal bands whilst features of DME depict a higher parafoveal outer nuclear layer to inner nuclear layer thickness ratio.11

Endpoints

Primary endpoints were change in CDVA from baseline, spherical equivalent (SE) accuracy and IOL efficacy represented by mean absolute difference from SE target at 3-month post operatively. The efficacy index was calculated as a measure of success (Postoperative Mean UDVA/Preoperative Mean CDVA measured by decimal). Secondary endpoints were incidence of CME and DME. Further secondary endpoint included change in CMT on OCT from baseline to 3 months post operatively. Safety outcomes were analysed as any adverse events from surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Deidentified data was collected and stored in an Excel spreadsheet prior to analysis with SPSS (V24. IBM, Chicago IL, USA). Standard demographic variables were collated (mean, standard deviation and range) for visual and refractive parameters. CDVA and CMT were compared to baseline using single-sided paired t-test. The incidence of CME and DME per DRSS grading was calculated using a chi square analysis. A p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Associations between the baseline factors and incidence of CME and DME were examined. Influence of baseline characteristics (age, CDVA, DRSS score, previous treatment with anti-VEGF) was evaluated by linear regression analysis. To avoid correlation bias, only one eye of any patient was included in the analysis.

Results

Twenty-five (25) eyes of 25 patients were recruited with all patients completing follow up to 3 months. Fifty‑two percent of patients underwent routine manual phacoemulsification surgery with the remaining 48.0% undertaking femtosecond-laser assisted surgery.

Overall, the mean age of patients was 74.3 ± 7.9 years (range 55 to 85 years) with 56.0% of the cohort female (40% male, 4% not identified). The preoperative demographic data is listed in (Table 1). The baseline CDVA ranged from 0.0 to 1.00 LogMAR (20/20 to 20/200). The DRSS grade of non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) was none 66.7%, mild 20.8%, moderate 12.5%. The average CMT at the time of surgery was 265.5 ± 38.5 microns. Overall, 16.0% of patients underwent pre-treatment with anti-VEGF prior to a confirmed period of stabilisation for 3 months prior to surgery. There were no adverse events from surgery.

Visual and Refractive Outcomes

The Efficacy Index was 1.32 indicating mean improvement in postoperative uncorrected visual acuity compared to preoperative corrected visual acuity. The 3-month postoperative visual outcomes of the cohort are shown in (Figure 3). Reflecting these values, 92.0% were within 0.50D and 100% within 1.00D of the spherical equivalent (SE) target refraction. Safety Outcomes

There were no adverse events during surgery.

Incidence of CME and Change in CMT

At 3 months, a clinical diagnosis of CME was present in 16.0% of all eyes (4/25 eyes). When analysed in terms of DRSS score, incidence of CME was 6.3% (1 eye) with a normal score and 37.5% (3/8 eyes) with a mild score. The overall change in CMT at 3 months is shown in (Figure 5). There was no change (or an improvement) in CMT in 8% of eyes, CMT increased 1 micron to 10 microns in 28.0% of eyes, CMT increased 11 microns to 25 microns in 24.0% eyes and CMT increased greater than 25 microns in 20.0% of eyes. A reduction in CMT was noted in 16% of eyes.

Distribution of change in central macular thickness (CMT) at 3 months.

The ratio of eyes with CME across DRSS classifications did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.053). Furthermore, in eyes which had previously undergone treatment with anti-VEGF, the incidence of CME was not found to be of statistical significance (p = 0.350).

Regression analysis demonstrated that none of the evaluated clinical variables significantly predicted the occurrence of CME. The change in overall CMT compared to baseline however was significantly predicted (r2=0.427, p= 0.015). Significant factors which contributed to change in CMT from baseline included age, previous treatment of maculopathy, baseline DRSS score.

There was no difference in incidence of CME between patients who underwent manual phacoemulsification surgery vs femtosecond laser -assisted surgery.

Incidence of DME and DME Recurrence

The incidence of new onset DME or DME recurrence was diagnosed in 8.0% (2 eyes) of cases at 3 months. The recurrence rate was lower in eyes with no or mild retinopathy (4.5%, 1/22) as predicted by baseline DRSS vs those eyes with moderate retinopathy (33.3%, 1/3). Correlation analysis indicated the risk of DME recurrence was higher in eyes with previous treatment (p < 0.001) and eyes with a moderate to severe DRSS score (p = 0.032).

Discussion

Cataract surgery in the setting of Type 2 DM has been shown to result in variable visual outcomes. Han et al, reports significantly greater improvements in CDVA in eyes with none or mild to moderate non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR).12 A systematic review by Kelkar et al reported improved visual outcomes in diabetic patients with early-stage cataract with a clear retinal fundus view where there was presumed early-stage retinopathy.13 Conversely, Mozaffarieh et al reported patients with advanced retinopathy showed minimal visual improvement.2 Overall visual recovery, even after uncomplicated cataract surgery and IOL implantation, would appear governed by the macular perfusion status, presence of DME and stage of retinopathy.

Our pilot study demonstrates that effective visual outcomes can be achieved in the presence of a visually significant cataract, irrespective of severity of NPDR, previous DME, and/or previous treatment with anti-VEGF if a standard protocol is followed.

A 3-month final refractive evaluation was utilized in this study which is comparable to other cataract studies of this nature where this is considered a standard timeframe for evaluating refractive stability. Also comparable is the use of the post-operative drop regimen, a combination of topical steroid, non-steroidal and antibiotics.

The incidence of CME in diabetic patients following uncomplicated cataract surgery in the literature ranges from 1.7% to 41.7%.4,5 We reported the incidence of clinically diagnosed CME in our study at 16% with no significant difference between eyes with mild or moderate NPDR. This is likely attributed to the small sample size in this pilot study but may also be related to our specific CME definition. Although the incidence of CME in eyes with mild vs moderate DRSS scores was not considered statistically significant, there remained a trend toward increased incidence of CME with increasing severity of retinopathy. Statistical analysis of CME incidence in mild vs moderate DRSS scores is suggestive of a trend toward increased incidence of CME with increasing severity of retinopathy. It is plausible that with more case numbers this is likely to reach statistical significance. This finding is suggestive of the notion that proceeding with cataract surgery in the presence of stable retinopathy, should lead to better visual outcomes. This is also supported in the current literature.14

In this study, there was no change in CME incidence in patients who underwent manual vs femtosecond laser-assisted surgery. Whilst it is theoretically postulated that laser pretreatment can lead to a release of inflammatory mediators possibly contributing to CME, there are likely to be several factors involved. This clinical aspect could be suitable for a larger study comparing these surgical modalities on the incidence of CME in this patient demographic.

The incorporation of the DRSS score in a surgical protocol can therefore serve to assist surgeons to accurately estimate probability of CME thus allowing effective preoperative patient counselling.

In our cohort, linear regression showed that overall change in CMT from baseline did have an impact on post-operative CME incidence. A normal range for CMT as measured by OCT typically falls between 170 and 250 microns. Notably, a further factor in the incidence of postoperative CME was a higher CMT value at baseline. It is plausible that in this patient demographic, patients with CMT measurements in the higher range of normal baseline pre-operatively may have a higher likelihood to develop subclinical CME post operatively as evidenced by mildly increased CMT seen in some patients.15,16

The incidence of DME in our study, which mostly reflected a recurrence of DME, was 8.0%, slightly higher than the previously mentioned reported incidence of 6.6%. Our study supports the current body of knowledge that DME recurrence is more likely in worsening retinopathy or in eyes with previous treatment of DME.

Several studies report that even with routine cataract surgery, the greatest impediment to visual gain is fluid accumulation in the macula.17 This may result from new onset of pseudophakic cystoid macular oedema or recurrence of DME. These two entities need to be differentiated. To our knowledge few studies in literature offer a definition of CME when evaluating outcomes in diabetic eyes following cataract surgery. This may impact on the interpretation of incidence of CME as reported in many of the previously mentioned studies. In our study CME and DME were distinctly defined using both clinical and OCT characteristics as mentioned in the Methods section. This adds to the accuracy of the data extrapolation of CME incidence and DME recurrence in our study despite the smaller study cohort.

Appropriate patient selection in this cohort of patients who are to undergo cataract surgery will ultimately determine optimal visual outcomes. A standard protocol can assist in achieving this with reference to a lower pre-operative DRSS, baseline CMT in the lower range of normal, and anti-VEGF treatment naïve for at least 3 months prior to surgery.

A limitation of this study was the small sample size. Furthermore, some baseline characteristics of this patient demographic were not considered including glycosylated hemoglobin status (HBa1c), other microangiopathy such as nephropathy, and overall glycemic control. Each of these factors may put patients at a higher overall risk of retinopathy or maculopathy affecting visual outcomes.18 A future study with a larger sample size taking these factors into consideration may further support our pilot analysis and provide translational information for surgeons and their patients.

Conclusion

Patients with Type 2 DM are undeniably at higher risk of visual compromise following routine, uncomplicated cataract surgery. Adhering to a reliable and repeatable surgical protocol as demonstrated in this study can improve the likelihood of a successful outcome after cataract surgery in this patient cohort.
