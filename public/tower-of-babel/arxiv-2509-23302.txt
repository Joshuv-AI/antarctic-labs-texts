Dual-Function Beam Pattern Design for Multi-Target ISAC Systems: A Decoupled Approach
Wilson de Souza Junior, Taufik Abrao, Amine Mezghani, Ekram Hossain

Abstract
We investigate beam pattern design problem for mono-static multi-user (MU) multi-point-target integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems, where a dual-function multiple-input multiple-output (DF-MIMO) base station (BS) performs downlink communication and radar sensing simultaneously. In ISAC systems, sensing and communication inherently compete for resources. As communication demand increases, the beam pattern is reshaped, which might degrade the direction of arrival (DoA) sensing accuracy, measured in terms of mean-squared error (MSE) and lower-bounded by the Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB) . Since conventional joint formulations of the sensing-based problem often overlook this trade-off, our work addresses it by decomposing the sensing-based problem into two subproblems (SPs) . This decomposition enables a more effective exploitation of the beam pattern’s physical properties, which we refer to as the Sensing-Guided Communication Dual-Function (SGCDF) beam pattern design. We further develop a low-complexity extension using the Riemannian Manifold Optimization (RMO) and convex closed-set projection. Simulation results confirm that the proposed method improves multi-target estimation accuracy, compared to traditional joint optimization strategies, by preserving the beam pattern, while the low-complexity version offers an excellent performance–complexity tradeoff, maintaining high accuracy with significantly reduced computational cost.



I
Introduction

The rapid evolution of wireless communication networks, coupled with the ever-increasing demand for intelligent and interconnected systems, drives the need for innovative technologies that transcend the traditional boundaries of communication. In this sense, future networks must not only provide high data throughput but also support emerging services that require real-time awareness and understanding of the surrounding environment. In this context, the upcoming
sixth-generation (6G)
wireless communication systems and
beyond six generation (B6G)
aim to encompass a wide range of advanced applications, such as autonomous vehicles, smart city infrastructure, industrial automation, and massive-scale
internet of things (IoT)
deployment. These applications require the simultaneous supply of ultra-reliable, high-speed, and precise environmental sensing, including object tracking and motion detection.

Motivated by these ambitious requirements, integrated sensing and communication (
ISAC
) has attracted significant attention in recent years. ISAC provides a promising paradigm that enables the joint use of
radio frequency (RF)
signals for both environmental sensing and data transmission concurrently. It merges traditional communication architectures with advanced sensing capabilities, by jointly designing a
dual-function radar-communication (DFRC)
beam pattern
[
1
]
. By unifying these functionalities within a shared hardware,
ISAC
offers significant gains in
spectral efficiency (SE)
, energy and spectrum saving, and sensing capabilities
[
2
]
.

Recent advances in signal processing, optimization techniques, and machine learning, jointly with some new enabling techniques such as
reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)
, have contributed to the development of
ISAC
-enabled systems, paving the way for the aforementioned applications. Despite this progress, the realization of practical
ISAC
systems remains a difficult task due to challenges in terms of resource allocation. Unlike traditional communication systems, where resources are optimized solely for payload data transmission,
ISAC
systems must simultaneously satisfy the performance requirements of both sensing and communication functionalities, which often have conflicting objectives. Key challenging problems, including beam pattern design, spectrum and interference management, security enhancement, and trade-offs between sensing accuracy and communication reliability, can be highly affected due to the conflicting nature of both functionalities.

Furthermore, resource allocation in
ISAC
systems is challenging due to the lack of a unified performance metric capable of jointly quantifying the quality of both communication and sensing functionalities. In this sense, since the communication and sensing functionalities use distinct nature criteria, the resource allocation problem in
ISAC
communication systems can be treated as a
multi-objective optimization (MOO)
problem

[
3
]
. Specifically, communication performance is typically evaluated using conventional metrics such as
sum rate (SR)
, individual user rate, or even
bit error rate (BER)
, denoted here as
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})
, whereas sensing performance is assessed through metrics such as estimation accuracy, detection probability, beam pattern gain, etc., denoted as
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})
. Both of these performance metrics are functions of the allocated system resources
\mathcal{R}
, which may include transmit power, bandwidth, time slots, etc.

To address the challenges imposed by the multi-objective nature of resource allocation in
ISAC
systems, two widely adopted approaches. The first approach involves prioritizing one objective function while constraining the other. This leads to two different strategies, denoted in this work as
sensing-based optimization
and
communication-based optimization
. This formulation is beneficial in scenarios where one functionality is mission-critical, and the other must remain within acceptable bounds. The second approach involves constructing a composite objective function, denoted herein as
weighted sum-based optimization
, that jointly incorporates both
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})
, enabling a more balanced optimization strategy.

ISAC
systems.

I-B

Motivations and Contributions

Designing dual-function beam patterns for
ISAC
systems is inherently challenging, as it requires a careful balance between sensing and communication performance. The overall performance is usually characterized by sensing metrics—such as beam pattern gain,
MI
, and the
MSE
—alongside communication metrics like the achievable rate, or the total sum-rate. Among these, specifically for sensing, the
CRLB
stands out in multi-target scenarios, since it establishes a fundamental lower bound on the estimation
MSE
, making it a powerful criterion for guiding effective beam pattern design. However, conventional approaches often adopt a joint formulation that optimizes sensing and communication objectives concurrently. Since the
MSE
is strongly influenced by beam pattern gain, joint dual-function
CRLB
-based formulations may yield inefficient beam patterns under stringent communication requirements, since they overlook the physical pattern. To address these limitations, we propose decomposing the sensing-based problem into two subproblems, which allows overcoming the inherent drawbacks of conventional joint formulations. This decoupled formulation enables us to account for two different sensing metrics: the
CRLB
as well as the beam pattern gain, while ensuring the communication performance.

Furthermore, unlike prior works that rely on alternative formulations to handle the
CRLB
in multi-target scenarios, which introduce relaxation gaps and increase computational complexity, our approach directly tackles the original problem formulation. The main contributions of this paper are threefold and summarized as follows.

•

First, we consider the dual-function beam pattern design for a mono-static
DF-MIMO
system, in which a multi-antenna
BS
performs downlink multi-user communication and
DoA
estimation for a multiple target scenario. The sensing-based optimization problem is split into two sub problems. The first sub-problem is dedicated to designing an adequate beam pattern specifically for sensing by solving the
CRLB
minimization problem, while the second one focuses on transforming the obtained beam pattern into a dual-function beam pattern by projecting it on the communication-feasible set by minimizing the mismatch with respect to the obtained sensing beam pattern. We show that both of the subproblems can be convex in the covariance matrix domain. This strategy is referred to as the
SGCDF
methodology.

•

Building upon the
SGCDF
framework, we further develop a low-complexity solution by solving the optimization problem in the beamforming domain, which reduces the problem’s dimensionality. To efficiently tackle this problem, we integrate
RMO
with a projection onto a convex closed set. This combination accelerates convergence, resulting in a computationally efficient algorithm well-suited for practical implementation. Moreover, we demonstrate that the proposed low-complexity solution covers the non-dedicated sensing stream case by appropriately projecting the initial point solution. This generalization broadens the applicability to different practical system configurations.

•

Finally, extensive simulations show that this two-step procedure better preserves the
inherent physical properties of the sensing-only beam pattern while meeting minimum communication
quality of service (QoS)
requirements. By decoupling sensing and communication design, it outperforms traditional joint optimization methods, achieving significantly improved
DoA
estimation accuracy. Additionally, the proposed low-complexity method is shown to offer an excellent performance-complexity trade-off.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces the system model. Section
III
presents the problem formulation, including the system assumptions, objectives, and constraints that guide the proposed approach. In Section
IV
, we describe the proposed solution to the formulated problem. Simulation results and a comprehensive performance analysis are presented in Section
VI
. Finally, Section
VII
concludes the paper and outlines potential directions for future research.

II
System Model and Assumptions

We consider a mono-static
ISAC
system with a coherence interval of
L
transmission blocks. In such a system, a
BS
with co-located
M_{T}
transmit antennas and
M_{R}
receive antennas serves
K
communication
user’s equipments (UEs)
while simultaneously detecting
T
targets. The dual function MIMO (DF-MIMO)
BS
is assumed to operate in full-duplex mode with perfect self-interference mitigation
[
11
]
. The transmitted signal
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}}
at time slot
\ell
can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{W}_{C}\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{W}_{S}\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}_{\ell},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}\triangleq[c_{1,\ell},...,c_{K,\ell}]^{T}
is the communication symbols intended to the communication
UEs
during the
\ell
-th transmission block, and
\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}
is the probing signal devoted to radar sensing. Finally,
\boldsymbol{W}_{C}=[\boldsymbol{w}_{c,1},\boldsymbol{w}_{c,2},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{c,K}]\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}\times K}
is the communication beamforming matrix applied to
\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}
, and
\boldsymbol{W}_{S}=[\boldsymbol{w}_{s,1},\boldsymbol{w}_{s,2},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{s,N_{t}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}\times M_{T}}
is the sensing beamforming matrix, applied to
\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}
. Let us define the joint dual-function beamforming augmented matrix
\boldsymbol{W}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{C},\boldsymbol{W}_{S}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}\times(K+M_{T})},
and the joint
dual-function data augmented matrix
\mathbf{\tilde{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C},\mathbf{S}\end{bmatrix}^{T}=[\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}_{L}]\in\mathbb{C}^{(K+M_{T})\times L}
.

Fig. 1:
Illustration of the
ISAC
system considered in this work.

When
L
is sufficiently large,
L\rightarrow\infty
, the communication data symbols can be assumed to be independent, satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{C}^{H}]=\frac{1}{L}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{C}^{H}=\mathbf{I}_{K}
. At the same time, the sensing signal is also carefully designed to satisfy
\frac{1}{L}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{S}^{H}=\mathbf{I}_{M_{T}}
. We assume that there is no correlation between sensing data and communication data, which means that
K+M_{T}
data streams are uncorrelated, satisfying

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}^{H}]=\frac{1}{L}\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}^{H}=\mathbf{I}_{K+M_{T}}.

(2)

Therefore, the sample covariance matrix of beam pattern
\boldsymbol{X}=[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}]
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{X}=\frac{1}{L}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{H}=\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{H}=\boldsymbol{R}_{C}+\boldsymbol{R}_{S}.

(3)

It is noteworthy that, in the adopted scheme, both communication signals and sensing beam patterns are precoded by
\boldsymbol{W}
, meaning that the covariance matrix has as many as possible
degree of freedom (DoF)
, i.e., the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{R}_{X}
is full-rank, given as
M_{t}

[
4
]
.

II-A

Communication Model

It is assumed that each
UE
is equipped with a single antenna. The signal vector received by the
k
-th
UE
is given by

\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{X}^{H}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}+\boldsymbol{n}_{k},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{k}
represents
additive white gaussian noise (AWGN)
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L})~\forall k
, while
\boldsymbol{h}_{k}
denotes the channel vector corresponding to
UE

k
. The
signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR)
of
UE

k
can be written as

\operatorname{SINR}_{k}=\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{w}_{c,k}\right|^{2}}{\sum_{j=1,j\neq k}^{K}\left|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{w}_{c,j}\right|^{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{M_{T}}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{w}_{s,t}|^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}},

(5)

where the term
\sum_{i=1,i\neq k}^{K}\left|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{w}_{c,i}\right|^{2}
is the multi-user interference and the term
\sum_{t=1}^{M_{T}}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{w}_{s,t}|^{2}
denotes the interference caused by the dedicated sensing signal to the communication. The normalized rate of the
k
-th
UE
can be given as

R_{k}=\log_{2}(1+\operatorname{SINR}_{k}).

(6)

The communication rate serves as a key metric for communication performance.

We assume a Rician fading environment for the communication users’ channel links. The channel matrix
\boldsymbol{H}=[\boldsymbol{h}_{1},\boldsymbol{h}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{h}_{K}]
, which is assumed to be perfectly known

1

1

Perfect
channel state information (CSI)
is assumed to evaluate the best achievable performance of the multi-target
ISAC
system. The proposed scheme can also be adapted to imperfect
CSI
scenarios, where beamforming is optimized to maximize the ergodic rate based on statistical
CSI
, which is more robust to estimation errors than instantaneous
CSI
.
, is given as

\boldsymbol{h}_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{k}\kappa}{\kappa+1}}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{{\rm LoS}}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{k}}{\kappa+1}}\boldsymbol{h}_{2,k}^{{\rm NLoS}},

(7)

where
\kappa
is the Rician
K
-factor, and
\beta_{k}
is the path-loss for
BS
-
UEs
link. Besides,
\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\rm LoS}=\boldsymbol{a}_{T}(\theta^{\operatorname{UE}}_{k})
represents the channels associated with the
line-of-sight (LoS)
component of
k
-th
UE
, while
\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\rm NLoS}
represents the channels associated with the
non-line-of-sight (NLoS)
component, whose entries are
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
and follow the complex Gaussian distribution with zero mean and unit variance.

II-B

Radar Model

The
DF-MIMO

BS
sends a sequence of probing signals, which will be reflected by the target back to the receiver antennas at the
DF-MIMO

BS
; the unknown
DoA
is then estimated by processing the received echo signals. The collection of received signals over
L
samples at the
DF-MIMO

BS
is thus given by

\boldsymbol{\tilde{Y}}=[\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{L}]=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{G}(\theta_{t})\right)\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{N},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{N}=[\boldsymbol{n}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{n}_{L}]
denotes the
AWGN
and
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{C}
is the complex-valued
radar cross section (RCS)
, which also includes the effect of round-trip path loss coefficient.

We consider a
LoS
propagation environment where no obstruction/scatter exists between the
DF-MIMO

BS
and each possible target location. The overall channel from the
DF-MIMO
transmitter to the
DF-MIMO
receiver via target reflection is given by

\boldsymbol{G}(\theta_{t})=\boldsymbol{a}_{R}(\theta_{t})\boldsymbol{a}_{T}^{H}(\theta_{t}),\quad\forall t

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{a}_{T}(\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{R}(\cdot)
are the transmit and receive steering vectors, respectively:

\boldsymbol{a}(\theta)=[1,e^{j\pi\sin(\theta)},\dots e^{j\pi(N-1)\sin(\theta)}]^{T}.

(10)

From a radar perspective, the goal is to estimate the parameters after
L
transmission blocks, where the performance is measured in terms of the
MSE
:

\operatorname{MSE}=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}\|^{2}\right],

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
denotes the vector of unknown parameters to be estimated. This paper focuses thoroughly on the estimation of target’s azimuth angles
\theta_{t}
, since they are embedded in a non-linear structure involving the array response. This is different from the
RCS

\alpha_{t}
, which can be estimated through straightforward techniques, such as
maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)
, once the azimuth angles are estimated
[
17
,
18
]
,
[
7
]
. Therefore, the unknown parameters vector, which remains constant over the coherence interval, is defined as

\boldsymbol{\zeta}=[\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 1}
.

Since a closed-form expression for
MSE
is hard to obtain, the
CRLB
is often assumed to be an optimization criterion, since it is a lower-bound for the variance of an unknown estimated with any unbiased estimator
[
19
]
:

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{t})\geq\operatorname{CRLB}(\theta_{t})=\Big[\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\Big]_{t,t},

(12)

with
\operatorname{Var}(\cdot)
being the variance operator and
\boldsymbol{F}
being the Fisher information matrix, where the element in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column is expressed as
[
20
]

[\boldsymbol{F}]_{i,j}=\frac{2L}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\left\{\alpha_{i}^{*}\alpha_{j}\operatorname{tr}\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{G}}(\theta_{j})\boldsymbol{R}_{X}\dot{\boldsymbol{G}}^{H}(\theta_{i})\right)\right\},

(13)

where
\dot{\boldsymbol{G}}(\theta_{t})=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{G}(\theta_{t})}{\partial\theta_{t}}
is the partial derivative of
\boldsymbol{G}(\theta_{t})
w.r.t.
\theta_{t}
. Besides,
\boldsymbol{F}
is a real symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
.

One can see that the performance of estimating
\theta_{t}
is critically dependent on the dual function beam pattern
\boldsymbol{X}
, which is, by itself, essentially related to the design of the dual function beamforming matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
. In the following, we will investigate the optimization of the
DF-MIMO
beamforming matrix.

III
Problem Formulation and Classical Solution

III-A

Problem Formulation

We consider an
ISAC
system, where the sensing task is integrated into the conventional communication system. Accordingly, the considered system is designed to optimize the covariance matrix of the dual-function transmit signal,
\boldsymbol{R}_{X}
, to guarantee
QoS
requirements for each
UE
in terms of rate, while simultaneously minimizing the overall estimation error of the
DoA
associated with each target. To this end, we employ the
Trace-Opt
criterion
[
21
]
, which represents the sum of each of the
CRLB
values regarding the
DoA
estimation. The joint sensing-communication problem is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{W}}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\right)

(14a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}

\displaystyle~\left[\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\right]_{m,m}\leq\frac{P_{\max}}{M_{T}}\quad\forall m

(14b)

\displaystyle~\Gamma_{k}|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}|^{2}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{K+M_{T}}|\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{H}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}|^{2}+\sigma^{2},~\forall k

(14c)

where
P_{\max}
denotes the total transmit power budget,
\Gamma_{k}=\left(1+\gamma_{k}^{-1}\right)
, with
\gamma_{k}=2^{R_{\min,k}}-1
, and
R_{\min,k}
is the minimum rate for the
k
-th
UE
. Constraint (
14b
) limits the consumed power per antenna, and constraint (
14c
) assures a minimum rate for each communication user.

The presence of the matrix inversion in the objective function, combined with the non-convexity of constraint (
14c
), makes problem (
14
) non-convex and challenging to solve. In what follows, we first revisit the classical
SDR
solution, and then propose a new method to solve (
14
).

III-B

Classical SDR Solution

By denoting
\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}=\boldsymbol{w}_{c,k}\boldsymbol{w}_{c,k}^{H}~\forall k
, and
\boldsymbol{R}_{C}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}
, problem (
14
) can be lifted to the covariance matrices domain, and cast as the following
semidefinite program (SDP)
:

\displaystyle\underset{\{\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}\},\boldsymbol{R}_{X}}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle~\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\right)

(15a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}

\displaystyle~\left[\boldsymbol{R}_{X}\right]_{m,m}\leq\frac{P_{\max}}{M_{T}}\quad\forall m

(15b)

\displaystyle~\hskip-22.76219pt\Gamma_{k}\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\right)\geq\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{R}_{X}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\right)+\sigma^{2}~\forall k

(15c)

\displaystyle~\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}\in\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}}_{+},~\forall k~\boldsymbol{R}_{X}-\boldsymbol{R}_{C}\in\mathcal{S}^{M_{T}}_{+}

(15d)

\displaystyle~\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k})=1,~\forall k.

(15e)

Constraint (
15d
) ensures the feasibility of
\boldsymbol{R}_{X}
,
\boldsymbol{R}_{S}
, and
\boldsymbol{R}_{C}
, and constraint (
15e
) is the non-convex unity rank constraint. Due to the construction of its objective function as well as the unity rank constraint (
15e
), (
15
) is a non-convex problem and challenging to solve. A reformulation will be required to to make the problem convex. First, we consider the
SDR
of the problem by removing (
15e
). To tackle the objective function, we should notice that the function
\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{X}^{-1})
is matrix decreasing on the positive definite matrix space
[
22
,
23
]
; thus, by defining
\boldsymbol{U}
, with
\boldsymbol{F}\succ\boldsymbol{U}
, we have that
\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{F}^{-1})<\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{U}^{-1})
. Therefore, the relaxed problem can be equivalently expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\underset{\{\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}\},\boldsymbol{R}_{X},\boldsymbol{U}}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}\right)

(16a)

\displaystyle~\eqref{eq:c11},\eqref{eq:c22},\eqref{eq:c3},

\displaystyle~\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{U}\succ\boldsymbol{0}.

(16b)

Problem (
16
) is a convex problem and can readily be solved by any convex solver. Due to the
SDR
, the solution of (
16
),
\{\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{C,k}\}
might not be rank-1. When the obtained solution is rank-1, the optimal solution is obtained; otherwise, we can extract a feasible solution for (
16
), denoted as
\boldsymbol{\tilde{w}}_{c,k}
, from the obtained
\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{C,k}
as follows
[
24

:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{w}}^{\star}_{c,k}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{C,k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k})^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{C,k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k},~~\forall k

(17a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{R}}^{\star}_{C,k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\tilde{w}}^{\star}_{c,k}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{w}}^{\star H}_{c,k}},\quad\forall k

(17b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{S}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{X}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\tilde{R}}^{\star}_{C,k}.

(17c)

Although the classical
SDR
methodology can provide a feasible solution for (
14
), we identify two major problems with respect to this solution. Both limitations are discussed in the following:

1.

High complexity:
The domain transformation from vectors
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{c,k}\}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}}
to covariance matrices
\{\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}\}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}\times M_{T}}
introduces optimization variables of higher dimensions, which intuitively results in prohibitively high computational complexity;

2.

Inefficient beam pattern:
Since the objective of the formulated problem is to minimize the overall
CRLB
associated with the estimation of
DoA
, while ensuring a minimum rate requirement for communication users, it does not explicitly account for the physical irradiated power toward the targets. This limitation may,
mainly under high communication rates
, lead to undesired power redistribution, weakening the main lobes in the direction of targets and jeopardizing the estimation accuracy.

Motivated by the aforementioned limitations, the next section proposes a new formulation for the sensing-based problem and discusses its solution.

IV
Revised Formulation and Proposed Solution

In order to circumvent the inherent limitations of the
SDR
solution, in this section, we introduce our proposed solution for problem (
14
). Firstly, we introduce a discussion about the general idea, which we term as
SGCDF
beam pattern, and then formulate the associated problems. Subsequently, a low-complexity algorithm is proposed.

IV-A

SGCD Beam Pattern Design

We adopt a structured approach that decouples the original problem (
14
) into two sequential sub problems (
SP
). The central idea is to formulate a sensing-based optimization problem, denoted as
\mathcal{SP}_{I}
, and optimize the beam pattern exclusively for sensing performance, while ignoring communication constraints. This sensing-based optimization yields an adequate beam pattern only for sensing and is utilized in the following as the sensing-reference beam pattern. In the second stage, we construct a communication-feasible beam pattern by projecting the previously obtained solution onto the set of beam pattern that satisfy the communication requirements, which is achieved by solving
\mathcal{SP}_{II}
. This projection step aims to preserve the sensing characteristics as much as possible, seeking to mitigate the
inefficient beam pattern
limitation while ensuring communication feasibility. This is different from the formulated approach in Eq. (
14
), which solves the joint problem simultaneously and aims to minimize the
CRLB
value, sacrificing the beam pattern.
We refer to this strategy as the
SGCDF
strategy.

IV-A
1
Subproblem I

The first
SP
focuses on designing a beam pattern that is well-suited for sensing functionality. To this end, we adopt a
CRLB
minimization framework, formulated without considering the communication constraints, as previously discussed. The choice of the
CRLB
is motivated by its practical relevance in target tracking scenarios, where it can be evaluated using coarse estimates of the target parameters. Additionally, the resulting beam pattern from this
SP
ensures sufficient beam pattern gain for each target. Inspired by the discussion in Section
III-B
, the first
SP
can be formulated as the following convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{SP}_{I}:\quad\underset{\boldsymbol{R}_{X},\boldsymbol{U}}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}\right)

(18a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}

\displaystyle\quad\eqref{eq:c11},\eqref{eq:c3},\eqref{eq:c5}.

(18b)

\mathcal{SP}_{I}
can be efficiently solved with any standard convex optimization solver. Let
\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{X}
be the optimal solution of (
18
). In the next step, we formulate the second
SP
in order to address the so-called
inefficient beam pattern
limitation, which may arise when the joint beam pattern is altered to accommodate communication constraints, by solving problem (
14
).

IV-A

MSE
-based problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{SP}_{II}:\quad\underset{\{\boldsymbol{R}_{C,k}\},\boldsymbol{\tilde{R}}_{X}}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\quad\|\boldsymbol{R}^{\star}_{X}-\boldsymbol{\tilde{R}}_{X}\|_{F}^{2}

(19a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}

\displaystyle\quad\eqref{eq:c11},\eqref{eq:c22},\eqref{eq:c3}.

(19b)

Problem (
19
) is also a convex problem, and can be solved with, e.g., CVX. If the optimal solution

is rank-1, then it is the optimal solution for the problem; otherwise, (

IV-B

Low-Complexity SGCDF Beam Pattern

In this section, we propose a low-complexity optimization framework to further reduce the computational burden of the original formulation of the proposed
SGCDF
. To this end, the
SGCDF
approach is addressed directly in the beamforming domain, rather than in the covariance matrix domain. Leveraging the Riemannian manifold optimization (
RMO
) approach, we begin by formulating and solving
\mathcal{SP}_{I}
, Eq. (
18
), as an unconstrained optimization problem. For
\mathcal{SP}_{II}
, (
19
), based on the obtained beam pattern in
\mathcal{SP}_{I}
, we incorporate a
second-order cone (SOC)
-projection within the
RMO
framework to enforce the communication-feasibility. The formulation and solution strategies for both
SPs
are revisited in what follows, under the lens of the
RMO
-based approach.

IV-B
1
Subproblem I

Following the formulation provided by Eq. (
14
), the
CRLB
minimization problem for the beamforming domain can be expressed as

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{W}}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\quad f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})\triangleq\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\right)

(20a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}

\displaystyle\quad\eqref{eq:c1}.

(20b)

Firstly, we should notice that the objective function of (
20
) is non-convex. Besides, it is intuitive that the transmit power should be fully utilized to enhance both functionalities’ performance
[
7
,
11
]
. To this end, we assume that each transmit antenna operates in the full power mode; thus, the constraint (
14b
) can be written as

\left[\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\right]_{m,m}=\|[\boldsymbol{W}]_{m,:}\|^{2}=\frac{P_{\max}}{M_{T}},\quad\forall m.

(21)

To address the challenging problem (
20
), we explore the fact that (
21
) forms a smooth manifold in the Euclidean space, also known as complex
\mathcal{OB}

[
25
,
26
]
, and defined as

\mathcal{OB}=\left\{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}\times(K+M_{T})}\big|~\|\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right]_{m,:}\|=\rho,~\forall m\right\}.

(22)

The
\mathcal{OB}(M_{T},K+M_{T})
defines the set of all
M_{T}\times(K+M_{T})
complex matrices with
\rho=\sqrt{P_{\max}/M_{T}}
row norm, i.e., each row of
\boldsymbol{W}
lies on a complex hyper-sphere of radius
\rho
.
This geometric structure allows us to reformulate the problem (
20
) as a constrained problem in Euclidean space to an unconstrained one over the manifold
\mathcal{OB}(M_{T},K+M_{T})
:

\mathcal{SP}^{LC}_{I}:\quad\underset{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{OB}}{\operatorname{minimize}}\quad f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}).

(23)

To tackle problem (
23
) with reduced computational complexity, we propose employing a gradient-descent-based algorithm.
Analogous to classical gradient descent in Euclidean spaces, this approach involves two fundamental operations: determining a descent direction that respects the manifold’s geometry and selecting a suitable step size along that direction. These operations are iteratively applied until the convergence criterion is met.

In the Riemannian setting, the manifold can be locally linearized around any point
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{OB}
via the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}}
, defined as
[
27
, Eq. 3.21]
:

\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}}(\boldsymbol{X})=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{T}\times(K+M_{T})}\Big|~\left<\boldsymbol{W,\boldsymbol{X}}\right>=0\right\},

(24)

where
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
is a Riemannian metric defined as the real inner product in the linear space of matrices
[
27
, Eq. 3.14, Eq. 3.16]
:

\left<\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X}\right>=\Re\left\{\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{X}^{H}\right)\right\}.

(25)

Within this framework, the Riemannian gradient
\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})
, is obtained by projecting the Euclidean gradient,
\nabla f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})
onto the tangent plane
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}}
, using the operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}}}(\cdot)
, expressed as follows:

\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}}}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{X}-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Re\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{X}^{H}\right)\odot\mathbf{I}_{M_{T}}\right\}\boldsymbol{W}.

(26)

Therefore, the Riemannian gradient can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}}}(\nabla f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})),

(27)

\displaystyle=\nabla f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Re\Big\{\nabla f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\odot\mathbf{I}_{M_{T}}\Big\}\boldsymbol{W}.

Besides, the Euclidean gradient of
f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})
in (
20
) can be computed as follows:

\nabla f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W})=2\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{W},

(28)

with
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
being an auxiliary matrix computed as follows:

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}[\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}]_{t,t}\left(\sum_{i\neq t,j\neq t}^{T}[\boldsymbol{F}_{t}^{-1}]_{i,j}\boldsymbol{A}_{j,i}-\sum_{i,j}^{T}[\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}]_{i,j}\boldsymbol{A}_{j,i}\right),

(29)

with
\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}\triangleq\frac{2L}{\sigma^{2}}\alpha^{*}_{i}\alpha_{j}\dot{\boldsymbol{G}}^{H}(\theta_{i})\dot{\boldsymbol{G}}(\theta_{j})
, and
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
being identical to
\boldsymbol{F}
except its
t
-th column has been replaced by
\mathbf{e}_{t}
, with
\mathbf{e}_{t}
denoting the
t
-th column vector of identity matrix.

Equipped with the Riemannian gradient obtained via projection mapping, we can deploy a steepest-descent procedure to find a locally optimal solution for (
23
). However, the steepest descent algorithm is known to exhibit slow convergence in practice. Hence, to accelerate convergence
conjugated gradient (CG)
algorithm is adopted. The Riemannian generalization of the
CG
, denoted as
Riemannian Conjugate Gradient (RCG)
, updates the descent direction
\boldsymbol{D}
at the
\ell
-th iteration as

\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}-\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}),&\ell=0\\
-\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})+\beta_{\ell}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}}}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell-1}),\hskip-4.2679pt&\operatorname{otherwise}\end{cases}

(30)

where
\beta_{\ell}
is the
CG
parameter at the
\ell
iteration. We should notice that
\operatorname{grad}f(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})
belongs to tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}}

and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell-1}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell-1}}
. Therefore, they
cannot be added together in a straightforward way. To this end, we should utilize
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}}}(\cdot)
, which
denotes the transport mapping operation needed to add/subtract points on different tangent spaces. Here, the Fletcher–Reeves rule is adopted to update
\beta
, which is expressed as
[
28
]
:

\beta_{\ell}=\frac{\|\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})\|^{2}_{F}}{\|\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell-1})\|^{2}_{F}}.

(31)

After the search direction
\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell}
is established at
\ell
-th iteration, the beamforming matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
can be updated as:

\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell+1}=\mathcal{R}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell}\right),

(32)

where
\mathcal{R}
is the
retraction
operator which is employed to map the point in the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}}
back onto the
\mathcal{OB}
, and
\alpha_{\ell}
is the step size at the
\ell
-th iteration.
\alpha_{k}
is selected by obeying the Wolf conditions
[
29
]
:

\displaystyle f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell+1})\leq f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})+c_{1}\alpha_{\ell}\left<f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}),\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell}\right>

(33a)

\displaystyle\left|\left<\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell+1}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell+1}}}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell})\right>\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\leq c_{2}|\left<\operatorname{grad}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}),\boldsymbol{D}_{\ell}\right>|,

(33b)

where inequality (
33a
) is the well-known Armijo condition
[
28
]
. By ensuring the Wolf conditions, the objective function is non-increasing. The retraction operator for the
\mathcal{OB}
can be computed as

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{W})=\rho(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\odot\mathbf{I}_{M_{T}})^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{W}.

(34)

With these operations,
RCG
proceeds iteratively and is expected to converge to at least a sub-optimal solution of (
23
). The optimization steps of
\boldsymbol{W}
for solving
\mathcal{SP}_{I}^{LC}

VII
Conclusion

A novel framework has been proposed for beam pattern design in a multi-target
MU

DF-MIMO

ISAC
system, where two
SPs
are properly designed. The first one is formulated to design an appropriate radar beam pattern, and the second guarantees the feasibility of the communication applications. Both of the problems are solved in the covariance domain by traditional convex tools. To further reduce the complexity of the problem, we have considered the problem in the beamforming domain. By modeling the per-antenna constraint as a manifold and the
SINR
constraints as
SOC
, we have proposed an efficient solution by utilizing
RMO
. The numerical results illustrate that the proposed schemes outperform the traditional schemes in terms of sensing performance. Besides, the proposed low-complexity algorithm offers performance similar to the original design at a much lower complexity, and therefore, it offers a favorable performance-complexity trade-off.
