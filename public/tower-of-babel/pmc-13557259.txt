Shared Pathways between Psoriasis and Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: Federica Trovato, Camilla Chello, Simone Amato, Antonio Di Guardo, Emanuele Amore, Luigi Bennardo, Antonio Giovanni Richetta, Teresa Grieco, Steven Paul Nisticò, Giovanni Pellacani, Annunziata Dattola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriasis and atopic dermatitis are leading examples of chronic inflammatory dermatoses with distinct clinical manifestations, pathogenesis, and demographic patterns. However, recent research has revealed overlapping immunological pathways that could serve as promising therapeutic targets.ObjectivesTo synthesize the principal immunological, epidermal barrier, and microbiome pathways shared by psoriasis and atopic dermatitis and to discuss their potential translational and therapeutic implications.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review of the literature on PubMed from January 2000 to June 2024, using keywords such as “psoriasis,” “atopic dermatitis,” “cytokine pathways,” “T cells,” and “environmental triggers.” Studies were selected based on their relevance to shared mechanisms between these diseases, focusing on cytokine profiles, immune cell activity, and skin barrier dysfunction.ResultsOur review identified significant overlaps in cytokine-mediated pathways, particularly the IL-17/IL-22 axis, which mediates keratinocyte hyperproliferation and inflammation in both diseases. T cell dysregulation, including Th17 and Th22 activity, plays a pivotal role in maintaining chronic inflammation. Both conditions also exhibit impaired skin barrier function, linked to filaggrin loss-of-function mutations in atopic dermatitis and abnormal keratinocyte differentiation in psoriasis.ConclusionThese findings suggest that targeting shared mechanisms, such as the Th17 and Th22 pathways, along with barrier repair strategies may enable dual therapeutic benefits. This review underscores the potential for integrative treatment approaches and personalized medicine in managing chronic inflammatory skin disorders.Keywords: Inflammatory skin diseases, Atopic dermatitis, Psoriasis, Shared pathways, Immune dysregulation

Introduction

Psoriasis and atopic dermatitis (AD) have traditionally been viewed as distinct and opposing inflammatory skin disorders, with psoriasis characterized by Th1/Th17-mediated immunity and AD dominated by Th2 polarization (1,2). However, emerging evidence increasingly supports the concept of a shared immunological continuum rather than a strict dichotomy. [3,4]Psoriasis is a chronic, systemic inflammatory disease affecting approximately 2% of the global population, clinically manifesting as erythematous and desquamative plaques. AD, with a prevalence of about 2.6%, represents the most common chronic inflammatory dermatosis, typically presenting with xerosis, eczematous lesions, and lichenification. [5,6]

Despite their apparent clinical and immunological divergence, recent findings reveal substantial overlap in cytokine profiles, particularly involving IL-17, IL-22, and IL-23, and in downstream epithelial responses. The acute phase of psoriasis is primarily mediated by Th1 and Th17 cells, whereas acute AD is driven by Th2 cytokines and IgE hyperproduction. [3,7]Nevertheless, chronic AD may display Th1/Th17 activity, while psoriasis may exhibit Th2 and Th22 involvement, highlighting a dynamic interplay rather than a fixed immune polarity. Furthermore, concomitant psoriasis and AD (psEma), reported in up to 16% of small cohorts, underscores the possibility of a shared pathogenic substrate. [8,9] Beyond T cell differentiation, both diseases encompass alterations in genetic susceptibility, epidermal barrier function, and microbiome composition. Advances in multi-omic and single-cell technologies have identified converging inflammatory pathways within the epithelial immune microenvironment (EIME), where keratinocytes emerge as active immunologic players. (10–12)Recognizing psoriasis and AD as interconnected entities within an immune-epithelial spectrum may refine current disease models and foster the development of targeted therapies addressing their shared mechanisms.

Objectives

This review aimed to synthesize the principal overlapping pathways underlying both diseases and to outline the translational implications of this evolving perspective.

Methods

We conducted a narrative review of the literature using the PubMed database to explore the immunological networks involved in skin inflammatory dermatoses. Our search focused on publications from January 2000 to October 2024, using the keywords “psoriasis,” “atopic dermatitis,” “cytokine pathways,” “T cells,” and “environmental triggers.” Studies were included if they provided primary data or significant insights into shared immunological pathways between psoriasis and atopic dermatitis. Research was organized into three thematic directions: i) common immunological pathways, particularly overlapping cytokine profiles and their epithelial microenvironmental functions; ii) the roles of T cells and dendritic cells; iii) shared environmental triggers that initiate inflammatory cascades. Data extraction prioritized cytokine profiles, genetic markers, and clinical outcomes. No formal quality assessment was performed, as this review aimed to synthesize key themes rather than systematically grade evidence.

Results

Psoriasis

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease that can affect skin, appendages, and joints as well as the whole system. [2,13] In fact, psoriasis is linked to several systemic diseases such as arthritis, Crohn’s disease, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) as well as to psychiatric disorders, metabolic syndrome, and renal disorders. [14–18]The pathogenesis of psoriasis is complex and not entirely understood, as it is considered an immune-mediated disease, involving the interplay between innate and adaptive immune systems, genetic predisposition, and environmental triggers. [19]

Genetic Factors and Immunological Pathways

The genetic background of psoriasis was first identified in 1976, when Karvonen et al. discovered the link between the onset of the disease before the age of 40, the human leukocyte antigen HLA-Cw6 and familial positivity for psoriasis. [20–23] This was followed by epidemiological studies showing a higher incidence in patients with a positive family history compared to the general population and a strong concordance in identical twins. The first genes to be associated with familial psoriasis were the psoriasis susceptibility genes 1–12 (PSORS1–12), of which PSORS-1 was the first to be linked to psoriasis and the one that showed the closest association with the disease. PSORS-1, in fact, encodes for the human leukocyte antigen Cw6 (HLA-Cw6), is located in 6p21.3, and is found in 20–50% of affected subjects. [22,23] PSOR-S genes and their functions have been summarized in Table 1. HLA-Cw6 has been found to be associated with increased latent period of psoriasis and arthritis, improved response to therapies (methotrexate and ustekinumab)(24), and improved cardiometabolic risk profile. Since the completion of the Human Genome Project in 2001, genomic discoveries have led to the identification of other genes linked to psoriasis, involving the innate immune system, the adaptive immune system, and the skin barrier.[20,22,25] The genes involved in psoriatic disease can be classified into three functional groups: skin barrier dysfunction, innate immune system, and adaptative immune system. [23]

Skin Barrier Dysfunction

The dysregulation of the skin barrier represents a key element in psoriasis’ pathogenesis. [26,27]Psoriatic plaque lesions result from keratinocyte hyper-proliferation and mis-differentiation, leading to structural damage and barrier disorders. According to immunohistochemistry and immunoblot studies, the expression levels of proliferation markers such as cyclin D1, cyclin E, pRb (Retinoblastoma protein), and Ki-67 are augmented in psoriatic lesions and decrease after treatment. In psoriatic skin, keratinocytes reach the stratum corneum from the basal layer in 6–8 days, whereas in normal skin, this process takes 40 days. (28–31). One of the major alterations found in psoriasis is the aberrant expression of keratins, with an induction of keratins pattern that is similar to the wound-healing response. Reflecting the abnormal differentiation state of keratinocytes, in psoriatic lesions, keratin 14 is expressed across the epidermis, despite its normal expression in the basal layers; it often links with keratin 10, which is normally expressed in suprabasal layers but is co-expressed with keratin 14 in psoriatic skin samples. Additionally, the presence of keratins 6, 16, and 17 (32),which are not ordinarily expressed in the epidermis, in keratinocytes represents a hallmark of psoriasis. The hyperproliferation of keratinocytes and aberrant expression of keratin profiles lead to finger-like papillae, with hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis (absent granular layer and nucleated corneocytes) and production of a number of chemoattractant proteins, including S100, beta-defensins, ICAM-1, CD40, IL-8, IP-10, and HLA-DR. [33,34]Keratinocytes also produce growth factors for endothelial cells such as VEGF and PDGF, which are responsible for the characteristic psoriatic angiogenesis, which results in the formation of aberrant blood vessels in the dermis: increased number, lumen diameter, and tortuosity. This leads to defects in the barrier function and classic psoriatic corneous desquamation. The dysfunction of the psoriatic skin barrier is also linked to the disruption of key proteins and lipids in the epidermis. Tight junction (TJ) proteins such as ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-4 are poorly expressed, contributing to increased water loss and inflammation. Adherens junctions (e.g. E-cadherins) are also under-expressed. Corneodesmosin is abnormally expressed deeper in the psoriatic epidermis. [33–43].

Innate Immune System

The innate immune system is a major player in the pathogenesis of psoriasis. In particular, some data in literature indicate a greater influence of the innate immune system in severe psoriasis and of the adaptive immune system in mild psoriasis. [21,44,45]The cells historically considered to be involved are mainly dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, antigen-presenting cells (APC), and neutrophils. They produce cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interferon α (IFN-α), interleukin-12 (IL-12), and interleukin-23 (IL-23), which play major roles in the development of psoriasis. Cytokines involved in the innate immune pathogenesis of psoriasis and their relative functions are described in Table 2 [46–50]. DCs are heterogeneous cells subclassified depending on site, origin, and function. Subtypes of DCs are identified in the blood and skin according to the inflammatory stage. In psoriatic lesions, plasmacytoid DCs and myeloid DCs are observed in the dermis. DCs play a pivotal role in the onset of psoriasis thanks to their ability to recognize and present self-nucleotides resulting from cellular distress, triggering the activation and proliferation of T cells through the identification of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) I antigen complexes. [51]Furthermore, DCs have the innate ability to recognize nucleic acids, which trigger the release of IFN-α and IFN-β, which are linked to the pathophysiology of psoriasis. The way macrophages contribute to the pathogenesis of psoriasis is mostly unknown. [52–55]Macrophages play a crucial role in the development and maintenance of psoriasis. They differentiate into different subtypes, mainly M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory), in response to various stimuli in the microenvironment. In psoriasis, there is a greater presence of M1, indicating a tendency towards pro-inflammatory responses, contributing to the characteristic chronic inflammation. The interaction between innate immune cells and keratinocytes (KC) is pivotal for disease progression. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of macrophages and DCs in both the onset and maintenance phases of psoriasis. The exact mechanisms of action of M1 macrophages in psoriasis remains unclear, but research has been conducted on the role of adenosine-adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) pathway, as A2AR is expressed in various immune cells, including macrophages, with pro-inflammatory action.[53,56–59] Recently, more attention has been paid to other immune cells involved in the innate immune response, including group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s), γδ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and natural killer T (NKT) cells. [53,60–63]These cells are linked to interleukin-17 (IL-17), which is also produced by the adaptative immune system and will be discussed more in the dedicated paragraph. ILC3 cells are a type of lymphoid cell, involved in early immune response and maintenance of barrier functions, which produce cytokines such as IL-17 and IL-22, contributing to the pathogenesis of psoriasis. In normal skin, ILC3s are mainly found in the superficial dermis and are generally NCR- (natural cytotoxicity receptor negative). In psoriatic lesions, there is an increased proportion of NCR+ ILC3s, which produce high levels of IL-22 in response to pro-inflammatory cytokine stimulation. Moreover, NCR–ILC3s may shift to NCR+ in response to cytokines secreted by KCs and DCs. [64–69]

Adaptative Immune System

Psoriasis is a TH1- and TH17-mediated disease associated with the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α ( tumor necrosis factor-α), IL-17A, IL-22, and IFN-γ. The IL-23/IL-17 immune pathway plays a crucial role in the maintenance of the disease. [70–72]The IL-17 pathway is responsible for the defense against fungi and extracellular bacteria. IL-17A acts as a bridge between the innate and adaptive immune systems, initiating immune responses at mucosal surfaces, with recruitment of neutrophils, which secrete IL-17, which amplifies the immune response. IL-17 acts directly on keratinocytes, reducing their proliferation and the production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines. This further stimulates inflammatory cells, fueling IL-17 production. There are six subtypes of IL-17, whose function is described in Table 3.[70–75]

The IL-17 pathway is closely linked to IL-23, which is believed to predominate in the pathogenesis of psoriasis. IL- 23 supports the survival, differentiation, and activation of Th17 cells, mast cells, and neutrophils, which release IL-17 into the affected skin. [67,76–81]

Atopic Dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is the most common chronic cutaneous inflammatory disease, with a prevalence of 2.6% worldwide. AD is characterized by itchy, eczematous cutaneous manifestations, often associated with rhinoconjunctivitis, allergic asthma, and food allergies, in a so-called “atopic march”.[1,82] Although the pathophysiology of AD is still not completely understood, immunological dysregulation and epidermal barrier dysfunction are the two primary theories that have been identified in the literature [6]. Defects in filaggrin (FLG) have been related to skin barrier dysfunction in AD; other associations include transglutaminases, keratins, and intercellular proteins. A compromised cutaneous membrane facilitates allergens and noxae to penetrate into skin and mucous membranes. [83–87] Recent evidence has also emphasized the contribution of the skin microbiome in sustaining chronic inflammation through dysbiosis and microbial metabolites capable of influencing keratinocyte and immune cell signaling. These findings have led to a broader view of AD as a systemic immune-epithelial disorder rather than a purely epidermal disease(10,27,88).

Genetic Factors

Atopic dermatitis is a prototypical multifactorial disease resulting from the interplay between genetic predisposition, epithelial dysfunction, and immune dysregulation. The pivotal role of genetics is evidenced by a high rate of heritability, estimated at around 75%. [89–92]One of the first genes identified in AD pathogenesis is filaggrin (FLG). [89,93–95] Patients with common ichthyosis, an inherited autosomal dominant disorder caused by loss-of-function (LOF) mutations in FLG, often present with atopic features. Although FLG mutations represent the most recognized genetic alteration associated with AD risk, they are detected in only 10–40% of patients. Other differentiation markers, such as loricrin, are also reduced in both lesional and non-lesional skin of AD patients and contribute to maintaining skin integrity; their alteration results in barrier impairment.[90,91,96] LOF mutations in FLG are associated with a characteristic AD phenotype characterized by increased transepidermal water loss (TEWL), enhanced allergic sensitization, and a tendency toward chronicity, often complicated by local infections. Genomic studies have also demonstrated associations between CD207 and Th2-related cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 (located on chromosome 15q31.1) as well as variations in IL4R, IL13, JAK1, JAK2, and TYK2. Furthermore, genes encoding TSLP and IL7R, implicated in the early stages of the disease, appear to be associated with both European and African populations developing AD(97–101).

In addition to genetic predisposition, increasing evidence highlights the role of epigenetic mechanisms in AD pathogenesis. [93,102,103]Alterations in DNA methylation, histone acetylation, and non-coding RNA expression (including microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs) have been shown to regulate genes involved in skin barrier integrity and immune signaling. AD-specific methylation changes have been identified particularly within lesional epidermis, correlating with altered transcription of genes related to keratinocyte differentiation and innate immunity.[102–105]

Histone modifications, especially acetylation, may further contribute to barrier dysfunction and inflammation, and preliminary studies suggest potential therapeutic implications for histone deacetylase inhibitors such as belinostat. Moreover, dysregulation of microRNAs such as miR-155, miR-146a, and miR-124 appears to modulate Th2 cytokine production and NF-κB–mediated inflammation, whereas altered expression of long non-coding RNAs (e.g., MALAT1) may influence keratinocyte proliferation and cytokine release.[ 102,103]

Collectively, these findings underscore that beyond static genetic mutations, epigenetic plasticity dynamically shapes the inflammatory and barrier phenotypes of AD, offering new opportunities for biomarker discovery and targeted therapeutic interventions.

Immunological Pathways

Although AD has traditionally been viewed as a paradigmatic Th2-driven immune disorder, growing evidence reveals the involvement of Th1, Th17, and Th22 responses in its complex inflammatory network. [106,107]Barrier impairment, whether genetically determined or environmentally induced, leads to the activation of keratinocytes, now recognized as key immune sentinels of tissue homeostasis. Once activated, keratinocytes release epithelial-derived cytokines such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL-25, and IL-33, which in turn stimulate resident dendritic cells and initiate a cascade of adaptive immune activation.[108,109] These cytokines promote the differentiation of naïve T lymphocytes into Th2 cells, resulting in the production of IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, and IL-13. [109]However, numerous studies have demonstrated that the cutaneous cytokine milieu evolves over the course of the disease: acute lesions are dominated by Th2 cytokines, while chronic lesions display increased expression of Th1-related molecules such as IFN-γ and TNF-α as well as Th17- and Th22-associated cytokines, including IL-17A, IL-17C, and IL-22. Activated T cells in AD express skin-homing markers such as cutaneous lymphocyte antigen (CLA) and CCR4, mediating epidermal infiltration and chronic inflammation.[109–113]

The cytokines TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33 also activate group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), which act as early amplifiers of type 2 inflammation through the release of IL-5 and IL-13. Studies have shown that ILC2s are significantly increased in AD lesions and peripheral blood compared with healthy controls, and animal models confirm their pivotal role: in C57BL/6 mice treated with vitamin D analogs, an AD-like phenotype develops, with elevated IL-5, IL-13, and cutaneous accumulation of CD25+IL-33R+ ILC2s.[64,65,114–118]

Recent transcriptomic studies have refined the immunological classification of AD, distinguishing between extrinsic and intrinsic endotypes. While Th2 activation predominates in extrinsic AD and correlates with disease severity and serum IgE levels, intrinsic AD exhibits stronger Th1, Th17, and Th22 signatures, with elevated expression of IFN-related genes (CXCL9, CXCL10, MX1) and cytokines such as IL-17A and IL-22. [107,119–122]This molecular heterogeneity underscores that AD is not a single disease entity but a spectrum of immune dysregulation shaped by genetic, environmental, and microbial factors.

Skin Barrier Dysfunction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) represents the prototype of diseases characterized by epidermal barrier dysfunction, involving both the skin and mucosal surfaces. A key genetic factor linked to AD is the FLG loss-of-function (LOF) mutation, coding for profilaggrin, which interacts with keratin intermediate filaments to flatten keratinocytes into their terminal shape, maintaining epidermal strength and integrity. Profilaggrin is subsequently processed into filaggrin, whose degradation products act as natural moisturizing factors (NMFs), contributing to water retention, regulation of cutaneous pH, and reinforcement of the stratum corneum. [26,27,123,124]

Increased TEWL due to impaired TJs, particularly those involving claudin-1, claudin-8, and claudin-13, plays a key role in AD pathogenesis. Altered filaggrin synthesis reduces the expression of loricrin, involucrin, corneodesmosin, and keratins 1 and 10, resulting in increased skin pH, decreased hydration, and enhanced permeability to allergens and microbes. Lipid composition is also compromised, with altered expression of enzymes regulating free fatty acid and ceramide synthesis in the stratum corneum, favoring colonization by Staphylococcus aureus and other opportunistic microorganisms.[26,123,125]

Beyond its structural role, the epidermis actively participates in immune regulation. Keratinocytes sense environmental and microbial stimuli through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as TLRs and NLRs, leading to the release of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-33, TSLP) that amplify Th2-mediated inflammation. This epithelial–immune crosstalk perpetuates chronic inflammation and contributes to disease persistence even after apparent clinical remission.[126,127]

Skin barrier dysfunction also amplifies the impact of environmental noxae in AD patients. The concept of the exposome, encompassing pollutants, climate, allergens, diet, stress, and microbiota, reflects the bidirectional interaction between the environment and health, shaping lifelong gene-environment dynamics in disease development. In AD, the exposome modulates the interplay between the impaired epidermal barrier, the skin microbiome, and immune dysregulation. [128–132]Recent studies have introduced the notion of a “cutaneous exposome signature” or cutaneotype, integrating microbiome, metabolome, and environmental exposure profiles as potential determinants of individual disease trajectories.[133–137]

Ultimately, disruption of the epidermal barrier triggers a chronic inflammatory process reinforced by microbial dysbiosis, oxidative stress, and neuroimmune imbalance. The resulting vicious cycle sustains disease flares and highlights the need for therapeutic strategies targeting not only immune pathways but also the restoration of barrier homeostasis and microbiome equilibrium(108,138–144).

Shared Pathways and Mechanisms

The traditional view of psoriasis and atopic dermatitis (AD) has long framed them as opposing immune disorders: psoriasis driven by Th1/Th17 polarization and AD dominated by Th2 immunity. However, recent insights increasingly challenge this dichotomy, suggesting that both conditions share convergent inflammatory and molecular circuits within a common epithelial–immune framework.[2,13,145]

Immunology

The immunopathogenesis of atopic dermatitis (AD) and psoriasis now appear as part of a dynamic spectrum rather than a binary model. Entities such as “psoriasiform eczema” and “AD-psoriasis overlap” exemplify this immunological continuum.[146–149] Both diseases display infiltration of Th1, Th17, and Th22 cells, with key cytokines (IL-17A, IL-17C, IL-22, IL-23) driving keratinocyte hyperactivation, epidermal remodeling, and chronic inflammation. [75,76,81,141,150–154]

Experimental studies demonstrate that Th17 cells can promote IgE production by naïve B cells, linking Th17 polarization to classical Th2 pathways. Chronic AD, much like psoriasis, shows increased expression of IL-17C, IL-17E ( IL-25), and IL-17F, though IL-17E seems particularly relevant in AD pathogenesis by amplifying barrier impairment and Th2 inflammation through keratinocyte and ILC2 activation. [155–157]The convergence of Th17, Th22, and Th1 immunity is especially prominent in intrinsic, pediatric, and Asian AD phenotypes, supporting the hypothesis of a mixed inflammatory endotype. [16,158–160]

In parallel with T helper cells, ILCs have emerged as key players in both diseases, bridging innate and adaptive immunity.[155–157,161–164] These cells are grouped into ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3 subsets, reflecting the cytokine profiles of Th1, Th2, and Th17/Th22 cells, respectively. In AD, ILC2s predominate and are activated by epithelial-derived cytokines such as TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33, released upon barrier disruption. Activated ILC2s produce IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, thereby amplifying type 2 inflammation, eosinophil recruitment, and pruritus. Elevated ILC2 levels have been demonstrated in AD lesional skin and in patients with FLG mutations, underscoring their role as early amplifiers of epithelial immune signaling.[155]

Conversely, in psoriasis, ILC3s constitute the dominant subset and release IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-22, cytokines central to keratinocyte proliferation and epidermal thickening. Increased frequencies of NKp44+ ILC3s have been observed in both lesional and non-lesional psoriatic skin, correlating with disease severity and decreasing after anti-TNF therapy. Experimental data indicate that IL-17 and IL-22 derived from ILC3s rather than from T cells may be sufficient to induce psoriasiform inflammation.[69,165]

Altogether, these findings highlight that ILCs act as innate counterparts of T helper subsets, orchestrating early immune responses and maintaining chronic inflammation. Their activation within the EIME strengthens the concept of AD and psoriasis as interconnected disorders sustained by shared innate and adaptive immunologic circuits. [64,115,123,133,166]

Skin Barrier Dysfunction

Although AD is the prototypical barrier-defect disorder, it shares with psoriasis profound structural and functional abnormalities of the epidermal barrier. In AD, primary defects include filaggrin insufficiency, tight-junction fragility (notably claudin-1/-8/-13 and occludin), and depletion of ceramides and free fatty acids, which drive increased TEWL, elevate surface pH, and facilitate microbial dysbiosis with Staphylococcus aureus overgrowth. Altered expression of loricrin, involucrin, corneodesmosin, and keratins 1/10 further compromises cornified-envelope formation and barrier integrity.

In atopic dermatitis, tight-junction dysfunction is a pivotal element in the barrier defect. [167,168]Reduced expression of claudin-1 (observed in both lesional and non-lesional skin) correlates with increased TEWL, enhanced allergen penetration, and greater clinical severity. Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) and IL-33 suppress the expression of claudin-1 and claudin-23, while IL-17 downregulates ZO-1, claudin-1, and claudin-4, contributing to acute barrier impairment. [169–172]Mechanical injury such as scratching further decreases claudin-1 and reinforces the itch–inflammation–barrier damage cycle. TJ disruption also alters interactions with Langerhans cells, facilitating dendrite penetration across the barrier and promoting allergen uptake. Moreover, downregulation of claudin-1 within hair follicles may convert follicular ostia into preferential entry routes for pathogens and antigens. These alterations, intensified by S. aureus colonization, perpetuate inflammation through epithelial alarmins and ILC2 activation. Overall, AD is characterized by a Th2/Th17-driven loss of claudin integrity and functional sealing capacity, linking TJ instability to the chronic epithelial–immune loop.[125,173–176]

In addition to structural protein defects, AD displays a marked alteration in epidermal lipid biosynthesis. [177,178] Expression of key lipidogenic enzymes, including ELOVL1, CerS3, acid sphingomyelinase (aSMase), and β-glucocerebrosidase (GBA), is significantly reduced in lesional epidermis, leading to a deficiency of long-chain ceramides and altered lipid lamellae organization. [137,177–179]These metabolic changes, primarily driven by IL-4 and IL-13, cause the accumulation of short-chain ceramide species and disruption of the corneocyte lipid envelope, further amplifying TEWL and barrier dysfunction. Restoration of enzymatic lipid synthesis through topical or systemic modulation (e.g., JAK inhibition, PPAR activation) is therefore emerging as a complementary therapeutic target alongside immune modulation. [179,180]

In psoriasis, tight-junction remodeling appears as an early event rather than a secondary phenomenon. In nascent lesions, occludin and ZO-1 extend beyond the granular layer, while claudin-1 and claudin-7 are downregulated and claudin-4 displays aberrant cytoplasmic localization. [181–183]Pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) induce a biphasic modulation of TJ resistance, initial reinforcement followed by decline, mirroring ex vivo observations of dynamic relocalization. Near transmigrating neutrophils, most TJ proteins are downregulated, facilitating leukocyte passage through the epidermis. This cytokine-driven remodeling results in early barrier leakage, enhanced antigen ingress, and amplification of IL-17/IL-22-mediated inflammatory circuits.[181–183]

Lipid metabolism is likewise profoundly altered in psoriasis. Reduced activity of ceramide-synthetic enzymes such as CERS6 and β-glucosylceramidase disrupts sphingolipid homeostasis, whereas upregulation of SPTLC2 reflects a compensatory but inefficient attempt to restore ceramide balance. The net effect is an abnormal ceramide profile, particularly a reduction in long-chain and ω-hydroxy ceramides, accompanied by disturbed lamellar structure and diminished lipid cohesion. [184–186]Cytokines such as IL-17A and TNF-α further perturb this enzymatic network, linking inflammatory signaling to metabolic derangement of the epidermis. [180,187]

Both conditions exhibit increased TEWL and a disturbed lipid architecture of the stratum corneum, albeit through distinct upstream drivers: genetic and epithelial in AD, proliferative and inflammatory in psoriasis.[145,188,189] Across AD and psoriasis, barrier breakdown enhances antigen and irritant access, activates keratinocyte pattern-recognition receptors (TLRs, NLRs), and induces epithelial alarmins (TSLP, IL-25, IL-33). This, in turn, fuels Th2/Th17/Th22 circuits within the epithelial immune microenvironment (EIME), creating a bidirectional barrier-inflammation-microbiome loop that sustains chronic disease. [123,166,190] Conceptually, this convergence supports therapeutic strategies that restore lipid and claudin balance, reinforce TJs, and modulate cytokine signaling to break the cycle.

Role of Skin Microbiome

The skin microbiome encompasses the symbiotic microorganisms that coexist with the human host, contributing to homeostasis without causing harm. Both the cutaneous and intestinal microbiota are key modulators of the immune system, maintaining a bidirectional relationship whereby immune signals influence microbial composition, and microbial shifts can drive immune activation and inflammation. Disruption of this equilibrium has been implicated in the onset and maintenance of inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis and AD. [10,191,192]

When microbes breach the epidermal barrier, they are recognized by pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) on keratinocytes and resident immune cells, including TLRs and NLRs. Activation of these receptors triggers the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, which in turn induce keratinocyte secretion of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). AMPs, together with cytokines and microbial metabolites, further shape adaptive immune responses by promoting T-cell differentiation and modulating antigen presentation.[ 140,193,194]

Commensal bacteria play an equally important protective role. Through the release of specific metabolites and surface molecules, they promote epithelial differentiation, regulate complement activity, and support the expansion of mucosa-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells and other regulatory lymphocyte subsets. Commensal-derived short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan metabolites can also induce epigenetic and transcriptional changes in keratinocytes, reinforcing immune tolerance and barrier integrity. [192,195–197]

In AD, the skin microbiome is typically dominated by Staphylococcus aureus, whose abundance correlates with disease severity, alongside S. epidermidis, S. haemolyticus, and non-Malassezia yeasts such as Candida albicans and Cryptococcus diffluens. Dysbiosis extends to the gut, characterized by elevated Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Clostridium, and Escherichia coli, with concomitant depletion of Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Ruminococcus, and Bifidobacterium species. [10,123, 191,198,199]

Psoriatic skin exhibits a partially overlapping microbial profile, with reduced Actinobacteria and Cutibacterium, but enrichment of Corynebacterium, Staphylococcus, and Streptococcus. In the gut, psoriasis-associated dysbiosis includes increased Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Faecalibacterium, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Megamonas, and Roseburia, alongside a relative decrease in Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Prevotella, and Eubacterium. [10,38,39,200–202]

Together, these microbial alterations intersect with barrier dysfunction and immune polarization, establishing a self-sustaining epithelial–microbial–immune loop. This reciprocal relationship between dysbiosis and inflammation underscores the rationale for microbiome-targeted strategies as potential adjuncts to cytokine-directed therapies in both AD and psoriasis.

Conclusions

Psoriasis and AD, while classically considered distinct entities with divergent immune profiles, increasingly emerge as part of a shared spectrum of chronic epithelial–immune disorders. Both conditions are characterized by an interplay between genetic predisposition, epidermal barrier dysfunction, and immune dysregulation within the EIME. In AD, Th2-driven inflammation predominates, particularly in acute phases, yet Th1, Th17, and Th22 pathways contribute significantly to chronicity and disease heterogeneity. Psoriasis, conversely, is primarily orchestrated by the Th1/Th17/IL-23 axis, sustaining keratinocyte hyperproliferation, and epidermal remodeling. [203–206]Despite these immunological differences, both diseases converge in key mechanisms: activation of innate immune sensors, ILC-driven cytokine release, and disruption of structural and lipid barrier components that perpetuate inflammation. [3,147,207]

Skin barrier dysfunction represents a central shared feature, though its origin diverges. In AD, filaggrin deficiency, tight-junction fragility, and impaired lipid synthesis underlie increased TEWL, microbial dysbiosis, and allergen penetration. In psoriasis, accelerated keratinocyte turnover and altered lipid enzymatic profiles produce a similarly compromised barrier, with secondary amplification by IL- 17A, IL-22, and TNF-α signaling. These processes establish self-sustaining loops of barrier breakdown, immune activation, and microbial imbalance, defining both disorders as systemic epithelial–immune diseases rather than isolated cutaneous conditions. [3,208–210]

Therapeutically, these shared mechanisms underscore the rationale for integrated strategies combining immune modulation and barrier restoration. Cytokine-targeted biologics (e.g., IL-17, IL-23, and IL-4/13 inhibitors) have transformed disease management, while adjunctive interventions aimed at restoring lipid metabolism, reinforcing tight junctions, and rebalancing the microbiome offer complementary benefits. Emerging agents such as bimekizumab, a dual IL-17A/F inhibitor, and JAK inhibitors [99,211,212]highlight this translational convergence, showing promising efficacy in both psoriasis and selected AD endotypes.

Future research should focus on delineating endotype-specific pathways, particularly the context-dependent role of IL-17 and IL-22 in intrinsic versus extrinsic AD, and on understanding how microbiome diversity and lipid enzymatic networks shape disease chronicity and treatment response. Unraveling these common inflammatory and barrier circuits will not only refine patient stratification but also foster the development of next-generation therapies targeting shared epithelial–immune pathways across chronic inflammatory dermatoses.
