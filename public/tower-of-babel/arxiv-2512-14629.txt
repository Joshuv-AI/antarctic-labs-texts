MuseCPBench: an Empirical Study of Music Editing Methods through Music Context Preservation
Yash Vishe ⋆ Eric Xue ⋆ Xunyi Jiang ⋆ Zachary Novack Junda Wu Julian McAuley Xin Xu †

Abstract
Music editing plays a vital role in modern music production, with applications in film, broadcasting, and game development. Recent advances in music generation models have enabled diverse editing tasks such as timbre transfer, instrument substitution, and genre transformation. However, many existing works overlook the evaluation of their ability to preserve musical facets that should remain unchanged during editing — a property we define as Music Context Preservation (MCP). While some studies do consider MCP, they adopt inconsistent evaluation protocols and metrics, leading to unreliable and unfair comparisons. To address this gap, we introduce the first MCP evaluation benchmark, MuseCPBench , which covers four categories of musical facets and enables comprehensive comparisons across five representative music editing baselines. Through systematic analysis along musical facets, methods, and models, we identify consistent preservation gaps in current music editing methods and provide insightful explanations. We hope our findings offer practical guidance for developing more effective and reliable music editing strategies with strong MCP capability 1 1 1 Our code for MCP evaluation is available in https://github.com/Yashvishe13/Music-context-preservation-benchmark .

Music Context Preservation

2.1
Evaluation Formulation

In this work, we introduce a unified evaluation framework to consistently assess music context preservation across editing systems.
Given an original music
x
and an editing instruction
e
, the editing system
\Theta
produces an edited music
\hat{x}=\Theta(x,e)
.
The instruction specifies only certain musical attributes that are expected to change, while all other attributes should remain preserved.
To evaluate whether MCP holds, we reorganize widely considered musical attributes into four music facets - Harmony, Rhythm & Meter, Structural Form, and Melodic Content & Motifs, covering fundamental dimensions of music.
For each facet, we define some corresponding metrics that measure the similarity between the reference
x
and the edited output
\hat{x}
along that dimension as shown in Figure
1
.
The detailed definitions of these facets and metrics are provided in §
2.2
.
This formulation offers a general and consistent pattern for evaluating MCP, enabling fair comparisons across diverse music editing techniques.

2.2
Music Facets and Metrics

For each piece of music, harmony, rhythm, structure form, and melodic content & motifs are key facets
[
7
]
.
Therefore, we evaluate the model from these four facets to measure the music context preservation.
Each facet is further examined with fine-grained metrics for a detailed evaluation.

Harmony.

Harmony is an important concept in music, representing the basic color of the music.
We measure this from high-level key and scale to low-level chords and chroma with three metrics.
As for key comparison, we use
circle-of-fifth distance

[
21
]
(normalized) where the circle of fifths is a way of organizing pitches as a sequence of perfect fifths; the distance is normalized, where 0 means harmonically identical and 1 means maximally distant.
As there is currently no consensus on chord evaluation metrics
[
13
]
, we adopt the
Major-Minor
score, which considers the chord root and whether it is major or minor, making it relatively robust and representative and it is normalized to the range [0–1], where 1 indicates complete agreement (same root and mode) and 0 indicates maximal difference.
Regarding pitch-class content, We use
dynamic time warping (DTW)
to measure similarity between chroma sequences. It is normalized to 0–1, where 1 means the two sequences are identical and 0 means completely different.

Rhythm & Meter.

As for the rhythm, we also use 3 metrics to ensure fault-tolerance and different levels of music pieces.
We measure the
beat difference
between the modified piece and original piece, which is a global value reflection. A value of 0 means there is no difference.
We also use
Beat F-measure
and
Information Gain
of the estimated beat-error histogram.
Beat F-measure measures how accurately estimated beats match the reference.
It ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 means perfect alignment and 0 means no correct hits.
Information Gain measures how much the estimated beats reduce information-theoretic entropy compared with a uniform-error baseline.
Higher values indicate better alignment beyond simple hit accuracy, and 0 means no improvement over random.
These two together offer both onset accuracy and temporal consistency under the
mir_eval
framework
[
13
]
.

Structural Form.

To assess higher-level musical organization beyond rhythm,
we evaluate the structural form such as intro, verse, and chorus of each piece.
To evaluate estimated structure’s quality, we use two representative metrics.
The first metric is the
boundary F-measure
.
It is calculated from the precision and recall of detected change points.
This metric shows how accurately the generated piece locates structural boundaries
[
17
]
.
The score ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 means perfect boundary detection and 0 means no correct boundaries.
The second is the
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)
, ranging from 0 to 1, which quantifies the agreement between estimated and reference segment groupings. ARI measures whether two frames belong to the same segmentation group. This clustering-based metric is grounded in the formulation of Rand’s
[
16
]
and applied to music segmentation in
[
9
]
.
A value of 0 means the grouping is no better than random and 1 means the two segmentations are exactly the same.
Together, boundary F-measure and ARI provide a concise yet comprehensive assessment of both boundary accuracy and higher-level structural grouping consistency.

Melodic Content & Motifs.

Melodic content and motifs depend on frame-level pitch accuracy and the preservation of recurring ideas.
We use three metrics:
voicing recall
,
motif overlap (3-gram) Jaccard
, and
motif overlap (3-gram) recall
.
Voicing recall measures how often melody in the reference is correctly predicted (0–1; 1 = all detected, 0 = none).
Motif overlap Jaccard measures shared melodic interval patterns (0–1; 1 = all match, 0 = none).
Motif overlap recall measures the fraction of reference patterns reproduced (0–1; 1 = all reproduced, 0 = none).
Such motif-based evaluation has been explored in
[
2
,
5
]
.

To comprehensively compare music context preservation of existing music editing works, we consider five representative systems with publicly available implementations: MusicGen, MusiConGen, MusicMagus, ZETA, and RefinPaint.
To ensure the authenticity and comparability of results, we adopt the evaluation protocols established by the existing baselines.

4
Experimental Results and Analysis

4.1
General Findings

Table
2
shows MCP evaluation results.

Vanilla.
The vanilla baseline achieves moderate scores across most facets.
For example, it obtains reasonable chroma similarity and structural alignment, and even outperforms some editing systems in voicing recall.
However, it performs poorly in Major-Minor Score and Motif Overlap Recall, indicating that purely generative outputs struggle to maintain some fine-grained musical attributes.
These observations highlight that while editing systems are expected to surpass Vanilla in MCP, some methods still fail to consistently outperform this vanilla baseline, underscoring the challenge of robust context preservation.

Methods.
Across the baselines, we observe clear differences in music context preservation after editing. RefinPaint performs best overall, particularly in melody and structure, while MusicGen shows notable weaknesses in rhythm/ meter. MusiConGen and ZETA achieve moderate balance across harmony and rhythm but struggle with melodic consistency.

4.2
Music Facet Analyasis

In this section, we further analyze the MCP capabilities for each music facet across the baselines.

Harmony.

MusicMagus and RefinPaint demonstrate very high Chroma DTW Similarity, suggesting strong retention of tonal alignment with the reference.
However, MusicMagus achieves this at the expense of rhythm (Beat F-measure = 0.030), which points to an architecture that may heavily optimize for spectral alignment while underweighting temporal structure.
Conversely, MusicGen, with the lowest Major-Minor Score, indicates weaker harmonic retention compared to methods like RefinPaint.
This may be due to MusicGen’s reliance on autoregressive generation, which can drift harmonically across longer spans.

Rhythm & Meter.

RefinPaint achieves the best Beat F-measure and Information Gain, suggesting it is particularly effective at aligning edits with the reference meter. Its diffusion-based iterative refinement architecture is well-suited to local rhythmic consistency, explaining these high scores.
In contrast, MusicGen struggles significantly, with the highest Delta BPM and relatively low Beat F-measure.
This instability reflects autoregressive architectures’ tendency to accumulate timing errors when tasked with longer or structurally altered generations.

Structural Form.

All editing systems achieve Boundary F-measure = 1, meaning segmentation boundaries are trivially preserved—likely due to explicit conditioning on edit points.
Differences emerge in the Adjusted Rand Index, where RefinPaint and ZETA outperform MusicMagus.
This suggests ZETA’s hybrid encoder-decoder structure may help maintain segment groupings, while MusicMagus overfits to tonal cues and disregards broader form.

Melodic Content & Motif.

RefinPaint outperforms with the highest Voicing and Motif Overlap Recall. Its iterative correction process likely helps preserve recurring motifs after edits. MusicGen and ZETA fare poorly here, suggesting that autoregressive & transformer-based editing struggle with long-range motif reappearance. MusiConGen balances harmonic and melodic preservation moderately, but its relatively low Motif Overlap Jaccard shows that while fragments of motifs are retained, their overlap with references remains partial.

Comprehensive Comparisons.
MusicGen and MusiConGen demonstrate strength in preserving local harmonic continuities, but overall perform relatively poorly compared to diffusion-based models.
We conjecture that the Transformer’s limited capacity for long-term memory also results in weaker motif retention, which explains its relatively low motif overlap scores.
By contrast, MusicMagus strongly prioritizes tonal similarity, achieving a high Chroma DTW Similarity, but this comes at the expense of temporal coherence, as seen in the system’s very low Beat F-measure.
Moreover, we strategically included RefinPaint, a refinement-based system, to validate our evaluation metrics. Unlike fully generative models, RefinPaint operates by iteratively correcting existing material, which naturally favors context preservation. As expected, this approach yields exceptionally high scores across harmony, rhythm, and motifs, not as a direct comparison to the generative systems, but as a confirmation that our metrics successfully capture the advantages of iterative correction in context-preserving tasks.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced MuseCPBench, a unified evaluation benchmark to evaluate the music context preservation ability across music editing methods.
By operationalizing music context evaluation across four core facets with widely used metrics, we establish a consistent methodology that enables fair and reproducible comparisons among diverse music editing methods.
Our empirical study of five representative editing systems provides a comprehensive analysis of music context evaluation.
We hope our findings can help understand the limitations and strengths of existing music editing tasks and give insights for constructing more reliable editing systems in the future.
