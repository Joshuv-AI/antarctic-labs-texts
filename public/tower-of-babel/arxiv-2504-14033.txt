Adaptive Diffusion Models for Sparse-View Motion-Corrected Head Cone-beam CT
Antoine De Paepe, Alexandre Bousse, Clémentine Phung-Ngoc, Youness Mellak, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract
Cone-beam computed tomography is a imaging modality widely used in head and neck diagnostics due to its accessibility and lower radiation dose. However, its relatively long acquisition times make it susceptible to patient motion, especially under sparse-view settings used to reduce dose, which can result in severe image artifacts. In this work, we propose a novel framework, joint reconstruction and motion estimation with adaptive diffusion model, that simultaneously addresses motion compensation and sparse-view reconstruction in head cone-beam computed tomography. Leveraging recent advances in diffusion-based generative models, our method integrates a wavelet-domain diffusion prior into an iterative reconstruction pipeline to guide the solution toward anatomically plausible volumes while estimating rigid motion parameters in a blind fashion. We evaluate our method on simulated motion-affected cone-beam computed tomography data derived from real clinical computed tomography volumes. Experimental results demonstrate that joint reconstruction and motion estimation-adaptive diffusion model substantially improves reconstruction quality over traditional model-based and learning-based baselines, particularly in highly undersampled scenarios. Our approach enables motion-robust and low-dose cone-beam computed tomography imaging, paving the way for improved clinical viability.



I
Introduction

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has become a valuable imaging modality for head and neck applications. CBCT systems use a flat-panel detector and a cone-shaped X-ray beam to acquire volumetric images in one rotation, and they are now widely used in dental, sinus, and cranial imaging. In these contexts, Cone-beam computed tomography often serves as a lower-dose and more accessible alternative to conventional multi-detector computed tomography
[
1
,
2
]
. Cone-beam computed tomography scanners are also more compact and can be deployed at the point of care (e.g. in outpatient clinics or operating rooms), offering advantages in portability and cost for head imaging
[
3

4

. While these features make cone-beam computed tomography particularly appealing, continued efforts are being made to further optimize patient safety—especially in terms of radiation dose.

One strategy to further reduce radiation exposure in head cone-beam computed tomography is to minimize the number of X-ray projections acquired. Due to the incompleteness of the data, the reconstruction problem becomes severely ill-posed, and standard algorithms—such as, for example, the Feldkamp-Davis-Kress
[
5
]
algorithm—yield severe streak artifacts, as shown in Figure
1

Traditional model-based iterative reconstruction algorithms leverage regularization, prior information, or compressive sensing techniques to compensate for missing projections
[
6

. More recently, deep learning methods have pushed reconstruction quality further. For example, convolutional neural network can be trained to refine Feldkamp-Davis-Kress-reconstructed sparse-view cone-beam computed tomography images
[

. However this kind of supervised methods can have drawbacks, especially when having out-of-distribution data. This limitation has motivated the use of less supervised and more robust alternatives.

On one hand, neural attenuation fieldss and related approaches

have been introduced for sparse view cone-beam computed tomography reconstruction. These methods leverages neural radiance fields—originally designed for synthesizing novel views in computer graphics—to represent the cone-beam computed tomography volume as an implicit neural function. On the other hand, methods at the intersection of model-based iterative reconstruction algorithms and deep learning approaches have been developed. Plug-and-play algorithms address image reconstruction inverse problems by integrating a restoration neural network into iterative processes, often with theoretical convergence guarantees. While early plug-and-play methods used Gaussian denoisers as restoration neural network, which are not well-suited for sparse-view cone-beam computed tomography,

Score-based diffusion models are generative models that involve training a prior probability distribution function by progressively converting a set of images into white noise using a stochastic differential equation. The reverse stochastic differential equation is then learned through score matching with a convolutional neural network, allowing for image generation starting from random white noise. Building on the foundational insights of diffusion models, diffusion posterior sampling methods

Despite advances in sparse-view reconstruction and in motion correction, there is a conspicuous gap in methods that tackle both simultaneously, especially in the case of head cone-beam computed tomography. Most prior works compartmentalize the problems: they either assume no motion when dealing with sparse-view data, or assume full-view data when correcting motion. Several studies have explored the integration of sparse-view acquisition with motion compensation in a reconstruction scheme
[

, or through deep learning approaches

. However, challenges persist—either in maintaining image quality in the former case or in extending these methods to three-dimensional applications in the latter. To the best of our knowledge, no existing approach integrates a modern learned-image prior into a joint reconstruction and motion estimation framework for the specific case head cone-beam computed tomography. This motivates this work: to offer a new solution that marries the latest advances in generative image modeling with a joint motion-image optimization scheme, to fill this crucial gap.

In this paper, we extend our previous adaptive diffusion model framework, developed for the sparse-view 4Dcomputed tomography context

In summary, the main contribution is the first motion-corrected sparse-view head cone-beam computed tomography framework, enabling dose reduction while preserving image quality. This is done by introducing a new alternating method, leveraging diffusion models as a probabilistic regularizer. The advantage of our method is that it can be applied for any geometry and any motion amplitude without retraining the model. To reduce the memory footprint, we employ wavelet diffusion model which allow to learn a probability distribution function defined on a compressed space such that the training of the diffusion model is less memory demanding.

In this context, an orthogonal discrete wavelet transform

IV
Discussion

This study highlights the potential of adaptive diffusion models for motion corrected sparse-view head cone-beam computed tomography imaging. That said, several limitations still need to be addressed before this approach can be fully applied in real-world clinical settings.

Although the computed tomography volumes we used are based on real acquisitions, we do not have direct access to the corresponding raw projection data. Hence, while we simulate realistic conditions such as Poisson noise and sparse-view acquisitions, we did not take into account for real-world effects, such as scatter or beam hardening. These factors can impact image quality and reconstruction fidelity in clinical practice, highlighting the need to validate our method on real projection data.

One major challenge lies in the computational cost requirements of the method. For example, running a 40-view joint reconstruction and motion estimation-adaptive diffusion model diffusion process takes about 20 minutes, which is not suitable for urgent clinical scenarios where rapid diagnosis is crucial, particularly in life-threatening situations. Our approach is currently better suited for follow-up cases.

To address this limitation, two potential strategies can be considered. First, motion estimation could be performed at a lower resolution than that of the reconstructed volume, which would reduce computational cost but may result in the loss of fine structural details. Second, incorporating differentiable X-ray rendering techniques—such as DiffDRR
[
44
]
—could enable direct optimization over the forward model’s geometry. This approach has the potential to eliminate the need for explicit volume warping followed by projection, thereby improving computational efficiency.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed sparse-view head cone-beam computed tomography image reconstruction with motion compensation. While prior research has made substantial progress in either sparse-view reconstruction or motion correction independently, our approach is the first to combine these two aspects within a single framework in the context of head cone-beam computed tomography, building upon our previous work on motion-corrected sparse-view 4DCT. By integrating a diffusion-based prior in a blind fashion, we enable robust reconstruction across arbitrary geometries and rigid motion patterns, while preserving fine anatomical details even under severely sparse acquisition conditions. Our proposed framework opens a promising path toward safer, lower-dose, and artifact-resistant cone-beam computed tomography imaging for the head, with strong potential for routine clinical practice.
