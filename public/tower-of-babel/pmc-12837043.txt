Impact of childhood trauma on depressive symptoms in bipolar adolescents: the mediating effect of sleep disturbance
Authors: Zhiying Wan, Yuan Hu, Yanfang Liu, Yue Sun, Zhongchun Liu, Meiyu Shen, Xiaofen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood trauma exacerbates bipolar disorder in adolescents, particularly intensifying depressive symptoms. While the potential mechanisms are unclear, sleep disturbances might mediate the link between childhood trauma and depression. This study investigated if sleep problems mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and depression severity in adolescents with bipolar disorder.Methods426 adolescents with bipolar I or II disorder were recruited from Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University in 2024. They completed questionnaires on the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Data analysis included Pearson correlations and a mediation analysis using the PROCESS macro to examine the proposed indirect effect.ResultsCorrelation analyses revealed significant positive associations between childhood trauma, sleep disturbance, and depressive symptoms (all p < 0.001). Controlled the variables of age, gender, and diagnosis classification, the mediation analysis confirmed that sleep disturbance served as a significant partial mediator in the relationship between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms. The indirect effect was significant (B = 0.071, 95% CI =[0.0495, 0.0944]), accounting for 38.59% of the total effect.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that sleep disturbance represented a critical mediating mechanism in the association between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms among adolescents with bipolar disorder. These results underscore the importance of systematically assessing and addressing sleep disturbances in both clinical care and therapeutic management, particularly for at-risk individuals with a history of early-life adversity.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Childhood trauma, Sleep disturbance, Depressive symptoms, Bipolar disorder, Adolescents

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe and recurrent psychiatric condition characterized by mood fluctuations, often resulting in significant lifelong functional impairments [1]. In recent years, the global morbidity rates of bipolar disorder have been notably high, especially among adolescents [2]. It affects approximately 2.9% of adolescents worldwide, with prior study indicating that 37.6% of individuals experience their initial onset of symptoms between the ages of 13 and 18 [3]. Compared to adults with bipolar depression, depressive episodes in adolescents are more complex and detrimental [4]. Moreover, in contrast to unipolar depression, children and adolescents with bipolar depression are more prone to additional comorbidities, such as behavioral disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder [5]. Depressive symptomatology predominates the longitudinal course of juvenile bipolar disorder and is the primary contributor to disability. These depressive episodes not only affect emotional states but also lead to functional deficits, reduced quality of life, and an increased risk of suicide [6, 7]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors and underlying mechanisms associated with depressive symptoms is crucial for formulating targeted intervention measures and improving clinical trajectories.

A history of childhood trauma (CT) is acknowledged as being associated with a more adverse progression of bipolar disorder and more severe depressive episodes [8]. Recent research highlights that childhood trauma, which includes emotional, physical, or sexual abuse, is linked to increased severity of depression and resistance to treatment [9]. Among various risk factors, childhood trauma emerges as a significant environmental stressor that is strongly correlated with the onset and unfavorable trajectory of bipolar disorder [10]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of affective disorders confirmed that adverse childhood experiences approximately double the risk of suicidal behavior [11]. The detrimental impact of CT extends beyond severe outcomes to affect fundamental daily functioning; for example, it is associated with impairments in social cognition, such as theory of mind and emotion identification (alexithymia), in patients with BD [12]. Nevertheless, the potential mechanisms connecting trauma to depressive symptoms remain inadequately understood.

Research has demonstrated a significant association between CT and the onset of sleep disorders, including difficulties in falling asleep, reduced sleep efficiency, and daytime dysfunction, with manifestations occurring both in the short and long term [13]. This correlational pattern is intricately linked to disruptions in neurobiological systems, particularly the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which is essential for the regulation of sleep-wake cycles [14]. Adolescents diagnosed with bipolar disorder exhibit elevated rates of sleep disturbances, such as insomnia and hypersomnia, which are further associated with emotional dysregulation and cognitive impairments [15]. A longitudinal study has shown that retrospective accounts of adverse childhood experiences are correlated with diminished sleep quality in young adults, indicating trauma-induced disruptions in circadian rhythms [16]. Additionally, a systematic review has highlighted a significant association between childhood sexual abuse and the prevalence of various sleep disorders in children and adolescents [17].

In individuals with BD, sleep disturbances are not merely secondary phenomena but are intrinsic characteristics intricately linked to the onset and prolongation of mood episodes [18]. Furthermore, sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among youth with BD and exhibit a strong correlation with the severity of depressive symptoms [18, 19]. Research indicates that even adult patients with bipolar disorder and depression in remission experience significantly poorer sleep quality and greater severity of insomnia compared to healthy individuals, with sleep disturbances independently associated with functional impairment [20]. Despite the well-documented associations between childhood trauma, sleep disturbances, and depression, there is a notable lack of studies that have explicitly investigated an integrated model in which sleep disturbances mediate the relationship between CT and depressive severity, particularly within the context of bipolar adolescents.

This study investigates the potential mediating role of sleep disturbances in the relationship between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms among adolescents diagnosed with bipolar disorder. Using cross-sectional data and mediation analysis, we aim to elucidate this plausible pathway and quantify the extent to which sleep disturbances contribute to the trauma-depression link. Establishing this significant association is a critical step toward identifying sleep as a viable intervention target for mitigating the burden of depression in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January to December 2024 at the outpatient and inpatient departments of psychiatry, Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University, China. The hospital is a large-scale comprehensive medical institution with a well-established adolescent mental health service system, ensuring a diverse and representative sample of adolescents with bipolar disorder.

Participants

The study population comprised adolescents aged 12 to 17 years who were primarily diagnosed with bipolar disorder. Diagnosis was established and confirmed by experienced child and adolescent psychiatrists using the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for bipolar disorder I or II as outlined in the DSM-5 [21]; (2) age between 12 and 17 years; (3) being in a clinically stable state for at least 4 weeks before screening, defined as no occurrence of a new acute mood episode (e.g., requiring emergency intervention or a marked change in treatment plan due to severe mania or depression) during this period; and (4) provision of informed assent along with written consent from a parent or legal guardian. The exclusion criteria included: (1) the presence of a major neurological disorder or intellectual disability; (2) a primary diagnosis of a schizophrenia spectrum disorder, autism spectrum disorder, or substance-induced mood disorder; and (3) incomplete responses on the key study measures.

Measures

General information questionnaire

A self-developed general information questionnaire was used to collect demographic data, including age, gender, diagnosis classification, previous medication usage, and common comorbidities. These factors were considered potential confounding variables that might influence the relationships between childhood trauma, sleep disturbance, and depressive symptoms.

Childhood trauma Questionnaire-Short form (CTQ-SF)

The CTQ-SF was translated and revised by Zhao Xingfu et al. [22], building upon the foundational work of Bernstein et al. [23]. This retrospective self-report questionnaire is designed to evaluate the frequency of childhood trauma experienced by individuals. It comprises 28 items that assess various dimensions of trauma, including emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores reflecting greater exposure to trauma. The instrument has demonstrated robust reliability and validity in prior research, thereby ensuring the precision of childhood trauma assessment within our sample. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.860.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The PSQI, originally developed by Buysse et al. [24] and subsequently translated by Liu Xianchen et al. [25], is designed to assess sleep quality over the preceding month. The instrument comprises 19 self-rated items and 5 observer-rated items, encompassing seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality, sleep onset latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep-promoting medications, and daytime dysfunction. Each dimension is evaluated on a scale from 0 to 3, resulting in a total PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21, with scores exceeding 7 indicative of poor sleep quality [25]. The PSQI is extensively utilized in both clinical and research contexts and has demonstrated robust reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.720.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 was developed by Spitzer et al. [26] to screen for and assess the severity of depressive symptoms. It consists of 9 items. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 27, 0–4 points indicating “no depression”, 5–9 points “mild depression”, 10–14 points “moderate depression”, and ≥ 15 points “severe depression”, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 has shown excellent psychometric properties for screening and measuring depression in adolescents. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.894.

Data collection procedure

Research assistants, who had undergone appropriate training, administered the questionnaires to the participants. Following the acquisition of informed consent from both the adolescents and their legal guardians, participants were provided with a quiet and comfortable setting to independently complete the questionnaires. The purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits of the study were thoroughly explained to them. In instances where adolescents encountered difficulties in comprehending the questions, the research assistants offered necessary clarifications in a neutral and non-leading manner. To ensure a high response rate, all questionnaires were collected on-site.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in strict adherence to the ethical principles delineated in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol, encompassing all procedures, informed consent/assent forms, and questionnaires, underwent rigorous review and received prior approval from the Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians, and written assent was secured from all adolescent participants, underscoring the voluntary nature of their involvement. Participants were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any adverse effects on their current or future medical care. In light of the sensitive nature of the topics addressed, such as childhood trauma and depressive symptoms, the research team was prepared to offer immediate psychological support to any participant experiencing distress. Additionally, all participants and their guardians were provided with contact information for the principal investigator and senior clinical staff to address any subsequent inquiries or concerns. All collected data were anonymized immediately after collection by using unique identification numbers instead of personal identifiers.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25.0 and the PROCESS macro for SPSS (version 4.2) developed by Andrew F. Hayes. First, descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the sample. Continuous variables were presented as means (M) and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Second, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the bivariate relationships between childhood trauma, sleep disturbance, and depressive symptoms. The core analysis tested the hypothesized mediation model using Model 4 in the PROCESS macro with 5000 bootstrap samples. Childhood trauma was entered as the independent variable (X), depressive symptoms as the dependent variable (Y), sleep disturbance as the mediator (M), and age, gender, and clinical diagnosis as covariates in the model. The significance of the indirect effect was determined if the 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval (CI) did not include zero. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics and clinical characteristics

A total of 426 adolescents with BD were included in the final analysis. The sample had a mean age of 14.991 years (SD = 1.50), and comprised 69.5% (n = 296) females and 30.5% (n = 130) males. Of these, 47.7% (n = 203) were diagnosed with bipolar I disorder and 52.3% (n = 223) with bipolar II disorder. 50.23% participants (n = 214) had never received pharmacotherapy for bipolar disorder; 22.07% (n = 94) had a history of medication use but were not on any psychotropic drugs for at least 4 weeks before the study; 27.70% (n = 118) were undergoing active pharmacotherapy. Common psychiatric comorbidities: 16.20% Anxiety Disorders; 0.47% Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD); 0.47% Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder. Correlation analysis

Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between childhood trauma, sleep disturbance, and depressive symptoms. Results showed a significant positive correlation between CTQ and PHQ-9 (r = 0.427, p < 0.001). There was also a significant positive correlation between CTQ and PSQI (r = 0.400, p < 0.001). Mediation analysis

To test the hypothesis that sleep disturbance mediates the relationship between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms, a simple mediation analysis was conducted using Model 4 of the PROCESS macro. Using the Boot⁃strap method, 5,000 repeated samples were performed to calculate the 95% confidence interval.CTQ was entered as the independent variable, PHQ-9 as the dependent variable, and PSQI as the mediator. Age, gender, and clinical diagnosis were included as covariates in the model.

Age (β=-0.036, 95% CI [-0.288, 0.229]), gender (β = 0.755, 95% CI [-0.103, 1.594]), and clinical diagnosis (β = 0.305, 95% CI [-0.471, 1.085]) have no statistically significant impact on PSQI. Gender (β = 2.402, 95% CI [1.235, 3.548]) and clinical diagnosis (β = 1.215, 95% CI [0.199, 2.258]) have significant effects on PHQ-9, while age has no statistically effect on PHQ-9 (β= -0.273, 95% CI [-0.601, 0.073]).

After controlling for age, gender, and clinical diagnosis, the total effect of childhood trauma on depressive symptoms was significant (B = 0.184, SE = 0.020, p < 0.001). This total effect model explained 24.70% of the variance in depressive symptoms (R² = 0.247). After including the mediator (sleep disturbance), the direct effect of childhood trauma on depressive symptoms remained significant (B = 0.108, SE = 0.019, p < 0.001), indicating partial mediation. The indirect effect of childhood trauma on depressive symptoms through sleep disturbance was significant (B = 0.071, BootSE = 0.012), accounting for approximately 38.59% of the total effect. Discussion

This study examined the potential mediating role of sleep disturbances in the relationship between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms among adolescents diagnosed with bipolar disorder. The findings demonstrated significant positive correlations among all three variables. Importantly, mediation analysis revealed that sleep disturbances partially mediated the relationship between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms in this clinically complex cohort, which was characterized by a moderate-to-severe depressive burden and pronounced sleep disturbances.

The significant positive correlation between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms identified in our study is consistent with previous research findings [27, 28]. Childhood trauma, including experiences of abuse or neglect, has the potential to disrupt normative psychological and neurobiological development [29]. Evidence indicates that such trauma may impair cognitive functions, leading to persistent negative thought patterns and pessimistic expectations about the future [28]. These cognitive disruptions can exacerbate the severity of depressive symptoms. Additionally, traumatic experiences may result in long-term alterations in the brain’s stress-response systems, particularly the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which is often dysregulated in individuals with depression [30]. These neurobiological changes may contribute to the onset and intensification of depressive symptoms in adolescents with bipolar disorder.

Our findings demonstrated a positive association between childhood trauma and sleep disturbances, which aligns with existing literature [14, 31]. Adolescence represents a critical developmental period during which traumatic experiences may exacerbate sleep disturbances by influencing emotional and behavioral issues [32, 33]. For instance, previous research has established a correlation between childhood trauma and heightened depressive and anxious symptoms in adolescents, which are closely linked to sleep disorders [34]. Moreover, sleep problems are more prevalent among traumatized youth, likely due to the impact of post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms [35, 36]. Childhood trauma may contribute to significant emotional distress and is associated with disruptions in the normal sleep-wake cycle. Traumatic experiences can alter neurophysiological and psychological processes, thereby affecting various aspects of sleep, including quality, efficiency, and duration [14, 31]. For example, experiences of abuse or neglect may lead to increased anxiety, hyperarousal, or post-traumatic stress symptoms, all of which can interfere with sleep. Additionally, trauma-induced HPA axis dysregulation can directly affect circadian rhythms and sleep mechanisms, contributing to insomnia, hypersomnia, or disrupted sleep patterns.

The identification of sleep disturbance as a partial mediator between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms represents a novel and clinically significant finding. This discovery is consistent with the results of a previous study [37].Guo et al. [38] also recognized sleep-related symptoms and emotional abuse as central and bridging symptoms within the network of childhood trauma, sleep disorders, and depressive symptoms, underscoring their transdiagnostic significance in this population. This suggests that sleep disturbance accounts for a substantial portion of the observed association between childhood trauma and more severe depressive symptoms in adolescents with bipolar disorder. Evidence indicates that even during periods of symptom remission, individuals with bipolar disorder continue to experience significantly impaired sleep quality [20]. Poor sleep quality can adversely affect emotional regulation, cognitive function, and neuroplasticity. Disrupted sleep may hinder individuals’ ability to manage negative emotions, potentially exacerbating depressive symptoms [39]. Goldstone et al. [19] showed that early adolescent sleep disturbances strongly predicted higher depressive symptoms a year later, independent of initial depression levels. Sleep issues can disrupt neurotransmitter systems like serotonin and dopamine, crucial for mood regulation, making sleep disturbance a key factor connecting childhood trauma to depressive.

It is crucial to acknowledge the probable bidirectional nature of these relationships. Although our model suggests that sleep disturbance acts as a pathway, depressive symptoms may also exacerbate sleep issues [40]. This reciprocal influence represents a limitation of cross-sectional data, which cannot disentangle temporal precedence. Consequently, the identified mediation model illustrates one plausible configuration of robust, interrelated associations, with causality necessitating validation through longitudinal studies.

The study also found that gender and bipolar type affect depressive symptoms in adolescents with bipolar disorder. Female patients, due to their distinct physiological and psychological traits, show more depressive symptoms [41]. Bipolar II patients experience greater depressive instability [42]. Clinicians should focus on depressive symptoms in female adolescents with bipolar II for tailored treatment and care.

Clinical and nursing implications

This study highlights the importance of routine, structured screening for childhood trauma (e.g., using brief tools like the CTQ-SF) and sleep quality (e.g., using the PSQI) in adolescents with BD to identify high-risk individuals efficiently. Psychiatric nurses should prioritize targeted sleep interventions, incorporating practical psychoeducation on sleep hygiene, such as maintaining regular bedtimes, limiting evening screen exposure, and reducing caffeine intake. For adolescents with a history of trauma, trauma-informed adjustments to the inpatient environment - such as minimizing nocturnal noise and avoiding non-urgent nighttime checks - can help reduce hypervigilance, promote a sense of safety, and directly address sleep disruptions rooted in anxiety. These concrete, feasible steps translate the research findings into actionable clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered: (1) The cross-sectional design limits causal conclusions, as the relationships between childhood trauma, sleep, and depression may be bidirectional. (2) Reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases; future research should use objective measures. (3) Uncontrolled confounding variables, such as comorbidities, the medication use, family dynamics, and socioeconomic status, may affect results. (4) Although we applied the clinical stability criteria for 4 weeks to enhance the reliability of self-reports, the heterogeneity of the clinical status during the assessment might have affected the symptom scores. (5) The sample, drawn from one hospital, may not be representative due to the severity of illness.

Based on these limitations, future research should employ longitudinal designs to track the temporal relationships between these variables. It should also integrate objective measurement tools to corroborate the role of sleep and explore how protective factors (e.g., social support, resilience) might buffer this pathogenic pathway.

Conclusion

This study identifies sleep disturbance as a significant partial mediator in the association between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms in adolescents diagnosed with bipolar disorder. The findings elucidate a crucial mechanistic pathway, highlighting sleep as a modifiable target for intervention. By addressing sleep disturbances, it may be possible to effectively interrupt the adverse trajectory from early adversity to depression within this vulnerable clinical population.
