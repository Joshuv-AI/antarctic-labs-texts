Perturbed Fourier transform associated with Schrödinger Operators
Shijun Zheng

Abstract
. We give an exposition on the L 2 L^{2} theory of the perturbed Fourier transform associated with a Schrödinger operator H = − d 2 / d x 2 + V H=-d^{2}/dx^{2}+V on the real line, where V V is a real-valued finite measure. In the case V ∈ L 1 ∩ L 2 V\in L^{1}\cap L^{2} , we explicitly define the perturbed Fourier transform ℱ \mathcal{F} for H H and obtain an eigenfunction expansion theorem for square integrable functions. This provides a complete proof of the inversion formula for ℱ {\cal F} that covers the class of short range potentials in ( 1 + | x | ) − 1 2 − ϵ ​ L 2 (1+|x|)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}L^{2} . Such paradigm has applications in the study of scattering problems in connection with the spectral properties and asymptotic completeness of the wave operators.

2.
Preliminaries on the Schrödinger operator

Suppose
H=-\Delta+V
is a Schrödinger operator on the real line, where
V

is a real-valued finite measure satisfying the short-range condition (
1
).

H
is the hamiltonian in the corresponding time-dependent
Schrödinger equation (
4
),
which describes the evolution of quantum wave packets under the action of
H
.
The solution is uniquely determined by the initial state
\psi(t,x)=e^{-itH}\phi(x)
,
\;t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Scattering theory deals with the wave function
\psi
, whose behavior in the past and in the
future are governed by the equation (
4
).

If assuming
\psi_{E}(t,x)=u_{E}(x)e^{-iEt}
in (4), where
E
is the energy, we arrive at the time-independent Schrödinger equation

(6)

(-\Delta+V)u_{E}(x)=Eu_{E}(x)\,

which is a typical model for the stationary waves
u_{E}
in an inhomogeneous medium.
An interpretation is that
H_{0}=-\Delta=-d^{2}/dx^{2}
describes the behavior of a free particle,
while
H_{0}+V
describes one particle interacting with an
external
field of forces
V(x)
.
In scattering experiment, the potential
V=V_{+}-V_{-}
is composed of repulsive and attractive components
V_{\pm}\geq 0
. The negative part
V_{-}
may introduce the possibility of localized disturbances, called
bound states
, in addition to the scattering solutions of (
6
).

It is well-known that under the condition (
1
) on
V
, a finite measure,
H
is defined as a selfadjoint operator by quadratic form with form domain
Q(H)

[
5
,
23
,
6
,
22
]
, also consult
[
35
,
36
]

and
[
34
, III, p.499]
,
[
8
,
25
]
for the case where
V
are locally integrable functions.
The form operator has spectral resolutions
H=\int\lambda dE_{\lambda}
in
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}
, where
dE_{\lambda}
is the associated spectral measure.
Let
\sigma(H)
denote the spectrum of
H
. Then
\sigma(H)\subset\mathbb{R}
consists of
\sigma(H)=\sigma_{ac}(H)\cup\sigma_{pp}(H)
with the singular continuous spectrum

\sigma_{sc}(H)
being empty.
There holds the
spectral decomposition

\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{ac}\oplus\mathscr{H}_{sc}\oplus\mathscr{H}_{pp}
, where
\mathscr{H}_{ac}
denotes the subspace of absolutely continuous vectors,

\mathscr{H}_{sc}
the subspace of singular continuous vectors, and
\mathscr{H}_{pp}
the subspace of eigen-vectors of

.

2.1.
Finite measures and their topology

Let

Denote

2.2.
The hamiltonian

6.
The resolvent for
H_{V}

The next few lemmas concern the resolvent of
H=H_{V}
when
V\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
is real-valued.
From the beginning of section
5
we know that
\sigma(H)=\sigma_{ac}\cup\sigma_{pp}
,
where
\sigma_{ac}(H)=[0,\infty)
,
\sigma_{sc}(H)=\emptyset
and

\sigma_{pp}(H)=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots\}\subset(-\infty,0)
.

If
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
, we let

R(z)=(H-z)^{-1}
and
R_{0}(z)=(H_{0}-z)^{-1}
. The unperturbed resolvent

R_{0}(z)
has an integral kernel

G_{0}(x,y;z)=-(2iz^{1/2})^{-1}e^{iz^{1/2}|x-y|}

where the value of the square root
z^{1/2}
is taken to have positive
imaginary part.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
V\in L^{1}
. Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H)
.
Then the operator
I+|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}\in{\cal B}(L^{2})
has a bounded inverse.

Proof.

Let
T_{z}
denote
I+|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}
.
We prove that
T_{z}
is a bijection on
L^{2}
.
We divide the proof of the lemma in two steps.

(a)

First we assume
V\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
.
Suppose
f\in L^{2}
is such that
T_{z}f=0
.
This means that
|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}f=-f
.
Let
g=R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}f
. Since
V^{1/2}f\in L^{2}
, we see
g\in{\cal D}(H_{0})
.
It is easy to verify that
Vg=-(H_{0}-z)g
. Hence

(58)

Hg=zg\quad in\ L^{2}\,.

Since
z
is not in the spectrum of
H
, it must be that
g=0
.
Since
f=-|V|^{1/2}g
, it follows that
f=0
. This shows that
T_{z}
is injective.
A similar calculation shows that
T_{z}
is surjective by noting that

f=(I-|V|^{1/2}R(z)V^{1/2})h
is a solution to
T_{z}f=h

in view of the second resolvent identity (
59
).
Thus, it follows from the inverse mapping theorem that
T_{z}
has a bounded inverse.

(b)

Now we relax the condition in (a) to assume
V\in L^{1}
only.
We shall prove the results in part (a)
using a limiting argument with
C^{\infty}_{0}\ni V_{n}{\rightarrow}V
a.e. and in
L^{1}
.
Let
f\in\mathrm{Ker}\ T_{z}
. Let
g=R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}f
.
First show
g\in D(H)\subset Q(H)=Q(H_{0})
.
Let
C^{\infty}_{0}\supset\{V_{n}\}\rightarrow V\;\text{ in}\;L^{1}\;\text{and}\;a.e.

with
|V_{n}(x)|\leq|V(x)|
.
Then
g_{n}=R_{0}(z)V_{n}^{1/2}f\in D(H_{0})
. Note
R_{0}(z)V_{n}^{1/2}f\rightarrow R_{0}(z){V^{1/2}}f=g\in L^{2}

(since
V^{1/2}f\in L^{1}
and
R_{0}(z):L^{1}\rightarrow L^{q}
,
\forall q\geq 1
). The convergence is in operator norm for
R_{0}(z)V_{n}^{1/2}
hence in
L^{2}
when it is applied to
f
.
This can be easily observed from the kernel
of
R_{0}(z)V_{n}^{1/2}-R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}
that is equal to

-\frac{1}{2iz}e^{iz^{1/2}|x-y|}(V_{n}^{1/2}-V^{1/2})\,,

which actually converges to zero in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. We have for all
h\in C^{\infty}_{0}
,

\displaystyle V_{n}^{1/2}f=(H_{0}-z)g_{n}\ {\Rightarrow}\ \langle V_{n}^{1/2}f,h\rangle=\langle(H_{0}-z)g_{n},h\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle g_{n},(H_{0}-{z})h\rangle\rightarrow\langle g,(H_{0}-{z})h\rangle\,.

On the left hand side,
\int V_{n}^{1/2}fh\rightarrow\int V^{1/2}fhdx=\langle V^{1/2}f,h\rangle

by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, noting that
V^{1/2}f\in L^{1}
.
We have shown that

L^{1}\ni V^{1/2}f=(H_{0}-z)g\qquad\text{in the sense of distributions}.

Now
T_{z}f=0
implies that
Vg=-V^{1/2}f
, i.e.,

\langle Vg,h\rangle=-\langle(H_{0}-z)g,h\rangle.

Therefore,
Vg=-(H_{0}-z)g
in distributions.
This implies
g\in D(H)
by regularity theorem for elliptic equations since
Vg=-V^{1/2}f\in L^{2}
. The remaining part of the proof goes the same as in part (a).

∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
V\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
.
Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H)
.
Then there exists a measurable function
G(x,y;z)
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
such
that

(R(z)f)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(x,y;z)f(y)\,dy

for all
f\in L^{2}
. Moreover:

(i)
G(x,\cdot\,;z)\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
for almost every

x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)
G(x,y;z)=G(y,x;z)
and
\overline{G(x,y;z)}=G(x,y;\overline{z})
for almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(iii)
G
satisfies the equation

G(x,y;z)=G_{0}(x,y;z)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{0}(x,w;z)V(w)G(w,y;z)\,dw

for almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
D(V)\supset D(H_{0})
using the resolvent equation

(59)

R(z)=R_{0}(z)-R_{0}(z)VR(z)

and Lemma
6.1
, it is easy to check the identity

R(z)-R_{0}(z)=-R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}(I+|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)V^{1/2})^{-1}|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)

Since
V
is integrable, the operator
|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)
is
Hilbert-Schmidt. Therefore
R(z)-R_{0}(z)
is Hilbert-Schmidt, and has
an integral kernel
A(x,y;z)
, measurable as a function of

(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|A(x,y;z)|^{2}\,dx\times dy<\infty.

In particular

A(x,\cdot\,;z)\in L^{2}
for almost every
x
.
Since
G_{0}(x,\cdot\,;z)\in L^{2}
for all
x
,
R(z)
has an integral kernel

G(x,y;z)=A(x,y;z)+G_{0}(x,y;z),

measurable as a function of
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that

G(x,\cdot\,;z)\in L^{2}
for almost every
x
.

Now according to the
resolvent equation (
59
), if
f,g\in L^{2}
, then

(60)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)G(x,y;z)g(y)\,dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)G_{0}(x,y;z)g(y)\,dydx

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)G_{0}(x,w;z)V(w)G(w,y;z)g(y)\,dydwdx.

These are
iterated
integrals with the indicated order of integration.
However, let
c,\epsilon>0
be chosen such that
|G_{0}(x,y;z)|\leq ce^{-\epsilon|x-y|}
. Define
a\in L^{1}
by
a(x)=e^{-\epsilon|x|}
. Then

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)G_{0}(x,y;z)g(y)|\,dydx\leq c\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|(a*|g|)(x)\,dx\leq c\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}}.

This shows that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)G_{0}(x,y;z)g(y)\,dydx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}G_{0}(x,y;z)\,f(x)g(y)\,dx\times dy.

The bound

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)A(x,y;z)g(y)|\,dydx\leq\|g\|_{L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|A(x,y;z)|^{2}\,dydx)^{1/2}

shows that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)G(x,y;z)g(y)\,dydx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}G(x,y;z)\,f(x)g(y)\,dx\times dy.

Finally, by similar arguments the triple integral
in (
60
) can be written in the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{0}(x,w;z)V(w)G(w,y;z)\,dw\right)\,f(x)g(y)\,dx\times dy.

The function

f(x)g(y)\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{0}(x,w;z)V(w)G(w,y;z)\,dw

is measurable as a function of
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
whenever
f,g\in L^{2}
.
It follows that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{0}(x,w;z)V(w)G(w,y;z)\,dw

itself is a measurable as a function of
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Now define

B(x,y;z)=G(x,y;z)-G_{0}(x,y;z)+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{0}(x,w;z)V(w)G(w,y;z)\,dw.

So far we know that if
f,g\in L^{2}
, then

(61)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}B(x,y;z)\,f(x)g(y)\,dx\times dy=0.

For
M>0
define
B_{M}(x,y;z)=\chi_{[-M,M]^{2}}(x,y)B(x,y;z)
. It is easy
to check that
B_{M}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for each

. By a standard argument
(
61
) implies that

\int_{[-M,M]^{2}}B_{M}(x,y;z)F(x,y)\,dx\times dy=0

for every

. Therefore
B_{M}(x,y;z)=0
for almost every

(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since this holds for all

,
B(x,y;z)=0
for
almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This proves
(iii)
.

To prove
{\emph{(}ii)}
, let

and write

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,(R(z)g)(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}((H-z)R(z)f)(x)\,(R(z)g)(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(R(z)f)(x)\,((H-z)R(z)g)(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(R(z)f)(x)\,g(x)\,dx.

This implies that
G(x,y;z)=G(y,x;z)
for almost every

(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Also,

,
so

for almost every

. This proves
{\emph{(}ii)}
.

It remains to prove that
G(x,\cdot\,;z)\in L^{1}
for almost every
x
.
Using the integral equation
{\emph{(}iii)}
and the symmetry of
G(x,y;z)

we have, for almost every

(62)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|G(x,y;z)|\,dy=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|G(y,x;z)|\,dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|G_{0}(y,x;z)|\,dy+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|G_{0}(y,w;z)V(w)G(w,x;z)|\,dwdy.

Again let

be chosen such that

and

. Note that

, and

for almost every

for all

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\underbrace{\varphi(\xi^{2})f^{\sharp}(\xi)}_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}}e(x,\xi)d\xi\qquad(pointwise)

\varphi(H)f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int f(y)dy\int\varphi(\xi^{2})e(x,\xi)\overline{e(y,\xi)}d\xi,

where the integral is convergent for a.e.
x
.

Now let
f\in L^{2}
. Pick a sequence
\{f_{m}\}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}

such that
f_{m}\rightarrow f
in
L^{2}
and
\varphi(H)f_{m}\rightarrow\varphi(H)f

a.e
and in
L^{2}
.
Thus, since for almost all
x
,
K(x,\cdot)
is in
L^{2}
,

\displaystyle\varphi(H)f(x)=

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\varphi(H)f_{m}(x)\quad a.e.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\int\varphi(H)(x,y)f_{m}dy=\int\varphi(H)(x,y)fdy.

The fact that

K(x,y):=\varphi(H)(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}
is a general property for Carleman type kernels
[
36
, Corollary A.1.2]
, which can also be easily verified
using the heat kernel estimate
[
30
,
42
]
.
∎

Proposition 9.1

Denote by

)

If
\varphi

, then we have

(89)

\displaystyle\varphi(H)\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}^{*}_{\pm}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}^{*}_{\pm}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\varphi(H_{0})

or, equivalently,

(90)

\displaystyle\varphi(H)P_{ac}=W_{\mp}\varphi(H_{0})W_{\mp}^{*}\,.

Proof.

For
u=\psi^{\pm}_{k}
,
\lambda=k^{2}>0
, using

\langle(-\Delta+V)u,\phi\rangle=\lambda\langle u,\phi\rangle
for all

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int(k^{2}-\zeta)\psi^{\pm}(x,k)\hat{f}(k)dk\,,

which implies that

\displaystyle(H-\zeta)^{-1}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}^{*}_{\pm}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}g=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}^{*}_{\pm}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}(H_{0}-\zeta)^{-1}g\,,\quad\forall g\in L^{2}\,.

Proof of Theorem
9.2

(a) Let

.
From (

\displaystyle U(t)\phi=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}^{*}e^{-itk^{2}}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}\phi

\displaystyle W_{\mp}U_{0}(t)\phi=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}^{*}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}^{*}e^{-itk^{2}}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\phi=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}^{*}e^{-itk^{2}}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\phi

\displaystyle U(t)\phi-W_{\mp}U_{0}(t)\phi=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}^{*}e^{-itk^{2}}\left(\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}-\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\right)\phi\,.

\displaystyle U(t)\phi-W_{\mp}U_{0}(t)\phi=\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}^{*}e^{-itk^{2}}\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{\pm}(\eta_{\mp})=U(t)\eta_{\mp}\,.

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{+}(\eta_{-})(k)\right|=\left|\frac{i}{2\sqrt{2\pi}|k|}\int\overline{\psi^{+}(y,k)}V(dy)\int\phi(x)e^{-i|k||x-y|}dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{1+|k|}\left\|V\right\|_{L^{1}_{2}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{1}_{1}}\,.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{\mathscr{F}}_{-}(\eta_{+})(k)\right|\leq\frac{C}{1+|k|}\left\|V\right\|_{L^{1}_{2}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{1}_{1}}\,.

On the other hand, it is easy to check the proof of

(b) Given
