Quasiprobability Thermodynamic Uncertainty Relation
Kohei Yoshimura, Ryusuke Hamazaki

Abstract
We derive a quantum extension of the thermodynamic uncertainty relation where dynamical fluctuations are quantified by the Terletsky–Margenau–Hill quasiprobability, a quantum generalization of the classical joint probability. The obtained inequality plays a complementary role to existing quantum thermodynamic uncertainty relations, focusing on observables’ change rather than exchange of charges through jumps and respecting initial coherence. Quasiprobabilities show anomalous behaviors that are forbidden in classical systems, such as negativity; we reveal that such behaviors are necessary to reduce dissipation beyond classical limitations and show that they are stronger requirements than that the state has quantum coherence. To illustrate these statements, we employ a model that can exhibit a dissipationless heat current, which would be prohibited in classical systems; we construct a state that has much coherence but does not lead to a dissipationless current due to the absence of anomalous behaviors in quasiprobabilities.



Introduction
.—
Finding universal lower bounds on the entropy production, which quantifies the irreversibility in the process, is one of the main tasks in nonequilibrium thermodynamics because they provide fundamental limitations beyond the second law of thermodynamics
Shiraishi 2023
.
The thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR) is arguably the most crucial example, having been intensively studied over the last decade
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2020
.
It consists of dissipation, quantified by the entropy production rate (EPR)
\dot{\Sigma}
, and a general current’s strength
J_{X}
and fluctuations
S_{X}
, typically given in the form

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}\geq\frac{2J_{X}^{2}}{S_{X}}.

(1)

Incorporating the information of fluctuations explicitly, it provides a universal finite lower bound that tightens the second law,
\dot{\Sigma}\geq 0
.

While extending the TUR to the quantum regime is a crucial problem gathering much attention recently
Agarwalla and Segal 2018
;
Ptaszyński 2018
;
Carollo et al. 2019
;
Hasegawa 2020
;
Van Vu and Saito 2022
;
Hasegawa 2023
;
Prech et al. 2025
;
Van Vu 2025
;
Brandner and Saito 2025
;
Guarnieri et al. 2019
;
Sacchi 2021
;
Miller et al. 2021
;
Das et al. 2023
;
Farina et al. 2024
, there is a fundamental issue of how to evaluate
dynamical
fluctuations.
TURs usually involve fluctuations in fluxes or observables’ change
Gingrich et al. 2016
;
Pietzonka et al. 2016
;
Proesmans and Van den Broeck 2017
;
Pietzonka et al. 2017
;
Dechant 2018
;
Timpanaro et al. 2019
;
Hasegawa and Van Vu 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Falasco et al. 2020
;
Koyuk and Seifert 2020
;
Tuan Vo et al. 2020
;
Dechant and Sasa 2020
;
Yoshimura and Ito 2021
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
Delvenne and Falasco 2024
;
Kwon et al. 2024a
;
Kolchinsky et al. 2024
;
Vo et al. 2025
;
Nagayama et al. 2025a
, hence they require statistics more than single time points.
However, in quantum mechanics, physical quantities at different times do not commute in the Heisenberg sense, which makes fluctuations elusive.

Conventionally, there are two approaches trying to characterize quantum fluctuations: one is based on the full counting statistics or continuous measurement
Agarwalla and Segal 2018
;
Ptaszyński 2018
;
Carollo et al. 2019
;
Hasegawa 2020
;
Van Vu and Saito 2022
;
Hasegawa 2023
;
Prech et al. 2025
;
Van Vu 2025
;
Brandner and Saito 2025
;
Guarnieri et al. 2019
, which identifies jumps as exchanging charges with the environment or detections of a signal in an experiment
Landi et al. 2024
.
However, it cannot explore fluctuations of more general observables that are not directly related to the jumps.
The other approach utilizes the two-point measurement
Sacchi 2021
;
Miller et al. 2021
;
Das et al. 2023
;
Farina et al. 2024
, which, however, ends up with discarding coherence regarding the measured observable we are interested in, because it includes invasive measurement steps
Esposito et al. 2009
.
Hence, these methods can fail to capture all the quantum effects on thermodynamic trade-off relations.

Our idea to overcome this difficulty is to focus on
quasiprobabilities
to fully describe the quantum statistics of observables that are not detected by jumps and may exhibit non-commutativity.
Quasiprobabilities are quantum extensions of the classical joint probabilities, and their peculiar behaviors have been attracting significant attention in recent years
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
;
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2024
.
While the classical probabilities satisfy positivity and linearity and lead to correct marginals, any quantum counterparts are known to violate at least one of these three properties
Perarnau-Llobet et al. 2017
;
Lostaglio et al. 2023
.
As the violation of positivity is connected to genuine quantumness called contextuality
Spekkens 2008
;
Lostaglio 2018
, quasiprobabilities that may take negative values have recently been studied extensively
Ferrie 2011
;
Allahverdyan 2014
;
Yunger Halpern et al. 2018
;
González Alonso et al. 2019
;
Levy and Lostaglio 2020
;
Hernández-Gómez et al. 2024a
;
Donelli et al. 2025
.

(a) In open systems, we cannot reduce two costs simultaneously: irreversibility, quantified by the entropy production rate
\dot{\Sigma}
, and fluctuations. That is represented by a universal trade-off relation called the thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR), where the product between
\dot{\Sigma}
and the dynamical fluctuation
S_{X}
of a physical quantity
X
is bounded by a current strength
J_{X}
. (b) We prove a quantum TUR with
S_{X}=m_{X}
[Eq. (
10
)], where the observable’s dynamical fluctuation is quantified by the quasiprobability, a quantum extension of the classical joint probability that may take negative values.

In this Letter, we derive a quantum TUR by quantifying the dynamical fluctuation of an observable with such a quasiprobability for the first time (see Fig.
1
).
We consider Markovian open quantum systems and show that the short-time variance of an observable’s change, as assessed by the Terletsky–Margenau–Hill quasiprobability
Terletsky 1937
;
Margenau and Hill 1961
, provides a short-time TUR bound on the EPR for any state and observable.
Moreover, on the basis of the non-classicality of quasiprobability, we elucidate criteria required for the anomalously large fluctuation absent in classical systems.
Combined with our TUR, the criteria serve as a more fundamental, basis-independent condition for the dissipationless current
Tajima and Funo 2021
;
Funo and Tajima 2025
than the abundance of the quantum coherence indicated in Ref.
Tajima and Funo 2021
.

Setup
.—
We consider completely positive Markovian quantum dynamics, which are generally described by the quantum master equation
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\mathcal{L}(\rho)\coloneqq-i[H,\rho]+\mathcal{D}(\rho),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\rho)\coloneqq\sum_{k}L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho\},

(3)

where
\rho
is the density operator,
H
the Hamiltonian,
L_{k}
jump operators,
[\cdot,\cdot]
the commutator, and
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
the anticommutator.
We refer to
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{D}
as the Liouvillian and the dissipator, respectively.
Given the initial data
\rho_{0}
, Eq. (
2
) is formally solved by
\rho(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}t}\rho_{0}
.
We indicate the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{D}
by
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}
.
For an observable (Hermitian operator)
X
, we describe the eigendecomposition as
X=\sum_{x}x\Pi_{x}
with eigenvalues
x
and projectors
\Pi_{x}
.
Its expectation value at time
t
is given by
\langle X\rangle_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\rho(t))=\sum_{x}xp(x,t)
, where
p(x,t)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\Pi_{x}\rho(t))
.
We divide the time derivative into the Hamiltonian part
J_{X}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=i\langle[H,X]\rangle_{t}
and the dissipative part
J_{X}^{\mathrm{d}}(t)=\langle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}(X)\rangle_{t}
as
d_{t}\langle X\rangle_{t}=J_{X}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)+J_{X}^{\mathrm{d}}(t)
.

To discuss multi-time statistics of
X
in the quantum dynamics beyond the single-time one
p(x,t)
, we introduce the quasiprobability
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
;
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2024
.
In particular, we focus on the two-time statistics given by the Terletsky–Margenau–Hill (TMH) quasiprobability
Terletsky 1937
;
Margenau and Hill 1961
, which is defined by

\displaystyle q(y,t+\Delta t;x,t)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big(\big\{e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Delta t}\Pi_{y},\Pi_{x}\big\}\rho(t)\Big).

(4)

The TMH quasiprobability is the real part of the Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobability
Kirkwood 1933
;
Dirac 1945
. While its marginals provide the single-time distributions as
\sum_{y}q(y,t+\Delta t;x,t)=p(x,t)
and
\sum_{x}q(y,t+\Delta t;x,t)=p(y,t+\Delta t)
, it can become negative, unlike the classical joint probability distribution, which is an emergence of genuine quantumness
Spekkens 2008
;
Lostaglio 2018
.

Analogously to the classical case
Gardiner 2009
, we define the moments of “change in
X
” by

\displaystyle\big\langle(\Delta X)^{n}\big\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}\coloneqq\sum_{x,y}(x-y)^{n}q(y,t+\Delta t;x,t).

(5)

It can be computed from the moment generating function
\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)
as
\langle(\Delta X)^{n}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}=(-i\partial_{\lambda})^{n}\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)|_{\lambda=0}
, where
\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big(\big\{e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Delta t}e^{i\lambda X},e^{-i\lambda X}\big\}\rho(t)\Big).

(6)

Theoretical studies have revealed that the moment generating function can be directly measured by devising an interferometric scheme
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
, which has been experimentally realized for
X=H

Hernández-Gómez et al. 2024b
.
Thus,
\langle(\Delta X)^{n}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}
is not only theoretically but also practically a reasonable measure of the dynamical fluctuation in a quantum object
X
.
Additionally, as shown in End Matter,
\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)
can be obtained by appropriately “quantizing” the classical counterpart.

We further define the short-time moments
m_{X}^{(n)}(t)
by
m_{X}^{(n)}(t)\coloneqq\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\langle(\Delta X)^{n}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}/\Delta t
.
We write
m_{X}^{(2)}(t)
simply as
m_{X}(t)
and call it the short-time fluctuation of
X
because the case
n=2
will be of the most importance due to its direct connection to the variance,
m_{X}^{(2)}(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\mathrm{Var}(\Delta X)/\Delta t
, where
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta X)\coloneqq\langle(\Delta X)^{2}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}-(\langle\Delta X\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t})^{2}
.
The limit is always well defined because the TMH quasiprobability is expanded as
q(y,t+\Delta t;x,t)=\delta_{xy}p(x,t)+T_{yx}\big(\rho(t)\big)\Delta t+O(\Delta t^{2})

with

\displaystyle T_{yx}(\rho)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(\{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Pi_{y},\Pi_{x}\big\}\rho\big)

(7)

when
\Delta t
goes to zero.
Because
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(I)=0
, we have
\sum_{y}T_{yx}(\rho)=0
.
The short-time moments are given by
T_{yx}
as

\displaystyle m_{X}^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{x,y}(x-y)^{n}T_{yx}(\rho(t)).

(8)

We refer to
T_{yx}
as the flux from
x
to
y
in analogy to the classical Markov processes.
Indeed, if
\rho(t)
commutes with
X
and
X
has no degeneracy, then
\rho
is diagonalized as
\rho(t)=\sum_{x}p(x,t)\Pi_{x}
with
\Pi_{x}=|x\rangle\langle x|
and we have
T_{yx}(\rho(t))=R_{yx}p(x,t)
with
R_{yx}=\sum_{k}|\langle y|L_{k}|x\rangle|^{2}
.
Now,
p(x,t)
and
R_{yx}
can be interpreted respectively as the occupation probability of the classical state labeled by
x
and the transition rate from state
x
to state
y

Gardiner 2009
;
Esposito and Mukamel 2006
.
The diagonal elements
T_{xx}(\rho(t))
also read
R_{xx}p(x,t)
, where
R_{xx}=-\sum_{x^{\prime}(\neq x)}\sum_{k}|\langle x^{\prime}|L_{k}|x\rangle|^{2}
, and
-R_{xx}
corresponds to the escape rate from state
x
.
Now that, even in the general (quantum) cases, we call the sum
\bar{\lambda}(\rho)\coloneqq-\sum_{x}T_{xx}(\rho)
the average escape rate.

Finally, we make a few assumptions to discuss thermodynamics.
We assume that each jump
k
has a unique counterpart
-k
and that jump
k
induces an entropy current
s_{k}
such that
s_{-k}=-s_{k}
. The jump operators are postulated to satisfy the local detailed balance
L_{k}=e^{s_{k}/(2k_{\mathrm{B}})}L_{-k}^{\dagger}

Maes 2021
;
Strasberg 2022
;
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
.
Then, we can define the entropy production rate by

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}\big(\rho(t)\big)\coloneq k_{\mathrm{B}}\frac{d}{dt}S(t)+\sum_{k}s_{k}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho(t)\big),

(9)

where
S(t)\coloneq-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\rho(t)\ln\rho(t))
is the von Neumann entropy.
Hereafter, we set the Boltzmann constant
k_{\mathrm{B}}
to one for simplicity.

Main result
.—
The following inequality is our main result:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}\big(\rho(t)\big)\geq\frac{2|J_{X}^{\mathrm{d}}(t)|^{2}}{m_{X}(t)}

(10)

for any state
\rho(t)
and observable
X
.
The numerator is the dissipative part of the time derivative of
\langle X\rangle_{t}
, so it quantifies the changing rate of
X
due to the dissipative dynamics.
On the other hand, the denominator, the short-time fluctuation, represents the dynamical fluctuation of
X
as evaluated by the TMH quasiprobability.
Thus, the inequality represents the universal trade-off between dissipation and fluctuations, i.e., the TUR
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2020
.
Specifically, it generalizes the so-called short-time TURs in classical systems
Dechant and Sasa 2018
;
Otsubo et al. 2020
, which have been utilized in estimating the EPR in classical systems
Seifert 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Otsubo et al. 2020
;
Manikandan et al. 2020
;
Dechant and Sasa 2021
.
This connection will be clear if we assume that
\rho
commutes with
X
; then, the numerator and denominator read
J_{X}^{\mathrm{d}}(t)=d_{t}\langle X\rangle_{t}
and
m_{X}(t)=\sum_{x,y}(x-y)^{2}R_{yx}p(x,t)
, which appear in the short-time TUR presented in Ref.
Otsubo et al. 2020
.
The TUR indicates that increasing the short-time fluctuation can lead to the reduction of the lower bound of dissipation.
As will be discussed in detail later, the quasiprobabilistic perspective has the practical advantage of clarifying physical requirements for such an increase in
m_{X}
.

Let us prove Eq. (
10
). We employ the following inequality derived by one of the present authors in Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2025
:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}\big(\rho(t)\big)\geq\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho(t)))|^{2}}{\mathfrak{D}_{X}(\rho(t))},

(11)

where
\mathfrak{D}_{X}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\rho(\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}(X^{2})-\{\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}(X),X\}))
is called the quantum diffusivity (the derivation is reviewed in Supplemental Material
smA
).
It is easy to see
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho(t)))=J_{X}^{\mathrm{d}}(t)
.
The nontrivial point is that we can also associate the denominator
\mathfrak{D}_{X}(\rho(t))
with
m_{X}(t)
. First, by expanding the definition, we find

\displaystyle m_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}(y-x)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Pi_{y},\Pi_{x}\}\rho(t))

(12)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(X^{2})\rho(t)\big)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(\{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(X),X\}\rho(t)\big).

(13)

Moreover, by using an identity
\{[H,X],X\}=[H,X^{2}]
, we can remove the Hamiltonian part from
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
.
Therefore, we find the equality

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{X}\big(\rho(t)\big)=\frac{1}{2}m_{X}(t).

(14)

Combining it with Eq. (
11
), we obtain the TUR (
10
).

Comparison with existing TURs
.—We compare our TUR (
10
) with existing quantum TURs.
First, we stress the difference from widely studied methods, the full counting statistics (FCS) approach
Landi et al. 2024
and the two-point measurement (TPM) approach
Esposito et al. 2009
.
In short, our result is complementary to those previous ones.
While the TURs based on the FCS
Carollo et al. 2019
;
Hasegawa 2020
;
Van Vu and Saito 2022
;
Hasegawa 2023
;
Prech et al. 2025
;
Van Vu 2025
;
Brandner and Saito 2025
;
Guarnieri et al. 2019
deal with current observables associated with the counting of jumps, our TUR is given by the fluctuations of intrinsic observables, which do not have to be connected to jumps.
It is noteworthy that in classical Markov jump processes, an intrinsic observable’s change is entirely given by keeping track of jumps, because every physical quantity takes determined values in classical states
Gardiner 2009
.
However, quantum states linked by quantum jumps are not eigenstates of every intrinsic observable (they may not be even for the Hamiltonian; see Model A in
Landi et al. 2024
).
In End Matter, we discuss a nontrivial intersection between the FCS and quasiprobabilities when
[X,L_{k}]=w_{k}L_{k}
holds for an observable
X
with certain weights
w_{k}
.

The TPM method, adopted to quantify dynamical fluctuations in Refs.
Sacchi 2021
;
Miller et al. 2021
;
Das et al. 2023
;
Farina et al. 2024
, inevitably leads to decoherence in the initial density operator due to invasive initial measurement, despite having a clear experimental perspective.
On the other hand, in our TUR, every initial coherence is incorporated owing to the employment of the quasiprobability, while maintaining experimental access by the interferometric method
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
;
Hernández-Gómez et al. 2024b
.

Finally, we mention the connection to the “TUR” [Eq. (
11
)] given in Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2025
.
Despite the apparent similarity, our TUR has a substantial advantage over the previous result.
As is clear from the proof, our TUR is derived by combining their result and the equality (
14
).
The latter equality gives a clear statistical meaning to
\mathfrak{D}_{X}(\rho)
, which was interpreted as a fluctuation measure only in classical cases in Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2025
.
Moreover, as discussed below, the quasiprobabilistic perspective brought by Eq. (
14
) enables us to understand a non-classical suppression of dissipation through non-classical behaviors of quasiprobabilities, which cannot be understood from Eq. (
11
) alone.

Anomalous scaling via non-classicality of quasiprobability
.—
We next explore the ramifications of non-classicality on the thermodynamic constraint.
As will be shown, the short-time fluctuation
m_{X}(t)
can exhibit an anomalous scaling when
X
is highly degenerate, which is forbidden in classical systems.
This scaling makes the TUR bound small and allows for reducing entropy production beyond the classical limit.
While the reduction has recently been
attributed to quantum coherence in
\rho

Tajima and Funo 2021
, we discuss that non-classical behaviors of the TMH quasiprobability are more fundamental.

First, we review the discussion in Ref.
Tajima and Funo 2021
.
The authors derived an inequality similar to Eq. (
10
) for
X=H
, the Hamiltonian, where they used the coherence in
\rho
regarding a specific eigenbasis of
H
instead of the short-time fluctuation
m_{H}
.
They showed that when
H
is highly degenerate, dissipationless heat current can be realized, as
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho(t))=O(1)
and
|d_{t}\langle H\rangle_{t}|=|J_{H}^{\mathrm{d}}(t)|=O(N)
, where
N
is the number of degeneracy.
In our terminology, this dissipationless current is enabled by
m_{H}(t)
scaling at
O(N^{2})
, while they attributed it to the coherence’s
O(N)
scaling.

To clarify what is crucial for the dissipationless current, we formulate the problem in a general way.
We assume that the eigenvalues of
X
are
N
-fold degenerate and examine conditions on
\rho(t)
for
m_{X}(t)
to scale more rapidly than the classical limitation
m_{X}(t)=O(N)
(detailed below).
We specifically refer to the growth of the short-time fluctuation faster than
O(N)
as
anomalous scaling
.
When an eigenbasis for eigenvalue
x_{s}
is given as
\{|s,j\rangle\}_{j=1}^{N}
,
X
is expanded as
X=\sum_{s,j}x_{s}\Pi_{s,j}
with
\Pi_{s,j}=|s,j\rangle\langle s,j|
.
We can define the flux from
(s,j)
to
(s^{\prime},j^{\prime})
as
T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Pi_{s^{\prime},j^{\prime}},\Pi_{s,j}\}\rho)
and the integrated fluxes as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}(\rho)&(s\neq s^{\prime}),\\
\sum_{j,j^{\prime}(\neq j)}T_{sj^{\prime}sj}(\rho)&(s=s^{\prime}).\end{cases}

(15)

While
T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}
and
\mathcal{T}_{ss}
are basis-dependent,
\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}
are not if
s\neq s^{\prime}
.
We further define the average escape rate
\bar{\lambda}(\rho)=-\sum_{s,j}T_{sjsj}(\rho)
.
They satisfy the equality
\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)-\bar{\lambda}(\rho)=0
.
Because the time dependence is not relevant hereafter, we write
m_{X}(\rho)
to indicate
m_{X}(t)
when
\rho(t)=\rho
.
The short-time fluctuation is given solely by the integrated fluxes as
m_{X}(\rho)=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}(x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}})^{2}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)
.

We first demonstrate
m_{X}(\rho)=O(N)
in the classical cases.
To this end, we define an eigenbasis to be classical if it satisfies that for each
(s,j,s^{\prime},j^{\prime})
, the number of jumps such that
|\langle s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|L_{k}|s,j\rangle|\neq 0
is
O(1)
, and that
|\langle s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|L_{k}|s,j\rangle|=O(1)
.
For a classical eigenbasis, we find
R_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}=\sum_{k}|\langle s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|L_{k}|s,j\rangle|^{2}=O(1)
and
-R_{sjsj}=\sum_{s^{\prime},j^{\prime}(\neq s,j)}\sum_{k}|\langle s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|L_{k}|s,j\rangle|^{2}=O(N)
.
Therefore, if
\rho
has no coherence with respect to the classical eigenbasis (i.e.,
\rho=\sum_{s,j}p_{sj}\Pi_{s,j}
holds), we obtain the scaling
\bar{\lambda}(\rho)=-\sum_{s,j}R_{sjsj}p_{sj}=O(N)
because
p_{sj}\geq 0
and
\sum_{s,j}p_{sj}=1
.
This scaling is intuitive as the target of escape grows by at most
O(N)
.
Because the absence of coherence in
\rho
also leads to the positivity of
T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}(\rho)
, we can conclude
m_{X}(\rho)=O(N)
from

\displaystyle m_{X}(\rho)=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}(x_{s}-x_{s}^{\prime})^{2}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)\neq 0}(x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}})^{2}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\max_{s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)\neq 0}(x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}})^{2}\bar{\lambda}(\rho)=O(N).

Here, we have assumed that
\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)\neq 0
implies
|x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}}|=O(1)
, which is true if, for example, there is a degeneracy-independent threshold
\Lambda
such that
|x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}}|>\Lambda
implies
\langle s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|H|s,j\rangle=0
and
\langle s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|L_{k}|s,j\rangle=0
(namely, transitions leading to a drastic change in
X
are forbidden).

The above discussion shows that if
\rho
has no coherence regarding the classical eigenbases,
m_{X}(\rho)
grows by at most
O(N)
.
On the other hand, if we adopt a non-classical eigenbasis, the coherence tells us nothing.
For example, consider
\rho
expanded as
\rho=\sum_{s}p_{s}|s,*\rangle\langle s,*|
with
|s,*\rangle
such that
X|s,*\rangle=x_{s}|s,*\rangle
and
|\langle s^{\prime},*^{\prime}|L_{k}|s,*\rangle|=O(N^{\alpha})
with
\alpha>1/2
.
Then,
\bar{\lambda}
grows by
O(N^{2\alpha})>O(N)
and an anomalous scaling can occur even though
\rho
has no coherence.
We will exemplify this drawback of coherence-based consideration through an example later.

The next statement is our second main result, which provides another viewpoint to examine the anomalous scaling:
if neither of the following conditions is satisfied for some eigenbasis,
m_{X}(\rho)
does not exhibit anomalous scaling
:

(Q1)

\displaystyle\exists s,s^{\prime},\;\;\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)}{N}=-\infty,

(Q2)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\bar{\lambda}(\rho)}{N}=\infty.

The first condition (Q1) represents significant negativity in the fluxes, which results in negative quasiprobability because then
T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}(\rho)<0
for several
j,j^{\prime}
and

\displaystyle q((s^{\prime},j^{\prime}),t+\Delta t;(s,j),t)

\displaystyle=T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}(\rho(t))\Delta t+O(\Delta t^{2})<0.

(16)

Note that the negativity of the quasiprobability also indicates the genuine quantumness called contextuality
Spekkens 2008
;
Lostaglio 2018
.
The other one (Q2) is also non-classical because it means the chance of escape grows more rapidly than the number of evacuation targets increases.
The statement shows that either of these quantumness conditions has to hold in
any
basis for the anomalous scaling to occur.
Such a property stands in contrast to the existence of coherence, which does not need to hold in every basis.
We depict their relationship with anomalous scaling in Fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
Hierarchy of conditions.
Condition (Q1) or (Q2) is always necessary for
m_{X}(\rho)
to scale anomalously, as is prohibited in classical systems.
(a) For classical eigenbases, these conditions are sufficient for the state to have coherence. Thus, this time, coherence is necessary for the anomalous scaling.
Density matrix
\rho^{+}
, discussed in Example, has a large coherence regarding a classical basis to exhibit anomalous scaling.
(b) If we take a non-classical eigenbasis, coherence regarding that basis loses its connection to the scaling (
\rho^{+}
may become “incoherent”). On the other hand, our two conditions remain relevant.
In both cases, there can be a state
\rho^{-}
that has as much coherence as
\rho^{+}
but does not satisfy (Q1) or (Q2) and thus does not show anomalous scaling.

Let us prove the claim.
First, because
\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)=\bar{\lambda}(\rho)
, the negation of (Q2) implies that
\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)
is
O(N)
.
Then, split the summation as
\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)=\mathcal{T}^{+}-\mathcal{T}^{-}
with
\mathcal{T}^{+}=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)\geq 0}\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{T}^{-}=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)<0}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)|
.
Due to the negation of (Q1), the negative part
\mathcal{T}^{-}
is at most
O(N)
. Therefore, the positive part
\mathcal{T}^{+}
can also be at most
O(N)
.
Finally,
m_{X}(\rho)
is bounded as

\displaystyle m_{X}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}(x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}})^{2}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)\neq 0}(x_{s}-x_{s^{\prime}})^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{+}+\mathcal{T}^{-}),

and thus is of order
N
.

Example
.—
We illustrate the above discussion through the model used in Ref.
Tajima and Funo 2021
.
Its Hamiltonian
H
has two
N
-fold degenerate eigenvalues,
0
and
\omega
.
The jump operators represent simultaneous jumps between energy levels; with an eigenbasis
\{|g,j\rangle,|e,j\rangle\}_{j=1}^{N}
such that
H|g,j\rangle=0
and
H|e,j\rangle=\omega|e,j\rangle
, they are defined as
L_{+}=\sqrt{\gamma_{+}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}|e,j\rangle\langle g,j^{\prime}|
and
L_{-}=\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}|g,j\rangle\langle e,j^{\prime}|
.
Anomalous scaling
m_{H}(\rho)=O(N^{2})
is observed if we set
\rho=\rho^{+}=p_{g}|g,+\rangle\langle g,+|+p_{e}|e,+\rangle\langle e,+|
with
|s,+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}|s,j\rangle
for
s=e,g
(see Supplemental Material
smA
).
The state
\rho^{+}
has a large
l_{1}
-coherence
C_{l_{1}}(\rho^{+})=O(N)
, defined as
C_{l_{1}}(\rho)=\sum_{s,j,s^{\prime},j^{\prime}|(s,j)\neq(s^{\prime},j^{\prime})}|\langle s,j|\rho|s^{\prime},j^{\prime}\rangle|
, to which Ref.
Tajima and Funo 2021
attributed the dissipationless current.
Now, note that the basis
\{|g,j\rangle,|e,j\rangle\}_{j=1}^{N}
, defining the
l_{1}
-coherence, is classical since there are only two jumps regardless of
N
and, for example,
\langle e,j^{\prime}|L_{+}|g,j\rangle=\sqrt{\gamma_{+}}=O(1)
.
On the other hand, if we consider a non-classical basis including
|g,+\rangle
and
|e,+\rangle
(note that
\langle e,+|L_{+}|g,+\rangle=\sqrt{\gamma_{+}}N
), the state
\rho^{+}
has no coherence regarding this basis.
This fact shows that
m_{H}(\rho^{+})
may exhibit anomalous scaling (that is, we may have dissipationless current) even if
\rho^{+}
has no coherence if the referred basis is not classical.
Nonetheless, we see that
\rho^{+}
fulfills either (Q1) or (Q2) for any eigenbasis, from the general discussion.

In addition, we can construct a state
\rho^{-}
that has as much coherence regarding the classical eigenbasis as
\rho^{+}
but does not yield dissipationless current, violating both of the quantumness conditions, (Q1) and (Q2).
Such a state is constructed by replacing
|s,+\rangle
in the definition of
\rho^{+}
with
|s,-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}|s,j\rangle
.
While it has the same amount of
l_{1}
-coherence as
\rho^{+}
, the integrated fluxes and average escape rate read
\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho^{-})=\gamma_{s}p_{s}\chi(N)
for
s\neq s^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{T}_{ss}(\rho^{-})=\frac{\gamma_{s}p_{s}}{2}(N-\chi(N))
, and
\bar{\lambda}(\rho^{-})=\frac{\gamma_{+}p_{g}+\gamma_{-}p_{e}}{2}(N+\chi(N))
, where
\gamma_{g}=\gamma_{+}
,
\gamma_{e}=\gamma_{-}
, and
\chi(N)
is the remainder when
N
is divided by
2

smA
.
Therefore,
\rho^{-}
does not satisfy either (Q1) and (Q2), and leads to
m_{H}(\rho^{-})=O(1)
.
Hence, anomalous dissipationless current does not occur for
\rho^{-}
, which cannot be understood by looking at the coherence alone.

Discussion

We have explored fundamental connections between quantum thermodynamics and quasiprobability; in particular, we have derived a quantum TUR evaluating fluctuations through the TMH quasiprobability.

As discussed, our TUR is complementary to existing TURs, which adopt different approaches when quantifying fluctuations.

I.1
Quantization of generating function

We show that the moment generating function (
6
) is obtained by appropriately quantizing the classical counterpart.
Consider classical Markov processes with discrete mesostates
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
The system’s state is described by a probability density
\bm{p}=(p_{i})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that
p_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
.
Its time evolution is generally written as the classical master equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{p}(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}\big(\bm{p}(t)\big)\coloneqq R\bm{p}(t),

(17)

where
R
is the rate matrix, which we assume time-independent
Gardiner 2009
.
Given the initial probability density
\bm{p}_{0}
, the classical master equation (
17
) is solved by
\bm{p}(t)=e^{Rt}\bm{p}_{0}=e^{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}t}\bm{p}_{0}
.

Let us consider a quantity that depends on the system’s mesostate,
\bm{f}=(f_{i})
.
Its expectation value at time
t
is provided as
\langle\bm{f}\rangle_{t}=\sum_{i}f_{i}p_{i}(t)
.
By using the standard inner product
\langle\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{i}v_{i}w_{i}
, it is rewritten as
\langle\bm{f}\rangle_{t}=\langle\bm{f},\bm{p}(t)\rangle
.
The Heisenberg picture can be discussed by defining
\bm{f}(t)=e^{R^{\mathrm{T}}t}\bm{f}
, where
\mathrm{T}
indicates transpose.
Because
(e^{Rt})^{\mathrm{T}}=e^{R^{\mathrm{T}}t}
, we find
\langle\bm{f}(t)\rangle_{0}=\langle\bm{f}\rangle_{t}
.
The adjoint of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is given by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\dagger}(\bm{f})=R^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{f}
.
It determines the time evolution of
\bm{f}(t)
as
\frac{d}{dt}\bm{f}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\dagger}(\bm{f}(t))
.

We next consider the statistics of the increment of
\bm{f}
.
The joint probability that the system is in state
i
at time
t
and in
j
at time
t+\Delta t
is given by
[e^{R\Delta t}]_{ji}p_{i}(t)

Gardiner 2009
.
Therefore, the change in
\bm{f}
, denoted by
\Delta\bm{f}
, has the moments

\displaystyle\big\langle(\Delta\bm{f})^{n}\big\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}=\sum_{i,j}(f_{j}-f_{i})^{n}[e^{R\Delta t}]_{ji}p_{i}(t).

(18)

As shown later, they are generated by the moment generating function defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda,\Delta t)\coloneq\Big\langle e^{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\dagger}\Delta t}(e^{i\lambda\bm{f}})e^{-i\lambda\bm{f}}\Big\rangle_{t},

(19)

where the exponential and product of vectors are interpreted entrywise;
e^{\bm{v}}=(e^{v_{i}})
and
\bm{v}\bm{w}=(v_{i}w_{i})
for any vectors
\bm{v}
and
\bm{w}
.
That is, we can prove

\displaystyle\big\langle(\Delta\bm{f})^{n}\big\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}=(-i\partial_{\lambda})^{n}\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda,\Delta t)|_{\lambda=0}.

(20)

Importantly, the TMH moment generating function (
6
) is led to by the following replacement

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}\to\mathcal{L},\;\;\bm{f}\to X,\;\;\bm{p}\to\rho,

(21)

and the introduction of the anticommutator between
e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Delta t}(e^{i\lambda X})
and
e^{-i\lambda X}
.
If the anticommutator is not applied,
\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{cl}}
will become the moment generating function corresponding to the Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobability
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
.

Let us prove Eq. (
20
).
First, expand
\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{cl}}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda,\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\lambda)^{m}}{m!}\frac{(i\lambda)^{l}}{l!}\big\langle e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Delta t}(\bm{f}^{m})(-\bm{f})^{l}\big\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{r}\frac{(i\lambda)^{r}}{r!}\binom{r}{l}\big\langle e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Delta t}(\bm{f}^{r-l})(-\bm{f})^{l}\big\rangle_{t}.

Differentiating by
\lambda
for
n
times and making
\lambda
zero, we finally find the terms corresponding to
r=n
in the summation; so we get

\displaystyle(-i\partial_{\lambda})^{n}\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda,\Delta t)|_{\lambda=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}\big\langle e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\Delta t}(\bm{f}^{n-l})(-\bm{f})^{l}\big\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}\sum_{i,j}[e^{R\Delta t}]_{ji}f_{j}^{n-l}(-f_{i})^{l}p_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}[e^{R\Delta t}]_{ji}p_{i}(t)\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}f_{j}^{n-l}(-f_{i})^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}(f_{j}-f_{i})^{n}[e^{R\Delta t}]_{ji}p_{i}(t)=\langle(\Delta\bm{f})^{n}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t},

which concludes the proof.

Let us focus on the second moment in the short-time limit.
By expanding
e^{R\Delta t}
, we find

\displaystyle\big\langle(\Delta\bm{f})^{2}\big\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}=\sum_{i,j}(f_{j}-f_{i})^{2}R_{ji}p_{i}(t)\Delta t+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(22)

where the zeroth order term vanishes because
(f_{j}-f_{i})^{n}\delta_{ji}p_{i}=0
for any
i
and
j
.
Thus, the short-time second moment
m_{\bm{f}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\big\langle(\Delta\bm{f})^{2}\big\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}/\Delta t
reads

\displaystyle m_{\bm{f}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}(f_{j}-f_{i})^{2}R_{ji}p_{i}(t)

(23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}f_{j}^{2}R_{ji}p_{i}(t)-2\sum_{i,j}f_{i}f_{j}R_{ji}p_{i}(t),

(24)

where we used
\sum_{j}R_{ji}=0
.
By remembering
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\dagger}=R^{\mathrm{T}}
, we can further rewrite this equation into

\displaystyle m_{\bm{f}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(t)=\big\langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\dagger}(\bm{f}^{2})-2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\dagger}(\bm{f})\bm{f}\big\rangle_{t}.

(25)

We can quantize this equation and recover Eq. (
13
) by
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{f})\bm{f}\to\{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(X),X\}/2
in addition to the replacement in Eq. (
21
).

I.2
Connection between FCS and quasiprobability

We discuss a nontrivial intersection between the FCS and quasiprobabilities.
In the former framework, we consider current observables
\mathcal{J}_{w}\coloneqq\sum_{k}w_{k}\mathcal{N}_{k}
during time interval
[t,t+\Delta t]
, where
w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is the weight and
\mathcal{N}_{k}
is the number of occurrences of jump
k
during the time interval.
Its moments are gained from the moment generating function
\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda,\Delta t)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(e^{\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\Delta t}\rho(t))
with
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\rho)=\mathcal{L}(\rho)+\sum_{k}(e^{i\lambda w_{k}}-1)L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}

Landi et al. 2024
.
As explained in the main text, the moments of
\Delta X
also have their own generating function
\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)
defined in Eq. (
6
).

While
\mathsf{G}_{t}
and
\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}
are different quantities, we can relate them when the jumps induce changes in
X
as

\displaystyle L_{k}=\sum_{x,y|y-x=w_{k}}a_{yx}|y\rangle\langle x|

(26)

with some
a_{yx}\in\mathbb{C}
.
This condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle[X,L_{k}]=w_{k}L_{k},

(27)

and then
\mathcal{J}_{w}
can be regarded as accumulating the change of
X
.
In this case, we can show

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)-\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)=\sum_{x,y}H_{yx}\rho_{xy}\sin\big(\lambda(y-x)\big),

(28)

where
\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)=\partial_{\Delta t}\mathsf{G}_{t}(\lambda,\Delta t)|_{\Delta t=0}
,
\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)=\partial_{\Delta t}\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda,\Delta t)|_{\Delta t=0}
,
H_{yx}=\langle y|H|x\rangle
, and
\rho_{xy}=\langle x|\rho(t)|y\rangle
.
The functions
\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)
and
\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)
provide the short-time moments because

\displaystyle(-i\partial_{\lambda})^{n}\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)|_{\lambda=0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\Delta t}\langle(\Delta X)^{n}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}|_{\Delta t=0}

(29)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\langle(\Delta X)^{n}\rangle_{t,t+\Delta t}}{\Delta t},

(30)

and the same discussion is possible for
\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)
.
From Eq. (
28
), we see that the counting statistics can provide the short-time statistics of
\Delta X
if Eq. (
27
) holds and
H
or
\rho
commutes with
X
.
If they do not, the unitary time evolution gives rise to changes in
X
, which cannot be detected through jumps.
Nonetheless, even in the presence of such noncommutativity, the even-order moments coincide;

\displaystyle(-i\partial_{\lambda})^{2n}\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)|_{\lambda=0}-(-i\partial_{\lambda})^{2n}\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)|_{\lambda=0}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{x,y}(y-x)^{2n}\sin\big(\lambda(y-x)\big)|_{\lambda=0}=0.

Consequently,
m_{X}
can be computed by monitoring jumps under Eq. (
27
).
Still, we stress that Eq. (
27
) is a highly restrictive condition, so that neither of the TURs implies the other in general.

Let us prove Eq. (
28
). It is not difficult to see that Eq. (
27
) leads to the following relations:

\displaystyle[X,L_{k}^{\dagger}]=-w_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger},

(31a)

\displaystyle[X,L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}]=0,

(31b)

\displaystyle e^{i\lambda w_{k}}L_{k}=e^{i\lambda X}L_{k}e^{-i\lambda X},

(31c)

\displaystyle e^{i\lambda w_{k}}L_{k}^{\dagger}=e^{-i\lambda X}L_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\lambda X}.

(31d)

Next, we can write
\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)
and
\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{fcs}}(\lambda)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\rho(t))=\sum_{k}(e^{i\lambda w_{k}}-1)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho)

(32)

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{i}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big(\big\{[H,e^{i\lambda X}],e^{-i\lambda X}\}\rho(t)\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big(\big\{L_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\lambda X}L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},e^{i\lambda X}\},e^{-i\lambda X}\big\}\rho(t)\Big).

(33)

The summand in
\mathsf{g}_{t}(\lambda)
is transformed as

\displaystyle\big\{L_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\lambda X}L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},e^{i\lambda X}\},e^{-i\lambda X}\big\}

\displaystyle=L_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\lambda X}L_{k}e^{-i\lambda X}+e^{-i\lambda X}L_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\lambda X}L_{k}

\displaystyle\quad-L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}e^{i\lambda X}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}e^{-i\lambda X}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\lambda X}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}e^{i\lambda X}

\displaystyle=2e^{i\lambda w_{k}}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}-2L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},

where we have used Eqs. (
31b
), (
31c
), and (
31d
) in the last line.
Therefore, we find

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{\mathrm{TMH}}(\lambda,t)=\frac{i}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big(\big\{[H,e^{i\lambda X}],e^{-i\lambda X}\}\rho(t)\Big)+\mathsf{g}_{\mathrm{jump}}(\lambda,t).

(34)

Rewriting the first term by the eigenbasis of
X
, we obtain Eq. (
28
).

.3
Derivation of Eq. (
11
)

We concisely review the derivation of Eq. (
11
) in Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2025
.
For the proof of unproven facts, please see that paper.

The fundamental strategy is as follows: 1) rewrite the entropy production rate as an inner product, 2) rewrite it as a squared norm, and 3) apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
First, notice that for each pair of jumps
(k,-k)
, we can take coefficients
\gamma_{\pm k}>0
and operators
\tilde{L}_{\pm k}
such that
L_{\pm k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\pm k}}\tilde{L}_{\pm k}
and
\tilde{L}_{-k}=\tilde{L}_{k}^{\dagger}
. The local detailed balance imposes
\gamma_{k}/\gamma_{-k}=e^{s_{k}}
(
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is set to be unity).
Then, we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}O&J_{-k}(\rho)\\
J_{k}(\rho)&O\end{pmatrix},

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}O&F_{-k}(\rho)\\
F_{k}(\rho)&O\end{pmatrix},

(36)

where

\displaystyle J_{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}\tilde{L}_{k}\rho-\gamma_{-k}\rho\tilde{L}_{k}),

(37)

\displaystyle F_{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq s_{k}\tilde{L}_{k}+[\tilde{L}_{k},\ln\rho].

(38)

Now,
\mathbb{J}_{k}(\rho)
and
\mathbb{F}_{k}(\rho)
are anti-Hermitian operators on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is the original Hilbert space.
By aligning them, we further define

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}(\rho)\coloneqq\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{J}_{k}(\rho),\quad\mathbb{F}(\rho)\coloneqq\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{F}_{k}(\rho),

(39)

where the direct sum is taken over the pairs of jumps (thus, if
k
is counted,
-k
is not).
They become operators on
\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathfrak{h}
is a complex vector space whose dimension is equal to the number of jumps.
Then, the entropy production rate (EPR) defined in Eq. (
9
) is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=\langle\mathbb{J}(\rho),\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rangle,

(40)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, defined as
\langle A,B\rangle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(A^{\dagger}B)
.

Next, we introduce a super-operator
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}
that maps operators on
\mathcal{H}
to those on
\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H}
.
It is defined by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}A\coloneqq[I_{\mathfrak{h}}\otimes A,\mathbb{L}],

(41)

where
I_{\mathfrak{h}}
is the identity operator on
\mathfrak{h}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{k}\begin{pmatrix}O&\tilde{L}_{-k}\\
\tilde{L}_{k}&O\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

It acts as a gradient, and its adjoint turns
\mathbb{J}(\rho)
into the dissipator as
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{\dagger}\mathbb{J}(\rho)=\mathcal{D}(\rho)
.

Finally, for a positive operator
G
, we introduce a super-operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{G}(A)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}G^{s}XG^{1-s}ds,

(43)

which satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))=\mathbb{J}(\rho)
with

\displaystyle\Gamma=\bigoplus_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{k}/2&0\\
0&\gamma_{-k}/2\end{pmatrix}.

(44)

We can define an inner product
\langle A,B\rangle_{G}=\langle A,\mathcal{M}_{G}(B)\rangle
and the induced norm
\lVert A\rVert_{G}=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle_{G}}
, which allows us to rewrite the EPR as

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=\lVert\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}.

(45)

By applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}\geq|\langle\mathbb{F}(\rho),\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}|^{2}.

(46)

Because
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))=\mathbb{J}(\rho)
holds, the right-hand side leads to
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))
.
On the other hand, we can generally show
\langle A,\mathcal{M}_{G}(A)\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(A^{\dagger}\{G,A\})
; by a direct calculation, we can derive that this upper bound leads to
\mathfrak{D}_{X}(\rho)
when
A=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X
and
G=\Gamma\otimes\rho
and obtain Eq. (
11
).

Appendix A
Computational details of Example

We provide the details of the computation in the example in the main text.
We aim to compute
\mathcal{T}_{s^{\prime}s}(\rho)
and
\bar{\lambda}(\rho)
for
\rho=\rho^{\pm}
and show
m_{H}(\rho^{+})=O(N^{2})
and
m_{H}(\rho^{-})=O(1)
.
Here, the model we consider consists of Hamiltonian
H=\omega\sum_{j}|e,j\rangle\langle e,j|
and jump operators
L_{+}=\sqrt{\gamma_{+}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}|e,j\rangle\langle g,j^{\prime}|
and
L_{-}=\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}|g,j\rangle\langle e,j^{\prime}|
.
The states of interest
\rho^{\pm}
are generated as
\rho^{\pm}=\sum_{s=g,e}p_{s}|s,\pm\rangle\langle s,\pm|
, where
|s,\pm\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}(\pm 1)^{j}|s,j\rangle
and
p_{s}
satisfies
p_{s}\geq 0
and
p_{g}+p_{e}=1
.

Because now we consider
X=H
, we need to compute

\displaystyle T_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}sj}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(\{\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}\Pi_{s^{\prime}j^{\prime}},\Pi_{sj}\}\rho\big).

(47)

For convenience, we define

\displaystyle\sigma_{s,j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big(|s,j\rangle\langle s,j^{\prime}|+|s,j^{\prime}\rangle\langle s,j|\big),

(48)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s,j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j^{\prime}}\sigma_{s,j,j^{\prime}}.

(49)

They satisfy

\displaystyle\sigma_{s,j,j^{\prime}}=\sigma_{s,j^{\prime},j},\;\;\sigma_{s,j,j}=\Pi_{s,j},

(50)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\sigma_{s,j,j^{\prime}}\rho)=\mathrm{Re}\langle s,j|\rho|s,j^{\prime}\rangle,

(51)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\Lambda_{s,j}=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}|s,j\rangle\langle s,j^{\prime}|,

(52)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\{\Lambda_{s^{\prime},j^{\prime}},\Pi_{s,j}\}=\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}\Lambda_{s,j}.

(53)

By using them, we obtain

\displaystyle L_{+}^{\dagger}\Pi_{s,j}L_{+}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{+}\delta_{s,e}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{g,j^{\prime}},

(54)

\displaystyle L_{-}^{\dagger}\Pi_{s,j}L_{-}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}\delta_{s,g}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{e,j^{\prime}},

(55)

and

\displaystyle L_{+}^{\dagger}L_{+}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{+}N\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{g,j^{\prime}},

(56)

\displaystyle L_{-}^{\dagger}L_{-}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}N\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{e,j^{\prime}}.

(57)

Then, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}\Pi_{s,j}

\displaystyle=\delta_{s,e}\bigg(\gamma_{+}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{g,j^{\prime}}-\gamma_{-}N\Lambda_{e,j}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\delta_{s,g}\bigg(\gamma_{-}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{e,j^{\prime}}-\gamma_{+}N\Lambda_{g,j}\bigg).

(58)

Consequently, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\{\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}\Pi_{s^{\prime},j^{\prime}},\Pi_{s,j}\}

\displaystyle=\delta_{s^{\prime},e}\delta_{s,g}\gamma_{+}\Lambda_{g,j}-\frac{\delta_{s^{\prime},e}\delta_{s,e}}{2}\gamma_{-}N(\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{e,j}+\sigma_{e,j,j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle+\delta_{s^{\prime},g}\delta_{s,e}\gamma_{-}\Lambda_{e,j}-\frac{\delta_{s^{\prime},g}\delta_{s,g}}{2}\gamma_{+}N(\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\Lambda_{g,j}+\sigma_{g,j,j^{\prime}}).

(59)

For density matrix
\rho
, the fluxes are given as

\displaystyle T_{ej^{\prime}gj}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{+}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}}\mathrm{Re}\langle g,j|\rho|g,j^{\prime\prime}\rangle

(60)

\displaystyle T_{gj^{\prime}ej}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}}\mathrm{Re}\langle e,j|\rho|e,j^{\prime\prime}\rangle

(61)

\displaystyle T_{gj^{\prime}gj}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{+}N}{2}\bigg(\mathrm{Re}\langle g,j|\rho|g,j^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{===}+\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}}\mathrm{Re}\langle g,j|\rho|g,j^{\prime\prime}\rangle\bigg)

(62)

\displaystyle T_{ej^{\prime}ej}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{-}N}{2}\bigg(\mathrm{Re}\langle e,j|\rho|e,j^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{===}+\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}}\mathrm{Re}\langle e,j|\rho|e,j^{\prime\prime}\rangle\bigg).

(63)

For
\rho=\rho^{+}
and
\rho^{-}
, because
\langle s,j|\rho^{+}|s,j^{\prime\prime}\rangle=p_{s}/N
and
\langle s,j|\rho^{-}|s,j^{\prime\prime}\rangle=(-1)^{j+j^{\prime\prime}}p_{s}/N
, we find

\displaystyle T_{ej^{\prime}gj}(\rho^{+})=p_{g}\gamma_{+},\;\;T_{ej^{\prime}gj}(\rho^{-})=(-1)^{j+1}p_{g}\gamma_{+}\frac{\chi(N)}{N},

(64)

where we used
\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}=-\chi(N)
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{eg}(\rho^{+})=p_{g}\gamma_{+}N^{2},\quad\mathcal{T}_{eg}(\rho^{-})=p_{g}\gamma_{+}\chi(N).

(65)

Note that
\chi(N)^{2}=\chi(N)
.
We also see

\displaystyle T_{gj^{\prime}gj}(\rho^{+})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{+}p_{g}}{2}(1+\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}N)

(66)

\displaystyle T_{gj^{\prime}gj}(\rho^{-})

\displaystyle=-\frac{(-1)^{j}\gamma_{+}p_{g}}{2}((-1)^{j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\chi(N)).

(67)

Therefore, we can get

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{gg}(\rho^{+})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{+}p_{g}}{2}N(N-1),

(68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{gg}(\rho^{-})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{+}p_{g}}{2}(N-\chi(N))

(69)

because

\displaystyle\sum_{j,j^{\prime}(\neq j)}(-1)^{j}(-1)^{j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}\bigg)^{2}-\sum_{j}(-1)^{2j}

(70)

\displaystyle=\chi(N)-N.

(71)

The other integrated fluxes are given in the same way.
Besides, we can compute the average escape rates as

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}(\rho^{+})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{+}p_{g}+\gamma_{-}p_{e}}{2}N(N+1),

(72)

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}(\rho^{-})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{+}p_{g}+\gamma_{-}p_{e}}{2}(N+\chi(N)).

(73)

Finally, from Eq. (
65
), we find

\displaystyle m_{H}(\rho^{+})

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}(\gamma_{+}p_{g}+\gamma_{-}p_{e})N^{2}=O(N^{2}),

(74)

\displaystyle m_{H}(\rho^{-})

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}(\gamma_{+}p_{g}+\gamma_{-}p_{e})\chi(N)=O(1).

(75)
