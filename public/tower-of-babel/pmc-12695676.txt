A Diagnosis Hidden in Plain Sight: A Rare Presentation of Isolated Dysarthria in Wilson's Disease—A Case Report
Authors: Kruthika Reddy, Maanini Singhvi, Archana B Netto, B Thejus, Leroy D Souza, Deepak B Shivananda, Samyuktha Vinu Nair, Deepak Rai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA clinical picture of isolated dysarthria is relatively uncommon, and the underlying condition causing it can be easily misdiagnosed. In this case, a 24‐year‐old Indian male presented with complaints of slurring of speech occurring for the past 9 months. The patient also complained of difficulty swallowing food using his tongue. There was also a history of excessive drooling of saliva. There was no history of involuntary movements suggestive of tremor, limb dystonia, Parkinsonism, ataxia, behavioral abnormalities, or any significant cognitive impairment. The patient's parents had a non‐consanguineous marriage, and there were no similar complaints among the patient's siblings. The patient was started on copper chelating agents and speech therapy and noticed remarkable improvement as per his own account. Clinical examination showed Kayser‐Fleischer rings in the eyes, raised serum ceruloplasmin, and significant MRI findings. If symptoms like isolated dysarthria are encountered, a thorough work‐up must be done to rule out any underlying neurological problems. The key teaching point revolves around the consanguinity of marriages in India should raise an index of suspicion for autosomal recessive conditions and movement disorders.Keywords: consanguinity, D‐penicillamine, dysarthria, KF rings, neuropsychiatric symptoms, Wilson's disease

Introduction

Wilson's disease or hepatolenticular degeneration is an autosomal recessive disease that results in an excess copper accumulation in the body. It primarily affects the liver, basal ganglia of the brain, cornea, as well as other organs [1]. The World Health Organization estimates the global prevalence of Wilson's disease to be between 1/10000 and 1/30000 [2].

Patients with Wilson's disease usually present with a combination of neurological symptoms like limb dystonia, Parkinsonism, and wing‐beating tremors. Isolated dysarthria and tongue dystonia in the absence of movement disorder manifestations such as tremor, Parkinsonism, choreoathetosis, ataxia, psychiatric, and cognitive impairment, as seen in this case, are a rare presentation of Wilson's disease.

The condition is caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene, which result in impaired excretion of copper in bile. Free copper is released into the bloodstream and is removed by urine instead of feces [3]. The clinical manifestations of Wilson disease are predominantly hepatic, neurologic, and psychiatric, with many patients having a combination of symptoms.

Wilson's disease usually has a delayed diagnosis due to variation in its presentation. Hepatic dysfunction manifestations start earlier than neurological or psychiatric manifestations. In nearly all cases of Wilson's disease, there are clinical or biochemical signs of liver dysfunction. It commonly manifests in the first 10 years of life and can vary from asymptomatic stage to liver cirrhosis [4].

In the neurological manifestations, extrapyramidal features are commonly seen in Indian cases. A study by Taly et al. [5] found Parkinsonism in two thirds of the cases, limb dystonia and ataxia in one third of the cases, and pyramidal features were seen in 16%, chorea, athetosis, and myoclonus in less than 10% of the cases.

The key clinical message of this case report is the fact that consanguinity of marriage is an extremely important aspect to evaluate, especially in the diagnosis of conditions like Wilson's disease. A thorough physical head‐to‐toe examination is required for observing details like KF rings. If this step is missed, the whole diagnosis can change, and hence physical examination is key to making a diagnosis.

Case Presentation

A 24‐year‐old Indian male patient presented with complaints of slurring of speech since the past 9 months. The patient also complained of difficulty swallowing food using his tongue. There was also a history of excessive drooling of saliva. There was no history of involuntary movements suggestive of tremor, limb dystonia, Parkinsonism, ataxia, behavioral abnormalities, or any significant cognitive impairment. The patient's parents had a non‐consanguineous marriage, and there were no similar complaints among the patient's siblings.

Clinical Findings

On examination, the patient was found to have a mixed dysarthria (spastic and ataxic), tongue dystonia, and Kayser‐Fleischer rings, which were present even on naked eye examination of both eyes. Refer Figure 1.

Images—Kayser‐Fleischer ring visible bilaterally.

Diagnostic Assessment

MRI of the brain including SWI was performed and it showed abnormal bilateral symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the bilateral lentiform nuclei, bilateral thalami, dorsal midbrain, and pons. Refer Figure 2.

Images—MRI of the brain, including SWI, showing abnormal diffuse symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the bilateral lentiform nucleus, bilateral thalamus, dorsal midbrain, and pons.

Ultrasound of the abdomen revealed that the liver was normal in size, with diffusely altered echotexture suggestive of diffuse liver disease, but the liver function tests (LFT) and serum ammonia levels were within normal limits.

Serum copper was 23.70 μg/dL (normal 74–130 μg/dL).

Serum ceruloplasmin was 5.00 mg/dL (normal 15–30 μmg/dL).

24‐h urine copper level was 256 μg/day (normal 15–60 μg/day).

There were no diagnostic challenges in this case.

Diagnosis

This is a case of Wilson's disease with the rare presentation of isolated dysarthria and tongue dystonia.

Therapeutic Intervention

The patient was treated with copper chelators and speech therapy.

The copper chelating agent given was—D‐Penicillamine, 250 mg, twice a day, and zinc acetate, 50 mg, three times per day. Supportive management to improve the patient's quality of life consisted of speech therapy. Three months into the above treatment regimen, the patient showed marked improvement in his speech. Parameters such as speech articulation, prosody (intonation), and resonance improved. The number of repetitions in 5 seconds improved from 5 to 8.

Discussion

Wilson's disease or hepatolenticular degeneration is an autosomal recessive disease which results in an excess copper accumulation in the body. It primarily affects the liver, basal ganglia of the brain, cornea, as well as other organs [1]. The World Health Organization estimates the global prevalence of Wilson's disease to be between 1/10,000 and 1/30,000 [2].

Wilson's disease typically presents with hepatic, neurologic, and psychiatric dysfunction, with many patients having a combination of symptoms. Wilson's disease usually has a delayed diagnosis due to variation in its presentation. Hepatic dysfunction manifestations start earlier than neurological or psychiatric manifestations. In nearly all cases of Wilson's disease, there are clinical or biochemical signs of liver dysfunction. It commonly manifests in the first 10 years of life and can vary from asymptomatic stage to liver cirrhosis [3].

In cases which present later in their course, psychiatric manifestations may dominate the clinical presentation [5]. Psychiatric features including mania, depression, suicidal tendencies, and schizophrenia‐like illnesses have been reported in 16%–43% of Indian Wilson's disease cases. While Wilson's disease is uncommon, if left unidentified and untreated, it can be fatal.

In atypical patients, the slightest hint of Wilson's disease in the preliminary investigations should evoke enough suspicion to probe further as the prevalence of consanguineous marriages in India is very high. If symptoms like dysarthria are encountered, a thorough work‐up must be done to rule out Wilson's disease or to diagnose it early.
