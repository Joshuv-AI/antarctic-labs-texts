Values and preferences of patients with severe mental illness regarding smoking cessation interventions: a systematic review with best-fit framework synthesis
Authors: Kristien Coteur, Laura Tops, Geertruida E Bekkering, Louise de Almeida Ferreira Fonseca, Floor van den Brand, Hanne Cloetens, Linan Zeng, Bert Aertgeerts, Nicolas Delvaux, Arnav Agarwal, Thomas Agoritsas, Mieke Vermandere

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo identify values and preferences regarding smoking cessation interventions among adults with severe mental illnessDesignSystematic review with best-fit framework synthesisData sourcesMEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, CINAHL and Scopus from inception to 14 November 2025.Study selectionStudies with any design, in any clinical and geographical setting, reporting on adult (18+) current or past smokers with a diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar or mood disorder, including major depressive disorder or post-traumatic stress disorder. We selected all quantitative and qualitative findings regarding patients’ values and preferences, including beliefs, attitudes, behaviours and perceived barriers and facilitators, in relation to smoking cessation interventions.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened studies. After a pilot to increase accuracy, data were extracted by one reviewer and verified by another. Risk of bias was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. We used the best-fit framework synthesis methodology to synthesise the data.ResultsOf 14 970 identified articles, 65 were included. Most studies were of moderate to high quality. Financial costs posed a significant barrier, while education and social support emerged as important facilitators. Patients preferred personal interactions with healthcare providers. Their motivation for cessation varied and was influenced by habits and perceived nicotine dependence. Health concerns and financial savings were primary drivers for reflective motivation. Beliefs regarding treatment varied. Although commonly used, scepticism about nicotine replacement therapy and concerns about the long-term safety of electronic delivery systems were reported. Overall, patients preferred personalised, flexible programmes and emphasised the need for tailored approaches. Digital interventions, especially mobile apps, that provide support, motivational content and relevant information, were perceived as appealing and helpful when sufficiently considering accessibility and usability aspects. Patients commonly perceived smoking as a coping mechanism for negative emotions. Developing new coping strategies and creating smoke-free environments were deemed to contribute to successful cessation.ConclusionsPatient-centred care for people with severe mental illness should leverage behavioural and pharmacological strategies for smoking cessation. Flexibility, accessibility and ongoing support appeared important for addressing stress, withdrawal symptom interpretation and relapse vulnerability in this population.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022337933.Keywords: PSYCHIATRY, Public health, Smoking Reduction

Introduction

Rationale

Severe mental illness (SMI) represents a significant public health challenge and includes schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and severe depression.1,4 Individuals diagnosed with SMI face a multitude of struggles, not only directly related to their psychiatric condition but also to the associated health disparities. Research indicates that individuals with SMI have a significantly reduced life expectancy, with estimates suggesting a staggering shortfall of 14 to 23 years compared with the general population.5,7

This risk of premature mortality among individuals with SMI is largely due to preventable causes, such as cardiovascular diseases, respiratory tract disease, metabolic disorders and related cancers.8 9 Smoking is one of the most significant risk factors.10 Smoking affects 50%–80% of individuals with SMI,11 with a notably higher percentage of heavy smokers compared with the general population.12 13 Despite concerted efforts to promote smoking cessation in broader public health initiatives, the challenge is particularly substantial within the context of SMI, where complex interactions between psychiatric symptoms, social determinants and healthcare access further complicate cessation efforts.14,16

People with SMI are likely to receive lower quality care compared with people without mental illness who have the same physical health problems.9 17 Unique barriers and facilitators in this population may include challenges related to mental health symptoms such as anxiety and stress, social factors such as stigma and social isolation, and systemic barriers including limited access to healthcare services tailored to the needs of individuals with SMI.15 16 Understanding the preferences and challenges surrounding smoking cessation among individuals with SMI is paramount in developing patient-centred and effective guidelines and interventions to mitigate the heightened risk of premature mortality in this population.

Objectives

This systematic review and best-fit framework synthesis aimed to map the values and preferences of adult patients with SMI regarding smoking cessation interventions, elucidating their beliefs, attitudes, behaviours and experienced barriers and facilitators. Through a systematic synthesis, we aimed to uncover nuanced insights into the factors influencing smoking cessation efforts within this vulnerable group.

Methods

To comprehensively analyse data on values and preferences regarding smoking cessation interventions among individuals with SMI, we adopted the best-fit framework synthesis methodology.18 19 This involves identifying an existing model or theoretical framework that is relevant to your topic, providing you with a pre-existing framework and themes for data synthesis. For this review, a best-fit framework with 6 categories and 21 concepts was developed, based on 11 articles. More details on the development process are found in online supplemental appendix 3. This method allowed for the integration of prior evidence about successful smoking cessation interventions while maintaining flexibility to accommodate the unique context of the study population. The framework was used as a structured lens for organising heterogeneous values and preferences data. This systematic review is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement20 and Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) guidelines.21 The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42022337933.

This review is accompanied by a linked systematic review and network meta-analysis summarising the efficacy and safety of smoking cessation interventions, such as nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), other medication aiming to help with reducing or quitting smoking, and non-pharmacological interventions.22 Both systematic reviews directly inform a BMJ Rapid Recommendation23 regarding smoking cessation interventions for patients with SMI. This practice guideline is produced by a panel of clinicians, researchers, methodologists and patient partners and adopts the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to move from evidence to recommendations.23 24

Patient and public involvement

This systematic review was conducted in consultation with an expert panel, in which four patients with SMI and lived experience using smoking cessation interventions participated. The expert panel was consulted during the development of the protocol and synthesis of the findings. The study’s results will be disseminated to patients as part of the BMJ Rapid Recommendation.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies addressing values and preferences pertaining to smoking cessation interventions and including adults aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of SMI. SMI was defined as schizophrenia, bipolar or mood disorder, including major depressive disorder or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Eligible studies included other reviews and qualitative, quantitative, mixed-method and case studies. We excluded studies that assessed patients with an intellectual development disorder, personality disorder, anxiety disorder other than PTSD, eating disorder, substance use disorder, subthreshold or mild depression or with less than 80% of the sample having a confirmed eligible diagnosis. We considered diagnoses as confirmed if they were made by a psychiatrist, extracted from patient records from a psychiatric care facility or followed the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders or the International Classification of Diseases. We excluded studies that included patients based on symptom-score checklists, such as the Beck Depression Inventory or the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), or general screening tools.

Search strategy

We searched the electronic databases of MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Web of Science, CINAHL and Scopus from inception to 14 November 2025 with a combination of keywords for the concepts of SMI, smoking cessation interventions and patient values and preferences (online supplemental appendix 1 for the search strategy). We screened the reference lists of eligible reviews to identify any additional missed studies. We did not limit the search nor eligibility based on time since publication nor language of publication. Preprints were included if meeting other eligibility criteria, but preprint databases were not specifically searched.

Selection process

After deduplication, paired reviewers (LT, GEB, KC, LdAFF, FvdB and HC) conducted title and abstract and full-text screening independently with Rayyan QCRI25 (figure 1). Disagreements were resolved by consensus. If a full text was not available, the first author was contacted once via email or their personal profile on ResearchGate to request the article.

PRISMA flow chart. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Data collection process

After a pilot exercise, paired reviewers (LT, GEB, KC, LdAFF, FvdB and HC) conducted data extraction using a prespecified standardised form (Microsoft Excel) for the first 11 articles, which included the a priori framework for qualitative data analysis. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Given high inter-rater reliability, the remaining articles (n=54) were extracted by a single reviewer with extracted data cross-checked by a second reviewer and disagreements resolved through discussion.

Data items

For all articles, publication details (first author, year, text type), research specifics (study aim, method, setting), sample characteristics (eg, sample size, age, gender, diagnosis, smoking status, previous smoking cessation attempts) and data regarding patient values and preferences about smoking cessation interventions were documented (online supplemental appendix 2). Quantitative outcomes were expected to include mainly descriptive measures such as ranking of interventions according to preference, usage rates or distribution of perspectives regarding harm in a sample. For qualitative data, a best-fit framework was developed, following the guidance of Carroll et al18 19 (development process described in online supplemental appendix 3. This framework consisted of several concepts and their subthemes, relevant to smoking cessation interventions, such as opportunity, social influence, capability, motivation and intervention preferences.

Study risk of bias assessment

Reviewers (LT, KC, LdAFF, FvdB and HC) used the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool26 to assess methodological quality of individual studies. We conducted the assessment independently and in duplicate for the first 10 studies, and by a single reviewer with data cross-checked by a second reviewer for the remaining articles. The two reviewers resolved disagreements by consensus.

Synthesis methods

We used (secondary) thematic analysis18 for synthesis of the qualitative data. Both original data, such as quotes, and their interpretation by the author(s), that is, published results, were coded against the themes of the best-fit framework (Microsoft Excel). When data did not fit in the framework, new themes were developed inductively and discussed during consensus meetings. Two researchers (LT and KC) reviewed the findings and independently provided a summary of key concepts and findings. We merged these summaries during a consensus meeting and then reviewed them with the entire team. An overview of the final framework and main findings per concept can be found in online supplemental appendix 4. Quotes are reported throughout the results to illustrate the variability in findings. We summarised the quantitative findings descriptively per concept.

Results

Study characteristics

From a total of 14 970 articles, 65 articles were included, reporting on 61 studies (figure 1). More than half reported on studies that were conducted in North America (n=41), while others were in Europe (n=13), Australia (n=6) and Asia (n=5). Articles were published between 1989 and 2025, with most (56/65) being published after 2011. The majority were reports on qualitative studies (n=33), quantitative descriptive studies (n=18) or mixed-method studies (n=9). Studies were generally of moderate to high quality; 49 studies (75%) met at least 80% of the methodological criteria (see online supplemental appendix 5). A detailed overview of study characteristics is reported in online supplemental appendix 6.

Synthesis of findings

The literature on values and preferences of adult patients with SMI regarding smoking cessation interventions is heterogeneous and fragmented because studies often target one specific behaviour or element in a smoking cessation strategy, one specific population or the evaluation of a newly developed or implemented intervention in different healthcare settings (online supplemental appendix 6). A synthesis of key findings is presented per concept of the best-fit framework (online supplemental appendix 4).

Capability

SMI was reported to complicate quitting for some individuals due to smoking serving as a coping mechanism for negative emotions.27,29 In line with this, stress was identified as a significant trigger for relapse.1630,33 Strategies such as developing new coping mechanisms3134,37 and creating smoke-free environments at home31 were identified as helpful for behavioural control. Quitting smoking was perceived as requiring a shift in identity and lifestyle34 38 39 leading to increased self-control and self-efficacy.38

I think I was stronger than I thought I was, because—I honestly didn’t think I could quit.38 (p.7)

Opportunity

A significant proportion of patients considered quitting smoking, but many faced barriers such as financial cost of the smoking cessation intervention40 41 or lack of insurance coverage.41 42 Education, social support, practical assistance and emotional guidance were reported by patients as crucial elements for successful cessation.1628 29 36 37 43,47 Personal interactions with healthcare providers (HCPs), over the telephone or face-to-face were preferred and considered as enablers to patients.2848,50 A statistically significant relationship was found between HCPs always recommending or discussing medication to quit smoking and a higher desire to quit in patients.51 Particularly older patients reported that there was insufficient information and encouragement to quit smoking.52 Ambivalence was found toward smoking restrictions, with bans unintentionally leading to cessation for some but others finding alternatives.28 53 54

When the manager is back, we will have to go near the rubbish bin near the entrance (of the community-based residential area) to smoke.54 (p.5)

Motivation

Automatic motivation was influenced by habitual smoking behaviours and perceived dependency on cigarettes, with patients reporting little or no dependence being more likely to quit.55 This could be related to prior unsuccessful quit attempts, as these have been reported to undermine self-efficacy and reinforce perceived dependence.32 56 Difficulty with managing their urges was also reported as an important barrier to cessation.42 47 Psychoeducation about nicotine withdrawal symptoms was emphasised by healthcare professionals because increased distress, anxiety, insomnia or other symptoms may be misinterpreted as a worsening of the psychiatric condition rather than withdrawal, leading patients to believe that smoking benefits their mental health.32 Learning about the interaction between smoking and the severity of psychiatric symptoms was also deemed important by patients.33

In one study, acceptance and commitment therapy helped veterans with PTSD to deal more effectively with tobacco dependence.57 Other patients perceived smoking as part of their identity: “I guess because the fact that I (sic) been doing it so long and it’s just it’s been an extension of me.”,38 and of their daily routine, with some expressing fatalistic and self-doubting beliefs: “Sometimes I think it’s in my DNA that…everything has been harder for me.”.38

Reflective motivation was primarily driven by health concerns, focus on financial savings and the desire to set a good example for others.1627 30 31 34 36 39 41,43 48 50 58 People with schizophrenia might be less motivated and less likely to consider quitting for health concerns and immediate reinforcement, for example, saving money.63 Some patients relativised smoking behaviour, “At least I’m not on dope”,28 (p.2745), or used contrary examples despite being aware of the health consequences of smoking: “…they take the attitude on that they know people who lived till they were 90 and smoked all their life long.”64 (p.110).28 64 Social influence, including disapproval of others for smoking, played a lesser role in motivation.63 65 66

Smoking was perceived as a multifaceted resource: enhancing self-perception, providing comfort, aiding with weight management, providing companionship and facilitating socialisation, offering mental health benefits despite known physical detriments, and representing a consistent element in their lives.1629,32 37 38 41 42 53 59 64 67

Sometimes [smoking] makes me feel more intelligent or grown up, you know, like I’m so sophisticated.38 (p.6)

It’s like a security blanket.30 (p.302)

And he said, ‘this [cigarette] is my friend; I talk to him while I’m smoking’.53 (p.7)

For some, the belief in the benefits of smoking cessation significantly outweighed perceived risks, with women valuing the positive impact on self-esteem significantly more highly than men but also expressing greater concern over negative affect and weight gain.70 For smokers with depression, those who accepted cessation treatment differed significantly from the non-accepters in reporting the goal of lifetime abstinence, greater perceived desire and expectancy for success.71

Varied treatment-related beliefs and attitudes were reported. Scepticism around NRT was found due to fears of addiction.31 In one study, nearly half of the participants expressed concerns about the long-term safety of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS), with mixed perceptions on their harm relative to traditional cigarettes.72 Another study found that attitudes towards the efficacy of cessation medications were slightly positive, with a preference for medication over counselling, which was viewed negatively.73 The willingness to quit ‘cold turkey’ received neutral sentiments.73

Social influences

Stigma and shame surrounding smoking were commonly reported.54 74 75 A non-judgemental approach by HCPs was preferred by patients.16 29 32 62 75 It was important to “talk to somebody who’s not judging me…not saying ‘oh, yeah, smoking is stupid’… kind of putting me down… she also helped me with believing in myself.”75 (p.3-5) Positive encouragement and both social and practical support from family—for example, living with them after treatment—friends, peers and HCPs, were perceived as pivotal for successful cessation.31 33 34 37 38 50 54 58 62 69 73 75 76

Social acceptance of smoking and exposure to smoking environments posed challenges to quitting.16 27 31 37 45 53 59 68 69 76 In one study, patients in a residential setting endorsed HCPs as role models and generally agreed that they should avoid smoking.47

Intervention preferences

Patients preferred personalised, flexible and holistic cessation programmes that catered to individual needs.1628 33,36 46 50 77 78 This included considering coping strategies tailored to the person,30 36 for example, individual sessions with a physical therapist instead of exercising in group because of self-image concerns: “look at my fat jiggle when I run”.36 (p.47). Patients reported preferring to have some control regarding when calls with HCPs occur or choosing to reduce smoking as an initial goal.46 A cut down to quit approach, that is, first cutting down and quitting later, was preferred by most patients despite associated challenges.31 48 62 75 79

It was hard because the woman running the group wanted us to cut down instead of stopping immediately. I knew I could have a cigarette only at certain hours. So in between, I was constantly thinking about having a cigarette. I was obsessed with it.79 (p.523)

High interest was reported in behavioural interventions, such as hypnosis, internet programmes and counselling, ranging from 40% to 70% of participants.40 42 Group exercise sessions were deemed to be a great opportunity to meet new people, provide and receive peer support, voice concerns, ask questions, learn from others, and increase accountability.36 44 58 “Walking and group sessions have helped me in staying positive and increasing my self confidence.”44 (p.27) Similarly, there was some interest in peer-based counselling50 as well as sessions in green, outdoor settings.37 The exposure to fresh air and positive sensory experiences helped them associate well-being with not smoking and offered cues that could help counter craving.37

Counselling and smoking cessation sessions were among the most helpful interventions once patients had decided to stop smoking.47 62 80 Telephone counselling was deemed very helpful,49 and could facilitate outreach by HCPs, which on its own was valued by patients46: “No other nurse or doctor has ever followed up or helped in any way”.46 (p.9) Participation in a smoking cessation programme could bring participants structure, which helps establish a daily purpose, engage in meaningful activities, and reduce boredom.32 37 62 Useful strategies that the patients could implement themselves were raising awareness by tracking cigarette count, carbon monoxide testing and practising mindfulness.29 81 82

Drug therapies, including NRT and other medications to promote reducing or quitting smoking, were commonly utilised, although with concerns about side effects and cost.3240,42 50 61 79 83 In one study, NRT was deemed to be the most helpful strategy when patients decided to quit smoking.80 Although inhaler, patches, gum, lozenges, and nasal spray were endorsed by patients,1642 84,86 ENDS, more specifically e-cigarettes and inhalers, were most preferred because they were perceived as more efficient and mirrored smoking behaviour without the smell.27 28 31 85 87 In one study, patients reported obtaining psychological reward from using an e-cigarette, describing it calmed them down, made them feel more awake, less irritable, helped with concentration and reduced hunger.88 In another study, patients reported NRT to be a highly effective strategy for coping with withdrawal symptoms and severe cravings, allowing them to focus more on the psychological and behavioural aspects of smoking cessation.33 While many patients expressed that NRT was helpful to them, some participants, particularly those who used NRT in addition to continuing smoking, reported problems with the use of NRT.16 These problems included worsening of a preexisting blood condition or dramatically deteriorated mood which led to the patch being contraindicated.16

She [her psychiatrist] said that it was contraindicated with one of my medications and so she told me that she highly recommended that I not take it. … I felt quite disappointed because I wanted to do it.16 (p.45)

Digital interventions, particularly mobile apps, were perceived as appealing and helpful, because they provide support, motivational content, and relevant information.81 82 89 Accessibility and usability aspects, for example, an accurate description of the app, text heaviness in the app, use of abstract symbols, and too subtle directions,90 require particular attention. According to one study, acceptability of app-based coaching was higher for persons with psychotic disorders than for those with PTSD.81

Discussion

This review synthesised the values and preferences of patients with SMI regarding smoking cessation interventions. In summary, patients with SMI preferred tailored interventions that address individual barriers and needs, that leverage social support networks, and that incorporate both behavioural and pharmacological strategies for smoking cessation. Included studies were mostly of moderate to high quality.

Flexibility emerged as an important component, with gradual reduction being seen as a more achievable and less daunting approach than abrupt cessation for some patients. Ambivalence was noted towards smoking bans, which in some cases inadvertently prompted cessation, while in others, patients sought alternative means to continue smoking. This ambivalence highlighted the need for supportive rather than punitive policies in clinical settings.

The findings of this review align with previous studies emphasising the need for personalised, flexible smoking cessation interventions. Complementing pharmacological treatments with behavioural therapies has been highlighted as particularly effective in this population.91 92 This reinforces the importance of a dual approach to not only address the physiological aspects of nicotine dependence but also provide the required psychological support, which includes coping with distress and installing social support mechanisms.31 36 37 58 93 Interestingly, in the accompanying network meta-analysis,22 effects of combination interventions were highly variable. There is some support for interventions combining non-pharmacological with pharmacological strategies tailored to the individual (low certainty of effects) while for other combinations the certainty of evidence was very low.22

The ambivalence towards smoking restrictions observed in this review is also consistent with prior research. While some patients viewed restrictions as an opportunity to quit, others developed workarounds to continue smoking.94 95 This variability underscores the importance of patient-centred care and supportive interventions that promote smoking reduction without stigmatising patients or imposing punitive measures. Studies already demonstrated that counselling and motivational enhancement therapies can lead to higher cessation success rates when tailored to individuals with SMI,96 97 with economic factors serving as a powerful motivator.3134 41,43 48 58 Due to the variability within the group of people with SMI, interventions should be tailored to individual preferences and needs, considering previous experience with smoking cessation. Although we did not perform subgroup analysis, some distinct motivational patterns were observed. Patients with schizophrenia appeared less motivated by health concerns and immediate rewards such as financial savings, compared with other SMI groups. For patients with PTSD, app-based coaching appeared to be less acceptable.

In other groups, digital interventions, particularly mobile apps, were generally accepted and viewed as beneficial because of their accessibility and motivational content. Previous research has advocated for user-centred design in digital health interventions, emphasising the need to accommodate the unique challenges faced by this population.98 99

Furthermore, the findings indicate that smoking serves as a complex coping mechanism for many individuals with SMI, helping them manage negative emotions and providing comfort and stability in their daily lives. Despite their awareness of health risks, many patients continue to smoke due to perceived benefits, such as stress relief and social connection.100 101 This insight underscores the necessity of addressing the underlying reasons for smoking and the role it plays in patients’ lives when designing cessation programmes. Smoking seems to fulfil psychological and social functions that cessation interventions must explicitly address. Interventions that fail to acknowledge this role might result in low engagement, particularly when withdrawal symptoms are misattributed to worsening of the psychiatric condition. Understanding these dynamics can lead to more effective interventions that resonate with the lived experiences of individuals with SMI, ultimately improving cessation outcomes.102

The strengths of this review include a comprehensive synthesis of diverse study designs, providing rich insights into patient preferences for smoking cessation interventions among individuals with SMI. The application of the best-fit framework synthesis enabled systematic integration of evidence across multiple sources, strengthening the validity of the findings. Additionally, the careful attention to multiple publication bias through the exclusion of duplicate reports enhances the quality of the synthesis.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The exclusion of studies involving certain comorbid conditions may limit the generalisability of the findings to broader patient populations. The synthesis relies heavily on patient-reported outcomes, introducing potential reporting bias due to varying levels of participant insight and motivation. Also, there was no separate synthesis of preferences of current or past smokers nor of patients who had participated in a smoking cessation intervention and those who had not. Study quality varied across included papers, and sample heterogeneity posed challenges for data synthesis. Finally, we did not prioritise a formal GRADE Cerqual assessment because we used a theory-informed framework synthesis, that is, best-fit framework synthesis, and integrated qualitative with quantitative data in our synthesis. Although such an assessment could provide insight into the confidence of our findings, it would unlikely change the overall value of our work for supporting the guideline development alongside complementary effectiveness and safety reviews.

The findings from this review have important implications for the design and implementation of smoking cessation programmes for individuals with SMI. Successful interventions will balance standardised evidence-based practices with individualised support that accounts for varying motivations, cognitive capabilities and social contexts. For clinicians, this means adopting adaptable treatment strategies that can accommodate gradual reduction and incorporate both individual and group-based support mechanisms. For policymakers, this means prioritising the development of accessible, integrated cessation programmes that leverage both in-person and digital delivery methods, while ensuring adequate funding for peer support initiatives and academic detailing. Several questions remain unanswered and warrant further investigation: the effectiveness of different motivational strategies across specific SMI diagnoses, the role of e-cigarettes for smoking reduction, the impact of interventions in green spaces within urban environments, the long-term sustainability of specific cessation approaches, and the real-world effectiveness of digital interventions. Future research should also explore how to optimise interventions in residential and ambulatory settings for this population, and better understand the variability in individual responses to different cessation strategies, particularly in relation to health status and economic motivators.

Conclusions

This review highlights patients’ preference for patient-centred and holistic approaches to smoking cessation in individuals with SMI. Interventions facilitating cessation can combine pharmacological strategies tailored to individual needs and behavioural strategies, emphasising social support networks and learning new coping strategies. Flexibility, accessibility and ongoing support emerged as important components for addressing stress, withdrawal symptom interpretation and relapse vulnerability in this population.

Addressing the multifaceted challenges associated with smoking in this population can improve cessation outcomes and help reduce health disparities. Future research should focus on identifying the most effective motivational strategies across different SMI subgroups and exploring the potential of digital tools as part of comprehensive cessation support.
