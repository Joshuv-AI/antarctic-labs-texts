Prevalence of insomnia and associated factors among postpartum mothers in Mattu City, Southwest Ethiopia: a community-based study
Authors: Solomon Seyife Alemu, Lema Fikadu Wedajo, Wubishet Gezimu, Biftu Geda, Mohammedamin Hajure Jarso
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsomnia during the postpartum period has short-term and long-lasting effects on the health of the mother and her baby. Hence, this study assessed the prevalence of insomnia and its associated factors among postpartum mothers in Mattu City.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among randomly selected 451 postpartum mothers in Mattu City from May 1 to June 1, 2022. A structured questionnaire was used to conduct a face-to-face interview. Athens’s Insomnia Scale (AIS) standardized tool was used to measure insomnia. EpiData version 4.4.2.1 and Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 26 were used for data entry and analysis, respectively. Factors significantly affecting insomnia were identified using a binary logistic regression analysis at a p-value of less than 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of insomnia was found to be 29.3% (95% CI: 25, 33.5). Parity (AOR = 3.36; 95% CI: 1.88, 5.60), unplanned pregnancy (AOR = 6.15; 95% CI: 5.23, 11.55), lack of postnatal follow-up (AOR = 3.82; 95% CI: 1.85, 7.90), partner dissatisfaction with the gender of the current baby (AOR = 5.73; 95% CI: 3.23, 10.2), partner daily alcohol use (AOR = 3.12; 95% CI: 1.66), and postpartum intimate partner violence (AOR = 2.60; 95% 4.67) were found to be factors significantly associated with insomnia.ConclusionsGenerally, three in ten mothers were suffering from insomnia in the study area. Therefore, health professionals and health extension workers need to endorse and enhance the counseling of the postpartum mother and her partner jointly. In addition, the media and other concerned bodies should focus on the identified factors to overcome them.

Background

Sleep is an important regulatory psychophysiological behavior in life, influencing mood, emotion, and impulse behaviors, which are key mediators of stress adjustments so commonly needed in the perinatal period [1]. Insomnia is a feeling of insufficient or non-restorative sleep despite an adequate opportunity to sleep. It is the most common sleep problem that varies in severity and duration and is diagnosed based on self-reported complaints of poor sleep quality concomitant with daytime disturbances [2, 3]. Insomnia is an indicator of common maternal mental illnesses like depression, anxiety, alcoholism, and self-harm [4]. It is commonly associated with comorbid medical and psychiatric conditions and exposure to drugs or other substances [5].

The postpartum period is a stressful time for the mother, as she carries extra duties and responsibilities to attain the maternity role and to care for the whole family [6]. In this period, mothers are more likely to experience mood disturbances due to physiological, psychological, and social changes, which can further affect the quality of sleep [7, 8]. In addition, sleep habits influence the mental health of the mother, the baby, and the whole family [9].

It is typical for women to experience difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep after giving birth [10]. Most women report that their sleep habits changed during the postpartum period; for instance, they took daytime naps, got less sleep at night, and became more worn out [11]. Insomnia can lead to maternal mental health problems that impair their quality of life, specifically during the postpartum period [12].

The global magnitude of insomnia among postpartum mothers ranges from 20.5 to 67.2% [13]. A systematic review conducted in twelve African countries found a 28% pooled prevalence of insomnia among postpartum mothers [14]. The magnitude in Ethiopia ranges from 21.8 to 24% [15, 16].

Insomnia causes various mental health (CMH) problems in the mother, including postpartum depression, postpartum psychosis, anxiety, and suicidal ideation [17–19]. It also affects the growth, mentality, and cognitive development of the infant [20–22] due to inadequate care and decreased mother-infant bonding [23–25]. Furthermore, it also affects the breastfeeding and immunization status of the infant [26, 27]. Given the significant effects of insomnia on health, well-being, and productivity, the impact of insomnia has economic consequences in both high- and low-income countries [28, 29].

Maternal age and parity [30], family size, known medical problems, maternal educational status, and occupation are significantly associated factors with insomnia [15]. In addition, marital status, social support, and depression were significantly associated with insomnia during the postpartum period [16].

Even though postpartum insomnia has a significant multifaceted effect on the health of the mother, infant, and the whole family, the distribution of insomnia and its attributes among this risky population has not been identified in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of insomnia and associated factors among postpartum mothers in Mattu City, southwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Study area, study design, and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was carried out from May 1 to June 1, 2022, in Mattu City. Mattu City is one of the city administrations of the Oromia Regional State. It is located about 600 km southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The city has six kebele (lower administrative units), including Abaa Mole, Gaddisa Odaa, Soor, Abbaa Saayaa, Taboo, and Qollo Korma. A total of 102,074 people were indwelling the city in 2021. Of which 51,217 and 50,857 were women and men, respectively. The total number of deliveries in 2021 was 2,840 [31].

Population and eligibility criteria

All selected postpartum mothers in the first year after childbirth and the permanent residents of Mattu City (who lived at least 6 months) were included in the study. However, those mothers who were critically ill during the data collection period were excluded from the study.

As a result, a total sample size of 451 was utilized.

A simple random sampling technique was used to select the study populations. Initially, we found the total number of postpartum mothers who gave birth in the last twelve months, 1850, from the Mattu City Administration Health Department as per the 2021 annual delivery report. Finally, the participants were accessed using their house codes obtained from the logbook.

Data collection tools and procedure

The data were collected through a face-to-face interview using a structured questionnaire. Three bachelor’s degree-holder midwifery professionals and two public health professionals collected and supervised the data collection process, respectively.

The tool contains socio-demographic characteristics, personal and social-related traits, obstetric characteristics, postpartum depression assessment questions, and intimate partner violence assessment questions. The standardized Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) was used to measure insomnia [32]. A validated Edinburg postnatal depression tool that has a minimum score of zero and a maximum score of thirty was used to assess postpartum depression [33]. An intimate partner’s violence (IPV) was assessed using items containing thirteen questions [34, 35]. A validated maternity social support scale that has six questions with a minimum and maximum score of one and five was used to assess maternal social support. The tool has a minimum score of six and a maximum score of thirty [36]. The tool containing 38 items that are classified as poor, medium, and rich was used to assess the household wealth index [37]. The reliability scores of AIS, Edinburg postnatal depression tool, intimate partner violence, maternal social support, and household wealth index were Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96, 0.87, 0.93, 0.89, and 0.95, respectively.

Data quality control measures

The questionnaire was translated into Afan Oromo and retranslated back to English to check consistency. In addition, a pre-test was conducted among 5% of the sample size (23 postpartum mothers) in Bedele Town three weeks before the actual data collection. The internal consistency of the tool was assessed by the reliability test (Cronbach’s alpha). The three-day training was given to the data collectors and supervisors. The investigators and supervisors checked and reviewed the completeness of questionnaires and gave the necessary feedback to data collectors daily. Data was cleaned before data entry to assure data quality.

Study variables

Prevalence of insomnia was the outcome variable of this study, whereas the socio-demographic characteristics (age, marital status, occupation, religion, ethnicity, educational level, and household wealth index) of the participants, personal and social traits (drinking coffee, alcohol use, khat (the leaves of an Arabian bush used for stimulating purpose) chewing, and social support, partner satisfaction to gender of current baby, postpartum depression and postpartum intimate partner violence), obstetric characteristics (parity, status of pregnancy during current infant, ANC visit during index pregnancy, place of delivery, mode of delivery, history of infant loss, PNC, and complications after delivery).

Operational definitions

Prevalence of insomnia

Those participants who scored ≥ 6 using the Athens insomnia scale were considered to have insomnia and coded as “1,” and those who scored ≤ 5 were considered to have no insomnia and coded as “0” [32].

Postpartum depression

Postpartum mothers whose Edinburg postnatal depression score was ≥ 13 were considered to have postpartum depression, whereas those who had a score < 13 were considered not to have depression [38].

Postpartum intimate partner violence

The participants who were exposed to at least one type of intimate partner violence during their first year after childbirth were considered to be victims of intimate partner violence [34].

Maternal social support

It was assessed using the Maternity Social Support Scale (MSSS). Participants who had the MSSS score of < 18, 19–23, and 24–30 were considered to have lower social support, medium support, and higher social support, respectively [36].

Length of inter-delivery interval

A birth interval < 24 months before the prior pregnancy was considered a short inter-delivery interval, whereas those who gave birth ≥ 24 months were considered to have a normal inter-delivery interval [39].

Household Wealth Index (HHWI)

It was assessed with items developed from related literature on the ownership of selected assets and properties. Then principal component analysis was done and divided into three equal parts: poor, which was labeled as “1”, medium, labeled as “2”, and rich, labeled as “3” [40].

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were cleaned, coded, and entered into Epi-Data version 4.4.2.1 software and then exported to the statistical software for social sciences (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. Inconsistencies related to the data were checked and corrected during the data cleaning processes. The data normality test was checked to select the appropriate statistical summary measures. Descriptive statistical analyses were described using frequency tables, proportions, and pie charts. The factors associated with insomnia were tested using a binary logistic regression analysis. Initially, a bivariable analysis was conducted, and the variables with a p-value of < 0.25 were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Around 40% of the participants attended a primary school. More than one-third of the participants fell under a medium wealth index. Regarding occupation, more than one-fourth (27.2%) of the participants were housewives (Table 1).

Prevalence of insomnia among participants

The prevalence of insomnia among the participants was found to be 29.3% (95% CI: 25, 33.5).

Factors associated with insomnia among participants

According to the bivariable analysis, factors including the status of previous pregnancy, postnatal visit, social support, mothers’ coffee and Khat use, postpartum intimate partner violence, postpartum depression, partner alcohol use, and partner satisfaction with the gender of the current baby were shown as having an association with postpartum insomnia at 95% CI with a p-value < 0.25. However, six variables, including parity, partner index of pregnancy, postnatal visit, partner satisfaction with the gender of the current baby, partner alcohol use, and postpartum intimate partner violence, were found to be significantly associated with insomnia in multivariable logistic regression at a p-value < 0.05.

The odds of postpartum insomnia were more than threefold (AOR = 3.36; 95% CI: 1.88, 5.60) higher among primiparous mothers compared to multiparous mothers. In addition, the odds of insomnia among mothers with unplanned pregnancy were 6.15 times higher compared to their counterparts (AOR = 6.15; 95% CI: 5.23, 11.55), and the odds of insomnia among mothers who didn’t have a postnatal visit were 3.82 times higher than those who have a postnatal visit (AOR = 3.82; 95% CI: 1.85, 7.90).

The odds of insomnia among mothers whose partners use alcohol were 3.12 times more likely when compared to those whose partners never use alcohol (AOR = 3.12; 95% CI: 1.46–6.68). Moreover, mothers who suffer from postpartum intimate partner violence had a twofold (AOR = 2.60; 95% CI: 1.45, 4.67) probability of insomnia compared to their counterparts. The odds of insomnia among mothers whose partners were not satisfied with the gender of their current baby were 5.73 times more likely than their counterparts (AOR = 5.73; 95% CI: 3.23, 10.2) (Table 4).

Discussion

The study revealed that the prevalence of insomnia was 29.3%. Parity, the partner index of pregnancy, the postnatal visit, partner satisfaction with the gender of the current baby, partner alcohol use, and postpartum intimate partner violence were identified as significantly associated factors.

As identified, the prevalence of insomnia was 29.30%, which is in line with the study conducted in Northwest Ethiopia at 24% [15]. This might be due to similarities in socio-demographic characteristics. However, it was higher than the finding of the study conducted in Bahirdar, Ethiopia, by 21.8% [32]. The discrepancy between these studies might be due to differences in the study settings and sociocultural variations. Furthermore, the finding of our study is higher than the studies conducted in Israel (10%) [41], and Canada (16%) [42]. The difference might be due to differences in sample size, study setting, tools, and socioeconomic variations. On the other hand, the current proportion is lower than another study conducted among urban residents in Ethiopia [43]. This might be due to the difference in study participants; the former study was conducted among adult people. Furthermore, this finding is lower than the global studies conducted in Korea [44], Nepal [45], Taiwan [46], and Iran [47], with findings of 42.9%, 50.5%, 50.2%, 61.6%, and 35.4%, respectively. This might be due to the difference in socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants as well as the difference in the duration of the studies. In actual fact, sleep quality is affected by sleep hygiene quality, technology like electronic devices, and different lights [48]. The technology, working culture, postnatal care culture, and sleep hygiene quality vary from country to country as well as from place to place, which might account for the discrepancy between those studies conducted in Finland [49] and Norway [50].

In this study, parity was significantly associated with insomnia. The likelihood of postpartum insomnia was more than threefold higher among primiparous mothers compared to multiparous mothers. This finding was not supported by a study conducted among a similar population. However, a study conducted among pregnant women identified a significant association between parity and insomnia [30]. This might be because becoming a new mother increases psychological stress among primiparous women, who are more likely to be affected by insomnia compared to multiparous women. In addition, primiparous mothers might experience fear regarding the care of newborns, breastfeeding, and taking care of themselves and their family members compared with multiparous mothers, so they cannot tolerate stressful events that might lead to poor sleep quality.

In addition, pregnancy status during the current infant is a significantly associated factor with insomnia. Postpartum mothers whose previous pregnancy was unplanned had sixfold higher odds of insomnia compared to their counterparts. This finding is congruent with a study conducted in northwest Ethiopia [32]. This might be because women whose partners did not intend to have a child at the index of pregnancy might ignore their infant and wife, which might lead to maternal stress and a feeling of loneliness that leads to insomnia. In addition, an unintended pregnancy might bring economic stress as the result of an increase in family size without a plan, which might lead to insomnia.

Partners’ daily alcohol use was significantly associated with insomnia during the first year after childbirth. This is supported by the study conducted in Bahirdar, Ethiopia [32] and the USA [51]. This might be because daily alcohol users do not have time for their families and infants, which might increase maternal responsibilities in the household and affect sleep quality. In addition, such partners might disturb their families and infants after alcohol use due to the effects of alcohol that lead to maternal sleep disturbance.

Furthermore, a lack of postnatal follow-up was significantly associated with insomnia. It might be expected that during postnatal follow-up, mothers might get psychological support and health education regarding mental health from healthcare providers. In addition, during postnatal follow-up, they might be screened for possible risk factors for insomnia, which they will discuss with their care providers, and that might enable them to overcome the problem when compared with those who do not have a postnatal follow-up. Similarly, partner satisfaction with the gender of the current baby is a significant factor associated with insomnia. This might be because the partner who was not satisfied with the gender of their current infant might use traumatizing words to their infant and partner, which might affect them psychologically and lead to sleep disturbances, and such partners might not give care for their children, which might increase maternal workloads.

Lastly, this study points out that postpartum intimate partner violence is a significantly associated factor with insomnia. This finding is in line with the evidence from the studies conducted in Northwest Ethiopia [32], Tanzania [35], and Switzerland [52]. This might be because those mothers who suffered from this problem lost their husband’s care and became stressed about the problem they were suffering from, which might affect their sleep patterns.

Limitations of the study

The limitations of this study were that it did not assess the current mental disorders as they might affect the sleep mood of the mothers. To overcome this, data collectors initially assessed previous histories of mental problems. Since this study utilized a cross-sectional design, it could not show a cause-effect relationship between insomnia and the identified factors. In addition, the findings of this study could be affected by self-report bias.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this study, three in ten postpartum mothers suffer from insomnia during the first year after childbirth. Parity, postnatal full follow-up, partner satisfaction with the gender of the current infant, status of the current pregnancy, partner daily alcohol use, and postpartum intimate partner violence were significantly associated with factitious insomnia among postpartum mothers in this study setting. From the findings, we recommend health professionals and HEWs strengthen postpartum counseling in clinical settings and outreach communities after discharge. In addition, the media should promote the importance of postnatal follow-up for reproductive mothers and communities. Furthermore, health extension workers screen for postpartum intimate partner violence.
