Glycopyrronium Bromide versus Droperidol for Prophylaxis of Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting in Hysteroscopic Day Surgery: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Non-Inferiority Trial
Authors: Xiaoqian Wang, Nenghong Sun, Ming Xiao, Ziyuan Chen, Xiaodan Yang, Haijuan Ren, Fanceng Ji, Hongyi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the efficacy of glycopyrronium bromide in preventing postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) in patients undergoing gynecological hysteroscopic day surgery.Patients and MethodsThis study is a prospective, randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial. The non-inferiority margin was set at 15%. Patients scheduled for elective gynecological hysteroscopic day surgery under general anesthesia from November 2025 to February 2026 were selected. A total of 260 eligible patients were randomly assigned to receive glycopyrronium bromide (Group G) or droperidol (Group F). Patients in Group G received intravenous glycopyrronium bromide 0.2 mg, while those in Group F received intravenous droperidol 1 mg. Surgery was performed under general anesthesia with remimazolam combined with alfentanil. The primary outcome observed was the incidence of PONV within 24 hours after surgery in both groups.ResultsWithin 24 hours postoperatively, the incidence of nausea and vomiting in Group G was non-inferior to that in Group F (37.1% vs 34.1%, risk difference 3.0%; 95% confidence interval −9.0 to 14.7; upper limit < non-inferiority margin of 15%). In the PACU, the incidence of nausea and vomiting in Group G and Group F was 22.6% vs 17.9%, respectively (risk difference 4.7%; 95% confidence interval −5.4 to 14.6). Regarding perioperative adverse reactions, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of hypotension between the two groups(30.6% vs 39.8%, P=0.130). The incidence of bradycardia was higher in Group F than in Group G (7.3% vs 1.6%, P=0.030). Furthermore, the incidence of parched mouth was higher in Group G than in Group F (67.7% vs 15.4%, P=0.001).ConclusionThis study found that glycopyrronium bromide is non-inferior to droperidol in preventing PONV within 24 hours after gynecological hysteroscopic day surgery.Keywords: glycopyrronium bromide, droperidol, nausea, vomit

Introduction

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is one of the common perioperative complications. PONV can not only cause severe discomfort to patients and prolong the postoperative hospital stay but may also lead to complications such as dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and wound dehiscence.1 Moreover, patients undergoing gynecological hysteroscopic surgery are mostly young women, and the majority have no history of smoking, placing them at a higher risk for PONV.2 Therefore, effective prevention of PONV is crucial for ensuring the smooth operation of gynecological day surgery, optimizing the utilization of medical resources, and enhancing the patient experience.3,4

Among the medications used for the prevention and treatment of PONV, butyrophenones and anticholinergics are two classic classes of agents.5 Droperidol, a representative butyrophenone, is a potent dopamine D2 receptor antagonist and is regarded as one of the “gold standard” antiemetics due to its excellent efficacy.6,7 Glycopyrronium bromide is an anticholinergic agent that can selectively act on the gastrointestinal tract, with the effects of inhibiting gastric secretion and regulating gastrointestinal motility. Compared to other drugs in its class, its core advantage lies in its difficulty crossing the blood-brain barrier, resulting in fewer central nervous system side effects.8 Previous studies have shown that glycopyrronium bromide has a certain prophylactic effect on postoperative nausea and vomiting and can effectively reduce the incidence of perioperative nausea and vomiting.9 However, there are no relevant studies in gynecological hysteroscopic surgery, and there is still a lack of relevant research to validate the antiemetic effect of glycopyrronium bromide in different surgical populations. Based on the above background and drug characteristics, we propose the hypothesis that glycopyrronium bromide is non-inferior to droperidol in preventing PONV in patients undergoing gynecological day surgery.

This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of glycopyrronium bromide compared with droperidol in preventing PONV in patients undergoing gynecological day surgery.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrollment

This study received approval from Weifang People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: KYLL20251024-2) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (available at www.chictr.org.cn; Registration No. ChiCTR2500113357, dated November 27, 2025). The research was conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the CONSORT guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their participation.

This was a randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial. Participants were recruited from among individuals scheduled for elective gynecological hysteroscopic day surgery under general anesthesia during the period from November 2025 to February 2026. Patients aged 18–65 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II and a body mass index ranging from 18 to 28 kg/m2 were enrolled in this study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of glaucoma or intraocular pressure measurement >21 mmHg; (2) preoperative heart rate >100 beats per minute; (3) known allergy to any study medication; (4) presence of acute or chronic nausea and vomiting, or gastrointestinal motility disorders preoperatively; (5) mental or cognitive impairment; (6) patients with hepatic or renal dysfunction.

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the glycopyrronium bromide group (Group G) or the droperidol group (Group F) by an independent researcher using a random number table. The randomization list was sealed in opaque envelopes. On the day of surgery, a designated anesthesia nurse who was not involved in the subsequent study prepared the study drugs according to the randomization results. Both glycopyrronium bromide 0.2 mg and droperidol 1 mg were diluted into 5 mL syringes and labeled as “study drug” to ensure the attending anesthesiologists were blinded to group allocation. Furthermore, since the researchers responsible for follow-up were not involved in the intraoperative procedures and were unaware of the intraoperative drug administration, they also remained blinded during outcome assessments in the PACU and at the 24-hour postoperative follow-up.

Anesthesia Management

Prior to surgery, all patients underwent standard preoperative fasting. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was initiated, which included non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry (SpO2), electrocardiogram, and the bispectral index (BIS). Both groups received the same anesthetic regimen and underwent preoperative assessment using the APFEL risk factor scoring system.10 Before the induction of anesthesia, all patients received an intravenous injection of flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg and dexamethasone 5 mg. Group G was then administered glycopyrronium bromide 0.2 mg intravenously, while Group F received droperidol 1 mg intravenously.

Anesthesia induction: Remimazolam 0.25 mg/kg was administered intravenously over one minute. After loss of consciousness, patients received alfentanil 20 μg/kg and mivacurium 0.2 mg/kg. Following three minutes of assisted mask ventilation, a laryngeal mask airway was inserted, and mechanical ventilation was initiated. Anesthesia maintenance: Remimazolam at 1 mg/kg/h and alfentanil at 40 μg/kg/h were continuously infused intravenously via syringe pumps. Additional doses of the muscle relaxant mivacurium were administered as needed based on the surgical conditions. All anesthetic infusions were discontinued at the end of surgery, and no reversal agents were administered to any patient. Upon emergence, the laryngeal mask airway was removed, and patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for observation. Patients were observed in the PACU for at least 30 minutes and were discharged back to the ward once their modified Aldrete score reached ≥9. Discharge from the hospital occurred after meeting the criteria of the Post-Anesthesia Discharge Scoring System (PADSS). For patients who experienced vomiting in the hospital, ondansetron 4 mg was administered as rescue antiemetic therapy. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 24 hours post-surgery by trained evaluators via electronic questionnaire links sent through WeChat and telephone interviews.

Assessment of Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of PONV within 24 hours after surgery.

Secondary outcomes: (1) Incidence of PONV in the PACU; (2) Severity of PONV within 24 hours after surgery (classified according to WHO criteria as: Grade I, no nausea or vomiting; Grade II, mild nausea or abdominal discomfort but no vomiting; Grade III, significant nausea and vomiting but no expulsion of gastric contents; Grade IV, severe vomiting with expulsion of gastric contents); (3) Time to loss of consciousness (time from start of remimazolam infusion for anesthesia induction to loss of eyelash reflex), anesthesia time (time from start of anesthesia induction to cessation of anesthetic infusion), and recovery time (time from cessation of anesthetic infusion to opening eyes on verbal command); (4) Doses of anesthetics used for induction and maintenance of anesthesia; (5) Intraoperative hemodynamic changes (mean arterial pressure [MAP], heart rate [HR]), recorded at the following time points: after entering the operating room (T0), at the start of anesthesia (T1), before laryngeal mask airway (LMA) insertion (T2), after LMA insertion and fixation (T3), at the start of surgery (T4), 5 minutes after start of surgery (T5), 10 minutes after start of surgery (T6), 15 minutes after start of surgery (T7), at the end of surgery when anesthetics were discontinued (T8), and at the time of LMA removal (T9); (6) The incidence of intraoperative hypotension and bradycardia in both groups (hypotension defined as mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg, bradycardia defined as heart rate < 50 bpm), and the incidence of parched mouth in the PACU.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome: the incidence of PONV within 24 hours after surgery. A pilot study found that the incidence of PONV within 24 hours post-surgery was 32% in the droperidol group and 30% in the glycopyrronium bromide group. Using the sample size calculation method for non-inferiority trials, with a non-inferiority margin of 15%,11 a power of 80%, and a one-sided α of 0.025, the required sample size was 120 patients per group, totaling 240 patients. Assuming a dropout rate of 5% to 10%, a total of 260 patients needed to be recruited, with 130 patients in each group.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of all measurement data. Data with a normal distribution, such as patient age and height, were presented as mean ± standard deviation and comparisons between groups were analyzed using the independent samples t-test. Continuous variables that were not normally distributed, such as anesthesia duration and time to loss of consciousness, were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1-Q3)] and analyzed using the rank-sum test. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and the results were presented as percentages. For continuous variables with repeated measurements, such as intraoperative blood pressure and heart rate, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of group, time, and their interaction. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA).

Ultimately, 260 patients were randomly assigned to Group G and Group F. In Group G, 6 patients were lost to follow-up, leaving 124 patients included in the analysis. The incidence of PONV in the PACU was 28/124 (22.6%) in Group G and 22/123 (17.9%) in Group F. The difference between the two groups met the non-inferiority criterion (Risk difference, 4.7%; 95% CI, −5.4% to 14.6%).

In the PACU, the incidence of postoperative nausea(PON) was 9.7% in Group G and 8.9% in Group F (P=0.843), and the incidence of postoperative vomiting(POV) was 12.9% in Group G and 8.9% in Group F (P=0.319). Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference in the severity of nausea and vomiting within 24 hours after surgery between the two groups (P=0.964) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study employed a non-inferiority design to compare the efficacy of glycopyrronium bromide and droperidol in preventing PONV among patients undergoing gynecological day surgery. The findings revealed that glycopyrronium bromide was non-inferior to droperidol in preventing PONV both within 24 hours after surgery and in the PACU. No significant difference was observed between the two groups in the severity of nausea and vomiting within 24 hours after surgery. Regarding perioperative hemodynamics, Group G demonstrated more stable intraoperative heart rate and a lower incidence of bradycardia. However, the incidence of postoperative parched mouth was significantly higher in Group G than in Group F. Additionally, although the incidence of hypotension was lower in Group G compared to Group F, the difference did not reach statistical significance.

All patients in this study were female, with Apfel scores of 2–3, classifying them as a moderate-to-high risk population for PONV. This study included patients who required hysteroscopic surgery. Cervical dilation during hysteroscopic surgery typically stimulates the vagus nerve.12 When the vagus nerve is stimulated, it can induce contraction of gastrointestinal smooth muscle, leading to nausea and even vomiting. In addition, remimazolam may be inferior to propofol in preventing nausea and vomiting.13 Therefore, all patients in this study were administered antiemetic interventions on the basis of dexamethasone. Among these, the antiemetic efficacy of droperidol has been well-established by numerous basic experiments and clinical studies.6 Our previous clinical study results showed that droperidol effectively reduces the incidence of PONV in patients undergoing gynecological day surgery, and its antiemetic effect is comparable to that of ondansetron.14 The dose of droperidol used in this study was 1 mg, which falls within the low-dose range, allowing for full antiemetic activity while minimizing the risk of adverse reactions. Throughout the study monitoring, no droperidol-related adverse reactions were observed in any patient.

Glycopyrronium bromide is a long-acting quaternary ammonium anticholinergic agent with potent and sustained peripheral anticholinergic effects. Its antimuscarinic potency is 5 to 6 times that of atropine, and its duration of action is 3 to 4 times longer. It offers advantages such as minimal central nervous system effects, a low incidence of tachyarrhythmias, and strong antisialagogue properties.15 It can be co-administered with reversal agents like neostigmine to antagonize residual neuromuscular blockade,16 playing an important role in multiple stages of perioperative management. Furthermore, glycopyrronium bromide selectively blocks M1 and M3 receptors in peripheral organs such as the gastrointestinal tract,17 inhibiting gastric acid secretion and excessive gastrointestinal motility and spasm. This reduces aberrant afferent signals from visceral nerves to the vomiting center, thereby contributing to the prevention of nausea and vomiting.18

Currently, there are few studies on the effect of glycopyrronium bromide on PONV in patients. Previous studies have mostly focused on patients under neuraxial anesthesia. Jain et al found that glycopyrronium bromide can prevent the occurrence of nausea and vomiting in cesarean section patients, and its effect is equivalent to 4 mg ondansetron.19 Ure et al also confirmed that glycopyrronium bromide can prevent nausea and vomiting in cesarean section patients.20 However, there is a lack of effective clinical studies on whether glycopyrronium bromide still has a PONV-preventing effect when used in patients under general anesthesia. Notably, a recent multicenter, prospective clinical study has provided new clinical evidence for the use of glycopyrronium bromide in the prevention of PONV in patients undergoing general anesthesia.9 The study found that intravenous injection of 0.2 mg glycopyrronium bromide can effectively reduce the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting in patients undergoing general anesthesia such as gynecological and gastrointestinal surgeries. In addition, Ma et al found that compared with intramuscular injection of 10 mg anisodamine hydrobromide, intravenous injection of 0.2 mg glycopyrronium bromide can effectively reduce the incidence of PONV within 24 hours after surgery.21 This study also verified that glycopyrronium bromide is non-inferior to droperidol in preventing PONV in patients undergoing gynecological day surgery. This new finding provides more sufficient evidence-based medical evidence for the application of glycopyrronium bromide in preventing nausea and vomiting during the perioperative period of general anesthesia. In the future, the different advantages of glycopyrronium bromide can be fully utilized to reduce the medical burden of patients and improve the quality of their postoperative recovery.

Glycopyrronium bromide has also gradually demonstrated certain advantages in perioperative hemodynamics. Previous research22 found that prophylactic intramuscular injection of glycopyrronium bromide effectively reduced the incidence of intraoperative hypotension in elderly patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia. In the present study, although the incidence of hypotension was lower in the glycopyrronium bromide group, the difference between the two groups did not reach statistical significance, which may be attributed to the mild circulatory inhibitory effect of the anesthetic agent used in this study—remimazolam.23 We also observed that heart rate was more stable in Group G during surgery, with a significantly lower incidence of bradycardia, which is consistent with the findings of Chamchad et al.24 The most common adverse effect of glycopyrronium bromide is transient parched mouth, which is an extension of its pharmacological action. This effect is typically mild rather than severe and requires no special treatment other than increased water intake for relief.

This study has several limitations. The administration of glycopyrronium bromide led to a certain degree of increase in heart rate, which may have potentially unblinded the attending anesthesiologists involved in the study. However, the follow-up assessors remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study. As this study was conducted in patients undergoing hysteroscopic day surgery who were discharged on the same day, further observation and investigation of central nervous system-related complications were not performed. Due to ethical considerations, a blank control group was not included in the study design. In addition, this was a single-center randomized controlled trial; therefore, further multicenter studies are needed to validate its generalizability and safety.

Conclusion

This study found that glycopyrronium bromide is non-inferior to droperidol in preventing postoperative nausea and vomiting within 24 hours after gynecologic hysteroscopic day surgery. This finding provides a reference basis for the use of glycopyrronium bromide in preventing postoperative nausea and vomiting in patients.
